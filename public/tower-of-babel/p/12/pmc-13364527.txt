Association Between Statin Initiation and Myasthenia Gravis Crisis
Authors: Woo Hyeon Yoo, Yunha Noh, Seung Woo Kim, Ha Young Shin, Ju-Young Shin, Hyesung Lee
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Background and PurposeStatins are widely prescribed for preventing cardiovascular disease, but concerns remain that they may trigger or exacerbate myasthenia gravis (MG). We evaluated whether statin initiation increases the risk of an MG crisis using a self-controlled case series (SCCS) design.MethodsThis SCCS study analyzed nationwide Korean claims data from 2007 to 2023. We included patients in whom statin therapy was initiated after an MG diagnosis and who experienced at least one MG crisis during a 730-day period spanning from 365 days before to 365 days after statin initiation. The primary outcome was an MG crisis during a 365 days follow-up. Incidence rate ratios (IRRs) with 95% confidence intervals (CIs) were estimated using conditional Poisson regression models.ResultsAmong 16,268 patients with MG in the claims database, 45 patients meeting the study inclusion criteria were included in the SCCS analysis. The risk of an MG crisis was higher during the first 30 days following statin initiation (IRR=3.60, 95% CI=1.50–8.64), but not during later periods (31–90 days: IRR=1.00, 95% CI=0.34–2.95; 91–180 days: IRR=0.87, 95% CI=0.34–2.23; 181–365 days: IRR=0.73, 95% CI=0.32–1.67). Subgroup analyses showed that the risk was higher in patients aged ≥65 years (IRR=10.31, 95% CI=2.59–41.05) and females (IRR=3.71, 95% CI=1.18–11.66). The findings were consistent across the sensitivity analyses.ConclusionsStatin initiation was associated with an increased risk of an MG crisis during the early postinitiation period, with no evidence of an elevated risk during longer-term use. Close monitoring is recommended during the early phase of statin therapy in patients with MG, particularly in the elderly, females, and users of lipophilic statins or concomitant beta blockers.

INTRODUCTION

Statins are widely used lipid-lowering agents that are essential for preventing cardiovascular disease. Although generally considered safe, statins induce muscle-related adverse effects in 10%–25% of patients.12 In 2023, pharmacovigilance agencies in Europe, the United Kingdom, Australia, Hong Kong, and Japan issued safety alerts regarding statin initiation potentially increasing the risk of onset or exacerbation of myasthenia gravis (MG), a rare autoimmune neuromuscular disorder characterized by fluctuating muscle weakness.34567 While many patients currently benefit from the available therapies for MG, up to 80% fail to achieve complete stable remission,89 and MG exacerbations can progress to a life-threatening MG crisis. This situation indicates the importance of identifying modifiable risk factors that can trigger symptom worsening.10 Although the biological mechanisms linking statin initiation and MG remain unclear, this potential association warrants further investigation.

The low prevalence of MG makes it challenging to establish drug–outcome associations in this population. Recent disproportionality and registry-based studies have identified that the risk of MG onset or exacerbation is increased with statin use.111213 Notably, a study from Hong Kong public hospitals found a 6.11-fold increase in the risk of new-onset MG within 1 month of statin initiation.11 However, disproportionality analyses can only identify potential relationships without establishing causal associations, and registry studies may lack generalizability by primarily including patients from specialized MG centers while underrepresenting milder cases in the community. The rarity of MG as well as the significant variations in MG diagnosis and treatment practices between countries have resulted in a critical need for further research in large, real-world populations and replication in different healthcare systems in order to better understand this association.14

This study aimed to determine the association between statin initiation and MG crisis among patients with MG using a self-controlled case series (SCCS) design. By leveraging within-person comparisons, an SCCS inherently controls for time-invariant confounders and reduces between-person variability, and so can achieve sufficient statistical power even for small samples. We additionally explored potential high-risk subgroups and investigated clinical factors that might influence the statin–MG association.

METHODS

Data source

We analyzed patient-level claims data obtained from the Health Insurance Review and Assessment Service (HIRA) in South Korea for the period from January 1, 2007 to February 28, 2023. The HIRA database covers the entire Korean population under the National Health Insurance System and includes all healthcare facilities. The database provides nationwide healthcare claims data that include detailed longitudinal information on diagnoses, procedures, and drug prescriptions.15

Study population

We included patients aged ≥20 years who were diagnosed with MG between January 1, 2007 and February 28, 2023, as defined by International Classification of Diseases (ICD)-10 code G70.0 (recorded in the primary diagnosis position) and the special exemption code V012. In the Korean National Health Insurance system, the V012 code is assigned to patients with neuromuscular disorders (ICD-10 codes G70–G71) to grant eligibility for expanded benefit coverage, and its requirement for specialist confirmation improves the diagnostic validity of MG case identification. We then included patients who received statin prescriptions during the study period (January 1, 2008 to February 28, 2022) and in whom statin therapy was initiated after the diagnosis of MG. We excluded patients who 1) died during the observation period (since MG crisis events could no longer be observed after death), 2) did not experience an MG crisis during the study period, 3) experienced an MG crisis during the washout period (365–730 days before the index date) to reduce potential bias from preceding events that might confound the association between statin initiation and MG crisis, or 4) experienced an MG crisis event on the same day as statin initiation. Following these inclusion and exclusion criteria, we identified patients in whom statin therapy was initiated for the first time after an MG diagnosis and who had a history of an MG crisis during the study period.

Study design

The SCCS design is a within-person comparison method that inherently controls for fixed individual-level confounders, since each patient serves as their own control.16 The index date was defined as the date of the first statin prescription, and the observation period extended from 365 days before to 365 days after the index date. We defined several distinct time windows during the observation period, with risk periods defined as 1–30, 31–90, 91–180, and 181–365 days after statin initiation, and the reference period defined as 1–365 days before the index date (Supplementary Fig. 1 in the online-only Data Supplement).

Statin exposure

Exposure to statins was identified based on prescription records from the HIRA claims data using national drug codes for simvastatin, atorvastatin, fluvastatin, rosuvastatin, lovastatin, pitavastatin, and pravastatin (Supplementary Table 1 in the online-only Data Supplement). These agents are categorized into lipophilic statins (simvastatin, atorvastatin, fluvastatin, lovastatin, and pitavastatin) and hydrophilic statins (rosuvastatin and pravastatin). Patients were considered to have been continuously exposed to statins from the first prescription date onward, regardless of treatment discontinuation or gaps in therapy.

Outcomes

The primary outcome was an MG crisis, defined as a severe exacerbation event with a primary diagnosis of MG (ICD-10 code G70.0) that required advanced respiratory support, including endotracheal intubation, mechanical ventilation, or noninvasive ventilation (continuous or bilevel positive airway pressure).17 These were identified using relevant diagnostic and procedure codes from the HIRA claims data (Supplementary Table 2 in the online-only Data Supplement). A repeat event was considered independent if it occurred at least 7 days after the end of the previous MG crisis episode, which ensured that repeat events met the independence assumption required for the SCCS analysis.18

Covariates

Age groups (in 10-year bins) and sex (male or female) were assessed at the time of the MG diagnosis. The duration of MG before statin initiation was calculated as the difference between the date of the first recorded MG event and the date of first statin prescription (the index date). Statin types were assessed through prescription records within 1 year after the index date and classified into lipophilic only, hydrophilic only, or both types. To account for the baseline MG status, patients were considered to have a history of immunosuppressant treatment if they met any of the following criteria during the 1-year period preceding the index date (first statin prescription): 1) an average dosage of systemic corticosteroids ≥20 mg/day (prednisone-equivalent) for at least two consecutive months; 2) at least one prescription of a nonsteroidal immunosuppressant (NS-IST), including azathioprine, cyclosporine, tacrolimus, mycophenolate mofetil, mycophenolate sodium, methotrexate, or cyclophosphamide; or 3) at least one administration of plasma exchange (PE) or intravenous immunoglobulin (IVIG).171920 Corticosteroid dosages were standardized based on prednisone-equivalent conversions (Supplementary Table 3 in the online-only Data Supplement). Concomitant use of medications—specifically beta blockers and nondihydropyridine calcium-channel blockers (non-DHP CCBs)—that are known to potentially exacerbate MG symptoms2122 was assessed based on prescription records within 1 year after the index date (Supplementary Table 1 in the online-only Data Supplement).

Statistical analyses

Baseline characteristics were summarized using descriptive statistics: categorical variables as frequencies and percentages, and continuous variables as medians and interquartile ranges (IQRs). We calculated the incidence rates of an MG crisis during the reference and risk periods by measuring the number of events and person-time at risk in each period. Using an SCCS design, we applied conditional Poisson regression models to estimate incidence rate ratios (IRRs) and their corresponding 95% confidence intervals (CIs). The models were adjusted for age (in 5-year bins), seasonality, and concomitant use of medications (beta blockers and non-DHP CCBs) as time-varying covariates.

To visualize the continuous temporal pattern of risk, we modeled the time relative to statin initiation using restricted cubic splines to allow for nonlinear trends in the risk of an MG crisis relative to the reference period of 1–365 days before the index date.

Subgroup analyses were conducted by age group (<65 years vs. ≥65 years), sex (male vs. female), statin type (hydrophilic vs. lipophilic), baseline MG status (with vs. without a history of immunosuppressant treatment), and concomitant use of beta blockers and non-DHP CCBs (users vs. nonusers). We conducted several sensitivity analyses to assess the robustness of our findings. First, we extended the grace period for defining independent MG crisis events from 7 to 30 days to evaluate the impact of this definition on our estimates. Second, we restricted the analysis to the first MG crisis event per patient to ensure that the results were not driven by patients with repeat events. Third, we performed a sensitivity analysis excluding patients with early statin discontinuation (defined as those with no subsequent prescriptions after the index date and a total prescription duration of <30 days) to minimize potential bias from reverse causality, where the occurrence of an event might lead to the immediate cessation of therapy.

Additionally, we examined healthcare visits before and after statin initiation to evaluate potential confounding by healthcare utilization bias, since a higher rate of healthcare utilization might influence both the likelihood of receiving statin prescriptions and the detection of MG crisis events.

All analyses were conducted using SAS Enterprise software (version 9.4.2, SAS Institute), with the cutoff for statistical significance set at 0.05. R software (version 4.4.2, R Foundation for Statistical Computing) was used for the graphical representations.

Standard protocol approvals, registrations, and patient consents

This study was approved by the Institutional Review Board of Sungkyunkwan University (approval number: 2024-11-011). The requirement for informed consent was waived by the ethics committee because this study involved retrospective analysis of de-identified claims data without direct patient contact.

RESULTS

Baseline characteristics

Among 16,268 patients with MG in the HIRA claims database, 45 patients meeting the study inclusion criteria were analyzed (Fig. 1). Most of the patients were female (60.0%) and aged 40–59 years (60.0%). The median MG duration before the index date was 5.3 years (IQR=2.1–7.0 years). Lipophilic statins were the most commonly prescribed type (64.6%). The median statin prescription duration was 308 days (IQR=106–340 days) during the 1-year follow-up period, and most patients (91.1%) continued taking statins thereafter (Supplementary Table 4 in the online-only Data Supplement). Prior to the index date, 80.0% had received immunosuppressant treatment: systemic corticosteroids at ≥20 mg/day (prednisone-equivalent) for ≥2 months (53.3%), NS-ISTs (64.4%), or at least one administration of PE or IVIG (44.4%). Regarding comedication within 1 year after index date, 26.7% used beta blockers and 6.7% used non-DHP CCBs. There were 52 MG crises during the 2-year study period, with a median of 1.0 event per patient (IQR=1.0–1.0) (Table 1).

Statins and incidence of an MG crisis

After adjustment for age, seasonality, and concomitant medication use (beta blockers and non-DHP CCBs), the risk of an MG crisis increased significantly during the first 30 days following statin initiation relative to the reference period (adjusted IRR=3.60, 95% CI=1.50–8.64). However, the risk rapidly returned to the baseline level thereafter, with adjusted IRR=1.00, 0.87, and 0.73 at days 31–90, 91–180, and 181–365, respectively (Table 2). The spline visualization in Fig. 2 illustrates this temporal pattern. Healthcare utilization patterns around the index date are shown in Supplementary Fig. 2 (in the online-only Data Supplement).

Subgroup and sensitivity analyses

Overall the subgroup analyses showed patterns similar to the main results, with elevated risks primarily observed during days 1–30. While most subgroups showed no significant associations during the later risk periods (days 31–365), the magnitude and statistical significance of the estimates varied between subgroups. During days 1–30, significant risk increases were observed in patients aged ≥65 years (IRR=10.31, 95% CI=2.59–41.05), females (IRR=3.71, 95% CI=1.18–11.66), users of lipophilic statins (IRR=3.36, 95% CI=1.08–10.42), and beta-blocker users (IRR=14.62, 95% CI=3.29–64.91), with no significant associations found in patients aged <65 years, males, or those with a history of immunosuppressant treatment. Estimates for hydrophilic statins, users of non-DHP CCBs, and patients without a history of immunosuppressant treatment were not available or estimable due to either insufficient sample sizes or a lack of events (Table 3).

The results from the sensitivity analyses were consistent with the main findings (Supplementary Table 5 in the online-only Data Supplement). When applying a 30-day grace period for MG crisis events, the IRR for days 1–30 was 3.92 (95% CI=1.63–9.40). Restricting the analysis to the first MG crisis event yielded an IRR of 3.95 (95% CI=1.66–9.44). In the analysis excluding early statin discontinuers, the point estimate indicated an increased risk (IRR=1.98, 95% CI=0.67–5.87). Although statistical significance was not reached, the wide CIs indicate limited precision in the estimates likely due to the insufficient sample size, the direction of the association remained consistent with the results from the primary analysis.

DISCUSSION

We evaluated the association between statin initiation and the risk of an MG crisis using an SCCS design based on nationwide Korean claims data. Our primary finding was a transient increase in the risk of an MG crisis during the early period following statin initiation (adjusted IRR=3.60), which subsequently returned to the baseline level during later periods. Subgroup analyses revealed that this transient risk increase was most pronounced among patients aged ≥65 years, females, and users of lipophilic statins. Notably, the concurrent use of beta blockers was associated with a markedly elevated risk that persisted beyond the initial phase. Sensitivity analyses showed the same pattern of transient risk increase and return to baseline, supporting the robustness of our main findings. These findings suggest that while statins may act as a short-term trigger for an MG crisis, the overall long-term risk appears to be limited.

Our findings are consistent with prior pharmacovigilance and registry-based studies that have found a higher risk of MG exacerbation following statin therapy, particularly during the early period after initiation. For example, a Hong Kong study identified a sixfold increase in the MG-related hospitalization risk during the first 30 days after statin initiation, which is consistent with our finding of a transiently increased risk. However, the risk observed in our study (adjusted IRR=3.60) was smaller than that found in the Hong Kong study, which is probably due to differences in outcome definitions and study populations. First, the Hong Kong study broadly defined MG exacerbation as any MG-related hospitalization in patients who had achieved 1-year remission, whereas our study strictly targeted cases of MG crisis that required mechanical ventilation or intubation. By focusing exclusively on this severe phenotype, our study provides a specific risk estimate for life-threatening respiratory complications. Second, the Hong Kong study restricted its analysis to patients in stable remission, whereas our study included a broader, real-world MG population without considering the remission status. Our approach minimizes potential selection bias and offers a more-conservative estimate that is highly generalizable to routine clinical practice, where statins are prescribed to patients with diverse disease activities.

The biological mechanism linking statin to MG remains unclear, but several mechanisms have been proposed.23 First, statins may impair mitochondrial function by inhibiting coenzyme Q10 synthesis and disrupting presynaptic transmission at the neuromuscular junction. The resulting energy deficit can critically reduce the neuromuscular safety margin, potentially triggering an MG crisis.24 Second, statins may exacerbate muscle weakness via direct myotoxicity, which could trigger MG deterioration even in the absence of immunological mechanisms.2425 Third, statins may upregulate proinflammatory cytokines and thereby promote antibody-mediated autoimmunity.2627 Our subgroup analyses provided further insights, although caution is required when interpreting the findings due to the sample smallness and wide CIs. We observed a higher risk in the elderly patients aged ≥65 years and in females, which may reflect age-related immunosenescence,28 and is consistent with previous studies.29 We also observed an increased risk among users of lipophilic statins, while risk estimates for hydrophilic statin users were not available due to the small number of cases. Although this pattern is biologically plausible given the higher tissue penetration capacity of lipophilic statins,2430 the insufficient sample size precluded meaningful comparisons between statin types, and so these findings should be considered exploratory. It was particularly interesting that no significant association was observed in patients with a history of immunosuppressant treatment (IRR=1.35). This implies that concurrent immunotherapy attenuates statin-induced immune activation and hence reduces the risk of a crisis. Regarding concomitant medications, we observed a higher risk with beta blockers, which is consistent with their known neuromuscular blocking effects. Beta blockers might exacerbate myasthenic symptoms via postsynaptic beta-adrenergic blockade or membrane-stabilizing activity, which further compromises the neuromuscular safety margin.21 Non-DHP CCBs are also thought to impair presynaptic acetylcholine release and exacerbate MG.2122 However, the small number of non-DHP CCB users in our cohort meant that we could not confirm this association.

This study had several strengths. First, to our knowledge, it is the first to utilize an SCCS design to specifically investigate cases of MG crisis that required intubation or mechanical ventilation. By focusing on life-threatening complications, we minimized outcome misclassifications and produced high-validity evidence relevant to critical care. Second, the SCCS design inherently controls for time-invariant confounding and is well suited for rare outcomes such as an MG crisis. Third, the availability of nationwide, longitudinal healthcare claims data covering both the public and private healthcare sectors facilitated the enrollment of a large and representative MG cohort and provided a better coverage of healthcare utilization. However, some limitations should also be noted. First, the applied adjustments might not have fully excluded residual time-varying confounding. Second, the reliance on claims-based data may introduce outcome misclassifications, although we attempted to minimize this risk by including the MG-specific rare-disease exemption code and using strictly defined outcomes. Third, limitations in data availability meant that we were unable to assess the statin dosage, which precluded investigations of potential dosage–response relationships. Fourth, the extreme rarity of MG crisis events resulted in zero occurring during the pre-exposure period (1–30 days before the index date) in the total study population. This prevented us from statistically assessing potential protopathic bias using the pre-exposure window, and caution is required when attempting to determine the temporal directionality of causality. Finally, variations in diagnostic pathways and healthcare coding practices for MG between countries may restrict the generalizability of our findings to other healthcare systems. Additional research using harmonized data across different national contexts is warranted to validate the risk patterns observed in this study.

In conclusion, we identified a significant increase in the risk of an MG crisis during the early period after statin initiation, suggesting that closer monitoring is warranted during this phase of statin therapy. These findings do not preclude statin initiation in patients with MG, but rather support individualized decision-making that carefully weighs cardiovascular benefits against short-term neuromuscular risks, with particular attention paid to high-risk populations such as elderly patients, females, and those comedicated with beta blockers.
