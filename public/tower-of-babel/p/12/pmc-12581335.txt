Predicting generalized anxiety disorder among Chinese depressed adolescents: an explainable machine learning approach
Authors: Shuang Geng, Jie Wang, Yulin Xia, Ben Niu, Xiuliang Deng, Xusheng Wu
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundThe co-occurrence of depression and anxiety in adolescents is associated with a greater risk than the presence of depressive symptoms alone. Predicting anxiety disorders among depressed adolescents is critical for interventions and therapeutic tools.MethodsWe recruited 2316 depressed adolescents through the Chinese Adolescent Depression Cohort (CADC) and collected 34 predictive factors for model construction. The Light Gradient Boosting Machine (LightGBM) prediction model and Shapley Additive Explanations (SHAP) algorithm were implemented for in-depth interpretation of the predictive importance of different factors. Furthermore, chi-square automatic interaction detection (CHAID) and ordinal logistic regression were used to explore the factor interactions and validate the importance of the SHAP value-based factors, respectively.ResultsNine key risk factors were identified. In addition to depressive severity, rumination, perceived stress, sleep quality, alexithymia, peer victimization, academic stress level, emotion-focused coping, and parental overprotect were recognized as key risk factors for the onset of anxiety. Resilience was recognized as a protective factor. Interaction analysis captured critical interactions between depression and six other risk factors in relation to different levels of anxiety risks. Interactions between the protective effect of resilience and four risk factors were also analyzed. High-risk subgroups and low-risk groups for different levels of anxiety were identified through the CHAID decision tree. The high-risk subgroups for severe anxiety include (1) adolescents with severe depression symptoms, (2) with moderate depression symptoms and high rumination, and (3) with severe depression symptoms and high alexithymia. The low-risk subgroups are (1) adolescents with low depression and rumination, (2) with low depression, low alexithymia, and more parental care, (3) with low depression, moderate rumination, and moderate academic stress.ConclusionsUtilizing an explainable machine learning approach enables us to identify the risk and protective factors for anxiety disorders among depressed adolescents. The SHAP analysis results suggest that depression severity was the most important predictor for co-morbid anxiety. CHAID decision tree further identified risk subgroups. These findings suggest that clinical workers take into consideration the above risk and protective factors as well as their interactions to develop appropriate therapies for the prevention of comorbid anxiety with depression.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12911-025-03225-y.Keywords: Anxiety disorder, Predictive modeling, Explainable machine learning, Factor importance, Factor interaction

Introduction

The mental health of adolescents has long been a focus of public attention. Adolescents who are affected by physical, psychological, interpersonal, and self-awareness changes are prone to high levels of loneliness, victimhood, and adverse emotional states such as depression and anxiety [1]. According to statistics, 14% of adolescents aged 10–19 worldwide suffer from mental health problems, with depression and anxiety rates of 8.2% and 3.9%, respectively [2], which indicates the critical situation of mental health problems.

Notably, depressive symptoms often manifest with co-occurring problems, such as anxiety. Studies have shown that the proportion of people with anxiety is between 15% and 75% among depressed adolescents [3]. Compared to those who experience anxiety alone, adolescents who are depressed with anxiety tend to have worse interpersonal relationships, poorer social functioning, and lower academic achievement than their healthy peers [4–6]. Furthermore, without timely intervention, those who suffer from depression and generative anxiety disorder have a higher risk of affective disorders and substance use disorders, which may lead to increased alcohol use, fighting, dropping out of school, and even suicide among adolescents [7]. As a result, disease assessment and treatment are becoming more challenging for clinicians. This highlights the necessity of identifying risk and protective factors for comorbid depression and anxiety that can help clinicians conduct early assessments and preventive actions.

At present, most studies on the risk and protective factors of comorbid depression and anxiety have used regression analysis approaches. The known correlated factors include adolescent demographic and sociological factors [8–10], such as sex, obesity, and nicotine intake; psychological factors [11–13], such as rumination, alexithymia, and negative coping styles; and social and family environmental factors [14, 15], such as childhood trauma and poor parenting behaviours. Nevertheless, the potential interactions between factors and nonlinear relationships between these factors and comorbid depression and anxiety are worthy of further exploration, which requires the adoption of advanced machine learning techniques with both predictive and explanatory capabilities [16].

Over the last decade, machine learning methods, such as the prediction and classification of psychiatric disorders [17–21], have received widespread attention and have been applied in the field of psychiatry because of their ability to process multidimensional and multimodal patient data. Compared to traditional statistical methods, machine learning offers out-of-sample predictive performance through holdout samples, enabling it to better capture underlying patterns in the data and demonstrating strong learning and generalization capabilities. Additionally, it has the ability to detect nonlinear relationships between predictive features and the target variable [18]. Considering the superior performance of machine learning techniques in predicting risk factors associated with psychiatric disorders [18, 19], we fully utilize machine learning methods to predict the comorbidities of depression and anxiety. Considering that simply providing model prediction accuracy is not sufficient for enabling the application of models in real-world scenarios [22, 23], we aim to address the “black box” nature of machine learning techniques and provide vivid examples of the nonlinear relationships between factors.

Therefore, we construct an interpretable machine learning model that employs the SHAP framework to identify the main factors influencing different levels of anxiety in depressed adolescents. The SHAP analysis quantifies the importance of each predictive feature and illustrates the interactions between the most relevant variables. We then performed a CHAID decision tree analysis to hierarchically interpret and visualize key feature combinations in different patient populations. This helps to identify the combinatory effects of high-risk and low-risk factors. After that, the study compares traditional ordered logistic regression models versus SHAP interpretation methods for influencing factor analysis. Multiple statistical analyses were designed to ensure a more robust interpretation of the role of anxiety risk predictors. We believe that exploring the predictive power of different risk factors and their combinations will provide both methodological and clinical insights into the prevention of depression and anxiety comorbidity.

Methods

Study design

This is a retrospective cohort analysis of Chinese adolescents with depression. The data were collected via the Chinese Adolescent Depression Cohort (CADC), a survey of depressed adolescents recruited to psychiatry outpatient clinics in 14 psychiatry hospitals across 9 provinces (Guangdong, Shanghai, Hubei, Zhejiang, Guangxi, Shandong, Jiangxi, Henan, and Anhui) in China from December 2020 to December 2021 [24, 25]. A three-stage sampling was used to select participants for this study. First, nine provinces in China were selected based on their economic status (three high, three medium, and three low); second, 14 psychiatric specialized hospitals in these nine provinces were selected (all of which were willing to participate in the survey and had professional research teams to conduct the study); and finally, outpatient and inpatient adolescents with depression were recruited through convenience sampling. The respondent screening process is as follows: when an adolescent was admitted to the psychiatric department of our partner hospital and he/she was diagnosed with depression by a psychiatrist, he/she would be included in the list of potential respondents of our study for the follow-up survey. All adolescent patients and guardians of individuals younger than the age of 16 were informed about the purpose and procedures of the study, and they voluntarily signed a consent form for joining the study.

The baseline inclusion criteria were as follows: (1) Patients who met the Diagnostic and Statistical Manual of Mental Disorders (DSM-5) criteria for depressive episode [26]; (2) aged 12–18 years; (3) had ≥ 6 years of education; and (4) had their families and other interested parties give their informed consent. The exclusion criteria were as follows: (1) had serious physical diseases, infectious diseases or immune system diseases; (2) had traumatic brain injury, epilepsy or other known severe neurological diseases or organic brain diseases; and (3) had a history of severe mental disorders such as schizophrenia or mental retardation. All participants underwent detailed assessments at baseline. This cohort survey collected demographic and self-reported clinical symptom, self-perception, and psychological information from 2,343 adolescents with depression at baseline.

According to Fig. 1, our research process comprises five stages: (1) data processing and preliminary statistical analysis, (2) predictive model construction, (3) model interpretation, (4) CHAID analysis for the combinatory effect, and (5) ordered logistic regression for a robustness check. Below, we provide the details of each stage.

Statistical Plan

Predictive variables and measurement

The outcome variable of this study was the level of anxiety experienced by depressed adolescents. A total of 34 predictive variables were selected through literature analysis and expert consultation, including the following: sociodemographic characteristics (i.e., age, sex, literacy), family environment (parent-child relationship, parental education level, etc.), lifestyle (smoking or drinking, mobile phone addiction), academic status, clinical symptoms (depression severity, sleep quality), and individual psychological traits (perceived stress, rumination, alexithymia, resilience, problem-focused coping, emotion-focused coping, reappraisal, suppression). Additional File 1 contains a complete list of all the variables used.

The Generalized Anxiety Disorder Scale (GAD-7) is used to evaluate the level of anxiety in depressed adolescents [27]. The scale comprises seven questions designed to measure the frequency of stress and worry experienced by patients during the past two weeks. The answers range from “not at all” (scored as 0) to “almost every day” (scored as 3), and the scores of the seven questions are summed and divided into four anxiety levels (0 to 4, minimal anxiety; 5 to 9, mild anxiety; 10 to 14, moderate anxiety; 15 to 21, severe anxiety). The Patient Health Questionnaire (PHQ-9) is used to evaluate depression severity, with higher scores reflecting more severe symptoms [28]. The Pittsburgh Sleep Quality Index (PSQI) is used to evaluate sleep quality in the most recent month [29]. The level of psychological stress is assessed using the Perceived Stress Scale (PSS-4) [30]. The assessment of resilience in response to stressful life events is conducted using the Connor-Davidson Resilience Scale (CD-RISC-10) [31]. The Toronto Alexithymia Scale (TAS) [32] was utilized to evaluate symptoms related to difficulties in emotional expression. The Ruminative Responses Scale (RRS) [33] is used to assess rumination, where higher scores indicate a stronger inclination toward ruminative thinking. The secondary school students’ coping style questionnaire is used to assess problem-focused coping and emotion-focused coping [34]. The Chinese Revision of the Emotion Regulation Questionnaire for Children and Adolescents (ERQ-CA-C) [35] was utilized to evaluate two types of emotion regulation strategies, namely, reappraisal and suppression. The Multidimensional Peer Victimization Scale (MPVS) [36] is used to evaluate peer victimization incidents. A parental bonding instrument is used to evaluate an individual’s perception of parenting styles during childhood, which includes two factors: care and overprotection [37].

Data processing and preliminary statistical analysis

Descriptive statistical analysis was conducted using SPSS 23.0. All categorical variables were recorded. After the variable coding process and the removal of missing variables, 2316 samples were ultimately retained. We, then, compared the 34 predictive variables for different anxiety level groups for preliminary data analysis. Noteworthily, the analysis results were not used for further feature selection in predictive model construction. For categorical variables, comparison was performed via chi-square test. For continuous variables, the Kruskal-Wallis test was used because the continuous variables were not under normal distribution according to the Shapiro-Wilk test. Moreover, Spearman analysis was also used to explore the relationship between scale variables and anxiety levels. All tests were two-sided at the significance level of α = 0.05. The results can be found in the section “Participant characteristics at baseline”.

Predictive model construction

We employ the Light Gradient Boosting Machine (LightGBM) to construct a predictive model and use the SHapley Additive exPlanations (SHAP) [38] to evaluate the importance of the predictors as well as their interactions. LightGBM implements two cutting-edge methods, exclusive feature bundling (EFB) and gradient-based one-sided sampling (GOSS), to enhance the optimization of ‌Gradient Boosting Decision Tree (GBDT) [39]. First, LightGBM has better performance in terms of running speed and accuracy than extreme gradient boosting (XGBoost) and other sorting-based decision tree algorithms [40]. Second, the algorithm directly supports the categorical feature input model.

To prevent model overfitting on the dataset and to ensure that the model has good generalizability, 80% (1852/2316) of the samples were randomly selected as the training set for model learning, and the remaining 20% of the samples (464/2316) were randomly selected as the test set for evaluating the model’s ability to generalize to unseen data. Then, the predictive model was trained using 5-fold cross-validation on the training set, with grid search employed to search for the optimal hyperparameters (parameters that achieve the highest AUC value). The model’s performance was subsequently evaluated on the test set.

The area under the curve (AUC) is an essential metric that quantifies the model’s ability to distinguish between patients and controls, with 1 representing ideal performance and 0.5 indicating random prediction. For the classification problem with four anxiety categories (none, mild, moderate, severe), we created six binary classification subproblems, in which we select samples from one of the four categories as positive samples and one of the left three categories as negative samples. The binary classification AUC is then computed using the true category and predicted category by the model. We took all the binary classification AUC values and averaged them to obtain the AUC value for the entire multi-categorization problem. During the model training process, the best AUC value obtained across the five iterations was 0.869, with an average AUC of 0.852. The AUC performance on the test data set was 0.847.

In the model training process, we fine-tuned parameters, such as learning_rate, num_leaves, and min_data, based on the official documentation of LightGBM to improve model performance. More details about parameter settings can be found at the official website [41]. In scenarios with a small data size, the leafwise growth of the model can introduce greater complexity and lead to potential overfitting. The maximum number of leaf nodes on each weak learner was set to 10. To speed up model convergence and increase the accuracy, the learning rate of the model was set to 0.05; the maximum number of iterations was set to 100. Moreover, the model is stopped early when the number of iterations is greater than 50 and the validation performance of the model is not improved, which ensures the convergence of the model. The final parameter settings are provided in Additional File 2.

Model interpretation

The SHAP method is a feature importance explanation technique based on cooperative game theory [38]. Compared to other feature importance measures that rely on the intrinsic characteristics of a model, SHAP offers additive consistency in its output, with the core idea of assigning a quantifiable marginal contribution (Shapley value) to each feature to assess its contribution to the model’s prediction. This aligns with the general concept of regression, where the model produces a predicted value for each sample. However, unlike traditional linear models, which measure a feature’s contribution based on the magnitude or sign of parameters, the SHAP method calculates the contribution of each feature through SHAP values, providing local explanations for individual data points. Additionally, by aggregating the contributions of all data points, SHAP can compute the global importance of each feature.

In this study, we adopted the TreeSHAP framework, which supports the tree integration algorithm. The TreeSHAP framework provides us with assistance in the following areas: (1) measuring the degree of influence of the predictor variables on the predicted outcome with the Shapley value; (2) distinguishing whether the predictor variables are risk factors or protective factors; (3) identifying the nonlinear relationship between the predictors and the predicted outcome; and (4) considering the interaction between the predictor variables.

All samples in the test dataset were considered in the interpretation steps. First, the Shapley contribution value of each feature was calculated for each sample in the test set, and the Shapley absolute value of each feature was averaged for feature importance ranking. Second, SHAP dependency plots were drawn. After a certain feature was selected, for each sample, the corresponding points were plotted on the axis, the feature values were plotted on the x-axis, and the corresponding Shapley contribution values were depicted on the y-axis. Dependency plots were generated to observe the relationships between the predictor variables and the predicted outcomes. Finally, the effect of variable interactions on the prediction was obtained by calculating the SHAP interaction values. The SHAP interpretation results over the entire dataset are also provided in Additional File 7 for comparison and validation.

CHAID analysis for combinatory effects

CHAID (Chi-square Automatic Interaction Detection) is a decision tree method primarily used for classification tasks. It recursively splits the dataset to identify optimal subgroups. Specifically, CHAID uses the chi-square test or ANOVA to optimally partition the data according to the type of variables and automatically determines the grouping of nodes by P value to generate a classification tree [42]. This method excels at identifying interactions between predictive factors and is used to classify nodes that have the greatest impact on the prediction model. This, helps to understand the influence of different feature combinations on predictions. Building upon the SHAP explanations, we leveraged CHAID decision trees to further segment high-risk and low-risk patient subgroups, offering more detailed explanations of the decision boundaries (ranges of feature values) for these groups.

We performed three binary classification tasks—no-anxiety and mild-anxiety, no-anxiety and moderate-anxiety, and no-anxiety and severe-anxiety—34 variables were included in the analysis. The classification criteria are as follows [43]. (1) The Pearson chi-square test was applied to divide nodes when p <. 05. (2) The pre-pruning method was used for tree pruning, and the decision tree’s maximum number of growth layers was restricted to 3. (3) The tree required a minimum of 50 samples for each parent node and 15 samples for each daughter node. If the number of samples in a node did not meet this criterion, the node was designated as a terminal node and would not undergo further splitting. Model fit evaluation was performed using tenfold cross-validation.

Ordered logistic regression for robustness checks

Ordinal logistic regression is suitable for analysing the relationship between independent variables and an ordered dependent variable. Based on the generalized linear model, ordinal logistic regression assumes that the dependent variable follows an ordinal distribution, and uses the logit function to model the cumulative probabilities for each category, outputting the effect of the independent variables on the dependent variable. We employ the ordinal logistic regression for robustness testing in the current study, which provides the regression coefficients reflecting the strength of the relationship between each independent variable and the dependent variable. The standardized coefficients reveal risk factors with varying degrees of influence. The significance level of the model was set at 5% (α = 0.05). The significance level of the model was set at 5% (α = 0.05). The significant coefficients obtained from this model are compared with SHAP values to analyse the differences in feature importance assessments between traditional linear models and SHAP-based interpretation methods.

Results

Participant characteristics at baseline

Among our sample participants, over 77% of the patients were girls, and the mean age was 20.44 years old. 85.3% (1975/2316) had overall anxiety symptoms, 19.8% (458/2316) had mild anxiety symptoms, 26.2% (606/2316) had moderate anxiety symptoms, and 39.3% (911/2316) had severe anxiety symptoms. The detailed descriptive characteristics are reported in Additional File 3. The Chi-square test preliminary analysis results suggest that no significant differences were found between groups concerning the discrete factors of residence site, childbirth, left-behind experience, BMI, education level of the father, education level of the mother, or parental marital status (P > .05). In contrast, significant differences existed between groups for each of the remaining discrete factors (P < .05). Spearman correlation analysis revealed that a total of 13 continuous variables were significantly associated with adolescent anxiety levels (P < .05), with depression severity, rumination, perceived stress, sleep quality, alexithymia, peer victimization, parental overprotection, emotional-focused coping, and suppression being positively correlated, and resilience, parental care, problem-focused coping, and reappraisal being negatively correlated. The Krusskal-Wallis test results also showed that there were significant differences (P < .05) in the mean scores between different anxiety groups. We found that the mean depression score of adolescents in the severe anxiety group (2.47 ± 0.45) was higher than that of the moderate anxiety group (1.96 ± 0.49, P < .05) and the mild anxiety group (1.46 ± 0.53, P < .05). The mean rumination score in the severe anxiety group (3.23 ± 0.52) was higher than that in the moderate anxiety group (2.85 ± 0.48, P < .05) and the mild anxiety group (2.57 ± 0.50, P < .05).

Higher levels of depression increase the probability of adolescents with low levels of alexithymia (< 3.5) being predicted as experiencing severe anxiety. For adolescents with high levels of alexithymia (> 3.5) and relatively low levels of depression, the SHAP analysis suggests that elevated alexithymia plays a greater role in predicting severe anxiety. For adolescents with lower levels of rumination (< 2.5), an increase in depression did not necessarily lead to a prediction of severe anxiety. However, adolescents with higher levels of rumination (> 2.5) and increased levels of depression are more likely to be predicted as experiencing severe anxiety. A higher level of reappraisal reduces the likelihood of patients being predicted as severely anxious. For adolescents with high levels of depression, their risk probability of severe anxiety became smaller as their reappraisal level increased. The protective effect of reappraisal becomes stronger among adolescents with low levels of depression. In the prediction of moderate anxiety risk, the adoption of emotional-focused coping styles increased the risk probability of moderate anxiety, and this effect was greater for adolescents with low levels of depression. Adolescents with moderate levels of rumination (between 2.5 and 3.5) and higher levels of depression are more likely to be predicted as experiencing moderate anxiety. Adolescents with lower levels of depression generally reported lower perceived stress, which contributed less to the prediction of anxiety risk. However, those with higher perceived stress were more likely to experience severe depression and be predicted as having moderate anxiety. In the prediction of the mild anxiety risk, adolescents with lower levels of alexithymia and depression were at lower risk probability of anxiety. When the levels of alexithymia and depression increase together, SHAP values increase and the risk probability of anxiety raise significantly. After the SHAP reached its peak, it then decreased as the levels of alexithymia and depression continued increasing. It confirms the results that high levels of alexithymia and depression contributed more to the prediction of moderate and severe anxiety. For adolescents employing less suppression strategies (< 3), higher levels of depression increase the risk probability of mild anxiety. The effect of depression level became weaker among adolescents employing more suppression strategies (> 3) who are at higher risk of mild anxiety.

Across different levels of anxiety, we can observe clear interactions between rumination and depression. The SHAP peak values shifted gradually rightwards from the mild anxiety group to the severe anxiety group, suggesting the increase of anxiety severity as the levels of rumination and depression increase together.

As shown in Fig. 5, adolescents with lower levels of resilience are more likely to be predicted as having depression and severe anxiety. A high value for resilience contributed to a reduction in the predicted risk for both severe depression and severe anxiety. Similar patterns were observed in the interactions between resilience and perceived stress in different anxiety groups. Adolescents with higher resilience tend to perceive less stress and they have a reduced predicted risk for anxiety. Regarding the interaction between resilience and problem-focused coping, the increasing adoption of problem-focused coping played a more important role in predicting mild anxiety risk. However, resilience played a more important role in predicting moderate and severe anxiety risk. Adolescents with high levels of resilience had lower risk probability of moderate and severe anxiety risk, although they prefer problem-focused coping strategies. Similar to problem-focused coping, the reappraisal had more predictive power in the mild anxiety group and less predictive power in the moderate and severe anxiety groups. Adolescents with high levels of resilience and reappraisal are less likely to be predicted as having moderate and severe anxiety.

CHAID analysis also revealed the interaction effects between certain predictive factors, which confirm the findings in SHAP analyses. For example, in the prediction of severe anxiety, the role of alexithymia in subgroups with high (score range: [2.000, 2.556]) and low (score range: <0.889) levels of depression is disparate. A total of 97.6% of the individuals in the high depression level group suffered from severe anxiety, and individuals with alexithymia had an increased probability of severe anxiety, while the range of change was small. 2% of the participants in the low depression level group suffered from severe anxiety. However, in this group, individuals with alexithymia had an increased risk probability from 2% to 17.6% for severe anxiety. Therefore, adolescents with low levels of depression were more likely to be affected by alexithymia than were those with high levels of depression.

In the prediction of moderate anxiety, the subgroup with high levels of depression (score > 1.556) had relatively high levels of perceived stress (score > 2.750) and a 96.1% probability of moderate anxiety, whereas the subgroup with low levels of depression (score range: 0.556, 1.000) had relatively low levels of perceived stress (score < 3.000) and the probability of moderate anxiety decreased to 3.8%. In the subgroup with moderate levels of depression (score range: 1.000, 1.556), the subgroup with higher levels of rumination had a higher probability of moderate anxiety compared to the subgroup with lower levels of rumination (score < 1.955).

Discussion

Principal findings

Based on the large Chinese Adolescent Depression Cohort (CADC), this study employed the LightGBM machine learning model and the SHAP interpretation method to predict and explain the influencing factors of anxiety in depressed adolescents, and used CHAID analysis to explore the combined effects of different influencing factors to provide tailored support for clinical interventions. The main findings of this study are as follows: (a) Depression severity, rumination, perceived stress, sleep quality, alexithymia, peer victimization, academic stress level, emotion-focused coping, and parental overprotection are nine key risk factors for comorbid anxiety. Resilience is an important protective factor for comorbid anxiety. (b) The SHAP interaction plots capture critical interactions between depression severity and six risk factors (alexithymia, rumination, suppression, emotional-focused coping, reappraisal, and perceived stress.) in relation to different anxiety risks. The interactions between the protective effect of resilience and four risk factors (depression severity, perceived stress, problem focused coping, and reappraisal) are also discussed. (c) The CHAID decision tree identified high-risk subgroups and low-risk subgroups associated with different levels of anxiety. In addition, the robustness check shows that the SHAP values of the predictive factors are generally consistent with those of traditional ordinal logistic regression analysis. SHAP values provide additional information about other potential risk factors and interactions between factors.

In our study, 85.3% (1975/2316) of depressed adolescents were co-morbidly anxious, wherein 19.8% (458/2316) of depressed adolescents suffered from mild anxiety, 26.2% (606/2316) of depressed adolescents suffered from moderate anxiety, and 39.3% (911/2316) of depressed adolescents had severe anxiety disorder. This proportion (85.3%) is higher than that reported in existing research. Previous studies show that 15.9% to 61.9% of depressed adolescents have both anxiety and depressive disorders [45], and 53% of patients with major depression exhibit anxiety symptoms [46]. One explanation is that depressed adolescents are prone to experiencing cognitive biases, negative thinking, difficulties with emotion regulation, and social and interpersonal influences, which further exacerbate their anxiety [47–49]. From the neurobiological perspective, comorbid depression and anxiety can be explained by several neurobiological components, including serotonin, dopamine, and norepinephrine. Functional abnormalities in these components could contribute to negative emotional temperaments, such as depression and anxiety in adolescents [50]. Sleep quality was found to be another clinical factor for anxiety prediction. Research has demonstrated that inadequate sleep and sleeping difficulty can substantially elevate the risk of depression and anxiety due to their detrimental effects on emotional regulation and stability [51]. According to the hyperarousal model of sleep, poor sleep quality mediates the relations between generalized anxiety disorder and major depressive disorder [52]. Sleeping problems were also considered to be the result of anxiety. Anxiety patients with high mental tension often experience poor sleep quality. Therefore, providing sleep coaching services or organizing sleep improvement programs can not only reduce the sleeping problem but also prevent anxiety.

Individual psychological characteristics are also important factors influencing their mental health status. Our findings suggest that rumination and perceived stress were key contributors to the prediction of co-morbid anxiety in depressed adolescents. This aligns with the results of related studies [53, 54]. Depressive rumination involves a maladaptive thinking process or cognitive vulnerability, which can worsen the depression and anxiety symptoms, including the duration and severity of symptoms [55]. This supports the findings in our study that the interaction between depression and rumination had a notable effect on the prediction probability of different levels of anxiety risk. As rumination and depression levels increased, adolescents gradually shifted from mild anxiety to severe anxiety. The correlation between perceived stress and negative mental health outcomes such as depression and anxiety in adolescents also aligns with findings in the existing literature [56]. Data from one study indicated that individuals who reported high perceived stress were six times more likely to develop mental disorders [57]. The cognitive vulnerability-stress paradigm suggests that psychosocial and environmental stressors can interact with cognitive factors, which further increase the risk of disease symptoms such as depression and anxiety [58]. Our results show that adolescents with higher depression levels were more likely to perceive higher stress, which contributes more to the prediction of anxiety risk. Several other studies have reported an association between perceived stress and rumination [59, 60]. The combined effects of perceived stress and rumination have an impact on adolescent mental health status. Specifically, rumination plays a significant role in amplifying perceptions of environmental pressures and daily stress, leading individuals to believe that they are incapable of coping effectively, thereby exacerbating anxiety and depression symptoms in adolescents [60]. These results suggest that clinical practitioners can adopt treatment strategies related to perceived stress and patients’ rumination patterns so that the risk of anxiety disorder can also be reduced among depressed adolescents.

Alexithymia was identified as another risk prediction factor related to adolescents’ psychological characteristics in this study. When a person’s emotion recognition is blocked, the adaptive emotion regulation process may be hindered, thus increasing vulnerability to mental health problems. People with depression and anxiety were found to theoretically have the characteristics of atypical alexithymia [61]. Our findings suggest that adolescents with low levels of depression are more susceptible to the impacts of alexithymia (scores around 3.5), thereby having a higher prediction risk of severe anxiety. The explanation found in existing research is that individuals only show significant alexithymia when the variable measure score reaches a specific threshold. Ertekin et al. (2015) reported that participants assessed as having alexithymia with a score of greater than 61 using the TAS-20 measure were more severely depressed and socially anxious [62]. As younger children or adolescents may have more difficulty in reflecting on and expressing their inner feelings, they may report higher scores of alexithymia than the general population [63]. Previous research showed that the occurrence of alexithymia decreases as adolescents develop and grow [64]. In the current study, adolescents are assessed as having alexithymia with a score greater than 70 according to the TAS scale [63, 64]. These patients exhibit obvious symptoms of alexithymia and are at an increased prediction risk of developing higher levels of anxiety. In addition, the interaction effect between depression and alexithymia aligns with existing studies [65]. Patients with high levels of depression generally exhibit alexithymia, while the low levels of depression may turn into high levels of depression and anxiety once the patients’ alexithymia reaches a certain threshold. These results suggest that clinicians need to focus on depressive adolescents’ alexithymia when intervening for comorbid anxiety risk and help them to better manage their emotions by improving their emotion recognition and expression skills.

Notably, our study also identified the interactions between adolescent depression and emotion regulation strategies in predicting the risk of different levels of anxiety. One interpretation is that adolescents with low levels of depression still have extra psychological resources to adopt emotion regulation strategies compared to those with severe depression and cognitive dysfunction. On the other hand, inappropriate emotion regulation strategies can significantly increase the risk of anxiety. Depressed adolescents tend to adopt emotion-focused coping styles such as patience, avoidance, fantasy denial, and emotional outbursts when faced with stressful events or emotional distress. Previous research showed that avoidance and fantasy may impair an individual’s ability to adapt to stressful events, thereby increasing the risk of anxiety and depression [66]. In contrast, increased use of problem-focused coping has been associated with alleviation of depressive symptoms in adolescents [67]. Reappraisal and suppression are two types of emotion regulation strategies commonly used by children and adolescents. Reappraisal is an emotion regulation strategy that involves reappraisal of the situation that evoked the emotion thereby changing the emotional experience, and suppression is the behaviour of adolescents that inhibits the emotional experience [68]. Our findings indicate that increased use of reappraisal strategies contributed to a lower prediction risk of severe anxiety, while higher use of suppression strategies increased the prediction risk of mild anxiety. Prior study also showed that depression and anxiety were strongly associated with inadequate utilization of reappraisal, and that individual anxiety showed an overreliance on suppression [69].

Moreover, adolescents with higher resilience tend to report lower levels of depression and stress, which in turn reduces the predicted risk of anxiety. Resilience enables individuals to actively cope with and adapt well after encountering adversity and stressful events. High resilience could effectively promote the recovery of adolescents’ mental health [70]. According to the compensatory and protective models of resilience, resilience counteracts the direct negative effects of risk factors such as stress [71], and the protective effects of resilience buffer against the negative outcomes of risk exposures and adversity [72]. Our findings also revealed a reinforced protective effect of resilience together with reappraisal and problem-focused coping. This result suggests that depressed adolescents can be helped in clinical interventions by building psychological resilience in the face of challenge and adversity, being encouraged to give positive cognitive appraisals, and being taught diverse problem-coping styles, such as seeking social support.

Our findings showed that peer victimization and academic stress are the two risk prediction factors that have the greatest impact on anxiety in the school environment. When adolescents suffer from bullying, they usually use cognitive styles such as rumination, disaster, and self-blame to cope with it. This passive lifestyle has been shown to be associated with higher levels of depression and negative mental health outcomes [73]. In addition, it is worth noting that Chinese students are facing great academic pressure [74]. Negative emotions (depression) can disrupt individuals’ original learning plans and reduce their interactions with teachers and classmates, which could increase the risk of anxiety and stress. A study showed that adolescents with depression during high school are more prone to experiencing comorbidity of anxiety and depression [75]. Parental overprotection was another risk factor that had the greatest impact on anxiety in the family environment. This is not unexpected. In the context of Chinese culture, family control can affect the occurrence of adolescent anxiety, especially for parents who believe that “achievement first” is an educational belief. Another possible reason is “intergenerational transmission”. Parental overprotective behaviour could be a mediator between parents’ anxiety and children’s adolescent anxiety. Finally, family factors such as parenting style and the parent-child relationship have a substantial impact on the transition of depressed adolescents from mild anxiety to moderate anxiety and subsequently to severe anxiety, which suggests that we can formulate targeted intervention measures.

The factor combinatory effects indicate that depression severity is the most critical predictor of risk for anxiety. The factor interactions revealed by CHAID analyses were generally consistent with those found in SHAP analyses. The decision trees effectively identify high-risk and low-risk subgroups associated with different levels of anxiety. The combination of high levels of depression, high levels of alexithymia, and a bad relationship with their mothers was associated with a substantially increased risk probability of severe anxiety. Moreover, the combination of higher levels of depression, higher perceived stress, and more peer bullying was associated with a 24.7-fold increased risk probability of moderate anxiety. These results highlight the necessity of conducting a comprehensive assessment of the risk factors instead of individual factors because of the existence of combinatory effects. The factor combinations in high-risk subgroups provide detailed references for clinicians in understanding patients’ risk of anxiety and developing tailored plans of treatment.

Limitations

Several limitations should be considered when interpreting these findings. First, we compared only the LightGBM prediction model and the feature importance ranking model. More machine learning models can be considered in the future together with the SHAP interpretation method. Second, all the scales employed in this study were self-report questionnaires. While we have provided standardized training to all study investigators, it is possible that some participants might not fully comprehend or misunderstand specific items. Third, it is important to note that this study adopted a cross-sectional design, which could constrain the interpretability of the causal analysis. Fourth, the sample size in this study was restricted, and we included only 34 variables in the models, which may have affected the predictive accuracy of the model. To enhance the predictive power of the models, future investigations should be conducted with larger sample sizes and encompass a wider variety of predictive factors. At last, neurobiological components such as serotonin, dopamine, and norepinephrine have been shown to be significantly associated with depressive comorbid anxiety. Due to limitations in research resources, we were unable to assess the nonlinear relationships and interactions of these factors, and it is promising for future studies to incorporate them into analyses in order to understand the complex interactive outcomes of risk factors from a neurobiological perspective.

Conclusions

In conclusion, our study demonstrated the effectiveness of interpretable machine learning methods for identifying anxiety-predicting factors in depressed adolescents and key risk factors. Their interactions were identified to compensate for the inadequate explanations of traditional regression methods. Practically, this study provides guiding implications for broadening the scope of interventions and treatments for anxiety in adolescents with depression.
