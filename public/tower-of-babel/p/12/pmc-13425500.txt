The Efficacy of Ultraviolet LED Cold Light Detoxification Technology in Removing Aflatoxin B1 from Peanut Oil and Its Application
Authors: Fan Qiao, Zihao Qi, Yu Xie, Huishuai Li, Liumin Wei, Huiyan Qin, Haibo Wang, Zhenning Li, Liming Huang, Feiyu Zhao, Binbin Yang, Dongjie Peng, Shaojun Li
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Peanut oil is prone to contamination with aflatoxin B1 (AFB1). Traditional ultraviolet (UV) irradiation methods demonstrate limited effectiveness in detoxification and may compromise the oil quality. To address this, this study developed a novel UV LED cold light source temperature control system, offering a new solution for AFB1 detoxification in peanut oil production. On the basis of this system, peanut oil samples spiked with varying concentrations of AFB1 were prepared and treated under different operational conditions. Then, the AFB1 content, acid value, and peroxide value of the decontaminated oils were quantitatively determined to identify the optimal UV wavelength, exposure duration, and intensity for AFB1 degradation. The system’s efficacy was also evaluated using oil pressed from mold-contaminated peanuts. In this study, the 365 nm UV LED cold light showed a higher degradation efficiency than its 254 nm. AFB1 concentrations in peanut oil progressively decreased with increasing irradiation duration and intensity, achieving over 30% enhancement. Similar effects were observed using peanut oil pressed from mold contaminated peanuts sourced from actual situation. Following UV LED cold light treatment, no significant alterations were noted in the oil’s acid value, peroxide value, indicating that the degradation process does not compromise lipid quality. In conclusion, the UV LED cold light system presents a superior, low-cost, and low-heat-loss solution for AFB1 detoxification in peanut oil, effectively overcoming the drawbacks of conventional thermal UV methods.

Introduction

Aflatoxins (AFs), classified as Group 1 human carcinogens by the International Agency for Research on Cancer (IARC), are furan-ring secondary metabolites produced by specific strains such as Aspergillus flavus and Aspergillus parasiticus. The double bond at the end of the furan ring directly determines its toxicity level. Among them, aflatoxin B1 (AFB1) exhibits the highest toxicity and widest distribution. Globally, AFB1 threatens human health through both acute poisoning and chronic exposure.AFB1 exhibits potent acute toxicity, and when large doses of AFB1 enter the body within a short time, the metabolite AFB1-8,9-epoxide interacts with DNA in target cells to form guanine adducts, leading to cellular damage. Research indicates that aflatoxin-phospholipid adducts can disrupt the integrity and function of cell membranes, mitochondria, and the endoplasmic reticulum, causing acute damage. Prolonged exposure to AFB1 ultimately induces liver fibrosis by inhibiting the RNA and DNA synthesis mechanisms. Furthermore, this factor carries potential risks for inducing systemic cancers such as gastric and renal carcinomas. Furthermore, immunocompromised individuals and fetuses exhibit significantly heightened susceptibility to these harmful effects due to their physiological characteristics, making them more vulnerable to damage, and this harmful agent can be transmitted through maternal-fetal pathways, causing adverse outcomes in newborns such as liver injury and developmental defects.

China, as a global core peanut-producing region, accounts for over 23% of the world’s peanut oil consumption. Data released by the WHO indicates that China bears an annual disease burden of 1.53 disability-adjusted years (DALYs) per 100,000 people due to liver cancer caused by exposure to AFB1 in food products, with peanut oil being the most severely contaminated source of AFB1. Currently, various physicochemical detoxification techniques (such as physical adsorption, high-temperature treatment, biodegradation, and alkali refining) are being employed in the market to degrade AFB1 in peanut oil. − However, AFB1 is highly heat-resistant, decomposing only at temperatures between 269 and 303 °C. Since such high temperatures degrade oil quality, achieving effective AFB1 degradation while preserving the quality of peanut oil remains a significant challenge. , Therefore, exploring methods to safely and efficiently remove AFB1 from peanut oil is increasingly becoming a global priority.

Currently, various technologies have been explored for the detoxification of AFB1 in food matrices, including physical separation, chemical treatments (such as ammonia treatment), adsorption using clays or activated carbon, and biological degradation using microorganisms or enzymes (Table ). However, these methods often suffer from limitations such as incomplete degradation, high cost, nutritional loss, or potential chemical residues. − Therefore, developing an efficient, safe, and nondestructive detoxification technology is of great significance. In contrast to this, we found that ultraviolet (UV) light exhibits strong penetration capability and efficient degradation performance, capable of instantly neutralizing toxins with significantly superior efficacy compared to chemical oxidation or biodegradation methods. Additionally, UV photodegradation offers advantages such as operational simplicity, cost-effectiveness, and ease of commercial-scale expansion. UV degradation of AFB1 primarily relies on high-energy photons breaking the double bonds and furan rings within AFB1, thereby reducing or completely eliminating its toxicity. Currently, mainstream UV degradation equipment on the market predominantly employs traditional UV pump lamp technology. However, prolonged exposure to UV radiation causes oil temperatures to rise again, which not only impacts the market competitiveness of peanut oil but also diminishes its quality. Therefore, UV photolysis technology, with its nonthermal effects and absence of chemical residues, has emerged as a key breakthrough for AFB1 contamination.

Comparison of Different AFB1 Degradation Methods and Their Degradation Efficiencies

This study employs UV LED cold light source technology, effectively resolving the heat generation issues inherent in conventional UV equipment. By establishing a UV LED cold light temperature control system, we compared its impact on oil temperature with that of conventional products under prolonged illumination. Our research indicates that UV LED cold light degradation is more effective than traditional methods for removing AFB1 from peanut oil. After comprehensively evaluating degradation efficiency and cost factors, the optimal UV LED cold light irradiation conditions were determined to be a wavelength of 365 nm, an irradiation duration of 6 min, and an intensity of 21,000 μW/cm2. Under these conditions, AFB1 in peanut oil pressed from moldy peanuts can be effectively degraded without altering the oil’s properties. It establishes a foundational basis for advancing AFB1 control technologies, holding significant practical implications for ensuring edible oil safety, accelerating technological upgrades among crude oil producers, and safeguarding public food health and safety.

Methods

Sample Acquisition

AFB1 standards (Yuanye Biotechnology Co, Ltd., Shanghai, China; B2405; 1–5 mg) were diluted to prepare peanut oil samples containing various concentrations of AFB1. Analyses were performed using ultrahigh performance liquid chromatography coupled with a Q-Exactive quadrupole-Orbitrap high-resolution mass spectrometry (UHPLC-MS) system (Thermo Fisher Scientific, Waltham, MA, USA). Simultaneously, normal peanuts were purchased from the market, and moldy peanuts removed from shelves were collected from the same market. Both types of peanuts were pressed in two batches using an oil press, yielding peanut oil with aflatoxin AFB1 concentrations of 6.432 μg/kg and 29.004 μg/kg, respectively. These were designated as Sample 1 and Sample 2, both originating from the same production batch.

Degradation Treatment Process

In this study, “cold light” refers to UV irradiation generated from a nonincandescent source, UV light-emitting diodes, which produces negligible infrared radiation and thus minimizes thermal effects, and we have also incorporated a corresponding temperature controlled cooling system. To validate the reliability of the UV LED cold light degradation device, we conducted an in-depth study on peanut oil containing AFB1 at varying concentrations and from different sources, examining changes before and after exposure to the UV LED cold light degradation machine. We used a minimum single oil batch size of 5 kg and set the degradation time to 4–6 min. Timing commenced when the first drop of peanut oil entered the inlet pipeline and concluded when the first drop exited the machine into the right-side storage tank. The machine was then shut off, and midsection output oil was collected as the test sample. Experiments were conducted to investigate the correlation between AFB1 degradation in peanut oil and UV LED irradiation duration and intensity by varying these parameters. To ensure testing accuracy, each experimental group was conducted independently. Oil pipelines and storage tanks were cleaned before and after testing, and parallel samples were used for repeated testing.

AFB1, Acid Value, and Peroxide Value Testing

When conducting the AFB1 test, 2.5 g of sample was placed into a 50 mL centrifuge tube. 10 mL of acetonitrile:water solution (84:16) was added, vortexed to mix, then shaken for 20 min, and centrifuged at 6000 rpm for 10 min. The supernatant was collected for later use. The UHPLC-MS conditions were set up. 4 mL of the supernatant was transferred, and the sample injection rate was controlled at 1–3 mL/min. After complete injection, it was eluted with 20 mL of water and then evaporated to dryness under vacuum. Acid value and peroxide value determination refer to standard GB 5009.229–2016.

Kinetics Fitting

The degradation-treated AFB1, acid value, and other indicators were imported into Origin 2024b software. Using the nonlinear curve fitting module, quasi-first-order and quasi-second-order kinetic models were tested. Where models were unavailable, custom functions were employed to create new kinetic equations. The Levenberg–Marquardt algorithm was used to optimize initial parameter values and iterate until convergence. Model goodness-of-fit was assessed using the coefficient of determination (R 2 ≥ 0.98) and adjusted R 2 as evaluation criteria, while residual sum of squares was calculated to validate model reliability. Based on reaction order, degradation kinetics are primarily categorized as zero-order kinetics, first-order kinetics, second-order kinetics, pseudo-zero-order or pseudo-first-order kinetics, and multiphase degradation kinetics.

Results

Structure and Principle of the UV LED Cold Light Degradation Unit

The structure of the UV LED cold light degradation unit is illustrated in Figure A,B, comprising a glass pipeline, UV LED disinfection lamps, a fluorescence sensor probe, and a PLC intelligent control system. Among them, the glass piping adopts a serpentine layout with oil-end connections to normally open solenoid valves, and the inlet oil line incorporates a transparent section housing a fluorescence sensor probe to detect fluorescent substances within the oil. UV LED disinfection lamps were mounted along the lateral surfaces of the glass piping array.

Overall configuration of the AFB1 degradation machine. (A) Structure of UV LED cold light degradation machine. (B) Schematic diagram of UV LED cold light degradation machine operation.

Peanut oil containing AFB1 transitions from its ground state to an excited state upon absorbing energy from UV LED lamps, thereby generating nontoxic photodegradation products. Throughout the degradation process, the PLC intelligent control system maintains the internal temperature of the degradation unit within a constant range, and the temperature of the oil entering the intelligent cold light AFB1 degradation unit remains essentially unchanged from that of the oil exiting after treatment; if the input oil is at a low temperature, the treated oil will also be at a low temperature upon exit. This prevents quality degradation caused by prolonged heating of the peanut oil and reduces lamp-life-span issues arising from high temperatures. Additionally, while synchronously regulating the cooling fan, sensors, and solenoid valves, one of the core tasks of the PLC intelligent control system is the precise control of the UV LED cold light lamps used for highly efficient sterilization.This enables the entire UV LED cold-light degradation machine to synergistically complete peanut oil degradation without compromising its quality.

Optimization of Wavelength for UV LED Cold Light Degradation of AFB1 and Analysis of Temperature Rise Effects

Since secondary temperature increases during the degradation process of peanut oil after pressing can affect its quality, we investigated the differences in thermal impact on peanut oil by comparing the temperature changes after 15 min of continuous exposure to UV LED cold light sources versus conventional lamps. The results indicated that the temperature increase in peanut oil under UV LED cold light irradiation was significantly lower (32.9–33.2 °C) compared to that under conventional UV lamps (45.2–46.1 °C), and the average rate of temperature rise in the UV LED cold light group was only about one-quarter of that observed in the conventional lamp group (2.3 vs 8.7 °C/min), a difference that was highly statistically significant (Figure A). Additionally, to compare the UV light intensity differences between the two illumination sources under total power conditions of 500 W, we measured the UV light intensity of both sources. The results showed that the intensity of the UV LED cold light source was 18,000 μW/cm2, while that of the conventional light source was 5500 μW/cm2. Under the same irradiation conditions, the light intensity of the UV LED cold light source was significantly higher than that of the conventional light source.

Study on wavelength optimization and temperature rise effects of UV LED cold light degradation of AFB1. (A) Changes in oil temperature between conventional UV lamps and UV LED cold light at the same time. (B) Effect of different UV LED cold light wavelengths on the degradation of AFB1.

Subsequently, to investigate whether AFB1 degradation before and after UV LED irradiation affects peanut oil characteristics, we prepared peanut oil contaminated with four different concentrations of AFB1 (0, 4, 10, 20 μg/kg) for degradation using a UV LED cold light system. The study revealed that both the color and transparency of the peanut oil at various concentrations showed no significant changes before and after UV LED degradation. This indicates that the UV LED degradation process does not alter the general quality or appearance of the peanut oil. Additionally, we took 200 mL samples of the degraded peanut oil and stored them in the dark at room temperature for 1 day, 7 days, and 15 days. Results revealed that peanut oil treated with the UV LED cold light lamp (AFB1 degradation rate > 90%) exhibited no color deviation or change in transparency (Table ).

To investigate the optimal wavelength of the LED UV cold light source for AFB1 degradation, previous literature indicates that wavelengths of 254 and 365 nm belong to ultraviolet C (UVC) and ultraviolet A (UVA), respectively, and are commonly used for degrading AFB1 in peanut oil. Therefore, this study employed ultraviolet light at these two wavelengths and prepared peanut oil with an AFB1 concentration of 30.1 μg/kg for conducting the relevant experiments. Experimental results indicate that both wavelengths of ultraviolet light effectively degrade AFB1 in peanut oil after 30 min of irradiation. Specifically, irradiation at 254 nm reduced AFB1 levels to 7.238 μg/kg, achieving a degradation rate of 75.95%. In contrast, irradiation at 365 nm was more effective, reducing AFB1 to 2.912 μg/kg with a degradation rate of 90.3%, indicating its superior efficacy (Figure B).

Effect of UV LED Cold Light Irradiation Duration on AFB1 Degradation Efficiency and Lipid Oxidation Indicators in Peanut Oil

To investigate the relationship between UV LED cold light irradiation duration and AFB1 degradation efficacy in peanut oil, five groups of peanut oil containing initial AFB1 concentrations of 20, 40, 80, 160, and 320 μg/kg were subjected to UV LED cold light irradiation treatment. Results indicated that within the irradiation duration range of 240 to 360 s, the concentration of AFB1 in peanut oil decreased with increasing UV LED cold light irradiation duration (Figure A). The degradation kinetic fitting curve demonstrated good fit (R 2 ≥ 0.9), with degradation results conforming to first-order kinetic curves (Figure B). This indicates that UV LED cold light degradation of AFB1 may be driven by a single dominant mechanism, whereby AFB1 concentration in peanut oil diminishes with prolonged UV exposure. Subsequently, we monitored changes in the acid value and peroxide value of peanut oil before and after different irradiation durations. Both acid value and peroxide value serve as key indicators reflecting the degree of oxidative deterioration in peanut oil; higher acid values and peroxide values indicate more severe lipid acidification and oxidation. Prior to irradiation, the mean acid value of peanut oil was 1.939 mg/g, and the mean peroxide value was 0.059 g/100 g. For each irradiation duration, five parallel experiments were conducted under identical conditions. The results indicated that altering irradiation duration did not significantly affect the acid value or peroxide value of peanut oil (Figure C, D).

To investigate the relationship between UV LED cold light irradiation intensity and AFB1 degradation efficiency in peanut oil, five groups of peanut oil containing different AFB1 concentrations were treated with varying irradiation intensities. Results indicated that within the irradiation intensity range of 12,000 to 21,000 μW/cm2, the concentration of aflatoxin AFB1 in peanut oil decreased with increasing UV LED cold light irradiation intensity (Figure A), and the degradation kinetic fitting curve demonstrated a good fit (R 2 ≥ 0.9), with degradation results conforming to first-order kinetic curves (Figure B). This indicates that UV degradation of AFB1 may be driven by a single dominant mechanism, whereby aflatoxin AFB1 concentration in peanut oil diminishes with enhanced intensity of UV radiation. Subsequently, we monitored the acid value and peroxide value in peanut oil before and after different irradiation intensities. Results indicated that altering the intensity duration did not significantly affect the acid value or peroxide value of peanut oil (Figure C, D).

Discussion

Compared to conventional UV degradation techniques, the utilization of UV LED cold light source technology maintains consistently low temperatures throughout the degradation process. Additionally, the quality of peanut oil postdegradation remains susceptible to storage duration and baking temperatures, and low-temperature conditions maximize the initial quality of the oil entering storage and cooking processes, fundamentally eliminating the detrimental thermal effects on the oil system. The degradation of AFB1 under UV LED cold light irradiation may be the result of direct photolysis and indirect photo-oxidation. AFB1 molecules can be absorbed by UV light from the ground state to the excited singlet state and further by the intersystem to form a more stable triple excited state; the excited state can be directly involved in the intramolecular rearrangement or bond rupture, and the excited state can directly participate in intramolecular rearrangement or bond breaking. At the same time, it can also indirectly transfer the energy to the surrounding oxygen molecules to generate reactive oxygen species such as monoclinic oxygen and then promote the photo-oxidative degradation of AFB1. , This explanation was also confirmed in the study of AFB1 degradation in aqueous systems. From the structural level, the key reaction sites of AFB1 are mainly focused on the terminal furan ring double bond and the lactone ring, where the former is closely related to its mutagenicity and carcinogenicity, while the latter plays an important role in its molecular stability and biological activity. It has been shown that the decrease in toxicity is often related to the destruction of the terminal furan ring double bond and the modification or cleavage of the lactone ring; in the peanut oil system, the main degradation products detected also support that the reaction is concentrated in the furan and lactone rings, and the oxidative/reducing transformations are predominant. , For peanut oil, the modulation of photochemical processes by medium effects should also be considered. However, due to the different fatty acid composition, color, initial peroxide value, and sample thickness of peanut oil, the degradation efficiency of different oil samples may differ from each other. Therefore, the degradation mechanism of AFB1 by UV LED cold light, although generally attributable to excited-state-driven oxidation/reduction and structural damage, still may have a matrix dependence in the actual oil system.

This study found that the degradation rate of AFB1 was significantly higher when irradiated with 365 nm UV light compared to the conventional 254 nm group, consistent with previous research findings. , This phenomenon can be rationalized from the photophysical properties of AFB1. The presence of a significant absorption peak of the AFB1 molecule in the long wave UV region near 360 nm implies that the photon energy of the 365 nm UV light coincides with its electron lepton energy level, which allows the furan ring and the lactone ring to absorb the light energy efficiently, directly inducing photochemical cleavage of their chromophores. Further kinetic analysis showed that the degradation of AFB1 under 365 nm UVC irradiation conformed to a first-order kinetic model with a rate constant of 0.001 cm2/mJ and a quantum yield of 4.25 × 10–3. Although 254 nm UVC has higher single photon energy, its wavelength deviates from the main absorption range of AFB1 and is difficult to be absorbed effectively; meanwhile, the high energy of UVC is easily absorbed and scattered by impurities, catalysts, or solution components in the reaction system, coupled with the fact that the peanut oil system is more viscous and less light penetrating and contains a large number of unsaturated fatty acids, and it has strong absorption properties in the range of 200–280 nm, which reduces the energy utilization. In addition, UVC irradiation may trigger undesirable side effects such as accelerated lipid oxidation, accompanied by thermal effects or free radical generation, which interferes with the degradation process of AFB1. Nevertheless, under some special conditions, such as in the ozone cotreatment system, the degradation of AFB1 by 254 nm UVC may play a synergistic role and show some potential for application.

Regarding the degradation of AFB1 in peanut oil, research indicates that the degradation rate of AFB1 increases significantly with an extended irradiation duration and heightened UV intensity, consistent with findings from other researchers. ,, The underlying mechanism lies in the fact that for the AFB1 degradation pathway dominated by direct photolysis, the reaction rate is primarily constrained by the number of photons absorbed by the target molecule per unit of time. Increasing irradiation intensity effectively elevates the flux of active photons per unit area while prolonging exposure duration and enhances the cumulative opportunities for photon collisions and interactions with AFB1 molecules. Consequently, heightened irradiation doses directly elevate the probability of AFB1 molecules undergoing photoexcitation and subsequent fragmentation of their toxic structures, manifesting as accelerated degradation kinetics. In addition, it has been shown that under the conditions of increased irradiation duration and shortened irradiation distance, the combined UV-formic acid treatment exhibited significant inhibition of both the growth of Aspergillus flavus and the synthesis of AFB1 toxin in dried red pepper powder. This further confirms that in practical application scenarios, the enhancement of UV intensity and the prolongation of irradiation time can effectively enhance the removal effect of photodegradation technology on the removal of AFB1 from complex matrices.Additionally, precisely because irradiation intensity is positively correlated with the degradation rate, and under identical conditions, the UV light emitted by UV LED cold light is more intense than that of conventional UV light. This further demonstrates that UV LED cold light technology offers greater energy savings and higher efficiency compared with conventional UV light.

One of the core objectives of food detoxification processes is “high-efficiency detoxification with no compromise to quality”. Research indicates that following UV treatment, peanut oil exhibited no significant alterations in acid value, peroxide value, or iodine value (p > 0.05). This confirms that the UV treatment process controls toxins without compromising the marketability of peanut oil. Compared with other detoxification techniques, hydrogen peroxide can oxidize aflatoxins into less toxic compounds. However, it typically requires combination with heating, radiation, or alkali, a process that may induce lipid oxidation and rancidity, and introduces other substances that affect peanut oil quality. Under conditions of 60 h ozone fumigation, AFB1 content in peanuts can be reduced by 89.4%, and the acid value and peroxide value of the peanut oil did not significantly increase post-treatment. However, ozone treatment necessitates strict control of concentration and duration, as excessive exposure may lead to lipid oxidation and rancidity. Although enzymatic and biological methods for degrading AFB1 offer distinct advantages such as mild reaction conditions, high specificity, and environmental sustainability, current research has also validated multiple viable technical pathways. For instance, screening Bacillus subtilis for biological fermentation and employing laccase and composite enzyme preparations for enzymatic degradation have both demonstrated efficient removal of AFB1 under experimental conditions. However, this technology remains largely confined to laboratory-scale development and preliminary application exploration. Its stability, universality, and economic viability have yet to meet the requirements for large-scale industrial implementation. − In summary, UV LED cold light technology offers the dual advantages of efficiently degrading AFB1 while preserving peanut oil quality. Moreover, the technology is mature and readily amenable to commercial-scale production.

Although LED cold-light UV technology demonstrates significant technical advantages in the removal of aflatoxin B1 from peanut oil, it is worth noting that LED light sources still face the following limitations in practical applications. The impact of light decay effects on the long-term stability remains unquantified. Existing research indicates that UV LED sources may experience intensity attenuation light decay during continuous operation due to factors such as chip aging and cumulative temperature effects; however, systematic quantitative studies in the field of oil detoxification remain lacking. Nevertheless, existing research findings indicate that UV-LED systems hold significant potential for the removal of toxic pollutants. In aqueous systems, UVA LED systems can effectively degrade AFB1 and AFM1 in pure water, and as the irradiation dose increases, the cytotoxicity of the degradation products toward HepG2 cells is reduced, suggesting that UV LED technology holds promising prospects for the removal of aflatoxins in aqueous systems. , Meanwhile, in the field of air pollution control, UV LED technology has also been proven effective in removing toxic pollutants such as volatile organic compounds (VOCs) from indoor air.
