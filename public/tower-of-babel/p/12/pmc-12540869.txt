Risk factors for unnatural mortality in persons with serious mental illness
Authors: Faith Dickerson, Sabahat Khan, Andrea Origoni, Kelly Rowe, Emily Katsafanas, Shuojia Yang, Paul Nestadt, Robert Yolken
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractRates of unnatural death in persons with serious mental illness greatly exceed those in the general population. These deaths largely result from suicides and accidents and are potentially preventable. There have been few prospective studies which have characterized clinical and biological risk factors for unnatural mortality in this population. A cohort of 1517 individuals with schizophrenia, bipolar disorder, or major depressive disorder was assessed at baseline. Unnatural cause mortality was determined from the US National Death Index with up to 23.9 years of follow-up and encompassing 18,952 person years of observation. Clinical, demographic, and serological variables were related to unnatural mortality using least absolute shrinkage and selection operators (LASSO) and Cox proportional hazard models. Sensitivity analyses were employed to determine risk factors in individuals who died due to definite or possible suicide. A total of 53 (3.5%) individuals died of unnatural causes, 29 as a result of suicide or possible suicide and 24 from accidental causes. Poisoning was the most common cause of death. Independent baseline factors that were associated with unnatural death were male sex, a diagnosis of major depressive disorder, low maternal education, more severe negative symptoms, younger age, and elevated levels of IgG antibodies to cytomegalovirus. Limitations of the study include the relatively small number of deaths due to suicide and accidents and also the exclusion of persons with primary substance use disorders. The factors associated with unnatural mortality in this study warrant further investigation in larger cohorts.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1038/s41598-025-20405-y.Subject terms: Biomarkers, Diseases, Medical research, Risk factors

Introduction

Persons with serious mental illnesses (SMI) such as schizophrenia, bipolar disorder, or major depression die at a younger age than persons in the general population1–5. Many of the premature deaths are due to natural causes such as cardiovascular disease and cancer, which are also the most common causes of death in the general population. However, a disproportionate number of deaths in individuals with SMI are due to unnatural causes such as suicides and accidents. The rate of deaths from unnatural causes is markedly higher in SMI as compared to that in the general population. Results of a meta-analysis indicate more than a 7-fold risk for unnatural cause mortality vs. a 1.8 risk for natural cause mortality in persons with mental disorders2.

Because of this alarming excess of death due to unnatural causes among persons with SMI and the fact that such deaths are potentially preventable, attention has focused on the determinants of unnatural death in this population. Most of the studies have focused on death by suicide and have highlighted risk factors including younger age3,6, male sex7, previous suicide attempts8, firearm access9 and substance misuse10–12. A smaller number of studies have focused on unnatural deaths including both suicides and accidents. These studies have largely used register-based data and are limited by the variables available in those datasets10,13–16. There have been few prospective studies of unnatural deaths, including both suicide and other unnatural deaths, in a cohort of persons across SMI diagnoses. To our knowledge, there have not been any studies of serological markers as predictors of subsequent unnatural death in this population.

The Sheppard Pratt Stanley cohort was established in 1999 and has enrolled individuals with schizophrenia, bipolar disorder, or major depression since that time. In this study we examine the relationship between baseline clinical, demographic, and serological factors and death from unnatural causes in 1517 individuals followed up through Dec 31, 2022.

Patients and methods

Participants were individuals who were enrolled in the Stanley Research Program at Sheppard Pratt in Baltimore, Maryland between February 1, 1999, and December 31, 2022. The inclusion criteria and the methods employed for data collection have been described previously17,18. The inclusion criterion for individuals with schizophrenia was a diagnosis of schizophrenia, schizophreniform disorder, or schizoaffective disorder. The inclusion criterion for individuals with bipolar disorder was a diagnosis of bipolar disorder including bipolar I disorder, bipolar II disorder, or bipolar disorder not otherwise specified. Those with major depressive disorder had either a single or recurrent episodes. The diagnosis of each psychiatric participant was established based on the Structured Clinical Interview for DSM-IV Axis 1 Disorders19 and available medical records. Participants in all groups met the following additional criteria: age 18–65; proficient in English; absence of any history of intravenous substance abuse; absence of intellectual disability by history; absence of HIV infection or immunodeficiency condition (e.g. HIV, cancer chemotherapy treatment); absence of serious medical disorder that would affect cognitive functioning; absence of a primary diagnosis of alcohol or substance use disorder per DSM-IV criteria. At time of enrollment, participants were receiving psychiatric services at Sheppard Pratt or an affiliated psychiatric agency.

All participants provided written informed consent and the study was approved by the Institutional Review Boards of Sheppard Pratt and Johns Hopkins School of Medicine following established guidelines. The study was performed in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki.

All participants were interviewed, and medical charts reviewed; information was obtained about demographic variables, co-occurring medical conditions from a review of systems, current tobacco smoking, history of substance abuse or dependence apart from nicotine or caffeine, and other variables listed in the Supplemental Text. Psychiatric symptom severity was assessed with the Positive and Negative Syndrome Scale (PANSS)20 after a structured interview. Cognitive functioning was assessed with the Repeatable Battery for the Assessment of Neuropsychological Status (RBANS)21. The RBANS scores were converted to normalized values (z-scores) having a mean of zero and a standard deviation of one, calculated by gender and race in a control population. Body mass index (BMI) was calculated from measured weight and height.

At baseline, a blood sample was drawn from which were measured antibodies to infectious agents and markers of inflammation employing immunoassays as previously described22,23. All reagents were obtained from IBL America, Minneapolis Minn. Assays were performed following the manufacturer’s instructions.

Mortality status at follow-up

The US National Death Index (NDI) provided the date and cause of death for decedents through December 31, 2022. The NDI is part of the Centers for Disease Control and is the best source of US cause-of-death data at a national level for research purposes24,25. The duration of observation was calculated for each patient as the time between the baseline study visit and December 31, 2022, or the date of death. Individuals who died from conditions other than unnatural causes, i.e., persons who died from natural causes such as cardiovascular disease or cancer, were excluded from the analyses.

Deaths were categorized based on the death certificate designations as unnatural (1) due to suicide or possible suicide based on the death certificate designation as due to intentional self-harm or undetermined intent, or (2) due to accidental causes26.

Data analyses

The relationship between factors measured at baseline and subsequent unnatural cause mortality was determined employing Cox proportional regression methods. The variables analyzed are those listed in Table 1 and in the Supplemental Text. First, the machine learning technique of least absolute shrinkage and selection operator (LASSO) was employed to identify covariates with non-zero coefficients. These covariates were then entered into a single Cox proportional hazards model to identify factors which were independently associated with increased hazards of unnatural cause mortality. A factor was considered to be significantly associated with a hazard of unnatural cause mortality if the p value was < 0.05. Significant continuous variables with a skewed distribution were also analyzed as dichotomous factors. Sensitivity analyses for death by suicide were done by performing the analyses leaving out accidental deaths and by analyzing only the group of individuals with a diagnosis of schizophrenia. Competing risk regression models27 were also employed to determine the possible effects of the individuals who died of natural causes on the associations with unnatural cause mortality. We also performed a sensitivity model consisting of Cox proportional hazards analysis of only the individuals with schizophrenia. For the sensitivity analyses, a p of < = 0.05 was considered to be significant and p > .05 and < 0.1 was considered to indicate a trend.

Results

Study sample

The study cohort consisted of 1517 persons: 731 with a diagnosis of schizophrenia, 579 with bipolar disorder, and 207 with major depressive disorder. Participants were followed for a mean observation period of 12.5 years (s.d. 6.5, range 30 days – 23.9 years) resulting in 18,962 person-years of observation. A flow chart of included and excluded individuals is shown in Fig. 1. Participant demographic and clinical characteristics are found in Table 1.

Consort diagram.

Causes of unnatural death

A total of 53 participants in the sample (3.5%) died from unnatural causes during the study period. More than half (29/53, 55%) were identified as suicide or possible suicide. As shown in Table 2, specific causes of suicide were poisoning with intent to self-harm (2 persons) or with undetermined intent (13), jumping from a high place (3), hanging (2), firearms (2), drowning (2), fire (1), self-harm with a sharp object (1) and other (3). The remaining 24 deaths were due to accidents including accidental poisoning (6), choking (5), exposure to heat or cold (4), motor vehicle accidents (3), drowning (2), exposure to fire (2), a fall (1) and unknown (1). There were no unnatural deaths due to homicide.

Predictors of mortality

We employed LASSO machine learning and Cox proportional hazards models to identify factors measured at baseline which were independently associated with subsequent unnatural cause mortality. As shown in Table 3, the significant independent predictors of unnatural cause mortality were: male sex (Hazard ratio (HR) 4.232, 95% Confidence Interval (CI) 2.000, 9.960, p = .0004); higher level of antibodies to cytomegalovirus (CMV) (HR 4.600, 95% CI, 2.456, 18.618, p = .0006); a diagnosis of major depression vs. schizophrenia (HR 8.684, 95% CI, 2.81, 26.831, p = .0009); low maternal education (HR 2.841, 95% CI 1.196, 6.753, p = .0.018); more severe negative symptoms (HR 1.060, 95% CI 1.007, 1.117, p = .020); and younger age (HR 0.967, 95% CI 0.938, 0.998, p = .022). In addition to being greater than in schizophrenia, unnatural cause mortality in major depression is also greater than in bipolar disorder (Wald test, chi-square = 6.22, p = .013).

Results of the sensitivity analysis with the suicide group alone, without inclusion of the accidental deaths, show the same significant predictors as for the total group except for the severity of negative symptoms, as shown in Supplemental Table 2. We also performed a sensitivity analysis using competing risk regression models to determine the effect of the 202 individuals in the cohort who had died of natural causes. Associations with unnatural mortality remained statistically significant for younger age at entry (HR 0.96, p = .002, 95% CI 0.93 − 0.98); male sex (HR 4.24, p < .001, 95% CI 1.99–9.901); levels of CMV IgG antibody (HR = 1.32, p < .001, 95% CI 1.18–1.47); a diagnosis of major depressive disorder (HR = 7.59, p < .001, 95% CI 2.31–24.9). There were also trends towards associations between unnatural mortality and the lowest level of maternal education (HR 2.59, p = .058, 95% CI 0.97-6.41) and more severe negative symptoms (HR = 1.05, p = .086, 95% CI 0.99–1.11).

We also performed a sensitivity model consisting of Cox proportional hazards analysis of only the individuals with schizophrenia. In this analysis, there remained significant associations between unnatural cause mortality and male sex (3.79, p < .005, 95% CI 1.49–9.67) and levels of CMV IgG antibody (HR = 1.34, p = < 0.001, 95% CI 1.18–1.51) as well as a trend towards an association with age at enrollment (HR = 0.97, p = .072, 95% CI 0.94 − 1.00).

Discussion

This report analyzes factors associated with unnatural cause mortality in a cohort of 1517 persons with schizophrenia, bipolar disorder, or major depression followed for a period of up to 23.9 years. A total of 53 (3.5%) of the cohort died of unnatural causes, 29 from suicide including those with possible suicide and 24 from accidental causes. Poisoning, accidental or intentional, was the cause of death for 21 persons (40% of the unnatural deaths). The mean age at time of death was 44.7 years and the mean number of years of potential life lost was 30.0. Based on LASSO and Cox proportional hazards analysis, the baseline variables that were significantly associated with subsequent unnatural mortality were male sex, elevated levels of antibodies to cytomegalovirus (CMV), a diagnosis of major depression vs. schizophrenia, low maternal education, more severe negative symptoms, and younger age.

While most studies of mortality in persons with serious mental illness have examined suicides and accidental deaths separately, it may be informative to look at these groups collectively given the inherent uncertainties in determining suicidal intent retrospectively and categorizing unnatural deaths and also the differences among medical examiners in making these judgments26,30,31. In addition, there has been little attention to the risks and prevention of serious accidents in SMI despite the fact that the rate of accidental death is as great or greater than that due to suicide in this population14.

Poisoning was the largest cause of unnatural death in our sample. There were nearly equal numbers of persons who died from poisoning classified as definite suicide, possible suicide, or accidental death. These results are consistent with other studies which have identified poisoning as a major cause of unnatural mortality in this population3. There have been fewer studies of accidental death, apart from suicide; the results of these studies vary as to the ranking of types of accidental death and the prominence of drug overdoses13,32,33. It is of note that we excluded from the cohort individuals with severe substance use at baseline, so deaths by drug overdose may be underrepresented in our population. In any case the identification and treatment of substance use is paramount in order to reduce the risk of death from ingestion of recreational drugs in individuals with serious psychiatric disorders.

We found that persons admitted to the hospital with major depressive disorder were at increased risk of unnatural death compared to those with schizophrenia, with a hazard of 8.9. The few previous studies comparing unnatural death between SMI diagnoses have also found that mood disorder patients are at the greatest risk16. While the lifetime risk of suicide in schizophrenia is relatively high, suicide death is most common in the early phases of the illness34; few of the schizophrenia participants in our sample were close to illness onset at the time of their baseline assessments. In addition, the persons in our sample with major depression were recruited largely from inpatient and day hospital settings, so they may represent the more severe end of the major depression spectrum. The number of deaths in persons with major depression was relatively small, adding to the exploratory nature of our findings.

In our study negative symptom severity as measured by the PANSS was associated with subsequent unnatural death, a finding that has not been previously reported. Higher ratings of negative symptoms may reflect social withdrawal or self-neglect, both of which could be conceivably related to a risk of unnatural death. It is of note in our study that many individuals died of accidents that might be associated with negative symptoms such as exposure to heat and cold, and drowning. Increased efforts should be made to treat negative symptoms as a way to decrease unnatural mortality due to these causes.

We measured maternal education as a proxy for socioeconomic status (SES) during childhood. We found that a very low level of maternal education, 8 years or below, was a significant predictor of unnatural mortality. To our knowledge there have not been other studies of maternal education or SES as a mortality risk factor in SMI. However, there are well established findings from the US general population about the association between lower SES, poverty, lower educational attainment and increased mortality, including from suicide35,36. The underlying mechanisms of this association are not known with certainty but may be mediated by health behaviors and by access to mental health resources37.

We also found an association between unnatural cause mortality and elevated levels of IgG antibodies to Cytomegaloviruses (CMV). Sensitivity analysis indicated an association between these elevated antibodies and death by suicide. Our findings are consistent with a recent population-based study in Denmark which also found an association between increased levels of antibodies to CMV and suicide behaviors38. Seropositivity to CMV has also been associated with increased rates of death by suicide as well as elevated levels of microglial activation in postmortem brains39. Associations between suicide behaviors and CMV have been found in some but not all other studies (reviewed in40). The biological mechanisms by which CMV might be associated with suicide behaviors are not known with certainty but we speculate may be related to immune activation within the CNS as occurs in both CMV infection and death by suicide41. One intriguing possibility is that the relationship between CMV and suicide is linked through interactions with toll-like receptors (TLRs) since TLRs are upregulated in the brains of individuals who die by suicide as well as in cells infected with CMV41,42. It is also of note that polymorphisms in TLR genes are associated both with suicide behaviors43 and susceptibility to CMV infection44 suggesting the possibility of genetic overlap between these conditions but these mechanistic explanations are speculative and were not directly tested in the data. It is also possible that CMV may act as a proxy marker for cumulative disadvantage45. However, the possibility that the association between CMV and unnatural cause mortality is an epiphenomenon relating to conditions of environmental exposure is rendered less likely by the fact that there were no significant associations between unnatural mortality and other human herpesviruses with similar epidemiological characteristics such as Herpes Simplex Virus type 1, Epstein Barr Virus and Varicella-Zoster virus. There is growing interest in the prevention of CMV infections through antiviral medications46 or vaccines47. Our finding about the association between unnatural death and CMV antibodies is preliminary and represents a hypothesis to be tested in further investigations.

Our study is limited by the relatively small number of deaths due to suicide and accidents and the geographic limitation of our study population. Also, we could not be certain of the self-harm intent in some cases and are limited by the determination, including the uncertainty, on the death certificate. Death certificate data are limited in the determination of intent; however, even with more information it may be difficult to determine intent as many persons are ambivalent about the possibility of death in the context of high-risk behavior such as opioid and other drug use. It has been suggested that accidental and intentional overdoses may represent a false dichotomy as both represent the same self-injurious act to varying degrees48. In addition, the PANSS symptom rating scale that was used to assess all of the participants is not an optimal instrument to measure psychiatric symptoms in persons with bipolar disorder and major depressive disorder. Also, we were not able to include all of the variables that are known to increase risk such as impulsivity and adverse childhood events. Finally, our sample was drawn from persons who received care at a tertiary care psychiatric system, who may have more severe psychiatric disorders than those treated in other settings.

Also, our sample excluded persons with HIV and those with primary substance abuse, groups with a high risk for unnatural cause mortality; however, even with these exclusions there was still substantial unnatural cause mortality in our cohort. In light of the study limitations, the results of our study should be considered preliminary and a basis for further investigation.

Strengths of our study are that it was prospective and included a detailed in-person assessment at baseline along with the collection of demographic, clinical, and serological variables. In addition, we included deaths due to suicide, possible suicide, and accidents, resulting in a fairly complete analysis of unnatural deaths in this population. Furthermore, we examined the specific causes and methods of death in order to identify potential preventive measures. Finally, we employed the National Death Index which provides an accurate and comprehensive source of mortality information throughout the United States.

In conclusion, unnatural deaths in individuals with SMI occur at a rate substantially higher than that in the general population. The identification of clinical and biological risk factors and an understanding of causes of unnatural death are essential in order to decrease early mortality in individuals with SMI.
