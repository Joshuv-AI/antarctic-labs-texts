Using a measure of the DSM-5 alternative model of personality disorder traits to define personality types in people seeking eating disorder treatment: a pilot study
Authors: Tanya Gilmartin, Caroline Gurvich, Gemma Sharp
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
BackgroundThere has been increasing research focus on the relationship between eating disorders and personality types and traits separately. In this pilot study, we present a mixed methods approach to classify individuals with an eating disorder as Overcontrol (OC; anxiousand perfectionistic), Undercontrol (UC; emotionally dysregulated) or resilient (without personality pathology). We then explore the Diagnostic and Statistical Manual of Mental Disorders, fifth edition, Alternative Model of Personality Disorder trait profiles associated with OC and UC presentations.MethodsTwenty individuals (12 women, 2 men, 6 gender-diverse) seeking treatment for eating disorders were classified as OC, UC or resilient based on a structured clinical interview and clinician rating scales supplemented by self-report measures of depression, anxiety and stress. Group differences in DSM-5 alternative model personality traits and eating pathology were examined.ResultsTwelve individuals were classified as OC, six as resilient, and two as UC. The OC group scored higher than the resilient group on the following DSM-5 alternative model of personality disorder traits: Anhedonia, Depressivity, Emotional Lability, Submissiveness and Separation Insecurity with large effect sizes (Cohen’s d = .0.80–1.84).ConclusionsOur research suggests the importance of assessing the public and private nature of problem behaviour within clinical assessment. In addition, our results indicated potential benefit of further research focused on understanding personality types in the context of DSM-5 pathological personality traits.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s40337-025-01413-9.

Introduction

The conceptualisation of personality pathology is an evolving construct. There has been a more recent shift towards understanding personality pathology from a dimensional perspective, concordant with current frameworks for understanding normative personality [8, 53, 56]. The Alternative Model of Personality Disorder (AMPD) was published in the Diagnostic and Statistical Manual of Mental Disorders, fifth edition (DSM-5-TR; [2]). The model encompasses 25 maladaptive personality traits conceptualised within five higher-order domains [18, 34]. Existing categorical PDs have been re-conceptualised as multi-dimensional constructs in the AMPD (APA, [2, 18, 27, 30, 66].

The developmental literature describes three personality types that form one bipolar dimension from overcontrol (OC) to undercontrol (UC) with a resilient type presenting with low psychopathology [4, 5, 9, 14]. The OC type is described as rigid, anxious and perfectionistic, and demonstrate features of obsessive–compulsive and avoidant PD. The UC type is defined by impulsivity and mood dependence, as well as elevations of features of Borderline PD [7, 22, 33, 41, 55, 65]. Consistent with current conceptualisations of normal and abnormal personality, personality types represent a continuous, normally-distributed multidimensional construct [32, 36, 47, 49, 53, 64].

There has been considerable research that has explored the presence of personality types among samples of individuals with eating disorders using a range of self-report measures, clinical interview and clustering methodology [10, 20, 22, 33, 51, 55, 65]. Eating Disorders are mental health concerns associated with serious physical health consequences [1, 57, 63]. The DSM-5 classification of eating disorders has been criticised due to concerns regarding substantial heterogeneity of diagnostic presentation [35], and high frequency of diagnostic migration (i.e. change in symptom expression that is more consistent with an alternate ED diagnosis; [12, 35, 60]). Furthermore, there is a need to develop and adapt treatments to improve outcomes for individuals experiencing eating disorders [13, 37, 46].

Research has indicated potential clinical utility of classifying individuals with eating disorders into personality type categories. For example, an early cluster analysis study among individuals with eating disorders found that grouping individuals into OC/UC/resilient types predicted adaptive functioning and aetiological variables more consistently than diagnostic categories [62]. Further research has suggested that among adults with anorexia nervosa, classification as UC has been associated with poorer treatment outcomes than OC and resilient types. In contrast, diagnosis based on DSM-IV classification did not significantly predict outcome [65]. Understanding personality type may provide a clinical treatment advantage in individuals with eating disorders [62, 65] In spite of such assertions, there are barriers to translating research into clinical practice. Firstly, there is considerable inconsistency in classification methodology across research studies to classify individuals as OC, UC or resilient [7]. Secondly, research has indicated that up to one-third of people who had been previously classified as UC, were then re-assessed as being OC [23, 25], suggesting a current lack of clarity regarding the assessment and classification of personality types.

When considering the translation of personality types theory and research into clinical practice, treatment protocols exist that are designed to specifically target features specific to OC and UC personality types [6, 7, 41]. Research conducted among individuals with eating disorders has indicated that Dialectical Behaviour Therapy (DBT) demonstrates positive treatment outcomes among UC individuals who engage in binge eating behaviour, while Radically-Open DBT (RO-DBT) has demonstrated promising treatment outcomes among OC individuals presenting with anorexia [6, 7, 41]. Within Lynch’s protocol for treating individuals presenting with a maladaptive OC personality type, there is a clear step-by-step protocol to assist clinicians in distinguishing OC individuals from UC presentation [41]. However, the process can be lengthy, and does require additional clinical training and supervision to confidently identify OC features. This likely creates a barrier to wider use. Integrating dimensional and typological frameworks may better translate research into practice, and a cluster analysis of Australian youth showed promise for applying the AMPD trait model to personality types. The results provided support for Lynch and AMPD theoretical models, in that traits conceptualised as indicative of obsessive–compulsive and avoidant PD (e.g. rigid perfectionism, perseveration, anxiousness) were indicative of membership in the OC cluster. In contrast, the UC cluster demonstrated elevations on all pathological personality traits, particularly those associated with Borderline PD (e.g. risk taking, impulsivity, grandiosity; [2, 41]. The current study

Although cluster analytical research has demonstrated consistency with theory regarding the AMPD traits that may be indicative of OC and UC personality types [21], at the time of writing, to our knowledge, no research had explored the relationship in a clinical sample. The aim of this cross-sectional, mixed methods pilot study was to explore personality types of individuals presenting for treatment with an eating disorders.

There were two objectives of our exploratory research:To classify individuals seeking eating disorder treatment as OC, UC, or Resilient, following Lynch’s [41] protocol.To examine AMPD trait profiles across these groups, with the expectation that OC traits (e.g., rigid perfectionism, perseveration) would align with OC, UC traits (e.g., grandiosity, callousness) with UC, and low trait scores with Resilient presentations.

Four step protocol for the classification of OC, UC and Resilient personality types

Self-report measures

Participants completed two measures designed to assess features consistent with OC and UC personality type. First, the Assessing Styles of Coping Word-Pair Checklist (ASC-WP; [41]) is designed to be indicative of OC or UC “caseness,” and asks participants to select the word that best describes them out of 47 word pairs. Second, the Overcontrol Trait Rating Scale (OC-TRS; [52]) which is a 24-item scale that is designed to measure OC traits. (The OC-TRS demonstrated acceptable internal consistency in our sample (Cronbach’s α = 76).

The Depression Anxiety Stress Scale-21 item version (DASS-21; [26]) was used to measure clinical presentation in our study. The DASS-21 demonstrated excellent internal consistency for the Depression (Cronbach’s α = 0.93), Anxiety (Cronbach’s α = 0.80) and Stress (Cronbach’s α = 0.83) subscales in our sample.

In order to assess AMPD pathological personality dimensions we used the PID-5 Short-Form (PID-5-SF; [42]) as a measure of AMPD domains. The PID-5-SF is a 100-item version of the original measure that measures the five domains (negative affectivity, detachment, antagonism, disinhibition, and psychoticism), and 25 facets featured within the PID-5. Within our sample, the majority of the PID-5-SF subscales demonstrated adequate to excellent internal consistency (Cronbach’s α = 0.70 – 0.96), with the exception of Irresponsibility (α = 0.50) and Restrictive Affectivity (α = 0.50). Due to the inadequate internal consistency, the results pertaining to the Irresponsibility and Restrictive Affectivity subscales have been moved to the supplemental material alongside a full description of the internal consistency data for our sample.

Eating pathology was measured in our study using the Eating Disorder Examination Questionnaire-Short version (EDE-Q; [19]). The scale asks participants to select on how many days over the previous 7 days they engaged in particular behaviours (The EDE-QS demonstrated good internal consistency in our sample (Cronbach’s α = 86).

Clinical interview

The Structured Clinical Interview for DSM-5 Personality Disorders (SCID-5-PD) is a semi-structured diagnostic interview designed to assess the ten DSM-5 PD categories (Avoidant Dependent, OCPD, Paranoid, Schizotypal, Schizoid, Histrionic, Narcissistic, Borderline, and Antisocial PD), in addition to dimensional scores for each PD [16].

Based on conceptualisation of OC tendencies as defined by Lynch [41] and the tendency for misdiagnosis [25], additional questions were asked to ascertain if behaviours were private (i.e., only displayed when alone or with immediate family) or public (ie. displayed more broadly). In line with recommendations by Lynch [41], participants were asked 11 supplemental questions, to identify the presence of OC traits, which are included in supplemental materials.

Clinical impression and clinician rating scales

Participants were rated on the degree to which they met features of OC on two clinician rating scales. First, the Clinician-Rated OC Trait Rating Scale (OC-TS) asks assessors to rate participants on a 7-point Likert scale from 1 (low) to 7 (high) based on eight different OC characteristics. The Overcontrolled Global Prototype Rating Scale (OC-PRS) consists of eight ratings across four categories to assess the presence of OC deficits following a clinical interview [41].

Procedures

Participants were recruited through clinicians (psychologists, dietitians, counsellors) and researchers working in the eating disorders field. More specifically, flyers were placed in clinic waiting rooms and in clinic newsletters in approximately eight private psychology, dietetic or counselling practices across Australia informing prospective clients of an opportunity to understand more about their personality and eating behaviour. Additionally, clinicians informed clients attending eating disorder treatment sessions of the study and provided interested clients with the link to the online survey. Prospective participants accessed the study through an online link, and first confirmed that they were aged 18 or over, living in Australia, and were seeking treatment for an eating disorder. They then read a more detailed description of the study and confirming their consent to participate in the study. The participants then began the demographic questions and psychometric measures. At the completion of the psychometric measures, participants were asked if they consented to be contacted to participate in the clinical interview component of the study and if so, to provide their email address. Participants were provided with a separate opportunity to enter their email address to go into a draw for a $100 AUD voucher of their choice. Study data was collected and managed using Research Electronic Data Capture (REDCap) electronic data capture tools (details included in Supplemental materials).

The Clinical Interview was conducted by using online videoconferencing software, Zoom. Within 1 week of completing the interview, the participants were provided with a brief report informing them of the outcome of the SCID-PD, specifically if they met criteria for a PD, and what features that they were found to endorse. Interviews were completed by researcher TG with the assistance of a research assistant for interviews and coding. Researcher TG, who is Level 3 trained in RO-DBT and has experience in identifying OC cases, completed rating scales following the Clinical Interviews to quantify the OC traits evident in the interview.

Of the interview recordings, six (30%) were watched by a research assistant and coded independently to the initial assessor. The consistency between the dimensional ratings of the SCID-PD was assessed using Interclass Correlation Coefficients (ICCs), with full details included in Supplemental materials. The ICC scores achieved in our study ranged from 0.86 (Histrionic PD) to 1 (Antisocial PD) indicating excellent interrater reliability, with the exception of Schizoid PD (ICC = 0.61), demonstrating consistency with past research [39]. Although lower ICC scores were achieved for Schizoid PD, raters agreed on overall diagnosis so no further discussion was required. Recordings were then transcribed using Zoom transcription software and the recordings destroyed.

Statistical analysis

Statistical calculations were implemented using SPSS version 27 (IBM [28]). There were minimal instances of missing data. For the PID-5-SF, one participant missed one item, and the associated facet and domain scores were prorated following recommendations by the test developer [42]. More specifically, the facet raw score was calculated by summing the answered item scores, and this score was then divided by 4 (the number of items in the facet score). To maintain consistency, we followed a comparable strategy with the other two missing data points, which were from separate participants in the OC-TRS.

For the first aim of the study, the continuous data obtained on the OC-TRS and ASC-WP were transformed into categorical data indicating OC “caseness.” The data was then assessed using descriptive statistics, namely the frequencies and percentages of PD diagnoses, OC-TRS categories and word pair categories. Means and standard deviations were calculated for continuous data obtained on the PID-5-SF, DASS-21 and EDE-QS. A content analysis was conducted on transcribed SCID-PD responses to assist on the classification of participants as OC or UC in line with [17]. The themes evident within the responses to three SCID-PD questions (Question 77: Frantic efforts to avoid real or imagined abandonment; and Questions 88–90: Inappropriate, intense anger, or difficulties controlling ager) and follow-up questions (described above) were the units of analysis. A deductive coding scheme was used, responses coded as OC demonstrated expression of vulnerable emotion when alone or only with immediate family/significant others. Responses coded as UC reflected a tendency to display vulnerable emotions in broader contexts, such as with acquaintances or colleagues, based on descriptions previously developed within the research literature [25], and in line with clinical manuals [41]. The coding was completed by a researcher with clinical training in the assessment of OC (TG) and any uncertain responses were discussed with the research team to ensure agreement and accurate classification. The data from the ASC-WP, OC-TRS, PD diagnoses as identified using the SCID-PD, content analysis codes, clinician rating scales and scores on the DASS-21 were all then systematically considered when classifying individuals as OC, UC and Resilient. A detailed flowchart has been included in the Supplemental Material.

For the second aim of the study, a series of Mann–Whitney U tests were used to calculate statistical differences between personality types on the PID-5-SF, DASS-21 and EDE-QS scores. A Benjamini–Hochberg Procedure controlling for the false discovery rate was conducted to control for possibility of Type-I error due to number of statistical tests. Charts were also completed using Microsoft Excel (Microsoft [44]) to allow researchers to observe the graphical representation of personality trait patterns between groups.

Step 4: clinical impairment

Participants who achieved DASS-21 scores in the severe or extremely severe category according to Lovibond and Lovibond [40] were considered to present with a maladaptive variant of their personality type. There was one exception, where this participant was found to meet criteria for four different PDs, yet scored in the mild to moderate ranges on the DASS-21 subscales. Given that they met criteria for PDs, the presentation was considered maladaptive and was classified OC.

Discussion

The overall purpose of our study was first to classify individuals seeking eating disorder treatment as OC or UC in line with the protocol outlined by Lynch [41]. Secondly, we aimed to explore the AMPD pathological personality dimensions that were associated with OC and UC presentations. As expected, elevated scores on measures of AMPD traits indicative of OCPD and avoidant PD such as rigid perfectionism, perseveration, depressivity, anxiousness, submissiveness, and anhedonia were associated with OC, and low scores on measures of AMPD traits were associated with resilient or adaptive personality presentations. There were not enough individuals in our study that were classified as UC to draw conclusions further regarding this type. We have explored our findings regarding each of the aims in more detail below.

Classification of personality types

The first aim of our study was to classify individuals as OC, UC and resilient based on previously outlined protocols [25, 41]. Although there is wide variation in the proportion of individuals with eating disorders who can be classified as each personality type, our research protocol classified sixty percent of the participants as OC, which is considerably higher than other studies (See [7], for a summary). The discrepancy between our findings and the findings from other studies may be explained by different sampling and classification strategies, which we expand upon below. Our results indicated four core findings related to the first aim of this study. Firstly, we found that recommended self-report measures outlined within the protocol [41] were inconsistent with the final classification in one-third of the participants in our study. Our findings therefore suggest that whilst the self-reported measures recommended by Lynch may be indicative of OC features, they may not be relied on alone for classification. Second, our findings provided some support for previous research that high rates of diagnostic co-occurrence among PDs which supports current arguments for adopting a more personalised, dimensional diagnostic system [3, 29, 30, 54, 58, 59]. Thirdly, participants with eating disorders in our study most frequently met criteria for and endorsed traits of OCPD followed by avoidant PD and borderline PD. Our research is therefore consistent with and provides an additional dimensional and sub-clinical perspective on existing research regarding the prevalence of PDs among samples of eating disorders [15, 31].

The fourth key finding within aim one of our study supported additional assessment to identify personality traits within a structured interview, specifically a need to enquire about reported behaviours as being observable or private [25, 41]. Our results highlighted that although emotional experiences consistent with a borderline PD presentation were endorsed by almost all participants, OC individuals described subtle or disingenuous expressions of experiences, in contrast to more direct or expressive responses. Our research therefore potentially suggests a need to ascertain underlying processes that may contribute to the behavioural expression of distress and what that expression might look like to fully understand an individual’s clinical presentation [25, 41].

Personality trait profiles and personality types

The second aim of the present study was to explore the AMPD pathological personality dimensions that were associated with adaptive and maladaptive presentations of OC and UC. In line with our expectations and previous research [21]. Traits associated with theorised OC PDs such as rigid perfectionism, perseveration, depressivity, submissiveness, and anhedonia were associated with OC. The OC group was also found to achieve low/normative scores on scales indicative of theoretical UC behaviour such as enjoying attention from others, grandiosity and callousness, consistent with previous research [7, 21, 22, 33, 55, 65].Contrary to our expectations and the findings of Gilmartin et al. [21], the OC group reflected published normative data on the withdrawal and intimacy avoidance subscales. This finding is curious given that the subscales are theorised to reflect theorised OC PDs (obsessive–compulsive and avoidant PDs), which were found to be highly prevalent in our sample. Previous research has indicated limitations of the PID-5 in measuring features of obsessive–compulsive and avoidant PDs [50, 61]. It is possible that the discrepancy between our research and previous findings highlights limitations in subscales designed to measure OC PDs, which deserve further research and scale development.

The resilient group tended to demonstrate a comparable response pattern to the OC group, although with lower scores on pathological trait domains. The exception from the consistent pattern was that the resilient group achieved low scores on depressivity, which is not surprising given that clinical severity was assessed in order to classify individuals as resilient or OC. What can be implied is that the resilient group is characterised by low scores on measures of AMPD pathological trait profiles.

Limitations

While our mixed-methods pilot study provided a novel approach to the understanding and classification of personality types, the small sample size limits the generalisability of the findings as well as the capacity to explore additional factors such as gender differences and cultural variability. In addition, our analysis were underpowered and therefore only significant differences with large effect sizes were able to be detected.

Only two participants were classified as UC in the current study, which was lower than found in other research [7]. There are two potential explanations for this discrepancy. First, the differing methodology may represent difference between our results and previous research. More specifically, a number of previous studies have used a cluster analysis within clinical samples to classify participants [20–22, 33, 43, 48, 55, 65], which is a contrast to the clinical interview process used in our study. Furthermore, several other studies have used a normative measure of personality as the basis for a cluster analysis [10, 20], which may yield some different results to our research focusing on pathological personality traits. The second potential explanation is the recruitment of participants seeking outpatient eating disorder treatment who self-volunteered to participate in the study, which differs from routine testing in eating disorder treatment centres [22, 33, 55, 65]. It is possible that UC individuals are less likely to volunteer for research, or may be more representative within the group of participants who did not complete the components of the study. Therefore, further research would benefit from replicating our research in larger samples, using clinical interviews and measures of pathological traits within more routine assessment of individuals presenting for treatment.

A further limitation of our study was the decision not assess the eating disorder diagnostic category of participant due to the heterogenous nature of diagnostic categories [38] and a tendency for diagnostic migration [12, 60]. However, we understand that this makes it difficult to compare our sample to other samples.

Implications

In spite of the limitations of our study, there are several important research and clinical implications that may be drawn from our results. In regard to areas for future research, our research suggests merit for further investigation of using the AMPD pathological personality traits as indicators of OC for individuals presenting for treatment of an eating disorder. Current clinical protocols have been developed to identify individuals who may be suited to treatment specific for OC individuals [41], and in addition to being lengthy, require specific training over and above standard clinical training. Our current research has provided preliminary indication that a brief, publicly-available assessment may potentially assist in identifying individuals who may be more appropriately suited to treatment targeting concerns resulting from OC behaviour, and doing so reduces time for the assessment process. Taken alongside the previous work of Gilmartin et al. [21], research currently indicates that elevated scores on rigid perfectionism, perseveration, depressively, submissiveness, and anhedonia combined with low scores on attention-seeking, callousness, deceitfulness, grandiosity, impulsivity, manipulativeness and risk-taking is indicative of a maladaptive OC presentation. Although further validation among larger samples is definitely required before clear clinical recommendations are made, comparable findings across two different samples using different methodology suggests that an AMPD composite that measures maladaptive OC may have the potential to bridge gaps between the research literature highlighting the relationship between personality types and mental illness [7]. Additional research is also required to explore the longitudinal outcomes of assessing OC using the AMPD.

Clinically, previous research suggests that classifying eating disorder presentations by personality type may offer stronger predictive validity than categorical diagnoses for symptoms and treatment outcomes [24, 65]. However, because the methods used to classify individuals as OC or UC differ from those supported in this paper, further confirmatory research is warranted. Our findings provide preliminary insight into AMPD traits that could indicate suitability for treatments targeting personality style, such as DBT and RO-DBT [6]. In addition, our results suggest the value of clinicians using assessment interviews to clarify how individuals experience and express distress, as well as how this is observed by others. Such assessments may help identify underlying personality factors linked to clinical presentation and improve the precision of treatment planning [25, 41].

Conclusions

The overall aims of our research were to classify individuals presenting for treatment of an eating disorder as OC or UC and to further explore the clinical utility of using the PID-5 as a measure of the AMPD to identify individuals with eating disorders as presenting for treatment as OC or UC. Our pilot study used a novel mixed-methods design that revealed that the AMPD pathological personality traits may potentially serve as a measure of OC and UC personality traits in an eating disorder sample. Our findings suggest that AMPD profiles, particularly rigid perfectionism and submissiveness, may serve as key indicators of maladaptive OC in ED populations.
