Penicillin or Ceftriaxone for Neurosyphilis in People With HIV: A Retrospective, Propensity Score–Matched Multicenter Study in Mexico
Authors: Rodrigo Ville Benavides, Héctor Rivera-Villegas, Obed Ocampo-Valdez, Rafael Franco-Cendejas, Jaime Federico Andrade-Villanueva, Luz Alicia González-Hernández, Pedro Martínez-Ayala, Andrea Cárdenas-Ortega, Paulina Carreño-Pérez, Jezer Lezama-Mora, Juan Carlos Rodríguez-Aldama, Edgar Pérez-Barragán, Pamela Alatorre-Fernández, Estefanía Sienra-Iracheta, Ana Patricia Rodríguez-Zulueta, Norma Eréndira Rivera-Martínez, Brenda Crabtree-Ramírez

ABSTRACT

Syphilis is a sexually transmitted infection caused by the spirochete Treponema pallidum, an obligate human pathogen that disseminates hematogenously soon after infection and progresses through primary, secondary, latent, and tertiary stages, with diverse mucocutaneous, systemic, ophthalmologic, otologic, and neurological manifestations. The central nervous system (CNS) can be invaded at any stage; however, the organism is frequently cleared spontaneously, and CNS invasion alone does not constitute neurosyphilis, which requires compatible clinical features, supported by serological and, when available, cerebrospinal fluid (CSF) findings. Neurosyphilis encompasses a spectrum of CNS manifestations associated with substantial morbidity, including irreversible neurological and sensory deficits [1, 2]. The global burden of syphilis has risen substantially, with annual incident cases increasing from 7.1 million in 2020 to 8.0 million in 2022 [3]. Treponema pallidum CNS invasion has been documented in up to 30% of individuals with syphilis, while clinical neurosyphilis accounts for ∼4%–10% of all reported cases [1, 2]. People with HIV-1 (PWH) are disproportionately affected, with higher rates of neurologic, ophthalmic, and otologic involvement, and an increased risk of treatment failure, recurrence, and reinfection [4, 5].

International guidelines group neurosyphilis, ocular syphilis, and otosyphilis together as conditions sharing a common treatment approach, recommending high-dose intravenous penicillin G as the standard therapy for all three [6–9]. However, this regimen requires prolonged inpatient care or continuous intravenous access, posing logistical, and financial barriers for patients and health systems [10–13]. Recurrent aqueous penicillin shortages, limited inpatient capacity, and high out-of-pocket costs further constrain access to standard neurosyphilis treatment, particularly in resource-limited settings [14, 15].

Ceftriaxone has emerged as a potential alternative owing to its prolonged half-life, adequate treponemicidal CSF concentrations, and once-daily dosing, which may facilitate outpatient administration [16–19]. Observational studies suggest comparable outcomes between ceftriaxone and intravenous penicillin; however, most were conducted in high-resource settings, included a limited number of PWH, focused on uveitis or lacked adjustment for confounding [11, 13, 16–24]. Robust comparative evidence focusing specifically on PWH remains scarce, particularly in resource-limited, real-world settings [12].

To address this gap, we conducted a multicenter retrospective cohort study of PWH diagnosed with neurosyphilis, ocular syphilis, or otosyphilis, at public hospitals and outpatient HIV clinics in Mexico. Using propensity score matching (PSM), we compared 6-month early serological response (ESR) between PWH treated with ceftriaxone or intravenous penicillin G.

METHODS

We conducted a multicenter, retrospective cohort study of adults with HIV treated for neurosyphilis, ocular syphilis, or otosyphilis at 5 public hospitals and 2 outpatient HIV clinics in 3 cities: Mexico City, Guadalajara, and Oaxaca, between January 2015 and January 2024.

Study Population and Inclusion Criteria

We included adults (≥18 years) with HIV with confirmed syphilis, defined by both a baseline serum nontreponemal titer (rapid plasma reagin [RPR] or Venereal Disease Research Laboratory [VDRL]) ≥1:4 and a positive Fluorescent Treponemal Antibody Absorption test, together with compatible neuropsychiatric, ophthalmologic, or otologic manifestations confirmed by specialist evaluation (ophthalmology, neurology, psychiatry, or otolaryngology, as clinically indicated), with or without CSF evaluation. When CSF data were available, confirmation could also be supported by CSF abnormalities, including a reactive nontreponemal test, pleocytosis (CSF white cell count ≥5 cells/µL), or CSF protein ≥45 mg/dL. In the absence of national Mexican criteria for these conditions, diagnostic definitions followed CDC guidelines [9].

Participants received intravenous penicillin G (18–24 million international units/day for 10–14 days) or ceftriaxone (1–2 g/day intravenously or intramuscularly for 10–14 days) according to clinician judgment. The ceftriaxone dose range reflects variation in prescribing practice across participating centers during the study period, consistent with published recommendations [6–9]. We excluded individuals with alternative diagnoses or incomplete baseline or 6-month follow-up data.

Data Collection

Between January and June 2024, we obtained data from electronic and paper medical records and entered them into a secure REDCap database. We extracted anonymized demographic, clinical, HIV-related, laboratory, and CSF variables using a predefined protocol. We extracted only objectively documented variables because provider-rated disease severity was not consistently documented across centers.

Outcomes

The primary outcome was ESR, defined as a ≥4-fold decline (or seroreversion) in serum RPR/VDRL titer at 6 months compared with baseline. As a secondary outcome, we evaluated early clinical response (ECR), defined as the resolution of neurological, ophthalmologic, or otologic manifestations among symptomatic individuals at baseline. Participants without baseline symptoms were classified as not evaluable for this outcome, rather than as missing. Cerebrospinal fluid normalization was not included as an outcome due to inconsistent follow-up lumbar puncture availability.

Statistical Analysis

We described baseline characteristics using χ2 or Fisher's exact test for categorical variables and Student's t test or the Wilcoxon rank-sum test for continuous variables, as appropriate. To address confounding by indication, we estimated propensity scores for receipt of ceftriaxone or intravenous penicillin using a logistic regression model incorporating clinically relevant covariates selected a priori based on biological plausibility: age, neuropsychiatric, ophthalmologic, or otologic manifestations, hepatitis B coinfection, hepatitis C coinfection, maximum baseline nontreponemal titer, HIV viral load, and advanced HIV disease (defined as <200 CD4 cells/mm3). Cerebrospinal fluid parameters were not included in the propensity score model, because lumbar puncture was not routinely performed at the outpatient HIV clinics where most ceftriaxone-treated participants received care. We then performed 1:1 nearest neighbor, without a caliper, targeting the average treatment effect in the treated, yielding 39 pairs (N = 78). We evaluated covariate balance using standardized mean differences (SMD <0.1 considered adequate). The robustness of the matching specification (0.2-SD caliper) and of residual imbalance (within-matched-cohort regression adjustment) was examined in additional sensitivity analyses (Supplementary Table 1).

The primary analysis estimated the odds ratio (OR) for ESR with ceftriaxone versus intravenous penicillin G using logistic regression in the matched cohort, with treatment as the sole predictor. We conducted 3 prespecified sensitivity analyses: (1) matched model adjusted for uncontrolled HIV-1 viremia and advanced HIV disease; (2) matched model adjusted for baseline maximum serum nontreponemal titer; and (3) a fully adjusted multivariable logistic regression model in the full cohort. We compared model fit using the Akaike Information Criterion (AIC) and Bayesian Information Criterion (BIC). In additional analyses, we repeated the comparison using a 12-month serological response (identical definitions), under a neurosyphilis definition in PWH of CSF white cell count ≥20 cells/µL [25] or reactive CSF VDRL, and under varying matching specifications (Supplementary Table 1, Supplementary Appendix). We handled missing data by complete-case analysis; 6-month titers were those recorded at the scheduled 6-month follow-up visit, and we assessed attrition by comparing participants with and without paired 6-month titers according to treatment, baseline symptoms, age, CD4 count, and HIV-1 RNA. The study was powered to detect a ≥15% absolute difference in ESR (2-sided α = .05, 80% power); however, given the retrospective design, this calculation was post hoc and is reported as a characterization of detectable effect size only. Treatment effects are presented as ORs with 95% confidence intervals (CIs) rather than as a formal noninferiority analysis, because no noninferiority margin was prespecified and the matched cohort was underpowered for such testing. Accordingly, all comparisons are interpreted descriptively. All analyses were conducted in R version 4.5.2.

Patient Consent Statement

All institutional review boards approved the protocol and waived informed consent given the retrospective design. The study followed the Declaration of Helsinki standards.

RESULTS

Study Population

Of 340 individuals assessed for eligibility, 143 met the inclusion criteria and had complete 6-month serological follow-up (Figure 1). Of these, 104 (73%) received intravenous penicillin G and 39 (27%) received ceftriaxone. Propensity score matching yielded 39 matched pairs (n = 78); 65 penicillin-treated participants remained unmatched. Baseline characteristics differed between groups on presentation, including the distribution of neurological and ophthalmologic manifestations (Table 1).

Attrition

Of 242 eligible participants, 99 (10%) lacked paired 6-month nontreponemal titers and were not included in the primary analysis. The proportion without paired titers was similar between treatment groups (penicillin 41% [72/176] vs ceftriaxone 41% [27/66]; P = 1.0), indicating nondifferential loss to follow-up by treatment. Participants without paired titers were more likely to have baseline symptoms (79% vs 59%; P = .002) but did not differ in age, CD4 count, or HIV-1 RNA, consistent with differences in care pathways and follow-up capture (symptomatic or inpatient care vs routine outpatient HIV follow-up) rather than treatment-related effects. Among those without paired titers, 32 had a documented follow-up visit but no recorded paired titer, indicating that part of the attrition reflected incomplete laboratory documentation rather than true loss to follow-up.

Determinants of Treatment Allocation

Before matching, participants treated with ceftriaxone had higher baseline nontreponemal titers and more frequent CSF abnormalities, whereas ocular and neuropsychiatric involvement were more common among those receiving intravenous penicillin G (Table 1).

Propensity score distributions showed adequate overlap between treatment groups in the unmatched cohort, supporting the use of matching (Supplementary Figure 1). Following PSM, baseline covariates achieved acceptable balance, with absolute SMD values below the recommended 0.1 (Supplementary Figure 2).

Primary Outcome: Early Serological Response

In the primary propensity score–matched model, ESR occurred in 74.4% of penicillin-treated participants and 71.8% of ceftriaxone-treated participants, OR 0.88 (95% CI .32–2.40; P = .80; Figure 2).

Sensitivity Analysis

Results were consistent across all prespecified sensitivity analyses; all point estimates were directionally similar to the primary analysis and no comparison reached statistical significance (P > .05; Figure 3). The matched model adjusted for HIV disease markers showed a directionally similar estimate; the likelihood-ratio test indicated improved model deviance relative to the primary matched model (P = .049) though this did not alter the treatment effect. The matched model incorporating baseline nontreponemal titer demonstrated the best fit among matched models (AIC: 82.1; BIC: 89.2) and produced estimates consistent with the primary analysis. The fully adjusted unmatched multivariable model showed substantially higher information criteria values (AIC: 162.5; BIC: 198.0), reflecting the larger dataset rather than poorer model quality, and yielded a consistent treatment effect estimate (OR 1.06, 95% CI .39–2.97). Covariate-specific estimates from the unmatched multivariable model are shown in Figure 4. Missing paired 6-month VDRL titers were similar between treatment groups: 67/171 (39%) in the penicillin group and 26/65 (40%) in the ceftriaxone group (P = 1.0). Participants without paired titers were more likely to have baseline symptoms than those with paired titers (80% vs 59%; P = .002), but did not differ by age, CD4 count, or HIV RNA. In a sensitivity analysis applying a 12-month response definition to a partially overlapping cohort of 85 participants, ESR occurred in 82.5% (47/57) of penicillin-treated participants and 75.0% (21/28) of ceftriaxone-treated participants before matching (OR 0.64, 95% CI .19–2.28; P = .57). After 1:1 PSM (28 pairs), response rates were identical between arms (21/28 [75%] each; OR 1.00, 95% CI .29–3.41; P = 1.0), consistent with the 6-month primary analysis (Supplementary Table 1). In a sensitivity analysis restricted to participants meeting a stricter CSF definition of neurosyphilis (CSF white cell count ≥20 cells/µL or reactive CSF VDRL; n = 78), propensity-score matching yielded 34 pairs; ESR occurred in 25/34 (73.5%) penicillin-treated participants and 23/34 (67.6%) ceftriaxone-treated participants (OR 0.75, 95% CI .26–2.14; P = .60), consistent with the primary analysis (OR 0.88; Supplementary Table 1).

Secondary Outcome: Early Clinical Response

Early clinical response applied only to symptomatic participants at baseline (85/143). Six-month symptom status was documented in 74/75 penicillin-treated participants and 10/10 ceftriaxone-treated symptomatic participants. The remaining 58 participants were asymptomatic at baseline and were therefore not candidates for this assessment, rather than lost to follow-up. Among evaluable participants (n = 84), ECR occurred in 55/74 (74.3%) of penicillin-treated participants and 9/10 (90.0%) of ceftriaxone-treated participants (OR 3.08, 95% CI .38–143.25; Fisher's exact P = .44).

At 6 months, CSF follow-up was available for 25/143 participants (17%), and only 11 had sufficient data for complete biochemical response assessment. Among these 11 participants, agreement between serological and CSF response was limited (5/11, 45%); discordance was mainly driven by 6 participants who met serological response criteria but did not achieve CSF normalization based on pleocytosis, protein concentration, and/or CSF serology.

DISCUSSION

In this multicenter retrospective cohort of PWH diagnosed with neurosyphilis, ocular syphilis, or otosyphilis, we found comparable rates of ESR at 6 months between participants treated with ceftriaxone and those treated with intravenous penicillin G. After PSM treatment effect estimates remained stable across sensitivity analyses, with no clinically meaningful differences in ESR and ECR. These findings align with previous observational studies suggesting comparable effectiveness of ceftriaxone and intravenous penicillin G for neurosyphilis [11–13]. However, much of the existing literature has been limited by small sample sizes, heterogeneous populations, combined outcome definitions, or insufficient adjustment for confounding [11, 17, 21–24, 26]. By focusing exclusively on PWH and applying a matched design, our study strengthens the evidence base for ceftriaxone in a population characterized by higher neurologic involvement, reinfection, and substantial barriers to care. Our cohort also carried a substantial burden of advanced HIV disease, ∼40% with CD4 < 200 cells/mm3 and about half with HIV-1 RNA >1000 copies/mL, reflecting late presentation common in Latin America. This contrasts with the comparative ceftriaxone-versus-penicillin literature, conducted predominantly in higher-resource settings with at most moderate immunosuppression and generally not assessing immunosuppression as an effect modifier [17, 21]. Reassuringly, our estimate was unchanged after adjustment for uncontrolled viremia and advanced HIV disease (OR 0.73, 95% CI .24–2.13), suggesting comparable ESR even in more immunocompromised individuals, a group underrepresented in prior studies [27].

Intravenous penicillin often requires hospitalization or prolonged intravenous access, whereas ceftriaxone's prolonged half-life and once-daily dosing facilitates outpatient administration. In our cohort, 95% of penicillin-treated participants required hospitalization, compared with 23% of those receiving ceftriaxone, illustrating a practical advantage of outpatient ceftriaxone administration where inpatient capacity is limited. Additionally, ceftriaxone is well tolerated, can be given intramuscularly, and offers an option for penicillin-allergic patients [9].

Intramuscular procaine penicillin G with oral probenecid (a guideline-recommended regimen permitting outpatient penicillin administration) has been discontinued or is recurrently unavailable in many settings, removing one of the few nonhospitalized penicillin options and further supporting ceftriaxone as a practical alternative. Oral regimens including doxycycline, amoxicillin–probenecid, and linezolid are under investigation but remain outside current guideline recommendations pending further evidence [11, 28–35]. Finally, the recent identification of mutations associated with partial β-lactam resistance and macrolides in contemporary T. pallidum strains further supports interest in alternative treatment options [36, 37], though clinical relevance of these findings requires prospective validation.

The comparable ESR observed with ceftriaxone and intravenous penicillin G in this cohort, together with ceftriaxone's practical advantages, is clinically relevant. Furthermore, higher baseline nontreponemal titers were weakly associated with ESR in the multivariable model (OR 1.006 per unit increase, 95% CI 1.002–1.012), suggesting that serological burden at diagnosis may modestly influence treatment response; these findings warrant confirmation in larger prospective studies. The observed imbalances in ocular disease and CSF abnormalities reflect treatment selection by clinical phenotype and care setting rather than differential ascertainment, as ophthalmologic and CSF evaluation were performed in nearly all participants in both groups, and CSF abnormality rates were similar across phenotypes among those examined. Ocular syphilis was preferentially treated with inpatient intravenous penicillin G, whereas nonocular, CSF-defined neurosyphilis was more often managed with outpatient ceftriaxone. Although PSM balanced clinical presentation, residual confounding from unmeasured differences in clinical decision-making, including clinician-perceived disease severity and referral patterns, cannot be excluded.

This study has several limitations. First, unmeasured confounding cannot be excluded despite successful matching; notably, CSF parameters, which differed substantially between groups, could not be incorporated into the propensity score model due to structural absence of lumbar puncture data at outpatient centers. Second, ECR analysis was limited by substantial missing follow-up data, precluding reliable comparisons; the small number of symptomatic ceftriaxone-treated participants yielded an imprecise estimate, rendering this exploratory comparison hypothesis-generating only. Third, we were unable to distinguish treatment failure from reinfection in a population with continuous exposure risk. Fourth, participating centers were predominantly urban and specialized, limiting generalizability to primary care or rural settings. Fifth, among the small subset with 6-month CSF follow-up, serological response appeared to overestimate improvement relative to CSF markers; however, the limited availability of repeat CSF assessments precluded its use for comparative effectiveness conclusions. Sixth, the retrospective design also precluded reliable syndromic staging of neurosyphilis; participants were grouped by affected system (neurological/psychiatric, ophthalmologic, or otologic) rather than by disease stage. Importantly, our study was not designed as a noninferiority study: no margin was prespecified, and the matched cohort was underpowered for formal noninferiority testing. Accordingly, although ESR was comparable between groups, our findings are consistent with, but cannot formally establish, noninferiority of ceftriaxone. Finally, only early outcomes were assessed; longer-term standardized serological and clinical outcomes warrant further study.

Despite these limitations, ceftriaxone represents a reasonable alternative to intravenous penicillin G for PWH with neurosyphilis when standard therapy is not feasible. Future prospective studies incorporating patient-centered outcomes, standardized clinical monitoring and CSF assessments with extended follow-up are needed to define the optimal role of alternative regimens across diverse care settings and populations.

CONCLUSIONS

In this multicenter cohort of PWH diagnosed with neurosyphilis, ocular syphilis, or otosyphilis, ceftriaxone showed comparable ESR to intravenous penicillin G. These findings support ceftriaxone as a reasonable alternative when standard therapy is not feasible due to penicillin shortages, limited inpatient capacity, or the financial and logistical constraints of prolonged intravenous administration, particularly in resource-limited settings.
