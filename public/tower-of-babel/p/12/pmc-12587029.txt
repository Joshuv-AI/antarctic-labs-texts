Decoding the Genome of the Komagataella phaffii Type Strain CBS 2612
Authors: Nadine Elpida Tatto, Lina Heistinger, Corinna Rebnegger, Minoska Valli, Diethard Mattanovich, Brigitte Gasser, Alexandra B. Graf
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTThe methylotrophic yeast Komagataella phaffii (formerly known as Pichia pastoris) is an essential host for biotechnological production. Here, we present the complete and annotated genome sequence of CBS 2612 T = NRRL Y‐7556T, the type strain of K. phaffii. CBS 2612 has a genome length of 9,387,549 bp with 5412 predicted and 5389 annotated genes, of which 144 are tRNA genes including the previously missing tRNAs for tryptophan, tyrosine, and serine, and 34 rRNA genes. In total, 4 SNPs were found compared to the biotechnologically most commonly used strain, CBS 7435. Additionally, 34 lncRNA candidates could be identified, including candidates that affect telomere‐regulation and flocculin genes.

Introduction

Komagataella phaffii is a methylotrophic yeast and valued expression system used to produce biopharmaceuticals and industrial enzymes (Ata et al. 2021; Barone et al. 2023). Previously known as Pichia pastoris, strains were later reclassified into the separate species K. phaffii and Komagataella pastoris (Kurtzman 2005, 2009). Today, the genus Komagataella contains seven methylotrophic species, all isolated from trees (Heistinger et al. 2022; Naumov et al. 2018). In the context of recombinant protein production, K. phaffii and K. pastoris are still often referred to by their old name, P. pastoris.

The ability of K. phaffii to grow on methanol as a carbon and energy source makes it an attractive host system to produce recombinant proteins. The strong inducible promoters of methanol utilization genes like AOX1 are routinely used for high‐level expression of recombinant proteins (Gasser et al. 2015; Weinhandl et al. 2014). Furthermore, K. phaffii efficiently secretes recombinant proteins to the culture supernatant, increasing its value as a production host (Puxbaum et al. 2015). The accumulated knowledge about K. phaffii genetics and cell biology facilitates successful cell‐ and metabolic engineering for metabolite and protein production (Peña et al. 2018; Zahrl et al. 2017). Also, the possibility of efficiently inducing peroxisome formation by methanol makes K. phaffii a sound microbial model system for peroxisome research (Farré et al. 2019; Oku and Sakai 2010). A prerequisite for in‐depth research on K. phaffii is the availability of high‐quality genome information. In 2009, the sequence of the GS115 strain (a histidine auxotroph mutant derived from the wild‐type strain NRRL Y‐11430 = CBS 7435) was published (De Schutter et al. 2009), followed by the sequence of the wild‐type CBS 7435 (Küberl et al. 2011). Later, the sequences and genome annotations of these strains were further improved by using long‐read sequencing, transcriptomics and proteomics data (Love et al. 2016; Sturmberger et al. 2016; Valli et al. 2016).

Despite CBS 7435 being the strain that is more often used in industry, CBS 2612 is the described type strain of the species K. phaffii (type strain NRRL Y‐7556T = CBS 2612 T; Kurtzman 2005), as it was the original isolate deposited by Herman Phaff in 1954 ((Phaff et al. 1956); also deposited as UCD‐FST K‐239, NRRL YB‐4290, and NCYC 2543 in different culture collections). Per definition, a type strain is the strain used when a species is described for the first time. Like other strains of the species, CBS 2612 was isolated from trees, specifically the exudate of black oak trees (Quercus kelloggii) in California, USA, and is used to produce recombinant proteins as well as in studies on the genetics and physiology of K. phaffii (Kurtzman 2009; Lopes et al. 2013; Prielhofer et al. 2018). Furthermore, methanol utilization pathway mutants and stable mating type strains of CBS 2612 are available (Heistinger et al. 2018; Zavec et al. 2018).

The genome sequence of CBS 2612 was included in comparative studies, and both raw data (Brady et al. 2020) and scaffolds (Opulente et al. 2023) from short‐read sequencing were deposited in the NCBI. However, a complete assembled genome sequence and annotation has not been available in public databases such as NCBI until now.

In their comparative study, Braun‐Galleani et al. (Braun‐Galleani et al. 2019) described in total 4 SNPs between CBS 2612 and CBS 7435 without analyzing the potentially affected proteins (and 69 SNPs between GS115 and CBS 7435). Brady et al. (Brady et al. 2020) reported just one SNP between CBS 2612 and CBS 7435, which affects the lengths of the transcription factor Rsf2 (and 74 SNPs between GS115 and CBS 7435). Recent findings suggest that NRRL Y‐11430/CBS 7435 is derived from the original type strain (Claes et al. 2024). To make the genome information of the K. phaffii type strain accessible, here we present the whole‐genome sequence of CBS 2612 ( = NRRL Y‐7556). We obtained the raw genome sequence of K. phaffii CBS 2612 through the long‐read sequencing technology PacBio. We performed the functional annotation, including the prediction of putative long non‐coding RNAs and a detailed comparison to the already published genome sequences of the K. phaffii CBS 7435 strain. This well‐annotated genome sequence of the K. phaffii type strain CBS 2612 facilitates its use for basic research and development of production strains.

Materials and Methods

Genome Sequencing

K. phaffii CBS2612 (obtained from CBS Yeast Collection, Westerdijk Fungal Biodiversity Institute, Netherlands) cells were grown in standard YP medium (10 g/L yeast extract, 20 g/L soy peptone) containing 2% glucose as a carbon source. Liquid cultures were grown overnight in a shake flask at 25°C. Genomic DNA was extracted using the Genomic‐tip 100/G kit (Qiagen). The extracted DNA was sequenced on the Pacific Biosciences, USA (PacBio) single‐molecule real‐time (SMRT) platform, resulting in 180,687 raw reads and 2,295,631,368 bases with a mean read length of 12,705 (N50 read length 17,495).

Genome Assembly

The assembly was performed using the native PacBio Hierarchical Genome Assembly Process HGAP3 (Myers et al. 2000) and Celera assembler v8.1 (Chin et al. 2013) followed by the unitig consensus caller of the sequencing center. Long‐read polishing was done using PacBios Quiver (SMRT Analysis 1.4) (Chin et al. 2013; Ferrarini et al. 2013). Mapping was conducted with BLASR v1 (Chaisson and Tesler 2012), which led to 147,523 mapped reads. The assembly process resulted in 29 contigs by Functional Genomics Center Zurich (FGCZ), which were later condensed into four chromosomes and the mitochondria by comparison with CBS 7435 and GS115. Chromosome and contig lengths were determined with bioawk (Li 2013).

Gene Prediction

Gene prediction was performed using MAKER2 (Holt and Yandell 2011). MAKER2 utilizes and combines several other tools: RepeatMasker (Smit et al. 2013‐ 2015) including RMBlast, Snap (Korf 2004), TandemRepeatFinder (Benson 1999), Exonerate (Slater and Birney 2005), GeneMark‐ES (Ter‐Hovhannisyan et al. 2008), Augustus (Stanke and Morgenstern 2005; Stanke et al. 2006), and CEGMA (Parra et al. 2007). Genes were predicted using only those contigs of the polished assembly, which correlate to the four chromosomes and the mitochondrion, namely: unitig 0 (chromosome 1), unitig 1 (chromosome 2), unitig 63 (chromosome 3), unitig 62 (chromosome 4), unitig 4 (mitochondrion). The following ORF naming convention was chosen: KP2612 Chr[number]‐[5‐digit‐number], starting with 00001 and steps of 11 digits between the genes for possible insertions or splits. tRNAs were predicted using tRNAScan‐SE (Lowe and Chan 2016; Lowe and Eddy 1997) and ARAGORN (Laslett 2004), and rRNAs were identified with barrnap v0.9 (Seemann 2013). The results of tRNAScan‐SE were cross‐checked and refined by conducting reciprocal blast searches with standard parameters against tRNAs of the K. phaffii strain CBS 7435. The protein‐coding genes of the mitochondria were determined using sequence comparison with known coding genes of CBS 7435 ASMa.

Sequence Statistics

GC content was determined using BioPython (Cock et al. 2009), contig and genome sizes were calculated by using bioawk (H. Li 2013), while the comparison of sequence length was conducted with in‐house Python scripts. Codon usage for the various strains was determined through EMBOSS cusp v6.6.0 (Rice et al. 2000). Furthermore, in‐house Python scripts were utilized to determine in‐frame up‐ and downstream ATGs for possible divergent ORF starts. Downstream ATGs were only searched within gene boundaries, whereas for upstream ATGs, a constraint of 114 bp was used. The average nucleotide identity between coding regions was calculated with OrthoANI v1.2 (Edgar 2010; Lee et al. 2016; Yoon et al. 2017).

Cross‐Strain/Cross‐Species Homology

Predicted coding genes were iteratively clustered using CD‐HIT‐EST to determine nucleotide identity between the selected species and strains (Huang et al. 2010; W. Li and Godzik 2006). Clustering was done in several steps to distinguish between distinct classes of sequences: Complete identity with 100% sequence identity over 100% sequence length; incomplete identity at least 90% sequence identity over 100% sequence length; and incomplete length with 100% sequence identity over any length. The workflow is depicted in Figure 1. The K. phaffii CBS 2612 sequence and both CBS 7435 genome assemblies were first clustered against themselves to remove isoforms and create a unique gene set. Next, CBS 2612 genes were clustered against the genes of CBS 7435 ASMa according to the constraints mentioned above. The representative sequences of each cluster were then compared to CBS 7435 ASMb. Lastly, CBS 2612 genes were clustered directly with CBS 7435 ASMb genes with the same constraints. A blastp was performed to investigate if the nucleotide differences between the two K. phaffii strains result in synonymous or nonsynonymous substitutions (Altschul et al. 1990; Camacho et al. 2009). For the 417 gene sequences of CBS 2612, which did not form clusters with any CBS 7435 ASMb sequence within the chosen constraints, a reciprocal nucleotide blast against all coding sequences of CBS 7435 ASMa and CBS 7435 ASMb was performed. RNA‐Seq data from Heistinger et al. (2018) (Heistinger et al. 2018) with the GEO Series accession number GSE111973 was used in a new alignment against K. phaffii CBS 2612 using the Tuxedo Suit (Gosh and Chan 2016) successors HISAT2 v2.1.0 (Kim et al. 2015; Pertea et al. 2015) and StringTie v1.3.5 (Pertea et al. 2015). The alignments were used to identify possible new transcripts and long non‐coding RNAs.

General clustering workflow for strain comparison on transcript nucleotide level for identification of homologous genes/transcripts and differences in annotation (created with Lucidchart).

To localize the centromere regions of CBS 2612, multiple sequence alignments were performed with ClustalW v2.1 (Larkin et al. 2007; Thompson et al. 1994) against the centromere repeat regions from CBS 7435 ASMa (Coughlan et al. 2016; Heistinger et al. 2018). Candidate regions were visually inspected in JBrowse (Skinner et al. 2009) using RNA‐Seq data support from Heistinger et al. (2018).

Long Non‐Coding RNA Identification

In‐silico discovery and annotation of long non‐coding RNAs were performed using the workflow from Mathew et al. (2019) with the following modifications: The Tuxedo Suit for de‐novo transcript detection was replaced with HISAT2 v2.1.0 (Kim et al. 2015) and StringTie v1.3.5 (Pertea et al. 2015). Coding potential was determined using CPC 2 beta (Atkinson et al. 2012; Uchida 2017). The coordinates of the resulting non‐coding transcripts longer than 200 bases (Atkinson et al. 2012; Uchida 2017) were compared to the K. phaffii CBS 2612 transcripts, as determined in the previous annotation steps. Potential domain coding subsequences were determined using hmmscan from HMMER 3.2.1 (06/2018) (Eddy 2011, 2015) and Pfam‐A. hmm v32.0 (El‐Gebali et al. 2019; Finn et al. 2016). Results were filtered for transcripts with an E‐value cutoff of 0.001. The remaining candidates were blasted against the fungi RefSeq protein database (O'Leary et al. 2016) with blastx (Altschul et al. 1990). Hsps with an identity above 50% and a length of over 30% were considered matches for potential coding regions. RNA‐Seq data from Heistinger et al. (2018) (Heistinger et al. 2018) was used to evaluate de‐novo predicted transcripts.

Synteny and Variant Calling

Synteny and variants were determined with Sibelia v3.0.7 (Minkin et al. 2013). The chromosomes of the CBS 2612 type strain were compared to the two available assemblies of the K. phaffii strain CBS 7435 (CBS7435ASMa and CBS7435ASMb). Variant calling was performed in the strain pairs CBS2612/CBS7435ASMb, CBS2612/CBS7435ASMa, and CBS2612/GS115ASMa, CBS2612/GS115ASMb. Circos v0.69‐6 (Krzywinski et al. 2009) was used to visualize both synteny and variant analysis (see Supplementary Figures S1– S5). An in‐house Python script was used to assign transcript names of the respective strains for SNPs located inside a gene for further evaluation. In‐silico identified SNPs underwent a wet‐lab examination. Genomic DNA of CBS 2612 and CBS 7435 were used as templates for PCR amplification of the respective regions using Q5 polymerase (NEB) followed by Sanger sequencing. Primers are given in Supplementary Table S1.

Visualization

An installation of a JBrowse 1.16.2 (Skinner et al. 2009) genome browser instance was created for the CBS 2612 sequences. The instance encompasses the following information: annotation of predicted genes utilizing the FeatureSequence plugin (TW 2016), RNAs, homologs to both CBS 7435 assemblies, blast hits to CBS 7435 ASMb using the plugin BlastView (Rasche 2017), detected variants, RNA‐Seq data from Heistinger et al. (2018) aligned with HISAT2 (Kim et al. 2015; Pertea et al. 2015) and StringTie (Pertea et al. 2015) to CBS 2612 using the MultiBigWig (Diesh and Raine 2016) and the WiggleSVGPlot (Hofmeister 2017b) plugin. Further plugins used for improved visualization are MotifDensity (Hofmeister 2017a), GC‐content (Diesh and Rasche 2016), QualityGlyphs (Diesh 2016a), SashimiPlot (Diesh 2016b), Bookmarks (Wilkey et al. 2015), and Fullscreen (Wilkey 2015).

Results

K. phaffii CBS 2612 genome sequence

The Komagataella phaffii type strain CBS 2612 genome was PacBio sequenced and assembled into 29 contigs, with sizes between 7813 and 2,903,793 bp (see Supplementary Table S2), with an N50 of 2,396,730 bp and a maximum contig length of 2,903,793 bp. The four longest contigs of the assembly correspond to the four chromosomes of K. phaffii, followed by the mitochondrial contig. The smaller contigs mapped to ribosomal regions and repeat regions like telomeres. Following a synteny verification utilizing Sibelia (Minkin et al. 2013), unitig_4 had to be shortened due to the assembly of the mitochondrion having twice the length of the reference and consisting of two identical sequence segments. Also, a 3995 nt long segment had to be repositioned to the beginning of the mitochondrial sequence to match the mitochondrium of CBS 7435 ASMa. Regarding chromosome 4, the synteny analysis in combination with the comparison to ATCC 76273 showed that the reference of CBS 7435 is flipped in orientation (see also Supplementary Figure S6). The results of the CBS 2612 assembly were compared to the publicly available assemblies of the K. phaffii strains CBS 7435 and GS115 and to the type strain of the genus Komagataella, K. pastoris DSMZ70382. The genomic vital data of all used strains and assemblies can be found in Table 2. With 9.39 Mb, the genome size of CBS 2612 is similar to the PacBio assembly of CBS 7435 ASMb. Gene prediction and manual curation resulted in 5196 chromosomal coding genes, 144 tRNAs (see also Supplementary Table S3), and 34 copies of the rDNA loci, which lies between the annotated chromosomal genes in CBS 7435 ASMa with 5301 and CBS 7435 ASMb with 5057 genes. The number of identified rDNA loci (34) is higher compared to CBS 7435 ASMa (29) and CBS 7435 ASMb (25). Notably, rDNA loci are, on the one hand, difficult to assemble due to their repetitive nature, and on the other hand, supposed to be variable in their number at different cell growth rates, which might explain the differences compared to previous assemblies (Sturmberger et al. 2016). 15 coding and 24 tRNA genes could be annotated on the mitochondrial sequence.

The GC content of CBS 2612 (41.2%) is similar to the GC content of the closely related CBS 7435 and GS115 strains (41.17%; 41.24% for PacBio‐based assemblies). Likewise, the GC content of the coding sequences (41.63%) corresponds to the average coding sequence GC content of the other PacBio sequenced assemblies and strains (41.64%). In contrast, the average GC‐content of the coding sequences from the Illumina sequenced assemblies (41.43%) is slightly lower.

The average nucleotide identity (ANI) (see Supplementary Table S4) was determined for a better insight into global sequence similarity between the strains and assemblies. Between CBS 2612 and the two CBS 7435 assemblies, it was found to be 99.97% (ASMa) and 99.96% (ASMb), respectively, and between the CBS 2612 and both GS115 assemblies, it was 99.95%. The average ANI with K. pastoris strains is only 89.68%, supporting the species separation between K. phaffii and K. pastoris and confirming that the CBS 2612 strain was correctly classified as K. phaffii. For the assignment of these unitigs to the according chromosomes, they were aligned to the chromosomes of CBS 7435 ASMa and CBS 7435 ASMb with LastZ (Harris 2007) and named accordingly (Table 3).

Gene Prediction and Annotation

Coding genes were predicted with MAKER2 (Holt and Yandell 2011) for the four chromosomes and the mitochondrial sequence, which resulted in 5172 protein‐coding genes, of which 480 contained introns. To determine the annotation of the genes, they were clustered with CD‐HIT‐EST (Huang et al. 2010; W. Li and Godzik 2006) together with both assemblies of the CBS 7435 strain by iteratively clustering highly similar sequences with > 90% identity. Isoforms (see final list of isoforms in Supplementary Table S5) present in each data set were removed before clustering since they cannot be distinguished in the annotation process. As depicted in Figure 1, sequences were divided into four groups: (a) Complete identity ‐ with 100% sequence identity over 100% sequence length, (b) incomplete length ‐ with 100% sequence identity over any length, (c) incomplete identity ‐ with at least 90% sequence identity over 100% sequence length, and (d) the rest of the sequences with incomplete identity and incomplete length. The split into groups of different sequence identities and sequence lengths was done to enable the identification of genes with differing intron/exon distribution (group b), with alternative start positions (group c) and genes not yet annotated in the other strains (group d). Clustering runs were performed for both CBS 7435 assemblies. Additionally, the results of each clustering step with CBS 7435 ASMa (PRJEA62483) were cross‐checked against the alternative assembly CBS 7435 ASMb (PRJNA304977) to evaluate possible differences between the two assemblies regarding the CBS 2612 (PRJNA1117054) strain. The comparison was made against both assemblies of CBS 7435 because CBS 7435 ASMa is more thoroughly annotated than CBS 7435 ASMb. In contrast, CBS 7435 ASMb has a better sequence quality due to the utilization of long‐read sequencing technology from PacBio.

Of the 5163 predicted coding genes of CBS 2612 (all in‐silico predicted genes without the isoforms), 84% had a homolog with complete sequence identity (group a) in the CBS 7435 ASMa assembly, and 11% a homolog with a sequence identity of 100% but a difference in predicted length (group b). There were no matches for group c. To investigate the differences between the remaining 417 predicted genes in CBS 2612 (group d) and the annotated genes of the CBS 7435 ASMa, those computationally predicted genes from strain CBS 2612 were subsequently subjected to manual curation based on the RNA‐Seq data from Heistinger et al. (2018) (Heistinger et al. 2018) and blast searches. This curation resulted in the split of 130 predicted genes into two or more separate open reading frames, the correction of the computational prediction of 363 genes, the removal of 33 in‐silico predicted genes due to overlaps with annotated genes on the opposite strand or because they were within the centromere (see Table 5), the annotation of 105 additional homologs via reciprocal blast and the addition of 150 genes which were missed by the computational prediction but were backed by the RNA‐Seq data. After manual curation, the gene set of CBS 2612 consists of 5,213 coding genes.

Non‐Coding RNA Prediction

A total of 144 tRNAs were predicted using tRNAScan‐SE [28] and ARAGORN (Laslett 2004), and 3 rRNAs were predicted with barrnap (Seemann 2013). tRNAs classified as “undetermined” by tRNA‐Scan‐SE (Lowe and Chan 2016; Lowe and Eddy 1997) were blasted against Saccharomyces cerevisiae and CBS 7435 tRNAs for determination. Thereby, classification could be assigned for 17 of the 27 undetermined tRNAs, including 4 for the so far missing amino acids: tryptophan and tyrosine, and 9 for serine. Ribosomal RNA prediction resulted in the following subunits: 3x 18S, 7x 28S, 22x 5S and 2×5.8S. Sixteen subunits are located on chromosome I, five on chromosome II, seven on chromosome III and six on chromosome IV (see Supplementary Table S6).

Long Non‐Coding RNA Prediction

Sun et al. (2019) predicted 208 lncRNAs in K. phaffii GS115 in‐silico. Until now, no long non‐coding RNAs were known for other K. phaffii strains. The RNA‐Seq data from Heistinger et al. (2018) was used to determine yet unknown transcripts by facilitating HISAT (Kim et al. 2015) and StringTie (Pertea et al. 2015) for the identification of lncRNAs. As the sequencing material was mRNA‐enriched instead of rRNA‐depleted, only 60% of potentially present long non‐coding RNAs are assumed to be found within this RNA‐seq data (Uchida 2017). From the resulting potential candidates, only those 700 with a length of more than 200 bases and where no homolog to a coding gene could be found in CBS 7435 ASMa were used as input for the candidate search of long non‐coding RNAs by conducting the workflow of Mathew et al. (2019). This analysis resulted in 34 lncRNA candidates. However, five were excluded since they span regions that contain annotated coding genes. The remaining candidates include 21 antisense lncRNAs and 8 sense lncRNAs. 10 of the 29 remaining candidates are intergenic lncRNAs, which are in proximity of genes. lncRNAs play an essential role in regulating gene expression of specific target genes (Sun et al. 2019; Yamashita et al. 2016). Among the possible target genes of the predicted lncRNAs, there is FLO11, which was also reported to be regulated by a lncRNA in S. cerevisiae (Yamashita et al. 2016), and two more flocculin‐related genes, BSC1, which is similar to FLO11, and the putative lectin‐like protein encoded by FLO200. Other putative lncRNA target genes are tRNA, telomere, and ribosome‐related genes (see Supplementary Table S7).

Variant Calling

Further analysis was performed to determine differences between the K. phaffii strains. A variant calling was conducted with C‐Sibelia (Minkin et al. 2013). Overall, the number of variants between the strains was low, with more nucleotide substitutions than nucleotide insertions or deletions (indels) for all comparisons (see Supplementary Table S8). The results also show apparent differences between assemblies, resulting from the different sequencing methods. A more detailed analysis of the variants revealed that most indels were in homopolymer‐repeat regions. Homopolymer repeat errors are a well‐known issue of the 454 Roche sequencing platform (Luo et al. 2012; Margulies et al. 2005; Quince et al. 2009). This circumstance is also supported by comparing the amount of indels in the two CBS 7435 assemblies, where the PacBio‐based assembly shows a significantly lower number of indels compared to the first short‐read‐based assembly (Figure 2).

Number of indels and substitutions over the complete genome and inside coding sequences comparing CBS 2612 versus CBS 7435 (see also Supplementary Table S9).

39 SNPs predicted between CBS 2612 and both CBS 7435 assemblies covering both homopolymer and non‐homopolymer regions were analysed by Sanger sequencing of PCR fragments from CBS 2612 and CBS 7435 genomic DNA (primers are given in Supplementary Table S1). Seven of the predicted SNPs were located in coding regions, while the others were intergenic. Sequence analysis confirmed only 7 of the 39 analysed SNPs (3 thereof in coding regions) as true differences between the CBS 2612 and CBS 7435, while 32 of them were actually present in both strains and are most likely due to sequencing artifacts (see also Supplementary Table S10). The SNPs in coding regions predicted for KP2612_Chr1‐08405 and KP2612_Chr3‐04368 were not confirmed by Sanger sequencing.

The Sibelia output from the comparison of GS115 ASMa and GS115 ASMb was filtered by setting a length threshold of < 20 nt for variants to be able to find SNPs by excluding longer ranges of indels. These comparisons resulted in the identification of 4 SNPs in coding regions (see Table 4) that were present in all comparisons between the CBS 2612 strain and the other K. phaffii assemblies (CBS 7435 and GS115). The SNPs in KP2612_Chr1‐06579 (SRB7) and KP2612_Chr1‐14730 (ESP1) cause a change in one amino acid, while the SNP in KP2612_Chr3‐07151 (ROP100) results in an earlier stop‐codon. PCR of the respective regions followed by Sanger sequencing confirmed their presence in the genome (Table 4). It was recently found that CBS 7435 contains a truncated version of the Golgi alpha‐1,6‐mannosyltransferase Hoc1, involved in N‐glycosylation (Claes et al. 2024; Offei et al. 2022). We can see the SNP leading to the presence of the full‐length gene in CBS 2612, thereby confirming that CBS 7435 is a derivative of the wild‐type lineage. Also, the SNP in Rop100 was clearly confirmed. In contrast, we do not see the SNP leading to a mutation in Rad18 that was described for CBS 2612 in Claes et al. (2024). Also the SNP leading to a mutation of Prp46 in the K. phaffii deposit NRRL Y‐7556 was not found in our study. For Sef1, there is a SNP between CBS 2612 and CBS 7435 ASMa, but not compared to the other assemblies, indicating that is most likely due to a sequencing error in CBS 7435 ASMa. Thus, we confirm the presence of serine (not cysteine) at position 315 for CBS 2612 as well as CBS 7435 ASMb and GS115. Interestingly, we find a SNP in Srb7 causing a non‐synonymous substitution at position 131 also in CBS 2612, while it was only found in the K. phaffii deposit NCYC 2543 (but not in CBS 2612) by Claes et al. 2024. The SNP alters the amino acid sequence from E (glutamic acid in CBS 7435) to Q (glutamine, in CBS 2612). Both E and Q are frequently occurring in Srb7 orthologs in different yeast species, and share similar physicochemical properties (e.g., polar, non‐hydrophobic), suggesting limited impact on protein function.

Centromere Region

In K. phaffii, as in other yeasts (Coughlan et al. 2016), centromeres are non‐transcribed regions. The centromere regions of K. phaffii were identified in 2016 in CBS 7435 ASMa and contain two almost identical copies of a sequence of approximately 2000 bp, separated by 1000 bp (Coughlan et al. 2016). We used the repeat sequences left and right from the centromere published by Coughlan et al. combined with the RNA‐Seq data of Heistinger et al. (Heistinger et al. 2018) to identify the centromeres in CBS 2612 (Table 5). ClustalW v2.1 (Larkin et al. 2007; Thompson et al. 1994) and blastn (Altschul et al. 1990; Camacho et al. 2009) were used to identify the repeat sequences in CBS 2612; the sequences and alignment results can be found in the Supporting files “SuppInfo_File1. fasta” and “SuppInfo_File2. docx”. CEN4 could be matched in both orientations (Figure 3) at the same positions, which was also observed by Coughlan et al. (2016) in K. phaffii CBS 7435.

Discussion

With 9.39 Mb, the genome size of K. phaffii CBS 2612 is similar to the PacBio assembly of CBS 7435 ASMb. Besides chromosome 2, which is 2593 bases shorter compared to CBS 7435 ASMa and 53 bases shorter compared to CBS 7435 ASMb, all other chromosomes and the mitochondria are between 6 and 12,603 bases longer compared to both assemblies of CBS 7435. The minimal differences in the chromosome lengths among the genome sequences of the K. phaffii strains originate in the various sequencing technologies, assembly tools, and, of course, the different assembly ages. However, the genome sequence of CBS 7435 ASMb is missing a repeat compared to this assembly, which is 5572 bp long in chromosome 4. Additionally, in CBS2612, we could identify a region on this chromosome that contains a reversed section at positions 1742528 to 1811407 compared to other strains, which could be connected to the mating type (see Supplementary Figure S5). The variations in the GC contents among the different assemblies are very small. The minor differences can be explained by the bias of Illumina libraries for sequences with a more moderate GC content. This results in an underrepresentation of GC‐rich regions in sequencing projects of genomes with heterogeneous base composition (Tilak et al. 2018).

Seven of the SNP candidates proved genuine by Sanger sequencing. Of the 4 SNPs affecting coding ORFs, three confirmed SNPs were previously reported in other K. phaffii strains. The transcription factor Rop100 contains a stop codon in CBS 7435 ASMa compared to CBS 2612, which results in an additional transmembrane signal peptide in the CBS 2612 version of the gene. Each of the other two confirmed SNPs resulted in a single amino acid change in its respective protein; however, the domain composition of both proteins remained unchanged. Also, the SNP leading to an elongation of Hoc1 was confirmed in our CBS 2612 genome assembly. In contrast, the SNP predicted in Ira1 for NRRL Y‐17741 (not part of this study) in Offei et al. (Offei et al. 2022) could not be found in CBS 2612 in this study. Claes et al. identified 4 further genes with SNPs in different K. phaffii deposits that were not detected in this study. The verification process of other SNPs and InDels via Sanger sequencing also substantiates the assumption that most in‐silico detected SNPs originate in homo‐polymer errors (Luo et al. 2012; Margulies et al. 2005; Quince et al. 2009).

A final reciprocal BLAST and repeated cluster analysis, following the manual corrections, identified 142 coding genes in CBS7435 ASMa that lacked homologs in CBS2612 and 30 coding genes in CBS2612 without corresponding homologs in CBS7435 ASMa. Another reciprocal blast against CBS 7435 ASMb resulted in 3989 homologs and 132 missing matches in CBS 2612, 10 matches were inconclusive. Supplementary Table S11 lists those genes for which no unambiguous or no match at all could be found in CBS 2612, CBS 7435 ASMa or CBS 7435 ASMb, respectively.

The meticulous manual curation of the in‐silico prediction, utilizing RNASeq data, yielded the identification of four (compared to the improved annotation of CBS 7435 ASMa) or nine (compared to CBS 7435 ASMb) new rDNA loci, respectively. This result stems not only from the long read technology but also the use of an additional annotation tool (barrnap) compared to the annotation of CBS 7435 ASMa. As rDNA loci are often located in high repetitive clusters the long read sequencing technology increased the detection efficacy. As a side effect of this study, 164 genes in CBS7435 ASMa will be corrected regarding intron‐exon structure or positions, respectively. 40 genes will be removed from and 113 will be added to the annotation of CBS 7435 ASMa. In contrast, 361 in‐silico predicted genes were corrected in position or intron‐exon structure, and 166 in‐silico indicated genes were subsequently split into multiple open reading frames in CBS 2612.

The finding of lncRNAs regulating the expression of flocculin‐related genes (KP2612_Chr1‐17051 [BSC1], KP2612_Chr3‐13498 [FLO200], KP2612_Chr3‐13520 [FLO5‐2]), suggests that also in K. phaffii lncRNAs are involved in gene regulation in response to environmental changes, as previously shown for S. cerevisiae (Yamashita et al. 2016). Like Till et al. (Till et al. 2018), we found lncRNAs regulating telomere‐related genes (KP2612_Chr3‐13630, KP2612_Chr3‐13645). Our candidates are absent in the publication of Sun et al. (Sun et al. 2019) regarding the lncRNAs in GS115 (208), but not all of the data is available for comparison in the publication. Additionally, lncRNAs are known to be not well conserved (Johnsson et al. 2014).

Despite the exclusion of non‐polyadenylated lncRNAs through the poly(A)‐based mRNA enrichment strategy used during RNA‐Seq library preparation, we were able to identify three lncRNAs not previously reported in K. phaffii. Although literature indicates that in yeast most lncRNAs are polyadenylated, a number of non‐poly(A) lncRNAs exist (Yamashita et al. 2016), which would have been missed in this analysis. Finding more lncRNA candidates in silico would be possible if rRNA‐depleted data were available instead of mRNA‐enriched RNASeq data (Uchida 2017), and by analyzing different environmental conditions.

The centromere region could successfully be identified and positioned by using the known repeat sequences from Coughlan et al. CEN4 could be matched in both orientations in contrast to the centromere repeats on the other three chromosomes. A comparable observation was made by Coughlan et al. (Coughlan et al. 2016).

Conclusion

The availability of comprehensive, high‐quality genome and annotation information of the patent‐free type strain of K. phaffii CBS 2612, presented in this paper, is a prerequisite for further in‐depth research on K. phaffii. Furthermore, we could use this information to further improve and correct the sequence and annotation information of other K. phaffii strains like CBS 7435.
