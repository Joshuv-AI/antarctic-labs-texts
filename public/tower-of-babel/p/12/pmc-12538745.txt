Background

With increasing life expectancy, the world population is gradually aging, with many countries becoming aging societies. Fractures of hip-related areas are common orthopedic conditions among elderly individuals. Prolonged bed rest can easily lead to pulmonary infections, pressure ulcers, lower limb thrombosis, and embolism complications, which threaten life. The most common treatment is hip joint surgery [1–7].

Elderly individuals usually have many diseases, including cardiovascular and cerebrovascular diseases, such as coronary heart disease, diabetes, hypertension, cerebral infarction, and cognitive impairment. In addition, some patients take anticoagulant drugs throughout the year, which makes it difficult to choose anesthesia methods for hip surgery. General anesthesia and neuraxial anesthesia often lead to hemodynamic fluctuations and hypotension, increasing the risk of cardiovascular and cerebrovascular diseases, and anticoagulant drugs also increase the risk of epidural hematoma [2–4, 8–10].

Lumbar plexus blocks combined with sacral plexus nerve blocks have often been used for postoperative analgesia after hip joint surgery. The advancement of ultrasound visualization technology in anesthesia has laid the foundation for the precise implementation of nerve block treatment. This method results in stable intraoperative hemodynamics and is a good choice for hip joint surgery in elderly patients with various diseases. However, on the basis of a comprehensive review of published research, there are significant differences in the clinical application of this method. This article provides a review on this issue.

Neural innervation of the human hip joint

The nerves that innervate the front of the hip joint are the femoral nerve and the obturator nerve of the lumbar plexus branch, whereas the nerves behind it are the superior gluteal nerve and sciatic nerve of the sacral plexus branch [11–14]. The femoral nerve (L2–L4) originates from the lumbar plexus and enters the thigh through the deep surface of the inguinal ligament to innervate the anterior side of the hip joint. The obturator nerve (L2–L4) is divided into anterior and posterior branches, with the anterior branch innervating the medial side of the hip joint (such as the pubococcygeal ligament) and the posterior branch distributed in the lower part of the joint capsule. The sciatic nerve (L4–S3) originates from the sacral plexus and runs along the posterior lateral side of the hip joint, and its branches (such as the inferior gluteal nerve and the quadriceps muscle branch) innervate the posterior lateral side of the joint capsule and some ligaments. The superior gluteal nerve (L4–S1) innervates the gluteus medius and gluteus minor muscles [11–14]. When performing hip joint surgery through a lateral skin incision, it is necessary to block the lateral femoral cutaneous nerve (L2–3) of the lumbar plexus branch, the lateral cutaneous branch of the iliohypogastric nerve (T12 and L1), and the T12 thoracic nerve cutaneous branch [11–14].

Anatomy and operation of a lumbar sacral plexus block

The sacral plexus is composed of the lumbosacral trunk (L4, 5) and the anterior branches of all sacral and caudal nerves. The main branches of the sacral plexus are the superior gluteal nerve (L4, 5, S1), inferior gluteal nerve (L5–S2), pudendal nerve (S2–4), posterior femoral cutaneous nerve (S1–3) and sciatic nerve (L4, 5, S1–3) [2, 3].

The lumbar plexus is composed of the anterior branches of the T12 nerves and the L1–4 nerves. Its branches include the iliopsoas nerve (T12, L1), the ilioinguinal nerve (L1), the genitofemoral nerve (L1, 2), the obturator nerve (L2–4), the lateral femoral cutaneous nerve (L2, 3), and the femoral nerve (L2–4) [2–4].

Sacral plexus block

A low-frequency convex array of 2–5 Hz ultrasound probes is placed at the lower edge level of the inner 1/2 of the line connecting the greater trochanter of the femur and the posterior superior iliac spine. The probe is subsequently moved up and down until the sacral plexus nerve, which appears as a high echo shadow located between the sacrum and iliac bone, is detected [2, 3]. The puncture needle is inserted from the outer side of the ultrasound probe toward the sacral plexus. When the puncture needle reaches the target nerve, plantar flexion movement of the foot is observed, and then local anesthetics are injected when the movement of the foot disappears after adjusting the nerve stimulator current to 0.4 mA [2, 3].

Lumbar plexus block

The lumbar plexus block technique is performed with the affected limb lying in the upper lateral position, using a low-frequency convex array of 2–5 Hz ultrasound probes. The spinous process, articular process, transverse process, psoas major muscle, and lumbar plexus deep to the transverse processes can be seen 4 cm above the intersection of the iliac crest line and the spine. Puncture is performed in the plane to observe the contraction of the quadriceps femoris muscle, and then local anesthetics are injected when anterior thigh movement disappears after adjusting the nerve stimulator current to 0.4 mA [2–4].

The complications of lumbar sacral plexus puncture

The complications of lumbar sacral plexus puncture include puncture injuries (such as nerve injury and abdominal organ injury), local anesthetic toxication, hematoma, and infection at the puncture site [2–4].

Nerve and abdominal organ injuries

Despite the assistance of ultrasound guidance and nerve stimulators, the puncture needle may still directly injure the lumbosacral plexus nerves and abdominal organs. During the operation, gentle movements should be made to avoid excessive punctures. Once nerves are injured, they may lead to sensory and motor dysfunction in the lower extremities [2–4].

Toxic reactions to local anesthetics

If the medication is accidentally injected into the blood vessels during administration, it may cause local anesthetic intoxication. To reduce this risk, careful aspiration should be performed before injection to ensure that the puncture needle is not in the blood vessel. Early symptoms of local anesthetic intoxication may include dizziness, tinnitus, and numbness around the mouth, whereas severe cases may present with convulsions, loss of consciousness, and other symptoms [2, 3, 15].

Hemorrhage

Blood vessels are distributed around the puncture site and may be damaged during the operation, resulting in bleeding. If larger blood vessels are damaged, a hematoma may form [2, 3, 11, 12].

Clinical and research status of lumbar sacral plexus block anesthesia for hip joint surgery

Implementation of lumbar sacral plexus block anesthesia for hip surgery

Patients who undergo hip surgery often suffer from severe pain before the operation. Because nerve block anesthesia needs to be performed in a lateral position, analgesic drugs are required to reduce pain before positioning. Lumbar sacral plexus blocks need to be precisely performed with the assistance of nerve stimulation instruments under ultrasound guidance based on anatomical positioning to avoid serious complications. After the operation, the analgesic scheme needs to be adjusted promptly according to the application of nerve block and local anesthetics to alleviate perioperative pain. Once the effect of nerve block is unsatisfactory or the nerve block operation fails, the anesthesia method should be adjusted promptly to ensure the normal progress of the operation [2, 11, 12].

Comparison of lumbar sacral plexus block anesthesia and other anesthesia methods for hip joint surgery

Lumbar sacral plexus block anesthesia has been compared with general anesthesia and spinal anesthesia on the basis of specific parameters, such as hemodynamics, analgesia time, stress response (C-reactive protein), inflammatory mediators, adrenaline, cortisol, cognitive function (Aβ−100 protein), cardiac function (troponin and atrial natriuretic peptide), renal function (creatinine and urea nitrogen), early and long-term postoperative hip joint function recovery, organ function recovery, and organ function evaluation [16–21]. Compared with other anesthesia methods, the lumbar sacral plexus block has superior performance on a variety of metrics.

Mixed drugs used for lumbar sacral plexus block anesthesia for hip joint surgery included lidocaine, bupivacaine, and ropivacaine, which were mixed at different doses and concentrations. By utilizing the additive or synergistic effects of similar drugs, perfect blocking could be achieved. However, there were significant differences in drug concentrations and dose ratios [2, 3, 22]. In one study, a 20 ml mixture of 0.5% levobupivacaine and 2% lidocaine with epinephrine (1:200,000), at a ratio of 1:1 by volume, was injected individually to block the lumbar and sacral plexuses of 70 patients [2]. In another study, 30 ml of a 60 ml mixture of 0.75% ropivacaine 20 ml, 0.5% bupivacaine 10 ml, 2% lignocaine with adrenaline 10 ml, and saline 20 ml were used for the lumbar plexus block, 20 ml was used for the sacral plexus block, and 10 ml was reserved for the intercostal block at the 11th ICS in 203 patients [3].

Different concentrations and doses of single drugs used in lumbar sacral plexus block anesthesia for hip joint surgery (Table 1)

Some authors reported using a single drug, such as ropivacaine in lumbar sacral plexus blocks, the concentration and dose were titrated to obtain the minimum effective concentration and dose [23–26]. In one study, 0.352% ropivacaine at a volume of 30 ml was titrated to provide a successful single-shot ultrasound-guided transmuscular quadratus lumborum block in 90% of patients who underwent total hip arthroplasty [23].

Lumbar sacral plexus block combined with T12 paraspinal block or local anesthetic infiltration at the surgical site or the application of analgesics for hip joint surgery (Table 1)

Due to the presence of T12 nerve innervation at the hip joint surgical site, some authors reported adding T12 paraspinal block or local anesthetic infiltration at the surgical site, or a small dose of analgesic drug supplement on the basis of lumbar sacral plexus block. In some studies the lumbar plexus blocks were replaced with lumbar nerve root blocks (L1–4), which could also meet surgical requirements [11, 12].

Local anesthetics combined with 1:200,000 adrenaline in lumbar sacral plexus blocks for hip joint surgery (Table 1)

To prevent local anesthetic intoxication, some local anesthetic drugs were mixed with adrenaline (1:200,000) [2].

Application of lumbar sacral plexus block anesthesia in special elderly patients undergoing hip joint surgery

Lumbar sacral plexus blocks in hip joint surgery are currently mostly used for specific groups of elderly patients, such as patients with ankylosing spondylitis, severe cardiovascular and cerebrovascular diseases, and anticoagulant drug use. No complications, such as increased intraoperative bleeding or hematoma at the puncture site, have been reported [11, 15, 27–32]. In four cases of ankylosing spondylitis 30 ml and 20 ml of 0.4% ropivacaine were injected individually for lumbar and sacral plexus blocks, respectively [11]. In an 87-year-old woman with severe aortic stenosis and fracture of the right trochanter due to a fall, 15 ml and 10 ml of 0.5% ropivacaine were injected individually into the paravertebral lumbar plexus and parasacral sciatic nerve block, respectively, to finish the operation [15].

Future research directions for lumbar sacral plexus block anesthesia in hip joint surgery

With the development of ultrasound visualization technology, lumbar sacral plexus nerve block methods may become increasingly simple and accurate in the future, and lumbar sacral plexus block methods should be explored on the basis of human anatomy [33–36].

Randomized controlled studies should be conducted to evaluate the onset time, duration of analgesia, and organ function recovery in patients undergoing hip surgery with lumbar sacral plexus block anesthesia using different concentrations and volumes of local anesthetics. These studies would help determine the optimal local anesthetic concentration and dosage for clinical use.

Blood and cerebrospinal fluid concentrations of local anesthetics should be monitored during lumbar sacral plexus nerve block anesthesia to prevent the occurrence of local anesthetic poisoning effectively [37].

Local anesthetic concentration and volume should be selected on the basis of different local anesthetic drug interaction mechanisms to optimize the combination of local anesthetics and reduce the occurrence of complications.

Conclusions

Lumbar plexus block combined with sacral plexus nerve block maintains stable intraoperative hemodynamics and is a good choice for hip joint surgery in elderly patients with various diseases. However, there are significant differences in the concentration and volume of local anesthetics in published reports.
