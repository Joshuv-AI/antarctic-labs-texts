The evolution of Australian cannabis policies, 1967 to 2024
Authors: Wayne D Hall, Danielle Dawson, Ben Johnson, Tesfa M Yimer
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractAimTo describe the outcomes of cannabis policy debates in Australia from 1967 to 2024.MethodsWe searched popular media, public reports, parliamentary inquiries and policy documents on cannabis in Australia since the 1960s and examined peer reviewed papers, national survey data and police records of cannabis‐related arrests.ResultsWe describe three phases in the development of Australian cannabis policy: a prolonged debate (1970–1999) about whether state governments should remove criminal penalties for cannabis possession and use; a less prominent but overlapping debate (1994–2016) about whether Australia should legally permit the medical use of cannabis; and a renewed debate (1999–2023) about whether adults should be allowed to use cannabis for non‐medical purposes and obtain it from a regulated legal market. The legalisation of medical cannabis in 2016, and the progressive liberalisation of its regulation, appear to have increased public support for adult cannabis legalisation in Australia and encouraged several so far unsuccessful attempts to pass legislation to do so.ConclusionsCriminal penalties for personal possession and use of cannabis have been removed in smaller states and territories of Australia, but the more populous states have diverted persons charged with cannabis use offences into education and treatment. The legalisation of medical cannabis use in 2016 appears to have increased public support for the legalisation of adult use, but there are major political obstacles to this policy change.Keywords: adult legalisation, arrests, Australia, cannabis policy debates, cannabis possession decriminalisation, diversion, medical cannabis

INTRODUCTION

Australia prohibited the non‐medical use of cannabis by adults nationally in 1967. Since then, debates about the merits of this policy have mirrored policy debates in other English‐speaking countries that share political cultures derived from their founding as settler colonies of the United Kingdom (UK) and that have shown very similar trends in illicit drug use, namely Canada, the United Kingdom and the United States (US). In this article, we describe the evolution of Australian policies toward non‐medical and medical use of cannabis from 1967 until 2024.

We describe major policy debates and their outcomes in terms of changes in criminal penalties for cannabis use, the implementation of diversion programs, medical cannabis legalisation and recent proposals to legalise adult cannabis use. We analyse publicly available national survey data and government reports on key quantitative indicators including cannabis use patterns, public attitudes toward different cannabis policies, cannabis‐related arrests and trends in medical cannabis prescriptions. We also examine how different state jurisdictions have responded to cannabis policy debates by tracking both successful and unsuccessful attempts at legal reform. We pay particular attention to the social and political factors that influenced policy outcomes in different Australian states and territories. Search strategy

We searched the scholarly and grey literature for government reports, parliamentary inquiries, policy advocacy documents, contemporaneous media accounts and histories of Australian cannabis policy during the period 1967 to 2024. We searched PubMed (including MEDLINE), Web of Science, Scopus, Google Scholar, records of the National Library of Australia (including its digital archive, TROVE), Criminal Justice Abstracts, the Australian Criminology Database and the website of the Australian Criminal Intelligence Commission (and its legacy websites). A comprehensive search was conducted in March 2024 and has been updated regularly since then. We used the following search terms: cannabis (and its variant terms), cannabis policy, Australia (including its states and territories), cannabis decriminalisation, cannabis legalisation and medical cannabis. We also conducted Factiva‐based searches (https://global.factiva.com/) of three leading metropolitan newspapers (The Age, The Canberra Times and the Sydney Morning Herald) between 1970 and 2023 to map the timing and content of articles on national debates about cannabis policy in Australia over the study period. We identified key themes and arguments about cannabis policy and legislative changes in the media and policy documents.

The origins of Australian cannabis prohibition

In Australia's federal system, state governments are responsible for criminal laws on the personal possession and use of cannabis and other drugs, and the Commonwealth government is responsible for Australia's compliance with United Nations (UN) drug control treaties. After Indian hemp was included along with opiates and cocaine as drugs under international control under the Geneva Convention of 1925, most Australian state governments made it a criminal offence to possess, use and traffic in cannabis [1, 2, 3]. Cannabis use was not prohibited at the national level, however, until 1967 when the Australian federal government passed legislation to comply with the 1961 UN Single Convention on Narcotic Drugs [2, 4]. This legislative change occurred around the time that the popular media first reported non‐medical cannabis use among young Australians.

TRENDS IN CANNABIS USE AND CANNABIS‐RELATED ARRESTS

The first mentions of recreational cannabis use in Australia in the 1960s were in tabloid newspapers and underground newsletters, which reported that young people were smoking cannabis that grew wild in the Hunter Valley of New South Wales (NSW) [5]. Cannabis use among young adults reportedly became more widespread after 1967 when US servicemen in Vietnam began to spend ‘rest and recreation’ leave in the red‐light district of Kings Cross in Sydney [2]. Young adults who visited Kings Cross spread cannabis use into Sydney's suburbs in the early 1970s [6] and cannabis use increased during the 1980s [7].

In the absence of national population surveys of drug use in the 1960s, policy makers responded to the rising number of cannabis arrests. These began to increase initially in NSW where the annual number of arrests for cannabis possession increased from 57 in 1966 to 365 in 1970 and 4300 in 1977 [2]. A conservative Liberal National Party coalition government responded by increasing criminal penalties for cannabis possession [2]. National arrest data for cannabis offences (that only became available in 1970) show a steady increase in per capita cannabis arrests in all states during the 1980s that peaked in the early 1990s before stabilising at a lower, but still substantial level (see Figure 1).

As an illicit cannabis market developed in the early 1970s, more potent herbal cannabis and cannabis resin were smuggled into Australia from Southeast Asia (primarily by sea) and sold at higher prices than local cannabis [6]. By the late 1970s potent forms of herbal cannabis were supplied by illicit domestic cultivation, some of which was controlled by criminal organisations [2]. The police use of aerial surveillance to identify outdoor plantations shifted production to indoor, hydroponic cultivation [2]. By the early 1990s, the Australian cannabis market was largely supplied by indoor hydroponic cultivation, but more potent imported hashish was still seized in substantial quantities (e.g. 478 kg in 1991 and 7158 kg in 1992) [23, 24].

The size of the illicit cannabis market appears to have increased in Australia in the 1980s and 1990s [5, 25, 26]. Jiggens [5] estimated that the economic value of cannabis in Australia increased from $550 million in 1982 to $3 billion by 1998. A Parliamentary Committee [25] produced a very similar estimate in 1988. In its 2020 to 2021 Illicit Drug Report [27], the Australian Criminal Intelligence Commission reported that the total amount of border seizures had increased between 2011 and 2012 and 2020 to 2021 from 17 kg to 819 kg and national seizures had increased from 7349 kg to 19 787 kg.

THE EVOLUTION OF AUSTRALIAN CANNABIS POLICIES

We follow Hughes [28] in dividing Australian cannabis policy debates into three overlapping periods: a long debate between 1970 and 1999 about whether criminal penalties should apply for the personal possession and use of cannabis; an overlapping debate from the early 1990s to 2016 about whether patients should be allowed to use cannabis to treat medical disorders; and a renewed debate since the late 1990s on whether Australia should follow the example of Canada and some US states by legalising adult non‐medical cannabis use.

Cannabis decriminalisation debates 1970 to 1999

In the late 1960s cannabis use was a mark of social non‐conformism and rebellion among Australia youth [5], as it was earlier in the United States [29]. The dominant political response from the state conservative governments then in power (see text Box 2) was to increase criminal penalties for possession with the aim of deterring cannabis use. Doubts about this policy began to be expressed in the mid‐1970s when the number of cannabis arrests increased steeply among young Australians and included middle‐class university students. Backbench members of the federal Labor government in power between 1972 and 1975 [2] and civil libertarians [5] argued that criminal penalties for personal possession caused more harm than cannabis use itself. They cited the Wootton Committee in the United Kingdom [30], the Le Dain Commission in Canada [31] and the Shafer Commission in the United States in 1972 [32] to support the proposal that criminal penalties for personal use of cannabis should either be removed or very substantially reduced [2].

In 1975 the federal Labor government, which was responsible for criminal laws in the Australian Capital Territory (ACT), replaced criminal penalties in the territory for possessing less than 25 g of cannabis with a fine of $100 [2]. In 1977, the Senate Standing Committee on Social Welfare in the federal parliament (the Baume Committee) [33] also recommended that penalties for personal cannabis use be reduced to ensure that legal controls did not ‘inhibit rehabilitation’, cause ‘more social damage than the use of the drug’ (p. 145). A 1977 discussion paper by the South Australian Royal Commission into the Non‐medical Use of Drugs [34] similarly recommended that criminal penalties for cannabis use and possession be removed for adults [35]. Its final report, however, proposed that persons arrested for cannabis possession should be referred to Drug Assessment and Aid Panels that would decide whether the person needed to undertake treatment for up to 6 months [36].

In 1977, the campaign to remove criminal penalties for cannabis use suffered a setback after the widely publicised murder of an anti‐cannabis campaigner, Donald McKay, in Griffith NSW, a centre of illicit cannabis cultivation [2]. In response to McKay's disappearance, the NSW Labor government and a federal Liberal National Party Coalition (LNP) government each established Royal Commissions into drug trafficking both of which concluded that Australia had a large illicit cannabis market. Each commission recommended increased funding for law enforcement [2], but did not recommend any change in penalties for cannabis possession. In the 1980s, state governments from both political parties increased criminal penalties for drug trafficking while retaining criminal penalties for personal possession of cannabis. With the exception of Queensland, the most politically conservative state, all states reduced the length of prison sentences for cannabis possession offences [2].

South Australia under a Labor government was the first Australian state to decriminalise cannabis possession in 1987 when it implemented the Cannabis Expiation Notice Scheme (CEN) [2]. Under the CEN cannabis possession and use and the cultivation of up to 10 cannabis plants (later reduced to 2 plants) for personal use, were penalised by a fine. If the fine was paid (‘expiated’), the person did not receive a criminal conviction [2].

In the early 1990s, in response to very high rates of cannabis arrests, the Ministerial Council on Drugs (MCDS), a committee of federal and state health and law enforcement ministers, established a National Task Force on Cannabis (NTFC) that comprised of state and federal health and law enforcement officials. In its 1994 report, the NTFC proposed that the ‘possession, unsanctioned cultivation, sale and non‐therapeutic use of cannabis in any quantity remain illegal’ [37], but proposed that state and territory governments ‘consider discontinuing…criminal penalties for the simple personal use or possession of cannabis’.

The Northern Territory was the only jurisdiction that implemented the Task Force's recommendations. In 1995, its conservative government passed legislation modelled on the CEN in South Australia and similar legislation passed in the ACT in 1992 [38].

In the 1990s, governments in Queensland (Labor) and Victoria (LNP) rejected expert committee recommendations to decriminalise or reduce criminal penalties for cannabis possession [39, 40]. A proposal to decriminalise cannabis use in NSW advocated at a Drug Summit was rejected by a Labor government [41], which introduced a police‐cautioning scheme for cannabis possession instead [42].

In 2004, a newly elected Labor government in Western Australia legislated to decriminalise cannabis use [43]. The bill included fines for possession and cultivation of small quantities of cannabis for personal use, but allowed those who were unable to pay fines to undertake counselling or treatment [43]. The policy was reversed in 2008 after the election of a (conservative) LNP government, which instead implemented a diversion program for cannabis offenders [28].

A national drug diversion program

In 1997, a recently elected LNP federal government responded to intense media coverage of heroin overdose deaths [41] by introducing a National Illicit Drug Strategy (NIDS) called ‘Tough on Drugs’, which promised ‘zero tolerance towards illicit drug use’ [41, 44]. In 1999, the Council of Australian Governments (COAG) (consisting of the prime minister and state premiers) agreed to implement a National Drug Diversion Initiative in which all persons charged with a first offence of possessing an illicit drug would be diverted into education and counselling. The majority of those who qualified for diversion were arrested for cannabis possession in states that retained criminal penalties. The states varied in how they implemented the diversion scheme, making it difficult to evaluate its impact [45, 46]. Diversion was generally offered at the discretion of the arresting officer, and cannabis offences continued to account for 70% of all drug arrests [47].

Cannabis policy in the 2000s

In November 2004, concerns about increased youth cannabis use among federal LNP politicians, then in power, led the MCDS to commission an expert group to develop a National Cannabis Policy. The resulting policy had four aims: (1) to educate the community—and young people in particular—about the adverse health effects of cannabis and its legal status; (2) to prevent cannabis use among young people by reducing its availability and reducing the social vulnerabilities of those most likely to use cannabis; (3) to prevent problems related to cannabis use among young people by encouraging cessation and reducing regular use among current users; and (4) to provide more effective help for cannabis users seeking help to stop.

After Western Australia's 2008 repeal of the 2004 expiation scheme, there were no legislative changes in state or federal cannabis policies until 2016 [28]. Rates of cannabis arrests remained high during this period, after declining from a peak in the late 1990s (see Figure 1). The use of diversion declined in NSW, Queensland, Tasmania, Victoria and Western Australia so more persons who were arrested for cannabis possession appeared before the courts [48]. The decline in diversion seems to have occurred because it was administratively easier for police officers to charge a cannabis offender than to divert them [28]. From 2010/2011 to 2014/2015, almost 95% of cases prosecuted in courts were found guilty of drug use or possession [48], but only 3.8% were incarcerated in correctional facilities or given suspended sentences. Most (96.2%) received fines or community supervision [48].

Debates about legalising medicinal cannabis 1992 to 2022

In the mid‐1990s, advocates from the Australian gay community proposed that the criminal law be changed to allow patients to use cannabis to treat symptoms of AIDS‐related illnesses [49, 50]. In 2000, the NSW Labor premier responded by appointing an advisory committee (chaired by the first author), which recommended that patients with cancer and HIV/AIDs should be permitted to use medical cannabis [50]. This recommendation (and similar recommendations by state parliamentary inquiries in the 2010s) was not implemented because the Australian Medical Association and leading medical colleges opposed the medical use of cannabis (see text Box 3) [51].

In February 2016, a conservative LNP federal government decided to legalise cannabis for medical use after a Victorian Law Reform Commission report recommended this step [51]. This change was brought about by the advocacy of parents who reported that cannabis relieved their children's intractable epilepsy [51, 52]. The media sympathetically reported these cases [52, 53] and provided positive accounts of medical cannabis programs in the United States, Canada, Israel and the Netherlands as models that Australia could follow.

The Australian federal government amended the Poisons Standard [54] to allow doctors to prescribe cannabis under the Therapeutic Goods Act, administered by Australia's medicines regulator, the Therapeutic Goods Administration (TGA). The Narcotic Drugs Act was also amended to enable an Office of Drug Control to license companies to cultivate and manufacture medical cannabis products [52]. The state governments of NSW, Queensland and Victoria passed matching legislation [51, 55].

The Australian Medical Association, the Royal Australian and New Zealand College of Psychiatrists and the Faculty of Pain Medicine (FPM) advised their members not to prescribe cannabis [56]. They argued that there was little evidence to support the medical use of cannabis and more effective treatments were available for those medical conditions even in those cases where there was some evidence of efficacy for cannabis [56, 57]. Many patients accordingly reported that it was difficult to find doctors prepared to prescribe cannabis [58] and there were very few cannabis prescriptions in the period 2016 to 2018 (see Figure 4).

In July 2018, federal and state governments created an online portal that enabled requests for a prescription to be concurrently assessed by Commonwealth and state authorities [59] and generally approved within 48 hours [56]. Family physicians were also authorised to prescribe a wide range of cannabis products for a broad range of medical indications and private for‐profit medical cannabis clinics began to prescribe cannabis via telehealth consultations [60]. These changes were followed by steep increases in the number of approved cannabis prescriptions that began in 2019 and increased sharply between 2020 and 2025 (Figure 4) [61, 62].

By 2022, the TGA had approved the use of more than 250 medical cannabis products. These included high Δ‐9‐tetrahydrocannabinol (THC) herbal cannabis [56] and other cannabis products used by recreational users in the United States, such as ‘bubble hash’, vapes and edibles [63, 64]. Many of these prescriptions were used to treat medical conditions for which there was weak evidence of safety and effectiveness from controlled trials, namely chronic pain (50%) and anxiety (27%) [65, 66, 67].

A renewed debate about adult cannabis legalisation

In the early 1990s, leading lawyers [68, 69, 70, 71] and physicians [70] had argued that the legalisation of cannabis production and retail sale for adult use would better protect public health and reduce drug‐related crime than cannabis prohibition. Their arguments were strongly opposed by leading, health professionals and researchers (e.g. Nahas and Latour, Walters) [72, 73]. They also did not attract majority public support in surveys (see Figure 5). Proposals to legalise cannabis began to attract more political support in the early 2020s [74, 75] after adult cannabis use had been legalised in 25 US states [76] and Canada in 2018 [77, 78]. Adult legalisation still does not enjoy majority public support (see Figure 5), but recent national surveys show a steady increase in support among Australians 14 years old and over. In 2019, support for legalisation (41%) overtook opposition (37%) for the first time [17, 75], and by 2022/2023 more adults supported (45%) adult legalisation than supported increased penalties for cannabis use (39%) [81].

Legislative attempts to legalise adult cannabis use 2018–2024

The legalisation of the production of cannabis for retail sale to adults in Australia requires changes in both federal and state legislation. The Commonwealth must remove the prohibition on non‐medical production, sale and use of cannabis. State governments must remove criminal penalties for personal use and legalise cannabis production and sale.

In 2018, the Greens party's policy platform included the legalisation of adult cannabis use for the first time [82]. In 2019, a Liberal‐Green coalition government in the ACT, legalised adult cannabis use to the extent permitted under Australia's federal system by removing criminal penalties for personal use, possession and cultivation of cannabis for personal use. These changes were implemented in 2020 [83]. The Legalise Marijuana Party introduced similar legislation in NSW, Victoria and Western Australia, but none of these bills passed [84, 85]. In 2023, the Greens introduced legislation into the Senate in the federal parliament to legalise non‐medical adult cannabis use [86]. The majority of a Senate Committee that examined the bill recommended against it being passed [87] and the bill was rejected in November 2024.

CONJECTURES ABOUT FACTORS INFLUENCING AUSTRALIAN CANNABIS POLICY

Our outline of the history of cannabis policy in Australia is necessarily provisional because there is limited archival documentation on the discussions that contributed to the formulations of policies within federal and state governments and bureaucracies. This article has been written to encourage historians, political scientists and drug policy analysts to undertake the detailed research needed to better understand the evolution of Australian cannabis policy. In the interim, we conclude with some conjectures about factors that have influenced Australian cannabis policy debates over the period of study.

In the late 1960s, state cannabis policies were driven by concerns about the adverse health and social effects of cannabis use on young people. The conservative LNP governments then in power federally, and in most states, believed that cannabis use was harmful and that increased criminal penalties were the best way to deter young people from using it. These governments accordingly increased criminal penalties for personal use. By the mid to late 1970s, the spread of cannabis to middle class youth and high rates of arrests for cannabis possession led civil libertarians and Labor politicians to propose that Australia should reduce criminal penalties for the possession of cannabis because criminal records produced more harm than cannabis use itself.

South Australia, Western Australia and the ACT and Northern Territory were the only jurisdictions that removed criminal penalties. These changes generally occurred under Labor governments. De facto decriminalisation was the preferred policy in the most populous Australian states, even in states with Labor governments, such as NSW and Queensland (in 1983). De jure decriminalisation was seen as lacking public support so de facto decriminalisation proved a more politically acceptable policy that retained criminal penalties in law while reducing the severity of the penalties that courts imposed on young people charged with cannabis possession [2, 88]. The major weakness in facto decriminalisation has been a decline in the police use of diversion over the past decade. The absence of legislative change in cannabis policy between 1999 and 2016 suggests that de facto and de jure decriminalisation reduced the political pressure for more radical policy changes. The continuing high rates of cannabis possession arrests does not seem to have produced as much public concern in this period as in the 1970s. One likely reason is that in the 2000s cannabis use declined among middle class youth and became concentrated among less well educated youth [89]. Another possibilibity is that persons arrested for cannabis possession had often been charged with other criminal offences that made them ineligible for police diversion.

The cannabis policy stasis that characterised the four decades since 1970 seems to have been disrupted by the decision in 2016 by federal and state governments to allow the medical use of cannabis as an unapproved medicine. The medical program began by limiting prescribing to specialist health practitioners who were encouraged to prescribe pharmaceutical cannabinoids for short list of medical conditions where there was evidence of safety and efficacy. Complaints from patient advocacy groups about the difficulty in accessing medical cannabis led to the progressive liberalisation of the program. These changes allowed general practitioners to prescribe a diverse range of cannabis products for a broad range of medical indications, including many where there was limited evidence on safety and efficacy, such as chronic pain and anxiety and sleep disorders. There are indications that some for profit clinics within Australia's medical cannabis program have implemented a de facto form of adult cannabis legalisation, as some argue occurred in Canada and some states in the United States [90].

The dramatic increase in cannabis medical prescriptions in Australia since 2019 has been accompanied by increased public support for the legalisation of adult cannabis use. A similar phenomenon occurred in the United States for the reasons outlined by Kilmer and McCoun [90], namely, the perception that drugs that are used as medicines are safe; the promotional activities of the medical cannabis industry, which has emphasised the benefits and discounted the harms of cannabis use; and the public growing accustomed to the presence of cannabis retail outlets and the use of cannabis for medical reasons by family, friends and acquaintances.

The liberalisation of Australia's medical cannabis program after 2018 has arguably made it easier to advocate for the legalisation of adult cannabis use. As happened earlier in Canada and the United States [90], the Australian medical cannabis program appears to be gradually evolving into a de facto legal cannabis market in which adults can obtain a prescription (via a telehealth consultation) that allows them to legally purchase high THC herbal cannabis for inhalation at a price that is competitive with the illicit market. These changes have made the legalisation of adult cannabis use a smaller policy change than was the case while all cannabis use was illegal.

Increased public support for legalisation has prompted smaller political parties in federal and state parliaments to unsuccessfully introduce legislation to legalise adult cannabis use. Major political obstacles remain to adult cannabis legalisation. The platforms of the two political parties that have traditionally formed government in Australia's state and federal parliaments, the Liberal‐National Party Coalition and the Australian Labor Party, both oppose the legalisation of adult cannabis use [88] and the Australian political system does not allow citizen‐initiated referenda [91], the mechanism used in some states in the United States to legalise medical and adult cannabis use since 2012 [76, 78].
