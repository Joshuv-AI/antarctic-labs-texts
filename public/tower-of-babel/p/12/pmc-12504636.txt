Eco-Inspired terraform networks emerge from material self-organization
Authors: Matteo Convertino, Enza Migliore, Amedeo Martines
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractIn natural ecosystems, large-scale structure and function arise from the spatial coordination of local habitat shaped by environmental pressures. Replicating such multiscale organization in synthetic systems—where emergent form and function are governed solely by material composition—remains a fundamental challenge in design and engineering. Here, we developed an experimental, analytical, and computational framework to create self-organizing material patterns—Terraforms—using a ternary mixture of clay, water, and binder subjected to uniform mechanical pressure. These networked patterns form instantaneously at material interfaces and exhibit 3D scale-free organization, positioned between random and regular configurations, mimicking diverse ecological patterns, with planar symmetry and microtopographic fractality. Systematic variation of relative material proportions (RMPs) revealed a critical regime—50% clay, 15–20% water, and 30–35% binder—with a water-to-binder (W/B) ratio close to the Pacioli golden ratio (1.62), yielding Pareto-optimal patterns. Within this regime, network structures minimize eco-stress, maximize hygroscopic capacity, and enhance systemic stability, thereby enabling their quantification. Transitions between pattern topologies follow gradients in the W/B ratio, consistent with a Fibonacci sequence, marking metastable states between disorder and order. Identical W/B ratios can produce distinct topologies, indicating multistability under fixed composition. In Terraforms, the W/B ratio governs short- and long-range bonding, analogous to ecological interactions, while clay acts as a foundational agent, comparable to habitat-forming species. Our results show that complex, functionally optimized network structures can emerge solely from material interactions dictated by RMPs, without systemic control, biological processes, or environmental heterogeneity. Introduction

“It’s in the anomalies that nature reveals its secrets.” Johann Wolfgang von Goethe.

Nature’s intelligence and the architecture of ecosystem patterns

Nature continually surprises us with the emergence of intricate patterns—whether signaling new species population organization and features, marking abrupt seasonal transitions, or revealing chronic environmental anomalies that may serve as early warnings for tipping points in ecohydrogeomorphological systems. Equally, such pattern emergence can arise serendipitously through experimentation, as occurred in our own material-based trials.

Historically, patterns have inspired thinkers and creators alike. Leonardo da Vinci’s illustrations for De Divina Proportione1, authored by Luca Pacioli, embodied the golden ratio as a principle of divine aesthetic and structural harmony2–4 in forms’ proportions. Echoing this, Gould and Lewontin5 reflected on how architectural forms—like the spandrels of San Marco—exemplify eco-evolutionary optimality, though formal analytical and empirical frameworks for this concept remain elusive. Scale invariance in ecosystems has long intrigued ecologists, yet triggering such invariance intentionally, especially in large-scale systems, remains a poorly understood frontier6.

Recent applied efforts have begun to explore biologically inspired material processes in eco-environmental and architectural contexts—using bacteria for dune stabilization7, or as agents of self-healing concrete and adaptive building foundations8,9. Despite these advances, the bulk of research remains focused on identifying and predicting ecosystem patterns10–17, rather than distilling design rules grounded in material constituents and ecological mechanics.

Nature’s intelligence—manifest through its adaptive, responsive, and evolving capabilities—is embodied in the organization of material flows and spatial configurations18–24. Among its clearest expressions are emergent spatial patterns. Yet, the influence of relative material proportions in governing such patterns—and the possibility of steering them through design—remains understudied. Human intelligence, crucially, lies not only in interpreting natural patterns but in learning how to co-engineer responsive systems by deriving new mechanisms from nature rather than mimicking surface patterns’ features.

Toward controlled self-emergence and functional ecosystem design

A central challenge in both natural and engineered systems is understanding how to guide self-emerging structures toward desired systemic functions. This involves not only unpacking the complexity of self-organizing systems but also identifying the critical structural elements and material distributions that control function. Bridging complexity with controllability is essential for enabling purposeful intervention in dynamic ecosystems at any scale.

Some studies suggest that reaction-diffusion or wave-like patterns emerge from predefined forcing networks—such as the spontaneous formation of Turing patterns observed in nature (where networks are encoded into the environmental heterogeneity)10,11,13,15–17—and from multiplex networks25–28.

To challenge this view, we asked: can “divine or golden proportions”1–4 arise from ecological mechanisms of self-emergence without imposed network structures? Can they be fabricated and interpreted through nature’s intelligent material organization logic, particularly in systems governed by water and related flows like sediment and carbon flows29–31? This study explores whether complexity can be synthesized and engineered by shaping multifunctional materials—specifically, whether the self-emergence of n-phasic complexes with precisely tuned system-level behaviors (i.e., terraforming) can be triggered by altering the proportions of material components.

Through a series of exploratory experiments, we developed a scale-free material recipe and examined its deviations from this property (Figs. 1 and 2), 3 given that scale-freeness is a hallmark of many natural systems that exhibit optimal or metastable states. When we applied pressure to clay derived from natural habitats using rubber tiles, emergent networked patterns appeared. These outcomes mirrored the structure of natural habitats—revealing the potential to build functional 3D geometries by simply adjusting material parameters. This process enabled the construction of physically embodied design prototypes, encoding ecological principles into tangible, scalable forms.

From environmental stress patterns to fractal Terraforms

Ecosystem pattern formation has historically been siloed within distinct eco-environmental domains—focusing on river hydrogeomorphology30–39, species distribution17, or climate systems. More recently, hyperuniform patterns have been identified in vegetation and sediment structures, even on extraterrestrial surfaces like Mars40,41. Parallel investigations have explored regime shifts, ecosystem stability10,11,15,16,42, and entropy-driven organization43, yet integrative models that explain how structure emerges across topologies and scales remain scarce.

Engineered systems often prioritize unique or local outcomes over systemic44, overlooking how emergent patterns reflect systemic responses to environmental pressure. In contrast, natural networks—whether hydrological, biological, or atmospheric—form as stress-distribution architectures, shaped by interactions among hydrodynamic forces, material composition, and historical environmental heterogeneity captured as information flows of ecosystems’ components45. These networks organize themselves into canonical topologies (random, regular, or hybrid), depending on the balance between environmental pressures and the collective adaptability of ecosystems’ components (e.g., water, sediments, species).

In this context, patterns encode information. They emerge from distributed stress and reflect how materials coordinate spatially and temporally. By interpreting these signals, we unlock a new paradigm of ecological design as information processing, where structure arises not from imposition but from responsiveness driven by relative material proportions. Notably, fractal structures—which exhibit scale invariance and power-law scaling of structural and functional features—represent optimally efficient systems. These configurations distribute stress across long- and short-range interactions, enhancing adaptive capacity while minimizing energy expenditure.

Building on these insights, our experiments demonstrate that triphasic clay–water–binder mixtures—prototypes of natural materials—spontaneously generate diverse, fractal-like patterns under uniform pressure. These arise not from pre-set forms, but from interactions defined by Relative Material Proportions (RMP). Their evolution, including pruning toward or away from optimal fractality, reveals how stability and function shift in relation to ecological balance. These processes offer a powerful platform for eco-environmental prototyping—where emergent patterns reflect not only form but function.

We propose Terraforms: synthetic, emergent ecosystems made from bio-materials that respond to ecological interactions. They embody aesthetic principles, ecological intelligence, and functional adaptability, offering applications ranging from life-origin modeling to eco-hydrological infrastructure. Their spontaneous form-generation reveals the potential for co-creation with nature, where low-tech, low-energy, circular design defines a new era of regenerative, resilient ecosystems.

Results and discussion

Relative material proportions as the blueprint of pattern emergence

The triphasic empirical model developed here demonstrates how Relative Material Proportions (RMP)—defined by the content of clay (biomass), water, and binder—serve as the blueprint for the emergence of diverse spatial patterns, including scale-free (SF) and non-fractal forms. Unlike traditional mechanistic, computational, or pattern-matching models that aim to replicate known geometries, this model is based on direct material interactions and emergent organization to fabricate distinct topologies along RMP gradients (Figs. 1 and 2, S1, and S2). Patterns are not imposed but instead arise spontaneously through the interaction of materials under uniform pressure, independent of external shape constraints, opening direction, or substrate material (Figs. 1, and S1-S6). Yet, RMP drives materials’ self-organization, resulting in eco-inspired patterns.

Across more than 100 experimental realizations with various substrates (rubber, clay, glass, iron, etc.), Terraform structures formed instantaneously with characteristic fractal and non-fractal geometries (Fig. 2), governed solely by the underlying RMP (see Table 1 and Figure S2). These configurations emerged regardless of substrate geometry or boundary conditions, underscoring the primacy of internal material composition over external forcing. Notably, scale-free patterns (SF) consistently optimized spatial stress distribution (Eqs. 3 and 4 for estimated local and systemic stress) and minimized energy dissipation under uniform pressure conditions (Figs. 4 and 5; Eqs. 4 and 6), establishing them as optimal topologies (with different motifs along a W/B gradient as shown in Figs. 2 and 4, and 5) from both structural and functional perspectives.

The complex material behavior—what we refer to as a complexus—arises not from additive material properties but from emergent, multiplicative high-order interactions governed by RMP. When proportions are precisely tuned (particularly around 50% clay and a specific water/binder ratio), micro- and macro-structure evolve in synchrony. Microstructures display chaotic motif diversity, yet the macro-topology remains predictably scale-free—an instantiation of the “commonality of rarity and rarity of commonality” observed in ecological systems (Figs. 2, S4-S6). This means that while system-level topology remains stable across realizations, the microgeometry varies chaotically, reflecting aleatoric uncertainty inherent even in deterministic systems.

The emergence of optimal fractal geometries at specific RMPs aligns with well-known scaling laws and mathematical constants. When water-to-binder (W/B) ratios follow the Fibonacci sequence, the resulting pattern exhibits golden-ratio symmetry—a condition of structural equilibrium between pressure and response fields. These conditions correspond to the golden proportion first articulated by Pacioli and observed by the German mathematician Simon Jacob46 in the convergence of Fibonacci numbers. Under these golden conditions, pattern elements scale allometrically: network area scales with pressure dissipation (see Figure S9 for the relationship between the Fibonacci sequence on W/B and the Information Dimension of patterns) in a manner equivalent to Kleiber’s law in ecosystems47,48, with an exponent near ¾ for scale-free networks.

Importantly, the stress encoded in the system is an intrinsic property of the RMP-defined response capacity (Eqs. 3, 4, 6, and 7) rather than the result of post-pattern evolution. Unlike simulated annealing or geological pattern evolution, Terraform structures manifest instantaneously—“frozen” into their topology at the moment of material interaction. This pattern encoding makes it possible to deduce internal stress flows and systemic energy dissipation directly from spatial configuration—a novel approach to material intelligence and ecohydrological analysis.

Furthermore, interfacial patterns (formed on the opposing pressure and response tiles) exhibit 3D symmetry only under optimal RMPs (Figures S4-S6). A 3D scale-free symmetry emerges only at specific Relative Material Proportions (RMPs) — C = 50%, W = 15–20%, B = 30–35% — which optimize the trade-off between local eco-stress (local force/area as in Eq. 3 and cumulated along the network as in Eq. 4) and water retention (Eq. 6, and Figs. 4 and 5, S4-S6, and S8). For non-optimal patterns, this symmetry breaks down, indicating functional inefficiencies. Such asymmetries mirror imbalances in ecosystems, where deviation from optimal species or water ratios results in reduced systemic functionality. Motifs function as ecotonal archetypes (that are recurring structures or dynamics that explain how systems behave at the interface), where the organization of smaller structural units defines the emergent systemic topology. The resulting coordinated geometry reveals the influence of underlying mechanics. When geometry arises purely as a byproduct of design, it can still shape mechanical properties at the system scale—as highlighted by Carpinteri and Pugno49, with analytical support from Carpinteri et al.50. Thus, scaling laws are not merely geometric outcomes but are grounded in fundamental mechanisms—whether biological or material—enabling the use of patterns to infer underlying processes51, and engineer desired functions.

On natural forms and 3D distributed ecohydrological stress

The emergence of Terraform structures — particularly scale-free networks — reflects a systemic response to ecohydrological pressure, not through imposed external design but through self-organization governed by Relative Material Proportions (RMP). A key insight is that even under uniform initial conditions and homogenous external pressure, the distribution of clay, water, and binder (i.e., the biomass in general sense) yields qualitatively distinct spatial configurations. These range from scale-free to exponential or log-normal and Poisson network-distributed patterns (Figs. 5 and S3), echoing ecological universality classes described by Maritan et al.52 and Banavar et al.53,54. This diversity implies a structural-mechanical underpinning behind ecosystem patterning, governed by interparticle interactions modulated by water availability and binder strength. The binder strength reflects the role of species or reactive sediments in real ecosystems. Pareto optimality—recognized as a universal evolutionary condition55—captures ecohydrological efficiency that arises from optimal proportions of species and materials, with water acting as both a primary morphogen and a structural chaperone (i.e., the agent shaping the process without directly producing the outcome). These optimal proportions for lognormally distributed biomass– including the particle-size distribution56 at smaller scales -- drive the emergence of scale-free networks as expressions of distributed stress, rather than from imposed design.

Suboptimal scale-free patterns (tree-like, or ‘’trees’’ due to their dendritic network organization) arise when foundational clay remains at 50%, while water-binder ratios shift the balance between long- and short-range particle interactions. Analogous to ecosystems, clay acts as a foundational species, structuring long-range flow and stress of sediment particles (Eqs. 3 and 4, and manifested by water stress as in Eqs. 6 and 7), while binder functions as an interaction modulator, influencing the short-range particle interactions (Fig. 5). Both clay and binder, mediated by water, define the system’s organization and resilience (Eq. 7). When the clay concentration is too high or too low (above or below 50% of the mixture), a topological transition is observed, where suboptimal random/patchy and regular/plain patterns are formed (Figs. 2, S3, S8, and S9). In particular, for high clay concentrations, the physical density of particles increases, and their capacity to form flows or distribute stress diminishes due to limited water-binder mobility. This results in higher entropy HT (see Eqs. 1 and 5 for the entropy of the material H(M) and the distributed entropy TE(M), and Fig. 4), characterizing structurally disordered states. These patterns reflect systemic inefficiency in water transfer and energy dissipation (Eqs. 6 and 7 for the effective water flow and the material capacity or free energy) — critical for understanding degraded or stressed ecosystems such as arid shrublands (with patchy vegetation) or disturbed wetlands and coastal systems (with disrupted drainage connectivity transitioning to tidal flats). Regular and random patterns, as extremes of the topological spectrum, show systemic divergence from scale-free/tree-like forms: they maximize stress or water retention locally, but accumulate systemic stress over time, reducing structural resilience and increasing material instability. In contrast, scale-free (SF) patterns, arising from golden RMP values, optimize stress distribution and reduce entropy (which leads to minimized systemic stress and maximized structural coherence), aligning with optimal functional outcomes like effective hygroscopy and material stability (Eqs. 6 and 7, and Figs. 4 and 5).

These findings resonate with earlier theoretical works32,33,53,54, which described hydrogeomorphological networks as flow-optimizing constructs. However, unlike those approaches, our results underscore that these networks are not merely flow paths observing basin-scale optimization principles of collective coordination, but emergent from precise and diffused RMP conditions — a finding that aligns more closely with Bejan and Lorente’s constructal law57, but goes further by identifying material proportions, not just geometry or pressure dynamics, as the key ontogenetic driver.

Critically, even in our triphasic mixtures, no perfectly symmetrical fractal was observed, reinforcing the idea that nature favors hybrid structures balancing randomness and order to guarantee adaptability. Patch patterns — often seen in drylands or cracked surfaces — showed Poisson-like distributions and a quasi-uniform pressure field (Fig. 5). Plain patterns, resembling tidal flats or floodplains, followed log-normal or exponential statistics which may result from broken power-law/tree-like network patterns. These patterns, whose structural networks reflects self-organizing material interactions, are analogs to real-world geomorphologies, supporting the claim that pattern ontogeny reflects deeper ecological processes58–62.

Moreover, the distributed microtopography — illustrated in Fig. 6 — supports the systemic functional interpretation: plain and patchy patterns exhibit lower and higher average elevation and random fluctuations (log-normal or exponential, and Poisson, respectively) like for stress (Fig. 5), while SF patterns show fewer but critical spikes with power-law height distributions. These insights suggest that elevation and surface geometry are secondary manifestations of deeper ecohydrological feedback mechanisms, evolving toward self-organized criticality for optimal fractals. Visibility networks—constructed by linking microtopographic extremes to all visible peaks—are equivalent to spatial networks, a phenomenon that underpins both the integrity of spatial organization and the complementarity of network-inference models. These networks echo neuronal and vascular systems63–65, where pressure minimization and dendritic maximization coexist for optimizing information flow with adaptable meta-stability.

In terms of practical inference, the ecology of pattern/network transitions observed here — especially the first-order shifts across topologies and the second-order variations in information dimension ID within each topology (Eq. 1) — provides a diagnostic and design framework for identifying and engineering ecosystem configurations (probabilistically defined by the distribution of material in Eq. 2) with desired function. For instance, as in ecosystems undergoing drought, water stress (Eqs. 6 and 7) is reflected by patch-type formations with small to medium interpatch distance (unvegetated or vegetated patches) and low flow velocity — pointing toward subcritical organization and inefficient energy dissipation. Vice versa, in ecosystems affected by persistent floods, water stress is reflected by plain-type formations with medium to high patch distance and high flow velocity, which are conditions manifesting supercritical organization and suboptimal function. Both subcritical/patch and supercritical/plain patterns are departures from the optimal ‘’water-neutral’’ tree-like/fractal patterns.

On collective abiotic intelligence and Nature-inspired design

Terraforms exemplify a new paradigm in ecological and material intelligence — where structural organization arises not from cognition or computation along the fabrication (as purely guided), but from intrinsic interaction properties embedded in matter a priori. This is abiotic intelligence: collective material behavior responding to systemic forcing via information-encoded interactions66. The same self-emergence can apply to biotic intelligence if relative species proportions are embedded into ecosystems.

Eco-stress, defined as force divided by response area (Eqs. 3 and 4, and Fig. 5), transfers through material bonds that form emergent networks with diverse strength and geometry shaped by Relative Material Proportions (RMP). Thus, interaction symmetry — or imbalance vive versa — critically determines how stress flows across the system. Perfect symmetry, like for regular and random patterns, defines suboptimality and imbalance of material interactions. SF patterns are balanced due to the non-random asymmetry in interactions and geometry.

Importantly, the pressure-response relationship in Terraforms requires no cognition or external programming; it is an emergent information-processing system operating purely via matter.

This bioinspired cybernetic view, akin to morphogenetic theory and cyber-physical eco-design, resonates with systemic intelligence models18,21,67. Morphogenesis occurs via chaperones (water and binder) and morphogens (clay), collectively defining flow topology. The symmetry between pressure and response tiles reveals 3D organization — an interface-based feedback mimicking ecotonal dynamics. Ecotones in natural ecosystems are interfacial habitats that form networks and stabilize ecosystemic function.

Practically, these findings open new directions for eco-functional infrastructure design: self-assembling, hygroscopically active surfaces for water harvesting (applicable to both natural and built environments outdoors and indoors); living infrastructures integrating soil-water-biota feedbacks; or mesocosms for testing ecosystem ontogeny and restoration. Since spatial patterns encode energy dissipation and hydrological function, pattern recognition (via the information dimension ID and statistical topology; see Eq. 1) becomes a predictive tool for system health and performance beyond system design based on optimal patterns’ features.

Furthermore, time is a derived property: a scalar manifestation of space. Spatial pattern reflects the system’s phase state and memory, where optimal RMP — especially the Fibonacci-derived (1.58) W/B ratio — encodes a history of ecohydrological stability and minimal entropy. These golden proportions of patterns correspond to minimum stress per unit area.

The emergence of Terraforms offers a new perspective on the intelligence of matter — defined not by cognition but by materials’ capacity to store, transmit, and adaptively organize information about environmental forcing. This brings abiotic systems closer to ecological intelligence paradigms where function arises through distributed coordination rather than central control. Extending Loreau et al.’s concept66 from biological communities, here abiotic collectives of clay, water, and binder exhibit systemic coherence via physicochemical interactions.

In this context, RMP acts as a morphogenetic code governing Geometry, energy dissipation, and ecohydrological performance. The golden proportions, particularly the 1.58 W/B ratio aligning with the Fibonacci sequence, emerge as the Pareto-optimal condition in this abiotic ‘’ecofolio’’ as complex mixture. The underpinning “golden interactions” lead to spontaneous formation of scale-free (SF) structures exhibiting optimal stress dissipation, pressure-response symmetry, and multiscale organization in 4D if time is included for the structural adaptation. Such self-organization parallels natural systems like river networks, tree branching, or neural structures63–65.

Patterns — patchy, plain, or scale-free — are diagnostic indicators of ecohydrological composition and function. Each topology, with characteristic statistics (Poisson, log-normal, or power-law distributions), signals a balance or imbalance among component interactions, pressure, and systemic adaptive capacity. Systemic stress per unit area is lowest in SF configurations, highest in rigid patterns limiting flow adaptability, and diffuse but inefficient in random patterns. These findings align with prior ecosystem modeling and evidence from ecosystems58,62, and emphasize that such outcomes can arise solely from controlled material proportions, independent of dynamic environmental evolution.

This abiotic design intelligence extends to bioinspired engineering. Guiding morphogenesis via material coding — without digital or mechanical control — suggests applications in ecological restoration (e.g., engineered soils), green infrastructure (e.g., self-forming water-retaining pavements), and indoor environments (e.g., humidity-stabilizing surfaces). Here, patterns are structural and functional (i.e., ‘’terraforming’’ at any scale), encoding hygroscopy (adsorption and absorption via connectivity), stress buffering, and adaptive capacity into material form.

Moreover, this approach entails an epistemic shift: ecosystems are emergent, programmable structures governed by interaction principles embedded in physical constituents. Terraforms become prototypes of natural archetypes — patterns and functions distilled into material form — whose ontogeny does not require temporal optimization but is instantaneously determined by blueprint-like proportion rules. This radically simplifies complex systems engineering, emphasizing “function follows composition” rather than “form follows function.”

In sum, Terraforms show how ecological structure and function can be encoded in simple materials through composition and pressure. They demonstrate that:

• Space encodes time: Golden spatial proportions from critical W/B ratios scale to temporal transitions (e.g., random to SF organization shift as selective environmental pruning), establishing space and time as mirrored dimensions in morphogenesis. Specifically, space defines effective hygroscopy and material stability.

Through this lens, Terraforms provide a platform for nature-positive design and engineering — three-dimensional realizations of nature’s structural and functional intelligence embedded in matter. They connect classical scaling theories (Fibonacci, Pacioli1, Pareto68, Kleiber69, Mandelbrot70, Bak71 with adaptive design, restoration ecology, and sustainable infrastructure. The ability to pre-program material systems toward desired structural and ecological outcomes opens a transformative avenue for understanding and engineering ecosystems, where abiotic matter is richly functional and adaptive to the environment.

Conclusions

The study’s core contributions can be distilled into three points:

Material proportions—not environmental pressure alone—govern emergent ecosystemic function and network structure.

The vast majority of literature discusses environmental pressure, spatial proportions and configurations, often with imposed shapes, but not how material proportions drive emergent patterns tied to systemic function. In contrast, we demonstrate that letting the proportions of materials (clay, water, binder) self-distribute over space generates spatially explicit patterns that manifest as macrostructures formed by micro-bonds independently of any other factor. These Terraforms—triphasic material complexes—self-organize even under uniform pressure, producing scale-free Pareto-like networks without requiring Pareto environmental extremes. Here, interactions among system components are not emergent byproducts, but primary drivers shaping both macro-topology and micro-motif geometries, as functions of proportion-based stress material flow and energy expenditure. Networks are emergent, stress-guiding architectures arising from mixture dynamics, revealing that engineerable function precedes structure.

These Terraforms—triphasic material complexes—self-organize even under uniform pressure, producing scale-free Pareto-like networks without requiring Pareto environmental extremes. Here, interactions among system components are not emergent byproducts, but primary drivers shaping both macro-topology and micro-motif geometries, as functions of proportion-based stress material flow and energy expenditure. Networks are emergent, stress-guiding architectures arising from mixture dynamics, revealing that engineerable function precedes structure.

Ultimately, Terraforms are prototypes of natural archetypes—physical network embodiments of nature’s morphogenetic intelligence. They provide a framework for ecosystem design, restoration, and enhancement rooted in material-driven self-organization. Their evolution follows no imposed plan but emerges from localized pressure–response dynamics embedded into materials’ proportions. These dynamics can be manipulated to fabricate aggregative vs. erosive networks, enabling targeted ecological functions such as network-driven hygroscopy, material stability, and distributed stress dissipation. Materials and methods

Triphasic mixture and pattern generation

The Terraform recipe consisted of a homogeneous triphasic mixture of fresh water (W), clay (C)—dried and finely sieved using a #120 mesh—and a binder (B) (Figures S1 and S2). This mixture is denoted here as M = M(C, W/B). Since clay properties vary locally, the drying time needed to remove embodied water (i.e., moisture already present in the clay, not the added water) varies by region. In our process, the clay lost nearly all of its water content after drying. The clay used in this study was collected from the Southern University of Science and Technology (SUSTech) campus, located in a historic inner wetland ecosystem in Shenzhen. It required approximately two weeks to fully dry, after which it was crushed and sieved. If any residual moisture remained, an additional week of drying was applied. This local “Shenzhen Red Soil”, typical of fluvo-aquic soils, consists of approximately 50–54% clay (< 0.01 mm), 16–23% silt (0.01–0.05 mm), and 34–36% sand (> 0.05 mm).

The binder used was Ethylene-Vinyl Acetate (EVA) glue, a readily available wood adhesive with ideal viscosity and adhesion for our selected substrate. The support material was black PVC rubber (4 mm thick), cut into tiles of 40 × 90 mm². Compared to wood, metals, plastics, and glass, PVC rubber offered superior flexibility, water resistance, and surface stickiness, making it the most effective substrate for Terraform formation.

We developed 17 different recipes (Figs. 2 and S2), systematically varying the proportions of the three components. The formulation followed the Triaxial Blend testing method—commonly used in the ceramic and pottery industries—to visualize and quantify how ingredients interact, particularly for glaze development. The mixtures combined clay, water, and binder in proportions ranging from 10 to 60% each, maintaining a total mass of 100 g per mixture. This method enabled us to explore how variations in composition influence material properties and ultimately pattern emergence. The recipe design aimed to support an objective generation process, promoting consistency and reproducibility throughout the study.

The fabrication process began by evenly spreading the mixture onto one rubber tile using a spatula (Figure S1). A second, clean tile was then placed on top to form a sandwich structure (tile–mixture–tile). To ensure uniform pressure, we designed a custom pressing device similar to a lithographic or screen-printing press. It featured two rails with thickness matching the tile stack and a blade (or squeegee) equal in length to the tiles. The rails guided the blade, ensuring smooth and even compression. As the blade was drawn across the rails, it applied homogeneous pressure on the sandwich, causing the tiles to adhere tightly with a thin layer of mixture slightly overflowing from the edges.

After pressing, the tiles were carefully separated (Figure S1), initiating pattern formation (Figure S3). The method of separation—whether pulling apart from the short or long edge, or center (Figures S4-S6)—determined the origin point of the branching pattern but did not affect the macro-topology or microstructural details. Each tile retained a mirrored version of the pattern, and only under golden proportion mixtures did the resulting scale-free (SF) patterns on both tiles exhibit perfect symmetry (Figures S4-S6). The topology of the pattern was closely tied to the specific material ratios used in each mixture (Figures S2 and S3).

Pattern digitization, information dimension, and divergence from optimality

To quantify the degree of pattern organization (Figs. 3 and S7), we applied a method for calculating the information dimension (ID)—also referred to as the information fractal dimension (IFD) for scale-free patterns. This approach, which evaluates fractal-like characteristics (as scale-free information stability) independently of a pattern’s strict physical fractality, was originally proposed by Chamorro-Posada83 and later refined by Convertino M84. through the incorporation of the maximum entropy principle. This enhancement identifies the scale-invariant entropy range over which the ID is computed; the larger the range, the higher the fractality of the pattern.

Convertino84 defines the information dimension as the most stable dimension across a range of spatial scales where local entropy is calculated. Importantly, scale-invariance in entropy can be observed even in non-fractal patterns, though the scale range typically narrows or widens (underlying information heterogeneity or full homogeneity) depending on the degree of randomness or regularity. In perfectly ordered patterns, entropy is minimal (approximating zero), spatially uniform, and constant across the domain. Conversely, in completely random patterns, entropy is maximal and exhibits significant spatial variability.

The distribution of local entropy determines the systemic entropy associated with each topology along connected elements through networks. Using Shannon entropy, the information dimension captures the distributed randomness of the pattern’s texture, rather than simply the number of pixels occupied by the material, as done by traditional box-counting methods. Crucially, differences in ID quantify the degree of suboptimal disorganization, with deviation from the optimal scale-free pattern—which exhibits an intermediate IFD between pure randomness and full regularity—serving as a measure of systemic inefficiency from the optimal fractality.

Network extraction and systemic stress as cumulated flow

Given the impossibility of precisely measuring microtopography at every point, we rescaled local entropy (interpreted as texture, following Convertino et al.88,89, to elevation (microtopography), which was assessed through direct measurements of the tiles and image-based digitization. This allowed us to derive Digital Information Networks (DIN) used to characterize the topology of the patterns. Local entropy quantifies the randomness of material distribution, while elevation reflects the physical height of the material—two distinct but related measures. Notably, a correlation exists: maximum entropy corresponds to maximum elevation with uniformly distributed peaks (indicating minimal flow), that is for the patchy/random pattern, whereas the strongest correlation between entropy and height is observed in scale-free (SF) patterns with the minimum entropy and intermediate average elevation.

Stress refers to the pressure transferred to the clay particles, which are the solid components of the mixture. This stress is influenced by the W/B ratio, which governs the formation of both long- and short-range bonds between clay particles (Fig. 5B). In principle, a lower W/B ratio results in greater pressure transfer to the clay due to reduced mobility or increased attraction, and the working hypothesis is that this also leads to higher pressure on the water phase (see Sect. 4.4). This is the typical example of clay-rich soils subjected to poor infiltration.

Free energy and systemic function: mixture stability and effective hygroscopy

Hygroscopy refers to the ability of a material to attract and retain water molecules from the surrounding environment, occurring through either absorption or adsorption. Absorption involves water penetrating into the bulk of the material and spreading through its volume, a process strongly influenced by material heterogeneity, such as porosity and internal connectivity. Adsorption, by contrast, occurs when water molecules adhere to the material’s surface and are taken up vertically. The latter mechanism is typically assumed to dominate based on the intrinsic properties of most materials. Taken together with the actual water content, absorption and adsorption jointly determine the water-holding or saturation capacity of the material.

Thus, while hygroscopy is generally regarded as an intrinsic material property, its effective performance depends on material connectivity, which governs water flow pathways and local saturation levels. Here, we explicitly incorporate network connectivity into the calculation of effective hygroscopy, in a manner analogous to systemic stress (Eq. 3). The effective drainage capacity is determined by the product of the connected drainage density (defined by Nij in Eq. 4 as the number of connections per unit area) and the drainage organization (informed by ID in Eq. 1, or P(M≥m) in Eq. 2 ~ HT)—with scale-free networks exhibiting the highest drainage organization as in natural ecosystems31,91. Although regular plain and random networks may have higher drainage densities31, their drainage organization is either too low or excessively high, which limits effective or optimal drainage. When Ci h/Wi > 1, or more precisely the ratio between vertical and surface water Wv /Wi > 1 (as for high binder content, B), the pattern is water-limited (analogous to Budyko’s theory) due to undrained water saturation; in this case patchy patterns arise where drought-like situations with high vertical fluxes are experienced. Drought-like states occur due of water limitation and higher, more homogeneous vertical water transpiration (dominating absorption) in random patterns. Conversely, when Ci h/Wi < 1, or Wv /Wi < 1 (for high water content, W), water capacity is very low because the clay is mostly washed away and absorption is energy-limited. In this case, plain patterns arise where flood-like conditions maximize surface water flows.

Thus, the ratio between potential absorption Ci h (water demand, that is in principle determined by the environment) and drained water capacity Wi (or water supply, where the available capacity emerges from relative material proportions) defines water stress, which impacts hygroscopy and structural stability of the complex material. Thus, material stress is effectively equivalent to water stress driven by RMP.
