Systemic inflammation modulates lipoprotein(a)-associated coronary stenosis in the chronic coronary syndromes
Authors: Lu Shen, Wenqing Zhai, Ping Jiang, Feng Liang, Ruonan Li, Dongju Xu, Qingna Zhang, Jing Zhang, Xingyong Tao
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
Highlights•Elevated lipoprotein(a) links to coronary stenosis in chronic coronary syndromes.•Systemic inflammation modulates lipoprotein(a)-associated coronary stenosis.•Strongest lipoprotein(a)-coronary stenosis association only in low inflammation.Keywords: Lipoprotein(a)·Coronary stenosis·Systemic inflammation ·Chronic coronary syndromes·Atherosclerosis

Introduction

Chronic coronary syndromes (CCS) represent a relatively long and broad " stability " phase of coronary artery disease (CAD), although the disease progression is not entirely stable but rather most often progressive[1]. Coronary artery stenosis or changes in the micro-circulation can lead to myocardial ischemia and ischemic chest pain, potentially triggering adverse cardiovascular (CV) events and destabilizing previously stable coronary conditions[1]. Hence, even in clinically apparently silent periods, CCS remains a serious issue that requires attention. Coronary artery stenosis is recognized as results from the lipid depositions and accumulation of atherosclerotic plaques in epicardial arteries, leading to an increase in morbidity, mortality, and a decline in quality of life[2]. Although the guidelines recommend that reducing low-density lipoprotein cholesterol (LDL-C) levels remains the primary means of reducing the risk of CAD, substantial residual risk has led to efforts to identify new lipoprotein biomarkers.

Lipoprotein(a) [Lp(a)] is a unique and highly distinguishable lipid parameter, with a profound genetic background, significant variations across populations, and stronger pro-inflammatory potential than LDL-C on an equimolar basis[[3], [4], [5]]. It acts through induction of vascular inflammation, atherogenesis, calcification, and thrombosis[[3], [4], [5]]. Recent studies strongly support a causal and continuous association between Lp(a) concentration and cardiovascular outcomes, and suggest that elevated Lp(a) is an absolute risk factor in advanced atherosclerotic lesions[6,7]. Lp(a) levels of 50 mg/dL or higher, as noted by the European Atherosclerosis Society, confer increased cardiovascular risk[3]. Numerous studies using optical coherence tomography have demonstrated that elevated Lp(a) levels accompanied by higher Gensini scores are independently associated with an increased coronary atheroma volume or atherosclerotic plaque in CAD patients, indicating a greater likelihood of the severity of coronary stenosis[8,9]. Despite this evidence, one study observed that Lp(a) was not an independent risk factor for CAD in African-Americans, while another cohort found no correlation between Lp(a) level and coronary stenosis[10,11]. This prompts researchers to consider additional modulating factors might influence the management of CCS.

These inconsistencies may be explained by inflammation's role as a potential modulator. Emerging data show Lp(a)'s cardiovascular risk depends on inflammatory status. Data from the European prospective population cohort studies have demonstrated that increased Lp(a) levels were associated with future atherosclerotic risk only in individuals with residual inflammatory risk[12]. Likewise, research from a Multi-Ethnic Study of Atherosclerosis (MESA) cohort in the general population also observed that only a concurrent rise in Lp(a) and inflammation was linked to an increased risk of cardiovascular atherosclerosis[7]. Also, studies in primary and secondary prevention settings suggest that the inflammatory burden mediates the prognostic capacity of Lp(a) in CV events[13]. These findings indicate that Lp(a)'s cardiovascular risk is potentiated by concurrent inflammation. However, whether coronary stenosis, one of the cardinal pathological manifestations of cardiovascular disease, is similarly mediated by inflammation during Lp(a) regulation remains unclear.

To better capture inflammation's role in coronary stenosis, robust biomarkers are needed. High-sensitivity C-reactive protein (hs-CRP) recommended by guidelines was considered the most promising inflammatory biomarker previously[1]. Beyond this conventional marker, given the nature of chronic inflammation in atherosclerosis, the systemic inflammatory response index (SIRI), systemic immune-inflammation index (SII) and neutrophil to lymphocyte ratio (NLR) effectively integrate counts of neutrophil, monocyte, platelet and lymphocyte to predict cardiovascular disease risks and all-cause mortality, and also provide superior predictions for vascular plaque and stenosis, offering mechanistic insights into chronic inflammation's role in stenosis[[14], [15], [16]]. These composite indices, derived from blood samples, may be more accurate and stable than absolute blood cell counts, serving as suitable biomarkers to mirror chronic inflammation.

Building on the well-established Lp(a) - coronary stenosis relationship, it is logical to hypothesize a potential association modified by systemic inflammation (quantified by SIRI) among Lp(a) and coronary stenosis in CCS. Our study specifically examines: (1) whether the association between Lp(a) and coronary stenosis might be modulated by accompanying systemic inflammation concentration among individuals in CCS, and (2) how the presence of other risk factors at baseline influence this interaction.

Methods

Our study was a post hoc analysis of an observational, case retrospective project, aiming to investigate the potential impact of systemic inflammation on Lp(a) and coronary artery stenosis in patients with CCS. The study was conducted in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki and the local ethics committees granted permission for accessing the medical electronic information system and provided the ethical approval (PJ-2025–03–47).

Study population

A flowchart of the study population derivation is presented in Fig. 1. In the first step, a total of 2 349 hospitalized individuals (aged > 18 years) undergoing elective coronary angiography, due to suspected or symptomatic CAD, were consecutively recruited from a single, large medical academic center (Department of Cardiovascular Medicine, The First Affiliated Hospital of Anhui Medical University, China) from June 2023 to February 2025. In the second step, we excluded 186 patients from the analysis due to missing critical detailed information such as the specific blood parameters or Lp(a) data. Those with acute coronary syndrome (ACS) or non-ST-elevation myocardial infarction (NSTEMI) were also excluded. Finally, we excluded those patients with systemic inflammatory or autoimmune diseases (n = 17), which may impact the organism’s inflammation or immune-related status. The final research sample involved 1 513 individuals.

Date collection and definitions

Demographic and clinical data, laboratory tests, and medication use were obtained from our medical center's electronic medical record system. These data were collected by well-trained clinical research coordinators and thoroughly reviewed by independent statisticians. The following variables were included:age, sex, body mass index (BMI), smoking status, alcohol consumption, educational level, medication use (statin treatment, aspirin intake), disease history (hypertension, diabetes, stroke), history of revascularization treatment, and laboratory values. The BMI was determined by dividing weight in kilograms by the square of height in meters. The smoking and drinking status were categorized into three classifications: previous, current, and ceased. The medication history of statin treatment and aspirin intake, and the history of revascularization treatment were obtained through detailed records in the patient's chief complaint and past medical history reported in the medical record system. Hypertension was still based on the standards outlined in the guidelines or the current utilization of anti-hypertensive medications, or on self-reported history of hypertension[17]. Diabetes mellitus (DM) was defined according to the American Diabetes Associations’ definitions, current use of hypoglycemic drugs or insulin, or a self-reported history of DM[18]. The definition of stroke was also based on the guidelines, or on a history of stroke. In terms of laboratory tests, fast blood sample testing was conducted within 24 h after admission or prior to undergoing angiography and other treatments. The basic blood cell test and the routine lipid profile test were required. The specific definition of CCS can be referred to the 2019 ESC guidelines[2].

Evaluation of coronary stenosis

The imaging data were collected in the catheterisation laboratory of our research institute. The coronary angiography and assessment of vascular stenosis for the patients were carried out by two experienced and certified interventional cardiologists, who followed standard operating procedures. Prior to this, they were blinded to this study. First, a visual inspection was conducted by two cardiologists to ascertain the extent of coronary artery stenosis and the main coronary arteries evaluated include the left main (LM), left anterior descending (LAD), and left circumflex coronary arteries (LCX), or dominant obtuse marginal (OM) branch and the right coronary artery (RCA). Typically, the assessment was conducted on two occasions, and the mean value was taken to minimize inter-observers differences. The extent and severity of CCS were evaluated using the Gensini Score (GS), a widely recognized method for quantifying angiographic atherosclerosis[19]. The GS was used to define the severity of vascular stenosis, with values assigned from 25 % obstruction to complete arterial occlusion respectively, according to the anatomical location in the coronary circulation. The angiographic outcomes were categorized into six groups based on the GS: eccentric or concentric obstruction ≤ 25 %, 25 % to 50 %, 51 % to 75 %, 76 % to 90 %, 91 % to 99 %, and 100 %. Each category was assigned a score of 1, 2, 4, 8, 16, and 32, respectively. The vessels at different positions within the dominant coronary arteries on the left or right sides are multiplied by different coefficients. Finally, the GS was calculated as the sum of the scores for all coronary branches. Patients with GS ≤ 20.5, 20.5 – 49.5, and > 49.5 were defined as the normal group, the mild group, and the severe group, respectively, in accordance with the quartile method.

Laboratory measurements

Baseline Lp(a) mass and systemic inflammation concentration were determined from the frozen stored blood samples and analyzed centrally in our central laboratory at the First Affiliated Hospital of Anhui Medical University. The unified testing of blood routine samples was conducted using the automatic blood analyser (XN-1000, Sysmex, Japan) and the concentrations of plasma triglycerides (TG), total cholesterol (TC), high-density lipoprotein cholesterol (HDL-C), LDL-C, very low-density lipoprotein cholesterol (VLDL-C), non-high-density lipoprotein cholesterol (N—HLDL-C), apolipoprotein A (Apo A), apolipoprotein B (Apo B), and Lp(a) were measured using an automatic biochemistry analyser (Cobas C8000, Roche, Germany). Reference ranges were provided for each biochemical and hematological indicator. The determination of Lp(a) mass was conducted using an immunoturbidimetry method, adhering to Chinese lipid test standards, with a reference range of 0 to 300 mg/L[20]. The European Atherosclerosis Society notes that Lp(a) levels above the 75th percentile increase ASCVD risk[3]; the 75th percentile was 351 mg/L in our database, which is close to the 300 mg/L threshold, supporting its validity and applicability. Accordingly, participants were categorized based on baseline Lp(a) mass as ≤ 300 mg/L or > 300 mg/L. SIRI, calculated by Neutrophil * Monocyte / Lymphocyte counts, was used to assess the inflammation risk, as a biomarker to reflect systemic inflammation[16]. Based on the quartiles of the continuous variable SIRI, patients with values < 0.64, 0.64 ≤ SIRI < 1.41, and ≥ 1.41 were respectively included in the low inflammation group, the moderate inflammation group, and the high inflammation group. SII (Neutrophil * Platelet / Lymphocyte counts) and NLR (Neutrophil / Lymphocyte counts) were similarly calculated and stratified.

Statistical analysis

Continuous parameters are given as mean and standard deviation (SD) or median and inter-quartile range (IQR, 25th and 75th percentile) and compared across the degree of coronary stenosis groups and Lp(a) group using either t-tests or Kruskal-Wallis H tests or Mann-Whitney U test depending on the distribution. Categorical variables are shown as absolute and relative frequencies and compared between Lp(a) and coronary stenosis using χ2 tests under different SIRI levels. We performed an analysis of using logistic regression with 1 513 observations at a 0.95 confidence level, producing two-sided confidence intervals for Lp(a) and the severity of vascular stenosis. To assess a possible impact of systemic inflammation concentrations on Lp(a)-associated risk for coronary stenosis, the multinomial models were adjusted three times, stratified by these composite index levels. SIRI was therefore selected as the primary biomarker of systemic inflammation for subsequent stratified analyses. Model 1 was a crude model, including Lp(a). Model 2 added age and sex, and model 3 incorporated all meaningful variables. Then the odds ratios (ORs) and 95 % confidence intervals (CIs) were generated by multivariate regression analyses. We also performed restricted cubic splines (RCS) regression analysis to visualize the risk of coronary stenosis across continuous variables, using the “rms” package in R software. RCS is a method used to assess nonlinear associations[21]. In RCS analysis, the core involves constructing regression splines, which are piecewise polynomial functions, while also incorporating linear functions at the two ends of the independent variable's data range. The independent variable was Lp(a) mass, the dependent variable was the coronary stenosis and the various influencing factors were covariates. Based on the principle of minimizing the Akaike Information Criterion (AIC), we chose three knots, which influence both the shape of the spline and its fitting effect. Further, sensitivity analyses were conducted to confirm our findings by separately eliminating four different influencing factors and using subgroup analyses stratified by systemic inflammation levels. All statistical analyses were performed using IBM SPSS Statistics ver. 25.0 software (IBM Corp., Armonk, NK, USA) and R 4.5.0 (https://www.r-project.org/China)) statistical packages. Statistical significance was defined as P < 0.05.

Distributions of Lp(a) and coronary stenosis across different systemic inflammation status

Overall, a significant association was observed between the distributions of Lp(a) levels and the severity of coronary stenosis (χ2 = 15.54, P < 0.001) as presented in Table 1. When stratified by SIRI levels, as shown in Table 2, a higher proportion of patients with Lp(a) > 300 mg/L were in the mild coronary stenosis group (50.4 %) compared to the normal (22.7 %) and severe (26.9 %) groups, with a significant association observed when SIRI was < 0.64. Interestingly, the trend of these distributions begins to weaken as SIRI increases. When SIRI was ≥ 1.41, no significant association was found. In summary, Lp(a) was associated with coronary stenosis with lower SIRI levels (< 0.64) in terms of quantity distribution, suggesting a potential interaction between SIRI and Lp(a) in influencing coronary stenosis.

Associations between Lp(a) and coronary stenosis stratified by systemic inflammation status

At this stage, we first found that Lp(a) levels are associated with an increased risk of coronary stenosis, and the association persists after adjusting for multiple potential confounding factors [Mild vs. Normal, OR 1.47, 95 % CI 1.11–1.96, P = 0.008; Severe vs. Normal, OR 1.68, 95 % CI 1.21–2.33, P = 0.002] (Additional File, Table A.3). Then, we analyzed whether baseline inflammation concentrations might modify the associations between Lp(a) or factors and risk of cardiovascular coronary stenosis. Assuming that SIRI ≥ 1.41 reflects a high residual inflammation risk, as well as better stratify and present the inflammatory state, the study population was divided according to SIRI quartile stratification (Fig. 2). Within the low inflammation (SIRI < 0.64), with the normal group as the reference, increased Lp(a) mass was closely linked to coronary stenosis, as shown in the central illustration [fully adjusted model: Mild vs. Normal, OR 2.03, 95 % CI 1.17–3.54, P = 0.012; Severe vs. Normal, OR 2.34, 95 % CI 1.24–4.44, P = 0.009]. As the concentration of inflammation in a moderate state (0.64 ≤ SIRI < 1.41), no connection was observed. Further stratification into SIRI group ≥ 1.41, however, revealed that increased Lp(a) was not associated with coronary stenosis in individuals having highly elevated SIRI concentration. The trend did not change after multivariate adjustment for covariables in Model 2, as well as in fully adjusted Model 3 [Mild vs. Normal, OR 1.27, 95 % CI 0.69–2.33, P = 0.445; Severe vs. Normal, OR 1.36, 95 % CI 0.69–2.69, P = 0.377] (Fig. 2; Additional File, Table A.4). We also sought to analyze whether there was a mediation effect between Lp(a) and SIRI in influencing the degree of coronary artery stenosis, but the results were not statistically significant (P = 0.298) (Additional File, Table A.5 and Figure A.1).

Sensitive analysis

Four sensitivity analyses were performed. Firstly, to mitigate the potential influence of statin or aspirin drugs on Lp(a) mass[22], 375 patients on statin treatment prior to admission and 388 patients taking aspirin were excluded. Secondly, 372 individuals with a history of DM at baseline were excluded to minimize the impact on coronary stenosis in previous studies[23]. Additionally, 255 patients who had undergone revascularization treatment may have lead to an underestimation of the link between Lp(a) and coronary stenosis and were excluded. When using continuous Lp(a) through restricted spline plots to reflect the risk of coronary stenosis stratified by SIRI levels after fully adjust variables, the summarized results of the sensitivity analyses were similar as presented in Figure A.2-A.5 (Additional File, Figure A.2-A.5). Identical analyses were also performed within SII and NLR-based inflammation stratification. The results demonstrated that these conclusions are stable and reliable.

Discussion

In 1 513 CCS participants from a medical academic center in China, elevated Lp(a) had a certain association with coronary stenosis. However, these associations were found to be mediated by systemic inflammation levels. The correlation was observed exclusively in patients with SIRI levels of < 0.64, whereas no such relationship was noted when stratified in moderate (0.64 ≤ SIRI < 1.41) and high inflammation (SIRI ≥ 1.41). These findings on coronary artery stenosis are novel, notably as such analyses have not been previously performed.

Previous studies have consistently revealed that inflammation is a key modulating factor influencing the risk of Lp(a)-related ASCVD in the primary and secondary prevention populations[12,13,24]. A comprehensive analysis of the Atherosclerosis Risk in Communities Study (ARIC), the Framingham Offspring Study (FOS) and the MESA revealed that elevated Lp(a) levels were significantly associated with an increased risk of ASCVD and coronary heart disease (CHD)[24]. The highest risk of ASCVD was observed in individuals with Lp(a) ≥ 50 mg/dl and hs-CRP ≥ 2 mg/dl [hazard ratio (HR) = 1.56, 95 % confidence interval (CI) 1.31–1.84], and a statistically significant interaction was found between the two factors (P < 0.05)[24]. Landmark trials like ACCELERATE (N = 12 092) showed that elevated Lp(a) levels during the statin treatment were related to the risk of CV events when hs-CRP levels were 2 mg/L or more, and the BiomarCARE Project studies, reported very similar results, showing that similar associations were seen only among individuals with hs-CRP ≥ 2 mg/L [HR = 1.34, 95 % CI 1.03–1.76)] in CHD individuals[12,13]. These studies focused exclusively on the differences in Lp(a) and inflammation in relation to CV events among predominantly European populations. Our results diverge from these cohorts in three key aspects: firstly, potentially due to the well known genetic variations may underlie inter-racial differences in Lp(a) and inflammation interactions. Moreover, these studies used more standardized endpoints (e.g., myocardial infarction, unstable angina pectoris, coronary death) for CV events, while we focused exclusively on the pathological condition of stenosis, measured during the stable phase of CCS. In advanced atherosclerosis, when coronary stenosis becomes difficult to precisely measure and assess functionally, clinical focus shifts to the direct outcome events of ACS as a more meaningful indicator of disease severity. Finally and notably, individuals who reached the endpoint events were excluded from the assessment of CCS. Our observational study provides a more novel perspective on the impact of Lp(a)-associated coronary stenosis, indicating that it depends on inflammation levels. However, the interdependence and capacity of Lp(a) with background systemic inflammation concentrations in modulating coronary stenosis severity rather than CV events remains under explored. Despite differences in population selections, designs, endpoints, Lp(a) measurements, and potential treatment effects on biomarkers, our findings had no conflicts with the established clinical consensus that both Lp(a) and inflammatory factors synergistically contribute to CHD events.

Recent studies showed a solid pathophysiological background for the tight interplay between Lp(a) and systemic inflammation[[25], [26], [27], [28]]. Elevated Lp(a) is linked to atherosclerosis, thrombosis, and arterial calcification, enhancing inflammation in endothelial cells, monocytes, and macrophages, via the oxidized phospholipids (oxPL), where the NLRP3 inflammasome–interleukin (IL)-1β / interleukin-6 / CRP pathway might act as danger-associated trigger mechanism[25,26]. The monocyte chemoattractant protein-1 carried by Lp(a) enhances the recruitment of monocytes to the vascular wall, promotes the release of reactive oxygen species and facilitates the formation of foam cells, accelerating the formation of atherosclerosis[29]. Our findings in individuals with SIRI concentration > 0.64 merit particular consideration. The Lp(a) was incorporated as a continuous variable in the piecewise polynomial model and the curve in nonlinear correlation diagram also exhibited no correlations at moderate and high inflammations. These findings shed new light on the complex role of Lp(a) in various states. Lp(a) has the capacity to trigger pro-inflammatory responses at both the molecular and cellular levels, but it can also exert anti-inflammatory effects in certain disease states. This is closely related to the dual pro-inflammatory and anti-inflammatory mechanism of OxPLs[30]. One study conducted by Maeda S et al. found that the concentration of Lp(a) in patients with acute inflammation at the time of admission was significantly lower than that in the recovery period, and the increase of Lp(a) in cases of acute myocardial infarction was delayed significantly, suggesting a synchronous and opposite relationship between Lp(a) concentration and inflammation[31]. In patients with severe sepsis, lipoprotein concentrations rapidly change and can be reduced to 50 % of recovery concentrations[32]. These indicate that varying baseline inflammatory levels can alter the effects or pathogenic potential of Lp(a). Moreover, a variety of oxidatively modified lipids and proteins like a double-edged sword have both been shown to modulate atherosclerosis that may activate the immune system, with evidence of both proatherogenic and atheroprotective effects[33]. Furthermore, a positive feedback loop exists among inflammatory factors. For instance, tumor necrosis factor-α (TNF-α) and IL-6 frequently increase concurrently and both activate the NF-κB and JAK/STAT signaling pathways, leading to a cumulative impact on lipid metabolism[34]. Systemic inflammation driven by mediators such as IL-6 and TNF-α through these inflammatory pathways accelerates plaque progression rather than being directly induced by Lp(a). These suggest that under conditions of elevated systemic inflammation, plaque stability may be governed by multi-factorial pathological processes rather than Lp(a)-mediated pathways alone, potentially favoring progression toward vulnerable plaque phenotypes. In conclusion, Lp(a) being regulated by the protective effects of oxidized lipids on atherosclerosis, the inverse concentration between Lp(a) and inflammation, and inflammatory factors dominating the progression of plaques seem to be able to explain that, in our research, the effect of Lp(a) on coronary stenosis exhibited no effect when in the state of moderate and high inflammation.

Although the mainstream anti-inflammatory applications of a selective IL-1β antagonist or low-dose colchicine therapies had been demonstrated recently[35,36], the mechanism underlying the interaction between inflammation and Lp(a) on coronary stenosis is not understood. Despite adjusting for numerous covariates, the presence of potential competing factors that can reduce atherosclerotic risk and their interactions is unavoidable. A recent meta-analysis has shown that statin treatments increase Lp(a) levels (11.6 %–20.4 % for pravastatin and 18.7 %–24.2 % for atorvastatin)[22]. In our sensitivity analysis, participants receiving medications that might affect Lp(a) mass and inflammation status were excluded. Similarly, it is well established that patients with diabetes have a greater cardiovascular burden[23], and the vascular benefits of revascularization therapy cannot be ignored. Indeed, we attempted to eliminate these potential confounders and their diluting effects, and the results of the sensitivity analysis in subgroups remain statistically unchanged. Moreover, we performed a mediation effect analysis and demonstrated that both Lp(a) and inflammation exerted significant independent impacts on the degree of stenosis, suggesting that each factor may contribute to the development of coronary stenosis through distinct pathways in the model. Furthermore, the correlations between Lp(a) and lipid parameters may partly reflect methodological influences, as Lp(a) quantification is affected by apolipoprotein(a) [apo(a)] isoform-dependent assay bias and by measurement approaches based on apolipoprotein B, which is covalently bound to apo(a)[37]. These factors further complicate the interpretation of the results.

Our study has several strengths: firstly, this is the first report demonstrating that the association between Lp(a) and coronary stenosis is influenced by background inflammatory status, with SIRI employed as a comprehensive non-traditional marker of systemic inflammation. Importantly, despite potential confounding from prior revascularization and statin use, a distinct Lp(a)-coronary stenosis association still persists under low inflammation. Secondly, the general population-based design represents a real-world, all-comer CCS individuals with no selection bias undergoing coronary angiography. Thirdly, the consistent findings across multiple sensitivity analyses enhance the robustness and reliability of the results. However, several limitations merit consideration. First, our cross-sectional observational study involves inherent limitations in drawing conclusions on causal associations among Lp(a), systemic inflammation and coronary stenosis. These associations must be investigated directly in prospective cohorts, randomized trials, or Mendelian randomization studies. Second, the present data may not be extrapolated to other ethnic populations, since the sample comes from a single study center in China, which may introduce bias. Third, coronary angiography provides information on stenosis severity but does not capture plaque burden, which better reflects the overall severity of CAD. Finally, we used SIRI, SII and NLR to define systemic inflammation. Other inflammatory biomarkers such as hs-CRP, IL-6 and TNF-α were not entirely measured in our population. These biomarkers’ expressions may also partly influence Lp(a) levels, contributing to a more complex and dynamic pathological process[38,39].

Conclusions

In our large cross-sectional study of 1 513 CCS participants, increased Lp(a) was robustly associated with coronary stenosis. However, this clear association was only observed under low systemic inflammation at baseline. In contrast, no correlations were noted in those at moderate and high inflammation state, providing further insight into the mechanisms underlying Lp(a)- associated coronary stenosis risk. Our results advocate for integrating inflammatory evaluation into current risk assessment and decision-making processes in CCS. It may prove useful for identifying individuals who might benefit the most from novel Lp(a)-lowering therapies, taking into account the inflammatory state and long-term nature of the CCS disease stage.
