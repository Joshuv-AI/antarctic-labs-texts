Imbalanced chromatin distribution in cellular senescence specifies paraspeckle dynamics
Authors: Joonwoo Lee, Jinmi Choi, Jeongeun Park, Seonduk Lee, Eun-Jung Cho, Youngdae Gwon
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
Cellular senescence is accompanied by extensive genomic reorganization, such as senescence-associated heterochromatin foci and expanded interchromatin compartments, to ultimately affect gene expression. Here, we demonstrate that chromatin structural changes in senescent cells drive significant alterations in the phase behavior and motility of paraspeckles, a type of interchromatin compartment condensate. We observe increased numbers, size, and elongation of paraspeckles harboring NONO and NEAT1_2, driven by elevated levels of those components, consistent with the micellization model of longitudinal growth rather than condensate coalescence. Enhanced paraspeckle motility is associated with HP1α-mediated heterochromatin condensation and interchromatin expansion found in cellular senescence.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s13059-025-03757-6.

Background

Chromatin plays a crucial role in determining the functional and structural properties of the nucleus [1]. Its dynamic transition between open and closed conformations, governed by the degree of compaction, dictates gene transcription [2]. Recent findings have delineated the pivotal role of liquid–liquid phase separation (LLPS), driven by multivalent interactions among chromatin components, in mediating these structural changes [3, 4]. Furthermore, chromatin organization provides specialized compartments around which the condensation of other biomolecules favorably occurs [5]. Such interchromatin compartment (IC) condensates include nuclear speckles, paraspeckles, and Cajal bodies, all of which contribute to RNA metabolism in relation to gene transcription.

Aside from these endogenous condensates, recent studies have explored the interplay between chromatin-associated condensates and chromatins by manipulating the valency of multivalent protein modules in the nucleus [6]. Utilizing these engineered protein modules has deepened our understanding of the relationship between condensates and genetic material in the nucleus. For example, when two condensates initially located at a distance coalesce during maturation, the associated DNA regions are repositioned into closer proximity [7, 8]. Otherwise, chromatin can act as a physical barrier regulating condensate behaviors, including mobility and coarsening [9]. Despite these insights, there remains limited understanding of how chromatin affects the phase behavior of endogenous IC condensates in actual cell physiology.

While organized with the nuclear lamina, heterochromatins are responsive to external stimuli such as mechanical forces and metabolic changes [10, 11]. Cellular senescence is another context in which heterochromatins are structurally modified. Senescent cells exhibit distinct profiles of histone modifications; meanwhile, their heterochromatins are detached from the lamina, which lie beneath the nuclear envelope and condensed relatively inward, leading to the formation of senescence-associated heterochromatin foci (SAHF) and uncontrolled gene expression [12]. Furthermore, nuclear condensates in senescent cells often exhibit aberrant dynamics, persisting for extended periods without mitosis-induced disassembly [13]. Consistent with this, we discovered the altered phase properties of nucleoli, including increased sphericity of subnucleolar phases and aberrant “inside-out” architectures under cellular senescence [14]. Here, we sought to correlate the structural features of chromatin, particularly the extent of compaction, with IC condensates by analyzing changes in the phase behaviors of paraspeckles, which are spatially adjacent to chromatin and involved in post-transcriptional regulation [15].

Results and discussions

Cellular senescence promotes growth dynamics of paraspeckles

To investigate whether cellular senescence-induced changes in phase behavior were also evident in other nuclear condensates beyond nucleoli, we first observed U2OS cells stably expressing green fluorescent protein (GFP)-attached Non-POU domain containing octamer-binding protein (NONO), a well-characterized paraspeckle protein (Fig. S1A). As previously described, NONO-GFP expressing U2OS cells treated with etoposide, a topoisomerase II inhibitor, showed evident signs of cellular senescence such as increase in both activity of senescence-associated β-galactosidase and expression of p21 (Fig. S2) [14, 16]. Etoposide-affected senescent cells displayed both increased numbers and area fractions of NONO foci (Fig. S1A–C). In accordance with the higher numbers and area fractions of NONO-GFP foci, the FCI values were also elevated in senescent cells (Fig. S1D–F). Furthermore, we revealed that genotoxic stress-induced senescence can establish an environment where incorporation of NONO-GFP into foci was favored, particularly to the cells expressing more NONO-GFP, as indicated by the steeper slope in our Ifoci + Idilute − FCI plot (Fig. S1G). To investigate the findings from the GFP-labeled NONO foci that were in accordance with paraspeckle, we stained their two core components, lncRNA NEAT1_2, which is required for the assembly of paraspeckle by accommodating multiple RNA-binding proteins, as well as endogenous NONO (Fig. 1A). Consistent with previous reports, NONO proteins showed strong colocalization with probes of fluorescence in situ hybridization (FISH) targeting the central region of NEAT1_2, in both unaffected and senescent cells (Fig. 1B) [17]. We observed the increased numbers and area fraction of paraspeckles that contained both NONO and NEAT1_2 upon the etoposide-induced cellular senescence (Fig. 1C–D). FCI of endogenous NONO immunoreactivity was increased as well (Fig. 1E). Since NEAT1_2 signals in the dilute phase were undetectable by FISH, we used integrated intensity as a measure, which also showed an increase in senescent cells, despite not reaching statistical significance (Fig. 1F). Western blot and qPCR further confirmed elevated levels of NONO protein and NEAT1_2 transcript, respectively (Fig. S4). Intriguingly, the expression of total NEAT1 transcripts composed of both NEAT1_2 and shorter NEAT1_1, incapable of paraspeckle formation, was unchanged, implying the specific elevation of paraspeckle-associated function under cellular senescence (Fig. S4C). In addition, we sought to examine the comparable changes of paraspeckles under cellular senescence in IMR90 cells, a lung-derived fibroblast line, using dual labeling of NONO and NEAT1_2 [18]. Senescent IMR90 cells also exhibited the increased numbers, area fraction, FCI of paraspeckles, and integrated intensity of NEAT1_2 (Fig. S5A–G). Of note, nuclear enlargement in senescent cells could contribute to the increased numbers of paraspeckles, as it was previously reported that paraspeckle abundance scales with nuclear size [19, 20]. Among the paraspeckles in senescent cells, particularly the larger ones, were often observed to have an elongated shape, whereas the foci in normal cells mostly had the circular shape of typical condensates (Fig. 1B, zoom-in images). To quantitatively assess the elongated shapes found in paraspeckles of senescent cells, we analyzed the aspect ratio of paraspeckles co-labeled with NONO and NEAT1_2, which is the ratio of the major axis to the minor axis, thereby serving as an effective metric for reflecting the elongation. Aspect ratio values were generally higher in senescent cells, with markedly elevated values in the top 25% largest paraspeckles (Fig. 1G, Fig. S5H, and Fig. S6A). This effect was more pronounced in U2OS cells expressing NONO-GFP. Although the overall increase of aspect ratio was not statistically significant, the top 25% largest foci showed a robust and significant rise (Fig. S6B–C), supporting preferential elongation of larger paraspeckles during senescence. Increased occupancy of elongated paraspeckles in the senescent cells might be largely relevant to their function, including RNA editing, because larger paraspeckles can accommodate more functional factors like inverted Alu repeat-containing transcripts [21, 22]. Besides, both circularity and roundness, other morphological metrics, of NONO-GFP foci in senescent cells are decreased, supporting their less circular shapes, though the latter was changed rather slightly (Fig. S6D–E).

The elongated form of large paraspeckles has been understood as the longitudinal growth through the sequential addition of copolymer micelle units composed of NEAT1_2 and RNA-binding proteins to pre-existing paraspeckles rod [23]. This micellization model differs from the coalescence of adjacent condensates, which discretely scale up their size while maintaining spherical shapes (Fig. 1H). The micelle units formed by the amphipathic properties of self-hybridized NEAT1_2 loops serve as structural blocks for paraspeckle assembly, allowing the core and shell to accommodate distinct groups of RNA-binding proteins [17, 23]. If paraspeckle elongation during cellular senescence occurs through micellization, this core–shell distinction should be maintained. To test this hypothesis, we examined the spatial distribution of TDP-43 and NONO, each shell and core-enriched protein, during the progression of cellular senescence [24]. High-resolution images revealed that NONO co-localized with FISH probes targeting the central region of NEAT1_2 that marked the core of elongated paraspeckles, while immunoreactivities to TDP-43 were detected rather at the periphery of them (Fig. 1I). This core–shell architecture as well as the elongation of paraspeckles determined by aspect ratio were continuously observed as the duration of etoposide treatment lasted nine days, addressing the micellization-driven growth of paraspeckles under cellular senescence (Fig. 1J, Fig. S7). Moreover, time-lapse observation showed that two NONO-GFP foci that contacted each other were rather separated again than ended up with coalescence, indicating that the growth of paraspeckle does not occur via immediate fusion between condensates (Fig. 1K). In conclusion, cellular senescence can affect the many aspects of phase behaviors of NONO-positive paraspeckles.

Compaction of heterochromatin-containing HP1α alleviates constraints on the movement of NONO-positive paraspeckles

Interestingly, we observed the enhanced movement of NONO-GFP foci in senescent cells (Video S1–2). To characterize the extent of NONO-GFP foci movement in both senescent and non-senescent cells, we executed the particle tracking of NONO-GFP foci over a decade of minute period (Fig. 2A). When cumulative mean square displacement (MSD) of NONO-GFP foci under the time series were plotted, NONO-GFP foci in senescent cells showed longer trajectories with higher terminal MSD than those in non-senescent cells (Fig. 2B–C). Cellular senescence generally loosens the chromatin by reducing the overall level of repressive histone code, leading to the increase of accessibility. Meanwhile, certain chromatin regions are assembled into severely condensed states like SAHFs [25, 26]. These loose-condensed imbalances permit the expansion of certain IC areas, providing more space for agile condensates like paraspeckles. To investigate whether imbalanced chromatin occupancy inside the nucleus is observed in our system, we analyzed the distribution of heterochromatin using DAPI, which has strong spatial colocalization with heterochromatin protein 1α (HP1α) and macro histone 2 A 1.2 (mH2A1.2), both established markers of condensed heterochromatin (Fig. S9) [27–29]. Non-senescent cells showed a relatively even distribution of heterochromatin, while senescent cells displayed pronounced condensation of heterochromatin with strengthened DAPI-positive signals as well as expanded areas of very weak DAPI staining, indicating an imbalanced heterochromatin distribution (Fig. S9). Intriguingly, the line profile analysis of DAPI and GFP fluorescence near NONO-GFP foci demonstrated that these foci found in senescent cells were located in DAPI-sparse regions (Fig. S10), well-matched with previous reports [30]. Consequently, we interpreted that the uneven distribution of chromatin in senescent cells caused the expansion of the IC. Thus, paraspeckles, as IC condensates, gain increased space for movement, which is reflected in the enhanced trajectories of NONO-GFP foci.

To reveal that excessive condensation of heterochromatin is responsible for the increase in paraspeckle mobility, we sought to mimic the state of heterochromatin in senescent cells by modulating the oligomerization of HP1α which is critical for heterochromatin condensation. By employing the chemogenetic switch operated by the reversible oligomerization of the BTB domain of BCL6 transcription factor, we succeeded in manipulating the HP1α condensation, which was visualized using mCherry fluorescence (Fig. S11A–D) [31]. The degree of HP1α condensation, represented using FCI values, was increased upon BI-3802 treatment and reversibly decreased upon the following treatment of BI-3812 (Fig. S11E). In addition, the induced HP1α condensates exhibited strong colocalization with H3K9me3-marked heterochromatin, aligning that HP1α is a reader protein of H3K9me3 (Fig. S11F). On the other hand, condensed BTB-HP1α did not colocalize with PML bodies (Fig. S11F). This is because BTB oligomerization-induced HP1α condensation did not initiate the formation of heterochromatin, which is mediated by PML bodies through the incorporation of H3.3 and the induction of H3K9me3, but promotes the assembly of pre-formed heterochromatin that is already methylated [32, 33]. Using this system, we monitored the movement of NONO-GFP in response to HP1α assembly and disassembly through live-cell imaging. Compared to cells untransfected with the inducible HP1α switch, both the length of trajectories and terminal MSD of NONO-GFP foci were increased by the promoted condensation of HP1α using BI-3802 (Fig. 2D–F). The following addition of BI-3812 to break down the preassembled HP1α condensates recovered the trajectories and terminal MSD of NONO-GFP foci, describing the correlation between paraspeckle movement and HP1α condensation (Fig. 2D–F). Therefore, these findings suggest that HP1α condensation, which constitutes condensed heterochromatin, can influence the dynamics of IC condensates near the chromatin even independently of the cellular aging process.

The altered landscapes of lysine modifications on the histone subunits are another hallmark of the senescent nuclei [34]. As previously reported, senescent cells under genotoxic stress exhibit increased acetylation of histone H3 at lysine 27 (H3K27ac) and decreased tri-methylation of histone H3 at lysine 9 (H3K9me3) (Fig. S12A–C) [25, 34]. Additionally, the levels of the condensed heterochromatin-enriched histone variant macroH2A1.2 (mH2A1.2) were elevated (Fig. S12A, D). To explore whether senescence-associated chemical modifications of histone proteins influence NONO-GFP foci dynamics, we treated cells with valproic acid (VPA), a pan-histone deacetylase inhibitor to enhance histone acetylation (Fig. S13A–B). Particle tracking and concomitant evaluation of MSD of NONO-GFP foci in time-series revealed that VPA treatment did not significantly affect their mobility (Fig. 2G–I). Meanwhile, no significant changes were detected in the FCI values of HP1α, which addressed the degree of its condensation, through the treatment of VPA (Fig. S13C–D). Taken together, these results suggest that the increased motility of the NONO-resident paraspeckles under cellular senescence arises not from altered modification profiles on lysine residues at the single nucleosome scale, but rather from structural changes coordinated by multiple chromatin regions, such as clustering of heterochromatins driven by HP1α condensation and expanded IC area.

Conclusion

Resident at IC, paraspeckles in senescent cells show distinct phase behaviors, including increased numbers, size, motility, and morphological elongation facilitated by the longitudinal growth through the micellization model (Fig. 2J). Furthermore, the increased motility of paraspeckles is driven by chromatin remodeling, particularly HP1α-mediated heterochromatin condensation and the associated expansion of IC space (Fig. 2J). These findings provide novel insights into the interplay between chromatin organization and nuclear condensates, highlighting the altered distribution of chromatin as a key regulator of paraspeckle dynamics during the cellular aging process.

Methods

Plasmid

To construct NONO-GFP and BTB-mCherry-HP1α, NONO and HP1α were cloned from a human cDNA library. The NONO fragment was inserted into the XhoI and BamHI sites of pEGFP-N1. B2342_BTB-mCherry was a gift from Chandra Tucker (Addgene plasmid # 215095). The HP1α fragment was inserted into the AgeI and NotI sites of BTB-mCherry by ligating mCherry and HP1α through the linker sequence ggtggtagtctcgag (GGSLE), using overlapping PCR.

Cell culture

The human osteosarcoma cell line U2OS and human fetal lung fibroblast cell line IMR90 were purchased from American Type Culture Collection and Korean Cell Line Bank, respectively. U2OS cells were cultured in DMEM (GenDepot) supplemented with 10% fetal bovine serum (FBS) (Gibco), 100 U/mL penicillin/streptomycin (Gibco), and 1% GlutaMAX (Gibco). IMR90 cells were cultured in MEM (Corning) supplemented with 10% FBS (Gibco), 100 U/mL penicillin/streptomycin (Gibco), and 1% GlutaMAX (Gibco). All cells were checked for mycoplasma contamination and incubated at 37 °C in a humidified atmosphere with 5% CO2.

Generation of U2OS cells stably expressing NONO-GFP

To generate a U2OS stably expressing NONO-GFP cell line, U2OS cells were transfected with NONO-GFP using ViaFect (Promega) and selected with G418 (InvivoGen) for two weeks. GFP-positive cells were subsequently sorted using a FACSAria Fusion flow cytometer (BD Bioscience).

Senescence induction

To induce genotoxic stress-induced senescence, U2OS and U2OS NONO-GFP stable cells were treated with 2 µM etoposide (Alfa Aesar) for 5 days, as described in a previous study [14, 16]. IMR90 cells at passage 21 were treated with 50 µM etoposide for 10 days, following the protocol of C Anerillas et al. [18].

For oxidative stress-induced senescence, U2OS NONO-GFP stable cells were exposed to 200 µM H2O2 for 2 h and subsequently incubated in normal medium for 5 days, as previously reported [14].

To analyze the progressive elongation of paraspeckles, prolonged senescence was induced by treating U2OS NONO-GFP stable cells with 2 µM etoposide for 9 days.

Senescence-associated β-galactosidase assay (SA-β-gal assay)

Etoposide-induced senescent U2OS NONO-GFP cells were identified using a senescence-associated β-galactosidase kit (Cell Signaling Technology), following the manufacturer’s instructions. β-galactosidase-stained (β-gal+) cells were imaged using the EVOS™ M5000 imaging system (ThermoFisher) equipped with a 20 × fluorite long working distance lens (NA 0.45, WD 6.23). A GFP LED light cube and the RGB transmitted light option were used to visualize NONO-GFP and β-galactosidase staining, respectively. The proportion of SA-β-gal+ cells was calculated as the ratio of β-gal+ cells to the total number of cells, with both groups restricted to cells exhibiting NONO-GFP signal.

Sequential RNA-Fluorescence in situ hybridization (RNA-FISH) and Immunofluorescence (IF)

To visualize the middle region of NEAT1_2, Stellaris FISH probes targeting the human NEAT1 middle segment with Quasar 570 (Biosearch Technologies) were used.

Cells were cultured on 18 mm coverslips (Marienfeld). For sequential RNA-FISH and IF, cells were fixed with 4% paraformaldehyde in PBS (Thermo Scientific) for 10 min at room temperature, followed by three 5-min washes with RNase-free PBS. Cells were then permeabilized in 0.2% Triton X-100 (v/v) in RNase-free PBS for 5 min at room temperature.

After permeabilization, cells were incubated with Wash buffer A (Biosearch Technologies) for 5 min at room temperature, then hybridized overnight at 37 ℃ in a dark humidified chamber with 125 nM FISH probes diluted in Hybridization buffer (Biosearch Technologies). Hybridized cells were washed with Wash buffer A for 30 min at 37 ℃ in the dark, followed by a 5-min wash with Wash buffer B (Biosearch Technologies).

After a brief rinse with RNase-free PBS, cells were blocked with RNase-free PBS containing 1% bovine serum albumin (BSA) (GeneDepot) for 30 min at room temperature.

For sequential immunofluorescence of NONO or TDP-43, cells were incubated overnight at 4 °C with the following primary antibodies diluted in RNase-free PBS containing 1% BSA: mouse monoclonal anti-NONO (1:300; Santa Cruz, sc-376804) or rabbit polyclonal anti-TDP-43 (1:300; Proteintech, 10,782–2-AP). After three 5-min washes with RNase-free PBS, cells were incubated for 1 h at room temperature with the corresponding secondary antibodies diluted in RNase-free PBS containing 1% BSA: Alexa Fluor 488-conjugated goat anti-mouse IgG (H + L) or Alexa Fluor 647-conjugated goat anti-rabbit IgG (1:300; Invitrogen). Following three final washes with RNase-free PBS, coverslips were mounted using ProLong™ Gold Antifade Mountant (Thermo Fisher Scientific).

Image acquisition and analysis of NONO-GFP foci

To measure the number, area fraction, and morphological metrics of NONO-GFP foci, as well as to calculate the Fluorescence Coexistence Index (FCI), fluorescence images acquired on fixed cells using an LSM710 confocal laser scanning microscope (ZEISS) equipped with a Plan-Apochromat 100 ×/1.4 Oil DIC M27 objective. A 488 nm laser was used for imaging NONO-GFP. For quantitative analysis of fluorescence intensity, imaging parameters—including LED intensity, gain, digital offset, and pinhole size—were kept identical across all groups.

All segmentation and quantitative measurements described below were performed using Image J software. To delineate NONO-GFP foci (foci) from the total NONO-GFP-expressing region (foci + dilute phase), two rounds of thresholding were applied. First, background intensity was subtracted using a rolling ball radius of 101 pixels, and the total region encompassing both foci and the dilute phase was outlined using Otsu thresholding. Second, to mask each focus, signals were normalized using the enhance contrast tool (0%). Primary ROIs for foci were then segmented using Otsu’s thresholding with a minimum size threshold of 8 pixels. If the primary ROIs encompassed a broader area than the actual foci, secondary ROIs were defined within the primary ROIs using Max Entropy thresholding.

Image acquisition and analysis of endogenous paraspeckles

To quantitatively analyze the growth dynamics of endogenous paraspeckles in senescent U2OS and IMR90 cells, NEAT1_2 and NONO-labeled fluorescent images were acquired using an LSM710 confocal laser scanning microscope (ZEISS) equipped with a Plan-Apochromat 100 ×/1.4 Oil DIC M27 objective. Excitation was performed using 488 nm and 561 nm lasers for NONO and NEAT1_2, respectively. For quantitative analysis of fluorescence intensity, imaging parameters—including LED intensity, gain, digital offset, and pinhole size—were kept identical across all groups. Additionally, both NEAT1_2 and NONO signals were acquired from the same single z-plane to evaluate their colocalization.

Paraspeckles were defined as foci positive for both NONO and NEAT1_2, identified using the same segmentation workflow described above. While the overall procedure was consistent, the combined region of foci and surrounding dilute phase for NEAT1_2 segmentation was defined using the NONO signal. Subsequent segmentation of individual NEAT1_2 foci was then performed on the NEAT1_2 image within the imported ROIs. Parameters related to paraspeckle growth dynamics were measured and calculated according to the same analysis procedure above.

Immunoblotting

To assess the cellular levels of p21, NONO-GFP, endogenous NONO, H3K9me3, H3K27ac, macro H2A 1.2, U2OS NONO-GFP stable cells, and U2OS cells were washed with PBS and lysed at 4 °C with RIPA (50 mM Tris–HCl pH 7.5; 150 mM NaCl; 0.5% Sodium deoxycholate; 2 mM EDTA; 1% Triton X-100, 0.1% SDS) containing a protease inhibitor cocktail (Quartett). The cell lysates were incubated on ice for 20 min and sonicated for 1 min using the Omni Sonic Ruptor 400 Ultrasonic Homogenizer (Omni International). Proteins were mixed with 4X SDS sample loading buffer (250 mM Tris–HCl pH 6.8; 0.02% Bromophenol Blue; 8% SDS; 8% 2-Mercaptoethanol; 40% Glycerol) and boiled at 100 °C for 8 min.

Protein samples were separated by SDS-PAGE for 120 min and transferred to a PVDF membrane (GVS Filter Technology) overnight. The membrane was blocked with 3% BSA in 0.1% Tween-20 in 1X Tris-buffered saline (TBS-T) on a rotating shaker for 60 min at room temperature (RT). The blocked membrane was incubated overnight at 4 °C with optimal concentrations of primary antibodies: rabbit polyclonal anti-p21 (Proteintech), mouse monoclonal anti-NONO (Santa Cruz, sc-376804), mouse monoclonal anti-β-actin (Santa Cruz, sc-47778), rabbit polyclonal anti-H3 (abcam, AB1791), rabbit polyclonal anti-macroH2A1.2 (Cell Signaling, #4827S), rabbit polyclonal anti-H3K27Ac (abcam, AB4729), and mouse monoclonal anti-H3K9me3 (Active Motif, #39285). After three washes with TBS-T for 10 min each, the membrane was incubated with horseradish peroxidase-labeled anti-mouse secondary antibodies (Invitrogen) at RT for 1 h. Immunolabeled proteins were visualized using an enhanced chemiluminescence (ECL) kit (Biomax) and detected using the LAS-3000 Imager (Fujifilm). Densitometric analysis of the detected proteins was performed using the ImageJ software.

Time-lapse imaging of NONO-GFP foci and particle tracking under genotoxic stress-induced senescence

For observation of the fusion between adjacent NONO-GFP foci and track their movements, time-lapse imaging was performed using a DMi8 microscope (Leica) equipped with a Dragonfly 302 spinning disk confocal system (Andor), a Zyla PLUS 4.2-megapixel USB3.0 sCMOS camera (Andor), and HC PL APO 63 ×/1.4 Oil CS2 objective. A 488 nm laser was used for imaging NONO-GFP (The BIORP of Korea Basic Science Institute, KBSI).

Cells were maintained in a chamber at 37 °C in a humidified atmosphere with 5% CO2 using the CU-501 programmable temperature and gas control system (Live cell instrument). Imaging was performed at a rate of 1 frame every 20 s for 10 min, with Z-stacks acquired at 0.5-µm intervals over a total thickness of 2 µm to minimize photobleaching and ensure clear visualization of foci.

Z-stack images were processed using the Max Intensity algorithm for Z-projection, and particle tracking was performed using the TrackMate plugin in ImageJ software. NONO-GFP foci were detected by applying a Laplacian of Gaussian (LoG) filter to the images with an estimated object diameter of 0.5 µm, and the quality of initial thresholding was set manually. Sequential trajectories were generated using the simple Linear Assignment Problem (LAP) tracking algorithm with a maximum linking distance of 1.0 µm, a maximum gap-closing distance of 1.0 µm, and a maximum frame gap of 1 frame. Measured coordinates were used to calculate the cumulative mean square displacement (MSD) over time, and the terminal MSD was determined from MSD values at the final time point.

Time-lapse imaging of NONO-GFP foci and particle tracking under oxidative stress-induced senescence

For tracking the NONO-GFP foci in U2OS NONO-GFP cells undergoing H2O2-induced senescence, time-lapse imaging was performed using an LSM710 confocal laser scanning microscope (ZEISS) equipped with a Plan-Apochromat 100 ×/1.4 Oil DIC M27 objective. A 488 nm laser was used to visualize NONO-GFP.

Cells were maintained in a chamber at 37 °C in a humidified atmosphere with 5% CO2. Imaging was performed at a rate of 1 frame every 20 s for 10 min, with Z-stacks acquired at 0.5-µm intervals over a total thickness of 2 µm to minimize photobleaching and ensure clear visualization of foci.

Stacked image processing and trajectory tracking of NONO-GFP foci were conducted using the same methods described above. In addition, cell drift was corrected by calculating the deviation of the nuclear center of mass in each frame relative to that in the first frame.

Immunofluorescence

U2OS NONO-GFP stable cells were cultured on 18 mm coverslips (Marienfeld). For immunostaining, the cells were fixed with 4% paraformaldehyde in PBS (Thermo Scientific) for 15 min at room temperature and washed three times with PBS for 5 min each. The cells were then permeabilized for 10 min with Triton X-100 0.5% (v/v) in PBS at room temperature, before blocking with PBS containing 2% BSA at room temperature for 60 min. Primary antibodies were applied in PBS supplemented with 2% BSA and incubated overnight at 4 °C with the optimal concentration of primary antibodies: mouse monoclonal anti-HP1a (Sigma-Aldrich, #05-689) and rabbit polyclonal anti-macroH2A1.2 (Cell Signaling, #4827S). Appropriate fluorescent secondary antibodies were applied at a dilution of 1:500 after washing three times with PBS supplemented with 2% BSA for 40 min at room temperature: Alexa 594-conjugated goat anti-mouse IgG (Thermo Fisher Scientific). After washing three times with PBS, the samples were stained and mounted with 4′6-diamidino-2-phenylindole (DAPI, 2 mg/ml, Molecular Probe) in 70% glycerol (v/v) in PBS.

Image acquisition and analysis of line profiles

To investigate the characteristics of NONO-positive paraspeckles as interchromatin compartment (IC) condensates within chromatin and to assess the formation of senescence-associated heterochromatin (SAHF), fluorescence images were acquired from fixed cells using a LSM700 confocal laser scanning microscope (ZEISS) equipped with a C-Apochromat 40 ×/1.2 W Korr M M27 objective. A 405 mm, 488 nm, and 555 nm lasers were used for imaging DAPI, NONO-GFP, and Alexa 594-labeled macro H2A1.2, respectively.

To analyze FCI values of HP1α and generate line plots comparing HP1α and DAPI signals, the microscopy setup previously used for time-lapse imaging was employed in this experiment without an incubation chamber at room temperature. A 405 mm and 561 nm lasers were used for imaging DAPI and Alexa 594-labeled HP1α, respectively.

For line profile analysis, NONO-GFP foci, DAPI foci, HP1α foci, and macro H2A 1.2 foci were identified using the same ROI settings described above. The mean fluorescence intensities for these foci were measured along the same line positions using the Plot Profile tool in ImageJ software. FCI values for HP1α were measured using the protocol as for NONO-GFP.

Reversible HP1α condensation using the BTB oligomerization system

To manipulate heterochromatin compaction, U2OS NONO-GFP stable cells were transfected with BTB-mCherry-HP1α using Polyfect (QIAGEN). HP1α condensation was induced by treating the cells with 1 μM BI-3802 (MedChemExpress) for 5 min. Following the 5-min treatment, 10-min live-cell imaging was performed under conditions where the chemical remained present, as described above, and visualized using a 561-nm laser. Subsequently, HP1α decondensation was induced by treating the same cells with 10 μM BI-3812 (MedChemExpress) for 10 min. Ten-minute live-cell imaging was repeated after the 10-min treatment, with the chemical still present during imaging. The treatment concentrations and durations were determined, as previously described [31].

Immunofluorescence after reversible HP1α condensation and de-condensation

To examine whether the induced HP1α condensates colocalize with HP1α-resident heterochromatin and PML bodies, condensation and subsequent decondensation of HP1α were induced as described above in U2OS cells transfected with BTB-mCherry-HP1α.

After induction, cells were subjected to immunofluorescence staining to detect H3K9me3 and PML. Cells grown on 18 mm coverslips were fixed with 4% paraformaldehyde in PBS (Thermo Scientific) for 10 min at room temperature and washed three times with PBS for 5 min each. The cells were then permeabilized for 10 min with 0.2% Triton X-100 (v/v) in PBS at room temperature. After permeabilization, cells were blocked with PBS containing 3% BSA for 1 h at room temperature.

Cells were incubated overnight at 4 °C with the following primary antibodies diluted in PBS containing 3% BSA: mouse monoclonal anti-PML (1:300; Santa Cruz, sc-966) and rabbit polyclonal anti-H3K9me3 (1:300; Abcam, ab8898). After three 5-min washes with PBS, cells were incubated for 1 h at room temperature with the corresponding secondary antibodies diluted in PBS containing 3% BSA: Alexa Fluor 488-conjugated goat anti-mouse IgG (H + L) and Alexa Fluor 647-conjugated goat anti-rabbit IgG (1:300; Invitrogen). Following three final washes with PBS, coverslips were mounted using ProLong™ Gold Antifade Mountant (Thermo Fisher Scientific).

Fluorescence images were acquired using an LSM710 confocal laser scanning microscope (ZEISS) equipped with a Plan-Apochromat 100 ×/1.4 Oil DIC M27 objective. Excitation was performed using 488 nm, 561, and 639 nm lasers for PML, BTB-mCherry-HP1α, and H3K9me3, respectively. PML, BTB-mCherry-HP1α, and H3K9me3 signals were acquired from the same single z-plane to evaluate their colocalization.

Statistical analysis

Statistical analyses were carried out using GraphPad Prism 10 software. Comparisons between two means were performed using an unpaired t-test. Comparisons between multiple means over two were performed using one-way analysis of variance (ANOVA) with Tukey’s test. All statistical analyses were conducted using the mean values from three independent biological or technical replicates per condition. In accordance with the central limit theorem, normality tests were not performed. A p-value of < 0.05 was considered statistically significant. The specific details for the number of foci and cells analyzed are provided in the relevant figure legends.
