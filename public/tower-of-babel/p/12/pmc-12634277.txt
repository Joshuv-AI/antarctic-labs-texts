Less is more: Evaluating the effectiveness of simplified two and three-element scores for predicting unfavorable outcomes in acute toxic alcohol exposure: A multi-center study
Authors: Asmaa Fady Sharif, Badar Abdullah Al Qasem, Muhannad Abdullah Alsaidan, Abdulaziz Adel Al Abdulkader, Sultan Abdulhamid Albisher, Mesfer Mubarak Hanif, Bandar Saleh Aldurayb, Mohammad Abdullah AL-Otaibi, Abeer Jaber Mohamed, Fahad aldosari, Khalid A. Al-Mulhim, Zahraa Khalifa Sobh
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
Toxic alcohols are readily available in multiple household products. Although acute toxic alcohol exposure is less frequently reported, it results in severe consequences. This study aimed to evaluate the effectiveness of the three elements score, PGI]pH, Glasgow Coma Scale (GCS), and Impaired systolic blood pressure (SBP)] score and its two elements modified PG (pH and GCS) score, in identifying high-risk alcohol-exposed patients, compared to the multivariable conventional poison severity score (PSS). This cross-sectional study was conducted among 80 patients admitted to two poison control centers over a five-year period. These patients were exposed to toxic alcohols, including methanol, glycols, and propanol. The PGI/PG scores were derived retrospectively by assigning one point for every adopted variable, including pH < 7.25, GCS < 13, and impaired SBP < 90 mmHg. Patients with unfavorable outcomes constituted 27.5 %, where 17.5 % underwent hemodialysis (HD), 6.3 % developed blindness, 5 % needed mechanical ventilation (MV), and 2.5 % died. Patients with unfavorable outcomes demonstrated significantly higher PSS, PGI, and PG scores (p < 0.05). PSS was superior in predicting blindness only. PGI and PG scores demonstrated higher capabilities for predicting unfavorable outcomes, as well as the need for HD and MV. PG achieved the highest areas under curve as an unfavorable outcome’s predictor (0.889), need for HD (0.819), and MV (0.993). The PGI and PG scores could serve as simple and reliable predictors of unfavorable outcomes, including the need for HD and MV among patients poisoned with acute toxic alcohol exposure.

Introduction

Toxic alcohols are readily available in multiple household products, including methanol, isopropyl alcohol, propanol and glycols. These agents have common metabolic properties. Although acute poisoning from toxic alcohol is less frequent than poisoning from other harmful substances, it still results in severe consequences, including death [1]. Methanol constitutes the majority of toxic alcohol exposure because of its widespread use in both industries and household products. Furthermore, methanol is often used as an adulterant in alcoholic beverages due to its physical similarities with ethanol. Consequently, ingestion of illegally manufactured spirits is a common cause of acute methanol poisoning [2], [3]. Additionally, a remarkable increase in mass methanol poisoning was reported during the COVID-19 pandemic. This incremental increase might be attributed to the misconception that consuming alcoholic beverages kills the virus and guards against infection [4], [5], [6]. The reported surge was associated with much higher mortality rates of 55 % [6], and 61.3 % [7].

Indeed, toxic alcohols potentiate the central GABA effects, which suppress the vasomotor center and induce circulatory shock. Additionally, these intoxicants can also result in metabolic acidosis, which is associated with altered sensorium [8]. Nevertheless, these signs are often non-specific and can be induced with several other intoxicants. This non-specific nature of symptoms can make diagnosing acute toxic alcohol poisoning quite challenging. Delayed or insufficient management is frequently linked to severe complications, which can include visual impairment, kidney failure, and neurological damage [1], [9].

Timed management is critical in handling patients of acute toxic alcohol exposure. Previous literature has focused on identifying high-risk patients early using various clinical scores, including the Poison Severity Score (PSS), Acute Physiology and Chronic Health Evaluation (APACHE) II score, Sequential Organ Failure Assessment (SOFA) score, Rapid Acute Physiology Score (RAPS), and Rapid Emergency Medicine Score (REMS). While these clinical scores have demonstrated relatively high accuracy in predicting adverse outcomes from toxic alcohol exposure, their complexity can make them challenging to apply, especially in emergency settings [10], [11].

In clinical settings with limited resources, utilizing the aforementioned prognostic scores during emergencies may prove impractical due to insufficient data collection and recording. Furthermore, predictors that are unavailable for testing in these resource-constrained environments or that take several days for reporting are unhelpful to clinicians making decisions in emergency situations [12], [13]. Therefore, there is an urgent need to develop simplified, objective, and rapid prognostic scores. It is considered essential to adopt two or three-element scores that can be obtained by a physician or nursing team upon patient admission.

Recently, PGI]pH, Glasgow Coma Scale (GCS), and impaired systolic blood pressure (SBP)] score is a three-element bedside tool that was designed to predict the mortality in acute aluminum phosphide (AlP) poisoning [14], [15], [16]. However, its effectiveness in assessing poisoning from other substances has not yet been investigated. The present study aimed to assess the effectiveness of the PGI score, in its current form, for predicting unfavorable outcomes in acute toxic alcohol exposure. Furthermore, we explored whether simplification, achieved by excluding the least significant element of the PGI, would influence its predictive utility. Thus, the modified forms of the PGI score (PG, including only pH and GCS) were also investigated as another early bedside prognostic tool that can predict unfavorable outcomes in acute toxic alcohol poisoning.

Subjects and methods

Study design and setting

This was a retrospective multi-center cross-sectional study that was conducted using medical records of patients diagnosed with acute toxic alcohol poisoning and admitted to King Fahad Medical City (KFMC) and King Saud Medical City (KSMC) Emergency Departments during the period 2019–2024.

Sampling and sample size calculation

A convenience sampling method was utilized to collect all medical records from patients who met the inclusion criteria. However, to ensure that the current study achieved its objectives, the minimal sample size was determined using R Statistical language (version 4.4.1) [17]. This calculation resulted in an estimated minimum of 72 patients, considering a 0.6 % prevalence of mortality, 2.1 % significant major life-threatening events in methanol-exposed patients, and 4.9 % of glycol-exposed patients [18]. Additionally, a receiver operating characteristic (ROC) curve power analysis was conducted, assuming an area under the curve (AUC) of 0.8 or greater, an alpha level of 0.01, a power of 90 %, and a control-to-case ratio of 4:1. This analysis indicated a requirement of at least 16 cases and 63 controls, with a minimal overall sample size of no less than 79 participants [19]. In ROC curve analyses, we employed a bootstrap method to estimate the 95 % confidence interval for the AUC, which provides more robust uncertainty estimates in small samples. Furthermore, instead of relying solely on global model performance, we identified and reported the optimal threshold based on Youden’s index, along with the associated sensitivity and specificity, to offer more clinically interpretable findings. Power testing was conducted for the ROC curve of each predicting score and outcome, showing a power of 99.8 % for PSS, and a unified power of 100 % for the PGI and PG as predictors of unfavorable outcomes [death, blindness, need for hemodialysis (HD) or mechanical ventilation (MV)], collectively.

Inclusion and exclusion criteria

All adults diagnosed with acute toxic alcohol poisoning and admitted during the study period were eligible. The diagnosis was confirmed by qualitative analysis using gas chromatography-mass spectrophotometry (GC-MS). The three investigated types of toxic alcohols were methanol, glycol, and propanol. We excluded patients with long-term chronic kidney disease, significant visual impairment, and chronic respiratory problems. Additionally, we excluded patients who received any form of hospital treatment prior to admission, as well as those referred from other poison control centers. Patients with a poisoning severity score (PSS) of 4 who arrived deceased were also excluded, along with those who had incomplete medical records or missing data.

Compliance with ethical standards

This study was conducted after obtaining ethical approvals from the Institutional Review Board (IRB) at both KSMC (approval number H1R1–07-Nov24–02) and KFMC (IRB Log Number: 24–608). The present study adopted the principles of the Declaration of Helsinki, of 1975, as revised in 2000. Given the retrospective and non-invasive nature of the research, the IRB committee exempted the informed consent. All patient’s relevant information was anonymized, and confidentiality was ensured using the coding system.

Data collection tool

A predesigned case report form was completed for every included patient, documenting the patient’s demographics and history of exposure, including the type of alcohol, manner of consumption, and delay time before seeking medical services. Additionally, we recorded the initial assessment data upon admission, before the patient received medical treatment, which included relevant clinical findings and laboratory investigations. Furthermore, all patients' scores on admission were reported, including the GCS and the PSS. The total GCS ranges from 3 to 15 [20]. The PSS assigns a score ranging from 0 to 4 based on a set of clinical signs and symptoms. This scoring reflects the most severe clinical effects experienced by the patient: a score of 0 indicates that the patient is asymptomatic, 1 denotes minor effects, 2 represents moderate effects, 3 indicates severe effects, and a score of 4 is assigned in the event of death. Consequently, any patient who dies is assigned a score of 4 [21] Furthermore, we calculated the PGI score using three items, pH < 7.25, GCS< 13, and impaired SBP < 90 mmHg, where every item was scored with a single point to end up with a total score ranging between 0 and 3 [14]. Likewise, the PGI score was reassessed as a two-parameter PG score without the I (impaired SBP) component, resulting in a total score that ranges from 0 to 2.

The results of the routinely conducted laboratory workup were reported, including serum glucose levels, serum electrolytes, arterial blood gas analysis, liver transaminases, serum bilirubin, urea and creatinine levels, and various blood indices. The standard medical treatment provided included fomepizole as an antidote, vasopressors, and intravenous fluids as the mainstay therapy. The patient is referred for intubation when one or more of the following conditions are present: impaired consciousness requiring airway protection, severe hypoxemic or hypercapnic respiratory failure, or hemodynamic instability. However, intubation is performed after the patient’s evaluation at the discretion of the on-call intensivist. The two centers where the patients were recruited follow a unified national protocol for management of toxic alcohol poisoning, which complies with the international guidelines [22], [23], [24]. The duration of hospital admission in hours was also stated.

Grouping and outcomes

The investigated patients were classified into favorable and unfavorable outcomes groups. The unfavorable outcome group included death or one or more of the following: blindness, need for HD or MV. Blindness was confirmed by ophthalmological assessment when the visual acuity was less than 6/60, or by fundoscopic examination [25], [26]. HD and MV were conducted whenever indicated [23], [27], [28].

Data analysis

Analyses were conducted using the R Statistical language (version 4.4.2; R Core Team, 2024). To evaluate the distribution of continuous numerical variables, the Shapiro-Wilk test for normality and q-q plots were utilized. For variables with a normal distribution, the Welch t-test was applied for comparisons. In contrast, the Wilcoxon rank-sum test was used for non-normally distributed variables and ordinal data. Spearman’s rank-order correlation assessed relationships between numerical and ordinal variables. The associations among categorical variables were examined using either Pearson’s Chi-square test or Fisher’s exact test, along with the Chi-square test for trend in proportions. ROC curves were generated to evaluate the discriminatory ability of the studied scores. For pairwise comparisons of the AUCs of correlated ROC curves, DeLong's test was used. A significance level of p < 0.05 was established for all statistical tests.

Results

The current study was conducted involving 80 patients diagnosed with acute toxic alcohol exposure. The males outnumbered the females (87.5 % versus 12.5 %), and the age of the studied patients ranged between 19 and 88 years, with a mean of 38.5 years. Patients with unfavorable outcomes constituted 27.5 % (n = 22), where 17.5 % of the studied patients underwent HD (n = 14), 6.3 % developed blindness (n = 5), 5 % were put on MV (n = 4), and 2.5 % died (n = 2). Methanol poisoning accounted for the vast majority of the enrolled cases, while glycols and propanol were reported less frequently in this study. No significant differences were observed between the types of toxic alcohols in terms of their ability to induce unfavorable outcomes (p > 0.05).

As Table 1 illustrates, there was no significant association between the reported patient’s demographics (age, sex, and manner) and unfavorable outcomes. Moreover, the delay time did not significantly alter the patient’s outcomes (p > 0.05). Likewise, all vital signs were comparable among all studied patients. Blurred vision was a significant presentation in patients with unfavorable outcomes (61.1 %) in comparison to those without unfavorable outcomes (38.9 %). Scoring on admission yielded those patients who experienced unfavorable outcomes demonstrated significantly lower GCS but higher PSS, PGI and PG scores (p < 0.05). Patients with unfavorable outcomes exhibited significantly elevated potassium levels (mean = 4.5 vs. 3.9), reduced pH (mean = 7.2 vs. 7.4), and decreased HCO3 levels (mean = 14.8 vs. 22.3 mEq/L) compared to those in the favorable outcomes group. There was also a significant increase in RBC count and hematocrit in this group. Although liver transaminases were higher in the unfavorable outcomes group, these differences did not achieve statistical significance. Discussion

Acute poisoning from toxic alcohols could be associated with serious and evenly life-threatening sequelae [3], [11], [29], [30]. Therefore, this study is concerned with predicting unfavorable outcomes following acute exposure to toxic alcohols, including death, blindness, and need for HD or MV. These unfavorable outcomes were reported in 27.5 % of enrolled patients. Unfortunately, the death was the tragic outcome of 2.5 % of our patients. Similarly, Ghannoum et al. [18], Hoyte et al. [31], and Sharif et al. [11] reported that small fractions of patients of acute poisoning with toxic alcohols died, which concords with current results. Nevertheless, several studies reported higher mortality percentages among patients with acute methanol poisoning [10], [32], [33], [34], which might be attributed to the relatively higher toxicity of methanol compared with other toxic alcohols [11].

In the present work, 6.3 % of the poisoned patients suffered from blindness. Meanwhile, Sharif et al. reported visual loss among 19.5 % of patients following exposure to toxic alcohols [11]. Nonetheless, the higher percentage of patients with severe poisoning according to PSS (18.3 % versus 8.8 % in the current study) could explain the variations in results. Visual impairment is one of the features of acute methanol poisoning, as formic-induced cytotoxicity leads to severe hypoxia. The retina and optic nerves are particularly vulnerable to hypoxia because they contain fewer mitochondria. Also, their high vascularity and metabolic demand facilitate the diffusion of formic acid [35], [36]. Most seriously, increased acidity—whether caused by methanol or concurrent ingestion of toxic alcohols—promotes format diffusion, thereby aggravating its ocular toxicity [37]. Accordingly, Mishra et al. [38] and Jafarizadeh et al. [35] emphasized the association between acidosis and visual impairment in acute methanol poisoning.

In the present work, requiring HD or MV was also considered among the unfavorable outcomes to be predicted because they indicate severe complications from poisoning with toxic alcohols [23], [27], [28]. Most importantly, access to these essential resources can vary greatly among healthcare facilities. By predicting which patients may require them early, providers can facilitate timely transfers to well-equipped facilities, ultimately improving patient prognosis [39], [40].

In the current study, the poisoning with methanol, glycol, and propanol was diagnosed using GC/MS along with history taking and clinical assessment. In many clinical settings, physicians diagnose acute poisoning with toxic alcohols empirically as acute methanol poisoning, since it is the most common adulterant in illicitly manufactured alcoholic beverages. Furthermore, the inconvenience of GC/MS hinders the ability to determine which type of toxic alcohol is responsible for acute poisoning. As a result, there is a scarcity of clinical studies on toxic alcohol poisoning [11]. On the contrary, there were extensive studies on prognostic tools for acute methanol poisoning [10], [33], [34], [41], [42].

It is worth noting that different toxic alcohols share common features in their toxicokinetic and toxicodynamic properties [1], [2], [11]. Indeed, toxic alcohols are CNS depressants that primarily exert their effects through GABAergic activity. Also, their ability to induce cytotoxicity contributes to CNS depression [2]. This explains the association between diminished GCS scores and the development of unfavorable outcomes in this study. Similarly, Mansour et al. [33] and Elbastawesy et al. [42] previously pointed to the high prognostic ability of GCS in predicting poor outcomes among methanol-poisoned patients, which aligns with the current study.

Noteworthy, metabolic acidosis is another important feature of poisoning with toxic alcohols [2]. Methanol, glycol, and propanol are metabolized into formic, glycolic, and propionic acids, respectively. The buildup of these acids in poisoned patients leads to metabolic acidosis. Furthermore, formic acid and, to a lesser extent, glycolic acid could interfere with cellular respiration, leading to increased lactic acid production and exacerbating metabolic acidosis. [35], [43], [44]. Thus, in the present research, the poisoned patients with unfavorable outcomes had significantly lower pH and HCO3 levels, along with a higher anion gap, which concords with previous studies [11], [18], [31].

The well-established ability of toxic alcohol to decrease pH and GCS among poisoned patients led to this study, which assessed, for the first time, the effectiveness of the PGI score in predicting the outcomes of toxic alcohol poisoning, alongside the PSS, the traditional score in clinical toxicology. Importantly, the PGI score has only been examined in acute AlP poisoning and has shown remarkable findings as a predictor of mortality and other adverse outcomes [14], [15], [16], [45]. Nevertheless, its performance as a prognostic tool in other types of toxic exposure has not yet been studied.

Upon admission, both PGI and PSS scores were significantly higher in patients who faced unfavorable outcomes in the present study. The PGI score outperformed the PSS score in predicting unfavorable outcomes overall. Furthermore, the PGI score exhibited superior prognostic capability in predicting the need for HD and MV. Conversely, the PGI score was found to have limited predictive power regarding the development of blindness. Yet, the current study indicated that PSS is a relatively accurate tool for predicting serious visual impairment following exposure to toxic alcohol. The low mortality rate observed in this study hindered the statistical assessment of mortality predictors.

In the present work, blood pressure did not show a significant association with unfavorable outcomes among poisoned patients. Notably, SBP, diastolic blood pressure (DBP), mean arterial pressure (MAP), and shock indices were comparable between patients who had unfavorable outcomes and others, indicating that toxic alcohols are unlikely to induce obvious hemodynamic instability. The non-significance of the third pillar of PGI led us to eliminate it and adopt a novel two-parameter PG score. Interestingly, the PG score, despite its simplicity, demonstrated the highest accuracy in predicting unfavorable outcomes and the need for HD and MV, with AUCs of 0.889, 0.819, and 0.933. In this regard, the differences in the performance of the three studied scores did not reach the level of statistical significance.

In addition to the significant predictors reported in this study, several others have discussed various prognostic factors in acute alcohol exposure, especially methanol poisoning. Yousefinejad et al. reported that hyperglycemia, the time interval from hospital admission, and respiratory arrest are significant predictors of poor outcomes in methanol poisoning [46]. Meanwhile, Shen et al. identified the duration of toxic exposure, delayed admission, and degree of acidosis as significant determinants of visual disturbance following methanol toxicity [47]. While Rafiei et al. stated that mortality significantly correlated with decreased GCS and reduced levels of pH and HCO3, as well as higher heart rate, potassium, creatinine, and blood sugar levels [48]. Additionally, various clinical scores, including the APACHE II score, SOFA score, RAPS, and REMS, were used to predict the adverse outcomes of methanol poisoning [10], [11].

In the era of artificial intelligence (AI), it is noteworthy to mention that previous studies have adopted eight machine learning algorithms to predict adverse outcomes and guide therapeutic management in cases of toxic alcohol exposure. AI models can improve early diagnosis, forecast treatment outcomes, and assist in clinical decision-making [49]. In their study about the role of AI in predicting the need for intubation in methanol-poisoned patients, Moulaei et al. compared the performance of deep and machine learning models. Forty-three features were incorporated into the developed models. The GCS at admission, ICU utilization, age, and biochemical markers constituted the main key predictors for intubation. The XGBoost model outperformed all others in terms of sensitivity, specificity, and other ROC curve-related metrics [50].

Rahimi et al. established a model for predicting the severity of methanol intoxication. The Gradient Boosting Classifier was the best model, incorporating 32 items, where the younger age, higher methanol ingested dose, respiratory symptoms, lower GCS scores, type of visual symptom, duration of therapeutic intervention, ICU admission, and elevated CPK levels were the most significant predictors [34]. From different perspectives, the prognosis of methanol-poisoned patients undergoing hemodialysis could be forecasted by machine learning-driven models using various variables, including age, endotracheal intubation, pH, past medical history, bicarbonate level, GCS, intensive care unit utilization, acute kidney injury, and potassium level [51].

The noticeable clinical importance and lack of statistical significance warrant adopting such simple, objective and resource-conserving tools (PGI and PG scores) rather than detailed and time-consuming scores. Adopting PGI and PG scores deemed worthy, considering the mutuality of the included items compared to other adopted efficient scores for predicting the outcomes of toxic alcohol poisoning, including the PSS, APACHE-II score, SOFA score, RAPS, and REMS [11]. Besides, PGI and PG scores have the advantage of being simpler and more applicable while preserving reasonable predictive power. The simplicity of these scores enables any healthcare provider, not necessarily a clinical toxicologist, to calculate them with ease [16]. Additionally, these scores encompass objective parameters that have proven to be feasible and replicable. The reliability and low inter-rater error are additional criteria that render the proposed PGI and PG scores appropriate for emergency settings, such as acute drug poisoning events. Indeed, one or more components of PGI/PG align with other established predictors of various adverse outcomes, including visual impairment [47], [52], mortality [48], [53], [54], [55], and the prognosis of poisoned patients undergoing hemodialysis [51]. Therefore, we recommend integrating the PGI and PG score into the existing clinical workflow in the setting of acute toxic alcohol exposure.

Conclusions

The current study aimed to identify high-risk patients shortly after exposure to toxic alcohol using a straightforward PGI score suitable for emergency contexts. Additionally, a derived PG score was employed for the same objective. PGI and PG showed a stronger ability to predict unfavorable outcomes, as well as the need for HD and MV. Among these, PG had the highest AUC values: 0.889 for predicting unfavorable outcomes, 0.819 for the need for HD, and 0.993 for the need for MV. However, PSS was more effective solely in predicting blindness. The current study recommends using the PG score as a simple and reliable tool to predict unfavorable outcomes in patients poisoned by toxic alcohols, including those who will require advanced medical interventions such as HD and MV. Therefore, the PG score could assist physicians in managing cases of toxic alcohol poisoning by enabling timely treatment decisions.

Strength and Limitations

This is the first study to identify the PGI score as a simple and reliable tool for predicting unfavorable outcomes among patients poisoned by toxic alcohol. In this context, a novel PG score was introduced, demonstrating superior performance compared to both the PGI score and the PSS. Nevertheless, several limitations should be considered when interpreting our findings. Subtle variations in therapeutic practices across centers, while likely minimal, may have introduced unmeasured confounding. The reliance on convenience sampling may have introduced selection bias, potentially limiting the generalizability of the results to the broader population. Moreover, the inherent rarity of toxic alcohol poisoning cases in the Arab world posed a significant challenge, contributing to a limited sample size. This, coupled with low event rates—particularly for mortality—may negatively impact the statistical power of this outcome. Therefore, to address the limitations of a small sample size, rigorous analytical approaches were employed. ROC curve analysis assessed predictive performance, with AUC confidence intervals derived via bootstrap resampling to ensure robust estimation. Youden’s index was used to determine optimal thresholds, with corresponding sensitivity and specificity reported to enhance clinical applicability. Power analysis was conducted for each ROC curve to support the adequacy and validity of the findings.

Future studies with larger sample sizes are needed to improve the validity of mortality prediction, especially given the limitations encountered in this analysis. We also emphasize the importance of externally validating the PG score in various clinical contexts to ensure its robustness and generalizability as a prognostic tool in acute toxic alcohol poisoning. Additionally, further investigation into the predictive performance of both the PGI and PG score in cases involving other toxicants may extend their clinical utility and inform broader toxicological practice.
