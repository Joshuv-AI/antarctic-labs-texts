Target trial emulation of DPP-4 Inhibitors in patients with T2DM for pulmonary tuberculosis: a nationwide observational data
Authors: Yi-Geng Chen, James Cheng-Chung Wei, Fu-Shun Yen, Chen-Yu Sung, Yu-Han Huang, Teng-Shun Yu, Fuu-Jen Tsai, Chii-Min Hwu, Chih-Cheng Hsu
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundDiabetes mellitus increases the risk of developing tuberculosis (TB) and negatively affects TB treatment outcomes. Dipeptidyl peptidase-4 (DPP-4) inhibitors are used in glycemic control but can also modulate immune pathways involved in immune-mediated diseases. Considering the immunoregulatory effects of DPP-4 inhibitors, this emulated target trial compared the risk of pulmonary TB in users and non-users of DPP-4 inhibitors with type 2 diabetes mellitus (T2DM).MethodsWe identified 328,842 pairs of DPP-4 inhibitor users and non-users from Taiwan’s National Health Insurance Research Database from January 1, 2007, and December 31, 2019. Cox proportional hazard models were used to determine the risk of new-onset pulmonary TB between the study and control groups.ResultsThe follow-up duration was 5.06 years for DPP-4 inhibitor users and 4.05 years for non-users. The incidence rates of new-onset pulmonary TB were 1.93 and 2.18 cases per 1,000 person-years in DPP-4 inhibitor users and non-users, respectively. Compared with non-users, DPP-4 inhibitor users had a significantly lower risk of developing pulmonary TB, with an adjusted hazard ratio (aHR) of 0.85 (95% CI: 0.81–0.90). Kaplan–Meier analysis showed a significantly lower cumulative incidence of new-onset pulmonary TB among DPP-4 inhibitor users than non-users (log-rank test, p < 0.001).Furthermore, a longer cumulative duration of DPP-4 inhibitor use was associated with a lower risk of pulmonary TB.ConclusionsIn patients with T2DM, the use of DPP-4 inhibitors was associated with a significantly lower risk of developing pulmonary TB compared to non-use. Background

Tuberculosis (TB) continues to be a significant global health challenge. According to the World Health Organization (WHO), approximately 10.8 million people worldwide developed TB in 2023, which translates to an incidence rate of 134 cases per 100,000 individuals [1]. In the same year, the number of global tuberculosis deaths was 1.25 million [1]. Various risk factors, such as human immunodeficiency virus (HIV), malnutrition, indoor air pollution, alcohol consumption, use of immunosuppressive drugs, tobacco smoke, and type 2 diabetes mellitus (T2DM), significantly contribute to the substantial burden of TB worldwide [2].

T2DM increases the risk of developing TB threefold and has a detrimental impact on TB outcomes [3]. The concurrence of T2DM and TB can lead to poorer health outcomes, a higher likelihood of secondary transmission, and an increased incidence of TB [3]. The International Diabetes Federation (IDF) estimated that 537 million adults worldwide were living with T2DM in 2021, with projections indicating that by 2045, this number will rise to 783 million [4]. Moreover, the majority of people with T2DM reside in low- and middle-income countries, where TB remains highly endemic [1, 4]. The increasing prevalence of T2DM is expected to contribute to a rise in TB cases [5].

Dipeptidyl peptidase-4 (DPP-4), also known as CD26, a lymphocyte cell surface protein, is a multifunctional enzyme that removes dipeptides from the N-terminal of peptides containing proline or alanine at the penultimate position [6]. DPP-4 is broadly expressed in specific cell types in different organs, such as the liver, lungs, kidneys, intestines, spleen, and pancreas, where it regulates various physiological processes through its enzymatic activity [6, 7]. DPP-4 can cleave and inactivate incretin hormones, such as glucagon-like peptide-1 (GLP-1) and glucose-dependent insulinotropic peptide (GIP). These peptides are critical in enhancing glucose-stimulated insulin secretion from β-cells, improving the body’s ability to lower blood glucose levels [8, 9].

Preventing TB in patients with T2DM has become increasingly important for improving patient prognosis and eliminating TB [10]. Several studies have shown that individuals with T2DM are at a higher risk of developing pulmonary TB, although oral antidiabetic medications may help reduce the risk of active TB infection in these patients [10–12]. However, research on the protective effects of DPP-4 inhibitors against TB infection remains limited and unclear [10]. On the other hand, DPP-4 inhibitors are not only effective for T2DM management but can also regulate immunological pathways involved in immune-mediated diseases, such as diabetic cardiovascular disease and inflammatory bowel disease [13]. This highlights the potential of DPP-4 inhibitors to prevent or alleviate the development of diseases associated with abnormal immune responses [13]. This emulated target trial [14] compared the risk of pulmonary TB infection between DPP-4 inhibitor users and non-users with T2DM.

Methods

Data source and population

This study utilized data from Taiwan’s National Health Insurance Research Database (NHIRD), a comprehensive resource derived from the National Health Insurance (NHI) program. The NHI is a single-payer, universal healthcare system established in 1995, providing coverage to 23.86 million residents of Taiwan [15]. The NHIRD includes detailed medical records, such as demographic information, diagnoses, procedures, and prescription data. Diagnostic codes in the database follow the International Classification of Diseases, Ninth and Tenth Revision, Clinical Modification (ICD-9/10-CM) standards. The study was approved by the Research Ethics Committee of China Medical University Hospital [CMUH112-REC1-117(CR-1)]. Patient privacy was ensured by anonymizing all data and using encrypted identification numbers. As anonymous data was used, the requirement for informed consent was waived.

Target trial emulation

We applied the framework proposed by Hernán and Robins (2016) [14] to emulate a clinical trial comparing DPP-4 inhibitors treatment with non-DPP-4 inhibitors treatment in patients with T2DM, using an unblinded design. First, we established the target trial emulation framework to align with the study objectives. This included defining key components, such as eligibility criteria, treatment strategies, allocation methods, follow-up procedures, outcomes of interest, causal comparisons, and the analysis plan (Additional file 1: Table S1). Next, we outlined the methodology for leveraging observational data to replicate these trial components, and then, perform the corresponding analyses (Additional file 1: Table S2). This approach enabled us to rigorously emulate the design and analytical strategies of a randomized controlled trial [16] while utilizing real-world data.

Eligibility criteria

We extracted 2007 to 2019 data from Taiwan’s NHIRD. From this dataset, we identified individuals with a new diagnosis of T2DM who had received antidiabetic medications for inclusion in our study. To ensure diagnostic accuracy, T2DM was defined as having at least two outpatient visits or at least one inpatient admission for T2DM within one year (Additional file 1: Table S3). The ICD coding algorithm used to identify T2DM was previously validated in a Taiwanese study, demonstrating a sensitivity of 90.9% and a positive predictive value of 92.0% [17].

Exclusion criteria included individuals who were older than 80 years or younger than 20 years at the time of T2DM diagnosis. Patients under 20 were excluded to minimize the likelihood of inadvertently including individuals with type 1 diabetes, which is more common in younger populations and could confound our results. Moreover, because immune and physiological maturity typically stabilize after age 20, whereas individuals aged over 80 may experience age-related immunosenescence and diminished physiological reserve, which may also influence outcomes [18]. Additional exclusions included those with a diagnosis of type 1 diabetes, missing data on sex or age, and individuals with a history of hepatic failure, heart failure, or tuberculosis. These conditions were excluded because previous studies have reported that certain DPP-4 inhibitors may worsen liver function or exacerbate heart failure [19–21]. Therefore, to avoid potential harm and reduce confounding effects, we excluded such patients from the analysis.

Assigned treatment strategies and groups

In the emulated target trial, we defined the index date as the first prescription date of a DPP-4 inhibitor (sitagliptin, vildagliptin, saxagliptin, or linagliptin). Patients who had never received a DPP-4 inhibitor served as the control group, with their index date determined by matching the time from T2DM diagnosis to the initiation of DPP-4 inhibitors. As DPP-4 inhibitors were first introduced in Taiwan in 2011, we set the index date for both the study and control groups as on or after January 1, 2011, to minimize selection bias. Study outcomes and follow-up

The primary outcome of this study was the incidence of new-onset pulmonary TB, defined as either at least two outpatient visits or one inpatient admission with a diagnosis of pulmonary TB (Additional file 1: Table S3) [22].

Follow-up commenced on the index date, which marked the initiation of the assigned treatment strategy. Participants were monitored until the first occurrence of one of the following events: diagnosis of pulmonary TB, death, or the end of the study period on December 31, 2020, whichever occurred first.

Emulation of the target trial

To approximate randomization, propensity score matching (PSM) was employed to estimate the probability (propensity score) of receiving a specific treatment (e.g., DPP-4 inhibitors vs. non-DPP-4 inhibitors) based on baseline characteristics. Patients who started on DPP-4 inhibitors were matched 1:1 with contemporaneous initiators of non-DPP-4 inhibitors. Statistical differences between the treatment and control groups were assessed using the chi-squared test (categorical variables) and the Student’s t-test (continuous variables). PSM was implemented to minimize baseline differences between DPP-4 inhibitor users and non-users. We employed a non-parsimonious multivariable logistic regression model during the propensity score estimation process to balance baseline characteristics between treatment groups, with DPP-4 inhibitor use as the dependent variable. Unlike parsimonious models, which aim to include only statistically significant predictors, a non-parsimonious approach includes a broader set of covariates, regardless of their statistical significance in predicting treatment assignment. Including all clinically relevant variables could improve the quality of the propensity score and leads to better balance after matching or weighting [23]. Matched pairs were formed using the nearest-neighbor algorithm with a caliper width of less than 0.01, ensuring balance between groups. Adequate matching was defined as standardized mean differences (SMD) below 0.1.

The matching process incorporated a wide range of potential confounding variables, based on data collected within the three years preceding the index date (Table 1). Patients without available medical records during this three-year baseline were excluded to minimize risks of misclassification and potential bias. These variables included demographics (sex, age groups, obesity, and smoking status), comorbidities (alcohol-related disorders, hypertension, dyslipidemia, coronary artery disease [CAD], stroke, peripheral artery disease [PAD], chronic kidney disease [CKD], chronic obstructive pulmonary disease [COPD], pneumonia, liver cirrhosis, rheumatologic diseases, cancers, psychosis, depression, human immunodeficiency virus [HIV] infection, dementia, organ transplantation, and Charlson Comorbidity Index [CCI] [24]) that were diagnosed within three years prior to the index date, medications (metformin, sulfonylureas, thiazolidinediones, alpha-glucosidase inhibitors, and sodium-glucose cotransporter 2 [SGLT2) inhibitors, insulin, statins, aspirin, corticosteroids, and immunosuppressants), T2DM severity indicators (the Diabetes Complication Severity Index [DCSI] score [25], and the number of oral antidiabetic drugs prescribed). We categorized both CCI and DCSI into three groups (0, 1, and ≥ 2) for propensity score matching to ensure a more balanced distribution and sufficient sample size across strata, which improves the quality and feasibility of the matching process. By incorporating these variables, the matching process aimed to reduce confounding and enhance the comparative validity between DPP-4 inhibitor users and non-users.

Analysis plan and statistical methods

The primary causal estimands in this study were the intention-to-treat effects of the assigned treatment strategies. Time-to-event analyses comparing DPP-4 inhibitor users with non-users were conducted using Cox proportional hazards models with robust sandwich standard error estimates. The results are presented as hazard ratios (HRs) with 95% confidence intervals (CIs). Kaplan–Meier curves were used to assess the cumulative incidence of new-onset pulmonary TB between the two groups.

Multivariable analyses were performed to evaluate the risk of new-onset pulmonary TB across various subgroups, including individual DPP-4 inhibitors (saxagliptin, linagliptin, sitagliptin, and vildagliptin), obesity, smoking status, comorbidities, and medication use. Additionally, we conducted an analysis comparing different cumulative durations of DPP-4 inhibitor use versus no use of DPP-4 inhibitors to assess their impact on the risk of new-onset pulmonary TB.

A two-tailed p-value of < 0.05 was considered statistically significant. All statistical analyses were performed using SAS version 9.4 and Stata SE version 11.0.

Results

Characteristics of the study participants

Between January 1, 2007, and December 31, 2019, 2,285,712 patients with newly diagnosed T2DM and prescribed antidiabetic medications were identified from the NHIRD. After applying exclusion criteria and conducting propensity score matching, 328,842 pairs of DPP-4 inhibitor users and non-users were included in the analysis (Fig. 1). The matched groups exhibited similar baseline characteristics, with SMDs below 0.1 (Table 1), indicating successful matching.

In the emulated target trial, the mean age of the participants was 60.5 years (SD = 11.6), and the proportion of male participants was 56.2% (Table 1). The mean (SD) follow-up duration was 5.06 (2.74) years for DPP-4 inhibitor users and 4.05 (2.87) years for non-users.

Primary outcomes

In the emulated target trial, the incidence rates of new-onset pulmonary TB were 1.93 and 2.18 per 1,000 person-years among DPP-4 inhibitor users and non-users, respectively (Table 2). The adjusted hazard ratio (aHR) for new-onset pulmonary TB among DPP-4 inhibitor users compared to non-users was 0.85 (95% CI: 0.81–0.90), indicating a significantly lower risk (Table 2). In the multivariable analysis, vildagliptin use was significantly associated with a lower risk of new-onset pulmonary TB compared to non-vildagliptin users, with an aHR of 0.93 (95% CI: 0.88–0.99).

Cumulative duration of DPP-4 inhibitor use

In the emulated target trial, the duration of DPP-4 inhibitor use was categorized into three groups: ≤ 540 days (109,981 patients [33.4%]), 541–1,416 days (109,245 patients [33.2%]), and > 1,416 days (109,616 patients [33.3%]). This categorization was chosen to ensure a relatively even distribution of patients across the three duration groups. Patients who used DPP-4 inhibitors for 541 to 1,416 days had a significantly lower risk of developing pulmonary TB (aHR: 0.79, 95%CI: 0.73–0.85) compared with those who did not use DPP-4 inhibitors. The risk reduction was even more pronounced in patients who used DPP-4 inhibitors for more than 1,416 days (aHR: 0.29, 95% CI: 0.26–0.32) (Table 3).

Multivariable analyses

Patients with alcohol-related disorders, stroke, CKD, COPD, pneumonia, cirrhosis, psychosis, dementia, higher CCI scores, and those using sulfonylureas, insulin, or immunosuppressants had a significantly higher risk of new-onset pulmonary TB compared with those without these comorbidities or medications. In contrast, patients with obesity, hypertension, dyslipidemia, coronary artery disease (CAD), and those using metformin or statins had a significantly lower risk of new-onset pulmonary TB compared to those without these conditions or medications (Table 2).

Discussion

This nationwide emulated target trial showed that the use of DPP-4 inhibitors was associated with a significantly lower risk of new-onset pulmonary TB compared to other antidiabetic agents in patients with T2DM. Moreover, a longer cumulative duration of DPP-4 inhibitor use was linked to a lower risk of new-onset pulmonary TB.

Current evidence suggests that the immune system often struggles to completely clear Mycobacterium tuberculosis (M. tuberculosis) infections [26]. While effective T-cell responses are critical for bacterial clearance and infection control, M. tuberculosis can exploit T-cell recognition pathways, potentially leading to immune exhaustion [27]. In humans, Th1 cells targeting M. tuberculosis are predominantly a C-X-C motif chemokine receptor (CXCR)3-expressing subset. Animal studies have shown that CXCR3-expressing T-cells play a critical role in controlling infection, both during natural exposure and in response to experimental vaccines [28]. Of note, Blauenfeldt et al. previously suggested that DPP-4 inhibitors may act as regulators of CXCR3-mediated Th1 cell migration to infection sites [29]. This finding highlights the potential of DPP-4 inhibitors as promising candidates for host-directed therapies against M. tuberculosis infections. Wang et al. conducted an analysis of 9,963 patients from a tertiary referral center in Taiwan between 2009 and 2016. Their findings indicated that the use of DPP-4 inhibitors did not significantly impact the incidence of pulmonary TB or nontuberculous mycobacterial lung disease, regardless of the severity of T2DM or the presence of comorbid conditions [30].

To the best of our knowledge, this present study may be the first to show that the use of DPP-4 inhibitors may reduce the risk of new-onset pulmonary TB. Our analysis of cumulative treatment duration showed that patients who used DPP-4 inhibitors for 540 days or less had a higher risk of developing pulmonary TB. The underlying cause remains uncertain, but it may be due to poor medication adherence in this group. It is also possible that patients in the control group (non-users of DPP-4 inhibitors) may have initiated DPP-4 inhibitor therapy during the follow-up period, while patients in the study group (users of DPP-4 inhibitors) may have discontinued therapy during the same period. Such treatment crossover could have contributed to the observed results. In contrast, among patients who used DPP-4 inhibitors for more than 540 days, longer treatment duration was progressively associated with a lower risk of pulmonary TB. To potentially reduce the risk of new-onset pulmonary TB, it is important to encourage patients with T2DM to maintain DPP-4 inhibitor therapy for at least 1.5 years. However, it is important to note that, as we did not perform a per-protocol analysis, the continuity of medication use among patients could not be fully verified. As a result, the cumulative treatment duration reported in this study may be overestimated.

Several potential mechanisms may explain how DPP-4 inhibitors contribute to a lower risk of new-onset pulmonary TB: First, DPP-4 inhibitors increase the level of glucagon-like peptide-1 receptor agonists, which have anti-inflammatory properties, thus, enhancing the immune response [31]. These effects may improve the host’s ability to control M. tuberculosis infection. Additionally, DPP-4 inhibitors can reduce the production of pro-inflammatory cytokines, such as tumor necrosis factor-α, I interleukin-6, and monocyte chemoattractant protein-1, which play a key role in chronic inflammation and immune regulation [9, 32]. By lowering these inflammatory markers, DPP-4 inhibitors may help create an immune environment less conducive to TB progression. Second, DPP-4 inhibitors may regulate T-cell responses by enhancing both the Th1 and Th2 immune pathways. Th1 responses are particularly important for TB control because they activate macrophages, which are essential for the containment and clearance of M. tuberculosis [9, 27, 33]. By optimizing T-cell-mediated immunity, DPP-4 inhibitors may enhance the host’s immune defense against TB. Third, DPP-4 inhibitors may affect the migration of immune cells, particularly CXCR3 + T-cells, which are critical for targeting TB-infected cells [29]. Evidence suggests that DPP-4 inhibition may enhance the trafficking of these immune cells to infection sites, thereby strengthening the immune response against M. tuberculosis and reducing the risk of TB. Taken together, these mechanisms suggest that DPP-4 inhibitors may provide immunomodulatory benefits beyond glycemic control, potentially reducing the risk of new-onset pulmonary TB in patients with T2DM.

Our multivariable analyses indicated that, among the various DPP-4 inhibitors, only vildagliptin was significantly associated with a lower risk of new-onset pulmonary TB compared to non-users. The exact underlying mechanism remains unclear. However, vildagliptin differs from other DPP-4 inhibitors in its binding characteristics and the duration of enzyme inhibition, which may affect host–pathogen interactions, particularly immune defense against M. tuberculosis [34]. Additionally, vildagliptin has been shown to more effectively suppress inflammatory cytokines than other agents in certain experimental studies, which may contribute to its potential protective effect against pulmonary TB [35]. Furthermore, in our study, metformin use was associated with a significantly lower risk of pulmonary TB compared to no metformin use, while sulfonylurea use was linked to a significantly higher risk of pulmonary TB compared to no sulfonylurea use. Previous reports have similarly found that metformin use is associated with a lower risk of pulmonary TB compared to non-metformin or sulfonylurea use [12, 36]. A study by Kai-Huang Lin et al. found that low-dose sulfonylureas were associated with a non-significantly higher risk of tuberculosis [10]. Additionally, other reports have shown that sulfonylurea use increases the risk of pneumonia and urinary tract infections in patients with diabetes [37]. In our study, patients with more comorbidities, weakened immunity, or those using immunosuppressants had a higher risk of developing pulmonary TB. These findings align with previous research [2, 5, 38, 39]. Conversely, patients who were obese or who used statins were found to have a lower risk of pulmonary tuberculosis, consistent with other studies [22, 40].

This study has several limitations. First, certain clinical and lifestyle-related variables were not reported in the NHI database, such as physical activity, nutritional status, family history, and socioeconomic status, due to inherent data constraints. Hence, the potential impact of these variables on pulmonary TB risk could not be assessed. The database also lacked blood glucose and hemoglobin A1C levels, thus, glycemic control among patients with T2DM could not be evaluated. To approximate patients’ overall health status, we used comorbidities such as obesity, alcohol-related disorders, and CCI scores as proxies. Similarly, DCSI scores, antidiabetic medication use, insulin use, and the number of oral antidiabetic drugs were used to better represent and control for T2DM severity in both study and control groups. Second, the follow-up periods differed between DPP-4 inhibitor users and non-users in this study, which may introduce bias into our results. Additionally, this study did not include a per-protocol analysis, which would have allowed for a more accurate assessment of the true effect of DPP-4 inhibitors and helped reduce potential bias arising from treatment crossover between the study and control groups. Moreover, inverse probability of treatment weighting (IPTW) sensitivity analyses were not conducted, which could have complemented propensity score matching (PSM) and the emulated target trial design to further minimize residual confounding effects. Third, this study primarily focused on the Taiwanese population, which may limit the applicability of the findings to other ethnic groups. Further studies involving diverse populations are needed to validate these results. Finally, as a retrospective cohort study, despite extensive adjustments for confounding variables and the application of an emulated target trial design, the potential influence of unknown or unmeasured confounders cannot be completely ruled out. In the future, prospective randomized controlled trials are necessary to confirm our findings.

Conclusions

This nationwide emulated target trial showed that DPP-4 inhibitor use was associated with a significantly lower risk of new-onset pulmonary TB in patients with T2DM. Additionally, a longer cumulative duration of DPP-4 inhibitor use was associated with a lower risk of pulmonary TB. These findings suggest that DPP-4 inhibitors may have a role in reducing the risk of pulmonary TB in patients with T2DM. However, further clinical trials are required to validate these results and establish the clinical implications.
