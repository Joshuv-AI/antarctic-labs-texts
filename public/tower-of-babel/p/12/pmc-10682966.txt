A mode of action-based probabilistic framework of dose-response assessment for nonmutagenic liver carcinogens: a case study of PCB-126
Authors: Yun Zhou, Qiran Chen, James E Klaunig, Kan Shao
Published: 2023

ABSTRACT
AbstractA main function of dose-response assessment is to estimate a “safe” dose in the target population to support chemical risk assessment. Typically, a “safe” dose is developed differently for cancer and noncancer effects based on a 2-step procedure, ie, point of departure (POD) derivation and low-dose extrapolation. However, the current dose-response assessment framework is criticized for its dichotomized strategy without integrating the mode of action (MOA) information. The objective of this study was, based on our previous work, to develop a MOA-based probabilistic dose-response framework that quantitatively synthesizes a biological pathway in a dose-response modeling process to estimate the risk of chemicals that have carcinogenic potential. 3,3′,4,4′,5-Pentachlorobiphenyl (PCB-126) was exemplified to demonstrate our proposed approach. There were 4 major steps in the new modeling framework, including (1) key quantifiable events (KQEs) identification and extraction, (2) essential dose calculation, (3) MOA-based POD derivation, and (4) MOA-based probabilistic reference dose (RfD) estimation. Compared with reported PODs and traditional RfDs, the MOA-based estimates derived from our approach were comparable and plausible. One key feature of our approach was the use of overall MOA information to build the dose-response relationship on the entire dose continuum including the low-dose region. On the other hand, by adjusting uncertainty and variability in a probabilistic manner, the MOA-based probabilistic RfDs can provide useful insights of health protection for the specific proportion of population. Moreover, the proposed framework had important potential to be generalized to assess different types of chemicals other than nonmutagenic carcinogens, highlighting its utility to improve current chemical risk assessment.Keywords: probabilistic dose-response, PCB-126, MOA, nonmutagenic carcinogens

Dose-response assessment, a critical component for human health risk assessment, typically involves 2 steps (ie, point of departure [POD] derivation and low-dose extrapolation) with a goal of identifying a “safe” dose level of a chemical for a target population (U.S. Environmental Protection Agency, US EPA, 2022). The approach of deriving PODs (eg, NOAEL, LOAEL, and benchmark dose [BMD/BMDL]) for noncancer effects primarily relies on a critical endpoint that is not necessarily the most severe effect, but typically the most sensitive adverse effect in the relevant species (US EPA, 2022). The estimated POD can be further divided by multiple uncertainty/variability factors as a way of nonlinear low-dose extrapolation to derive a reference dose/concentration (RfD/RfC) which is viewed as a safe dose level for noncarcinogenic chemicals (US EPA, 2002). In contrast, the default approach for cancer risk assessment derives a cancer slope factor (CSF) via linear low-dose extrapolation as a potency estimate of substances having carcinogenic potential under the assumption that carcinogenic is equivalent to mutagenic (US EPA, 2005). The dichotomized low-dose extrapolation methodology for cancer and noncancer effects (US EPA, 2022) is straightforward but may be inadequate and sometimes confusing in the context of risk interpretation and decision-making. First, whether the “safe” level is sufficient to characterize overall toxicity of a chemical when only focusing on a single critical event (ie, without considering mode of action [MOA]) remains questionable (Julien et al., 2009; Meek et al., 2013; National Research Council (NRC), 2009; Simon et al., 2014). Unlike a CSF, a typical RfD/RfC cannot quantitatively estimate the health risk given a certain exposure level to chemicals (Chiu and Slob, 2015). Additionally, heavily depending on study designs and experts’ experience, selecting a critical effect itself may cause substantial uncertainty, even though a comprehensive grasp of chemical toxicity is mandatory in hazard identification step. On the other hand, simply assuming carcinogenic hazards are mutagens with a linear extrapolation by default, a CSF may produce overly conservative risk estimates by neglecting the fact that nonmutagenic carcinogens may exhibit threshold-type dose-response relationship in the low-dose region. Moreover, little consideration of intra/interspecies-related variability in CSFs hinders a comparison/extrapolation of cancer risks. Overall, neither of the current low-dose extrapolation approaches (ie, nonlinear or linear) for dose-response assessment of noncancer and cancer effects is impeccable, especially from the point of view of integrating biological mechanisms and appropriately quantifying uncertainty and variability (NRC, 1994, 2009, 2014).

To improve the default dose-response assessment framework, the latest Guidelines for Carcinogen Risk Assessment recommended by US EPA suggested that plausible biological pathways (eg, modes of action or MOA) should be considered in extrapolating PODs to a low-dose region for carcinogenic substances (US EPA, 2005). Specifically, an RfD/RfC rather than a CSF can be used when strong evidence suggests a nonlinear mechanism for cancer development. However, MOA information has not been fully utilized in dose-response assessment, given that the RfD/RfC is still derived from a single critical effect. Moreover, low-dose extrapolation methodology per se (ie, linear or nonlinear) does not change. Recent progress in modern toxicology (eg, high-throughput screening assays) (Gwinn et al., 2017; Judson et al., 2011; Villeneuve et al., 2019; Zhu et al., 2014) provides an opportunity to synthesize various data sources so that we can bring elaborate MOA information rather than individual endpoints into dose-response assessment. Based on a series of identified key events, our study (Chen et al., unpublished data) quantitatively integrated MOA information of TCDD-induced liver tumor to estimate MOA-based PODs which were in line with the traditional estimates reported by US EPA and the Agency for Toxic Substances and Disease Registry (ATSDR). This result indicated the feasibility and plausibility of incorporating a complete biological pathway into dose-response modeling.

Going beyond the traditional point-estimate RfD/RfC, a framework of probabilistic dose-response assessment which is capable of unifying low-dose extrapolation methods for various effects has been proposed (Chiu and Slob, 2015; Chiu et al., 2018; Jang et al., 2023; World Health Organization and International Program on Chemical Safety, 2018). Aligned with recommendations of NRC (1994, 2009, 2014), the primary advantage of this approach was that it expanded the use of Bayesian methods by quantitatively estimating uncertainty and variability under a unified probabilistic framework. This concept also offers an opportunity to further develop the MOA-based POD (Chen et al., unpublished data) to a probabilistic RfD, while utilizing proper distributions to unambiguously quantify uncertainty.

The objective of this study was to extend the MOA-based dose-response assessment strategy recently developed in Chen et al. (unpublished data) to a probabilistic framework that quantitatively synthesizes a biological pathway in a dose-response modeling process to estimate the risk of substances with carcinogenic potential. The major improvements of the framework proposed in the current study included (1) using a Bayesian method to derive a MOA-based probabilistic POD and (2) extrapolating the POD to a MOA-based probabilistic RfD. This new probabilistic framework was illustrated through a case study of 3,3′,4,4′,5-pentachlorobiphenyl (PCB-126). Despite the restriction on the manufacture and use of PCBs in the 1970s (Lehmann et al., 2015), PCBs are widely distributed in various environmental media and accumulated through food chain, and can potentially induce liver tumors in human due to the continuing exposure (ATSDR, 2000; IARC Working Group on the Evaluation of Carcinogenic Risks to Humans, 2016; Lai et al., 2010; Sari et al., 2022). As a dioxin-like compound with the most potency in the PCB family (National Toxicology Program, NTP, 2006a), PCB-126 binds to and activates the aryl hydrocarbon receptor (AhR), which is considered necessary for downstream responses. Although existing evidence linking PCB exposure to the development of human cancers and human relevance of the proposed MOA remain debatable (ATSDR, 2000; Golden and Kimbrough, 2009; IARC Working Group on the Evaluation of Carcinogenic Risks to Humans, 2016), PCB-126 is still a valid example to demonstrate the proposed framework because the mechanism-related uncertainty can also be quantitatively captured by the pathway dose-response assessment. Relatively sufficient studies (eg, adequate dose-response information) and potential risk (ie, being upgraded as a carcinogenic substance [group 1] to humans; IARC Working Group on the Evaluation of Carcinogenic Risks to Humans, 2016) make it worth focusing on this chemical.

Materials and methods

Overall structure of the approach

One key feature of the proposed framework was probabilistic. So, instead of estimating a point-estimate RfD, this study aimed to derive a probabilistic RfD of PCB-126 based on the quantitative integration of various biological processes over the entire dose continuum to harmonize low-dose extrapolation methods. To achieve the goal, 4 steps were proposed and graphically presented in Figure 1. In this study, “endpoint” and “event” were 2 distinct terms distinguished by temporal attributes. An “event” was defined as a particular biological response or change (ie, an “endpoint”) with a time stamp, so different events could be the same endpoint occurring at multitime points.

The overall structure of the dose-response modeling framework.

The 4 steps in the framework were briefly introduced here. In Step 1, we identified and extracted a series of biological events pertaining predefined key quantifiable events (KQEs) through peer-reviewed literature to prepare necessary data for the pathway dose-response relationship which can quantitatively integrate overall MOA information. In Step 2, we used the BMD methodology for individual events to estimate cumulative doses (in order to make the doses estimated from various studies on the same scale) that triggered the next KQE and were used as the value of x-axis for the pathway dose-response relationship. For an endpoint measured at more than one exposure duration (eg, pigmentation was measured at 31, 53, and 105 weeks), only one event can be selected as a component of the pathway dose-response relationship, mainly based on toxicological considerations (ie, considered sufficient to induce the next effect). For example, we will choose pigmentation measured at 31 weeks rather than 53 or 105 weeks, if toxicological evidence shows that pigmentation was remarkably observed at this time point following a chronic exposure. All events that constitute the pathway dose-response relationship were essential events (ie, the endpoints measured at the identified sufficient time point) and the cumulative doses (eg, BMDs or BMDLs) estimated from them were called essential doses. In Step 3, these essential doses were coupled with their time stamps to form a pathway dose-response curve where “dose” referred to the log-transformed essential dose and “response” referred to the normalized exposure duration. To facilitate the MOA-based probabilistic POD derivation, we employed a full Bayesian method to model the pathway dose-response relationship using a Beta regression. The distributional POD estimate consisting of posterior samples was utilized in Step 4 for low-dose extrapolation and probabilistic RfD estimation based on the approach described in Chiu and Slob (2015).

Step 1: KQE identification and extraction

We assumed that the MOA can represent biologically sequential alterations at molecular, cellular, and organ levels as both dose and time increase, which was in line with the modified Hill criteria (US EPA, 2005). To quantitatively characterize a MOA, KQEs, considered as building blocks in the framework, were assumed to be sequentially activated along the MOA. The KQEs can include key events, associative events, and modulating factors (ModFs) that were essential for the incidence of PCB-126 induced liver tumor.

KQEs were identified and extracted based on peer-reviewed literature from the PubMed database. The search string that we used was as follows: (3,3′,4,4′,5-pentachlorobiphenyl OR PCB-126) AND (female Sprague Dawley rats) AND (liver OR hepatic) AND (cancer OR tumor OR neoplasm). The reason of selecting female Sprague Dawley rats pertained data availability and distinct sensitivity between genders. In vivo studies were preferred over in vitro ones because the former might introduce less uncertainty in the modeling framework. Sufficient evidence has indicated that sustained AhR activation of dioxin-like compounds can be the initiating event for the incidence of liver tumors (Becker et al., 2015). Thus, sustained AhR activation was specified as the first KQE in this study. All retrieved articles ranging from AhR activation to liver tumors were further examined based on the same criteria as described in Chen et al. (unpublished data), ie, briefly, eligible studies should be properly designed and provide explicit dose-response data with at least 3 dose levels. KQEs and corresponding dose-response data were extracted from the eligible studies following the paper screening. It was worth mentioning that for our proposed MOA, some biological events belonging to different KQEs may not be strongly or directly relevant to sustained AhR activation, but these events still can be incorporated in the MOA and modeled as a way to consider mechanistic uncertainty in our modeling framework.

Step 2: Essential dose calculation using the BMD methodology

Multiple biological endpoints can belong to a particular KQE. An endpoint may be quantitatively characterized by more than one dose-response dataset, because it would be measured at multiple exposure durations. Daily doses (unit: ng/kg/day) reported in the original studies were first converted to cumulative doses (unit: ng/kg) so that the dose levels of individual endpoints with different exposure durations can be integrated to the same scale to form the pathway-based dose-response relationship, based on the following equation: Cumulatve dose (ng/kg)=Daily dose (ng/kg/day)× days per week (day/week)×total exposure weeks (week). For an endpoint with more than one time stamp, the endpoint measured earliest was usually selected as the essential event, but biological plausibility of inducing the effect at that time point and data quality were additional factors considered. For each essential event to be integrated into the pathway dose-response relationship, its essential dose was estimated from the corresponding dose-response dataset using Bayesian model-averaged (BMA) approach, as introduced in Shao and Shapiro (2018).

Although acute transcriptional changes, especially altered core battery genes (eg, Cyp1a1), are an indicator of AhR activation, they have the limited ability to represent sustained AhR activation (Becker et al., 2015; NTP, 2006a). However, we retained acute changes of gene expression in the MOA for 2 reasons. First, altered core battery genes best acted as biomarkers, at least, to show AhR activation (Becker et al., 2015). Second, it was reasonable to incorporate this KQE to facilitate the activation of next KQE. In this study, in vitro transcriptomic responses based on rat hepatocytes with a 48-h exposure duration (access number: GSE144555) (Carlson et al., 2009) were chosen to evaluate genomic changes using the Bayesian BMD methodology (Ji et al., 2022). Additional in vitro to in vivo extrapolation (IVIVE) was conducted for the genomic data to ensure the consistency of the dose measurement (https://ice.ntp.niehs.nih.gov/downloads/UserGuides/IVIVE_userguide.pdf). Moreover, 1% and 10% extra risk, 1% and 10% relative change, and 0.1 and 1 SD (NTP, 2018) were used as BMRs for dichotomous, continuous, and genomic dose-response data to estimate the essential doses, respectively.

Step 3: MOA-based probabilistic POD estimation based on the pathway dose-response relationship

The pathway dose-response relationship could be viewed as a quantitative representation of the proposed MOA of PCB-126 induced liver tumor, because it described overall biological process for tumor formation as a function of both dose and time (Step 3 in Figure 1). Relying on all essential events, we can characterize a pathway dose-response relationship by specifying the essential doses on a log-scale (ie, ln(BMD) or ln(BMDL)) and corresponding normalized exposure durations (ie, all exposure durations were divided by the longest exposure duration of the identified KQEs, resulting in a response ranging between 0 and 1 on the y-axis) as the values of x- and y-axis, respectively. Given the dependent variable was a noncount proportion value between 0 and 1, Beta regression was an appropriate modeling strategy. Particularly, dose-response link functions were used to model the mean response and a full Bayesian method featuring the Markov Chain Monte Carlo (MCMC) algorithm was employed to estimate the parameters in the link functions. Parameters a and b were estimated using the MCMC algorithm implemented in the RStan package “rstanarm” (Muth et al., 2018).

After defining adversity (ie, specifying a proper value on the y-axis) based on toxicological considerations, we can calculate a MOA-based probabilistic POD, which is the dose level corresponding to the defined adverse effect on the fitted pathway dose-response curve. This MOA-based POD estimated from the MCMC algorithm contained a large number of posterior samples that can be used to characterize the distribution and calculate important statistics of the POD. Specifically, 31 weeks (ie, y = 31/105 = 0.295) and 53 weeks (ie, y = 0.505) were 2 potential options to define the adversity on the y-axis given that the events occurring at these exposure durations were early events but can be considered as adverse from a toxicological perspective (eg, irreversible effect).

Step 4: MOA-based probabilistic RfD analysis

Coupled with Monte Carlo simulation, the framework for probabilistic dose-response assessment was employed to extrapolate the estimated POD in Step 3 to a MOA-based probabilistic RfD by integrating probability distributions of a dosimetric adjustment factor (DAF), animal-to-human uncertainty (UFA), human variability (UFH), and other potential uncertainty factors (OUF) (Chiu and Slob, 2015; Manual and Guidance, 2021; Simon et al., 2016). The concept and equation for estimating probabilistic RfDs are shown as Step 4 in Figure 1. Simply, it was implemented through 3 basic steps, ie, (1) deriving animal doses with a specified magnitude of effect (M) based on an effect metric, (2) calculating the dose for median humans by incorporating uncertainty in interspecies and other factors, and (3) estimating the dose that induces I% of population who would experience the outcome or satisfies (1-I)% population protection (Chiu and Slob, 2015).

In addition to the temporal information, the y-axis of the pathway dose-response relationship also reflected the severity of an effect, which exactly represented the essence of M. Therefore, M can be specified as the magnitude of PCB-126 induced liver effects that culminated in tumors, in the form of the toxicological adversity (eg, 31 weeks). Serving as a starting point, the estimated POD in Step 3 was ultimately extrapolated to a MOA-based probabilistic RfD, ie, the 5th percentile of target human dose (HDMI) where I% of the population would experience effects of severity M or greater (Chiu and Slob, 2015). For example, HD31weeks1 means that a human dose where 1% of population would experience liver effects with the adversity more severe than cell proliferation indicated by bromodeoxyuridine (BrdU) labeling index. It was worth noting that M may reflect the magnitude of a group of liver effects with similar severity (instead of a single effect) given that the pathway dose-response curve was characterized by a series of essential events collectively.

DAF, UFA, and UFH, 3 essential uncertainty components for low-dose extrapolation, were considered in this study, whereas OUF should be accounted for on a case-by-case basis. For DAF, we assumed that the allometric scaling exponent satisfied a normal distribution with mean of 0.7 and SD of 0.024 (Chiu and Slob, 2015), and default body weight values of animals and humans were utilized (US EPA, 1994). UFA was assumed to be lognormally distributed with geometric mean (GM) of 1 and geometric SD (GSD) of 1.95 (Chiu and Slob, 2015). UFH was also thought to be lognormally distributed with GM equal to 6.93 (3.91) and GSD equal to 2.57 (1.94) to achieve 99% (95%) population protection coverage based on equation 10 as indicated in Chiu and Slob (2015). There was no need to adjust exposure duration (ie, nonchronic to chronic uncertainty) when estimating a probabilistic RfD, because the pathway dose-response curve and the MOA-based POD were developed based on a complete MOA that represents long-term/chronic biological processes. Detailed descriptions of individual uncertainty factors can be found in Chiu and Slob (2015).

MOA-based probabilistic PODs

The pathway dose-response curves fitted by distinct link functions (ie, Logistic, Probit, and Cloglog models) using the Bayesian MCMC beta regression are presented in Figure 3. The 3 models captured a similar sigmoidal pattern as both dose and duration increased. Compared with black dots (ie, essential events) shown in Figure 3, all models tended to better depict events measured at 14, 31, and 53 weeks, whereas they slightly missed the events occurring at lower and upper ends (ie, 2 days and 105 weeks). This may be attributed to the study design for dose levels and the fitting capability of models themselves. Despite slightly steeper slopes detected for the Cloglog model (Figure 3c), the difference among the 3 models with the Bayesian approach was limited.

In contrast, a remarkable decrease in MOA-based PODs estimated from BMD10, BMDL10, BMD1, and BMDL1 was observed (eg, 44.6 > 16.2 > 10.9 > 1.8 ng/kg/day [31 weeks using Logistic model]). Median MOA-based PODs estimated based on BMD10 were roughly 3.0 ∼ 4.1 times more than those based on BMD1, whereas a larger ratio (ie, 6.3 ∼ 8.9) was detected when comparing median estimates based on 2 BMDLs, which may be caused by the larger uncertainty in BMDL estimation.

Comparison of MOA-based probabilistic RfDs with traditional estimates

A traditional RfD (ie, based on one single endpoint) was estimated by dividing the POD (preferably BMDL) by 100 (ie, UFA = 10 and UFH = 10 by default) (Martin et al., 2013). Given that 31 and 53 weeks can be categorized into chronic exposure (Klaassen, 2019; Organization for Economic Co-Operation and Development, 2018) and a comprehensive toxicological database was available for evaluating the toxicity of PCB-126, we need no additional adjustments for the traditional RfD estimation. The point estimate of traditional RfD based on BMDL10 and BMDL1 are listed in Table 4. To ensure comparability, the MOA-based probabilistic RfDs using BMD10 and BMD1 were considered in the comparison. Moreover, based on the parameters we leveraged in the probabilistic framework, the 95% CIs of the distributions for both UFH and DAF/UFA included the commonly used value of 10 (Chiu and Slob, 2015). Therefore, MOA-based RfDs estimated at target incidences of 1% and 5% were both eligible to be compared.

The traditional RfD estimates and MOA-based probabilistic RfD were not necessarily lower or higher than each other. For example, considering the 31-week endpoints/events as adverse, the MOA-based RfD ranged from 0.00223 to 0.281 ng/kg/day, whereas the traditional RfD varied between 0.001 and 2.675 ng/kg/day. It was noticeable that the MOA-based RfD had a smaller variation range than the traditional counterpart, probably because the difference between various endpoints was mitigated by integrating KQEs in the pathway dose-response modeling. A similar pattern can be observed when comparing different RfDs at 53 weeks as well. Although the MOA-based probabilistic RfD was generally comparable to the traditional RfD, the most important improvement of the former one was that it explicitly specified the effect severity and quantified the protection level (eg, 99% or 95% of the population). We also compared the probabilistic RfDs listed in Table 3 with the HDMIs (proposed in Chiu and Slob, 2015) estimated given the default settings as shown in Supplementary Table 5. The comparison suggests that the MOA-based probabilistic RfDs and HDMIs are not necessarily lower or higher than each other, indicating that the estimates are generally comparable.

The proposed framework is also limited by a few factors, especially the availability of quantifiable dose-response data used to construct the pathway dose-response relationship. Although acute/short-term change in gene expression may not be representative of sustained AhR activation, additional data in low-dose region will be helpful for reducing the uncertainty in the pathway dose-response relationship. In addition, diverse data with various exposure durations can result in a more informative pathway dose-response relationship, which will benefit the adversity selection. It is possible that somewhere between 31 and 53 weeks would be a better choice to define adversity for MOA-based probabilistic POD/RfD derivation, but it remains unknown at this moment due to the lack of data with exposure duration in that range. Moreover, additional uncertainty from IVIVE would affect the accuracy of MOA-based PODs/RfDs. In this study, the only in vitro dataset used was associated with altered gene expression. Equivalent administered dose (ie, in vivo dose) was estimated by a linear extrapolation from a plasma concentration; however, whether such a linear transformation approach exhibits a real biological process has not been well justified.

Discussion

The main goal of this study was to develop a dose-response assessment framework that provided a probabilistic RfD estimation for nonmutagenic carcinogens that both quantitatively integrated MOA information and characterized uncertainty and population variability. The framework included 4 steps: (1) KQE identification and extraction, (2) essential dose calculation using the BMD methodology, (3) MOA-based POD derivation based on the pathway dose-response relationship, and (4) probabilistic low-dose extrapolation to generate MOA-based probabilistic RfD. Six KQEs characterized by total 24 quantifiable events were identified to form the MOA of PCB-126 induced liver tumor. Events along this pathway showed biologically subsequent processes (Becker et al., 2015; Budinsky et al., 2014). Additionally, the constructed pathway dose-response relationship explicitly revealed the increased severity as a function of both dose and time. Therefore, the estimated MOA-based PODs and RfDs based on the pathway dose-response relationship no longer correspond to a single event, but the overall MOA.

Typically, a linear approach is applied for low-dose extrapolation of carcinogenic substances, if the MOA information is insufficient to suggest the existence of a threshold (US EPA, 2022). Dichotomized low-dose extrapolation approaches for cancer and noncancer effects may lead to incomparable results in various situations (eg, PODs/RfDs based on NOAEL/LOAEL method and BMD approach, or RfDs vs CSF). Additionally, due to differences in study designs, higher PODs/RfDs estimated from early/mild effects may also confound researchers. Conversely, one significant improvement of the proposed framework was to continuously extend the curve into the low-dose region for substances by building a MOA-incorporated dose-response relationship. Thus, low-dose extrapolation for different chemicals can be unified to the idea of “letting MOA information decides,” that is, integrating the earlier key events into the pathway dose-response curve to inform the shape in the low dose region. It is evident that this approach can be generalized to noncancer risk assessment by simply applying the 4 steps mentioned above to identify chemical-specific apical effect and associated pathway and quantify MOA-based POD and RfD. By combining MOA-based PODs and the quantification of uncertainty and variability, this framework can derive MOA-based probabilistic RfDs that are more transparent, informative, and scientifically rigorous than the traditional RfDs. In the framework proposed in the present study, the adversity defined to estimate MOA-based PODs can also serve as the magnitude M to indicate the severity of effects. Comparing with the M defined in the original probabilistic RfD framework proposed in Chiu and Slob (2015) which was primarily reflected through the choice of benchmark response (BMR) level in the BMD methodology for the critical endpoint selected, the definition of M in the current framework is easier to understand and better aligned with toxicological intuition because more toxicological information has been incorporated. Therefore, the framework proposed in the present study is more biologically oriented and toxicologically meaningful than the Chiu and Slob approach.

Two reported NOAELs/LOAELs of PCB-126 in literature included (1) the NOAEL of 10 ng/kg/day based on serum biochemical and histopathological changes in rats measured at 13 weeks (Chu et al., 1994) and (2) the LOAEL of 740 ng/kg/day from a series of rat-based studies with an exposure duration of 13 weeks (ATSDR, 2000). In terms of these 2 studies, a plausible range of PODs of PCB-126 was 10∼74 ng/kg/day after converting the LOAEL to NOAEL based on the default uncertainty factor. For our approach, using the Logistic model as an example, the 5th percentile of MOA-based PODs were 2.6 and 13.5 ng/kg/day at 31 and 53 weeks, respectively, given BMD1 as individual essential doses, whereas the counterparts were 12.4 and 55.7 ng/kg/day given BMD10 as individual essential doses (Table 2). Therefore, MOA-based PODs using BMD1 or BMD10 were within the range between 2.6 and 55.7 ng/kg/day and generally comparable to the traditional PODs.

The RfD analysis in section “Comparison of MOA-based probabilistic RfDs with traditional estimates” exhibited that the traditional RfDs were comparable to the MOA-based RfDs (ie, the 5th percentiles of HDMI) estimated in the present study (Tables 3 and 4) as they were within 90% CI of the HDMI. To further examine the plausibility of MOA-based RfDs, we also compared MOA-based PODs with the deterministic RfDs estimated from reported NOAEL/LOAEL (ie, 10 ∼ 740 ng/kg/day). Corresponding deterministic RfDs were 0.033 ∼ 0.25 ng/kg/day by dividing NOAEL/LOAEL by default uncertainty factors (ie, UFS = 3, UFA = 10, UFH = 10, and LOAEL-to-NOAEL adjustment [UFL = 10]) (Martin et al., 2013; Office of Environmental Health Hazard Assessment, 2008). The statistical lower bounds (shown in bold) calculated based on the BMD10 or BMD1 as essential doses at 31-week duration generally fell in the range characterized by the deterministic RfDs. The plausibility of the estimated MOA-based RfDs can also be justified by its compatibility with the regulatory RfDs of TCDD. Oral RfDs and chronic minimum risk levels of TCDD were 7 × 10−4 ng/kg/day (US EPA, 2012) and 1 × 10−3 ng/kg/day (ATSDR, 1998) derived with a UF of 30 and 100, respectively, whereas the magnitude of most MOA-based RfDs ranged from 10−3 to 10−1 ng/kg/day (Table 3). This observation (ie, an approximately 10-fold reduced toxicity of PCB-126) also agreed with dioxin toxic equivalency factor (TEF) of 0.1 for PCB-126, the relative toxicity of PCB-126 in comparison with TCDD (TEF = 1) (NTP, 2006a,b).

As shown in Table 3, in addition to the distributional uncertainty and variability factors, the MOA-based RfDs calculated in this study were mainly affected by 2 factors that impacted the MOA-based POD estimation, ie, the individual essential doses used to form the pathway dose-response relationship, and defined adversity level (eg, 31 weeks vs 53 weeks). Using BMDL as the individual essential doses to form the pathway dose-response relationship can certainly lower the MOA-based PODs, compared with the counterparts generated from the corresponding BMD-based essential doses (eg, BMDL10 vs BMD10), because the BMDL-based pathway dose-response curve can be considered as the BMD-based curve shifting to the lower end (ie, left). However, we do not think using the statistical lower bound at the step of estimating essential doses is a suggested strategy for 2 reasons. First, instead of generating a dose level that can cause a predetermined change in response with the consideration of uncertainty, we more focus on estimating a plausible essential dose that can initiate the next KQE. Therefore, a dose level less impacted by various factors in the BMD modeling process is the choice that fits the purpose better. Second, from a risk management perspective, the uncertainty in dose-response modeling should be considered collectively after the pathway dose-response modeling is completed rather than taking a conservative measure at every single step in the proposed modeling framework. As shown in section “MOA-based probabilistic PODs,” different definitions of adversity (eg, 31-week ratio on the y-axis) will certainly have major impact on the estimated MOA-based PODs. Just like there is no right or wrong answer for the choice of the BMR value in the BMD methodology, the important thing is that definition of adversity should be justifiable from a toxicological perspective. In the case of PCB-126, the events occurring at either 31 or 53 weeks can be treated as adverse, but, from the viewpoint of curve fitting, the pathway dose-response relationship better captured essential events at 31 weeks rather than 53 weeks because more information on liver effects was available at 31 weeks.

In addition to the factors shown in Table 3 and discussed above, the selection of essential events to be included in the pathway may also impact the MOA-based POD/RfD estimation. A sensitivity analysis was performed to examine the effect of essential event selection on both PODs and RfDs by only choosing the earliest event (instead of the most toxicologically plausible event) for an endpoint with various exposure durations. It showed that our framework was relatively robust to uncertainties caused by this factor, especially supported by the results at 53 weeks (Table 2 vs Supplementary Table 3; Table 3 vs Supplementary Table 4).
