Nonlinear relationship between BMI and 30-day in-hospital mortality in ICU sepsis patients: a multicenter retrospective cohort study
Authors: Pan Zhou, Fang-xi Wang, Xiao-jia Liu, Li Zhou, Zhe Deng
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundWhile obesity paradox has been observed in various critical conditions, its role in sepsis remains unclear. This study aimed to investigate the relationship between body mass index (BMI) and 30-day in-hospital mortality among intensive care unit (ICU) patients with sepsis.MethodsThis retrospective study analyzed data from 17,510 adult sepsis patients (aged ≥ 18 years) with ICU stays between 24 h and 30 days, complete mortality data, and available BMI measurements, using the eICU Collaborative Research Database (eICU-CRD v2.0) collected in the United States between 2014 and 2015. BMI was the exposure variable, and 30-day in-hospital mortality was the outcome. Covariates included demographics, clinical parameters, comorbidities, and treatments. Univariate and multivariate logistic regression models examined the association between BMI and mortality, while generalized additive models with smooth curve fitting explored potential nonlinear relationships.ResultsThe median BMI among the 17,510 patients was 28.91 kg/m². The overall 30-day in-hospital mortality was 16.7% (95% CI: 16.2%–17.3%). A non-linear relationship between BMI and mortality was identified with an inflection point at 21.5 kg/m². Below this point, each unit increase in BMI was associated with a 9% decrease in mortality risk (OR: 0.91, 95% CI: 0.87–0.94, p < 0.001). Above this point, the association became insignificant (OR: 0.99, 95% CI: 0.99-1.00, p = 0.054).ConclusionIn ICU patients with sepsis, BMI is nonlinearly associated with 30-day in-hospital mortality, showing a significant negative correlation when BMI is ≤ 21.5 kg/m². These findings provide valuable insights for clinical risk assessment.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12879-025-11780-y.Keywords: Body mass index, 30-day in-hospital mortality, Sepsis, Nonlinear, Multicenter study

Introduction

Sepsis remains one of the leading causes of death in intensive care units (ICU) worldwide, claiming over 6 million lives annually despite modern medical advances [1, 2]. In the United States alone, sepsis affects 535 per 100,000 population with hospital mortality rates reaching 30% [3, 4], while in China, it claimed 986,929 lives in 2020, resulting in 17.1 million years of life lost [5]. These stark statistics have prompted the World Health Organization to recognize sepsis as a major public health concern [6], emphasizing the critical need for improved patient outcomes.

Concurrent with the sepsis challenge, obesity has emerged as a significant global health burden [7–9]. The prevalence of obesity has reached alarming levels [10], affecting approximately 25% of adults in Western nations and 42% in the United States [8, 11, 12], with severe obesity impacting roughly 3% of the population [13]. This trend extends to ICU settings, where recent studies show an increasing proportion of obese patients [14, 15]. Intriguingly, recent evidence has revealed a complex relationship between body mass index (BMI) and mortality in hospitalized patients. This phenomenon, termed the “obesity paradox,” suggests that while obesity increases the risk of various diseases, overweight and moderately obese patients demonstrate better survival rates during acute illness phases [7, 13]. This paradox has been documented across multiple conditions, including heart failure [16], coronary artery disease [17], end-stage kidney disease [18], pneumonia [19], and critically ill patients overall [20, 21]. However, despite the widespread recognition of the obesity paradox, several critical questions remain unanswered. First, specific BMI thresholds indicating improved survival rates have not been well established. Second, the underlying mechanisms of this paradox, particularly in sepsis patients, remain poorly understood. Most importantly, there is a notable lack of direct epidemiological evidence connecting BMI to sepsis outcomes within the ICU setting, where patients present unique physiological profiles.

To address these knowledge gaps, we hypothesized a non-linear relationship between BMI and mortality in sepsis patients, specifically that elevated BMI would confer survival benefits within a defined range, with this protective effect potentially diminishing beyond a certain threshold. Therefore, we conducted a comprehensive analysis of the eICU-CRD v2.0 to examine the association between admission BMI and 30-day in-hospital mortality among ICU patients with sepsis. This comprehensive analysis aims to provide robust evidence for future clinical practice and research directions.

Methods

Study design

This study is a retrospective cohort study. BMI is the independent variable, while 30-day in-hospital death in sepsis patients is the dependent variable, categorized as either survival and death.

Data source

Philips Healthcare and the Massachusetts Institute of Technology (MIT) collaborated to create a comprehensive public database called the eICU-CRDv2.0. Between 2014 and 2015, date of excellent quality was gathered from approximately 200,859 patients in 208 American hospitals who were hospitalized in ICUs and stored in this database. Included in the information are important signs, plans for nursing care, severity of illness, diagnoses, and details about treatment [22]. Access to data is available to the public upon registration, with data collection procedures following the guidelines set by MIT Ethics Committee and adhering to the principles of the Helsinki Declaration.

Study population

Sepsis was defined as a life-threatening organ dysfunction caused by a dysregulated host response to infection [23]. Organ dysfunction was identified as an acute change in total Sequential Organ Failure Assessment (SOFA) score ≥ 2 points consequent to infection [23].

From the eICU database (n = 200,859), we included 17,510 eligible patients with sepsis after applying the following exclusion criteria: (1) patients without sepsis (n = 177,380); (2) patients younger than 18 years (n = 8); (3) patients with ICU stay less than 24 h or greater than 30 days (n = 4,817); (4) patients with incomplete in-hospital mortality data (n = 196); (5) patients with missing BMI information (n = 593); and (6) patients with extreme BMI values (minimum or maximum 1%) (n = 355). Variables

BMI was first determined as continuous variable by dividing weight in kilograms by height square in meters. Based on WHO guidelines, people were categorized as being underweight (< 18.5 kg/m²), normal weight (18.5–24.9 kg/m²), overweight (25.0–29.9 kg/m²), obese (30.0–39.9 kg/m²), and morbidly obese (≥ 40.0 kg/m²) [7, 12].

Covariates

Factors were chosen according to clinical expertise and prior study results [24–28]. The variables included in the study consisted of categorical variables such as gender, race, acute respiratory failure (ARF), atrial fibrillation (AF), acute coronary syndrome (ACS), congestive heart failure (CHF), chronic obstructive pulmonary disease (COPD), gastrointestinal bleeding(GB), diabetes mellitus, hypertension, use of glucocorticoids, use of carbapenems, use of cephalosporins, use of vancomycin, and mechanical ventilation; as well as continuous variables like age, hemoglobin concentration (Hb), platelet, blood urea nitrogen(BUN), serum creatinine (SCr), albumin (ALB), and Acute Physiology and Chronic Health Evaluation-IV score (APACHE-IV score). Baseline characteristics were evaluated within first day of being admitted to the ICU.

Missing data processing

In this study, there were 112 individuals (0.64%) who did not have Hb data, 213 (1.22%) without platelet data, 57 (0.34%) lacking BUN data, 66 (0.38%) with missing SCr data, 1384 (7.90%) with ALB data missing, and 2,877 (13.52%) without APACHE-IV data. To tackle this issue, multiple imputations were carried out [29], including factors such as race, age, ARF, AF, ACS, CHF, COPD, GB, diabetes, hypertension, glucocorticoids, carbapenems, cephalosporins, vancomycin, and mechanical ventilation into the imputation process. The analysis used the missing-at-random assumption (MAR) [30].

Statistical analysis

In this study, participants were classified into five groups based on their BMI. Continuous variables were presented as the mean and standard deviation for normally distributed data, and as the median and interquartile range for non-normally distributed data. Categorical variables were presented as numbers and percentages. We used one-way analysis of variance for normally distributed data, the χ2 method for categorical variables, and the Kruskal–Wallis H test to examine variations among BMI groups.

To examine the independent linear relationship between BMI and 30-day inpatient mortality

After conducting collinearity screening and constructing three models using both univariate and multivariate binary logistic regression, while following the STROBE statement [31], we investigated the relationship between BMI and 30-day in-hospital mortality without excluding any covariates (Table S1, where no covariates were excluded). Collinearity among variables was assessed using variance inflation factor (VIF), with values > 5 considered indicative of significant multicollinearity. As shown in Table S1, all VIF values were below 2, confirming absence of substantial collinearity among the included variables. The three models used were: a basic model with no adjustments made; a model with minimal adjustments including gender, age, and ethnicity; and a fully-adjusted model with additional adjustments for various factors such as Hb, Platelet, BUN, SCr, ALB, APACHE-Ⅳ score, ARF, AF, ACS, CHF, COPD, GB, diabetes mellitus, hypertension, glucocorticoid, carbapenems, cephalosporins, vancomycin, and mechanical ventilation). Measurements of effect sizes were documented along with their corresponding 95% confidence intervals (95% CI). Adjustments for covariates were implemented if their incorporation into model resulted in a modification of the odds ratio (OR) by a minimum of 10% [31]. Robustness of results was assessed through subgroup analysis. Furthermore, the study examined potential unmeasured confounding factors for BMI and 30-day in-hospital mortality using E-values.

Subgroup analysis

Subgroup analyses were conducted using a stratified binary logistic regression model to examine the impact of sex, ethnicity, ARF, AF, ACS, CHF, COPD, GB, diabetes mellitus, hypertension, and mechanical ventilation. Besides the stratification factor, every category was modified for variables factors such as age, gender, race, Hb, platelet, BUN, SCr, ALB, APACHE-Ⅳ score, AF, ACS, CHF, COPD, GB, diabetes mellitus, hypertension, glucocorticoids, carbapenems, cephalosporins, vancomycin, and uses of mechanical ventilation. To assess potential effect modification, interaction terms between BMI and each stratification factor were included in separate fully-adjusted models. The significance of each interaction was evaluated using likelihood ratio tests comparing models with and without the interaction term [32, 33].

Examining the nonlinear correlation between BMI and 30-day inpatient mortality

We utilized generalized additive models (GAMs) and smooth curve fitting (penalized spline method) to explore potential nonlinearity in the association between BMI and 30-day in-hospital mortality. Using a recursive algorithm, our approach identified the inflection point initially when nonlinearity was present. Afterward, we developed a binary logistic regression model with two components on each side of the inflection point [34]. A log-likelihood ratio test was utilized to determine the optimal model for explaining the relationship between BMI and the mortality rate within 30 days of hospitalization.

Findings are in accordance with the guidelines outlined in the STROBE statement [31]. The analysis was performed with the statistical software R (version 3.4.3) and EmpowerStats (version 4.2). P < 0.05 (two-sided) was considered statistically significant.

Demographic characteristics

Gender distribution varied among different weight categories, with a higher proportion of males in the normal, overweight and obese groups, and a higher proportion of females in the underweight and morbidly obese groups (p < 0.001). Caucasians represented the majority across all groups, followed by African Americans, Hispanics, Asians, and others (p < 0.001). The mean age was lowest in the morbidly obese group and highest in the overweight group (p < 0.001).

Clinical parameters

Parameters such as Hb, platelet count, BUN, SCr, ALB, and APACHE-IV score exhibited statistically significant differences across BMI categories (all p < 0.05), suggesting potential associations between BMI and these clinical indicators. In particular, increased BMI correlated with elevated levels of Hb, BUN, SCr, ALB, and lower APACHE-IV scores (all p < 0.05).

Comorbid conditions

AF, ACS, CHF, COPD, GB, diabetes, and hypertension exhibit distinct prevalence distributions across different body mass index (BMI) categories (all p < 0.05).

Treatment

Therapy with glucocorticoids (p = 0.029), and the need for mechanical ventilation (p < 0.001), differed among different BMI groups.

Outcome

Mortality rates also exhibited a statistically significant difference among BMI categories (p < 0.001), with a decreasing trend observed as BMI increased.

Outcomes of mortality within 30 days of hospital admission

Table 2 display the mortality rates within 30 days of hospitalization, categorized by BMI groups, for a group of 17,510 sepsis patients. The total mortality rate within 30 days of hospital admission was 16.7%, with 2929 out of 17,510 patients passing away. There is a clear correlation between BMI categories and rate of 30-day in-hospital deaths. The mortality rates for underweight, normal weight, overweight, obesity, and morbid obesity were 21.6% (95% CI: 18.8–24.3%), 18.3% (95% CI: 17.2–19.3%), 16.6% (95% CI: 15.5–17.7%), 15.2% (95% CI: 14.2–16.3%), and 13.7% (95% CI: 12.1–15.4%), respectively.

Results of univariate analyses utilizing a binary logistic regression model

Table S2 summarizes the findings of the univariate regression analysis aimed at identifying factors linked to 30-day in-hospital mortality. Higher BMI, Hb, and ALB levels were associated with lower odds of mortality (all p < 0.05). On the other hand, Older age, high BUN, high SCr, and high APACHE-Ⅳ score are correlated with a higher risk of death during hospitalization within 30 days (all p < 0.05). Among concurrent health conditions, having ARF, AF, ACS, CHF, and GB were linked to higher chances of 30-day in-hospital death (all p < 0.05). In contrast, mortality was not significantly linked to COPD, diabetes mellitus, or hypertension. Regarding treatment factors, the use of glucocorticoids, carbapenems, and vancomycin were associated with higher odds of mortality, while the use of cephalosporins was associated with lower odds (all p < 0.05). The use of mechanical ventilation was significantly linked to higher chances of 30-day in-hospital death, with an odds ratio of 2.94 and a confidence interval of 2.72–3.19, p < 0.0001.

Results of multivariate analyses utilizing the binary logistic regression model

In Table 3, the relationship between BMI and 30-day in-hospital mortality is shown in different logistic regression models. The basic model showed that an increased BMI was linked to a reduced chances of 30-day in-hospital death (OR 0.98, 95% CI 0.98–0.99, p < 0.0001). The inverse association between BMI and mortality remained consistent even when accounting for demographic variables (age, gender, and race) in Model I (OR 0.99, 95% CI 0.98–0.99, p < 0.0001) and subsequently adjusting for clinical features, coexisting conditions, and therapy in Model II (OR 0.99, 95% CI 0.98–0.99, p < 0.0001). When examining the association between BMI categories and mortality, several key findings emerged. In comparison to individuals of normal weight, those identified as being underweight had a notably associated with an increased chance of of 30-day in-hospital death across all three models (Model II: OR 1.30, 95% CI 1.07–1.57, p = 0.0071). Conversely, individuals classified as overweight, obese, and severely obese exhitied a reduced risk of 30-day inpatient mortality in comparison to those in the normal weight category(all p < 0.05). These results indicate that a higher BMI could be linked to a reduced likelihood of 30-day in-hospital death among sepsis patients, even when considering different demographic and clinical variables. The E-value of 1.08 indicates that an unmeasured confounder would need to have a minimum relative risk association of 1.08 with both BMI and mortality to completely explain away the observed association, suggesting relative robustness to potential unmeasured confounding.

Addressing nonlinearity with GAM

In our research, we employed GAM to investigate potential non-linear relationships between BMI and the rate of 30-day in-hospital deaths, as shown in Fig. 3. After accounting for various factors such as age, gender, race, Hb, Platelet, BUN, SCr, ALB, APACHE-Ⅳ score, AF, ACS, CHF, GB, diabetes mellitus, hypertension, sepsis, glucocorticoid, carbapenems, cephalosporins, vancomycin, and mechanical ventilation (p < 0.0001 based on the Log-likelihood ratio test), the findings in Table 5 offer a more detialed insight into the correlation between BMI and 30-day in-hospital mortality among sepsis patients, utilizing a two-segment linear regression model. The analysis identified an inflection point of BMI at 21.5 kg/m2. The relationship between BMI and mortality was more pronounced below this turning point, showing an odds ratio of 0.91 (95% CI: 0.87-0.94, p<0.001). This indicates that for every unit increase in BMI below the inflection point, the odds of mortality decreased by 9%. However, when BMI was >21.5 kg/m², this association was attenuated. This indicates that the beneficial impact of increased BMI on mortality weakens after reaching the inflection point (OR 0.99, 95% CI: 0.99-1.00, p=0.054). The statistical significance of the log-likelihood ratio test between the standard logistic regression model and the two-piecewise logistic regression model (p<0.001) suggesting that the two-piecewise model offered a notably improved fit to the data. This highlights the importance of considering a non-linear relationship between BMI and mortality in sepsis patients, with a potential at a BMI of 21.5 kg/m2.

Discussion

This study suggests a non-linear relationship between BMI and 30-day in-hospital mortality in ICU patients with sepsis, with an inflection point observed at BMI 21.5 kg/m². Our analysis of 17,510 patients from 208 American hospitals revealed that mortality risk increases more steeply below this threshold, while the relationship becomes more gradual above it. This finding provides novel insights into the “obesity paradox” in critical care, suggesting that the protective effect of increased body mass is most pronounced in patients with lower BMI values.

Our findings align with several previous studies while also offering unique insights into the relationship between BMI and mortality in sepsis patients. The observed protective effect of higher BMI is consistent with Prescott et al.'s study of 1,404 severe sepsis patients [35], which found that obesity was associated with decreased mortality (adjusted OR 0.59; 95% CI: 0.39-0.88). However, our study extends beyond this by identifying a specific BMI inflection point at 21.5 kg/m², below which mortality risk increases more steeply. This finding adds nuance to the existing literature and may help explain some previously observed inconsistencies. In contrast, Kuperman et al.'s multicenter study of 792 septic patients found no significant association between obesity and mortality [36], possibly due to their smaller sample size and different analytical approach. Our study's larger sample size (17,510 patients) and sophisticated statistical modeling, including the use of GAM and two-piecewise regression, provides more robust evidence for this relationship. This results aid in comprehending the "obesity paradox" in sepsis, indicating that the beneficial impact of increased BMI on 30-day in-hospital mortality is strongest when BMI is less than 21.5 kg/m², decreasing as BMI levels rise. This study's unique contribution lies in its comprehensive analysis of a large, multi-center dataset using sophisticated statistical methods, which has revealed a more nuanced understanding of the BMI-mortality relationship in sepsis. These insights suggest that healthcare providers should pay particular attention to sepsis patients with BMI below 21.5 kg/m², as they may benefit from more intensive nutritional support and closer monitoring. Additionally, our findings challenge the traditional view of obesity as uniformly detrimental, suggesting that maintaining some excess weight might offer protective benefits during acute illness. For clinical practice, we recommend implementing routine BMI screening upon ICU admission.

Our research, as far as we know, is the first to show a non-linear correlation between BMI and 30-day hospital death in sepsis patients in the ICU. Several biological mechanisms may explain the observed BMI-mortality relationship. First, the increased mortality risk below BMI 21.5 kg/m² likely reflects the critical role of nutritional status in sepsis outcomes. Our data (Table 1) showed that patients with lower BMI exhibited significantly reduced levels of BUN, SCr, and albumin (all p < 0.001), indicating potential malnutrition [37]. During critical illness, these patients may lack adequate metabolic reserves to meet the increased energy demands of sepsis, leading to rapid depletion of essential nutrients and reduced physiological resilience [38, 39]. Secondly, selection bias may influence the admission of obese patients to the ICU. Clinicians may have a lower threshold for admitting younger, healthy obese patients for surveillance to prevent potential complications [7]. This is supported by our study's findings (Table 1), where higher BMI was associated with younger age, lower APACHE-Ⅳ scores, and higher ALB levels (all P < 0.001). Additionally, individuals with obesity may be prescribed reduced weight-dependent amounts of fluids and vasopressors, which could help lessen the adverse reactions associated with these treatments [7]. Moreover, future research should focus on elucidating the underlying mechanisms of this protective effect, particularly investigating the role of adipose tissue-derived factors in modulating the inflammatory response during sepsis, and evaluating the effectiveness of BMI-specific intervention strategies.

Our study possesses several notable strengths that enhance the reliability and generalizability of our findings. First, we utilized a large-scale dataset comprising 17,510 patients from 208 American hospitals, providing substantial statistical power and broad geographical representation. Second, our analytical approach was particularly robust, employing advanced statistical methods including generalized additive models and two-piecewise regression analysis, which enabled us to identify a precise BMI inflection point at 21.5 kg/m². Third, we implemented comprehensive strategies to address potential methodological challenges, including multiple imputation for missing data and calculation of E-values to assess unmeasured confounding. Furthermore, our extensive subgroup analyses and interaction testing across various demographic and clinical parameters demonstrated the consistency of our findings across different patient populations. The application of sophisticated statistical techniques, combined with thorough covariate adjustment and rigorous sensitivity analyses, strengthens the validity of our conclusions.

Several limitations of this study should be acknowledged. First, as an observational study, we could only demonstrate associations between BMI and mortality rather than establish causality. Second, despite our comprehensive adjustment for multiple covariates and the calculation of E-values, unmeasured confounding factors might still exist. Third, our study excluded patients with ICU stays shorter than 24 hours or longer than 30 days, which might limit the generalizability of our findings to these specific patient populations. Fourth, the exclusion of cases with missing BMI data and extreme BMI values could have introduced selection bias, although the proportion of such cases was relatively small. Additionally, while the eICU database included comprehensive clinical information, some potentially relevant variables such as nutritional status, muscle mass, and long-term outcomes beyond 30 days were not available. Furthermore, the retrospective nature of our study and the need for multiple imputations for missing data might have affected the accuracy of our findings. Future prospective studies with more complete data collection and longer follow-up periods would be valuable to validate our results.

Conclusion

In conclusion, this study identifies a potential nonlinear association between BMI and 30-day in-hospital mortality in ICU sepsis patients. Notably, our study reveals a significant inflection point: a marked inverse association between BMI and mortality exists when BMI ≤ 21.5 kg/m², whereas this association is rendered statistically insignificant when BMI exceeds 21.5 kg/m². These unique findings offer a new reference for clinical assessment of in-hospital mortality risk in ICU sepsis patients.
