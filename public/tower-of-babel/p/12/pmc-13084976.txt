Chemoselective Alkylating Esterification of Thiophosphinic Acids: An Experimental and Theoretical Study
Authors: György Keglevich, Dorka Nagy, Zoltán Mucsi, Konstantin Karaghiosoff, Mátyás Czugler, László Drahos, Gömöry Ágnes
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Preparative experiments including the alkylation of diphenylthiophosphinic acid with a series of C1‐C4 haloalkenes at 75/115°C in the presence of triethylamine in toluene suggested chemoselective formation of the corresponding S‐ (thiolic) ester. No O‐(thionic) ester was formed. For this, we wished to clarify the confusion regarding the outcome of such alkylations described in the literature. A few analogous derivatives were also synthesized and characterized for comparison. First in the literature, crystal structures of two thiolic esters were analyzed in detail. The alkylation led to non‐centrosymmetric crystal structures. Moreover, the two isostructural thiolic compounds formed chiral crystals, a rather unusual feature of achiral molecules. Weak interactions such as C–H … O bridges and dispersion dominate and tailor the structure of the crystals. As regards the mechanism, three possible pathways were investigated at the B3LYP/6‐31G(d,p)/PCM[toluene] level of theory. The activation enthalpy for the rate‐determining step was significantly lower for the triethylamine‐catalyzed route than for the base‐free variation. Moreover, in the base‐catalyzed protocol, the difference of 30.8 kJ mol–1 between the enthalpy of activation for the S and O‐alkylations justified the S‐selectivity. The pathway involving the anion from the thiophosphinic acid was found unfavorable and hence was excluded.

Introduction

Thiophosphinic acids and derivatives are important starting materials or intermediates and may exhibit biological activity [1]. Thiophosphinic acids principally exist under the equilibrium of the thionic acid (>P(S)OH), and the thiolic acid (>P(O)SH) forms shifted towards the previous one [2, 3, 4, 5]. Theoretical calculations also confirmed the overwhelming presence of the >P(S)OH species [6]. The classical preparation of thiophosphinic acids involves the hydrolysis of the corresponding phosphinic chlorides (Scheme 1A) [7]. It is worth noting that the reaction of phosphinic chlorides (>P(O)Cl) with H2S also leads to the corresponding thiophosphinic acid (>P(S)OH) [8]. The reaction of secondary phosphine oxides with elemental sulfur offers a more atomic efficient method for the synthesis of the compounds under discussion. The first example was the preparation of tert‐butyl‐phenyl‐thiophosphinic acid (Scheme 1B) [9]. Optically active starting phosphine oxide allowed the formation of enantiomerically pure product [10].

Two routes for the preparation of thiophosphinic acids.

Later on, the procedure was refined and extended to other model compounds [11, 12]. An odorless synthesis involved the conversion of secondary phosphine oxides to the respective thiophosphinic oxides applying 2,4,6‐trimethoxybenzylthiol as the thionating agent [5]. It is, however, surprising that in a study applying (R)‐(+)‐tert‐butyl‐phenylthiophosphinic acid as a chiral resolving agent, the tBuPhP(O)SH thiolic structure was assigned to it [13] that might be incorrect based on our current findings. A noteworthy observation is that the reaction of dialkyl phosphites ((RO)2P(O)H) with elemental sulfur affords (RO)2P(O)SH as the predominant tautomer formed by the insertion of S into the P–H bond [14].

As the thiophosphinic acids, like other heteroatomic acids, in the first approach, do not undergo direct esterification with alcohols, the reaction of thiophosphinyl chlorides with an alcohol is the method of choice (Scheme 2). The reaction of phosphinyl chlorides with thioalcohols affords the corresponding thiolic (S‐) esters [15]. However, these methods are not atomic efficient and not “green.”

Possible synthesis of thiophosphinates.

Alkylating esterification is another possibility for the preparation of thiophosphinates. According to this, thiophosphinic acids are reacted with alkyl halogenides. The claimed disadvantage of this method is that a mixture of thiolic and thionic esters is formed (Scheme 3). Furthermore, the product ratio was found to be dependent on the nature of the alkyl halogenide [16, 17]. There was no doubt that the thiolic ester was the major component.

Alkylating esterification of thiophosphinic acids.

It was a step further, when a chemoselective procedure involving the alkylation of thiophosphinic acids by bromoalkenes under solid–liquid phase transfer catalytic conditions applying K2CO3 and Bu4NBr was developed (Scheme 4) [18]. The thiolic esters were obtained in 85–96% yields. The variety of the substituents did not affect the selectivity.

Chemoselective alkylation of thiophosphinic acids under phase transfer catalytic conditions.

A few examples are also known, when alkenes or alkynes were applied as alkylating agents towards thiophosphinic acids [19, 20].

The lack of selectivity claimed in early publications [16, 17] motivated the researchers to try alternative methods for the synthesis of thiolic esters. In these attempts, secondary phosphine oxides were the starting materials. The oxidative coupling of secondary phosphine oxides (SPOs) with thioalcohols in the presence of CuI/bipyridyl catalyst in pyridine/acetonitrile at room temperature gave the thiolic esters (Scheme 5/(1)) [21]. The second approach utilized the photoredox condensation of SPOs with trimethylsilyl thioether using Ni(acac)2/dtbbpy catalyst and an Ir photoredox catalyst at 26°C in acetonitrile (Scheme 5/(2)) [22]. According to the next method, SPOs were reacted with thiosulfonates at 26°C in acetonitrile (Scheme 5/(3)) [23]. An innovative protocol coupled SPOs with sulfinic acids at room temperature in iPrOAc as the solvent. The role of the triphenylphosphine reagent was to remove the oxygen atom of the S = O unit in the intermediate (Scheme 5/(4)) [24]. Thiolic esters were also obtained in the reaction of SPOs and sulfonyl hydrazides applying Bu4NI under aerobic conditions at 75°C in DMF (Scheme 5/(5)) [25]. Last but not least, the corresponding thiolic ester was prepared by the alkylation of the in situ formed diphenylthiophosphinic acid with 1,1‐diphenyl‐allylalcohol [26].

Possibilities for the conversion of diphenylphosphine oxide to the corresponding thiophosphinate.

It was a challenge for us to revisit the alkylation of thiophosphinic acids with alkyl halogenides. We wished to clarify the problem of chemoselectivity, as earlier articles seemed to be misleading regarding the outcome, causing uncertainty. It was also a consequence that efforts were made to develop alternative (often more difficult) methods for the synthesis of simple thiolic esters. We also wished to study the structure of the target compounds, the thiolic esters. Furthermore, we wished to investigate the mechanism and energetics of the model reaction to clarify the problem of selectivity.

Results and Discussion

The Synthesis of Diphenylthiophosphinates

The starting diphenylthiophosphinic acid (1) was prepared from diphenylphosphine oxide by reaction with elemental sulfur at 26°C in dichloromethane (Scheme 6). The crude product obtained after filtration and evaporation was suitable for further transformations.

Preparation of the starting diphenylthiophosphinate.

Thiophosphinic acid 1 was alkylated with 1.5 equiv. of haloalkanes, such as iodomethane, bromoethane, 1‐bromopropane, and 1‐bromobutane (at 75°C) and 2‐bromopropane (at 115°C), in the presence of 1 equiv. of triethylamine (TEA) in toluene (Scheme 7). 31P NMR analysis of the crude mixtures obtained after filtration and evaporation referred to the presence of only a single component in each case. After purification by column chromatography, the products (2a‐e) coming from alkylations were isolated in 65–89% yields. On the basis of 31P and 13C NMR data, it was not a question that the alkylation took place in an S‐selective manner resulting in the formation of the thiolic ester. No O‐ (thionic) ester was present in the crude mixtures.

Chemoselective alkylation of diphenylthiophosphinic acid (1).

The thiophosphinates (2a‐e) were also characterized by 1H NMR spectral data and identified by HRMS.

One can see that under the common conditions applied, the alkylation of diphenylthiophosphinic acid (1) was completely S‐selective. Hence, the earlier observations [16, 17] suggesting the formation of both the thiolic and thionic esters in similar type alkylations have now been ruled out. Contrary to the earlier supposition, the nature of the alkyl halogenide had no impact on the selectivity of the reactions. A further study to confirm the general validity of the 100% S‐selectivity is in process.

For comparison purposes (see below), thiophosphinate Ph2P(S)OBu (3A), dithiophosphinate Ph2P(S)SBu (3B), and phosphinate Ph2P(O)OBu (3C) were also prepared. The syntheses of species 3A‐C are shown in Scheme 8.

Synthesis of analogous P‐esters.

The 31P NMR chemical shift of 80.9 for Ph2P(S)OBu (3A) is the most downfield. For Ph2P(O)SBu (2e) and Ph2P(S)SBu (3B), the shifts are ca. 43, while Ph2P(O)OBu (3C) exhibits the most upfield shift of 31.1. Hence, the 31P chemical shifts are rather informative for the >P(X)Y‐ scaffolds. In case of >P(O)S‐ and >P(S)S‐, MS is also needed to be able to decide on the P‐function. As regards the 13C NMR data, the chemical shifts for the SCH2 and OCH2 moieties appearing at ca. 32.6 and 64.7, respectively, may be of diagnostic value. On the other hand, the 1 J(P, C) coupling constant detected for Cα (appearing in all cases at a chemical shift between 132 and 135) may be informative. In the instances, when the P‐function incorporates at least one sulfur atom, such as in species 2e, 3A, and 3B, the 1 J(P, C) coupling is ca. 107–110 Hz, while for 3C with a >P(O)O‐ unit, the coupling is significantly larger (137 Hz). X‐Ray Crystallographic Study of Two Thiophosphinates and a Phosphinate

The molecular and crystal structures of thiophosphinates 2b and 2d, as well as that of phosphinate 3C, were determined by single‐crystal X‐ray diffraction. Both thiophosphinates crystallize in the orthorhombic space group P212121 (No. 19) with four formula units in the unit cell. These two crystal structures are not only isostructural with a cell similarity index π = 0.04 [27] but also crystallize in chiral space groups. Thus, like conglomerate‐like crystallization occurred. This is unusual, as the P atoms in these molecules are not chiral, but only prochiral in the best case. Thus, while these crystals grew, there must have been some kind of chiral induction present, probably of supramolecular origin coming from disparity in molecular interactions. Phosphinate 3C crystallizes in the monoclinic space group Pc with four formula units in the unit cell and displays two crystallographically independent molecules. Atom distances and bond angles of P‐esters 2b, 2d, and 3C are in accord with those reported in the literature for analogous compounds.

The isostructural thiophosphinates 2b and 2d display very similar hydrogen bonding interactions in the solid state. They always involve the O1 oxygen atom of the P = O units, while the H‐atoms show the following H‐bonding pattern both in 2b and in 2d. Hydrogen bonding involves the para hydrogen atom and one meta hydrogen atom of one phenyl group, whereas only the para hydrogen of the other phenyl group is involved (Table 3, Figures 4 and 5). This pattern means that the two phenyl groups play no longer equivalent roles in the crystalline state. Whether this inequality is the cause or consequence of the chiral crystalline forms remains open. These hydrogen bonds lead to the formation of helical chains along the a‐axis as well, which are interconnected by further H‐bonds to a three‐dimensional network forming the crystal.

Theoretical Investigation of the Mechanism: A DFT Study

The alkylation of diphenylthiophosphinic O‐acid, Ph2P(S)OH (1), with 1‐bromobutane (BuBr) in an apolar solvent (toluene) in the presence of TEA affords the S‐alkylated product, formulated as Ph2P(O)SBu (2e), whereas the O‐alkylated isomer, Ph2P(S)OBu (3), cannot be detected. To rationalize this chemoselectivity, the reaction was investigated computationally at the B3LYP/6‐31G(d,p)/PCM[toluene] level of theory to gain mechanistic insights into the origin of the observed chemoselectivity. The thermodynamical analysis revealed that the parent thionic acid tautomer, Ph2P(S)OH (1A), is by ≈ 25 kJ·mol−1 more stable than its thiolic counterpart Ph2P(O)SH (1B). Regarding the potential products, the related O‐alkylated isomer (3) seems to be somewhat (by −4 kJ·mol−1) more stable than the S‐alkylated product (2e).

To explore this selectivity, three mechanistic pathways (Routes A–C) were considered. In Route A (base‐free pathway), the chalcogene atom of the P = S or P = O function of the neutral acid attacks the α‐carbon atom of BuBr (Scheme 9). The related intermediates (4 and 5, respectively) remain protonated, and the proton is removed after the loss of the bromide ion. In this scenario, the energy of the transition state (1A‐TS‐4) belonging to the S‐attack is 8.7 kJ mol−1 lower than that (1B‐TS‐5) connected with the O‐attack.

Possible mechanisms for the alkylation of diphenylthiophosphinic acid (1) with BuBr obtained at the B3LYP/6‐31G(d,p)/PCM[toluene] level of theory suggesting kinetic S‐selectivity. The enthalpy values are given in kJ mol–1.

Route B (TEA‐assisted proton transfer): Here, TEA is explicitly involved in the process. Proton transfer from the OH or SH group to TEA occurs simultaneously with the nucleophilic attack on the α‐carbon of BuBr by the ambient acid connecting a TEA molecule (Scheme 9). This concerted deprotonation significantly reduces the activation enthalpy of transition states 6‐TS‐8 and 7‐TS‐9 as compared to the base‐free pathway, and here again, the S‐alkylation route is favored. However, in this case, the greater difference of 30.8 kJ mol−1 between the energy level of the two transition states (6‐TS‐8 and 7‐TS‐9, respectively) indeed justifies the selectivity experienced in the syntheses. Hence, the complete selectivity was explained by kinetic reasons. Overall, these results explain that kinetic control is behind the selective S‐alkylation. No matter that the O‐alkylated product is somewhat more stable, the significantly lower activation barrier for S‐alkylation determines the outcome. Computations clearly demonstrated that the selectivity is governed by kinetic control and that the involvement of TEA as a base is essential for lowering the activation barriers.

Experimental

General

The 31P, 13C, 1H NMR spectra were taken on a Bruker DRX‐500 or Bruker Avance‐300 spectrometer operating at 202, 126, and 500 MHz or 122, 75, and 300 MHz, respectively. The 31P chemical shifts are downfield relative to H3PO4, while the 13C and 1H chemical shifts are downfield relative to TMS. The couplings are given in Hz. High‐resolution mass spectrometric measurements were performed using a Q‐TOF Premier mass spectrometer (Waters Corporation, 34 Maple St, Milford, MA, USA) in positive electrospray mode. The melting points were determined using a Setaram Differential Scanning Calorimetry 92 device.

General Procedure

A mixture of 0.30 g (1.48 mmol) diphenylphosphine oxide and 0.15 g (4.69 mgatom, = 0.59 mmol) elemental sulfur were dissolved in dichloromethane (6 mL), and the resulting mixture was stirred at room temperature for 24 h under a nitrogen atmosphere. The excess elemental sulfur was removed by filtration. The excess dichloromethane was removed under reduced pressure.

Then the crude product was subjected to alkylation using 2.2 mmol of various haloalkanes (iodomethane: 0.14 mL, bromoethane: 0.16 mL, 1‐bromopropane: 0.20 mL, 2‐bromopropane: 0.21 mL, and 1‐bromobutane: 0.24 mL) in the presence of 0.21 mL (1.48 mmol) of triethylamine and of toluene as the solvent under a nitrogen atmosphere. In the reactions with n‐propyl‐bromide and n‐butyl‐bromide, 10 mL of toluene was used and the mixtures were stirred in round‐bottom flask, while the other reactions were performed in a bomb tube using 6 mL of toluene. The reactions were carried out for 24 h at 75°C (in case with 2‐bromopropane at 115°C). After completion of the reaction, the mixture was filtered and the toluene was removed under reduced pressure. The resulting crude product was purified by column chromatography (silica gel, dichloromethane–methanol (95:5) as the eluent) to afford products 2a‐e.

Computational Methods

All stationary points were optimized at B3LYP/6‐31G(d,p) [51] with PCM(toluene) by G16 program [52]. Frequency analyses confirmed minima (no imaginary frequencies) or transition states (one imaginary frequency), and supplied ZPE and thermal corrections (298.15 K, 1 atm). Intrinsic reaction coordinate calculations connected each TS to the corresponding reactants and products. Reported free energies are G(298 K) in kJ·mol−1. The IEFPCM method was also applied to model the solvent effect, by using the default settings of G16, setting the for toluene [53].

Conclusions

It was found that the alkylation of diphenylthiophosphinic acid with a series of C1‐C4 haloalkenes at 75/115°C in the presence of triethylamine in toluene took place in a chemoselective manner, resulting in the exclusive formation of the thiolic S‐ester, and no thionic O‐ester could be detected in the reaction mixture. An X‐ray study of the two thiophosphinates showed non‐centrosymmetric crystal structures. Interestingly, the two isostructural thiolic compounds formed chiral crystals that is relatively rare in case of achiral molecules. Weak interactions, such as C–H … O bridges and dispersion, determine the final arrangement in the crystals. Three possible mechanisms were investigated at the B3LYP/6‐31G(d,p)/PCM[toluene] level of theory. The activation barrier for the rate‐determining step was significantly lower for the triethylamine‐catalyzed alkylations than for the base‐free variations. Moreover, during the amine‐catalyzed route, the difference of 30.8 kJ mol–1 between the enthalpy of activation for the S and O‐alkylation was in good accord with the S‐selectivity. The protocol starting with the deprotonation of the thiophosphinic acid proved to be unfavorable due to the high enthalpy of activation.
