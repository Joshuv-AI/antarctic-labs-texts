Antiretroviral therapy clients’ experience of Follow-up care in tshwane, Gauteng province, South Africa
Authors: Kulani James Mabasa, Sibusiso Zuma
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundAccess to antiretroviral therapy (ART) has improved substantially in resource-limited settings in Africa, Asia, and South America, where 90% of people living with human immunodeficiency virus and acquired immunodeficiency syndrome (HIV/AIDS) reside. Despite the effective roll-out of free life-prolonging antiretroviral therapy (ART) in public clinics of South Africa since 2004 and the proven efficacy of ART, some people living with HIV (PLHIV) become lost to follow-up (LTFU) from treatment for various reasons. This study aimed to explore and describe the follow-up care experiences of clients on antiretroviral therapy in South Africa.MethodsThe researcher selected a qualitative research approach, used an exploratory and descriptive research design, collected data using in-depth unstructured interviews, and analysed data using thematic analysis. The ART clients were selected using purposive sampling, and data were collected from a sample of fifteen individuals at a primary healthcare facility to a point of data saturation.ResultsThe study revealed two categories of findings, personal experiences and healthcare services experiences. On a personal level, ART clients experienced fear of disclosing HIV status, stigma, increased non-adherence to HIV treatment influenced by alcohol use, communication problems with healthcare providers, medication side effects, and challenges with transportation to access primary healthcare (PHC) facilities. The healthcare systems were negatively experienced due to factors such as prolonged waiting times, a shortage of healthcare providers, staff attitude, facility operating hours, and a lack of integration in clinical healthcare services.ConclusionThe study showed that ART clients choose alternative treatment sites where they receive better services, including non-biomedical settings.Keywords: Non-adherence, Loss to follow-up, Primary healthcare, ART regimens, Non-biomedical, Stigma, HIV, Antiretroviral therapy, Client experience, Follow-up care

Background

Access to antiretroviral therapy (ART) has improved substantially in resource-limited settings in Africa, Asia, and South America, where 90% of people living with human immunodeficiency virus and acquired immunodeficiency syndrome HIV/AIDS reside [1]. South Africa has made notable progress towards attaining the Joint United Nations Programme on HIV/AIDS (UNAIDS) 95-95-95 targets of 2030 by achieving 94% on the first 95 UNAIDS targets of people living with HIV (PLHIV) who know their status, as well as achieving 77% of PLHIV enrolled on ART, and 92% of those on ART having suppressed viral load by June 2023 [2]. South Africa has committed to achieving the 95-95-95 UNAIDS targets for HIV diagnosis, treatment, and viral suppression set out in the Global AIDS Strategy 2021–2026 to end AIDS as a public health threat by 2030 [3]. In addition, the South African National AIDS Council (SANAC) has developed the National Strategic Plan (NSP) for HIV, Tuberculosis (TB), and Sexually Transmitted Infections (STIs) for the period 2023–2028 to guide the country to ending HIV AIDS by 2030.

Globally, ART loss to follow-up rates vary from 32.7% in America and 12.1% in Europe to between 39.4 and 79.4% in different parts of Africa [2]. The term ‘loss to follow-up’ (LTFU) means that the client has not taken an ART refill for 90 days or longer since the last visit/date of refill, but has not yet been captured as ‘dead’ or ‘transferred out’. The term amalgamates several possible outcomes, including death, defaulting, and self-transfer to another facility [1]. However, health facilities have strategies to track LTFU clients and successfully trace clients to achieve outcomes that might differ from LTFU status [4]. Despite the effective roll-out of free, life-prolonging antiretroviral therapy (ART) in public clinics in South Africa since 2004, as well as the proven efficacy of ART, some PLHIV become LTFU from treatment for various reasons [3]. Data from the District Health Information System (DHIS) shows that South Africa experienced a 4% decline in retention in ART care during the COVID-19 Pandemic [5].

Poor ART adherence poses a challenge to both individuals and facilities. The consequences of not adhering to ART may result in patients developing resistance to the first-line regimen, which may require switching to the second-line regimen [6]. Patients who are switched are subjected to greater pill burdens and must take medication frequently [7]. The other challenge of poor ART adherence is that second-line and third-line regimens are limited and sometimes unavailable in developing countries [8]. This may lead to patients transmitting HIV to others during sexual intercourse, and patients may develop Acquired Immune Deficiency Syndrome (AIDS) and die [9]. The Primary Healthcare (PHC) facilities present several challenges for PLHIV. A survey conducted in nineteen countries globally showed that, due to HIV status, clients were denied healthcare services at PHC facilities [10]. In a survey conducted in the Gauteng, KwaZulu-Natal, North West, and Western Cape provinces of South Africa regarding PHC services, clients reported high dissatisfaction with first-contact accessibility and follow-up care, while community orientation was also high on the list [8]. Although the challenges have been identified, patients still rely on services from PHC facilities [11].

Multiple studies conducted in the KwaZulu-Natal, Limpopo, Western Cape, and Eastern Cape provinces of South Africa revealed significant challenges that lead to ART non-adherence [12]. Many quality improvement innovations, such as the implementation of a patient appointment system and a differentiated model of care, have been tested and applied to improve the PHC facility’s response to ART patients’ management in the Republic of South Africa (RSA) [13]. However, they did not produce the quality of care desired. The challenges include inadequate healthcare providers, long waiting times, poor quality healthcare services, poor control and prevention practices, and old infrastructure [13]. There have been many instances where clients have been turned back from primary health care and subsequently developed complications and died [14].

Methods

Study aim

This study aimed at exploring and describing the experiences of follow-up care for clients on antiretroviral therapy in Tshwane, Gauteng Province, South Africa.

Study approach

The research approach is a plan and procedure that includes data collection, analysis, and interpretation. This plan consists of the proposed research methodology and discussions of the research outcome [15]. The researcher selected a qualitative research approach for this study because of the advantages it yields in conducting a field study. Conducting a study using a qualitative approach helped in capturing the true meaning of the participants’ experiences through their own words. It further assisted in answering the research question and was appropriate for exploring and describing the experiences of follow-up care of ART clients.

Study design

An exploratory study takes place at the beginning of a research effort and is a way of learning about a relatively under-researched topic by evaluating different literature related to the subject [15– 16]. A descriptive research design is used for a study that seeks to answer questions about what, where, who, how, and when [17].

This study used explorative and descriptive qualitative research to obtain rich participant information [18]. The selection of an exploratory design assisted in gathering rich information about the ART clients’ experience of follow-up care in a healthcare setting. In-depth interview processes and a literature search were used for this study, both of which assisted in an expanded understanding of the problem statement. The research questions concerning how ART clients experience follow-up care at PHC facilities were designed with the aim of extracting more information about events, behaviours, and attitudes concerned with the phenomenon under study.

Study setting

This research was conducted at two PHC facilities in the Tshwane Health District, which fall under South Africa’s Region One and Region Six. The facilities are in a semi-urban area of the Tshwane Health District. The two PHC facilities have the largest ART enrolments in the country, with ART facility A having over 15,000 and facility B having over 9,000 registered clients [19]. The two facility catchment areas also serve many people from outside the Gauteng Province who are there seeking work opportunities and a better life. The two facilities were selected because they constitute high-burden ART sites in the Tshwane Health District. Despite both facilities being fully resourced, both face significant loss to care challenges [19]. The selected facilities also face challenges of poor infrastructure, exacerbated by a lack of integration of services – meaning that ART clients have been allocated a separate waiting area and consulting rooms, a situation that promotes stigma amongst people living with HIV.

Study population and sampling strategy

The target populations in the study were PLHIV, between the ages of 18 to 60, both males and females, who were enrolled in the facility for ART services and resided in Tshwane. The participants were selected from those who had missed clinic appointments at least twice in a year. Participants were selected as part of the sample using a patient electronic register (Tier.Net). The focus sample was ART clients with more than twenty-four months of experience on ART. Purposive sampling was used as a strategy to select participants for the study. Fifteen participants were carefully selected to form part of the study, considering attributes such as having been enrolled on ART for twenty-four months and having missed facility appointment visits twice in one year. Selection was followed by the making of appointments with the identified participants during their next scheduled facility visits.

Data collection

For this study, data were collected using in-depth unstructured interviews in which a researcher and participants had a face-to-face conversation about their experiences of ART treatment follow-up care. The pilot study was conducted before actual data collection with four participants who met the requirements for the study. Before data collection, the researcher discussed ethical principles of informed consent, confidentiality, and protection from harm with study participants and further received signed consent forms [18]. Data collection was conducted on a day on which participants reported for their ART follow-up care visits at the facility. All fifteen of the sampled participants agreed to take part in the study. However, data collection was conducted with only twelve of them because of repetition of the answers to the questions provided by the participants [20]. The unstructured interviews were flexible and encouraged dialogue, while the researcher performed follow-ups to clarify questions. The interviews yielded rich information that assisted the researcher in understanding the experiences of clients receiving ART follow-up care. The English language was used as the medium to collect data, and participants were all well conversant with the language used. The grand tour question for the study was, “How do ART clients experience follow-up care at PHC facilities?“. The researcher used an audio recorder and jotted down notes for transcribing during data collection [21].

Data analysis

The current study followed a qualitative analytical structure of identifying important concepts, comparing the concepts, identifying a few themes, reviewing the initial analysis, turning the analysis into findings, and writing the findings. This process required rigorous reading and re-reading of the transcripts to establish themes and sub-themes [18]. The thematic analytical process followed six steps, beginning with sorting transcripts, coding, identifying themes, reviewing themes, naming themes, and writing an analytical report. The study analysis was done in consultation with a professional co-coder to verify themes and sub-themes. The process of co-coding included listening to recorded interviews and searching transcripts to extract themes. These themes were then discussed with the researcher for alignment. Thematic analysis was a relevant procedure for this study because of the need to abstract themes from the information shared by the participants during data collection [22]. The data analysis process established five themes and seventeen sub-themes. The study fulfilled its purpose and objectives with the selected analytical procedure.

Results

The researcher in the study used experienced ART clients who complied with follow-up care visits at PHC facilities as participants. For each theme and sub-theme, direct quotations from participants were included to reinforce and illustrate the findings.

Summary of patient demographic details

Demographic information

The twelve sampled participants were segregated according to gender and specific age grouping (Table 1). The gender split of the study was 67% female and 33% male. The 67/33 female/male representation reflects the typical patterns of clinic utilisation in South African healthcare facilities. Participant age ranged between 29 and 59 years, with an average age of 44. All participants in the study had missed clinic appointments twice within a year, and all had twenty-four months of enrolment experience on ART. Facility A (n = 6) and Facility B (n = 6) each accounted for fifty per cent of participants.

Patient-related experiences of follow-up care

Patient-related experiences were challenges that ART clients encountered during follow-up care at PHC facilities. These experiences influence ART clients to respond positively or negatively to follow-up care visits at PHC. Some of the reported experiences recorded as sub-themes included being fearful of disclosing HIV status, the effects of alcohol use, language barriers, and access to means of overcoming healthcare barriers.

Disclosure of HIV status

Study participants indicated that disclosing their HIV status was challenging because of fear of stigma and humiliation. Participants indicated that they experienced challenges such as being forced by healthcare practitioners to disclose their HIV status during clinic visits. The methods of delivery of medication by healthcare providers also compromise ART clients’ privacy and confidentiality. Some ART clients had resorted to collecting their medication from distant clinics for fear of disclosing their HIV status to other clients visiting their local facility. The quotes below further reflect this issue.

“… I have been asked by the nurse in front of people to go collect my HIV treatment at the far end HIV room. It was difficult for me to stand because I was [sitting] with other people, and I acted like she [was] not talking to me”. (Participant 12-M-37)

“My condition is private and personal, and I would like it to stay that way for now; the only people allowed to know are healthcare providers and nobody else. The thing is, I am not yet ready to tell the world about it”. (Participant 7-M-37)

Use of alcohol

Excessive use of alcohol was reported as one of the experiences that hinders ART adherence among young people living with HIV. The use of alcohol was linked to bad associations and the influence of friends. There is sufficient knowledge amongst ART clients regarding alcohol abuse and ART – provided by healthcare providers through health education – but some clients choose to ignore this knowledge and indulge in continuous drinking. The representative quotes below reflect this further.

“… Sometimes I forget to take medication when drunk”. (Participant 12-M-37)

“Eish! I blame my friends for my excessive alcohol consumption. I also blame myself, and I thought I would leave, but it is not that easy”. (Participant 9-M-46)

Language barrier

The language barrier between ART clients and healthcare providers was cited as a challenge. Some participants cited being illiterate as a reason for not understanding their doctor’s instructions during follow-up care. Intense probing of the language barrier issue revealed that ART clients sometimes fail to understand the correct usage of prescribed medication [9]. The existence of a language barrier between ART clients and healthcare providers leads to incorrect dosing of medication and non-adherence to treatment. After further probing into why they did not seek help from friends and family, as to the proper usage of their medication, prompted participants to indicate that doing so would compromise their privacy. A 49-year-old male participant indicated that he did not use the facility frequently because of language-related misunderstandings.

“My reason for not coming all the time is because sometimes I do not hear nurses when they speak; I just agree so that I move from their presence because I know they are rude”. (Participant 8-M-49)

Barriers to access to healthcare

Barriers to access to healthcare amongst ART clients were prominent, and the main reported contributor was unemployment, which prevents patients from finding transport fares to go to the clinic. Many healthcare facilities were reported to be on the outskirts of the communities and did not provide the liberty of walking to the clinic. Instead, transport is required to access these health facilities. The poverty level experienced by some ART clients prevents them from keeping up with their clinic visits [21]. The two participants quoted below, one male and one female, expressed their concerns regarding transport fares and unemployment status, which limited their access to healthcare facilities.

“I walk a very long distance to come here because I cannot afford the taxi fare; I also plan to arrive on time to get my medication because if they turned me back, I would not return the following day because of the distance. If I am ill, I sit at home hoping they deliver”. (Participant 8-M-49)

“… I sometimes do not have enough money to come to the clinic; I take transport and am not working”. (Participant 10-F-26)

Healthcare-related experiences

Responding to the study’s open-ended interview questions, participants provided their views on healthcare-related experiences. There was a combination of events that ART clients found positive or negative during the follow-up care visit at the PHC. Participants mentioned issues such as long waiting times, the attitude of nurses, medication availability, and infrastructural problems in this regard.

Facility waiting times

Facility waiting times during follow-up care were reported as contributing to negative experiences amongst all the ART clients surveyed. The main contributors to waiting times were staff shortages and poor health systems. Some participants reported that poor infrastructure and lack of an appointment system contributed to long waiting times that result in ART clients spending whole days at the facility – and sometimes having to go home without being seen by a health professional [18, 23]. The consequences of long waiting times were poor treatment outcomes and patients leaving the facility without being attended to, which causes health risks. Participants reported dissatisfaction with the long waiting times and expressed that this was a constant challenge in the facility.

“… It is a norm that we should wait for a long time to see a nurse in this facility, especially in the morning when it is difficult to enter; I am not happy at all with the queues at the facility”. (Participant 12-M-37)

“The problem of waiting times has been a constant challenge, and we have been writing and submitting in the suggestion box, but there is no solution…”. (Participant 7-M-37)

Medicine availability

The availability of ART medication in healthcare facilities was one of the positive attributes that all participants indicated. Participants reported that, at times, facilities would dispense multiple months’ worth of medications to ART clients during times of festivities and when they had to travel for work purposes. Some participants indicated that they received their ART medication even when they were visiting for other illnesses. This provision is necessary to enable clients to manage their own HIV infection and to promote treatment adherence. Most of the study participants reported that the key to treatment adherence was to ensure that ART medication was always available and accessible because the populations that use ART medication are poor and may not be able to afford it [24].

“Every time I visit the facility, they give me ART medication, even if I am not coming for a follow-up visit, so I have enough bottles”. (Participant 11-F-59)

“I have never seen a moment where the medication will finish in this clinic. There are times when they give us two or three bottles, and that keeps us for long”. (Participant 3-F-29)

“Because of the nature of my work, I always get more containers, sometimes two or three, to keep me for a long time while I am away”. (Participant 9-M-46)

Healthcare provider attitude

The attitudes of healthcare providers drew both positive and negative responses from the ART clients. Some participants indicated that healthcare providers are friendly people who treat them with respect, encourage them to adhere to treatment, and often remind them of their follow-up care visits to the facility. The attitude of healthcare providers can encourage or discourage ART clients regarding follow-up care visits at PHC. The positive experiences of participants included being offered advice on medication, good treatment practices, and being reminded of follow-up visits at the facility. The other reported positive experience with healthcare providers was that they gave easy-to-understand explanations, allowing participants to ask questions and share their concerns during consultations, something that encourages adherence to ART treatment [25].

“What I know is the nurses are doing a good job. They always remind us of our next clinic appointment. During the consultation session, nurses ask if we take medication on time”. (Participant 4-M-49)”.

“… We as patients go through difficult moments sometimes, but nurses help by clarifying what is difficult for us, allowing us to ask many questions without being offended”. (Participant 6-M-44)

Some participants reported having experienced negative attitudes from healthcare providers. One participant complained that nurses are more focused on administrative and computer work than on their nursing duties, and nurses could be observed using phones for personal matters while in consultation rooms. Participants reported that factors contributing to patients not adhering to treatment include provider relationships and service delivery [12]. The negative attitude of healthcare providers triggers the disengagement of ART clients from their follow-up care in the facility, which in turn carries consequences such as regimen changes and sometimes death.

“Aaah! It is better to be assisted by a clerk than a nurse; in this clinic, nurses do not work but do management issues; the facilities here are far, that is why I am still here”. (Participant 11-F-59)

“The nurses give poor service; they shout at us without doing anything wrong, and we are left stranded on benches while [they are] doing their things”. (Participant 8-M-49)

“… One nurse was showing a negative attitude toward me for no reason; I think she heard me complaining about poor services”. (Participant 1-M-44)

Availability of healthcare providers

The shortage of healthcare providers was reported as an experience that ART clients encounter during follow-up care at healthcare facilities. Participants indicated that the shortage of healthcare providers causes delays and compromises the rendering of quality services during follow-up care [18, 22]. The challenges of staff shortages cause facilities to be congested. Through further probing on experiences related to a lack of staff, participants indicated that they sometimes received clinical services from non-clinical staff, such as cleaners and guards. Participants reported that the lack of available staff in the facility was a long-term challenge that they always experienced during follow-up care visits.

“We have complained enough about waiting for long in the clinic, and the message always is that they are short-staffed, so I think our government must hire more nurses to assist us so that we get services faster”. (Participant 2-F-53)

“The nurses here try to assist us, but I think they are insufficient. That is why we wait forever”. (Participant 10-F-26)

“Sometimes we find ourselves assisted by non-clinical staff such as cleaners and guards to do our blood pressure reading and vital signs because of staff shortages in this clinic”. (Participant 1-M-44)

Facility operating hours

The majority of ART clients reported facility operating hours as a challenge, especially for those in employment. Participants indicated that the healthcare facilities in Tshwane operate five days a week (Monday to Friday) from 07h30 to 16h00. Limited clinic operational hours were deemed to negatively affect people who get sick over the weekend and after hours. ART clients reported that they woke up early and travelled to the facility for follow-up care visits because they feared spending the whole day waiting for services or being forced to return home when the facility closed. ART clients expressed frustration with facility operating hours, and some resorted to using alternative facilities or buying medication [18, 23].

“Where I stay, I use transport to come to the clinic, so I had to wake up early to get transport, but I find the clinic not open, and then we had to follow the queue outside to register”. (Participant 10-F-26)

“… If you want to collect your medication in this clinic, you must be mindful of time; by 15h30, nurses begin closing the clinic, and we must return the following day”. (Participant 8-M-49)

“Some of us are working, and the way this clinic operates does not help us because when we go to work, they are still closed, and when we knock off from work, they are closed”. (Participants 7-M-37)

Discussion

This study aimed to explore and describe the follow-up care experiences of clients on antiretroviral therapy in the Tshwane Health District. Our findings were categorised into three different segments. From these three categories, the main findings were on ART clients’ positive and negative experiences during follow-up care visits at the facilities. During thematic data analysis, negative experiences reported by ART clients during follow-up care emerged as more prominent than positive ones. The following sections discuss these findings in detail.

The first finding was based on personal experiences encountered by ART clients during follow-up care at the facilities. These were related to a fear of disclosing HIV status, increased non-adherence to treatment related to the use of alcohol, communication problems, and challenges in accessing healthcare. Further probing found that HIV patients do not disclose their status to their relatives because of fear of rejection [20]. This harms patients because it causes them to not adhere to HIV treatment. The fear of disclosure is a major factor for ART clients when it comes to not adhering to treatment because they do not want other people to know about their HIV status [12].

In the PHC facilities of Tshwane, a gap was identified regarding the personal experiences of ART clients during follow-up care. All the reported personal experiences were negative.

All the personal experiences reported on have in common the problem of ART clients failing to adhere to their treatment regimes [16]. Most study participants indicated that they struggled with disclosing their HIV status to family members for fear of being ostracised [26]. Meanwhile, participants who abuse alcohol carry a high risk of HIV re-infection as they tend to engage in unsafe sexual practices when intoxicated. Through further probing, participants indicated that alcohol abuse is a challenge to their ART journey. This often leads to failure to adhere to treatment, at times causing them to become a loss to follow-up (LTFU) at the health facility [27]. Communication problems bring the risk of using the wrong dosage, which can render the medication ineffective [18]. Challenges with accessing healthcare due to the unaffordability of transport fares carry a risk of failure to manage HIV and failure to honour follow-up appointments at the facility [23]. The Tshwane Health District should intensify public health education to avert social ills and should also look into taking proactive measures in this regard, such as bringing health services to places where people live.

There were both positive and negative findings as regards healthcare-system-related experiences. These included long waiting times, a shortage of healthcare providers, medication availability, healthcare providers’ attitudes, facility operating hours, and the lack of integration of clinical services. The positive findings concerned medication availability, where ART clients indicated that they always received their medication and that the facilities they used never ran out of stock [18]. Some of the study participants indicated that they had experienced positive attitudes from healthcare providers in the facilities they used and that they were encouraged to adhere to their treatment and received reminders of their upcoming appointment dates.

The shortage of healthcare providers in PHC facilities of Tshwane was one of the main negative experiences amongst ART clients who, as a result, were dissatisfied with congestion and long waiting times. Also related to staff shortages were the poor quality services that ART clients were subjected to because nurses and doctors wanted to cover as many clients as possible. Long waiting times in the Tshwane facilities were seen to arise from a combination of many factors, such as staff shortages, the attitudes of healthcare providers, and facility operating hours. ART clients were dissatisfied with the amount of time spent in the facilities waiting for service [20]. Among the consequences of poorly run healthcare systems are that ART clients may seek better service elsewhere [26].

Treatment-related experience included the effectiveness of ART medication and side effects. The effectiveness of ART medication was a major success for ART clients. All participants indicated that, since they started taking ART, their health had improved. The positive results witnessed by participants encouraged them to continue with ART adherence and not to miss follow-up care visits at the facility [25]. However, a small number of participants were concerned about the side effects of ART medication. More studies should be conducted at the Tshwane facilities to determine ART client experiences related to side effects. Healthcare providers should encourage ART clients to continue adhering to treatment based on improvements in their health during follow-up consultations.

Strengths and limitations

To our knowledge, this is the first study in the Tshwane Health District of South Africa to explore and describe ART clients’ follow-up care experiences at PHC facilities. The study shows the levels of positive and negative experiences that ART clients encounter during follow-up care at PHC facilities. The study limitations were that it focused on only two out of ninety facilities in the Tshwane Health District, and that the focus was on only two sub-districts out of seven. The study focus was restricted to sizeable ART facilities with a high ART burden. The study findings might have been different had the focus been on smaller facilities with a low ART burden. The study explored the experience of ART clients’ follow-up care and did not account for the experiences of healthcare providers regarding follow-up care. The study concentrated on ART clients in a PHC setting, whereas many factors outside the facility setting also affect follow-up.

Conclusion

The study achieved its objectives, answered the research question, and suggested easy-to-apply recommendations concerning follow-up care for ART clients. Applying study recommendations may improve healthcare-related experiences. These recommendations concern addressing staff shortages, the attitude of healthcare providers, long waiting times and improving facility operating hours. The study could contribute to patient-related experiences, such as enhancing knowledge about medication, dealing better with stigma and discrimination, and tempering the use of alcohol and alternative treatment options. The study could benefit ART clients during follow-up care in PHC settings in the Tshwane Health District of South Africa.
