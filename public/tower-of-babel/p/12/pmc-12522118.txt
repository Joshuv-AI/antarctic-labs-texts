Mouse X-linked microRNA cluster regulates the meiotic checkpoint and Prdm9-driven hybrid sterility in a copy number–dependent manner
Authors: Petr Jansa, Giordano Tanieli, Kim Vucinic, Diana Lustyk, Karel Fusek, Barbora Valiskova, Kento Morimoto, Seiya Mizuno, Kristian Ullrich, Linda Odenthal-Hesse, Jan Provaznik, Vladimir Benes, Lucie Pfeiferova, Michal Kolar, Václav Gergelits, Jaroslav Pialek, Jiri Forejt
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
SignificanceWe show that the number of copies of the Mir465 miRNA genes plays a crucial role in the infertility of hybrids between Mus musculus musculus and Mus musculus domesticus subspecies of the house mouse by participating in the DM incompatibility with the Prdm9 gene. Deletion of the Mir465 gene cluster restores the fertility of sterile interspecific hybrids, improves incomplete meiotic synapsis of homologous chromosomes, and increases the global recombination rate. Furthermore, the dependence of spermatogenic differentiation on the Mir465 genes also holds true for chromosomal sterility in the case of an autosomal translocation heterozygote, highlighting the role of the Mir465 gene cluster as a pachytene checkpoint guardian.

Hybrid sterility is one of the reproductive barriers that facilitates speciation by restricting the gene flow between diverging taxa. This universal mechanism occurs in all sexually reproducing species and obeys two main rules. According to Haldane’s rule, if one sex in hybrids is affected, it is the heterogametic sex (e.g., XY in mammals, or ZW in birds and butterflies), while the large X-effect, or Coyne’s rule, refers to the disproportionate involvement of the X chromosome in hybrid male sterility (1, 2). Genetic mechanisms explaining these rules as well as the genetic architecture of hybrid sterility are poorly understood (3–6). The Dobzhansky–Muller model suggests that hybrid sterility is a consequence of the incompatibilities that arise when genes, which have evolved separately, interact. Although the model is widely accepted, there are still no concrete examples of gene incompatibilities leading to hybrid sterility. This may be because most studies have focused on hybrids between evolutionarily distant species, where polygenic incompatibilities are often a consequence rather than a cause of sterility (3).

Over the past fifty years, we have studied a model of hybrid male sterility between two recently evolved subspecies of house mouse, Mus musculus musculus and Mus musculus domesticus (hereafter abbreviated as musculus and domesticus) (7–9). These subspecies can interbreed, but in nature they form a hybrid zone, reflecting their incomplete reproductive isolation (10, 11). Our model (7, 9, 12) refers to complete male sterility of F1 hybrids between musculus PWD/Ph inbred strain females (abbreviated PWD) and predominantly domesticus C57BL/6 J (abbreviated B6) males. The sterile (PWD × B6)F1 hybrids display >50% reduction of testis mass, meiotic arrest at late pachytene stage, and absence of sperm in the epididymis. Formation of synaptonemal complexes and synapsis of homologous chromosomes is impaired in ~80% of pachynemas of sterile males (12, 13). The genetic architecture of (PWD × B6)F1 hybrid male sterility consists of three interacting components, the PR domain containing 9 (Prdm9) gene encoding histone methyltransferase, the Hybrid sterility X2 (Hstx2) locus on chromosome X (Chr X), and musculus/domesticus F1 hybrid heterozygosity. The Prdm9 heterozygous for alleles from PWD (Prdm9msc1)and B6 (Prdm9dom2) strains interacts with the Hstx2PWD allele to cause complete male sterility on the musculus/domesticus F1 hybrid genetic background but not in pure domesticus B6 genome (14, 15), (Fig. 1 A–C; for reviews, see refs. 8, 9, 12). The X-linked Hstx2 locus controls the asymmetry of reciprocal hybrids; the (PWD♀ × B6♂)F1 hybrid males carrying the Hstx2PWD allele are completely sterile while the reciprocal F1 hybrids with the Hstx2B6 allele are semifertile (Fig. 1 A and B) (16–18). Besides the hybrid sterility factor Hstx2 locus harbors the Meir1 genetic factor controlling the meiotic recombination rate (19). To study the molecular mechanism of Prdm9-driven hybrid sterility, it was important to identify the interacting hybrid sterility gene(s) within the Hstx2 locus. To this end, the Hstx2 locus was progressively reduced by repeated backcrossing to an interval of 2.70 Mb (X:65.55 to 68.25 Mb; genome assembly GRCm39) (18), which contains eight protein-coding genes and two clusters of 19 and 3 miRNA genes, respectively. Curiously, the protein-coding genes were gradually excluded as Hstx2 candidates, and the miRNA clusters were found not to be essential for normal spermatogenesis after their removal in the B6 (mostly domesticus) mouse strain (18, 20, 21).

Here, we show that the Mir465 gene cluster is responsible for not only the Hstx2 phenotype but also for the Hstx1 (22) and the Meir1 (19) phenotypes, which are associated with fertility parameters and the recombination rate. The Mir465 gene meets the definition of a pachytene checkpoint gene as it prevents exit from the pachytene stage when meiotic recombination or chromosome synapsis is incomplete (23–25). We demonstrate that deleting the Mir465 cluster overcomes pachytene arrest and restores fertility in sterile (PWD × B6)F1 and semifertile (B6 × PWD)F1 intersubspecific hybrid males. Mir465 also controls a pachytene checkpoint in the case of chromosomal sterility by releasing meiotic arrest caused by a “male-sterile” reciprocal chromosomal translocation.

Results

Structure of the Mir465 Cluster in the PWD Mouse Strain of Musculus.

Previous analysis by Bionano optical genome mapping revealed that the Hstx2 interval spanning the Mir465 cluster is ~22 kb longer on Chr XPWD compared to Chr XB6 (18). Here, we employed Oxford Nanopore long-read sequencing to analyze this enlarged Mir465 cluster in the PWD genome. The BAC clone #266-j04 from a previously prepared PWD BAC genomic library (26) was selected according to the genomic insert ends that mapped to the orthologous genomic region of the Mir465B6 cluster. The long reads generated by nanopore sequencing were de novo assembled into a genomic contig overlapping the Mir465PWD cluster (Dataset S01). The BLAST-N alignment of the PWD genomic contig with the corresponding B6 region revealed three main structural variations: deletions of two long terminal repeats (LTRs), 5.4 kb and 3.1 kb long, and an enlargement of Mir465PWD cluster by 22.3 kb compared to the Mir465B6 cluster (Fig. 1D). The Mir465PWD cluster revealed an array of 13 Mir465 paralogs (one copy of Mir465-d, nine copies of Mir465-b and three copies of Mir465a) and occupies a 38,816 bp region, whereas the Mir465B6 cluster consists of six Mir465 genes (one Mir465-d, one Mir465-a, two Mir465-b, and two Mir465-c paralogs) and spans a 16,469 bp interval (Fig. 1E and SI Appendix, Table S1). All mature PWD and B6 Mir465-5p miRNAs (a, b, c, and d) carry the same seed sequence as well as nucleotides at positions 1 to 12, but nucleotides at positions 13 and 14 are polymorphic. In contrast to the nonpolymorphic mature Mir465-3p (-a, -b, -c) full-length miRNAs, the mature Mir465-d-3p is distinct (SI Appendix, Table S1). The mature Mir465 miRNAs belong to a set of miRNAs that are preferentially expressed in primary spermatocytes and round spermatids (21, 27).

Deletion of the Mir465 Cluster Restores Fertility in Sterile Intersubspecific Hybrids.

To explore the role of the Mir465 cluster in sterile intersubspecific hybrids, we generated mouse lines with deletion of the entire Mir465B6 and Mir465PWD clusters using the CRISPR/Cas9 technology (Materials and Methods). The characteristic phenotypes of Prdm9-driven hybrid male sterility include small testes and absence of spermatozoa in the epididymis. Synapsis of homologs is incomplete at the pachytene stage of meiotic prophase I, followed by arrest at mid-late pachytene. To evaluate the effect of the Mir465 cluster knockout in males of the B6 and C57BL/6 J-ChrX.64-69PWD/Ph/Forej (here dubbed as B6-DX) parental strains and in sterile domesticus × musculus hybrids (B6-DX × PWD)F1 and (B6 × PWD)F1 (Fig. 1 A and B), we determined their relative testes weight (rTW), sperm count (SC) in paired epididymis and frequency of pachynemas with one or more asynapsed autosomal pairs. B6-DX is a congenic strain carrying musculus PWD sequence (4.34 Mb) at Hstx2 locus on domesticus B6 genetic background (18). Consistent with the previous reports (20, 21), deletion of the Mir465 cluster did not change fertility parameters in B6-Mir465KO males and slightly increased rTW without changing SC in B6-DX.Mir465KO males (Dataset S02 and SI Appendix, Fig. S1). The male progeny of the B6-DX.Mir465KO/wt mother and PWD father are intersubspecific F1 hybrids with the Prdm9dom2/msc1 − Hstx2PWD incompatible allelic combination (9). They segregate wild type or deleted Mir465PWD cluster. As expected (18), the hybrids with the intact Mir465 cluster were completely sterile with small testes [median rTW 2.12 (2.07 to 2.22) (95% CI)] and zero sperm in the epididymis (Fig. 2A and Dataset S02). Remarkably, deletion of Mir465 cluster resulted in fertility rescue, twofold increase of testes size [median rTW 4.82 (4.16 to 4.85) (95% CI) and spermatozoa reappeared from zero to a median value of 13.0 × 106, (3.8 × 106 to 21.8 × 106) (95% CI) (Fig. 2A]. A less dramatic but significant effect of Mir465B6 ablation was observed in (B6-Mir465KO/wt × PWD)F1 hybrid males carrying Mir465B6 allele. Again, ablation was associated with significantly improved fertility, with median rTW increased to 5.13 (5.05 to 5.36, 95% CI) and median SC to 9.30 × 106 (7.05 × 106 to 11.30 × 106, 95% CI) (Fig. 2A) compared to the semisterile siblings with intact Mir465B6 cluster [median rTW 3.85 (3.67 to 4.06) (95% CI) and SC median 2.15 × 106 (1.80 × 106 to 2.80 × 106, 95% CI) (Fig. 2A). Histological sections confirmed complete meiotic arrest in (B6-DX × PWD)F1 males and restored spermatogenesis and spermiogenesis after Mir465PWD cluster knockout (SI Appendix, Fig. S2). Curiously, if B6 and PWD Mir465 allelic variants were the only cause of the difference in fertility parameters between (B6 × PWD)F1 and (B6-DX × PWD)F1 hybrid males, then the difference in fertility parameters should disappear completely after their deletion. This was not the case, as a small but significant reduction in rTW and SC persisted in (B6-DX × PWD)Mir465KO males. (SI Appendix, Table S2). Fertility rescue after Mir465 knockout was further confirmed in crosses of six (B6- Mir465KO/WT× PWD)F1 hybrids each with two B6 females for 2 mo. While three Mir465WT male hybrids yielded 25 pups in two litters, their Mir465KO brothers sired 80 pups in 9 litters (SI Appendix, Table S3A). A similar effect of deletion of Mir465PWD cluster on fertility was observed in (B6-DX.Mir465KO/WT× PWD)F1 males. As expected, the three (B6-DX.Mir465WT × PWD)F1 males did not sire any offspring, in contrast to their (B6-DX.Mir465KO × PWD)F1 siblings which sired 53 pups in 8 litters (SI Appendix, Table S3B). Taken together, the rescue of fertility in (musculusPWD × domesticusB6)Mir465KO intersubspecific hybrids identified the Mir465 miRNA gene cluster as the long-sought Hstx2 Dobzhansky–Muller incompatibility interacting partner of Prdm9.

Mir465 Is Responsible for the Pachytene Checkpoint in Sterile Intersubspecific Hybrids.

Prdm9-driven hybrid sterility is associated with persistent unrepaired DNA DSBs and disrupted synapsis of homologous chromosomes during the first meiotic prophase followed by activation of the pachytene checkpoint and meiotic arrest at the mid-late pachytene stage (13, 28). The percentage of anomalous pachynemas carrying one or more asynapsed autosomes correlates with the reduced rTW and the number of produced spermatozoa (reviewed in ref. 12). It has been proposed that asynapsis in sterile (PWD × B6)F1 hybrid males is linked to the evolutionary divergence of noncoding DNA between subspecies. In particular, the reduced symmetry of PRDM9 binding to homologous chromosomes at recombination hotspots reduces the chance of homologous meiotic recombination and successful synapsis (28–30). To test whether the fertility rescue in Mir465KO hybrids is accompanied by changes in chromosome synapsis, we determined the percentage of pachynemas showing asynapsed chromosome axes in one or more pairs of autosomes by immunostaining synaptonemal complex protein 3 (SYCP3) and HORMA domain containing 2 (HORMAD2) protein on meiotic spreads. The results were unequivocal, in both hybrids deletion of the Mir465 cluster resulted in a significant increase in pachynemas with fully synapsed autosomes. The asynapsis in (B6 × PWD) Mir465KO males was reduced from 47.4% (33.3 to 61.4, CI 95%) to 34.7% (30.4 to 39.1, CI 95%, P = 0.034) and in (B6-DX × PWD) Mir465KO males from 67.2% (56.9 to 77.5, CI 95%) to 28.9% (25.0 to 32.7, CI 95%, P < 0.0001) (Fig. 2 A and C and SI Appendix, Table S4). However, compared to musculus × domesticus hybrids with allelic combination other than Prdm9msc1dom2 (14, 15), the rescue of fertility phenotypes and meiotic synapsis after Mir465 ablation was not complete. To conclude, deletion of the Mir465 cluster improves synapsis of homologous chromosomes and rescues fertility in (B6-DX × PWD)F1 musculus × domesticus intersubspecific hybrid males.

Hybrid Male Sterility Depends on the Number of Mir465 Paralogs.

Next, we generated the transgenic strain B6-TG(Mir465B6)1PeJ (abbreviated B6-TG) harboring the bacterial artificial chromosome (BAC) RP23-152G20 with miRNA Mir465B6 cluster (31). The transgene was transferred by backcrossing to B6-DX and B6-DX-Mir465KO strains. The idea was to distinguish between the copy number–dependent effect and the effect caused by the unique composition of individual paralogs in the Mir465PWD cluster. The impact of Mir465 transgene on rTW, SC and asynapsis rate were compared in the hybrid male offspring of B6 or B6-DX females hemizygous for transgene and PWD males. The Mir465 transgene reduced rTW and SC in (B6 × PWD)F1 males to values not significantly different from completely sterile (B6-DX × PWD)F1 hybrid males. In sterile (B6-DX × PWD)F1 males the transgene further reduced rTW and increased the percentage of pachynemas with asynapsis. (Fig. 2B). When the data from Mir465 cluster ablation and transgenesis were combined, the male hybrids with three copies of Mir465 cluster showed the highest asynapsis rate (91%), no sperm, and low rTW [2.0, 1.7 to 2.2 (CI 95%)]. Males with two copies had either the PWD cluster (genotype – B6-DX × PWD)F1 or two copies of B6, one endogenous and one transgenic (genotype – B6-TG × PWD) (SI Appendix, Table S4). They did not differ in asynapsis rate (68.0%, 72.2%, P = 0.75), rTW (2.1, 2.0, P = 0.99), and in SC (0, 0), showing that the copy number of Mir465 rather than a specific property of the Mir465PWD allele determines the hybrid sterility phenotypes (SI Appendix, Fig. S3). Taken together, the number of Mir465 gene copies corresponds to the degree of meiotic breakdown in intersubspecific hybrids. However, the contribution of individual Mir465 paralogs to Hstx2 meiotic phenotypes remains to be determined.

Mir465 Controls the Meiotic Recombination Rate.

The number of crossovers per meiosis is a genetically controlled trait in a wide variety of species (19, 32, 33). The MutLγ homologs, MLH3 and MLH1, members of the DNA mismatch repair proteins, are required for the resolution of Holliday junctions and crossover formation. The recombination nodules, detected as MLH1 foci by immunostaining correspond to the positions of ~90% of the crossovers in each pachynema (Fig. 3A) (34). Previously, we found that the Hstx2 locus not only modulates Prdm9-driven hybrid sterility but also modifies the average number of crossovers per meiosis. The hypothetical genetic factor within the Hstx2 locus was named Meiotic recombination 1, Meir1 (19). The Meir1PWD allele from the high recombination rate strain PWD acted transgressively in the low recombination rate strain B6 by further reducing the number of crossovers (18, 19). In this study, we asked whether Mir465 determines Meir1 phenotypes, particularly in the domesticus B6 genome and in (PWD × B6)F1 hybrids. However, unlike previous studies in fertile mice where only pachynemas confirming the “one obligatory crossover per autosomal pair” rule (35) were considered, we counted MLH1 or MLH3 foci in all mid to late pachynemas. The reason was that the “one obligatory crossover” rule does not apply in the meiosis of sterile hybrids. We found the crossover rate significantly increased by ablation of the Mir465 cluster in B6.Mir465KO males [mean 24.22, (23.61 to 24.82) (95% CI)] compared to B6.Mir465 wild type [mean 22.10, (21.67 to 22.54)], and in B6-DX.Mir465KO [24.04, (23.76 to 24.31)] compared to B6-DX.Mir465WT [mean 21.58, (21.11 to 22.06)] (Fig. 3B and Dataset S03).

We then compared the effect of Mir465 ablation on the recombination rate in semifertile (B6 × PWD)F1 and sterile (B6-DX × PWD)F1 hybrids (Fig. 3B). In the former hybrids the median of MLH1 foci was 23, (23 to 24, 95%CI), which increased to 26 foci (25 to 27, 95%CI) after Mir465 knockout. A significant reduction in MLH1 foci in sterile (B6-DX × PWD)F1 hybrids (median 10, (7 to 13, (95% CI) was reversed to physiological levels, median 24, (23 to 25, 95%CI) after Mir465 knockout (Fig. 3B and Dataset S03). Thus, the effect of Mir465 knockout on meiotic recombination rate together with a reversal of chromosome synapsis and fertility parameters (Fig. 2A) identified Mir465 with both genetic factors, Meir1 and Hstx2.

Compared to MLH1, MLH3 showed slight but significant increase of foci in B6 (MLH1 22.10 - MLH3 23.05) and B6-DX males (MLH1 21.58 – MLH3 22.16), which persisted after Mir465 knockout in B6-DX (MLH1 24.04 - MLH3 24.61) but disappeared in B6 (MLH1 24.22 - MLH3 24.39) (Fig. 3C). Unexpected was the uncoupling of MLH1, median 10, (7 to 13, 95% CI) and MLH3, median 21, (20 to 21, 95% CI) in (B6-DX × PWD)F1 sterile males (Fig. 3C and Dataset S04), suggesting that in the sterile hybrids MLH3 binds prospective crossover sites earlier in the pachytene stage, before the formation of MLH1/ML3 heterodimers, and that the ratio of noncrossovers to crossovers is already established and marked by the MLH3 foci at this stage. Localization of MLH3 to prospective crossover sites before MLH1 at the early pachytene stage was reported previously (36).

Mir465 Controls the Meiotic Checkpoint in Sterile Carriers of a Chromosomal Rearrangement.

The reciprocal translocation between mouse Chr 16 and Chr 17 T(16;17)43H (hereafter abbreviated to T43 (Fig. 4A and SI Appendix, Materials and Methods), belongs to a subset of chromosomal rearrangements in mouse and human that cause male limited meiotic arrest and sterility when heterozygous, whereas the homozygotes for the same translocation, if available, are fertile (37–40). The characteristic feature of these “male-sterile” chromosomal translocations is incomplete synapsis of rearranged autosomes, attraction of unsynapsed chromatin to XY body and failure of MSCI (Meiotic Sex Chromosome Inactivation, reviewed in refs. 41, 42). The T43 translocation breakpoint at the proximal 30 Mb of Chr 17 is followed by the whole of Chr 16 except the centromere16 in Chr 1716. Chr 1617 starts with the centromere16 followed by the distal part (from 30 Mb breakpoint to telomere) of Chr 17 (Fig. 4A). The sterile T43/+ translocation heterozygotes show meiotic arrest, small testes and the absence of sperm in the epididymis, local transcriptional downregulation of Chr 17 genes near the translocation breakpoint and incomplete MSCI (43, 44), but unlike the sterility of (PWD × B6)F1 hybrids, the T43/+ sterility does not depend on Prdm9–Hstx2 interaction and operates on an inbred domesticus background. To determine whether Mir465 functions as a meiotic checkpoint gene in this chromosomal sterility, the B6-DX.Mir465KO/wt females were mated to T43/T43 males and their male progeny with and without Mir465 knockout were compared. The increase in fertility phenotypes was remarkable. The Mir465 knockout increased mean rTW to 3.86 (3.70 to 4.01) (95% CI) compared to 2.28 (2.20 to 2.37) (95% CI) observed in T43/+ Mir465wt and the mean number of sperm in epididymis increased from zero to 3.75 × 106 (2.18 to 5.31) (95% CI) (Fig. 4B). However, the number of spermatozoa was still less than 10% of that in fertile B6 males (18) and 90.3% (87.5 to 93.2) (95%CI) of the sperm heads displayed abnormal morphology (SI Appendix, Table S5) showing that loss of Mir465 removed the meiotic checkpoint, but not the cause of infertility.

To determine whether the rescue of spermatogenesis was related to the Mir465 control of meiotic recombination, we assessed the mean number of MLH1 foci per pachynema. In the Mir465 knockout the global recombination rate increased from 22.05 ± 2.54 to 23.98 ± 3.00 crossovers (P < 0.0002) (SI Appendix, Fig. S4 and Dataset S05), both values being in the physiological range. We then focused on the recombination between rearranged Chrs 16 and 17 at pachytene and metaphase I. When a chiasma, a cytological counterpart of a crossover, occurs in the proximal 30 Mb of Chr 17, the translocated chromosomes form a quadrivalent, whereas the absence of a crossover leads to two pseudobivalents and a synapsis failure in the same chromosomal interval. (45) (Fig. 4 C and D and Dataset S06). The proportion of quadrivalents was found to be similar in Mir465 knockouts and wild type at both meiotic stages (Dataset S06 and SI Appendix, Fig. S5), indicating that Mir465 does not change the recombination rate and synapsis of translocated chromosomes. In addition, the frequency of quadrivalents was found to be consistent between pachytene and metaphase I (Fig. 4E), indicating that selection against later prophase I stages is not related to recombination of translocated chromosomes.

Finally, we examined the effect of Mir465 on the synapsis of the translocated autosomes 16 and 17 by immunostaining the SYCP3 and SYCP1 proteins of the lateral and central elements of the synaptonemal complexes, respectively. Asynapsis spanning the translocation break interval was observed as an SYCP1 discontinuity in more than 80% of translocation quadrivalents regardless of Mir465 deletion (SI Appendix, Fig. S6). To conclude, changes in the global recombination rate controlled by Mir465, the distribution of crossovers in translocated chromosomes and synapsis of translocated autosomes are unlikely to be involved in the spermatogenic rescue by the Mir465 knockout.

Copy Number–Dependent Expression of Mir465 Genes.

To test whether the miRNA expression mirrors the number of Mir465 genes, we used the Mir743 expression as a control. Expression of miRNAs was assessed by RT-qPCR using a miRCURY panel of locked nucleic acid (LNA) probes targeting the specific miRNAs in 12- and 18-d-old juvenile males from parental B6 a PWD strains and from (PWD × B6)F1 and (B6 × PWD)F1 reciprocal hybrids. The expression of all Mir465 miRNAs was higher in the PWD parental strain than in B6 in a dose-dependent manner (Fig. 5A), reflecting the PWD to B6 paralog copy number ratio (Fig. 1). The ratios were as follows: Mir465a = 3:1, Mir465b = 9:2, and Mir465d = 1:1 (Dataset S07). The reciprocal hybrids (PWD × B6)F1 and (B6 × PWD)F1 showed an even greater increase of the Mir465PWD and the X-linked Mir743 miRNAs expression than the parental strains (Fig. 5B), most likely due to the failure of MSCI (46) and the associated increase in transcriptional activity of the entire Chr X. An increase in expression levels was also observed between 12- and 18-d-old males in both parental strains and hybrids. The probe mmu-miR-465c-5p showed the expected expression results in B6 and (B6 × PWD)F1 samples but failed to work in PWD and (PWD × B6)F1 samples, confirming the absence of the Mir465c gene in the PWD genome.

Mir465 Targets a Small Group of Genes in the First Wave of Meiotic Prophase I.

The primary function of microRNAs is to modulate gene expression by downregulating target genes at the mRNA and protein levels. To identify the targets of Mir465, we examined the transcriptomes and proteomes generated from the testes of the juvenile male hybrids carrying the null mutation of Mir465 cluster and their wild-type siblings. As the testes of adult wild type (B6 × PWD)F1 and mutant (B6.Mir465KO × PWD)F1 hybrids show uneven cell composition (Fig. 2A), we examined the testicular expression in 12- and 18-d-old hybrids representing the first wave of meiosis in which the cellular compositions is expected to be similar. Comparison of the (B6 × PWD)F1 and (B6.Mir465KO × PWD)F1 juvenile hybrids revealed enrichment of Mir465 targets within differentially expressed genes (SI Appendix, Fig. S7 and Dataset S08). Cumulative proportion of target genes was plotted as a function of their p-values ranks using empirical cumulative distribution function (ECDF) (Fig. 5 C, 5 D). Among them, the most prominent, albeit modest, upregulation was observed for Tiprl mRNA (Log2FC=0.06 and 0.3 in 12- and 18-d-old (B6.Mir465KO × PWD)F1 hybrids, respectively. SI Appendix, Fig. S7 and Dataset S08). Furthermore, a Mir465 copy number–dependent expression of Tiprl was observed in (PWD × B6)F1 hybrids (two Mir465 copies, the lowest Tiprl expression), (B6 × PWD)F1 (one Mir465 copy, intermediate expression) and (B6.Mir465KO × PWD)F1 hybrids (null copy, highest Tiprl expression) (Fig. 5E). Next, we used mass spectrometry to examine testicular proteome of 12- and 18-d-old (B6-DX.Mir465 × PWD)F1 and (B6-DX.Mir465KO × PWD)F1 hybrids. The proteomic analysis disclosed 48 and 256 differentially expressed proteins (DEPs) in 12- and 18-d-old males, respectively (FDR <0.2). The most upregulated predicted target protein in DX-Mir465KO juvenile hybrids was again TIPRL (Dataset S08). The enrichment of the Mir465 target proteins was tested by plotting ECDF curves, which demonstrated a leftward up shift, indicating enrichment of Mir465 targets among more significant p-value ranks (Fig. 5 F and G). Importantly, the 3’-untranslated region of the Tiprl mRNA harbors two target sites for both Mir465a-5p and Mir743a(b)-3p (miRbase, Fig. 5H) supporting the concept that Tiprl is downregulated by the Mir465 and Mir743 microRNAs. Tiprl, TOR signaling pathway regulator-like is an inhibitory regulator of protein phosphatase-2A and plays a role in the ATM/ATR signaling pathway controlling DNA replication and repair. However, it would be premature to consider any role of Tiprl in the fertility rescue of interspecific hybrids before testing the effect of Tiprl mRNA insensitive to the Mir465 microRNA.

Mir465 Copy Number Varies Between Musculus and Domesticus Populations.

Having established that the size of the Mir465 cluster within the Hstx2 locus is an important and necessary component of Prdm9-driven hybrid sterility, we wanted to get an insight into the variation in the number of Mir465 copies within and between natural populations of Mus musculus subspecies in Europe. Provided that musculus and domesticus subspecies differ in Mir465 copy number in a similar way as PWD and B6 mouse strains, then Mir465 could be a candidate for one of the anticipated X-linked “speciation genes” preventing the gene flow across the European musculus/domesticus hybrid zone (47, 48). To this aim, we estimated the size of Mir465 cluster using Bionano-Saphyr optical genome mapping at 500 bp resolution (18) in five wild-derived musculus strains, SKA, SKE, STUS, STUF, and MBS and in four wild-derived domesticus strains, DGA, DJO, SIN, and SCHUNT (49). All wild-derived inbred strains studied were generated by brother × sister mating of the founder pairs of mice trapped in seven widely separated European sites (Fig. 6A). The Mir465 B6-DX and B6 genomic intervals were used as reference for musculus and domesticus subspecies. After alignment against the B6 reference genome, the Mir465 locus (Chr X:65,828,091 to 65,950,947, GRCm39) exhibited an enlargement in all musculus strains, similar to that previously observed in PWD genome (insertions between 13 kb to 25 kb (SI Appendix, Table S6). The STUS (musculus) strain was an exception as it showed a much larger insertion (107 kb) (Fig. 6B) and integration of additional mapping anchors (DLE1 sites), suggesting a more complex genomic event. The increased interval and complexity of STUS mice appear to be driven predominantly by a lack of comparable mapping anchors in the B6 reference genome. In addition, the previous study (17) found that the hybrid sterility of (STUS × B6) males did not depend on the allelic forms of the Hstx2/Mit465 locus.

The green line depicts domesticus/musculus hybrid zone (10, 11) (B and C) Optical maps across the candidate Hstx2 locus on Chr X: 65,828,091 to 65,950,947 (GRCm39) marking the interval with Mir465 cluster in musculus (B) and domesticus (C) wild-derived strains.

The Mir465 clusters in all domesticus strains tested were either identical to the B6 reference (strain DGA) or were smaller (3.5 to 6.8 Kb) (Fig. 6B and SI Appendix, Table S6). The same pattern of musculus-specific amplification of Mir465 interval was observed when PWD was used as a reference (SI Appendix, Fig. S8).Combined with our previous findings (18), the chromosomal interval encompassing the Mir465 cluster was enlarged by imperfect duplications in mice from all seven European musculus populations whereas all four domesticus strains tested showed either complete identity or deletions when compared to the B6 reference. The duplication of musculus Mir465 cluster refers to the B6 reference without any evolutionary inferences. Without further phylogenetic analysis, it is impossible to distinguish between a deletion of Mir465 in the domesticus species and a duplication in the musculus species. Further studies are needed to verify the role of Mir465 in maintenance of the hybrid zone between the two subspecies.

Discussion

MicroRNAs are a class of small noncoding RNA molecules of 21 to 23 nucleotides in length that regulate gene expression by silencing mRNA transcripts in a wide range of eukaryotes (50). Mir465 belongs to a family of miRNAs located in the evolutionarily conserved region bounded by the Slitrk2 and Fmr1 genes on chromosome X. In mice, the miRNA subfamily closer to Slitrk2 is designated SmiR or SpermiR-L and contains 19 miRNA genes including the Mir465 cluster. The other subfamily, closer to Fmr1 and designated FmiR or SpermiR-R carries three miRNAs (21, 51). The Slitrk2 - Fmr1 region is syntenic in Chr X of all mammals and marsupials together with a variable number of poorly conserved SmiR and FmiR orthologs. Their predominantly spermatogenesis-specific expression has been reported in all mammalian species tested, suggesting a conserved spermatogenesis-related function (51). Unexpectedly, deletion of individual miRNAs and even the entire block of 19 SmiR genes showed a rather weak effect on male fertility (21). In particular, an attempt to identify a hybrid sterility gene in the Hstx2 locus by deleting the Mir465 cluster showed no impairment of spermatogenesis and male fertility in B6 males (20).

Here, we have shown that Mir465 is the predicted hybrid sterility gene within the Hstx2 locus. Deleting the Mir465 cluster restores the fertility of (PWD × B6)F1 intersubspecific hybrids and in sterile male carriers of a chromosomal translocation. It also increases the number of crossovers in hybrids and in the B6 parental strain. Thus, the Mir465 cluster plays a dual role in male meiosis: It ensures the posttranscriptional regulation of the meiotic checkpoint genes in cases of hybrid and chromosomal sterility, and it controls the meiotic recombination rate as the Meir1 modifier located in the Hstx2 locus (19). The meiotic checkpoint is a surveillance mechanism that signals errors in DNA DSB repair or in homologous chromosome synapsis and leads to meiotic cell cycle delay or arrest if the defects persist (23, 52–54). By this sensu stricto definition, removal of a pachytene checkpoint gene allows partial or complete rescue of the meiotic cell cycle arrest. As an example, the ATM (ataxia teleangiectasia mutated) kinase is considered to be such a sensor in mice because its deletion allows Trip13mod/mod spermatocytes with unrepaired DSBs to progress from early to late pachytene stage (55). Similar effect occurred following the knockout of the Mir465 cluster: The meiotic cycle and spermiogenesis were restored in sterile intersubspecific hybrids and in sterile carriers of a chromosomal translocation.

Prdm9 and Mir465 interaction in musculus × domesticus F1 hybrid background are the necessary and sufficient components to reconstitute F1 hybrid sterility between these two subspecies (9, 12). In mice, humans, and most other mammals, PRDM9 histone methyl transferase trimethylates histone 3 at lysine 4 and lysine 36 at allele specific binding sites, which are then targeted by the protein SPO11 to create the developmentally programmed DNA DSBs in early meiotic prophase I (56, 57). The DSBs are subsequently repaired by a cascade of enzymatic reactions using the homologous chromatids as templates to generate crossovers or noncrossovers (58, 59). In sterile PWD × B6 hybrids approximately 70% of PRDM9 hotspots are heterozygous due to the independent historical erosion of PRDM9 binding sites during the evolution of both subspecies, making them difficult or impossible to repair and resulting in incomplete pachytene synapsis of homologs (9, 29). Deletion of the Mir465 cluster enhances expression of the Mir465 differentially expressed targets directly or indirectly involved in the recombination/pachytene checkpoint, reducing the proportion of asynapsed autosomes and restoring fertility. There are many possible ways to achieve this, including increasing the ratio of crossovers to noncrossovers, allowing sister chromatids to be used as templates for DSB repair and/or extending the time window for DSB repair. Such mechanisms could increase the number of successful crossovers per chromosome required for synapsis of homologues above a necessary threshold, thus allowing fertility rescue. However, an increase in the number of crossovers per chromosome is unlikely to explain the disappearance of the pachytene checkpoint in Mir465KO T43/+ translocation heterozygotes.

Both Prdm9-driven hybrid sterility and T43 sterility are male-specific; females with the same genotype are fertile. Male-limited also is the transcriptional inactivation of the X and Y chromosomes. While both X chromosomes are active during the first meiotic prophase of oogenesis, a single X chromosome becomes epigenetically silenced in pachytene spermatocytes, in a process known as meiotic sex chromosome inactivation (MSCI) (60). The MSCI is a specific form of meiotic silencing of unsynapsed chromatin (MSUC), a more general a surveillance mechanism for chromosome asynapsis first identified in Neurospora crasa (61). MSCI is initiated by the DNA damage response (DDR) recognizing unsynapsed X and Y chromosomes. The ATR serine/threonine-protein kinase phosphorylates histone H2AX at serine 139 (γH2AX), which is a signal for downstream histone posttranslational modifications (62). We predicted a failure of MSCI in males with “male-sterile” chromosomal rearrangements (63), which was later confirmed by finding the disruption of transcriptional inactivation of X chromosome in sterile hybrids (13) and in sterile T43 heterozygous males (44). The question of whether the Mir465 checkpoint operates via control of MSCI remains to be answered after the differentially expressed target genes involved in the rescue of meiotic arrest are identified.

We have shown that Mir465 is a Dobzhansky–Muller incompatibility partner of Prdm9 in our hybrid male sterility model. Unlike Mir465, the Prdm9 gene is highly polymorphic in both musculus and domesticus subspecies (49), and only a few musculus/domesticus allelic combinations yield sterile or semisterile hybrids (14, 15). In contrast to Prdm9, the duplication of the Mir465 cluster appears to be musculus specific. All seven musculus populations examined [present data and (14)] shared roughly twice the length of the Mir465 cluster compared to five domesticus populations studied. In addition, Mir465 may act as a hybrid sterility gene independently of Prdm9. It has been shown that this part of Chr X, which has been identified as the Hybrid sterility 1 (Hstx1) QTL, reduces male fertility when transferred to the domesticus B6 background (22). In addition, using the genomic markers, several independent studies found the X chromosome intervals with the reduced introgression across the hybrid zone, indicating the presence of the reproductive isolation genes. Among the strongest were the sites overlapping or close to the Mir465 cluster (48, 64, 65). We can therefore speculate that Mir465 may be one of the predicted X-linked “speciation” genes operating in the European musculus/domesticus hybrid zone (48, 6466, 67). Considering a similar modus operandi in other mammals, Davies and coworkers (68) reported hybrid male sterility in two interspecies cat hybrids. Seven of the top 50 GWAS markers of candidate hybrid sterility intervals on Chr X were close to the Fmr1 gene. The 3.4 Mb interval between Slitrk4 and Fmr1 in the cat is syntenic to the 2.0 Mb interval between the same two genes in mice. In the cat, it carries five rapidly evolving SmiR and seven FmiR (51). Further studies on Mir465 orthologs and other testis-expressed X-linked miRNAs in mice and other mammalian species may elucidate their role in the pachytene checkpoint and their function as speciation genes.

In conclusion, our finding that the Mir465 microRNA genes act as a copy number–dependent sensor of unrepaired DSBs or asynapsed autosomes introduces microRNA as a novel form of genetic control underlying male-limited infertility and reproductive isolation during speciation.

Materials and Methods

Mice.

The mice used in this study were maintained in the Pathogen-Free Facility at the Institute of Molecular Genetics, Czech Academy of Sciences, in accordance with animal care protocols approved by the Institute’s Committee on the Ethics of Animal Experiments (No. 45/2020). The PWD/Ph strain (PWD)F1 is a wild-derived mouse strain from a single pair of musculus mice trapped in Central Bohemia, Czech Republic, in 1972 (69). The C57BL/6 J (B6) inbred strain originates from The Jackson Laboratory, Bar Harbor, ME, USA. B6-DX subcongenic strain carries 4.34 Mb of PWD sequence spanning the Hstx2 locus on B6 genetic background (18). The wild-derived musculus and domesticus inbred strains used in this study are maintained in the breeding facility of the Institute of Vertebrate Biology in Studenec (49, 70). The T43 strain with radiation-induced reciprocal translocation between Chr 16 and Chr 17 (40) was kindly provided to J.F. by Mary F. Lyon in the 1970s. The translocation was transferred on C57BL/10 (B10) background and later to B6 background. The current inbred T43 strain is on mixed B10/B6 background (37).Transgenic mouse lines were generated at the Transgenic Unit of the Czech Centre for Phenogenomics (CCP) - Institute of Molecular Genetics, Czech Academy of Sciences, BIOCEV division, in Vestec. Animal care adhered to the Czech Republic Act for Experimental Work with Animals (Decree No. 207/2004 Sb and Acts Nos. 246/92 Sb and 77/ 2004 Sb), fully compatible with the corresponding European Union regulations and standards (Council Directive 86/609/ EEC and Appendix A of the Council of Europe Convention ETS123).

Copy Number Editing of the Mir465 MiRNA Genes.

To test the Mir465 miRNA as a candidate for the Hstx2 hybrid sterility gene, we manipulated the Mir465B6 and Mir465PWD clusters in mice with a domesticus (B6) background. We generated two strains with deletions of the Mir465 cluster using CRISPR/Cas9 (SI Appendix, Fig. S9), the B6-Mir465KO mice with 16,469 bp deletion as previously described (20) and the B6-DX.Mir465KO mice with 38,816 bp deletion. The B6-DX is a subconsomic strain that on the domesticus B6 background carries a 4.34 Mb sequence (Chr X: 63.91 to 68.25 Mb, GRCm39) of musculus Chr XPWD encompassing the Mir465 cluster (Fig. 1A) (18). Finally, we generated the transgenic strain B6-TG(Mir465B6)1PeJ (hereafter abbreviated B6-TG) carrying a 165 kb B6 genomic insert (Chr X: 65.75 to 65.91 Mb, GRCm39, SI Appendix, Materials and Methods) encompassing the Mir465B6 cluster from the bacterial artificial chromosome RP23-152G20 (31). Next, females of the B6 strains with deletion or extra copy of the Mir465 cluster were crossed with PWD males to produce hybrid males, and their fertility phenotypes were tested.
