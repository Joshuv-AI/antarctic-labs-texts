Concurrent fabry disease and monoclonal gammopathy of renal significance: a case report
Authors: Xiao-Ying Li, Qian-Ni Chen, Jie-Ni Fang, Zhi-Cong Zeng, Yan Pan
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundFabry disease (FD) is a rare X-linked lysosomal storage disorder caused by genetic variant in the GLA gene, resulting in reduced α-galactosidase A (α-Gal A) activity and accumulation of glycosphingolipid substrates in multiple organs. Monoclonal gammopathy of renal significance (MGRS) refers to a group of disorders where monoclonal immunoglobulins produced by low-tumor-burden B-cell or plasma cell clones induce renal injury. Background

Fabry disease (FD) is a rare X-linked lysosomal storage disorder caused by genetic variant in the GLA gene, which encodes α-galactosidase A (α-Gal A). Reduced or absent α-Gal A activity disrupts glycosphingolipid metabolism, leading to accumulation of the metabolic substrate globotriaosylceramide (Gb3) and its derivative deacetylated Gb3 (globotriaosylsphingosine, Lyso-Gb3) in multiple organs, resulting in multisystemic pathology. Clinical manifestations of FD include angiokeratomas, corneal and lens opacities, hypohidrosis, neuropathic pain, gastrointestinal complaints, cerebrovascular disease, and renal and cardiac disorders [1]. Left ventricular hypertrophy (LVH) is observed in approximately 40% of FD patients [2]. However, in clinical practice, relatively uncommon diseases such as FD and cardiac amyloidosis are frequently overlooked, whereas LVH without hypertension or aortic stenosis is usually attributed to hypertrophic cardiomyopathy (HCM).

Monoclonal gammopathy of renal significance (MGRS) fills a diagnostic gap in traditional hematologic classification for conditions characterized by “low tumor burden but organ damage”. It describes any B-cell or plasma cell clonal proliferative disorder that does not meet the diagnostic criteria for malignant hematologic diseases (e.g., multiple myeloma, Waldenström’s macroglobulinemia, chronic lymphocytic leukemia) but produces nephrotoxic monoclonal immunoglobulin (MIg) leading to renal injury [3]. Proliferative glomerulonephritis with monoclonal immunoglobulin deposition (PGNMID) is a subtype of MGRS characterized by disordered MIg deposition in glomeruli, clinically manifesting as proteinuria, hematuria, and elevated serum creatinine.

Herein, we report the first documented case of concurrent FD and MGRS (PGNMID, IgG1-λ type), highlighting diagnostic challenges and therapeutic considerations.

Case presentation

A 69-year-old male patient was admitted in June 2024 due to “chest tightness and shortness of breath for 2 hours”. On admission, oxygen saturation level was 80% and the blood pressure was 267/142 mmHg. Electrocardiogram (ECG) revealed ST-T segment abnormalities. N-terminal pro-B-type Natriuretic Peptide (NT-proBNP) was 2735.5 pg/ml. Both high-sensitivity C-reactive protein (hs-CRP) and white blood cell counts were elevated. Acute heart failure was suspected. His medical history included hypertension (10 years), HCM, diabetes mellitus (5 years), and chronic obstructive pulmonary disease (COPD, 6 years). Regarding family history, his brother also had a history of HCM.

Physical examination revealed a barrel-shaped chest with moist rales auscultated in the lower lobes of both lungs. The apical impulse was located 1 cm lateral to the left midclavicular line in the 5th intercostal space. The relative cardiac dullness border was expanded to the leftward and downward. Heart rhythm was regular, with no pathological murmurs detected in any valve auscultation area. Moderate edema was present in both lower extremities.

Echocardiography showed ventricular septum thickening of about 30 mm and left ventricular posterior wall (LVPW) thickness of 19.5 mm, with a normal left ventricular end-diastolic diameter. Left atrial enlargement with diastolic dysfunction was noted. The left ventricular ejection fraction (LVEF) was 58%. No abnormalities were detected in qualitative Bence-Jones proteinuria, serum and urine free light chains, serum protein electrophoresis (SPEP), or urine immunofixation electrophoresis (UIFE). After admission, the patient was given treatments including anti-heart failure, blood glucose control, and anti-infection. The patient was discharged after symptom resolution.

In August 2024, the patient underwent implantable cardioverter-defibrillator (ICD) placement at another hospital. Contrast-enhanced coronary CT revealed a myocardial bridge in the mid-segment of the left anterior descending artery (LAD), ruling out coronary artery occlusion.

In October 2024, the patient was readmitted with chest tightness, shortness of breath, and pitting edema in both lower extremities. Laboratory results showed: NT-proBNP 3664.00 pg/ml, D-dimer 496.0 ng/mL, albumin 26.0 g/L, potassium 2.81 mmol/L, high-sensitivity troponin I (hs-TnI) 0.2213 µg/L, elevated myocardial enzyme profile, and urinalysis revealed proteinuria (2+) and hematuria (2+). Echocardiography findings were consistent with the earlier results. The patient’s condition improved following anti-heart failure therapy and potassium supplementation, and he was discharged.

In July 2025, the patient was readmitted for 4 months of persistent proteinuria and elevated serum creatinine. Laboratory testing revealed multisystem abnormalities (Table 1): mild anemia on complete blood count (CBC); renal function impairment characterized by elevated serum creatinine, uric acid, significant proteinuria, and markedly increased urine microalbumin/creatinine ratio; decreased complement C3 and C4 levels, positive cytoplasmic antineutrophil cytoplasmic antibody (cANCA), and negative multiple autoantibodies (anti-U1-RNP/Sm, anti-Sm, anti-SSA, anti-SSB, anti-Scl-70, anti-JO-1, anti-ribosomal P protein) on immunological tests; and detection of IgG1-λ type M protein on serum immunofixation electrophoresis. Additionally, cardiac function markers (NT-proBNP, hs-TnI) and selected cardiac enzymes (lactate dehydrogenase, creatine kinase) were elevated. ECG revealed sinus bradycardia, ST-segment J-point elevation, and T-wave abnormalities (Fig. 1). Echocardiography still showed interventricular septal thickening (21–32 mm), left ventricular posterior wall thickening (16–22 mm), left atrial enlargement with left ventricular diastolic dysfunction, and LVEF of 56% (Fig. 2).

To further clarify the diagnosis, whole-body bone scintigraphy, abdominal wall fat biopsy, bone marrow biopsy, and renal pathology testing were performed. Whole-body bone scintigraphy showed diffuse mild technetium-99m pyrophosphate (99mTc-PYP) uptake in the myocardium that was lower than that in the ribs, excluding amyloid transthyretin cardiomyopathy (ATTR-CM). The abdominal wall fat biopsy revealed fibrofatty tissue with negative Congo red staining. Bone marrow biopsy demonstrated scattered plasma cell infiltration without clonal aggregation, negative κ light chain expression, positive λ light chain expression, and negative Congo red staining. Granulocyte, erythroid, and megakaryocyte hyperplasia were found, along with modest monoclonal plasma cell proliferation (1.5%) and essentially normal myelopoiesis. Immunofluorescence of renal pathology showed petal-like deposits of IgG (+++) and IgG1 (+~++) along capillary loops and mesangial areas, with λ light chains (++). Ultrastructure revealed electron-dense deposits in the subendothelial and mesangial regions, diffuse fusion of podocytes (> 95%), and mild homogenous thickening of glomerular basement membranes (GBM). Histologically, there were thickened basement membranes, mesangial matrix insertion, double-track formation, deposition of eosinophilic material in the mesangial/subendothelial space, and moderate-to-severe widespread proliferation of mesangial cells and matrix. Tubular epithelial cells showed vacuolation and granular degeneration. The pathological diagnosis was consistent with membranoproliferative glomerulonephritis. According to immunofluorescence, IgG1-λ expression is prominent. In conjunction with clinical presentation, PGNMID is highly suspected (Fig. 3).

Despite confirming the diagnosis of PGNMID via renal biopsy, the IgG deposits in PGNMID are primarily confined to the glomerular mesangium and capillary walls, with limited ability to penetrate vessel walls for extensive deposition in the myocardial interstitium. PGNMID alone cannot reasonably explain the patient’s concurrent significant cardiac lesions, prompting a systematic differential diagnosis of the etiology underlying his myocardial hypertrophy. Although the patient had a history of hypertension, his asymmetric severe myocardial hypertrophy (ventricular septal thickness up to 30 mm, ventricular septum/posterior wall ratio > 1.3) was inconsistent with the typical manifestations of hypertensive heart disease. Meanwhile, abdominal wall fat biopsy and 99mTc-PYP bone scintigraphy ruled out cardiac amyloidosis. Additionally, the classic presentation of HCM is apical hypertrophy accompanied by left ventricular outflow tract obstruction. The patient’s onset age of 69 years, combined with renal dysfunction, did not support isolated primary hypertrophic cardiomyopathy. Although characteristic myelin bodies of FD were not identified on renal biopsy, this may be attributed to masking by prominent PGNMID lesions or sampling limitations. We ultimately prioritized FD for differential diagnosis given the patient’s family history of myocardial hypertrophy and the fact that late-onset FD may lack typical manifestations presenting solely with cardiac and renal involvement. Consequently, α-Gal A enzyme activity assay, plasma Lyso-Gb3 level measurement, and GLA gene sequencing were performed.

Genetic analysis identified a heterozygous pathogenic variation, c.640-801G > A (GRCh37: chrX:100654735), in the fourth intron of the GLA gene (NM_000169.3). This variant, also frequently reported as IVS4 + 919G > A, is located in the intronic region, and its pathogenicity has been confirmed in multiple studies [4, 5]. Functional studies [6, 7] clearly demonstrate that this variant causes abnormal mRNA splicing, which in turn results in the production of a truncated protein and a significant reduction in α-Gal A activity, meeting the criteria for a pathogenic variant. Consistent with the genetic diagnosis, the patient’s plasma Lyso-Gb3 level was significantly elevated to 14.25 nmol/L (reference range: <2.54 nmol/L), and α-Gal A enzyme activity was decreased to 14.70 nmol/h/mg (reference range: >34.76 nmol/h/mg). These findings collectively confirm the diagnosis of FD at both the molecular and biochemical levels.

Additionally, FD is clinically classified into the classic phenotype and the late-onset phenotype [8]. The patient in this case presented in middle age with predominant cardiac involvement (severe asymmetric left ventricular hypertrophy, recurrent heart failure) and absence of classic clinical manifestations, which is consistent with the clinical characteristics of late-onset phenotype.

The patient was diagnosed with FD complicated by MGRS. Enzyme replacement therapy (ERT) was initiated with intravenous agalsidase alfa (12 mg every 2 weeks). Daratumumab (1800 mg on days 1, 8, 15, 22) and dexamethasone (10 mg on days 1, 8, 15, 22) were used in the MGRS treatment. The patient maintained good treatment adherence and has completed three doses of ERT and one full course of targeted therapy. No serious adverse events were observed during treatment, including daratumumab-related infusion reactions, infections, or other unexpected complications. Discussion

Diagnostic experience and lessons learned

This is the first documented case of concurrent FD and MGRS (PGNMID, IgG1-λ type). Prior to confirmation of FD via genetic testing, the patient’s chronic LVH had long been misdiagnosed as HCM. In contrast, the diagnosis of MGRS is mostly dependent on renal biopsy to verify monoclonal immunoglobulin deposition.

The heart and kidneys are the most commonly affected organs in FD-related multisystem dysfunction. Nevertheless, FD exhibits substantial interindividual variability in clinical manifestations. Cardiac involvement is primarily characterized by heart failure, arrhythmias, and LVH, with the latter observed in approximately 40% of FD patients [2]. However, it is difficult to differentiate FD-induced LVH from other cardiac diseases (e.g., hypertrophic cardiomyopathy, hypertension, aortic stenosis, amyloidosis). According to reports, it takes an average of 14.8 years for FD patients to receive a formal diagnosis, with cases stretching several decades [9]. In this case, recurrent heart failure occurred in the absence of classic FD manifestations (e.g., angiokeratomas, corneal whorl opacities), leading to the long-standing misdiagnosis of LVH as HCM. The diagnosis of FD was only confirmed through comprehensive genetic testing.

FD is clinically classified into the classic phenotype and the late-onset phenotype [8]. The classic phenotype is more common in males. Onset typically occurs in childhood, presenting with acroparesthesia, hypohidrosis, corneal vortex opacities, and angiokeratomas. By adulthood, multi-organ involvement including the kidneys, heart, and central nervous system may occur. In contrast, the late-onset phenotype is more frequently observed in females, with onset usually occurring between 40 and 70 years of age, primarily affecting the heart and kidneys. Although the patient in this case is male, he presented in middle age with predominant cardiac involvement and absence of classic clinical manifestations (e.g., angiokeratomas, corneal opacities, neuropathic pain), thereby being classified as having the late-onset phenotype. Furthermore, the IVS4 + 919G > A genetic variant carried by the patient is a hotspot variant associated with late-onset FD in the Chinese population [10].

The concurrent presence of MGRS is a distinctive aspect of this instance. Based on the presence or absence of MIg deposition, MGRS are divided into two major groups: those with MIg deposition (direct mechanism) and those without MIg deposition (indirect mechanism). Renal injury caused by MIg deposition is further categorized as ordered or disordered based on the ultrastructural characteristics of the deposits. PGNMID represents a type of disordered deposition, with key histological manifestations including mesangial proliferation, intraglomerular hyperplasia, and membranoproliferative glomerulonephritis. Deposits are confined to the glomerulus, predominantly localized in the subendothelial and mesangial regions, with sporadic subepithelial deposits [3]. PGNMID deposits, which frequently manifest as proliferative lesions, are mechanistically composed of intact MIg with complement-activating ability. Renal biopsy is crucial for diagnosis since around 30% of PGNMID patients have undetected pathogenic clones and half have serum-free M proteins [11]. In this instance, the renal biopsy revealed thicker basement membranes, double-track formation, moderate to severe widespread mesangial and stromal proliferation, and eosinophilic material deposition that were consistent with PGNMID. Nephrotoxic immunoglobulins produced by MGRS cause irreversible renal damage [12]. Therefore, early identification of MGRS and targeted therapy against the clonal cells (B lymphocytes or plasma cells) are essential for preserving organ function and improving patient prognosis.

This patient’s serum and urine immunofixation electrophoresis showed positive λ-type M protein. Combined with cardiac hypertrophy, significantly elevated NT-proBNP, proteinuria, and increased serum creatinine, clinicians initially considered light chain amyloidosis affecting the kidneys and heart. However, renal pathology confirmed the final diagnosis of PGNMID. Most reported cases of FD concurrent with IgA or IgM nephropathy were suspected of FD after myelin bodies were incidentally identified on renal pathology, with diagnosis subsequently confirmed via genetic testing [13, 14]. Pathological alterations in PGNMID may mask the characteristic myelin bodies of FD, while the prominent vacuolation of glomerular endothelial cells and tubular epithelial cells observed in this case is likely associated with FD.

This instance demonstrates that physicians should not limit their focus solely to plasma cell disorders when MIg is detected in middle-aged or elderly patients with cardiac and renal dysfunction. It should be acknowledged that monoclonal immunoglobulins may coincidentally coexist with another inherited or acquired illness, or even be induced by the latter’s persistent immunological dysregulation [15]. FD should be considered in patients with unexplained left ventricular hypertrophy, particularly those with a family history. Renal biopsy and genetic testing are crucial for diagnosing FD.

Pathophysiological correlation between FD and MGRS

It is extremely rare for FD and monoclonal gammopathy to coexist. Only three cases of FD in conjunction with either multiple myeloma (MM) or monoclonal gammopathy of undetermined significance (MGUS) have been reported in prior research [16–18]. And no cases of FD concurrent with MGRS have been documented to date. In contrast, compared to FD, Gaucher disease (GD), another lysosomal storage disorder, has a much higher co-occurrence rate with MGUS/MM (19% for MGUS, 1.6% for MM) [19]. This discrepancy may reflect the underestimation of the true incidence of FD or suggest an inherent difference in the immunopathogenic mechanisms between FD and MGRS.

The pathophysiological relationship between FD and MGRS remains unclear. On the one hand, it’s possible that FD and MGRS coexist accidentally. In this case, IgG proteins or λ light chains were not detected during the initial heart failure episode. Renal biopsy confirmed that IgG1-λ monoclonal immunoglobulin was the primary pathogenic factor causing renal damage.

On the other hand, there may be a causal link between the occurrence and development of FD and MGRS. Gb3 structurally resembles nephropathogenic glycopeptides [20], which may be recognized as a “foreign” antigen. This potentially induces monoclonal IgG to be produced that subsequently deposits in glomeruli and triggers PGNMID. Accumulation of Gb3 and Lyso-Gb3 activates the innate immune system through Toll-like receptor 4 (TLR4), promoting the release of proinflammatory factors such as interleukin-1 (IL-1), interferon-α (IFN-α), and tumor necrosis factor-α (TNF-α) [21]. This sustained pattern recognition receptor (PRR) signaling can mediate chronic immune stimulation, potentially leading to immune dysregulation [13]. According to some studies, the accumulation of glycosphingolipid substrates creates chronic antigenic stimulation, which stimulates the release of cytokines and in turn results in hypergammaglobulinemia [22, 23]. Tissue damage caused by FD releases hidden antigens, potentially activating autoreactive B-cell clones through an epitope spreading-like mechanism [21]. Consistent with the pathophysiological characteristics related to epitope spreading, the literature reports significantly elevated prevalence of autoantibody in FD patients (45% for antiphospholipid antibodies, 39% for antinuclear antibodies), with mechanisms involving damage-associated molecular patterns (DAMPs) and abnormal antigen presentation [24]. Following ERT, M protein levels in FD patients remain stable without progression [17], suggesting that clearance of glycosphingolipid substrates may weaken antigenic drive on clonal B cells.

Additionally, FD impairs the integrity of the filtration barrier by inducing vacuolar degeneration in podocytes, endothelial cells, and renal tubular epithelial cells. In theory, this makes it easier for circulating monoclonal IgG to deposit in the glomerular mesangium and endothelium. This represents a secondary renal insult in FD.

Selection of treatment regimens

Treatment approaches for FD with MGRS should be carefully selected because glycosphingolipid buildup may cause immunological dysregulation. ERT is the cornerstone of FD treatment, as it can significantly reduce Gb3 and Lyso-Gb3 accumulation while maintaining low levels long-term [25]. Research has shown that ERT improves or stabilizes LVH and reduces the incidence of clinical events [26].

In recent years, chaperone therapy has been introduced, with migalastat being the most widely used agent. It binds to defective α-Gal A, stabilizes protein structure, and enhances or restores enzymatic activity. Similar to ERT, migalastat effectively reduces plasma Lyso-Gb3 levels, preserves renal function, and improves LVH [14].

For the treatment of MGRS, there are currently no unified guidelines. The core principle of MGRS treatment lies in the implementation of targeted therapy, which specifically targets the clonal cells producing pathogenic monoclonal immunoglobulins. Simple immunosuppression is essentially ineffective [27, 28]. The primary therapeutic goal is to prevent kidney injury by reducing monoclonal immunoglobulin levels. Effective renal protection requires at least a very good partial remission (VGPR), which is defined as a reduction of more than 90% in the involved free light chains or a difference between the involved and uninvolved free light chains of less than 4 mg/dL [29].

For this patient, the treatment regimen targeting the clonal plasma cells consisted of a combination of daratumumab and dexamethasone. Daratumumab specifically addresses PGNMID by targeting and eliminating plasma cells that produce pathogenic IgG. Dexamethasone has anti-inflammatory and supplemental immunosuppressive properties. Serum free light chain levels and renal function need to be closely monitored during treatment. If urinary protein or creatinine levels show no improvement after 2–3 cycles, the regimen should be modified [3, 27].

The patient has now completed the third course of ERT with stable cardiac and renal function, indicating that this regimen is safe and feasible. However, the long-term efficacy remains to be observed. In particular, it is necessary to evaluate whether ERT lowers Lyso-Gb3 while reducing the production of monoclonal IgG, as well as whether anti-plasma cell therapy improves glomerular pathology beyond merely suppressing proteinuria. The efficacy observation of this case can provide experience and a paradigm for the treatment of similar comorbidities.

GLA genetic variant and AGALopathy correlation

FD is recently redefined to include AGALopathy, a novel pathogenic pathway distinct from classic lysosomal substrate deposition: specific GLA variants (predominantly coding-region missense variants) encode misfolded α-Gal A protein, which accumulates in the endoplasmic reticulum (ER) and triggers sustained unfolded protein response (UPR) and ER stress [30]. This cascade independently induces inflammatory activation and organ damage [31], and may coexist with Gb3/Lyso-Gb3 deposition, being a key mechanism underlying treatment refractoriness in some FD patients.

The patient in this study carries the GLA deep intronic variant c.640-801G > A (IVS4 + 919G > A), whose pathogenicity is mediated by abnormal pre-mRNA splicing leading to truncated, non-functional α-Gal A protein synthesis [6, 7]. This intronic variant does not alter the α-Gal A amino acid sequence or cause protein misfolding, and thus cannot induce ER retention, UPR activation or subsequent AGALopathy. The patient’s FD is solely driven by the classic pathogenic pathway: α-Gal A functional deficiency results in impaired glycosphingolipid catabolism and Gb3/Lyso-Gb3 lysosomal deposition, which is consistent with his late-onset cardiac-predominant phenotype and elevated plasma Lyso-Gb3 levels.

Limitations

This report is a single-case observation. It is unable to prove a connection between FD and MGRS. In addition, cytokine levels were not measured to support the immune activation hypothesis. Furthermore, the patient has only been receiving treatment for a brief period of time, and long-term follow-up information is still being collected.

Conclusion

The prevalence of FD coexisting with MGRS may be underestimated due to the diverse clinical presentations and significant interindividual variability of FD. Therefore, for patients who have both unexplained LVH and monoclonal immunoglobulin-related renal damage, the possibility of FD coexisting with MGRS should be considered. Genetic tests and renal biopsy are crucial for confirming the diagnosis. FD has been found to coexist with numerous immune-mediated diseases, which may be related to long-term glycosphingolipid accumulation-induced immune stimulation. As the first reported case of FD coexisting with MGRS, this presentation may serve as a starting point for exploring the pathophysiological mechanisms underlying their co-occurrence.
