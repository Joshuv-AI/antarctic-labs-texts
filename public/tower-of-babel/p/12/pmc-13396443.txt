GPI‑anchored protein nanoclusters license migrasome expansion and serve as exocytic platforms for migrasome
Authors: Xiaopeng Li, Ying Li, Renxiang Xie, Haifeng Jiao, Li Yu
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractMigrasomes are dynamic organelles that form on migrating cells and mediate intercellular communication through secretory cargo release. Expansion of migrasomes has classically been attributed to tetraspanin-enriched microdomains (TEMs). Here we show that nanoclusters of glycosylphosphatidylinositol-anchored proteins (GPI-APs) act upstream to license expansion, while TEMs provide the stabilizing scaffold. We find that GPI-AP biosynthesis is important for migrasome formation, and that insertion of the GPI anchor alone is sufficient to drive precursor expansion, producing unstable migrasomes that retract. Stimulated emission depletion (STED) microscopy resolves a meshwork of GPI-AP nanoclusters interlocked with, yet largely segregated from, tetraspanin domains in stable migrasomes. Exocytic machinery localizes to GPI-AP regions, where secretion occurs. In cells that naturally generate unstable migrasomes, elevated tetraspanin levels convert them into stable vesicles. We propose a two-module architecture generating unstable, secretion-specialized migrasomes in some cells and stable migrasomes as extracellular extensions of the secretory pathway in others.Subject terms: Membrane trafficking, Cell migration, Organelles

Introduction

Migrasome biogenesis, a multistep cellular phenomenon governed by biochemical cues and mechanical forces, unfolds in three consecutive stages: nucleation, maturation, and expansion. During nucleation, localized sphingomyelin synthesis—catalyzed by sphingomyelin synthase 2 (SMS2)—converts ceramide to SM at the plasma membrane, providing essential structural scaffolding1. Maturation proceeds via de novo production of PI(4,5)P₂ at the migrasome formation site (MFS), which selectively recruits and concentrates the PI(4,5)P₂-effector Rab35. Rab35, in turn, clusters integrins at the MFS, anchoring the nascent migrasome to the extracellular matrix (ECM)2,3. Expansion is characterised by the targeted accumulation of tetraspanins at the MFS, driving the lateral fusion of tetraspanin-enriched microdomains (TEMs) into mature migrasomes. Functionally, TEM coalescence augments membrane rigidity, thereby enhancing migrasome stability4.

Recent live‑cell imaging and biomimetic reconstitution of migrasomes on retraction fibers (RFs) reveal that migrasome expansion proceeds in two ordered phases5. First, elevated membrane tension generates local swellings on RFs that are largely negative for tetraspanin 4 (T4). In the subsequent phase, T4 is recruited to these swellings, promoting their progressive enlargement to micrometer‑scale structures and their maturation into bona fide migrasomes. Thus, accumulation of T4 at nascent swellings is indispensable for sustained growth and final stabilization of migrasomes. A recent study further indicates that the switch from the initial swelling, driven by tension fluctuations, to subsequent T4‑dependent expansion and stabilization is modulated by extrinsic cues such as Ca²⁺ acting through the Ca²⁺‑binding protein synaptotagmin‑1 (Syt1)6. Whether additional regulatory events are required to bridge this transition remains unresolved.

Glycosylphosphatidylinositol-anchored proteins (GPI-APs) are a diverse class of cell-surface molecules tethered to the outer leaflet of the plasma membrane via a conserved glycosylphosphatidylinositol anchor7. Beyond their well-established roles in signal transduction, cell adhesion, and lipid raft organisation, GPI-APs are known to form cholesterol- and sphingolipid-dependent nanoclusters that serve as dynamic platforms for membrane trafficking and signal compartmentalisation8. Given that migrasomes are enriched in cholesterol and sphingomyelin, and require robust membrane scaffolding, we hypothesised that GPI-AP nanoclusters might act as early symmetry-breaking elements that couple lipid reorganisation to mechanochemical forces during migrasome formation.

Recent studies have established migrasomes as dynamic organelles of secretion, capable of locally releasing signal peptide–containing proteins such as cytokines, chemokines, and growth factors9–11. In addition to localized secretion, certain cell types, including monocytes9, can shed migrasomes into the circulation, enabling systemic delivery of signaling molecules to distant tissues. Mechanistically, secretory vesicles derived from diverse trafficking routes—including constitutive secretion (Rab8/VAMP2)10, recycling endosomes (Rab11/VAMP3)10, and Rab10/CAV1-positive pathways11—are actively transported along actin bundles within retraction fibers by Myosin-5a. These vesicles are then targeted to migrasomes, where SNARE-mediated exocytosis (R-SNAREs such as VAMP2/3 engaging with Q-SNAREs like SNAP23 on the migrasome membrane) ensures efficient release, a process tightly regulated by calcium signaling. Such mechanisms allow migrasomes to serve dual roles: mediating localized secretion into the extracellular milieu, or packaging and dispatching intact organelles for long-range signaling. Functional studies in embryonic development, angiogenesis, and immune responses underscore that this secretory versatility represents a central physiological role of migrasomes, although how cells decide between localized versus systemic release remains unresolved.

In this study, we combine CRISPR-mediated genetic perturbations, super-resolution imaging, and live-cell secretion reporters to dissect the contribution of GPI-APs to migrasome biogenesis. We find that GPI-APs initiate the earliest stage of migrasome expansion through the insertion of their glycosylphosphatidylinositol (GPI) anchors and, for a subset of GPI-APs, through interactions with the extracellular matrix, leading to the formation of nanoclusters. These nanoclusters interlock with tetraspanin-enriched microdomains to generate a bipartite membrane architecture that underpins migrasome stability and secretory capacity. Our findings indicate that migrasome growth is not a single-step process driven solely by tetraspanins, but a modular program in which GPI-APs act as initiators of membrane expansion, while tetraspanins serve as stabilisers that extend organelle lifespan. Moreover, we show that exocytosis of secretory proteins is compartmentalised within GPI-AP nanocluster domains during the initiation phase of migrasome expansion, producing transient secretion platforms that can be stabilised by recruitment of tetraspanins. This framework provides a mechanistic basis for understanding how migratory cells balance transient exocytosis on migrasomes with the packaged release of migrasomes for long-distance cell–cell communication.

Results

GPI-anchored protein nanoclusters are enriched on migrasomes

By serendipity, we discovered that Cholera toxin subunit B (CTxB), a biomarker for cholesterol- and sphingolipid-enriched membrane nanoclusters12, effectively labels migrasomes in L929 cells, which are commonly used to study migrasome biogenesis (Fig. 1a). CTxB also labels migrasomes in all cell types capable of producing them, including various cultured cell lines (Supplementary Fig. 1a) and primary neutrophils (Fig. 1b). Additionally, CTxB labels neutrophil-derived migrasomes isolated from mouse blood (Supplementary Fig. 1b). Given that CTxB is a well-known probe for GPI-anchored protein nanoclusters12,13, we hypothesised that GPI-APs are enriched in migrasomes. Immunostaining further verified that Glypican-1 (GPC1), a GPI-anchored protein abundant in L929 cells, is enriched in migrasomes (Supplementary Fig. 1c). Furthermore, overexpression of mCherry-tagged GPC1 or CD157 (another GPI-AP), regardless of whether they are expressed in L929 cells, results in their enrichment on migrasomes (Fig. 1c; Supplementary Fig. 1d). This finding, together with our previous data showing that cholesterol4 and sphingomyelin1 are highly concentrated in migrasomes (Supplementary Fig. 1e), suggests that GPI-AP nanoclusters are enriched in migrasomes.

Biosynthesis, maturation, and trafficking of GPI-APs are involved in migrasome formation

Next, we investigated whether GPI-APs are involved in migrasome formation. To test this hypothesis, we first treated cells with manogepix14,15, a synthetic anti-fungal agent that inhibits fungal Gwt1, the functional ortholog of mammalian PIGW (phosphatidylinositol glycan anchor biosynthesis class W). Manogepix inhibits inositol palmitoylation of GPI anchors—a modification essential for stable membrane anchoring of GPI-APs¹⁵. Accordingly, high-dose treatment markedly reduced surface levels of GPC1, a representative GPI-AP, in L929 cells (Supplementary Fig. 2a) and concomitantly suppressed migrasome formation (Fig. 1d, e).

Given the ubiquitous expression of GPI-anchored proteins, we hypothesised that their contribution to migrasome biogenesis is broadly conserved. To test this, we treated multiple cell lines and primary neutrophils with manogepix and observed a consistent blockade of migrasome formation (Fig.1f, g; Supplementary Fig. 2b, c). We then dissected this requirement genetically by sequentially targeting the biosynthesis, lipid remodelling, and membrane trafficking of GPI-APs (Fig. 1h).

First, we knocked out PIGK, the catalytic subunit of the GPI transamidase complex, which transfers the GPI anchor to the C-terminus of target proteins7. We found that PIGK knockout (KO) in L929 cells significantly reduced migrasome formation (Fig. 1i, j; Supplementary Fig. 3a–c). Expression of wild-type PIGK, but not a catalytically inactive PIGK mutant16, restored migrasome formation in PIGK KO cells, suggesting that GPI transamidase activity is essential for migrasome formation (Fig. 1i, j). Together, these data suggest that the biosynthesis of GPI-APs is involved in migrasome formation.

Next, we targeted the maturation step of GPI-AP biosynthesis. After the GPI anchor is synthesised and attached to proteins, additional modifications are necessary for proper function on the cell surface. PGAP3 facilitates the removal of an unsaturated fatty acid from the GPI anchor, which is then replaced by a saturated fatty acid in a subsequent step catalyzed by PGAP27. This lipid modification is critical for GPI-AP nanocluster formation17. We found that knocking down either PGAP2 or PGAP3 blocked migrasome formation (Supplementary Fig. 4a–c), and CRISPR-mediated deletion of PGAP2 further confirmed this result (Fig. 1i, j; Supplementary Fig. 4d–f). These observations suggest that lipid modification and maturation of GPI-APs are essential for migrasome formation.

GPI-APs, synthesised in the endoplasmic reticulum (ER), must be transported through the Golgi apparatus to reach the plasma membrane. Myosin 1, an unconventional myosin family member, mediates the transport of vesicles containing GPI-APs to ensure their proper delivery to the cell surface18. We observed that Myosin1c (Myo1c) is markedly enriched in migrasomes (Fig. 1k). Consistent with previous reports18, we found that Myo1c KO led to the accumulation of GPI-APs in intracellular vesicles, reducing their presence on the plasma membrane (Supplementary Fig. 5a, b) and impairing migrasome formation (Fig. 1l, m). Expression of wild-type Myo1c, but not mutants lacking ATP-binding and hydrolysis activity or PIP2-binding activity, restored migrasome formation, indicating that functional Myo1c capable of membrane binding is required for this process (Supplementary Fig. 5c, d).

Besides Myo1c-mediated trafficking, it has been established that GPI-APs are transported to the plasma membrane via the secretory pathway19. Recently, we reported that secretory vesicles are selectively delivered to migrasomes by Myosin5a (Myo5a) and subsequently fuse with the migrasomal membrane through SNARE-mediated fusion10.

Consistent with this earlier observation, we found that pharmacological inhibition of Myo5a using pentabromopseudilin (PBP), a selective Myo5a inhibitor, markedly decreased GPC1 accumulation at the plasma membrane (Supplementary Fig. 5e). This result supports a functional role for Myo5a in GPI-AP transport to the cell surface. To validate this mechanism genetically, we examined Myo5a knockout and overexpression cells. Myo5a knockout significantly reduced GPC1 levels on both migrasomes and retraction fibers, concomitantly impairing migrasome formation, whereas Myo5a overexpression increased GPC1 abundance and markedly enhanced migrasome biogenesis (Supplementary Fig. 5f–h).

These findings suggest that, in addition to Myo1c-mediated trafficking of GPI-APs to the plasma membrane, the targeted delivery of GPI-AP–enriched secretory vesicles into migrasomes also contributes to their formation by locally supplying GPI-APs at the migrasome membrane.

In summary, these experiments demonstrate that the biosynthesis, maturation, and proper trafficking of GPI-APs are essential for migrasome formation, suggesting that GPI-APs play a specific and critical role in this process.

GPI‑anchored proteins promote migrasome formation through GPI‑anchor insertion and protein‑specific activities

To determine whether specific GPI-APs are essential for migrasome formation, we first conducted transcriptome analysis to identify the top five most abundant GPI-APs in L929 cells (Fig. 2a). We then performed RNAi screening to assess their roles in migrasome formation. Our results revealed that GPC1, a cell surface heparan sulfate proteoglycan and the most abundant GPI-AP in L929 cells, and Bone Marrow Stromal Antigen 2 (BST2), also known as Tetherin, are involved in migrasome formation (Supplementary Fig. 6a–c). CRISPR-mediated knockout of GPC1 and BST2 independently validated their essential roles in migrasome formation (Fig. 2b, c; Supplementary Fig. 6d). In contrast, the other three highly expressed GPI-APs were not involved, indicating that only a subset of GPI-APs plays specific roles in migrasome formation (Fig. 2a; Supplementary Fig. 6a–c).

GPC1 is a GPI-anchored proteoglycan that carries three heparan sulfate (HS) chains (Fig. 2d); these anionic glycans engage a broad spectrum of extracellular ligands, including integrins and other positively charged matrix proteins. We therefore hypothesised that the HS chains of GPC1 are critical for migrasome biogenesis.

To test this, we performed rescue experiments in GPC1 KO cells with either wild-type GPC1 or a GPC1 mutant (S485A, S487A, S489A) which cannot be modified with HS chains. Re-expression of wild-type GPC1 fully restored and even enhanced migrasome formation, whereas the HS mutant only partially rescued the defect, demonstrating that the HS chains are important for GPC1-mediated migrasome formation (Fig. 2e, f).

To directly confirm the presence and function of HS chains on migrasomes, we first immunostained migrasomes with an anti-HS antibody. Endogenous HS signals were readily detected on migrasomes and were markedly reduced in cells expressing the HS‑negative GPC1 mutant (Supplementary Fig. 7a, b). Next, we treated cells with a mixture of Heparanase I, II, and III to enzymatically remove cell‑surface HS; efficient cleavage was confirmed by loss of anti‑HS staining (Supplementary Fig. 7c). HS cleavage significantly impaired migrasome formation (Supplementary Fig. 7d, e), providing direct evidence that HS chains are required for efficient migrasome biogenesis. Furthermore, a heparin competition assay—in which soluble heparin was added to competitively block HS–ECM interactions—also inhibited migrasome formation (Supplementary Fig. 8a, b), supporting a model in which HS‑mediated adhesion to the ECM promotes migrasome formation. Consistent with the role of HS chains in interacting with extracellular proteins, GPC1 has been reported to interact with integrin β120.

Furthermore, the transmembrane GPI-anchored protein BST2 (also identified in our screen; Fig. 2a) uses its ectodomain as an adhesion molecule21. To test whether this adhesive function contributes to migrasome formation, we treated cells with a previously characterised BST2‑derived peptide (B18 peptide) that competitively inhibits BST2–ECM binding22. B18 peptide treatment significantly reduced migrasome formation (Supplementary Fig. 8c, d), confirming that BST2’s adhesive activity participates in the process. Collectively, these data suggest that individual GPI-anchored proteins promote migrasome formation at least partially by modulating migrasome–substrate adhesion.

Since the HS-negative GPC1 mutant partially rescued migrasome formation, we next asked whether mere insertion of the GPI anchor—devoid of any glycan-mediated interactions—is sufficient to support migrasome biogenesis. To test this, we generated a minimal mCherry-GPI construct23 and introduced it into GPC1-knockout cells. Expression of the mCherry-GPI restored migrasome formation (Fig. 2g, h), indicating that GPI anchoring alone contributes substantially to migrasome formation. We next overexpressed mCherry-GPI in wild-type L929 and NRK cells; both lines exhibited a marked increase in migrasome formation (Supplementary Fig. 9a-d). Consistent with this, stable expression of PRNP—a GPI-anchored protein dispensable for migrasome formation based on our knockdown screen—in GPC1 knockout cells significantly rescued migrasome formation (Supplementary Fig. 9e, f), further supporting that enrichment of GPI anchors themselves, rather than protein-specific functions, contributes to migrasome biogenesis.

Together, these data show that GPI‑anchor insertion—together with ECM‑binding activities mediated by specific GPI‑APs such as GPC1 and BST2—cooperatively drives efficient migrasome biogenesis. Because depletion of any single GPI‑AP only modestly reduces the overall GPI‑AP pool, and because GPI‑anchor insertion is likely the primary driver of migrasome initiation, this framework explains why loss of some—but not all—of the five most highly expressed GPI‑APs impairs migrasome formation. Consistent with this model, GPC1 appears to act through a dual modality (anchor‑dependent insertion plus protein‑specific/ECM‑binding activity), which likely underlies its prominent role in promoting migrasome formation.

GPC1 initiates migrasome expansion

To further understand how GPC1 promotes migrasome formation, we analysed its recruitment relative to other migrasome markers, including PH-GFP, which labels PI(4,5)P₂ generated during the maturation stage, and T4-BFP, a marker of the expansion stage. Time-lapse imaging revealed that GPC1 recruitment is synchronised with the de novo synthesis of PI(4,5)P₂ on migrasome precursors, occurring earlier than T4-BFP recruitment (Fig. 3a, b). As previously reported2, PH-GFP signals fade during the expansion stage, while GPC1 and T4-BFP signals remain relatively consistent throughout expansion. These observations suggest that GPC1 plays a role in both the maturation and expansion stages. Interestingly, we found that the expansion stage begins as soon as GPC1 is recruited to the migrasome precursor, prior to T4 recruitment. Consistent with a general role for GPI-APs in initiating migrasome expansion, live-cell imaging confirmed that BST2, another GPI-AP, is also recruited to migrasome precursors prior to T4 accumulation (Supplementary Fig. 10a, b). To dissect how integrin signaling and Myo1c intersect with GPC1 at the onset of migrasome formation, we compared their recruitment kinetics with the expansion-stage marker T4-BFP. Using triple-colour time-lapse imaging, we found that integrin alpha-5 (ITGα5)-GFP and GFP-Myo1c arrive at the nascent migrasome precursor in near-perfect synchrony with mCherry-GPC1, and all three signals precede the appearance of T4-BFP (Fig. 3c, d; Supplementary Fig. 10c, d). These data indicate that integrins and Myo1c act together with GPC1 to license the precursor for immediate expansion. This is consistent with our previous finding that anchoring migrasome precursors at the formation site via integrins is essential for migrasome expansion.

GPC1 assembles into larger clusters on migrasomes

Most GPI-APs are present as monomers, with a small fraction existing as cholesterol-sensitive nanoscale clusters24. Given the high concentration of “raft lipids” like cholesterol and sphingomyelin in migrasomes, we hypothesised that GPC1 would be more readily assembled into nanoclusters on migrasomes. To test this, we performed stimulated emission depletion (STED) microscopy on migrasomes and found that most mCherry-GPC1 molecules assemble into nanoclusters ranging from 42 to 446 nm in size (Fig. 3e, f). These nanoclusters densely cover the migrasome surface, forming a network-like pattern. By contrast, GPC1 nanoclusters on retraction fibers were markedly smaller and exhibited lower intensity compared with those on migrasomes (Fig. 3e–g). Together, these data suggest that migrasomes are enriched in GPI-APs, likely through the lateral diffusion and assembly of smaller clusters from retraction fibers, in combination with the local delivery of GPI-APs via Myo5a-mediated trafficking.

Mature migrasomes consist of interlocked GPC1 microdomains and tetraspanin-enriched microdomains

As described above, STED analysis revealed that GPC1 forms a network of microdomains. Our previous work showed that mature migrasomes are also composed of tetraspanin-enriched macrodomains (TEMAs), which are built from TEMs4. Given the presence of both GPC1 nanoclusters and TEMs on migrasomes, we investigated how these two microdomains are spatially organised. Using STED microscopy on cells co-expressing mCherry-GPC1 and T4-GFP, we found that the GPC1 and T4 microdomains are interlocked on migrasomes. Most of these microdomains are distinct but closely associated, with a small fraction being double-positive for both GPC1 and T4 (Fig. 3h, i). This suggests that migrasomes are composed of two types of tightly interlocked microdomains.

GPI-AP-induced migrasomes are unstable

We observed that overexpression of mCherry-GPI enhances migrasome formation; interestingly, the migrasomes promoted by mCherry-GPI expression are unstable. After forming, many of them retract back into the retraction fibre (Supplementary Video 1). In contrast, migrasomes promoted by T4 expression are much more stable, with only a small fraction retracting. As a result, T4-induced migrasomes have a significantly longer lifespan compared to those induced by mCherry-GPI. In cells co-expressing mCherry-GPI and T4, migrasomes that are positive for both proteins also exhibit longer lifespans than GPI-only migrasomes (Fig. 4a–c). To test whether the ability to form transient migrasomes is a general feature of GPI-anchored proteins, we overexpressed mCherry-GPC1 in L929 cells. Similar to mCherry-GPI, mCherry-GPC1 promoted the formation of unstable, retractile migrasomes (Supplementary Fig. 10e–g), confirming that this phenotype is not unique to the minimal GPI reporter but extends to endogenous GPI-APs.

Given that GPI-APs are recruited to migrasome precursors before T4 and can initiate the expansion of precursors into unstable migrasomes, while T4 stabilizes migrasomes, we propose that migrasome expansion involves an initiation step driven by GPI-APs and a stabilization step mediated by T4. To test this hypothesis directly, we knocked down PIGK and GPC1 in T4-overexpressing cells and observed a significant reduction in migrasome number (Fig. 4d, e). This result suggests that GPI-APs are required for T4 overexpression-driven migrasome formation.

GPC1 nanoclusters as sites of exocytosis on migrasomes

Previously, we showed that once secretory vesicles reach migrasomes, a subset fuses with the migrasomal membrane, releasing cargo by exocytosis10. To visualize the trafficking event in situ, we assembled a triple-expression cassette comprising T4-BFP, GFP-GPC1, and mCherry-Myo5a. Time-lapse imaging revealed that Myo5a and GPC1 co-traffic along retraction fibers (Fig. 5a), and our pharmacological and genetic pharmacological data indicate that Myo5a activity is required for efficient GPC1 enrichment at the plasma membrane (Supplementary Fig. 5e–g) and in migrasomes (Supplementary Fig. 5h). This indicates that actin-based, Myo5a-driven transport is involved in the surface delivery of GPI-anchored cargo.

Exocytosis occurs on migrasomes via SNARE-mediated fusion between secretory carriers and the migrasome membrane, which is enriched in T-SNARE proteins, making it more conducive to vesicle fusion than the plasma membrane. Given the dual microdomain structure of migrasomes, we next investigated whether vesicle fusion occurs preferentially on GPC1-positive or T4-positive microdomains.

We first tracked fusion events in cells co-expressing VAMP2-pHluorin, GPC1, and T4 tagged with different fluorescent proteins. Before fusion, pHluorin is localized in the acidic lumen of VAMP2-positive secretory vesicles, where its signal is quenched. Upon vesicle fusion, pHluorin becomes exposed to the neutral pH of the culture medium, emitting a fluorescent signal that marks fusion. Time-lapse imaging revealed that fusion coincides with GPC1 recruitment and occurs before T4 recruitment (Fig. 5b, c), indicating that exocytosis may occur at the GPC1-positive stage, prior to T4 recruitment.

To further test this, we monitored the release of GFP-tagged TNF-α, which confirmed that exocytosis begins at the GPC1-positive, T4-negative stage of migrasome expansion (Fig. 5d, e). STED analysis of cells expressing mCherry-GPC1 and TNF-α-GFP confirmed that TNF-α signal was restricted to GPC1-positive microdomains (Fig. 5f, g). Consistent with this observation, SNAP23, a plasma membrane T-SNARE that mediates fusion, was also localized to GPC1-positive regions, with minimal SNAP23 and TNF-α signal in T4-positive microdomains (Fig. 5h, i). These findings suggest that GPC1-positive microdomains are the primary sites of exocytosis on migrasomes (Fig. 5j).

The preferential localization of exocytic activity to GPC1-positive regions raised the question of whether T4, beyond its well-established role in stabilizing migrasome structures, might also actively modulate secretion. To test this directly, we designed an experiment to isolate the effect of T4 on secretion from its role in promoting migrasome formation. We cultured cells under conditions that minimise migrasome formation (high cell density ~80% confluence, without fibronectin coating) and compared cytokine secretion from control cells versus T4-overexpressing cells. Under these conditions, T4 overexpression significantly reduced the amount of M-CSF secreted into the culture medium compared to controls (Supplementary Fig. 11a, b). This result indicates that when the effect of T4 on increasing migrasome numbers is minimised, its intrinsic effect on secretion per structure is inhibitory. This finding aligns with our live imaging data showing that T4 enrichment coincides with the attenuation of active exocytosis, suggesting that T4 may function not only as a structural stabiliser but also as a molecular brake on secretion during migrasome maturation.

This inhibitory effect, however, is context-dependent. By contrast, under conditions that promote robust migrasome formation (low cell density, fibronectin-coated), T4 overexpression enhances overall cytokine secretion by dramatically increasing migrasome numbers10, despite the reduced secretory activity per individual migrasome. Thus, the net effect of T4 on total secretion depends on the balance between two opposing forces: (i) promotion of migrasome formation (increasing the number of secretion platforms), and (ii) stabilization-induced reduction in exocytic activity per platform. The physiological relevance of this dual role likely varies across cellular contexts, depending on the relative stoichiometry of GPI-AP initiators and tetraspanin stabilisers, as well as the adhesive properties of the extracellular environment.

GPI-APs promote localized secretion of secretory proteins

The ability of GPI-APs to drive the formation of unstable migrasomes, along with the fact that GPC1-positive microdomains are sites of exocytosis, raises the intriguing possibility that these unstable migrasomes serve as temporary exocytosis platforms, retracting back to retraction fibers once localized secretion is complete. To test this, we conducted time-lapse imaging of cells co-expressing mCherry-GPI and VAMP2-pHluorin. The pHluorin signal was activated on mCherry-GPI-positive migrasomes, indicating fusion events. Over time, these mCherry-GPI/VAMP2-pHluorin-positive migrasomes retracted, confirming that GPI-AP-driven migrasomes serve as sites of localized exocytosis (Fig. 5k).

To visualize this localized secretion in real time, we performed live-cell imaging of cells co-expressing mCherry-GPI and TNF-α-GFP. The dual fluorophores simultaneously co-localized on nascent migrasomes at formation sites and subsequently faded as these structures fully retracted, confirming that GPI-AP-driven migrasomes serve as transient exocytic platforms (Fig. 5l). Notably, in this process, no detached migrasomes were left behind as extracellular vesicles.

We then screened for cell types that generate unstable migrasomes and found that in Lewis lung carcinoma (LLC) cells, most migrasomes were unstable (Fig. 6a; Supplementary Video 2). LLC-derived migrasomes were enriched in cholesterol and sphingomyelin (Fig. 6b), and their formation was reduced by knockdown of PIGK or GPC1 (Fig. 6c, d). Transmission electron microscope analyses further showed that the lumen of each migrasome was densely packed with secretory vesicles (Fig. 6e; Supplementary Fig. 12a, b) bearing canonical exocytic markers (Fig. 6f; Supplementary Fig. 12c) and specific cargoes (Fig. 6g). Live-cell imaging using GFP-tagged membrane-associated secretory cargos showed that cargo signals appeared on migrasomes during their growth phase and disappeared when the migrasomes retracted (Fig. 6h). This confirmed that migrasomes can function as localized secretion sites without necessarily producing detached extracellular vesicles. Furthermore, overexpression of T4 markedly stabilised these migrasomes, extending their lifetime and almost eliminating retraction (Fig. 6i–k), supporting the notion that GPI-AP–initiated migrasome expansion is stabilised by tetraspanins.

Discussion

Our study establishes that GPI-APs are not passive passengers in migrasome biogenesis but active organisers of the process. Using genetic perturbations and high‑resolution imaging, we define a two‑tiered mechanism in which an initiator module (GPI‑AP nanoclusters) precedes—and templates—a stabiliser module (tetraspanin‑enriched macrodomains). The balance between these modules converts transient membrane swellings into either long‑lived migrasomes or short‑lived exocytic boutons, depending on their relative stoichiometry (Fig. 6l).

The earliest resolvable event is the coincident arrival of GPC1, integrin α5 and Myo1c at the plasma‑membrane patch that becomes the migrasome precursor. The GPI anchor itself is necessary and, notably, partially sufficient to initiate migrasome expansion: a minimal mCherry-GPI chimera rescues migrasome formation in GPC1‑KO cells and enhances it in wild‑type cells. These observations argue that the saturated acyl chains of the GPI anchor nucleate liquid‑ordered nanodomains enriched in cholesterol and sphingomyelin—the latter synthesised locally by SMS2 during nucleation1. In this context, GPC1 may function as a multivalent scaffold: its heparan sulfate chains can interact with extracellular matrix components, while its GPI anchor coalesces raft lipids. The resulting microdomain is both adhesive and deformable, providing the mechanical compliance required for subsequent expansion.

STED super‑resolution imaging shows that GPC1 nanoclusters form a contiguous meshwork interdigitated with, yet largely segregated from, tetraspanin‑enriched microdomains: ~60% of the GPC1 signal lies outside T4‑positive regions. This interlocked but segregated architecture explains why migrasomes containing only the initiator module (GPC1/GPI) are unstable: limited overlap with TEMs leaves the membrane too pliant. Conversely, T4 overexpression in LLC cells enlarges the TEM area and shifts the GPC1/T4 overlap beyond a critical threshold, converting unstable migrasomes into stable structures and demonstrating that the stabiliser module can be titrated independently of the initiator module.

Live‑cell imaging with VAMP2–pHluorin and TNF‑α–GFP cargoes reveals that exocytosis begins almost immediately after GPC1 recruitment and ceases as the migrasome retracts. STED localises fusion sites to GPC1‑positive microdomains, whereas SNAP23 is excluded from T4‑positive regions. This spatial segregation suggests that the initiator module not only nucleates membrane curvature but also concentrates the exocytic machinery. The transient nature of GPI-AP‑driven migrasomes thus represents a programmed “exocytose‑and‑retract” event that returns the secretory apparatus to the retraction fibre once localized delivery is complete. This behavior is likely to be especially relevant in cells requiring rapid, polarized secretion without releasing migrasomes as extracellular vesicles.

Several questions remain. First, what molecular cue triggers the transition from the GPC1‑based initiator to the T4‑dependent stabiliser? Mechanical tension may gate T4 recruitment, as suggested by biomimetic experiments4,5. Second, how are GPI‑AP nanoclusters assembled? Given the enrichment of Myo1c on migrasomes, a Myo1c‑driven assembly and/or transport mechanism is plausible. Third, what biophysical features explain why GPI‑AP nanoclusters alone fail to stabilize migrasomes whereas TEMs are successful—do differences in protein–lipid packing, membrane viscosity or cytoskeletal coupling confer distinct mechanical properties on these microdomains? These important questions warrant future investigation.

In summary, we redefine migrasomes as programmable secretion devices whose lifetime and cargo output are dictated by the sequential assembly of GPI‑AP nanoclusters and tetraspanin macrodomains. This modular design equips migratory cells with a versatile toolkit for either transient, localized secretion or persistent, long‑range signaling, extending their functions beyond canonical roles in adhesion and locomotion.

Methods

Mice

All animal procedures were reviewed and approved by the Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee of Tsinghua University (protocol numbers: 22-YL2 and 25-YL2) and were performed in accordance with the applicable governmental guidelines and the institutional animal welfare policies of Tsinghua University. Mice were housed in ventilated cages in a specific pathogen-free animal facility under a 12 h light/12 h dark cycle at 20–26 °C with 40%–70% humidity. Animal welfare was assessed once daily by trained animal care staff. At the study terminus, euthanasia was performed by carbon dioxide (CO₂) inhalation until cessation of breathing and heartbeat. C57BL/6 J mice (male and female, 6–12 weeks old, three per experiment) were obtained from the Animal Center, Tsinghua University. Isolation of mouse bone-marrow neutrophils

Mouse bone-marrow neutrophils were isolated on ice. Femurs and tibiae from one mouse were dissected, flushed with 10 mL cold F-buffer (1% FBS in PBS), filtered through 70 µm mesh, pelleted (300 g, 4 °C, 10 min) and layered on a 3 mL/3 mL gradient (1.071 over 1.119). After 700 g, 20 °C, 30 min centrifugation, the neutrophil band was collected, washed twice in DPBS, resuspended in HBSS and kept at 4 °C for 1 h. Cells were seeded on fibronectin-coated plates and stimulated with 1:1000 N-formyl-methionyl-leucyl-phenylalanine (fMLP) to induce migrasomes.

Isolation of neutrophil-derived migrasomes from mouse blood

The mouse was injected with 10 μg of anti-CD41 antibody (16-0411-85; eBioscience) 16 h prior to blood collection to induce platelet depletion. Platelet-depleted blood was centrifuged at 1000 × g for 5 min at 4 °C to remove blood cells, followed by centrifugation at 20,000 × g for 60 min at 4 °C. The crude migrasome pellet was resuspended in PBS and incubated with anti-Ly-6G MicroBeads (130-120-337; Miltenyi Biotec) for 1 h at 4 °C. The bead-bound neutrophil-derived migrasomes were then captured using DynaMag™-Spin magnet (12320D; Invitrogen) for 24 h at 4 °C. While the tube remained in the magnetic rack, the supernatant containing unbound contaminants was carefully aspirated and discarded. Following three gentle washes with PBS, the bead-bound neutrophil-derived migrasomes were finally resuspended in 50 μL of PBS for subsequent experiments.

RNA expression analysis

Total RNA was extracted from L929 cells using TRIzol reagent (Thermo Fisher Scientific) according to the manufacturer’s protocol. RNA samples were sent to Novogene Co., Ltd. (Beijing, China) for library construction, sequencing, and data analysis. Raw reads were filtered to remove adapter sequences and low-quality reads using fastp (v0.20.0). Clean reads were mapped to the Mus musculus reference genome (GRCm39/mm39) using HISAT2 (v2.2.1). Gene expression levels were quantified as fragments per kilobase of transcript per million mapped reads (FPKM) using StringTie (v2.1.4). GPI-anchored proteins were identified by cross-referencing differentially expressed genes with the UniProt GPI-anchored protein database.

Heparinase cleavage

A heparinase I/II/III mixture containing heparinase I (1 unit, Aladdin, #H766335), heparinase II (1 unit, NEB, #P0736S), and heparinase III (0.5 unit, Aladdin, #H766341) was applied to cells in FBS-free DMEM at 30 °C for 1 h. Cells were then seeded onto 35 mm dishes pre-coated with fibronectin (10 µg/mL) and cultured for 14–16 h.

Secretion assay

To minimise migrasome formation, cells were cultured at high density ( ~ 80% confluence) without fibronectin coating. Briefly, 2.5 × 10⁶ cells were seeded per 10 cm dish. After 4 h, the medium was changed from DMEM containing 10% FBS to DMEM containing 1% FBS. After 16 h (when cell density reached ~80% confluence), both the culture supernatant and cell bodies were collected.

For supernatant processing, samples were centrifuged at 1000 × g for 10 min at 4 °C, filtered through a 0.22 μm membrane, and concentrated using 10 kDa molecular weight cut-off centrifugal filter units. Equal amounts of protein from cell body lysates were loaded for normalisation. Concentrated supernatants were analysed by Western blotting.

Immunofluorescence staining

Confocal glass-bottom dishes were incubated with 10 μg/mL fibronectin (or 10 μg/mL fibronectin and 1 μg/mL Laminin 521) for 1 h at 37 °C to promote cell adhesion, seeded with cells adjusted to reach 40%–60% confluence after 8–12 h, fixed with pre-warmed 4% PFA for 10 min, washed three times with PBS (5 min each), blocked and permeabilised with PBS containing 10% FBS and 0.05 % saponin for 40–60 min at room temperature, incubated with primary antibody for 2–4 h at room temperature or overnight at 4 °C followed by three PBS washes, labeled with fluorophore-conjugated secondary antibody (1:500) for 1 h at room temperature protected from light and washed again three times with PBS in the dark, and finally imaged by confocal microscopy or stored at 4 °C for up to 1 week.

Imaging

Glass-bottom confocal dishes (35 mm) were coated with fibronectin (10 µg/ml, 37 °C, 1 h). Cells seeded for fixed imaging were cultured for 12–14 h; those for live imaging were allowed to adhere for 4–6 h. Images were captured on either a Nikon A1RSi HD25 or an AXR NSPARC laser-scanning confocal microscope at 1024 × 1024 pixels within a 37 °C, 5% CO₂ incubator chamber. Raw data were deconvolved using NIS-Elements and processed in ImageJ. Super-resolution data were acquired with an Abberior STEDYCON super-resolution confocal microscope and segmented with Nikon NIS-Elements Image Analysis Software. Intensity Diffraction Tomography (IDT) imaging was performed on a ZIRCON SC3000.

Transmission electron microscopy

LLC cells were seeded on 35 mm dishes pre-coated with fibronectin (10 µg/mL) and Laminin 521 (1 μg/mL) and cultured for 12–14 h. Culture medium was gently aspirated and replaced with an equal volume of fresh medium containing 2.5% glutaraldehyde for 10 min at room temperature. The cells were then fixed in 2.5% glutaraldehyde in 0.1 M phosphate buffer (pH 7.4) for 2 h at room temperature. After three brief rinses with PBS, samples were dehydrated through a graded ethanol series (50%, 70%, 90%, 95%, 100%), 8 min per step, followed by infiltration and embedding in SPON12 resin. Polymerization was performed at 60 °C for 48 h. Ultrathin sections (70 nm) were cut with a diamond knife, mounted on Formvar-coated 100-mesh copper grids, double-stained with 2% uranyl acetate and lead citrate, and imaged at 80 kV on an HT7800 transmission electron microscopy.
