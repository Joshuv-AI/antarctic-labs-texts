Depression may not be an independent risk factor for mortality in patients with cardiovascular disease: data from NHANES 2011–2018
Authors: Bao The Nguyen, Yi-Chang Li, Hoang Nhat Dang, Hoa Thai Nguyen, Nhung Thi Hong Thai, Dung Thanh Nguyen, Quyen Thuy Nguyen, Duy Tran Khanh Nguyen, Huyen Thi Ngoc Le, Vy Ngoc Bao Nguyen, An Viet Tran
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundCardiovascular disease (CVD) is the leading cause of mortality worldwide, while depression is highly prevalent in this patient population and has long been regarded as an independent risk factor for increased mortality. However, recent evidence suggests that this association may be influenced by symptom overlap and residual confounding that has not been fully accounted for.ObjectivesThis study aimed to evaluate the association between clinically significant depressive symptoms and the risk of all-cause, cardiovascular and non-cardiovascular mortality in adults with CVD.MethodsWe conducted a secondary analysis of 2064 adults with a history of CVD using data from the National Health and Nutrition Examination Survey 2011–2018 linked with the National Death Index. Depression was defined using the Patient Health Questionnaire-9 with a cut-off score of 10 or higher. Primary outcomes were all-cause, cardiovascular and non-cardiovascular mortality. Statistical analyses were performed using R software. We employed multivariable Cox regression models as well as propensity score matching and inverse probability weighting to control for potential confounders.ResultsOver a median follow-up of 4.67 years, 403 deaths were recorded. In an adjusted multivariable Cox model controlling for age, sex and race, depression was associated with an increased risk of all-cause mortality (HR 1.33; 95% CI 1.02 to 1.75) and non-cardiovascular mortality (HR 1.46; 95% CI 1.04 to 2.05). However, after applying propensity score matching and inverse probability weighting, these associations were no longer statistically significant for any mortality outcome.ConclusionsAfter rigorous adjustment for confounders and comorbidities, depression was no longer identified as an independent risk factor for mortality among patients with CVD.

Introduction

Cardiovascular disease (CVD) remains the leading cause of mortality worldwide, accounting for approximately 32% of all deaths in 2022.1 Meanwhile, depression is one of the most common psychiatric disorders accompanying chronic illnesses.2 3 It is particularly prevalent among patients with CVD, with an estimated prevalence of 20.8%,4 which is two to three times higher than that observed in the general population.5 6 Over the past decades, depression has been widely recognised in the literature as an independent risk factor that substantially increases mortality in individuals with CVD. Evidence from meta-analyses, particularly the study conducted by Barth and colleagues, has supported this view by demonstrating that depression was associated with a more than twofold increase in all-cause mortality (OR=2.61; 95% CI 1.53 to 4.47).7

Nevertheless, recent evidence has raised important concerns regarding the reliability of this association, suggesting that it may be influenced by unresolved methodological limitations.8 First, the substantial symptom overlap between depression and advanced CVD (eg, fatigue or sleep disturbance) may lead to screening tools such as the Patient Health Questionnaire-9 (PHQ-9) to reflect the severity of cardiac illness rather than identify a distinct depressive syndrome.9 Second, depression may merely represent a comorbid manifestation or a marker of more severe underlying pathology, whereby the observed association with mortality could be confounded by unaccounted disease severity.10 11 Third, most earlier studies primarily examined all-cause mortality, whereas emerging evidence indicates that this relationship is driven mainly by non-cardiovascular deaths, thereby questioning the plausibility of a direct causal pathway between depression and cardiovascular outcomes.12

These knowledge gaps necessitate a re-evaluation of the association using large, representative datasets and rigorous analytical approaches. From a health management perspective, clarifying the true nature of this relationship is essential, as it directly informs the design of screening strategies and integrated models of care. Suppose depression merely serves as a marker of more severe illness rather than an independent risk factor. In that case, healthcare resources should be prioritised toward optimising the management of underlying CVD rather than standalone psychological interventions. Conversely, establishing an independent effect of depression would provide strong justification for implementing multidisciplinary care pathways between cardiology and psychiatry to improve treatment quality, reduce readmission rates and optimise costs. Against this background, we conducted the present study to re-examine the association between clinically significant depressive symptoms and the risks of all-cause, cardiovascular and non-cardiovascular mortality, using data from US adults with a history of CVD in the National Health and Nutrition Examination Survey (NHANES) 2011–201813 14 linked to the National Death Index (NDI).15

Materials and methods

Study design and population

We conducted a secondary analysis using data from the NHANES 2011–2018, a nationally representative, cross-sectional survey of the non-institutionalised US population. NHANES applies a stratified, multistage probability sampling design with deliberate oversampling of specific subgroups (eg, older adults, non-Hispanic black, Hispanic and Asian populations) to ensure adequate statistical power and generalisability to the US population.

Eligible participants were adults aged ≥18 who reported a history of CVD in the Medical Conditions Questionnaire (MCQ). This in-home interview module was administered by trained personnel using a computer-assisted personal interviewing system to minimise interviewer bias and ensure standardised data collection. CVD status was defined as a self-reported physician diagnosis of one or more of the following conditions: congestive heart failure (MCQ160B), coronary heart disease (MCQ160C), angina/angina pectoris (MCQ160D), myocardial infarction or heart attack (MCQ160E) or stroke (MCQ160F). The MCQ instrument is adapted from the US National Health Interview Survey and undergoes rigorous quality assurance and editing protocols to enhance completeness and internal validity of self-reported diagnoses.16 We required mortality linkage eligibility and available follow-up in the Linked Mortality Files (LMF). Participants were excluded if they: (1) reported no CVD, (2) had missing responses to all CVD items or (3) had missing PHQ-9 data. Regarding covariates, most variables with missing values were retained; however, participants missing data on two covariates with low rates of missingness were excluded to minimise potential bias from imputation.17 This approach maximised the analytical sample while preserving data integrity. The cohort derivation process and the number of participants excluded according to each exclusion criterion are detailed in online supplemental figure S1. The final analytical cohort included 2064 participants, and all NHANES protocols were approved by the National Center for Health Statistics (NCHS) Research Ethics Review Board.

Outcome variables

Mortality and underlying cause of death were ascertained through linkage of NHANES participants to the NDI maintained by the NCHS. Mortality information was provided in the 2019 public-use LMF, with follow-up through 31 December 2019. The NCHS conducted probabilistic matching between NHANES survey records and NDI death certificates using a validated algorithm, which has been shown to achieve high linkage accuracy. The public-use LMF contains adult records only and applies disclosure-limiting perturbation to a small subset of cases, whereby follow-up time or underlying cause of death may be replaced with synthetic values; vital status is not altered.18

Person-time was calculated from the date of the household interview (PERMTH_INT) until the date of death or 31 December 2019, whichever came first. Because cause-of-death variables may be subject to perturbation, all-cause mortality was designated as the primary outcome. Cause-specific outcomes were classified according to the UCOD_LEADING variable (Underlying Leading Cause of Death: Recode), derived from the UCOD_113 recode of International Classification of Diseases, 10th Revision (ICD-10) underlying causes of death issued by NCHS, including diseases of heart, malignant neoplasms, chronic lower respiratory diseases, accidents (unintentional injuries), cerebrovascular diseases, Alzheimer’s disease, diabetes mellitus, influenza and pneumonia, nephritis/nephrotic syndrome/nephrosis and all other causes. Cardiovascular mortality was defined as deaths attributed to diseases of the heart (ICD-10 I00–I09, I11, I13, I20–I51) or cerebrovascular diseases (I60–I69), whereas non-cardiovascular mortality included all remaining UCOD_LEADING categories. However, because the public-use linked mortality files apply data perturbation with synthetic substitution for underlying cause of death in select records, cause-specific mortality based on UCOD_LEADING may be subject to potential misclassification.19

Depression database

Depression was assessed using the PHQ-9, which was administered as part of the NHANES Mental Health–Depression Screener during household interviews. The PHQ-9 is a widely validated 9-item instrument that evaluates the frequency of depressive symptoms over the previous 2 weeks, with each item scored on a 4-point Likert scale ranging from 0 (‘not at all’) to 3 (‘nearly every day’). Total scores range from 0 to 27, with higher values indicating a greater burden of depressive symptoms. The instrument has been extensively validated in both the general population and cardiovascular cohorts, and a cut-off score of 10 or higher is widely recognised as indicating clinically significant depression (major depression) in diagnostic accuracy and validation studies.20 21 In line with this threshold, depression status in the present analysis was dichotomised into clinically significant depression (PHQ-9 ≥10) and no depression (PHQ-9 <10), consistent with several prior studies.2224

Covariates

Socio-demographic variables included age, measured as a continuous variable in years, sex (male or female) and race/ethnicity categorised as Mexican American, other Hispanic, non-Hispanic white, non-Hispanic black, non-Hispanic Asian or other, based on NHANES demographic files. Educational attainment was derived from the variable DMDEDUC2, which was originally reported in five categories (<9th grade, 9th to 11th grade, high school graduate/general educational development, some college/associate degree and college graduate or above) and subsequently recategorised into three levels for analysis: high school or lower, college and postgraduate. Marital status was assessed using DMDMARTL and grouped as married or living with a partner, single (never married) or divorced/separated/widowed. Lifestyle variables included smoking status, determined from SMQ020, with participants classified as ever-smokers if they had smoked at least 100 cigarettes in their lifetime and never-smokers otherwise, consistent with standard epidemiological definitions. Sleep disorder status was assessed from SLQ050 and coded as positive if a participant reported ever being told by a health professional that they had a sleep problem.16

Clinical covariates included body mass index, calculated from measured weight and height (kg/m²) and categorised according to the WHO cut-offs for adults in European and North American populations: underweight (<18.5 kg/m²), normal weight (18.5–24.9 kg/m²), overweight (25.0–29.9 kg/m²) and obese (≥30.0 kg/m²).25 Hypertension was determined from self-reported physician diagnosis using BPQ020 (‘Ever told you had high blood pressure?’) and classified as Yes or No (details on handling missing values are provided in the Data analysis section). Pre-diabetes and diabetes were defined using DIQ010. Participants who answered ‘Yes’ (diabetes) or ‘Borderline’ (pre-diabetes) were classified as having pre-diabetes/diabetes, whereas those responding ‘No’ or ‘Don’t know’ were classified as not having diabetes. In addition, major cardiovascular comorbidities, including congestive heart failure, coronary heart disease, angina, heart attack and stroke, were included as individual covariates in the analysis.16

Statistical analysis

Missing data were addressed prior to analysis. Because the overall proportion of missingness was modest, a pragmatic strategy was applied. Participants with missing responses on two covariates with low rates (<10%) of missingness were excluded using listwise deletion.17 For hypertension, responses were originally categorised as Yes, No, Refused or Do not know; the latter two were collapsed into the No category. Approximately 25% of hypertension responses (n=521) were missing, and to preserve sample size, we applied simple single imputation by replacing missing values with the most frequent response (mode), resulting in a binary classification of Yes or No.26 As a sensitivity analysis, we additionally modelled hypertension using a missing-category (indicator) specification with three levels (No, Yes, Missing).27 Effect estimates were materially unchanged across approaches, supporting the robustness of our findings to alternative handling of missing hypertension data.

All statistical analyses were performed using R software (V.4.5.0; R Foundation for Statistical Computing, Vienna, Austria).28 Continuous variables were summarised as means with SDs, and normality was checked using the Shapiro-Wilk test. Group differences were evaluated with the independent t-test for normally distributed variables and non-parametric alternatives for skewed variables. Categorical variables were presented as counts and percentages, with comparisons based on the χ2 test.

Cumulative incidence and survey cycle-specific incidence of all-cause, cardiovascular and non-cardiovascular mortality were estimated with sampling weights, strata and primary sampling units to provide nationally representative results (survey package). By contrast, Cox proportional hazards regression models were fitted without weighting because the primary objective was to evaluate causal associations rather than produce nationally generalisable HRs. Specifically, our primary aim was to estimate conditional aetiological HRs as association parameters rather than nationally representative effect estimates. Therefore, we adopted a model-based approach and adjusted for key baseline demographics aligned with NHANES sampling domains, including age and race and ethnicity, as well as sex, under which unweighted regression can be appropriate when selection and non-response are approximately ignorable given the modelled predictors.2931 Proportional hazards assumptions were assessed using Schoenfeld residuals.32 The global test indicated no major violation (p=0.078), although sleep disorder showed a modest deviation (p=0.031). Visual inspection of Schoenfeld residual plots confirmed the absence of systematic time-varying effects, and stratified sensitivity analyses further confirmed that the main results were robust. Both univariable and multivariable Cox models were estimated, with variables showing p<0.20 in univariable analysis considered for entry into multivariable models, which adjusted for socio-demographic, lifestyle and clinical covariates, including the key non-modifiable factors of age, sex and race/ethnicity.33 HRs with 95% CIs were reported, and statistical significance was defined as a two-sided p value <0.05.

To further mitigate confounding, we constructed a 1:1 propensity-score-matched cohort using nearest-neighbour matching without replacement (MatchIt package). The propensity score was defined as the conditional probability of exposure given observed covariates and was estimated using multivariable logistic regression. The propensity score model included all prespecified socio-demographic, lifestyle and clinical variables. We assessed the positivity or overlap assumption by inspecting the propensity-score distributions across exposure groups and confirmed adequate common support, where applicable, observations in regions of non-overlap were excluded in sensitivity analyses.34 Covariate balance was summarised in a standardised differences table and visualised with Love plots before and after matching, with standardised mean differences (SMD) <0.1, the commonly recommended threshold, considered indicative of acceptable balance (cobalt package). In the matched cohort, associations were re-estimated using Cox models that accounted for the matched design (stratification by matched pair and robust sandwich SEs) to ensure valid variance estimation. Because results from propensity score analyses can be sensitive to the choice of matching algorithm and weighting strategy, additional sensitivity analyses were performed. Specifically, we re-estimated treatment effects using 1:2 propensity-score matching with a calliper of 0.2 SD of the logit of the propensity score, as well as inverse probability weighting (IPW) derived from the propensity score (WeightIt package). For IPW, we examined weight diagnostics and used stabilised weights to reduce the influence of extreme weights, with robust variance estimation applied to avoid anticonservative inference.35 These complementary approaches yielded consistent findings, thereby strengthening the robustness and credibility of the causal inferences.36

Results

The socio-demographic characteristics showed significant differences between participants with and without depression (table 1). Individuals with depression were younger on average than those without depression (61.7±13.0 vs 66.6±12.3 years, p<0.001). Women constituted a higher proportion of the depression group compared with men (59.5% vs 40.5%, p<0.001). Obesity was more prevalent among those with depression (p<0.001). Racial distribution also differed between the two groups (p<0.021). Marital status was associated with depression, as single individuals accounted for 12.2% of the depression group compared with 7.4% in the non-depression group (p<0.001). Educational attainment was lower among depressed participants, with 65.2% having a high school education or less, compared with 52.4% in the non-depression group (p<0.001). Regarding comorbidities, depression was more frequent among participants with hypertension, sleep disorder, pre-diabetes and diabetes, congestive heart failure, angina pectoris and stroke, while no significant differences were observed for coronary heart disease or heart attack. Depressed patients also tended to smoke more frequently than non-depressed patients (65.5% vs 59.8%, p=0.044).

During a median follow-up of 4.67 years, corresponding to 8841.2 person-years, a total of 403 deaths were documented. Of these, 161 were attributed to cardiovascular causes and 242 to non-cardiovascular causes. As shown in table 2, the overall incidence rate of all-cause mortality was 45.6 per 1000 person-years, with a weighted incidence of 17.1% across survey cycles from 2011 to 2018. Cardiovascular mortality accounted for an incidence rate of 18.2 per 1000 person-years, representing a weighted incidence of 6.3%, while non-cardiovascular mortality occurred at an incidence rate of 27.4 per 1000 person-years, corresponding to a weighted incidence of 10.8%.

Discussion

Our analysis of NHANES 2011–2018 data indicates that, among US adults with a history of CVD, clinically significant depressive symptoms (PHQ-9 ≥10) were not independently associated with mortality after rigorous adjustment for confounders. Initially, depression was linked to higher all-cause, cardiovascular and non-cardiovascular death rates adjusted for non-modifiable factors, including age, sex and race/ethnicity. These associations disappeared with full multivariable adjustment and advanced methods, such as propensity score matching and IPW. In fully adjusted models, depression conferred no significant increase in risk for any mortality category. Our findings challenge the view that depression is a causal or independent risk factor for mortality in patients with CVD and suggest that the observed association is largely attributable to coexisting risk factors and comorbidities.

Depression is undeniably common in individuals with CVD, affecting an estimated 20–25% of patients with CVD,4 and numerous studies have documented a strong association between depression and adverse outcomes. Meta-analyses in post-myocardial infarction and other cardiac populations have shown that depressed patients experience approximately a twofold higher risk of mortality compared with their non-depressed counterparts. For example, a comprehensive analysis by Barth et al reported that, beyond 2 years post-infarction, patients with clinical depression had more than double the risk of all-cause mortality relative to those without depression.7 Similarly, among stroke survivors, meta-analytical data indicate that post-stroke depression is associated with about a 59% increase in the hazard of death.37 These and other findings have led to the widespread recognition of depression as an important prognostic factor in CVD, to the extent that professional guidelines have advocated routine depression screening for patients with cardiac issues.38 The prevailing explanation is that depression may exacerbate cardiovascular risk through both behavioural and biological pathways. Patients with depression are more likely to engage in unhealthy behaviours—such as smoking, physical inactivity and poor diet—and to have lower adherence to medications and cardiac rehabilitation, all of which can adversely affect outcomes.39 Concurrently, depression is associated with physiological changes like heightened sympathetic activity, hypothalamic–pituitary–adrenal (HPA) axis dysregulation, increased inflammation and reduced heart rate variability.39 These mechanisms can promote atherothrombosis and arrhythmogenesis, offering a plausible link by which depression might influence cardiovascular events and mortality. Given this backdrop of evidence, it is noteworthy that our study did not find an independent depression-mortality association after accounting for other risk variables.

Several factors may explain why our findings diverge from the anticipated depression–mortality relationship. First, the impact of depression appears to be largely mediated by concomitant health behaviours and clinical characteristics. In a well-known prospective study of outpatients with stable coronary disease, Whooley et al demonstrated that the association between depressive symptoms and cardiovascular events was essentially nullified after accounting for differences in lifestyle factors, particularly physical inactivity.40 We observed a similar pattern in which depressed participants in our cohort were generally older and carried a higher burden of comorbidities such as hypertension, pre-diabetes and diabetes, sleep disorder, congestive heart failure, angina and stroke, as well as risk factors including smoking, compared with non-depressed participants. These variables emerged as the dominant predictors of mortality. Indeed, the European Society of Cardiology has noted that much of the risk associated with depression in patients with cardiac issues stems from poorer adherence to preventive strategies and adverse lifestyle profiles.39 Second, depression may not be an independent cause of mortality but could instead reflect overall health status and disease severity. Some depressive symptoms overlap with the manifestations of advanced CVD—for example, profound fatigue, low interest or poor appetite can reflect cardiac dysfunction or cachexia. Intriguingly, an analysis of NHANES data noted that certain PHQ-9 items, such as ‘feeling tired or having little energy’ and ‘poor appetite or overeating’ were independently associated with higher all-cause and CVD mortality.41 This may suggest that elevated depression scores in patients with CVD might, in part, be capturing those with more severe illness or frailty rather than a distinct psychiatric effect. Indeed, a meta-analysis in heart failure found that depression’s prognostic impact was strongest in older patients and those with short follow-up, and the authors explained that late-life depression may serve as an indicator of advanced heart failure rather than an independent risk factor.42 Our study did not have direct measures of CVD severity, such as ejection fraction or angina class. However, the absence of an association after adjustment raises the possibility that any link between depression and mortality may have been influenced by unmeasured disease severity. Overall, our findings support the growing view that depression itself may not directly increase mortality in patients with CVD once medical risk factors are adequately controlled. This association may have been overestimated in some settings because of publication bias and low study quality.43

The strengths of the present study include its large, nationally representative sample and the use of standardised PHQ-9 screening to capture depressive symptoms. By leveraging linked NDI data through 2019, we could examine cause-specific mortality (cardiovascular vs non-cardiovascular) and all-cause mortality. We employed robust statistical approaches—multivariable regression with extensive covariate adjustment, propensity score matching and IPW—to rigorously control for confounding. The consistency of our results across these methods lends credibility to the null findings regarding depression. Our analysis therefore provides a timely re-evaluation of the depression-mortality causality in CVD. Notably, the lack of an independent association in our study is concordant with some other recent investigations. For example, a case-controlled study in individuals with stroke similarly found that depression was not a risk factor for mortality.44 Similarly, the DECADE cohort study demonstrated that depression did not increase the risk of all-cause mortality or major adverse cardiovascular events in patients with CVD,11 which may be explained by a ceiling effect of risk. In patients with advanced CAD characterised by extensive atherosclerotic burden, impaired cardiac function and arrhythmias—factors that are already strong predictors of mortality—the additional impact of depression becomes marginal and overshadowed. Such convergent evidence reinforces the idea that depression’s influence on survival may be indirect. It is worth emphasising, however, that absence of evidence is not evidence of absence—our findings do not show that depression has no impact on patient outcomes. Instead, any effect is likely explained by or hidden behind other factors.

Despite its strengths, this study has several limitations. First, the observational and cross-sectional design of NHANES (with depression assessed at a single baseline point) precludes any conclusions about causality or temporal ordering. We cannot determine whether depression might contribute to mortality over longer periods or whether successful depression treatment would improve survival—only that in our data, baseline depressive symptoms did not confer additional risk once baseline health status was accounted for. Second, there is the possibility of residual confounding by unmeasured variables. Although we adjusted for a wide range of socio-demographic, lifestyle and clinical factors—and complemented conventional multivariable modelling with propensity score matching and stabilised IPW to improve covariate balance—propensity score-based approaches can only address confounding by measured baseline covariates and depend on key assumptions, including adequate overlap and correct model specification. Self-reported hypertension had non-trivial missingness. We therefore applied a prespecified single imputation for BPQ030 for model feasibility and conducted a missing-category/indicator sensitivity analysis with materially similar estimates; however, we did not implement multiple imputation, and some underestimation of uncertainty may remain if missingness was informative. Consequently, we cannot fully exclude bias from unmeasured factors such as social support, treatment adherence or CVD severity, and the findings should be interpreted as robustly adjusted associations rather than definitive causal effects.45 46 We adjusted for many socio-demographic, lifestyle and clinical factors, but factors such as social support, treatment adherence or severity of CVD were not captured and could influence both depression and mortality. Relatedly, depression status was based on a PHQ-9 score ≥10 at one time point, which may misclassify some individuals, for example, those with transient depressive symptoms or those on antidepressant therapy. Depression is a dynamic condition and some participants categorised as non-depressed at baseline may have developed depression during follow-up. Conversely, some participants classified as depressed at baseline may have experienced symptom resolution over time or initiated or changed antidepressant treatment during follow-up. The PHQ-9 is responsive to change, underscoring that depressive symptom burden can vary meaningfully across time.47 As a result, relying on a single baseline PHQ-9 assessment may introduce time-varying exposure misclassification and regression dilution, which typically attenuate true associations toward the null when exposures fluctuate and are measured with error.48 In addition, if changes in depressive symptoms are correlated with evolving clinical status or treatments during follow-up, standard baseline-only models may not fully capture time-dependent confounding structures.49 Third, the reliance on self-reported CVD history means we could not differentiate specific diagnoses or disease durations—the sample included a heterogeneous mix of coronary disease, stroke, heart failure, etc. The heterogeneity in underlying cardiovascular conditions could mask an association in a particular subgroup. Lastly, the median follow-up time in our analysis was moderate; it remains possible that depression could impact very long-term outcomes beyond the follow-up period.

In summary, our findings suggest that depression, when rigorously evaluated in a large cardiovascular cohort, may not be independently associated with an increased risk of mortality after adjustment for other prognostic factors. These findings are consistent with the possibility that depression may function as a risk marker rather than a direct risk factor in this context. Nevertheless, the clinical significance of depression remains considerable since it continues to impair quality of life and functional capacity. In practice, screening and recognition of depression should be regarded as a strategic opportunity to identify patients at highest risk and to implement comprehensive and integrated care pathways focusing on the management of comorbidities and lifestyle-related factors. Therefore, while treatment to alleviate depressive symptoms is necessary, it may not directly prolong survival and the survival benefit is more likely to result from intensive control of overall cardiovascular risk factors that depression reflects. Further investigations are warranted to confirm these observations and to determine whether specific patient subgroups may derive survival benefit from depression-targeted interventions. Meanwhile, clinicians and healthcare policymakers should continue to integrate psychosocial care into standard cardiovascular management, acknowledging that improving depressive status confers substantial benefits even when its direct relationship with mortality remains complex.

Conclusion

Our results suggest that depression is unlikely to be an independent risk factor for mortality in patients with CVD after adjustment for confounders and comorbidities. Instead, its apparent effect may reflect underlying disease severity, warranting confirmation in well-designed prospective cohorts with detailed severity assessment.
