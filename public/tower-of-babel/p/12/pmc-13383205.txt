To the Editor:

Severe asthma is a heterogeneous condition requiring multifaceted management. Inhaled corticosteroids (ICS) remain the cornerstone of treatment; however, some patients remain uncontrolled or exhibit ICS resistance, necessitating biologic therapy. While biologics primarily target type 2 (T2)-driven inflammation, some exhibit non-T2, often neutrophilic, airway inflammation [1]. Accurate assessment of airway inflammation is crucial for precision medicine and phenotype-based treatment in severe asthma [1, 2].

Blood eosinophils and exhaled nitric oxide fraction (FENO) are used to infer airway eosinophilia but do not consistently reflect inflammation [3]. Sputum cytology offers a more direct assessment of airway inflammation but remains limited in routine practice due to perceived technical challenges and lack of real-world evidence [4].

We report our experience implementing a clinically delivered sputum cytology service for severe asthma, describing inflammatory phenotype distribution, concordance with standard biomarkers and its impact on clinical decision-making.

We conducted a retrospective observational study at the Birmingham Regional Severe Asthma Service between October 2022 and May 2025, approved by University Hospitals Birmingham NHS Foundation Trust as a service evaluation (CARMS23615). Adults meeting European Respiratory Society (ERS)/American Thoracic Society criteria for severe asthma [5], uncontrolled on standard and/or biologic therapy, were invited to provide sputum samples. Smoking status and maintenance oral corticosteroid (OCS) use were recorded. Comorbidity data were not systematically collected.

Samples were obtained spontaneously or by induction as per ERS standards [6]. A weekly sputum clinic was established within the respiratory department, staffed by trained nurses and physiotherapists. If spontaneous sputum failed, induction was performed with bronchodilator premedication and spirometric monitoring. Visits typically lasted 30–60 min. Samples were transported on ice to the pathology department, processed within 2 h, and differential counts reported by a certified cytologist and consultant clinical pathologist. Samples with >30% squamous contamination were excluded. Only differential counts were analysed; microbiology was performed when clinically indicated.

Inflammatory phenotypes were defined as eosinophilic (≥3% eosinophils), neutrophilic (≥60% neutrophils), mixed or paucigranulocytic [7]. Sensitivity analyses using higher neutrophil thresholds (≥70% and ≥80%) were performed to account for age-related increases [8]. Blood eosinophils, FENO and spirometry were recorded. Results were discussed by the asthma multidisciplinary team to guide treatment. Primary outcomes were sample adequacy and impact on treatment decisions. Secondary outcomes examined associations between sputum eosinophils and standard biomarkers. Continuous variables were analysed with Spearman's correlation and categorical variables were analysed with Chi-squared tests (p<0.05).

Among 220 patients, sputum samples were obtained spontaneously (n=148) or by induction (n=72). Adequate samples were achieved in 58.2% (n/N=128/220): 59.5% spontaneous and 55.5% induced, with 22% (n/N=16/72) of induced following previously inadequate spontaneous attempts.

Demographics among patients with adequate samples were comparable to the full cohort (mean±sd age 51.7±15.6 years; 70% female; mean body mass index 32.1±9.0 kg·m−2; never smoked 54.7%, ex-smokers 32%, current smokers 9.4%, unavailable smoking status 3.9%). At sampling, all were receiving medium- to high-dose ICS, 7% (n=9) receiving maintenance OCS for asthma (5–20 mg·day−1 prednisolone) and 4% (n=5) hydrocortisone for secondary adrenal insufficiency (20 mg·day−1 in divided doses). Median sputum eosinophils were 2.0% (interquartile range (IQR) 1.0–9.0%) and neutrophils 70.5% (IQR 45.5–82.5%); median blood eosinophils 0.14×109 per L (IQR 0.06–0.28 per L); FENO 21.0 ppb (IQR 12.0–38.0 ppb). Median forced expiratory volume in 1 s (FEV1) was 1.9 L (IQR 1.32–2.79 L), mean FEV1 % predicted 69.3±25.1%. Inflammatory phenotypes were eosinophilic (19.5%), neutrophilic (34.4%), mixed (27.3%) and paucigranulocytic (18.8%). Neutrophilia remained common using higher thresholds (≥70%: 52%; ≥80%: 35.2%).

Sputum eosinophilia (≥3%) was present in 48% (n/N=61/128); 41% (n/N=25/61) had blood eosinophils <0.15×109 per L and 70% (n/N=43/61) <0.3×109 per L. Conversely, 36% (n/N=24/67) of noneosinophilic samples had blood eosinophils ≥0.15×109 per L and 12% (n/N=8/67) ≥0.3×109 per L. FENO was <25 ppb in 48% (n/N=23/57) and <50 ppb in 75% (n/N=43/57) of eosinophilic samples. Sputum eosinophils correlated moderately with blood eosinophils (ρ=0.359, p<0.0001) and FENO (ρ=0.362, p=0.0001). Categorical associations were significant (table 1).

Among patients receiving biologics (n=48), 29% had sputum eosinophilia despite low blood eosinophils (<0.15×109 per L) versus 12.5% of biologic-naïve patients. In anti-interleukin (IL)-5/IL-5 receptor (R) therapy recipients (n=29), 41% (n/N=12/29) had sputum eosinophilia despite blood eosinophils (<0.15×109 per L), and 42% (n/N=5/12) had both blood eosinophils <0.3×109 per L and FENO <50 ppb (table 1).

For patients with available data (n=92), the median number of exacerbations requiring OCS in the preceding 12 months was 2 (IQR 1–4). Exacerbation frequency was not stratified by biomarker discordance; therefore, clinical significance cannot be determined here. Sputum cytology informed treatment changes in 58.6% (n/N=75/128), including biologic initiation (34.7%) or switch (18.7%), macrolide initiation (44%), or macrolide discontinuation (2.7%).

Contrary to the belief that sputum analysis is impractical, we show its feasibility and clinical value in severe asthma, representing one of the first real-world demonstrations of routine sputum cytology. Most samples were obtained spontaneously, using induction when required; over half were adequate for analysis (58.2%), which is comparable to prior reports (57–90%) [9, 10]. Sputum neutrophilia was more common, yet eosinophilia was detected in a substantial proportion of samples despite low blood eosinophils or FENO, highlighting the limitations of relying solely on peripheral biomarkers. Discordance between sputum and peripheral biomarkers has been reported [11, 12], underscoring severe asthma heterogeneity and supporting routine sputum-based phenotyping.

Among patients receiving anti-IL-5/IL-5R therapy, some demonstrated persistent sputum eosinophilia despite suppressed blood eosinophils. Although mechanistically unexpected, this has been variably reported, and may reflect methodological and population differences. In the BenRex study, sputum collection during exacerbations with high rates of missed events potentially under-represented eosinophilia [13]. Real-world data from Manchester, UK, showed sputum eosinophils in a minority of benralizumab-treated patients and a larger proportion receiving mepolizumab [14]. Our service selected patients with persistent disease burden despite therapy, possibly enriching for partial/nonresponders. Although anti-drug antibodies, serum drug levels and adherence were not assessed, suppressed blood eosinophils argue against suboptimal exposure as a primary explanation. Persistent airway eosinophils in tissue or coexistent pathology may contribute, highlighting the complexity of airway inflammation and limitations of peripheral biomarkers.

FENO often aligned with sputum eosinophilia but discordance shows a normal FENO cannot always exclude it. Smoking suppresses FENO, yet discordance persisted in our cohort despite inclusion of smokers/ex-smokers. Sputum neutrophilia was common (62%), remaining frequent even at higher thresholds accounting for age-related increases, supporting its robustness and clinical relevance for guiding macrolide therapy [15]. Sputum cytology informed treatment changes in 58.6% of cases, although longitudinal follow-up is needed to elucidate the clinical impact of sputum-guided management.

Eosinophilic airway inflammation is temporally variable, and our analysis reflects blood, FENO and sputum measurements obtained at a single time point. Serial assessments may better characterise endotypes and biomarker concordance. Our cohort comprised complex, predominantly biomarker-low patients with ongoing disease burden despite advanced therapy, limiting generalisability.

Phenotypes may have been influenced by treatment or nonadherence among home-care patients, although consistently suppressed blood eosinophils on benralizumab suggests this was unlikely. Maintenance OCS may have influenced biomarkers; data on acute rescue courses were unavailable. Lack of routine airway infection and microbiology assessment limits interpretation of neutrophilic profiles. We could not assess whether sputum-defined phenotypes or biomarker discordance influenced clinical outcomes like exacerbation frequency or symptom burden across biologic therapies, limiting conclusions regarding clinical significance. Comorbidities including bronchiectasis were not captured but may influence sputum profiles.

Sputum cytology is feasible and deliverable in routine severe asthma care, providing direct insight into airway inflammation when peripheral biomarkers are inconclusive and influencing treatment decisions in a substantial proportion of patients. Integration into severe asthma pathways may enhance diagnostic precision and personalised care, although further standardisation and longitudinal outcome data are needed.
