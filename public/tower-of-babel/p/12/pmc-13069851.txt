Revealing cancer glycome drivers using CRISPR activation screens
Authors: Mathieu Decloquement, Matthew S Macauley
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractIn this study, Wisnovsky and colleagues1 investigate the genetic determinants underlying cancer-associated glycome remodeling. Using genome-wide CRISPR-based approaches, they identified regulators of cell surface glycosylation. Specifically, genetic networks are discovered that upregulate cancer-related ligands of the sialic acid-binding immunomodulatory Siglecs.

Main text

Immune checkpoint therapies primarily target immunoinhibitory receptors, blocking interactions with their ligands. However, a substantial proportion of patients fail to respond, partly due to tumor heterogeneity and the presence of numerous inhibitory receptors that restrain immune activation. Malignant transformation is frequently accompanied by changes in glycosylation, including hypersialylation,2 which can serve as ligands for immunoinhibitory sialic acid-binding immunoglobulin-type lectins (Siglecs). The development of innovative ways to block Siglec-glycan interactions is hindered by an incomplete description of the identity and regulation of their sialoglycan ligands.

Deciphering the regulatory networks that shapes Siglec ligands is challenging because glycosylation pathways can have redundancy, with overlapping enzyme function and context-dependent gene expression.3 Building on previous CRISPR interference (CRISPRi),4 wherein loss-of-function screens identified contributors to Siglec-7 ligand expression in chronic myeloid leukemia, the authors implemented a complementary gain-of-function strategy as a way to address the challenge of redundancy. Specifically, they implemented a dCas9-based transcriptional activation system (CRISPRa) coupled with a SunTag-VP64 scaffold, enabling systematic upregulation of each of 20,800 genes within a single cellular background.1 The power of this approach is its ability to reveal genes with low basal expression (∼50% in K562 cells) that may nevertheless exert strong functional effects.5

Using K562 cells, the authors screened for binding to soluble Fc-fused Siglec-7, -9, -10, and -15 (Figure 1A). Cells were sorted by flow cytometry into high- and low-binding populations, and by comparative single-cell RNA sequencing, coupled with a Cas9 high-throughput maximum likelihood estimator (CasTLE) scoring identified genes whose overexpression significantly enhanced or reduced Siglec binding. A key conceptual advance of the study is the demonstration that cancer-associated hypersialylation does not depend on a single dominant gene. Rather, multiple genetic routes converge to generate Siglec ligands, which is dependent on the expression of glyco-genes and their carriers.

One central finding is that modification of a galactose residue constitutes a bottleneck in ligand formation, reflecting competition between GlcNAc-branching enzymes (GCNT and B3GNT families) and sialyltransferases. Several members of the ST3GAL family emerged as potent enhancers of Siglec-7, -9, and -10 binding (Figure 1B). In contrast, genes involved in fucosylation, core N-glycan branching, and CMP-sialic acid biosynthesis were not prominent hits in this CRISPRa setting, suggesting that these pathways are not limiting drivers of Siglec ligand expression in this model. Thus, glycome remodeling appears to result from a dynamic balance across intersecting biosynthetic routes rather than from a single master regulator.

Consistent with previous work,4,6 Siglec-7 ligand was predominantly associated with sialylated core-1 O-glycans, especially on CD43 and mucin-type proteins such as MUC22. Beyond ST3GAL1 and ST3GAL2, a notable discovery was the identification of GAL3ST4, a sulfotransferase that modifies core-1 O-glycans, as a top positive regulator. Conversely, GCNT1, which promotes core-2 O-glycan structures, and MUC1 reduced Siglec-7 binding when overexpressed. Functional validation in LN-229 glioma cells showed that GAL3ST4 knockout decreased Siglec-7 binding by approximately 80%, whereas re-expression restored binding. These findings highlight the strength of CRISPRa in uncovering the functional impact of genes with low expression, such as GAL3ST4, whose sulfation activity generates critical structural elements for Siglec-7 recognition in specific tissue contexts, particularly glioma. The data suggest competition between sulfation and sialylation at the 3-OH position of galactose in core-1 structures, leading to the identification of a “3-sulfo-6-sialyl core-1” configuration as a potential Siglec-7 ligand. The enrichment of GAL3ST4 expression in glioma further supports the idea that tissue-restricted sulfation programs may promote immune evasion in selected cancers.

Siglec-9 ligands exhibited greater structural plasticity, with contributions from both N- and O-glycans. Redundancy among glycosyltransferases implies that perturbation of a single gene may be insufficient to abolish ligand expression. While GlcNAc-branching enzymes, such as GCNT2 and B3GNT2/5, appeared as negative regulators, nearly all ST3GAL enzymes (except ST3GAL5) enhanced Siglec-9 binding upon overexpression as previously reported.7 CD44 and CSPG4 also emerged as strong hits, suggesting roles as potential carrier proteins or facilitators of ligand presentation.

Although Siglec-10 ligands have previously been associated with N-glycans, the CRISPRa data indicate a broader recognition of α2-3- and α2-6-linked N-glycans rather than dependence on a single carrier protein.8 Notably, CD24 was dispensable for Siglec-10 binding in K562 and THP-1 cells, which is in line with another recent study,9 calling into question CD24 as a ligand for Siglec-10. Accordingly, these results support a model in which Siglec-10 recognizes a diverse repertoire of N-linked sialoglycans.

As Siglec-targeted therapeutics progress toward clinical application, understanding tumor-specific glycosylation programs becomes increasingly important. The CRISPRa dataset provides a framework for developing predictive gene-expression signatures to identify patients most likely to benefit from Siglec-directed therapies. The identification of key carrier proteins (e.g., sulfated mucins) and enzymatic drivers (e.g., GAL3ST4 in glioma) opens avenues for biomarker development, patient stratification, and combination strategies designed to overcome pathway redundancy. The authors highlighted GAL3ST4 as particularly attractive owing to its restricted expression and ability to upregulate ligands of both Siglec-7 and Siglec-9.

The study emphasizes functional redundancy among sialyltransferases, substrate competition within glycosylation pathways, cell-type-specific enzyme hierarchies, and context-dependent compensation. Importantly, CRISPRa complements CRISPRi by revealing gain-of-function phenotypes that loss-of-function screens may overlook, especially for genes with low baseline expression. Several limitations are acknowledged. CRISPRa relies on artificial overexpression systems that may not fully recapitulate physiological enzyme stoichiometry or glycan structures. Moreover, gain-of-function strategies may be less informative when genes are already highly expressed.5 Expanding analyses to additional cellular models would capture the diversity of glycosylation programs.

Future work will need to address how these glycan alterations impact immune cell signaling within intact tumor microenvironments. Translating results to in vivo systems is nonetheless complicated by incomplete conservation of Siglec biology between humans and mice. Integrating structural glycomics, enzymology, and in vivo immune modeling will be essential to validate the functional significance of the identified pathways.

In summary, Daly et al.1 establish a comprehensive functional genomic framework to dissect cancer glycome remodeling. Leveraging genome-wide CRISPR activation screens, they reveal the genetic competition and redundancy underlying tumor-associated hypersialylation and Siglec ligand expression. Their findings reframe glycome remodeling as a dynamic, network-based process rather than a single-driver event, with important implications for future immunotherapy and precision oncology strategies.10
