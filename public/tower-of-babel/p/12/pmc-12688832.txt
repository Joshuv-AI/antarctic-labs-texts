Epidemiological, radiological, therapeutic, and evolutionary aspects of distal radius fractures in adults in South Kivu: a multicenter cross-sectional study
Authors: Mupenda Mwenibamba Rodrigue, Daniel Safari Nteranya, Bisimwa Wabene Christian, Nechi Nakashenyi Alexandre, Eben-Ezer Genda, Fabrice Kibukila, Mbilizi Kasilembo Didier, Alumeti Munyali Desiré, Muyisa K. Séverin, Tsongo Kibendelwa Zacharie, Uwonda Akinja, Tshimbila Kabangu Jean Marrie Vianney
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
Background:Distal radius fractures, common upper limb injuries, lack of multicenter data from sub-Saharan Africa. This study profiles their epidemiology and management in Bukavu, South Kivu, DR Congo.Methods:We conducted a retrospective cross-sectional analysis of 192 adults with distal radius fractures across 16 hospitals (2018–2023), reviewing medical records for epidemiological, clinical, therapeutic, and outcome data.Results:Mean age was 40.46 ± 16.16 years (range: 17–90), with male predominance (60%) and urban origin (82.3%). Traffic accidents caused 44.8% of fractures, primarily hyper-extension compression injuries (88.3%). Castaing type I (Pouteau-Colles fracture) prevailed (70.3%), treated mostly with intrafocal pinning (60.9%), with a mean hospital stay of 4.49 ± 4.37 days and fracture union achieved in an average of 47.56 ± 6.75 days. Complications were rare (e.g. five cases of malunions).Conclusion:This first multicenter DR Congo study links distal radius fractures to traffic trauma, suggesting stricter regulations could reduce incidence in resource-limited settings.

Introduction

Distal radius fractures are the predominant fracture type observed in emergency departments globally, comprising 17.5% of all fractures[1–3]. In the United States, from 2016 to 2019, these injuries accounted for 3.6 per 1000 emergency room-reported injuries and 10.8 per 1000 upper limb injuries[4]. Their incidence varies widely across regions, with European data reflecting an upward trajectory. In Germany, for instance, an estimated 100 000 distal radius fractures occurred annually by 2018[5], ranking them as the third most common fracture type – after femoral neck and pertrochanteric fractures – with 79 087 cases in 2019, or 106 per 100 000 individuals[6].

These fractures frequently result in suboptimal functional recovery. In England, only half of affected individuals report satisfactory outcomes 6 months post-injury, with nearly 30% facing chronic complications; lifetime risks are estimated at 1.9% for men and 16% for women from age 50 onward in the UK[7]. In Africa, comprehensive data on adult distal radius fractures are sparse, often limited to single-institution reports. A six-month prospective study in 2009 at CHU Ampelifiloha HJRA Antananarivo, Madagascar, identified 25 cases (6 men, 16 women), while a 2024 retrospective review by Dworkin et al[8] documented 1440 cases (888 men, 545 women) across four Malawian public hospitals.

The primary therapeutic goal is to restore pain-free function[9]. For patients with elevated functional expectations, achieving anatomical reduction and stable fixation is critical for favorable outcomes. Treatment decisions hinge on age and functional demands, though subjective interpretations of these needs often complicate clinical guidance[10]. Management spans nonoperative methods to surgical options, including intra- and extra-focal pin osteosynthesis, external fixation (with or without wrist bridging), and dorsal or palmar plate osteosynthesis with or without locking screws[10,11]. Arthroscopy enhances outcomes in articular fractures[12], and phosphocalcic cements outperform grafting for stabilizing extensive metaphyseal defects[11]. While diverse fixation techniques maintain reduction, each presents unique challenges, and no single approach is universally endorsed due to limited comparative evidence. Modern palmar plates have notably reduced secondary displacement[11,13]. Following 4–6 weeks of immobilization, a 6-week rehabilitation phase targets pain relief, swelling reduction, joint mobility restoration, and wrist strength enhancement[14].

In eastern Democratic Republic of Congo, the frequent presentation of distal radius fractures in hospital emergency departments has necessitated this investigation into the epidemiological, clinical, radiological, therapeutic, and evolutionary profiles of distal radius fractures among adults in the three Bukavu health zones, South Kivu. This study addresses a critical gap in regional data, aiming to inform the development of tailored interventions in a resource-constrained, conflict-affected setting.

Patients and methods

This cross-sectional study involved a retrospective analysis of 192 cases of distal radius fractures in adults, compiled from the surgical departments of hospitals and university medical centers within the three Bukavu health zones, South Kivu, eastern Democratic Republic of the Congo, spanning January 1, 2018, to December 31, 2023. Data were extracted from a standardized collection form utilizing medical records, consultation logs, operative report registries, and hospital databases across 16 facilities distributed among the three Bukavu health zones. Inclusion criteria encompassed patients aged 17 years or older diagnosed with a distal radius fracture, possessing a complete medical record, and having been admitted, treated, and followed up within the study period at one of these institutions. All the included patients have been followed for 3 months.

Fractures were classified using the Castaing system, as modified by Kapandji to ensure consistent radiological categorization. The Castaing classification divides distal radius fractures into two main groups based on whether they are caused by compression-extension (usually with dorsal displacement) or compression-flexion (usually with volar displacement). Thus, the cases evaluated in this paper were distributed as follows according to the Castaing system:

Extra-articular fracture without displacement (Type 0): 1

Extra-articular fracture with posterior tilt without comminution (Type 1): 135

Extra-articular fracture with posterior tilt and posterior comminution (Type 2): 1

Sagittal T-shaped fracture (Type 4): 3

Anterior tilt or Goyrand-Smith fracture (Type 8): 46

Sagittal T-shaped articular fracture + fracture of the lower end of the ulna (Type 10): 1

Malunion (fractures admitted between D28 and D30 after traditional treatment) (Type 11): 5

The primary aim of the Castaing classification is to objectively assess the radiological outcome of distal radius fractures by measuring specific parameters like frontal tilt, sagittal tilt, radio-ulnar joint line, and the degree of radio-carpal arthritis[15]. It assists physicians in assessing the stability and severity of fractures as well as the efficacy of various therapies by offering a numerical scale for these variables[15]. As previously done by Benabid et al[16], we employed this classification to describe the patients’ radiological profiles and fracture mechanisms. Data were entered into a pre-designed Excel spreadsheet (Microsoft Corporation, Redmond, WA, USA) and analyzed with R software (version 4.4.3 for Windows, R Foundation for Statistical Computing). Quantitative variables were reported as means (± standard deviation), while nominal variables were presented as frequencies. For contingency table analyses, Fisher’s exact test was employed in lieu of the Chi-square test when cell counts fell below 5, enabling assessment of variable interdependence with a significance threshold set at P < 0.05.

This study adheres to the Strengthening the Reporting of Observational Studies in Surgery guidelines, ensuring comprehensive and transparent methodological reporting[17].

Results

Epidemiology

The average age of patients in this study was 40.46 ± 16.16 years (range, 17–90 years). Males (116, 60%) outnumbered females (76, 40%), as shown in Table 1. Fractures were most prevalent among informal workers (87, 45.3%), followed by civil servants (35, 18.23%), students (15, 7.81%), and retailers (14, 7.29%) (Fig. 1 and Table 2). Urban dwellers accounted for 158 cases (82.29%). Among comorbidities, diabetes was observed in 6 patients (3.13%), hypertension in 4 (2.08%), and osteoporosis in 3 (1.56%), while those without prior medical history predominated (170, 88.54%). Clinical and radiological data

Hyperextension compression emerged as the predominant injury mechanism for distal radius fractures, occurring in 160 cases (88.33%). Road traffic accidents were the leading cause (86, 44.79%), followed by falls from height (61, 31.67%) and sports injuries (20, 10.42%). Fractures more frequently involved the left wrist (102, 53.13%) than the right (89, 46.35%). According to the Castaing classification (modified by Kapandji), type I fractures prevailed (135, 70.31%), with type VIII (46, 23.96%) and type XI (5, 2.60%) less frequent (Table 3). Therapeutic study and evolution

Intrafocal pinning was the predominant treatment modality (117, 60.94%), followed by plaster immobilization (51, 26.96%) and screwed plate osteosynthesis (21, 10.94%). Treatment selection showed a significant association with fracture type per the Castaing classification (modified by Kapandji) (Fisher’s exact test, P = 6.44 × 10−6). Hospitalization averaged 4.49 ± 4.37 days (range, 1–37 days), with 70 patients (36.5%) discharged after 3 days. In this series, complications included malunion (five cases), wrist stiffness (three cases), superficial surgical site infections (three cases), pin migration (two cases), and algodystrophy (two cases). The majority of patients (173) experienced no complications. The occurrence of complications showed no significant association with the treatment methods used at the facilities (Fisher’s exact test, P = 0.637), indicating they arose independently of the therapeutic approach.

Discussion

Our multicenter study of 192 distal radius fractures in adults across Bukavu, South Kivu (2018–2023), identified a distinct profile in this resource-limited setting: a relatively young cohort (mean age 40.46 ± 16.16 years), male predominance (60%), and a strong association with traffic accidents (44.8%) as the primary etiology, reflecting the region’s urban and socioeconomic dynamics. Radiologically, hyperextension compression injuries (88.3%) and Castaing type I fractures (70.3%) prevailed, with intrafocal pinning emerging as the dominant treatment (60.9%), significantly tied to fracture type (P = 6.44 × 10−6). Therapeutically, this approach yielded a mean consolidation time of 47.56 ± 6.75 days and a low complication rate (9.9%), independent of treatment modality (P = 0.637), underscoring its efficacy in a context with limited access to advanced fixation options. These findings provide a foundation for understanding distal radius fracture patterns and management in sub-Saharan Africa, highlighting both clinical success and public health implications.

Epidemiological data

Age

In our cohort, the mean age was 40.46 ± 16.16 years, ranging from 17 to 90 years, with a marked decline in frequency beyond age 60. These findings align closely with observations by numerous authors[8,18–20]. However, our reported incidence is lower than that in the series by Cognet et al, and Lemai[21,22], which primarily involved younger patients (<60 years per WHO criteria). Mahfoud (2006), Obert et al, Lemai, and Coudane et al have noted that distal radius fractures are increasingly prevalent among young, active individuals due to rising road traffic accidents, alongside sports, occupational injuries, or falls from height[11,22,23]. The predominance of younger adults in our study reflects their status as the most active socio-professional group in Africa and Asia, where over half the population is youthful, and thus more exposed to high-energy trauma[10,11,22,24].

Sex

Our study revealed a male predominance, with 116 (60%) men affected compared to 76 (40%) women. This is consistent with findings from Zyluk et al, Aggarwal et al, and Dworkin et al[8,18,25], who attribute higher male incidence to riskier occupations. Conversely, the series by Cognet et al, Nemmar et al, Ralahy et al, Najdi et al, Khadka et al, Bertrand et al, and Lemai[20–22,26–29] report a greater frequency in women, linked to osteoporosis and menopause. The male predominance likely reflects increased occupational and traffic-related risk exposures, aligning with high-kinetic trauma patterns[10,11,30].

Profession

Non-professionals (45.3%) topped the occupational distribution in our series, followed by civil servants (35, 18.23%), students (15, 7.81%), and shopkeepers (14, 7.29%). The prominence of young adults – representing the most active and populous socio-professional group – explains the higher proportion of students and civil servants, a pattern echoed in Dworkin et al’s[8] African series. This suggests that socioeconomic dynamics heighten trauma exposure, contrasting with sedentary profiles in Western contexts.

Location

Distinct from Dworkin et al[8], 158 (82.29%) of our patients hailed from urban areas (Table 1). This urban bias may reflect shorter trauma-to-consultation intervals and the proximity of referral centers, potentially underrepresenting rural fracture profiles.

Comorbidities

Diabetes affected 6 (3.13%) patients, hypertension 4 (2.08%), and osteoporosis 3 (1.56%), while 170 (88.54%) had no prior medical history. These comorbidities mirror those reported by Nemmar et al and Abdulbari et al[20,31]. The prevalence of diabetes in our setting, linked to bone fragility via insulinopenia (type I) or reduced bone mineral density (type II), alongside hypertension, may elevate fracture risk. Osteoporosis further contributes to skeletal vulnerability, as corroborated by Obert et al, Chabra et al, and Vanderlinden et al[10,11,30].

Clinical and radiological data

Mechanisms of injury

Hyperextension-compression dominated as the leading mechanism (160, 88.33%), consistent with Brallet, Nemmar et al, Aggarwal et al, and Anil et al[18–20,32]. This high-energy pattern aligns with the prevalence of road traffic accidents and the youthful demographic most susceptible to such trauma.

Etiology

Road traffic injuries accounted for 86 (44.79%) cases, followed by falls from height (61, 31.67%) and sports injuries (20, 10.42%). These findings resonate with Parupalli et al, Dworkin et al, Khadka et al, Nemmar et al, Ando et al, Lemai, and Rundgren et al[1,8,20,22,28,33,34]. The dominance of traffic-related etiologies reflects the urban, young, and professionally mobile population’s daily exposure to such risks[10,11,13,22,24,30].

Affected side

Left-sided fractures (102, 53.13%) slightly outnumbered right-sided ones (89, 46.35%). This distribution varies across studies, opposing Mabrouk et al, Parupalli et al[2019,31,34], but matching Khadka et al, Zyluk et al, Nemmar et al, and Lemai[20,22,25,28]. The affected side appears incidentally, as no consistent pattern dictates initial ground contact in falls.

Castaing classification

Per Kapandji’s modified Castaing classification, type I fractures predominated (135, 70.31%), followed by type VIII (46, 23.96%) and type XI (5, 2.60%). These align with Parupalli et al, Khadka et al, Mabrouk et al, Anil et al, Dworkin et al, Ando et al, and Ralahy[8,19,26,31,33–35]. Extra-articular fractures prevail across series despite varied classifications. Dumontier notes that classifications must describe lesions, guide treatment, and predict outcomes – yet none fully meet these criteria, tying them to specific eras and therapies[11]. Our study links hyperextension-compression to extra-articular predominance.

Therapeutic study

Therapeutic techniques

Surgical intervention was employed in 139 (89.06%) cases, with intrafocal pinning most common (117, 60.94%), followed by plaster immobilization (51, 26.96%) and bolted plate osteosynthesis (21, 10.94%). Treatment choice strongly correlated with Castaing fracture type (P < 0.05). Our results diverge from Dworkin et al[8] but align with Anil et al[19] and Ando et al[33]. The surgical preference reflects our young cohort’s high functional demands and fracture anatomy, consistent with Vanderlinden et al, Chhabra et al, Obert et al, Wolfe, and Dahmi et al[10,11,13,24,30].

Evolution

Length of hospital stay

The mean hospital stay was 4.49 ± 4.37 days (range: 1–37 days), with 70 patients discharged after three days. Stay duration varied significantly by treatment modality (P = 3.10−6), approximating Jacques Barsotti et al[36]. The predominance of intrafocal pinning with plaster supports this brief hospitalization, facilitating outpatient follow-up.

Consolidation period

Fracture consolidation averaged 47.56 ± 6.75 days (range: 30–60 days), with 107 (55.7%) cases healing by 45 days. Healing time depended significantly on the treatment method (P < 0.05), aligning with the typical 4–6-week window noted by Barosotti[36] and Wolfe[13]. This suggests optimal initial reduction and stabilization in our cohort, despite potential adverse prognostic factors[37].

Complications

Complications included malunion (five cases), wrist stiffness (three cases), superficial surgical site infections (three cases), pin migration (two cases), and algodystrophy (two cases), with 173 patients complication-free. Unlike earlier cases, complication rates here showed no statistical significance (P > 0.05, error probability 1.5%). While variable, our complication frequency is slightly lower than that reported by Nemmar et al, Dahmi et al, and Gourbier et al[20,24,38], reflecting effective management in this series.

Conclusion

This multicenter study of 192 distal radius fractures in Bukavu, South Kivu (2018–2023), delineates a distinct regional profile, with a young cohort (mean age 40.46 years) and traffic accidents as the primary etiology (44.79%). Predominantly managed with intrafocal pinning (60.94%), fractures consolidated effectively (mean 47.56 days) with minimal complications, affirming its utility in resource-limited settings. These findings highlight the intersection of orthopedic trauma and public health, suggesting that stricter traffic regulation enforcement could substantially reduce fracture incidence. Prospective studies are warranted to validate these insights and explore long-term functional outcomes.

Limitations

This retrospective study of 192 distal radius fracture cases across 16 Bukavu hospitals (2018–2023) is limited by reliance on potentially inconsistent medical records, excluding patients with incomplete data and biasing the sample toward urban cases (82.29%). The small cohort restricts statistical power for rare outcomes (e.g. osteoporosis, 1.56%), while the lack of a control group and unadjusted confounders (e.g. age, fracture severity) limits causal inferences. Treatment variability, resource constraints, and the absence of patient-reported outcomes further narrow the findings’ scope and generalizability, necessitating prospective studies for validation.
