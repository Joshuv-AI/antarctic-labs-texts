The Editor,

We sincerely thank Kwakernaak and Klimek for their thoughtful comments on our article, “Network Meta-analysis of the Efficacy of Different Music Therapy Interventions for Delirium in Adult Intensive Care Unit Patients.” We appreciate the opportunity to clarify several methodological and interpretative issues raised in their Letter to Editor.

First, the authors state that the transitivity assumption is not met. We agree that transitivity is central to the validity of any network meta-analysis, particularly in a clinically heterogeneous intensive care unit (ICU) population. However, we would like to clarify that transitivity was not overlooked in our study. As described in the Methods section, we assessed the plausibility of transitivity qualitatively by considering key potential effect modifiers, including patient age, ICU population or diagnosis, delirium assessment instruments and schedules, baseline sedation and ventilation status when reported, and intervention dose, frequency, and total course. We also explicitly acknowledged that some effect modifiers were incompletely reported, and therefore, indirect comparisons required cautious interpretation. Thus, our position was not that perfect exchangeability existed across all trials, but that the available randomized evidence could be synthesized with appropriate methodological caution.

Second, we agree that music-based interventions are heterogeneous. Music type, delivery mode, frequency, total intervention duration, and co-intervention components varied across the included trials. This was described in the Results section and summarized in the intervention characteristics table. For this reason, we categorized interventions into separate nodes, namely simple music (SM) therapy, traditional chinese medicine (TCM) five-element music, family voice stimulation (FS) combined with music, and combined music (CM) therapy, rather than pooling all music-related interventions into a single category. We also agree that future studies should adopt more standardized reporting frameworks, such as template for intervention description and replication (TIDieR)-based descriptions, to improve reproducibility and enable more refined analyses of intervention components.

Third, regarding the statement that study-level specification was lacking, we respectfully clarify that study-level intervention characteristics were reported in our article, including intervention type, control condition, outcome measures, assessment tools, and intervention dose. Nevertheless, we acknowledge that the reporting quality of the original trials was variable, and some intervention details were not fully available from the primary studies. This limitation was one reason why our conclusions regarding relative effectiveness were deliberately cautious.

Fourth, regarding the phrase “commonly used ICU music-based programs,” we agree that this wording may be interpreted too broadly if read as implying global clinical commonality. Our intention was to refer to the intervention categories commonly represented in the available randomized ICU evidence included in the review, rather than to suggest that all interventions are equally common internationally. In particular, we acknowledge that TCM five-element music is culturally specific and that most included studies were conducted in China. We also noted in our clinical applicability table that TCM five-element music is most relevant in settings where TCM is integrated into care and that non-Chinese evidence remains limited. Therefore, the generalizability of findings for TCM five-element music should be interpreted with appropriate cultural and healthcare-contextual caution.

Fifth, we agree that usual care (UC) was not a universal comparator. In our study, UC referred to the control condition as defined in each original randomized trial, such as routine ICU care, routine nursing care, or standard ICU treatment and nursing. We acknowledge that UC may vary across hospitals, countries, sedation practices, staffing models, family visitation policies, and delirium prevention protocols. This variability may contribute to heterogeneity and limit generalizability. However, this does not invalidate the direct randomized comparisons between each music-based intervention and its trial-specific UC condition; rather, it highlights the need for clearer reporting and standardization of UC in future trials.

Sixth, we agree that the evidence network was predominantly star-shaped, with UC as the common comparator and limited direct head-to-head comparisons between active interventions. This point was explicitly stated in the Results and Discussion sections of our article. Therefore, the distinction is important: Comparisons between each music-based intervention and UC were informed by direct randomized evidence, whereas comparisons among active interventions were mainly based on indirect evidence anchored on UC. Accordingly, the statement that all comparative estimates relied on indirect evidence is too broad; the limitation applies mainly to between-active-intervention contrasts. Because closed loops were largely absent, formal inconsistency testing was not feasible for most comparisons, and we explicitly reported this limitation.

Finally, regarding surface under the cumulative ranking curve (SUCRA) rankings, we agree that ranking probabilities should not be overinterpreted. This was also the interpretation presented in our article. Although CM therapy ranked highest, we emphasized that differences between active interventions were not statistically significant, that the CM node was informed by few and mostly small trials, and that the ranking should be considered hypothesis-generating rather than evidence of definitive superiority. We also stated that clinical selection among music-based programs should consider feasibility, patient preference, local resources, cultural acceptability, and ICU workflow rather than ranking alone.

In summary, we appreciate the authors' constructive comments and agree that heterogeneity, contextual variability, sparse head-to-head evidence, possible small-study effects, and risk of bias limit the certainty of comparative conclusions. These limitations were acknowledged in our original article and informed our cautious interpretation. Our principal conclusion is that music-based interventions may serve as promising adjunctive nonpharmacological approaches compared with UC, whereas reliable differentiation among specific music-based interventions requires adequately powered, multicenter, head-to-head randomized controlled trials (RCTs) with standardized intervention reporting, harmonized delirium assessment protocols, and clear descriptions of UC.
