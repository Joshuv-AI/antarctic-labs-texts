Endovascular treatment of posterior circulation aneurysms with flow diverters with hydrophilic polymer coating in patients receiving prasugrel single antiplatelet therapy: a multicenter case series presenting complication and occlusion rates
Authors: Ali Khanafer, Alexander Sirakov, Donald Lobsien, José E Cohen, Mohammad almohammad, Marie-Sophie Schüngel, Florian Hennersdorf, Thomas Borgmann, Zakarya Ali, Andrei Filioglo, Hansjörg Bäzner, Philipp von Gottberg, Kamran Hajiyev, Pablo Albiña-Palmarola, Stephan Felber, Michael Forsting, Stefan Schob, André Kemmling, Joachim Klisch, Stanimir Sirakov, Hans Henkes
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundThe present study reports a multicenter experience of using hydrophilic polymer-coated (HPC) flow diverters (FDs) with prasugrel single antiplatelet therapy (SAPT) to treat posterior circulation aneurysms (PCAs).MethodsA prospectively maintained database was retrospectively reviewed to identify all cases of intracranial PCA treated with HPC-coated FDs under SAPT (prasugrel). The clinical presentation and outcomes, periprocedural and postprocedural complications, and degree of occlusion at follow-up (FU) were evaluated.ResultsA total of 74 patients were treated (45.9% female). Ischemic complications were experienced by two patients (2.7%) and were dependent on the use of FDs. No cases of aneurysm rupture or hemorrhagic complications related to antiplatelet therapy or the FD treatment were recorded. The rate of complete occlusion was 77.9% in the early FU period (3–6 months) and 90.3% in the initial 12-month period.ConclusionIn this single-arm retrospective study, HPC-coated FDs with prasugrel SAPT were associated with high safety in the treatment of ruptured and unruptured PCA and high occlusion rates at early- and mid-term FU.Keywords: Aneurysm, Flow Diverter, Subarachnoid Hemorrhage

Introduction

Posterior circulation aneurysms (PCAs) are a particularly challenging subset of intracranial aneurysms with persistently elevated mortality and morbidity risks.1 2 Given the complex location of PCAs within the posterior fossa—and their anatomical proximity to the brainstem and diencephalon, brainstem perforators, and cranial nerves—microsurgical treatment poses a substantial challenge.3 Consequently, endovascular treatment (EVT) has been the prevailing approach for these aneurysms during the past three decades.4 5

The advent of the flow diversion technique has since led to widespread use for intracranial aneurysms. However, the use of flow diverters (FDs) in the posterior circulation (PC) continues to be a topic of considerable debate, largely because of the potential for ischemic complications and associated consequences, particularly in the brainstem.6 7

Several recent studies have reported the safety and effectiveness of the implantation of various FDs in the PC in patients receiving dual antiplatelet therapy (DAPT).6,8 However, whether these procedures might also be possible in patients receiving single antiplatelet therapy (SAPT) remains unclear.

The objective of this study was to present the findings of a multicenter investigation of the use of p48/64 MW (movable wire) hydrophilic polymer-coated (HPC) devices (Wallaby Phenox, Bochum, Germany) in conjunction with prasugrel SAPT for the management of intracranial PCAs.

Methods

Patient population

Multicenter retrospective data were collected from eight neurointerventional centers in Germany (Stuttgart, Erfurt, Marburg, Koblenz, Halle, Tübingen, Offenbach, Regensburg) and three international centers (Sofia, Jerusalem, Chisinau). The study included all patients who had ruptured or unruptured intracranial PCAs treated with either p48 MW HPC or p64 MW HPC implantation and were receiving prasugrel SAPT. Cases were included in the analysis even if the procedure was a retreatment with EVT, or other devices in addition to the FD device were used. All treatments with other antiplatelet agents, such as clopidogrel, ticagrelor, or acetylsalicylic acid (ASA), as well as patients with dolichoectatic basilar artery (BA) aneurysms, were excluded from this study.

We recorded demographics, aneurysm morphology and location, platelet function test results, FD type and size, intraprocedural and postprocedural complications, clinical outcomes, and angiographic follow-up (FU) data.

Endovascular treatment

All centers performed PCA procedures with a biplane angiography system in patients under general anesthesia. Intravenous (IV) heparin was administered immediately after groin puncture in cases with unruptured aneurysms (usually 3000–5000 IU unfractionated heparin IV). Arterial access was obtained with a 6–8 French (F) sheath, usually in the right groin, and a standard 6–8 F guiding catheter. The use of an intermediate catheter was at the discretion of the physicians at each center, depending on vertebral artery (VA) condition. All FDs were deployed over a 0.021 inch microcatheter. FD diameter and length were selected according to two- or three-dimensional measurements of the parent artery diameter.

Antiplatelet medication

In unruptured aneurysms, patients received SAPT with either 1×10 mg prasugrel orally (PO) daily for a minimum of 5 days before treatment or a loading dose of 30–60 mg prasugrel 24–48 hours before the procedure. A multiplate analyzer (Roche Diagnostics, Mannheim, Germany) and VerifyNow (Accriva, San Diego, CA) were used to assess the sufficiency of platelet inhibition on the day of the intervention and at regular intervals after FD implantation. If the responses to the SAPT regimens were too low or excessive, the doses were modified to twice or half the standard dose, respectively. The target values included an area under the curve (AUC) <30 in the ADP Multiplate test and P2Y12 reaction units (PRUs) <100 in the VerifyNow test. In cases with a normal response, a dosage of 10 mg prasugrel PO was administered for a period of 6 months, after which the regimen was permanently replaced with a dosage of 100 mg ASA PO once per day. For the long-term treatment, all patients were recommended SAPT with 100 mg ASA PO daily lifelong.

In patients with ruptured aneurysms, a loading dose of 30–60 mg prasugrel was administered orally via a gastric tube 3 hours before implantation. The efficacy of the antiplatelet regimen was tested 2–3 hours later. After effective antiplatelet therapy was confirmed, patients were immediately taken to the angiography suite for FD implantation. In the remaining patients, a body weight-adjusted bolus of IV eptifibatide (Integrilin; GlaxoSmithKline, Munich, Germany), cangrelor (Kengrexal; Chiesi Farmaceutici, Parma, Italy), or tirofiban (Aggrastat; Carrevio, Vancouver, Canada) was administered before flow diversion, and a loading dose of 30–60 mg was administered after implantation with an overlapping, body weight-matched continuous IV infusion of eptifibatide or cangrelor.

The daily post-procedural dose of prasugrel ranged from 1×10 mg to 2×20 mg PO in cases with ruptured aneurysms. The dosage was optimized according to multiplate analyzer and VerifyNow platelet aggregation inhibition results.

Follow-up

Patients underwent MRI with T2 and diffusion-weighted imaging (DWI) within 72 hours after treatment of unruptured aneurysms, depending on the strategies and capabilities of each treatment center. The number of lesions was documented as part of the FU. In view of the challenging clinical and management circumstances encountered in patients with ruptured aneurysms, who are frequently ventilated, no post-procedural MRI was performed.

Planned angiographic examinations were conducted at early (3–6 months), mid-term (9–18 months), and long-term (>19 months) FU. Aneurysm occlusion was assessed with the O’Kelly-Marotta (OKM) scale, which uses the following definitions: A (total filling), B (subtotal filling), C (entry remnant), and D (no filling).9 Neurological examinations were conducted during the periprocedural period (within 24 hours after the procedure); at discharge; and during early, mid-term, and long-term FU. Outcomes were assessed with the modified Rankin Scale (mRS).10

Results

Patient demographics and aneurysm characteristics

A total of 74 patients with PCAs (mean age 58.9 years, 45.9% women) were treated with one or more p48MW/p64MW HPC stents between January 2018 and September 2024. Of the 74 aneurysms treated, 65 (87.8%) were classified as small (<10 mm) and nine (12.1%) were categorized as large (10–25 mm). The mean maximum aneurysm diameter was 5.3 mm.

The aneurysm locations were as follows: BA tip (n=14, 1.9%), BA trunk (n=19, 25.7%), posterior cerebral artery (n=6, 8.1 %), posterior inferior cerebral artery (PICA) (n=9, 12.2%), superior cerebral artery (SCA) (n=4, 5.4%), and VA V4 intradural (n=22, 29.7%).

In 69 aneurysms (93.3%), the FD was implanted in the initial procedure, whereas the remaining five aneurysms (6.7%) had previously been treated with the following methods: coiling (n=4) and coils and pCONus (n=1). In addition, coils were used as supplementary devices in six procedures, accounting for 8.1% of the total.

The mean diameter of the distal landing zone was 2.6 mm (range 1.1–3.5 mm), and that of the proximal landing zone was 3.2 mm (range 1.4–3.7 mm).

In 66 cases (89.2%), a single FD was implanted. In seven cases (9.5%), two FDs were implanted. In one case (1.3%), three FDs were implanted. Implantation of the p64 HPC was performed in 55 aneurysms (74.3%), whereas p48 HPC implantation was conducted in 18 aneurysms (24.3%). In one aneurysm, a combination of both FDs was used (figure 1).

An aneurysm at the base of a fenestration in the proximal part of the basilar artery (BA) was treated with a flow diverter (FD) under prasugrel as a single antiplatelet therapy. Digital subtraction angiography (DSA) with contrast injection into the right vertebral artery (VA) confirmed the BA fenestration aneurysm (A). Implantation of two FDs in both fenestration segments’ FDs (FD 1: p64 MW HPC 3/15; FD 2: p48 MW HPC 2/12) (B, C). DSA follow-up at 6 months showed complete aneurysm occlusion and a separation of the fenestration segments (D). HPC, hydrophilic polymer-coated; MW, movable wire.

Technical difficulties

In the treatment of one of the aneurysms, a significant shortening of the implanted FD was observed after deployment, thus resulting in inadequate aneurysm coverage and requiring implantation of a second device. The deployment of all FDs was successful, and neither twisting nor insufficient wall adaptation was documented. Consequently, no mechanical re-adaptation of the FD was required post-implantation.

Peri- and post-procedural complications associated with flow diversion treatment or SAPT

All treated aneurysms demonstrated no rupture or intracerebral hemorrhage, in both ruptured and unruptured cases, during the clinical stay or after discharge.

One patient was treated for a ruptured blister aneurysm of the BA with subarachnoid hemorrhage (SAH) using an FD. The device was implanted after detection of the aneurysm and administration of a loading dose of 60 mg prasugrel. The effectiveness of prasugrel was successfully confirmed with VerifyNow and multiplate analyzer. Although the EVT was uncomplicated, the patient exhibited motor aphasia and right hemiparesis after extubation after a prolonged ventilation period, which were precipitated by severe pneumonia and impaired lung function. Subsequent MRI and digital subtraction angiography (DSA) revealed extensive ischemia in the pons, without any evidence of in-stent stenosis, thrombosis, or cerebral vasospasm. Repeated MP and VN indicated hyporesponsiveness to SAPT (10 mg prasugrel daily). The patient’s medication was therefore switched to DAPT to avoid further ischemia. The patient was discharged with an mRS score of 4.

In the course of the treatment of an SCA aneurysm, an FD (p64 HPC) was implanted in the basilar artery. There were no documented complications periprocedurally. However, the patient returned within a week with mild right leg paresis and dysarthria (mRS 2). The MRI and DSA FUs revealed a new fish mouth deformation in the proximal part of the FD and pons ischemia. This FD deformation was hemodynamically irrelevant; therefore, the patient was treated conservatively with a modification of medication to DAPT. The patient subsequently improved and was discharged again with persistent low-grade dysarthria (mRS 1). No deterioration in the FD position or width had been documented in the predischarge, mid- and long-term FUs. Consequently, no mechanical treatment was performed, and the medication was changed to SAPT after 1 year.

Angiographic follow-up

Regular FU examinations were not possible in all cases, because of variations across centers, the patients’ clinical condition after discharge, and their rehabilitation duration, particularly after treatment for ruptured aneurysms. Two in-hospital deaths were documented during the clinical period, and two additional deaths occurred in the first 6 months after discharge but were not associated with flow diversion treatment. Angiographic FU was not possible for these four SAH patients.

It is notable that seven patients declined to undergo an FU DSA examination. One of the cases under consideration was that of a patient suffering from severe ischemic complications resulting from FD treatment of a ruptured BA aneurysm following SAH. The patient continued to exhibit severe neurological deficits after being discharged.

Consequently, FU angiographic examination was performed in 63 of the 70 surviving patients (90%).

Early FU (FU1, 3–6 months) was performed at a median of 3 months post-procedure in 59/63 patients (93.6%).

Complete aneurysm occlusion (OKM D) was observed in 46 of 59 (77.9%), 44 of 50 (88%), and 24 of 26 (92.3%) cases in early, mid-term, and long-term FU, respectively. The complete aneurysm occlusion rate in the initial 12 months was 90.3% (56/62).

At the latest possible FU examination (mean 18.4 months; median 12 months), DSA indicated OKM D in 61 of 63 patients (96.8%) and OKM C in 1/63 (1.6%) patients.

The occlusions did not lead to ischemic complications in the affected posterior flow territory, which was supplied by the posterior communicating artery of the ipsilateral internal carotid artery (figure 2). Pre-discharge MRI

Among the 37 patients treated for non-ruptured aneurysms, 33 (89.2%) received pre-discharge MRI scans, of whom 28 (84.8%) showed no DWI lesions. In contrast, four (12.1%) of the MRIs showed five or fewer DWI lesions, whereas one (3.1%) showed more than five DWI lesions. No instances of territorial infarcts, SAHs, or intracerebral hemorrhages were detected. None of the patients with treated ruptured aneurysms underwent a planned post-procedural MRI scan.

Clinical follow-up

In the total number of cases, which amounted to 74, FD treatment was the cause of clinical deterioration in only two patients (2.7%).

In the cohort of 37 patients (50%) treated for ruptured aneurysms following SAH, two patients (2.7%) died during the clinical period. One patient died from progressive generalized cerebral edema following SAH, and the other from massive SAH and severe pneumonia. In both cases, severe brain damage due to SAH was the primary cause of death, regardless of FD treatment. In this cohort, 21.6% (8/37) of patients were discharged with poor outcome (mRS 3–5). Of these, six patients were already admitted with severe clinical conditions (mRS 3–5), and two patients deteriorated during their clinical stay. One patient deteriorated due to severe cerebral vasospasm, and another showed clinical deterioration from mRS 1 to mRS 4 due to an FD-related ischemic complication following treatment of ruptured BA aneurysms. Two patients from this cohort died within 6 months of being discharged, regardless of FD treatment, and two others remained in poor clinical condition. The four remaining patients demonstrated improvement over the initial 6-month period and exhibited favorable clinical conditions (mRS 0–2) at the first FU. Twenty-nine patients (78.4%) of the 37 patients treated for ruptured aneurysms were discharged with mRS between 0 and 2.

In the cohort of 37 patients (50%) treated for non-ruptured aneurysms, only one case documented a deterioration in mRS from 0 to 2 in early FU and then to 1 in long-term FU. This deterioration was attributable to a post-procedural ischemic complication of the FD treatment. It is evident from the available documentation that no deterioration in clinical condition was observed in this group between the time of admission and discharge.

Discussion

The development of flow diversion has resulted in a substantial expansion in the use of FDs for the management of intracranial aneurysms in the past decade.11 One of the most notable advancements in these devices is the enhancement of their coatings, which has markedly decreased the thrombogenicity complications associated with these stents. HPC, a hydrophilic multilayer glycan-based polymer coating that can be applied to nitinol surfaces, is currently the only coating indicated for use with SAPT. Recent studies have demonstrated the efficacy of HPC-coated FD in both unruptured and ruptured aneurysms. Hellstern et al12 have reported high complete occlusion rates of 83.8% at mid-term FU (9 months) in the treatment of 132 unruptured intracranial aneurysms in the anterior circulation with p64 MW HPC FDs in patients receiving prasugrel SAPT. Similarly, HPC-coated FDs (p64 and p48 HPC) have demonstrated a high occlusion rate in a recently published multicenter study on the treatment of acutely ruptured intracranial aneurysms. Among the treated ruptured aneurysms, 90.2% exhibited complete occlusion at mid-term FU.13

In our multicenter experience, we observed a complete PCA occlusion rate (OKM D) of 77.9% (46/59) in the early FU period (3–6 months). Additionally, 11.9% (7/59) of the aneurysms exhibited only a residual neck (OKM C). The complete aneurysm occlusion rate (OKM D) in the initial 12 months was 90.3% (56/62), and 4.8% (3/62) of the aneurysms showed OKM C. Our findings in this series suggested that treatment of PCAs with HPC-coated FDs with prasugrel SAPT improved occlusion rates. Bender et al have reported complete occlusion rates of 68% at 6 months and 78% at 12 months in 59 cases of PCAs treated with the Pipeline Embolization Device (PED, ev3, Medtronic Inc, Dublin, Ireland).14 Kulcsár et al, in 12 patients with BA aneurysms treated with the Silk FD, have reported a complete occlusion rate of 58% in short-term FU among patients who could be followed up.15 Additionally, a study by Wakhloo et al has reported a complete occlusion rate of 72.7% 6 months after PCA treatment with the Surpass FD.16 In the treatment of PCA with uncoated p64 FDs, 56% of lesions (28/50) exhibited an OKM score of C or D after 3–6 months, whereas 75.6% of lesions demonstrated this outcome after 9–12 months.8 The proportion of complete closure observed in these studies was lower than that in our study.

Flow diversion treatment of PCA remains a topic of ongoing debate, because previous studies have reported elevated rates of morbidity and mortality due to ischemic complications.7 17 In a study examining the efficacy and safety of the PED in PC, Bender et al14 have reported severe ischemic stroke complications in 8% of total cases. In a separate series, Philips et al have documented three cases (14%) of perforator infarcts after PED treatment of PCA in patients receiving DAPT.18 Kulcsár et al15 have reported that the use of the SILK FD in PC resulted in ischemic complications in 25% of cases. In a study of 34 aneurysms examining the efficacy of the Derivo2Heal FD (Acandis), three PCAs were treated. One patient had thalamic infarctions after treatment of a V4 aneurysm, and a second patient developed partial in-stent thrombosis with ischemia in posterior cerebral artery and BA perforator territories. Consequently, both patients were discharged with an mRS score of 2.19 The use of uncoated p64 FDs for saccular PCA treatment resulted in ischemic complications in 4/54 patients (7.4%), and only 1/54 patients (1.9%) exhibited permanent neurologic deterioration, with an mRS score of 2.14 In those studies, FD implantation was performed in patients receiving DAPT. In comparison, in our multicenter experience, ischemic complications were reported in two patients (2.7%), and the incidence of ischemic complications in PCA treatments with HPC-coated FDs was lower than reported in those studies, despite our use of SAPT medication.

Although FD treatment with only prasugrel as SAPT has been associated with concerns regarding thromboembolic complications, recent studies have demonstrated the safety and low ischemic complications, as retrospectively observed in both ruptured and unruptured aneurysms in the anterior circulation in patients treated with HPC-coated FDs and receiving SAPT with 5.3% and 4.7%, respectively.12 Beyond the antithrombogenic coating, FD porosity is another factor contributing to ischemic complications. The p64 classic, p64 MW, and p64 MW HPCs consist of 64 nitinol wires with a porosity of 60%. However, this percentage can decrease to 51% depending on the diameter of the vessel and the size, position, and adaptation of the device.20 Accordingly, decisions regarding FD diameter for all operators were based on a slight oversizing in comparison to the parent vessel, to avoid shortening of the device and a corresponding decrease in porosity. Despite the FD oversizing, four patients exhibited a side vessel occlusion in the FU examination. However, none of those patients demonstrated ischemic complications or neurological deficits. The newly developed software and algorithm, specifically designed to increase the accuracy of the device selection process, can facilitate FD treatment with simpler, safer device implantation.21 22

In our study, 89.2% of patients with unruptured PCAs underwent a post-procedural MRI examination within 48 hours after treatment. Clinically silent microemboli were observed in 15.2% of the controls in the DWI sequences. In contrast, a meta-analysis by Diestro et al reported a 67% incidence of silent microemboli after FD implantation.23 The low number of these lesions in our studies is likely to be attributable to the antithrombogenic HPC coating, as observed in similar recent studies using Pipeline Shield, which has a phosphorylcholine polymer coating. In a prior study, only 18.2% of patients exhibited post-procedural silent microemboli on MRI.24 Silent microinfarctions after EVT, particularly after FD implantation, are a significant risk factor, particularly in conjunction with the use of antiplatelet inhibitors.25,27 In our study, no fatal hemorrhagic complications were documented, a finding consistent with the use of prasugrel alone and the avoidance of DAPT.

Several recent studies have demonstrated the safety of prasugrel SAPT in the context of FD treatments. Those studies have investigated the use of prasugrel in a range of vessels, including those with wide diameters in the proximal region, as well as distal vessels and even in the setting of ruptured aneurysms.12 13 28 29

Prasugrel is a third-generation oral thienopyridine that irreversibly binds to the platelet P2Y12 receptor, thereby inhibiting ADP-induced platelet aggregation. Prasugrel is a prodrug that is converted by liver enzymes into its active metabolite and binds irreversibly to P2Y12 receptors. The influence of genetic polymorphisms on the metabolism of prasugrel is minimal, thus resulting in no significant resistance to the drug. It has been demonstrated that platelet inhibition in response to prasugrel is more potent than that elicited by ASA. In addition, the response to prasugrel persists for a greater duration than that elicited by ticagrelor. This is presumably due to the fact that these agents possess different mechanisms of action, with irreversible inhibition of the P2Y12 receptor by prasugrel and reversible direct antagonism by ticagrelor.30

Consequently, prasugrel was the SAPT medication of choice at centers participating in this study. However, patients’ individual responses must always be considered as an additional factor to avoid hypo- or hyper-responses with possible complications. Therefore, platelet function tests remain advisable, despite the high efficacy of prasugrel.

Limitations

The principal limitation of this study is its retrospective, single-arm design. In addition, the lack of a control group and the lack of independent assessment of both clinical and imaging data are relevant limitations. The findings are limited to the two FD devices (p64/p48 MW HPC) implanted and cannot be extrapolated to other FDs or FD-associated procedures. A comparison of the safety and occlusion rate of HPC-coated FDs under prasugrel as SAPT versus the results obtained using the uncoated FD variant of these devices implanted under DAPT is required.

Conclusion

In our single-arm retrospective multicenter study, the use of p64/p48 MW HPC FDs with prasugrel SAPT for PCA was associated in our collective study with safety from rupture of the aneurysm or hemorrhagic and ischemic complications, such as high occlusion rates at early, medium- and long-term FU.
