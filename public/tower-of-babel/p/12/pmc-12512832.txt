Cerebellar transcranial direct current stimulation combined with virtual reality training for the balance of older adults: study protocol for a randomized controlled trial
Authors: Mengjie Zhang, Ting He, Yidi Xu, Chenrui Ma, Xindi Guo, Yuning Shen, Wenxin Niu
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundFalls are a major public safety issue, and improving the posture control ability of elderly people is highly important for preventing falls. Transcranial direct current stimulation (tDCS) and virtual reality-based balance training (VRBT) have been proven to be effective in improving postural stability and balance ability. The combination of these two methods may achieve better therapeutic effects. At present, there is a lack of research on the effects of tDCS combined with VRBT on postural stability and fall prevention in elderly individuals.MethodsThis 4-week single-blind, parallel-group randomized controlled trial will allocate 64 participants into the experimental or control group at a 1:1 ratio. The experimental group will undergo cerebellar tDCS combined with VRBT, whereas the control group will receive sham cerebellar tDCS paired with conventional exercise guidance. The outcome measures will be single leg stance with eyes closed, timed up and go, activities-specific balance confidence scale, modified falls efficacy scale, center of pressure measurements, and functional near-infrared spectroscopy. Assessments will be conducted at 0, 4, 8 and 12 weeks.DiscussionStudies examining the effects of cerebellar tDCS combined with VRBT on postural stability and fall risk among older adults are lacking. This study aims to comprehensively assess the effects of cerebellar tDCS combined with VRBT on postural stability and balance function through clinical scales and objective instrumental assessments. The findings of this study are anticipated to enhance existing intervention strategies for fall prevention and inspire researchers to develop innovative treatment methods for older adults.Trial registrationChinese Clinical Trial Registry, ChiCTR2300078998, Registered on 22 December 2023.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12906-025-05114-3.Keywords: Transcranial direct current stimulation, Virtual reality, Postural stability, Fall prevention, Balance, Functional near-infrared spectroscopy

Introduction

Falls represent the leading cause of accidental injury-related fatalities among those aged 65 and older in China [1]. Falls are a main cause of injury, impairment, and mortality in older persons, significantly diminishing their quality of life and imposing a substantial economic burden on families and society [2, 3]. As individuals age, postural control deteriorates, accompanied by balance impairments and gait abnormalities, which can jeopardize the independence of older adults and increase their risk of falls and injuries [4]. Enhancing postural stability is essential for effective fall prevention [5].

The cerebellum functions as a vital neuronal region for the regulation of postural control. This crucial brain region supports motor and cognitive activities while maintaining strong links with the motor cortex and prefrontal cortex [6, 7]. The cerebellum is essential for maintaining posture and balance, as it integrates and interprets information from the vestibular, somatosensory, and visual-auditory systems. Moreover, the vermis of the cerebellum is crucial for the modulation of axial muscles, which are vital for balance and postural stability. Age-related impairments in balance, postural stability, and motor performance may correlate with changes in the structural integrity of cerebellar white matter and a decrease in the volume of the cerebellar vermis [8, 9]. Moreover, aging is correlated with a reduction in functional connectivity within brain neuronal networks [10]. Functional connectivity denotes the correlation among the neurophysiological activities of different cerebral cortex areas. Thus, employing ways to proficiently modulate the neuronal excitability of the cerebellum and its related networks may improve motor performance and postural stability in elderly individuals, consequently reducing the risk of falls.

Transcranial direct current stimulation (tDCS) is a noninvasive neuromodulation technique that employs a constant, low-intensity direct current to modulate cortical neuronal activity. The underlying mechanism by which tDCS exerts its neuromodulatory effects involves the regulation of neuronal excitability and synaptic plasticity [11]. Typically, the anode electrode facilitates neuronal excitation in the cerebral cortex, while the cathode electrode inhibits neuronal excitability [12]. Furthermore, research has indicated that long-term application of tDCS can induce structural plasticity changes, particularly through the enhancement and maintenance of dendritic structures [13]. Consequently, tDCS holds promise for positively influencing cerebellar structural changes. Its capacity to directly target the central nervous system and modulate cortical excitability and neuroplasticity has garnered increasing attention, leading to its initial applications in enhancing postural stability and balance performance [14, 15].

In addition to enhancing postural control among the elderly through neuromodulation technology, the latest clinical practice guidelines strongly advocate for balance exercises to improve balance capabilities and prevent falls, with an evidence level of A [16]. Furthermore, the guideline indicate that exercise, when combined with nonexercise interventions, can significantly enhance the physical function of older adults.

More researchers have focused on balance training incorporating virtual reality (VR) technology to bolster postural stability and mitigate fall risk [17, 18]. VR technology uses computers and other devices to create immersive virtual environments, enabling older adults to engage in real-time interactive training within these simulated settings. Within a VR context, elderly participants can interact naturally with their surroundings, thereby stimulating interest and motivation for rehabilitation, enhancing subjective engagement, and improving overall rehabilitation efficacy. Compared with traditional exercise training, VR games combined with physical activity can more effectively reduce fall risk among older adults; Additionally, these VR-based exercises have been shown to improve static balance and dynamic balance more effectively than conventional daily activities [19, 20].

Recent studies have demonstrated that tDCS can increase motor gains during motor training by promoting cortical excitability in motor areas, thereby facilitating motor learning and neural plasticity [20]. The integration of tDCS with VR training represents a novel approach that has been shown to improve upper limb function in stroke patients and balance function in children with cerebral palsy [21–23]. Two systematic reviews have also indicated that the combination of tDCS and VR yields positive health outcomes for both healthy individuals and patients [23, 24]. However, to date, no research has reported the application of tDCS combined with VRBT to enhance postural stability in elderly individuals.

Therefore, this study will design a randomized controlled trial to investigate the effects of cerebellar tDCS in conjunction with VRBT on postural stability and fall prevention among the elderly population. The hypothesis is that this scheme can significantly improve postural stability and reduce the risk of falls in elderly individuals.

Methods

The study protocol adheres to the Standard Protocol Items: Recommendations for Interventional Trials (SPIRIT) guidelines (Table S1). A summary of the study schedule, which includes participant recruitment, assessments, and intervention, is presented in Fig. 1.

Study design and setting

This study is a single-center, single-blind randomized controlled trial. Figure 2 illustrates the flow diagram of the study design. Participants will be recruited from Shanghai Yangzhi Rehabilitation Hospital and the surrounding communities using informational posters. Eligible individuals will be randomly assigned to either the experimental group or the control group at a 1:1 ratio. The experimental group will undergo real cerebellar tDCS in conjunction with VRBT, whereas the control group will receive sham cerebellar tDCS paired with conventional exercise guidance. The participants will undergo a four-week intervention, with outcomes assessed prior to the intervention, immediately following its completion and at four and eight weeks postintervention. The study is anticipated to commence in January 2025 and conclude in December 2025.

Flow diagram of the study design

Inclusion and exclusion criteria

Participants will be included in the study if they meet the following criteria: (1) aged 60 years or older; (2) capable of standing independently for a minimum of 30 s without support; and (3) free from a history of neurological or musculoskeletal disorders that result in disability. Conversely, individuals will be excluded from participation if they present any of the following conditions: (1) severe visual, auditory, or sensory impairments; (2) any central nervous system disorders, vestibular dysfunction, or episodes of dizziness; (3) a history of brain surgery or the presence of a brain tumor; (4) peripheral nervous system lesions or severe postural disorders; (5) intracranial metal implants; (6) mental health disorders or cognitive impairments; (7) the use of medications for neurological conditions or psychotropic drugs; or (8) musculoskeletal disorders, such as rheumatoid arthritis, that significantly limit the range of motion in the lower limbs.

Sample size estimation

The required sample size was calculated via the G*Power of 3.1.9.4, employing a two-factor repeated measures analysis of variance. Previous studies have indicated that anodal tDCS enhances the dynamic balance abilities of elderly individuals, with an effect size of f = 0.3. We established a significance level of α = 0.05 and a desired statistical power of 1-β = 0.8. The analysis involved four repeated measurements, with a correlation coefficient of repeated measures set at 0 and a sphericity test correction factor of 0.5. The total sample size calculated was 52 participants. Taking into account a projected 20% attrition rate and maintaining a 1:1 ratio between the experimental and control groups, a minimum of 32 participants is required in each group, resulting in a total sample size of 64 individuals.

Randomization and allocation

The randomization sequence will be generated by a neutral researcher, who is not involved in subject recruitment, experimental assessment, intervention, or data analysis, via the website Randomization.com (http://www.randomization.com). To maintain allocation concealment, the allocation information will be securely placed in an opaque envelope that is sequentially numbered. Upon enrollment of a subject, the neutral researcher will sequentially draw the envelope and communicate the allocation information to the experimenter.

Blinding

The assessor-blinded approach will be used in this study. All outcome measures for the participants will be evaluated by an assessor who is unaware of the participants' group assignments.

Intervention

Cerebellar tDCS

The subjects will be randomly assigned to receive either cerebellar tDCS or a sham tDCS regimen. The stimulation intensity is set at 2 mA, with each session lasting 20 min, and administered three times a week over four consecutive weeks, resulting in a total of 12 sessions. Prior to the intervention, the demographic information of the subjects will be collected, and the experimental procedures, along with safety precautions, will be thoroughly explained to them. tDCS is delivered to the cerebellum using a battery-operated, high-definition constant-current stimulator (Soterix Medical, USA) through 3.14cm2 Ag/AgCl gel electrodes. The anode is positioned 10% below Oz, following the 10–20 international EEG electrode placement system, while the return electrodes are placed at Oz, O2, P8, and PO8. The current gradually increases to 2 mA over a 30-s baseline phase, remains at this level throughout the stimulation, and then automatically decreases to 0 mA within 30 s at the conclusion of the stimulation. The electrode configuration for the sham stimulation group mirrors that of the experimental group. However, in the sham group, the current is activated only for 30 s at both the beginning and end of the session, with no current delivered during the stimulation phase, thereby providing a placebo effect for the control group. Throughout the stimulation process, the procedure will be halted immediately if any subject reports discomfort associated with the stimulation.

VRBT

The experimental group will participate in VRBT utilizing the Doctor Kinetic gaming system (iMove 3.0-Standard, DIH Technologies Co., USA), which comprises a Kinect sensor and a 48-inch monitor. Balance training tasks, including one-legged stand balance, bipedal stand balance, and dynamic balance will be tailored to the individual needs of each subject. A therapist will be present to monitor the subjects' safety and provide verbal cues as necessary. Throughout the training sessions, participants in the experimental group will receive immediate feedback via the computer screen and auditory prompts, enabling them to adjust their performance in subsequent balance training exercises. Concurrently, the subjects will undergo cerebellar tDCS, with each balance training session lasting 20 min, three times per week for four weeks.

Conventional exercise guidance

The exercise guidance encompasses traditional balance training techniques, which include forward and backward center transfers while standing, full-foot straight standing, and single-foot standing exercises. The design of the balance tasks will adhere to several key principles: a gradual progression from stable to unstable positions, a systematic reduction in the body's support base, an elevation of the body's center of gravity, a transition from open to closed eye conditions, and a shift from static to dynamic balance activities.

Outcomes

Single Leg Stance with eyes closed (SLS)

The SLS test is a widely recognized measure for assessing static postural stability in older adults [25]. During the assessment, participants close their eyes, place their hands on their hips, and stand on their dominant leg while bending the knee of their non-weight-bearing leg, ensuring that the foot does not touch the ground or the supporting leg. The duration for which the posture is maintained is recorded in seconds, and the average time is calculated from three trials. The SLS test has strong reliability and validity among older adults and serves as a predictive indicator for fall risk [26, 27].

Timed Up and Go (TUG)

The TUG is a rapid and quantitative assessment tool for evaluating balance and functional mobility in older adults [28]. The participants are instructed to rise from a chair with armrests, walk forward at a normal pace, proceed beyond a 3-m line, turn around, return to the chair, and sit down. The total time taken to complete this sequence is recorded in seconds. The TUG is recommended as a screening tool to identify fall risk among older adults [29]. A completion time exceeding 12 s is indicative of an increased risk of falls, and the TUG can also serve to predict the extent of functional impairment in daily living activities among this population [28].

Center of Pressure (COP) measurement

A Kistler force plate (type 9260AA6, Kistler Instruments Inc, Switzerland) will be used to sample the COP data while participants performing the balance task, including COP displacement and sway velocity in the anteroposterior (AP), and mediolateral (ML) directions, as well as the COP sway area. Stabilometry is a widely used method to evaluate balance by quantifying the displacement of the COP over a force platform, with high sensitivity and accuracy [30, 31].

Activities-specific Balance Confidence scale (ABC)

The ABC scale is a subjective measure used to assess an individual's confidence in maintaining balance during various dynamic activities [32]. This scale comprises 16 items, each of which participants self-rate on a scale ranging from 0 to 100%. A score of 0% indicates no confidence, while a score of 100% signifies complete confidence. An average score below 67% suggests an increased risk of falling [33, 34].

Modified Falls Efficacy Scale (MFES)

The MFES is a tool specifically designed to assess the self-efficacy of older adults concerning fall prevention in daily activities [35]. Comprising 10 items, this scale employs a scoring system from 0 to 10, where higher scores reflect increased confidence in preventing falls. It is primarily applicable to community-dwelling older adults as well as those residing in health service facilities, particularly individuals with reduced mobility and balance. The scale has demonstrated strong reliability and validity, establishing it as an effective instrument for evaluating fall-related self-efficacy [36].

Functional near-infrared spectroscopy data collection

We will employ a functional near-infrared spectroscopy (fNIRS) system (NirScan, Danyang Huichuang Medical Equipment Co., Ltd., China) to record cortical activity at wavelengths of 740 nm and 850 nm, with a sampling rate of 10 Hz. This system is equipped with 24 emitters and 24 detectors, with each optode spaced 3 cm apart, resulting in a total of 65 measurement channels. Data collection will utilize a block-designed standing balance protocol, encompassing two types of balance tasks: bilateral standing and unilateral standing. Simultaneously, COP data will be collected alongside fNIRS data. The bilateral standing tasks include standing with both feet together, standing with the left foot in front of the right, and with the right foot in front of the left. Each task lasting 30 s. The unilateral standing tasks consist of standing on the left leg and standing on the right leg, each for 15 s. Each balance task will be repeated three times, with a 30 s rest interval between repetitions. An experienced therapist will remain close to the patient throughout the procedure, providing necessary assistance and ensuring patient safety.

fNIRS data processing

Data preprocessing and analysis will be conducted using MATLAB 2013b (MathWorks Inc., USA) and the open-source toolkit Homer2 [37]. Initially, the raw signal is converted into changes in optical density. A spline interpolation algorithm is subsequently applied to correct for motion artifacts induced by head movement [38]. The corrected signal is then filtered via a third-order bandpass filter (0.01–0.08 Hz) to eliminate physiological noise, such as fluctuations due to blood pressure, heartbeat, and respiration. The optical density signal is subsequently transformed into blood oxygenation metrics, including measurements of oxygenated hemoglobin, deoxygenated hemoglobin, and total hemoglobin, via the modified Beer‒Lambert law [39]. Given that oxygenated hemoglobin has greater sensitivity in data response [40], this study will focus on this parameter for further analysis.

Adverse events

During the intervention period, the side effects and adverse reactions associated with tDCS will be systematically assessed. These include sensations such as itching, tingling, and burning under the electrodes, as well as headaches or pain experienced during or following the intervention. The assessment will utilize the Numeric Rating Scale, which ranges from 0 to 10, where 0 indicates no pain and 10 represents the most severe pain imaginable.

Data management and confidentiality

Assessment data will be collected using case report forms, followed by an electronic data acquisition system for real-time data entry and monitoring. Data entry personnel are professionally trained and follow a dual entry verification mechanism to ensure data accuracy. The subjects' data will be anonymized, and only the members of the data monitoring committee and the researchers will have access to it. In addition, the ethics committee will conduct regular reviews and supervision to ensure the research's compliance.

Statistical analysis

Statistical analyses will be performed using IBM SPSS Statistics version 25.0. To evaluate the normality of the analysis parameters, the Kolmogorov‒Smirnov test will be used. Continuous data will be reported as the means ± standard deviations, while categorical data will be represented as proportions. The Chi-square test will be used to compare sex and age distributions between groups. For continuous data that meet the assumptions of normality and homogeneity of variance, independent samples t-tests will be conducted to compare group means. Two-way repeated measures analysis of variance will be employed to compare the differences in the main outcomes between the experimental group and the control group. The level of significance will be set at p < 0.05. When continuous data do not satisfy the criteria for normality and homogeneity, the Friedman test will be used. For participants who completed the baseline assessment but did not complete the intervention and follow-up, the intention-to-treat analysis will be used.

Discussion

Currently, no studies have examined the effects of cerebellar tDCS combined with VRBT on postural stability and fall risk among older adults. This study aims to assess postural stability and balance function comprehensively through clinical scales and objective instrumental assessments, including the use of fNIRS to measure changes in oxygenated hemoglobin concentrations as an indicator of cortical activity.

The SLS is used to evaluate static balance function, whereas the TUG test is used to assess dynamic balance and walking ability. Additionally, COP data provide a more accurate reflection of postural control capabilities. The ABC scale and the MFES serve as reliable indicators of the subjects' balance confidence and fall-related self-efficacy during daily activities. By integrating these evaluation results, we anticipate that our hypothesis will be effectively validated.

The fNIRS is an emerging, noninvasive neuroimaging technique that detects dynamic local hemodynamic changes in the cerebral cortex during neural activity stimulation, thereby providing an indirect measure of neural activity in specific cortical regions [41]. Research has demonstrated that external stimuli or environmental changes can elicit electrophysiological and neurochemical responses in the brain, resulting in local hemodynamic alterations characterized by a significant increase in oxygenated hemoglobin and a slight decrease in deoxygenated hemoglobin [42]. These changes serve as indicators of neuronal activity. Furthermore, fNIRS can assess cortical activity associated with postural control tasks in real-time by establishing a correlation between variations in cerebral cortical activation and motor abilities [43]. Currently, fNIRS is widely utilized in clinical research to evaluate the activation levels of specific cortical areas during various balance functions and postural control tasks [44, 45].

The findings of this study are anticipated to enhance existing intervention strategies for fall prevention in this population, inspire researchers to develop innovative treatment methods and provide new insights into balance function rehabilitation and postural training for older adults.
