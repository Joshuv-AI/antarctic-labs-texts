Prognostic value of serum gasdermin d in predicting major adverse cardiovascular events in patients with unstable angina: a prospective observational study
Authors: Lijie Wu, Zhonghua Zhou, Xue Yang, Qi Wu, Wei Tan
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractObjectiveThis study investigated the prognostic significance of serum gasdermin D (GSDMD) in unstable angina (UA) and its association with inflammatory cytokines, myocardial injury markers, and 1-year major adverse cardiovascular events (MACE).MethodsA total of 221 UA patients were prospectively enrolled and followed for 12 months. Serum levels of GSDMD, IL-6, IL-1β, TNF-α, and CRP were measured by ELISA. NT-proBNP, CK-MB, and cTnI were quantified using automated immunoassays. Patients were stratified by 1-year MACE outcomes.ResultsSerum GSDMD, IL-6, and IL-1β were significantly higher in the MACE group (all p < 0.01), while TNF-α and CRP showed no differences. GSDMD levels correlated with IL-6 and NT-proBNP (p < 0.001). ROC analysis showed GSDMD predicted MACE with an AUC of 0.800 (95% CI: 0.741–0.859, p < 0.001). Logistic regression identified serum GSDMD levels (OR = 2.601, 95% CI: 1.652–3.701, p < 0.001) and NT-proBNP (OR = 1.055, 95% CI: 1.030–1.080, p < 0.001) as independent predictors of MACE.ConclusionElevated GSDMD was associated with adverse outcomes in UA and served as an independent prognostic biomarker, suggesting a potential role of pyroptosis-related inflammation in acute coronary syndromes.Keywords: Unstable angina, Gasdermin D, Pyroptosis, Biomarkers, Inflammation

Introduction

Unstable angina (UA) is a prevalent manifestation of acute coronary syndrome (ACS), characterized by chest discomfort or pain resulting from inadequate myocardial perfusion without evident myocardial necrosis [1–3]. Epidemiological data highlights the considerable clinical burden and heterogeneous prognosis of UA. A recent epidemiological study demonstrated that among 10,316 patients with ACS followed over 12 years, the cumulative incidence of sudden cardiac arrests (SCAs), including sudden cardiac deaths (SCDs), was significantly notable in patients with UA, amounting to 5.7% over the follow-up period [4]. Furthermore, in a population-based analysis involving 9,694 patients referred for coronary angiography, UA was associated with substantially higher mortality compared to stable angina (SA), with a 1-year mortality rate nearly double that observed in SA patients [5]. Thus, identifying predictive factors for adverse outcomes in UA patients remains crucial for effective risk stratification and management. Biomarkers have emerged as essential tools for predicting prognosis in UA patients, facilitating timely risk stratification and clinical decision-making [6–8]. Gasdermin D (GSDMD), a key executor of pyroptosis, has recently drawn attention due to its involvement in inflammatory responses associated with cardiovascular diseases [9, 10]. GSDMD activation significantly enhances neutrophil and monocyte mobilization into the infarcted myocardium, exacerbating cardiac inflammation, enlarging infarct size, and impairing cardiac function. Conversely, genetic deletion or pharmacological inhibition of GSDMD mitigates these inflammatory responses and improves cardiac outcomes [11]. Similarly, research utilizing GSDMD-deficient mouse models of atherosclerosis indicates reduced lesion formation, diminished inflammatory cell infiltration, and attenuated macrophage and endothelial cell pyroptosis, highlighting the pathological relevance of GSDMD-mediated inflammation in atherosclerotic disease progression [12]. However, the specific role and prognostic significance of serum GSDMD levels in patients with UA remain unexplored. Thus, evaluating serum GSDMD expression and its potential relationship with clinical outcomes could provide valuable insights for improved prognostic stratification and management of UA patients.

Given the potential role of GSDMD in inflammatory processes associated with cardiovascular disease, this study aimed to investigate serum GSDMD levels in patients with UA and explore their association with inflammatory markers, cardiac enzyme profiles, and clinical outcomes. Additionally, this research evaluated whether serum GSDMD levels could serve as a novel biomarker for predicting major adverse cardiovascular events (MACE) within 1-year in UA patients. By identifying such biomarkers, this study aims to contribute to the early identification and improved management of high-risk UA patients, potentially reducing cardiovascular morbidity and mortality.

Methods

Study population

This prospective observational study enrolled 221 patients with UA who were admitted and treated at our hospital between January 2022 and June 2024. The diagnosis of UA was based on the 2012 ACCF/AHA focused update incorporated into the ACCF/AHA 2007 guidelines for the management of patients with unstable angina/non–ST-elevation myocardial infarction [13]. Specifically, the diagnostic criteria for UA included: (1) typical angina symptoms; (2) typical ischemic electrocardiographic changes, such as new or transient ST-segment depression ≥ 0.1 mV or T-wave inversion ≥ 0.2 mV; and (3) normal levels of cardiac biomarkers, including cardiac troponin T (cTnT), cardiac troponin I (cTnI), or creatine kinase-MB (CK-MB).

The inclusion criteria were as follows: (1) age ≥ 18 years; (2) diagnosis of UA confirmed by cardiologists based on clinical symptoms, ECG findings, and troponin levels; and (3) provision of informed consent. Exclusion criteria included: (1) history of myocardial infarction, coronary artery bypass grafting, or heart failure; (2) presence of other cardiovascular conditions such as stable angina, myocardial bridge, congenital or valvular heart disease, cardiomyopathy, or coronary artery anomalies; (3) severe hepatic or renal impairment; (4) comorbidities that may affect inflammation or immune response, including autoimmune diseases, malignancies, hematological disorders, or active infections; and (5) recent use (within the past month) of medications with potential anti-inflammatory or immunomodulatory effects, such as corticosteroids, nonsteroidal anti-inflammatory drugs (NSAIDs), antibiotics, or immunosuppressive agents; (6) incomplete clinical data or loss to follow-up.

The study protocol was approved by the Ethics Committee of our Hospital, and written informed consent was obtained from all participants prior to enrollment, in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki.

Serum sample collection and ELISA assays

Fasting peripheral venous blood samples (5 mL) were collected from all enrolled patients within 24 h of hospital admission and prior to the initiation of any antiplatelet or anti-inflammatory therapy. Blood samples were drawn into EDTA-containing tubes and immediately centrifuged at 3,000 rpm for 10 min at 4 °C to isolate the serum. The obtained serum was aliquoted and stored at − 80 °C until further analysis. Serum levels of GSDMD, interleukin-6 (IL-6), interleukin-1β (IL-1β), tumor necrosis factor-α (TNF-α), and C-reactive protein (CRP) were measured using commercially available enzyme-linked immunosorbent assay (ELISA) kits according to the manufacturers’ instructions. All assays were performed duplicate to ensure reliability. The ELISA kits for GSDMD, IL-6, IL-1β, TNF-α and CRP were purchased from Nanjing Jiancheng Bioengineering Institute (Nanjing, China).

Collection of clinical data

Upon admission, detailed demographic and clinical information was recorded for all patients, including age, sex, body mass index (BMI), smoking status, medication use, history of hypertension and diabetes mellitus.

Peripheral venous blood samples (5 mL) were collected on the day of admission, prior to the administration of any antiplatelet, lipid-lowering, or anti-inflammatory medications. Complete blood count (CBC) parameters, including white blood cell (WBC) count, hemoglobin (Hb) concentration, and platelet (PLT) count, were measured using an automated hematology analyzer (XN-Series, Sysmex Corporation, Kobe, Japan). Biochemical parameters were analyzed using a fully automated biochemical analyzer (AU5821, Beckman Coulter, USA), and included total cholesterol (TC), triglycerides (TG), high-density lipoprotein cholesterol (HDL-C), low-density lipoprotein cholesterol (LDL-C), alanine aminotransferase (ALT), and aspartate aminotransferase (AST). Cardiac function was evaluated by measuring serum levels of N-terminal pro–B-type natriuretic peptide (NT-proBNP), CK-MB, and cTnI. All laboratory tests were performed in the central laboratory of our hospital in accordance with standardized protocols and internal quality control procedures.

Follow-up

All enrolled patients received standard treatment in accordance with the clinical practice guidelines of our hospital. This study was observational in nature and did not involve any intervention in patient management.​ Patients were followed for a period of 12 months from the date of hospital admission. The occurrence of MACE during the follow-up period was documented. MACE was defined as the composite of the following cardiovascular events: sudden cardiac death, heart failure, cardiogenic shock, recurrent myocardial infarction, recurrent angina, coronary heart disease-related death and hemodynamically unstable arrhythmias. Based on the occurrence of MACE within the 12-month follow-up period, patients were categorized into two groups: the MACE group (patients who experienced at least one MACE event) and the non-MACE group (patients who did not experience any MACE events).​.

Statistical analysis

All statistical analyses were performed using IBM SPSS Statistics version 25.0 (IBM Corp., Armonk, NY, USA). Continuous variables were tested for normality using the Shapiro-Wilk test. Normally distributed continuous variables were expressed as mean ± standard deviation (SD) and compared between groups using the independent samples t-test. Non-normally distributed continuous variables were presented as median (IQR) and compared using the Mann-Whitney U test. Categorical variables were expressed as frequencies and percentages, and comparisons between groups were made using the chi-square test or Fisher’s exact test, as appropriate.​ Spearman’s rank correlation analysis was employed to assess the relationships between serum GSDMD levels and inflammatory or myocardial injury markers. Receiver operating characteristic (ROC) curve analysis was conducted to evaluate the predictive value of serum GSDMD levels for the occurrence of MACE within 1-year in patients with unstable angina. Furthermore, multivariate binary logistic regression analysis was performed to determine whether serum GSDMD level was an independent risk factor for the occurrence of MACE within 1-year, adjusting for potential confounding variables.​ A two-tailed p-value of less than 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

The clinical characteristics and laboratory measurements of the study participants are summarized in Table 1. Age, sex distribution, body mass index (BMI), and smoking status were comparable between the MACE group (n = 41) and the non-MACE group (n = 180). Similarly, there were no significant differences between the two groups regarding the history of hypertension, diabetes, and hyperlipidemia. Laboratory parameters including WBC count, Hb, PLT count, TC, TG, LDL-C, ALT, AST, cTnI and CK-MB were also comparable between the groups. Serum levels of GSDMD and inflammatory cytokines in UA patients with and without MACE

Serum levels of GSDMD and inflammatory cytokines in patients with UA were further compared between the MACE group and the non-MACE group. Serum concentrations of GSDMD were significantly elevated in patients experiencing MACE compared to those without MACE (Fig. 1, p < 0.001). Similarly, significant differences were observed in serum levels of inflammatory cytokines, including IL-6 and IL-1β, with both cytokines markedly increased in the MACE group (both p < 0.01). Conversely, serum concentrations of TNF-α and CRP did not differ significantly between the two groups.

Correlations among serum GSDMD levels, inflammatory cytokines, and cardiac enzymes in UA patients

Spearman’s rank correlation analysis was performed to assess the relationships among serum GSDMD levels, inflammatory cytokines, and cardiac enzyme profiles in UA patients. Serum GSDMD showed a significant positive correlation with IL-6 levels (Table 2, r = 0.261, p < 0.001) and IL-1β (r = 0.174, p = 0.010). Additionally, serum GSDMD was positively correlated with NT-proBNP (r = 0.359, p < 0.001). However, there were no significant correlations identified between serum GSDMD and other inflammatory cytokines or cardiac enzymes.

Multivariate logistic regression analysis of risk factors for 1-year MACE in UA patients

To further identify independent risk factors associated with the occurrence of MACE within 1-year in patients with UA, univariate logistic regression analysis was first performed. Variables with a p-value < 0.10 in the univariate analysis were subsequently included in the multivariate binary logistic regression model. After adjusting for potential confounding factors, elevated serum GSDMD levels (OR = 2.601, 95% CI: 1.652–3.701, p < 0.001) and NT-proBNP (OR = 1.055, 95% CI: 1.030–1.080, p < 0.001) were identified as independent predictors of MACE occurrence (Table 4).

Diagnostic value of serum GSDMD levels in predicting 1-year MACE in UA patients

ROC curve analysis was performed to evaluate the predictive value of serum GSDMD levels for MACE within 1-year in patients with UA. Serum GSDMD demonstrated significant predictive capability with an area under the curve (AUC) of 0.764 (95% CI: 0.675–0.852, p < 0.001). The optimal cutoff value of serum GSDMD was identified at 4.20 ng/mL, which yielded a sensitivity of 68.3% and specificity of 78.9% (Fig. 2).

Discussion

UA remains a critical clinical condition due to its potential to progress into acute myocardial infarction and its association with a heightened risk of recurrent ischemic events [14, 15]. Despite advances in diagnosis and treatment, the identification of reliable biomarkers that can predict adverse outcomes in UA patients remains limited. In this study, we found that elevated serum GSDMD levels and NT-proBNP were independent predictors of 1-year MACE in patients with UA. Importantly, GSDMD showed a strong association with adverse cardiovascular outcomes, underscoring its potential role as a novel biomarker for risk stratification. These findings suggest that incorporating GSDMD into current clinical assessment may improve prognostic evaluation and guide early intervention strategies in UA patients.

A growing body of evidence supports the clinical utility of various serum biomarkers in predicting outcomes in UA patients. Elevated neutrophil-to-lymphocyte ratio (NLR) has been shown to correlate with SYNTAX scores and is independently associated with long-term cardiovascular mortality in patients with UA, indicating its role as a systemic inflammation marker for risk stratification [16]. Similarly, the uric acid to albumin ratio (UAR) has demonstrated prognostic value, with higher UAR levels significantly predicting increased cardiac and all-cause mortality after percutaneous coronary intervention in UA patients [17]. Growth differentiation factor-15 (GDF-15), a stress-responsive cytokine, has also been found to be elevated in UA compared with stable angina, suggesting its potential as a diagnostic biomarker [18]. Additionally, elevated serum total bilirubin (STB) levels have been linked to poorer coronary flow and increased risk of adverse events, further supporting their prognostic relevance [19]. Among inflammatory markers, IL-6 has consistently emerged as a strong and independent predictor of cardiovascular events and mortality in UA patients, particularly in the context of stent placement without statin pretreatment [20]. In line with these findings, our study also identified significantly higher IL-6 levels in UA patients who subsequently developed MACE, reinforcing its prognostic importance. In addition to IL-6, we observed that GSDMD levels were also elevated in high-risk patients, suggesting its potential value as a novel biomarker worthy of further investigation.

Recent studies have increasingly highlighted the potential value of GSDMD as a circulating inflammatory biomarker across multiple disease settings. In the field of neurodegenerative disorders, elevated cerebrospinal fluid levels of GSDMD were found in patients with Alzheimer’s disease and vascular dementia, where they showed good discriminatory ability and correlated with inflammatory markers such as IL-1β and IL-6 [21]. Similarly, in patients with end-stage kidney disease undergoing hemodialysis, high serum GSDMD levels were associated with more severe vascular calcification and poorer clinical outcomes, suggesting its utility as a prognostic biomarker in systemic inflammatory states [22]. In cardiovascular settings, a recent study in STEMI patients demonstrated that elevated serum GSDMD concentrations were linked to greater infarct size, lower left ventricular ejection fraction, increased microvascular injury, and higher incidence of MACE during follow-up [23]. Another study showed that GSDMD expression in peripheral blood mononuclear cells was significantly elevated in acute myocardial infarction patients and independently associated with increased MACE risk, underscoring its relevance in acute coronary syndromes [24]. Collectively, these findings provide strong support for the role of GSDMD as a novel systemic marker reflecting pyroptosis-related inflammation and disease severity in both cardiac and non-cardiac pathologies. Building upon this evidence, our study is, to our knowledge, the first to demonstrate a significant elevation of serum GSDMD levels in UA patients who developed MACE, and to identify GSDMD as an independent prognostic factor in this population. These findings extend the role of GSDMD from acute myocardial injury and chronic inflammation into the realm of unstable angina.

The potential involvement of GSDMD in the pathophysiology of UA is supported by emerging mechanistic evidence linking GSDMD-mediated pyroptosis to atherosclerosis progression and plaque instability. Pyroptosis, driven by inflammasome activation and caspase-1-dependent cleavage of GSDMD, leads to membrane pore formation, release of proinflammatory cytokines, and eventual cell lysis. In murine models of myocardial infarction, IRF2 has been shown to transcriptionally activate GSDMD under hypoxic conditions, thereby exacerbating myocardial injury through enhanced pyroptotic cell death and inflammation [25]. More importantly, in atherosclerosis models, GSDMD expression is predominantly localized in plaque-residing macrophages and is critically involved in the formation of unstable lesions. Deletion of GSDMD in ApoE-/- mice resulted in reduced lesion size, diminished necrotic core formation, and a shift from pyroptosis to apoptosis within plaques, suggesting improved plaque stability [26, 27]. Mechanistically, GSDMD contributes to mitochondrial DNA release and STING pathway activation, promoting NF-κB-driven cytokine expression and further macrophage recruitment, thereby amplifying vascular inflammation [27]. These findings imply that elevated GSDMD levels in UA patients may reflect active pyroptotic activity within atherosclerotic plaques, contributing to their vulnerability and predisposing patients to MACE. Our current results showing increased serum GSDMD levels in UA patients who subsequently experienced MACE is in line with these mechanistic insights, supporting its clinical relevance as both a biomarker and potential therapeutic target in atherosclerotic cardiovascular disease.

Several limitations of this study should be acknowledged. First, this was a single-center study with a relatively limited sample size, which may restrict the generalizability of our findings. Second, although we demonstrated a significant association between serum GSDMD levels and adverse cardiovascular outcomes, the causal relationship and underlying mechanisms were not fully elucidated and require further mechanistic or longitudinal validation. Third, the study did not include serial measurements of GSDMD, limiting our understanding of its dynamic changes during disease progression. Fourth, our primary endpoint was a broad composite MACE, which combined both “hard” events and less severe outcomes; although the breakdown of individual components is reported, the study was not powered for component-specific analyses, and we avoided post hoc hard-endpoint–only models to reduce instability and multiplicity. Lastly, potential confounding factors such as medication use, dietary status, or subclinical infections were not fully controlled and could have influenced biomarker levels. In particular, we did not exclude patients with prior statin use to preserve the clinical representativeness of UA care, and thus residual effects from long-term exposure cannot be fully ruled out.

Conclusion

Our study identifies serum GSDMD as a promising inflammatory biomarker in patients with unstable angina, with elevated levels independently associated with the risk of 1-year major adverse cardiovascular events. These findings suggest that GSDMD may serve as a valuable tool for early risk stratification in UA and highlight the potential clinical relevance of pyroptosis-related pathways in atherosclerotic cardiovascular disease.
