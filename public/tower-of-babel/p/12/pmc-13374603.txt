Contact Lenses and Vision-Related Quality of Life: Evidence and Clinical Insights
Authors: Eugene Appenteng Osae, Kwaku A Osei, Natalie C Edwards, Edward Wygonik, Caroline A Blackie
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundContact lenses are a cornerstone of vision correction for ametropia and can be of particular value for management of irregular astigmatism and corneal ectasia. Beyond objective measures of visual acuity and corneal health, patient-reported outcomes and vision-related quality of life (VR-QoL) have emerged as critical endpoints. This review aimed to synthesize evidence on VR-QoL outcomes with contact lenses.MethodsWe conducted a narrative review of studies published between 2000 and 2025 on the influence of various contact lens modalities including soft conventional lenses, rigid gas-permeable (RGP) lenses, scleral and mini-scleral lenses, orthokeratology, and specialty designs on VR-QoL across myopia, keratoconus, dry-eye disease, and other conditions. Descriptive tables were developed for all identified studies, and a synthesis of the impact of contact lenses on VR-QoL findings is presented. Evidence for VR-QoL measurement and instrumentation, special considerations for pediatric/adolescent populations, and barriers/limitations to sustained contact lens wear were also appraised.ResultsVR-QoL evaluations using validated instruments demonstrate favorable findings with contact lenses. VR-QoL gains with contact lenses are more favorable compared with spectacle correction, particularly in appearance, activities, peer perception, and psychological domains. In keratoconus, RGP and scleral lenses dramatically improve NEI-VFQ-25 composite scores, even in cases of advanced disease. Barriers to sustained wear of contact lenses mostly relate to lens discomfort and dry eye due to ocular surface complications, although studies in patients with ocular surface disease/dry eye still derive VR-QoL benefits with contact lenses. There is a lack of head-to-head trials for certain modalities (eg, an absence of studies directly comparing multifocal contact lenses to spectacles for fall rates).ConclusionContact lenses provide significant and clinically meaningful gains in VR-QoL among adult and pediatric patients with refractive error, keratoconus, ocular surface disease or dry eye, and other indications. The VR-QoL benefits of contact lenses may exceed those achieved with spectacles.Keywords: contact lenses, quality of life, myopia, keratoconus, ocular surface disorders

Introduction

Contact lenses are a cornerstone of vision correction for ametropia and can be of particular value for visual rehabilitation of irregular astigmatism and corneal ectasia. Introduced clinically in the 1950s, contact lenses have undergone transformative advances in materials, designs, and fitting philosophies.1 From daily-disposable soft lenses to large-diameter sclerals that vault the entire cornea, contact lenses now address conditions once considered untreatable without surgery.2

It is estimated that more than 140 million people globally use contact lenses,3,4 representing approximately 1.7% of the world’s population of about 8.3 billion.5 This figure is projected to grow to over 170 million by 2033,4 driven by rising myopia prevalence (affecting nearly 27% of the global population and expected to reach 50% by 2050).6 In certain age groups in several Asian countries, the prevalence of myopia is over 80%.7 Among late teenagers and young adults in Korea, Taiwan, and China the prevalence is now between 84% and 97%.7 Additional reasons for the growth in contact lens usage include an aging demographic needing multifocal options and increasing adoption in urbanizing regions like Asia-Pacific, where youth and cosmetic use are surging.6 In the United States (US), contact lens use is more prevalent, with approximately 45 million wearers in 2025—about 13% of the adult population.8

The primary goal of any vision correction is not only optimal visual acuity but also enhancement of functional vision and overall quality of life (QoL). Vision-related quality of life (VR-QoL) encompasses visual function, socio-emotional well-being, convenience, appearance, and independence from optical aids. Measuring VR-QoL among contact lens wearers is critical because clinical measures such as visual acuity, wearing time, or even objective signs of ocular surface health consistently fail to capture the full patient experience and predict long-term success.3,9,10 For example, some contact lens wearers achieve 20/20 vision yet still experience chronic contact lens-associated dry eye, fluctuating vision, end-of-day discomfort, increased risk of infections, or restrictions in work, sports, and social activities that erode overall satisfaction and eventually lead to reduced wearing time or permanent dropout9 (reported rates ranging from 15 to 30%).11 These burdens are highly individualized and may be subjective and only detectable through validated patient-reported outcome measures (PROMs).10

Although some previous studies have summarized the literature evaluating the impact of contact lenses on VR-QoL in more restricted populations (ie, patients with keratoconus,12–14 long-term wear among patients with myopia,15 and patients with dry eye disease and associated ocular surface disorders),16 to our knowledge, there is an absence of contemporary literature reviews summarizing the evidence for VR-QoL across all types of contact lens wearers. This narrative review aimed to synthesize evidence from 2000 to 2025 on VR-QoL outcomes with contact lenses. Narrative literature reviews summarize, synthesize, and interpret existing literature to describe what is known on a topic while conducting a subjective examination and critique of an entire body of literature, without following the strict protocols of a systematic review.17 Narrative reviews are often useful for topics that require a meaningful synthesis of research evidence that may be complex or broad and that require detailed, nuanced description and interpretation.18 The influence of various contact lens modalities including soft conventional lenses, rigid gas-permeable (RGP) lenses, scleral and mini-scleral lenses, orthokeratology, and specialty designs on VR-QoL across myopia, keratoconus, dry-eye disease, and other conditions was evaluated.

Materials and Methods

We conducted a narrative review of studies published between 2000 and 2025 using PubMed, Scopus, and Web of Science databases.

The search terms that were utilized were “contact lens” and “quality of life” and the search was restricted to English language. Inclusion criteria were studies that measured VR-QoL and included contact lens interventions. Studies were excluded if they did not contain primary research data (ie, excluded literature reviews, commentaries, editorials, study designs, etc.). The reference lists of included articles and relevant review articles were manually screened to identify any potentially eligible studies missed by the electronic searches. The search was conducted on December 8, 2025.

Data extracted included the journal citation, publication year, country of analysis, sample size, indication, correction method, the VR-QoL instrument used, and VR-QoL findings. The contact lens and VR-QoL evidence were grouped into the following clinical indications: refractive error (myopia, hyperopia, astigmatism, and presbyopia) for adult and pediatric populations separately, keratoconus and irregular corneal conditions, ocular surface disease/dry eye, and other indications. Descriptive tables were developed for all identified studies. Evidence for VR-QoL measurement and instrumentation, special considerations for pediatric and adolescent populations, and barriers and limitations to sustained contact lens wear were also appraised. Along with the summarized extracted data, a narrative synthesis of the impact of contact lenses on VR-QoL findings is presented.

VR-QoL Measurement and Instruments

Over the past 25 years, PROMs for evaluating vision correction have evolved from simple non-validated satisfaction surveys to robust, validated instruments that capture VR-QoL.19–21 Optimal contact lens VR-QoL instrument selection is dependent upon the specific eye condition and patient population evaluated.20 The ideal instrument should be validated for contact lens populations, sensitive to subtle issues like end-of-day discomfort, dryness, or visual fluctuations, and brief enough to minimize patient burden.15,20 The most commonly used instruments are presented in Table 1.

The National Eye Institute Visual Function Questionnaire (NEI-VFQ) was developed to assess VR-QoL with respect to emotional well-being and social function as well as difficulty with tasks and symptoms22 The NEI-VFQ-25 has been used extensively in the evaluation of VR-QoL with refractive correction and keratoconus. It is comprised of 25 items yielding 12 subscales and a composite score (0–100). Composite NEI-VFQ-25 scores of <60 indicate severe impairment, whereas scores >90 approximate normal vision. The instrument has been validated in Arabic,33 Persian,34 Turkish,35 and other languages, and is currently the most commonly utilized instrument for assessing VR-QoL.

To better capture the effects of current and proposed therapeutic regimens on refractive error, especially surgical ones, the NEI Refractive Error Correction Quality of Life (NEI-RQL) questionnaire development study was initiated.23 Its purpose was to develop and test an instrument that more appropriately and effectively captures the more subtle aspects of functioning associated with refractive error and its correction in patients with corrected vision of 20/30 or better from the patients’ perspective.23 The NEI-RQL-42 measures vision-targeted, health-related QoL for persons with well-corrected refractive error.23 Study findings have demonstrated the reliability and construct validity of the NEI-RQL.36

The 20-item Quality of Life Impact of Refractive Correction (QIRC)24 questionnaire is a validated questionnaire for quantifying the VR-QoL among people with refractive correction by spectacles, contact lenses, and refractive surgery in the prepresbyopic age group. The use of Rasch scaling allows scores to be treated as a valid continuous variable. QIRC has broad applicability for cross-sectional and outcome research.24,29

When dry-eye and contact-lens comfort are primary concerns, the Ocular Surface Disease Index (OSDI),26 Dry Eye-Related Quality-of-Life Score (DEQS),27 and Standard Patient Evaluation of Eye Dryness (SPEED)28 are frequently implemented. The OSDI is a 12-item symptom scale with scores ranging from 0 to 100. Scores >33 denote moderate-to-severe dry eye, the leading cause of contact lens discontinuation. The DEQS is a 15-item scale that focuses on the frequency and intensity of dry eye symptoms (ie, dryness, soreness, redness, light sensitivity, heavy sensation) and how much they affect VR-QoL (eg, difficulty reading, driving at night, eye fatigue affecting work or hobbies) Patients rate both frequency (Never/Occasionally/Often/All the time) and severity (1=no trouble to 4=very severe), with final score ranging from 0 to 100, where a score >15 is commonly used as a cutoff for clinically meaningful dry eye, and >33 indicates severe impact. The SPEED is an 8-item questionnaire designed to quickly assess the frequency and severity of dry eye symptoms (ie, dryness, grittiness/scratchiness, soreness/irritation, burning/watering, and eye fatigue) over the past 72 hours (with optional 3-month recall). Patients rate frequency on a 0–3 scale (Never to Constant) and severity on a 0–4 scale (No problem to Intolerable), yielding a total score of 0–28, where higher scores indicate greater symptom severity. Scores ≥8 are often considered clinically significant, while scores >13–16 typically indicate moderate-to-severe dry eye warranting intervention.

The Contact Lens Impact on Quality of Life (CLIQ)29 and Pediatric Refractive Error Profile (PREP and PREP2)30–32 are age-specific instruments that provide detailed information on gains in appearance, confidence, activities, and peer perception with contact lenses. The Children’s Visual Function Questionnaire (CVFQ)37 is a vision-specific VR-QoL instrument designed for use with children up to 7 years of age. The CVFQ subscales quantified meaningful differences among pediatric patient groups who were chosen to address key research questions commonly posed in ophthalmic clinical trials.37

Other less commonly used instruments include the Keratoconus Outcomes Research Questionnaire (KORQ),38,39 the Impact of Dry Eye on Everyday Life (IDEEL) VR-QoL questionnaire,40,41 the Computer Vision Syndrome Questionnaire (CVS-Q),41,42 the Pediatric Eye Questionnaire (PedEyeQ),43,44 and various custom quantitative and qualitative VR-QoL questionnaires.41,45–48 The Vision Quality of Life Index (VisQoL) is a vision-related utility instrument for the health economic evaluation of eye care and rehabilitation programs.25,49 Time trade-off and standard gamble questionnaires have also been used to directly derive health-related QoL utility measures.50,51

VR-QoL with Contact Lenses in Refractive Error (Myopia, Hyperopia, Astigmatism, and Presbyopia)

Refractive error includes nearsightedness (myopia), farsightedness (hyperopia), astigmatism, and presbyopia.52 Refractive error affects approximately 60% of the American population and is the most common problem resulting in visits to eye care professionals.52 There is a rapidly increasing prevalence of myopia across the world commonly attributed to increasing intensive education and increasingly common indoor lifestyles.53 Globally, myopia affected 1.45 billion people or 27% of the world’s population in 2010.6 However, in some parts of the world, notably in East and Southeast Asia, the prevalence of myopia is over 80%.6 Among late teenagers and young adults in Korea, Taiwan, and China the prevalence of myopia is now between 84% and 97%.6 The increasing prevalence is a growing global public health problem, in terms of rates of uncorrected refractive error and significantly, an increased risk of visual impairment due to myopia-related ocular morbidity.6,53 Concerns about cosmetic appearance, personal health and safety, difficulties in day-to-day activities, inconveniences rendered in daily life, and economic impact have been identified as the most important themes in how refractive error affects myopic patients’ VR-QoL.54

Adult Populations

Across multiple validated questionnaires (ie, NEI-RQL-42, NEI-VFQ-25, QIRC), multiple types of contact lenses, and multiple countries, contact lenses have demonstrated significant and clinical meaningful impacts on adults’ VR-QoL (Table 2).41,45,50,55–73 Studies comparing contact lenses and spectacles among adults showed that contact lens wearers consistently rate their VR-QoL markedly higher than spectacle users, particularly in regard to cosmetics, peripheral vision, and activity participation.50,55–58 One study by Queiros et al (2012)58 found that soft contact lenses were associated with a slightly larger reduction in VR-QoL compared to emmetropes than spectacles (−17.3% vs −15.8%); however, no statistical comparisons were made. Also, Pucker et al (2025)41 found reduced comfort across the wear day with toric lenses.

Overnight corneal reshaping orthokeratology lenses have also been shown to improve myopic symptoms,59–65 and wearers have reported greater satisfaction with vision, activities, and overall compared to spectacle wearers,60 and less activity limitations, less trouble with symptoms, and less dependence on refractive correction compared to soft contact lens wearers.61

Studies comparing contact lenses and refractive surgery (LASIK/PRK) mostly found more favorable VR-QoL results with surgery;57,58,70,73 however, the findings were not completely consistent and some evidence suggested that well-fitted contact lenses may match or sometimes exceed the VR-QoL benefits of surgery.63

Patients with presbyopia were also shown to have significant benefit in VR-QoL with use of contact lenses.68,71 However, the study by Richdale et al68 showed worse clarity of vision and increased symptoms with multifocal/monovision lenses and Novillo-Diaz et al71 found higher discontinuation rates due to poor distance/near vision with specific presbyopic designs. Presbyopia often coincides with aging, and poor near or peripheral vision can contribute to trips and falls. Multifocal or progressive spectacles have been linked to increased fall risk due to distortions in the lower visual field (eg, blurred ground view when looking down), which affects depth perception and toe clearance during walking. A large randomized controlled trial (n=606 older adults) found that switching multifocal glasses wearers to single-vision distance glasses for outdoor activities reduced falls by 40% in active participants, implying that multifocal glasses elevate baseline risk.74 Contact lenses (multifocal, bifocal, or monovision) may offer advantages to spectacles presbyopia because they move with the eyes, potentially providing a more natural field of view without fixed lower-field blur or “image jump.”75 Hence, wearers do not need to tilt their head to avoid the wrong zone to see obstacles on the ground.75 Studies directly comparing multifocal contact lenses to spectacles for fall rates were not identified in the literature review; therefore, this could be an important area of research.

Pediatric Populations

Multiple studies have demonstrated large and clinically meaningful VR-QoL advantages of contact lenses in myopic children, adolescents, and young adults (Table 3).32,44,46,51,55,60,76–85 Contact lens wear dramatically improves how children and teens feel about their appearance, peer interactions, and participation in activities, leading to greater satisfaction with their refractive error correction.32,44,46,51,55,60,76–85 Across studies conducted between 2003 and 2025 involving more than 3,000 myopic children and adolescents from Asia, Europe, and North America, contact lens wear (ie, soft daily-disposable, conventional soft, multifocal soft, or orthokeratology) was shown to be associated with higher VR-QoL compared with spectacle correction44,46,51,55,60,76,77,79–84 Longitudinal data showed that the benefits of contact lenses were sustained over months to years of wear and that scores continue to improve as children gain experience with lens handling Parents of contact lens wearers consistently rate their children’s VR-QoL higher than parents of spectacle wearers, particularly in activity participation and self-esteem related to appearance.44,60,80

VR-QoL with Contact Lenses in Keratoconus and Irregular Corneal Conditions

Keratoconus, a progressive ectatic corneal disorder, significantly impacts VR-QoL beyond reduced visual acuity.12 Multiple factors contribute to reduced VR-QoL in keratoconus including refractive errors (leading to challenges with spectacle and contact lens correction, impacting comfort and convenience), decreased contrast sensitivity (hindering daily activities like driving and reading), ocular pain and discomfort (often stemming from the disease itself or treatment), and associated comorbidities (such as vernal keratoconjunctivitis, further compromising ocular health).12 Many studies have been conducted to evaluate VR-QoL in patients with keratoconus and contact lenses (Table 4).39,45,47,86–106 The studies have confirmed that keratoconus profoundly impairs VR-QoL, with NEI-VFQ-25 composite scores typically in the 55–75 range in uncorrected or spectacle-corrected patients.86,88 Successful fitting with RGP or scleral lenses produces dramatic VR-QoL improvement in patients with keratoconus.39,45,47,86–106 Different lens types cater to varying disease severities and patient needs.12 Contact lens comfort, complications, cost, and handling issues can influence the overall impact on VR-QoL, varying with lens type and individual patient factors.12 Most studies showed that modern specialty contact lenses (RGP, hybrid, scleral) improved VR-QoL more than spectacles in patients with keratoconus;95 however, two studies found more nuanced or contrasting results. Kandel et al (2023)39 observed that contact lens wearers had lower overall scores on the Keratoconus Outcomes Research Questionnaire compared to those using spectacles. Panthier et al (2020)94 found that rigid gas-permeable lenses did not yield better VR-QoL than untreated patients overall, although they provided better general vision in advanced cases (with increased ocular pain noted in that subgroup). Also, a qualitative study by Fan et al (2024)92 highlighted some patient-reported challenges with contact lenses such as inconvenience, cost, and lifestyle adjustments associated with contact lens wear.106 Pediatric keratoconus is aggressive and socially debilitating; however, evidence evaluating contact lens VR-QoL among pediatric keratoconus is scant (Table 4).86

VR-QoL with Contact Lenses in Patients with Ocular Surface Disease/Dry Eye

Dry eye causes irregularity of the ocular surface and reduces the quality of vision.107 An intact, regular tear film is essential for high-quality retinal images; however, visual tasks requiring sustained gazing can disrupt the tear film, eventually degrading visual function.108 Across studies published between 2007 and 2024 involving patients with severe ocular surface disease – including chronic graft-versus-host disease, Stevens-Johnson syndrome/toxic epidermal necrolysis, Sjögren syndrome–associated dry eye, and other causes of limbal stem-cell deficiency or profound dryness – specialty contact lenses (scleral, mini-scleral, silicone hydrogel scleral, and prosthetic soft/scleral devices) were consistently associated with statistically and clinically significant improvements in VR-QoL in patients with ocular surface disease or dry eye (Table 5).48,109–114 Contact lens-related dry eye remains a leading cause of discontinuation; however, modern lens materials and designs mitigate symptoms and have dramatically reduced dropout rates.115

VR-QoL with Contact Lenses in Other Indications

Garcia-Romo et al (2018)116 assessed the feasibility, effectiveness and acceptability of using prosthetic occluding contact lenses among patients with moderate amblyopia. The authors found that NEI VFQ-25 composite scores showed significant improvement with the occluding contact lens at 12 months compared to eye patching for amblyopia. Rajak et al (2006)117 and Severinsky et al (2016)118 showed that tinted contact lenses markedly improved VR-QoL in children with stationary cone dystrophies and degenerative retinal diseases and offered an alternative for the management of photophobia. The ability to use contact lenses to treat pain and wound healing and improve VR-QoL after corneal crosslinking (CXL) was demonstrated in a study by Soeters et al (2020).119 Finally, a case series in Japan by Tanaka et al demonstrated VR-QoL benefits of antihistamine-releasing contact lenses for patients with severe allergic conjunctivitis.120

Discussion

Contemporary evidence from diverse populations and validated VR-QoL instruments consistently demonstrates that contact lenses confer significant and clinically meaningful improvements in VR-QoL. Studies have shown that VR-QoL improves more with contact lenses than with spectacles in patients with refractive errors and in patients with keratoconus and irregular corneas. Gains are largest in appearance, activities, psychological well-being, and independence domains. In keratoconus and corneal ectasia, successful contact lens fitting frequently restores near-normal QoL and delays or avoids keratoplasty. The evidence comparing VR-QOL with contact lenses directly to refractive surgery (LASIK/PRK) mostly found more favorable VR-QoL results with surgery;57,58,70,73 however, the findings were not completely consistent and some evidence suggested that well-fitted contact lenses may match or sometimes exceed the VR-QoL benefits of surgery.63

Ongoing challenges of contact lenses may include cost, access in lower-resource settings, and managing lens-related dry eye and an increased risk of eye infections. Access may be more limited in lower-resource settings, where professional fitting, regular eye examinations, supply chains for quality lenses, and education on proper use are more challenging. Contact lens-associated dry eye affects approximately 30–50% of wearers and is a leading cause of discomfort, fluctuating vision, reduced wearing time, and discontinuation.3,9 Patients can often ameliorate symptoms through frequent use of preservative-free rewetting drops, daily disposable lenses, proper hydration, humidifiers, and screen breaks, while clinicians help by optimizing lens materials (eg, silicone hydrogel with better wettability), assessing the ocular surface, and prescribing anti-inflammatory therapies when needed.121,122 Similarly, although the reported risk of microbial keratitis low (2–20 cases per 10,000 wearers annually), it is higher with overnight wear and can lead to more serious complications.123,124 This risk is reduced through strict patient hygiene practices including handwashing, fresh solution use, avoiding water exposure, and never sleeping in lenses unless approved, combined with clinician education, regular monitoring, and preferential fitting of daily disposables.123,125 Innovations in contact lens materials, fitting philosophy, and adjunctive therapies continue to widen the therapeutic window. These developments collectively may help mitigate limitations, making contact lenses more viable and effective for a wider range of patients.

As patient populations and contact lens innovations continue to evolve, clinicians and researchers should continue to utilize validated VR-QoL instruments to quantify contact lens treatment success given that clinical measures such as visual acuity, wearing time, or even objective signs of ocular surface health consistently fail to capture the full patient experience and predict long-term success or satisfaction.3,9,10 PROMs for evaluating vision correction have evolved over the past 25 years to robust, validated instruments that effectively discern VR-QoL19–21 and are sensitive to subtle issues like end-of-day discomfort, dryness, and visual fluctuations.15,20

To our knowledge, this is the only contemporary literature review that summarizes the evidence for VR-QoL across all types of contact lens wearers. Some previous studies, however, have summarized the literature evaluating the impact of contact lenses on VR-QoL in more restricted populations. Two recent literature reviews evaluated VR-QoL among patients with keratoconus more broadly. Rafizadeh et al (2025)12 summarized the economic burden of keratoconus and the effectiveness of various treatment modalities in improving VR-QoL outcomes. Another study by Sari et al (2025)13 assessed the VR-QoL of patients with keratoconus who had not undergone surgical interventions by evaluating domain scores such as symptoms, activity limitation, psychological changes, mental health and social wellbeing. A literature review by Mushtaq et al (2025)14 assessed the long-term effectiveness (ie, visual acuity, comfort, and VR-QoL) of scleral lens treatment in the management of keratoconus. Kandel et al (2022)15 summarized evidence on VR-QoL with long-term contact lens wear among patients with myopia. Finally, Chaudhary et al (2023)16 summarized the role of soft lenses and rigid gas-permeable scleral lenses in the management of dry eye disease associated and associated ocular surface disorders. Consistent with our literature review, these previous literature reviews also found favorable VR-QoL impacts of contact lenses.

Limitations of this review mostly relate to the heterogeneity of the patients, range of conditions and condition impairment, variation of study designs, and method of VR-QoL evaluation. Although nonrandomized cohort studies or observational studies may provide more “real-world” representations of patient outcomes, differences in the population and study characteristics can increase the variation in results. These differences may make it challenging to synthesize the findings across studies. There were also some gaps in the literature that were identified. Pediatric keratoconus is aggressive and socially debilitating; however, evidence evaluating contact lens VR-QoL among pediatric keratoconus is scant.86 There is also a lack of head-to-head trials for certain modalities. For example, there was an absence of studies directly comparing multifocal contact lenses to multifocal spectacles for fall rates. Multifocal or progressive spectacles have been linked to increased fall risk due to distortions in the lower visual field.74 Contact lenses may offer advantages because they move with the eyes;75 therefore, this could be an important area of future research.

Conclusions

Despite some gaps in evidence, this literature review demonstrated the existence of substantial data reporting on the VR-QOL benefits of contact lenses among adult and pediatric patients with refractive error (myopia, hyperopia, astigmatism, and presbyopia), keratoconus and irregular corneal conditions, ocular surface disease/dry eye, amblyopia, photophobia, post-corneal crosslinking, and allergy. The VR-QoL benefits of contact lenses frequently exceed those achieved with spectacles. Evidence comparing VR-QOL with contact lenses directly to refractive surgery (LASIK/PRK) mostly found more favorable VR-QoL results with surgery; however, the findings were not completely consistent and some evidence suggested that well-fitted contact lenses may match or sometimes exceed the VR-QoL benefits of surgery. Our research provides a comprehensive summary of contemporary published evidence for the impact of contact lenses on VR-QoL. The incremental estimates of VR-QoL impact that were observed may provide useful benchmark references against which other studies of contact lens VR-QoL can be compared.
