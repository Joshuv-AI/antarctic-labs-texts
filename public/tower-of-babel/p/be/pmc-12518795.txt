Frequency, temperature, and strain effects on dielectric tunability and energy storage performance of P(VDF-TrFE-CFE) terpolymer
Authors: Ying Chieh Hu, Su Min Lee, Kyung Hoon Kim, Hyun Soo Ahn, Jong Hun Kim, Jong Hoon Jung
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractIn this study, the dielectric and ferroelectric responses of P(VDF-TrFE-CFE) terpolymer were systematically investigated under various DC electric fields, frequencies, temperatures, and mechanical strains. The dielectric constant exhibited pronounced decrease at low frequencies under the influence of DC bias. This effect was particularly evident at temperatures where dielectric dispersion was significant. These results indicate that the dynamics of polar nanoregions are of critical importance in both high dielectric tunability (67% at 100 MV/m) and frequency-dependent dielectric behavior. The polarization–electric field hysteresis loops became increasingly slim at elevated temperatures and at high frequencies. These results indicate that the thermal activation and dynamic behavior of polar nanoregions significantly influence the high discharged energy density (3.8 J/cm3 at 200 MV/m) and storage efficiency (65% at 102 Hz and 300 K). Additionally, the inherent mechanical flexibility of the P(VDF-TrFE-CFE) film leads to highly stable dielectric and ferroelectric properties under compressive/tensile strains and after extensive bending cycles. This work demonstrates that the control of polar nanodomains is a key strategy to enhance the dielectric tunability and energy storage performance of P(VDF-TrFE-CFE) terpolymers for portable and wearable electronics applications.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1038/s41598-025-19461-1.Keywords: P(VDF-TrFE-CFE), Relaxor ferroelectric, Dielectric tunability, Energy storage, Polar nanoregionsSubject terms: Engineering, Materials science, Nanoscience and technology, Physics

Introduction

The development of materials that combine mechanical flexibility with high electrical performance has become a key research focus, driven by the growing demand for portable and wearable devices in the era of the Internet of Things (IoT)1−3. Compared to bulk materials, thin films have attracted considerable attention due to their lightweight nature, high integration density, and low power consumption4−7. Among flexible dielectric films, PVDF-based polymers have emerged as promising candidates to address these requirements. These polymers not only offer easy processability but also allow for tunable mechanical and electrical properties through the introduction of defects, or by modifying solvents and crosslinking agents8−11.

The P(VDF-TrFE-CFE) terpolymer exhibits a characteristic property known as relaxor ferroelectricity12. Similar to ceramic-based relaxor ferroelectrics, the P(VDF-TrFE-CFE) shows both a high dielectric constant and a slim ferroelectric hysteresis loop near room temperature. Numerous studies have reported enhanced dielectric tunability and increased energy storage density by quenching the polymer during crystallization and by introducing organic or inorganic defects13−20. Most previous studies have focused on evaluating material properties under limited conditions, while systematic investigations into the changes in dielectric and ferroelectric behaviors under external stimuli, such as electric field, frequency, and temperature, remain insufficient. In particular, there is a lack of research on the stability of material properties under varying mechanical strain, which is critical for ensuring the operational reliability of flexible devices.

In this paper, we fabricated high-quality P(VDF-TrFE-CFE) terpolymers and systematically investigated their dielectric tunability and energy storage characteristics under a range of external stimuli: DC bias fields (0–100 MV/m), frequencies (1–103 Hz or 102–106 Hz), temperatures (260–360 K), and mechanical strain (−1.5–+1.5%). Notably, enhanced dielectric tunability (67%) was observed at low frequencies and specific temperature ranges, likely due to contributions from the dynamic response of polar nanoregions (PNRs). Furthermore, the increased energy density (3.8 J/cm3) was observed in low frequency and elevated temperature due to the thermal activation and dynamic behavior of PNRs. Both the dielectric tunability and energy storage density remained stable under compressive/tensile strains of ±1.5% and repeated bending cycles of 104.

Results and discussion

Figure 1a shows a digital image of a bent Au-coated P(VDF-TrFE-CFE)/ITO-PET film with dimensions of 2 cm × 2 cm. The film is flexible and can be bent with fingers. It consists of a uniform P(VDF-TrFE-CFE) layer with a thickness of ~ 330 nm on a 127 μm-thick transparent and flexible ITO-PET substrate (Fig. S1). As shown in Fig. S2, the coated polymer surface exhibits a uniform microstructure with no visible cracks or voids. The surface roughness (Rq) is measured to be ~ 2.6 nm, indicating a smooth and uniform surface. The film also demonstrates optical transparency, with a transmittance exceeding 80% in the visible range (Fig. S3).

Figure 1b shows the Fourier-transform infrared (FT-IR) spectra of the P(VDF-TrFE-CFE) terpolymer. The spectrum reveals that the polymer contains a mixture of chain conformations, including TG (α-like phase), Tm>4 (β-like phase), and T3G (γ-like phase) sequences21,22. Each peak corresponds to the vibrational mode associated with a specific chain conformation, providing qualitative insight into the polymer’s microstructure. Note that the β-like phase consists of all-trans (TTTT) conformation and exhibits ferroelectric behavior, whereas the α-like phase adopts an alternating trans–gauche (TGTG′) conformation and is paraelectric. The γ-like phase, which has an intermediate TTTGTTTG′ structure, lies between the two in both conformation and polarity. Approximate analysis of the FT-IR spectra revealed that the chain conformation of P(VDF-TrFE-CFE) is dominated by TG and T3G sequences, while all-trans (Tm>4) sequences are present in relatively small fractions (Note S1). These results indicate that the presence of CFE monomers in the polymer suppresses the formation of the ferroelectric phase and instead promotes relaxor ferroelectric behavior by altering the chain structure12,23. Consistently, the X-ray diffraction (XRD) pattern in Fig. 1c shows no distinct peaks corresponding to the α- or γ-like phases typically associated with PVDF; instead, a (110)/(200) peak characteristic of the relaxor ferroelectric phase is observed21,24.

Figure 1d presents the leakage current density–electric field (J−E) curve of the P(VDF-TrFE-CFE) terpolymer. The measured leakage current density exhibited asymmetric behavior depending on the polarity of the applied field, which is attributed to the difference in contact properties between the top Au electrode and the bottom ITO electrode. At an electric field of 100 MV/m, the leakage current density was approximately 10-5 to 10-6 A/cm², indicating that the terpolymer possesses adequate insulating properties. In the log–log plot of the J−E curve shown in Fig. S4, the slope ranges from about 1.13 to 1.57. This suggests that conduction is primarily governed by a thermally activated free-electron process following a linear Ohmic mechanism within the measured field range (0–100 MV/m)25. Such Vogel–Fulcher–type relaxor behavior originates from the dynamics of polar nanoregions (PNRs)28,31,32. Above the freezing temperature, the PNRs remain dynamically active, which enables them to readily respond to external stimuli even at room temperature.

Figure 2 explores the dielectric tunability of the P(VDF-TrFE-CFE) terpolymer under varying electric fields, frequencies, and temperatures. Figure 2a and b show the dielectric constant and loss tan δ, respectively, as functions of the applied DC bias at room temperature. Both properties decrease significantly as the DC bias increases, as similarly observed in relaxor ceramics33–36. This behavior is particularly pronounced at low frequencies (102–104 Hz) for the dielectric constant and at high frequencies (104–106 Hz) for the dielectric loss. Accordingly, the dielectric tunability remains around 67% at low frequencies, and begins to decrease above 104 Hz, reaching 56% at 105 Hz and 46% at 106 Hz (Fig. 2c). Note that, the dielectric tunability was calculated using the relation of [ε(0) − ε(E)]/ε(0)×100%, where ε(0) and ε(E) are the dielectric constant without bias and under a given DC field, respectively. Note that, Wang et al.37, reported the 80% of dielectric tunability at high field of 240 MV/m in highly aligned molecular structure of P(VDF-TrFE-CFE). It suggests that enhanced dielectric tunability can be further achieved through the molecular alignment and process optimization. Moreover, the dielectric tunability of P(VDF-TrFE-CFE) relaxor ferroelectric polymer is comparable to that of (Ba, Sr)TiO3 (~ 86%)38, Pb(Zr, Ti)O3 (~ 80%)33,34, and Pb(Mg1/3Nb2/3)O3–PbTiO3 (~ 78%)35 relaxor ferroelectric ceramics and epitaxial thin films.

To further investigate the frequency-dependent dielectric behavior under DC bias, we analyzed the dielectric constant in the frequency range from 10² Hz to 10⁴ Hz using the empirical relation of (Fig. S6) and extracted the values of the intercept (ε0) and slope (α)33–36. This approach enabled us to isolate the extrinsic contribution associated with PNR dynamics in the P(VDF-TrFE-CFE) terpolymer. In relaxor ferroelectric ceramics, dielectric tunability has primarily been explained by the motion of domain walls. In contrast, relaxor ferroelectric polymers are composed of ferroelectric and paraelectric chains, and amorphous regions. The ferroelectric chains are polar and form a domain, which is surrounded by paraelectric domains and amorphous regions. Such ferroelectric domains could be considered polar nanoregions, while the domain wall is difficult to define. Therefore, the dynamics of PNRs, rather than domain walls, are more appropriate to explain the large dielectric tunability observed in relaxor ferroelectric polymers.

Figure 2d shows the variation of the intercept ε₀ as a function of applied DC bias. The curve can be divided into two distinct linear regions, with a transition occurring around 60 MV/m. In the low-field region (20–50 MV/m), the intercept εlow reflects a combination of intrinsic and extrinsic contributions. The corresponding slope αlow mainly represents the tunability of the extrinsic response and indicates how extrinsic influences are suppressed under increasing DC field. In the high-field region (70–100 MV/m), PNRs are aligned with the electric field and their size becomes saturated. The intercept εhigh corresponds to the intrinsic dielectric constant, while the slope αhigh reflects the residual tunability of the intrinsic response, which also becomes suppressed under strong DC fields. Notably, αlow is nearly three times larger than αhigh, suggesting that extrinsic contributions are more easily suppressed by the applied field. Similarly, εlow is approximately twice as large as εhigh, confirming the dominant role of extrinsic effects in the low-field regime.

Under high DC bias, the enlarged and aligned PNRs suppresses their dynamics, which can explain the observed decreases in dielectric constant and loss tanδ shown in Fig. 2a and b, respectively. The P(VDF-TrFE-CFE) terpolymer forms localized PNRs due to the disruption of long-range ferroelectric ordering, which is induced by the incorporation of CFE units as structural defects into the ferroelectric P(VDF-TrFE)34. These features were confirmed by FT-IR analysis (Fig. 1b), which revealed the predominant presence of paraelectric (TG) and weakly ferroelectric (T3G) chain conformations, along with minor fractions of ferroelectric all-trans (Tm>4) conformations. Under an applied electric field, dipole alignment along the field direction becomes energetically favorable, inducing a conformational transition from TG and T3G sequences to the all-trans sequences via dipole switching–based chain rotation. Simultaneously, amorphous chains may also align with the electric field direction39–41. These field-induced structural transitions directly influence the formation, growth, and alignment of PNRs. In particular, the initial TG and T3G sequences are nonpolar or weakly polar, contributing little to PNR formation. However, upon transformation to all-trans sequences under an electric field, these domains acquire a locally ordered polar structure, thereby functioning as active PNRs. As a result, new PNRs may be generated, or existing ones may grow and align in response to the applied field. As the electric field increases, PNRs based on all-trans sequences become increasingly aligned along the field direction, leading to a gradual suppression of dipolar dynamics. This behavior has a direct impact on the dielectric response, including a reduced dielectric constant and changes in loss tan δ.

The temperature dependence of dielectric tunability was further investigated in Fig. 2e and f at 10³ Hz and 10⁶ Hz, respectively. A common trend observed in both curves is that the electric field corresponding to the dielectric maximum shifts toward higher fields as the temperature decreases. This implies that at lower temperatures, stronger electric fields are required to induce dipole alignment due to enhanced ferroelectric order. However, as the temperature decreases, the overall dielectric constant declines, and the difference between the maximum and minimum dielectric constant (i.e., dielectric tunability) is reduced. This behavior can be attributed to the reduced PNR dynamics at low temperatures, which limits the overall dielectric response. Conversely, at elevated temperatures, the field corresponding to the dielectric maximum shifts closer to zero, indicating a transition toward paraelectric behavior, where dipoles can be more easily reoriented even at the weak external field. Nonetheless, the difference between the maximum and minimum dielectric constant also decreases with increasing temperature, likely due to excessive dynamic activity of PNRs, which weakens field-induced dipole alignment. As a result, the effect of DC field on the dielectric response is reduced, and hence the dielectric tunability decreases. Figure 2g quantitatively illustrates the variation in dielectric tunability with temperature. The maximum tunability is observed near 300 K at 103 Hz and around 320 K at 106 Hz. These temperatures are close to the dielectric phase transition temperature, i.e. Tm, for each frequency, indicating that tunability is more strongly influenced by the temperature sensitivity of the polar response. The temperature dependence of dielectric tunability closely follows the temperature profile of the dielectric constant, with maximum tunability occurring near the phase transition. This reflects the enhanced dynamic behavior of PNRs in the vicinity of the transition temperature42.

Figure 3 investigates the effects of electric field, frequency, and temperature on the energy storage behavior of the P(VDF-TrFE-CFE) terpolymer. Figure 3a, b, and c display unipolar polarization–electric field (P–E) hysteresis loops measured as a function of electric field, frequency, and temperature, respectively. At room temperature and a frequency of 100 Hz, increasing the electric field from 20 MV/m to 200 MV/m results in increases in both the maximum polarization (Pmax) and remanent polarization (Pr). The P–E loops are narrow with small remanence, which is a characteristic feature of relaxor ferroelectric materials. This behavior is attributed in part to the enhanced alignment of PNRs with increasing field strength, together with field-induced conformational transitions of TG and T3G sequences into all-trans sequences via dipole switching. Under a fixed electric field of 200 MV/m at room temperature, increasing the frequency from 1 Hz to 103 Hz leads to a gradual decrease in Pmax. This is attributed to the inability of PNRs to follow the rapid changes in the applied field at higher frequencies, resulting in reduced dipole alignment. At a fixed frequency of 102 Hz and an electric field of 200 MV/m, the P–E loops measured at different temperatures reveal that Pmax decreases at lower temperatures. This reduction is attributed to suppressed chain mobility due to limited thermal energy, which hinders dipole rotation and conformational transitions43. Conversely, an increase in Pr at higher temperatures is likely associated with enhanced chain mobility, leading to delayed dipole relaxation and increased dielectric loss44.

As the electric field increases, the discharged energy density increases almost linearly, reaching approximately 3.8 J/cm3 at 200 MV/m. This enhancement is attributed to increased alignment of PNRs and pronounced dipole switching, which lead to a larger polarization difference between Pmax and Pr. Meanwhile, the storage efficiency remains around 60% at electric fields above 50 MV/m, indicating relatively low hysteresis loss even under high-field conditions. In the frequency-dependent analysis, the highest discharged energy density (3.8 J/cm3) and storage efficiency (65%) were observed at 102 Hz. These values are comparable to those previously reported in P(VDF-TrFE-CFE)47. It also implies that there remains considerable potential for further improvement through strategies such as polymer blending, multilayer structuring, and nanocomposite design13–20. As frequency increases, PNRs are unable to respond to the rapidly changing electric field, limiting dipole alignment. Consequently, both energy density and efficiency decrease at high frequencies. On the other hand, conduction losses also contribute at low frequencies, leading to a further reduction in efficiency. As temperature increases, the discharged energy density increases up to approximately 330 K. This is due to enhanced chain mobility at elevated temperatures, which facilitates dipole reorientation and conformational transitions. However, at excessively high temperatures, increased chain mobility leads to higher dielectric losses, resulting in reduced efficiency. The maximum storage efficiency (65%) is observed at 300 K, which can be interpreted as an optimal balance between dipole alignment and energy loss. These results demonstrate that external stimuli—such as electric field, frequency, and temperature—strongly influence the energy storage performance of the P(VDF-TrFE-CFE) terpolymer through the dynamic behavior of PNRs and field-induced chain conformational transitions.

Figure 4 investigates the mechanical stability of dielectric tunability and energy storage performance of the P(VDF-TrFE-CFE) terpolymer. As a preliminary step, the resistance of the ITO-PET substrate was measured under various bending strains (Fig. S7). The resistance remained nearly constant up to a strain of 2.0%, but increased rapidly beyond this point, likely due to the formation of microcracks in the ITO layer. These results indicate that the electrical, dielectric, and ferroelectric properties of the bent P(VDF-TrFE-CFE) film can be reliably evaluated under strains below 2.0%, where the resistance of the bottom ITO electrode remains stable. This strain range also corresponds to the elastic deformation regime of the P(VDF-TrFE-CFE) terpolymer. Details of the strain calculation from the bending radius of the P(VDF-TrFE-CFE)/ITO-PET film are provided in the Note S2 and Fig. S8. To precisely apply compressive and tensile strains, an aluminum holder with a defined curvature was fabricated (see Fig. S9), and the P(VDF-TrFE-CFE)/ITO–PET film was fixed onto the holder.

The strain varied from −1.5% to +1.5% at the interval of 0.5%. Figure 4a and b present the dielectric constant and unipolar polarization–electric field (P–E) hysteresis loops of the P(VDF-TrFE-CFE) terpolymer measured at room temperature under varying strains from − 1.5% (compressive) to + 1.5% (tensile). Both the frequency-dependent dielectric response and unipolar polarization characteristics showed a slight decrease under compressive strain and a slight increase under tensile strain. These variations are attributed to changes in polymer chain alignment and microstructural deformation induced by the applied mechanical strain48. When the P(VDF-TrFE-CFE) is subjected to compressive and tensile deformation, the ferroelectric and paraelectric domain alignments would be altered along the strain direction. In addition, compressive and tensile strains can also induce changes in the geometrical characteristics, such as the size of the microstructure in P(VDF-TrFE-CFE).

Figure 4c and d present the variations in dielectric tunability and discharged energy density at 103 Hz as a function of applied mechanical strain. The dielectric tunability ranged from 67.2 to 68.3%, while the discharged energy density varied slightly between 3.71 J/cm3 and 3.75 J/cm3. These results suggest that, compared to temperature or electric field, mechanical strain has a relatively minor influence on the dielectric and polarization properties of the P(VDF-TrFE-CFE) terpolymer. Figure 4e evaluates the mechanical durability of dielectric tunability and discharged energy density under repeated bending. After 10,000 bending cycles, the dielectric tunability slightly decreased from 67.2 to 63.9%, while the discharged energy density remained nearly unchanged. As shown in Fig. S10, the dielectric response and unipolar polarization characteristics before and after bending cycles showed negligible difference, demonstrating strong mechanical robustness and reliability under mechanical stress. These findings confirm that the P(VDF-TrFE-CFE) terpolymer exhibits excellent mechanical stability, making it suitable for use in flexible electronic device applications.

Conclusions

In this study, we have investigated the effects of DC bias, frequency, temperature, and strain on the dielectric tunability and energy storage performance of spin-coated 330 nm-thick P(VDF-TrFE-CFE) terpolymers. Fourier-transform infrared spectroscopy reveals that the P(VDF-TrFE-CFE) terpolymer consists mainly of TG and T3G chain conformations, along with minor amounts of Tm>4. Strong dielectric dispersion with respect to both temperature and frequency is indicative of relaxor ferroelectricity, with an activation energy of 124 mV and a freezing temperature of 191 K. The dielectric constant significantly decreases at low-frequency, compared to high-frequency, under DC bias due to the diminished dynamic movement of aligned and enlarged polar nanoregions. Consequently, the dielectric tunability of P(VDF-TrFE-CFE) is estimated to be 67% at 102 Hz and 46% at 106 Hz at room temperature. The dielectric tunability closely follows the temperature and frequency profile of the dielectric constant so that it is maximized at 300 K at 103 Hz and 320 K at 106 Hz. It may reflect that the temperature-dependent dynamic behavior of PNRs strongly affects the dielectric tunability of P(VDF-TrFE-CFE) terpolymer. The maximum polarization gradually decreases while the hysteresis loop becomes slim with increasing frequency due to the finite frequency response of PNRs and reduced dielectric loss. Consequently, the maximum discharged energy density (3.8 J/cm3) and efficiency (65%) are obtained at 102 Hz. The discharged energy density increases with temperature due to the enhanced chain mobility induced dipole reorientation and conformational transition. However, the increased dielectric loss at high temperature causes a slight decrease of temperature for maximized efficiency. Both the dielectric tunability and energy storage performance are demonstrated to be unaffected by compressive/tensile strains of ±1.5% and excessive bending cycles of 10,000. This work provides a systematic investigation of the dielectric and ferroelectric properties of P(VDF-TrFE-CFE) under various stimuli and provides a plausible route to enhance the dielectric tunability and energy storage performance for the application of future electronic devices, including wearable dielectric modulation components, energy storage systems, and field-effect transistors.

Methods

Fabrication

P(VDF-TrFE-CFE) terpolymer powder (63/30/7 mol%, Arkema Piezotech, France) was dissolved in N, N-dimethylformamide (purity > 99.8%, Sigma Aldrich, USA) at a concentration of 5 wt%. The solution was stirred at room temperature for 12 h to obtain a clear and homogeneous solution. The prepared P(VDF-TrFE-CFE) solution was filtered using a 0.2 μm PTFE syringe filter, and cast onto an ITO-PET substrate (Sigma Aldrich, USA) under a dried Ar atmosphere in a glove box at room temperature. The cast solution was subsequently spin-coated at 3,000 rpm for 30 s to form a uniform film. The P(VDF-TrFE-CFE)/ITO-PET film was dried on a hot plate at 60 °C for 1 h to evaporate the organic solvent. Then, the film was annealed at 120 °C for 6 h in a dry oven to enhance the crystallinity.

Characterization

A high-resolution X-ray diffractometer (SmartLab SE, Rigaku, Japan) with Cu-Kα radiation was used to identify the crystal structure and polymorphic phase of P(VDF-TrFE-CFE). A Fourier-transform infrared spectrometer with attenuated total reflectance mode (VERTEX 80 V, Bruker, USA) was used to analyze the volume fraction of chain conformations. The surface morphology and thickness of the film was examined using a field-emission scanning electron microscopy (SU8010, Hitachi, Japan) as well as an atomic force microscopy (FX-40, Park Systems Corp., Republic of Korea), and a surface profilometer (Dektak 150, Veeco, USA), respectively. Optical transmission of the P(VDF-TrFE-CFE)/ITO-PET film was measured by UV-Vis-NIR spectrophotometer (Cary 5E, Varian, USA). Complex dielectric constant and ferroelectric hysteresis loop at various temperatures were investigated using an LCR meter (4284 A, Hewlett-Packard, USA) and a ferroelectric tester system (TF 1000, aixACCT systems, Germany), respectively, with a l-N2 cooled probe station (MPS-LN, Nextron). The P–E hysteresis loops were obtained in the frequency range of 1–103 Hz and in the temperature range of 250–340 K after the consideration of the limitation of instruments and the detailed investigation of relaxor behaviors, respectively. Leakage current and electric breakdown were examined using the two-probe method (Keithley 6517 A, Keithley instruments, USA). Prior to dielectric, ferroelectric, and electrical measurements, Au was deposited by a sputter (Sputter coater 108auto, Cressington, UK) to form a top electrode. A custom mechanical system (Jaeil Optical system, Republic of Korea) and carved aluminum holders were used to apply mechanical stress to the film.
