Biomimetic hybrid nanocarriers hitchhike neutrophils for targeted ginsenoside Rc delivery to BMSCs: ameliorating postmenopausal osteoporosis via iron homeostasis regulation
Authors: Yi He, Depeng Fang, Binjie Huang, Xiang Chen, Zhengmin Zhang, Zihan Wang, Qing Jiang, Baosheng Guo, Weidong Xiao
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Postmenopausal osteoporosis (PMOP), a prevalent skeletal disorder among postmenopausal women, is hallmarked by heightened fracture susceptibility. Estrogen deficiency in this condition impairs the endogenous antioxidant defense system, triggering iron overload within the bone marrow mesenchymal stem cell (BMSC) niche and disrupting bone metabolic homeostasis. Guided by this mechanistic framework, we focused on ginsenoside Rc (Rc), a bioactive phytochemical endowed with capacities to enhance antioxidant defenses and potential iron-regulating activities. We engineered DT-NPNs/Rc via a pathogen-mimetic strategy, entailing hybridization of bacterial outer membrane vesicles (OMVs) with BMSC-derived membranes for encapsulation of Rc. These nanoconstructs exploit the bone marrow-homing properties of circulating neutrophils for hitchhiking, followed by specific internalization by BMSCs to achieve targeted Rc delivery. Functional assessments revealed that DT-NPNs/Rc mitigates lipid peroxidation through SIRT1/p53 axis, thereby restoring the osteogenic differentiation capacity of BMSCs under iron overload. Notably, in vivo studies confirmed that DT-NPNs/Rc effectively attenuates ovariectomy (OVX)-induced bone loss. Collectively, by overcoming the limitations of conventional delivery systems in targeting, DT-NPNs/Rc offered an innovative, highly efficient, and clinically translatable platform for PMOP treatment.Graphical Abstract The study developed DT-NPNs/Rc using a pathogen-mimicry strategy, hybridizing bacterial outer membrane vesicles (OMVs) with BMSCs membranes and loading ginsenoside Rc. The hybrid version with capabilities of penetrating the bone marrow-blood barrier (BMBB) and enhancing BMSCs targeting is a promising research pathway to both optimize the treatment effects and to develop individualized treatments to improve outcomes. Introduction

Postmenopausal osteoporosis (PMOP) ranks among the most prevalent metabolic bone disorders globally. It is defined by reduced bone mineral density and impaired bone microarchitecture, which collectively lead to a marked increase in fragility fracture risk [1, 2]. Current clinical management of PMOP relies primarily on traditional interventions, including bisphosphonates, calcitonin preparations, and estrogen replacement therapy [3]. However, these conventional treatments are hindered by significant clinical limitations: long-term bisphosphonate administration carries risks of severe complications such as bisphosphonate-related osteonecrosis of the jaw and atypical femoral fractures, fail to satisfy clinical demands [4, 5].

Bone marrow mesenchymal stem cells (BMSCs) have emerged as increasingly promising therapeutic targets in PMOP, owing to their inherent capacity to facilitate bone regeneration and repair [6, 7]. In bone metabolic homeostasis, the differentiation of BMSCs into osteoblasts constitutes a pivotal process in bone formation [8, 9]. However, postmenopausal estrogen deficiency induces systemic iron overload in patients, resulting in ferroptosis of BMSCs [10]. Ferroptosis, a distinct iron-dependent form of regulated cell death driven by lipid peroxidation, is characterized by excessive reactive oxygen species (ROS) accumulation, elevated lipid peroxidation byproducts (e.g., malondialdehyde (MDA), 4-hydroxynonenal (4-HNE)), and hallmark mitochondrial perturbations [11]. Notably, the mitochondrial dysfunction intrinsic to ferroptosis, such as impaired electron transport chain integrity and attenuated ATP synthesis, exacerbates the redox and bioenergetic imbalance in BMSCs, further amplifying their functional impairment [12, 13]. Ferroptosis in BMSCs significantly impairs their osteogenic differentiation potential while enhancing the bone resorptive activity of osteoclasts, which disrupts the balance between bone formation and resorption and ultimately results in bone loss [14]. Thus, the development of targeted therapeutic strategies that selectively modulate BMSC ferroptosis and enhance osteogenic differentiation represents a pivotal approach to addressing the unmet challenges in PMOP management.

Panax, traditionally valued for treatment of osteopenia and fortify bone integrity [15–17]. Ginsenoside Rc (Rc), a key Panax constituent, inherits this osteoprotective potential for modern osteoporosis intervention [18]. Accumulating evidence indicated that ginsenosides Rc exerted regulatory effects in counteracting oxidative damage [19, 20], senescence [21], cancer [22], inflammation [23] and metabolic diseases [24]. Recent studies demonstrated that Rc enhanced BMSC osteogenesis via Runx2/Osterix and suppressed osteoclastogenesis through NF-κB/RANKL inhibition, directly targeting core pathogenic mechanisms of bone loss. However, its role in regulating lipid peroxidation and iron metabolism in BMSCs under iron overload remains poorly defined. Meanwhile, its clinical application is hindered by poor bioavailability and non-specific tissue distribution.

The bone marrow-blood barrier (BMBB) poses a critical obstacle for osteoporosis therapy by limiting drug access to the bone marrow niche [25]. An innovative strategy in bone - targeted drug delivery has emerged, utilizing bacterial membrane-coated nanoparticles to exploit neutrophil chemotaxis for precise cargo delivery [26]. Bacterial membranes are taken up by neutrophils via pathogen-associated molecular patterns (PAMPs) [27], enabling drug enrichment in bone marrow by chemokine CXCL4/CCL2. Meanwhile, BMSC membrane proteins enable secondary recognition and internalization by skeletal stem cells, thus facilitating intracellular drug delivery [28]. This biomimetic strategy synergistically leverages the innate migratory properties of neutrophils and the cell-specific recognition mechanisms of BMSCs membranes to concentrate therapeutic payloads within the bone marrow [29]. This strategy synergizes neutrophil-mediated BMBB penetration and BMSC-specific targeting, concentrating drug in bone marrow and markedly enhancing therapeutic precision in PMOP).

Based on the iron-overload environment in PMOP, this study proposes a pathogen-mimic nanoparticle strategy that hitchhikes on circulating neutrophils. By hybridizing bacterial outer membrane vesicles (OMVs) with BMSC membranes and loading them with ginsenoside Rc, the nanoparticles are recognized and internalized by neutrophils during circulation, enabling homing to the bone marrow. Simultaneously, they are specifically taken up by BMSCs, achieving targeted drug delivery. BMSCs from OVX mice exhibited reduced osteogenesis and increased lipid peroxidation

To explore the possible pathogenesis of postmenopausal osteoporosis, an OVX mouse model was established (Fig. 1a). The results of Micro-CT analysis indicated that the trabecular bone in the OVX group was significantly thinner than control mice, with a marked decrease in bone mineral density (BMD). Similarly, the ratio of bone volume to total volume (BV/TV) and trabecular number (Tb.N) were decreased in the OVX group compare to control group (Fig. 1b). Furthermore, BMSCs of two groups were isolated and cultured in the vitro for further analysis. Western blot revealed that the expression of osteogenic marker proteins COL1A and OCN in the BMSCs of OVX mice were significantly lower than in normal mice, indicating a reduced osteogenic differentiation capacity of BMSCs (Fig. 1c).

We conducted transcriptome sequencing on BMSCs derived from OVX mice and normal mice. The heatmap of differentially expressed genes showed notable differences in the gene expression between OVX and control groups (Fig. 1d). In comparison to the normal group, 2524 upregulated and182 downregulated genes were identified in the BMSCs of OVX mice (Fig. 1e). Gene Ontology (GO) analysis was performed on differentially expressed genes to elucidate their involvement in biological processes (BP), cellular components (CC), and molecular functions (MF) (Fig. 1f). Predominant processes included inflammatory responses, lipid metabolism, and cell differentiation; cellular components focused on the cell membrane, cytoplasm, and mitochondria; and molecular functions mainly involved protein binding, metal ion, among others (Fig. 1g). Additionally, genomic enrichment analysis was performed on these differentially expressed mRNAs, revealing changes in osteogenic ability, mineralization capacity, mitochondrial membrane potential regulation, reactive oxygen species metabolism, iron ion homeostasis, and response to iron ions in the BMSCs of OVX mice (Fig. 1h). These findings suggest that in lipid metabolism, mitochondrial function, and iron ion homeostasis may play a critical role in the BMSCs of OVX mice.

To further validate the sequencing analysis, we employed BDP 581/591 C11 lipid peroxidation probe (BDP staining) on BMSCs to evaluate changes in intracellular lipid peroxidation (Fig. 2a). The results showed that BMSCs in the control group predominantly exhibited red fluorescence with minimal green signals, while cells in the OVX group displayed green fluorescence, signifying elevated lipid peroxidation levels. Furthermore, Western blot analyses revealed that the levels of lipid peroxidation biomarkers MDA and 4-hydroxynonenal (4-HNE) were significantly heightened in the OVX group than control group (Fig. 2b). These results confirmed that accumulation of lipid peroxidation in BMSCs from OVX mice. Previous studies have shown that cellular lipid peroxidation and its product 4-HNE trigger mitochondrial apoptosis and ferroptosis. Transmission electron microscopy (TEM) revealed obvious mitochondrial alterations in OVX group, including vacuolization, reduced cristae, and membrane rupture (Fig. 2c). Mitochondrial superoxide staining (Fig. 2d) showed increased reactive oxygen species (ROS) production, consistent with mitochondrial dysfunction. Given the reported association between mitochondrial function and sirtuin (Sirt) family proteins, mRNA levels of Sirt1-Sirt7 were quantified (Fig. 2e). Notably, Sirt1 expression was significantly downregulated in OVX group, suggesting its potential role in mitochondrial impairment. Based on the critical role of mitochondria in diverse cell death mechanisms, TUNEL staining was performed (Fig. 2f). TUNEL staining showed increased apoptosis in OVX group than control group, corroborated by upregulated cleaved caspase-3 and BAX, and downregulated BCL-2 (Fig. 2g). Iron ion probe staining revealed stronger green fluorescence in OVX group (Fig. 2h), indicating iron overload, which may drive lipid peroxidation. Elevated expression of ferroptosis-related proteins P53, SLC7A11, and ALOX12 were found in OVX group compare to control group (Fig. 2i), further confirmed the process of ferroptosis in OVX group. Collectively, these findings demonstrated that excessive phospholipid peroxides accumulate in OVX group inducing mitochondrial damage and ferroptosis. The downregulation of Sirt1 may represent a key contributing factor in this pathological process.

Fabrication and Characterization of DT-NPNs/Rc nanoparticles

To achieve targeted drug delivery to BMSCs, a biomimetic nanodrug delivery vehicle (DT-NPNs/Rc) was constructed by hybridizing mouse BMSCs cell membrane (BMSCs-CM) with Escherichia coli (E. coli)-derived outer membrane vesicles (OMVs), followed by encapsulation of ginsenoside Rc-loaded nanoparticles (Fig. 3a). The biomimetic vehicle could hitchhike circulating neutrophils for bone marrow migration, while the surface antigens of BMSCs-CM enable specific targeting to BMSCs. Transmission electron microscopy (TEM) imaging (Fig. 3b) revealed a transparent membrane coating on the surface of DT-NPNs/Rc particles compared to NPN/Rc, confirming successful encapsulation by the hybrid membrane. Subsequent characterization of particle size (Fig. 3c) and zeta potential (Fig. 3d) showed that DT-NPNs/Rc had an average diameter of ~150 nm (slightly larger than NPN/Rc) and a surface potential of −5 mV, significantly higher than NPN/Rc. As shown in Fig. 3e, OmpA and CD90 were both expressed on DT-NPN/Rc, confirming the successful fusion of OMVs and BMSCs-CM. To further validate BMSCs-CM/OMVs hybridization, OMVs were double-labeled with fluorescent dyes NBD-PS and Rhodamine PE (Fig. 3f). Under a fluorescent spectrometer, a strong emission peak was observed at around 588 nm (red line). Mixing BMSCs with labeled OMVs increased the emission at 544 nm and decreased the emission at 588 nm (black line). The decreased fluorescence resonance energy transfer (FRET) effect was the fusion of OMVs and BMSCs-CM which extended the surface area of the vesicles and enlarged the distance between NBD-PS and Rhodamine PE. Meanwhile, different fluorescent dyes were used to label BMSCs-CM and OMVs separately. Fluorescence microscopy (Fig. 3g) showed overlapping red and green fluorescence signals in the hybrid membrane-based nanocarriers, conclusively demonstrating successful fabrication of the biomimetic nanoparticles. As is shown in Fig. 3h, DT-NPNs/Rc reached a release equilibrium at 12 h. This release profile suggests that the coated cell membrane acts as a physical barrier, enhancing the stability of DT-NPNs/Rc, providing sufficient time for effective targeting of bone marrow via venous circulation.

DT-NPNs/Rc nanoparticles promote osteogenic differentiation of BMSCs and inhibit osteoclastogenesis in vitro

Several investigations have documented iron accumulation in postmenopausal osteoporosis patients wherein excessive iron burden may disrupt the homeostasis of bone metabolism [30, 31]. An iron overload model was established using 200 µmol/L ferric ammonium citrate (FAC) to mimic iron excess in vitro. Under iron-overloaded conditions, BMSCs were treated with PBS, ginsenoside Rc, or DT-NPNs/Rc. Following 3 days, cellular viability was assessed via live/dead fluorescent staining. As showed in Fig. 4a, the DT-NPNs/Rc group exhibited little red fluorescence which indicated no significant cell death and confirming its good biocompatibility. After 7 days of different treatment, qRT-PCR for the expression of osteogenic differentiation markers (Runx2, Ocn, Opn, Alp, Col1a, and Bmp2) was performed in BMSCs. Notably, both Rc and DT-NPNs/Rc treatment restored osteogenic markers suppressed by iron-overloaded, while DT-NPNs/Rc exerted superior osteogenic recovery (Fig. 4b). Alkaline phosphatase (ALP) staining corroborated with qRT-PCR findings (Fig. 4c), as the DT-NPNs/Rc group exhibited dense positive staining that effectively restored the osteogenic capacity of BMSCs. Given the pivotal role of osteoclasts in postmenopausal bone resorption, the impact of DT-NPNs/Rc on osteoclastic differentiation was investigated. Murine bone marrow-derived macrophages (BMMs) were isolated and subjected to osteoclastogenic induction under iron-overloaded conditions. Seven days post-induction, RT-qPCR analysis revealed that iron overload potently upregulated osteoclastic marker genes (Nfatc1, Rankl, Fos, Cox2), whereas DT-NPNs/Rc treatment significantly attenuated this upregulation (Fig. 4d). Similarly, the staining of TRAP was also consistent with the results of qPCR. Collectively, these data demonstrate that DT-NPNs/Rc effectively ameliorated iron-overload-induced aberrant osteoclast differentiation, underscoring its therapeutic potential in osteoporotic bone loss.

DT-NPNs/Rc nanoparticles alleviate lipid peroxidation and mitochondrial dysfunction of BMSCs induced by iron overload

Accumulation of lipid peroxidation plays a critical role in iron overload-induced cell death. Under iron excess conditions, the expression of SIRT1 in BMSCs was significantly downregulated, whereas treatment with ginsenoside Rc and the biomimetic nanocarrier DT-NPNs/Rc potently activated SIRT1/p53 axis expression (Fig. 5a). Detection of lipid peroxidation and ferroptosis-related markers revealed that DT-NPNs/Rc reduced the expression of 4-HNE, while upregulated SLC7A11 (Fig. 5a). These results indicated that DT-NPNs/Rc alleviates iron overload-induced lipid peroxidation injury and ferroptosis in BMSCs by activating SIRT1. BDP fluorescence probe staining showed similar findings (Fig. 5b), as the DT-NPNs/Rc group exhibited significantly weakened green fluorescence, indicating the lowest level of lipid peroxidation among all groups. Further investigation of mitochondrial function showed that FAC treatment markedly increased mitochondrial superoxide production in BMSCs, which was effectively suppressed by DT-NPNs/Rc (Fig. 5c). TEM observation (Fig. 5d) revealed that FAC induced typical pathological changes in BMSCs mitochondria, including vacuolization, cristae reduction or disappearance, and increased membrane permeability. Although both Rc and DT-NPNs/Rc groups performed improved mitochondrial morphology, partial mitochondrial shrinkage was still observed in the Rc group, suggesting incomplete repair, whereas DT-NPNs/Rc-treated mitochondria showed near-normal structure. TUNEL staining (Fig. 5e) and apoptosis-related protein analysis (Fig. 5f) showed that FAC induced increased apoptosis in BMSCs, while DT-NPNs/Rc significantly reduced the expression of pro-apoptotic proteins (C-Caspase3, BAX) and upregulated anti-apoptotic protein BCL2. Collectively, iron overload in vitro induces lipid peroxidation and ferroptosis in BMSCs, leading to mitochondrial structural and functional damage. DT-NPNs/Rc restores mitochondrial homeostasis and inhibits apoptosis by activating the SIRT1/p53 axis, providing a novel therapeutic target for iron overload-related bone metabolic disorders.

Validation of neutrophil-mediated delivery of DT-NPNs/Rc to mouse bone marrow

To study whether the pathogen-mimicking DT-NPNs/Rc nanoparticles could delivery to the bone marrow via neutrophil chemotaxis, we labeled the nanoparticles with the fluorescent dye DiD and administered them via tail vein injection. Fluorescence imaging at 4 h revealed that DiD-labeled NPNs/Rc (DiD-NPNs/Rc) primarily accumulated in the liver, lung and spleen whereas DiD-DT-NPNs/Rc exhibited preferential localization in the liver, bone, and lung. At 24 h, DiD-NPNs/Rc persisted mainly in the liver, lung and spleen, with minimal signal detected in the bone. In contrast, DiD-DT-NPNs/Rc demonstrated widespread organ distribution, with robust fluorescence intensity observed in the bone tissue (Fig. 6b, d). Quantitative region-of-interest (ROI) analysis of femoral DiD signals confirmed significantly higher accumulation of DiD-DT-NPNs/Rc compared to DiD-DT-NPNs (Fig. 6c). In addition, a small amount of fluorescence signals of the DiD-DT-NPNs/Rc can be detected in bone tissue and liver, while no obvious fluorescence was observed in other tissues; the fluorescence intensity in both bone and liver decreased at 14 d post-injection. These results indicated that DT-NPNs/Rc nanoparticles possessed favorable bone-targeting ability and sustained retention in bone tissue.

To further investigate the cellular mechanisms underlying this targeting effect, bone marrow cells were harvested and analyzed by flow cytometry. As shown in our flow cytometric analysis, we identified and quantified the following cell populations in murine bone marrow: Ly6Ghi. neutrophils (CD45+, CD11b+, Ly6Ghi), basophils (CD45+, CD11b, Ly6G−, c-Kit+, Siglec-F), eosinophils (CD45+, CD11b+, Ly6G−, c-Kit−, Siglec-F+), macrophages (CD45+, CD11b+, Ly6C−, F4/80+), Ly6Clow monocytes (CD45+, CD11b+, F4/80−, Ly6G−, Ly6Clow), and BMSCs (CD45−, CD90+, CD29+) (Figure S2a). Notably, at both 4 h and 24 h post-injection, the proportion of DID+ cells within the Ly6Ghi neutrophil population was significantly elevated in the DT-NPNs/Rc group compared to controls which indicated that neutrophils are a major cellular target for the labeled nanoparticles (Fig. 6e and f). While DID+ frequencies were also increased in Ly6Clowmonocytes (considered mature monocytes) (Figure S2c) and macrophages (Figure S2d), the absolute counts of these cell populations were markedly lower than those of Ly6Ghineutrophils (Figure S2b). This observation strongly suggests that neutrophils are the primary cellular vehicles for the drug’s delivery to the bone marrow. The proportion of DID+ cells within BMSCs were significantly higher in DT-NPNs/Rc group compared to controls (Fig. 6g and h). This result suggests that DT-NPNs/Rc are effectively delivered to and internalized by BMSCs within the bone marrow microenvironment.

Collectively, these results demonstrated that pathogen-mimicking DT-NPNs/Rc nanoparticles exploited neutrophil trafficking to achieve targeted delivery to the bone marrow, where they are subsequently internalized by BMSCs (Fig. 6h).

DT-NPNs/Rc nanoparticles ameliorate osteoporosis in vivo

To evaluate the efficacy of DT-NPNs/Rc in postmenopausal bone loss, we employed an OVX mouse model. Treatments were administered via retro-orbital venous plexus injection twice weekly for 8 weeks. Micro-CT analysis of distal femurs revealed significant preservation of trabecular bone architecture in DT-NPNs/Rc-treated mice compared to OVX controls (Fig. 7a). Quantitative parameters, including BMD, BV/TV, BS/TV, and Tb.N, were employed to assess the quality of bone microarchitecture. The date performed that the BMD, BV/TV, BS/TV, and Tb.N were significantly elevated in the DT-NPNs/Rc group compared to that PBS and Rc groups (Fig. 7b), suggesting treatment of DT-NPNs/Rc could markly attenuate OVX-induced bone loss. Hematoxylin and eosin (HE) staining confirmed a significant increase in trabecular bone mass in DT-NPNs/Rc-treated mice compared to other groups (Fig. 7c). Masson’s trichrome staining further revealed enhanced collagen deposition and osteoid formation in the DT-NPNs/Rc group (Fig. 7d). Immunofluorescence analysis of OCN, a marker of osteoblast activity, showed robust positive staining in DT-NPNs/Rc-treated bone sections, indicating enhanced osteoblast differentiation and mineralization (Fig. 7e and Figure S3b). As expected, TRAP staining (Figure S3a) showed that the group of DT-NPNs/Rc exhibited a lower osteoclast content than other treatment groups. Meanwhile, we observed decreased levels of TNF-α and COX2 in the Rc and DT-NPNs/Rc groups by ELISA (Figure S3c). These findings demonstrated that DT-NPNs/Rc improve the inflammatory conditions in mice by reducing the expression of pro-inflammatory factors. To investigate the molecular mechanism underlying these effects, we assessed expression of SIRT1, a key regulator of osteogenesis and antioxidant defense. Immunofluorescence staining (Fig. 7f and Figure S3b) revealed significant upregulation of SIRT1 in DT-NPNs/Rc-treated bone tissues, suggesting that the nanoparticle formulation enhances intracellular delivery of Rc and potentiates SIRT1 activation. In addition, as shown in Figure S4a, no notable changes were found in the major organs after 8 weeks of administration. Besides, there was also no marked difference in liver and kidney function, such as ALT, AST, UREA, and CREA, after treatment of DT-NPN/Rc for 8 weeks (Figure S4b, c). All these results confirm that such bioengineered DT-NPN/Rc had remarkable in vivo biosafety and holds potential as a targeted drug nanocarrier in clinical applications.

Discussion

Our study introduced DT-NPNs/Rc, a biomimetic hybrid membrane nanocarrier that hitchhikes neutrophils to target BMSCs, delivering ginsenoside Rc to alleviate bone loss by regulating iron homeostasis.

The innovative design of DT-NPNs/Rc addresses key challenges in drug delivery for bone disorders. By hybridizing OMVs with BMSC membranes, the nanocarriers utilize PAMPs on OMVs to exploit neutrophil chemotaxis, a natural process where neutrophils migrate to bone marrow microenvironment. This “hitchhiking” strategy significantly enhances targeted accumulation in the bone marrow, overcoming the poor bioavailability and off-target effects of ginsenoside Rc in previous studies [32, 33]. Moreover, the integration of BMSC membranes enables specific recognition and internalization by BMSCs, ensuring precise drug release at the cellular level which is critical for modulating BMSC function under iron overload. This dual biomimetic approach, combining pathogen-neutrophil interactions and cell-cell recognition, offers a more efficient and cell-specific alternative to traditional nanoparticle systems lacking such biological homing mechanisms.

Postmenopausal estrogen deficiency is known to disrupt antioxidant defenses [34, 35], leading to iron accumulation in the BMSC niche [36]; however, the direct link between iron overload-induced ferroptosis in BMSCs and impaired osteogenic differentiation has only recently emerged as a key pathological mechanism. Our study shows that DT-NPNs/Rc-mediated delivery of ginsenoside Rc, thereby inhibiting lipid peroxidation induced by ferroptosis and restoring BMSC osteogenic capacity via the SIRT1/p53/SLC7A11 axis. This aligns with prior reports of SIRT1’s role in suppressing ferroptosis in other cell types and extends these findings to BMSCs under PMOP-specific iron overload. Importantly, the reduction in OVX-induced bone loss with DT-NPNs/Rc confirms that targeting BMSC ferroptosis via SIRT1 activation rebalance bone formation and resorption, addressing a critical gap in PMOP therapy and while current treatments (e.g., bisphosphonates) primarily focus on inhibiting osteoclast activity rather than enhancing osteoblast function [37, 38].

The therapeutic efficacy of DT-NPNs/Rc also highlights the potential of natural compounds like ginsenoside Rc in metabolic bone disorders. Ginsenoside have long been recognized for their anti-inflammatory and antioxidant properties, but their clinical translation has been limited by pharmacokinetic issues [39]. By overcoming these barriers through targeted delivery, our study validates ginsenoside Rc as a potent SIRT1 activator in PMOP and provides a framework for repurposing other natural products with similar mechanisms. This is particularly relevant given growing interest in nutraceutical-based therapies for age-related diseases, which often have favorable safety profiles compared to synthetic drugs.

Based on the pathogen mimicry strategy, this study has successfully constructed neutrophil-like nanoparticles. Leveraging the circulatory homing property inherent to neutrophils, the research achieved efficient bone marrow-targeted delivery of ginsenoside Rc. By reducing the level of lipid peroxidation in BMSCs, the DT-NPNs significantly promoted the differentiation of BMSCs into osteoblasts and exerted a marked effect in ameliorating bone loss. DT-NPNs/Rc represents a promising strategy to overcome the clinical limitations of conventional anti-osteoporotic drugs, such as poor bone marrow targeting, low bioavailability, and long-term side effects, thereby offering a novel precision therapeutic approach for PMOP. This platform could be utilized in combination with conventional drugs to reduce their required dosage and associated adverse effects (e.g., bisphosphonate-related osteonecrosis of the jaw). Moreover, through platform modification, its application could be extended to other iron overload-related bone metabolic disorders, such as senile and steroid-induced osteoporosis.

Meanwhile, DT-NPNs/Rc showed excellent stability in vivo that the phospholipid bilayer structure prevents rapid degradation of the PLGA core in the circulatory system. As shown in Fig. 6b-f, DT-NPNs/Rc is preferentially accumulated in the bone tissue at 24 h post-injection, with decresed distribution in the liver and lung. In contrast, the uncoated NPNs/Rc is rapidly sequestered by the liver/spleen with almost no bone marrow accumulation, confirming the specific targeting effect of the hybrid membrane. No obvious long-term retention of DT-NPNs/Rc was observed in major organs (heart, spleen, lung and kidney) at 7 d and 14 d post-administration in vivo fluorescence imaging (Figure S1c and d). Based on the biodistribution results and the physicochemical properties of DT-NPNs/Rc, a small portion of un-hitchhiked DT-NPNs/Rc may be cleared by Kupffer cells in the liver. Meanwhile, histological staining of major organs (Figure S4a) showed no obvious pathological damage (such as inflammation, necrosis, fibrosis) in the DT-NPNs/Rc treatment group compared with the control group. And serum biochemical indexes (ALT, AST, UREA, CREA) showed no significant differences between the DT-NPNs/Rc group and the control group, indicating few liver or kidney toxicity.

Despite such efforts, there still are some problems needs to solve in the present study. DT-NPNs are encumbered by substantial limitations pertaining to manufacturing and lack of pharmacokinetic/pharmacodynamic (PK/PD) validation in large-animal models. Batch-to-batch consistency is undermined by the variable membrane protein profiles of BMSCs which imped standardization. Future research will focus on targeted strategies spanning manufacturing optimization and PK/PD validation in large-animal models.

Conclusion

In this study, bioinformatics analysis identified ron homeostasis imbalance as key drivers of impaired BMSCs osteogenic differentiation. Based on these findings, we developed DT-NPNs/Rc using a pathogen-mimicry strategy, OMVs with BMSCs membranes and loading ginsenoside Rc. DT-NPNs hitchhike on circulating neutrophils, leveraging their bone marrow-homing property. DT-NPNs/Rc inhibited lipid peroxidation through activated SIRT1 to restore BMSCs’ osteogenic differentiation under iron overload. Notably, DT-NPNs/Rc effectively alleviated OVX-induced bone loss. This study offers novel insights into nano-drug delivery for bone-related diseases.

Materials and methods

Materials

The antibodies against OCN (A20800) and COL1A (AC012) were purchased from ABclonal. Primary antibodies targeting p53 (# 2524), Cleaved-Caspase 3 (#9661S), Bax (#2772S) and Bcl-2 (# 3498 S) were acquired from Cell Signaling Technology (Beverly, USA). The antibodies against SIRT1 (13161-1-AP), 4-HNE (68538-1-Ig), SLC7A11 (26864-1-AP) and MDA (ab27642) were purchased from proteintech and abcam.

Micro-CT analysis

Femoral bone architecture was assessed via micro-CT analysis. Micro-architectural properties of the calvaria were analyzed using a micro-CT scanner (VivaCT80; Scanco Medical AG, Switzerland). Scanning was performed at 70 kV, 114 µA, with a spatial resolution of 15.6 μm per pixel. A square region of interest (ROI) measuring 4 × 4 mm was selected around the midline suture. Bone mineral density (BMD) and total porosity percentage were subsequently quantified using SkyScan software.

Cell culture

Six-week-old male C57BL/6 mice were euthanized via cervical dislocation and immersed in 75% ethanol for 10 min. Next, hindlimb skin was removed and bone marrow flushed using a syringe preloaded with complete medium. The cell suspension was collected in a 15 mL centrifuge tube. After centrifugation at 300 × g for 10 min, the supernatant was discarded, and the pellet resuspended in 1 mL of complete medium. Cells were seeded into culture dishes and incubated at 37 °C with 5% CO₂. Upon reaching 70%–80% confluence, cells were passaged using standard trypsinization protocols.

Western blotting

Proteins were extracted by lysing cells in RIPA buffer (Nanjing Keygen Biotech Co., Ltd, China) and proteins were separated via SDS-PAGE and transferred to PVDF membranes. Membranes were blocked with 5% skim milk at 4 °C for 5 h, then incubated overnight at 4 °C with primary antibodies. The next day, membranes probed with HRP-conjugated secondary antibodies for 1 h at room temperature. Chemiluminescent signals were detected using ECL substrate (Cat# KF8003, Affinity Biosciences, China) and imaged.

Quantitative real-time PCR (qPCR) analysis

Total RNA was extracted from samples using Trizol reagent (RC112, Vazyme, China), followed by reverse transcription to cDNA (R122-01, Vazyme, China). qPCR was performed on a Real-Time PCR Detection System using SYBR Green Master Mix to measure target genes expression levels. β-actin served as the internal reference gene, and specific primer sequences for target genes are provided in Table S1 (Supporting Information).

RNA sequencing analysis

Total RNA from OVX group or control group was isolated using Trizol reagent. RNA integrity was validated via spectrophotometric analysis (NanoDrop, Thermo Fisher) to ensure A260/A280 ≥ 1.8 and A260/A230 ≥ 2.0. Library preparation employed the TruSeq Stranded mRNA Library Prep Kit, with poly (A) selection, followed by paired-end 150-base-pair sequencing on a NovaSeq 6000 platform (S4 flow cell, 2 × 150 bp, ~ 50 million reads per sample). Differentially expressed genes (DEGs) were defined by q value of < 0.05 and a fold change of > 2 or < 0.5. Functional enrichment analysis of DEGs was performed using the Gene Ontology (GO) and Kyoto Encyclopedia of Genes and Genomes (KEGG) databases via the cluster Profiler package (v4.8.1) for further investigation.

BDP assay

Lipid peroxidation was detected using BDP 581/591 C11kit (L267, Dojindo Molecular Technologies, Inc.). Cells at a concentration of 2 × 10⁵ cells/mL were pre-seeded into 0.16 mm confocal dishes and cultured in a CO₂ incubator. After different treatment, 200 µl of BDP 581/591 C11 working solution was added to each confocal dish, followed by incubation at 37 °C in a CO₂ incubator in dark for 20 min. The pictures of fluorescent signal were captured by confocal microscope.

TEM observation of mitochondrial

Cells were obtained via trypsinization and fixed in 2.5% glutaraldehyde (0.1 M sodium cacodylate buffer, pH 7.4) overnight at 4 °C. Then, samples underwent graded dehydration in ethanol (50%–100%) and acetone, followed by infiltration and embedding in resin. Ultrathin Sect. (70 nm) were prepared using a Leica UC7 ultramicrotome and stained with uranyl acetate and lead citrate. Samples were visualized using by TEM operated at 80 kV.

Detection of mitochondrial superoxide

Cells (3 × 10⁴ cells/mL) were seeded into confocal dishes and cultured overnight at 37 °C in 5% CO₂. After thrice washing with HBSS, cells were stained with 100 nmol/L Mito Tracker (KGE2706-100, Nanjing Keygen Biotech Co., Ltd, China) work solution for 30 min at 37 °C. Samples were imaged using confocal microscopy.

TUNEL staining

Cells were fixed in 4% paraformaldehyde in PBS for 15 min at 25 °C, permeabilized with 0.1% Triton X-100 for 10 min. Samples were incubated with TUNE (Cat: T2130, Beijing Solarbio Science & Technology Co., Ltd, China) reaction mixture at 37 °C for 60 min in dark. Following incubation, cells were washed twice with PBS, counterstained with 1 µg/mL DAPI for 5 min. Fluorescence images were captured using a confocal laser scanning microscope.

Preparation of Bacterial Outer Membrane Vesicles (OMVs)

OMVs derived from E. coli (Trans T1 strain) were prepared as previously describe. The bacterial culture was initiated by inoculating E. coli into Luria-Bertani (LB) medium with shaking at 200 rpm and 37 °C for 12 h. The suspension was then diluted 1:100 into fresh LB medium and cultured for an additional 4 h until the optical density (OD600 = 1.0). Then, the culture was centrifuged at 4000 × g for 10 min at 4 °C to remove cellular debris, followed by vacuum filtration of the supernatant through a 0.45 μm membrane and concentrated by 100 KDa ultrafiltration. Final OMV isolation used ultracentrifugation (200,000 × g, 2 h, 4 °C), with pellets resuspended in water and stored at −80 °C.

Preparation of primary BMSCs membrane vesicles

BMSCs were collected and washed with PBS, then resuspended in Tris-HCl buffer (pH 7.4) for sonication lysis on ice (20 kHz, 10 s pulse/15 s interval, 6 cycles). The lysate underwent differential centrifugation at 4 °C: 1,000×g (10 min) to remove intact cells/nuclei, 10,000×g (20 min) to eliminate organelles, and 100,000×g (1 h) to pellet plasma membranes. The pellet was resuspended in PBS, layered onto a 20%–60% sucrose velocity gradient, and ultracentrifuged at 100,000×g (4 °C, 2 h). The plasma membrane fraction was collected from the gradient interface via needle aspiration, washed with PBS, and further purified by ultracentrifugation (100,000×g, 4 °C, 1 h) to remove gradient residues. Purified membranes were resuspended in Tris-HCl buffer containing protease inhibitors, aliquoted, and stored at −80 °C.

NP preparation

PLGA nanoparticles were prepared via a double emulsion-solvent evaporation method. Briefly, 100 mg PLGA (20 kDa) was dissolved in 2 mL DCM, emulsified with 2 mL water (105 W sonication, 5 cycles), followed by secondary emulsification with 10 mL 1% PVA. Solvent removal was achieved by stirring with 8 mL 2% IPA overnight, and nanoparticles were collected by centrifugation (10,000 ×g, 15 min), washed, and resuspended in PBS for storage.

Preparation of hybrid membrane-coated nanoparticles.

Hybrid membrane-coated nanoparticles were prepared by mixing purified cell membranes and bacterial OMVs at 1:1, sonicating on ice (20% amplitude, 3 s on/3 s off, 12 min total) to form vesicles, and stabilizing at 37 °C for 1 h. The hybrid membranes were then combined with nanoparticles (1:9 ratio), sonicated on ice to induce interaction, and incubated at RT for 1 h to facilitate spontaneous membrane wrapping. Size homogenization was achieved via extrusion through polycarbonate membranes (400 nm, 10–20 passes; 100 nm, ≥ 10 passes) using a lipid extruder, ensuring a bubble-free surface and airtight seal to generate uniformly sized hybrids.

Characterization of nanoparticles

Transmission electron microscopy (TEM) was used to visualize the morphology and size distribution of nanoparticles, enabling assessment of surface structural changes in hybrid nanoparticles. Particle size and zeta potential were determined using Dynamic Light Scattering (DLS). Samples were diluted in ultrapure water and added into a cuvette for size analysis, followed by zeta potential measurement in a dedicated cell at 25 °C. Each sample was equilibrated for 60 s and analyzed in triplicate to ensure reproducibility.

Fluorescence detection of NPs fusion

Hybrid formation was verified by labeling OMVs with PKH-26 (D0030, Solarbio, China) and BMSCs-CM with PKH-67 (D0031, Solarbio, China) (37 °C, 30 min, dark), followed by centrifugal purification (100,000 × g, 90 min). Labeled components were mixed, sonicated (4 °C) to induce fusion, and extruded through a 100 nm membrane to ensure homogeneity. Confocal microscopy was performed for imaging.

Osteogenic induction and staining analysis

Under iron excess conditions (ferric ammonium citrate, FAC, 200 µmol/L), BMSCs were cultured in osteogenic differentiation medium supplemented with equimolar concentrations of PBS, ginsenoside Rc, or DT-NPNs/Rc. The osteogenic induction medium was refreshed every 2 d. Alkaline phosphatase (ALP) activity was evaluated on day 7 post-differentiation induction using the BCIP/NBT kit (Beyotime) following fixation in 4% paraformaldehyde. for staining area quantification. And qRT-PCR assay was conducted to detect osteogenic markers. For BMSCs, osteogenic markers (Runx2, Ocn, Opn, Alp, Col1a and Bmp2) were assessed.

Distribution of DT-NPNs/Rc in mice

Did-DT-NPNs/Rc and Did- NPNs/Rc were i.v. injected into mice (n = 3 for each group) at DiD-equivalent dose of 1.8 µg/mouse. In vivo fluorescence imaging was performed at 4 h and 24 h post-injection using an IVIS system with excitation/emission wavelengths of 640/675 nm. Moreover, after 24 h, the major organs (heart, liver, spleen, lung, kidney, and bones) of mice were harvested and subjected to ex vivo fluorescence imaging.

Distribution of DT-NPNs/Rc in bone marrow neutrophils

DiD-DT-NPNs/Rc and DiD- NPNs/Rc were i.v. injected mice at an equivalent dose of DiD (n = 3 for each group). 4 h and 24 h later, neutrophils and monocytes with DiD-DT-NPNs/Rc and DiD- NPNs/Rc in bone marrow were detected by flow cytometry.

Animal and treatments

All male C57BL/6J mice were purchased (GemPharmatech Co., Ltd) and kept housed in the experimental animal center at the Medical School of Nanjing University. Female C57BL/6 mice (8-week-old) were selected to construct an ovariectomized (OVX) model. Mice were randomly divided into 4 groups (Control, OVX, OVX + Rc, OVX + DT-NPNs/Rc) using a random number table method, with 6 mice per group initially. And 5, 6, 6 and 6 mice in Control, OVX, OVX + Rc, OVX + DT-NPNs/Rc group in the endpoint. For the treatment groups, 200 µL of PBS, Rc and DT-NPNs/Rc was injected into tail vein respectively twice a week for 8 weeks. Randomization was performed by an independent researcher not involved in the subsequent experiments and data analysis. All in vivo experiments and data analysis were conducted in a double-blind manner. The researchers performing the operations and data analysis were unaware of the group allocation of the mice, and group labels were only unblinded after all data were collected and analyzed.

Histological and immunofluorescence analysis

Bone specimens were fixed in 4% paraformaldehyde for 24 h at 4 °C, followed by decalcification in 10% EDTA solution (pH 8.0) with gentle agitation for 21 days. The decalcification solution was refreshed every 48 h. Specimens were then dehydrated through a graded ethanol series (70%−100%), cleared in xylene, and embedded in paraffin. Serial Sect. (5 μm thickness) were prepared.

Hematoxylin and Eosin (H&E) Staining

Sections were deparaffinized in xylene, rehydrated through graded ethanol, stained with Mayer’s hematoxylin for 5 min, differentiated in 1% acid alcohol, blued in Scott’s tap water substitute, counterstained with eosin Y for 2 min, dehydrated, cleared.

Masson’s Trichrome Staining: Sections were processed using a commercial kit (Sigma-Aldrich) following the manufacturer’s protocol.

Immunofluorescence staining

Sections were deparaffinized, rehydrated, and subjected to antigen retrieval in citrate buffer (pH 6.0) using a pressure cooker for 3 min. Endogenous peroxidases were quenched with 3% hydrogen peroxide in methanol for 10 min. Sections were permeabilized with 0.3% Triton X-100 in PBS for 15 min and blocked with 10% normal goat serum in PBST for 1 h at room temperature. Primary antibodies (diluted in blocking buffer) were applied overnight at 4 °C in a humidified chamber. After three washes in PBST, sections were incubated with Alexa Fluor-conjugated secondary antibodies (1:500; Thermo Fisher Scientific) for 1.5 h at room temperature in the dark. Nuclei were counterstained with 4′,6-diamidino-2-phenylindole (DAPI, 1 µg/mL) for 5 min. Images were acquired using a confocal laser scanning microscope (Zeiss LSM 880) with appropriate excitation/emission filters and processed using ZEN Blue software (Zeiss).

Statistical analysis

Data were demonstrated as means ± SDs and an analyzed with SPSS software (version 22.0), of which the P < 0.05 was considered significant. The data were obtained from results of at least three independent experiments. Unpaired Student’s t-test were used for comparisons between the two groups. A one-way ANOVA with Tukey’s multiple-comparison test was performed for the multiple group comparisons.
