Efficacy of upadacitinib induction for pediatric inflammatory bowel disease
Authors: Evan Greenhall, Nina Gautam, Paula Prieto‐Jimenez, Elizabeth Hilow, Ashish Patel, Brad Pasternak
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractObjectivesUpadacitinib, a second‐generation Janus kinase (JAK) inhibitor, is approved for moderate to severe Crohn's disease (CD) and ulcerative colitis (UC) in adults. Its efficacy in pediatric patients remains unclear, though early reports suggest benefits in refractory inflammatory bowel disease (IBD). However, data indicate potential relapse after dose reduction. This study evaluates upadacitinib's sustained efficacy in pediatric IBD, particularly when transitioning from induction to maintenance dosing.MethodsWe conducted a case series of 16 pediatric patients (mean age 16.1 years) treated with upadacitinib for refractory IBD between 2023 and 2024. Treatment responses were analyzed in 8 patients who completed induction (45 mg daily) and transitioned to maintenance dosing (30 or 15 mg daily based on provider preference). Relapse rates and responses to dose adjustments were assessed.ResultsAmong the 16 patients, 8 had UC, 7 had CD, and 1 had IBD‐unclassified. Seven (43.8%; 95% confidence interval [CI], 19.4%–68.1%) lost response after transitioning to maintenance, with a median time to relapse of 81.5 days. Of these, 6 (85.7%; 95% CI, 42%–99%) regained response after increasing the dose back to induction dosing.ConclusionOur findings suggest that pediatric patients may require longer induction, higher maintenance doses, or individualized dosing due to pharmacokinetics. Given the high relapse rate in our small cohort, close monitoring and tailored treatment strategies are essential.

INTRODUCTION

Upadacitinib is a second‐generation selective Janus kinase (JAK) inhibitor, approved by the Food and Drug Administration (FDA) to treat moderate to severe Crohn's disease (CD) and ulcerative colitis (UC) in adults. Standard induction dosing consists of 45 mg daily by mouth for 8 weeks for UC and 12 weeks for CD, with a subsequent decrease to maintenance dosing of 15 or 30 mg daily. 1 , 2 In adults, higher doses have shown minimal additional exposure‐response benefit, and are associated with increased adverse effects. 3 These adverse effects include acne, anemia, nasopharyngitis, headaches, and serious infections such as Pneumocystis jirovecii pneumonia, cytomegalovirus, and herpes zoster. 2 , 3 There is a paucity of case report/series of upadacitinib use in pediatric inflammatory bowel disease (IBD). 4 , 5 , 6 , 7 , 8 , 9 Two case series do describe a relapse in multiple patients when transitioned to maintenance dose after achieving remission with standard induction dosing. 7 , 8 In Spencer et al.'s case series, two patients experienced a flare after dose reduction out of a total of 20 patients descripted. A patient with UC (age 12) was reinduced at 25 weeks post‐dose reduction, while a patient with IBD‐Unclassified (IBD‐U) (age 17) was reinduced just 4 weeks after transitioning to maintenance. 7 In addition, Kontaki et al.'s case series revealed one patient with CD (age 17) out of 12 with a relapse of symptoms after a decrease in upadacitinib to maintenance dose. 8 Nonetheless, both series reported overall success in their pediatric populations with upadacitinib.

METHODS

A retrospective chart review was performed to identify all patients aged 0–18 who were started on upadacitinib for refractory IBD at our institution between 2023 and 2024. Exclusion criteria included patients who were still in the induction phase at chart review, patients with incomplete serological or clinical information to determine response, and patients started on upadacitinib after 18 years of age. Patients were deemed to be responders to upadacitinib based on a decrease in pediatric ulcerative colitis activity index (PUCAI) from baseline by 20 or if decreased to less than 10, decrease in pediatric Crohn's disease activity index (PCDAI) of 15 from baseline or decrease to less than 10, or based on physician global assessment (PGA) with a decrease in assessment. Measures of central tendency were used to describe demographic and cohort characteristics. Given the small sample size, a nonparametric Mann–Whitney U test was used to assess the differences in number of previous failed therapies between those who lost response to those who maintained remission. This was again used when evaluating induction length and loss of response. Loss of response was defined by increased clinical symptoms related to IBD, PUCAI/PCDAI, biochemical abnormalities (increased inflammatory markers), or endoscopic evidence of active disease.

Ethics Statement

This study was reviewed and approved by the Institutional Review Board at Phoenix Children's Hospital (Approval Number: IRB‐24‐255).

RESULTS

We identified 16 pediatric patients receiving upadacitinib for refractory IBD at our institution. The average age was 16.1 years, and 56.3% of patients were assigned female at birth. Of the 16 patients, 8 had UC, 7 had CD, and 1 with IBD‐U. Of the eight with UC, five had acute severe UC defined by PUCAI > 65 and or six bloody stools per day and or one of the following, tachycardia, fever, anemia, or elevated erythrocyte sedimentation rate (ESR) at diagnosis. The number of previously failed treatments ranged between 1 and 4 with a median of 2 (all have failed anti‐tumor necrosis factor antibody). All 16 patients completed induction dosing of 45 mg with a median duration of induction of 11 weeks (range: 7–15 weeks). Of note, most of our inductions were longer than 8 weeks due to lack of data in pediatrics as well as physician preference. Prolonged induction longer than recommended was not correlated to loss or improvement in response (U = 28.5, p = 0.789). Fourteen patients were transitioned to 30 mg daily and 2 were transitioned to 15 mg daily based on patient weight and provider preference. Of the 16 patients, 7/16 (43.8%; 95% confidence interval [CI], 19.4%–68.1%) lost response when transitioned to maintenance dose. This consisted of one of the two patients on the 15 mg maintenance dose and 6/14 (42.8) on the 30 mg maintenance dose. Of the seven, all responded initially to upadacitinib. The two patients that did not have sufficient decrease in PCDAI/PUCAI at the level determined to be responders were determined to respond by PGA (Table 1). One patient (patient 1) had a decrease from mild to quiescent on PGA and another (patient 4) PGA decreased from moderate to quiescent by the end of induction. The median number of days to loss of response was 81.5 (range: 14–217 days). All seven patients who lost response suffered increased clinical symptoms, including abdominal pain and increased bowel movement frequency and diarrhea. Mean change in PUCAI/PCDAI from completion of induction to relapse was +20 (range 0–35). Three of the seven patients (patients 1, 5, and 7) underwent endoscopic evaluation with evidence of macroscopic and histologic inflammation. Patient 5, who had no change in PUCAI, had endoscopic evidence of loss of response (Table 1). Laboratory evaluation was performed in six patients. Of these, fecal calprotectin was elevated in three patients with a median of 2470 mcg/g (range: 170–3550 mcg/g), ESR was elevated in two patients with a median of 64 mm/h (range: 53–75 mm/h), c‐reactive protein (CRP) in two patients with a median of 14.15 mg/L (range: 7.2–21.1 mg/L), decreased albumin reported in one patient at 2.9 g/dL (normal 3.8–5.1 g/dL), and decreased hemoglobin in two patients, both at 11.9 g/dL (normal 12–15 g/dL). Only patient 3 had worsening clinical symptoms without laboratory or endoscopic evaluation and was determined in relapse by change in PUCAI (Table 1).

DISCUSSION

This retrospective study describes the increased rate of relapse in pediatric patients when transitioning from induction to maintenance dosing of upadacitinib. The U‐ENDURE trial reported worsening of CD in 19% of patients in the 30 mg maintenance dose group. 2 Although a much smaller sample size, this is significantly different from the 43.8% observed in our pediatric population.

Pediatric patients may require a longer induction period. Some adult studies have suggested that an extended induction period of up to 16 weeks at 45 mg daily could improve clinical remission rates in those that do not respond by Week 8. 1 , 10 While all patients in our study were successfully induced into remission by the end of their induction period, continuation of this higher dose may be necessary to sustain remission. There was no significant association between the number of previously failed therapies and likelihood of response.

In addition, pharmacokinetics may play a role. Pediatric patients may require higher dosing due to higher drug clearance. 11 , 12 , 13 A case series on ustekinumab, an interleukin‐12 and 23 antagonist also approved for adult IBD but used off label in pediatrics, revealed lower trough levels in patients less than 18 years old after standard induction. 10 In addition multiple patients required re‐induction to maintain clinical remission. 10 Though the pharmacokinetics of ustekinumab differ from upadacitinib, with a clear exposure‐response relationship, it indicates the possible need for more aggressive induction in pediatrics due to higher clearance rates. This concept also holds true for infliximab and vedolizumab where pediatric patients often need higher doses to achieve remission. 12

Although this case series was limited in power and generalizability by a small sample size as well as the variability in assessing disease relapse, based on our analysis, it appears pediatric patients may require higher induction doses, maintenance doses, or a longer duration of induction to sustain remission. Ideal trough levels for upadacitinib have not been determined, despite well‐established therapeutic levels for anti‐tumor necrosis factor antibodies. 13 Further investigation on upadacitinib pharmacokinetics and optimal dosing in pediatrics is clearly warranted.

CONCLUSION

Our findings suggest loss of response in pediatric patients when transitioning from induction to maintenance dosing of upadacitinib for refractory IBD. Compared to adult data, relapse rates in children appear higher, raising the hypothesis that pediatric patients may require alternative dosing strategies. These may include prolonged induction periods or increased maintenance dosing. Possible explanations could include that pediatric patients appear to have accelerated drug clearance and altered pharmacokinetics as seen with other advanced therapies. These observations warrant further investigation on upadacitinib pharmacokinetics and optimal dosing in the pediatric population.
