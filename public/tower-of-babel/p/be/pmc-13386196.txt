Neurophysiological Response of Spinal Mobilization and Manipulation in Individuals With and Without Musculoskeletal Spine Disorders: A Scoping Review
Authors: Mostafa Mehraban Jahromi, Dylan Basset, Jordan A Borrell, Neena K Sharma
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTSpinal manipulation (SMA) and spinal mobilization (SMO) are two of the most widely used manual therapy techniques (SMT) for spine‐related musculoskeletal pain management. Although their neuromuscular effects have been reported, emerging evidence suggests that SMT may also induce neurophysiological changes within the central nervous system (CNS). This review aimed to synthesize the current literature on the neurophysiological effects of SMT with multiple brain imaging and neurophysiological methods. A scoping review was conducted according to PRISMA guidelines. Eight databases were searched for studies using brain imaging techniques (e.g., transcranial magnetic stimulation [TMS], functional magnetic resonance imaging [fMRI] or electroencephalography [EEG]) to assess the impact of SMT on healthy individuals or those with musculoskeletal spine pain. Twenty‐six studies were included: eight TMS, seven fMRI, five somatosensory evoked potential (SEP) and six with other imaging methods (e.g., EEG, positron emission tomography [PET] or magnetic resonance spectroscopy [MRS]). Most studies reported significant postintervention neurophysiological changes, particularly in motor cortex excitability (TMS), functional connectivity (fMRI) and sensorimotor integration (SEP). However, heterogeneity in study design, manipulation protocols, imaging outcomes and timing of assessments limited generalizability of the results. Although many studies reported reductions in pain intensity, none of them linked cortical changes directly to clinical improvements. Overall, SMT can induce changes in cortical activity and connectivity, particularly in pain processing and motor control regions. However, significant methodological variation across studies limits the strength of conclusions. Future research should aim for standardized protocols and investigate the clinical significance of neurophysiological changes in large, diverse populations.Keywords: brain, neurophysiological response, pain, spinal manipulation, spinal mobilization

Introduction

Spine‐related musculoskeletal (MSK) pain is a leading contributor to disability worldwide, with neck and low back pain (LBP) accounting for 10% of the total years lived with disability (James et al. 2018). Although this pain can originate from peripheral structures such as ligaments, intervertebral discs, myofascial tissue or facet joints, the majority of people with LBP and neck pain are diagnosed with nonspecific conditions (Will et al. 2018) without a clear cause (Binder 2007; Maher et al. 2017). Emerging evidence suggests alterations in brain structure and function in individuals with neck (de Zoete et al. 2022; Gao et al. 2025) and LBP (Chen et al. 2025; Medrano‐Escalada et al. 2022). These studies indicate structural, functional and neurochemical changes associated with chronic pain. Thus, potential therapeutic targets for neck and back pain should be evaluated in reference to neurophysiological effects within the brain.

Spinal manual therapy (SMT), including spinal manipulation (SMA) and spinal mobilization (SMO), is a common intervention performed by many providers such as physiotherapists, osteopaths and chiropractors (Young et al. 2024), and its effects on the brain have been explored by few studies. According to Maitland's classification, manipulation is a segmental, low‐amplitude, high‐velocity technique that extends beyond physiological limits without reaching anatomical boundaries (Maitland 1986; Outeda et al. 2022). Mobilizations are low‐velocity passive oscillatory movements (Lascurain‐Aguirrebeña et al. 2016). Both SMT techniques are widely used evidence‐based interventions, recommended by several Clinical Practice Guidelines for management of spinal disorders (Organization 2023; Paige et al. 2017; Reed et al. 2020). The physiological effects of SMT extend beyond the primary targeted tissues and include multiple mechanisms, including neurophysiological, biomechanical, neuromuscular and neurological (Keter et al. 2025). However, the underlying mechanisms of SMT are still evolving, specifically related to its impact on the brain. Several recent studies suggest SMT has neurophysiological effects within the central nervous system (CNS), resulting in alteration of somatosensory integration and central pain processing (Robinault et al. 2021), leading to improved clinical outcomes.

Brain imaging techniques such as functional magnetic resonance imaging (fMRI) (Ellingsen et al. 2018; Hotz‐Boendermaker et al. 2016; Zhou et al. 2024), electroencephalography (EEG), somatosensory evoked potentials (SEP) (Haavik‐Taylor and Murphy 2007; Lelic et al. 2016; Provencher et al. 2021) and transcranial magnetic stimulation (TMS) (Baarbé et al. 2018; Fryer and Pearce 2012; Haavik et al. 2018) have provided valuable insights into the neurophysiological impacts of SMT. These modalities have been used to explore how SMT influences functional connectivity (Zhou et al. 2024), cortical excitability (Baarbé et al. 2018) and sensorimotor integration (Lelic et al. 2016). Employing these imaging techniques may advance our understanding of SMT neurophysiological effects (e.g., brain function) and whether such changes relate to clinically relevant outcomes (Meyer et al. 2019).

Tan et al. (2020) in a longitudinal prospective study showed that six sessions of SMA resulted in increased activation within default mode network (DMN) related to self‐regulation. Likewise, Weber Ii et al. (2019) found that SMA decreased neurologic pain signature by altering pain processing regions in the brain in both healthy individuals and those with neck pain.

To date, only three systematic/scoping reviews have reported the effects of SMT on the CNS (Bonanno et al. 2024; Gyer et al. 2019; Meyer et al. 2019). In a systematic review, Meyer et al. (2019) reported that SMA can modulate brain function in both symptomatic and healthy subjects; however, limited evidence and inconsistent findings from the included studies make the interpretations from the findings less robust (Meyer et al. 2019). The review mainly included studies with neck pain (n = 10), and only one study with experimentally induced back pain published in 2019. Gyer et al. (2019) additionally reported that SMA may activate descending pain inhibitory pathways, engage the neuroendocrine system and induce segmental inhibition. In addition, a recent scoping review (published in 2024) of fMRI and EEG studies showed that global osteopathic approaches can modulate brain activity (Bonanno et al. 2024). However, the review included a wide range of osteopathic techniques, including joint, soft tissue and visceral techniques, for example, myofascial release, visceral manipulation and cranial‐sacral techniques, and limited neuroimaging approaches to EEG and fMRI.

Although these studies provide valuable insights into brain functions following manual techniques, they included (1) fMRI and EEG methods only; (2) relatively few studies of LBP, which is the most prevalent spinal pain condition; and (3) a wide range of manual techniques. As a result, evidence from imaging methods like TMS, SEP and functional near‐infrared spectroscopy (fNIRS) has been overlooked. Second, current reviews underrepresent the neurophysiological impacts of joint‐specific SMT, the most commonly used manual approaches, on cortical function and brain network dynamics. Each of these brain imaging methods represents a distinct aspect of the impact of SMT that may ultimately influence clinical decision‐making.

To address these gaps, this scoping review aims to evaluate the effects of SMA and SMO on the CNS, drawing on evidence captured through diverse neuroimaging approaches. The primary objective is to identify the effects of SMA and SMO on neurophysiological changes, including functional connectivity, hemodynamic responses, electrophysiological measures and somatosensory evoked potentials, in both healthy individuals and those with spine‐related MSK pain disorders. Secondary objectives include assessing the impact of SMA and SMO on pain and pain‐related outcomes (e.g., pain intensity and pain sensitivity), psychological stress, quality of life, function and disability measures.

Materials and Methods

This Scoping review was registered with PROSPERO (CRD420250650483) and prepared in accordance with the Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Reviews and Meta‐Analysis extension for scoping reviews (PRISMA‐ScR).

Inclusion and Exclusion Criteria

The inclusion criteria for this review were defined according to the population, intervention, comparator and outcome (PICO) framework. Studies were considered eligible if they included (1) healthy individuals or participants with spinal‐related disorders, such as LBP or neck pain; (2) the primary intervention of interest as spinal mobilization or manipulation based on Maitland's criteria (Maitland 1986); (3) comparators of therapeutic interventions, no intervention, placebo or sham treatments, or baseline measurements; and (4) brain‐related neurophysiological responses from brain imaging techniques as the primary outcomes. Secondary outcomes included pain, psychological stress (e.g., kinesiophobia) and disability‐related measures. Study designs of randomized controlled trials (RCTs), longitudinal studies, cross‐sectional studies, crossover studies, case–control studies, quasiexperimental studies and cohort studies published in English language were included.

Studies were excluded if they (1) involved animals, paediatric populations or individuals with neurological or systemic conditions unrelated to MSK spine pain or disorders; (2) included the primary intervention other than spinal mobilization or manipulation (e.g., exercise‐only interventions and soft tissue mobilization alone); and (3) were qualitative studies, review articles, meta‐analyses, letters to the editor, study protocols, case series or case reports.

Search Strategy

Relevant studies were identified using eight databases of PubMed, Web of Science, Embase, CINAHL, ovid MEDLINE, ScienceDirect, CENTRAL and PEDro published from inspection to January 8, 2026. The initial search was conducted between 15 February 2025 to 15 March 2025 and updated on 8 January 2026 using the exact search strategy. The main terms to prepare specific syntaxes for each database were a combination of relevant keywords of manual therapy, healthy individuals or individuals with spine pain and brain imaging modalities (full terms and their corresponding abbreviations). Boolean expressions ‘AND’ and ‘OR’ were used to combine or modify search terms according to each database (Appendix S1). The syntaxes were designed to search for the selected keywords within the title, abstract and keyword fields of each database.

Study Selection

Study selection and screening process was facilitated using COVIDENCE (Covidence systematic review software, Veritas Health Innovation, Melbourne, Australia; available at www.covidence.org), a web‐based tool for review articles management. The reference list of each database was uploaded in the COVIDENCE separately, and two independent reviewers (MM and DB) screened the title and abstracts of the articles to identify eligible studies for full text review. Any disagreements between the two reviewers were resolved through discussion, and a third reviewer (NS) was consulted to make the final decision if consensus could not be reached. The eligibility of the selected articles for full‐text review was determined using the same screening process.

Data Extraction

The data from the included studies were extracted based on the predefined objectives, using a customized form. The extracted information included sample size, population characteristics, study design, brain imaging methods, intervention and control groups, level of manipulation or mobilization, number of interventions, assessment time points, primary and secondary outcomes and the corresponding results. Regular meetings between the authors were held during the data extraction period to ensure consistency and maintain the accuracy of the extracted data.

Results

Study Selection

We identified 2497 records from eight databases. After removing 1088 duplicates, 1409 records remained for title and abstract screening. Of these remaining articles, 1347 were excluded based on title, abstract and keyword screening for not meeting the inclusion criteria, leaving 62 articles for full‐text review. Consequently, 26 articles were included in the study, whereas 36 were excluded and considered ineligible (Figure 1). All the studies were original studies.

PRISMA flow chart.

Study Characteristics

Out of 26 studies included, 11 were crossover design (Fryer and Pearce 2012; Haavik et al. 2017; Haavik et al. 2018; Haavik Taylor and Murphy 2010; Inami et al. 2017; Isenburg et al. 2021; Lelic et al. 2016; Navid et al. 2019; Ogura et al. 2011; Taylor and Murphy 2008, 2010), 8 were RCTs (Baarbé et al. 2018; Didehdar et al. 2020; Gay et al. 2014; Haavik‐Taylor and Murphy 2007; Provencher et al. 2021; Sparks et al. 2017; Zhou et al. 2024) and 7 were non‐RCTs (Clark et al. 2011; Dishman et al. 2002; Ellingsen et al. 2018; Haavik et al. 2016; Hotz‐Boendermaker et al. 2016; Kharazinejad and Didehdar 2024). All the studies implemented SMA techniques except for Hotz‐Boendermaker et al. (2016) that used SMO techniques with low force (e.g., 30‐N force), and Ellingsen et al. (2018) compared the effects of manipulation versus mobilization in a three‐visit study. Eight of the studies used TMS (Baarbé et al. 2018; Clark et al. 2011; Dishman et al. 2002; Dishman et al. 2008; Fryer and Pearce 2012; Haavik et al. 2016; Haavik et al. 2018; Taylor and Murphy 2008), seven used fMRI (Ellingsen et al. 2018; Gay et al. 2014; Hotz‐Boendermaker et al. 2016; Isenburg et al. 2021; Sparks et al. 2017; Zhou et al. 2024; Zhou et al. 2023), five used SEP (Haavik‐Taylor and Murphy 2007; Haavik et al. 2017; Haavik Taylor and Murphy 2010; Lelic et al. 2016; Taylor and Murphy 2010), two used EEG (Navid et al. 2019; Provencher et al. 2021), two used PET scan (Inami et al. 2017; Ogura et al. 2011) and two used Proton Magnetic Resonance Spectroscopy (1H‐MRS) (Didehdar et al. 2020; Kharazinejad and Didehdar 2024) as the main imaging modality (Baarbé et al. 2018; Didehdar et al. 2020; Ellingsen et al. 2018; Gay et al. 2014; Isenburg et al. 2021; Navid et al. 2019; Ogura et al. 2011; Provencher et al. 2021; Sparks et al. 2017; Zhou et al. 2024; Zhou et al. 2023). Eighteen of the studies included secondary outcomes (Baarbé et al. 2018; Clark et al. 2011; Didehdar et al. 2020; Ellingsen et al. 2018; Fryer and Pearce 2012; Gay et al. 2014; Haavik et al. 2017; Haavik et al. 2016; Inami et al. 2017; Isenburg et al. 2021; Kharazinejad and Didehdar 2024; Navid et al. 2019; Ogura et al. 2011; Provencher et al. 2021; Sparks et al. 2017; Taylor and Murphy 2008; Zhou et al. 2024; Zhou et al. 2023). Of these, 14 studies (Baarbé et al. 2018; Didehdar et al. 2020; Ellingsen et al. 2018; Gay et al. 2014; Haavik et al. 2017; Inami et al. 2017; Isenburg et al. 2021; Kharazinejad and Didehdar 2024; Navid et al. 2019; Ogura et al. 2011; Provencher et al. 2021; Sparks et al. 2017; Zhou et al. 2024; Zhou et al. 2023) reported clinical outcomes (e.g., pain intensity, disability and psychological stress). Eleven of the studies used pain intensity measurement (Baarbé et al. 2018; Didehdar et al. 2020; Ellingsen et al. 2018; Gay et al. 2014; Isenburg et al. 2021; Navid et al. 2019; Ogura et al. 2011; Provencher et al. 2021; Sparks et al. 2017; Zhou et al. 2024; Zhou et al. 2023). Other secondary outcomes mainly consisted of spinal neurophysiological responses.

Twelve of the studies tailored manipulation or mobilization for lumbar (or lumbosacral) region (Clark et al. 2011; Didehdar et al. 2020; Dishman et al. 2002; Dishman et al. 2008; Ellingsen et al. 2018; Fryer and Pearce 2012; Gay et al. 2014; Hotz‐Boendermaker et al. 2016; Isenburg et al. 2021; Kharazinejad and Didehdar 2024; Zhou et al. 2024; Zhou et al. 2023), six for cervical (Baarbé et al. 2018; Haavik‐Taylor and Murphy 2007; Haavik Taylor and Murphy 2010; Ogura et al. 2011; Taylor and Murphy 2008, 2010), six for the whole spine (Haavik et al. 2017; Haavik et al. 2016; Haavik et al. 2018; Inami et al. 2017; Lelic et al. 2016; Navid et al. 2019) and two for the thoracic spine (Provencher et al. 2021; Sparks et al. 2017). Study by Ellingsen et al. (2018) did not specify the spine region that received manipulation or mobilization. Figure 2 summarizes study characteristics.

TMS

Table 1 summarizes the main characteristics and results of the TMS studies. Overall, 202 participants were assessed across eight TMS studies. Three of the studies were conducted on healthy participants (Dishman et al. 2002; Dishman et al. 2008; Fryer and Pearce 2012), two on general spinal pain (Haavik et al. 2016; Haavik et al. 2018), two on neck pain (Baarbé et al. 2018; Taylor and Murphy 2008) and one on chronic LBP (Clark et al. 2011). All the studies reported motor evoked potentials (MEPs) induced from the TMS stimulation as the primary outcome. Two of the studies reported cortical silent period (CSP) (Haavik et al. 2018; Taylor and Murphy 2008) and one reported cerebral brain inhibition (CBI) (Baarbé et al. 2018). All the studies reported significant changes in the primary outcome except Fryer and Pearce (2012) and Clark et al. (2011), where no significant change was reported in the MEP latency or amplitude. Only Baarbé et al. (2018) included baseline pain intensity measure, and they did not report the changes in pain intensity following the intervention.

fMRI

Table 2 summarizes the main characteristics and results of the fMRI studies. Overall, 283 individuals were included across seven studies with fMRI as the main brain imaging method. Six of the studies were conducted on LBP or healthy individuals with history of LBP (Ellingsen et al. 2018; Gay et al. 2014; Hotz‐Boendermaker et al. 2016; Isenburg et al. 2021; Zhou et al. 2024; Zhou et al. 2023), and only one study was conducted on neck pain population (Sparks et al. 2017). Although the studies differed in the brain regions analysed, outcomes of interest and fMRI approaches (resting‐state vs. task‐based), all reported significant changes in brain region activity following SMT intervention. Pain as the secondary outcome decreased in all the studies (Ellingsen et al. 2018; Gay et al. 2014; Isenburg et al. 2021; Sparks et al. 2017; Zhou et al. 2024; Zhou et al. 2023); however, it is not clear that changes in pain in Sparks et al. (2017) study was significant or not.

SEP

Table 3 summarizes the main characteristics and results of SEP studies. Overall, 73 participants were included across five studies with SEP as the main brain imaging method. Three of the studies were conducted on pain‐free individuals with a history of neck pain or chronic neck and upper extremity pain (Haavik et al. 2017; Haavik Taylor and Murphy 2010; Taylor and Murphy 2010), and two were conducted on subclinical pain or recurring spinal pain (Haavik‐Taylor and Murphy 2007; Lelic et al. 2016). SEP ratio of P22‐N30 peak complex (Haavik et al. 2017; Haavik Taylor and Murphy 2010; Taylor and Murphy 2010) or N30 peak amplitude (Haavik‐Taylor and Murphy 2007; Lelic et al. 2016) was the main outcome reported by all of the included studies (Table 3). All studies reported a significant decrease in P22‐N30 peak complex or N30 peak amplitude following the intervention.

Other Brain Imaging

Table 4 summarizes the main characteristics and results of the studies that used other brain imaging methods. Secondary Outcomes

Pain was the most consistently reported secondary outcome among the studies, with 14 studies (Baarbé et al. 2018; Didehdar et al. 2020; Ellingsen et al. 2018; Gay et al. 2014; Haavik et al. 2017; Inami et al. 2017; Isenburg et al. 2021; Kharazinejad and Didehdar 2024; Navid et al. 2019; Ogura et al. 2011; Provencher et al. 2021; Sparks et al. 2017; Zhou et al. 2024; Zhou et al. 2023) assessing pain outcome (e.g., pain intensity or pain unpleasantness). Regardless of intervention duration, study design or statistical significance, all studies reported a decrease in pain scores. Baarbé et al. (2018) was the only study that reported pain intensity only at the baseline. Other reported outcomes included function, disability or quality of life (Didehdar et al. 2020; Kharazinejad and Didehdar 2024; Ogura et al. 2011; Zhou et al. 2024; Zhou et al. 2023), neurophysiological outcomes derived from TMS (Clark et al. 2011; Fryer and Pearce 2012; Haavik et al. 2016; Taylor and Murphy 2008) and stress‐related outcomes (Ellingsen et al. 2018; Inami et al. 2017; Isenburg et al. 2021; Ogura et al. 2011).

Discussion

To our knowledge, this is the first scoping review to comprehensively synthesize evidence on the effects of SMT on the brain activity across multiple brain imaging and neurophysiological methods. Additionally, this review critically evaluates the methodological approaches of the included studies, which can provide an integrated picture of the cortical effects of SMT. Studies mainly used TMS, fMRI and SEP as the main imaging methods measuring brain activity (e.g., cortical excitability), neural network activity connectivity via blood‐oxygen‐level‐dependent (BOLD) signals and electrical signals of the sensory pathway respectively. Although many of the studies reported significant changes in the neurophysiologic brain activity, lack of standardized method for treatment and assessment makes them incomparable. However, changes in the prefrontal cortex (PFC); sensorimotor regions, including the primary motor and somatosensory cortices; the thalamus; anterior cingulate cortex (ACC); anterior and posterior insula; and the periaqueductal grey (PAG) have been reported more consistently across studies. Although none of the studies examined the association between these neural alterations and pain intensity (which was typically reported as a secondary clinical outcome), these regions play key role in the physical, sensory, cognitive, emotional and autonomic dimensions of pain perception and modulation, as well as in descending pain control (de Ridder et al. 2021; Gan et al. 2022; Hemington and Coulombe 2015; Leite et al. 2017; Li et al. 2024; Ong et al. 2019; Ziegler et al. 2023). Collectively, the findings suggest that SMT exerts central effects across multiple brain regions.

TMS

TMS was the most frequently used imaging method with 8 of 24 studies using this approach. All the studies except two of them (Clark et al. 2011; Fryer and Pearce 2012) reported significant changes in their primary outcomes following SMA compared to the control intervention. These changes were mainly attributed to increases or decreases in cortical processing or corticospinal output (Haavik et al. 2016; Taylor and Murphy 2008), CBI (Baarbé et al. 2018) and CSP (Haavik et al. 2018; Taylor and Murphy 2008). In particular, SMA appears to have immediate and transient effects in both asymptomatic individuals and individuals with spinal pain, causing enhanced activity of corticomotoneurons or α motor neurons (Clark et al. 2011) innervating target muscles (e.g., paraspinal or limb) and resulting in increased muscle strength. However, no firm conclusion can be drawn due to the heterogeneity of the results among studies.

Additionally, a major challenge in interpreting these results lies in methodological differences among the studies and the timing of postmanipulation assessments, which is a critical factor given the transient effects of SMT. Dishman et al. (2008) reported transient increases in MEP amplitude as early as 10 s postlumbar SMA in healthy subjects that returned to baseline by 20 s. In another study, they indicated that the facilitation occurred 20 to 60 s (derived from gastrocnemius muscle) postlumbar manipulation (Dishman et al. 2002). In contrast, Clark et al. (2011) reported no significant changes in MEPs in erector spinae postlumbo‐sacral SMA in asymptomatic and individuals with LBP when postassessment occurred 10 min after the application of SMA. The lack of changes in MEPs postintervention is likely due to delay in the data collection beyond the first 2 min. Fryer and Pearce (2012) reported decreased MEPs after lumbo‐sacral SMA in healthy individuals.

Dishman et al. (2002, 2008), Clark et al. (2011) and Fryer and Pearce (2012) implemented lumbo‐sacral manipulation, whereas others implemented the manipulation for at least one cervical level (Baarbé et al. 2018; Taylor and Murphy 2008) or the whole spine, including the sacral region (Haavik et al. 2016, 2018). Cervical manipulation across both studies reported intracortical (e.g., sensorimotor integration) facilitation or shortened CBI following the manipulation.

Studies using whole spine including sacral SMA reported increased limb muscle strength due to shortening CSP and descending cortical drive. On the other hand, studies using lumbosacral manipulation reported a transient increase in cortical excitability and no systematic effect or reduction in corticospinal excitability. This may suggest that level of manipulation could affect the results. Besides methodological differences in assessment time and level of manipulation, studies also varied in the type of TMS coil, stimulation intensities (e.g., percent of maximum stimulator output) and EMG recording sites. This highlights a need for standardized framework of TMS‐based spinal manipulation research intervention protocols and outcome measures to enhance the interpretability of future findings and their replicability by other researchers. Collectively, the SMT appears to induce short lived central nervous system effects detectable with TMS, but methodological variability across studies restricts definitive interpretation.

fMRI

Seven studies used fMRI with various types of analyses (e.g., BOLD response, regions of interest, resting state functional connectivity of pain processing network, salience network, default mode network and dorsal attentional network), collectively targeting all three aspects of pain processing, for example, sensory discrimination, cognitive and affective emotional and pain modulating regions. Two studies compared SMO versus SMA versus light touch/pseudo SMT, one included SMO, three included SMA in participants with LBP and one study included thoracic spine SMA on individuals with neck pain. All fMRI studies reported significant changes post‐SMT. Across the studies, spinal manipulation was associated with changes in both regional brain activation and functional connectivity in networks involving pain processing, sensorimotor integration, cognitive‐emotional integration and regions of descending pain inhibition. Sensory motor cortex was the most commonly reported brain region among studies (Ellingsen et al. 2018; Gay et al. 2014; Hotz‐Boendermaker et al. 2016; Isenburg et al. 2021; Sparks et al. 2017; Zhou et al. 2023). It is worth mentioning that reporting different regions and using different fMRI techniques (resting state vs. task‐based fMRI) can add to the complexity of interpretation of these results highlighting the need for a standardized framework for future studies.

Results may also vary depending on whether the study population includes healthy individuals or participants with spine‐related pain (e.g., LBP or neck pain). Whereas Isenburg et al. (2021) found no significant changes in Salience Network (SLN) connectivity premanipulation or postmanipulation in healthy participants, Hotz‐Boendermaker et al. (2016) reported reduced activation of secondary somatosensory cortex area in individuals with chronic LBP compared with healthy controls postmanipulation. Moreover, findings from Ellingsen et al. (2018) suggest that spinal manipulation may modulate BOLD response more effectively in individuals with chronic LBP than in healthy participants in regions associated with pain expectation, sensory integration, emotional regulation and fear of movement. Overall, fMRI evidence indicates that SMT may modulate central pain‐related networks through changes in brain activation and functional connectivity, particularly in clinical populations; however, methodological variability across studies precludes firm conclusions.

SEP

Across the five SEP studies (Haavik‐Taylor and Murphy 2007; Haavik et al. 2017; Haavik Taylor and Murphy 2010; Lelic et al. 2016; Taylor and Murphy 2010), decreased N30 amplitude reflecting a negative wave occurrence in 30 ms post a nerve stimulation and P22‐N30 ratio, an index of central sensory integration, were consistently reported. All studies included cervical SMO in participants with neck pain except the study by Haavik Taylor and Murphy (2010) that included healthy individuals. Collectively, the findings from these studies suggest that cervical spine SMO alters somatosensory processing and sensorimotor integration at the cortical level. Pain scores were reported only by Haavik et al. (2017), and this study was the only study that included 12 weeks of intervention; all remaining studies used one SMO session. These changes were attributed to the basal ganglia, thalamus, premotor cortex and primary motor cortex (Haavik et al. 2017). These findings support the hypothesis that spinal manipulation may induce neuroplastic changes within the sensorimotor networks and are consistent with the findings from fMRI studies. It is worth mentioning that from the five SEP studies (Haavik‐Taylor and Murphy 2007; Haavik et al. 2017; Haavik Taylor and Murphy 2010; Lelic et al. 2016; Taylor and Murphy 2010), four (Haavik‐Taylor and Murphy 2007; Haavik et al. 2017; Haavik Taylor and Murphy 2010; Taylor and Murphy 2010) of them were published by the same research team. Additionally, only two (Haavik Taylor and Murphy 2010; Lelic et al. 2016) of the studies were randomized, highlighting the need for better study design from diverse research groups to verify the generalizability and mechanistic underpinnings of these effects. Collectively, SEP evidence suggests that cervical spine mobilization consistently modulates cortical somatosensory processing and sensorimotor integration, though limited study rigour warrants cautious interpretation.

Other Brain Imaging

Both EEG studies reported no significant changes in the recorded brain activity but reported changes in pain measures. Navid et al. (2019) tested temperature‐induced pain in subclinical pain population and suggested that spinal adjustments may help to reset the balance between excitatory and inhibitory mechanisms involved in pain habituation.

Provencher et al. (2021) used d laser heat pulses to induce pain in healthy participants and mentioned C‐fibres inhibition or changes in pain amplification process as the central mechanisms of SMA on pain inhibition. However, these theoretical framework bases were not seen in the reported EEG results. Regardless of the assessment and imaging method, as both studies aimed to report the hypoalgesia following SMA, it is hard to differentiate the true neurophysiological effects of the SMA or SMO with those studies on healthy population.

Didehdar et al. (2020) and Kharazinejad and Didehdar (2024) used 1H‐MRS and reported increased neurometabolites in many regions of pain processing along with decreased pain and disability following five consecutive weeks of lumbosacral manipulation on individuals with nonspecific chronic LBP. Ogura et al. (2011) and Inami et al. (2017) used PET scan and also reported alterations in glucose metabolisms in several cortical and subcortical regions following cervical spine SMO in people with neck pain. Improvement in neurometabolites in the regions of pain processing reported by these studies suggest improved synaptic activity.

Collectively, current evidence suggests that SMT can produce measurable neurophysiological changes within the brain at multiple levels, including cortical excitability, functional connectivity, synaptic activity, sensorimotor integration and sensory and motor neuron excitability, leading to restoration of spinal and limb muscle activity. SMT techniques are commonly used manual techniques for the treatment of back and neck pain, and their underlying mechanisms continue to evolve. The current scoping review adds to the growing body of evidence related to potential mechanisms of spinal SMT responsible for pain relief and restoration of muscle activity and functional ability by summarizing and integrating the current evidence from cellular to functional levels. However, the magnitude, durability and clinical implications of these neuroplastic changes remain highly unclear due to considerable methodological heterogeneity. Differences in study populations, imaging modalities, timing of outcome assessments and manipulation level, all contribute to the complexity of interpretation of the results from current evidence. Notably, studies using TMS, fMRI and SEP demonstrate more consistent patterns of enhanced or altered sensorimotor integration post intervention, specifically in individuals with spinal pain, suggesting that SMT may modulate central pain processing and cortical reorganization. In contrast, evidence from EEG, PET scan and 1H‐MRS remain limited, precluding firm conclusions. Moreover, evidence on the clinical relevance of these neurophysiological changes within the CNS and long‐term pain/symptoms relief (e.g., pain and function) has not yet been explored. Taken together, although there is growing support for the neurophysiological changes at brain level following SMT, the current literature lacks methodological rigour and clinical signification. Standardization in the treatment/assessment protocol is needed for firm conclusions. It is worth noting that of the 26 included studies, more than half were conducted by a small number of research groups, each group contributing multiple studies employing similar techniques. This clustering of evidence may limit the generalizability of the findings attributed to the true effects of SMT on CNS.

Conclusion

This multimodal brain imaging scoping review synthesizes evidence from 26 studies investigating neurophysiological effects of SMT interventions, primarily using three brain imaging techniques. Although the findings support that SMT can modulate brain activity specifically PFC, sensorimotor cortex, thalamus, ACC and insula, the overall evidence is mixed and limited by significant methodological variability. TMS studies suggest changes in motor excitability, potentially resulting in improved muscle strength. SEP studies highlight altered somatosensory processing, whereas fMRI studies demonstrate changes in brain activity and functional connectivity. Although no study directly examined the relationship between CNS changes and pain intensity, the reported increases in activity within the somatosensory regions may suggest modulation of pain processing and potentially reduced pain following SMT. However, the clinical relevance of these changes (e.g., pain relief and functional improvement) remains inadequately understood.

Therefore, future studies should prioritize standardized intervention/assessment protocols. Larger, well‐designed randomized controlled trials are also needed to validate existing findings and explore possible relationships between cortical changes and clinical improvements.
