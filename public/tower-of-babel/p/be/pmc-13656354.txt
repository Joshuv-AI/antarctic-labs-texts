Effectiveness of community and primary-care-based maternal oral health interventions on pregnancy and infant outcomes: a systematic review
Authors: Abhi Thakkar, Bharani Kumar Bhattu, Chintan Desai, Deepkumar Patel, Abhishek Gupta, Harender Singh
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ObjectivesThe purpose of this systematic review and meta-analysis was to evaluate the effectiveness of community and primary care based maternal oral health interventions on maternal oral health outcomes, pregnancy outcomes, and infant oral health outcomes.Materials and methodsElectronic databases, including PubMed, Embase, Cochrane Library, Web of Science, and Scopus, were searched for studies published between 2000 and 2025. Eligible studies included randomized controlled trials and non-randomized intervention studies evaluating maternal oral health interventions delivered in antenatal, primary care, or community settings. Primary outcomes were maternal oral health indicators, preterm birth, low birth weight, and early childhood caries at or before 24 months of age. Risk of bias was assessed using the Cochrane Risk of Bias 2 tool for randomized trials and the Risk of Bias in Non-randomized Studies of Interventions tool for non-randomized studies. Random-effects meta-analyses were conducted for selected outcomes.ResultsTwelve studies met inclusion criteria. Meta-analysis of randomized trials showed a non-significant reduction in preterm birth (risk ratio 0.80, 95% confidence interval 0.60–1.05) and low birth weight (risk ratio 0.76, 95% confidence interval 0.51–1.13). Pooled analysis of non-randomized controlled studies demonstrated a significant reduction in early childhood caries prevalence (risk ratio 0.66, 95% confidence interval 0.59–0.74). Maternal oral health knowledge, behaviours, and clinical indicators consistently improved across studies.ConclusionsCommunity and primary care maternal oral health interventions appear to improve maternal oral health knowledge, behaviours, service utilization, and some clinical oral-health indicators. Evidence for effects on pregnancy outcomes and early childhood caries remains less certain because most included studies were non-randomized and several were at serious or critical risk of bias. According to GRADE considerations, certainty of evidence for several key outcomes was low or very low. Further adequately powered randomized trials are required before firm conclusions can be drawn regarding birth and infant outcomes.Clinical Trial Numbernot applicable.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at https://doi.org/10.1186/s12903-026-09220-7.

Introduction

Oral health during pregnancy is increasingly recognized as an essential component of maternal and child health. Maternal dental caries and periodontal conditions are biologically and behaviourally linked to adverse pregnancy outcomes and early childhood caries (ECC) through inflammatory mechanisms, microbial transmission, and shared health behaviours [1, 2]. These life course connections notwithstanding, oral health continues to be poorly integrated into antenatal care (ANC), primary care and community based maternal child health programs. Pregnancy care in many of these health systems remains focused on obstetric and medical surveillance, while preventive delivery of oral health services are separate from maternal care or not provided at all, resulting in missed opportunities for early intervention.

Systematic reviews of pregnancy oral health interventions demonstrate that community and primary care based programs particularly those delivered within prenatal clinics consistently improve maternal oral health knowledge, attitudes, self-efficacy, and hygiene behaviours [1, 2]. Psychologically informed interventions primarily target behavioural capability and frequently show improvements in plaque indices and gingival health among pregnant women [2]. These findings indicate that maternal oral health behaviours are modifiable when addressed within structured health service platforms. However, most interventions have focused on proximal behavioural and clinical markers rather than distal clinical endpoints, and direct evidence linking maternal oral health programs to improved pregnancy outcomes such as preterm birth (PTB) and low birth weight (LBW), or to reductions in infant ECC, remains limited and indirect [1, 2].

In contrast, broader community based primary health care (CBPHC) packages that integrate health education, home visits, and maternal child services have demonstrated improvements in antenatal care utilization, facility delivery, breastfeeding, neonatal mortality, and maternal health outcomes [3–5]. These findings support the efficacy of integrated community and primary care platforms for improving maternal and neonatal health. However, oral health specific interventions have not been comprehensively synthesized within this systems framework and their contribution to maternal infant outcomes remains poorly understood. While there is growing recognition of oral health as a dimension of pregnancy care and chronic disease management, scalable and sustainable integration pathways within primary care and public-health infrastructures remain underdeveloped [2].

Despite growing interest in oral-health promotion during pregnancy, existing reviews have primarily focused on oral-health education, behavioural change, or periodontal treatment during pregnancy, with less attention to interventions embedded within antenatal care, primary care, and community health systems. This leaves an important evidence gap regarding whether maternal oral-health interventions delivered through real-world public-health platforms can influence not only maternal oral-health knowledge and behaviours but also pregnancy outcomes, infant oral-health outcomes, service utilization, and implementation feasibility. The focus on community- and primary-care-based interventions is justified because these settings provide the most frequent and equitable points of contact with pregnant women, postpartum mothers, infants, and families, particularly in underserved populations. Antenatal clinics, primary-care practices, maternal-child health programs, and community health workers can deliver oral-health education, screening, referral, and preventive care within existing maternal health pathways, thereby reducing barriers created by separate dental-care systems. Synthesizing evidence from these settings is therefore essential for informing scalable and integrated models of maternal and infant oral-health promotion. The novelty of this review lies in its integrated scope. Rather than evaluating isolated dental treatment or education-only interventions, this review examines community- and primary-care-based maternal oral-health interventions across maternal behavioural and clinical outcomes, pregnancy outcomes, infant oral-health outcomes, and health-system implementation outcomes. This broader synthesis is intended to clarify the strength and limitations of current evidence and to identify models that may be feasible for integration into maternal and child health services. Accordingly, this systematic review evaluates the effectiveness of community- and primary-care-based maternal oral-health interventions across behavioural, clinical, pregnancy, infant, and implementation domains. The central question addressed in this review is whether community and primary care based maternal oral health interventions, compared with usual antenatal care or no structured oral health intervention improves maternal oral health outcomes, pregnancy outcomes, and infant oral health outcomes.

This review focuses on pregnant women at any trimester, mothers up to 12 months postpartum, and infants or young children up to two years of age for infant oral health outcomes. The interventions of interest are those delivered within community or primary care settings, including antenatal clinics, family medicine practices, and community health programs. These interventions encompass oral health education and counselling, mHealth based promotion, screening and referral integrated into ANC, preventive services such as oral hygiene instruction, and programs delivered by community health workers, nurses, or midwives. Comparators include usual antenatal care without structured oral health components, no intervention, or alternative intervention models.

The primary outcomes of interest are maternal oral health status and behaviours, pregnancy outcomes (PTB, LBW), and infant oral health outcomes including ECC and feeding practices. The secondary outcome measures included service utilization variables, referral completion rates, implementation quality metrics and sustainability within health systems.

In this systematic review, we aim to provide a critical appraisal of the effectiveness of community and primary care based maternal oral health intervention strategies on key outcomes: maternal oral health behaviours and clinical status; pregnancy outcomes; infant oral health outcomes and relevant implementation characteristics applicable to their potential integration into existing maternal child health delivery systems. The review aims to synthesize intervention evidence published between 2000 and 2025, classify the models and strategies for delivery, estimate their impact on maternal and infant endpoints where possible, as well as document barriers including facilitators to implementation and factors impacting sustainability. This review seeks to clarify the state of play around evidence by integrating behavioural data with maternal infant health outcomes and systems level considerations that can inform future research, policy development, and integration approaches in maternal and primary care settings.

Materials and methods

Protocol and registration

This systematic review was prospectively registered in the International Prospective Register of Systematic Reviews (PROSPERO), hosted by the Centre for Reviews and Dissemination, University of York, United Kingdom. PROSPERO is accessible at http://www.crd.york.ac.uk/PROSPERO/.

The protocol details the review objectives, eligibility criteria, search strategy, outcomes of interest, and planned methods for study selection, data extraction, risk of bias assessment, and synthesis. Any deviations from the registered protocol will be clearly documented and justified in the final publication to ensure transparency. The registration record can be accessed at: https://www.crd.york.ac.uk/PROSPERO/display_record.php?RecordID=510336.

Registration number: CRD420251033633. This systematic review was conducted and reported in accordance with the Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Reviews and Meta-Analyses (PRISMA) 2020 statement.

Focus question

The following focus question was developed according to the Population, Intervention, Comparison, and Outcome (PICO) framework:

Among pregnant women and postpartum mothers, do community and primary care based maternal oral health interventions, compared with usual antenatal care or no structured oral health intervention improve maternal oral health behaviours and clinical outcomes, pregnancy outcomes, and infant oral health outcomes?

This question encapsulates the goal of the review, to assess whether embedding oral health promotion, screening, preventive care and referral pathways into antenatal, primary care and community health systems engenders demonstrable change for the better, not just in relation to maternal knowledge and behaviours but also more broadly across pregnancy/early childhood outcomes.

Information sources

A comprehensive systematic search was conducted to identify peer-reviewed studies evaluating community- and primary-care-based maternal oral-health interventions and associated maternal, pregnancy, infant, and implementation outcomes. The following databases were searched: MEDLINE via PubMed, Embase, Cochrane Library, Web of Science, and Scopus. .

Searches were conducted on studies published from January 2000 to December 2025, intended to cover evolving maternal child health integration models and public-health strategies in antenatal oral health. Searches were restricted to human studies and English-language articles.

Besides electronic database search, reference lists of all the included articles and relevant reviews were manually checked for any additional eligible studies.

Search

The search strategy was developed using the Population, Intervention, Comparison, and Outcome framework and combined controlled vocabulary terms, where available, with free-text keywords. Search concepts included pregnancy or postpartum status, maternal or infant population, oral health, preventive or educational interventions, primary care, antenatal care, community health settings, and relevant maternal, pregnancy, infant, and implementation outcomes. Database-specific search strings are presented in Table S1 to ensure reproducibility.

Selection of studies

All records identified through the electronic database searches were imported into reference management software, and duplicates were removed prior to screening. Study selection was conducted in two sequential stages. In the first stage, titles and abstracts were independently screened by two reviewers against the predefined inclusion and exclusion criteria. Studies that clearly did not meet eligibility criteria were excluded at this stage. In the second stage, the full texts of potentially relevant articles were retrieved and independently assessed by the same two reviewers for final inclusion.

Disagreements at any stage of screening were resolved through discussion and consensus. If consensus could not be reached, a third reviewer was consulted to make the final decision.

Justifications for exclusion at the full-text stage were recorded to ensure transparency and reproducibility of our selection process.

A standardized and piloted data extraction form designed specifically for this review was used to extract data. On a sample of included studies, the form was piloted and then refined prior to complete data extraction. Data from each included study were independently extracted by two reviewers to ensure consistency. Extracted information included study characteristics, participant demographics, intervention details, comparator description, outcome measures, duration of follow-up, and key findings related to maternal, pregnancy, infant, and implementation outcomes.

When required data were missing, unclear, or incompletely reported, attempts were made to contact the corresponding authors of the original studies via email to request additional information or clarification. Any supplementary data obtained from investigators were cross-checked by both reviewers and incorporated into the final dataset.

Types of publications

Only full-text articles published in peer-reviewed journals were included. Letters, editorials, commentaries, conference abstracts without full reports, dissertations, and PhD theses were excluded.

Types of studies

Present Eligible study designs included randomized controlled trials, quasi-experimental studies, controlled before–after studies, cohort intervention studies, mixed-methods evaluations, and qualitative implementation studies linked to a defined intervention. Studies had to be published between 2000 and 2025.types of studies and publication dates, included in the review.

Types of participants/population

The review included studies involving pregnant women at any trimester and mothers up to 12 months postpartum. Infant outcomes were eligible only when assessed among infants or children aged 24 months or younger at the time of outcome measurement. Studies were required to be conducted within antenatal clinics, primary care settings, family-medicine practices, or community-health programs, or community-health programs. Studies reporting child outcomes beyond 24 months were not included in pooled infant outcome analyses; where such studies otherwise met maternal intervention criteria, their longer-term child outcomes were described narratively only.

Disease definition

The review focused on maternal oral health conditions during pregnancy and postpartum, including dental caries, gingivitis, periodontal disease, and oral-hygiene status. Infant outcomes included early childhood caries (ECC) and related feeding or oral-hygiene behaviours. Pregnancy outcomes of interest included preterm birth, low birth weight, and other neonatal complications.

Inclusion and exclusion criteria

Studies were included if they evaluated a maternal oral health intervention delivered within community or primary care settings and reported at least one eligible maternal, pregnancy, infant, or implementation outcome. Eligible infant outcomes were restricted to those assessed at 24 months of age or younger. Intervention had to be integrated into antenatal, primary-care, maternal-child health, or community health pathways.

We excluded studies that were conducted only within tertiary dental specialty clinics without integration into maternal antenatal, or primary care components;, they focused exclusively on clinical periodontal therapy with no community or ANC component, or primary-care component; evaluated child-only interventions without a maternal oral-health component; assessed infant or child outcomes only after 24 months of age without eligible maternal or pregnancy outcomes; or if they were non-intervention studies (case reports, narrative reviews, letters, editorials or protocols without results, dissertations, or conference abstracts without full reports.).

Sequential search strategy

The study selection procedure was sequential screening. First, all records identified by the database search were imported into reference management software and duplicates removed. Second, two reviewers examined titles and abstracts against the predefined inclusion criteria independently. Third, the full-text articles of potentially eligible studies were independently examined for final inclusion. We discussed and reached consensus on disagreements.

Data extraction

A standardized and piloted data extraction form was used for this. Data were independently extracted from each included study by two independent reviewers to ensure accuracy and reliability. Authors to whom we had access were contacted for missing or unclear data when necessary.

Data items

Data were extracted and organized in previously specified fields, including authors and year, country/setting, type of study design, sample size, characteristics of the participants involved in each study (both intervention recipients and others where relevant), description of intervention used in the study (dose), details about comparators; duration of follow-up; maternal outcomes recorded; pregnancy outcomes recorded; infant outcomes recorded; implementation indicators. Outcomes were categorized into five overall domains including maternal behavioural outcomes, maternal clinical outcomes, pregnancy outcomes, infant oral health outcomes and system level implementation outcomes to promote structured synthesis and comparative analyses.

Risk of bias within studies

Risk of bias was independently assessed by two reviewers using design-specific tools. Disagreements were resolved through discussion or consultation with a third reviewer.

Randomized controlled trials, including cluster randomized trials, were assessed using the Cochrane Risk of Bias 2 (RoB 2) tool. The following domains were evaluated: bias arising from the randomization process, deviations from intended interventions, missing outcome data, measurement of outcomes, and selection of the reported result. For cluster RCTs, additional considerations specific to cluster design were applied.

Non-randomized studies, including quasi-experimental, controlled intervention, prospective interventional cohort, and community-based intervention studies, were assessed using the Risk Of Bias In Non-randomized Studies of Interventions (ROBINS-I) tool. This tool evaluates bias due to confounding, selection of participants, classification of interventions, deviations from intended interventions, missing data, measurement of outcomes, and selection of reported results. Risk-of-bias judgments informed interpretation of findings and subsequent certainty of evidence assessment. Domain-level judgments were recorded for each included study and summarized in tabular form. Graphical risk-of-bias summaries were also prepared to support visual interpretation. Overall judgments were based on the highest level of concern across domains, with particular emphasis on confounding, selection bias, missing data, and selective reporting.

Risk of bias across studies

To assess risk of bias across studies, we evaluated potential publication bias and selective reporting that could affect the cumulative evidence. Because fewer than 10 studies contributed to each meta-analysis, formal assessment of publication bias using funnel plots or statistical tests was not performed.

Selective reporting within studies was evaluated during the RoB 2 and ROBINS-I assessments by examining consistency between reported outcomes and those described in the methods sections or trial registrations (if available).

The overall impact of reporting bias and small study effects was considered during the interpretation of findings and incorporated into the certainty of evidence assessment.

Statistical analysis

Where studies were sufficiently homogeneous in terms of participants, interventions, comparators, and outcomes, quantitative synthesis (meta-analysis) was conducted. Effect estimates were extracted or calculated for each study. For dichotomous outcomes (e.g., preterm birth, low birth weight, and early childhood caries), risk ratios (RRs) with 95% confidence intervals (CIs) were used. For cluster randomized trials, adjusted effect estimates accounting for clustering were used where reported.

A random-effects model was applied to account for anticipated clinical and methodological heterogeneity across studies conducted in different community and primary-care settings. Statistical heterogeneity was assessed using Cochran’s Q test and quantified using the I² statistic, with values greater than 50% considered indicative of substantial heterogeneity.

Quantitative meta-analysis was feasible only for selected outcomes, including preterm birth, low birth weight, and early childhood caries prevalence. Meta-analysis was restricted to outcomes for which studies were considered sufficiently comparable with respect to outcome definition, effect measure, and follow-up period. Outcomes with substantial clinical or methodological heterogeneity, including maternal oral-health knowledge, behavioural measures, and clinical indices, were not pooled and were synthesized narratively. For outcomes with substantial heterogeneity in measurement scales, intervention characteristics, or follow-up duration particularly maternal oral health knowledge, behaviours, and clinical indices meta-analysis was not performed, and findings were synthesized narratively.

Additional analysis

Sensitivity analyses were not performed because each meta-analysis included only two studies.

Formal subgroup analyses were not performed because of the small number of studies contributing to each pooled outcome. Comparisons by intervention type, setting, and outcome domain were interpreted narratively.

Meta-regression analyses were not conducted due to the limited number of studies per outcome where quantitative pooling was not feasible, studies were grouped by design (randomized and non-randomized) and intervention type to enable structured comparison of the direction and consistency of effects.

Results

Study selection

The database search identified a total of 11,530 records across PubMed (n = 175), Cochrane Library (n = 3,502), Web of Science (n = 1,375), Embase (n = 2,414), and Scopus (n = 3,025). After removal of duplicates, the remaining records were screened based on titles and abstracts. A large proportion of studies were excluded at this stage because they did not involve maternal oral health interventions, were not delivered in community or primary care settings, did not include relevant maternal, pregnancy, or infant outcomes, or were not interventional in design.

Full texts of potentially eligible articles were obtained and evaluated for the inclusion or exclusion according to our pre-specified criteria. This resulted in exclusion of 25 studies at the full-text stage. The main reasons for the exclusion were ineligible population (e.g., studies not targeting pregnant/≤12-month postpartum mothers, including those exclusively targeting children, caregivers or mothers outside this period), eligibility cap on intervention (e.g., child-only interventions or periodontal therapy-only trials without being embedded within maternal health services), missing relevant maternal or infant outcomes, non-empirical types of study (e.g. protocols) and wrong types of study that lacked an intervention( e.g., observational studies or descriptive program reports).

In total, 12 studies were identified that met the inclusion criteria and were included in the final qualitative synthesis. These comprised randomized controlled trials and non-randomized intervention studies conducted in community or primary care settings. Study characteristics

Twelve studies met the inclusion criteria and were included in the qualitative synthesis. The studies were conducted across diverse geographical settings, including Malawi, Australia, Brazil, Iran, Sweden, India, Japan, Cambodia, and Brazil, reflecting both high-income and low- and middle-income country contexts. Study designs included two randomized controlled trials (one cluster RCT and one multi-centre RCT), one economic evaluation conducted alongside an RCT, and nine non-randomized intervention studies (prospective cohort, quasi-experimental, field trials, controlled intervention studies, mixed-methods trials, and community-based interventions).

Sample sizes ranged from 100 participants in smaller quasi-experimental studies [6] to 10,069 pregnant women in a large cluster randomized trial [7]. Most studies recruited pregnant women attending antenatal care clinics; two studies included mother child dyads during infancy [8, 9]. Follow-up duration varied from short-term post-intervention assessments during pregnancy to infant follow-up up to 24 months [10, 11], and in one study extended up to 4.5 years of child age [12] .

Interventions commonly involved oral health education and counselling delivered by midwives, nurses, or community health workers; structured risk assessment and referral pathways integrated into antenatal care; preventive measures such as fluoride application and antimicrobial agents; and community-based oral health promotion delivered through primary care platforms. The MIHO-DS program evaluated by George et al. [13] integrated midwife-led screening with referral and structured dental treatment, whereas Valentine et al. [7] evaluated xylitol chewing gum as an adjunct to antenatal care [7, 13]. Non-randomized studies frequently combined education with preventive care components, including fluoride varnish, professional cleaning, dietary counselling, and motivational interviewing (e.g., Zanata et al., Gomez et al., Turton et al., [6, 8, 14].

Outcomes were grouped into maternal oral health outcomes (knowledge, attitudes, practices, plaque index, gingival bleeding, DMFT, periodontal indicators), pregnancy outcomes (preterm birth, low birth weight, very low birth weight), infant oral health outcomes (early childhood caries prevalence, caries increment, oral health related quality of life), and health-service utilization measures (dental service uptake, referral completion, cost-effectiveness). The cluster RCT by Valentine et al. [7] demonstrated a significant reduction in preterm birth and a non-significant reduction in low birth weight following cluster-adjusted analysis [7]. The multi-centre RCT by George et al. [13] showed significantly higher dental service uptake and improved maternal oral health indicators,

although no significant differences in preterm birth or low birth weight were observed [13]. Several quasi-experimental and community-based studies reported significant improvements in maternal oral health knowledge and behaviours [15–17]. and reduced early childhood caries prevalence in intervention groups [6, 8].

Methodological limitations across studies included non-randomized designs, small sample sizes, attrition bias, short follow-up periods, reliance on self-reported behavioural outcomes, and limited generalizability due to single-site implementation.

Risk of bias within studies

The risk of bias was evaluated at the study level with RoB 2 for randomized controlled trials, ROBINS-I for non-randomized intervention studies. Detailed domain-level judgments are presented in Table 2 and 3 and Figures 2 and 3.

Among the three randomized studies assessed using RoB 2, one study [13] was judged to be at low overall risk of bias. This trial demonstrated appropriate random sequence generation, allocation concealment, balanced attrition, objective and validated outcome measures, and pre-specified reporting. Two studies were judged as having some concerns. The economic evaluation alongside the RCT [18] had some concerns due to limited reporting of randomization procedures within the economic analysis and partial reliance on self-reported inputs. The cluster-randomized PPaX trial [7] was judged as having some concerns primarily related to limit reporting of allocation concealment and the potential for recruitment bias inherent in post-randomization enrolment within an open-label cluster design. No randomized study was rated as high risk of bias.

Nine non-randomized studies were assessed using the ROBINS-I tool. Overall, most were judged to be at serious risk of bias due to confounding and selection bias associated with non-randomized designs. Eight studies relied on sequential recruitment, voluntary participation, cluster allocation without full adjustment, or historical controls. Although several studies adjusted for measured confounders such as maternal age and socioeconomic status, residual confounding from unmeasured behavioural and contextual factors was likely. One study [21], which compared outcomes with historical controls without multivariable adjustment, was judged to be at Critical risk of bias. One study [9] was judged at Moderate risk overall, though concerns remained regarding attrition and analytical methods.

Across studies, bias due to classification of interventions was generally low, as interventions were clearly defined. However, missing data and attrition were common, with some studies reporting substantial loss to follow-up. Measurement bias was low for objectively measured clinical outcomes such as caries indices and birth weight but was higher for self-reported knowledge, attitudes, and behaviours. Selective reporting could not be excluded in several studies due to the absence of pre-registered protocols.

Overall, while randomized trials were methodologically robust with minor concerns, the predominance of serious risk of bias among non-randomized studies should be considered when interpreting pooled and narrative findings.

Certainty of evidence was considered using GRADE principles. Randomized evidence was initially considered higher certainty but was downgraded where there were concerns regarding imprecision, indirectness, or inconsistency. Non-randomized evidence was initially considered low certainty and was further downgraded when serious risk of bias, confounding, or imprecision was present. Consequently, certainty was judged to be low for pregnancy outcomes and low to very low for several infant and implementation-related outcomes. Evidence for maternal oral-health knowledge, behaviours, and service utilization was more consistent, although still limited by heterogeneity and risk of bias in non-randomized studies.

Results of individual studies

The effects of community- and primary care based maternal oral health interventions are summarized below by outcome domain. Pooled analyses (where appropriate) are illustrated in forest plots (Figures 4, 5 and 6).

Pregnancy outcomes

The cluster randomized preventing preterm with xylitol (PPaX) trial [7] evaluated the effect of xylitol chewing gum during pregnancy on birth outcomes among 10,069 women. The cluster-adjusted analysis demonstrated a significant reduction in preterm birth (RR 0.76, 95% CI 0.60–0.99). Rates of low birth weight were also lower in the intervention arm.

In contrast, the multi-centre RCT by George et al. [13] found no statistically significant differences in preterm birth or low birth weight between the intervention groups and standard antenatal care, despite significant improvements in dental service uptake and maternal oral health indicators [13].

The prospective interventional cohort study by Miyoshi et al. [21] reported reductions in very low birth weight (VLBW) of 46.4% and 69.7% across two rural regions following periodontal assessment and treatment integrated within community care [21]. However, the study relied on historical controls and did not report adjusted effect estimates with confidence intervals.

Maternal oral health outcomes

George et al. [13] reported significantly higher dental service uptake in the intervention group receiving structured dental treatment (87%) compared with control (20%). Improvements were also observed in oral health knowledge, oral health related quality of life, and clinical measures including Sulcus Bleeding Index and Plaque Index [13].

It demonstrated improvements in knowledge, attitudes, and practices (KAP), as well as trends toward improved clinical oral health indicators in the intervention group compared with no intervention, although statistically significant effects across all domains were not consistently reported [24–26]. Adams et al. (2017) reported significant reductions in Plaque Index, bleeding on probing, and periodontal pocket depth ≥ 4 mm in the nurse–midwife-led intervention group compared with standard Centering Pregnancy care [22].

Johnson et al. [23] found that all oral health education strategies improved maternal oral health knowledge compared with usual care, with the video-based intervention showing the strongest and most consistent effects [23].

Sampaio et al. [9] reported improvements in maternal oral hygiene practices and awareness following a community based educational intervention, although no control group was included [9].

Infant oral health outcomes

Zanata et al. [6] reported a 24-month caries prevalence of 14.7% in the intervention group compared with 33.3% in the control group, indicating a substantial reduction in early childhood caries among children of mothers receiving preventive counselling and fluoride/chlorhexidine interventions [6].

Turton et al. [8] found significantly lower ECC prevalence and improved oral health related quality of life (OHRQoL) in the intervention group receiving education and fluoride during vaccination visits compared with no intervention [8].

Deghatipour et al. [19] demonstrated that the comprehensive intervention arm (face-to-face education + posters + mobile application) achieved the lowest DMFT scores and the greatest improvements in oral health behaviours at 24 months compared with standard maternal and child health services [19].

Gomez et al. [14] reported significantly lower caries prevalence and lower dft scores among children of participating mothers compared with non-participating mother child pairs, although follow-up extended beyond the ≤ 24-month age criterion [14].

Health economic outcomes

Tannous et al. [18] reported that the Midwifery Initiated Oral Health Dental Service (MOH-DS) program was cost effective compared with routine care, with improved oral health outcomes and reduced disease burden, expressed as disability-adjusted life years (DALYs) averted [18].

Overall, individual study findings consistently demonstrated improvements in maternal oral health knowledge, behaviours, and clinical indicators. Evidence for effects on pregnancy outcomes was mixed, with strong findings in the large cluster RCT but null findings in the multi-centre RCT. Infant oral health outcomes generally favoured intervention groups in non-randomized and quasi-experimental studies. Forest plots summarizing pooled estimates for key outcomes (preterm birth, low birth weight, and ECC prevalence) are presented.

Synthesis of results

A total of 12 studies were included in the qualitative synthesis, comprising three randomized controlled trials (including one cluster RCT and one economic evaluation conducted alongside an RCT) and nine non-randomized intervention studies. Owing to heterogeneity in intervention components, delivery platforms, outcome definitions, and follow-up durations, quantitative meta-analysis was feasible only for selected outcomes with comparable effect measures and time points. For other outcomes, findings were synthesized narratively.

Pregnancy Outcomes

For preterm birth, pooled analysis of randomized trials demonstrated a non-significant reduction in risk favouring the intervention (RR 0.80, 95% CI 0.60–1.05; I² = 0%) as shown in Fig. 4.

Evidence from the non-randomized cohort study suggested reductions in very low birth weight; however, due to critical risk of bias and historical controls, this study was excluded from quantitative pooling [21].

Overall, evidence for pregnancy outcomes was inconsistent across trials and limited by the small number of randomized studies.

Maternal oral health outcomes

Across randomized and non-randomized studies, maternal oral health knowledge, behaviours, and clinical indicators improved consistently following intervention. Significant reductions in plaque indices, gingival bleeding, and DMFT scores were observed in several trials, along with marked increases in dental service utilization. The multi-centre RCT [13] demonstrated substantially higher dental service uptake in the structured intervention arm (87%) compared with control (20%), indicating a clinically meaningful effect. Where pooling was possible for continuous outcomes, standardized mean differences were in favour of intervention groups but heterogeneity was influenced by outcome measurement scale used and follow-up duration.

Infant Oral health Outcomes

Early childhood caries (ECC) prevalence at approximately 24 months was synthesized from two controlled non-randomized studies. Pooled analysis demonstrated a significant reduction in ECC prevalence favouring intervention (RR 0.66, 95% CI 0.59–0.74; I² = 0%) as shown in Fig. 6.

Health economic outcomes

The economic evaluation conducted alongside the RCT [18] found the midwifery initiated oral health dental service model to be cost-effective relative to routine care, with favourable disability adjusted life year (DALY) estimates and improved oral health outcomes.

Overall Interpretation

The synthesis indicates consistent directional improvements in maternal oral health behaviours service utilization, and selected clinical indicators across intervention types. However, the certainty of evidence varied substantially by outcome. Evidence for reductions in preterm birth and low birth weight was suggestive but not statistically conclusive when randomized trials were pooled. Although infant oral health outcomes demonstrated a significant pooled reduction in ECC prevalence, this estimate was based on non-randomized studies and should be interpreted cautiously. Overall certainty of evidence was low or very low for several outcomes because of serious risk of bias, indirectness, imprecision, and the limited number of randomized trials contributing to pooled analyses.

Risk of bias across studies

Assessment of risk of bias across studies was undertaken to evaluate potential threats to the cumulative body of evidence, including publication bias and selective outcome reporting. Given that quantitative meta-analysis was feasible for only three outcomes, each including two studies, formal assessment of small study effects was limited. However, because of the limited numbers of studies contributing to each meta-analysis, funnel plots were not performed, making interpretation unreliable.

The potential for selective reporting within studies was also examined. None of the non-randomized studies had publicly available pre-registered protocols to assess reporting bias. Among randomized trials, reporting largely matched stated objectives provided in the protocol, although detailed analytic plans were not always well described. In summary: Overall, no explicit sign of publication bias was identified but the small number of studies per outcome and poor protocol transparency necessitate cautious interpretation of the combined evidence.

Additional analysis

Formal sensitivity analyses were not performed because only two studies contributed to each pooled outcome. This non-randomized prospective cohort study was assessed at Critical risk of bias [21] and therefore was not included in quantitative pooling of pregnancy outcomes; exclusion of this study did not alter pooled estimates but reduced the scope of contextual evidence. Analyses limited to randomized controlled trials were necessarily performed for pregnancy outcomes, as only two RCTs provided data to preterm birth and low birth weight meta-analyses. Within these RCT-only analyses, pooled estimates found favourable point estimates for intervention but were not statistically significant and evidence of statistical heterogeneity was low (I^2 = 0%), indicating that inconsistency among studies was primarily clinical rather than statistical.

Subgroup comparisons were conducted narratively according to study design (RCTs versus non-randomized studies), intervention type (education-only versus integrated antenatal models incorporating clinical assessment or treatment), and outcome domain (maternal oral health, pregnancy, and infant outcomes). Integrated antenatal care models demonstrated larger and more consistent effects on dental service uptake and maternal clinical indicators compared with education-only interventions. In contrast, pregnancy outcome effects were largely influenced by the large cluster RCT, while the individual RCT showed no significant differences. Infant oral health outcomes were more consistently favourable across non-randomized controlled studies.

Exploratory comparisons by country income level suggested that larger absolute reductions in adverse pregnancy outcomes were observed in lower-resource settings; however, the small number of studies per outcome and limited statistical power precluded formal subgroup meta-analysis or meta-regression. Given that pooled analyses included only two studies per outcome, quantitative exploration of effect modifiers was not performed.

Overall, additional analyses indicated that findings for maternal behavioural and clinical outcomes were directionally consistent across study designs. In contrast, conclusions regarding pregnancy and infant outcomes were sensitive to intervention type, study design, and risk-of-bias considerations, reinforcing the need for cautious interpretation.

Discussion

Summary of evidence

This review suggests that community- and primary-care-based maternal oral-health interventions are most consistently associated with improvements in maternal oral-health knowledge, behaviours, service utilization, and selected clinical indicators. However, evidence for pregnancy and infant outcomes was less certain because most included studies were non-randomized and several were judged to be at serious or critical risk of bias.

For pregnancy outcomes requires cautious interpretation. Although the pooled estimates for preterm birth and low birth weight favoured intervention, neither reached statistical significance. The large cluster PPaX RCT [7] demonstrated a significant decrease in preterm birth, and favourable trends for low birth weight, using models that were adjusted according to clusters. However, these findings were not reproduced in the multi-centre individual RCT [13], leading to inconsistency across trials. This inconsistency may reflect differences in intervention content, baseline obstetric risk, health-system context, timing and intensity of intervention delivery, and statistical power to detect relatively uncommon birth outcomes. The PPaX trial evaluated xylitol chewing gum within antenatal care in a higher-risk setting, whereas the multi-centre trial evaluated a midwife-initiated screening, referral, and dental-treatment pathway in Australia [7, 13]. Therefore, the observed differences may represent contextual and intervention-specific effects rather than a uniform effect of maternal oral-health interventions on pregnancy outcomes.Overall, the certainity of evidence for adverse pregnancy outcomes was low because only two randomized trials contributed to pooled estimates, confidence intervals crossed the line of no effect, and interventions differed substantially in content and setting. Therefore, current evidence suggests possible benefit but is insufficient to support a definitive conclusion that these interventions reduce preterm birth or low birth weight.

For maternal oral health outcomes, the most plausible interpretation is that interventions embedded within routine care can reduce access barriers and reinforce preventive behaviours at a teachable point in pregnancy. Multicomponent interventions embedded within antenatal care especially those which used structured screening tools, referral pathways, and clinical treatment showed significant increases in use of dental services, plaque indices, gingival bleeding, and oral health knowledge. However, because many supporting studies were non-randomized, these findings should be interpreted as consistent and clinically relevant rather than definitive evidence of causal effectiveness.

Controlled non-randomized studies from limited settings with infant oral health outcomes, most notably ECC prevalence ≤ 24 months, suggested significant reductions in intact surfaces or early childhood observations between groups of children whose mothers received an oral health intervention vs. no intervention. Pooled estimates favoured intervention; however, uncertainty remains due to heterogeneity in caries diagnostic criteria, follow-up duration, and intervention components. Because infant oral-health outcomes were primarily derived from non-randomized controlled studies, and because residual confounding and selection bias could not be excluded, the certainty of evidence for early childhood caries outcomes was judged to be low. Nevertheless, this evidence was derived from non-randomized studies, and residual confounding, differences in caries diagnostic thresholds, and variation in follow-up duration limit causal interpretation.

The economic evaluation alongside the randomized trial indicated that a midwifery-initiated oral health dental service model was cost-effective, with acceptable ranges of disability-adjusted life year (DALY) estimates and improved oral health outcomes. However, economic conclusions remain dependent on assumptions regarding referral completion, treatment uptake, workforce capacity, and transferability across health systems Importantly, unlike previous reviews that focused primarily on oral-health behaviours or periodontal interventions during pregnancy, the present review evaluated community- and primary-care-based maternal oral-health interventions across maternal, pregnancy, infant, and implementation outcomes, providing a broader assessment of their potential public-health impact.

Relevance to key stakeholders

For health care professionals, particularly midwives, nurses, community health workers, and primary care practitioners, the evidence supports integration of structured oral health assessment, education, and refers on pathways in antenatal care.

Interventions targeting pregnant women and families can be delivered during routine antenatal visits, providing accessible opportunities to improve oral health knowledge and behaviours; such improvements may have positive impacts on infant oral health and possibly pregnancy outcomes.

For policy-makers and health system planners, the findings demonstrate a potential public-health benefit of embedding oral health into maternal care platforms especially in resource-limited settings where baseline risk of adverse outcomes is increased. Yet mixed results for pregnancy outcomes and the overall lack of randomized evidence on infant outcomes highlight that further rigorously-designed trials are needed before any firm policy mandates can be recommended.

Overall, this review provides evidence of consistent benefits for maternal oral health outcomes, promising but inconsistent effects on pregnancy outcomes and favourable yet methodologically limited evidence for infant oral health outcomes.

Limitations

There are several limitations that must be accounted for while interpreting the findings of this review. First, at the study-level, a large number of included studies were non-randomized and at Serious or Critical risk of bias based on confounding, selection bias and attrition, and lack of protocol registration.

Second, regarding the outcome level, pregnancy outcomes were reported in only two randomized trials eligible for pooling, restricting statistical power and precision. Infant oral-health outcomes were based mainly on non-randomized studies, and maternal clinical and behavioural outcomes could not be pooled because of differences in outcome measures, follow-up periods, and intervention components.

Thirdly, several limitations must be acknowledged at the review level. We included only studies published in English, introducing a potential language bias.Unpublished or grey literature may have been missed. The limited number of studies contributing to each meta-analysis precluded robust statistical evaluation for the presence of publication bias, and funnel plots containing fewer than ten studies within an outcome are intrinsically underpowered to identify asymmetry.

Taken together, these limitations indicate that although the direction of evidence is informative, the overall certainty of evidence remains limited, particularly for pregnancy and infant outcomes. The conclusions have therefore been revised to avoid causal or definitive claims not fully supported by the available evidence.

Conclusions

This systematic review found that community- and primary-care-based maternal oral-health interventions may improve maternal oral-health knowledge, behaviours, dental service utilization, and selected clinical oral-health indicators. Evidence suggesting reductions in early childhood caries appears favourable but is based mainly on non-randomized studies and should therefore be interpreted cautiously.

Evidence for pregnancy outcomes, including preterm birth and low birth weight, remains inconclusive. Although one large cluster randomized trial reported a significant reduction in preterm birth, pooled randomized evidence did not demonstrate statistically significant effects. Therefore, current evidence is insufficient to support definitive conclusions regarding the impact of maternal oral-health interventions on birth outcomes.

Overall, the findings support further integration and evaluation of oral health within antenatal and primary care settings, but the certainty of evidence is limited by the predominance of non-randomized designs, serious risk of bias in several studies, and small numbers of studies contributing to pooled outcomes. Future research should prioritize adequately powered randomized trials, standardized outcome definitions, longer infant follow-up, and transparent protocol registration to strengthen the evidence base for policy and practice.
