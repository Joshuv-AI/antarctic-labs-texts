Assessing the effects of naltrexone‐bupropion on hepatic steatosis and fibrosis in patients with T2DM and overweight or obesity: Insights from a placebo‐controlled trial
Authors: Alina N Saidi, Laura A M Konings, Carmen A W Dietvorst, Vivian D de Jong, Willem Pieter Brouwer, Aart‐Jan van der Lelij, Marco Alings, Martien van Wenum, Simone P Rauh, Weiwei Xu, Manuel Castro Cabezas
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundMetabolic dysfunction‐associated steatotic liver disease (MASLD) is prevalent among individuals with overweight or obesity and type 2 diabetes (T2DM), increasing the risk for liver‐related and cardiovascular complications. Lifestyle changes to reduce body weight are the primary intervention to decrease the risk of MASLD and liver fibrosis. This study aimed to evaluate the impact of a pharmacological intervention with naltrexone/bupropion (NB) on hepatic steatosis and fibrosis risk in patients with T2DM using non‐invasive tests.Materials and MethodsIn this post hoc analysis of a 56‐week, randomized, double‐blind, placebo‐controlled trial 505 adults with T2DM and overweight or obesity were randomized to either NB or placebo (2:1), both combined with structured lifestyle counselling. Hepatic outcomes were measured using the Hepatic Steatosis Index (HSI), Metabolic Dysfunction‐Associated Fibrosis‐5 (MAF‐5) and Fibrosis‐4 (FIB‐4) indices. Changes in liver‐related indices were analysed by treatment arm and weight loss strata (<5%, ≥5%, ≥10%), and multivariable regression was used to identify predictors of hepatic improvement.ResultsAt Week 56, the NB group achieved significantly greater body weight loss (−6.3 ± 7.2 kg vs. −2.5 ± 5.2 kg, p < 0.001). Lifestyle intervention with NB treatment compared with placebo significantly reduced HSI (−2.9 vs. −1.2, p < 0.001) and MAF‐5 (−0.80 vs. −0.31, p = 0.003), while FIB‐4 scores remained unchanged in both groups. The best improvements in HSI and MAF‐5 were observed in those achieving ≥5% or ≥ 10% weight loss compared with those with <5% (all p < 0.001). Weight change correlated strongly with HSI reduction (r = 0.796, p < 0.001) and moderately with MAF‐5 (r = 0.488, p < 0.001), but not with FIB‐4 (r = 0.034, p = 0.590). Multiple regression analysis identified weight change as the strongest predictor of improvements in hepatic markers (HSI, MAF‐5, ALT and AST), with no significant contributions from other factors.ConclusionWeight loss was the key driver of hepatic improvements in patients with T2DM and overweight or obesity. Treatment with NB resulted in significantly greater weight reduction and significantly greater improvements in HSI and MAF‐5 compared with placebo, suggesting an added benefit for liver health.Keywords: liver fibrosis, liver steatosis, MASH, MASLD, naltrexone‐bupropion, weight loss

INTRODUCTION

Metabolic dysfunction‐associated steatotic liver disease (MASLD), formerly known as non‐alcoholic fatty liver disease (NAFLD), has emerged as a significant global health challenge, affecting approximately 30% of the general population. MASLD is defined by the presence of hepatic steatosis along with at least one cardiometabolic risk factor. 1 MASLD encompasses a spectrum of liver conditions, starting with simple steatosis and potentially progressing to metabolic dysfunction‐associated steatohepatitis (MASH), the inflammatory form of MASLD. This inflammatory stage can further advance to fibrosis, cirrhosis and, in some cases, hepatocellular carcinoma (HCC), all in the absence of secondary factors like excessive alcohol use or other liver conditions. 1 , 2 , 3

MASLD is highly prevalent in patients with type 2 diabetes mellitus (T2DM), impacting over 65% of patients with T2DM. 4 Cohorts with liver histology data available show that approximately 66% of patients with T2DM have MASH, underscoring the substantial burden of advanced liver disease in this group. 5 Obesity, which commonly coexists with T2DM, further increases the likelihood of MASLD, with rates as high as 90% reported among obese patients. 4 With the global rise in obesity and T2DM rates, the prevalence of MASLD is expected to increase as well, posing an ongoing and growing public health concern. 4 , 6 , 7

A key driver of MASLD progression is liver fibrosis, which also serves as a strong predictor of serious complications such as liver failure and cardiovascular disease (CVD). Consequently, early detection and effective management of both MASLD and liver fibrosis are essential to prevent disease progression and reduce cardiovascular (CV) risk.

Currently, lifestyle interventions aimed at achieving weight loss remain the cornerstone of MASLD management, as even modest reductions in body weight can improve steatosis. 5 , 8 , 9 In addition to lifestyle modification, various pharmacological treatments are currently under investigation for MASLD, such as incretins, PPAR agonists and others. 10 Among these, the combination of naltrexone sustained‐release (SR), and bupropion SR (NB), which combines an opioid receptor antagonist and a dopamine/norepinephrine reuptake inhibitor, has been studied for its potential to reduce appetite by modulating the brain's reward system. 11 This combination could offer a useful option for overweight or obese patients with T2DM and MASLD.

While liver biopsy remains the gold standard for MASLD diagnosis, its invasive nature, high cost and potential complications make it unsuitable for routine screening or large‐scale implementation. 1 , 12 To address this, several non‐invasive tests (NITs) have been developed. These include imaging techniques such as vibration‐controlled transient elastography (VCTE; FibroScan®), as well as various scoring algorithms. 1 To estimate hepatic steatosis, the Hepatic Steatosis Index (HSI) has been validated, and it has been widely adopted in both clinical and research contexts. 13 , 14 For fibrosis assessment, the recently validated Metabolic Dysfunction‐Associated Fibrosis 5 (MAF‐5) index, especially relevant for individuals with metabolic disorders such as T2DM or obesity, or the widely utilized Fibrosis‐4 (FIB‐4) index could be utilized. 12 , 15 , 16 , 17

This study aimed to investigate the effects of NB in combination with structured lifestyle counselling on hepatic steatosis and fibrosis risk in patients with T2DM and overweight or obesity, using NITs.

MATERIALS AND METHODS

Study design

This is a post‐hoc analysis of a 56‐week, double‐blind, placebo‐controlled trial in which the effects of NB were evaluated. 11 The study enrolled 505 patients with overweight or obesity and T2DM, all of whom received a standardized lifestyle intervention. The protocol was approved by each site's Institutional Review Board. Written informed consent was obtained from all participants, and the study adhered to the Declaration of Helsinki and the principles of Good Clinical Practice standards. 18

Study population

The study included men and women aged 18 years to 70 years with T2DM, a BMI between 27 kg/m2 and 45 kg/m2, fasting blood glucose levels below 70 mg/dL and haemoglobin A1c (HbA1c) levels ranging from 7% (53 mmol/mol) to 10% (86 mmol/mol). Patients were either not using diabetes medications or had maintained stable doses of oral antidiabetic drugs (OADs) for at least 3 months prior to enrolment. Patients had systolic blood pressure <145 mmHg and diastolic blood pressure < 95 mmHg. A comprehensive list of inclusion and exclusion criteria is provided in a previous publication. 11

Data collection

Participants were randomized (2:1) via a computer‐generated randomization schedule to receive either 32 mg/day naltrexone SR plus 360 mg/day bupropion SR (NB) or placebo, stratified by baseline HbA1c (≤64 mmol/mol or >64 mmol/mol) and sulfonylurea use. NB dosing was initiated at one tablet daily and escalated to two tablets twice daily by Week 4. Adherence and adverse events were assessed via phone at Week 2. Study visits occurred at screening, baseline and every 4 weeks thereafter. Participants followed a hypocaloric diet (500 kcal/day deficit) with dietary and lifestyle counselling based on the World Health Organization (WHO) algorithm for calculating resting metabolic rate. Participants received nutrition counselling and instructional booklets adapted from the Exchange Lists for Weight Management, in alignment with guidelines established by the American Diabetes Association and the American Dietetic Association. Additionally, participants received written instructions on behavioural modification strategies, including recommendations to engage in at least 30 minutes of walking per day. Blood glucose was monitored twice daily for 4 weeks, with protocol‐defined OAD adjustments if glycaemic control deteriorated. Full details are available in the original publication. 11

Outcome measures

The primary outcome measures were changes in hepatic steatosis and fibrosis, assessed using the HSI, MAF‐5 and FIB‐4 indices, respectively. Hepatic steatosis was evaluated at baseline and at Week 56, with a reduction in HSI indicating improvement. HSI was calculated using the following formula: HSI = 8 × AST/ALT + BMI (+2 if female, 0 if male). HSI cut‐off points were used to classify hepatic steatosis: values below 30 ruled out steatosis, and values above 36 ruled in steatosis. The risk of hepatic fibrosis was assessed at baseline and Week 56 using the MAF‐5 index, calculated as: MAF − 5 = −11.367 + WC × 0.0282 − BMI × 0.1761 + WC × BMI × 0.0019 + 2.0763 for diabetes + ln (AST) × 2.9207 – platelets × 0.0059. A MAF‐5 score of <0 indicated a low risk of fibrosis, while ≥1 indicated a high risk. FIB‐4 was calculated as: age × AST/(platelets × √ALT), and interpreted using standard cut‐offs: <1.3 (low risk),1.3 to 2.67 (intermediate risk) and >2.67 (high risk). 1 Multiple regression models included factors such as baseline glucose levels, lipid parameters and weight change, selected for their relevance to MASLD progression.

Statistical analysis

Data are presented as mean ± standard deviation (SD), unless otherwise specified. Paired samples t‐tests were used to assess within‐group changes in HSI, MAF‐5 and FIB‐4 from baseline to Week 56 in both the treatment and placebo groups. Subgroup analyses, stratified by weight loss categories (<5%, ≥5% and ≥10%) were conducted to evaluate differential responses in NITs. Correlation analyses were performed using Pearson or Spearman correlation coefficients, as appropriate. One outlier, identified based on extreme weight change, was excluded based on scatterplot analyses. Multiple regression analysis was performed to identify independent factors associated with changes in HSI, MAF‐5, FIB‐4 and liver enzymes. All analyses were conducted using IBM SPSS Statistics version 28.0.0.0 (IBM SPSS, New York, USA), with statistical significance set at p < 0.05.

RESULTS

The study cohort included 285 females (56.5%) and 219 males (43.5%), with a mean age of 53.8 ± 9.3 years, mean weight of 104.4 ± 18.2 kg and mean BMI of 36.4 ± 4.7 kg. All the participants fulfilled the criteria for MASLD at baseline. Baseline characteristics of the overall population are presented in Supplementary Table S1, and treatment group‐specific baseline characteristics in Supplementary Table S2. The NB group showed a significantly larger weight reduction compared with the placebo group (−6.3 ± 7.2 kg, vs. −2.5 ± 5.2 kg, p < 0.001). In addition, fasting glucose levels decreased significantly in the NB group (mean change −10.9 mg/dL, p = 0.002), while the decrease in the placebo group was not statistically significant (−6.7 mg/dL, p = 0.163). At Week 56, hepatic indices improved significantly in both groups. HSI decreased from 49.9 ± 5.7 to 48.7 ± 5.6 in the placebo group (p < 0.001), and from 50.7 ± 5.9 to 47.7 ± 6.5 in the NB group (p < 0.001), with a greater reduction in the NB group (mean change: −2.94 vs. –1.18; p < 0.001) (Figure 1A). Similarly, MAF‐5 scores decreased from 3.0 ± 1.8 to 2.7 ± 1.7 in the placebo group (p = 0.006), and from 3.1 ± 2.1 to 2.3 ± 2.1 in the NB group (p < 0.001), with a significantly greater reduction in the NB group (−0.80 vs. –0.31; p = 0.003) (Figure 1B). In contrast, FIB‐4 scores did not change significantly within (NB: p = 0.731; placebo: p = 0.137) or between groups (p = 0.217) (Figure 1C).

Subgroup analyses hepatic steatosis and fibrosis

Baseline clinical and demographic characteristics by weight loss subgroups (<5%, ≥5% and ≥ 10%) are presented in Table 1. From baseline to Week 56, significant reductions in BMI were observed in participants with ≥5% and ≥10% weight loss in both the placebo and NB groups (all p < 0.001). Among participants with <5% weight loss, BMI remained stable in the placebo group (p = 0.490) and showed a small but statistically significant decrease in the NB group (p = 0.045) (Supplementary Table S3). Improvements in HSI were significant among participants who achieved ≥5% or ≥10% weight loss, regardless of treatment group, with the largest reductions observed in those achieving ≥10% weight loss. In the placebo group, HSI significantly decreased in both the ≥5% (51.0 ± 5.9 to 46.3 ± 5.1, p < 0.001) and ≥10% (50.4 ± 4.1 to 43.3 ± 4.9, p < 0.001) subgroups, while no significant change was seen in the <5% subgroup (p = 0.783). Similarly, in the NB group, significant reductions were seen in the ≥5% (51.1 ± 5.6 to 45.9 ± 5.8) and ≥10% (50.7 ± 5.2 to 43.9 ± 5.3) subgroups (both p < 0.001), but not in the <5% subgroup (p = 0.172) (Table 2). MAF‐5 scores in the placebo group improved significantly in participants with ≥5% weight loss (p = 0.002), whereas those with <5% weight loss showed no significant change (p = 0.177). The ≥10% group showed a numerical improvement that did not reach statistical significance (p = 0.112). In the NB group, MAF‐5 improved significantly in both the ≥5% and ≥10% subgroups (both p < 0.001), while the <5% group showed no significant change (p = 0.258) (Table 3). FIB‐4 scores remained unchanged across all weight loss categories in both the placebo and NB groups (placebo: p = 0.388; NB: p = 0.572) (Supplementary Table S4).

Correlation and regression analyses

Weight change was strongly correlated with reductions in HSI (r = 0.796, p < 0.001) and moderately with MAF‐5 (r = 0.488, p < 0.001) (Figure 2A,B). In contrast, no significant correlation was found between weight change and FIB‐4 score (r = 0.034, p = 0.590) (Figure 2C). Multiple regression analyses confirmed weight change as the strongest independent predictor of changes in HSI (β = 0.441, p < 0.001), MAF‐5 (β =0.096, p < 0.001) (Supplementary Tables S5 and S6), ALT (β = 0.559, p < 0.001) and AST (β = 0.202, p = 0.011) (Tables 4 and 5). Other variables, including age, sex and baseline lipid levels, were not significant predictors (all p > 0.05).

DISCUSSION

This study assessed the effect of NB combined with structured lifestyle counselling on liver health in patients with T2DM and overweight or obesity. The original study demonstrated that NB results in greater weight loss compared with lifestyle modification alone. 11 Building upon these findings, our study shows that NB may also improve hepatic steatosis and fibrosis, as measured by HSI and MAF‐5. The current study identified weight loss as the primary determinant of improvements in hepatic steatosis and fibrosis, reinforcing its central role in the management of MASLD, aligning with existing literature. 5 , 8 , 9 Improvements in hepatic steatosis were observed primarily in participants who achieved at least 5% weight loss, with greater benefits seen at ≥10%. In contrast, weight loss below 5% was not sufficient to produce meaningful changes in hepatic steatosis in either group. Similar trends were observed for hepatic fibrosis, although the ≥10% weight loss placebo subgroup did not reach statistical significance, likely due to a small sample size. The observed differences are unlikely to be explained by the use of concomitant antidiabetic medications such as metformin or thiazolidinediones (TZDs), as these were similarly distributed between groups as reported in the original study. 11

In comparison with existing literature, studies investigating GLP‐1 receptor agonists such as semaglutide and liraglutide similarly emphasize the central role of weight loss in improving liver outcomes in MASLD. For instance, a phase 3 trial of semaglutide in patients with biopsy‐confirmed MASH and significant fibrosis demonstrated substantial resolution of steatohepatitis and fibrosis alongside an average weight loss exceeding 10%. 19 Similarly, a recent study comparing liraglutide to matched lifestyle‐induced weight loss in non‐diabetic MASLD patients found that comparable weight reductions yielded similar improvements in hepatic steatosis and disease activity, highlighting weight loss as the primary mediator of liver benefit. 20 Notably, liraglutide also provided additional metabolic advantages, such as improved glucose regulation, underscoring potential incretin‐related effects beyond weight reduction. 21

In contrast, resmetirom, a thyroid hormone receptor‐β agonist, exerts its therapeutic effects through direct modulation of hepatic lipid metabolism. While resmetirom is not a weight‐loss agent per se, in a phase 2 trial of resmetirom, patients who achieved ≥5% weight loss experienced greater hepatic improvements, suggesting that weight loss amplifies its liver‐specific benefits. 22 These findings underscore weight loss as a central driver of hepatic improvement across therapies, while also illustrating that agents like resmetirom may exert additional, weight‐independent benefits.

To the best of our knowledge, this is among the first analyses reporting beneficial effects of NB on hepatic outcomes in patients with T2DM and overweight or obesity using NITs. A prior pooled RCT reported ALT improvements with NB but did not focus solely on patients with T2DM, and used FIB‐4, 11 a marker known to have limited accuracy in metabolic populations. 23 , 24 , 25 , 26 , 27 Consistent with evidence suggesting FIB‐4's suboptimal performance in patients with T2DM, our study did not show any changes, while the metabolic‐specific NIT MAF‐5 was more responsive to weight‐related hepatic changes, supporting its use in patients with T2DM. 15

Several limitations should be considered. Although HSI and MAF‐5 include BMI and waist circumference, and may partly reflect weight‐related changes, the fact that weight loss was the strongest predictor of ALT and AST changes supports our conclusion of hepatic improvement. Additionally, while validated, these non‐invasive markers cannot replace liver biopsy or advanced imaging for definitive diagnosis and staging. Furthermore, our analysis was limited by the absence of data necessary to calculate other established non‐invasive steatosis and fibrosis markers, which restricted the scope of comparative assessments in this study. Future research should consider a wider variety of NITs. Moreover, this study is a post hoc analysis of a trial primarily designed for other endpoints than MASLD‐related outcomes. Given this, the findings should be considered hypothesis‐generating and warrant confirmation in prospective studies. Finally, while the NITs used to assess hepatic steatosis and fibrosis were not originally developed for longitudinal follow‐up, emerging evidence supports their potential utility in this context. 28 , 29 , 30 Future studies with longer follow‐up periods and larger, more diverse cohorts are needed to confirm the durability of NB's benefits and to further validate these markers against histological or imaging endpoints. Such studies are essential to establish the role of NB as a potential adjunctive treatment in the metabolic management of MASLD.

CONCLUSION

In conclusion, this study demonstrates that weight loss is the primary factor driving improvements in hepatic steatosis and fibrosis, as assessed by HSI and MAF‐5, among patients with T2DM and overweight or obesity. Importantly, treatment with NB resulted in significantly greater weight loss compared with lifestyle intervention alone, which was accompanied by more pronounced reductions in hepatic indices. These findings suggest that NB provides an added benefit by facilitating further weight loss, thereby enhancing liver health beyond lifestyle measures.
