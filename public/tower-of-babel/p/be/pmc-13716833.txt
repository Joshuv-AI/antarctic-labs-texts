Base editing reveals an essential role for NANOG in human embryogenesis
Authors: Oliver J Bower, Ana E R Orsi, Riley McMahon, Desislava Staneva, Josephine R Blagrove, Kashish Singh, Claire S Simon, Afshan McCarthy, Patricia Garcia, Valerie Shaikly, Mohamed Taranissi, Martin Wilding, Paul Serhal, Rabi A Odia, Mina Vasilic, Meenakshi Choudhary, Athanasios Papathanasiou, Kay Elder, Phil Snell, Leila Christie, Mandana Arbab, David R Liu, Mary Herbert, Katarina Harasimov, Kathy K Niakan
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractUnderstanding how the first cell lineages in human development are specified and maintained has fundamental importance and clinical implications for regenerative medicine, infertility and pregnancy loss. Although mouse models have provided valuable insights into transcription factors regulating early development, translating these findings to human embryos has been limited by ethical, technical and biological constraints. Functional studies of transcription factors in human embryos have been hindered by nuclease-based genome editing approaches that induce genotoxicity1–3. Here, to overcome this, we applied ABE8e adenine base editing4,5 to precisely target an exon splice donor site, resulting in a splicing defect and functional knockout of the developmental regulator NANOG in human embryos. This approach did not trigger genotoxicity and showed limited off-target editing. Loss of NANOG disrupts pluripotent epiblast specification and instead cells differentiate towards a primitive endoderm (yolk sac) or trophectoderm (placental) transcriptional programme. Retention of primitive endoderm differentiation in NANOG-edited human embryos reveals a functional compensation that is distinct from mouse, underscoring the importance of directly investigating human development. Our findings demonstrate an essential role for NANOG in human pluripotency and epiblast specification and highlight the utility of base editing for functional interrogation of human development.Subject terms: Genetic engineering, Embryology, Stem cells, Genomic instability, Embryonic stem cells

Main

The genetic regulation of lineage specification in the human embryo remains poorly understood, particularly the mechanisms by which key transcription factors establish and maintain the pluripotent embryonic epiblast. The homeodomain transcription factor NANOG is required for epiblast development in the mouse6,7. Moreover, NANOG contributes to stabilizing a pluripotency gene regulatory network that is required to maintain mouse and human embryonic stem cells (ESCs) in an undifferentiated state8,9. In human preimplantation embryos, NANOG expression is specifically restricted to the epiblast, the precursor population of the embryo proper, prior to the restriction of other epiblast-associated factors, such as OCT4 and SOX210–12. The functional role of NANOG and the mechanisms by which it regulates epiblast specification and pluripotency maintenance in human embryos remain unclear partly owing to technical challenges associated with genetic perturbation in human embryos.

The use of CRISPR–Cas9 mutagenesis can trigger genomic instability, chromosomal rearrangements and unintended mutations at on-target sites due to error-prone resolution of DNA double-strand breaks1–3. Human zygotes are particularly vulnerable to these adverse effects due to inefficient DNA double-strand break repair, leading to high rates of karyotypic abnormalities after editing1,3,13. These limitations hinder the use of the originally described method of CRISPR–spCas9 for studying human embryogenesis and pose a significant barrier to its clinical translation.

NANOG mutant hESCs lose stem cell identity

To overcome genotoxicity we used base editing, a precision genome editing technology that uses a deaminase enzyme fused to a Cas9 nickase protein to modify individual bases in the DNA of living cells without the generation of DNA double-strand breaks14. We selected the adenine base editor variant ABE8e for its enhanced catalytic activity following phage-assisted continuous evolution5. We designed a single guide RNA (sgRNA) to target the splice donor sites of NANOG (Fig. 1a), reasoning that it would induce a splicing defect leading to intron retention or exon skipping, ultimately producing mutant proteins15. To minimize the use of valuable human embryos, sgRNAs targeting NANOG were first screened in human ESCs (hESCs)16 (Extended Data Fig. 1a), where its function remains incompletely characterized and knockdown or knockout phenotypes are variable11,17–21.

We selected an sgRNA targeting the splice donor site of exon 1 (Fig. 1a), converting the canonical -GT splice donor motif to -GC with 77.6% efficiency following transient transfection (Extended Data Fig. 1b,c). We targeted the AAVS1 safe harbour locus as a control for delivery and activity and generated AAVS1-edited naive hESCs that represent an earlier epiblast-like state20 (Extended Data Fig. 1d). Despite multiple replicates and methods, NANOG editing in naive hESCs was inefficient and colonies were rapidly lost (Extended Data Fig. 1d,e). We therefore focused on the role of NANOG in primed hESCs that represent a peri- and early post-implantation epiblast-like state22, in which we were able to base-edit the gene and observed loss of protein (Extended Data Fig. 1a–e). We observed that NANOG ABE8e-edited cells displayed lower cell density compared with AAVS1-edited controls (Extended Data Fig. 1d,f). Bulk RNA sequencing (RNA-seq) of NANOG- and AAVS1-base-edited hESCs showed clustering with previously published primed hESCs and away from naive hESCs, indicating that transcriptome-wide, they retained their expected stem cell state (Extended Data Fig. 2a,b). NANOG-edited cells showed significant reduction in NANOG, PRDM14, DPPA2, DPPA5, DNMT3B, SUSD2 and ZSCAN2, indicating a loss of pluripotency marker expression (Extended Data Fig. 2c,d and Supplementary Table 1). NANOG transcript may be downregulated owing to nonsense-mediated decay and/or differentiation of hESCs triggered by destabilization of pluripotency following NANOG perturbation. NANOG-edited cells upregulated neuroectoderm-associated molecular markers, including increased expression of LRNN3, ZEB2, PAX6, PAX9, OTX2, SOX2 and SOX9 (Extended Data Fig. 2c–e and Supplementary Table 1).

We next explored whether we could propagate NANOG-null mutant hESC lines. We performed single-cell colony cloning23 of NANOG- and AAVS1-base edited hESCs and cultured the resulting clones for 14 days (Extended Data Fig. 2f–h). After 14 days, 95.8% of AAVS1-edited clones formed viable, stable colonies, whereas only 41.7% of NANOG-edited clones formed colonies at day 7, with none persisting by day 14 (Extended Data Fig. 2h). Remaining NANOG-edited cells were sparse, highly differentiated and larger in size compared with AAVS1-edited controls (Extended Data Fig. 2g). Altogether, this demonstrates that adenine base editing of a splice donor site is an effective strategy for disrupting gene function. This also shows that NANOG is essential for maintaining pluripotency and self-renewal in hESCs, highlighting a critical role in hESC maintenance. The phenotype that we observed in hESCs contrasts with Nanog-null mouse ESCs that remain pluripotent, undergo indefinite self-renewal, express pluripotency-associated factors Oct4, Sox2, Dppa3, Klf4, Esrrb and Zfp42, and chimerically contribute to all somatic lineages and the germline24,25, which may reflect stem cell state, culture condition or species differences. The role of NANOG in hESCs is reminiscent of that reported in primed mouse epiblast stem cells, where it functions to inhibit somatic cell lineages, including neuroectoderm differentiation26, consistent with previous studies of hESCs in equivalent primed culture conditions18. G-band karyotyping showed that the NANOG-edited hESCs are karyotypically normal (Extended Data Fig. 2i) indicating that base editing does not lead to partial or whole chromosome gains or losses as previously reported for spCas91,3.

Reduced genotoxicity and off-target editing

We next determined the efficiency of ABE8e editing in mouse embryos. We designed a corresponding mouse sgRNA targeting the 3′ region of Nanog exon 1 (Extended Data Fig. 3a). Although this sgRNA could be used to optimize delivery methods in mouse embryos, the lack of a compatible protospacer adjacent motif (PAM) sequence at the exon–intron boundary prevented the targeting of the splice site to generate an equivalent splicing mutation. We electroporated mouse zygotes with ABE8e mRNA and Nanog sgRNA and collected blastocysts at 4.5 days post-fertilization for DNA extraction and next generation target amplicon sequencing (Extended Data Fig. 3b). We observed no significant difference in rates of blastocyst formation between Nanog ABE8e-edited embryos (n = 13) and unperturbed controls (n = 7) (Extended Data Fig. 3c). Nanog-edited embryos exhibited high frequency of detectable A•T>G•C edits at the on-target locus (Extended Data Fig. 3d,e). Single-nucleotide editing frequency was on average 82.4% in individual embryos (n = 6), with biallelic editing detected in 83% of embryos (5 out of 6). Pooled groups of 10 embryos showed a single-nucleotide editing frequency of 94.3% (n = 3 replicates, n = 30 embryos) (Extended Data Fig. 3e). In keeping with previous reports, we observed silent bystander mutation of neighbouring bases, targeting a proximal adenine base pair because it falls within the editing window of ABE8e27 (Extended Data Fig. 3d). To further optimize our delivery approach, we performed purification of ABE8e protein for human embryo experiments (Extended Data Fig. 3f).

We next proceeded to target human embryos and gametes donated for research. We used a co-injection method that has previously been shown to reduce mosaicism and increase the efficiency of CRISPR–Cas9-mediated editing28. Cryopreserved MII-stage oocytes and sperm were thawed. Using intracytoplasmic sperm injection (ICSI) sperm was co-microinjected with ABE8e mRNA or purified protein and NANOG sgRNA (Fig. 1a,b and Extended Data Fig. 3g). Based on our prior experience, approximately 50% of human embryos form blastocysts following microinjection of spCas9 protein16, consistent with typical developmental outcomes observed with in vitro fertilization in clinical settings29. Consistent with this frequency, 43% of NANOG-edited embryos initiated blastocyst formation and expansion (n = 12 out of 28 embryos) (Fig. 1c). We confirmed NANOG protein loss in the NANOG-edited embryos by immunofluorescence analysis (Fig. 1d). Co-staining and quantification of trophectoderm and primitive endoderm markers GATA3 and GATA4 showed the retention of these extraembryonic lineages despite the loss of NANOG (Fig. 1e–j). To maximize the information gained from embryos used in this study, we also analysed arrested human embryos to evaluate editing frequency (Extended Data Fig. 3g–i). Sequencing of embryos that arrested prior to the blastocyst stage revealed an editing frequency of 62.5% (n = 10 out of 16 embryos) (Extended Data Fig. 3i). However, given that arrested human embryos are developmentally compromised and frequently carry karyotypic abnormalities30, this editing rate may not be representative of embryos that are capable of developing beyond this stage.

As blastocyst formation was not compromised, we next sought to investigate the consequences of NANOG loss on human preimplantation embryos (Fig. 1a). Blastocyst quality was scored using clinical embryology grading, which showed that compared with controls NANOG-edited blastocysts had a poorer quality inner cell mass (ICM) (Fig. 2a). NANOG-edited blastocysts were laser microdissected to enrich for a polar region comprising epiblast, primitive endoderm and polar trophectoderm separated from mural trophectoderm cells (Fig. 1a and Extended Data Fig. 4a). Mural trophectoderm was further microdissected and genotyped, whereas the polar regions were dissociated for subsequent single-cell RNA sequencing (scRNA-seq) and transcriptome analysis (Fig. 1a). We observed on-target T•A>C•G editing at the NANOG exon 1 splice donor site in 84.8% of trophectoderm biopsies from edited blastocysts (n = 12 embryos, 39 out of 46 biopsies) (Fig. 2a and Extended Data Fig. 4b). Biallelic editing was variable among biopsies, with homozygous mutation in 37.0% of biopsies and either heterozygous or mosaic mutations in 47.8% of biopsies (n = 17 and n = 22 out of 46 biopsies, respectively) (Fig. 2a). As previously described, we also observed bystander complement T•A>C•G editing of a thymine located three base pairs upstream (5′) of the target sequence in 10 out of 12 (83.3%) blastocysts and 10 out of 16 (62.5%) arrested embryos (Fig. 2b and Extended Data Fig. 4b,c). Exclusive on-target base editing was observed in 2 out of 12 blastocysts (16.7%) (Fig. 2b). The bystander base change is a silent mutation that does not alter the amino acid sequence of NANOG.

To assess aneuploidy and chromosomal loss following editing, we performed whole-genome sequencing on trophectoderm biopsies (Fig. 2c and Extended Data Fig. 4d). No loss of chromosomal material was detected at the on-target loci or the top 5 predicted off-target sites in any of the biopsies analysed (n = 23 biopsies) (Fig. 2c and Extended Data Fig. 4d). NANOG sgRNA is predicted to edit an off-target NANOG pseudogene at an intergenic region, and other loci were predicted in silico to have low rates of editing (Fig. 2d and Extended Data Fig. 4e). The majority of predicted off-target sites were either in introns or intergenic regions with a single exonic region for a microRNA MIR485 predicted (Extended Data Fig. 4e). We performed next generation amplicon sequencing of 10 predicted off-target sites in edited blastocysts (n = 10 embryos, 30 biopsies in total) and observed 0.15% or less edits (Fig. 2d and Extended Data Fig. 4e).

Previous versions of deaminase base editors have been suggested to lead to RNA off-target edits, particularly using the cytidine deaminase base editor31–34. To assess possible RNA off-target edits following ABE8e editing, we transfected hESCs and collected cells for genome-wide total RNA-seq 48 h following puromycin selection (Fig. 2e). We applied a previously published pipeline to determine edits in RNA-seq reads31 and observed A-to-G editing rate at all sites increased by 1.2% in ABE8e NANOG-edited cells compared with controls (n = 348 loci in control, n = 415 loci in ABE8e NANOG-edited) (Fig. 2e). Notably, we observed similar percentages of base changes at other nucleotides not associated with adenine base editing conversion, for example 1.6% increase of C-to-G base conversion in ABE8e NANOG-edited cells compared with controls (n = 91 loci in control, n = 145 loci in ABE8e NANOG-edited), indicative of technical DNA polymerase or sequencing error rates. Overall, these data demonstrate effective base editing in human embryos, absence of genotoxicity, low off-target editing and human blastocyst initiation despite the loss of NANOG.

NANOG is essential for epiblast specification

The retention of GATA4 in NANOG-edited human embryos was unexpected because Nanog-null mutant mouse embryos are defective in primitive endoderm specification, including the absence of GATA435,36. To investigate the transcriptome-wide effects of NANOG loss for human lineage specification, we performed scRNA-seq on dissociated polar regions. We isolated biopsies from 12 NANOG-edited embryos and single cells (359 cells) from 11 out of 12 of these because one biopsy lysed prior to scRNA-seq. We compared these to cells isolated from two AAVS1-edited controls (42 cells from 2 polar regions) (Extended Data Fig. 4f) and previously published reference transcriptome datasets37–39. Genotyping of biopsied embryos indicated homozygous mutant, heterozygously or mosaically edited, or wild-type alleles at the NANOG locus (Fig. 2a). Although these groupings informed further analysis, trophectoderm biopsies represent only a portion of the embryo and may not reflect the genotype of the embryo as a whole. In NANOG-edited cells, scRNA-seq reads exhibited GT-to-GC mutation at the NANOG splice donor site, aberrant intron-retaining transcripts following exon 1 and absence of mapping to subsequent exons, suggesting a splicing defect (Extended Data Fig. 5). By contrast, in AAVS1-edited controls, scRNA-seq reads mapped to the 3′ untranslated region and all NANOG exons, with evidence of correct splicing across exonic regions (Extended Data Fig. 5). Reads mapping to the GAPDH locus in both the NANOG- and AAVS1-edited cells showed correct splicing and reads mapping to only exons.

To further understand the transcriptome-wide consequences of the loss of NANOG, we focused on the embryos in which trophectoderm biopsies suggested a homozygous NANOG mutant genotype compared with AAVS1 and reference control embryos. Global gene expression analysis revealed that compared with control cells, NANOG-edited embryos 4, 7 and 8 exhibited significantly reduced expression of epiblast-associated markers, including NANOG, LEFTY1, LEFTY2 and FGF4 (Figs. 3e,f and 4a and Supplementary Table 2). We found reduced expression of SOX2, ETV5, PRDM14, VENTX and NODAL transcripts in the NANOG-edited ICM cells compared with controls, although the difference was not statistically significant (Figs. 3e and 4a and Supplementary Table 2). This suggests that epiblast specification is disrupted in the absence of NANOG.

Primitive endoderm in NANOG mutant human but not mouse

To compare the role of NANOG in mouse and human, we utilized a spCas9 editing strategy in mouse zygotes to target the orthologous 3′ region of Nanog exon 1 (Extended Data Fig. 6a). We validated high on-target editing efficiency (Extended Data Fig. 6b), resulting in loss of NANOG and GATA4 protein expression in mouse embryos (Extended Data Figs. 6c–e). Although 4 out of 5 mouse blastocysts that we evaluated following spCas9 genome editing had a normal karyotype, 1 out of 5 was aneuploid at the on-target chromosome (Extended Data Fig. 6f), consistent with the approximately 20% aneuploidy that we and others previously reported following the use of spCas9 enzyme in embryos1. Off-target loci predicted in silico included intergenic and intronic regions (Extended Data Fig. 6g). We amplified seven in silico predicted off-target sites using PCR of pooled samples of three Nanog genome edited mouse embryos and performed next generation deep sequencing (Extended Data Fig. 6g). Four out of 7 off-target predicted sites lacked observed insertion deletion mutations in mouse Nanog-edited embryos, and 2 out of 7 sites had 10% and 2% reads with off-target editing (Extended Data Fig. 6g). We performed scRNA-seq on Aavs1- versus Nanog-edited mouse blastocysts and enriched for ICM cells in the polar region by laser microdissection (Extended Data Fig. 6a). RNA-seq reads mapping to the Nanog locus confirmed successfully edited cells (Extended Data Fig. 7a). Following integration with previously published scRNA-seq of mouse embryos from embryonic day 3.5 to 4.5 (ref. 40) (Extended Data Fig. 7b,c), we observed distinct clustering of Nanog-edited cells compared with Aavs1-edited controls, with divergent lineage marker expression (Extended Data Fig. 7d,e). Notably, Nanog-edited mouse embryos did not establish discrete epiblast or primitive endoderm cells, consistent with previous studies6,7,35,36. Instead, they clustered with ICM and trophectoderm cells (Extended Data Fig. 7b–d). The Nanog-edited ICM-like cells expressed Nanog, Pou5f1, Tbx3, Zfp42, Klf2 and Klf4, but downregulated markers such as Etv4, Nodal, Lefty1 and Lefty2 (Fig. 4a and Extended Data Figs. 7e and 8a and Supplementary Table 3). Expression of Sox2 and Fgf4 transcripts was reduced in the Nanog-edited ICM cells compared with controls, although this did not reach significance (Extended Data Figs. 7e and 8a and Supplementary Table 3). Moreover, the Nanog-edited ICM-like cells significantly upregulated trophectoderm-associated markers Gata2, Gata3, Krt7, Cdx2, Epcam, Tfap2c, Hand1 and Aqp3, and gene ontology enrichment further indicated that lineage specification was compromised (Fig. 4b and Extended Data Figs. 8b,c and 9a and Supplementary Table 3). The inability of both mouse and human embryos to specify the pluripotent epiblast indicates conservation of this phenotype between these species (Figs. 3e and 4a, Extended Data Figs. 7e and 8a–c and Supplementary Table 4).

The downregulation of Fgf4 expression in the mouse is likely to account for the failure of primitive endoderm differentiation (Fig. 4c,d and Extended Data Fig. 7e), because FGF4 is a key NANOG-regulated ligand that is essential for primitive endoderm maturation36,41,42. In contrast to the mouse, we note that human NANOG-edited cells differentiated to the primitive endoderm, despite the absence of FGF4 transcripts (Figs. 3c–f and 4c,d). This is surprising, given the role of receptor tyrosine kinase (RTK) signalling via the FGF–MEK–ERK pathway in human primitive endoderm specification43,44. We confirmed that NANOG-edited cells express a number of key primitive endoderm-associated markers such as GATA6, GATA4, PDGFRA, SOX17, FOXA2 and OTX2 and basement-membrane associated factors such as LAMA1, LAMB1, LAMC1 and COL4A1 (Fig. 4c and Extended Data Fig. 9b).

Given the lack of FGF4 transcription in NANOG-edited cells, we investigated the expression of genes encoding alternative ligands and any receptors associated with RTK signalling (Fig. 4d and Extended Data Figs. 9c). We identified expression of genes encoding FGF receptors (FGFR1, FGFR242, FGFR3 and FGFR4), insulin receptor genes (INSR and IGFR1), FGF ligands (FGF1, FGF12 and FGF18) and insulin growth factor ligands (IGF1). This suggests either that alternative ligands bind to FGF receptors or that RTK signalling is activated by alternative ligands and receptors that compensate for the loss of FGF4. We previously showed crosstalk between FGF and IGF signalling in stem cell proliferation and that treatment with exogenous IGF1 led to increased numbers of ICM cells with embryos in a diapause-like state45,46; it would therefore be interesting to determine whether FGF18, IGF1 or alternative RTK signalling components can compensate for the loss of NANOG and whether they may have a role in primitive endoderm specification in a non-perturbed context.

Overall, we show that adenine base editing is a precise and effective strategy for functional genetic interrogation in early human embryos, overcoming some of the genotoxic limitations of conventional CRISPR–spCas9-mediated genome editing. Here we apply adenine base editing to disrupt NANOG, revealing a requirement for this gene in human pluripotency and epiblast specification (Figs. 3c–f and 4e). In contrast to mouse, we observed that NANOG-edited human embryos retained primitive endoderm cells despite the absence of FGF4 (Fig. 4e), and our data suggest a number of plausible RTK signalling components that would be interesting to investigate in the future. The inability of Nanog-null mutant mouse embryos to give rise to epiblast or mature primitive endoderm cells, and the expression of trophectoderm-associated transcripts in the ICM, is consistent with previous studies6,7,35,36. This suggests divergence in gene function between the human and mouse, consistent with our previous studies indicating differences in OCT4 function16.

Whereas base editing of exon–intron splice sites has been reported in growth hormone receptor in porcine cells, major histocompatibility complex in immune cells and to target fluorescent proteins15,47,48, to our knowledge, this is the first demonstration of base editing used to disrupt a conserved splice donor site to induce a splicing defect and generate a loss-of-function mutation in a developmental regulator across any cell type. This approach will be broadly applicable to other developmental regulators and a substantial improvement over other CRISPR–Cas9 approaches. Given that G•C>A•T substitutions underlie nearly half of all known pathogenic variants5, base editing approaches have been proposed as potential tools for disease modelling and therapeutic correction. Although the present study was not designed to assess clinical applicability of base editing in the human germline, our findings do demonstrate reduced aneuploidy following genome editing compared with previous Cas9-mediated approaches1–3. Although these observations suggest potential for base editing to investigate gene function in early-stage human embryos, substantial basic research is necessary before potential therapeutic correction. This includes extensive evaluation of safety, such as further assessment of off-target activity, genotoxicity and developmental potential in a larger cohort and across more genomic loci in basic research before consideration for clinical use in heritable genome editing. Base editor variants such as ABE8e-V106W5 and CBE6b-V106W49 show improved safety through reduced DNA and RNA off-target editing in cell and animal studies, but their performance in human embryos is not yet characterized. Crucially, clinical translation of genome editing in human embryos must not proceed unless there is broad societal debate and support50–52. Caution, underpinned by rigorous ethical inquiry and oversight together with extensive public discourse, are needed. Together, these findings define a previously unappreciated requirement for NANOG in human development and provide a generalizable strategy for precise and minimally disruptive genome engineering in early embryos.

Methods

Ethics statement

The human embryos and gametes used in this work were donated to the University of Cambridge and Francis Crick Institute following assisted reproductive treatment at UK clinics. Oversight of this research and licencing approvals were conducted by the UK Human Fertilisation and Embryology Authority (HFEA) under research license numbers R0162 and R0152 at centres 0397 and 0401. License approval involved independent review by the HFEA License and Executive Committees, as well as the UK Health Research Authority’s Research Ethics Committee IRAS projects 308099, 252286 and 272218. All human embryo research conducted was compliant with the HFEA Code of Practice, and regular audits and inspections were performed.

Informed consent was obtained from all donors by suitable qualified clinic staff not involved in the research or directly involved in treatment. Human oocytes, gametes and embryos donated as supernummary to assisted conception treatment requirements were not financially compensated. Alternatively, human oocytes were donated as part of an ‘egg sharing’ programme or by non-patient donors. A subsidy of £1,500 or an additional cycle of treatment was provided to donations made through the egg sharing programme, while non-patient donors received a £500 financial compensation per donation cycle. Before giving consent, donors were provided with all of the necessary information about the research project, including details of the biochemical and genetic studies, such as genome editing, that would be performed. Donated embryos and gametes were cryopreserved and transferred to the University of Cambridge and Francis Crick Institute, where they were thawed and used in the research project.

Egg retrieval and fertilization

Oocytes were retrieved via ultrasound-guided follicle aspiration, and the surrounding cumulus cells were removed using hyaluronidase (HYASE; Vitrolife). Metaphase II (MII) oocytes were identified by the presence of the first polar body and subsequently fertilized through ICSI using sperm specifically donated for this study. The experiments were not randomized, and investigators were not blinded to group allocation during the procedures or outcome assessments.

Human embryo thaw and culture

Human MII oocytes were thawed using the RapidWarm Oocyte Kit (VitroLife, 10122). Human oocytes or embryos were alternatively thawed using the Oocyte/Embryo Thawing Media kit (Kitazato, VT602). All kits were used per the manufacturer’s instructions.

Dishes were prepared with G-IVF (VitroLife, 10134) for oocytes or Gx-TL (VitroLife, 10172) for zygotes. Oocytes were cultured at 37 °C, 6% CO2 and 5% O2 prior to ICSI. Following ICSI the fertilized eggs were cultured at 37 °C and 6% CO2 in EmbryoSlide+ culture dishes (VitroLife, 16450) inside an EmbryoScope+ time-lapse incubator (VitroLife). Culture medium was overlaid with mineral oil (Cooper Surgical, ART-4008-5P). Dishes were prepared following the manufacturer’s instructions.

Human sperm thawing

Frozen semen samples (~1 ml) were thawed at 37 °C in atmospheric gas incubator or water bath. A density gradient was prepared using 80% and 40% Puresperm solutions (Puresperm and GMOPS, Vitrolife). The gradient was assembled in 15 ml Falcon tubes, and the semen was layered on top. Samples were centrifuged at 300g for 5 min.

After centrifugation, the supernatant was removed and the sperm pellet was washed in G-MOPS (Vitrolife), followed by a second centrifugation under the same conditions. The final pellet was resuspended in 100 µl G-MOPS. Sperm quality was briefly assessed prior to ICSI by observing a small aliquot under the microinjection microscope.

Human ICSI

An injection plate was prepared with multiple wash droplets of G-MOPS and G-IVF. One droplet of 10% polyvinylpyrrolidone (PVP) (Cooper Surgical, ART-4005-AA) was used to wash the ICSI injection needle to prevent sticking and a 10 μl streak of 10% PVP was used to reduce sperm motility. Mineral oil was overlaid and the plate was warmed to 37 °C before use. Injection (Cooper Surgical, MIC-SI-30) and holding pipettes (Cooper Surgical, MPH-MED-30) were used for ICSI.

One microlitre of the sperm preparation was pipetted into the bottom of the PVP streak and sperm that swam to the top were selected. Sperm were immobilized by striking the tail and were then drawn into the injection pipette. To perform adenine base editing coincident with ICSI, a solution of 50 ng μl−1 sgRNA and 100 ng μl−1 ABE8e mRNA or 100 ng μl−1 ABE8e protein was prepared in 1:10 Buffer TE (Invitrogen, 12090015) diluted in nuclease free water. Ribonucleoprotein (RNP) complexes were produced by incubating the mixture at 37 °C for 15 min. The solution was kept on ice until needed. A 10 μl droplet of the solution was prepared on the injection plate. The injection pipette containing sperm was expelled until the sperm was at the opening of the ICSI needle, then the sgRNA/ABE8e mRNA solution was aspirated to approximately two times the length of a sperm from the tip of the head to the end of the tail.

Oocytes were placed into the G-MOPS droplets. Oocytes were held with the polar body at the 12 or 6 o’clock position and injection needle approaching at the 3 o’clock position. The solution inside the injection pipette was expelled until the sperm had approximately one sperm length of medium ahead of it in the pipette, halving the volume of the aspirated base editing solution. The injection needle was inserted into the oolemma at a position approximately two-thirds the length of the oocyte. The ooplasm was gently aspirated until the oolemma visibly broke. The sperm was then rapidly and gently expelled into the oocyte. The pipette was removed and the holding pipette released the oocyte to allow membrane closure. Eggs were fertilized in groups of fewer than three and washed in Gx-TL before returning to an incubator for culture in an EmbryoScope incubator at 37 °C and 5% CO2 for culture until the blastocyst stage, 6 days post-fertilization.

Human embryo single-cell collection

For single-cell collection, lysis buffer was prepared using the SMART-Seq v4 Ultra Low Input kit (Takara Bio, 634891) per manufacturer’s instructions. Laser microdissection (RI Saturn 5 Active Laser, Cooper Surgical) was used to enrich for polar region, comprising epiblast, primitive endoderm and overlying polar trophectoderm. The mural trophectoderm was further microdissected to collect replicate biopsies for genotyping and karyotyping.

Dishes were prepared with 500 μl TryplE Express (Thermo Fisher, 12604013) and warmed to 37 °C. The polar region was washed twice and deposited into the TryplE Express solution. Incubation was at 37 °C for 8–10 min.

Polar regions were then washed in three droplets of G-PGD medium (VitroLife, 10074). Glass capillaries (World Precision Instruments, TW100F-6) had been previously prepared using a Pipette Puller (Sutter Instruments, Model p-1000). Capillaries were broken with bore sizes ranging from 40 μm to 100 μm.

Using the glass capillary, polar regions were mouth pipetted up and down until single cells became detached. Cells were picked immediately once detached and deposited into lysis buffer. Cells were spun down and snap frozen on dry ice in Lysis Buffer from the SMART-Seq v4 Ultra Low Input kit (Takara Bio, 634891).

ABE8e expression and purification

ABE8e protein was prepared following previously established protocol53. The pABE8e plasmid was transformed into Escherichia coli BL21 Star (DE3) competent cells (Thermo Fisher Scientific). Protein expression was induced in a 1 l culture grown in Terrific Broth supplemented with 25 µg ml− 1 kanamycin at 37 °C to an OD600 of 1.3 to 1.5. The culture was cold-shocked in ice water for 1 h and protein expression was induced by addition of l-rhamnose (Sigma) to a final concentration of 0.8% (wt/vol). The induced culture was incubated at 18 °C with shaking for 24 h. Cells were collected by centrifugation at 4,000g for 20 min, flash-frozen in liquid nitrogen, and stored at −80 °C.

Frozen cell pellet from 1 l of culture was resuspended in 30 ml of bacterial lysis buffer (1 M NaCl, 100 mM Tris-HCl pH 8.0, 1.5 mM TCEP, and 20% (vol/vol) glycerol supplemented with two EDTA-free cOmplete protease inhibitor tablets (Roche) per 50 ml buffer). Cells were lysed by sonication and the lysate was clarified by centrifugation at 20,000g for 20 min at 4 °C. The clarified supernatant was supplemented with imidazole to a final concentration of 30 mM using a 3 M stock solution at pH 8.0. Ni-NTA resin (1.5 ml; Qiagen) was added to the lysate and incubated with gentle rotation for 1 h at 4 °C to allow protein binding. The resin-lysate mixture was transferred to a disposable gravity-flow chromatography column, washed twice with 20 column volumes of high-salt buffer (1 M NaCl, 100 mM Tris-HCl pH 8.0, 1.5 mM TCEP, and 20% (vol/vol) glycerol), and bound protein was eluted using four column volumes of elution buffer (0.45 M NaCl, 100 mM Tris-HCl pH 8.0, 1.5 mM TCEP, 20% (vol/vol) glycerol, and 300 mM imidazole). The eluate was diluted tenfold with low-salt buffer (100 mM Tris-HCl pH 8.0, 1.5 mM TCEP, and 20% (vol/vol) glycerol) and immediately loaded onto a 5 ml HiTrap SP HP cation exchange column (Cytiva). The column was washed with three column volumes of low-salt buffer. Proteins were eluted using a linear gradient from 100% low-salt buffer to 80% high-salt buffer over a total volume of 50 ml at a flow rate of 5 ml min−1. Fractions containing only the ABE8e protein (185 kDa) were pooled, sterile-filtered using a low protein-binding 0.22-µm PES membrane filter (Merck) and concentrated using 100 kDa molecular weight cutoff centrifugal filters (Merck). The protein concentration was measured by NanoDrop One spectrophotometer (Thermo Fisher Scientific) and the final concentration was 4.3 mg ml−1. Protein aliquots were flash-frozen in liquid nitrogen and stored at −80 °C.

Mouse embryo collection and culture

At the Francis Crick Institute, F1 (C57BL6 × CBA) 4- to 8-week-old females were super ovulated and mated to F1 (C57BL6 × CBA) 2- to 12-month-old male mice. The study was approved by the Animal Ethics Committee and by the UK Home Office licence number 70/8560, following all relevant institutional and national guidelines and regulations. At the University of Cambridge Physiology, Development and Neuroscience Department, CD1 10- to 12-week-old females were naturally mated to CD1 6- to 12-month-old male mice. All mice were kept at 21 °C, 50% humidity and subject to a standard light cycle of 12 h. Embryos were collected and cultured as described54. Zygotes were collected in EmbryoMAX FHM Mouse Embryo medium (Millipore, MR-122-D) and cumulus cells were removed with hyaluronidase (Sigma, H4272). Mouse embryos were placed in pre-equilibrated EmbryoMAX Advanced KSOM (Millipore, MR-101-D) overlaid with mineral oil (Origio; ART-4008-5P) at 37 °C and 5% CO2.

Mouse embryo electroporation

In brief, mouse zygotes were transferred to droplets of FHM medium overlaid with mineral oil then underwent zona drilling to generate a single hole using a RI Saturn 5 Active Laser (Cooper Surgical). Zygotes were then washed in droplets of OptiMEM (Gibco, 31985-062) before being loaded into the electroporation plate. Zygotes were electroporated in OptiMEM on a NEPA21 platinum electrode plate (NepaGene, CUY501P1-1.5) using a NEPA21 Super Electroporator. Cas9 enzyme (LabOmics, Cas9-TOO-250) was pre-incubated with sgRNA for 15 min at 37 °C to form RNPs. The program used was 20 V, pulse length 25 ms, pulse interval 50 ms, 2× pulses. Embryos were then washed through FHM medium then washed and cultured in Advanced KSOM until the blastocyst stage.

hESC culture

H9 hESCs (WiCell) were cultured on Matrigel-coated 6-well dishes (BD Biosciences, 356231). Cells were split at a 1:10–1:20 ratio every 3–4 days depending on confluence using ReLeSR (STEMCELL Technologies, 05872). Cells were fed daily with mTeSR1 (STEMCELL Technologies, 85831), supplemented with 1% penicillin/streptomycin (Gibco, 15140122). Cell pellets were resuspended in mTeSR1 supplemented with 1× CloneR2 (STEMCELL Technologies, 100-0691) prior to plating. hESCs were screened for mycoplasma presence on a monthly basis. hESCs were assessed for cell line identification and karyotype on a quarterly basis.

Lipofection of primed hESCs

Seventy per cent confluent hESCs were dissociated to single-cell density using Accumax (Thermofisher Scientific, 00-4666-56). Cells were counted using a haemocytometer. Cells were then plated at a density of 3 × 105 cells per well of a 6-well plate. Twenty-four hours later, cells were lipofected using Lipofectamine Stem Reagent (Thermo Fisher, STEM00003), following manufacturer’s instructions. Plasmid (1.5 µg) was used per well of a 6-well dish. The Francis Crick Institute Viral Vector Core produced an ABE8e (Addgene plasmid #138489) plasmid, by substituting the Cas9 region from pX459 (Addgene plasmid #62988) for ABE8e. The resultant plasmid contained an sgRNA sequence cloning site, ABE8e and puromycin resistance gene. After 24 h, medium was replaced with mTeSR1 supplemented with 0.5 µg ml−1 puromycin for 48 h. Cells were fed with mTeSR1 until collection after 3–7 days. The pX459 backbone was a gift from F. Zhang (Addgene plasmid #62988).

Single-cell cloning of NANOG-edited hESCs

H9 hESCs were first lipofected as described above. After 2–3 days following puromycin selection, when colonies were of medium size, they were picked manually for cloning. In brief, colonies were scored and picked using a p20 pipette, then transferred into a 96-well plate containing Accumax. Cells were then incubated for 10 min at 37 °C. Afterwards, cells were gently pipetted to a single-cell density then deposited into Matrigel-coated 12-well plates containing mTeSR1 with 1× CloneR2 and cultured as described above.

Design and ligation of sgRNAs

sgRNAs were designed using a combination of the in silico design tools CRISPOR, Benchling and Synthego Design Tool55. Guides were selected primarily based on having lowest predicted off-target binding to other regions of the genome. Other key considerations for sgRNAs for use in diverse human genetic backgrounds have been previously described23. For design of base editing sgRNAs, the position of the target sequence at an exon–intron boundary was prioritized. sgRNAs were selected which had the target A base of the exon–intron boundary motif at positions 13–17 distal from the PAM sequence. sgRNA sequences were ligated into pX459 or ABE8e-based plasmids as previously described56. The NANOG and Nanog sgRNA sequences used are provided in Fig. 1a, Extended Data Fig. 3a and Supplementary Table 6.

Generation of ABE8e mRNA

Plasmids containing a T7-ABE8e sequence (Addgene #138489) were linearized using the SpeI-HF restriction enzyme (NEB; R3133S) at 37 °C overnight. Linearized plasmid DNA was cleaned by phenol/chloroform extraction. DNA was concentrated by ethanol precipitation using 3 M sodium acetate.

In vitro transcription was performed using the mMESSAGE mMachine T7 ULTRA kit (Invitrogen, AM1345) per the manufacturer’s instructions, with incubation for 2 h. RNA was cleaned using the QIAGEN RNeasy kit (QIAGEN, 74104) per the manufacturer’s instructions.

Genomic DNA extraction and assessment of editing efficiency

Collected cells and embryos were processed using QIAGEN DNeasy Blood and Tissue Kit (QIAGEN, 69506) or by low-input whole-genome amplification using the RepliG Single Cell Kit (Qiagen, 150343), respectively. Both kits were used per the manufacturer’s instructions.

On- and off-target genome editing was assessed by PCR amplification of the target region. PCR reactions used the Q5 high-fidelity kit (New England BioLabs, M04925). Primers included the addition of Illumina MiSeq adaptor sequences. The PCR reactions were cleaned using AMPure XP beads (Beckman Coulter, BECLA63881) following the manufacturer’s instruction. Subsequently, target amplicon sequencing was performed either by Sanger sequencing or Illumina MiSeq (paired end reads of 250 base pairs). The sequencing primers used are reported in Supplementary Table 7.

Human embryo immunofluorescence

Human blastocyst stage embryos were fixed in 4% methanol-free formaldehyde (Thermo Scientific, 28906) in phosphate-buffered saline (PBS, Sigma-Aldrich, D8537) for 20 min at room temperature. Fixed embryos were permeabilized in PBS (Sigma-Aldrich, D8662) containing 0.2% Triton X-100 (Sigma-Aldrich, 93443) for 10 min at room temperature, then blocked in 1% BSA (Fisher BioReagents, BP1605-100) in PBS (Sigma-Aldrich, D8662) overnight at 4 °C. Primary antibodies were diluted in 1% BSA in PBS and incubated overnight at 4 °C. Secondary antibodies were diluted in 1% BSA in PBS and DAPI used for nuclear staining. All primary and secondary antibodies and the dilutions used are listed in Supplementary Tables 8 and 9.

Confocal imaging and image analysis of human embryos

Confocal imaging was performed in PBS (Sigma-Aldrich, D8662) with 3 mg ml−1 polyvinylpyrrolidone (Sigma-Aldrich, P0930) under paraffin oil in a 35 mm dish with a no. 1.5 glass coverslip. Images were acquired with SP8 confocal laser scanning microscope (Leica) using a plan apochromatic 40× 1.10 NA water-immersion objective. Nuclei were detected in the DAPI channel using the Spot Detection function in Imaris (Bitplane). The spot diameter was set to 8 µm. Following manual correction, mean fluorescence intensities were extracted for the GATA3, GATA4 and NANOG channels for each detected nucleus. Cells were classified automatically according to the channel with the highest mean fluorescence intensity. Spots/nuclei with mean fluorescence intensities below 2.5 arbitrary units in all three channels were classified as negative or unassigned, as the signal was considered too low for reliable classification. Images were acquired in 8-bit format, with an intensity range of 0–255.

Mouse embryo immunofluorescence

Mouse embryos were washed in PBS then placed into 4% paraformaldehyde in PBS for 1 h at 4 °C. Embryos were then washed 3 times in PBS containing 0.1% Triton (PBS-T). To permeabilize, embryos were incubated in PBS-T (0.5%) for 20 min at room temperature. Blocking was performed by placing embryos in PBS-T (0.1%) supplemented with 5% donkey serum and 1.5% bovine serum albumin for 1 h. Primary antibodies were added by diluting in blocking solution and embryos incubated in this solution overnight at 4 °C. The following morning, embryos were washed several times in blocking solution, then incubated in PBS-T (0.1%) containing secondary antibodies for 1 h at room temperature. After washing 3 times in PBS-T, embryos were placed in 1:30 DAPI-Vectashield mounting medium (Vector Labs) in PBS-T (0.1%). All primary and secondary antibodies and the dilutions used are listed in Supplementary Tables 8 and 9.

hESC immunofluorescence

hESCs were washed with PBS then fixed in 4% paraformaldehyde in PBS for 1 h at 4 oC. hESCs were then washed 3 times with PBS containing 0.1% Triton (PBS-T). For permeabilization, hESCs were placed in PBS-T (0.5%) for 20 min at room temperature. hESCs were blocked in PBS-T (0.1%) supplemented with 10% donkey serum, or 5% donkey serum and 1.5% bovine serum albumin for 1 h. Primary antibodies were added by diluting in blocking solution and cells were incubated in this solution overnight at 4 °C. The following morning, hESCs were washed several times in blocking solution, then incubated in PBS-T (0.1%) containing secondary antibodies for 1 h at room temperature. After washing 3 times in PBS-T, 1:30 DAPI-Vectashield mounting medium (Vector Labs) in PBS-T (0.1%) was added to the hESCs prior to imaging. All primary and secondary antibodies and the dilutions used are listed in Supplementary Tables 8 and 9.

Immunofluorescence imaging

Mouse embryos and hESCs were imaged on a Leica SP8 inverted microscope (Leica Microsystems). Images were captured with dimensions of either 512 × 512 or 1,024 × 1,024, line averaging was set to 2 and bidirectional x was used. For z-stacks, section thickness was set at 1 µm or 2 µm. Magnification used 10×, 20× or 40× oil-immersion objectives.

Bulk RNA-seq

Primed hESC were used for the bulk RNA-seq experiments. hESCs were dissociated using ReLeSR. 1 ml PBS was added and gentle pipetting was performed to remove all cells from the surface of the well. The cell suspension was then centrifuged and the supernatant removed.

RNA was isolated using the Monarch Total RNA Miniprep (NEB, T2010S) according to the manufacturer’s instructions. RNA was then submitted to the Crick’s Genomics Facility for further processing. The facility performed library preparation using the NEBNext Ultra II Directional PolyA mRNA kit (NEB, E7760S). RNA sample quality was measured using a ScreenTape Assay on a 4200 TapeStation (Agilent). Sequencing was performed using a NovaSeq 6000 (Illumina) with average sequencing depth of 25 million reads.

scRNA-seq

Single cells from mouse and human experiments were collected as described above. Cells were thawed and processed using the SMART-Seq v4 Ultra Low Input kit (Takara Bio, 634891), following manufacturer’s instructions for single-cell processing. Single-cell RNA-seq libraries were prepared using a Nextera XT DNA Library Preparation Kit (Illumina) according to the manufacturer’s protocol. Sequencing was carried out on a NovaSeq 6000 (Illumina) or AVITI (Element Biosciences) machine at a depth of ~12 million reads per cell.

Karyotype analysis

For low-pass whole-genome sequencing, samples were subject to whole-genome amplification (Qiagen RepliG Single Cell Kit, 150343) followed by low-pass next generation MiSeq sequencing (Illumina) platform to approximately 1× coverage. An analysis pipeline based on the QDNASeq Package was used to generate chromosome copy number profiles. In brief, the genome was subdivided into 1,000-kilobase sections, with the number of reads mapping to a region calculated. A log2 ratio of reads mapping to each locus versus the average number of reads mapping to all other loci is calculated to predict gain or loss of a chromosomal region.

Bioinformatic analysis of sgRNA efficiency

FASTQ alignment was performed using BWA57 to map paired end reads to the genome. SAMtools58 was then used for processing. Analysis was performed using the CrispRVariants package in R59. Further details have been previously described23. The following Github scripts were used: https://github.com/jzohren/crispr-miseq. To control for PCR amplification and sequencing errors, Cas9 edited mouse embryo reads had uncategorized (‘Other’) reads and single nucleotide variants of <1% each incidence filtered, and mouse embryo and hESC adenine base editing reads were assessed for editing at the on-target and bystander bases only. Human off-target DNA editing was assessed using CRISPResso260.

Bioinformatic analysis of scRNA-seq

Cutadapt version 4.2 package was used to remove sequencing adaptors from FASTQ files of scRNA-seq reads, with minimum read length set to 50. FastQ Screen version 0.13.0 and FastQC version 0.11.8 were used for assessing read quality. Alignment was performed using HISAT2 version 2.1.0 for mouse, and STARsolo 2.7.1b for human61,62. The mouse genome (Mus musculus GRCm38 release-89) or the human genome (Homo sapiens GRCh38 release-89) were used. For mouse, BAM files were generated using SAMtools version 1.18. Count matrices were generated using HTseq version 2.0.563. Integration with previously published human embryo samples37–39 was performed using Seurat version 5.0.264.

Dimensionality reduction and feature expression was performed using the R package Seurat version 5.0.2. For mouse cells, sample filtering was performed to exclude cells with fewer than 5,000 features, greater than 5% mitochondrial read content, and cells with less than 5 million or more than 20 million total RNA counts. For human cells, sample filtering was performed to exclude cells with fewer than 1,000 features, greater than 20% mitochondrial read content, and cells with fewer than 20,000 total RNA counts. Features expressed in fewer than three cells were also excluded. Cells were clustered using the Seurat FindNeighbors and FindClusters functions. In human embryos, after clustering, UMAP visualization was used to re-assign 14 trophectoderm cells and 4 primitive endoderm cells to local clusters.

Differential gene expression was performed with Seurat using the MAST test. Gene set enrichment analysis was performed using Enrichr65.

Bioinformatic analysis of bulk RNA-seq

FASTQ files of bulk RNA reads were trimmed and aligned and a count matrix was generated using the methods described for scRNA-seq. Differential gene expression was performed using the R package DESeq2 version 1.42.166. Features expressed in fewer than three samples or with fewer than ten counts were removed. False discovery rate was calculated using the Benjamini–Hochberg method. Gene set enrichment analysis was performed using Enrichr65.

RNA off-target editing analysis

For human experiments, three wells of H9 hESCs were lipofected with plasmids containing Cas9 only or plasmids containing NANOG gRNA and ABE8e. Cells were collected after 48 h and processed using the Monarch Total RNA Miniprep (NEB, T2010S) according to the manufacturer’s instructions. RNA off-target editing was performed using a previously established pipeline31 availble in the following GitHub repository: https://github.com/aryeelab/RNAseq_BE_editing. The dbSNP reference was used to account for natural variation in human samples67,68. The following edits to the analysis were made: control (ABE8e only) and NANOG-edited hESCs were merged and mapped onto the same space. A genomic position required a minimum coverage of 50 reads to be analysed. To account for remaining SNVs, Single nucleotide variants, PCR or sequencing errors identified in both control and NANOG-edited hESCs across all experimental repeats were filtered out.

Statistical information

The number of cells or embryos analysed (n), statistical tests and P values are all stated in each figure or figure legend. The tests performed in this paper are unpaired two-tailed Student’s t-test. For multiple comparison of RNA-seq analysis, false discovery rates were controlled with the Benjamini–Hochberg method (false discovery rate (FDR) < 0.05). Differential gene expression of scRNA-seq data was performed using the MAST test. Differential gene expression of bulk RNA-seq data was performed using DESeq2. Data are represented as mean ± s.d. Unless otherwise noted, each experiment was performed at least three times. Statistical methods were not used to predetermine sample size for experiments and experiments were not performed blinded.
