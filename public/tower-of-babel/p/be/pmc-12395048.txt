Natural Tooth Count and Sleep Quality in Older Adults: The Mediating Effect of Temporomandibular Joint Pain
Authors: Jingting Pi, Zhi Wang, Lin Song, Xia Deng, Xiaoli He, Deping Sun
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractPurposeThe study aimed to explore the relationship between tooth count and sleep quality and duration in the elderly, investigating the potential mediating role of temporomandibular joint (TMJ) pain in this relationship.MethodsUsing data from the China Health and Longevity Longitudinal Survey (CLHLS), a cohort analysis was conducted on 5941 older Chinese adults. Multivariate linear regression and mediation analysis were applied to assess the associations, adjusting for sociodemographic factors, health behaviours, and comorbidities.ResultsThe participants had a mean natural tooth count of 10.1 (SD = 10.4), a mean sleep quality score of 2.38 (SD = 0.93; lower scores indicate better quality), and a TMJ pain prevalence of 6.93%. Linear regression showed that more natural teeth were associated with better sleep quality (coefficient = −0.0036, P = .012), meaning each additional tooth correlated with a small improvement in sleep quality. TMJ pain was linked to poorer sleep quality (coefficient = 0.2175, P < .001), indicating worse sleep in those with TMJ pain. Mediation analysis revealed that TMJ pain partially mediated the relationship: the indirect effect (average causal mediation effect [ACME] = 0.0004, 95% CI: 0.0002 to 0.0005, P < .001) accounted for 11.5% of the total effect, while the direct effect of tooth count on sleep quality remained significant (ADE = −0.0039, P = .006).ConclusionMore natural teeth are associated with better sleep quality in older adults, and TMJ pain partially mediates this relationship.Clinical RelevanceThese findings highlight the importance of oral health (tooth retention and TMJ pain management) in improving sleep quality among older adults, providing new insights for clinical intervention in sleep disorders.Key words: Tooth loss, Sleep quality, Temporomandibular joint, Pain, China Health and Longevity Longitudinal Survey, Older adults

Introduction

Temporomandibular joint (TMJ) disorders are a prevalent group of conditions that can significantly impact an individual’s quality of life, with TMJ pain being a common complaint among patients seeking dental care. The aetiology of TMJ pain is complex and multifactorial, involving both local oral factors and systemic conditions.1,2 Emerging evidence from a Thai cohort3 study highlights that specific awake and sleep oral parafunctional behaviours (eg, sleep bruxism, tongue thrusting), as quantified by the Oral Behaviors Checklist (OBC), are strongly associated with TMJ pain chronicity. However, the exact mechanisms linking oral behaviours and TMJ pain remain poorly understood.

One aspect that has received increasing attention in recent years is the potential influence of natural dentition on the development and perpetuation of TMJ pain.4,5 The number of natural teeth has been associated with masticatory efficiency, occlusal stability, and overall oral health.6,7 Loss of teeth can lead to changes in jaw biomechanics, muscle activity, and TMJ load distribution, which may contribute to the onset of TMJ pain.8,9 Moreover, the relationship between oral health and sleep outcomes is well-established, with poor oral health being linked to sleep disturbances and disorders.10,11 Given that TMJ pain can disrupt sleep patterns and reduce sleep quality,12,13 it is plausible to consider TMJ pain as a mediator in the association between natural dentition and sleep outcomes. Prior studies have primarily focused on direct associations between tooth loss and sleep quality, but have not systematically examined how TMJ pain might mediate this relationship. The current study aims to address this gap by investigating the role of TMJ pain as a mediator in the relationship between tooth count and sleep quality/duration.

The scientific rationale for this study is supported by a growing body of evidence that suggests a bidirectional relationship between oral health and general health. For instance, studies have shown that periodontal disease is associated with sleep apnea,14,15 and that oral appliance therapy for sleep apnea can improve TMJ pain.16,17 Additionally, the impact of stress on both TMJ pain and sleep quality has been recognised, with potential common pathways involving the central nervous system and stress-related hormones.18,19

The feasibility of this study is underpinned by the availability of diagnostic tools and assessment protocols for TMJ pain and sleep quality, as well as the increasing awareness among healthcare professionals of the importance of a holistic approach to patient care. Furthermore, the study will build upon previous research that has explored the links between dental factors, TMJ pain, and sleep.20,21

In conclusion, this study aims to contribute to the existing literature by providing a comprehensive analysis of the mediating effect of TMJ pain on the relationship between natural dentition and sleep quality and duration. The findings could have significant implications for the management of TMJ disorders and the promotion of better sleep health through oral health interventions.

Material and methods

Etonogestrel implant data source and study design

This study utilises data from the China Health and Longevity Longitudinal Survey (CLHLS), capturing a representative sample of China’s elderly population. Focusing on the 2011 and 2014 waves,22 which provide detailed oral health metrics, including natural teeth count and TMJ pain prevalence, our analysis is guided by a detailed flowchart (Figure 1). Initially, 9765 Chinese seniors were enrolled in 2011, with subsequent follow-ups through 2014. An additional 1126 individuals were enrolled in 2014, followed to 2018. Merging these datasets and applying stringent inclusion/exclusion criteria, we refined the sample to 5941 participants. Inclusion criteria were as follows: (1) age ≥60 years at survey (2011 and 2014 wave); (2) complete self-reported data on Natural tooth count, TMJ pain symptoms and sleep quality and duration;(3) complete records on key covariates including socio-demographic characteristics, health behaviours, and chronic conditions. Exclusion criteria included: (1) missing or invalid data for core variables; (2) implausible data, such as reporting >32 natural teeth; (3) self-reported severe neurological or psychiatric disorders. (4) Loss to follow-up before the 2014 or 2018 wave; (5) inadequate proxy responses for oral health or sleep questions.

Participant recruitment and eligibility criteria.

Comprehensive data on socio-demographics, behaviours, and health status were collected, enabling a thorough examination of factors affecting oral health and sleep quality in China’s aging demographic. The CLHLS study was approved by the Research Ethics Committee of Peking University (IRB00001052-13074), and all participants or their surrogate respondents provided a written informed consent.

Number of natural teeth and prosthetic dentistry

In our study, ‘number of natural teeth’ was ascertained through direct examination by trained enumerators,23 excluding prosthetic teeth, to accurately assess oral health’s impact on sleep among the elderly.

Sleep quality and duration

In our study, sleep quality was gauged using a 5-point Likert scale from self-reports, with ‘1’ for ‘very good’ sleep to ‘5’ for ‘very poor’,24, 25, 26 while sleep duration was reported as the average hours slept nightly.25,27 These measures are practical for large epidemiological studies, offering a reliable snapshot of sleep health in the elderly population. However, self-reported measures have limitations: sleep quality assessments may be prone to recall bias (eg, memory-related overestimation or underestimation), and sleep duration lacks validation by objective tools like polysomnography (PSG), the gold standard for sleep assessment. Future studies could integrate subjective reports with PSG to enhance accuracy.

TMJ pain and its scores

In our investigation, TMJ pain was evaluated through participant questionnaires, gauging pain frequency (yes or no) and intensity on a 1 to 10 scale, a standard practice in epidemiological studies.28, 29, 30 This method helped to pinpoint the prevalence of TMJ pain, key for examining its relationship with sleep and general health outcomes. However, these self-reported assessments lacked objective clinical evaluations (eg, TMJ palpation or MRI) as outlined in the Diagnostic Criteria for Temporomandibular Disorders (DC/TMD), and may undercount mild symptoms or overreport due to psychological factors (eg, anxiety). Future studies could use standardised clinical exams to improve validity.

Statistical analysis

This study used R software (version 4.3.2, R Core Team, Vienna, Austria) for analysis. Initial descriptives set the stage, followed by multivariate linear regression to probe the influence of natural tooth count and TMJ pain on sleep quality and duration. Logistic regression assessed TMJ pain’s influence on sleep disturbances. Restricted cubic splines tested potential nonlinear relationships between tooth count, TMJ pain score, and sleep outcomes. Balancing flexibility and stability with 3 to 5 knots, they explored nonlinear trends in continuous variables (eg, threshold effects of tooth count on sleep quality). Subgroup analyses by demographics and oral health nuanced these findings. Adjusted for confounders like age, gender, and health behaviours, the study ensured internal validity Models were adjusted for confounders, including age, gender, and health behaviours, ensuring internal validity. Mediation analysis, utilising the ‘mediation’ package, emerged as a critical component of the study. It underscored the significant mediating role of TMJ pain, thereby elucidating the pathways through which oral health influences sleep quality.

Results

Participant characteristics and descriptive statistics

Participant demographics (N = 5941) revealed a mean age of 82.4 years, with the majority lacking false teeth (63.1%) and a minority reporting TMJ pain (6.93%). Sleep quality averaged 2.38, and sleep duration was 7.56 hours. The majority resided in rural areas (55.3%) and had low education levels (53.9%), which may reflect limited access to healthcare and health information, potentially influencing both oral health and sleep quality. Most were married or living with a spouse (44.4%), which may provide social support, possibly buffering against sleep disturbances. High rates of nonsmokers (64.8%) and non-drinkers (67.0%) suggest lower levels of behaviours associated with poor oral health and sleep problems. Common health issues included hypertension (29.5%) and diabetes (4.26%), both of which are known to affect sleep quality and may confound the relationship between oral health and sleep. The majority had a normal BMI (53.8%), potentially indicating fewer individuals with obesity-related sleep disturbances. Limited ‘NA’ responses indicated robust data completeness, ensuring the study’s analytical validity (Table 1).

Teeth and TMJ pain in relation to sleep quality and duration: a linear regression analysis

This study’s linear regression analyses revealed a significantly positively relationship between natural tooth count and sleep quality (unadjusted: β = −0.003, P < .01; adjusted: β = −0.004, P < .01), with no significant effect on sleep duration. The presence of TMJ pain was paradoxically linked to worse sleep quality (unadjusted: β = 0.249, P < .001; adjusted: β = 0.217, P < .001) and shorter sleep duration (unadjusted: β = −0.585, P < .001; adjusted: β = −0.423, P < .05). Higher TMJ pain scores were associated with diminished sleep quality (unadjusted: β = 0.049, P < .001; adjusted: β = 0.044, P < .001) and reduced sleep duration (β = −0.095, P < .001) (Table 2). Restricted cubic splines confirmed a linear association for tooth count and sleep quality and a strong linear effect for TMJ pain score on both sleep quality and duration (P-overall < .05), without significant nonlinear effects (P-overall > .05) (Figure 2).

Teeth’s link to TMJ pain and score

The linear regression analyses conducted in this study revealed a significant positive correlation between the count of natural teeth and the TMJ pain score (unadjusted β = 0.011, adjusted β = 0.008, P < .001). Furthermore, logistic regression models substantiated that an increased number of natural teeth is associated with a greater propensity for TMJ pain, as evidenced by an unadjusted odds ratio (OR) of 1.026 (P < .001) and an adjusted OR of 1.033 (P < .001), as detailed in Table 3. The restricted cubic splines analysis, as depicted in Figure 3, affirmed a significant nonlinear relationship between the number of natural teeth and the odds of TMJ pain (P-overall < .001) as well as the TMJ pain score (P-overall < .001), with pronounced nonlinear effects (P-non-linear < .01).

Stratified analysis of oral health and TMJ pain on sleep outcomes

The stratified analysis of oral health and TMJ pain’s impact on sleep outcomes, as detailed in Supplementary Tables 1, 2, and 3, uncovers nuanced insights into demographic-specific effects. Among males aged 60 to 80 with prosthetic dentistry, higher natural teeth count significantly improves sleep quality (β < 0, P < .05). Conversely, females over 90 without prosthetic dentistry exhibit a negative correlation between increased natural teeth count and sleep duration (β < 0, P < .05). For females within the 60 to 80 and 81 to 90 age brackets, elevated TMJ pain is linked to reduced sleep quality (β > 0, P < .05) and, among those with prosthetic dentistry, also to decreased sleep duration (β < 0, P < .05). Table 3 further supports this, highlighting that female aged 60 to 80 with TMJ pain experience both diminished sleep quality (β > 0, P < .05) and shortened sleep duration (β < 0, P < .05).

Supplementary Figures 1, 2, and 3 utilise stratified restricted cubic spline (SRCS) analyses to dissect the intricate links between oral health and sleep in the elderly. These analyses, segmented by age, gender, and prosthetic dentistry, underscore a beneficial relationship between natural teeth count and enhanced sleep quality. SRCS analysis affirms a straightforward, negative correlation between tooth presence and sleep quality, while also demonstrating a consistent, significant linear degradation in sleep quality and duration with escalating TMJ pain scores. Notably, no substantial nonlinear effects are detected (Figure 2), reinforcing the linearity of these associations. The findings highlight the value of oral health in predicting sleep outcomes among older adults. These stratified analyses, adjusted for covariates such as age, gender, and dental prosthetics, provide a comprehensive view of how oral health and TMJ pain affect sleep.

Mediation analysis results for the impact of TMJ pain on sleep duration

Figure 4A, using a binary TMJ pain metric, reveals a significant mediation effect of TMJ pain on the relationship between natural teeth and sleep quality. The analysis shows a substantial average causal mediation effect (ACME) of 0.000411 (P < 2e-16), indicating that TMJ pain significantly mediates the effect of natural teeth on sleep quality. The average direct effect (ADE) is −0.003988 (P = .012), and the Total Effect is −0.003577 (P = .014). This suggests that TMJ pain accounts for approximately 11.5% of the variance in sleep quality, highlighting its important role in the oral health-sleep quality relationship. Clinically, this implies that addressing TMJ pain may significantly improve sleep quality in older adults with poor oral health. Figure 4B, employing a continuous TMJ pain score, corroborates these findings with an ACME of 0.000374 (P < 2e-16) and an ADE of −0.003950 (P = .008), pointing to a mediation proportion of approximately 10.5%. Together, these results highlight the influential role of TMJ pain in the relationship between oral health and sleep, emphasising its management for sleep improvement strategies.

Discussion

The study’s findings have demonstrated a substantial correlation between the number of remaining natural teeth and sleep quality among older adults, where a lower count of natural teeth is associated with poorer sleep. This evidence complements prior research underscoring the pivotal role of oral health in overall wellness and implies that preserving a greater number of natural teeth might be beneficial for sleep quality.11,31,32 Improved masticatory efficiency resulting from a higher number of natural teeth33 may facilitate digestion and mitigate gastrointestinal issues that can disturb sleep. Moreover, individuals with fewer natural teeth might experience social discomfort or self-consciousness, culminating in stress and anxiety,34 both detrimental to sleep quality. The nervous system plays a central role in maintaining both oral health and regulating sleep patterns; tooth loss could alter sensory input and neural pathways,35 influencing sleep behaviour. Notably, our mediation analysis further clarifies this relationship by identifying TMJ pain as a significant mediator, accounting for 11.5% of the total effect. This has notable clinical implications: for older adults with fewer natural teeth, targeted intervention for TMJ pain (eg, physical therapy or pain management) may serve as a feasible pathway to improve sleep quality – bridging oral health and sleep health in geriatric care. This finding highlights TMJ pain as a modifiable target for reducing sleep disturbances in dentally compromised elderly populations.

Mechanisms linking tooth count, TMJ pain, and sleep quality

An intriguing aspect of the study reveals a positive correlation between the quantity of natural teeth and the likelihood of experiencing TMJ pain, where a higher tooth count corresponds with increased pain scores. This relationship can be explained through several interconnected mechanisms:

Mechanical strain: More natural teeth might impose greater mechanical strain on the TMJ and masticatory muscles during chewing and other oral functions,36 potentially triggering discomfort or pain, thus suggesting a biomechanical basis for the observed link.

Occlusal complexity: A larger number of teeth could engender more intricate occlusal relationships, potentially fostering parafunctional habits like bruxism.37,38 These behaviours add further stress to the TMJ, contributing to the onset or exacerbation of TMJ pain.

Age-related degeneration: Aging is characterised by inherent degenerative changes in the TMJ, rendering elderly individuals with more natural teeth potentially more susceptible to pain due to these age-related modifications.39

Inflammatory processes: Oral health, specifically dental status, influences the level of inflammation within the TMJ. A more complex oral environment, often associated with a higher number of teeth, may pose a greater risk of inflammation, enhancing the prevalence of TMJ pain.40

In conclusion, the connection between the number of natural teeth and TMJ pain is multifarious, intertwining mechanical, anatomical, and inflammatory factors. This underscores the need for a holistic approach to TMJ pain management that takes into account broader oral health considerations. Further investigation is needed to clarify the underlying mechanisms and devise targeted interventions for individuals with varying dental statuses.

Interplay between tooth count, TMJ pain, and sleep quality

The interplay between tooth count, TMJ pain, and sleep quality offers valuable insights into the ways oral health impacts overall well-being. TMJ pain serves as a mediating factor in the relationship between tooth count and sleep quality. The biomechanical stress on the TMJ and masticatory muscles, resulting from a higher number of teeth, can lead to TMJ pain that disrupts the normal sleep cycle, causing nocturnal discomfort and lighter, less restorative sleep.41

Additionally, the complexity in occlusal relationships exacerbated by more natural teeth may induce parafunctional habits such as bruxism. The TMJ pain arising from these actions not only directly causes discomfort during sleep but can also generate psychological stress,42,43 which is known to interfere with sleep quality.

Furthermore, as aging naturally affects the TMJ, having more natural teeth may compound these age-related changes, and the pain accompanying these alterations can serve as a mediator affecting sleep quality. Finally, heightened inflammation within the TMJ, potentially more common with a larger number of teeth, can lead to TMJ pain and the release of inflammatory mediators that disturb sleep architecture. It is noteworthy that sleep is intricately regulated by inflammatory cytokines44; elevated levels can lead to sleep disruptions, thereby emphasising the indirect pathway through which dentition influences nocturnal sleep quality via TMJ pain.

Study limitations and future directions

The study’s methodological approach, while robust in its statistical adjustments for various confounders, is not without its limitations. Although direct examination reduces self-report bias, interexaminer reliability for tooth counting (eg, distinguishing between natural teeth and implants) may introduce minor errors. The reliance on self-reported data for TMJ pain and sleep quality introduces recall bias and subjective interpretation errors, as older adults may misestimate pain duration or sleep onset times. This self-reporting could lead to an overestimation or underestimation of the true prevalence and severity of TMJ pain and sleep disturbances.

Moreover, the study’s failure to fully adhere to the DC/TMD presents a significant limitation.1 Although some literature suggests that TMJ pain can be diagnosed through questionnaires alone,24, 25, 26 the absence of physical examinations (TMJ palpation) and diagnostic imaging, such as MRI or CT scans, could result in misdiagnosis. Conditions like external auditory canal disorders or otitis media, which can mimic TMJ pain, may have been incorrectly classified, leading to an inaccurate representation of TMJ pain prevalence in the study population.

Additionally, the study did not consider genetic predispositions, which are known to play a role in the development of TMJ disorders and could influence an individual’s susceptibility to TMJ pain.45,46 The inclusion of genetic data could provide a more nuanced understanding of the factors contributing to TMJ pain and its relationship with sleep quality.

The impact of individual stress responses on both TMJ pain and sleep quality was also not accounted for in the study. Stress is a recognised modifier of pain perception and can exacerbate TMJ pain, as well as disrupt sleep patterns.47,48 The absence of data on stress levels and individual stress management strategies may have introduced confounding variables that could affect the study’s findings.

In light of these limitations, future research should adopt multimodal assessments: dental radiography to verify the number of natural teeth, PSG for objective evaluation of sleep architecture, and standardised clinical examinations including TMJ palpation or MRI imaging for precise diagnosis of TMJ conditions. Longitudinal study designs would allow for a better understanding of the temporal relationships between tooth loss, TMJ pain, and sleep quality. Additionally, the inclusion of genetic data and measures of stress could provide a more comprehensive analysis of the factors influencing these relationships. By addressing these limitations, future studies can enhance the validity of the findings and contribute to the development of targeted interventions for the management of TMJ pain and sleep disturbances.

Implications for clinical practice and future research

Despite these limitations, the findings have important implications for clinical practice. They suggest that dental professionals should consider the potential impact of tooth loss on sleep quality and screen for TMJ pain when treating patients with sleep disturbances. Early identification and management of TMJ disorders, along with strategies to maintain natural teeth, could improve sleep quality in elderly individuals. Public health policy should also take these findings into account by promoting preventive oral health measures, especially in aging populations. Investing in oral health education can raise awareness of the link between oral health, systemic health, and sleep quality, encouraging individuals to adopt proper oral hygiene practices.

In summary, this research has highlighted the importance of considering oral health as a contributing factor to sleep quality and TMJ health, opening doors for interdisciplinary collaboration and innovative research endeavours. By addressing the limitations identified and pursuing the proposed avenues of inquiry, future research will undoubtedly contribute significantly to refining diagnostic and therapeutic approaches, enhancing overall health, and improving quality of life for individuals suffering from these interconnected conditions.

Conclusion

In conclusion, this study has provided significant insights into the intricate relationship between dentition, TMJ pain, and sleep quality in the elderly population. The findings underscore the mediating role of TMJ pain in the association between the number of natural teeth and sleep outcomes, suggesting that preserving natural teeth and effectively managing TMJ pain may improve sleep quality in older adults that preserving natural teeth and managing TMJ pain can improve sleep outcomes. To drive practical change, we recommend: (1) regular dental check-ups and preventive care for older adults to maintain natural teeth and detect TMJ issues early; (2) training healthcare providers to consider oral health when assessing sleep quality in elderly patients; and (3) policymaker support for affordable dental care initiatives targeting the elderly. Future research should address current limitations and further explore the oral health–sleep connection to develop targeted interventions. By integrating these recommendations into clinical practice and policy, we can enhance the overall well-being of older adults through a holistic approach that prioritises both oral and sleep health.
