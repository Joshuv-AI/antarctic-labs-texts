Phage vB_KpnM_JYSS3 encodes a novel polysaccharide depolymerase that exhibits specific activity against K2-type carbapenem-resistant Klebsiella pneumoniae
Authors: Shi-Yan Jiao, Xia Wang, Jie Wang, Ying-Ying Liu, Lu Zhang, Xiao-Cui Peng, Jia-Le Song, Chun-Miao Wang, Wei Zhang, Xiao-Jun Li
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
BackgroundCarbapenem-resistant Klebsiella pneumoniae (CRKP), a major nosocomial pathogen, poses an increasingly serious threat to human health.ResultsIn the quest for innovative therapeutic modalities to treat CRKP infections, a newly discovered phage, vB_KpnM_JYSS3, which can specifically infect and lyse K2-type CRKP, was isolated and subjected to an array of comprehensive characterizations and genome sequencing. Furthermore, an analysis of the expression of the tail protein (ORF17) and the detection of its functions were carried out. Double-layer agar plates containing CRKP were used to isolate phages from hospital sewage samples. The phage's optimal multiplicity of infection (MOI), lytic spectrum, bactericidal activity, stability, and one-step growth curve were tested. The biofilm development ability of the CRKP strain 21AA2216 and the phage antibiofilm activity were determined using a 96-well microtitration plate. vB_KpnM_JYSS3 prevented biofilm generation and broke down mature biofilms. The whole-genome sequencing results revealed that vB_KpnM_JYSS3 is a toxic phage (nontemperate). The genome size of vB_KpnM_JYSS3 is 47,244 bp, and the G + C content is 48.48 mol%. A total of 71 coding sequences (CDSs) were predicted. Protein function prediction revealed that the tail protein (ORF17) of vB_KpnM_JYSS3 may possess depolymerase activity, consistent with the halo phenomenon around the plaque. ORF17 was expressed, purified, and named Dep17. Dep17 can degrade the capsular polysaccharide (CPS) surrounding K2-type CRKP, and it can also enhance the activity of polymyxin B against K2-type CRKP biofilms.ConclusionsPhage vB_KpnM_JYSS3 and its novel depolymerase Dep17 may represent promising strategies for combating infections resulting from biofilm-forming and drug-resistant K2-type CRKP.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12866-025-04251-6.

Introduction

Klebsiella pneumoniae (KP) is a common gram-negative pathogen in China that causes community-acquired and hospital-acquired infections [1]. KP can be pathogenic to immunocompromised individuals, leading to urinary tract infection (UTI), liver abscess, and pneumonia [2]. Consequently, the United States Centers for Disease Control (CDC) has categorized KP as an immediate and critical threat to public health [3]. Among carbapenem-resistant Enterobacteriaceae, carbapenem-resistant Klebsiella pneumoniae (CRKP) accounts for approximately 72.7%, and the annual detection rate in China is increasing annually, as is the drug resistance rate [4]. CRKP is linked to high mortality rates and is a global public health concern. Thus, CRKP is considered a high-priority drug-resistant pathogen [5].

Capsular polysaccharide (CPS) is one of the key pathogenic factors of KP. It is present on the surface of bacterial cells or is secreted into the extracellular environment. Additionally, CPS released by KP contributes to biofilm development [6], conferring high endurance to antibacterial drugs and probably leading to grave ongoing infections [7, 8]. Over 130 KP capsular serotypes (K types) have been depicted, with significant differences in CPS structure. Reports have indicated that types K1, K2, and K20 are closely linked to hypermucus viscosity, a hallmark of hypervirulence [9]. Concerns about antibiotic resistance and overuse demand the development of alternative or supportive antibacterial approaches.

Many alternative methods for treating CRKP infections have been explored, including the utilization of bacteriophages (referred to as phages). Phages can selectively infect bacteria. They have been investigated as antibacterial agents for nearly a century, with their first clinical use occurring in 1919. Interest in phage therapy has declined with the introduction of antibiotics; however, the current threat of increasing drug resistance in bacteria has led to renewed interest in these viruses as antibacterial tools. Several effective CRKP-targeting phages have been identified, and their potential therapeutic efficacy has been validated through experimental infection studies using both Galleria mellonella larvae and mice models [10–14]. Several clinical trials have also evidenced that phage therapy can cure patients with CRKP infections with no observed adverse effects [15–17].

Furthermore, as antibacterial agents, phage-derived proteins (including holins and lysins) have been developed [18, 19]. Phage-encoded polysaccharide depolymerases have also shown considerable promise in combating infections with various gram-negative bacteria [20–22]. The typical feature of polysaccharide depolymerases is the halos surrounding the plaques, which increase in diameter as the incubation time increases [23]. Phages expressing depolymerase possess the capability to break down polysaccharides, including CPS, thereby removing the physical barrier for accessing host receptors [24]. Additionally, the depolymerization of CPS causes encapsulated bacteria to lose a crucial protective shield and become more prone to the host's immune defense system and antibacterial drugs. Various depolymerases against 20 different KP strains have been identified and reported. These depolymerases have demonstrated possible in managing lethal infections triggered by certain CPS-forming KP strains in animal models [25–27]. Majkowska-Skrobek et al. [26] reported that injecting depolymerase into CRKP-infected G. mellonella larvae significantly increased their survival rate and survival time. Tu et al. [28] interpreted the molecular structure and mechanism of action of a depolymerase that targets K1-type KPs and demonstrated that the depolymerase could significantly increase the survival ratio of mice infected with a high dose of KP. The CRKP phage-derived depolymerase Depo32 can promote the endocytosis and bactericidal activity of macrophages in CRKP-infected mice and has significant bacterial scavenging properties [29]. Depolymerases also can achieve significant bacterial clearance and have the potential to be utilized in combination with antibiotics in the treatment of CRKP infections.

In this study, we first report the isolation and characterization of a lytic phage, vB_KpnM_JYSS3, and a depolymerase (Dep17) against K2-type CRKP. Our data indicate that phage vB_KpnM_JYSS3 can effectively lyse CRKP (strain 21AA2216) and degrade its biofilm. Moreover, the depolymerase Dep17 could specifically degrade the CPS of K2-type CRKP. We also investigated the potential application of Dep17 in combination with polymyxin B for the control of CRKP through biofilm degradation.

Methods

Bacterial strains

Ninety-six CRKP clinical isolates were isolated at the First Affiliated Hospital of Hebei North University (Table S1). K types were detected via genomic sequencing [30]. Blood agar was utilized to culture the isolates overnight at 37 °C. Colonies on blood agar were inoculated to 5 mL of LB broth and then incubated overnight at 37 °C while being shaken continuously at a speed of 150 rpm [31].

Phage isolation and identification

For phage isolation, a CRKP clinical strain 21AA2216 of the K2 type obtained from the sputum of a patient was utilized as the host strain. Phages were isolated from the sewage treatment center of the First Affiliated Hospital of Hebei North University. Briefly [31], a total of 100 mL of unsterilized sewage water was centrifuged at 10,000 × g for 15 min at 4 °C. The supernatant was removed and filtered through a 0.22 μm filter. For phage enrichment, 2 mL of filtered supernatant and 5 mL of LB broth were inoculated with 2 mL of exponential host cells and cultured for 16 h at 37 °C while being shaken at 150 rpm. The mixture was centrifuged at 10,000 × g for 15 min at 4 °C, and the supernatant was collected and filtered through a sterile 0.22 μm filter to remove bacteria. The double-layer agar plate method was used to isolate and purify the phage. A single plaque was picked for purification with a sterile pipette tip until uniform plaques were obtained. Phage titer was measured as the number of plaque-forming units per mL (PFU/mL) [32]. The purified phages were stored as an aqueous suspension of glycerol (30%, v/v) at − 80 °C.

Host range analysis

The host range was determined for 96 CRKP clinical isolates via the standard spot test and the double-layer agar plate method [32]. These experiments were all conducted independently three times.

Transmission electron microscopy (TEM) observation

The fresh phage culture was dropped onto a 200-mesh copper grid on a slide and stained using phosphotungstic acid (2% w/v). The samples were then examined using TEM (Hitachi-7650, Japan) at an acceleration voltage of 80 kV [10].

Determination of optimal multiplicity of infection (MOI)

The phage mixture was mixed with the CRKP culture mixture at volumes of 100, 10, 1, 0.1, 0.01, 0.001, 0.0001, 0.00001, and 0.000001 and incubated for 15 min. The phage titer was measured [32].

Examination of one-step growth curve

The CRKP strain 21AA2216 was cultured to the logarithmic stage and mixed with the phage mixture at the optimal MOI. A one-step growth curve was drawn with the infection time as the abscissa and the logarithm of the phage titer as the ordinate [11].

Assessment of phage thermal and pH stability

To determine the phage stability under different temperature conditions, 1 mL of the phage culture was divided into microcentrifuge Tubes. The Tubes were then incubated at 4 °C, 20 °C, 25 °C, 30 °C, 37 °C, 42 °C, 50 °C, 60 °C, 70 °C, or 80 °C. The phage titer was detected via the double-layer agar plate method [28]. The phage stability at different pH values was measured via LB broth ranging from pH 2 to 13 in separate microcentrifuge tubes [28].

Bacterial cell-killing assay

The CRKP strain 21AA2216 was exposed to the phage at MOIs of 0.01, 0.1, 1, and 10, and untreated CRKP was utilized as the control group. The cultures were incubated at 37℃ and then collected for optical density at 600 nm (OD600) measurement hourly for 12 h [14]. Three experiments were independently performed, with three replicates for each experiment.

The Calcein/PI Cell Viability/Cytotoxicity Assay Kit (Beyotime Institute of Biotechnology; China) was used to detect the effect of the phage on CRKP cells. The CRKP cells were visualized via confocal laser scanning microscopy (CLSM) via an Olympus LX83-FV3000 microscope (Japan) [33].

Biofilm formation assay

The biofilm formation of the CRKP strain 21AA2216 was measured via the 96-well microtiter plate method [34]. The specific method can be found in the Additional file 1.

Determination of the effect of phage on CRKP biofilms

Standard CV staining, viable cell counting, and scanning electron microscopy (SEM; Hitachi-SU8000; Japan) were utilized to determine the ability of phage vB_KpnM_JYSS3 to inhibit and remove CRKP biofilms [34]. The specific method can be found in the Additional file 1.

Genome sequence analysis

The complete genome sequence analysis was commercially conducted on the DNBSEQ-T7 sequencing platform (Hong Kong, China). The genome was extracted and subjected to DNA quality assessment, after which a sequencing library was prepared [35]. The resulting sequencing data were transformed into raw datasets for subsequent analysis. Fastp was utilized for filtering and quality control of the obtained raw sequencing data [36]. The processed reads were utilized for de novo assembly into contigs, with the assembly executed by multiple k-mers via metaSPAdes [37]. The tRNA genes, rRNA genes, and protein-coding sequences were predicted via Prokka (v1.12), with their functional annotations determined via BLASTP [38]. The genes were also functionally annotated via eggNOG-mapper, which contains annotation information from the COG, GO, KEGG, Cazy, Bigg, and Pfam databases. Gene sequences were analyzed against the Virulence Factors of Pathogenic Bacteria (VFDB) database to predict potential virulence-associated genes. Resistance analysis was conducted via Resfinder to predict the presence or absence of resistance genes in the genome. PHaTYP was utilized to predict the phage’s lifestyle. The Virus Intergenomic Distance Calculator (VIRIDIC, http://rhea.icbm.uni-oldenburg.de/VIRIDIC) was used to calculate the pairwise intergenomic distances/similarities of the phage genomes. Phyre2 and SOPMA were used to predict protein structure [39].

Phylogenetic analysis

BLASTn was utilized to align the complete genome sequence, the DNA polymerase genes, and terminase large subunit genes of phage vB_KpnM_JYSS3 to the NCBI database. A phylogenetic tree was constructed via ViPTree (https://www.genome.jp/viptree/) for the phage genome sequences, and evolutionary relationships was analyzed via MEGA11 based on the terminase large subunit and DNA polymerase genes. Phylogenetic trees were constructed via MEGA11 based on the protein sequences of Dep17 and 20 other phage depolymerases to determine their similarity relationships.

Expression and purification of the depolymerase

The gene encoding the putative phage depolymerase ORF17 was amplified from the purified phage vB_KpnM_JYSS3. The specific method can be found in the Additional file 1.

Determination of the thermal and pH stabilities of the depolymerase

Spot assays were used to detect the activity of the depolymerase after various treatments [40]. A total of 5 µL of two-fold serially diluted depolymerase solution was spotted onto CRKP lawns and incubated for 24 h at 37 °C.

Bacterial cell-killing assay of the depolymerase

The bacterial cell-killing activity of the depolymerase against the CRKP strain 21AA2216 was examined at 37 °C [41]. Cell counts were carried out at 0 min, 30 min, 1 h, 2 h, 3 h, 4 h, and 6 h.

Tests of the activity of depolymerase against CPS and biofilms

The CRKP strain 21AA2216 was employed to test the preventative effects of the depolymerase on biofilm formation, and tests were carried out as mentioned above [42]. The specific method can be found in the Additional file 1.

Combined antibacterial assay of depolymerase and polymyxin B

The CRKP strain 21AA2216 was co-cultured with polymyxin B and/or depolymerase for 48 h to examine the effects of combined treatment with depolymerase and polymyxin B on biofilm formation. Briefly, the wells were divided into four groups: (1) the control group, with sterile PBS and CRKP cells added; (2) the depolymerase group, with depolymerase and CRKP cells added; (3) the polymyxin B group, with polymyxin B and CRKP cells added; and (4) the combination group, with depolymerase, polymyxin B, and CRKP cells added. Biofilm formation was induced in 24-well plates for 48 h, after which the biofilms were washed thrice. The effects of the combination of polymyxin B and depolymerase on mature biofilm degradation were also assessed.

Unformed or formed biofilms were treated with sterile PBS, depolymerase, or polymyxin B. After treatment, the cells were washed, and the residual biofilm was stained with the Calcein/PI Cell Viability/Cytotoxicity Assay Kit. CLSM was employed to observe the biofilms [33].

Statistics

GraphPad Prism (version 10) was used for analysis. The data are presented as the means ± standard deviations (SDs). The t test for independent variables was used to analyse the data in the bivariate analysis. For multiple comparisons, one-way analysis of variance (ANOVA) was used. P < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Results

Phage isolation, purification, and host range

Phages were enriched from a hospital sewage sample. Upon isolation, the phages presented morphological differences. We purified and named the phage vB_KpnM_JYSS3. Ninety-six CRKP strains isolated from patients’ sputum, urine, or secretions were used to test the host range. The results showed that phage vB_KpnM_JYSS3 produced clear plaques with surrounding translucent halos on the CRKP lawn of the K2-type strain 21AA2216 (Fig. 1a) and produced translucent halos on the CRKP lawn of the K2-type strain 21AA0026 (Table S1). Using TEM, we observed that phage vB_KpnM_JYSS3 possessed a capsid diameter of 62–64 nm and an approximately 88 ± 2 nm tail with a length of approximately 150 ± 4 nm, characteristic of the Myoviridae phages (Fig. 1b). Phage vB_KpnM_JYSS3 had a relatively short incubation period, lasting approximately 5 min. Phage titers increased exponentially between 5 and 50 min postinoculation, reaching a plateau after 50 min (Fig. 2c). The burst size was 230 PFU/cell after the exponential phase.

Phage vB_KpnM_JYSS3 was mixed with the CRKP strain 21AA2216 in the prelogarithmic growth phase. The outcomes revealed that, following 5 h of co-incubation, the growth of CRKP cells treated with phage vB_KpnM_JYSS3 at MOIs of 0.000001, 0.00001, 0.0001, 0.001, 0.01, 0.1, 1, 10, and 100 was entirely inhibited, and this inhibitory effect persisted for 12 h. The OD600 of each group of cultures gradually decreased (Fig. 2d). There was a conspicuous disparity in the absorbance value between the experimental and control groups at each temporal point and MOI. Overall, phage vB_KpnM_JYSS3 effectively inhibited the growth of the strain in vitro.

The viability of phage vB_KpnM_JYSS3 remained relatively stable range from 4 °C to 60 °C, showing no statistically significant changes. However, the phage titer was significantly decreased by approximately 99% at temperatures higher than 80 °C for 20 min (Fig. 2e), and less than 1% viability was noted after 60 min of incubation. The stability of phage vB_KpnM_JYSS3 showed no marked variation after incubation at pH 4–10. However, when incubated at pH values of 2, 3, or 11, phage viability significantly declined. The phage lost viability at pH values of 1, 12, 13, and 14 (Fig. 2f). The results indicated good stability of vB_KpnM_JYSS3.

We utilized CLSM to obtain more evidence of the morphological changes in the host strain in response to the phage. In the control group, the green fluorescence was obvious, indicating that the bacterial survival rate was high. The effect of vB_KpnM_JYSS3 on the growth of CRKP after 24 h was also determined. Compared with no treatment, treatment with phage vB_KpnM_JYSS3 for 4 h resulted in a lower live bacterial content (Fig. 3c, 3 d). Compared with that of the negative control, the biofilm biomass was significantly inhibited by the addition of phage at MOIs of 0.1, 0.01, 0.001, and 0.0001 at 12, 24, and 48 h. To assess the activity of phage vB_KpnM_JYSS3 on mature biofilms, biofilms were cultivated for 48 h to reach a mature state, followed by treatment with the phage. The addition of phage at MOIs of 0.1, 0.01, 0.001, and 0.0001 remarkably decreased the number of viable cells within the biofilm during formation and in the mature biofilm at 48 h post-incubation (P < 0.0001; Fig. 4c, 4 d).

SEM analysis confirmed that the CRKP strain 21AA2216 could form large biofilms. The size of the CRKP biofilm co-cultured with phage vB_KpnM_JYSS3 decreased after 48 h. The size of the preformed CRKP biofilm decreased after treatment with phage vB_KpnM_JYSS3 for 4 h. The cellular ultrastructures of the CRKP biofilms treated with the phage were also observed via SEM. Untreated CRKP cells exhibited a rod shape with smooth surfaces, an intact structure, and a normal morphology. In contrast, CRKP cells infected with phage vB_KpnM_JYSS3 exhibited extensive shrinkage and membrane collapse. Disruptions of the cell wall and cytoplasmic membrane were observed, leading to pore formation. Phage treatment also increased the volume of cell debris (Fig. 4e, 4f). These results indicate that treatment with the phage vB_KpnM_JYSS3 significantly reduces biofilm biomass at the forming and mature biofilms.

Genetic characterization of vB_KpnM_JYSS3

Whole genome of vB_KpnM_JYSS3 was deposited in the GenBank database with the accession number PQ589211. vB_KpnM_JYSS3 has a double-stranded DNA genome consisting of 47,244 bp with a 48.48 mol% G + C content. In total, 71 open reading frames (ORFs) were predicted, and no transfer RNAs (tRNAs) were detected. Among the 71 ORFs, 50 (70.4%) were annotated as having known functions, and 21 ORFs were predicted to be hypothetical proteins. The visualization of functional proteins in the phage vB_KpnM_JYSS3 genome, which involves four main modules, namely, phage lysis, DNA replication, DNA packing, and structural proteins, is shown in Fig. 5. No genes linked to integrases, allergenicity, virulence factors, or antibiotic resistance were identified. PHaTYP result revealed that the phage vB_KpnM_JYSS3 is a virulent phage, with an identification value of 100%. Analysis of predicted genes encoding a depolymerase

The tail protein ORF17 was analysed and found to have depolymerase activity; we named it Dep17 (accession number: XMN72219). Modelling via Phyre2 was successful, with a confidence level of 99.9% (Fig. 8a). PSI-BLAST indicated that Dep17 was homologous to the K2-type hydrolase (PDB: 8IQ9) with the identity of 28%, suggesting that Dep17 might have a function related to K2-type CPS degradation. Dep17 contains 668 amino acids with a predicted molecular Weight of 73.59 kDa. Depolymerase activity and host range of Dep17

The predicted protein Dep17 was expressed and purified from the supernatant (Fig. 8b). The concentrated liquid was removed for subsequent experiments. The activity of Dep17 against the host strain 21AA2216 was tested, and a semitransparent round hole was observed at the site of the high-concentration depolymerase drip plate, with the minimum concentration of activity ranging from 47 to 94 ng. The results showed that Dep17 had good activity against the host strain.

Ninety-five other CRKP strains were selected for the host range test. Only strain 21AA0026 presented distinct halo formation, which was also of the K2 type (Table S1). This finding suggests that the protein Dep17 probably has host specificity. In addition, the lowest active concentration of Dep17 varied between strains 21AA2216 and 21AA0026 (Fig. 8c, 8 d).

The stabilities of Dep17 are shown in Figure S5. Dep17 had stable enzymatic activity in the temperature range of 4 °C–60 °C. Dep17 was inactivated at 70 °C. The activity of Dep17 was stable in a pH 2–11 environment, and no significant decrease was detected after 60 min of incubation. When the pH increased to 12, the activity of Dep17 was maintained to a certain extent. These results showed that the depolymerase has good thermal and pH stability.

Discussion

KP is part of the normal microbiota of the human body. KP is usually harmless to the human body; however, it can cause infections in cases of low immunity, long-term application of hormones, long-term use of many broad-spectrum antimicrobial drugs, organ transplantation, and tumor chemotherapy. In recent decades, with the development of medicine and the extensive use of broad-spectrum antibiotics, the rate of bacterial resistance has been increasing annually, and KP has been declared one of the most serious “ESKAPE” pathogens that are effectively resistant to antibiotics and that currently cause hospital-acquired infections worldwide [43]. The carbapenemase-producing strains are the prime antibiotic-resistant bacteria for which new antibiotics need to be developed [44]. Phage therapy is being reconsidered as an alternative antimicrobial method to overcome the public health problems associated with antibiotic resistance. We isolated, characterized, and named a new phage, vB_KpnM_JYSS3. Its antibacterial activity in vitro and ability to destroy bacterial biofilms were tested. vB_KpnM_JYSS3 has a narrow host range and acts on only one strain (21AA2216) of K2-type CRKP, forming plaques surrounded by a translucent halo on the bacterial lawn. When bacterial growth reaches a certain period, the proliferation of phages either halts or decelerates notably, and the area of the plaques no longer increases, whereas the zone of halos produced around the plaques continues to increase, indicating the presence of polysaccharide depolymerase [45]. TEM revealed that vB_KpnM_JYSS3 has a icosahedral head and a nontelescopic tail. The total length is 150 ± 4 nm, the capsid diameter is 62–64 nm, and the tail length is 88 ± 2 nm. According to the latest classification method published by the International Committee on Taxonomy of Viruses (ICTV), vB_KpnM_JYSS3 is a myovirus.

Phage vB_KpnM_JYSS3 had a high adsorption rate and antibacterial activity. The optimal MOI of phage vB_KpnM_JYSS3 is 0.0001. A smaller optimal MOI of phages indicates that fewer phages are needed to lyse the same number of bacterial cells. In this case, the phage achieves the most effective proliferation, and the titer of progeny phages reaches 3.7 × 1010 PFU/mL. One-step growth curve revealed that the incubation period of vB_KpnM_JYSS3 is only 5 min. The short cryptic and latent periods can help the phage complete a single lysis cycle as soon as possible. Therefore, vB_KpnM_JYSS3 is a highly efficient phage for lysing bacteria. A phage with long lysis times and short latency times is a desirable alternative for clinical applications. Temperature and pH are important external environmental factors that affect phage activity and stability [46]. The activity of phage vB_KpnM_JYSS3 remains extremely stable after incubation in the 4 °C–60 °C temperature range and at pH 4–11. This stability is a significant advantage of phage vB_KpnM_JYSS3 for medical applications compared with other K2-type phages, such as vB_KpP_HS106 [47]. It has also been demonstrated that vB_KpnM_JYSS3 is extremely efficient in disrupting the biofilms of CRKP, suggesting that vB_KpnM_JYSS3 could be employed as a potential therapeutic agent to address the lack of drugs in the postantibiotic era.

In our study, PhaGCN analysis demonstrated that vB_KpnM_JYSS3 belongs to the family Chaseviridae of the class Caudoviricetes in the phylum Uroviricota. However, the BLAST results and all the phylogenetic trees revealed that vB_KpnM_JYSS3 should belong to the genus Jedunavirus of the Jameshumphriesvirinae subfamily in the class Caudoviricetes (Table S3). Interestingly, in the ICTV database, the subfamily Jameshumphriesvirinae and the family Chaseviridae are parallel taxonomic units, and there is no hierarchical relationship between them. Therefore, we speculate that there might be a certain connection between the subfamily Jameshumphriesvirinae and the family Chaseviridae. In light of the findings presented above, and adhering to the classification criteria widely accepted for most phages, we classify phage vB_KpnM_JYSS3 into the genus Jedunavirus within the subfamily Jameshumphriesvirinae of the class Caudoviricetes.

Safety and nontoxicity are important for the ultimate success of phage therapy [48]. Some reports have indicated that there are no adverse reactions related to the therapy [49]. However, the phage therapy safety has only been tested in a few trials, and there are potential risks associated with it. Phages can self-replicate and grow within the body. There is a possibility that virulence or antibiotic-resistance genes could be transferred to infected bacteria through temperate phages, potentially leading to the appearance of strains with high pathogenicity. No antibiotic resistance-, virulence-, or lysogeny-related genes were found in the genome sequence of vB_KpnM_JYSS3. No key genes that control the phage's entry into the lysogeny cycle in the host bacterium (such as integrase genes or immune deterrent-related genes) were discovered, suggesting that phage vB_KpnM_JYSS3 is a virulent phage in the lysis cycle [50]. Therefore, phage vB_KpnM_JYSS3 may be safe and feasible for clinical treatment. However, it cannot be completely ruled out that phage vB_KpnM_JYSS3 may acquire new lysogen-associated genes during co-evolution with bacteria. In addition, over 50% of the proteins have hypothetical functions; thus, further studies are needed to assess the safety of the phage. Some in vivo experiments have shown that phage therapy is associated with drug resistance, potentially due to the constant confrontation between bacteria and phages, leading to the development of resistant bacteria in vivo. However, the number of resistant bacteria is very small, and the body's immune system can eliminate them [51]. We observed that tiny bacteria grew on the plaque of vB_KpnM_JYSS3 after a long incubation period. Combining phages with other antimicrobial agents, such as antibiotics or antimicrobial peptides, could be considered for the purpose of averting the development of phage-resistant bacteria.

Several case reports regarding phage clinical therapy have been documented [52, 53]. For example, doctors in the Netherlands have successfully treated kidney transplant patients with recurrent UTIs caused by CRKP via a combination of meropenem and phage. However, phage therapy is relatively new and has not yet been approved for clinical trials. Thus, there remains considerable opportunity for the development of phage therapy in China.

Compared with antibiotics, phages can specifically lyse pathogenic bacteria without damaging the growth of other probiotics in the body, thereby effectively protecting the normal microbiota. However, the narrow lysis spectrum is the primary limitation of phages. Several studies have shown that depolymerases seem to have a broader host range than phages do and are easier to produce and purify [54]. Additionally, the depolymerases appear to be more targeted and specific toward the target bacteria, which may be beneficial or at least not harmful. It has been suggested that depolymerase could serve as another potential therapeutic agent. Disruption of the capsule can increase the sensitivity of the targeted KP to immune or antibiotic challenges [55].

Depolymerases that target nearly 30 K-type KP strains have been identified [56]. However, only a few depolymerases have been expressed and purified, and their corresponding serotypes have been identified and functionally assayed, showing activity against KP infection [57, 58]. Several depolymerases that target K2-type KP strains have been reported. Depo32 from phage vB_KpnM_GH-K3 degraded K2-type CPS and was shown to be protective against KP infection in mice, but it was poorly stabilized, maintaining enzymatic activity only at 4–37 °C [29]. Some depolymerases, such as the depolymerase ORF1, which is encoded by the phage ΦFK1979 [59], are not expressed or assayed for activity. We aligned our depolymerase Dep17 with other K2-type depolymerases, including OmpK36 (accession number: CDO15604), KpV763-43 (accession number: KX591654), Dep_kpv74 (accession number: APZ82768.1), and others. Dep17 was found to have low concordance (≤ 27.5%) with all other published K2-type depolymerases. K2-type KP strains are highly virulent major pathogens with high mucus viscosity and are more lethal than other KP strains [60]. In summary, we believe that studying the K2-type specific depolymerase Dep17 is worthwhile.

Evseev et al. [61]. have shown that depolymerases can be found in tail proteins. We hypothesized that the tail protein ORF17 or ORF22 might provide the depolymerase activity of the phage. The pET28a vector was constructed and expressed in prokaryotes. The ORF17 gene encodes the depolymerase gene, and an active depolymerase was obtained.

We tested different induction conditions for soluble expression. Through BLAST comparison, Dep17 had the highest homology with the tail protein (YP 010684740.1) of KP phage 1611E-K2-1, with an identity of 99.25% and a query coverage of 100%. The remaining highly homologous proteins were labelled hypothetical proteins or tail proteins. However, the functions of these proteins have not yet been studied. The activity and stability of Dep17 were determined. Dep17 acts only on K2-type CRKP. Even under extreme conditions, Dep17 shows strong enzymatic activity and stability against the host strain compared with other depolymerases. Its high specificity may contribute to the applicability of K2-type KP strains as promising molecular markers for future therapeutic development, with potential uses, including lethality reduction and vaccine development [62]. Further studies on phage vB_KpnM_JYSS3 and its depolymerase Dep17 are warranted.

The formation of biofilms plays a significant role in the capabilities of microorganisms to evade host defense systems and is thus considered a major pathogenic factor in the clinical setting [63]. CRKP is highly capable of forming biofilms and accumulating bacteria in the early stages of infection [64]. It is difficult for the host immune defense and antimicrobial agents to eradicate biofilms, enhancing the severity of CRKP infections, and antibiotic treatment concentrations may be elevated 1,000-fold, as required for planktonic bacteria [65]. There is a pressing necessity for new treatments to combat the increasing number of CRKP infections, which is an unmet medical need. CPS is a major component of biofilms and is regarded as one of the primary virulence determinants of bacteria, including that of KP. CPS can help pathogens evade the role of phagocytes and the complement system and promote bacterial colonization and invasion in organisms. Phage depolymerase can identify, attach to, and break down the CPS, decrease bacterial virulence, disarm pathogens, and expose pathogen-associated surface antigens, enhancing the phagocytosis of phagocytes by activating the immune complement pathway [66]. Phages encoding depolymerases reportedly have high antibiofilm activity [67, 68].

In this study, Dep17 had no bactericidal effect on bacteria, which is consistent with the findings of previous studies [69]. However, CV staining revealed that both the 10 μg/mL and the 1 μg/mL Dep17 treatments reduced biofilm biomass, both in mature biofilms and in biofilm formation, which is in line with the findings of previous studies. Depolymerase alone or in combination with antibiotics can effectively inhibit or degrade biofilms [70–72]. This effect may be because CV staining responds to the total biomass of biofilms, and because depolymerase can disrupt bacterial biofilm formation, resulting in a decrease in total biomass. The decreased values were slightly lower than those of the phage-treated group.

Viable bacteria counting revealed that Dep17 treatment also reduced the number of viable bacteria. This finding may be due to the degradation of biofilm polysaccharide components, resulting in reduced stability after a certain dose of Dep17. This effect may cause a reduction of live bacteria count. Cui et al. [11] showed that the treatment of bacteria with depolymerase did not inhibit but rather increased biofilm formation. This difference may be because bacteria degrade the depolymerase proteins and use them to form thicker and tighter biofilms. Guo et al. [73] reported that Dpo42 cannot remove young or mature biofilms. This finding may be due to the different strains having different virulence and growth characteristics, and even depolymerases targeting the same capsular type have different activities and sites of action. Few depolymerases prevent biofilm formation and distroy mature biofilms, making Dep17 a good therapeutic candidate.

Prior investigations have provided evidence that the bacterial cells set free from biofilms possess a high degree of virulence [74, 75]. The effect of biofilm cell proliferation during the pathogenic process of infection remains unclear. Notably, Dep17 is not a bactericidal agent. The combined use of depolymerase and antibiotics should be considered to eliminate cells dispersed from biofilms [76]. The combination of depolymerase and antibiotics led to a significant decline in the number of CRKP biofilms cells, whereas antibiotics alone could not show obvious effect [77]. Reportedly, there are synergistic effects of DepoKP36 and gentamicin in combination, as well as an obvious improvement in gentamicin activity [78]. Therefore, combination therapy with antibiotics and depolymerases may be an effective therapeutic strategy for bacterial infections. The combined action of Dep17 and polymyxin B on CRKP biofilms was more effective than Dep17 or polymyxin B alone. This finding may be because Dep17 destroys the CPS of bacteria, leading to a better and more invasive effect of polymyxin B on the interior of the bacteria to exert its bactericidal effect.

The capsular serotype assay employed in this study has certain limitations. Generally, depolymerase typically shows only depolymerization activity against a single serotype [79]. However, some depolymerases can act on more than one capsular type, possibly due to the diversity and complexity of the CPS chain composition [80].

Encouragingly, novel approaches, such as depolymerase cocktail combinations, can be utilized to expand the range of depolymerase activity. Depolymerases against different serotype strains can be applied together. Alternatively, depolymerases could be genetically engineered to share some of the domains involved in recognition and binding. In addition, the combination of depolymerase with antibiotics, plant-derived antibiotics, or antimicrobial peptides may enhance the antibacterial effect against CRKP.

Conclusions

In this study, we isolated and identified a new K2-type phage, vB_KpnM_JYSS3, and its encoded depolymerase, Dep17. These results demonstrated that vB_KpnM_JYSS3 and Dep17 could remarkably prevent biofilm formation and destroy mature biofilms. Our data indicate that Dep17 has high degradation activity on CPS. Moreover, when Dep17 is combined with polymyxin B, it has a synergistic effect on the antibiotic activity of K2-type CRKP biofilms. These data suggest that phage vB_KpnM_JYSS3 and depolymerase Dep17 are potentially promising candidates for controlling CRKP infection. In combination with antibiotics, these findings lay the foundation for further relevant studies.
