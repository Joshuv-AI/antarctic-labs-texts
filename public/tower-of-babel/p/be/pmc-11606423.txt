Loss‐of‐function of LIGULELESS1 activates the jasmonate pathway and promotes maize resistance to corn leaf aphids
Authors: Shijie Huang, Chuanhong Wang, Ling Wang, Shuai Li, Tengyue Wang, Zhen Tao, Yibing Zhao, Jing Ma, Mengjie Zhao, Xinqiao Zhang, Lei Wang, Chuanxiao Xie, Peijin Li
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
SummaryCorn leaf aphids (Rhopalosiphum maidis) are highly destructive pests of maize (Zea mays) that threaten growth and seed yield, but resources for aphid resistance are scarce. Here, we identified an aphid‐resistant maize mutant, resistance to aphids 1 (rta1), which is allelic to LIGULELESS1 (LG1). We confirmed LG1's role in aphid resistance using the independent allele lg1‐2, allelism tests and LG1 overexpression lines. LG1 interacts with, and increases the stability of ZINC‐FINGER PROTEIN EXPRESSED IN INFLORESCENCE MERISTEM (ZIM1), a central component of the jasmonic acid (JA) signalling pathway, by disturbing its interaction with the F‐box protein CORONATINE INSENSITIVE 1a (COI1a). Natural variation in the LG1 promoter was associated with aphid resistance among inbred lines. Moreover, a loss‐of‐function mutant in the LG1‐related gene SPL8 in the dicot Arabidopsis thaliana conferred aphid resistance. This study revealed the aphid resistance mechanism of lg1, providing a theoretical basis and germplasm for breeding aphid‐resistant crops.

Introduction

Maize (Zea mays) is a major source of food, feed and chemical raw materials, with a global kernel production of ~1.147 billion tons per year (Yang and Yan, 2021), providing an essential resource for economic development worldwide. However, insect pests threaten the growth, development and reproduction of maize plants (Meihls et al., 2012). The corn leaf aphid (Rhopalosiphum maidis) is a destructive phloem‐feeding insect pest of maize, causing major yield losses in the crop as well as significant damage to the larger ecosystem (Stewart et al., 2020), as it is a common vector for hundreds of plant viruses and diseases (Guo et al., 2023). In field management practice, aphid control currently relies mainly on pesticides, which are costly and environmentally harmful (Bass and Nauen, 2023). Moreover, Bacillus thuringiensis (Bt) products and transgenic plants, which have shown great success in killing lepidopteran insect pests, are ineffective against aphids (Lumbierres et al., 2004). Breeding aphid‐resistant maize lines is therefore an attractive alternative approach, but known genetic resources for the isolation of genes conferring aphid resistance are scarce.

Jasmonic acid (JA) is crucial in plant resistance to insect infestation; in fact, its biosynthesis is rapidly activated when plants suffer damage from pests (Yan et al., 2018a). The F‐box protein CORONATINE INSENSITIVE1 (COI1) is a component of the E3 ubiquitin ligase complex Skip–Cullin–F‐box (SCFCOI1) that modulates JA signalling (Katsir et al., 2008; Sheard et al., 2010). Indeed, JA and its isoleucine conjugate JA‐Ile can trigger the formation of a complex between COI1 and JASMONATE‐ZIM DOMAIN (JAZ), which induces JAZ degradation in a COI1‐dependent manner via the 26S proteasome (Yan et al., 2018b). In a naïve state without insect attack, JAZ proteins bind to transcriptional repressors that inhibit the activity of the basic helix–loop–helix (bHLH) transcription factor MYC2, thereby suppressing JA signalling. When plants are injured, however, JAZ is degraded, alleviating MYC2 repression and allowing MYC2 to bind to the promoters of JA‐responsive genes, conferring insect resistance (An et al., 2017). Numerous studies have shown that manipulation of JA signalling components can disturb insect defence (Berens et al., 2017). For instance, in the dicot Arabidopsis (Arabidopsis thaliana), the triple mutant myc2 myc3 myc4 and quadruple mutant myc2 myc3 myc4 myc5 exhibit high sensitivity to herbivorous insects (Cheng et al., 2011; Song et al., 2017). In rice (Oryza sativa), two JA‐deficient lines with knockout of ALLENE OXIDE CYCLASE (AOC) or MYC2 showed greater sensitivity to the brown planthopper (BPH, Nilaparvata lugens (Stål)) (Xu et al., 2021); likewise, ZmMYC2s were shown to bind directly to the promoters of benzoxazinoid (BXD) biosynthetic genes and promote defence against herbivores by inducing benzoxazinoid biosynthesis (Ma et al., 2022).

Several quantitative trait loci (QTLs) associated with aphid resistance have been mapped in maize, for example by using the aphid‐susceptible variety B73 and the aphid‐resistant variety Mo17 (Betsiashvili et al., 2015). Similarly, two aphid resistance loci were mapped to chromosomes 5 and 7 using the accumulation of 5‐hydroxynorvaline as an indicator of aphid resistance in different maize inbred lines (Yan et al., 2015), and three Bx7 homologues – Bx10a, Bx10b and Bx10c – were identified using a mapping population from the inbred lines B73 and CML322 based on the contents of defence‐related glycosides (Meihls et al., 2013). However, the identities of the genes and molecular mechanisms underlying aphid resistance have remained unclear.

SQUAMOSA PROMOTER‐BINDING PROTEIN‐LIKE (SPL) proteins, first identified in snapdragon (Antirrhinum majus), are plant‐specific transcription factors (Klein et al., 1996), and there is growing evidence that SPLs are important in plant development and tolerance of abiotic stress. For instance, the SPL member LIGULELESS1 (LG1) regulates ligule and leaf angle development in rice and maize (Lee et al., 2007; Li et al., 2017a). In Arabidopsis, the microRNA156 (miR156)–SPL regulatory module mediates the response to recurring high‐temperature stress, integrating stress response and development (Stief et al., 2014). In terms of biotic stress, the miR156–SPLs signalling module plays a central role in regulating the plant immune system (Yin et al., 2019), as miR156–SPL9 can attenuate the increases in JA and JA‐Ile levels in seedlings attacked by herbivorous insects (Mao et al., 2017). Maize harbours ~30 ZmSPLs (Wei et al., 2018), whose functions have been little explored, especially in the context of resistance to biotic stress.

Here, we report a maize mutant designated resistance to aphids 1 (rta1) with significant resistance to aphids. Gene cloning and transgenic experiments indicate that the aphid resistance phenotype of rta1 is caused by a mutation in LG1. We show that LG1 interacts with and stabilizes ZINC‐FINGER PROTEIN EXPRESSED IN INFLORESCENCE MERISTEM (ZIM1), a repressor of the JA pathway. Moreover, the LG1–ZIM1–COI1a module affects benzoxazinoid biosynthesis, and sequence variation in the LG1 promoter is associated with the degrees of LG1 expression and aphid resistance among maize inbred lines. Importantly, a loss‐of‐function mutation of SPL8, homologous to LG1, in Arabidopsis also conferred increased resistance to aphids, underscoring the potential of LG1 and related proteins in breeding aphid‐resistant crops.

Results

The rta1 mutant shows resistance to aphids

In a screen for aphid resistance in an ethyl methanesulfonate (EMS)‐mutagenized population of the maize inbred line B73, we identified a mutant strongly resistant to aphids, which we designated resistance to aphids 1 (rta1). To characterize the mutant, we infested wild‐type B73 and rta1 plants grown in the field with aphids; at the mature stage, we observed many aphids on B73 plants, but few on rta1 plants (Figure 1a). Moreover, the survival rate, body weight and reproduction rate of aphids infesting rta1 were all significantly lower than those infesting B73 (Figure 1b,c, Figure S1a–c). Aphids infesting rta1 also excreted much less honeydew, an indicator of aphid feeding ability, than those infesting B73 (Figure 1d, Figure S1d). To investigate whether aphid behaviour differed when feeding on B73 and rta1 plants, we monitored aphid movements for 30 min by continuously capturing the position of multiple aphids on leaf discs from B73 or rta1 as previously described (Kloth et al., 2017) (Figure 1e). We determined that the aphids residing on rta1 leaf discs spend much more time exploring the disc for suitable feeding sites, and therefore travelled significantly (~15 times) longer distances than those on B73 discs (Figure 1f,g), suggesting that rta1 was not conducive to aphid feeding. To explore how rta1 affects aphid feeding behaviour, we performed an electrical penetration graph (EPG) assay (Prado and Tjallingii, 1994), in which a single aphid and the plant are each connected to an electrode; feeding behaviour is visualized as increase output voltage. Aphids feeding on B73 plants produced multiple E2 (phloem ingestion) waveforms, with a cumulative duration of several hours, whereas for aphids feeding on rta1 plants the number and duration of E2 waveforms were significantly lower (Figure 1h,i). In addition, the non‐probing phase (np) and stylet‐probing (C) waveforms were significantly more frequent for aphids feeding on rta1 plants than on B73, which is consistent with the aphid movement tracking described above (Figure 1h,i). An estimation of f‐SEP (the time to reach the first sieve element) showed that aphids took much longer to find suitable feeding regions on rta1 than on B73 plants (Figure 1h,i). We also detected several xylem ingestion (G) waveforms, with a cumulative duration of ~2 h for aphids feeding on rta1, in contrast to ~20 min on B73. Longer duration of G waveforms may reflect a compensatory strategy for aphids to maintain their water balance despite lower phloem ingestion (Figure 1h,i). These results indicate that rta1 possesses the ability to limit aphid infestation.

rta1 is allelic to LG1

During the experimental process, we also observed a narrower leaf angle and an absence of pulvinus structure in the rta1 mutant compared to B73. This leaf angle phenotype is reminiscent of the lg1 mutant phenotype (Moreno et al., 1997). We thus sequenced the LG1 locus (Zm00001d002005) in rta1 by Sanger sequencing, revealing one point mutation in the first exon that introduces a premature stop codon located after 119 amino acids instead of 399 in B73 (Figure S1a). We hypothesize that RTA1 is likely identical to LG1. To confirm this notion, we counted the aphids infesting B73, rta1 and F1 plants derived from the B73 × rta1 cross: the number of aphids on F1 plants was comparable to that on B73 plants, and much higher than on rta1 plants (Figure S1b,c). Thus, rta1 is a recessive mutation conferring aphid resistance. We divided the F2 population derived from the B73 × rta1 cross into three groups based on RTA1 genotype, RTA1/RTA1, rta1/RTA1 and rta1/rta1 and scored the number of aphids infesting each group (Data S1). Importantly, we counted roughly equal numbers of aphids on the wild‐type and heterozygous groups, but far fewer on the homozygous mutant group (Figure S1d). We also observed a significant and positive correlation between leaf angle and aphid resistance phenotypes (r = 0.729) (Figure S1e).

Confirmation of RTA1 (LG1) function in resistance to aphid

To confirm that RTA1, an allele of LG1, loss of function causes aphid resistance, we generated the independent lg1‐2 mutant in the 707 M background via clustered regularly interspaced short palindromic repeats (CRISPR)/CRISPR‐associated nuclease 9 (Cas9)‐mediated gene editing, meanwhile, we renamed the first mutant rta1‐1 as lg1‐1 in the B73 background. For allelism tests, we crossed lg1‐1 to lg1‐2 to obtain F1 hybrid plants, using a B73 × 707 M cross as a control. We also obtained transgenic maize lines overexpressing LG1 cloned in‐frame with the sequence for a FLAG tag (LG1‐FLAG OE: LG1‐OE) in the B104 background. Two independent transgenic lines were selected for subsequent experiments using bar rapid test strips (Figure S1a). RT‐qPCR showed that LG1 was significantly upregulated in both lines, and immunoblot analysis indicated a significant accumulation of LG1‐FLAG (Figure S1b). We scored the aphid survival rate, weight, fecundity rate and honeydew excretion from these genotypes. As with lg1‐1, the lg1‐2 and lg1‐1 × lg1‐2 F1 plants showed strong resistance to aphids, whereas LG1‐OE lines were more sensitive to aphids than the non‐transgenic control (Figure 2a–i, Figure S1c–k). These results confirm that the inactivation of LG1 can significantly enhance maize resistance to aphids.

Molecular analysis of LG1

Aphids prefer to aggregate on maize leaf sheaths at the vegetative stage and on tassels during the reproductive stage. We asked whether aphid infestation sites are related to LG1 expression by measuring LG1 transcript levels in various tissues by RT‐qPCR analysis, which showed that LG1 is highly expressed in the tassels, ears, pulvinus and flag leaves, with lower expression in stems and roots (Figure 2j). An RNA in situ hybridization analysis in leaf sheaths showed that LG1 transcripts are abundant in the cells around the vascular cells and sclerenchyma cells (Figure 2k). These sites of expression are similar to the sites preferentially attacked by aphids and are consistent with a role for LG1 in maize resistance to aphids (Shi et al., 2021).

To assess whether LG1 expression is influenced by aphid infestation, we examined LG1 expression in maize seedlings at different times after initial aphid infestation. We determined that LG1 expression levels decreased after 24 h of aphid infestation, and returned to pre‐infestation levels after the aphids were removed (Figure 2l). We investigated the subcellular localization of LG1 by transfecting maize protoplasts with an LG1‐GFP construct encoding a fusion between LG1 and the green fluorescent protein (GFP), using SV40‐dsRed1 (a fusion between a nuclear localization signal encoded by the genome of the polyomavirus SV40 and the red fluorescent protein from Discosoma sp.) as a nuclear marker. We detected green fluorescence mainly located in the nucleus and overlapped with SV40‐dsRed1 fluorescence, indicating that LG1 is a nuclear protein (Figure 2m).

Comparative transcriptome analysis of the wild‐type B73 and lg1‐1

We performed transcriptome deep sequencing (RNA‐seq) to investigate the differentially expressed genes (DEGs) under LG1 control, sequencing 12 cDNA libraries: three replicates each from non‐infested B73 (B73‐C), non‐infested lg1‐1 (lg1‐1‐C), infested B73 (B73‐T) and infested lg1‐1 (lg1‐1‐T). Principal component analysis (PCA) illustrated a clear separation between B73‐C and lg1‐1‐C and between the aphid‐infested and control groups (Figure S4a). We randomly selected nine genes for RT‐qPCR validation of the RNA‐seq data: indeed, the two transcriptome methods produced largely congruent results (Figure S4b).

We determined that DEGs between B73‐C and lg1‐1‐C are enriched in multiple Kyoto encyclopaedia of genes and genomes (KEGG) pathways, including ‘plant‐pathogen interaction’, ‘benzoxazinoid biosynthesis’, ‘alpha‐linolenic acid metabolism’ and ‘biosynthesis of secondary metabolites’ (Figure S5a). Between B73‐T and lg1‐1‐T, the KEGG pathways ‘benzoxazinoid biosynthesis’, ‘metabolic pathways’, ‘biosynthesis of secondary metabolites pathways’ and ‘alpha‐linolenic acid metabolism’ were enriched among DEGs (Figure S5b). As benzoxazinoid biosynthesis and alpha‐linolenic acid metabolism, which are related to JA pathways (Ma et al., 2022; Zhu et al., 2022), were significantly enriched in both comparisons, we focused on JA‐related genes, noting that their expression levels are elevated in lg1‐1 following aphid infestation, whereas TIFY genes related to JAZs were expressed at lower levels under the same conditions (Figure S6a). BX1–BX7 expression was elevated in aphid‐infested lg1‐1, while that of BX8–BX11 was decreased (Figure S6b). Subsequently, we measured the JA content using the standard curve method (Figure S7a) and found that it was not significantly different between B73 and lg1‐1 or B104 and LG1‐OE#2 before aphids infestation. However, JA content was higher in lg1‐1 than in B73 and lower in LG1‐OE than in B104 after aphids infestation (Figure S7b,c). We conclude that the aphid resistance conferred by lg1‐1 may be associated with the JA pathway.

LG1 interacts with ZIM1, a central component of the JA pathway

Some SPL family members, to which LG1 belongs, interact with JAZs from the JA response pathway in Arabidopsis (Mao et al., 2017). We thus asked whether LG1 might interact with JAZ proteins from maize. Since the JAZ gene family in maize is large, we turned to Arabidopsis SPL8 (accession number: Q8GXL3), which is most similar to LG1 (Figure S8a). Of the 12 Arabidopsis JAZs, SPL8 interacted only with JAZ9 (accession number: Q8W4J8), as evidenced by bimolecular fluorescence complementation (BiFC) assays in Arabidopsis protoplasts (Figure S8b). The maize proteins most similar to Arabidopsis JAZ9 are ZIM1 (accession number: B4FSX6) and ZIM35 (accession number: C0PCB2); however, we could not amplify a ZIM35 cDNA by PCR, possibly owing to a very low expression level. We therefore chose ZIM1 for the study (Figure S8c).

We examined the subcellular localization of LG1 and ZIM1 by co‐infiltrating Nicotiana benthamiana leaves with the constructs LG1‐GFP and ZIM1‐dsRed1; the two proteins co‐localized in the nucleus (Figure S9a). We performed several complementation experiments to verify the interaction of LG1 and ZIM1. In a yeast two‐hybrid (Y2H) assay, only yeast cells co‐transformed with the constructs BD‐LG1 (a fusion between LG1 and the DNA‐binding domain of yeast GAL4) and AD‐ZIM1 (a fusion between ZIM1 and the activation domain of GAL4) could grow on the selection medium (Figure 3a). In a luciferase (LUC) complementation imaging (LCI) assay, we detected strong LUC activity following co‐infiltration of N. benthamiana leaves with the constructs LG1‐nLUC (encoding a fusion between LG1 and the C‐terminal half of LUC) and cLUC‐ZIM1 (encoding a fusion between ZIM1 and the C‐terminal half of LUC), but not in the controls (Figure S9b). Similarly, in BiFC assays conducted in maize protoplasts transfected with the constructs YNE‐LG1 (encoding LG1 fused to the N‐terminal half of the yellow fluorescent protein [YFP]) and YCE‐ZIM1 (encoding ZIM1 fused to the C‐terminal half of YFP) or in N. benthamiana leaves co‐infiltrated with the two constructs, we observed reconstitution of YFP, indicative of a positive interaction (Figure 3b, Figure S9c). For a His pull‐down assay, we purified recombinant His‐tagged ZIM1 and glutathione S‐transferase (GST)‐tagged LG1 from Escherichia coli; after incubation of GST‐LG1 with ZIM1‐His or His control, we detected GST‐LG1, among the ZIM1‐His, but not in His pulled‐down proteins (Figure 3c). Finally, we performed a co‐immunoprecipitation (Co‐IP) assay with anti‐FLAG and anti‐GFP antibodies using protein extracts from maize protoplasts co‐transfected with the constructs LG1‐GFP or GFP and ZIM1‐FLAG; consistent with the above results, an immunoblot analysis detected LG1‐GFP, but not GFP, among the proteins co‐precipitated with ZIM1‐FLAG (Figure 3d).

LG1 stabilizes ZIM1 protein

The interaction between LG1 and ZIM1 prompted us to dissect their relationship and investigate whether ZIM1 stability is regulated by LG1. To this end, we transfected a 35S:ZIM1‐GFP construct into protoplasts prepared from B73 or lg1‐1. We detected significantly less fluorescence intensity for ZIM1‐GFP in lg1‐1 protoplasts than in B73 protoplasts (Figure 4a,b). We obtained the same result in protoplasts prepared from lg1‐2 and its wild type, 707 M (Figure S10a,b). Using a fusion between ZIM1 and a different reporter, firefly LUC, we determined that the relative LUC activity derived from the ZIM1‐LUC reporter was significantly weaker when this construct was transfected into lg1‐1 protoplasts than in B73 protoplasts. Again, we obtained similar results with the same construct transfected into lg1‐2 protoplasts compared to 707 M protoplasts (Figure 4c, Figure S10c).

To confirm the role of LG1 on ZIM1 stability, we extracted total proteins from ZIM1‐FLAG transgenic maize lines, mixed them with total proteins extracted from B73 or lg1‐1, and then collected samples immediately (0 min) or after 60 min. Immunoblotting with an anti‐FLAG antibody revealed a much lower abundance of ZIM1‐FLAG when co‐incubated with lg1‐1 extracts relative to that with B73 extracts after 60 min (Figure 4d). We also co‐infiltrated N. benthamiana leaves with the constructs ZIM1‐FLAG and LG1‐GFP, or GFP as control. At 48 h after infiltration, we observed a higher abundance of ZIM1‐FLAG in the presence of LG1‐GFP than in the presence of GFP (Figure 4e). Finally, we co‐transfected maize protoplasts with ZIM1‐FLAG and different amounts of LG1‐GFP plasmid DNA, resulting in the significantly greater accumulation of ZIM1‐FLAG with increasing LG1‐GFP levels; moreover, treatment with the 26S proteasome inhibitor MG132 inhibited this decrease in ZIM1‐FLAG protein abundance (Figure 4f,g). Taken together, these results suggest that LG1 can stabilize ZIM1.

LG1 stabilizes ZIM1 by interfering its interaction with COI1a

In Arabidopsis, JAZ proteins are marked for 26S‐proteasome‐mediated degradation in a COI1‐dependent manner (Yan et al., 2018b); we therefore tested whether ZIM1 is subject to COI1‐mediated degradation in maize. Maize had six COI genes, previous studies have found that there is an interaction between COI1a (accession number: C4J3Z7) and ZIM1 in the presence of JA‐Ile or COR (Ma et al., 2021; Qi et al., 2022). When we co‐transfected ZIM1‐GFP with an empty vector (EV) or a COI1a overexpression (COI1a‐OE) construct into B73 protoplasts, we observed significantly weaker fluorescence signals from ZIM1‐GFP in the presence of COI1a than in the EV control (Figure S11a,c). When we treated the protoplasts with methyljasmonate (MeJA), the signal intensity of ZIM1‐GFP decreased to a larger extent in the comparison between COI1a and the EV control (Figure S11b,d), compared with the results without MeJA treatment (Figure S11a,c). We obtained similar results with the independent reporter construct ZIM1‐LUC: relative LUC activity decreased in the presence of COI1a, and dropped further upon MeJA treatment (Figure S11e,f).

LG1 stabilizes ZIM1 (Figure 4a–g), whereas COI1a promotes ZIM1 degradation (Figure S11). We thus investigated whether LG1 regulates ZIM1 stability by affecting the COI1a‐ZIM1 interaction. LCI assays in N. benthamiana leaves co‐infiltrated with the COI1a‐nLUC and cLUC‐ZIM1 constructs showed that the COI1a‐ZIM1 interaction is disrupted in the presence of LG1 (Figure 4h). We also performed in vitro His pull‐down assays using a constant amount of recombinant purified ZIM1‐His and GST‐COI1a and increasing amounts of GST‐LG1, showing a gradual decrease in the ability of ZIM1‐His to pull down GST‐COI1a as GST‐LG1 levels increased (Figure 4i,j). Taken together, these results indicate that LG1 stabilizes ZIM1 by interfering with its interaction with COI1a.

Overexpression of ZIM1 results in susceptibility to aphid infestation

To elucidate the function of ZIM1 in aphid resistance, we obtained transgenic maize lines overexpressing ZIM1 cloned in‐frame with a FLAG tag sequence (ZIM1‐FLAG OE: ZIM1‐OE) in the B104 background. We obtained 19 independent transgenic lines (Figure S12a), all of which had higher ZIM1 expression levels than in B104 (Figure S12b). We randomly chose five representative lines – ZIM1‐OE #6, #21, #29, #32 and #41 – for analysis (Figure 5a); we confirmed the presence of the transgene with bar rapid test strips (Figure S12c) and the accumulation of ZIM1‐FLAG by immunoblot analysis (Figure 5a).

We tested the five lines for the survival rate, honeydew excretion and body weight of aphids. Whereas aphids showed a gradual decrease in survival rate when infesting B104, all five ZIM1‐OE lines maintained a constant and nearly 100% survival rate (Figure 5b). After aphids were allowed to reproduce for 20 days, significantly more aphids propagated on the ZIM1‐OE lines than on B104 (Figure 5c). Aphids also produced more honeydew and grew better on the ZIM1‐OE lines than on B104 (Figure 5d,e), which was reflected in higher body weights when infesting the ZIM1‐OE lines (Figure 5f). Together, these results indicate that the ZIM1‐OE lines are more susceptible to aphids than the wild type.

Benzoxazinoids play an important role in aphid resistance (Ahmad et al., 2011), and previous studies have found that JA treatment induces the accumulation of HDMBOA‐Glc, a metabolite of the benzoxazine pathway (Oikawa et al., 2002). In maize, MYC2s can bind to the BX promoters, thereby regulating benzoxazinoid biosynthesis (Ma et al., 2022). We also noticed high expression of benzoxazinoid biosynthesis genes in lg1‐1 in our RNA‐seq data (Figure S6b), prompting us to examine their expression levels in ZIM1‐OE lines. RT‐qPCR analysis showed that BX‐related genes are significantly downregulated in ZIM1‐OE lines relative to B104 (Figure 5g), suggesting that LG1 and ZIM1 may regulate aphid resistance by jointly influencing benzoxazinoid biosynthesis. To test the effect of benzoxazinoid treatment on aphids, we thus applied 500 μg/g of DIMBOA evenly on B73 or lg1‐1 leaves and counted the survival rate of aphids. Aphid survival rates were much lower on DIMBOA‐treated B73 than on mock‐treated B73, with the survival rates on lg1‐1 plants being at least as low as that of DIMBOA‐treated B73; by contrast, ZIM1 overexpression did not affect the aphid survival rate regardless of DIMBOA treatment (Figure S13a,b). The content of DIMBOA‐glc in leaves of DIMBOA‐treated B73 was significantly higher than that in mock‐treated B73 (Figure S13c). We also carried out a feeding bioassay in which aphids were fed with an artificial diet containing 500 μg/g of DIMBOA. As the results show in Figure S13d, the survival rates were significantly lower than the control.

As resistance to biotic stress often negatively affects plant growth and development (Züst and Agrawal, 2017), we examined several agronomic traits in wild‐type and lg1 mutants. The upper and lower leaf angles of lg1‐1 and lg1‐2 plants were significantly smaller than those of their respective wild‐type controls, in agreement with RTA1 being LG1 (Figure S14a,b). lg1‐1 and lg1‐2 plants were shorter than the respective wild types (Figure S14c,d). Moreover, the strength required to puncture lg1‐1 or lg1‐2 sheaths was significantly greater than the wild types (Figure S14e,f).

Natural variation in the LG1 promoter is associated with maize resistance to aphid

Given that LG1 expression can be downregulated by aphid infestation (Figure 2l), we wondered whether natural variation in the LG1 promoter might modulate aphid resistance across maize varieties. We thus analysed the LG1 promoters of 114 maize varieties but did not find a major haplotype for LG1 (Yang et al., 2014). Considering that insertion/deletion mutations (InDels) may be missed in published resequencing data, we chose 95 varieties and sequenced a 1700‐bp LG1 promoter fragment from each one. We identified nine InDels of >8 bp, based on which we defined six major LG1 haplotypes (Figure 6a). We scored the reproduction rate of aphids on accessions from each LG1 haplotype (Data S2). We obtained comparably low reproduction rates for aphids infesting accessions harbouring LG1 haplotype 1 or 2, and significantly much higher rates for those infesting accessions carrying LG1 haplotypes 3, 4, 5 or 6 (Figure 6b).

We wished to know whether LG1 expression levels correlated with maize resistance to aphids. Since LG1 haplotypes 2 and 3 contained the largest number of varieties, we randomly selected 10 varieties each in haplotype 2 and haplotype 3 and measured LG1 transcript levels. An RT‐qPCR analysis indicated that LG1 is significantly less expressed in the aphid‐resistant accessions from haplotype 2 than those from haplotype 3 (Figure 6c). Since aphid resistance was similar between accessions carrying haplotypes 1 and 2, and between haplotypes 3, 4, 5 and 6, we formed two groups: all accessions with haplotype 1 or haplotype 2 (hap1,2) and all accessions with haplotypes 3, 4, 5 or 6 (hap3,4,5,6). The aphid reproduction rate was significantly lower in the hap1,2 group than in the hap3,4,5,6 group (Figure 6d). Of the six haplotypes, the presence/absence of InDel 2 and InDel 3 distinguished the accessions based on their resistance to aphid (Figure 6a). To assess the contribution of InDels 2 and 3 to LG1 expression, we placed the LUC reporter gene under the control of 1700‐bp LG1 promoter fragments (upstream of the ATG) cloned from haplotype 2 accession GEMS33 (deletion at InDel 2; insertion at InDel 3) and from the haplotype 3 accession CML122 (insertion at InDel 2; deletion at InDel 3). Following the transfection of maize protoplasts with these constructs individually, we measured significantly lower relative LUC activity from the haplotype 2 promoter than from the haplotype 3 promoter (Figure 6e). Importantly, swapping the sequence covering InDel 2 and InDel 3 between the haplotype 2 and the haplotype 3 promoters changed their transcriptional activation, with the insertion of GTGCATGCACGC and the deletion of 14 × (G) in the Hap 2 promoter leading to higher relative LUC activity, whereas the deletion of GTGCATGCACGC and the insertion of 14 × (G) in the Hap 3 promoter resulted in lower relative LUC activity (Figure 6e). Taken together, these data suggest that natural variation in the LG1 promoter sequence may change LG1 expression and contribute to aphid resistance across maize accessions.

An Arabidopsis mutant of the LG1 ‐ homologous gene AtSPL8 is resistant to green peach aphids

Might LG1‐related proteins have a conserved role in different plant species – for example, in dicots? To explore this idea, we obtained a mutant in the LG1 homologous gene SPL8 in Arabidopsis (spl8, SAIL_816_E01) with a T‐DNA insertion in the 3′ untranslated region (3′ UTR) (Figure S15a). SPL8 expression level was about half that in the wild‐type Col‐0, making this mutant an SPL8 knockdown allele (Figure S15b). We performed a two‐host choice test with spl8 and Col‐0: almost no aphids moved to the mutant, whereas Col‐0 was covered with aphids (Figure S15c). A no‐choice survival test showed that the number of aphids on spl8 is also significantly lower than on Col‐0 (Figure S15d). These results indicate that spl8 is also resistant to aphids, suggesting that the aphid resistance conferred by loss of LG1 function (or that of its homologue) may be conserved between maize and Arabidopsis. Seed yield was similar between Col‐0 and spl8 in the absence of aphids; importantly, the seed yield of spl8 was much less affected by aphid infestation than that of Col‐0 (Figure S15e,f).

Discussion

Plants and insects have been coexisting and engaged in an arms race for ~400 million years, during which the defence mechanisms in plants have continuously evolved (Schatz et al., 2017). Aphids, the main phloem‐feeding insect pests of maize, cause unpredictable damage to maize; thus, there is an urgent demand to identify genetic and germplasm resources for breeding aphid resistance. We report here a maize mutant with significant aphid resistance ability, uncovering a previously missed biotic function for LG1, besides its well‐known role in setting leaf angle.

Many insect resistance pathways in plants are associated with phytohormones, of which JA is one (Wang et al., 2019). Although plants can enhance their resistance to insects by promoting JA signalling, JA often significantly inhibits plant growth, making it impractical to achieve insect resistance by simply activating the JA pathway or directly spraying plants with JA (Wang et al., 2019). In the current study, LG1 inactivation enhanced JA responses and upregulated several aphid‐resistance pathways, leading to the upregulation of aphid‐resistance genes including BX genes (Figures S5, S6). Interestingly, we found that the upstream genes of the benzoxazinoid pathway (Bx1 – Bx7) were up‐regulated in the lg1 mutant, whereas the downstream genes were down‐regulated (Figure S6b). This implies that biosynthesis of DIMBOA may be increased, which may increase callose formation and provide better defence against aphids (Meihls et al., 2013). We also found that the content of DIMBOA‐glc increased and endogenous DIMBOA remained unchanged in DIMBOA‐treated leaves (Figure S13c). DIMBOA‐Glc, the main active form of benzoxazinoid pathway in maize, is rapidly converted to DIMBOA when infested by insects, increasing resistance to insects (Gierl and Frey, 2001). Importantly, LG1 expression level decreased in response to aphid infestation (Figure 2l), leading to increased resistance to aphids in maize; upon removal of the aphid threat, LG1 expression returned to its basal level (Figure 2l). Thus, LG1 may act as a reversible switch in maize resistance to aphids, which may also provide a strategy to minimize the possible negative effects on maize development in the absence of aphids. In addition, we identified variations in the LG1 promoter region in different maize accessions, which may influence LG1 expression and contribute to maize aphid resistance (Figure 6).

Mostly reported in Arabidopsis, the JAZs, negative regulators of the plant JA signalling pathway, can be degraded by COI1 via the ubiquitin–proteasome complex, which in turn activates JA‐related gene expression and insect resistance (Chung and Howe, 2009; Yan et al., 2018b). We showed here that overexpressing ZIM1, a maize JAZ member, led to high aphid susceptibility in maize (Figure 5). Y2H, BiFC, protein pull‐down and Co‐IP assays all indicated that LG1 interacts with ZIM1 (Figure 3), and LG1 could stabilize ZIM1 to regulate resistance to aphid by disrupting the COI1a–ZIM1 interaction (Figure 4). Consistent with this idea, ZIM1 protein abundance was lower in COI1a‐OE lines than in the B73 control (Figure S11). At the transcriptional level, LG1 did not affect the transcript levels of ZIM1, suggesting that LG1 mainly regulates ZIM1 at the protein level (Figure S10d,e).

In agriculture, higher planting density has been an effective means to improve maize yield per unit area (Wu et al., 2019). However, high‐density planting tends to provoke shading of the undercanopy, which is associated with diminished plant immunity to pathogens and insect pests (Huang et al., 2023); it would therefore be desirable to isolate genes that can narrow the maize leaf angle and increase pest resistance simultaneously. In a previous study, loss of LG1 function resulted in smaller maize leaf angles, which can be used to increase planting density and improve yield (Li et al., 2017a). Here, we demonstrated that loss of LG1 function also increases plant aphid resistance (Figure 1). The coordinated role of lg1 in plant architecture and pest resistance may have great application potential in maize breeding and agricultural production. More importantly, the fact that the gene‐edited allele lg1‐2 also exhibited aphid resistance (Figure S3) suggests that the widely used CRISPR technology (Li et al., 2017a) may be applied in future to create various LG1 mutant alleles, to generate maize germplasm resistant to aphid infestation. Moreover, we observed aphid resistance in an Arabidopsis knockdown mutant in its LG1 homologue SPL8, suggesting a potential application for LG1 homologues in both dicot and monocot crops (Figure S15).

In summary, we discovered a previously unrecognized function for LG1 in conferring aphid resistance when inactivated, and elucidated the molecular mechanism of this resistance, relying on LG1‐mediated stabilization of ZIM1 by disrupting the COI1a–ZIM1 interaction, thereby modulating JA signalling and insect resistance pathways. We propose that LG1 may play an important role in coordinating plant leaf angle and aphid resistance. These findings will provide theoretical support and germplasm resources for aphid resistance breeding in crops.

Materials and methods

Plant materials and growth conditions

To screen for maize resources resistant to aphids, we employed ethyl methanesulfonate (EMS) mutagenesis to induce mutations and obtained mutated seeds. Meanwhile, we ordered seeds from both the EMS mutagenized maize library and the ChinaMu mutant library (Liang et al., 2019; Lu et al., 2018). About 500 mutants were planted in the field, alternating with the wild type and subjected to natural aphid infestation to screen for insect‐resistant mutants. We defined an insect‐resistant mutant when both neighbouring wild types were infested by aphids and significantly less aphids were present on the intermediate mutant. Conversely, an insect‐sensitive mutant was defined as one where aphid infestation was more serious on the intermediate mutant than on the flanking wild type. This approach was used for the first step screening. The rta1‐1 (lg1‐1) mutant is an EMS mutagenesis type mutant and the wild type B73 is from the stock of our laboratory. The wild‐type 707 M and the lg1‐2 mutant were provided by Dr. Chuanxiao Xie from the Institute of Genetics and Development, Chinese Academy of Sciences (Li et al., 2017a). The F1 lg1‐1/lg1‐2 plants for gene allelic tests were generated from a cross between lg1‐1 and lg1‐2. The ZIM1‐OE transgenic plants were obtained by Beijing BomeiXingao Technology Company (Beijing, China). The LG1‐OE transgenic plants were obtained by WIMI Biotechnology Company (Changzhou, China). For field experiments, the maize plants were grown in Sanya, Hainan Province, China (N18°21′, E109°10′) and Hefei, Anhui Province, China (N31°49′, E117°13′) as described previously (Zeng et al., 2020). For in‐house experiments, the maize seedlings were grown in a greenhouse under 16 h of light at 28 °C/8 h of darkness at 22 °C, with a light intensity of 400 μmol/m2/s.

Aphid bioassays

All aphid bioassays were conducted in a greenhouse as described previously (Wang et al., 2023a). The populations of the maize aphid Rhopalosiphum maidis and Arabidopsis aphid Myzus persicae (green peach aphid, the predominant aphid species affecting Arabidopsis) were consistently reproduced and kept in our laboratory. The aphids were maintained in 59 × 61 × 91 cm net cages to prevent external insect disturbance, colonized with seedlings of the susceptible maize variety B73 and Arabidopsis variety Col‐0, and propagated for several generations. The same batch of aphids was chosen manually for each experiment.

The performances of aphids on different materials were evaluated. For the survival rate experiments, 20 apterous adult aphids were placed on the leaves of each plant and the number of aphids on each plant was counted daily for 1 week. The survival rate was calculated as the number of surviving aphids divided by the total number of aphids included in the assay. For fecundity assays, a one‐day‐old aphid was placed on the back of the second leaf, close to the pulvinus, of each plant, and the number of aphids in every day and the total number of aphids after 30 days were counted and recorded. The honeydew experiment was set up similarly to the survival rate experiments. To collect the honeydew, the filter paper was cut in the middle and placed horizontally around the rootstock of each maize seedling. After 2 days, the filter paper was collected, treated with ninhydrin in 0.1% (w/v) acetone solution, and dried at 60 °C for 30 min. Honeydew contains amino acids that can be stained and produce purple colour (Du et al., 2009). The areas of purple spots were captured and quantified using ImageJ software. The seedlings at the four‐leaf stage were used for the honeydew experiments, and all the seedlings were grown in 59 × 61 × 91 cm net cages to ensure no external insect disturbance throughout the experiments. We set up six replicates for each experiment.

For the two‐host choice assay of aphids, Atspl8 and Col‐0 were planted together with their seedlings touching each other and the same number of aphids (Myzus persicae) was placed on the two plants. After 48 h, the distribution of aphids on Atspl8 and Col‐0 was recorded, respectively. For the no‐choice survival test, Atspl8 was planted separately from Col‐0. Two green peach aphids were placed on each plant, and the total number of aphids on each plant was counted after 15 days.

The statistical analysis for aphid resistance of different maize varieties was conducted as reported previously (Meihls et al., 2013). For each maize variety, three replicates were planted in different trays to avoid contact between different plants.

Tracking of aphid movement assay

To track the feeding behaviour of aphids growing on different maize plants, a previously described method was used, with minor modifications, to monitor the movement of aphids (Kloth et al., 2017). In brief, B73 and lg1‐1 seedlings at the four‐leaf stage with uniform growth were chosen; leaf discs of similar size (diameter: 1.5 cm) were punched out at the same positions with a hole puncher and placed side by side in a 24‐well plate with one disc in each well and adaxial side facing upward, which was placed under a camera for photography. Aphids were starved for 15 min and then placed at the centre of each leaf disc and photographed continuously over 30 min at the speed of 25 frames per second. The incubator was kept closed during this time to avoid external disturbances. The trajectories of aphid movement were determined using Tracker‐6.1.2‐Windows‐x64 software and quantified using AutoCAD (2020) software.

Electrical penetration graph (EPG) assay

The feeding behaviour of adult aphids on maize plants was recorded using an EPG (DC EPG AMPLIFIER GIGA‐8d) system. Briefly, a thin gold wire was attached to the back of an adult aphid with conductive water‐based silver adhesive. The wired aphid was placed on a plant that was connected to a conductivity recording system via a copper electrode inserted into the soil. During EPG recording, the plant and the insect were both kept in a Faraday cage to avoid external electrical noise. The EPG signal was recorded continuously for over 8 h. Each aphid and maize seedling was used only once. Different feeding waves were visualized and manually labelled using Stylet + a (Giga‐8dd Basic 8 channel EPG recording system, Wageningen, the Netherlands) (Zhang et al., 2022). The characterization of aphid feeding wave patterns was analysed according to previous descriptions (Prado and Tjallingii, 1994). The experiments were conducted in a Faraday cage under a 16‐h light/8‐ h dark photoperiod with 28 °C (day)/22 °C (night) cycles.

Measurement of JA content in maize leaves

The JA content was measured using enzyme‐linked immunosorbent assay (J38954; ELISA, GILED BIOTECHNOLGY, Wuhan, China). Maize plants at the four‐leaf stage were infested with aphids. After 24 h, leaves were collected and rapidly frozen in liquid nitrogen for the determination of JA. The method of determination was carried out as described previously (Yi et al., 2020).

Measurement of DIMBOA‐glc and DIMBOA in maize leaves

The measurement was conducted by Wuhan Metware Biotechnology Company (Wuhan, China). 100 μL of 500 μg/g DIMBOA was evenly applied to maize leaves (6 cm × 3.5 cm) at the four‐leaf stage. After 24 h, samples were taken from areas adjacent to the application site to determine the content of DIMBOA‐glc and DIMBOA. Biological samples were freeze‐dried under vacuum in a lyophilizer (Scientz‐100F) and ground (30 Hz, 1.5 min) to a fine powder using a tissue grinder (MM 400; Retsch). A 50‐mg tissue powder was extracted in 1.2 mL of 70% (v/v) methanol extraction buffer and vortexed every 30 min for 30 s for a total of six times. After centrifugation (13 523 g for 3 min at 4 °C), the supernatant was collected and filtered through a microporous membrane (0.22‐μm pore size) and stored in an injection vial for UPLC‐MS/MS analysis (Fraga et al., 2010).

Transcriptome analysis

The transcriptome analysis was conducted by Wuhan Metware Biotechnology Company (Wuhan, China). Total RNA was isolated from each sample and purified using an RNAprep Pure Plant Kit (DP441, Tiangen, China). The quality and integrity of the total RNA were assessed using a NanoPhotometer spectrophotometer (IMPLEN, California, USA), a Qubit 2.0 fluorometer (Life Technologies, California, USA), and an Agilent Bioanalyzer 2100 system (Agilent Technologies, California, USA). A fragmentation buffer was added to break RNA into short segments. The segmented RNA was reverse transcribed to first‐strand cDNA with six‐base random primers, and double‐stranded cDNA was synthesized using DNA polymerase I. The cDNA (~200 bp) was ligated to sequencing adapters and size‐selected using AMPure XP beads. Following amplification and purification, cDNA libraries were obtained and sequenced using an Illumina Novaseq6000 system (Chen et al., 2021). Fragments Per Kilobase of transcript per Million fragments mapped (FPKM) were used as a measure of transcript and gene expression levels (Liao et al., 2014). Differential gene expression analysis between sample groups was performed using DESeq2 to obtain differentially expressed gene (DEG) sets between the different samples (Love et al., 2014). Following DEG analysis, multiple hypothesis testing probabilities (P‐value) were corrected using the Benjamini‐Hochberg method to obtain a false discovery rate (FDR). The DEGs were identified based on |log2Fold‐Change| ≥ 1 and FDR <0.05. Heatmap analysis was performed using the Metware Cloud, a free online platform for data analysis (https://cloud.metware.cn).

Gene expression analysis

Total RNA was extracted using an RNAiso Plus kit (ALF1045A, TaKaRa, Dalian, China). All reverse transcription (RT) assays were performed with the PrimeScript™ RT reagent Kit with gDNA Eraser (Perfect Real Time, RR047A; TaKaRa, Dalian, China) according to the manufacturer's instructions. The RT‐qPCR was performed using FastStart SYBR Green (4 673 492 001; Roche, Basel, Swiss). The gene encoding glyceraldehyde 3‐phosphate dehydrogenase (GAPDH) was used as an internal control. The primer sequences for each gene are listed in Table S1.

For the analysis of LG1 expression patterns, different tissues of maize seedlings at mature stage were harvested, including roots, stems, tassels, ears, pulvinus, sheaths, leaves and flag leaves. For the analysis of LG1 expression patterns upon aphid infestation, multiple four‐leaf stage maize seedlings were collected from seedlings at 0, 1, 6, 24, 48, 72 and 96 h after 15 aphids had been infested on the second leaf of each maize seedling. After 96 h of aphid infestation, the aphids were removed and the maize samples were collected at 1, 6, 24, 48, 72 and 96 h. Each experiment was performed with three independent replicates.

Subcellular localization analysis of proteins

For the construction of LG1‐GFP and ZIM1‐DsRed1, a high‐fidelity Taq enzyme was used to amplify the coding regions of all the genes. The PCR fragments were cloned into pEasy‐T3 and sequenced, and the correct fragments were cloned into pCambia1300‐GFP and pCambia1300‐modified‐DsRed1 vectors. The LG1‐GFP and ZIM1‐DsRed1 vectors were transferred into Agrobacterium cells for subcellular analysis in N. benthamiana leaves. Briefly, after Agrobacterium was cultured overnight, the cells were collected and re‐suspended in infiltration buffer (0.01 MES, 0.01 mm MgCl2, 0.1 mm acetosyringone), to an OD600 of 1.0, and infiltrated into N. benthamiana leaves. After 48 h, the leaves were imaged using a Zeiss confocal microscope (Zeiss LSM800, Jena, Germany). The primer sequences for the constructs of each gene are listed in Table S1.

For the construction of ZIM1‐GFP, the ZIM1 coding region was amplified, cloned into pEasy‐T3 and sequenced. The correct fragment was then cloned into the pCambia1300‐GFP vector for the transformation into maize protoplasts (Tian et al., 2019). The ZIM1‐GFP fluorescence intensity was captured and quantified as previously described (Wang et al., 2020).

RNA in situ hybridization

Maize leaf sheaths at the four‐leaf stage were collected, fixed in fixative (4% paraformaldehyde, 0.1 M PBS, pH 7.4, prepared in DEPC‐treated water) for 2–12 h, dehydrated, sliced and then digested by adding proteinase K (20 μg/mL) (G1234; Servicebio, Wuhan, China) at 37 °C. Pre‐hybridization and hybridization were then performed (Weng et al., 2003). After washing this tissue away the hybridization solution with 2× SSC (G3015; Servicebio, Wuhan, China) at 37 °C for 10 min, 1× SSC at 37 °C for 2 × 5 min and 0.5× SSC for 10 min at room temperature, normal rabbit serum was added dropwise and incubated at room temperature for 30 min. The blocking solution was discarded, mouse anti‐digoxigenin‐conjugated alkaline phosphatase (anti‐DIG‐AP, 200‐052‐156: 1:400; Jackson, Pennsylvania, USA) was added dropwise and the mixture was incubated for 40 min at 37 °C and then washed four times for 5 min each time. BCIP/NBT (AR1023, 1 μM; Boster, Wuhan, China) colour developing solution was added dropwise and signals were observed and photographed under a microscope (Nikon, Nikon Eclipse CI; imaging system: Nikon, Nikon DS‐U, Tokyo, Japan).

Y2H analysis

The experiment was performed by using a Matchmaker Gold Yeast Two‐Hybrid kit according to the manufacturer's instructions (PT4084‐1; CLONTECH Company, Dalian, China). The LG1 coding region was fused in‐frame to the GAL4 DNA binding domain (BD) of pGBKT7 vector, and the ZIM1 coding region was fused in‐frame to the GAL4 activation domain (AD) of pGADT7. The pGBKT7‐p53 and pGADT7‐T vectors were used as positive controls. The yeast cells transformed with different constructs were grown on a solid selection medium without leucine and tryptophan for 3 days. Then multiple monoclonal clones were picked and cultured in a liquid medium without leucine and tryptophan for 24 h. Then the cells were precipitated, and washed with synthetic defined (SD) medium without tryptophan, leucine, histidine and adenine three times. Finally, the yeast concentration was determined (measuring the OD600), and the same amount of yeast cells were spread on a solid culture medium without leucine, tryptophan, adenine and histidine. Clone growth was subsequently checked, compared and photographed.

Bimolecular fluorescence complementation (BiFC) assays and luciferase complementation imaging (LCI)

The BiFC assays were performed as previously described (Wang et al., 2020). The LG1 and SPL8 coding regions fused in‐frame to the N‐terminal region of YFP (pUC‐SPYNE), while the ZIM1 and AtJAZs coding regions were fused in‐frame to the C‐terminal region of YFP (pUC‐SPYCE). The YNE‐LG1 and YCE‐ZIM1 constructs were transformed into maize protoplasts, and the YNE‐SPL8 and YCE‐AtJAZs constructs were transferred into Arabidopsis protoplasts. The interaction between AtCUL1 and AtSSF414D was used as a positive control (Wang et al., 2020). The transformed protoplasts were incubated for 24 h in the dark and imaged using a Zeiss confocal microscope (Zeiss LSM800, Jena, Germany). Meanwhile, the LG1 and ZIM1 coding regions were fused in‐frame to pCambia‐1300‐YNE and pCambia‐1300‐YCE, and transformed into Agrobacterium strain GV3101 (p19 + pSoup), respectively. The agrobacteria were then infiltrated into N. benthamiana leaves for fluorescence analysis as described above.

For the LCI analysis using benthamiana leaves (Wang et al., 2023b), the LG1 and ZIM1 coding regions were fused in‐frame to the N‐ or C‐regions of luciferase (LUC), respectively. The resulting constructs were introduced into Agrobacterium strain GV3101. The strain was then infiltrated into N. benthamiana leaves. After 48 h incubation at room temperature, the leaves were sprayed with D‐luciferin (5 mg/mL), kept in the dark for 5 min and the luminescence was monitored using a Tanon 5200 chemiluminescence imaging system (Tanon, Shanghai, China).

Pull‐down assay

For protein pull‐down experiments, the LG1 coding region was cloned into the pGEX‐GST vector, while the ZIM1 coding region was cloned into the pET28a‐His vector, which was introduced into E. coli BL21 (DE3) strain, respectively. The protein extraction, purification, and pulldown analysis were performed as described previously (Wang et al., 2020). The target proteins were detected with anti‐GST (M20007S, dilution: 1:5000; Abmart, Shanghai, China) or anti‐His (E12‐004, dilution: 1:2000; EnoGene, Nanjing, China) antibodies.

The ZIM1 and COI1a interaction was carried out in a similar way with the addition of coronatine (COR), a structural analogue of JA‐Ile (Toda et al., 2013; Zeng et al., 2021). To test whether the influence of LG1 on the ZIM1‐COI1a interaction is concentration dependent, an additional pulldown assay of ZIM1‐COI1a interaction was performed together with the increasing of GST‐LG1 or GST concentration. Proteins were enriched with His beads and detected by an anti‐GST antibody (M20007S, dilution: 1:5000; Abmart, Shanghai, China).

Co‐immunoprecipitation (Co‐IP) analysis

For Co‐IP experiments, the LG1‐GFP and ZIM1‐FLAG were transferred into the maize protoplasts, respectively. After incubation in the dark for 48 h, total protein was extracted using a protein extraction buffer (50 mm Tris–HCl (pH 9.0), 2.5 mm EDTA, 0.1% IGEPAL, 150 mm NaCl, 10% glycerol, 1 mm PMSF (phenylmethylsulfonyl fluoride), 1× Roche protease inhibitor cocktail, 10 mm BME (b‐mercaptoethanol), 10 μM leupeptin) and incubated with FLAG‐beads (B26101; Selleck.cn, Shanghai, China) for 3 h. The immunoprecipitated samples were washed five times with extraction buffer and separated by SDS polyacrylamide gel electrophoresis (PAGE), and subjected to immunoblot analysis with GFP (11 814 460 001, dilution: 1:1000; Roche, Basel, Swiss) or anti‐FLAG (M185‐3L, dilution: 1:5000; MBL, Sapporo, Hokkaido, Japan) antibodies.

Dual‐luciferase assay

The ZIM1 coding region was cloned into the pCAMBIA0800‐35S‐LUC vector to generate 35Spro:ZIM1‐LUC, and the COI1a coding region was cloned into the pCAMBIA1300 vector to generate 35Spro:COI1a (COI1a‐OE). To test the influence of COI1a on ZIM1, the 35Spro:ZIM1‐LUC construct was transfected into B73 and lg1‐1 protoplasts, or the 35Spro:ZIM1‐LUC and COI1a‐OE constructs were co‐transfected into B73 protoplasts, using the empty vector pCAMBIA1300 as a control. After 1 day of incubation, the protoplasts were collected for the quantitative analysis of LUC activity. The activity of Fluc (Firefly luciferase) activity and the Rluc (Renilla luciferase) activity were measured using a DualucifTM Firefly & Renilla Assay Kit (cat: F6075M; BioScience, Shanghai, China) through a TriStar2 LB 942‐Multimode Microplate Reader according to the manufacturer's instructions.

Protein degradation assay

Protein extraction buffer (50 mm Tris–HCl [pH 9.0], 2.5 mm EDTA, 0.1% IGEPAL, 150 mm NaCl, 10% glycerol, 1 mm PMSF [phenylmethylsulfonyl fluoride], 1× Roche protease inhibitor cocktail, 10 mm BME [β‐mercaptoethanol], 10 μM leupeptin) was used to extract total proteins from maize protoplasts co‐transfected with the constructs ZIM1‐FLAG and LG1‐GFP, using different amounts of each plasmid. For the control samples, MG132 was added to the extraction buffer at a final concentration of 50 μM. Proteins were separated by SDS‐PAGE gel and detected with an anti‐FLAG antibody (M185‐3L; dilution: 1:5000; MBL, Sapporo, Hokkaido, Japan).

DIMBOA treatment on maize leaves

DIMBOA (S89132; YuanYe, Shanghai, China) was dissolved in DMSO (D8371; Solarbio, Beijing, China) to form a 100 mm stock solution, which was diluted with sterile water to the concentrations of 500 μg/g (Varsani et al., 2019). A final concentration of 0.02% (v/v) Tween‐20 as surfactant was added to the solution (Li et al., 2017b). For the aphid survival rate experiments, maize seedlings at the four‐leaf stage were thoroughly sprayed with different concentrations of DIMBOA solution. After 24 h, aphids were placed on the maize leaves, and the number of aphids was counted at different time points. All the maize seedlings were grown and treated in a net box to prevent external insect disturbance.

DIMBOA treatment in aphid artificial diet

Aphid artificial diet was purchased from TROPHIC Animal Feed High‐Tech Company (Nantong, China). Aphid feeding assays were conducted using the artificial diet containing different amounts of DIMBOA as previously described (Meihls et al., 2013), and the control aqueous solution (0.3% [v/v] DMSO) was used for all artificial feeding trials.

Measurement of agronomic traits

The agronomic traits of the wild type B73 and lg1 were measured when the maize grew to 50 days. The leaf angles were measured as previously described (Tian et al., 2019) and the plant height was measured from the highest point of the plant tassel to the ground position. For the puncture strength tests, the same position of B73 and lg1 stems were measured with a plant stem strength tester (YYD‐1; Zhejiang Topu Yunnong Technology Company, Hangzhou, China).

Statistical analysis

Histograms and boxplot analyses were performed with GraphPad Prism 8.0.1 (San Diego, CA, USA), and statistical analyses were carried out using SPSS 16.0 with two‐tailed unpaired Student's t‐tests (Chicago, IL, USA).
