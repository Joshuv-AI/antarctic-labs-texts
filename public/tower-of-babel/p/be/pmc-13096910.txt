Enteric and Controlled-Release Budesonide in IgA Nephropathy: Expanding Access While Refining Evidence
Authors: Kenar D Jhaveri, Heather N Reich
Published: 2026

See Clinical Research on Article 106412 and Research Letter on Article 106368

The therapeutic landscape of IgA nephropathy (IgAN) has evolved rapidly. The renaissance for the treatment of IgAN began with the development of nefecon, a targeted-release formulation budesonide (Tarpeyo or Kinpeygo).1,2 By delivering corticosteroid selectively to the distal ileum to modulate gut-associated lymphoid tissue, nefecon demonstrated meaningful reductions in proteinuria and preservation of kidney function in randomized trials.1,2 However, cost and limited global availability restrict its use in many low income countries such as South Asia where IgAN is highly prevalent.

Two contemporary studies in the current issue of the journal now explore whether more widely available formulations, generic controlled-release (CR) budesonide and enteric-coated budesonide, which are used in inflammatory bowel diseases might serve as pragmatic alternatives, particularly in resource-limited settings. Bagchi et al.3 conducted a retrospective single-center study of generic CR-budesonide in 57 patients with biopsy-proven IgAN and persistent proteinuria despite optimized renin-angiotensin system blockade. At 9 months, 43.8% achieved remission (complete or partial), with a mean 38.5% reduction in proteinuria and relative stability of estimated glomerular filtration rate (eGFR). Importantly, serious infections were not observed, contrasting with previous local experience using systemic corticosteroids. Relapses of IgAN after withdrawal, however, occurred in more than half of responders during short follow-up.3

In parallel, Gulati et al.4 performed a prospective, multicenter, open-label, single-arm study of enteric-coated–budesonide in 168 immunosuppression-naïve adults with IgAN. At 9 months, geometric mean urinary albumin-to-creatinine ratio declined by 66.3%, with 75.7% achieving ≥50% reduction. Mean eGFR improved modestly (+5 ml/min per 1.73 m2), and no serious adverse events were reported. Notably, this study included patients with advanced chronic kidney disease (eGFR < 35 ml/min per 1.73 m2), a group underrepresented in previous landmark trials.4

These studies add to previously reported data supporting the efficacy of alternate formulations of budesonide. These include an open-label study of 32 patients with IgAN treated with CR-budesonide (Budenofalk) for 24 months, demonstrating clinically and statistically significant reduction in proteinuria and eGFR preservation up to 36 months of follow-up.5 A small single-center open-label randomized controlled trial compared 9 months treated with a CR-budesonide (n = 27) to standard-of-care (n = 26), after a 6-month run-in on maximized renin-angiotensin-aldosterone system inhibition.6 By 9 months, patients in the budesonide arm had greater reduction in proteinuria and stabilization of eGFR.

Mechanistically, nefecon differs from enteric-coated or CR formulations in its targeted terminal ileal delivery, designed to modulate Peyer’s patches specifically through the use of specialized encapsulation technology.7 Budesonide absorbed in the ileum will undergo first-past metabolism to products with low glucocorticoid activity, to minimize systemic glucocorticoid effects.8 Distal enteric-coated and CR formulations dissolve more broadly in the small intestine and proximal colon, potentially resulting in broader mucosal immunomodulation and variable systemic exposure. Whether this broader exposure compromises specificity—or enhances efficacy—remains uncertain.

Methodologically, important limitations must temper interpretation. The study by Bagchi et al.3 was retrospective and single-centered. The study by Gulati et al.,4 although prospective and multicentered, lacked a control arm. Neither can definitively attribute observed improvements solely to the intervention, because regression to the mean, optimized supportive care, and improved adherence may contribute.

A randomized controlled trial comparing nefecon to other budesonide formulations is unlikely to be conducted. However, the results of the highly anticipated GRACE-IgAN study will be an important addition to our knowledge and will be of particular interest to countries where nefecon is difficult access.9 This multicenter, randomized, adaptive platform trial will compare the effects of 2 years of treatment with oral steroids, gut-directed budesonide, hydroxychloroquine, mycophenolate mofetil, and nonsteroidal mineralocorticoid receptor antagonists, on proteinuria and kidney function. The study is underway, targeting enrollment of 585 subjects in South Asian adults at high risk of disease progression.

From a practical standpoint, access to treatment options requires consideration of regulatory status, cost, access pathways, and real-world monitoring logistics. In the US, nefecon (Tarpeyo) is US Food and Drug Administration–approved for IgAN at risk of progression, which facilitates prescribing but introduces prior authorization requirements and substantial cost considerations that may limit equitable access, particularly outside academic centers. Kinpeygo is approved by the European Medicines Agency and funding varies by country. Nefecon is also approved by regulatory agencies in several Pacific Asian countries, with variable funding support. In Canada, and South America, nefecon is not approved.

Practical use of budesonide demands structured proteinuria monitoring, concurrent optimization of background renin-angiotensin-aldosterone system blockade and sodium-glucose cotransporter-2 inhibition, and vigilance for steroid-related adverse effects—even if reduced compared with systemic glucocorticoids. Therefore, though gut-targeted budesonide is conceptually appealing and increasingly evidence-based, its real-world deployment hinges as much on reimbursement frameworks and health-system infrastructure as on clinical trial efficacy. Widening disparities in global access to less toxic IgAN therapies merits attention and discussion because emerging treatment classes such as complement and APRIL inhibition are not likely to be more financially accessible.

Nevertheless, both studies provide valuable real-world data in populations often underrepresented in global trials. They suggest that accessible budesonide formulations may offer short-term antiproteinuric benefit with acceptable safety in selected patients. Prospective randomized comparisons to other accessible treatment options are required to confirm long-term efficacy and to clarify their position relative to nefecon.
