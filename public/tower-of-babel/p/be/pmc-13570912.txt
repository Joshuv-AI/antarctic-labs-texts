Expanding Patient Access to Advanced Therapies
Authors: Elena Guillen, Ana Hidalgo-Simon
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractAdvanced therapy medicinal products (ATMPs) promise transformative clinical potential, but their use remains limited by complex manufacturing, high prices, and fragmented market access. To explore the existing and potential regulatory pathways to increase access to ATMPs, we conducted a product-level analysis of ATMPs and biologic medicines submitted to the European Medicines Agency up to May 2026, and a review of regulatory pathways and policy frameworks. While certain scientific principles from biologic and biosimilar regulation, such as totality of evidence, fit-for-purpose data, and tailored approaches are transferable, the biosimilar pathway may not be suitable for certain ATMPs. Other approaches, including bio-hybrid applications or prior knowledge-based dossiers, may be better suited to support competitive entry and second-wave innovation. Tailored follow-on pathways, open and well governed platform technologies, and supportive industrial and data sharing policies, are key to facilitate the translation of the scientific promise of ATMPs into sustainable and equitable patient access.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at https://doi.org/10.1007/s40259-026-00797-7.

Key Points

Almost half of currently marketed ATMPs are expected to lose market exclusivity by 2030, prompting critical discussions on how to foster competition, improve affordability, and increase patient access.Lessons from biosimilar regulation—totality of evidence, fit-for-purpose, and tailored approaches—can guide ATMP follow-on product development but may be suitable only for a subset of ATMPs.Alternative pathways include prior-knowledge applications, bio-hybrids, or platform technologies. Redevelopment using full access to originator data and optimized manufacturing may improve access for safe and efficacious but commercially vulnerable ATMPs.

Introduction

Advanced therapy medicinal products (ATMPs) are defined as medicinal products based on genes, tissues, or cells [1, 2]. In terms of innovation, they are arguably the most transformative and exciting products on the market today. Currently, 30–40 ATMPs are approved globally, with some projections estimating 50–75 new approvals by 2030 [3]. Clinical development is expanding rapidly, with over 1500 ongoing ATMP trials registered on ClinicalTrials.gov (Cell & Gene 2023 market outlook) and more than 300 clinical trials ongoing in the European Union (EU) [4]. The regulatory environment supporting ATMP development is also maturing. Converging initiatives, including unified European Medicines Agency (EMA) guidelines [5], PRIME (priority medicines) eligibility [6], and the proposed EU Biotech Act [7], are all collectively aimed at streamlining development. Despite their transformative potential, ATMPs continue to face significant hurdles in manufacturing scalability, regulatory approval, integration into healthcare systems, and cost management, which collectively limit patient access. Most authorized ATMPs target rare diseases with high unmet medical needs; and accordingly, a large proportion qualify for orphan designation [8].

By 2030, around half of currently approved ATMPs will have reached the end of their market exclusivity protection [8, 9], prompting critical discussions on how to foster competition while maintaining affordability. The need to balance continued innovation, sustainable market dynamics, and equitable patient access has become increasingly pressing. A particularly acute challenge concerns ATMPs that, despite demonstrating substantial long-term societal benefit and life-saving potential for patients, remain commercially nonviable under conventional reimbursement frameworks, creating a structural gap between clinical value and market sustainability.

This article explores policy and regulatory strategies to enhance access to ATMPs. Using a EU dataset encompassing ATMP and biologic submissions to the EMA, we identify a subset of ATMPs that exhibit characteristics supportive of traditional follow-on development. Moreover, drawing lessons from the evolution of biosimilar regulation, we propose a continuum of approaches, including hybrid models, platform technologies, and a potential “redevelopment ATMP” pathway, designed to enable scalable manufacturing, sustainable pricing, and expanded patient access across Europe.

Methods

We conducted a product-level analysis of all ATMPs and biological medicines submitted to EMA up to 30 May 2026, complemented by a targeted review of regulatory pathways and policy frameworks.

For ATMPs, product data were extracted from publicly available information from EMA [10]. Information regarding approved indication(s), prevalence of conditions, orphan designation, and efficacy data was extracted from Orphan Maintenance Assessment Reports (OMARs), European Public Assessment Reports (EPARs), and withdrawal assessment reports (ARs). For selected products, pricing information was derived from published sources [11] and, where needed, verified through follow-up with relevant experts. Publicly available list prices were used as reference values. Confidential rebates were not included; prices are presented for illustrative purposes rather than precise economic modeling. Information on loss of exclusivity was collected to estimate timelines for potential follow-on development.

For biological medicinal products, data were extracted from publicly available information from EMA [10] and commercial databases [12, 13]. For each biologic, we identified whether biosimilar versions had been developed and the characteristics of the corresponding active substances (Supplementary Table 1). The EMA database served as the primary reference source, while commercial databases were used for cross-validation. In cases of discrepancies between sources, EMA records were used as the authoritative dataset. Duplicates were defined as multiple biosimilar versions of the same active substance developed by different manufacturers, including cases of staggered or indication-specific development strategies, and were retained in the dataset to reflect development and market dynamics.

For both datasets, inclusion was based on the submission of a marketing authorization application to EMA. Products were classified according to their regulatory status, following EMA framework [14]. Categories included: authorized (European Commission [EC] marketing authorization decision), positive opinion (Committee for Medicinal Products for Human Use [CHMP] opinion issued prior to EC decision), withdrawn, and expired/lapsed products. In our dataset, withdrawn products were defined as products that had obtained marketing authorization and were subsequently removed from the market. Products withdrawn prior to authorization were not included, as they never entered the market. Although the analytical cut-off was 30 May 2026, selected factual fields (e.g., dates of CHMP opinion adoption or EC marketing authorization decisions) were updated where necessary to reflect publicly available information released after this date and are reported in the final tables.

To identify strategies for improving patient access to ATMPs, we conducted a literature review focusing on regulatory pathways and enabling policy instruments. Sources included publicly available guidance documents, regulatory reports, and relevant legislation. Particular emphasis was placed on EMA documents. Additional perspectives were drawn from comparable frameworks and policies from the US Food and Drug Administration (FDA), Japan’s Pharmaceuticals and Medical Devices Agency (PMDA), and Health Canada to provide international context.

In this study, “follow-on” refers broadly to subsequent-entry products that rely, to some extent, on the data package of an already authorized reference product following loss of regulatory protection, including but not limited to biosimilars.

The ATMP Landscape in the EU

Up to 30 May 2026, 31 ATMPs have been authorized or hold a positive opinion in the EU. Only 22 are still in the market (Table 1). Nine (29%) were withdrawn or not renewed following company decisions. Reasons were primarily commercial, including limited market uptake and unfavorable reimbursement outcomes. Two withdrawals had a clinical performance element: Alofisel, which was withdrawn following insufficient clinical benefit to justify its use in the confirmatory ADMIRE-CD II trial [15], and Zalmoxis, while formally withdrawn for commercial reasons by the marketing authorization holder, had also reported unfavorable results from its post-approval phase III trial, a condition of its conditional authorization [16].

Four additional ATMP products not included in Table 1 were submitted to EMA but never entered the market: Fanskya and Amtagvi were withdrawn by the applicant before a final CHMP opinion [17, 18], and Jelrix and Elevidys received a negative CHMP opinion/refusal [19, 20].

Among the 22 marketed ATMPs, 18 (82%) are gene therapies. Overall, 19 (86.4%) have an orphan status, i.e., target a rare disease affecting no more than 5 in 10,000 people and either has no satisfactory treatment available or offers significant benefit over existing options. [21]. Of note, not all products that met the criteria applied for orphan designation. Of the 19 ATMPs with an orphan designation, 7 (36.8%) address indications with no satisfactory treatment, while the remaining were designated on the basis of significant benefit despite available alternatives.

Of all ATMPs listed in Table 1, only five (16%) cover ultrarare indications, i.e., prevalence under 0.2 in 10,000 [21]. Additionally, certain approved ATMPs with orphan designation (i.e., Kymriah, Breyanzi, and Casgevy) have individual indications that meet the orphan designation prevalence threshold; however, owing to approval for multiple indications, their overall treated population exceeds this limit.

The 31 approved ATMPs cover seven therapeutic areas, with oncology being the most represented. Overall, 14 address hematological disorders (7 malignancies, 5 hemoglobinopathies/bleeding disorders, and 2 metabolic/leukodystrophies with hematopoietic involvement), and 16 cover genetic conditions. More than half have overlapping indications; 5 treat B cell malignancies, 2 multiple myeloma, 2 hemophilia B, 2 transfusion-dependent β-thalassemia, and 3 hematopoietic stem cell transplantation (HSCT)-related conditions.

Of the 22 marketed ATMPs, 9 (41%) will lose their regulatory market exclusivity within 4 years. To the best of our knowledge, no approved biosimilar or direct follow-on competitors are in development for these products.

These biologics cover 206 distinct active substances. Existing Pathways that can be Used to Increase Access to ATMPs

The Biosimilar Pathway

The biosimilar pathway relies on two key principles: (1) a robust analytical and functional characterization, rooted in decades of experience with comparability of manufacturing changes of originators [22, 23] and (2) the totality of evidence approach, where the need for additional data is based on the remaining uncertainties in the analytical and functional characterization [24, 25].

The EU legislation [26], together with the overarching EMA guideline [27], provides a legal framework that does not preclude the development of biosimilar ATMPs. Although the overarching framework and legal basis exists, additional adaptation at the level of ATMP-specific guidance will likely be necessary, and practical implementation faces numerous challenges and uncertainties.

Lessons from biosimilars can help inform ATMP follow-on development. Principles that can be applied include: (1) tailored case-by-case approaches, where evidence requirements are adapted to the specific product and clinical context rather than applying a one-size-fits-all framework [23, 28]; (2) the totality of evidence principle [25, 28], particularly relevant in the context of ATMPs, where data scarcity and uncertainty are common; and (3) fit-for-purpose data, meaning data are specifically designed to address a relevant scientific or regulatory question. Equally important is managing the burden of proof; only the evidence necessary should be generated, avoiding unnecessary studies that consume resources or may introduce uncertainty.

However, several elements of the biosimilar framework do not directly translate to ATMPs. First, access to an adequate reference product. While 15–30 reference batches are generally considered appropriate for the biosimilarity exercise [28], such numbers are often infeasible for ATMPs owing to small-scale, patient- or donor-specific manufacturing and ethical concerns around using human material solely for comparability [29]. In addition, access to a reference ATMP for side-by-side comparability is itself a major constraint, particularly for autologous and genetically modified cell therapies, which are not routinely available through standard pharmacy supply chains and are unlikely to be provided by the original marketing authorization holder. Second, ATMPs exhibit exceptionally high inherent variability, which limits the statistical validity of similarity assessments and makes it difficult to define robust comparability ranges, challenges that, as documented in the literature, are among the most frequent issues identified in the assessment of ATMP regulatory dossiers [30]. Third, adequate characterization methods are still lacking. The biosimilarity exercises strongly rely on extensive structural and functional characterization [28], but for most ATMPs, potency and safety depend on complex cellular properties that cannot yet be fully captured with current in vitro tests. The biosimilar framework approach may be more feasible for ATMPs such as gene therapy products based on RNA or DNA (plasmid), where molecular characterization is more advanced and quality comparability could, in principle, be more readily established. Fourth, traditional pharmacokinetic and confirmatory efficacy studies are frequently impractical. Pharmacokinetics cannot be reliably measured in many ATMPs, and clinical trials often involve extremely small patient populations, on occasions fewer than ten individuals, further restricting statistical power [31]. Compared with other biologicals, ATMPs are more prone to critical deficiencies in clinical data, resulting in higher residual uncertainty at the time of authorization [32]. In addition, complex dose definitions (e.g., vector genomes/mL, cell dispersion for infusion), administration procedures, and limited shelf life further complicate quality and clinical comparability.

Importantly, the legislation and some overall guidance are already in place. For instance, the current EMA framework for comparability of manufacturing changes in ATMPs already reflects some of the scientific principles that could inform future follow-on development models. The EMA Q&A on ATMP comparability explicitly recognizes that there is no fixed or “one-size-fits-all” number of reference batches and that the amount of data required must be justified case by case, depending on product variability, assay sensitivity, type of change, and process knowledge [33]. The guidance also acknowledges the statistical limitations associated with highly variable biological products and small batch numbers, and allows flexible, risk-based approaches, including the use of historical data, split-batch designs, and representative donor material when side-by-side testing is not feasible. Certain approaches could help provide a starting point from a practical perspective; for instance, experience with the batch numbers of innovator type 2 variations may provide guidance for designing follow-on comparability studies. Broader applicability of the biosimilar pathway may follow as comparability methodologies mature and allogeneic platforms advance.

Although the biosimilar framework provides a scientifically grounded model for follow-on development, its broader impact must also be considered in light of real-world implementation. The pathway was originally developed to increase patient access to biological medicines by fostering competition and reducing prices [34]. While it has successfully increased competition for some widely used biologics, uptake remains concentrated in a limited subset of products. As presented in Table 2, approximately a quarter of biologic medicines have an approved biosimilar, representing less than 15% of active substances and only 11 product classes. This proportion must be interpreted in context, as not all biologics have reached loss of exclusivity. However, whether a biosimilar can be technically developed for a given biologic does not fully explain the low coverage. Market dynamics and other practical realities also play a crucial role, as discussed later in this manuscript.

Bio-hybrids

Another potential framework is the so-called bio-hybrid pathway. It is a distinct legal basis for off-patent biologic versions of existing medicines that do not meet the strict biosimilar medicine and stand-alone legal definition. The revision of the pharmaceutical legislation in the EU currently being discussed contains a proposal that opens the door for such hybrid routes under Article 12, including repurposing opportunities. This builds on the existing hybrid application framework from Article 10(3) of Directive 2001/83/EC, which already permits such flexibility when bioequivalence cannot be demonstrated [35], and has been used for nonbiologic follow‑ons, such as teriparatide products [36]. The concept mirrors the US FDA’s 505(b)(2) pathway, which leverages existing data (e.g., published clinical studies or data from another product) to support approval of a differential or follow‑on product [37]. Omnitrope (somatropin) was approved in the USA via a 505(b)(2) approach before the dedicated biosimilar pathway existed [38]. Biohybrids acknowledge that a follow-on product may present more differences than strictly allowed in the biosimilar framework (e.g., new formulations, different delivery systems, or improved manufacturing technologies), while still relying principally on reference product data. This approach may enable “second-wave innovation,” where developers expand the therapeutic use of an existing product beyond what the originator company achieved through the product’s life cycle during the exclusivity period. Although no individual biological product has yet been approved using this pathway, it has the merit of already existing in legislation, and could provide a pragmatic option for ATMPs, particularly cellular products, where strict biosimilarity may be unattainable.

Applications Built on Literature and Prior Knowledge

Another possible route is the use of literature-based data, where prior knowledge is leveraged to support parts of the regulatory dossier. This model allows applicants to build on published data and regulatory experience with similar products, reducing the need for extensive de novo evidence generation, although care must be taken to not overlook product-specific critical quality attributes. The EMA has explored how external and proprietary knowledge can justifiably replace or complement specific data in a regulatory submission [39]. When full analytical and functional characterization is not possible, literature-based evidence could complement and streamline the overall package (e.g., criticality designation, justifying ranges of process parameters, predicting issues with manufacturing scale-up and stability). In addition, data from non-similar but related products may inform developers about which attributes are critical for clinical performance and which data may be redundant. Subsequent regulatory experience from licensed products may serve to validate or refute these assumptions.

Chimeric antigen receptor T cell (CAR-T) therapies may represent viable candidates for this route. Although different vectors and CAR constructs classify them as distinct products, they converge around similar targets and clinical uses. In such cases, relevant clinical experience from related products, such as safety data, could still be leveraged to support applications built on prior knowledge.

Discussion

As presented in Table 1, approximately one in three ATMPs authorized in the EU has been withdrawn from the market within around a decade of approval (9 of 31 products). While the majority of withdrawals were driven by commercial considerations, clinical performance was also a contributing factor in two cases. This high postauthorization attrition rate suggests that regulatory approval does not necessarily translate into market sustainability.

Among the marketed ATMPs, orphan designation alone does not seem to ensure sustainability, nor is ultrarare targeting the primary driver of market fragility. For example, although Breyanzi initially received orphan designation, subsequent indication expansions have enlarged its cumulative treatable population to nearly double the standard orphan threshold. Moreover, the 31 authorized ATMPs cluster within a limited number of therapeutic areas, showing early signs of intraclass competition and the emergence of competitive micro-markets. Certain subfields with relatively common diseases (hematology, oncology, and autoimmune diseases) are attracting increasing developer interest, with follow-on or second-wave entrants clustering. These are also the areas where highly effective novel biologics, including T cell engagers, are advancing rapidly, intensifying cross-class competition alongside intraclass rivalry. In these areas, timing of entry may be critical, and commercial windows narrow.

Almost half (41%) of currently marketed ATMPs are expected to lose market exclusivity by 2030, with no approved biosimilar or direct follow-on competitors visibly in development. This suggests that expiry of regulatory exclusivity alone may not be the competition landmark that exists for biosimilars. As observed by other authors, in the ATMP field, layered patent thickets, extensive trade secrecy surrounding manufacturing processes, and fragmented intellectual property estates may create barriers that are structurally more complex and less transparent than those typically encountered for conventional small-molecule or biologic medicines [40]. Moreover, next-generation approaches (e.g., allogeneic CAR-T platforms, improved lentiviral vectors, in vivo delivery systems, clustered regularly interspaced short palindromic repeats (CRISPR)-based editing, and universal donor cell technologies) are advancing in the pipeline, and may introduce competitive pressure. Although these represent iterative innovations rather than the direct product-specific competition characteristic of the biosimilar pathway, whether such pipeline activity will translate into meaningful competitive pressure at the point of exclusivity expiry remains uncertain.

Experience from biosimilar development further demonstrates that commercial entry is shaped by a complex interplay of market forces beyond scientific feasibility. Of 206 approved biologic active substances, only 29 (14.1%) are covered by at least one authorized or positively reviewed biosimilar. Moreover, biosimilar development is highly concentrated. Of all authorized biosimilars, 74.4% target just two product classes: monoclonal antibodies (62.8%) and granulocyte colony-stimulating factors (11.5%). At the molecule level, clustering is even more pronounced: up to 8–29 biosimilars compete for a single high-value active substance (e.g., denosumab, ustekinumab, and adalimumab), rather than one biosimilar per molecule across the full set of eligible products. The concentration of biosimilar development is further illustrated by the 2025 surge in approvals: 41 biosimilars were authorized by the EMA that year alone. Interestingly, 23 referenced denosumab, probably driven by the convergence of market exclusivity expiry and a high market valued at over 1.2 billion USD [41].

This indicates that conventional indicators such as large patient populations or multiple approved indications do not consistently predict biosimilar uptake. For instance, despite high prevalence and well-characterized biology, insulins have seen limited biosimilar entry, largely due to market concentration and low profitability, with three companies controlling approximately 88% of globally registered products [42]. Similarly, although trastuzumab has multiple indications, biosimilar uptake has been shaped by shifts in standard of care and formulation changes, including the introduction of second-generation products such as trastuzumab emtansine and transitions from intravenous to subcutaneous administration [43]. In other cases, alternative regulatory or manufacturing routes, as observed with teriparatide—where synthetically manufactured versions may enter as generics—have bypassed the classical biosimilar pathway [36].

Timing is equally critical: The optimal entry point occurs after reimbursement pathways are secured, yet before markets become saturated or the therapy is displaced by therapeutic innovation. Collectively, these observations indicate that follow-on development is determined not solely by regulatory possibility or scientific comparability but also by economic scale, competitive dynamics, intellectual property constraints, and market positioning.

These dynamics are nonetheless subject to change. Advances in manufacturing technologies and anticipated regulatory adaptations (e.g., streamlined clinical development for biosimilars) may progressively lower barriers to follow-on entry and attract new competitors. Yet, experience from the biosimilar field confirms that commercial potential remains the primary driver of follow-on development. Moreover, even where competitive entry occurs, real-world patient access remains contingent on national reimbursement frameworks, which adds an additional layer of market fragmentation.

Determinants of Follow-On ATMP Development and Sustainability

Commercial attractiveness for any medical product is determined by a sufficient patient population and market size. Most approved ATMPs target rare conditions, where a large proportion of eligible patients have already been treated in the context of clinical trials. Even in less rare diseases, one-time curative ATMPs may lead to shrinking patient pools over time [40]. However, a growing number of approved ATMPs that remain on the market have expanded indications and shifted toward earlier treatment lines. For example, Breyanzi and Carvykti were initially approved in later lines of therapy but have since gained approval for use following earlier relapse [44, 45]. Considering these factors, we estimate that approximately one-quarter of approved ATMPs in Table 1 have the prevalence and breadth of indication to constitute a potentially attractive market for developers (e.g., CAR-T therapies) when considering patient numbers alone.

A second key factor is the extent to which production can be standardized and variability minimized. Gene therapies have a primary functional component (e.g., genetic sequence) that can, in principle, be more readily replicated and delivered via a vector [9]. However, under current EMA definitions, gene therapies also include ex vivo products such as CAR-T cells, which involve genetically modified cellular components. Thus, this perceived “simplicity” applies primarily to in vivo gene transfer therapies (e.g., Luxturna, Zolgensma, Roctavian, or in vivo gene-silencing nonviral platforms such as small interfering RNA [siRNA] or messenger RNA [mRNA]-based therapies). Some of these therapies can be defined by a specific, known transgene and vector, and their identity can be confirmed by sequencing and vector analysis. Other types of gene therapy, such as ex vivo, gene addition (e.g., CAR-T or hematopoietic stem cell gene therapy), in vivo gene editing (CRISPR, zinc finger nucleases [ZFN], and transcription activator-like effector nucleases [TALENs]), and ex vivo gene editing would be less easily replicated [46]. Consequently, CAR-T could focus on replicating or standardizing the viral vector rather than the final cell product, since the latter is highly variable and patient-specific. How such an approach could fit within existing regulatory pathways remains unclear.

Cell therapies would likely be more challenging, as they exhibit subtle but complex differences in phenotype, function, and behavior, even when manufactured using the same protocol. Such variability arises not only from differences in starting materials (e.g., donor-to-donor heterogeneity in both autologous and allogeneic settings) but also from differences in manufacturing steps (e.g., cell culture conditions, expansion protocols, and handling) [47].

Tissue-engineered products may be among the least attractive, as they combine living cells with scaffolds or devices, and are often manufactured on a personalized basis, making standardization difficult [48].

Logistic considerations are another clear driver. Decentralized infrastructure could enable local production, reducing reliance on a single centralized facility and subsequently facilitating scale-out and broader patient access [49]. At the same time, it poses important regulatory challenges, particularly in defining the boundary between manufacturing under Good Manufacturing Practices (GMP) and activities that fall within medical or pharmacy practice. While decentralized manufacturing is increasing for certain ATMPs, especially autologous cell therapies, and other initiatives at the EU level (e.g., EMA pilots that support the academic exploration of solid manufacturing leading to products), regulatory frameworks are still evolving to accommodate these models, and full implementation is not yet standard practice. Another consideration is the mode of delivery; therapies that require highly specialized administration in a small number of expert centers where teams retain significant tacit knowledge of the process, face substantial barriers to wider uptake because the complexity of replication extends beyond the product and into the delivery chain. A strong EU Biotech Skills agenda will therefore be essential to ensure adequate preparedness in the healthcare community and to prevent workforce and skills bottlenecks [7, 50, 51].

Finally, costs and pricing are key elements in future development of follow-on ATMP decisions. ATMP manufacturing is likely to remain expensive, even with increasing streamlining of manufacturing processes. Although estimating true commercial ATMP manufacturing costs is difficult, academic initiatives offer some insight. For instance, Hospital Clínic de Barcelona produces an in-house anti-CD19 CAR-T under the hospital exemption pathway at an estimated 89,270 € per treatment, covering consumables, cleanroom maintenance, and staff salaries, but excluding research and development [52]. Another example is Quartemi, offered in India at ₹35–50 lakhs (33,800–48,300 €) [53]. Although these estimates may be based on relatively small-scale production and are likely owing to lower cost of local manufacturing and supportive national policies, current market prices for commercial CAR-Ts in the EU typically range from 250,000 € to 350,000 € per treatment [11]. These price differences highlight that manufacturing is only one component of pricing. Others include the need to recoup high development investments, the complexity of the delivery, and the cost of alternative treatment options, such as stem cell transplantation, which can set an implicit benchmark for payer willingness to reimburse high-cost curative therapies.

Reimbursement policies are another critical factor in this equation. Market authorization does not automatically translate into patient access. Despite substantial and rapidly growing global expenditure on ATMPs—reaching 5.9 billion USD in 2023, a 38% increase compared with 2022 [3], the reimbursement landscape remains highly fragmented, even within Europe. A 2024 IQVIA market access analysis shows marked national differences: Germany has granted positive national reimbursement or funding decisions for 92% of authorized ATMPs, followed by France at 75%. In contrast, countries such as Luxembourg and Switzerland reimburse fewer than 42% of authorized products. Notably, reimbursement rates do not appear to correlate with gross domestic product [3]. This is partially explained by the fact that cost-effectiveness assessments are challenging for ATMPs (e.g., high unit costs, combined with the limitations of traditional value frameworks in capturing the long-term benefits of one-off curative therapies, complicate standard health economic modeling), and that the solutions being tested currently, such as outcome-based managed entry agreements, public–private partnerships, and risk-sharing agreements, are having limited success.

While our analysis is necessarily EU-centric, these dynamics must be considered within a rapidly evolving global context. In 2025, Asia-Pacific has overtaken North America in ATMP clinical trials (990 versus 916, with 716 in China alone); global venture funding reached 11.1 billion USD, and the FDA approved six ATMPs [54]. This expanding international landscape not only intensifies competitive pressure, accelerates pipeline development, and may drive manufacturing cost reductions but also risks increasing regulatory and market fragmentation. All these factors shape the commercial viability and follow-on development prospects of ATMPs in the EU and beyond.

Future Solutions to Improve ATMP Access

Platform technologies have the potential to streamline ATMP development by enabling elements, such as vector platforms, manufacturing processes, analytical methods, or cell lines to be shared across multiple products. This can increase efficiency and standardization, reduce operating costs, facilitate knowledge share [7, 9, 40], and simplify regulatory processes.

Platforms are now formally embedded in the new EU pharmaceutical legislation, with specific provisions facilitating their development and authorization for innovative medicinal products [55]. The term “platform” may encompass anything from shared starting materials (such as viral vectors or cell lines) to fully validated manufacturing processes common across multiple products, each sitting at different distances from the final product. Broadly, platforms refer to technologies that directly generate a product or an intermediate step toward it, but not biomarkers, devices, or ancillary tools. However, their definition, scope, and level of application remain open.

Use of a platform should ensure that outputs are identical or highly similar across users, yet there is no consensus on the level of data needed to demonstrate such similarity. Moreover, a platform does not necessarily require proprietary intellectual property and could be developed collaboratively or remain in academic hands that allow wider access. When incorporated into regulatory filings, platform technologies may also qualify for expedited review, as envisioned in recent legislative efforts such as the FDORA Act [56].

However, a critical hurdle is the definition of responsibilities for maintaining the platform, pharmacovigilance, and governance. To address some of these concerns, and in the interest of public health perspective, we believe platform technologies should ideally be developed as open platforms, accessible to multiple stakeholders. This would allow developers to benefit from validated and regulatory-compliant technologies to accelerate development timelines and reduce costs. Ideally developers should rely on or cross-refer to a platform technology without necessarily being their owner, while a separate mechanism ensures that the platform is kept scientifically and regulatory up to date.

Ultimately, platforms represent not just a technical tool but also a pathway for fostering competition and allow products and technologies to be built in already existing know-how. Conceptually, they could be compared with a shared “trunk” of validated science, manufacturing and regulatory validity, from which diverse “branches” of products emerge. The higher up the trunk one defines the platform, the more closely related the derived products will be. Properly structured, platforms could lower entry barriers, support sustainable development models, and expand patient access by increasing the number and diversity of available therapies. Moreover, shared platforms, such as vectors and allogeneic cell lines, could meaningfully reduce the capital requirements for market entry, opening the field to smaller developers.

Regulatory sandboxes are another approach being discussed under the review of the pharmaceutical legislation in the EU [7, 55]. The provision of a controlled environment for developers to innovate under regulatory supervision [57] is likely to be a key developmental space for ATMP regulatory innovation, including exploring incentives for both originators and follow-on developers. Within such a framework, developers could test strategies under the supervision of regulators, reducing uncertainty and facilitating learning. To ensure broader impact, key findings should be made public to inform guidance, support standardization, and guide future developers. However, careful governance will be needed to maintain a clear boundary between regulatory oversight and commercial interests.

To address therapies that are withdrawn from the market owing to insufficient commercial viability, we propose a new pathway, the “redevelopment” pathway, for situations of commercial failure. As illustrated in Table 1, safe and effective therapies are withdrawn from the market owing to lack of profit. The “redevelopment” pathway is applicable when the originator company, or a developer with full access to the complete dossier, undertakes redevelopment. This assumes (1) full and unrestricted access to the reference product’s data through expiration of data exclusivity or voluntary data transfer agreements between originator and redevelopment entity and (2) a clear potential for substantial improvement in the manufacturing process, enabling reductions in cost and complexity while preserving, or improving, safety and efficacy.

Manufacturing changes are normally introduced through regulatory variations [58]. However, variations can be time-consuming, expensive, and may be insufficient when fundamental redesign is needed. In such cases, re-engineering the process from the ground up may be more efficient than incremental modification. Under the “redevelopment” approach, developers could reconstruct key manufacturing elements, such as switching to improved vectors, adopting more practical cell sources, or introducing automation to generate a therapy that is more scalable and affordable while still leveraging the existing clinical and regulatory evidence base. This is an area where regulators could exercise flexibility to enable improved follow-on versions of clinically valuable products that would otherwise leave the market. It should be noted that no dedicated regulatory pathway currently exists for the redevelopment of withdrawn products. Under the current EU framework, substantially redeveloped products would generally be considered new medicinal products and would therefore require a new marketing authorization under an appropriate legal basis.

Strimvelis—a gene therapy for a rare immune deficiency—may illustrate this concept [59, 60]. Its current manual manufacturing is costly and limited to a single center. Re-engineering the process by switching from retroviral to lentiviral vectors, using peripheral blood as a source instead of mobilized bone marrow, and adopting semiautomated methods could dramatically reduce costs and timelines while improving product quality and patient reach. The knowledge and technologies required for such improvements are already available; however, their implementation is constrained within the currently approved product framework. The redevelopment pathway proposed may be analogous to existing EMA initiatives such as medicine repurposing, intended to facilitate the reintroduction of clinically valuable therapies that have failed for commercial rather than clinical reasons [61].

Finally, patient access to follow-on ATMPs would benefit from redefining precompetitive and competitive boundaries and reshaping interactions across the value chain. Developers face barriers from patents, trade secrets, and complex manufacturing [9, 40]. Private–public contractual arrangements could be key to enabling knowledge sharing while preserving competitive advantage. Success will depend on combining scientific rigor with innovative business models that support a sustainable and collaborative market.

Of note, the pathways discussed in this manuscript sit at different points on a continuum between full biosimilarity and independent innovation. The evidence required will depend on the residual uncertainties of each product, and tailored, case-by-case approaches will remain most appropriate. In practice, pathways may overlap or be combined. For instance, literature- and prior knowledge-based approaches may support a hybrid or redevelopment application, while platform technologies may support either a follow-on or a novel product.

A final note on the global ATMP arena. The principles of the EU biosimilars regulatory pathway have been adopted by many other highly regulated jurisdictions, sometimes with minor variations. A similar approach could occur in future ATMPs, provided there is a legal basis and sufficient regulatory alignment. In this context, the EMA’s proactive role in facilitating solutions that increase patient access to ATMPs within the current regulatory framework may provide a basis for other jurisdictions. Given the global nature of ATMP development, international regulatory convergence and greater reliance among regulatory agencies could reduce duplication, accelerate access, and expand therapeutic options worldwide. Notably, international harmonization will be most achievable once at least one functioning regulatory framework has produced a concrete model on which convergence efforts can be built.

Conclusions

As ATMPs reach the end of regulatory market exclusivity and begin to move beyond niche indications, wider patient access is expected. Scientific feasibility is a necessary but insufficient condition for viable ATMP follow-on development. Developers must also navigate small or shrinking patient pools, high and often inflexible manufacturing costs, delivery complexity, and heterogeneous reimbursement and intellectual property landscapes, all of which fundamentally shape commercial attractiveness. Within this landscape, a spectrum of alternative regulatory routes and ATMP-specific follow-on models offer potential options to leverage existing data and experience, particularly for ultrarare indications.

Lessons from other complex biologicals highlight that a one-size-fits-all approach is not feasible and that ultimately, a sustainable ATMP follow-on ecosystem will require enabling conditions beyond regulation alone. Achieving an appropriate balance between scientific rigor and practical and economic considerations will be essential to translating the transformative potential of ATMPs into sustainable and equitable patient access.
