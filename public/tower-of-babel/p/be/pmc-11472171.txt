Fifty Years of NIA Research: A New Journal’s Perspective
Authors: Steven M Albert, Karen J Jung, Jennifer Tehan Stanley
Published: 2024

A recent tribute to the first 50 years of the National Institute of Aging (NIA), a division of the National Institutes of Health (NIH), ends with a ringing call to extend aging science: “As we embark on the next 50 years, we look forward to … welcoming a new generation of investigators from the full breadth of scientific disciplines, geriatricians and other aging-focused clinical providers, and others to an aging research career. Individuals who are interested in these fields are in the right place at the right time!” (Kelley et al., 2024). NIA has amply fulfilled its mission “to conduct genetic, biological, clinical, behavioral, social, and economic research on aging” to reduce the burden of diseases and conditions associated with growing older.

In 2022, NIA congressional appropriations approached $4.5B (Kelley et al., 2024). These funds support a robust intramural and extramural research program and a series of centers that have anchored aging science solidly in academic medicine, allied and public health, and the social and behavioral sciences. No one can deny the supercharging of aging research made possible by NIA’s support for the 37 Alzheimer’s Disease Research Centers, 8 Nathan Shock Centers of Excellence, 15 Edward R. Roybal Centers for Translational Research, 15 Claude D. Pepper Older Americans Independence Centers, 15 Centers on the Demography and Economics of Aging and AD/ADRD, and 18 Resource Centers for Minority Aging Research.

How has this effort affected a new journal devoted to publishing “innovative, conceptually sound, methodologically rigorous research on aging and the life course that has high potential for translating scientific knowledge to improve older adults’ health, functioning, and well-being?” Such is the charge of Innovation in Aging (Albert, 2021). Like NIA, Innovation in Aging seeks research that draws on multiple disciplines to solve problems that stand in the way of optimal aging. As a small and newer journal (launched in January 2017), we may have an advantage over NIA in our ability to take risks and push the boundaries of aging research. As we have written, Innovation in Aging’s focus on translational significance forces authors to consider the implications of their research for changing some components of aging “to enhance our ability to address a challenge posed by aging bodies, minds, relationships, and societies.” Innovation in Aging requires each article to make a clear statement of translational significance, which is published as part of the article beneath the abstract.

How does NIA affect such a journal? NIA is far and away our biggest funder. Of the first 500 papers published in Innovation in Aging, 83% reported having at least one funding source. Of 254 articles reporting one funding source, 31% reported funding from NIA, and with each year this proportion has increased. In fact, this number likely underestimates the impact of NIA, because authors may simply report NIH funding without specifying an Institute or indicating a Center that is clearly NIA-funded but without full attribution. The breakdown of other funding sources includes NIH or other NIH agencies (14%), internal departmental/institutional grants (12%), government or government-affiliated agencies (25%), and others (e.g., nonprofit organizations, private funding) (18%). For articles reporting more than two funding sources, 47% included funding by the NIA.

Another way to assess the impact of NIA on a journal is to examine its most highly cited and most often read papers. Of the nine distinct papers meeting these criteria (as of September 2024), a third reported NIA funding. This number would likely be higher but for the large number of international investigators who draw on funding from home countries and the inclusion of systematic reviews that do not require external funding.

Finally, the impact of NIA can be seen in the proportion of journal associate editors who have had NIA funding across their careers. A quick look at NIH Reporter (https://reporter.nih.gov/) suggests about two thirds have had such funding. Again, this number would likely be higher if we exclude editors with international affiliations, an important and growing element in journal leadership.

NIA is central to gerontology and to the journals that seek to publish the best research in aging. Innovation in Aging is positioned well to disseminate this research. Topics that NIA leadership has identified as cutting edge in our discipline, such as assessing healthspan, defining the aging exposome, advancing behavior change for individuals and organizations, developing data science resources for aging, and building a more diverse aging workforce, are all well represented in the journal. Innovation in Aging looks forward to the next 50 years of NIA investment.
