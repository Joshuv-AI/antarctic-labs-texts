Expression of HSP70 in Human Skin After Cryolipolysis Treatment
Authors: W Grant Stevens, Justin L Perez, Linda D Pham, Joel N Jimenez Lozano
Published: 2023

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundCryolipolysis nonsurgically targets and reduces subcutaneous fat through controlled cooling of skin and underlying fatty tissue. Although skin changes after cryolipolysis treatment have been observed clinically, the mechanisms by which these occur are not well understood.ObjectivesThe aim of this study was to investigate the expression of heat shock protein 70 (HSP70) in the epidermal and dermal layers of human skin following cryolipolysis treatment.MethodsSubjects (N = 11; average age, 41.8 years; average BMI, 29.59 kg/m2) were recruited to receive cryolipolysis treatment with a vacuum cooling cup applicator (−11°C/35 minutes) prior to abdominoplasty surgery. Treated and untreated abdominal tissue samples were harvested immediately after surgery (average follow-up, 15 days; range, 3 days to 5 weeks). Immunohistochemistry for HSP70 was performed on all samples. Slides were digitized and quantified in epidermal and dermal layers.ResultsThere was higher epidermal and dermal HSP70 expression in cryolipolysis-treated pre-abdominoplasty samples vs untreated samples. There was a 1.32-fold increase of HSP70 expression in the epidermis (P < .05) and a 1.92-fold increase in the dermis (P < .04) compared with untreated samples.ConclusionsWe found significant induction of HSP70 after cryolipolysis treatment in epidermal and dermal layers. HSP70 has potential therapeutic benefits and is recognized to have a role in skin protection and adaption after thermal stress. Although cryolipolysis is popular for subcutaneous fat reduction, cryolipolytic HSP induction in the skin may prove valuable for additional therapeutic applications, including skin wound healing, remodeling, rejuvenation, and photoprotection.Level of Evidence: 4

Noninvasive fat reduction has gained popularity with aesthetic patients. Among the many available noninvasive technologies, cryolipolysis uniquely targets subcutaneous fat through controlled cooling. During treatment, skin is in a supercooled, nonfrozen state for the length of the treatment.

The effects of cryolipolysis treatments on the skin are not fully understood.1 Our feasibility study showed, by means of molecular techniques and immunohistochemistry (IHC) quantification, the first evidence of dermal remodeling following cryolipolysis. Molecular analysis revealed a steady upregulation of dermal mRNA collagens following treatment. IHC staining showed an increase in procollagen type I, tropoelastin, transforming growth factor-β, and heat shock protein 47 (HSP47).2

The present study further explores induction of heat shock protein 70 (HSP70) after cryolipolysis. It is known that cold or heat exposure on diverse cells can induce the expression of HSPs. HSP47 is recognized as a collagen-specific chaperone, as it sets procollagen filaments in the correct conformation to make chains and is a valuable marker of active skin fibroblasts.3 As shown in our previous study, the higher dermal expression of HSP47 in treated samples permitted identification of active fibroblasts and HSP47 participating in collagen synthesis.2

Another HSP of interest is HSP70, which has been linked to proper folding and transport of newly synthesized proteins, anti-inflammatory processes, and protection against photodamage.4–7 HSP70 is known to be expressed by keratinocytes, melanocytes, fibroblasts, and adipocytes.8–11

Our objective was to study HSP70 response after cryolipolysis treatment in the epidermal and dermal layers of human skin.

METHODS

This feasibility study was multicenter, prospective, open-label, and nonrandomized. The protocol was approved by an independent review board (Salus IRB, Austin, TX). Eligible subjects were male or female, at least 18 years of age, with a scheduled surgical removal of abdominal fat and skin. All subjects provided written informed consent prior to participating in the study.

Abdominal standard cryolipolysis treatments were performed in the study subjects with a commercially available cryolipolysis system (CoolSculpting; ZELTIQ Aesthetics, Pleasanton, CA), applying 1 treatment cycle (−11°C for 35 minutes) per treatment site delivered by a cooled cup vacuum applicator (CoolAdvantage applicator). Cryolipolysis was delivered to one side of the lower abdomen while the contralateral side served as the untreated control. At the end of the treatment cycle, the applicator was removed, and a 2-minute manual massage was given in the area. Whole-tissue abdominoplasty samples were taken from each subject, and consisted of 1 treated and 1 untreated control sample, approximately 1 × 1 × 1 inch3 through the skin and fat.

For this study, additional subjects after the study presented in Stevens et al were recruited for HSP70 investigation; the total numbers of samples and time points are shown in Table 1.2 The 11 enrolled study subjects were female with an average age of 41.8 years and an average BMI of 29.5 kg/m2. Posttreatment time points ranged from 3 days to 5 weeks (average, 15 days).

HSP70 IHC was performed with an automated Leica Bond immunostainer and a rabbit-host anti-HSP70 antibody (Cell Signaling #4872, lot 4) at 1:200 dilution. Samples were stained along with a human skin-positive control and a no-primary-negative control. Leica Bond Epitope Retrieval Buffer 2 (pH 9.0, EDTA buffer) was used for 20 minutes to perform heat-induced antigen retrieval. Novolink Protein Block was used for 10 minutes to block nonspecific antibody binding. The primary antibody was applied for 1 hour. For 30 minutes, Novolink Polymer (Leica, lot 6058461, catalog RE7280-CE) or anti–rabbit poly-HRP-IgG antibody was applied. Novolink Peroxide Block (Leica, lot 6058461, catalog RE7280-CE) was used for 10 minutes to block endogenous peroxidase. For visualization, a hematoxylin nuclear counterstain (blue) was used and detection of anti-HSP70 antibody was visualized with 3,3′-diaminobenzidine (brown).

Bright-field whole-slide images were captured with a digital slide scanner (Panoramic SCAN; 3DHistech, Budapest, Hungary). Automated image analysis was carried out with integrated management software (imageDx; Reveal Biosciences, San Diego, CA).

HSP70 was quantified in all samples from digital images by utilizing imageDx to automatically detect biomarker positivity in epidermis and dermis (Figure 1). Statistical analysis of quantified data was determined with a standard 2-tailed paired t test for means.

Whole slides were scanned, and digital images were then processed to identify tissue and regions of interest, and to classify areas stained by the biomarker. Biomarker positivity was then quantified separately in epidermal and dermal regions.

RESULTS

Five representative samples of treated and untreated tissue are shown in Figure 2 with IHC staining for HSP70. The IHC brown staining indicates positive 3,3′-diaminobenzidine, and the blue counterstain shows nuclear hematoxylin. Higher expression of HSP70 was detected in epidermal and dermal layers in all treated samples compared with the untreated controls. Based upon the histological digital image analysis, quantification identified HSP70 skin changes that showed a 1.32-fold increase (P < .049) in the epidermis and a 1.92-fold increase (P < .04) in the dermis, as illustrated in Figure 3.

DISCUSSION

HSPs are found in all living organisms within the nucleus, mitochondria, endoplasmic reticulum, and cytosol of cells. Many of these HSPs have essential expression, although expression can be induced in others by heat shock and other types of stress, such as toxic compound exposure, oxidizing conditions, and pathological and physiological processes. HSPs belong to a multigene family and are commonly named according to their molecular size, which ranges from 8 to 150 kDa. For example, the 70-kDa protein is referred to as HSP70. HSPs are rapidly induced by way of transcription (mRNA production from DNA occurring in minutes) and translation (protein production from mRNA) mechanisms.

The precise functions of proteins in the HSP70 family have not been fully characterized. There is a profound degree of conservation of these proteins across species. This, coupled with their importance in cell survival, suggests that HSPs are critical for both normal cellular function and survival after stress.12 The production of HSP70 is associated with development of tolerance to different kinds of stresses, including hypoxia, ischemia, acidosis, energy depletion, cytokines (such as tumor necrosis factor-α), and ultraviolet radiation.

The specific functioning of HSPs as chaperones lies in their ability to act in response to thermal stress by supporting denatured proteins within the cell to refold to their native, functional conformations, thereby re-establishing the homeostasis of the cell environment. In concurrence with the role of HSP70 in protein refolding, there are various mechanisms by which they can save cells from thermal shock.13 HSP70 is involved in guiding severely denatured proteins for degradation over the proteasomal pathway and interference in cell death by downregulating apoptotic signals within the cell through its interaction with BAX (a proapoptotic protein).

The cytoprotective functions of many HSPs are known, including those in the HSP70 family, and include protein folding and unfolding, resisting protein denaturation, the maintenance of structural proteins, the transport of proteins across membranes and into intracellular compartments, as well as the degradation of unstable proteins.14,15,16

The expression of HSPs in human skin occurs due to cellular stress conditions, such as heat, light, and healing. The basal or constitutive expression of HSP70 is thought to reflect its physiological significance in these cells, which are subjected to various environmental stressors, needing the continuous protective activity of HSP70.

HSP70 may provide therapeutic benefit by functioning to help in skin adaptation after thermal insult and providing skin protection. The expression of HSP70 within the epidermis and dermis is indicative of newly synthesized proteins, anti-inflammatory processes, and photodamage safeguard. The upregulation of HSP70 following cryolipolysis is consistent with our previous study showing steady upregulation of skin dermal collagens after treatment with increased procollagen type I, tropoelastin, transforming growth factor-β, and HSP47.2 Although cryolipolysis is popular for subcutaneous fat reduction, HSP induction in the skin may have additional aesthetic implications, including repair, remodeling, rejuvenation, and photoprotection. The results of the current feasibility study are limited by the small subject/sample size and the single location (abdominal skin). A more comprehensive study should be carried out to characterize the time response after treatment by including shorter and longer time points, as well as other skin anatomic locations. The identification of specific cell(s) expressing HSP70 was not investigated and should be assessed in future studies. For this study, we decided to focus our analysis on the skin and highlight these new findings as an addition to our previous publication about the skin. We are preparing a separate, more comprehensive analysis to assess changes of adipose tissue after cryolipolysis, covering topics such as programmed cell death, inflammatory response, and extracellular matrix remodeling. We cannot specify if HSP70 in the skin has a direct correlation to observed clinical differences in fat reduction across subjects because our feasibility study is limited by small sample size and pre-abdominoplasty subjects are not followed for fat layer reduction assessment.

CONCLUSIONS

This study uncovered significant production of HSP70 in the skin following cryolipolysis. Digital image analysis quantified changes in HSP70 IHC within the epidermal and dermal layers, and showed that treated samples had a 1.32-fold increase in the epidermis and a 1.92-fold increase in the dermis. HSP70 appears to be involved in extracellular matrix remodeling by supporting the production and assemblage of structural elements such as collagen. Further investigation of specific mechanisms and additional traits of cryolipolytic HSP induction are warranted across a larger patient population.
