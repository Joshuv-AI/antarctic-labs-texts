Recurrent Pericarditis in a Rugby Player
Authors: Abdallah Abuawad, Osamah Badwan, Muhammad Ehsan, Chadi Thabaja, Faysal Massad, Muhannad Mahmoud, Besir Besir, Abdelrahman Ahmed, Jibran Ikram, Tom Kai Ming Wang, Allan Klein
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundRecurrent pericarditis in young athletes is challenging, as relapse risk, treatment duration, and return to play must be balanced.Case SummaryA 26-year-old rugby player developed recurrent pericarditis after atrial septal defect repair. At initial presentation, she had moderate pericardial late gadolinium enhancement on cardiac magnetic resonance (CMR) and a high pericarditis risk score, and she achieved clinical remission on rilonacept. At taper initiation, she was asymptomatic with normal inflammatory biomarkers yet showed mild residual late gadolinium enhancement on CMR. After tapering of rilonacept, she relapsed 11 months later but responded rapidly to reinitiation of therapy.DiscussionThis case highlights the challenges of recurrent pericarditis in a high-risk athlete and discusses athlete-specific considerations, including exercise restriction and individualized return to activity. It also suggests that serial CMR and clinical risk scores may provide supportive context for cautious tapering, although evidence remains limited.Take-Home MessagesThis case demonstrates the complexity of recurrent pericarditis management in a high-risk athlete despite clinical and CMR improvement. Serial CMR and clinical risk scores may provide supportive, hypothesis-generating context for individualized care and cautious tapering.Key words: athletes, cardiac MRI, interleukin-1 blockade, late gadolinium enhancement, recurrent pericarditis, rilonacept

History of Presentation

A 26-year-old female Olympic rugby player presented 2 days after robotic atrial septal defect (ASD) closure with severe, pleuritic, midsternal chest pain radiating to the left arm, exacerbated by exertion and relieved by leaning forward. She was afebrile, hemodynamically stable, and euvolemic. Cardiac examination showed normal S1/S2 without murmurs, rubs, or gallops.

Past Medical History

The patient had a history of secundum ASD (repaired in 2019), pulmonary embolism and left renal infarction after anterior cruciate ligament repair while taking oral contraceptives, and childhood urticaria.

Differential Diagnosis

Considerations included pericarditis, pulmonary emboli, acute coronary syndrome, pneumonia, costochondritis, and postoperative incisional pain.

Investigations

At initial presentation, inflammatory markers were markedly elevated, with C-reactive protein (CRP) 24.3 mg/dL (normal range: <0.9 mg/dL), and erythrocyte sedimentation rate (ESR) 87 mm/h (normal range: <20 mm/h). Electrocardiogram showed sinus rhythm. Chest computed tomography excluded pulmonary embolism but revealed a sterile right pleural effusion requiring drainage. Transthoracic echocardiography (TTE) demonstrated a trivial pericardial effusion. She improved on naproxen and colchicine.

Six months later, while training with the Olympic team, she developed recurrent pleuritic chest pain with an elevated ESR of 38 mm/h. The TTE was normal. She was treated with a prednisone taper starting with 20 mg and 0.6 mg colchicine twice daily for 6 months.

Nine months later, she presented with pleuritic chest pain and an elevated CRP of 10.4 mg/dL. The electrocardiogram showed sinus bradycardia at 53 beats/min; the TTE was unrevealing. She required treatment with prednisone and nonsteroidal anti-inflammatory drugs (NSAIDs) followed by maintenance colchicine.

Approximately 2.5 years after index presentation, while taking colchicine 0.6 mg daily, she experienced another episode of pleuritic chest pain with a CRP of 11.7 mg/dL. Her treatment was intensified with 0.6 mg colchicine twice daily, NSAIDs, and a prednisone taper.

Several months before referral, rilonacept had been initiated at an outside institution but was discontinued after approximately 2 months because of concern for urticaria. Subsequent rilonacept challenge testing did not confirm hypersensitivity.

At referral to a tertiary pericardial center, she was asymptomatic with borderline normal inflammatory biomarkers, including an ultrasensitive CRP of 1.5 mg/L and an ESR of 2 mm/h. CMR demonstrated circumferential moderate pericardial late-gadolinium enhancement (LGE) and the absence of pericardial edema on T2–short-tau inversion recovery (STIR) sequence (Figure 1A). Her pericarditis risk score for prediction of pericarditis recurrence was 10, placing her in the high-risk category for recurrence.1 Rilonacept was started, and she was advised to refrain from strenuous activity, consistent with American College of Cardiology (ACC) guidance on exercise restriction during active pericarditis.2 Although she was clinically quiescent at referral, this threshold was applied as a precautionary measure given her high-risk profile and persistent moderate pericardial LGE on CMR.

One year after rilonacept initiation, the patient reported stable symptoms, with normal inflammatory markers. CMR still showed residual moderate LGE, largely unchanged from her prior CMR (Figure 1B). She was instructed to begin initially with light activity, followed by gradual progression based on symptoms, clinical status, and tolerance, with close monitoring. During treatment with rilonacept, she was able to resume recreational pickleball as part of this staged return to activity.

At the 26-month follow-up after rilonacept initiation, the patient remained asymptomatic, with normal inflammatory markers. CMR demonstrated interval improvement, with mild residual pericardial LGE (Figure 1C).

After over 2 years of clinical stability and shared decision-making with the patient, rilonacept was tapered slowly over 4 months and discontinued, with daily colchicine 0.6 mg initiated as maintenance therapy. After 6 months of stopping rilonacept, the patient remained without symptoms, with normal inflammatory markers. CMR showed mild residual circumferential pericardial LGE (Figure 1D).

After 11 months of stopping rilonacept, after a viral upper respiratory infection and resumption of high-intensity training, she developed pleuritic chest pain and an elevated ultrasensitive CRP of 42 mg/L. Rilonacept was restarted with weekly dosing, resulting in symptom resolution within 1 week, with tapering of ibuprofen and colchicine.

Management

The patient's early course was managed with NSAIDs, corticosteroids, and colchicine. After 3 recurrences, rilonacept was initiated with sustained response. Therapy was tapered almost 2.5 years after prolonged remission and improvement in LGE on CMR (Figure 1C). After almost 11 months of stopping rilonacept, the patient developed a recurrence of the pericarditis, rilonacept was restarted, and exercise restrictions were reinstated, her symptoms abated, and inflammatory markers improved rapidly.

Outcome and Follow-Up

At virtual follow-up after 2 months of restarting rilonacept, she remained asymptomatic with normal inflammatory markers. Plans were made for CMR at 6-month intervals to help guide future tapering decisions based on LGE resolution. Discussion

After undergoing robotic ASD repair, our patient developed postcardiac injury syndrome pericarditis, which is an auto-inflammatory pericardial condition that occurs in almost 20% of patients who undergo adult congenital heart disease repair.3

Rilonacept and the IL-1 pathway

Recurrent pericarditis is increasingly recognized as an IL-1–mediated inflammatory syndrome, and rilonacept is an established therapy for corticosteroid-dependent or recurrent disease.4 However, the optimal duration of IL-1 blockade and the appropriate approach to discontinuation remain uncertain.2

Risk stratification for recurrent pericarditis

Risk stratification tools may provide useful clinical context in recurrent pericarditis, but their limitations should be acknowledged.1 In particular, the Pericarditis Risk Score–Klein score may classify some patients as higher risk on the basis of variables that are common in routine practice, including female sex, colchicine use, heart rate, and preserved left ventricular function, which may reduce its discriminatory value in individual cases.1 In our patient, the score was not used in isolation to determine management. It provided supportive context alongside the recurrent clinical course, inflammatory markers, serial CMR findings, and athletic considerations. Management decisions remained grounded in the overall clinical picture, with the score serving primarily to reinforce a cautious approach to tapering and follow-up rather than to determine therapy duration.

Role of CMR and LGE

CMR is valuable in recurrent pericarditis, but the biological meaning of pericardial LGE requires careful interpretation. Pericardial LGE severity was described qualitatively according to the 2025 ACC concise clinical guidance framework, based on circumferential extent, enhancement thickness, and the number of involved basal, mid, and apical short-axis levels.2 T2-STIR images primarily reflect edema, whereas pericardial LGE is less specific and may reflect hyperemia, increased vascular permeability, granulation tissue, chronic inflammation, or evolving fibrosis.5 Baseline severity of LGE has been linked to higher recurrence rates and prolonged disease activity.6, 7, 8 Notably, a study by Cremer et al6 reported that even patients with none or trace LGE after approximately 18 months of rilonacept could experience recurrence after drug withdrawal, indicating that improvement or near-resolution of LGE alone does not guarantee durable remission after treatment cessation.8

In our patient, at referral, T2-STIR was negative and baseline CMR showed moderate circumferential LGE, which improved to mild residual LGE on follow-up imaging. The absence of T2-STIR edema at referral may have been influenced, at least in part, by antecedent anti-inflammatory therapy, including rilonacept, prednisone, colchicine, and NSAIDs, which may have partially suppressed active edema despite persistent LGE. That uncertainty likely influenced the decision to taper therapy, because persistent LGE without T2-STIR abnormality was interpreted as suggestive of residual inflammatory activity. Recurrence after discontinuation therefore suggests that persistent imaging abnormalities may mark incomplete disease resolution in our high-risk patient. This case should be interpreted alongside the Cremer RHAPSODY substudy, which was hypothesis-generating and underscored the uncertainty surrounding the relationship between pericardial LGE and recurrence after IL-1 blockade withdrawal.

Athlete-specific considerations

Athletes are particularly vulnerable to relapses. High heart rates, mechanical stress, and adrenergic surges can exacerbate latent inflammation.9 Contemporary expert guidance supports exercise restriction during active disease, followed by a graduated and individualized return to activity once symptoms improve, inflammatory markers normalize, and cardiac evaluation is reassuring.2,10 The 2025 ACC concise clinical guidance and ESC 202511 recommends exercise restriction for at least 1 month with a maximal heart rate <100 beats/min during active pericarditis until clinical remission.2 In our patient, although she was clinically quiescent at referral, this heart rate threshold was adopted as a precautionary measure given her high-risk profile, recurrent disease course, and persistent moderate pericardial LGE on CMR. AHA/ACC 2025 scientific statement recommends avoidance of exercise during active pericarditis and return to sport only after complete resolution of active disease and normalization of inflammatory markers, with reassessment before return in more severe cases.12 Progression is ideally guided by symptoms, tolerance, and physiologic parameters such as heart rate or perceived exertion, with shared decision-making tailored to the athlete's sport and goals. In our patient, the return-to-play strategy was therefore intended as an individualized, consensus-informed approach rather than a disease-specific validated protocol. During treatment with rilonacept, she underwent a staged return to activity, progressing from rest to gradual increases in intensity as tolerated. The patient was able to resume recreational pickleball within this staged reconditioning plan.

Conclusions

This case highlights the complexity of recurrent pericarditis in a high-risk athlete. Recurrence after discontinuation of prolonged IL-1 blockade despite clinical and CMR improvement underscores the challenges of assessing disease activity. Serial CMR and clinical risk scores may offer supportive, hypothesis-generating context for individualized care and cautious tapering of rilonacept.
