Nanocarriers for targeted drug delivery in the vascular system: focus on endothelium
Authors: Xiuxiu Cong, Zebin Zhang, He Li, Yong-Guang Yang, Yuning Zhang, Tianmeng Sun
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
Endothelial cells (ECs) are pivotal in maintaining vascular health, regulating hemodynamics, and modulating inflammatory responses. Nanocarriers hold transformative potential for precise drug delivery within the vascular system, particularly targeting ECs for therapeutic purposes. However, the complex interactions between vascular ECs and nanocarriers present significant challenges for the development and clinical translation of nanotherapeutics. This review assesses recent advancements and key strategies in employing nanocarriers for drug delivery to vascular ECs. It suggested that through precise physicochemical design and surface modifications, nanocarriers can enhance targeting specificity and improve drug internalization efficiency in ECs. Additionally, we elaborated on the applications of nanocarriers specifically designed for targeting ECs in the treatment of cardiovascular diseases, cancer metastasis, and inflammatory disorders. Despite these advancements, safety concerns, the complexity of in vivo processes, and the challenge of achieving subcellular drug delivery remain significant obstacles to the effective targeting of ECs with nanocarriers. A comprehensive understanding of endothelial cell biology and its interaction with nanocarriers is crucial for realizing the full potential of targeted drug delivery systems.Graphical Abstract

Introduction

Nanocarriers have become crucial in modern pharmacotherapy due to their efficient drug delivery, targeted specificity, and controlled release properties [1–3]. Recent advancements in nanocarrier technology have introduced novel strategies to improve both the efficacy and safety of conventional drugs [4–6]. Despite these benefits, challenges remain regarding the controlled distribution and metabolism of nanodrugs within the body [7]. A major issue is the rapid clearance of nanocarriers from the bloodstream, which impedes the maintenance of effective drug concentrations at target tissues [8, 9]. Vascular ECs are crucial in this process, as they influence not only the circulation time of nanocarriers in the bloodstream but also their distribution within tissues [10, 11].

ECs, as integral components of the vascular wall, are responsible for regulating blood flow, vascular permeability, and inflammatory responses [12–14]. In various diseases, including atherosclerosis and cancer, endothelial dysfunction is pivotal, rendering these cells crucial targets or key factors influencing drug efficacy [15–17]. Recent studies indicate that for most nanocarriers, ECs located outside the target tissues are primarily responsible for their systemic clearance [18, 19]. In healthy vasculature, ECs, through interactions with macrophages and the reticuloendothelial system, facilitate the rapid clearance of nanocarriers from the bloodstream [3, 17]. This clearance mechanism restricts the precise accumulation of nanodrugs in target tissues, reducing their therapeutic concentrations and ultimately impacting clinical outcomes [17]. Therefore, optimizing the pharmacokinetics of nanocarriers requires minimizing their interactions with ECs in healthy vessels to extend circulation time and enhance delivery to target sites.

Conversely, in diseased tissues, ECs can facilitate the accumulation of nanocarriers, thereby enhancing therapeutic outcomes [11, 20]. For instance, in tumor therapy, it is traditionally believed that tumor ECs exhibit abnormal permeability, known as the enhanced permeability and retention effect (EPR), which facilitates nanocarriers’ entry into the tumor microenvironment [20, 21]. However, the large size of nanocarriers and the structural characteristics of tumors may hinder their diffusion and penetration, resulting in suboptimal therapeutic efficacy [21, 22]. Through investigations into the interactions between nanocarriers and tumor ECs, Zhou et al. proposed a novel method to enhance drug delivery efficiency based on the transcytosis mechanism. Leveraging the high expression of gamma-glutamyl transferase (GGT) on tumor ECs and adjacent tumor cells, they developed a strategy involving GGT-responsive polymer nanocarriers, converting passive diffusion into active transport [22, 23]. This approach enhanced the interaction between nanocarriers and tumor ECs, leading to pronounced antitumor effects.

In the case of vascular diseases, it is crucial to enhance the interaction between nanocarriers and diseased ECs in order to promote treatment outcomes [24, 25]. By specifically targeting molecular markers on the surface of ECs, nanocarriers can selectively accumulate in pathological tissues thereby enhancing drug concentration while minimizing side effects in non-target tissues [26]. In cardiovascular diseases, including atherosclerosis and hypertension, endothelial dysfunction is closely associated with disease progression, leading to notable changes in the expression levels of various molecules on EC surfaces [27, 28]. These alterations present potential therapeutic targets. The design of nanocarriers targeting ECs can be optimized by adjusting properties such as shape, size, and ligand density to improve adhesion and penetration, thereby enhancing therapeutic efficacy [10, 29–32]. Therefore, understanding the interaction patterns between nanocarriers and ECs, along with the development of corresponding regulatory strategies, is essential for enhancing the therapeutic effects of nanodrugs.

Upon entering the vascular system, nanocarriers can interact with ECs, potentially affecting drug delivery efficiency and inducing cellular stress responses [33, 34]. In certain instances, nanocarriers may exhibit toxicity towards vascular ECs, resulting in acute or chronic inflammation [34, 35]. For example, certain metal nanocarriers, such as silver and zinc oxide nanoparticles, or cationic polymeric materials like polyethyleneimine (PEI), have been reported to induce oxidative stress in ECs at elevated concentrations or prolonged exposure, leading to membrane damage and apoptosis [20, 35–38]. This cellular damage not only compromises vascular integrity but also triggers a cascade of inflammatory responses, further impairing disease progression [11]. To mitigate these issues, researchers are developing several strategies to reduce the toxicity of nanocarriers towards ECs. One approach involves surface modification to minimize direct contact between nanocarriers and ECs, thereby reducing toxic reactions. For instance, polyethylene glycol (PEG) modification can significantly enhance the biocompatibility of nanocarriers, thereby reducing interactions with ECs [39–41]. Additionally, the development of controlled-release nanocarriers that gradually release drugs to minimize immediate impact on cells represents another strategy to reduce toxicity [39].

Investigating the pivotal role of ECs in nanocarriers and their targeting strategies offers substantial research value for enhancing the therapeutic efficacy and safety of nanodrugs. Effective targeting of ECs with nanocarriers necessitates a comprehensive understanding of the complex mechanisms of endothelial uptake, identification of factors influencing interactions between nanocarriers and ECs, and the implementation of strategies to enhance specificity and efficiency. This review seeks to summarize and analyze recent advancements in endothelial cell-targeted drug delivery using nanocarriers, discussing design strategies, challenges, and future directions. Furthermore, it will provide comprehensive insights and references to facilitate the understanding of interactions between nanocarriers and ECs, as well as the development of regulatory methods and strategies.

Nanocarriers in drug delivery

The emergence of nanomedicine has profoundly impacted the field of precision medicine [2, 42]. Nanoparticle-based drug delivery systems facilitate the targeted delivery of therapeutic agents to specific cells or tissues, thereby improving drug bioavailability and therapeutic efficacy [2]. Numerous types of nanocarriers have been developed for drug delivery, including polymeric nanoparticles, inorganic nanoparticles, and lipid-based nanoparticles [18]. Recent advancements in nanotechnology are focused on overcoming four major biological barriers: pulmonary mucus, gastrointestinal mucus, the placental barrier, and the blood–brain barrier (BBB) (Fig. 1) [12]. Nanocarriers are proficient at surmounting these biological obstacles, thereby enabling efficient drug delivery to targeted sites, which is essential for achieving optimal therapeutic outcomes [1, 43]. For example, liposomes and polymeric nanoparticles are capable of crossing the blood–brain barrier to deliver drugs directly to brain tissue, providing promising therapeutic strategies for neurodegenerative disorders, such as Alzheimer’s disease [44].Fig. 1Successful strategies of nanotechnology to overcome biological obstacles. Nanocarriers exhibit significant potential in drug development, cancer therapy, gene therapy, diagnostic imaging, and vaccine development, thus becoming a central focus of biomedical research [45–47]. The nanocarriers composed of alginate and chitosan, demonstrate superior biocompatibility and biodegradability [48]. They are especially effective in delivering anticancer agents, such as paclitaxel and gemcitabine [48]. Solid lipid nanocarriers and nanostructured lipid carriers improve therapeutic delivery in skin cancer treatments, enhancing systemic bioavailability and site-specific targeting [43]. Additionally, Zhao et al. proposed a smart spatiotemporal drug release system utilizing multifunctional nanocarriers for synergistic chemotherapy and photothermal therapy of melanoma, which significantly improved tumor imaging and photothermal treatment efficacy in vivo [49]. Furthermore, nanomedicine-based drug delivery systems possess substantial potential in gene therapy [2, 50, 51]. These advanced systems are capable of delivering nucleic acids, including DNA and RNA, to targeted tissues and cells, while ensuring both safety and efficacy [18]. The development of nanomedicine-based drug delivery systems provides unparalleled opportunities for precise and effective gene therapies, advancing novel approaches for treating genetic disorders, particular malignancies, and viral infections [4, 52].

Physiological characteristics and functions of ECs

The general structure of blood vessels

Blood vessels function as conduits that transport blood, nutrients, and oxygen throughout the body [60]. They consist of three layers: the innermost layer, known as the tunica intima (consisting of connective tissue and ECs), the middle layer, called the tunica media (containing smooth muscle cells), and the outermost layer, termed the tunica adventitia (composed of fibroblasts, collagen, and elastic fibers) (Fig. 2A) [60, 61]. The vascular endothelium consists of a uniform, continuous monolayer of tightly packed ECs, forming a cobblestone-like structure. It covers approximately 5,000 square meters, making it one of the largest organs in the human body (Fig. 2B) [9, 24, 60, 62]. Beyond acting as a selective barrier between blood and tissues, ECs regulate vascular permeability, maintain hemodynamic balance, participate in blood coagulation, modulate vascular tone, promote angiogenesis, and mediate immune responses [24, 50]. The endothelium under physiologic conditions

ECs are vital for maintaining vascular health. Under physiological conditions, they establish cell–cell junctions via molecules such as CD31 (PECAM-1) and VE-cadherin which are essential for maintaining endothelial barrier function [63]. Healthy ECs maintain vascular homeostasis by expressing molecules such as thrombomodulin (TM) and endothelial nitric oxide synthase (eNOS), which modulate blood flow and permeability through endothelin [64]. eNOS is a crucial enzyme for the production of nitric oxide (NO), which is essential for vascular integrity and function [65]. eNOS facilitates vasodilation and inhibits platelet aggregation [64]. Additionally, vascular endothelial growth factor (VEGF) is a vital regulator of endothelial cell physiology, specifically targeting ECs to enhance their survival and proliferation (Fig. 2C) [9].

The endothelium under dysfunctional conditions

Pathological conditions significantly impact EC function [24]. Factors including turbulent blood flow, inflammation, oxidative stress, and dyslipidemia contribute to endothelial dysfunction [15, 66, 67]. The activation of VEGFR2 by VEGF induces the internalization and degradation of VE-cadherin, thereby impacting cell–cell junctions [63]. Reduced levels of NO and TM contribute to thrombus formation, disrupt blood flow, and can lead to inflammation and thrombosis [24]. Additionally, the upregulation of inflammation-related adhesion molecules, including P-selectin, E-selectin, intercellular cell adhesion molecule-1 (ICAM-1), and vascular cell adhesion molecule-1 (VCAM-1), on ECs facilitates leukocyte adhesion and transmigration across the vascular wall [68, 69]. This process contributes to plaque formation and reduces vascular integrity, thereby worsening the progression of vascular diseases (Fig. 2D) [24].

A thorough understanding of EC physiology in both health and disease is crucial for developing novel therapeutic strategies and targeting the various drivers of EC pathology [28, 70]. ECs express a range of surface molecules, the expression levels of which markedly alter in disease conditions, rendering them potential targets for therapeutic intervention [15, 16]. However, the high mobility of ECs and the rapid clearance of substances from the bloodstream present significant challenges to the effective interaction between delivery nanocarriers and ECs [11]. For example, in vascular-targeted drug delivery utilizing nanocarriers, researchers can exploit the specific characteristics and vulnerabilities of ECs in pathological states to design nanocarriers that selectively target these cells [14, 71]. This approach enables targeted delivery of therapeutics to pathological vascular sites, reducing systemic side effects.

Interaction between nanocarriers and ECs

Nanocarriers enhance drug solubility, biodistribution, and controlled release, leading to improved drug bioavailability and reduced side effects [3, 13, 39]. Additionally, engineered nanocarriers can target specific tissues, cells, and biomolecules with high precision [18, 30, 72]. This precision targeting has enabled applications in medical imaging, cell tracking, and in vivo biomarker identification, highlighting their potential in the biomedical field [4, 73, 74].

Upon intravenous administration, nanocarriers circulate through the bloodstream, targeting organs and tissues with dense vascular networks [2]. However, for nanocarriers to leave the bloodstream and enter the interstitial tissues, they must overcome the barrier posed by vascular ECs [3, 12]. The ability of nanocarriers to penetrate the endothelial monolayer depends on their physicochemical properties and the biological and physiological characteristics of the vascular system. Nanocarrier interactions with the endothelial layer can enhance drug delivery by expanding endothelial gaps [21]. However, these interactions also present challenges, including nanocarrier metabolism, toxicity, and unintended impacts on vascular permeability [9, 30, 75]. Therefore, understanding the mechanisms and effects of nanocarrier-endothelial cell interactions is crucial for developing effective therapeutic strategies.

Reduced interaction with healthy ECs

The interaction between nanocarriers and vascular ECs may initially appear straightforward. However, it is actually a complex process [17]. ECs play a crucial role in the systemic clearance of nanocarriers. This interaction significantly impacts their circulation, targeting, and therapeutic efficacy. Intravascular enzymes, including proteases and nucleases, degrade active ingredients, constituting the primary intravascular barrier [3]. Nano drugs also face an intravascular barrier due to sequestration by the mononuclear phagocytic system (MPS). The MPS includes phagocytic components, primarily resident macrophages in the liver (Kupffer cells) spleen (splenic macrophages), and monocytes in the bone marrow [76]. Upon entering the circulation, plasma proteins adsorb onto the surface of nanoparticles, forming protein coronas, a process referred to as “opsonization.” Opsonized nanoparticles are recognized by the MPS through specific receptor binding and are subsequently internalized by phagocytes (Fig. 3A) [3]. To extend circulation time, nanocarriers should be engineered to minimize interactions with ECs, thereby reducing clearance, avoiding blood flow obstruction, and preventing adverse effects from EC damage (Fig. 3B) [17]. PEGylated lamella nanomaterials (PLM) activate vascular ECs via L-type calcium ion channels. ECs generally facilitate the clearance of nanocarriers through interactions with macrophages and the reticuloendothelial system (RES) [79]. This interaction between ECs and the immune system leads to the rapid removal of nanocarriers from the bloodstream, limiting their effectiveness [79]. Hence, optimizing nanocarrier design to account for EC characteristics is essential for improving drug delivery efficiency.

In a healthy vascular system, the size of nanocarriers significantly influences their fate [30]. Nanocarriers smaller than 10 nm are usually cleared through renal excretion, whereas those between 20 and 200 nm are predominantly removed by the RES; and nanocarriers larger than 500 nm are generally captured by the microvascular system [30]. Enhanced interaction with diseased tissue ECs

Nanocarriers tend to accumulate in tumor and inflammatory lesions (Fig. 3C). Tumor ECs often exhibit abnormal permeability, known as the EPR effect, which increases nanocarrier penetration into the tumor microenvironment [22, 80]. Although the EPR effect partially facilitates drug accumulation, it is insufficient by itself for optimal drug delivery [81]. Recent studies have proposed endocytosis as a new mechanism for drug penetration, enhancing targeted drug delivery through transcellular transport by ECs [22, 81]. Additionally, functionalized nanocarriers, including those targeting tumor-associated antigens and lipid-based nanocarriers, have shown potential in improving drug delivery efficiency and increasing drug accumulation within tumors [24, 82].

Targeting diseased ECs

Notably, in diseases affecting ECs, the persistent interaction between nanocarriers and vascular ECs can significantly enhance drug distribution and targeting within these cells, thereby improving therapeutic outcomes. In cardiovascular diseases such as atherosclerosis and hypertension, endothelial dysfunction is often associated with disease progression, making ECs key therapeutic targets [15, 17]. The application of vascular-targeted polyphenol nanocarriers enhances drug permeability and retention in diseased blood vessels, thereby significantly improving drug bioavailability. Additionally, this approach has improved photodynamic therapy by augmenting the production of reactive oxygen species (ROS) and increasing the incidence of late-stage apoptosis [83]. Nanocarriers can be designed to specifically target these diseased ECs, thereby enhancing therapeutic efficacy (Fig. 3D) [17]. For example, targeting specific adhesion molecules on the surface of ECs, such as VCAM-1, can enable nanocarriers to selectively accumulate in diseased vessels and exert therapeutic effects [26, 29].

Safety considerations

Interactions between nanocarriers and ECs may also lead to potential adverse effects [84]. Excessive uptake by ECs can result in cytotoxicity or pro-inflammatory responses, particularly under prolonged or high-dose exposure conditions [35, 38]. Studies have demonstrated that smaller-sized SINPs (< 20 nm) can significantly induce apoptosis and necrosis in human ECs [85]. Additionally, exposure to copper oxide nanoparticles has been shown to disrupt mitochondrial dynamics in vascular ECs [38]. Zinc oxide nanoparticles have been demonstrated to induce oxidative stress across diverse cell types, such as ECs [37]. Oxidative stress induced by these nanocarriers can lead to cellular damage and apoptosis [26]. The toxicity of zinc-based nanoparticles is primarily due to the generation of ROS [26]. Furthermore, the biocompatibility of the nanocarriers and their metabolic pathways are critical for safety, necessitating comprehensive research and evaluation. Therefore, understanding the interactions between nanocarriers and ECs is critical for optimizing drug delivery systems and minimizing potential risks.

Mechanisms and strategies for targeting ECs with nanocarriers

Endothelial cell uptake pathways

EC uptake is a fundamental aspect of targeted drug delivery [86, 87]. Nanocarriers are internalized by ECs through several distinct endocytic pathways, such as clathrin-mediated endocytosis, caveolin-mediated endocytosis, phagocytosis, macropinocytosis, and non-clathrin/non-caveolin-mediated endocytosis [87]. The type of endocytosis undergone by nanocarriers affects their intracellular fate and is influenced by factors such as cell type, nanocarrier size, and receptor interactions [2, 88, 89]. Clathrin-mediated endocytosis is a predominant pathway for nanocarrier uptake in non-specialized mammalian cells [2]. This process depends on receptor-mediated, hydrophobic, or electrostatic interactions between the nanocarriers and clathrin-coated regions of the cell membrane [90–92]. Conversely, vesicle-mediated endocytosis is linked to the transport of smaller nanocarriers via lymphatic vesicles [93]. Phagocytosis and macropinocytosis are involved in processing larger nanostructures via initial endosomal pathways [94]. Non-clathrin-mediated endocytosis, however, can internalize unmodified small molecular drugs [95]. Additionally, the activation of these endocytic pathways is affected by the rigidity of nanocarriers [2]. Although findings may vary, stiffer nanocarriers are generally absorbed more readily. Both experimental and theoretical analyses suggest that rigid particles require less energy for endocytosis [96, 97]. Consequently, nanocarrier uptake is influenced by various factors, with the selection of endocytic pathway determined by the physicochemical properties of the nanocarriers, such as size, surface charge, and surface modifications, which also affect the subsequent endocytic process (Fig. 5A) [18, 86]. Subsequently, ECs internalize nanocarriers via these pathways, enabling their transport to different cellular compartments [29]. Research on nanocarrier design underscores the significance of comprehending uptake mechanisms and intracellular transport pathways to improve drug delivery efficiency. Similarly, Cheng et al. highlighted the essential role of blood flow and glycocalyx integrity in the intravenous delivery of nanocarriers, particularly in the context of cardiovascular disease therapy [98]. Moreover, external factors such as flow rate and shear stress considerably affect the efficacy of nanocarrier internalization [39, 99]. In specific disease contexts, such as overcoming the BBB, Moya et al. explored the interaction between Poly (lactic-co-glycolic acid) copolymer (PLGA) nanoparticles and human brain-like ECs, emphasizing the pivotal role of surface modifications in enhancing drug delivery [100].

Targeting mechanisms of nanocarriers

Nanocarriers, as critical tools for targeted drug delivery systems, involve complex mechanisms including their physicochemical properties, ligand-receptor interactions, and intracellular uptake pathways. These mechanisms encompass passive targeting, active targeting, and endogenous targeting, which can be utilized independently or in combination to modulate the accumulation sites of nanocarriers in vivo (Fig. 6) [18]. Determinants of endothelial targeting

Various molecules located on the surface of ECs present potential targets for therapeutic interventions [17, 29]. Targeting specific EC surface markers, such as cell adhesion molecules (CAMs) with monoclonal antibodies, can improve interactions between nanocarriers and endothelial surface molecules [29]. This enhancement facilitates the effective delivery and penetration of drugs into ECs. Unique clusters of molecules identified on the EC surface demonstrate notable variations across different disease states [15, 16, 27, 68]. These markers are not only specific to general endothelial characteristics but may also exhibit selectivity for various vascular beds, vessel types, or states of endothelial activation [16]. For example, in conditions such as lung injury and acute inflammation, the expression levels of these markers may increase, decrease, or remain relatively stable, thereby establishing a basis for selecting drug targets (refer to Table 2) [9, 10, 17, 30, 101–106]. Strategies for targeting ECs with nanocarriers

Recent advancements in nanotechnology have markedly improved the precision of drug delivery systems for targeting ECs within the vascular system [9, 30, 107]. Various nanocarrier platforms, such as liposomes, polymer nanoparticles, and inorganic nanoparticles, have been investigated for their capacity to specifically deliver therapeutic agents to ECs while minimizing off-target effects [30]. This discussion centers on the primary strategies currently employed to direct nanocarriers toward ECs.

Surface modification with targeting ligands

A common strategy to enhance endothelial cell targeting involves modifying the surface of nanocarriers with specific ligands [9, 18, 72]. These ligands may encompass antibodies, peptides, or small molecules that recognize receptors overexpressed on ECs under pathological conditions [32]. For example, adhesion molecules such as VCAM-1 are frequently upregulated on ECs during processes such as angiogenesis and inflammation [15, 32]. Nanocarriers can achieve passive endothelial targeting by exploiting the elevated expression of these adhesion molecules and active targeting through the use of targeted peptides and bioengineered surface proteins specific to VCAM-1 [26, 72]. Functionalized nanocarriers can target activated ECs under high shear stress by employing ligands such as E-Selectin-binding peptides and anti-ICAM-1 antibodies, showing increased adhesion under pathological shear stress conditions [43]. Hossen et al. demonstrated a peptide-modified nanocarrier designed for precise delivery to ECs, which maintained structural integrity and avoided lysosomal co-localization [108]. Voigt et al. reported that the surface modification of ECs with anionic polyelectrolytes can result in varied nanocarrier uptake patterns and enhance absorption via targeted caveolae [109]. The FLT4/VEGFR3 receptor, which is prominently expressed on the ECs of Schlemm’s canal, represents a promising target for improving drug delivery in glaucoma therapy. Researchers have engineered polymeric nanocarriers that incorporate lipid-anchored targeting ligands to enhance their interaction with the FLT4 receptor. These nanocarriers were synthesized with various PEG spacer units and subsequently incorporated into micellar assemblies. This approach aims to optimize drug absorption by targeted cells, reduce off-target effects, and improve the efficacy of therapies for glaucoma cell softening [110]. Responsive nanocarriers

Responsive nanocarriers are precisely engineered to exhibit structural modifications or release therapeutic payloads in response to specific stimuli within the vascular microenvironment [112]. Mészáros et al. investigated the use of glutathione and alanine to target human ECs, thereby facilitating the penetration of peptide nanocarriers through a BBB model [113]. In a separate study, Lu et al. developed an intelligent nanocarrier designed to treat cerebral ischemia and reperfusion injury by targeting ischemic regions and remodeling the neurovascular unit [114]. Furthermore, Yan et al. developed a microenvironment-responsive co-delivery nanosystem to mitigate acute kidney injury by interrupting feedback loops induced by calcium overload and ROS [115]. Additionally, Myocardial ischemia–reperfusion (MI/R) injury represents a substantial clinical challenge, frequently resulting in endothelial barrier dysfunction [116]. In response to this challenge, recent research has developed a biomimetic nanoparticle (Fe@PLP-TR-A) that integrates with platelet membranes for the targeted delivery of angiopoietin-like 4 (ANGPTL4) and ROS scavengers (Fig. 8A) [117]. This strategy enhances endothelial barrier protection and mitigates MI/R injury. The platelet membrane directs the nanoparticles specifically to the injured endothelium, where thrombin cleavages the specific peptide and triggers extracellular release of ANGPTL4 which stabilizes VE-Cadherin by binding to receptors on cell membranes [117]. Nanocarrier coating and stealth strategies

The application of biocompatible polymer coatings, such as PEG, to nanocarriers can reduce their recognition and elimination by the RES, thus prolonging circulation time [41]. Research has resulted in the development of PEGylated target-sensitive liposomes (TSLs) containing C–C motif chemokine receptor 2 antagonists linked to specific peptides recognized by endothelial VCAM-1 [118]. This provides preliminary evidence that nanocarriers capable of accurately delivering and releasing chemokine inhibitors at pathological sites can facilitate targeted binding to mitigate chemokine-driven inflammatory processes both in vitro and in situ [118]. Ariyandi et al. utilized cell surface biotinylation to identify specific receptors involved in the uptake of nanocarriers by ECs. The study revealed that a vitronectin corona effectively targets integrin receptors, thereby significantly augmenting the uptake of nanocarriers by ECs in both the brain and liver. This approach offers a refined understanding of nanocarrier interactions with cell surfaces [119]. Biomimetic nanocarriers, produced by coating synthetic nanoparticles with platelet membranes, facilitate interaction with activated endothelium, foam cells, and collagen. This strategy enables multifaceted biological targeting and the detection of atherosclerosis [120].

The utilization of nanocarriers in targeted drug delivery provides notable benefits; however, recent studies underscore several limitations and potential drawbacks that need to be addressed. Principal challenges encompass the stability, bioavailability, and regulatory pathways related to nanocarriers, which could hinder their effective application in clinical environments. For example, intrinsic instability may result in premature drug release, thereby diminishing therapeutic efficacy [121]. Additionally, short circulation half-lives and rapid metabolism can restrict the bioavailability of drugs administered through nanocarriers [122]. Furthermore, challenges such as poor tissue distribution and insufficient penetration, especially across the BBB, represent significant obstacles [39]. The transition from preclinical studies to clinical applications is beset with regulatory hurdles, potentially delaying the commercialization of promising nanocarrier systems [123]. Additionally, scaling-up processes complicate the production of nanocarrier-based therapies, thereby impacting their availability in clinical settings [123].

Factors influencing endothelial targeting of nanocarriers

Targeting nanocarriers to ECs involves a complex interplay of physical forces, receptor-ligand interactions, and binding affinities [72]. Various factors influence the efficacy of nanocarriers targeting ECs. These factors encompass the intrinsic properties of the nanocarrier (such as size, charge, geometric shape, and deformability), the characteristics of the target (including surface density, aggregation, and accessibility of binding sites), and hemodynamic and blood composition variables [17, 31]. The interactions between nanocarriers and target factors ultimately determine the specificity, selectivity, efficacy, fate, and safety.

Intrinsic factors of the nanocarriers

Key factors affecting the targeting of nanocarriers to ECs include particle size, shape, and charge (Fig. 9) (refer to Table 3) [2, 124, 125]. The size of nanocarriers, ranging from 100 nm to several micrometers, directly impacts their behavior in the bloodstream and their adhesion efficiency to ECs [126]. Smaller particles are correlated with prolonged circulation times and enhanced specificity in binding to ECs [31, 126]. Non-spherical nanocarriers, such as rod-shaped or disc-shaped particles, demonstrate higher targeting specificity and longer circulation times than spherical particles due to improved alignment with blood flow, allowing them to evade collisions with blood cells [127, 128]. The role of nanocarrier binding affinity in vascular targeting

The binding affinity between nanocarriers and target molecules within the vascular system significantly influences targeting efficacy [72, 145]. This includes their affinity characteristics, specific recognition of molecular targets, and adherence to target surfaces (refer to Table 4). The ligands on the surface of nanoparticles directly affect their binding affinity with ECs [17, 146]. Insufficient ligand density may lead to reduced affinity, while excessively high ligand density can cause “ligand crowding,” thereby decreasing binding efficiency [147]. Other factors

Non-affinity factors, such as hemodynamics and blood components, significantly impact targeting efficiency, especially the effects of red blood cells and associated components (e.g., glycocalyx, red blood cell collisions, and leukocyte phagocytosis) [31, 72, 99, 157]. The glycocalyx, a protective layer on the EC surface, may alter or degrade under inflammatory conditions, thereby affecting the adhesion and delivery efficiency of nanoparticles [157]. Furthermore, shear forces in blood flow substantially affect the targeting efficiency of nanoparticles by influencing their adhesion to the vascular wall and lysosomal delivery rates [99, 158]. Consequently, there is a continued need for comprehensive parameter studies on molecular-specific and geometrically-specific platforms when designing therapeutic and diagnostic interventions for both healthy and diseased vascular endothelium [32].

Optimization strategies for nanocarrier vascular targeting

To optimize vascular targeting, several strategies may be implemented: (1) reducing nanocarrier size to the submicron level can enhance targeting efficiency, although excessively small sizes may limit drug loading capacity and overall efficacy; (2) employing non-spherical geometries, such as filaments, discs, and ellipsoids, can prolong circulation time within blood vessels; (3) flexible nanocarriers typically demonstrate longer circulation times compared to rigid particles; (4) increasing antibody density can enhance interactions between the nanocarrier and cells, but potential limitations in binding efficiency should be assessed before reaching maximum surface density; (5) elevating nanocarrier concentration can improve targeting efficiency, yet it is essential to balance this with drug loading capacity and potential toxicity risks; (6) careful selection of target molecules is crucial for effective targeting during cellular processes such as upregulation, internalization, and transport, as variations among different targets can significantly impact efficacy and safety [31]. These strategies aim to optimize nanocarrier design for improved vascular targeting and therapeutic outcomes.

Applications of endothelial targeting nanocarriers in diseases

ECs play a crucial role in the development of various diseases, including cancer, inflammatory disorders, cardiovascular diseases, and neurodegenerative syndromes [20, 28, 159–161]. Targeting these cells with nanocarriers has demonstrated promising outcomes by improving drug localization at the disease site and minimizing systemic adverse effects, thereby enhancing therapeutic efficacy [3, 60, 162].

Cancer treatment

Recent studies have reported promising results in cancer treatment using nanocarriers specifically designed to target ECs. Paris et al. explored nanocarriers designed to target tumor vasculature, which circumvent the need for tissue penetration [163]. Zhou et al. proposed a novel method to enhance drug delivery efficiency based on the transcytosis mechanism. Leveraging the high expression of GGT on tumor ECs and adjacent tumor cells, they developed a strategy involving GGT-responsive polymer nanocarriers, converting passive diffusion into active transport (Fig. 10) [23]. Nanocarriers targeting P-selectin have exhibited effective vascular traversal, offering a precise therapeutic strategy [164]. Additionally, ongoing research aims to develop lipid nanocarriers for nucleic acid delivery to ECs, demonstrating potential for targeted cancer therapy [24]. Moreover, lipid nanocarriers specific to αvβ3-integrin have been utilized for tumor targeting, especially in ECs, underscoring the potential of nanocarriers in cancer therapy [165]. Inflammatory diseases

Nanocarriers have also shown potential in the treatment of respiratory diseases, including pneumonia. By specifically targeting pulmonary ECs, these nanocarriers are capable of effectively delivering therapeutic agents to infected or inflamed sites [87]. For example, anti-ICAM-1 antibody-modified nanostructured lipid nanocarriers for drug delivery, including dexamethasone and simvastatin, exhibit promise as targeted treatments for acute lung injury (ALI) [166, 167]. Utilizing passive, active, or physicochemical targeting mechanisms, nanocarriers can interact with pulmonary epithelial/ECs and inflammatory cells, potentially reversing aberrant processes associated with acute respiratory distress syndrome [168]. Furthermore, ceramides have been identified as potential surface receptors on ECs, applicable for targeted pulmonary therapies [169]. Additionally, research has developed anti-E-selectin immunoliposomes that can simultaneously deliver rapamycin, specifically targeting ECs activated by E-selectin and TNF-α, thereby selectively inhibiting inflammatory responses in these cells [170]. pH-sensitive nanocarriers coated with anti-ICAM-1 antibodies are designed to release TPCA-1 in the acidic environment of inflamed lungs, thereby alleviating acute lung inflammation and injury [171]. Gene-engineered cell membrane-coated nanocarriers exploit the specific affinity between very late antigen-4 (VLA-4) and VCAM-1 to target inflammation and deliver dexamethasone to inflamed lung tissues (Fig. 11) [172]. In conclusion, the application of endothelial-targeted nanocarriers for pneumonia shows substantial potential for enhancing therapeutic outcomes and reducing the impact of respiratory infections. Cardiovascular diseases

ECs are crucial in the pathogenesis of cardiovascular diseases, positioning them as potential targets for therapeutic interventions [27, 63]. Lipid-based nanocarriers, when precisely engineered, have demonstrated the ability to deliver nucleic acids specifically to ECs, showcasing substantial potential for targeted drug delivery in cardiovascular research [24, 173]. Recent advancements have introduced a novel nucleic acid delivery system targeting ECs for the treatment of myocardial infarction. This system utilizes a combination of polycationic siRNA (siR-ICAM1) and plasmid DNA (pCXCL12), which has been demonstrated to enhance cardiac function, contractility, anti-inflammatory responses, and angiogenesis in the context of MI treatment [173]. Furthermore, Yeh et al. have demonstrated that endothelial thioredoxin domain containing 5 (TXNDC5) is significantly elevated in human atherosclerotic lesions and is upregulated in response to disturbed blood flow (DF) both in vivo and in vitro [174]. The application of nanoparticles for the delivery of TXNDC5-targeted CRISPR-Cas9 plasmids significantly enhances eNOS protein levels in ApoE−/− mice and mitigates the progression of atherosclerosis (Fig. 12) [174]. Additionally, research has investigated the use of iron oxide nanoparticles for molecular imaging in cardiovascular diseases, underscoring the feasibility of targeted imaging and diagnostics [175]. Overall, nanocarriers specifically engineered for ECs exhibit significant potential for improving drug delivery efficiency, imaging techniques, and therapeutic outcomes in cardiovascular diseases [73, 74, 176]. Neurological diseases

Strategies for directing nanocarriers across the BBB to address neurological disorders have also been explored [177, 178]. In neurobiology, methods to manipulate BBB permeability using mechanical and biological techniques, such as laser-induced stimulation of endothelial-targeted nanocarriers, have been investigated [179]. Nanocarriers engineered to specifically target the BBB possess distinct biomaterial properties and have shown potential in managing neurological disorders [124]. Targeted nanocarriers for ECs are anticipated to enhance drug delivery efficacy and therapeutic outcomes across various central nervous system diseases [180, 181]. Recent studies indicate that targeted nanocarriers can utilize the natural migration pathway of leukocytes from the lungs to the brain to deliver therapeutic agents to affected brain regions [182]. This approach offers a viable method for precise intervention in acute brain injuries. Specifically, anti-ICAM/dexamethasone liposomes have been demonstrated to reduce cerebral edema and induce anti-inflammatory M2 polarization in brain macrophages (Fig. 13) [182]. Additionally, targeting lipid nanoparticles to the BBB represents a promising approach for enhancing outcomes in acute ischemic stroke [180]. Challenges and future outlook

In the field of nanomedicine, the efficient delivery of drugs to ECs presents a promising therapeutic avenue for various diseases [24, 84]. However, it also faces significant challenges [39]. These challenges include issues of biosafety, the complexity of in vivo pharmacokinetics, difficulties in achieving subcellular targeting, and the formidable task of translating these systems into clinical applications [7, 72, 200, 201].Biosafety challenges: Nanocarriers injected intravenously may accumulate in systemic circulation and interact directly with vascular ECs, which can affect vascular physiology [202]. Previous research has demonstrated that certain inorganic nanomaterials, such as copper oxide and silica, may damage ECs [33, 37, 38]. Therefore, improving the targeting efficiency of drug-delivery nanocarriers while maintaining their biocompatibility is crucial [39]. Furthermore, the in vivo fate and toxicity mechanisms of nanocarriers, along with their interactions with key cell types such as ECs, immune cells, and hematopoietic stem cells, remain insufficiently explored, leading to unclear mechanisms.In vivo dynamics challenges: The movement of nanocarriers within the body is a dynamic process, characterized by a kinetic competition between targeting ECs and nonspecific tissue retention [200, 203]. Although ligand-functionalized nanocarriers hold promise, achieving precise targeting across diverse disease states and EC subpopulations remains a significant challenge. This requires a more thorough examination of molecular and geometric specificity parameters to improve therapeutic strategies and diagnostic techniques. Additionally, the complex microenvironment, including hemodynamics and blood components, is not well understood in terms of its impact on drug behavior and drug delivery systems.Complexity of subcellular targeting: Ideally, optimal drug efficacy is achieved within the target cells. Attaining maximal drug efficacy at the subcellular level within ECs is essential for therapeutic success but continues to be a significant challenge [204]. The intricate interactions between nanocarriers and cells are profoundly influenced by the characteristics of both the nanocarriers and the cells, increasing the complexity and challenge of designing effective delivery systems [201].Future outlook: In the current research landscape, developing new technologies and methods is essential for achieving precise diagnosis and treatment of ECs. It is crucial to address existing challenges, such as the use of biocompatible and biodegradable materials like poly(lactic acid) (PLA) and poly(vinyl alcohol) (PVA), which are FDA-approved, can mitigate toxic reactions [205, 206]. Surface modification of nanocarriers with hydrophobic molecules, such as PEG, or the conjugation of specific targeting ligands can reduce non-specific interactions with ECs. This approach enhances targeting efficiency and minimizes toxicity to healthy ECs.

It is crucial to address existing challenges, such as the use of biocompatible and biodegradable materials like poly(lactic acid) (PLA) and poly(vinyl alcohol) (PVA), which are FDA-approved, can mitigate toxic reactions [205, 206]. Surface modification of nanocarriers with hydrophobic molecules, such as PEG, or the conjugation of specific targeting ligands can reduce non-specific interactions with ECs. This approach enhances targeting efficiency and minimizes toxicity to healthy ECs.

Strategies for addressing in vivo pharmacokinetic challenges include optimizing nanocarrier size and surface properties. Particles with diameters ranging from 50 to 200 nm generally exhibit improved in vivo distribution and targeting [207]. Dynamic tracking techniques, such as magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) and fluorescence imaging, facilitate real-time monitoring of nanocarrier distribution and kinetic behavior within the body [208]. The combination of multiple targeting strategies, including the use of specific ligands and passive targeting characteristics, can further enhance targeting efficiency. For example, nanocarriers modified with monoclonal antibodies have demonstrated successful targeted delivery in various types of ECs [209].

Addressing the complexity of subcellular targeting involves the development of nanocarriers capable of releasing drugs within specific organelles, such as endosomes or mitochondria in ECs [210]. For instance, pH-responsive nanocarriers that release drugs in acidic environments are effective for targeting endosomes within cells [211]. Furthermore, employing intracellular navigation technologies, such as receptor-mediated endocytosis, can improve the intracellular targeting of drugs. Nanocarriers designed with receptor-mediated endocytosis mechanisms have exhibited promising intracellular targeting capabilities in tumor cell therapy [212]. The combination of multimodal imaging techniques, such as fluorescence microscopy and electron microscopy, provides detailed observations of nanocarrier distribution and localization within cells, thereby optimizing the design of drug delivery systems [213]. Furthermore, using artificial intelligence to assist nanocarrier design for improved targeting efficiency. Future research should prioritize the development of nanocarriers with improved delivery efficacy, synergistic therapeutic effects, and minimal toxicological impact.

In conclusion, the targeted delivery of drugs to vascular ECs via nanocarriers demonstrates considerable promise for the diagnosis and treatment of conditions such as tumors, inflammatory disorders, and cardiovascular diseases. A comprehensive understanding of the interactions between nanomedicines and ECs is crucial for devising methods and strategies aimed at improving therapeutic efficacy. Achieving this objective necessitates exploring their mechanisms comprehensively, developing personalized treatment strategies, and promoting interdisciplinary collaborations to advance both theoretical and empirical insights. This will facilitate the transition from laboratory research to clinical applications and introduce innovative therapeutic strategies for vascular and inflammatory conditions, thereby significantly enhancing disease management and prognosis.
