Association Between Intrauterine Growth Restriction and Chronic Kidney Disease: A Birth Cohort Study Analysis
Authors: Daniela Aparecida Lorencini, Paulo Cesar Lopes, Heloísa Bettiol, Marco Antonio Barbieri, Eduardo Barbosa Coelho
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
Rationale & ObjectiveDiabetes mellitus and hypertension are the main risk factors for chronic kidney disease (CKD), but factors affecting fetal development also contribute to later kidney loss. Various birth-related factors and definitions, including low birth weight (LBW), intrauterine growth restriction (IUGR), and prematurity, complicate the understanding of this relationship. Therefore, this study aimed to evaluate the effect of these fetal conditions on the risk of CKD by the end of the third decade of life.Study DesignA single-center, birth cohort study.Setting & ParticipantsThe cohort was established between 1978 and 1979 in Brazil, which included 6,827 newborns (98% of births). The follow-up visit occurred in 2016/2017, and 1,564 participants were included in the study.ExposurePrematurity (gestational age < 37 weeks), LBW (<2,500 g), and IUGR defined as body weight ratio ≤0.85.OutcomesThe primary outcome was the presence of CKD. Secondary outcomes were the values of estimated glomerular filtration rate (eGFR) and urinary albumin-creatinine ratio (UACR).Analytical ApproacheGFR was modeled using multivariable linear regression. UACR was analyzed using a quantile regression (median) model. CKD risk was estimated using a logistic regression model to calculate the odds ratio (95% CI).ResultsPrematurity was associated with a lower eGFR (105.9 ± 13.1 vs 108.8 ± 13.0 mL/min/1.73 m2, P < 0.05), while IUGR was linked to a higher prevalence of CKD (25.3% vs 18.1%, P < 0.05). In multivariate analysis, factors such as body mass index, homocysteine level, C-reactive protein level, and UACR were associated with lower eGFR. Female sex, high blood pressure, glycated hemoglobin levels, C-reactive protein levels, and pulse wave velocity were associated with increased UACR. None of the birth exposure factors impacted eGFR or UACR. IUGR (1.50 [1.10-2.21], P = 0.047), diabetes mellitus (1.60 [1.05-2.46], P = 0.03), and female sex (2.18 [1.62-2.90]) were associated with an increased risk of CKD. Neither LBW nor prematurity shows significant associations with CKD risk.LimitationsThe cohort design, with participant loss because of dropouts during the follow-up visits.ConclusionsIUGR was associated with a higher risk of CKD, particularly in women.

Introduction

Chronic kidney disease (CKD) is defined as abnormalities of kidney structure or function, present for a minimum of three months, with health implications. CKD is classified based on cause, glomerular filtration rate (GFR), and albuminuria range.1 In 2017, the prevalence of CKD was estimated as 9.1% in the world’s population. Globally in 2017, there were 697.5 million cases of CKD, and 1.2 million people died from this disease. People with an estimated GFR (eGFR) less than 45 mL/min/1.73 m2 (CKD stage 3b) and moderately increased albuminuria, corresponding to 42.8% of all CKD-diagnosed cases in the world, have a higher risk of lost kidney function and will need hemodialysis or renal transplantation in the future.2

Although diabetes and hypertension are the leading risk factors and causes of CKD, there is evidence showing the contribution of factors affecting fetal development with loss of kidney function in later life.3 Barker et al.4,5 observed an inverse relationship between birth weight and the risk of cardiovascular disease, suggesting a developmental programming process for the occurrence of adult diseases. Intrauterine growth restriction (IUGR) is associated with a reduction in the number of nephrons at birth.6 Additionally, low birth weight (LBW) and prematurity have been identified as risk factors for later kidney disease and hypertension.3 This association is explained by cellular and molecular mechanisms involved in the renal compensatory process, which occurs in response to a reduced number of nephrons and can lead to further kidney injury because of compensatory hypertrophy and glomerular hyperfiltration.7,8

The main objective of this study was to evaluate the effect of fetal conditions on the risk of kidney disease by the end of the third decade of life. Additionally, because birth weight, gestational age, and IUGR may be associated with CKD later in life and often overlap, we decided to study each condition separately to better understand the risk of CKD associated with each factor.

Methods

Study Design and Sample

Data came from a longitudinal birth cohort study performed in the city of Ribeirão Preto, São Paulo state, Brazil. The cohort was established between June 1978 and May 1979, with the registration of 6,827 non-twin live newborns, corresponding to 98% of all local births. The last cohort follow-up occurred between 2016 and 2017, in which 1,954 individuals, 38/39 years old, were evaluated. In the current study, participants with missing gestational age, height, or weight at birth data, for whom imputation (see following) was not possible, were excluded, totaling 6,718 participants from the original cohort. Furthermore, participants without data regarding kidney function or albuminuria were excluded. Thus, 1,564 participants remained from the 2016/2017 follow-up visit. To address selection bias because of a loss to follow-up, we analyzed the original 1978/1979 birth cohort and the 2016/2017 sample (Table S1 in the supplementary appendix). Baseline characteristics—including maternal smoking, family income at birth, maternal education, parity, and participant sex—were similar between the 2 groups, with no differences. These findings suggest that the follow-up sample remained broadly representative of the original cohort and that attrition was unlikely to be associated with key perinatal or sociodemographic factors. However, concerning exposure factors, we observed a reduction in the prevalence of all studied variables. The mortality rate in 1978/1979 in Brazil was very high, and the babies with LBW, prematurity, and IUGR were the most prone to death, partially explaining these findings. More details about this cohort's characteristics can be obtained from previous publications.9,10

Participants in the 2016/2017 visit were evaluated and interviewed at the Clinical Research Centre, Ribeirao Preto, University of São Paulo, using socioeconomic questionnaires. Informed consent was obtained, and the study was approved by the institutional review board (protocol 1.282.710). Recruitment occurred through phone calls, invitation letters to birth addresses, searches in health system records, and advertisements on social media, newspapers, and television.

Variables and Biochemical Tests

All participants were assessed consecutively upon arrival at the study center, following a standardized protocol. The research staff—including physicians, nurses, laboratory technicians, and administrative personnel—were unaware of each participant's perinatal exposure at the time of evaluation. This ensured that all clinical measurements, interviews, and sample collections were conducted without knowledge of group allocation, thereby minimizing the risk of bias. Blood pressure (BP; mmHg), weight (kg), and height (m) were measured. The body mass index (BMI) was calculated by dividing the weight by height squared (kg/m2). Blood samples were collected, processed, and stored in a freezer at −80°C until the moment of biochemical tests. Concentrations of urinary albumin-to-creatinine ratio (UACR) in urine and serum levels of creatinine, total cholesterol, high-density lipoprotein, low-density lipoprotein, triglycerides, cystatin C, homocysteine, high-sensitivity C-reactive protein (CRP), and glucose were quantified in an automated biochemical analyzer (Atellica CH, Siemens Healthineers). Glycated hemoglobin (HbA1c) level was determined using high-performance liquid chromatography (Bio-Rad D-10). The skin color was auto-referenced and classified as white, black, or brown. Black and brown subjects were analyzed together and classified as non-white. BP was measured using calibrated and validated digital equipment (OMRON 742INT), in accordance with the 2017 recommendations of the American College of Cardiology/American Heart Association.11 Hypertension was defined as a systolic blood pressure (SBP) of ≥ 130 mmHg and/or a diastolic blood pressure (DBP) of ≥ 80 mmHg, or the use of antihypertensive medication.11 To compare BP values, 214 individuals receiving antihypertensive therapy were excluded from the analysis. The pulse wave velocity was measured using a portable device (SphygmoCor), as previously described.12 Diabetes mellitus was defined as a fasting glucose level ≥ 126 mg/dL, an HbA1c level ≥ 6.5%, or the use of medication to control serum glucose. In the same way, a homocysteine level > 15 μmol/L and an ultrasensitive C-reactive protein level > 0.3 mg/dL were considered above the values for normality based on the reference value of the methods. The analysis of socioeconomic classes was conducted using the Brazilian Criteria of Economic Classification questionnaire, specifically the 2016 version.13 This questionnaire categorizes individuals into distinct socioeconomic groups based on their household income. For our study, these groups were condensed into two categories, labeled high-middle income (A-C) and low income (D and E).

The primary data of the initial cohort of 1978/1979 were registered using interviews and questionnaires applied to the parents on the birth of the newborn, collecting information related to the mother's characteristics and state of health, life habits, and socioeconomic condition. Data were collected on the number of gestations, maternal educational level (measured in years studied), parents’ smoking history, and household income, which was calculated based on family salaries standardized to the minimum Brazilian wage per month in 1978.

Variables of Exposure

The exposure variables studied were as follows: prematurity (gestational age < 37 weeks), LBW (< 2,500 g), and IUGR (birth weight ratio [BWR] < 0.85). Gestational age was calculated based on the date of the last normal menstrual period reported by the mother. This date was classified as unknown when not recalled by the mother. In cases of birth weight incompatible with the date of the last menstruation (identified using the cutoff points suggested by Altman and Coles14 based on English reference data), or newborns with an unlikely gestational age (20 or 50 weeks), the gestational age was reclassified as unknown. All cases with missing or ignored gestational age were imputed in a regression model.15 The characteristics that were used to impute gestational age were birth weight, parity, family income, and newborn sex. Participants without this information were excluded. The concept of IUGR was defined using the BWR, which is the ratio of the newborn’s weight to the mean weight for gestational age based on a sex-specific reference curve, based on Intergrowth 21st.16 A BWR greater than 0.85 was defined as adequate for gestational age, while a BWR of 0.85 or less was classified as IUGR.17 This classification was used to select patients with IUGR, because they typically exhibited significantly lower weight, length, head circumference, and BMI at birth compared with those in the control group of term infants, reinforcing the diagnosis of IUGR.18 Also, this approach allows for the detection of births with a weight higher than 2,500 g but with IUGR, considering the gestational age.

Outcome Variables

The creatinine level measurements follow the isotope dilution mass spectrometry traceable method according to the National Institute of Standards and Technology guideline. The eGFR was estimated using the serum creatinine and cystatin C levels in the CKD-Epidemiology Collaboration 2021 equation. Values of the UACR were used as a continuous variable or used based on the categorization as normal (< 30 mg/g) or albuminuria (higher than 30 mg/g). Additionally, the presence of CKD was considered if eGFR < 60 mL/min/1.73 m2 or UACR > 30 mg/g or the participant had a clinical history of dialysis or kidney transplantation.

Statistical Analysis

The data were analyzed using the Stata program, version 14.219 (StataCorp. Stata statistical software: Release 14. StataCorp LLC;2015). Univariate analysis was performed using a t test for the continuous variables or a chi-square test to compare proportions to determine the effect of each variable on birth weight, gestational age, or IUGR. The effect of exposure variables on eGFR was modeled using multivariable linear regression. To mitigate multicollinearity in the multiple linear regression analysis, a collinearity matrix approach was used. Variables with a high collinearity ratio (variance inflation factor > 5) were systematically eliminated to ensure the robustness and reliability of the regression model. To assess the affet of exposure variables on the UACR, the values were normalized using a transformation of the UACR values to log UACR + 1, and a quantile regression model, considering the 0.5 quartiles (median), was applied. The affect of birth variables on CKD was calculated using a stepwise logistic multiple regression model with backward elimination, used to control for confounding factors. Variables with P < 0.20 upon univariable analysis were imputed in all models, and the best fit for each model was compared using the variance inflation factor, Akaike information criterion, and Bayesian information criterion, according to the model used. The results were expressed as coefficients or odds ratios (ORs) and 95% confidence intervals (95% CIs). The P value considered significant was < 0.05.

Discussion

More than 35 years ago, Brenner et al.20 proposed that a reduced number of nephrons at birth, influenced by perinatal or intrauterine factors, could be linked to hypertension and future renal disease. Specifically, LBW (less than 2,500 g) is thought to be associated with a diminished number of nephrons, considering that renal maturation occurs until the 36th week of gestation, and LBW is strongly correlated with prematurity. Experimental and epidemiological studies support the plausibility of this hypothesis.2,3 However, only a limited number of clinical studies and nationwide birth cohorts have examined the potential association between IUGR and/or prematurity and the risk of developing CKD later in life.21

The Helsinki Birth Cohort Study (n = 20,431) demonstrated that LBW and prematurity (defined as birth before 34 weeks) were associated with an increased risk of CKD in adulthood, particularly among females.22 Similarly, a Swedish nationwide cohort (n = 4.2 million) found that individuals born extremely preterm (< 28 weeks) had a threefold higher risk of developing CKD before age 44, with the most significant risk observed during childhood (0-9 years).23 In Norway, a population-based study following over 2.6 million individuals for up to 5 decades reported a stepwise increase in KFRT risk according to the number of adverse birth conditions (LBW, IUGR, or prematurity), with the highest risk seen in those exposed to all three risk factors.24

Our study adds to this literature, showing that IUGR, rather than LBW or prematurity alone, was independently associated with a higher prevalence of CKD in early adulthood, even after adjusting for traditional risk factors. This finding supports the hypothesis that IUGR may represent a more severe and sustained intrauterine insult, potentially leading to long-term structural and functional alterations in renal development, beyond the reduction in nephron numbers alone. Several factors may explain these discrepancies with the above studies. First, the underlying causes of LBW and prematurity may differ between high-income and middle-income settings, because these conditions are more likely to reflect maternal undernutrition, infections, or inadequate prenatal care, rather than the elective preterm births or other etiologies common in high-resource environments. Second, we used BWR as a marker of IUGR, which may offer greater specificity for true fetal growth impairment than LBW or gestational age alone. Furthermore, the number of births with extreme prematurity and the prevalence of KFRT were low in our context. Finally, given the relatively small number of participants with LBW and prematurity, we should consider the limited statistical power in these subgroup analyses.

Our findings indicate that IUGR was associated with a higher prevalence of CKD, but not with eGFR or UACR values alone. Additionally, female sex and diabetes mellitus were factors linked to a CKD risk, even in young individuals. Another noteworthy observation from the present study was the prevalence of approximately 20% of CKD in a young population representative of the local demographic characteristics. These findings may reflect the high prevalence of hypertension detected in the study sample according to the American College of Cardiology/American Heart Association cutoff criteria (≥ 130 × 80 mmHg) and the high rate of overweight, which may be associated with increased peripheral insulin resistance and subsequent hyperglycemia. A higher risk of CKD in women has been reported in other studies, including data from 34 multinational cohorts with more than 5.2 million individuals from 28 countries.25,26 The higher prevalence of CKD in women is further supported by other studies conducted in Latin America and the Caribbean.27,28 Moreover, within the CKD-affected population, conditions such as diabetes and obesity were more commonly observed in females than in males, which may help explain the increased prevalence of CKD among women.

A reduced risk of CKD was attributed to the presence of a BMI greater than 30 kg/m2 within the age group included in this study. Obesity is a well-known risk factor for CKD,29 and a potential explanation for our findings lies in the fact that obesity is associated with an increase in GFR at the initial stages of exposure to this condition.30,31 Our study demonstrated an association between IUGR and CKD, but not with prematurity or LBW. In the univariate analysis, LBW was associated with lower eGFR values, but this effect was not observed after adjusting for multivariate analysis. IUGR, which encompasses the adaptive factors related to this condition as extensively discussed in the developmental origins of health and disease theory,32 appears to have a greater impact than LBW alone, which is often associated with a reduced number of nephrons. Although there is biological plausibility, supported by experimental studies, that a lower number of nephrons at birth is linked to hyperfiltration and premature loss of the remaining nephrons, this factor alone does not seem sufficient to explain the increased risk of CKD in individuals with LBW. For example, human kidney donors experience an acute decrease of nearly 30% in their baseline GFR. Compared with matched healthy non-donors, kidney donors had an increased risk of KFRT over a median of 7.6 years;however, the magnitude of the absolute risk increase was small.33 The GFR is influenced by 4 key determinants of glomerular ultrafiltration: renal plasma flow, systemic oncotic pressure (πA), the hydraulic pressure gradient (ΔP), and the glomerular ultrafiltration coefficient (Kf). The Kf is defined as the product of the hydraulic conductivity of the glomerular capillary membrane and the surface area of the glomeruli.34 The most plausible explanation for ameliorating the glomerular hyperfiltration adaptive response in kidney donors is a simultaneous increase in renal plasma flow and Kf, while ΔP remains unchanged.35 This finding is consistent with the developmental growth of kidneys in healthy rodents, where GFR increases alongside kidney size because of nearly identical increases in both enal plasma flow and Kf, with ΔP remaining stable.36 Although similar experiments have yet to be conducted in cases of prematurity or LBW exposure, our data suggest that these conditions alone could impact the risk for CKD less than the presence of IUGR, likely because of the occurrence of similar adaptive processes following nephron loss and the more aggressive insult to the kidneys in IUGR-exposed newborns. Our study has several limitations. Cohort studies are prone to significant participant loss over time, which can introduce biases. It was not possible to identify the presence of CKD and KFRT among participants who did not attend the final visit. Data related to maternal health conditions, except for smoking status, were not collected. Urine collection for albuminuria was conducted at different times because of participants returning throughout the day, which may lead to inaccurate results. CKD diagnosis in our study was based on a single measurement of serum creatinine and urinary UACR. Although used in observational cohorts, this approach may lead to an overestimation of CKD prevalence because of transient increases in albuminuria or reductions in eGFR. Repeat testing after 3 months, as recommended for CKD confirmation, was not feasible because of logistical constraints inherent to large-scale cohort designs. Therefore, our findings reflect a screening-based definition of CKD, commonly called “apparent CKD,” as applied in several population-based studies. Furthermore, the sample returning for the final visit of the cohort had a higher prevalence of males compared with the original sample, which could also introduce biases. Finally, residual confounding by unmeasured factors influencing both exposure and outcome cannot be fully excluded.

In contrast, this study has several advantages. The eGFR was calculated using the levels of cystatin C and creatinine measurements, which provide more accurate GFR results in ranges close to normal filtration. The quantification of albuminuria increased the prevalence of CKD cases in the sample. The IUGR classification was determined by calculating the relationship between birth weight and the 50th percentile of reference values from Intergrowth 21st, which includes data from the Brazilian population. In conclusion, IUGR was associated with a higher risk of CKD, particularly in women from a middle-income parental background. Using birth weight normalization based on the 50th percentile of the reference weight values for gestational age was more accurate in estimating the risk of CKD than information on prematurity or solely birth weight.
