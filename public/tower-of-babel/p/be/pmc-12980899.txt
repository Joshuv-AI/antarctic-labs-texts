Evaluation of static and dynamic pupil responses in patients with acromegaly with automatic pupillometer
Authors: Seyfettin Erdem, Mehmet Emin Dursun, Sedat Ava, Mine Karahan, Leyla Hazar, Doruk Can Sevuk, Zafer Pekkolay, Ugur Keklikci
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundAcromegaly is a chronic disorder caused by excessive secretion of growth hormone and IGF-I, usually from a benign pituitary adenoma, leading to systemic complications and increased mortality. Abnormal pupillary responses may indicate neurological or ophthalmic dysfunction. This study investigates pupillary response alterations in acromegaly patients using automatic pupillometry.MethodsThis prospective cross-sectional study involved 50 individuals diagnosed with acromegaly and an equal number of age- and sex-matched healthy controls. All subjects underwent a complete ophthalmological examination. Pupillary measurements were obtained static conditions (scotopic, mesopic and low-high photopic) and dynamic (rest pupil diameter, contraction amplitude, latency, contraction duration, contraction velocity, dilation latency, resting duration and velocity) using an automatic pupillometer.ResultsThe mean age of the patients with acromegaly was similar to that of the healthy control group (44.80 ± 4.46 and 42.52 ± 4.40 years, p = 0.85). According to the results of our study, mesopic and low- and high-photopic pupil diameter (PD) were significantly lower in patients with acromegaly (p = 0.003, p < 0.001, and p < 0.001, respectively). In addition, when the dynamic pupillometry parameters of these patients were examined, it was found that the resting diameter, contraction amplitude, duration, and velocity (p < 0.001, all values), and dilatation latency and velocity were significantly lower (p < 0.001, both values), while only the latency of pupil contraction was higher (p = 0.02).The mean age of patients with acromegaly was comparable to that of the healthy control group (44.80 ± 4.46 vs. 42.52 ± 4.40 years, p = 0.85). Analysis revealed that pupil PD under mesopic, low photopic, and high photopic conditions were significantly reduced in the acromegaly cohort (p = 0.003, p < 0.001, and p < 0.001, respectively). Furthermore, evaluation of dynamic pupillometry parameters demonstrated that resting pupil diameter, contraction amplitude, contraction duration, and contraction velocity were all significantly decreased (p < 0.001 for each). Similarly, both dilatation latency and dilatation velocity were markedly diminished (p < 0.001 for both), whereas the latency of pupil contraction was significantly prolonged (p = 0.02).ConclusionsOur findings indicate that both static and dynamic pupillometry parameters are compromised in patients with acromegaly. Consequently, the use of an automatic pupillometry system in these patients may serve as a convenient, non-invasive screening method for assessing pupil function. Nonetheless, a more comprehensive understanding of these effects necessitates multicenter studies with larger sample sizes and longer durations.

Introduction

Acromegaly is a chronic, progressive disorder characterized by the excessive secretion of growth hormone (GH) and insulin-like growth factor 1 (IGF-I). While the condition typically results from a benign pituitary adenoma, it has also been documented to arise from glandular hyperplasia or ectopic secretion of pituitary hormones (GH/GHR) [1, 2]. The prevalence of acromegaly, which predominantly affects middle-aged adults, ranges from 3 to 70 cases per million. Prolonged exposure to elevated hormone levels leads to progressive somatic deformities and widespread systemic symptoms, which are associated with increased mortality [3–5].

Previous research has documented ophthalmological manifestations in patients with acromegaly, including proptosis, eyelid swelling, ophthalmoplegia, enlargement of extraocular muscles, and ptosis [6, 7]. Additionally, optic nerve and retinal nerve fiber layer (RNFL) damage have been observed, attributed to optic chiasm compression, enlarged retrobulbar tissues, and papilledema in individuals with acromegaly [8, 9].

The pupillary light reflex (PIR) is characterized by the pupil’s response to light, functioning as a component of the autonomic nervous system (ANS). The ANS includes the parasympathetic nervous system (PSS), which is responsible for pupil constriction, and the sympathetic nervous system (SNS), which facilitates pupil dilation through the radial muscles [10]. Disorders affecting the visual system may influence pupillary responses. The parasympathetic nervous system regulates the sphincter pupillae muscles, resulting in pupil constriction in response to increased light intensity. In contrast, the sympathetic nervous system activates the dilator pupillae muscles, leading to pupil dilation under low light conditions or during stress. Abnormalities in these pathways can lead to impaired pupillary light reflexes, potentially indicating underlying neurological or ophthalmic disorders [11–13]. Automatic pupillometry enables the objective and noninvasive measurement of both static and dynamic pupillary responses under varying light conditions [14, 15].

This technique enables precise quantification of pupillary light reflex parameters, including latency, amplitude, and velocity. It is extensively utilized in both clinical and research contexts to evaluate ANS function. Furthermore, automatic pupillometry aids in the early detection of neurological disorders by identifying subtle abnormalities in pupillary dynamics. In clinical practice, the evaluation of the ANS can be conducted using various invasive, labor-intensive methods, such as blood pressure measurement, heart rate variability, and blood pressure variability [16]. Therefore, we sought to ascertain whether there are any changes in pupillary responses, either dynamic or static, in patients with acromegaly by employing automatic pupillometry.

Materials and methods

Study design

In this cross-sectional prospective study, 100 participants were involved, comprising 50 individuals with acromegaly and 50 control subjects. Approval was secured from the ethics committee. Conducted in line with the Declaration of Helsinki, the study involved taking measurements after obtaining written consent from all participants. Patients diagnosed with acromegaly were referred from the Endocrinology Department to the Ophthalmology Department for an eye examination. Participants were eligible if they did not have any additional systemic diseases that could affect pupil function (e.g., parkinson’s disease, multiple sclerosis, other demyelinating diseases, or autonomic neuropathy), no iris or pupil abnormalities, no pseudoexfoliation, glaucoma, or inflammatory eye diseases, no history of intraocular surgery, and were not using systemic anticholinergic medications or eye drops that could influence pupillary reactions. A thorough ophthalmological assessment was performed on all participants.

Subject and measurements

Pupillary measurements were conducted using the MonPack One pupillometer (Metrovision, Pérenchies, France) set to Automatic mode, allowing for standardized and precise data acquisition across various lighting environments. All recordings were taken monocularly from the right eye of each participant. These measurements encompassed both static and dynamic parameters to comprehensively assess pupillary behavior. Static measurements included pupil diameter under scotopic, mesopic, and both low and high photopic light conditions, capturing the baseline size and light-adaptive changes of the pupil. Dynamic assessments involved detailed analysis of the pupillary light reflex characteristics such as resting pupil diameter, contraction amplitude and latency, contraction duration and velocity, as well as dilation latency, resting duration, and velocity, thereby providing insights into the functional responsiveness of the pupil to light stimuli. To ensure consistency and minimize variability, all measurements were performed by a single clinician between 09:00 and 12:00, thereby controlling for circadian influences known to affect pupillary responses. Three consecutive measurements were taken for each parameter, and the average of these measurements was taken to reduce the effect of transient fluctuations or measurement noise (Figure).

Statistical analysis

Statistical analyses were conducted using SPSS version 26.0 (Chicago, IL, USA). Descriptive statistics were applied to summarize the demographic characteristics of the study population, with continuous variables presented as means accompanied by their respective standard deviations to indicate variability within the data. To assess differences between groups, inferential statistical tests were employed: the independent t-test was used to compare means of continuous variables between two independent groups, while the chi-square test was utilized to examine associations between categorical variables. These methods allowed for a comprehensive evaluation of both central tendencies and relationships within the dataset, ensuring robust and reliable interpretation of the results.

Results

The mean age was found to be similar between the two groups in the study, with values of 44.80 ± 4.46 years and 42.52 ± 4.40 years, respectively, and a p-value of 0.85. The study cohort comprised 52 females (52.0%) and 48 males (48.0%) (Table 1). It was observed that the mesopic, low, and high photopic PD, which are parameters of static pupillometry, were significantly reduced in patients with acromegaly compared to the healthy group (p = 0.003, < 0.001, and p<;0.001, respectively) (Table 2). Examination of the dynamic pupil parameters revealed that the resting diameter, contraction amplitude, duration, and velocity were all significantly lower in acromegaly patients than in healthy controls (p<;0.001 for all values), as were the dilation latency and velocity (p < 0.001 for both values). However, the latency of pupillary contraction was higher in acromegaly patients compared to healthy subjects (p = 0.02) (Table 3). In patients with acromegaly, both the duration of the disease and IGF-1 levels showed a significant negative correlation with all static and dynamic pupillometry measurements, except for scotopic PD, mesopic PD, and the duration of pupil dilation (p < 0.001 for all values) (Table 4). The study demonstrated that patients with acromegaly exhibit significant alterations in both static and dynamic pupillometric parameters compared to healthy controls. Moreover, the analysis identified a significant negative correlation between disease duration and IGF-1 levels with nearly all pupillometric parameters, excluding scotopic and mesopic PD and the duration of pupil dilation (Fig. 1).

Discussion

In this study, we sought to determine whether there is a change in pupillary responses, which serve as an evaluative measure of the ANS in patients with acromegaly. Our findings indicate that both static and dynamic pupillometry parameters are impaired in individuals with acromegaly.

GH exerts significant autocrine and paracrine effects on various tissues, thereby influencing both physiological and pathological processes. These effects are mediated through GH’s interaction with tissue-specific receptors, which subsequently affect cellular functions such as proliferation, differentiation, metabolism, and repair. In conditions such as hyperhidrosis and goiter, GH signaling results in abnormal tissue growth or function, whereas in osteoarthritis and colon polyps, it influences tissue remodeling and inflammation. GH’s role in metabolic disorders and sleep apnea underscores its impact on systemic metabolic and respiratory functions [17, 18].

The tissue-specific expression of GH-related genes underpins its diverse local effects, including those on the cardiovascular and muscular systems. GH contributes to cardiac hypertrophy and hypertension by promoting myocardial growth and vascular remodeling. In diabetes mellitus, GH affects insulin sensitivity and glucose metabolism. Its involvement in arrhythmia, muscle weakness, and myopathy reflects its role in maintaining muscle integrity and electrophysiological stability. These localized actions highlight GH’s complex physiological roles beyond its classical endocrine functions, emphasizing the importance of gene expression patterns in tissue-specific outcomes [19–21].

Studies has indicated alterations in both central corneal thickness and intraocular pressure in patients with acromegaly [22]. Moreover, changes in corneal thickness and intraocular pressure may predispose patients to secondary ocular complications, underscoring the need for regular ophthalmologic monitoring. Retinal pigment epithelial cells in culture have been shown to express mRNA for the IGF receptor [23]. Furthermore, an increased concentration of IGF-I in the subretinal fluid has been observed in diabetic patients with acromegaly [24]. This condition has also been associated with vitreoretinal diseases, including proliferative diabetic retinopathy and retinal detachment [25, 26]. These ocular manifestations are thought to arise from the direct effects of excess growth hormone and IGF-I on orbital tissues.

Studies have identified ANS dysfunction through heart rate variability (HRV) analysis, establishing a correlation between decreased HRV and increased morbidity and mortality [27]. In the context of acromegaly, various cardiac autonomic functions and HRV indices have been reported as impaired, indicating a sympathovagal imbalance [28]. Additionally, these patients exhibit parasympathetic impairment and altered QT variability [29]. Other studies have demonstrated significant improvements in HRV parameters following treatment in these patients [30]. Furthermore, it has been observed that the cardiac autonomic response during physical exercise is compromised in individuals with acromegaly [31].

By analyzing pupillary responses, insights into the ANS, which regulates these reactions, can be obtained. The PNS predominantly facilitates pupillary constriction, whereas the SNS exerts a minimal influence. Consequently, the SNS determines the resting PD, while the PNS is influential in modulating PD under illuminated conditions and in the development of pupil function parameters in response to light. Nonetheless, both systems contribute to the re-expansion process. A delay in pupillary constriction under light conditions is indicative of PNS dysfunction, whereas a delay in pupillary dilation in dark conditions suggests SNS dysfunction. Research has indicated that abnormalities in pupillary responses can be detected prior to the manifestation of cardiovascular system symptoms associated with ANS dysfunction [32–34].

Studies have reported structural and microvascular retinal changes in acromegaly. OCT studies have shown that it may be accompanied by variable retinal nerve fiber layer (RNFL) measurements. Furthermore, layer-by-layer OCT analyses have highlighted potential subclinical changes in individual retinal layers in acromegaly [35, 36]. OCT angiography studies have reported that acromegaly may be associated with decreased macular capillary perfusion or ocular hypoperfusion [37].

It has been reported that between 45% and 80% of patients with acromegaly have Obstructive Sleep Apnea (OSAS) and even if these patients are treated, the risk of increased cardiovascular mortality continues in these patients [38]. There are also studies reporting an increase in blood pressure, ANS dysfunction, and urinary catecholamine levels in OSAS patients [39]. Similarly, the prevalence of diabetes mellitus in different series in acromegaly has been reported between 19 and 56% [40]. In addition, diabetic autonomic neuropathy (DAN) is a common complication affecting cardiovascular, gastrointestinal, genitourinary and ocular systems in patients with diabetes mellitus as a major cause of morbidity and mortality [41, 42]. We think our results, in which we detected impaired pupilometry values in these patients, are not a surprise due to the frequent coexistence of acromegaly with diseases such as OSAS and diabettes mellitus (DM), which can cause autonomic dysfunction. Therefore, our results in this study may lead to further studies to highlight the autonomic neuropathy mechanism that can occur in patients with acromegaly.

Nonetheless, multicenter studies with larger sample sizes and extended follow-up periods are necessary to gain a more comprehensive understanding of these effects.
