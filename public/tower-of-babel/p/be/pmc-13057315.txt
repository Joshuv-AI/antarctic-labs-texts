Ag2O-decorated TiO2 for ultrasensitive SERS detection of crystal violet
Authors: Jinghan Wang, Peng Hou, Qingwei Yao, Shuang Fu, Liyuan Chao, Huimin Sui, Lu Han, Jiafeng Wang, Guowei Wang, Hongguang Zhang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Surface-enhanced Raman scattering (SERS) technology has emerged as a powerful analytical tool for trace-level environmental pollutant detection, with its performance critically dependent on the physicochemical properties of substrate materials. While noble metal substrates (e.g., Au/Ag) demonstrate superior SERS enhancement through surface plasmon resonance (SPR), their practical deployment is hampered by inherent limitations including high material costs and susceptibility to oxidation-induced degradation. Herein, we report the rational design of Ag2O/TiO2 composite substrates via a facile wet-chemical precipitation method, wherein Ag2O nanoparticles are uniformly decorated onto pre-synthesized TiO2 nanospheres. Upon application in crystal violet (CV) detection, the optimized Ag2O/TiO2 composite substrate exhibits remarkable SERS performance with an enhancement factor (EF) of 6.7 × 106 and an ultra-low detection limit (LOD) of 1.0 nM (1.0 × 10⁻9 M). Systematic investigations reveal that the Ag2O modification induces photogenerated electron accumulation on the TiO2 surface, promoting photoinduced charge transfer (PICT) between the substrate and adsorbed CV molecules. This work not only presents a cost-effective and stable SERS platform for environmental contaminant monitoring but also provides mechanistic insights into the rational engineering of semiconductor-based SERS substrates with enhanced sensitivity and durability.

Introduction

Crystal violet (CV), a synthetic triphenylmethane dye, has been widely utilized in industrial processes including textile printing, dyeing, and food additive formulations due to its intense violet hue and economic viability1. However, extensive toxicological evaluations have revealed that CV exhibits multiple hazardous effects on human health, encompassing teratogenic, carcinogenic, mutagenic, and reproductive toxicities2. The molecular mechanisms underlying these toxicities involve three principal pathways: (1) intercalation into DNA double helices causing replication errors, (2) disruption of cellular redox balance through generation of reactive oxygen species (ROS), and (3) induction of mitochondrial dysfunction via cytochrome c oxidase inhibition. These findings have prompted stringent regulatory actions; notably, the European Chemicals Agency (ECHA) classified CV as a Substance of Very High Concern (SVHC) under Annex XIV of the EU REACH Regulation (EC No 1907/2006), imposing authorization requirements for its continued use. Despite these regulatory constraints, CV persists in environmental and food matrices, especially in aquaculture systems, where its potent antiseptic and broad-spectrum bactericidal properties promote illicit use to extend the shelf life of seafood3. Such practices raise significant public health concerns, as CV residues can bioaccumulate in the food chain, resulting in chronic exposure even at trace concentrations.

Conventional detection methodologies, such as high-performance liquid chromatography (HPLC) and spectrophotometry4,5, frequently exhibit inadequate sensitivity or selectivity for monitoring CV at concentrations below sub-ppb levels within complex matrices like food and water samples. This deficiency underscores the critical need for rapid, accurate, and field-deployable analytical platforms, including nanomaterial-based sensors, immunoassays, and advanced spectroscopic techniques, to mitigate risks and ensure compliance with global safety regulations. Surface-enhanced Raman scattering (SERS) spectroscopy has emerged as a powerful analytical tool, distinguished by its exceptional sensitivity, molecular fingerprinting specificity, minimal sample consumption, and label-free detection capabilities6,7. These attributes have positioned SERS as a versatile platform with extensive applications in environmental monitoring, food safety assurance, and biomedical diagnostics. Two essential mechanisms are generally recognized to underpin SERS enhancement: electromagnetic enhancement (EM) and chemical enhancement (CM)8–10. The EM mechanism originates primarily from localized surface plasmon resonance (LSPR). In contrast, the CM mechanism relies on the photo-induced dynamic charge-transfer (CT) effect occurring between probe molecules and the nanostructure substrate11. In the EM mechanism, noble metal substrates such as gold (Au) and silver (Ag) typically exhibit a pronounced SERS effect by leveraging the LSPR effect under visible light excitation12,13. However, their practical utility is constrained by limitations such as poor long-term stability, high material/fabrication costs, low reproducibility, and inadequate selectivity14. The advent of highly sensitive semiconductor materials has significantly broadened the repertoire of SERS substrates15. Notable candidates, including TiO216, MoO317, SnS218, and ZnO19 nanomaterials, have demonstrated promising SERS activity. Nevertheless, semiconductor-based SERS substrates typically exhibit markedly lower enhancement factors (EFs) in the ranging from 10 to 102 and limit of detection (LODs) values on the order of ~ 10⁻3 M compared to noble metal counterparts (EFs: ~106 to 1010; LODs: ~10⁻10 M). These performance gaps impede their widespread implementation in practical applications. Consequently, the development of innovative strategies to substantially enhance the SERS activity of semiconductor substrates remains a high-priority research area.

In semiconductor-based SERS systems, charge-transfer (CT) resonance serves as the primary enhancement mechanism. Recent investigations have revealed that metal-semiconductor heterojunctions can effectively modulate the population of surface-trapped electrons, leading to substantial enhancement of SERS performance20–22. The incorporation of metal nanoparticles introduces complexity in mechanistic interpretations, as overlapping plasmonic effects may mask the intrinsic contributions of the semiconductor component. Consequently, the development of purely semiconductor-based substrates that are devoid of plasmonic interference is critical for preserving the unique advantages of semiconductor materials in SERS applications. Semiconductor-semiconductor heterojunctions facilitate efficient separation of photogenerated electron-hole pairs, thus improving the utilization efficiency of charge carriers23,24. According to charge-transfer theory, such heterostructures promote photoinduced charge transfer (PICT) in semiconductor-molecule systems, resulting in enhanced SERS activity of semiconductor substrates. This mechanistic clarity underscores the potential of semiconductor-based architectures to advance SERS technologies beyond the limitations imposed by plasmonic materials.

In this study, TiO2 nanospheres were synthesized via a sol-gel method under controlled hydrolysis conditions, as confirmed by X-ray diffraction (XRD) and Fourier-transform infrared (FT-IR) spectroscopy. Subsequently, p-type semiconductor Ag2O nanoparticles were uniformly deposited onto the TiO2 surface through a facile chemical precipitation process at room temperature, resulting in a well-defined Ag2O/TiO2 heterojunction. The as-prepared Ag2O/TiO2 composite was then utilized as a highly sensitive SERS-active substrate for the detection of trace-level CV. Its reproducibility and SERS enhancement mechanisms were systematically investigated to elucidate the underlying physicochemical processes.

Experimental

Materials

All chemicals were of analytical grade and used without further purification. Tetrabutyl titanate (TBOT, ≥ 99.99%), absolute ethanol (EtOH, ≥ 99.8%), silver nitrate (AgNO3, ≥ 99.8%), sodium hydroxide (NaOH, ≥ 98.0%), and crystal violet (CV, ≥ 99.0%) were purchased from Shanghai Aladdin Biochemical Technology Co., Ltd. (Shanghai, China).

Methods

Synthesis of TiO2, Ag/TiO2, and Ag nanoparticles

In a typical synthesis procedure, 1.0 mL of tetrabutyl titanate (TBOT, 99.99% purity) was gradually added dropwise at a controlled rate of 0.2 mL/min to a homogeneous solution comprising 45 mL of absolute ethanol (≥ 99.8%) and 2.5 mL of deionized water (18.2 MΩ·cm resistivity). The reaction was conducted under vigorous magnetic stirring (600 rpm) at room temperature (25 ± 2 °C) for 6 h to facilitate complete hydrolysis. The resultant white precipitate was subsequently isolated via centrifugation at 8000 rpm for 5 min and subjected to four successive washing cycles using ethanol and deionized water to eliminate unreacted precursors and byproducts. Following purification, the product was dried in an oven at 80 °C for 12 h to yield the final TiO2 sample. For the preparation of Ag/TiO2 composite, 0.1 g of TiO2 powder was added to 20 mL of AgNO3 solution (1.0 × 10⁻3 mol/L) and then stirred in the dark for 15 min. The suspension was then irradiated for 0.5 h with a high-pressure mercury lamp (125 W) positioned vertically above the reaction vessel. The resulting solid product was collected by centrifugation, washed thoroughly with deionized water, and dried at 60 °C for 6 h to obtain the Ag/TiO2 composite sample. For comparison, a pure Ag sample was prepared under identical conditions but without the addition of TiO2.

Synthesis of Ag2O/TiO2 p-n heterojunction

The Ag2O/TiO2 p-n heterojunction nanocomposites were synthesized via a meticulously controlled wet-chemical precipitation method. In a typical procedure, a precisely weighed quantity of pre-synthesized TiO2 was uniformly dispersed in 20 mL of freshly prepared aqueous silver nitrate (AgNO3) solution (0.017 M, ≥ 99.0% purity) using ultrasonic treatment for 10 min. The resulting suspension was magnetically stirred (600 rpm) at room temperature (25 ± 2 °C) for 1 h under dark conditions to avoid premature photochemical reactions. Subsequently, 50 mL of sodium hydroxide (NaOH) solution (0.1 M, prepared from analytical grade pellets) was gradually introduced into the suspension using a peristaltic pump at a controlled flow rate of 1 mL/min, while maintaining vigorous stirring (600 rpm). The reaction was allowed to proceed for an additional 1 h in the dark to ensure complete precipitation of Ag2O nanoparticles onto the TiO2 surfaces. The resultant product was isolated via centrifugation at 8000 rpm for 10 min and sequentially washed three times with absolute ethanol (≥ 99.8%) and deionized water (18.2 MΩ·cm resistivity) to eliminate residual ions and byproducts. Finally, the purified Ag2O/TiO2 nanocomposites were dried in an oven at 60 °C for 12 h to yield the final material for subsequent characterization and application evaluations. For comparative analysis, pure Ag2O nanomaterials were also synthesized using only an aqueous AgNO3 solution (0.017 M, ≥ 99.0% purity) and NaOH solution (0.1 M, 50 mL) as reactants, following an identical precipitation protocol.

Preparation of SERS substrate

For the fabrication of SERS substrates, 0.01 g of the synthesized Ag2O/TiO2 composite material was dispersed in 30 mL of crystal violet (CV) solutions with varying concentrations (10⁻5, 10⁻6, 10⁻7, 10⁻8, 10⁻9, and 10⁻10 M) and subjected to ultrasonic treatment for 30 min to ensure uniform dispersion. Subsequently, 20 µL aliquots of the resulting suspension were drop-cast onto tin foil-coated glass slides and oven-dried at 60 °C overnight to yield the final Ag2O/TiO2 SERS-active substrates. The Ag/TiO2, TiO2, and Ag substrates were synthesized following the identical procedure described for the Ag2O/TiO2 composite, differing only in the starting materials used.

To evaluate the applicability of the Ag2O/TiO2 SERS substrate under realistic conditions, an additional experiment was conducted using tap water as a complex matrix. The composite was dispersed in a 1.0 × 10⁻6 M CV solution prepared with local tap water, which contained common interfering species such as dissolved ions and residual chlorine. The substrate was then fabricated following the same dispersion, drop-casting, and drying procedures, thereby simulating its performance in environmental water samples.

The reusability of the Ag2O/TiO2 substrate was evaluated through a cyclic stability test. For each cycle, a substrate loaded with CV (1.0 × 10⁻6 M) was filtered and transferred into deionized water, where it was irradiated under a 300 W xenon lamp for photocatalytic degradation of the adsorbed molecules. After irradiation, the substrate was thoroughly rinsed with deionized water, dried at ambient temperature, and then analyzed by Raman spectroscopy. This procedure defined one regeneration cycle, and a total of five consecutive cycles were performed.

Instruments

The crystalline structure of the as-prepared samples was characterized using powder X-ray diffraction (XRD; Shimadzu XRD-6000) using Cu-Kα radiation (λ = 0.15405 nm) at a scan rate of 2°⋅min− 1 over the 2θ range of 10–80°. Morphological analysis was conducted using a CIQTEK SEM5000X scanning electron microscope (SEM) (Hefei, China). Fourier-transform infrared (FT-IR) spectroscopy was performed on a Nicolet iS50 spectrometer (Thermo Fisher Scientific, Waltham, MA, USA). Diffuse reflectance ultraviolet-visible (UV-Vis DRS) spectra were recorded using a Hitachi U-4100 spectrophotometer (Hitachi High-Tech, Tokyo, Japan). Photoelectrochemical (PEC) and electrochemical (EC) measurements were performed using an IVIUM V13806 electrochemical workstation with a conventional three-electrode system employed. Furthermore, the surface-enhanced Raman scattering (SERS) response activity of the substrate toward crystal violet solutions with varying concentrations was evaluated using a Renishaw inVia Reflex spectrometer system equipped with a 532 nm laser source. Raman spectra were acquired at room temperature using a ×10 objective lens to focus the laser light (10 mW) to a ∼1 μm spot on the sample. Each spectrum was recorded with an integration time of 1 s, and the signal was accumulated over 10 cycles (resulting in a total accumulation time of 10 s per spectrum). To assess SERS reproducibility, spectra were collected from 20 randomly selected positions across the substrate, covering the spectral range 500–1750 cm⁻1.

Results and discussion

XRD analysis

The phase structures of pure Ag2O, pure TiO2, and Ag2O/TiO2 p-n heterojunction were characterized by X-ray diffraction (XRD), as illustrated in Fig. 1. For pure Ag2O (Fig. 1a), the diffraction peaks observed at 2θ values of 33°, 38.3°, 55.3°, and 66° correspond to the (111), (200), (220), and (311) planes of the cubic Ag2O phase, respectively (JCPDS No. 41–1104). In contrast, the characteristic peaks of pure TiO2 at 2θ = 25.3° (101), 37.8° (004), 47.8° (200), 54.5° (211), 62.8° (204), 69.5° (220), and 75.3° (215) are unambiguously assigned to the anatase crystalline phase of TiO2 (JCPDS No. 21–1272) (Fig. 1b). In the Ag2O/TiO2 heterojunction pattern (Fig. 1c), the characteristic peak at ~ 38.3° exhibits a clear positive shift of ~ 0.5° compared to the pure TiO2 (004) peak at 37.8°. This shifted peak is therefore attributed to the overlap of the TiO2 (004) reflection and the Ag2O (200) reflection. The observed shift, together with the reported assignment of the peak near 38.4° to the (200) plane of Ag2O in similar composites25, confirms the presence of Ag2O. Furthermore, the absence of the strongest Ag2O peak at ~ 33° is consistent with literature observations for moderate loadings where this peak can become weak26. Furthermore, the XRD pattern of the p-n heterojunction exhibits no discernible peak shift for the anatase phase, only a slight reduction in peak intensity, indicating that Ag2O nanoparticles are surface-deposited on TiO2 without lattice incorporation (Fig. 1c).

FT-IR spectra analysis

To further verify the formation of the Ag2O/TiO2 p-n heterostructure, FTIR spectra of Ag2O, TiO2, and Ag2O/TiO2 were measured, as illustrated in Fig. 2. The broad peak at ~ 3300 cm⁻1 and the sharp peak at ~ 1629 cm⁻1 is attributable to the stretching and bending vibrations of O-H bonds from adsorbed water molecules, respectively. The broad absorption band observed in the 400–900 cm⁻1 region corresponds to Ti-O stretching and Ti-O-Ti bridging stretching modes27, confirming the effective formation of TiO2. The FTIR spectrum of pure Ag2O exhibits two characteristic peaks at 1371 and 1453 cm⁻1, assigned to Ag-O vibrational modes28. After the formation of the p-n heterostructure, the Ag2O/TiO2 sample displays not only the characteristic peaks of TiO2 but also a distinct vibrational peak at ~ 1374 cm⁻1, attributable to the Ag-O characteristic vibration.

SEM analysis

The surface morphologies of pristine TiO2 and the Ag2O/TiO2 p-n heterostructure were investigated through characterization using a field-emission scanning electron microscope (SEM). As depicted in Fig. 3a and b, the synthesized TiO2 exhibits a well-defined and uniform spherical morphology. Quantitative analysis based on the particle size histogram (Fig. 3c) reveals a narrow size distribution with an average diameter of 2.30 μm and a standard deviation of 0.45 μm, confirming the uniformity of the microspheres. Furthermore, the microspheres possess smooth surfaces and are interconnected via mutual adhesion, forming a continuous network-like structure. After the deposition of Ag2O, a remarkable transformation in the surface morphology of TiO2 microspheres is clearly discernible (Fig. 3d and e). The surface of the TiO2 microspheres becomes significantly roughened, with flocculent Ag2O particles being deposited on their surfaces. Statistical analysis of the composite (Fig. 3f) shows a similarly uniform distribution, characterized by an increased mean particle diameter of 2.88 μm and a standard deviation of 0.32 μm.

Ultraviolet-visible absorption spectroscopy analysis

The optical properties of pure Ag2O, pure TiO2, and the Ag2O/TiO2 p-n heterostructure were characterized using ultraviolet-visible (UV-vis) absorption spectroscopy. As depicted in Fig. 4a, pure Ag2O exhibited strong light absorption across the entire measured wavelength range (250–800 nm), whereas pure TiO2 displayed optical absorption only below 450 nm. Notably, the deposition of Ag2O nanoparticles onto TiO2 surfaces significantly enhanced the visible-light response of the resulting Ag2O/TiO2 p-n heterojunction compared to pristine TiO2. The bandgap energies (Eg) were determined by calculating the Kubelka-Munk function. As shown in Fig. 4b and d, the calculated bandgaps of TiO2, Ag2O, and the Ag2O/TiO2 p-n heterojunction were 2.75 eV, 1.42 eV, and 1.96 eV, respectively. The reduction in the bandgap of the heterojunction compared to that of pure TiO2 indicates a modification of the electronic structure during heterojunction formation.

SERS activity of Ag2O/TiO2 substrates

Crystal violet (CV), a potentially hazardous organic dye posing risks to the environment and food safety, was selected as the Raman probe to investigate the surface-enhanced Raman scattering (SERS) performance of the Ag2O/TiO2 p-n heterojunction for trace detection. Upon excitation with a 532 nm laser, the Ag2O/TiO2 p-n heterojunction probe adsorbed with CV molecules exhibited characteristic Raman vibrational bands of CV, as shown in Fig. 5a. As summarized in Table 1, the Raman band at 913 cm⁻1 is assigned to the ring skeletal vibration of the radical orientation. The bands at 730, 760, and 808 cm⁻1 correspond to out-of-plane bending vibrations of the ring C-H bonds, while the band at 1177 cm⁻1 is attributed to in-plane vibration of the ring C-H bond. The peak at 1371 cm⁻1 is assigned to N-phenyl stretching, whereas the bands at 1294, 1533, 1584, and 1619 cm⁻1 are associated with aromatic ring C-C stretching modes. The Raman signal intensity of CV decreased with decreasing CV concentration (Fig. 5a). Even at an ultralow concentration of 1.0 × 10⁻9 M, the characteristic Raman vibration peaks of CV at 1294, 1371, and 1584 cm⁻1 remained clearly observable, indicating the excellent trace detection performance of the Ag2O/TiO2 p-n heterojunction. However, when the CV concentration was further reduced to 1.0 × 10⁻10 M, these diagnostic vibrational features were no longer detectable. This result demonstrates that the limit of detection (LOD) for CV molecules on the Ag2O/TiO2 p-n heterojunction reaches 1.0 nM (1.0 × 10⁻9 M). Figure 5b and c illustrate the relationship between the logarithmic concentration of CV and the SERS spectral intensity at 1177 cm⁻1 and 1584 cm⁻1, respectively. A highly linear response is clearly observed within the concentration range of 1.0 × 10⁻5−1.0 × 10⁻9 M. For the peak at 1177 cm⁻1, the linear equation is given by I = 137019.2 + 15142.8Lg(c), with a coefficient of determination (R2) of 0.98383 (where c represents the CV concentration). Similarly, for the peak at 1584 cm⁻1, the linear relationship is I = 144256.4 + 15521.1Lg(c), with R2 = 0.99571. These results confirm the excellent SERS performance of the Ag2O/TiO2 p-n heterojunction substrate. The linear correlation enables reliable quantitative SERS detection, highlighting the significant potential of the Ag2O/TiO2 p-n heterojunction substrate for analytical applications utilizing SERS technology. To further assess the practical applicability of the proposed SERS substrate, the detection performance of CV was investigated in real tap water as a representative complex matrix. As shown in Fig. 5d, the characteristic Raman peaks of CV (1.0 × 10⁻6 M) remained clearly identifiable when measured in tap water using the Ag2O/TiO2 substrate, confirming its detection capability in the presence of common background constituents. Notably, the Raman intensity of CV in tap water was lower than that in deionized water, which may be attributed to potential interference from chlorine or other reactive species in tap water that could partially reduce the SERS activity of the substrate. This observation underscores the method’s sensitivity to matrix effects while demonstrating its functionality in non-ideal sample environments. These results provide preliminary evidence supporting the feasibility of applying the Ag2O/TiO2 p-n heterojunction substrate for SERS-based detection in real-world scenarios.

SERS activity of TiO2, Ag, Ag/TiO2, and Ag2O/TiO2 substrates

To investigate whether the enhancement of the CV Raman signal is associated with the formation of the Ag2O/TiO2 p-n heterostructure, the SERS performance of a pure TiO2 substrate toward CV was evaluated, as shown in Fig. 7a. As expected, pure TiO2 exhibited significantly weaker SERS activity compared to the Ag2O/TiO2 p-n heterojunction. Under 532 nm laser excitation, the minimum detectable CV concentration for pure TiO2 was only 1.0 × 10− 6 M, which is markedly inferior to the 1.0 × 10− 9 M detection limit achieved by the Ag2O/TiO2 p-n heterojunction. However, compared to the Raman signal of CV in the absence of any substrate, the presence of the pure TiO2 substrate still significantly amplified the Raman signal of CV (at 1.0 × 10− 4 M). The Raman enhancement observed for 1.0 × 10− 4 M CV on TiO2 is significantly more intense than that of 1.0 × 10− 4 M pure CV molecules in the absence of any substrate and is even comparable to the enhancement observed for 0.1 M pure CV molecules without a substrate. This enhancement is primarily attributed to chemical mechanisms, including photoinduced charge transfer at the CV-TiO2 interface, which increases the Raman polarizability of surface-adsorbed CV molecules. Figure 7b presents a comparative analysis of the SERS enhancement performance of Ag, Ag/TiO2, and Ag2O/TiO2 substrates toward 1.0 × 10− 6 M CV under 532 nm laser excitation. Among them, the pure Ag substrate demonstrates the strongest SERS enhancement. This prominent enhancement is predominantly ascribed to the EM, arising from the intense LSPR of the Ag nanostructures. The Ag2O/TiO2 heterostructure substrate shows moderate but clearly distinguishable enhancement, which is notably superior to that of the Ag/TiO2 composite. In contrast, the Ag/TiO2 composite exhibits the weakest SERS signal under the same experimental conditions, likely due to the absence of this optimized charge-transfer pathway and a less favorable interfacial structure.

Based on the EF calculation formula and the experimental data presented in Table 2, the EF of the Ag2O/TiO2 substrate for the CV solution was determined to be 6.7 × 106. This enhancement effect is significantly stronger than that of previously reported semiconductor nanomaterials and is even comparable to the performance of noble metal substrates with hot spots documented in the literature. A systematic comparison in Table 3 indicates that the Ag2O/TiO2 substrate developed in this work achieves a lower detection limit (1.0 × 10⁻9 M) compared to previously reported Ag2O/TiO2 systems (4-MBA), along with a higher enhancement factor (6.7 × 106) than composites such as Ag/PPy/TiO2. Although certain newly developed substrates (BP/Ag/TNR) exhibit outstanding performance in a single metric, their fabrication typically involves more complex processes. The key advance of this work lies in the straightforward synthesis of a high-performance Ag2O/TiO2 heterojunction, which achieves a well‑balanced combination of high sensitivity, low detection limit, and excellent stability, offering a practical and feasible material platform for real‑world SERS applications.

Enhancement mechanism

The formation of the Ag2O/TiO2 p-n heterojunction and the charge transfer between them were confirmed through photoelectrochemical experiments (Fig. 9). The photocurrent curves (Fig. 9a) demonstrate that the photocurrent density of Ag2O/TiO2 is significantly higher than that of pure TiO2, providing compelling evidence that the fabricated p-n heterojunction markedly enhances charge separation. Notably, under 530 nm monochromatic light illumination (close to the 532 nm laser used in our SERS measurements), Ag2O/TiO2 still exhibits a substantially enhanced photocurrent compared to TiO2 (Fig. 9b). This result directly confirms that Ag2O contributes to visible-light harvesting and charge generation at the SERS operational wavelength, further supporting the role of the heterojunction in the charge-separation process. Furthermore, the efficiency of interfacial charge migration was evaluated using electrochemical impedance spectroscopy (EIS). As illustrated in the inset of Fig. 9a, the arc radius of Ag2O/TiO2 is smaller than that of TiO2, indicating a lower charge transfer resistance in the heterojunction, which facilitates the rapid separation and transfer of charge carriers.

The band structures of TiO2 and Ag2O were investigated through Mott-Schottky measurements. The positive slope of the Mott-Schottky plot indicates that TiO2 is an n-type semiconductor, while the negative slope of the Mott-Schottky plot suggests that Ag2O is a p-type semiconductor. According to the Mott-Schottky plots, the flat band potentials (Efb) of TiO2 and Ag2O were determined to be −0.62 and + 1.54 V vs. Ag/AgCl, respectively. By converting the reference electrode from Ag/AgCl to the normal hydrogen electrode (NHE) at pH = 7, the conduction band (CB) potential of TiO2 and the valence band (VB) potential of Ag2O were further deduced to be −0.42 and + 1.74 V vs. NHE, respectively. The conduction band potential (ECB) of TiO2 and the valence band potential (EVB) of Ag2O were derived to be approximately − 4.08 eV and − 6.24 eV vs. vac, respectively. Therefore, based on the calculated semiconductor bandgap energies (Fig. 4b and c) and the empirical formula Eg = EVB − ECB, the EVB of TiO2 and the ECB of Ag2O were calculated to be − 6.83 eV and − 4.82 eV vs. vac, respectively. Based on the calculated band structure and relevant literature reports, prior to the contact between Ag2O and TiO2, the Fermi level (Ef) of n-type TiO2 is slightly below the ECB of TiO2, while the Ef of p-type Ag2O is slightly above the EVB of Ag2O. Upon contact between n-type TiO2 and p-type Ag2O, the Ef of p-type Ag2O and n-type TiO2 align to form a continuous new Ef, thereby establishing a p-n heterojunction at the interface26. Due to the differences in the relative positions of the ECB and EVB with respect to the Fermi level (Ef) in Ag2O and TiO2, a new band structure is formed in the interfacial region, as illustrated in Fig. 10c. The formation of this functional p-n heterojunction is directly confirmed by the rectifying I-T characteristics and the significantly reduced charge-transfer resistance in the EIS Nyquist plot (Fig. 9a). When the sample is irradiated with a 532 nm laser (2.33 eV), four photoinduced charge transfer (PICT) processes occur: (1) electrons from the VB of p-Ag2O are excited to the CB and subsequently transferred to the low-energy LUMO of CV (PICT1); (2) electrons are transferred from the HOMO of CV to the CB of n-TiO2 (PICT2); (3) the excited electrons located in the CB of p-Ag2O can be transferred to the CB of n-TiO2, and the electrons trapped and accumulated in the CB of n-TiO2 can be further excited to the LUMO level of CV (PICT3); and (4) direct electron transfer from the VB of p-Ag2O to the LUMO of CV (PICT4). The feasibility of these PICT pathways is underpinned by the energy band diagram constructed from the Mott-Schottky analysis (Fig. 10a and b), while their exceptional efficiency is corroborated by the markedly enhanced photocurrent response of the Ag2O/TiO2 heterojunction under 530 nm irradiation (Fig. 9b), compared to pure TiO2. Collectively, these PICT processes, facilitated by the rationally designed heterojunction, induce alterations in the electron cloud distribution of the CV molecules, consequently leading to the enhancement of Raman signals.

Conclusion

In summary, this study designed and fabricated a novel Ag2O/TiO2 p-n heterojunction SERS substrate via a simple solution-based process. Under 532 nm laser excitation, the Ag2O/TiO2 substrate demonstrated exceptional SERS activity toward CV molecules, achieving an EF of 6.7 × 106, a detection limit as low as 1.0 nM (1.0 × 10⁻9 M), and an RSD below 5%. The observed SERS enhancement on the Ag2O/TiO2 p-n heterojunction significantly surpasses that of the pure TiO2 substrate, underscoring the synergistic benefits of the heterostructure. The remarkable SERS enhancement is attributed to the fact that four distinct PICT processes are triggered. These involve sequential electron transfers among the VB and CB of p-Ag2O, the HOMO and LUMO of CV, and the CB of n-TiO2. Such PICT processes induce significant changes in the electron cloud distribution of CV molecules, ultimately resulting in the enhancement of their Raman signals. Critically, the entire fabrication is conducted at room temperature and based on solution processing, which endows this substrate with inherent cost-effectiveness and strong scalability potential. This work provides a foundational approach for developing high-performance SERS substrates suitable for ultra-sensitive pollutant monitoring and other analytical applications.
