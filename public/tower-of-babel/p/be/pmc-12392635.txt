Redefining intimacy: a qualitative study on sexual function experiences and perspectives among migrant and refugee women in South Australia
Authors: Negin Mirzaei Damabi, Jodie C Avery, Mumtaz Begum, Salima Meherali, Zohra S Lassi
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundSexual function is a fundamental aspect of sexual health, yet migrant and refugee women from Low- and Middle-Income Countries (LMICs) often face unique challenges in navigating intimacy and sexual function post-migration. While sociocultural norms, migration-related stressors, and healthcare access influence their experiences, yet these perspectives remain underexplored.MethodsThis qualitative exploratory study explored the perspectives and experiences of sexual function among first-generation migrant and refugee women from low- and middle-income countries residing in South Australia. Semi-structured interviews were conducted with 20 reproductive-aged cisgender heterosexual women from diverse cultural backgrounds, recruited through multiple culturally appropriate approaches including community organisations supporting migrant and refugee populations and online outreach via the purpose-built RISE website. Although multilingual support and professional interpreters were available, all participants opted to be interviewed in English. Interviews were conducted by a trained qualitative researcher with shared migrant background, and reflexive, inductive thematic analysis guided by social constructivist epistemology was used to identify key influences on sexual health and intimacy.ResultsParticipants reported that cultural norms, sociocultural expectations, and migration-related stressors shaped their sexual experiences. Many described difficulties in navigating cultural taboos, communication barriers, and limited access to culturally sensitive healthcare services. Conversely, while migration provided opportunities for increased sexual autonomy, self-discovery, and improved partner communication, deeply ingrained cultural beliefs and emotional struggles continued to impact their sexual well-being.ConclusionsThe findings highlight the need for culturally sensitive, gender-appropriate sexual health services and the removal of financial, linguistic, and systemic barriers to healthcare access. Healthcare providers, policymakers, and community organisations play a crucial role in fostering inclusive environments that support migrant and refugee women’s sexual health and well-being.Keywords: Sexual function, Migrant, Refugee, Women

Background

Sexual health is a fundamental aspect of overall wellbeing and quality of life, and the World Health Organization (WHO) defines it as “a state of physical, emotional, mental and social well-being in relation to sexuality” that requires a positive and respectful approach to sexual relationships, and the possibility of pleasurable and safe sexual experiences, free of coercion, discrimination, and violence [1]. Female Sexual Dysfunction (FSD) is a central component of sexual health and refers to persistent problems with sexual desire, arousal, orgasm, or sexual pain that lead to significant distress or interpersonal difficulty [2]. Understanding the scope and variation of FSD is crucial, as research demonstrates its widespread prevalence across diverse populations. A recent systematic review and meta-analysis encompassing 95 studies and over 105,000 premenopausal women worldwide estimated the global pooled prevalence of FSD at approximately 41% (95% CI 37.1–44.7%), with significant variation in rates depending on geographic regions, age groups, and diagnostic criteria [3]. In Australia, 20.6% of women aged 18–39 years were found to have at least one clinically significant sexual dysfunction, and 50.2% reported sexually related personal distress, according to a large community-based study involving 6986 participants [4].

While these statistics highlight the widespread nature of sexual health challenges, it is important to recognise that the risk of experiencing poor sexual health is not equally distributed. Women from migrant and refugee backgrounds face unique and compounding challenges that increase their vulnerability to sexual health difficulties [5]. These challenges are shaped by intersecting factors such as displacement, sociocultural dislocation, gender norms, and systemic marginalisation in host countries [6]. Such factors often compromise both access to Sexual and Reproductive Health (SRH) services and the ability to engage in safe, informed, and autonomous sexual relationships [7]. Consequently, research has consistently reported that migrant and refugee women are more likely to experience lower levels of sexual knowledge, reduced sexual desire and arousal, and heightened levels of sexual dissatisfaction [8]. These disparities are often rooted in pre-migration experiences and cultural contexts. Evidence from global literature indicates that many migrant and refugee women arrive in host countries with limited sexual health knowledge and conservative attitudes toward sexuality [9]. This is often shaped by sociocultural environments where sex-related discussions are considered taboo and sexual education is either absent or restricted [10].

This research gap is particularly significant in the Australian context, given the nation’s increasing cultural diversity. As of 2024, 31.5% of the Australian population was born overseas (and 29.8% of South Australians, where this study was conducted, according to the 2021 Census), and more than half have at least one parent born abroad, with large and growing communities originating from South Asia, the Middle East, Africa, and Southeast Asia [11]. Within these groups, women from Low- and Middle-Income Countries (LMICs) backgrounds face layered disparities stemming not only from migration status but also from pre-existing gender inequities, socioeconomic disadvantage, visa precarity, and racialisation [11–13]. Although Australia is often regarded as having a comprehensive healthcare system, these women remain underserved in SRH contexts [14]. Barriers such as language, cost, cultural taboos, and fear of stigma hinder access to appropriate care and restrict open conversations about sexual wellbeing [8].

Given these challenges and the notable absence of Australian-based research examining sexual function among LMIC migrant and refugee women, this study explores the lived experiences of first-generation migrant and refugee women in South Australia as they navigate sexual health following migration.

Methods

Study design and aim

This qualitative exploratory study was conducted to examine the perspectives and experiences of sexual function among migrant and refugee women in South Australia. Specifically, the study sought to understand participants’ knowledge of sexual function, changes in sexual function post-migration, and their experiences engaging with healthcare providers, including the facilitators and barriers they encountered.

A qualitative exploratory approach was chosen because it is well-suited for examining under-researched and sensitive topics, such as sexual health, where limited prior knowledge exists. This design enables an in-depth exploration of participants’ lived experiences, allowing themes and insights to emerge naturally from the data without relying on pre-existing theories or frameworks [15].

Participants and eligibility

The study recruited 20 reproductive-aged women (18–49 years) from migrant and refugee backgrounds who had migrated from LMICs and were currently residing in South Australia. Purposive sampling was employed to recruit individuals with direct lived experience relevant to the study aims. Eligibility criteria included being a first-generation migrant or refugee, identifying as a cisgender heterosexual woman, and sexually active (defined as having had sexual intercourse within the past four weeks [16]. Women who were born in Australia, those living with intellectual disabilities, and those unwilling to participate were excluded from the study.

This study intentionally focused on cisgender heterosexual women to maintain cultural and experiential consistency in exploring sexual function changes before and after migration. In most LMIC contexts, heterosexuality is not only normative but often institutionally and culturally enforced, while same-sex relationships remain stigmatised, criminalised, or hidden [17]. Therefore, the inclusion of sexual minority women, who may have concealed or constrained pre-migration sexual experiences, could have made comparative analysis across time and context less meaningful. This approach allowed for direct examination of shifts in sexual autonomy, intimacy, and health-seeking behaviours within the dominant sociocultural paradigm of participants’ countries of origin [18]. Furthermore, recent evidence confirms that sexual minority women report significantly different patterns of sexual well-being and distress [19], which suggests that sexual minority women’s experiences warrant separate and dedicated qualitative exploration, rather than inclusion within studies focused on heterosexual trajectories. Given the sensitivity of the research topic and recruitment challenges within marginalised populations, this focused approach ensured analytical clarity, participant comfort, and cultural coherence.

Sample size was guided by a reflexive approach to adequacy, prioritising the richness, diversity, and depth of the data rather than adherence to a fixed numerical target. In line with qualitative principles, data collection was concluded when the dataset offered sufficient complexity and variation to support the development of coherent, well-supported themes that meaningfully addressed the research aims [20].

Recruitment strategy

Recognising the sensitive and culturally taboo nature of the topic, recruitment utilised multiple culturally appropriate approaches to ensure accessibility and trust-building. Flyers with the heading “We want to hear your story” were distributed through organisations supporting migrant and refugee populations, including the Australian Migrant Resource Centre, International Organisation for Migration, Refugee Advocacy Service of South Australia, Australian Refugee Association, Vinnies Refugee & Asylum Seeker Service, and FBW Gynaecology Plus Clinic. Social media outreach through institutional networks, such as the University of Adelaide, Robinson Research Institute (RRI), and Healthy Development Adelaide (HDA), further complemented these efforts. To enhance accessibility and participant engagement, the research team developed the Refugee and Immigrants Sexual Health Endeavour (RISE) website which featured multi-language translation capabilities, comprehensive study information, an online appointment booking system, and downloadable participant information sheets.

Interview protocol development

The development of the interview protocol was grounded in a comprehensive methodological framework. As there were no directly comparable instruments addressing the sexual function of migrant and refugee women in the Australian context, the interview schedule was developed specifically for this study. A foundational component of this development was our prior scoping review which systematically synthesised evidence and identified conceptual and empirical gaps in the literature [8]. This review revealed key thematic domains such as cultural taboos, migration-related stressors, healthcare inaccessibility, and sexual agency which directly informed the content and structure of the interview guide.

Additionally, the development of the interview schedule was informed by Berry’s Acculturation Theory, which posits that migrants adopt different strategies to negotiate the relationship between their heritage culture and the dominant culture of the host country, namely: integration, assimilation, separation, and marginalisation [21]. This framework is especially relevant for understanding how cultural values, sexual beliefs, and healthcare behaviours are transformed, maintained, or resisted post-migration. The theory was selected for its utility in exploring the dynamic, bidirectional process of cultural adaptation, particularly in areas such as gender roles, intimacy, and healthcare-seeking practices, which are often heavily influenced by cultural and social norms. Guided by this model, the interview schedule was designed to explore shifts in sexual function and behaviour in response to changing cultural contexts, as well as the barriers to and facilitators of access to SRH healthcare.

The interview schedule was divided into two main sections: demographic questions gathered basic information about participants’ age, ethnicity, migration history, marital status, and other relevant background details. While thematic questions consisted of open-ended questions that explored participants’ experiences and perceptions of sexual function, focusing on cultural influences, migration-related changes, and interactions with healthcare services. Data collection procedures

Semi-structured interviews were conducted in participants’ preferred languages to ensure comfort, inclusivity, and the establishment of trust. The research team’s multilingual capabilities, including fluency in Urdu, Persian, Hindi, and Dari, enabled direct communication with participants, while professional interpreters were engaged when additional language support was needed. For participants speaking languages beyond the team’s expertise, arrangements for qualified translators were made. However, all participants opted to conduct their interviews in English. All interviews were conducted by the first author (NMD), a trained qualitative researcher with a shared migrant background and substantial experience in sensitive, cross-cultural interviewing.

Each interview lasted approximately 40 to 60 min, depending on participant engagement and comfort. Interviews were conducted either in-person or virtually, depending on participants’ preferences and logistical needs. Private, safe settings were prioritised to facilitate open and honest discussions, recognising the personal and sensitive nature of the topic. Interviews were audio-recorded with participants’ consent and transcribed verbatim for analysis.

Data analysis

Reflexive, inductive thematic analysis, guided by social constructivist epistemology, was employed to identify, analyse, and interpret patterns within the interview data [22]. This approach followed the six-phase framework outlined by Braun and Clarke’s Reflexive Thematic Analysis, which emphasises active researcher interpretation, inductive coding, and reflexivity throughout the process [22]. The analysis process was systematic and iterative. Initially, team members independently reviewed a subset of interview transcripts to generate first-order codes, such as “sexual function changes after migration,” “how Australia is different,” and “barriers to accessing sexual health services.” These codes were then discussed, refined, and organised into broader themes during team meetings to ensure a consistent analytical approach.

In addition to conducting the interviews, the first author also led the data analysis. This dual role is a widely accepted practice in qualitative research and, particularly in reflexive thematic analysis, is recognised for enhancing analytic rigour. As Braun and Clarke (2021) note, when researchers are deeply involved in both data collection and interpretation, they are better positioned to engage reflexively with participants’ accounts, make sense of the data in context, and interpret meaning beyond surface-level content. Therefore, this continuity between interviewer and analyst enabled a more nuanced understanding of participants’ experiences and strengthened the credibility and coherence of the thematic findings [23].

NVivo software was used to facilitate data management and coding. Themes were developed through continuous movement between data, codes, and conceptual categories. Reflexivity was central to the process, with the research team actively reflecting on their own cultural positions and how these shaped interpretation. This approach supported rich, layered thematic development aligned with the principles of interpretive qualitative inquiry.

Ethical considerations

This study was conducted in accordance with the National Statement on Ethical Conduct in Human Research and the Declaration of Helsinki [24, 25], and was approved by the University of Adelaide Human Ethics Research Committee (approval: H-2024-011). All participants were provided with an information sheet outlining the study’s purpose, procedures, potential risks, confidentiality measures, and their rights as participants. They were given the opportunity to ask questions before providing written informed consent. Verbal consent was also reaffirmed at the beginning of each interview to ensure ongoing willingness to participate.

Participants were informed that their involvement was voluntary and that they could withdraw at any time without consequence. However, due to the nature of qualitative research, it was explained that data already collected could not be withdrawn after the interview was completed. All participants also provided written consent for the publication of anonymised data with assurance that no identifying information would be included in any published material. All signed consent forms are stored securely.

To acknowledge the time and emotional labour involved, participants received a $50 gift card after the interview. This approach aligns with NHMRC guidelines, which support modest reimbursement for time, effort, and inconvenience, provided it does not constitute coercion or undue influence [26].

Researchers’ reflexivity

The research team employed several strategies to ensure both methodological rigour and cultural sensitivity throughout the study. A shared migrant background among team members, including the lead researcher, provided significant cultural insights that enriched the study. This common experience facilitated a deeper understanding of the participants’ lived realities, fostering empathy and trust, which were especially important when discussing sensitive topics such as sexual health.

While cultural familiarity proved to be a valuable asset, the research team remained mindful of its potential drawbacks, often described as a “double-edged sword” [27]. Participants may have assumed the researchers’ familiarity with their experiences, potentially leading to less detailed or nuanced disclosures during interviews. To mitigate this risk, the researchers emphasised open-ended questioning, active listening, and the importance of participants sharing their unique perspectives, ensuring their voices were fully heard and understood.

To ensure inclusivity and respect for participants’ diverse needs, interviews were offered in participants’ preferred languages, and access to study materials was made available via the multilingual RISE website. This platform enabled participants to access comprehensive information, further enhancing the study’s accessibility.

The multilingual RISE website and interviews offered in participants’ preferred languages ensured inclusivity and respect for participants’ diverse needs. The lead researcher maintained a reflexive approach throughout the study, continuously reflecting on their positionality and the impact it may have on data collection and interpretation. By prioritising the participant comfort and agency, the researcher created a space where participants felt heard and respected. This participant-centered approach was crucial in navigating conversations around sexual health, a subject often considered taboo in many cultural contexts.

Challenges encountered during recruitment, such as cultural sensitivities and stigma surrounding the topic of sexual health, were met with proactive strategies. The team demonstrated the study’s commitment to confidentiality and clearly communicated its objectives, providing transparency to potential participants. Collaboration with local community organisations played a pivotal role in building trust, while multilingual materials and the RISE website ensured accessibility for participants from varied linguistic backgrounds.

Additionally, post-interview support was offered to participants through information on sexual health services, both online and in-person, ensuring they had access to continued support if necessary.

Themes and sub-themes identified from thematic analysis

These themes collectively provide insight into the complex and deeply personal experiences of sexual function among migrant and refugee women in South Australia. The findings illustrate how pre- and post-migration factors interact to shape women’s perceptions, behaviours, and access to care.

Theme 1—cultural and sociocultural factors influencing sexual function

The analysis revealed that cultural beliefs and practices profoundly influenced participants’ sexual experiences, attitudes, and behaviours. This influence unfolded across three interconnected subthemes: [1] cultural norms and family expectations in the country of origin [2], the consequences of cultural practices such as Female Genital Mutilation (FGM), and [3] the impact of host-country norms on sexual health and function. Each subtheme demonstrates how deeply rooted cultural frameworks shaped the way women understood, experienced, and navigated intimacy.

Subtheme 1.1: cultural norms and family expectations in the country of origin

Cultural norms and family expectations significantly shaped participants’ understanding and communication about sexual function, especially in cultures where discussing sexuality is taboo or even considered illegal. Particularly in some cultures, particularly in the Middle East and South Asia, sexuality remains a highly sensitive subject. One participant described the severity of this taboo in her country:

“In my country, sexual activity is very much taboo. Even now in 2024, it’s getting a little better, but still… it’s not something we discuss openly. It’s especially difficult when you’re not married—then it becomes not just taboo but socially unacceptable. You have to hide relationships from your family, from police, from everyone.” (Middle Eastern—Age 28).

This quote reflects how deeply internalised norms shape not just attitudes toward sex, but also emotional safety and social visibility. Building on this cultural constraint, for many participants, the pressure to adhere to these cultural and familial expectations created significant internal conflict. One participant described the emotional toll of living in a new country while still carrying the weight of their cultural programming:

“This is very complicated for me… [long pause] Being 35 and having my first relationship here, I feel like a teenager sometimes. But I also carry all these years of cultural… how to say… programming? I often feel guilty, even though I know I’m not doing anything wrong. The hardest part is that I can’t talk to my family about any of this…Sometimes after being intimate with my partner, I cry because I feel like I’m living a double life. But at the same time, I feel I deserve to experience love and relationship like anyone else.” (Middle Eastern—Age 35).

Here, the participant expresses cognitive dissonance, a conflict between cultural loyalty and emergent personal autonomy, illustrating how guilt becomes a bridge between past and present selves. Similarly, shame and fear of judgment were common emotional residues carried into their new lives.:

“ I still carry a lot of guilt and shame when I have sexual activity. Sometimes I think, ‘What would my parents think if they knew?’ or ‘Am I taking advantage of their trust?’ There’s also worry about… if this relationship doesn’t work out, what will my next partner think about my previous relationship? " (East Asian—Age 39).

This illustrates how emotional burdens surrounding sexuality are not limited to single events, but shape women’s entire sense of identity, trust, and relationship viability. Furthermore, even in marriage, the absence of open communication about sex perpetuated misunderstanding and discomfort.:

“I’ve never had an open conversation with my mom about my sex life. Even though I was getting married, she didn’t tell me anything, and I didn’t have any sisters. So back in my mind, I think I’m still not very open to try new things or even talk about it, which sometimes, you know, like, creates misunderstanding between the couple as well.” (South Asian—Age 38).

These accounts demonstrate how silence around sex becomes generational, perpetuating sexual uncertainty and relationship strain even in more liberal contexts.

Subtheme 1.2: cultural practices and their consequences: Female Genital Mutilation (FGM) and sexual function

Moving beyond cultural attitudes to examine specific practices, participants from African and some Middle Eastern countries highlighted the continued cultural relevance of Female Genital Mutilation (FGM), though their personal experiences varied. Some women had undergone the practice themselves, while others were aware of its prevalence in their communities but had not experienced it directly. Regardless of personal exposure, FGM was widely recognised as a harmful yet culturally embedded tradition. Many participants acknowledged the existence of FGM in their home countries but expressed discomfort discussing it. None of the participants, however, differentiated between the types or stages of the practice as classified by the World Health Organization (WHO) [28]. This general discomfort suggests a shared cultural sensitivity, where even awareness of FGM is often accompanied by silence or partial knowledge. One participant, who had not undergone FGM herself, described the cultural belief systems surrounding it in her country:

“It’s like a traditional belief that it helps the female become more fertile. They think if they do this, they can conceive better.” (African—Age 20).

This illustrates how FGM is framed by communities as enhancing womanhood or reproductive health, despite its well-documented risks and points to a deeper link between control of female sexuality and social expectations around fertility. Expanding on these cultural justifications, another participant who had not undergone FGM but lived in a context where it was expected, reflected on the societal justifications commonly used to support the practice:

“They say .ladies should undergo that process so that they cannot cheat in their marriages.” (African—Age 29).

Here, the rationale is overtly disciplinary, with FGM seen as a mechanism to ensure marital fidelity. The implication is that women’s sexuality must be managed to preserve family honour, a logic that reinforces patriarchal control. By contrast, one woman who had personally undergone FGM as an infant reflected on her retrospective understanding of the practice:

“I was being circumcised while I was still a baby… But now that I’ve grown up, I see the way they do it. It’s not good. I don’t like it anymore… I wouldn’t have allowed someone to circumcise me because they do use unsterilised equipment.” (African—Age 32).

This testimony is powerful not only for its clarity but also for its transformation, from passive acceptance to critical rejection. Her shift reflects how education, migration, and access to health knowledge can reshape women’s views of childhood experiences that were previously normalised.

Collectively, these accounts demonstrate the spectrum of FGM exposure and awareness. For some, it was a lived bodily experience; for others, it was a cultural backdrop marked by silence, myths, and stigma. Regardless of personal history, participants recognised FGM as a violation of women’s rights and health, yet also acknowledged the enduring grip of tradition.

Subtheme 1.3: influence of host country norms on sexual health and function

Migration to Australia played a pivotal role in the personal growth and self-esteem of many participants, offering them a chance to regain control over their lives and sexual well-being. The shift in their circumstances was often accompanied by newfound freedom, allowing them to make decisions independently, without the constraints of family or societal expectations from their home countries. This sense of autonomy was reflected in various aspects of their lives, such as living arrangements, financial independence, and personal choices. However, despite this greater sense of freedom, some participants found it challenging to fully embrace it, as traditional beliefs and values from their home countries remained deeply ingrained. As one participant reflected:

“Now I live alone in a first-world country, and I can make my own decisions. There’s no family asking where I’m going, no [social] police to be afraid of. I can go out, date, be in contact with people… But it’s interesting because even with all this freedom, those traditional beliefs are still deep inside me.” (Middle Eastern—Age 32).

This testimony highlights the complex nature of cultural transition - while physical distance provided freedom from external constraints, internalised cultural values remained influential in shaping participants’ decisions and experiences. Building on this complexity, participants frequently highlighted the stark differences between Australian sexual health norms and those of their home countries, particularly regarding gender dynamics and sexual agency. The Australian context was characterised by greater openness and respect for individual choice, contrasting sharply with more restrictive norms in their countries of origin. One participant illustrated this contrast in discussing women’s sexual agency:

“They [Australians] communicate openly… sexual life here, versus the one in my home country, is kind of different; everyone seems to respect each other’s opinion and choice here. But in over there [home country], even a married woman can never say no to a husband [when it comes to sex].” (African—Age 42).

This sentiment was echoed by another participant who emphasised the transformative impact of Australia’s more open cultural environment:

“Being in Australia, I’ve learned to be outspoken about my feelings, be expressive about sexual desires… But in my home country, when you talk about stuff like that, it’s like you’re degrading yourself or you’ve been cheap.” (East Asian—Age 33).

The navigation between different cultural expectations emerged as a significant challenge for participants. This was particularly evident in how they reconciled traditional values with new cultural norms regarding sexual behaviour and expression. One participant, for instance, described the complex dynamics of sexual expectations, particularly the pressure women face to preserve their sexual autonomy and avoid being labelled as “cheap”:

“In our culture, sex for women is kind of treasure you hold, and if you give it away too easily, then you’re cheap… So that kind of gives you this weird concern when you have sex with even your boyfriend, they’re like, oh, he hasn’t proposed… Would he think that I’m an easy girl?” (East Asian—Age 39).

For some participants, religious beliefs added another layer of complexity to this cultural navigation. As one 32-year-old participant explained:

“I felt like I needed to be more intentional with, first of all, I am a Christian, and I had to make a personal decision not to do things which are opposite of what is expected of me as a Christian… So that is one of the factors.” (African—Age 32).

Theme 2—sexual function transitions after migration

Having explored the cultural foundations that shape sexual experiences, the analysis now turns to examine how migration specifically impacts sexual function. Migration brings profound changes to various aspects of life, including intimate relationships and sexual experiences. Participants in this study described a complex journey of sexual adaptation and discovery following their migration, encompassing challenges in maintaining intimacy, shifts in sexual desire, experiences with sexual pain, understanding of orgasm, and overall sexual satisfaction. Their narratives reveal how the intersection of cultural transition, increased access to information, and personal growth shaped their sexual experiences in their new country.

The initial period of migration presented significant challenges that impacted participants’ sexual function and intimate relationships. These challenges were primarily related to settlement stress, changes in living arrangements, and navigating cultural and healthcare differences. These life changes often caused a temporary decline in sexual activity or desire, as daily survival took precedence over intimacy:

“When we were new here, we were under a lot of stress, like, looking for a place and work. We weren’t probably doing much because we both were under stress. But down the line, once we got our rental place, once we both had our jobs, it was pretty much easier.” (South Asian—Age 44).

“Sexual functionality was at an all-time low because we were going through a lot of changes …. My husband had to take up a night job, it was just hard, and the kids started sleeping with me.” (South Asian—Age 29).

These quotes reflect how financial instability and disrupted family routines created barriers to sexual connection. Once practical needs were stabilised, intimacy often began to recover—indicating that sexual function was closely tied to broader environmental and emotional security.

Subtheme 2.1: shifts in sexual desire and expression

As participants overcame initial settlement challenges and adjusted to their new environment, many experienced shifts in their sexual desire and expression. Migration often provided a more open and encouraging environment, fostering greater sexual arousal and willingness to explore new experiences, despite busy lifestyles:

“I feel more sexually aroused here, and I am more willing to try various things… But still, the environment here encourages me to explore more.” (African—Age 49).

Notably, some women experienced a transformation in their sexual agency, describing a sense of empowerment to initiate intimacy, contrasting with the more passive roles often expected in their home countries:

“I think I’m now willing to engage. Sometimes I will even be the one to engage in the activities that I have the desire to engage in.” (African—Age 29).

Subtheme 2.2: understanding and managing sexual discomfort

Alongside changes in desire and expression, many participants also had to navigate physical challenges. Sexual pain emerged as a common concern, particularly in early post-migration experiences. However, some participants reported improvement as they gained access to better sexual education, increased communication with partners, and personal experimentation:

“Initially, I experienced very bad sexual pain, but it got better after practising and having more sexual activity. I think my partner and I progressed as we proceeded. We did research and learned how everything could be better.” (African—Age 33).

This quote highlights the value of experiential learning and mutual adaptation in improving sexual wellbeing. Pain was not static but responsive to trust, patience, and communication.

In other cases, participants attributed pain to poor communication or misconceptions about anatomy and sexual health, suggesting that limited sexual education in their home countries left them unprepared:

“It’s quite easy to feel painful during sex if the person is not doing it right. I think I am probably allergic to semen because if we don’t use a condom, I get quite a burning sensation afterwards.” (East Asian—Age 33).

This account shows how health literacy and open medical conversations are crucial for identifying treatable causes of discomfort—something many participants lacked pre-migration.

Subtheme 2.3: exploring and interpreting orgasm

Beyond addressing discomfort, participants also described a journey of sexual discovery, particularly regarding orgasm. Understanding and experiencing orgasm emerged as a complex journey influenced by cultural background, education, and personal discovery. Several women expressed initial confusion about the concept of orgasm, highlighting the impact of limited sexual education in their home countries. One participant shared:

“I didn’t understand what orgasm actually is. So, I had to research it, it’s really rare for me to experience it. I think it has been constant. I experienced it [lack of orgasm] back in my home country, and also here.” (African—Age 49).

This quote highlights both the challenges faced by some participants in understanding their own sexual experiences and the self-initiated process of learning about orgasm after migration. It also reflects that, despite the geographical and cultural transition, the frequency of experiencing orgasm remained unchanged for them. For other participants, emotional and psychological factors emerged as barriers to experiencing orgasm, reflecting internalised beliefs and societal expectations:

“I think this is just something which is really hard for women to experience. So, I was just, maybe thinking I could experience it one day. I don’t know. But maybe it depends also on the person I’m with.” (Middle Eastern—Age 39).

Despite these challenges, the post-migration environment provided opportunities for self-education and exploration, though some participants continued to navigate cultural and personal barriers:

“I have not quite had actual systematic sexual education or a set discussion. So, I know I can definitely get an orgasm by myself. I can also get good pleasure from sex with my partner, but it feels different. It’s not quite the same thing. So, I’m not entirely sure if that means I don’t get an orgasm from sex, or it’s just that there are different types of orgasm.” (African—Age 42).

This statement highlights the complexity and fluidity of sexual pleasure, as well as the uncertainty that can arise in the absence of comprehensive education or open discussions about sexual health.

Subtheme 2.4: reconstructing sexual satisfaction

Encompassing all these changes in desire, comfort, and understanding, the impact of migration on sexual satisfaction was complex. While some women continued to face internal or relational barriers, others described migration as a turning point in their sexual self-awareness and fulfilment.

“In general, I am not satisfied… I have never been. I have always had problems with sexual activity. These are things that are inside me, I think.” (African—Age 32).

Here, the participant situates dissatisfaction as internal, suggesting that migration alone is not always enough to overcome deeply embedded discomfort or trauma. By contrast, others described transformation:

“It’s changed immensely… I was able to learn, and I was able to get to know myself in that area… I was exposed to resources that helped me gain this idea about the recent changes.” (South Asian—Age 39).

This quote reflects how information access, emotional autonomy, and cultural validation of female pleasure can improve sexual satisfaction. The positive changes were often self-driven and made possible through access to education and healthcare systems that validated women’s sexual needs.

Theme 3—navigating sexual healthcare services and systemic challenges

While the previous themes explored personal and cultural aspects of sexual function, this final theme examines how participants engaged with formal healthcare systems. This theme highlights the ways in which participants engaged with the Australian sexual and reproductive healthcare system post-migration. Their experiences reveal a mix of cultural transitions, emotional discomfort, and systemic obstacles that shaped how and whether they sought care. While some women described growing openness and confidence in clinical settings, others encountered challenges including stigma, financial barriers, limited awareness of services, and difficulties finding culturally appropriate support. These narratives offer insight into both the barriers and potential enablers of equitable, accessible sexual healthcare for migrant and refugee women.

Subtheme 3.1: cultural dynamics in sexual health communication

Even when participants were ready to seek help, communication remained a significant challenge. Participants described complex dynamics in discussing sexual matters with their partners, shaped by both internalised cultural norms and new opportunities for openness post-migration. While some women reported increased comfort in communication, many continued to struggle, especially around expressing dissatisfaction or discomfort.

“I just have the fear of the unknown because if I say, I don’t experience orgasm, maybe they could blame themselves.” (African—Age 44).

This quote reflects a fear of emotional fallout from sexual honesty, where the act of expressing one’s needs is viewed as risky or potentially damaging to the relationship. Even when sexual difficulties were recognised, socialised deference and emotional caution often constrained open discussion.

To navigate these communication challenges, other participants adopted non-verbal methods of communication to avoid direct confrontation, relying on subtle cues to express pain or discomfort during intimacy:

“I never bring it up. So, for example, if we’re in bed and something he did actually hurts me, I’ll stop him, but I’m not going to say much about it. I’m not going to say, oh, you can’t push from that angle. It hurts me. I’ll just pull away and hope that over time, he’s going to learn not to do it in a certain way because I’ll always pull away from that direction.” (East Asian—Age 33).

Here, the participant demonstrates a patterned strategy of avoidance, where bodily cues substitute for verbal clarity, a reflection of both personal restraint and cultural conditioning. This behaviour signals the emotional burden that many women carry when expected to navigate intimacy without the language, tools, or confidence to advocate for their needs.

The transition to Australian healthcare systems introduced new cultural expectations around openness. For some women, this environment encouraged communication with healthcare providers; for others, the shift highlighted the cultural dissonance between provider expectations and patient comfort. In particular, discussing sexual issues remained taboo or shame-inducing, even in clinical settings.

Subtheme 3.2: systemic barriers to accessing sexual and reproductive health services

Beyond communication challenges, participants encountered numerous structural obstacles when attempting to access care. Participants reported facing significant structural and cultural barriers to accessing SRH services. Common obstacles included lack of awareness, financial insecurity, limited cultural competence in services, and logistical hurdles like referral delays or clinician shortages.

“I was too shy to reach out to doctors. I just tried to search online, finding podcasts or going on social media to help myself.” (South Asian—Age 44).

This quote illustrates how shame and uncertainty often diverted women away from professional help toward informal, self-guided sources of information. The act of not seeking care was not a sign of neglect but rather an adaptive strategy within a context of stigma and confusion.

Financial insecurity was another key deterrent, especially for women on temporary visas or without Medicare:

“They [healthcare services] are expensive as a migrant who doesn’t have Medicare. Sometimes I have more urgent needs rather than my… orgasm problems.” (South Asian—Age 29).

This response underscores how economic precarity prioritises survival needs over sexual health. Sexual wellbeing becomes deprioritised, not because it lacks importance, but because the system renders it inaccessible or unaffordable. Further, some women were not even aware of the existence of specialised sexual health clinics:

“I’ve never heard of that [sexual health clinic]. Honestly, it did not even cross my mind to search for some sort of sexual health clinic.” (African—Age 29).

This lack of awareness reveals an information gap — where health literacy intersects with cultural taboos to keep sexual health invisible. Even when services exist, they are often unknown or misunderstood, making proactive health-seeking unlikely. Access was also obstructed by Australia’s complex healthcare bureaucracy, which, although designed for privacy and consent, often resulted in exclusion through long waitlists or inability to find appropriate clinicians:

“Some GPs and specialists are not taking new patients. I tried two specialists, and both refused because they were full.” (South Asian—Age 39).

Here, the systemic bottleneck becomes an additional stressor, particularly when paired with a desire for gender- and culturally-matched providers.

Subtheme 3.3: facilitators to access and service expectations

Despite these numerous barriers, participants identified specific conditions and preferences that made SRH access more comfortable and effective. These included provider gender, cultural background, language compatibility, affordability, and respectful communication.

The most consistent preference was for female providers:

“I would become very uncomfortable in sharing this personal sort of thing with a male person. Probably, I would be more comfortable with a female.” (African—Age 44).

This highlights how gender congruence can act as a protective factor, reducing shame, increasing honesty, and building trust. For many women, a male provider created an implicit risk of judgment or misunderstanding.

Views on provider cultural background were more mixed. While some preferred clinicians from outside their own community to preserve privacy:

“I prefer female doctors who aren’t from my background… our community is small here; everyone knows each other. I don’t want someone who might know me or my family.” (Middle Eastern—Age 33).

Others felt that cultural similarity improved understanding and reduced the need to “explain” complex beliefs or values.:

“It doesn’t have to be someone from my home country, but maybe similar. They understand how things are different in our cultures when it comes to sexuality.” (South Asian—Age 44).

This reveals a core tension: the need for cultural understanding versus the fear of cultural exposure. Both positions reflect legitimate strategies for navigating healthcare with dignity and autonomy.

Language was also pivotal. For some, English offered emotional distance and a sense of professionalism.

“I prefer speaking English for these topics. Saying these words in my native language feels… wrong. In English, it’s like I’m a different person—more professional, more open.” (Middle Eastern—Age 28).

But others found that using their native language enabled deeper and more precise communication:.

“If I can communicate and the other person can grasp the message in my own language, then I’ll be more open. Language is the first barrier.” (Middle Eastern—Age 33).

These insights suggest that multilingual options and flexible communication strategies are not simply logistical supports, they are central to emotional safety and healthcare equity.

Finally, participants stressed the need for affordable services and community-based education to raise awareness and build trust. Financial insecurity and lack of information were not isolated problems but systemic access issues, disproportionately affecting migrant women.

Discussion

This study contributes to growing evidence on the ways migration, culture, and gender intersect to shape sexual well-being. Through qualitative exploration, the findings revealed that migrant and refugee women’s experiences of sexual function are not static, but deeply entangled with the cultural, emotional, and structural contexts they navigate before and after migration. The women in this study described a range of influences, from cultural taboos and family expectations to healthcare accessibility and sexual autonomy, that shaped how they understood, expressed, and managed their sexual function. Rather than presenting a uniform narrative, participants offered diverse and sometimes contradictory experiences, reflecting the fluid and negotiated nature of sexual function in the migration context. This complexity was reflected in three interrelated domains, each representing a key influence on participants’ sexual function and well-being. The following discussion examines these domains in detail, beginning with how cultural expectations shape sexual identity transformation.

Navigating cultural expectations and redefining sexual identity

The primary themes identified centre around cultural norms, traditional beliefs, and the challenges of navigating between different cultural contexts. A significant finding was the pervasive impact of cultural taboos and family expectations from participants’ countries of origin. These deeply ingrained cultural norms created persistent internal conflicts, particularly regarding sexual expression and relationships. Participants frequently reported experiencing guilt, shame, and emotional distress when their sexual behaviours conflicted with traditional values, even after relocating to Australia. This was especially evident among participants from Middle Eastern and Asian backgrounds, where sexuality remains a highly sensitive and often prohibited topic of discussion. These findings align with the study conducted by Alvarez et al. [29] who highlights how migrant women often carry enduring beliefs and attitudes about sexuality and sexual propriety from their home countries. Viewed through Berry’s framework, these internal conflicts reflect acculturative stress associated with attempts to either preserve cultural identity (separation strategy) or balance it with new sociocultural norms (integration strategy) [21, 30].

Migration, while bringing cultural and emotional baggage, can also create opportunities for renegotiating sexual norms and behaviours. A nuanced understanding emerged, indicating that the impact of migration on sexual function and autonomy is not uniform and varies based on individual circumstances, resilience, and the broader socio-cultural environment in the host country. For some participants, migration acted as a catalyst for increased sexual autonomy and willingness to explore sexuality. This reflects Berry’s “integration” pathway, where individuals adopt certain aspects of the host culture while maintaining ties to their heritage values [21, 31].

The exposure to a more open cultural environment in Australia, combined with reduced social surveillance and greater access to sexual health resources, allowed these women to reconstruct their sexual identities and feel more empowered in expressing their sexual desires. The findings point to a pattern of adaptation when these women maintain their sexual well-being while negotiating conflicting cultural expectations. This dual narrative underscores the complexity of migration experiences. On one hand, cultural norms and values from the country of origin may persist, creating internal barriers to sexual freedom and self-expression. On the other hand, migration can also serve as an opportunity for liberation from these constraints, enabling women to reframe their sexual identities in a more accepting and less judgmental cultural context. These two trajectories are not mutually exclusive but rather reflect different facets of the same migration experience, shaped by personal, cultural, and situational factors.

Consistent with findings from other studies, participants’ sexual beliefs and behaviours were also shaped by education and women with limited education or from lower socioeconomic backgrounds were more likely to exhibit reserved or submissive sexual behaviours, while those with higher education levels tended to express themselves more openly about sexuality, were more inclined to seek sexual health services, and maintained more communicative and transparent relationships with their partners and broader social networks [29].

While cultural conditioning strongly influenced participants’ early experiences, migration itself emerged as a powerful turning point, introducing both challenges and opportunities for transformation in sexual relationships, behaviours, and beliefs. This transformation process is examined in greater detail in the following section.

Evolving intimacy and sexual agency in the post-migration context

The second major area of inquiry focused on how women’s sexual function changed over time following migration, revealing multifaceted adaptations in both intimate relationships and personal attitudes. During the initial settlement period, participants faced considerable challenges that directly impacted their sexual functioning, primarily stemming from settlement stress, altered living arrangements, and adaptation to new cultural contexts. These immediate post-migration stressors often resulted in decreased sexual activity and disrupted intimacy patterns within relationships. However, as settlement progressed, a different pattern emerged. The study revealed that the new cultural environment often facilitated greater sexual autonomy and willingness to explore sexuality, with some women reporting increased comfort in initiating intimate encounters - a marked shift from traditional gender roles in their countries of origin. This finding suggests that migration can serve as a catalyst for sexual empowerment and the reconstruction of sexual identity.

Particularly concerning were the revelations about pre-migration sexual health knowledge and attitudes. Notably, participants also described how restrictive cultural attitudes and insufficient sexual health education in their home countries led to problematic conceptualisations of sexuality and consent. For instance, one East Asian participant revealed how intense cultural shame around sexual desire led to the development of concerning sexual fantasies as a means of reconciling pleasure with cultural prohibitions. However, exposure to more sex-positive cultural attitudes post-migration enabled many participants to develop healthier perspectives on sexual agency and intimacy. These findings underscore how excessive cultural control over sexuality, combined with inadequate sexual health literacy, can foster potentially harmful ideas about sex, highlighting the critical importance of comprehensive sexual education and the need for culturally sensitive approaches to sexual health support for migrant women.

Our findings on the transitions in sexual function following migration align with recent research while offering new perspectives on this complex issue. A 2022 study by Ussher et al. found that migrant women from Middle Eastern and North African backgrounds experienced significant changes in sexual practices and pleasure post-migration [32], which is consistent with our observations of increased sexual autonomy and exploration among some participants. The results also offer new perspectives on the concept of orgasm among migrant women, revealing confusion and a lack of clarity for some participants.

The varying experiences of sexual pain and satisfaction reported in our study align with recent work by Hawkey et al. (2021), which found that cultural beliefs and practices significantly impact sexual health outcomes for migrant women [33]. However, our study provides a more nuanced understanding of how these factors interact with the migration experience over time, offering insights into both challenges and opportunities for growth in sexual function.

Despite these positive changes, significant communication barriers persisted throughout the post-migration period. The results of this study highlight the evolving comfort of migrant and refugee women in discussing sexual health as they adapt to new cultural environments. However, significant barriers persist, particularly in openly addressing issues related to sexual satisfaction or discomfort. These challenges often stem from deeply rooted cultural taboos and the fear of judgment from both partners and healthcare providers. Many participants resorted to non-verbal communication strategies to navigate these sensitive topics. These findings align with prior research, such as Metusela et al., which emphasised that migrant women often face difficulties in discussing sexual health due to cultural norms and language barriers [34].

Our study also suggests that the sexual agency of migrant and refugee women is shaped by the intersection of patriarchal cultural and religious narratives, with these factors influencing their relationships with their spouses and the psychological and practical consequences of resisting these norms. Echoing the work of Hawkey et al. (2018), our findings emphasise that migrant and refugee women are not a homogeneous group, and it is crucial for healthcare providers and sexual health educators to be mindful of the cultural and religious nuances that shape how women navigate these issues within their marital relationships [35].

These communication challenges were further compounded by systemic barriers within the healthcare system itself.

Culturally responsive care and system navigation

The findings identified several systemic barriers that hinder access to sexual health services included limited awareness of available resources, financial constraints, and the complexities of navigating the healthcare system. These barriers were especially pronounced among migrants without access to Medicare, prompting some participants to seek help through informal networks or online platforms. A consistent theme across interviews was the call for more accessible, affordable, and culturally sensitive healthcare services to meet the needs of these women.

These reflections on barriers to care naturally extend into how participants experienced, interpreted, and navigated the healthcare system in practice, particularly their preferences and expectations for culturally safe, inclusive SRH services.

Participants also frequently highlighted the importance of gender-congruent and culturally attuned healthcare. Many expressed a preference for providers from similar cultural backgrounds to mitigate the risk of judgment and ensure confidentiality. This preference preferences reflect the layered considerations migrant women navigate when seeking culturally safe care.

Language was another key factor influencing participants’ comfort and access. While some participants preferred speaking in English, others felt more at ease using their native language, believing it ensured more accurate and meaningful interactions. These findings resonate with the results of a systematic review by Sánchez et al., which highlighted similar barriers in SRH service access. According to the review, the most common barriers to SRH services included lack of information (57%), language barriers (43%), cultural differences (39%), economic challenges (25%), administrative hurdles (25%), and discrimination (14%) [36].

Implications for theory, policy, and practice

The findings of this study offer important insights that align with and extend existing theoretical frameworks, particularly Berry’s Acculturation Theory. Berry’s influential model identifies four primary acculturation strategies that individuals may adopt when navigating cultural change: integration (maintaining heritage culture while adopting host culture), assimilation (adopting host culture while abandoning heritage culture), separation (maintaining heritage culture while rejecting host culture), and marginalisation (rejecting both heritage and host cultures) [37]. While our findings generally align with Berry’s framework, they reveal important nuances specific to the domain of sexual health among migrant and refugee women.

Our study suggests that Berry’s “integration” strategy takes on particular complexity when applied to sexual health experiences. Many participants described selectively adopting aspects of Australian sexual health norms, such as openness in partner communication or accessing health information, while continuing to uphold traditional beliefs related to modesty, shame, or familial expectations. This selective approach appears to be more nuanced than traditional integration, reflecting what could be understood as domain-specific acculturation within sexual health. Rather than wholesale adoption of either cultural framework, women demonstrated strategic selection of practices and beliefs that aligned with their personal values, relationship contexts, and well-being needs.

This selective engagement was not linear or uniform, but shaped by factors such as relationship context, religious identity, and emotional readiness. For instance, the same participant might embrace Australian approaches to contraception and sexual health services while maintaining traditional communication patterns with family members about sexuality. This suggests that acculturation in the sexual health domain may be more compartmentalised and context-dependent than Berry’s model initially suggests, with women actively negotiating between cultural frameworks depending on the specific aspect of sexual health in question.

The study also highlights the adaptive capacity demonstrated by many participants in navigating these cultural negotiations. Despite facing cultural taboos, emotional conflict, and systemic healthcare barriers, many women demonstrated resourcefulness in maintaining their sexual well-being. This included engaging in self-education, seeking culturally or linguistically appropriate providers, and setting personal boundaries within intimate relationships. These behaviours reflect a capacity to negotiate conflicting cultural messages while prioritising personal agency and health—an adaptive response that emerges from, rather than despite, cultural tension.

Limitations

This study offers several strengths, including the cultural diversity of participants, a reflexive methodological approach, and multilingual recruitment strategies that enhanced cultural sensitivity and participant comfort. However, several limitations should be acknowledged to contextualise the findings.

First, the sample size was relatively small and drawn from a single geographic region, which may limit the generalisability of findings to broader migrant and refugee populations in Australia.

Second, although participants were offered interviews in their preferred languages, all opted for English. As such, the study only reflects the perspectives of women with sufficient English proficiency. This introduces a potential bias, as the experiences of women with limited or no English literacy, who may face distinct barriers in sexual and reproductive health, were not captured. Additionally, as English was a second language for all participants, certain linguistic nuances or emotionally complex reflections may have been constrained.

Third, despite efforts to build trust and cultural safety, the sensitive nature of the topic may have influenced participants’ willingness to fully disclose their experiences. Social desirability bias or discomfort discussing intimate topics may have limited the depth of disclosure in some interviews.

Lastly, structural and digital access barriers likely contributed to sampling bias. Women with limited literacy, minimal digital access, or who are socially isolated due to cultural expectations may have been unintentionally excluded, reducing the representation of more marginalised subgroups within migrant and refugee communities.

Conclusion

This study highlights the cultural, emotional, and systemic factors that shape the sexual function experiences of migrant and refugee women in South Australia. The findings point to the urgent need for culturally competent healthcare, affordable SRH services, and targeted policy support. Healthcare providers, policymakers, and community organisations must act collectively to remove access barriers and deliver inclusive care. Addressing these needs is essential for improving health equity and supporting the wellbeing and integration of migrant women in an increasingly diverse Australia.
