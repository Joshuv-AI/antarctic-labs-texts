Enhancing the preoperative differentiation of ameloblastoma and odontogenic keratocyst using pathomics-guided radiomics: a pilot study
Authors: Liang Li, Shengfu Huang, Kai Yang, Zimo Huang, Yuhong Li
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT

Introduction

Ameloblastoma (AM), one of the most prevalent odontogenic tumors, and odontogenic keratocyst (OKC), one of the most common jaw cysts, are both frequently encountered in clinical practice [1, 2]. Although both AM and OKC comprise multiple histopathological subtypes with slightly different biological behaviors, in general, AM exhibits more aggressive and infiltrative growth patterns than OKC. Therefore, AM often requires more aggressive surgical management, such as resection and reconstruction, whereas OKC is more commonly managed with relatively conservative approaches, such as marsupialization or enucleation, although its high recurrence potential requires careful case-specific management and follow-up [3]. However, distinguishing patients with these two lesions before surgery is challenging, as they share similar clinical presentations such as slow-growing painless swelling, tooth displacement, as well as overlapping age of onset and lesion location [4]. Therefore, accurate preoperative diagnosis of AM and OKC is critical for selecting effective and personalized treatment planning.

A variety of clinical approaches are used in the evaluation of AM and OKC, including cone-beam computed tomography (CBCT), aspiration and histopathological biopsy [5]. Among these, histopathological examination remains the gold standard for definitive diagnosis, whereas CBCT is one of the most commonly used non-invasive tools for preoperative assessment of jaw lesions, providing valuable information for preliminary differentiation; however, some cytological and architectural changes may precede radiographic manifestations [6]. Several radiological manifestations, such as lesion border, size, expansion pattern and lesion-tooth relationship, have been utilized as diagnostic indicators for preliminary assessments of AM and OKC [7]. However, as the identification and interpretations of radiographic information depend heavily on clinical experience, diagnostic opinions may vary among physicians. As a result, radiomics was proposed to achieve more standardized radiographic evaluation, involving key steps such as feature extraction, feature selection, and model building [8]. Unlike routine radiographic reporting that relies on qualitative visual assessment, radiomics can convert medical images into quantitative high-dimensional data, enabling objective computational analysis and machine learning (ML)–based disease classification that improves diagnostic accuracy across various diseases [9]. Nevertheless, the classification performance for jaw lesions in current radiomics studies remained unsatisfactory, which may be attributed to the large amount of redundant information present in CBCT and the lack of specific feature selection strategies tailored to classification tasks [10]. Therefore, adopting new strategies to identify radiological features containing more diagnostic information may improve CBCT-based preoperative differentiation between AM and OKC.

In clinical practice, histopathological biopsy could provide the most accurate and comprehensive diagnostic information, including cell density, morphology and other microscopic details [11]. Driven by the same motivation as radiomics, pathomics, a technique automatically capturing quantitative pathological characteristics across multiple tissue levels from whole slide images (WSIs), has emerged [12]. Notably, although biopsy is regarded as the gold standard for diagnosing jaw lesions, it is an invasive method requiring tissue specimens obtained through surgical resection, thereby not directly enhancing diagnostic accuracy before surgery. However, if the diagnostic information contained in postoperative pathological images could guide radiomic-based decision-making, it may improve the preoperative classification accuracy. Yet, this raises a critical question: how to address this temporal paradox?

In fact, CBCT and WSI reflect the same pathological information at different spatial scales and resolutions, from which radiomics and pathomics can respectively extract analogous quantitative features, such as textural and intensity-based features [11]. Meanwhile, the cross-modal correlations between the features extracted from radiological and pathological images have been identified across various diseases [13, 14]. Therefore, we hypothesized that such correlations may help propose a novel pathomics-guided radiomic feature selection strategy to improve the preoperative differentiation accuracy. However, the clinical application value of these correlations has not been validated in the aforementioned studies. Moreover, no research to date has even investigated these radio-pathomic correlations in AM and OKC.

To fill this gap, we extracted radiomic and pathomic features from CBCT and WSI of AM and OKC patients to comprehensively identify their cross-modal correlations at two levels, directly between features, and between latent factors derived from features via factor analysis. Additionally, based on these correlations, we proposed a novel pathomics-guided feature selection strategy that could identify radiomic features strongly associated with diagnostically significant pathomic features from CBCT scans. Finally, we evaluated whether using our proposed feature selection strategy could enhance the classification performance of various classical ML models, compared to traditional feature selection methods. To the best of our knowledge, this is the first study to enhance the differentiation between AM and OKC from a multi-omics perspective. We proposed a promising strategy to incorporate postoperative pathological information into preoperative CBCT-based decision-making, thereby enhancing the diagnostic accuracy of jaw lesions at an early stage and achieving more personalized treatment planning.

Materials and methods

Data

The use of patient data and waivers of informed consent were approved by the Institutional Ethics Committee of the School and Hospital of Stomatology, Wuhan University (WHUSS) (approval number 2020-B22). All patient data in this study were fully anonymized before analysis, and any potentially identifiable information was removed.

CBCT acquisition

We retrospectively collected CBCT imaging data in Digital Imaging and Communications in Medicine (DICOM) format from the WHUSS, from 2015 to 2019.

The tube voltage, tube current, and exposure time were set to 110 kV, 6 mA, and 9.4s, respectively. The images were reconstructed with a slice interval of 0.3 mm and a slice thickness of 0.3 mm. The matrix size was 512 × 512 × 512 voxels. The inclusion criteria were as follows: (i) complete clinical records, (ii) histological confirmation of AM or OKC, and (iii) availability of high-quality preoperative CBCT. The exclusion criteria included (i) cases with multiple AM or OKC lesions, (ii) cases with recurrent lesions, (iii) nevoid basal cell carcinoma syndrome (NBCCS), (iv) CBCT images with apparent artifacts. Ultimately, 50 AM cases and 58 OKC cases met these criteria and were included in the study.

WSI acquisition

Hematoxylin and eosin (H&E)-stained formalin-fixed paraffin-embedded (FFPE) tissue specimens were obtained from surgical resections of tumors in all 108 patients. WSIs were digitized in SVS format at 20× magnification using a Pannoramic scanner (3DHISTECH Ltd., Budapest, Hungary). Each WSI was manually reviewed to exclude those with artifacts for further analysis.

Radiomic feature extraction

The overall experimental workflow is illustrated in Fig. 1. To label the lesions accurately, the original CBCT images were first imported into 3D Slicer software (version 4.10.2), where the regions of interest (ROIs) were manually segmented by two radiological experts with over 20 years of experience. In cases where consensus was not reached, a third expert was consulted. The final ROIs were then reconstructed in three dimensions and saved in NRRD format for subsequent feature engineering (Fig. 1A).

To ensure compliance with IBSI guidelines and improve feature reproducibility, all CBCT volumes were resampled to an isotropic voxel size of 0.3 × 0.3 × 0.3 mm³ using linear interpolation. Prior to feature extraction, voxel intensities were normalized using z-score normalization. Gray-level discretization was applied with a fixed bin width of 25 [15, 16]. Subsequently, we used the PyRadiomics V3.0.1 software package in Python for automated feature extraction. A total of 107 radiomic features were automatically extracted across seven categories: (1) 3D shape features; (2) first-order features; and five second-order texture categories for each patient, including (3) grey-level co-occurrence matrix (GLCM); (4) grey-level dependence matrix (GLDM); (5) grey-level run length matrix (GLRLM); (6) grey-level size zone matrix (GLSZM); (7) neighboring grey tone difference matrix (NGTDM) (Fig. 1B). Detailed definitions of these features were provided in Supplementary Material 1. All features were generated based on the original image without applying any image filters.

To assess intra-observer reliability, 20 CBCT images (10 AM and 10 OKC) were randomly selected and re-segmented by the same observer after a one-month interval. To assess inter-observer reliability, the same 20 images were independently segmented by a second observer. The intraclass correlation coefficient (ICC) was calculated to evaluate feature reproducibility for both intra- and inter-observer agreement.

Pathomic feature extraction

The QuPath software (version 0.5.1) was used for WSIs region delineation, where the regions covering the largest possible tissue area with vivid histopathologic characteristics were outlined by two experts in oral histopathology.

By applying the “cell detection” function from the analysis module, cell nuclei were segmented using the watershed-based cell detection algorithm, with all parameters set to default values [13, 17]. The quality of the automated cell detection was also checked by the expert. Additionally, the “show detection measurements” function was used to obtain the centroid coordinates of all nuclei located in the ROI on each WSI. Then these coordinates were further loaded in Python to generate cell density maps at multiple spatial resolutions (25 μm, 50 μm, 100 μm, and 200 μm), as shown in Fig. 1A [18]. Specifically, each WSI was divided into non-overlapping tiles at the target resolution, and the number of nuclei within each tile was counted and converted into a proportional grayscale intensity, resulting in a cell density map that reflects the spatial distribution of nuclei.

Subsequently, pathomic features were automatically extracted from each resolution level of the WSI using PyRadiomics, which belonged to the same categories described in the radiomics extraction, including 2D shape features, first-order features, and second-order texture features (GLCM, GLDM, GLRLM, GLSZM, and NGTDM). A total of 102 pathomic features were extracted from each level, resulting in 408 pathomic features for each WSI. In addition, in line with previous studies, for patients with more than one slide, the value of each feature was averaged across WSIs [13, 19]. Thus, 408 pathomic features were ultimately obtained for each patient.

Radio-pathomic analysis to identify the cross-modal correlations

To comprehensively identify the cross-modal correlations between radiomic and pathomic features, correlation analyses were conducted separately for AM and OKC at two levels, directly between features, and between latent factors extracted via factor analysis (Fig. 1C).

Correlation analysis at feature level

To preliminarily investigate these cross-modal correlations, correlation analyses were first conducted at the feature level. To reduce redundancy, a Spearman correlation-based filter was applied to pathomic and radiomic features, where for any pair of features with a correlation coefficient (ρ) > 0.9, the feature with the higher mean absolute correlation across all others was removed [20]. Among the remaining features, the top 50 features with the lowest mean absolute Spearman correlation were retained for subsequent feature-level correlation analysis, balancing dimensionality reduction with retention of a sufficiently broad exploratory feature space in this pilot study.

Subsequently, Spearman correlation analysis was conducted between the filtered radiomic and pathomic features separately in AM and OKC. P values were adjusted using the false discovery rate (FDR) method to control for errors caused by multiple comparisons, with an FDR-adjusted p value (q value) < 0.05 considered statistically significant. The strength of correlation was categorized as: very weak (0.00–0.19), weak (0.20–0.39), moderate (0.40–0.59), strong (0.60–0.79), and very strong (0.80–1.00) [21].

Correlation analysis at factor level

To assess whether these cross-modal correlations remained after dimensionality reduction, factor analysis was applied to derive latent factors from the original features, which were then used for subsequent correlation analysis. First, all features were standardized using z-score normalization to ensure comparability. A shrinkage-based approach was then applied to estimate a regularized correlation matrix, which served as the basis for subsequent decomposition [22]. To determine the optimal number of latent factors, parallel analysis was conducted, retaining only those components that explained more variance than expected by chance [23]. Additionally, maximum likelihood factor analysis was performed to identify the underlying latent factors separately in AM and OKC, including both radiomic factors (RFs) and pathomic factors (PFs). Subsequently, the score of each latent factor for each case was calculated by mapping its normalized features onto the latent space, which were used for correlation analyses at the factor level. Finally, factor loadings were employed to interpret how individual features contributed to each latent factor [13].

Radiomic feature selection strategies for differentiating AM and OKC

To identify the most discriminative radiomic features from CBCT for differentiating AM and OKC, we employed two feature selection approaches: a traditional filter-based method and a novel pathomics-guided radiomic strategy (Fig. 1D).

Traditional filter-based selection strategy

First, all features were standardized via z-score normalization to ensure fair comparison during selection. Subsequently, the top 15 radiomics features were identified using traditional filter-based methods, including Analysis of Variance (ANOVA) and Spearman correlation analysis. ANOVA was used to identify features that showed significant differences between lesion categories, while Spearman correlation analysis helped remove redundant features by evaluating pairwise correlations.

Pathomics-guided selection strategy

The top 15 pathomic features were first identified by the traditional filter-based method described above to guide the subsequent radiomic feature selection. Here, we proposed a Spearman correlation-based strategy to select the most pathology-relevant radiomic features. Specifically, all radiomic features and the top 15 pathomic features were standardized using z-score normalization. Then each top pathomic feature was matched with its own top 20 most strongly correlated radiomic features, which were determined by Spearman correlation analyses. Subsequently, we counted how many times each radiomic feature appeared across these top 20 lists of all 15 pathomic features. Radiomic features that appeared in at least half (≥ 8) out of 15 lists were selected. Among these, the top 15 radiomic features with the highest average absolute ρ to all pathomic features were finally retained as the pathomics-guided radiomic feature set.

Classification model evaluation across different feature selection strategies

To evaluate the generalizability and robustness of our proposed feature selection strategy, we employed six classical ML models, including Support Vector Machine (SVM) [24], XGBoost [25], Ridge [26], Lasso [27], Logistic Regression [28], and Random Forest [29], and evaluated their performance using stratified 10-fold cross-validation (Fig. 1E), in which the proportion of AM and OKC cases was preserved as closely as possible in each fold. The optimal hyperparameters for each ML model were determined using the grid search method, and the corresponding hyperparameter search ranges are provided in Supplementary Table 1. As differential diagnosis can be considered a binary classification task, the default metrics in our study treated AM as positive and OKC as negative. To prevent information leakage, all analytical steps, including pathomic feature selection, radiomic feature selection, pathomics-guided radiomic feature selection, and grid-search-based hyperparameter tuning, were performed exclusively within the training data of each fold, and the test data was used only for final evaluation.

We compared the diagnostic performances of models trained by radiomic features selected by the traditional method and our pathomics-guided strategy. Meanwhile, the performances of models based on the top 15 pathomic features were also evaluated, given that pathological information is considered the diagnostic gold standard.

Model performance was evaluated using several metrics, with the area under the receiver operating characteristic curve (AUROC) as the primary metric. Additional metrics included precision, recall, F1-score, accuracy, and the area under the precision-recall curve (AUPRC). The final results of each metric were reported as the mean ± standard deviation across the ten folds. In addition, DeLong tests were performed to compare the AUROCs between the traditional radiomics-based models and the corresponding pathomics-guided radiomics-based models.

Results

Patient characteristic

The clinical characteristics of patients with AM and OKC involved in this study were generally comparable (Table 1). The mean ages of the two lesions were similar (p = 0.45), with AM patients ranging from 6 to 72 years and OKC patients ranging from 10 to 80 years. Both AM and OKC included a smaller proportion of female patients (p = 0.30) and these lesions predominantly occurred in the mandible rather than the maxilla (p = 0.18). In terms of lesion volume, AM cases showed slightly larger average lesion volumes than OKC cases (p = 0.08). For the reproducibility of the extracted features, the intra-observer ICCs for the first and second observers were 0.95 and 0.93, respectively, and the inter-observer ICC was 0.90, indicating good ROI segmentation and feature reproducibility.

The radio-pathomic correlations at the feature level

Correlation analysis results at the feature level were presented in Table 2. Eight significant radio-pathomic feature pairs, including one in the AM cohort (ρ = 0.58, q = 0.05) and seven in the OKC cohort (all |ρ| > 0.50, q < 0.03), were revealed. The radio-pathomic correlations at the factor level

Parallel analysis identified six RFs and five PFs in the AM cohort, and five RFs and six PFs in the OKC cohort (Supplementary Fig. 1). In AM, RF1 showed a positive correlation with PF3 (ρ = 0.50, q = 0.01) (Fig. 2). Discussion

Previous studies on oral cancers or cysts typically investigated radiomics and pathomics in isolation. In this study, we investigated the cross-modal correlations between radiomic and pathomic features separately in AM and OKC. Subsequently, we proposed a novel pathomics-guided radiomic feature selection strategy based on these correlations, which showed potential to improve diagnostic performance compared with traditional methods when evaluated using classical ML models. Therefore, these cross-modal associations may provide preliminary insights for future translational research.

Correlation analyses at the feature level highlighted the importance of radiomic feature “shape_sphericity”, a geometric metric quantifying how spherical a lesion appears [30]. In AM, this feature demonstrated a significant positive correlation (ρ = 0.58) with the pathomic feature GLCM_JointAverage, where higher JointAverage values reflected more uneven gray-level intensity distributions in cell density maps [31]. This strong positive correlation suggests a possible association between a more spherical lesion shape in AM and greater tissue heterogeneity and complexity. In OKC, shape_sphericity was negatively correlated with pathomic features including LongRunEmphasis, LargeDependenceEmphasis, and JointEnergy, whose lower values indicated greater tissue complexity and irregularity. This finding suggested that morphologically regular OKC lesions may be associated with greater tissue heterogeneity [32]. This cross-modal relationship was also observed at the factor level.

In terms of model evaluation, pathomics-based ML models consistently achieved the best performance among three groups. This suggests that nuclear density, a type of very limited pathological information in WSIs, has already achieved greater diagnostic accuracy than radiomic features. This observation was the fundamental basis for enhancing radiomics-based diagnostic accuracy through pathomics guidance, consistent with a previous study [18]. Moreover, pathomics-guided radiomic feature set achieved enhanced classification performance compared to the traditional radiomic feature set in nearly all six models, suggesting the potential utility of our proposed strategy in improving the differentiation of AM and OKC, although these improvements reached statistical significance only in some models and will require further validation in future studies.

This improvement may be further interpreted by the difference between the features selected by the traditional and pathomics-guided selection strategy (Supplementary Table 2). The latter feature set contained more GLRLM features, which mainly reflect the local consistency of gray-level patterns, and fewer NGTDM features, which are more sensitive to large-scale intensity variations [33]. Overall, the pathomics-guided strategy enhanced CBCT-based diagnostic accuracy possibly by emphasizing specific feature categories and textural details. However, whether this selection preference remains consistent across other diseases needs further investigation.

In this study, the SVM classifier consistently outperformed the other ML models. This observation may be partly related to several methodological factors. First, compared with tree-based models (XGBoost and random forest) and linear models (logistic regression or Lasso/Ridge), SVMs may be better suited for datasets with limited sample size [34, 35]. In addition, because many radiomic features may exhibit moderate inter-feature correlations [36, 37], the performance of SVMs may be less affected by collinearity than that of some linear models. However, these explanations remain interpretive rather than directly tested in the present study, and therefore this interpretation remains speculative and should be validated in future studies.

However, this study also has several limitations. First, as a pilot study, radiomic and pathomic features were only extracted from the original images, without the application of filtered image transformations, such as wavelet or Gradient, which will be addressed in our subsequent studies [38]. Second, although we attempted to include multiple WSIs per patient whenever available, the pathological images still could not be precisely matched to specific radiographic subregions, which may have introduced sampling bias into the radio-pathomic association analysis. In addition, the selection of WSI regions was performed manually, which may introduce sampling bias. Because histopathological characteristics can vary across different areas of the same lesion, manually selected regions may not fully represent the overall tissue heterogeneity. Third, external or multi-center validation was not performed. In addition, this study did not impose restrictions on the histopathological subtypes of AM during the inclusion and exclusion process. Nevertheless, as different AM subtypes may present slightly distinct radiological and pathological characteristics, future research should further explore the radio–pathomic correlations across specific AM subtypes [15, 39].

Future studies should further validate our findings in larger multi-center and independent external cohorts to improve robustness and generalizability. In addition, prospective studies are needed to evaluate how this pathomics-guided radiomics framework can be integrated into real clinical workflows to support preoperative diagnosis and treatment planning.

Conclusion

This pilot study is the first to reveal significant cross-modal correlations between radiomic and pathomic features in both AM and OKC, suggesting a potential link between morphological regularity and textural complexity of lesions on CBCT and tissue heterogeneity in corresponding histopathological images. In addition, our proposed pathomics-guided radiomic feature selection strategy contributed to better classification model performance over traditional methods, although these improvements reached statistical significance only in some models. Overall, pathomics-guided radiomics may serve as a feasible exploratory approach for CBCT-based preoperative differentiation between AM and OKC.
