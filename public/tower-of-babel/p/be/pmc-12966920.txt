Introduction

Primary tumors of the epididymis are rare, with approximately 80% being benign. Of these, adenomatoid tumors are the most common, followed by leiomyomas. Papillary cystadenoma of the epididymis (PCE) accounts for about 5% of epididymal tumors [1, 2, 3, 4, 5, 6]. Malignant tumors are even rarer, with only a limited number of cases of adenocarcinoma and squamous cell carcinoma reported in the literature [2]. We herein describe a rare case of clear cell carcinoma associated with PCE. Along with a literature review, we highlight the clinical course, including MRI and clinicopathological characteristics, and discuss its distinction from other malignant tumors of the epididymis.

Case Presentation

A 77‐year‐old man presented with an induration of the left scrotum. His past medical history included hypertension and diabetes mellitus, with no notable family history. At the initial visit, vital signs were normal; both urinalysis and blood tests revealed no remarkable findings. Serum levels of testicular tumor markers, including alpha‐fetoprotein and soluble interleukin‐2 receptor, were normal. Ultrasonography revealed a solid round mass with a mosaic pattern in the left epididymis, and MRI revealed an area of abnormal heterogeneous signal intensity in the left testis and epididymis on T2‐weighted imaging (Figure 1A, black arrowheads). Diffusion‐weighted imaging (DWI) revealed a region of high signal intensity. The apparent diffusion coefficient map showed a low signal intensity on the head of the epididymis (Figure 1A–D, white arrowheads). A left high inguinal orchiectomy was performed to exclude a malignant tumor. Macroscopically, the excised specimen revealed a yellowish–white cystic lesion arising from the epididymis (Figure 2, black arrowheads) with a solid white component in the head of the epididymis (Figure 2, white arrowheads). The solid white area corresponded to the region with low signal intensity on DWI in MRI. Histopathological examination revealed the yellowish‐white area corresponded to the benign component, consisting of a well‐circumscribed cyst. Within the cyst, tumor cells showed small round nuclei and large clear cytoplasm, proliferating mainly in a papillary pattern with focal solid growth. A characteristic feature was aligned tumor cell nuclei in a single row away from the basal layer, resembling piano keys (Figure 3A). These findings were consistent with PCE. Any differential diagnosis should consider both metastatic clear cell renal cell carcinoma and malignant mesothelioma for primary malignant tumors of the epididymis. The former was excluded by the absence of a renal mass, and positive cytokeratin 7 (Figure 3B) and negative CD10 (Figure 3C) immunostaining. The latter was excluded by negative staining for calretinin (Figure 3D) and WT‐1 (Figure 3E). In contrast, the solid white area represented the malignant component, characterized by solid tumor growth with nuclear enlargement, atypical mitotic figures, and invasion into surrounding tissues (Figure 3F,G). These features are incompatible with papillary cystadenoma and were therefore diagnosed as a malignant transformation to clear cell carcinoma. Positivity in Ki‐67 immunostaining in tumor cells in a benign (Figure 4A,C) was clearly low compared with those in malignant (Figure 4B,D) areas. The postoperative course proved uneventful, and follow‐up was via imaging every 3 months, and 18 months postoperatively, recurrence or metastasis has not been observed.

Discussion

Primary tumors of the epididymis are rare, with approximately 80% benign; the most common are adenomatoid tumors (56%), leiomyoma (28%), with PCE representing about 5% [1]. PCE is a benign neoplasm that occurs either infrequently or together with von Hippel–Lindau disease (VHLD) [5]. About one‐third of male patients with VHLD develop PCE and about 40% of PCE cases are associated with VHLD [6]. In VHLD‐associated cases, more than half are bilateral, whereas sporadic cases are commonly unilateral [6]. In this case, no personal or family history of VHLD existed. No tumor was identified in the contralateral epididymis. Therefore, this case was considered to represent malignant transformation of a sporadically arising PCE. While most PCE cases are benign, the possibility of malignant transformation has rarely been described. Only a few reports have suggested such transformation, and detailed pathological documentation is limited. To date, five cases suggest malignant transformation of PCE [3, 4, 7, 8, 9], which provided detailed pathological descriptions. In three cases, tumors exhibited histological features resembling PCE, with cells having small round nuclei and abundant clear cytoplasm and proliferating in a papillary or intracystic papillary pattern. Additionally, some areas demonstrated atypical mitotic figures, necrosis, and invasive growth toward surrounding tissues, leading to a diagnosis of malignant transformation associated with PCE. Patients' ages were 27–82 years, and three cases of which had metastasis including ipsilateral testis or around ureter. No cases of recurrence were observed after treatment. In these prior cases, no description of MRI findings was provided. In the present case, however, the area showing restricted diffusion on MRI corresponded histopathologically to regions with nuclear atypia and invasive features, and was also strongly positive for Ki‐67 immunostaining (Figure 4A–D). Therefore, this is considered the first report to identify malignant transformation of PCE on MRI.

Malignant tumors arising in the epididymis are even more uncommon. Distinguishing between primary malignant tumors of the epididymis and metastatic lesions is important [3]. Specifically, distinguishing tumors from metastatic clear cell renal cell carcinoma is crucial, and immunohistochemical evaluation plays an essential role [4]. In terms of epithelial neoplasms, to date, 39 cases of adenocarcinoma and six cases of squamous cell carcinoma have been reported; however, some may have represented PCE or adenomatoid tumor misdiagnosed as carcinoma [2]. Therefore, the actual number of true carcinomas is presumed to be even smaller. Compared to these primary malignant tumors originating from the epididymis, PCE is thought to be slow‐growing and with a favorable prognosis. Urabe et al. [10] reviewed 26 cases of primary adenocarcinoma of the epididymis, one of which corresponded to the case reported by Kurihara et al. [3]. Of the remaining 25 cases, nine patients died of cancer and three experienced postoperative recurrence, indicating a relatively poor prognosis. Imaging findings revealed metastasis at the time of diagnosis or during postoperative follow‐up in seven cases, of which five died within 6–30 months. As for treatment strategy, epididymal tumors are rare, and characteristic imaging findings are not well established; therefore, surgical excision should be firstly considered. Although malignant transformation of PCE is thought to follow an indolent course with a favorable prognosis, the presence of areas with higher proliferative activity in Ki‐67 positivity, as observed in this case, suggests that proactive radical excision should be recommended.

Conclusion

We experienced an extremely rare case considered to represent the malignant transformation of PCE originating from the epididymis. Although this tumor may follow an indolent course, the presence of areas with higher proliferative potential suggests surgical excision be recommended as definitive treatment.
