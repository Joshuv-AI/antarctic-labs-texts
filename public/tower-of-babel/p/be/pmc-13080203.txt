Associations of selected atypical non-neuroendocrine cancers with multiple endocrine neoplasia type 1
Authors: Iina Yliaska, Joona Tapio, Timo A Kumpula, Vesa-Matti Pohjanen, Tapani Ebeling, Olavi Ukkola, Outi Kuismin, Minna Koivikko, Katri Pylkäs, Pasi I Salmela
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractContextMultiple endocrine neoplasia type 1 (MEN1) is a hereditary tumor predisposing syndrome, characterized by typical neuroendocrine tumors in parathyroid, pituitary, and endocrine pancreas tissues (“3P”). MEN1 is also associated with non-neuroendocrine cancers, which are less studied.ObjectiveTo assess the prevalence of certain non-neuroendocrine cancers associated with MEN1 in Finnish patients and compare the findings with a local control population.DesignRetrospective registry-based cohort study.Methods113 MEN1 patients (51 males) treated at Oulu University Hospital between 1982 and 2023, with an average follow-up of 56 patient-years, were compared to a local control population (n = 1 045 520 males, average follow-up of 73 patient-years). Data were analyzed using descriptive statistics, survival curves, and Cox regression models.ResultsSeven women with MEN1 (11.5%) had breast cancer (BC), 7 (6.2%) with papillary thyroid carcinoma (PTC), and 5 (4.4%) with renal cancers (RC). The mean age at BC diagnosis was 49 years compared to 60 in the general Finnish population. The hazard ratio (HR) for BC in female MEN1 patients was 3.79 (95% CI: 1.66-8.60) compared to controls. Pathogenic variants in established BC risk genes were not present in our MEN1 patients. The HR for PTC and RC were 9.37 (3.59-24.41) and 7.80 (2.74-22.25), respectively.ConclusionThis study demonstrates a significantly increased prevalence of BC, PTC, and RC in MEN1 patients compared to the Finnish general population. The findings support earlier BC screening in women with MEN1 and highlight the need for further research into non-neuroendocrine cancer risks in these patients.Keywords: MEN1, MEN1 gene, menin, breast cancer, thyroid cancer, renal cancer

Multiple endocrine neoplasia type 1 (MEN1) syndrome is a rare inherited tumor syndrome caused by germline pathogenic variants in the MEN1 gene coding the menin protein [1]. Loss of heterozygosity and inactivation of both alleles of this gene predispose to MEN1 syndrome [2, 3]. Menin is a scaffold chromatic adaptor protein, which interacts with various cellular proteins, including different nuclear receptors, epigenetic and cellular transcription factors, which are involved in gene regulation, DNA repair, and signaling transmission [2, 3]. Menin has contradictory effects both as an oncogenic cofactor and as a tumor suppressor, depending on tissue type and cellular context [3, 4]. In MEN1, abnormal structures and functions of menin are assumed to cause several changes in cellular functions and predispose to tumorigenesis [2-4]. Clinically, MEN1 syndrome is typically characterized by an increased risk of neuroendocrine tumors (NETs) [2, 5, 6]. These include parathyroid (pHPT), pituitary (PitNET), and pancreatic NETs (PNET), and less often other gastro-intestinal NETs. Other well-known but rarer tumors are thymic (TNET) and bronchopulmonary NETs [2, 5]. Adrenal tumors (AdrT) are also present in more than 20% of MEN1 patients [5].

In addition to neuroendocrine tumors, MEN1 syndrome has also been associated with more than 20 different neoplasms [2, 5-7]. Several studies have reported an increased prevalence of breast cancer (BC) in MEN1 compared to the general population [8, 9]. There are also reports of possible associations between MEN1 and thyroid [10, 11] and urogenital cancers [11, 12].

The aims of this study were (1) to evaluate the prevalence of BC, thyroid, and renal cancers (RC) previously reported in the literature to be associated with MEN1 in a registry-based retrospective Finnish MEN1 cohort and (2) to compare the findings with a representative local control population.

Materials and methods

Study population

The data were collected at the Department of Endocrinology at Oulu University Hospital between November 1982 and December 2023 and all patients diagnosed with MEN1 during that period were included in the data collection (n = 113, 45.1% males).

The criteria for MEN1 diagnosis were applied according to previous guidelines [2, 5]. All but one of our patients had a genetically confirmed diagnosis of MEN1. The patient without genetic MEN1 confirmation had pHPT and Zollinger-Ellison syndrome caused by a gastrinoma, meaning she had a clinical MEN1.

The Oulu Project Elucidating Risk of Atherosclerosis (OPERA) was utilized as a control population. OPERA is an epidemiological cohort study originally designed for addressing cardiovascular disease (CVD) risk factors and clinical endpoints. Subjects (n = 1045, 50% hypertensives, 50% males, age 40-62 years) were middle-aged hypertensives and their age- and sex-matched controls who were randomly selected between 1990-1993 from the local area, as previously described in detail [13]. At baseline, participants were interviewed, examined, and tested in the University of Oulu research laboratory. Mortality and hospital events of the population have since been followed and the project is ongoing, with the latest major data collection in 2014.

A flowchart presenting the study setup, and the analyses carried out is presented in the supplementary data (Fig. S1) [14].

The study protocol was approved by the medical ethics committee of the North Ostrobothnia Hospital District and board reviews at Oulu University Hospital. Genetic studies of the MEN1 gene in the patients have been conducted as part of routine clinical work and counseling process. All participants of the OPERA cohort gave written informed consent. The Declaration of Helsinki 1975 was adhered to during the study.

Clinical data and outcome classification

Patient data, including clinical information such as sex, age at diagnosis of MEN1, family history, characteristics of tumors, their treatment, and outcomes, were obtained from both electronic medical records and clinical charts. Data on MEN1 patients were collected until the end of 2024, and on the control population until the end of 2014.

The outcome “breast cancer” included all recorded BCs, including lobular carcinomas and ductal carcinomas. The outcome “thyroid cancer” included all recorded thyroid carcinomas. In both cohorts, all recorded thyroid cancers were papillary thyroid carcinomas (PTC). The outcome “renal cancer” (RC) included all recorded renal cell carcinomas (RCCs), including clear cell (CCRCC) and papillary (PRCC) carcinomas.

Breast cancer gene panel testing

The presence of pathogenic or likely pathogenic variants in established breast and/or ovarian cancer genes was investigated with next-generation sequencing based gene panel testing (ATM BARD1 BRCA1 BRCA2 BRIP1 CDH1 CHEK2 DICER1 EPCAM FANCM MCPH1 MLH1 MSH2 MSH6 NF1 PALB2 PMS2 PTEN RAD50 RAD51C RAD51D STK11, and TP53 genes) with Twist Bioscience Comprehensive exome library prep kit and run using Illumina NextSeq550 platform (2 × 151 bp paired-end reads). SOPHiA DDM pipeline, or DRAGEN enrichment and ANNOVAR, were used for data analysis.

Statistical methods

Prior to variance analysis, continuous variables were checked for skewness and kurtosis. For comparisons between 2 groups, one-way ANOVA was used for normally distributed variables, Kruskal–Wallis's test for non-normally distributed variables, and Pearson's chi-square test for categorical variables. Subjects with missing data were omitted from individual analyses. Information on missing data for each variable is reported in the supplementary materials (Table S1) [14]. For longitudinal analyses, all outcomes were analyzed separately and the follow-up ended in case of the event of interest or death.

Kaplan–Meier curves and Cox proportional hazards models were used to estimate the associations of MEN1 syndrome with the outcomes. For the Cox hazards models, both the MEN1 cohort and the control cohort were pooled. The hazards model included MEN1 syndrome as the only categorical explanatory variable. Based on descriptive statistics, all outcomes were also analyzed separately for males and females.

Characteristics of MEN1 patients diagnosed with breast cancer

Seven women, but no men, with MEN1 syndrome were diagnosed with BC during the study period (11.5% of females) (Tables 3 and S2) [14]. They were diagnosed at a mean age of 49.9 years and had MEN1 diagnosed at the age of 40.6 years (Table 3). MEN1 variants differed between patients with BC and those without (Tables 3 and S2, P = .014) [14]. Among the women with BC, 5 had the c1356_1367del12 gene variant, 1 had c.466G > C, and 1 had c.1252G > A. During the management of BC in our patients, none had clinical indications for germline BC risk gene analysis based on the oncological guidelines valid at that time. Afterwards, other pathogenic/likely pathogenic variants in known BC susceptibility genes were analyzed and excluded in those 6/7 BC patients, with available DNA for testing.

Of MEN1-associated typical tumors, pHPT was detected in 7 BC cases (100%), PNETs in 3 cases (50.0%, one with missing data), PitNET (all prolactinomas, 28.6%), and nonfunctional adrenal adenomas in both cases. None had DNET or other NET. Two BC patients also had PTC, being more common in MEN1 patients with BC compared to those without (Tables 3 and S2, P = .012) [14]. One of these PTC patients also had RC (Table 3).

Patient-specific characteristics of MEN1 patients with BC are presented in Table 4. Histological findings in 6 out of 7 women were invasive ductal carcinoma. One woman had invasive lobular carcinoma. Two patients had lymph node metastasis, and 1 patient's BC was widely spread at the time of diagnosis. Three patients are still alive (Table 4).

Characteristics of MEN1 patients diagnosed with thyroid cancer

The prevalence of PTC in all MEN1 patients was 6.2% (7 cases) (Table 3). They were diagnosed at the age of 44.4 years and had MEN1 diagnosed at an average age of 42.4 years (Table 3). Their predisposing genetic variants differed from those of patients without PTC (Tables 3 and S3, P = .001) [14]. Two patients had c.1356_1367del12, 2 had c.1546dup, 1 had c.1579C > T, and one had c.1252G > A and was also diagnosed with BC. One patient's gene MEN1 variant c.1064G > A is considered a VUS (variant of unknown significance).

All the 7 patients with PTC were diagnosed with pHPT and 3 had PNET (1 gastrinoma, 1 PPoma, and 1 nonfunctional PNET). PitNET was diagnosed in 3 patients (1 prolactinoma and 2 nonfunctioning adenomas), and 1 patient had AdrT. Two PTC patients also had BC, being more common in MEN1 patients with PTC compared to those without (Table S3, P = .012) [14], and one of these patients also had RC (Table S3) [14]. Patient-specific characteristics of MEN1 patients with PTC are presented in Table 5. One of them had PTC metastases in the lungs and another in the lymph nodes.

Cumulative risk of non-neuroendocrine cancers in MEN1 patients compared to the OPERA control cohort

Finally, we evaluated the cumulative risk for developing each non-neuroendocrine cancer in patients diagnosed with MEN1 (n = 113, 45.1% males, average follow-up time 56.1 patient-years) compared to the representative local OPERA control cohort (n = 1045, 49.8% males, average follow-up time 73.8 patient-years) (Table S5) [14].

A total of 33 women (3.2%) in the OPERA control cohort were diagnosed with BC compared to the 7 women in the MEN1 cohort (6.2%). In the control cohort, the average age of BC diagnosis was 62.2 years, significantly higher when compared to the MEN1 patients’ 49.9 years (P = .001, Table S5) [14]. In a crude Cox regression model, the hazard ratio (HR) with 95% confidence interval (CI) for BC in all MEN1 patients was 4.48 (1.97-10.18) (Fig. 1A, Table S6) [14] and 3.79 (1.66-8.60) in female MEN1 patients (Fig. S2A and Table S6) [14], respectively.

In the OPERA control cohort, only 11 patients (1.1%) were diagnosed with PTC, a significantly lower prevalence than in the MEN1 cohort (6.2%) (P < .001, Table S5) [14]. The average age of PTC diagnosis in the control cohort was 49.6 years compared to 44.4 years in the MEN1 patients (Table S5) [14]. In a crude Cox regression model, the HR for PTC in all MEN1 patients was 9.37 (3.59-24.41) (Fig. 1B), 12.87 (3.70-45.03) in female MEN1 patients (Figure S2B) [14], and 5.74 (1.14-28.8) in male MEN1 patients (Fig. S3A) [14], respectively.

Thirteen patients (1.2%) were diagnosed with RC in the OPERA control cohort, a significantly lower prevalence than in the MEN1 patients (4.4%) (P = .025, Table S5) [14]. The average age of RC diagnosis in the control cohort was 58.8 years compared to 59.6 years in the MEN1 patients (Table S5) [14]. In a crude Cox regression model, the HR for RC in all MEN1 patients was 7.80 (2.74-22.25) (Fig. 1C), 5.18 (1.03-26.14) in female MEN1 patients (Figure S2C) [14], and 12.35 (3.10-49.17) in male MEN1 patients (Fig. S3B) [14], respectively.

Discussion

Our retrospective MEN1 cohort consists of 113 patients. The aim of this study was to evaluate the occurrence of non-neuroendocrine cancers in MEN1. Breast cancer (BC) was diagnosed in 11.5% of women with MEN1, papillary thyroid carcinoma (PTC) in 6.2%, and renal cancer (RC) in 4.4% of all MEN1 patients. The age at BC diagnosis in women with MEN1 was significantly lower than in the OPERA control cohort. All these findings indicate a higher prevalence compared with age-adjusted, population-based statistics from the Finnish Cancer Registry (FCR, https://cancerregistry.fi/reports-and-publications/annual-report-on-cancer-in-finland/, page accessed on May 1, 2025) or with the local OPERA control cohort.

Heterozygosity of a pathogenic MEN1 (NM 001370259.2) variant was confirmed in every MEN1-BC patient in our cohort. Five of them carried an in-frame deletion c.1356_1367del12 (p.Gln453_Arg456del) in exon 10. Two BC MEN1 women carried missense variants, one had c.1252G > A (p.Asp418Asn) on exon 9 and the other c.466G > C on exon 3 (p.Gly156Arg). The prevalent variant is 1 of our 2 founder mutations in Northern Finland [16]. All these variants are supposed to result in a structurally and functionally altered menin protein. Curiously, there were no women with BC in our other large pedigree of MEN1 patients with the pathogenic variant c.1546dup in exon 10. Genetic testing is the only definitive confirmatory test for the diagnosis of MEN1. The traditional consensus has been that there are no genotype–phenotype correlations in genetically confirmed MEN1 [2]. This conclusion has recently been challenged [17], and our data suggest that in the case of some non-neuroendocrine cancers in MEN1, such correlations require further studies.

Previous studies have reported BC with a mean age of diagnosis ranging from 48 to 56 years (median age of 45 years) in women with MEN1 [7, 8, 11, 18-26]. In our study, the mean age of diagnosis of BC was 50 years in women with MEN1, lower than in female OPERA controls (62 years) or in the average Finnish female population (65 years) (FCR). BC was diagnosed in 11.5% of our MEN1 women (on average after 58 patient-years of follow-up) compared to 6.3% in female OPERA controls (on average after 73 patient-years of follow-up) and 8.3% to 11.2% in female population statistics from the Finnish Cancer Registry (70-80-year-old females) (FRC). The actual lifetime point prevalence in Finnish women to develop BC is 13.4% (FCR), and the incidence of BC in Finland has more than doubled from 1980 to the year 2020. In recent MEN1 cohorts, BC has been reported in 1.7% to 4.2% of MEN1 female patients [11, 25, 26]. When compared to our local OPERA control cohort, the risk for developing BC in our MEN1 women was 3.79 (1.66-8.60), slightly higher than the previously reported risks from other cohorts, ranging from 2.31 to 2.83 [8]. Our data suggest that BC can manifest in MEN1 women significantly earlier than in the average population, but data on the overall point prevalence of BC in MEN1 is currently lacking.

The MEN1 gene codes for the protein is menin [1]. It is widely expressed in various tissues and mostly expressed in the cell nucleus [2]. It is a scaffold nuclear protein, which has interactions with more than 50 different nuclear proteins and functions through such interacting partners, especially with those which regulate and control transcription, epigenetic regulation, chromatin modifiers, DNA repair proteins, and cell signaling proteins [2-4]. In typical NETs in MEN1, loss of menin and its tumor suppressive effects is thought to lead to the disruption of antiproliferative gene expression programs and to development of tumors [27, 28]. In certain sporadic solid tumors such as BC, previous studies have found that menin can also have a pro-oncogenic effect and could be involved in BC pathogenesis [3, 28-30]. Moreover, menin inhibitors have been tested as an adjunct to BC treatment [3, 31]. Menin binds and is a coactivator for the nuclear protein estrogen receptor alpha (ERα) and regulates the expression of the gene ESR1 coding ERα, which are important drivers for BC tumorigenesis [30, 32-34]. However, in sporadic non-MEN1 BC tumors, the role of menin in tumor pathogenesis is still uncertain and somewhat contradictory as both tumor promoter and tumor suppressive effects have been found [28, 34-36]. In experimental MEN1 models, there are reports suggesting tumor suppressive effects of menin in the normal mammary epithelium [34, 35, 37]. In BC women with MEN1, there are studies reporting a reduced expression of menin and a complete loss of heterozygosity of the MEN1 gene locus in breast tumor samples [8, 23]. MEN1-BC tumors have been luminal-like, expressing ERα and/or progesterone receptors, and have been histologically classified as invasive ductal carcinomas [8, 23]. In our MEN1 women with BC, histologically, a lobular carcinoma was found in 1 and an invasive ductal carcinoma in 6 of them. In 2 women, the ductal carcinoma was aggressive and led to death. We had immunohistochemical data in 6 BC women with MEN1 and in 4 of them, BC was luminal B-like and luminal A-like in 2 of them.

Although clinical and experimental data suggest an association of MEN1 syndrome and BC, the causal relationship remains uncertain [7, 38]. Breast carcinogenesis is a multistep and complex process involving the accumulation of genetic alterations and is influenced by various environmental and hormonal risk factors [29, 30, 39, 40]. In a Dutch study of MEN1 patients, various BC risk factors were analyzed and the findings indicated that the increased BC risk in MEN1 carriers was not related to other known BC risk factors or familial cancer history [9]. About 5% to 10% of BC patients have inherited predisposition due to germline mutations of various BC risk genes [41]. However, there are only 2 previous reports where BC risk genes have been studied in MEN1 patients [42, 43]. To the best of our knowledge, our study is the only one in which known BC risk genes have been reported in MEN1 BC women. We analyzed an extensive panel of pathogenic or likely pathogenic variants of BC genes available in 6 out of 7 of our BC patients. We did not find highly penetrant BRCA1 and BRCA2 gene variants on them, which have been previously noted in case reports with simultaneous mutations in both MEN1 and BRCA2 or MEN1 and BRCA1 genes [42, 43]. Also, other pathogenic or likely pathogenic variants in established BC genes were excluded in our patients. Previous large studies on genetic predisposition to BC have been unable to identify the MEN1 gene as a significant susceptibility gene in women with sporadic BC [44, 45] or in women with hereditary BC [46, 47], but MEN1 somatic mutations seem to have a driver role in women with metastatic BC [48]. In conclusion, menin seems to have a role in BC tumorigenesis, but in BC of MEN1 patients, the pathogenesis is uncertain, and it is thought that menin alone is not sufficient for BC development [28].

A higher prevalence of PTC was observed in our MEN1 patients (6.2%) compared to the OPERA controls (1.1%). Based on the Finnish Cancer Registry, the risk of developing PTC before the age of 70-80 years is 0.53% to 0.65% in the general population (FCR), indicating a similar prevalence as in our OPERA control cohort. The HR for PTC in our MEN1 patients was 9.37 (3.59-24.41) compared to the OPERA control cohort. Thus, our data suggest a higher prevalence of PTC in MEN1 patients compared to the general population. Although there are reports of a possible association between MEN1 and thyroid cancer [11, 49-51], this relationship is thought to be incidental, possibly at least partially related to increased use of ultrasound examination [52], and no loss of heterozygosity of the MEN1 locus has been observed in PTC tumor tissue in MEN1 patients [53-55]. It has been concluded that there is no robust evidence from in vitro, animal, or human studies linking germline mutation of the MEN1 gene with the development of PTC [50, 53]. Although we report an increased prevalence of PTC in MEN1 patients, this finding may be due to surveillance bias, and there is currently insufficient data to support a causal association between MEN1, and the occurrence of PTC and further studies are warranted.

There are only a few previous studies on the possible association between MEN1 syndrome and RC [11, 12, 49]. In our study with a small number of cases (n = 5), a higher prevalence of RC was observed in the MEN1 patients (4.4%) compared to OPERA controls (1.2%). Based on the Finnish Cancer Registry, the risk of developing RC before the age of 70 to 80 years is 0.70% to 1.16% (FCR), suggesting a similar prevalence as in our OPERA control cohort. The HR for RC in our MEN1 patients was higher (7.80) compared to the OPERA control cohort with a longer follow-up period, suggesting a higher risk of RC in MEN1 patients compared to the general population. In a murine Men1 knockout model, heterozygous Men1 mutant mice did not develop RCs [37]. Therefore, the data about the causal relationship between MEN1 and the occurrence of RC is uncertain.

Although our data are consistent with current knowledge, several validity issues must be considered. We lack reliable information on socioeconomic and behavioral factors such as smoking and occupation, established cancer risk factors, and details on how certain cancers were initially diagnosed. It can also not be excluded that an unknown genetic factor closely linked to MEN1 could contribute to the increased risk of cancer types observed in our MEN1 patients. Our OPERA control cohort does not directly represent a general population cohort, is not designed for cancer incidence evaluation, and is not age-matched or matched for key clinical breast cancer (BC) risk factors relative to our study population. Because the OPERA cohort was not originally intended as a control cohort for MEN1, information on several important risk factors for our main outcomes is missing, and therefore, these factors could not be included in our regression models.

For BC, however, the most important risk factors according to the World Health Organization are female sex and age. Both are accounted for in our models, as Cox regression inherently adjusts for age, and we performed separate analyses for men and women. In addition, we excluded the possibility of known high-risk BC genes such as BRCA1/2 in our MEN1 patients with BC, thereby addressing another major category of BC risk factors. Nevertheless, we lack information on reproductive history and postmenopausal hormone therapy in both the OPERA controls and the MEN1 cohort, meaning that residual confounding may partly explain the magnitude of the observed hazard ratios.

The National Breast Cancer Screening Program in Finland was launched in 1987 for women aged 50 to 59 years and expanded to include women aged 50 to 69 years in 2007. Invitation coverage increased among women over 60 years of age until 2016, reaching over 95% in 2014. Thus, although the data collection periods for the OPERA cohort and the MEN1 cohort differ slightly, BC screening practices were largely comparable between the 2 groups. Moreover, the OPERA control cohort is slightly older and has a longer follow-up period than our MEN1 cohort, suggesting that if anything, the OPERA cohort should have a higher cancer prevalence than an age-matched control cohort. The OPERA population is also local, and the study period (1990s-2000s) aligns well with the time frame of most MEN1 patient data. Finally, our findings are further supported by comparisons with population-based data from the Finnish Cancer Registry. The demographics of our MEN1 cohort are similar to those reported in three large MEN1 cohorts from the Netherlands, Italy, and France [6, 26, 56].

In summary, in this retrospective study, the risk of BC, PTC, and RC was higher in MEN1 patients compared with controls. Among women with MEN1 in whom other germline mutations in BC risk genes had been excluded, BC was diagnosed, on average, more than a decade earlier than in the control subjects. Our findings support the original Dutch and subsequent recommendations to initiate BC screening in women with MEN1 at the age of 40 years [9, 57-59].
