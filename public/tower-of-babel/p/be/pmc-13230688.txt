Rhythmic skills mediate the link between music training and cognition via attention and phonological processing
Authors: Fernando Lizcano-Cortés, Pablo Ripollés, Fernando A Barrios, M Florencia Assaneo
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractRhythmic abilities are linked to performance across a range of cognitive tasks, yet the mechanisms underlying this relationship remain unclear. We evaluated 98 Mexican participants using the Speech-to-Speech Synchronization Test (SSS-Test), a self-report measure of musical training, and an extensive cognitive battery. Analyses revealed that phonological language processing and temporal attention are the core cognitive skills associated with auditory-motor rhythmic proficiency (as assessed by the SSS-Test). Importantly, this relationship appears thresholded: cognitive benefits emerge only when rhythmic abilities exceed a specific level, with further refinements conferring no additional advantage. Moreover, we show that the link between musical training and cognition is fully mediated by this rhythmic performance threshold. These findings provide a unifying framework for understanding how rhythmic skills impact cognition and clarify the mechanisms through which musical training may confer cognitive benefits.Subject terms: Sensorimotor processing, Human behaviour

Introduction

Auditory-motor rhythmic skills (hereafter referred to simply as “rhythmic skills”), understood as the ability to coordinate different body movements with a sound cue, is a trait intrinsically linked to the human experience—a prerequisite for dancing and playing in a band1, and thought to support aspects of our ability to speak2,3. Research in both developmental and adult populations has shown that enhanced rhythmic abilities positively correlate with performance across a broad range of cognitive abilities, as assessed by standardized tasks designed to isolate specific cognitive domains. For instance, greater alignment of motor actions with auditory tempo is associated with better performance in phonological awareness4, statistical learning5,6, reading7–10, rapid naming11 and verbal memory12. Furthermore, individual differences in rhythmic abilities have been associated both with the risk of speech and language developmental disorders13 and with age-related cognitive decline14,15. However, each of these studies has typically focused on the specific relationship between rhythmic skills and a single task or a set of tasks within the same cognitive domain. This leaves an important question unanswered: Could there be a small set of core cognitive mechanisms that account for the advantages afforded by rhythmic abilities across a range of cognitive tasks? For example, it has been proposed that predictive processing may be the link between rhythmic abilities and cognitive skills16.

Although auditory–motor synchronization abilities have been studied through the alignment of various motor actions (such as tapping4,10,13,15, drumming9,11,12, clapping8 or walking7) to different rhythmic auditory stimuli, they remain largely underexplored in the context of speech. To address this gap, in a previous study, we introduced a deceptively simple behavioral test to assess rhythmic skills in the context of speech: the Speech-to-Speech Synchronization Test (SSS-Test)17,18. This test evaluates participants’ ability to synchronize their speech motor production with a rhythmic audio, revealing that the general population can be divided into two groups: high synchronizers, who can proficiently align their produced rhythm with the perceived one, and low synchronizers, who struggle to align their motor output with the sound cue. Since its introduction, this test has gained widespread use as a valuable tool for accounting for individual differences, enabling a more precise characterization of different cognitive phenomena5,6,19–25. Building on the effectiveness of this task in identifying groups with markedly different rhythmic abilities and its proven success in advancing our understanding of cognitive processes, we propose that the SSS-Test may serve as a useful tool to further explore the relationship between rhythmic and cognitive skills.

Importantly, given the close relationship between rhythmic abilities and musical training, understanding the link between rhythmic skills and general cognitive mechanisms could provide valuable insights into the longstanding debate regarding the impact of musical training on cognition. On one side of this debate, “pro-music” researchers argue that musical training enhances a variety of cognitive skills. On the other side, “neutral” researchers contend that no such effect exists. This controversy stems from conflicting findings: while a considerable number of studies point to a positive effect of musical training on cognition26–37, others report a subtle or even nonexistent correlation between cognition and musical practice38–41. These discrepancies are further reflected in meta-analyses and systematic reviews42–48. Interestingly, more recent studies that consider the “two dimensions of music” (i.e., melody and rhythm) consistently show that it is rhythmic, rather than melodic, training that confers benefits on specific cognitive skills, such as phonological awareness and memory49, and inhibition50.

Synthesizing insights from the studies discussed above, we recently proposed a set of interconnected hypotheses, providing a framework to reconcile conflicting findings regarding the link between musical training and cognitive abilities51. Specifically, we hypothesized that: (1) rhythmic skills are positively linked to fundamental cognitive mechanisms; (2) cognitive enhancements emerge only after reaching a minimum threshold of rhythmic proficiency; and (3) musical training benefits cognition primarily through its effects on rhythmic skills. The present study aims to test these hypotheses by combining the SSS-Test with a comprehensive cognitive battery and a detailed assessment of individual musical training.

Methods

Participants

Participant recruitment was conducted through social media advertisements, which allowed for a broad and inclusive sampling, ensuring a representative cohort from diverse backgrounds. An initial cohort of 203 participants underwent the screening phase of the study, during which they were evaluated using an online version of the Speech-to-Speech Synchronization Test (see section SSS-Test). Participants were recruited through advertisements posted on social media platforms (i.e., Facebook and Instagram). All individuals were native Mexican Spanish speakers, aged 18 to 45 years, and residing in Mexico at the time of the evaluation. A total of 27 participants were excluded based on the SSS-Test exclusion criteria (see SSS-Test section).

From the initial cohort of 176 individuals, 100 participants were selected ensuring a balanced representation of high and low synchronizers, as determined by the SSS-Test outcome (see section SSS-Test). This balanced cohort completed all three follow-up sessions of the study. Data from two participants were not recorded, resulting in a final analyzed dataset comprising 98 native Mexican Spanish speakers (62 female, based on a demographic questionnaire), aged 18–43 years (mean age = 28 ± 5), with an average of 16 years (± 4) of formal education. No data on race or ethnicity were collected. Since there was no explicit requirement regarding participants’ musical background for study enrollment, we assume that this cohort is representative of the general young adult Mexican population. This cohort included three participants with more than six years of formal musical training and five additional participants identifying themselves as musicians. Participants completed the Gold-MSI Music Training subscale, yielding a mean score of 14.5 ( ± 7.5), with scores ranging from 7 to 42.

Additionally, these same participants were invited to complete two separate post hoc control sessions, each consisting of a distinct task: one session involved a modified version of the Processing Speed task, while the other included the Eriksen Flanker task. A total of 82 participants (54 females, mean age = 27.9 ± 5.4 years) completed the Processing Speed task, of which 68 participants (45 females, mean age = 28.1 ± 5.2 years) also completed the Eriksen Flanker task.

All participants self-reported having no neurological or hearing disorders, provided informed consent, and received financial compensation for their participation. The study was approved by the Ethics Committee of the Instituto de Neurobiología, Universidad Nacional Autónoma de México (protocol 096.H), and was conducted in compliance with the federal guidelines of the Mexican Ministry of Health.

Sample size

Our intended sample size of 100 participants was based on previous studies reporting significant differences between high and low synchronizers on various cognitive skills. For example, differences in statistical learning have been reported in samples of 24 high and 20 low synchronizers17, as well as in studies with nearly 100 participants examining how rhythmic features of stimuli interact with phonotactics6. Other work has shown effects of rhythmic speech production on perception in samples of 30 high and 29 low participants19, and differences in temporal auditory processing have been observed in samples including 35 high and 20 low synchronizers22.

For a clear interpretation of the results, we conducted sensitivity power analyses using G*Power (v.3.1). With α = 0.05 and power = 0.80, the analyses indicated that: (i) for correlations computed in the full sample (N = 98), the minimum detectable effect size was r = 0.28; (ii) for within-group correlations (N = 47), the minimum detectable effect size was r = 0.39; and (iii) for group comparisons between high and low synchronizers (n = 51 vs. 47), the study was powered to detect effects of d = 0.58. Throughout the manuscript, when interpreting significant and non-significant results, we explicitly acknowledge that the study was designed to detect medium-to-large effects, which we consider the range of effect sizes likely to have meaningful ecological relevance.

General experimental design

The entire study was designed and hosted on the Gorilla Experiment Builder platform52, enabling remote data collection. The tasks included in the protocol can be accessed via the following link: https://app.gorilla.sc/openmaterials/1205864.

The study consisted of a screening phase followed by three experimental sessions conducted on different days (Fig. 1). During the screening phase, a large cohort of participants completed the SSS-Test and a demographic questionnaire. Based on the SSS-Test results, participants were categorized as high or low synchronizers, and a stratified quasi-random sampling procedure was applied to obtain a smaller new sample with a balanced representation of both groups (see section SSS-Test). The selected balanced sample of participants then completed the three experimental sessions. Sessions 1 and 2, each lasting approximately 1 h, involved the administration of different sequences of cognitive assessments. One sequence included the following tasks: Arithmetic, Digit Span Forwards, Digit Span Backwards, Eriksen Flanker, Phonological Verbal Fluency, Semantic Verbal Fluency, Phonological Statistical Learning, Visuospatial Working Memory, and Vocabulary Size. The other sequence comprised: Go/NoGo, Local/Global, Non-word Repetition, Processing Speed, Speech in Noise, Spoonerism, and Wisconsin Card Sorting. Within each session, the sequence of tasks was fixed; however, the order of Sessions 1 and 2 was randomly assigned for each participant. For further details about the tasks, refer to the section Cognitive Battery. Finally, during Session 3, participants completed the musical training subscale of the Goldsmiths Musical Sophistication Index (Gold-MSI) self-report questionnaire (see section Gold-MSI). The three experimental sessions were conducted within a one-month period, with at least three days between each session.

SSS-test: speech-to-speech synchronization test

This test evaluates an individual’s auditory-motor synchronization abilities in the context of speech. It has been shown to effectively categorize the general population into two groups18,54 and has previously been used to investigate various cognitive processes6,17,19,20,22,55. In this study, we employed the explicit accelerated version, as described in ref. 18. Participants were instructed to repeatedly whisper the syllable ‘tah’ while listening to a rhythmic train of syllables, with explicit instructions to synchronize their speech with the external rhythm. The produced speech was recorded, and each individual’s degree of synchrony was calculated as the phase-locking value (PLV) between the 4–5 Hz–filtered envelopes of the produced and perceived speech signals. A detailed description of the task procedure and data analysis is provided in ref. 18.

Following the exclusion criteria outlined in ref. 18, all collected audio recordings were carefully listened to and visually inspected in Praat56. Recordings were excluded if: (i) the participant spoke aloud instead of whispering; (ii) the recording contained high environmental noise, preventing reliable reconstruction of the speech envelope; (iii) silence gaps between “tah” utterances exceeded 3 s; or (iv) the spoken rate was equal to or lower than 2 Hz (i.e., the participant produced two or fewer “tah”s per second).

Since we relied on the SSS-Test outcome to classify participants as low or high synchronizers, we first verified the presence of a bimodal distribution in the synchronization measurements obtained during the screening phase. To this end, we fitted Gaussian mixture models with one and two components to the obtained PLVs and compared their goodness-of-fit. Both the Akaike Information Criterion (AIC) and the Bayesian Information Criterion (BIC) identified the two-component model as providing the best fit (unimodal fit: AIC = −55, BIC = −49; bimodal fit: AIC = −122 BIC = −106). Notably, a visual inspection of the histogram further supports this quantitative assessment (see Fig. 2). Building on this result, the Gaussian mixture model with two components was used to classify participants as high or low synchronizers. Specifically, participants with a PLV above or below the intersection of the two distributions were classified as high or low synchronizers, respectively (see Fig. 2). Next, a stratified quasi-random sampling procedure was applied to select a sample of 100 participants, ensuring a balanced representation of high and low synchronizers. These participants then completed the remainder of the study, and their Speech-to-Speech Synchronization scores (PLV) were treated as binary categories, given the confirmed bimodal distribution of this measure.

Cognitive battery

All tasks comprising the battery are widely used in the literature and have been previously validated as effective assessments of various cognitive skills. For this study, selected tasks were adapted for remote administration to Mexican Spanish speakers. Accordingly, only a brief description of the tasks and the applied adaptations are provided below, and the reader is referred to existing literature for detailed descriptions of the tasks.

Arithmetic

To evaluate the ability to engage with abstract numerical concepts and perform numerical operations, we adapted the Arithmetic subtest from the WAIS-IV battery57. This test required participants to mentally solve 15 arithmetic problems within a specified time limit. The problems were translated into Spanish, presented visually on a screen, and participants were instructed to type their solutions into a response box. Each problem was presented for a maximum of 60 s, and participants immediately proceeded to the next problem upon typing their answer. A “catch” trial was incorporated within the set of 15 problems to ensure that participants were not using a calculator. In this trial, participants were asked to solve the square root of 6942.

The final score for this test was computed as the percentage of correct responses, with participants answering with more than two digits of precision on the catch trial being scored as “NaN”.

Digit span forward and backwards

To assess working memory, we adapted the Digit Span Forward and Backward subtests from the WAIS-IV battery57 for remote administration. Participants were visually presented with sequences of numbers of increasing length, with a visual cue indicating the end of each sequence and signaling the participants to type the previously presented sequence. In the Forward test, sequences were expected to be typed in the presented order, while in the Backward test, participants were required to type the sequence in reverse order.

The length, defined as the number of digits in the sequence, increases every two trials. For the Forward test, sequences ranged from 5 to 9 digits, and for the Backward test, sequences ranged from 3 to 8 digits, resulting in a total of 10 and 12 trials, respectively. Each digit was displayed for 600 ms, with a 700 ms inter-stimulus interval (ISI) between digits.

To ensure participants understood the task instructions and were focused, a validation phase procedure was implemented prior to the main task. Participants were required to correctly recall two consecutive sequences of a fixed length: 4 digits for the forward task and 2 digits for the backward task. If they failed to recall both sequences correctly, they were presented with another pair of sequences of the same length. This process was repeated up to a maximum of four times. If participants failed to correctly recall both sequences within any of these four attempts, they were excluded from the entire experiment. However, no participants were excluded for this reason.

To address the less controlled experimental conditions associated with online assessment, we included three additional “catch” trials. Two short, 2-digit-long “catch” trials were included to ensure participants’ attention, while a third, 12-digit-long “catch” trial was used to verify that participants were not writing down the sequences.

For both tests (i.e., Forward and Backward), performance was scored as the total number of correctly recalled sequences, weighted by the number of digits in each sequence. Participants who failed either of the 2-digit-long trials or succeeded in the 12-digit-long trial had their data replaced with ‘NaN’.

Eriksen Flanker

To evaluate selective attention and inhibitory control, we adapted the Eriksen Flanker task58,59. Participants were visually presented with a horizontal line of five arrows and were instructed to indicate, as quickly and accurately as possible, whether the central arrow was pointing to the left or to the right. Each visual stimulus was displayed for 1 second, or until the participant’s keypress, whichever occurred first, followed by a 0.25-second inter-trial interval (ITI) featuring a fixation cross.

The main task comprised of 120 trials, which were divided equally into congruent conditions, where all arrows pointed in the same direction (⇨⇨⇨⇨⇨), and incongruent conditions, where the orientations of the arrows varied (⇨⇦⇦⇨⇦⇨).

Given the online nature of our study, two preparatory steps were administered before the main assessment. First, participants completed a training session designed to ensure comprehension of the instructions. This session consisted of 10 trials, during which participants received feedback indicating whether their responses were correct or incorrect. The first 4 trials had no time limit, while the remaining trials required participants to respond within 1500 ms after the stimulus presentation. If participants exceeded the time limit in these timed trials, a “Try faster” feedback message was displayed. Second, participants completed a validation phase to ensure they had a basic understanding of the task and were actively engaged. This phase consisted of 10 trials without feedback, each with a 1250 ms response time limit. Participants were required to answer at least seven trials correctly to proceed to the main task, with up to five attempts. None of our participants required more than four attempts to complete this phase.

After these two preparatory steps, participants continued to the main task. For each participant, the score was calculated by dividing the percentage of correct responses by the 5% trimmed mean response time for correct answers (in seconds).

An additional version of the task was developed for the post-hoc control experiment. In this version, the stimuli were presented at unpredictable intervals. Specifically, the only modification to the previous design involved varying the duration of the ITI from a fixed 0.25 s to a random duration between 0.25 and 2.5 s. To achieve this, the total interval range (0.25–2.5 s) was divided by the number of stimuli, resulting in fixed increments of approximately 18 ms. These ITIs were then randomized to eliminate any potential temporal predictability.

Go/NoGo

To evaluate inhibitory control, we adapted a Go/NoGo task60. In this task, a 2 × 2 matrix of four stars was displayed at the center of the screen, and every 500 ms, one of the stars was replaced by a letter (P, T, or R) for 250 ms. Participants were instructed to press the spacebar as quickly and accurately as possible whenever a non-target letter appeared (Go trials) but to withhold their response if the letter matched a predefined target (No-Go trials). Responses were allowed until the subsequent letter presentation (i.e., participants had 750 ms to either emit or omit their response).

The main Go/NoGo task consisted of two sequential blocks, each featuring a different experimental condition: “R-NoGo” and “P-NoGo”. In these conditions, participants were instructed to withhold their response to the target letter “R” or “P,” respectively. Each condition encompassed 80 letter presentations, 24% of which were randomly distributed as NoGo targets and the rest was equally distributed between the two Go options (i.e., T and P for the “R-NoGo” condition; T and R for the “P-NoGo”).

As in the Eriksen Flanker task, two additional preparatory steps were included to address the challenges of the online assessment condition. Initially, participants completed a training session to facilitate comprehension of the instructions. This session consisted of six trials, during which they received immediate feedback after each trial regarding their performance. The training trials were presented at a slower rate: the four stars were displayed for 1000 ms, and the letter stimulus was displayed for 500 ms. Following the training session, participants completed a validation phase to verify their understanding and engagement. In this phase, participants completed 10 trials without feedback. The temporal parameters of the stimulus were adjusted to approximate that of the main task, with the letter presented for 250 ms and the four stars displayed for 625 ms. This phase was repeated until reaching a minimum of 70% accuracy. None of the participants required more than three attempts to advance to the main task.

The score for this task was quantified using the d’ (d-prime) coefficient, calculated from responses recorded during the main task. Hits were defined as the percentage of trials in which the participant correctly pressed the spacebar during a Go trial. False alarms were defined as the percentage of trials in which the participant incorrectly pressed the spacebar during a No-Go trial.

Local–Global

To assess attentional shift, an adaptation of the local-global task60 was used. In this task, participants were presented with a larger figure (the global element) composed of smaller, identical figures (the local elements) and were instructed to identify either the global or local figure based on the color of the image. The stimuli were constructed from three basic shapes: circles, triangles and squares, either blue or red. The task consisted of four phases: global training, local training, validation phase and the main experimental phase.

During global training, images were presented in blue, and participants were instructed to indicate the number of sides of the global element by pressing the corresponding number key (one for circles, three for triangles, and four for squares). This training began with three example stimuli presented without a time limit, providing feedback indicating whether the response was correct or incorrect. Participants then completed four timed trials with the same feedback, followed by 10 timed trials without feedback. In timed trials, participants had a limit of 2 s to respond, and the ITI was set to 700 ms. Next, during the local training, images were presented in red, and participants were instructed to respond based on the local element using the same number keys. As in the global training, participants completed three trials without time limit and with feedback, 4 timed trials with feedback, and 10 timed trials without feedback.

To ensure understanding of the task instructions, participants then completed a validation phase. This phase consisted of 10 timed mixed trials (five local in red and five global in blue, presented in random order) without feedback. To proceed to the main experimental phase, participants were required to attain a minimum accuracy of 60%, with a maximum of five attempts. No participant required more than three attempts to proceed to the main phase.

In the main experimental phase, images were presented in either red or blue, requiring participants to apply the previously learned rules (i.e., red for local elements and blue for global elements). This phase comprised 48 timed trials without feedback (24 local and 24 global, presented in randomized order).

The performance score, calculated for the main experimental phase only, was determined by dividing percentage of correct responses by the 5% trimmed mean response time for correct answers (in seconds).

Non-word repetition

To assess phonological working memory and phonological assembly, participants completed a non-word repetition task61. Participants were auditorily presented with valid Spanish syllable sequences that formed no real words and were required to repeat them as soon as they finished, within a maximum period of four seconds. Following established methods62, 49 non-words of varying lengths were presented. Specifically, the sample included 23 three-syllable non-words and 23 four-syllable non-words, and an extra 3 three-syllable non-words for a training session. All audio stimuli were recorded by a female native Mexican Spanish voice-over artist to ensure high-quality and consistent pronunciation.

Prior to the main task, participants completed three practice trials comprising three-syllable non-words, during which the correct spelling of the non-word was presented both auditory and visually after each repetition.

Three independent researchers reviewed each participant’s recordings to assess the accuracy of non-word repetitions. To be considered correct, responses had to match the target non-word exactly, with no errors in syllable or phoneme pronunciation. Discrepancies among researchers (fewer than 4 of items per participant) were resolved through discussion until consensus was reached. Performance was quantified using a combined accuracy and speed score, calculated by dividing the percentage of correct responses by the 5% trimmed mean reaction time in seconds.

Phonological statistical learning

This protocol is based on the established cognitive procedure for assessing phonological statistical learning, in which participants are presented with isochronous syllable sequences and are required to identify combinations of syllables that consistently co-occur5,63.

Two pseudo-languages -designated as “language 2” and “language 4” in a previous study6- were used, each composed of four trisyllabic pseudowords. Participants completed two runs of this task, one for each pseudo-language. On each run, they were initially presented with a 2-min audio stimulus comprising a continuous concatenation (in a randomized fashion) of the four pseudowords that made up the language. Subsequently, they completed eight two-alternative forced-choice questions. In each question, a three-syllable combination forming a pseudoword was presented, paired with an incorrect combination (two syllables from one pseudoword paired with one from another). Participants were instructed to select the combination that sounded “more familiar.”

The learning score was calculated as the percentage of correct responses across both runs.

Processing speed

To evaluate processing speed, defined as the ability to rapidly process information, we adapted the pattern comparison task described in the NIH Cognition Toolbox64,65. On each trial, a pair of images was presented simultaneously on the screen and participants were asked to press a key as quickly as possible to indicate whether the images were identical or different. The main task consisted of 48 trials, with half of the pairs containing identical images and the other half containing different images. Differences included variations in color or alterations where part of the image was removed. Each pair remained on the screen until the participant responded, or for a maximum of 1.5 s, though responses were typically made well within this time frame. If no response was recorded, the trial was considered incorrect. Regardless of the trial outcome, the next trial began after a 0.25-s ITI with a blank screen.

Given the online nature of our assessment, two preparatory steps were implemented before the main task to ensure participants’ understanding and attention. First, participants completed a training phase with 12 untimed trials, receiving accuracy feedback. Next, they proceeded to a validation phase, in which they completed 8 timed trials without feedback. To advance to the main task, participants needed to answer at least seven trials correctly. This phase was repeated until the accuracy criterion was met. All participants successfully achieved the required performance within two attempts.

The processing speed score was calculated by dividing the percentage of correct responses by the 5% trimmed mean reaction time of correct responses (in seconds), computed only on the responses from the main task.

An additional version of the task was developed for the post-hoc control experiment. In this version, the stimuli were presented at unpredictable intervals. Specifically, the only modification to the previous design was the duration of the ITI. Instead of having a fixed duration of 0.25-s, which allowed participants to predict when the next pair of images would appear, the blank screen now lasted a random duration between 0.25 and 2.5 s. This was achieved by dividing the total interval range (0.25–2.5 s) by the number of stimuli, resulting in increments of approximately 54 ms. The ITIs were randomized in their order of presentation. This ensured that the intervals were evenly distributed across the specified range and prevented participants from anticipating the onset of the next stimulus.

Speech in noise

In order to measure the ability to focus and understand a speaker in a noisy environment, we adopted the Quick Speech in Noise (QuickSIN) test66. In this task, participants transcribed auditory sentences presented with background noise. On each trial, they listened to a sentence embedded in noise on a single occasion and typed it as accurately as possible. Their typed response was displayed on the screen, allowing for corrections. There was no time limit for completing each trial.

A total of 36 sentences were presented, all of which were grammatically correct and constructed with low transition probabilities between words to minimize the likelihood of guessing based on context66. The sentences were recorded by a female voice-over artist of Mexican Spanish, and background “social noise” was added to the recordings at a signal-to-noise ratio of -9 dB. This background noise was generated by combining the speech of five native Mexican Spanish speakers reading passages from different texts.

Following the established procedure66, each sentence contained five target words, and performance was assessed based on the total number of these words correctly transcribed divided by five.

Spoonerism

To assess phonological awareness, participants completed an adaptation of the spoonerism task described in ref. 67. On each trial of this task, participants were auditorily presented with name–surname pairs, and were instructed to repeat the pair while exchanging the initial phonemes (e.g., “Denisse Lara” became “Lenisse Dara”) within seven seconds upon hearing the pair. To ensure understanding of the task, four practice trials were provided, during which participants received feedback with the correct response presented both visually and auditorily. Following this training, participants completed 26 test trials.

The name pairs were selected from common Mexican names and surnames and recorded by a female Mexican Spanish voice-over artist. Pairs were carefully designed to avoid phonological violations after the phoneme exchange and reduce ambiguities, such as excluding names beginning with “H”, which is silent in Spanish.

Three independent researchers reviewed each participant’s recordings to determine whether the phoneme exchanges were performed accurately. Each name and last name within a pair was evaluated separately and considered an individual item. Items on which the researchers disagreed (less than 3 items per participant) were resolved through discussion until a consensus was reached. The final score was calculated as the percentage of correctly completed items.

Verbal phonological fluency

This task assesses an individual’s ability to generate words starting with a specific phoneme68. Participants completed three blocks of this task, each with one target letter: “A”, “F”, and “S”. In each block, participants were instructed to say as many words as possible that began with the target letter for as long as a green dot was displayed on the screen. Each block lasted 1 min. Participants were instructed to avoid repetitions, derivatives (e.g., happy, happiness, unhappy), and proper nouns.

The first author of the study reviewed each participant’s block recordings and counted the number of correct words (i.e., words starting with the target letter, excluding repetitions, derivatives, and proper nouns). The Phonological Fluency score was calculated as the total of correct words produced across the three blocks.

Verbal semantic fluency

This task assesses an individual’s ability to generate words from a specific semantic domain69. The design and data analysis were identical to the Verbal Phonological Fluency task. However, participants completed two blocks in this task. In one block, they were instructed to name as many animals as possible, and in the other, they were asked to enumerate fruits and vegetables. The same guidelines regarding repetitions, derivatives, and proper nouns applied. Scoring was done following the same procedure as in the Verbal Phonological Fluency task.

Visuospatial working memory

In order to assess visuospatial working memory, excluding the verbal component, the task described in ref. 70 was adapted.

On each trial of this task, participants were instructed to focus on a sequence of squares appearing on the screen and to remember their positions. Each trial began with the simultaneous appearance of two blue squares within a blank 3 × 4 grid for 1 s. These squares then came in completely different positions and remained for another 1 s. Finally, they appeared into a third new position, where they remained for a final 1 s (a pair of squares with three different positions, resulting in six positions across the trial). After the final presentation, participants completed a two-alternative forced-choice question. Two 3 × 4 grids, each with six of the twelve positions filled, were displayed on the screen. Participants were required to select the grid containing the combined positions of the previously presented squares. They had 3750 ms to respond, followed by 250 ms of accuracy feedback. A 500 ms ITI with a fixation cross was presented between trials. The main task consisted of three distinct blocks, each containing 8 trials, with a 3-s interval between blocks.

Participants underwent training prior to the task. During training, participants completed four example trials of gradually increasing difficulty with feedback after each response. The first two example trials showed a single-colored square in three different positions, while the next two showed two colored squares in three different positions, as previously described. After training, participants completed a validation phase to confirm their understanding of the task. They were presented with three trials, identical to those in the main task, and needed to answer at least two correctly to proceed. If they failed, the phase was repeated up to four times. All participants met the criterion within two attempts.

Scoring was based on the percentage of correct responses across the three blocks of the main task divided by the 5% trimmed mean reaction time in seconds for correct responses.

Vocabulary size

To assess vocabulary size in a Mexican Spanish-speaking population, we adapted the Spanish version of LexTALE71,72. In this task, participants are presented with visual strings of letters, some of which are real Spanish words (e.g., trasnochar, meaning to stay up all night) while others are pronounceable but meaningless pseudowords (e.g., galoche) and are asked to determine if each item is or not a real word from which they know the meaning.

To account for differences in word and nonword frequencies between Spanish from Spain and Mexican Spanish, new stimuli were developed. Using Sketch Engine73, we calculated the frequency of the original LexTALE stimuli in Spanish from Spain corpora. We then consulted the LEXMEX database of Mexican Spanish word frequencies74 to select words and non-words with frequencies exactly matching those of the original LexTALE stimuli.

On each trial, a word or nonword was displayed on the screen. Below the stimulus, two answer buttons were displayed, one green labeled ‘word’ and one red labeled ‘nonword.’ The next trial was presented immediately following the participant’s response. Participants completed 90 untimed two alternative forced-choice trials, of which 65 contained real words and 25 contained nonwords.

Vocabulary size score was calculated by averaging the percentage of correctly identified words and the percentage of correctly rejected nonwords.

Wisconsin Card Sorting Test

To assess abstract reasoning and the ability to shift cognitive strategies in response to changing environmental demands we adapted the Wisconsin Card Sorting Test (WCST75,76;). In this task, participants had to discover a hidden rule for sorting cards and adjust their strategy whenever the rule changed.

On each trial, four “response” cards were displayed horizontally at the top of the screen. Each card varied in three attributes: shape (triangle, star, diamond, or circle), number (one to four items), and color (red, green, yellow, or blue). A single “target” card was presented below the response cards, and participants had to match it to one of the four response cards based on an initially unknown rule (i.e., matching by shape, color, or number).

Over 64 untimed trials, participants selected their match by clicking on one of the response cards. After each response, feedback indicating “right” or “wrong” was displayed for 700 ms. The matching rule changed after every 10 consecutive correct responses, requiring participants to adapt their strategy. While participants were informed that the matching rule could vary, they were not told what the initial rule was or when it would change.

The final score was calculated as the percentage of correct trials divided by the corresponding 5% trimmed mean reaction time in seconds.

Gold-MSI

The Goldsmiths Musical Sophistication Index (Gold-MSI77) is a validated self-report that measures individual differences in musical experience. Research has demonstrated links between musical sophistication, as measured by the Gold-MSI, and various cognitive domains, including auditory working memory78, cognitive flexibility79, domain-general working memory (auditory, visual-spatial, and verbal)80. To assess the musical training of our participants, we used the Music Training subscale of this Index. This subscale consists of seven Likert-type items designed to assess the duration and intensity of musical training. Participants responded to questions such as: “How many hours did you practice daily at the peak of your interest?” with options ranging from 0 to 5 or more hours (0, 0.5, 1, 1.5, 2, 3–4, or 5+). Another example is: “Indicate your years of formal training in music theory” with options from 0 to 7 or more years (0, 0.5, 1, 2, 3, 4–6, or 7+). Responses were transformed into a 1-7 scoring system, with two items reverse-scored as they reflected negative views about musical training (e.g., “I would not consider myself a musician”). The final score was calculated by summing all item scores, yielding a total score ranging from 7 to 49.

Data analysis

Building on our previous opinion work51, we defined a preplanned analysis pipeline with a fixed order of steps. These preplanned analyses included: (i) group comparisons across tasks; (ii) an exploratory factor analysis (EFA), conducted conditional on observing significant group differences, aimed at identifying latent task dimensions distinguishing the groups; (iii) group comparisons across the resulting factor scores; (iv) regression analyses examining the relationship between PLV and factor scores separately within each group; and (v) planned correlation analyses between factor scores and Gold-MSI measures. In cases where significant associations between factor scores and Gold-MSI measures were observed, we further conducted planned mediation analyses to test whether these relationships were mediated by speech synchronization group membership, as motivated by hypotheses articulated in our prior opinion work.

Comparison between high and low synchronizers across cognitive tasks

We conducted non-parametric independent samples Mann–Whitney–Wilcoxon tests to compare scores between high and low synchronizers across the 16 cognitive tasks. Observations were independent, variables were ordinal or continuous, and comparable distributional shapes were verified through visual inspection. Scores exceeding 3 standard deviations (SD) were excluded for each task. Effect sizes were calculated using rank-biserial correlations, which can be interpreted as a Spearman correlation coefficient or as the difference between the proportions of favorable and unfavorable evidence. Confidence intervals for rank-biserial correlations were estimated using bootstrap resampling with 2000 iterations. Multiple comparisons were controlled for using FDR correction. Detailed statistical results are provided in Supplementary Table 1.

Exploratory factor analysis of six scores differentiating groups

To explore the fundamental cognitive skills shared by the six tasks in which highs outperform lows, we conducted an exploratory factor analysis (EFA) on their corresponding scores. Data preprocessing included z-scoring all scores, identifying outliers beyond 3 standard deviations, and replacing them with the respective score means. The suitability of the data for EFA was verified using Bartlett’s test of sphericity (p < 0.001) and the Kaiser-Meyer-Olkin (KMO) measure of sampling adequacy (KMO = 0.71)81. A scree plot analysis82, together with the Kaiser criterion83 (i.e., the number of eigenvalues greater than 1) (i.e., the number of eigenvalues greater than 1), suggested retaining two factors (see Fig. 3). The EFA was performed using Python’s FactorAnalyzer package84 with principal axis factoring and Varimax rotation.

Scree plot from the conducted EFA.

Eigenvalues as a function of the corresponding factor numbers. The dashed line indicates the threshold of 1, commonly used as a criterion for selecting the number of factors to retain.

The statistical analysis for comparing high and low synchronizers across the factor scores was identical to that applied to the independent scores from the 16 tasks (as described in the previous section). While we recognize that significance was expected—given that the factors were derived from the tasks that distinguished between groups (a form of “double dipping”)—our aim was to assess whether these differences were present across all factors or restricted to only one. To explore the correlation between the degree of synchrony (i.e., phase locking value) obtained from the SSS-test and the factor scores within groups, we performed non-parametric Spearman’s rank correlations after removing data points exceeding 3 SD. Monotonic relationships between variables were verified by visual inspection. Confidence intervals were estimated using bootstrap resampling with 2000 iterations.

Relationship between Gold-MSI scores, cognitive factors and synchronization group membership

Differences in the musical training background between low and high synchronizers were assessed by comparing scores on the training subscale of the Gold-MSI. Mann–Whitney–Wilcoxon tests were used to test for significant differences, and rank-biserial correlations were calculated to determine effect sizes. Non-parametric Spearman’s rank correlations were performed to explore the relationship between Gold-MSI scores and the cognitive factors (i.e., Attention and Phonological Processing).

Since music training scores differed significantly between high and low synchronizers and were positively correlated with both cognitive factors, we conducted two mediation analyses to explore whether the relationship between music training and cognition is, at least in part, mediated by synchronization group membership.

Two mediation analyses were conducted, with the Gold-MSI score as the independent variable and synchronization group membership (a dichotomous variable: high or low synchronizer) as the mediator. In one analysis, the dependent variable was the participants’ score on Factor 1 (Attention), and in the other, the dependent variable was the score on Factor 2 (Phonological Processing). The mediation analyses were conducted using the pingouin library in Python85. A bootstrapping procedure with 5000 iterations was applied to assess the significance of the indirect effect.

Post-hoc experiment analysis (exploratory)

Two-sided Mann–Whitney–Wilcoxon tests were used to assess significant differences between high and low synchronizers across task designs, and rank-biserial correlations were calculated to determine effect sizes. To explore whether low synchronizers show no effect in performance due to predictable designs, we used Bayes factors (BF₀₁). The BF₀₁ reflects the likelihood that the data arise from the null model (i.e., the probability of the data given H₀ relative to H₁). Values between 1 and 3 are interpreted as anecdotal evidence, while values between 3 and 10 are considered moderate evidence for the null hypothesis. These factors were computed using JASP software with default priors.

Results

We conducted an initial screening on a large cohort of participants (N = 203) using the SSS-Test. From this cohort we selected the experimental population ensuring a balanced representation of high (N = 47) and low (N = 51) synchronizers (see Fig. 1 and “Methods”). No significant differences were found between high and low synchronizers in age, gender, or educational level (see Supplementary Fig. 1). These participants completed three follow-up sessions on separate days. In the first two sessions, they performed a comprehensive battery of cognitive tasks, and in the final session, they answered the training subscale of the Goldsmiths Musical Sophistication Index (Gold-MSI; Fig. 1). The tasks in the battery were thoughtfully chosen to assess a broad range of cognitive skills, while the Gold-MSI training subscale provided a reliable measure of participants’ musical training experience. A detailed description of the evaluations is provided in the Methods section.

From the obtained data, we computed an overall performance score for each task for each individual. As an initial exploration of these cognitive scores, we compared the performance of high and low synchronizers. Results revealed that high synchronizers outperformed low synchronizers in 6 out of the 16 evaluated tasks after correcting for multiple comparisons (Fig. 4A; Supplementary Table 1). These tasks included: Eriksen Flanker (Nhigh = 47, Nlow = 51; rank-biserial correlation provided as a measure of effect size r = 0.32, 95% CI [0.10, 0.53]; Mann–Whitney–Wilcoxon test U = 643, two-sided pfdr = 0.021); Local/Global (Nhigh = 46, Nlow = 50; r = 0.48, 95% CI [0.28, 0.68]; U = 696, pfdr = 0.001); Processing Speed (Nhigh = 47, Nlow = 50; r = 0.41, 95% CI [0.19, 0.62]; U = 465, pfdr = 0.003); Spoonerism (Nhigh = 46, Nlow = 49; r = 0.46, 95% CI [0.25, 0.65]; U = 616, pfdr = 0.001); Phonological Verbal Fluency (Nhigh = 46, Nlow = 51; r = 0.39, 95% CI [0.17, 0.58]; U = 432, pfdr = 0.004) and Phonological Statistical Learning (Nhigh = 47, Nlow = 51; r = 0.31, 95% CI [0.10, 0.52]; U = 656, pfdr = 0.021).

After identifying the six tasks in which high synchronizers outperformed low synchronizers, we addressed the question posed in the Introduction: could a small set of core cognitive mechanisms account for the advantages conferred by rhythmic abilities across multiple tasks? Specifically, we examined whether the differences observed across these six tasks could be explained by variations in a smaller set of underlying cognitive mechanisms. To this end, we conducted an Exploratory Factor Analysis on these task scores, applying a varimax rotation. To this end, we conducted an Exploratory Factor Analysis on these task scores, applying a varimax rotation to the factors. The suitability of the data for EFA was verified using Bartlett’s test of sphericity (p < 0.001) and the Kaiser-Meyer-Olkin (KMO) measure of sampling adequacy (KMO = 0.71)81. A scree plot analysis82, together with the Kaiser criterion83 (i.e., the number of eigenvalues greater than 1), suggested retaining two factors (see “Methods” Fig. 3). Upon examining the composition of these factors, we found that the first factor had high loadings on the Eriksen Flanker, Processing Speed, and Local/Global tasks, while the second factor was primarily associated with Phonological Statistical Learning, Phonological Verbal Fluency, and Spoonerism tasks (Fig. 4B). This allowed us to interpret the two factors as representing two distinct underlying core cognitive skills that support performance on these six tasks: one related to attention (Factor 1) and the other to phonological language processing (Factor 2). When exploring how participants scored on these two factors, we found that both factors differentiate between high and low synchronizers, with high synchronizers showing higher scores (Fig. 4 C-D; Factor 1: Nhigh = 47, Nlow = 50; rATT = 0.35, 95% CI [0.13, 0.56]; U = 698, pfdr ATT = 0.003; Factor 2: Nhigh = 47, Nlow = 51; rPHON = 0.52, 95% CI [0.32, 0.70]; U = 690, pfdr PHON < 0.001).

Next, we explored whether, beyond group membership, finer variations in synchronization skills correlate with cognitive performance. Given the bimodal distribution of the synchronization measurements—indicating two qualitatively distinct behavioral profiles (i.e., high and low synchronizers)—and previous findings showing that certain behavioral effects are restricted to high synchronizers, we conducted separate analyses for each group. Specifically, within each group, we examined whether attentional and phonological language processing scores (i.e., factor scores) correlated positively with the degree of synchronization measured by the SSS-Test. The results show that none of these correlations were significant (Fig. 4E, F; Highs: N = 47; Spearman correlation rATT = 0.00, 95% CI = [−0.28 0.29], two-sided pATT = 0.981; rPHON = −0.18, 95% CI = [−0.49 0.16], pPHON = 0.226; Lows: N = 51; rATT = −0.01, 95% CI = [−0.29 0.27], pATT = 0.924; rPHON = 0.06, 95% CI = [−0.26 0.37], pPHON = 0.689). These results highlight the thresholded nature of the relationship between cognition and rhythmic skills: once a certain level of rhythmic ability is achieved, further improvements in synchronization do not predict additional enhancements in cognitive performance.

Relationship with musical training

Building on this, we investigated how this pattern of results could be integrated with participants’ music training history. First, we replicated the observation that high synchronizers have more musical training than low synchronizers17,86, as shown by the significant differences in the training subscale scores of the Gold-MSI between the two groups (Fig. 5A; Nhigh = 43, Nlow = 51; r = 0.26, 95% CI [0.01, 0.48]; U = 768, p = 0.033). Second, we assessed whether scores from the training subscale of the Gold-MSI correlated with attentional and phonological language processing scores (i.e., Factors 1 and 2). We found that this was indeed the case, with participants having more musical training performing better on both cognitive factors (Fig. 5B; Factor 1, Attention: N = 93; r = 0.22, 95% CI = [0.02 0.40], p = 0.031; Factor 2, Phonological Processing: N = 94; r = 0.23, 95% CI = [0.03 0.41], p = 0.028). To better understand the relationship between musical training and cognition, we conducted two mediation analyses. These analyses investigated whether the positive correlations between musical training and attention, as well as between musical training and phonological processing, could be explained by participants’ auditory-motor synchronization skills, as measured by the SSS-Test (Fig. 5C, D). The results showed that, after accounting for the mediator (i.e., being a high or a low synchronizer), the direct effect of musical training on cognition was no longer significant in either analysis (Factor 1, Attention: b = 0.14, 95% CI = [−0.06 0.35], p = 0.163; Factor 2, Phonological Processing: b = 0.15, 95% CI = [−0.04 0.34], p = 0.114); while bootstrapping with 5000 iterations revealed a significant indirect effect on both attention (b = 0.41, 95% CI = [0.09 0.97], p = 0.005) and phonological language processing (b = 0.60, 95% CI = [0.24 1.22], p < 0.001). These findings suggest that the relationship between musical training and cognitive skills is mediated by rhythmic abilities, with the distinction between high and low synchronizers accounting for the observed cognitive differences.

Characterizing the attentional factor

Finally, to further understand which “kind” of attention87 does Factor 1 represent, we examined the shared characteristics of the cognitive tasks with high loadings on this Factor: the Eriksen Flanker, Processing Speed, and Local/Global tasks. Although these tasks were not explicitly designed to assess implicit temporal attention, they inherently involved this aspect, as each trial began after a fixed interval following the participant’s motor response. This allowed participants to predict when the “probe” (i.e., arrows in Eriksen Flanker, images in Processing Speed, and composite figures in the Local/Global) would appear. Such predictability could provide an advantage to participants who effectively used this temporal cue to allocate cognitive resources efficiently over time. To investigate whether this temporal aspect conferred the performance advantage to high synchronizers, we conducted a final post-hoc experiment. Participants were invited back to complete a modified version of the Eriksen Flanker and the Processing Speed tasks (the ones with the highest loadings on Factor 1). In these modified versions the onset of the next trial occurred at a random interval following their motor response (i.e., no temporal prediction was possible; Fig. 6A). This control experiment was conducted post hoc, based on initial data patterns, and is presented as exploratory.

Discussion

Over the past decades, a growing body of evidence has emerged linking rhythmic skills to general cognitive abilities4–12,14,15. However, the diverse range of cognitive tasks used in evaluations, combined with the lack of a standardized method for assessing rhythmic skills, has hindered a precise understanding of the extent of this relationship. In the present study, we evaluated a large cohort of participants grouped according to their speech–rhythm synchronization abilities using an extensive cognitive battery. Participants in the high-synchronizer group outperformed low synchronizers on a subset of tasks, including phonological statistical learning, phonological verbal fluency, spoonerisms, local–global processing, the flanker task, and processing speed. An exploratory factor analysis of performance on these tasks revealed two core cognitive mechanisms—phonological language processing and temporal attention—enhanced in individuals with greater rhythmic ability. Importantly, this pattern offers a unifying explanation for previously reported isolated effects, as these mechanisms support a broad range of human cognitive functions.

The identification of these two cognitive mechanisms (i.e., phonological language processing and temporal attention) relies on the interpretation of the loadings of the two latent factors underlying the performance patterns that distinguish between groups. One factor, which we associate with an attentional mechanism, loads highly on the Eriksen Flanker, Processing Speed, and Local-Global tasks. Although each task engages additional domains—such as inhibitory control, rapidity of information processing under time constraints, visual perceptual integration—their primary and shared strength lies in measuring distinct aspects of attention64,88,89. Furthermore, we found that when temporal predictability in the stimulus presentation was removed, the performance difference between groups disappeared, linking the benefit to enhanced temporal processing. Specifically, we propose that this difference may reflect an implicit timing mechanism, as defined by Coull and Nobre90, which emerges as a by-product of temporal regularity in perceptual input, where temporal expectations arise incidentally from the regular stimulus structure. The other factor showed high loadings on Spoonerism, Phonological Statistical Learning, and Phonological Verbal Fluency tasks, and we associate it with phonological language processing. As with the previous factor, we acknowledge that completing these tasks recruits a variety of cognitive skills—such as working memory, prior linguistic knowledge, or inhibition—but what unites these tasks is their reliance on phonological processing6,91,92, defined as the capacity to perceive and manipulate the sound units of language independently of meaning. Further supporting this interpretation, high synchronizers outperform low synchronizers in Phonological Verbal Fluency but not in Semantic Verbal Fluency. In summary, we recognize that the tasks defining these factors inevitably engage multiple cognitive processes. While our exploratory approach highlights phonological language processing and implicit timing as the primary mechanisms distinguishing the groups, further studies specifically designed to evaluate these domains are needed to validate our findings.

Additionally, the findings of this study provide a valuable framework to help clarify the longstanding debate regarding the cognitive benefits of musical training on academic performance44,46. First, the mediation model aligns with the hypotheses that the positive correlation between musical training and cognition is mediated by rhythmic skills. Second, we show that cognitive benefits plateau once a minimum level of rhythmic ability is reached. These findings may help explain why some studies fail to detect differences in cognitive abilities between musicians and non-musicians. Our interpretation is as follows: while musical training increases the likelihood of surpassing this rhythmic skill threshold, some individuals naturally achieve it without formal training—as evidenced by high synchronizers with no musical expertise. Consequently, the outcomes of comparisons between musicians and non-musicians depend heavily on the composition of the non-musician group, particularly whether it includes individuals who inherently meet the rhythmic skill threshold. This observation aligns with our previously proposed hypothesis51, which introduced the “thresholded rhythmic skills model” as a parsimonious explanation for reconciling the pro-music and neutral perspectives on the cognitive effects of musical training. Moreover, these findings offer valuable insights for refining existing hypotheses on the relationship between musical training and language-related skills93–95.

From a methodological perspective, relying solely on the SSS-Test to index rhythmic skills limits the scope of our conclusions, as the observed relationships are restricted to auditory–motor speech synchronization abilities; the extent to which these findings generalize to other types of rhythmic skills remains to be explored in future work. Nevertheless, this study highlights the suitability of the SSS-Test—a simple protocol requiring only about five minutes to complete—for distinguishing individuals with qualitatively distinct cognitive profiles. First, we replicated the bimodal distribution of synchronization scores, indicating the existence of two distinct groups within the general population, a feature not captured by traditional sensorimotor tasks such as finger tapping to tones, which typically yield unimodal distributions. Second, we demonstrated that this group distinction was essential for identifying the thresholded relationship between rhythmic skills and cognition. Furthermore, in previous research, we demonstrated that certain cognitive traits, previously thought to be universal, are in fact restricted to high synchronizers. For instance, articulatory suppression impairs phonological statistical learning in high synchronizers but does not affect the performance of low synchronizers5. Similarly, speaking rhythmically modulates speech perception in high synchronizers, whereas no such effect is observed in low synchronizers19. Adding to these findings, our current study suggests potential differences in how temporal cues may be processed by these groups. Specifically, only high synchronizers appeared to benefit from the predictable presentation of stimuli, which could indicate that implicit timing—a cognitive process typically assumed to be universal 90,96,97—might function differently in the low-synchrony population.

Limitations

Three primary limitations warrant consideration. First, because the study focused on the general Mexican population, the sample included only a small number of participants with extensive formal musical training (three with more than 6 years of training and five self-identifying as musicians). Consequently, the present observations—including the mediation results—may not generalize to populations with high levels of musical expertise. Future work is needed to determine whether these findings extend to expert or professional musicians. Second, the correlational design precludes causal inference. Although the proposed mediation model is theoretically grounded, alternative explanatory models remain plausible; the observed indirect effect should therefore be interpreted with caution. Finally, sensitivity analyses indicated that the study was powered to detect medium-to-large effects, meaning that smaller, yet potentially meaningful, effects may have gone undetected.
