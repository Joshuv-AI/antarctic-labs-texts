Staged matrix and micrografting reconstruction for no-option CLTI: A case report
Authors: Dimitrios Miserlis, Lucas Ferrer, Reza M. Mizani, Mark G. Davies, Panagiotis Koutakis
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Venous arterialization has emerged as a limb-salvage option for selected patients with no-option chronic limb-threatening ischemia, particularly those with type 2 diabetes, extensive pedal occlusive disease, and small artery disease (distal tibial and inframalleolar disease) in whom conventional bypass or endovascular revascularization is not an option. We present a case of a man in his late 70s with diabetes and severe distal occlusive disease who underwent endovascular venous arterialization for a threatened limb with gangrene, followed by staged wound reconstruction after open transmetatarsal amputation. Reconstruction was performed in a stepwise manner with serial debridement and wound-bed preparation, application of an Integra dermal regeneration template to augment granulation tissue formation, and subsequent Meek fractional skin grafting for definitive coverage. The wound healed over approximately 7 months, and the patient regained the ability to ambulate. This case highlights the concept that venous arterialization should be viewed not as an isolated revascularization maneuver, but as the perfusion-enabling stage of a broader reconstructive pathway in no-option limb salvage.

Introduction

Chronic limb-threatening ischemia (CLTI) remains the most severe clinical presentation of peripheral arterial disease and is associated with high rates of limb loss, functional decline, and mortality when there is no option for limb salvage. The challenge is even greater in patients with diabetes, renal and metabolic comorbidity, and severe below-the-ankle disease, where the distal arterial tree may be profoundly compromised by small artery disease (distal tibial and inframalleolar disease), and a conventional target for bypass or endovascular revascularization may be absent.

In this setting, venous arterialization has emerged as a salvage strategy for carefully selected “no-option” patients. Recent case reports in the literature1,2 have emphasized that venous arterialization can create a perfusion platform for wound healing when standard revascularization pathways are exhausted. These reports also reinforce a practical point that is highly relevant to limb salvage: successful revascularization alone is often insufficient, and definitive healing frequently depends on staged reconstruction, infection control, offloading, serial debridement, and biologic wound coverage.

Here, we present a case of a patient in his late 70s with diabetes and small artery disease (distal tibial and inframalleolar disease) who underwent endovascular venous arterialization followed by staged wound reconstruction after open transmetatarsal amputation. Particular emphasis is placed on the wound reconstructive sequence, including Integra use for granulation augmentation and subsequent Meek fractional skin grafting, culminating in wound healing at 7 months and recovery of ambulation.

Case presentation

A man in his late 70s with diabetes mellitus on Metformin with HbA1C 7 and advanced distal occlusive disease presented with tissue loss of the foot in the setting of severe chronic limb-threatening ischemia. The overall clinical picture was consistent with advanced small artery disease (distal tibial and inframalleolar disease), with inadequate distal arterial runoff for standard reconstructive bypass or conventional endovascular revascularization. The patient had progressive tissue loss and a limb at high risk for major amputation. Additional comorbidities include coronary artery disease, type 2 diabetes, hyperlipidemia, and baseline medications: Aspirin 81 mg, Lipitor 80 mg, Xarelto 2.5 mg BID, previous smoking history, and ambulatory independence in his activities of daily life. Our patient had a remote history of a peripheral angiogram with angioplasties, no bypass or stenting history in the ipsilateral leg. Had a history of prior saphenous vein bypass in the contralateral leg for peripheral arterial disease.

Preoperative vascular assessment demonstrated no satisfactory distal arterial target for conventional revascularization. The wound infection burden and tissue compromise required urgent source control with extensive guillotine transmetatarsal amputation (not amenable to flap closure because of the extent of tissue loss) and staged reconstruction limb salvage planning. At presentation, the limb demonstrated gangrene; specifically, the wound grade was W3, based on necrosis/gangrene involving the entire forefoot, as shown in Figure 1(a). The foot-infection grade was fI2, based on clinical wet gangrene. The ischemia grade was not formally assigned; there were no dorsalis pedis or posterior tibial arterial pulses on clinical examination, presenting clinical concern for critical limb-threatening ischemia (but no available perfusion measurement for formal WIfI ischemia grading). The ABI was noncompressible, toe pressure was not obtained, and baseline TcPO was unavailable. Therefore, the objective perfusion data required to assign a reliable WIfI ischemia grade were incomplete; accordingly, we report the case as W3, I indeterminate, fI2. After a peripheral angiogram, we identified the absence of a reconstructible distal target, including no distal target for conventional revascularization, no distal tibial arteries patent, and no pedal arch patent (Figure 1(b)). Revascularization strategy and operative technique

Because the patient lacked a suitable distal arterial target (distal tibial or inframalleolar artery target), endovascular venous arterialization was selected as the limb-salvage strategy. The goal is to redirect oxygenated arterial inflow through the venous system to the ischemic foot after appropriate valve disruption and flow direction to venule – capillary level. In an endovascular fashion, access was from the ipsilateral CFA (common femoral artery), with crossover from the PT (posterior tibial) artery to the PT vein. Specifically, after access to the ipsilateral CFA and distal into the lateral plantar vein (with ultrasonic and fluoroscopic guidance), traversed the mid posterior tibial artery and vein retrograde, a connection was established using the VAST technique (crossing over with a needle perforating both vein and artery mid-calf level and using snare loops creating a through-and-through wire). 3 The crossover and the venous valves were disrupted using balloon valvulotomy, and collaterals were covered with 5 mm x 150 mm Viabahn stents to direct arterial flow to the plantar venous plexus (Figure 1(c)). For this technique, we did not use a commercial system, only off-label products and custom techniques. Completion angiography confirmed forward flow into the venous arch to reperfuse the foot. Aspirin 81 mg and Xarelto 2.5 mg were used postoperatively (the patient was on the regimen for severe PAD prior to his presentation). We have also adopted this regimen for our venous arterialization patients lifelong, unless there is a contraindication, in lieu of contralateral leg PAD. TcPO2 increased postoperatively and, after 8 weeks, remained at approximately 44 mmHg. For surveillance after venous arterialization, patency was monitored with serial duplex studies and clinical visits with hand-held Doppler. No re-interventions were required to maintain patency at 7 months.

Staged wound reconstruction after guillotine transmetatarsal amputation

Limb salvage in this case depended on a staged reconstructive approach rather than revascularization alone. The patient underwent open transmetatarsal amputation for source control and removal of nonviable tissue. The wound was then managed with serial debridement and interval wound-bed optimization. This phase is critical in advanced ischemic limb salvage, because residual infection, necrotic burden, and mechanical stress can negate the biologic benefits of restored perfusion if not properly addressed.

The amputation in our case, after the forefoot guillotine, involved recurrent muscles and tendons excision, sharp debridement, and bone resection at the metatarsal level, down to healthy bleeding tissues. We guided our debridements based on the clinical picture of necrosis and granulation of the tissue.

After serial surgical sharp excisional deep (bone muscle level) and superficial (subcutaneous skin level) debridements with negative-pressure wound therapy, once the wound had been converted into a cleaner and more reconstructible bed, Integra bilayer dermal regeneration matrix template (Figure 1(e)) (Integra LifeSciences Corp., Plainsboro, NJ, USA) was applied to augment granulation tissue formation (especially over the metatarsals) and improve the quality of the soft-tissue substrate for definitive grafting (required Integra application twice with silicon layer removal approximately at 30 days each time).

Supportive measures with antibiotics used during reconstruction (vancomycin and Zosyn empirically; the original cultures showed polymicrobial infection; discontinued after 10 days; clinical infection resolved) on an as-needed basis if signs of cellulitis were present. We did not use margin bone cultures or quantitative tissue cultures to guide our treatment, and the reconstructive plan was based on clinical progress and granulation (Figures 1(d) and 1(f)).

After sufficient granulation and wound-bed maturation (average wound 12 cm x 8 cm), definitive coverage was performed using Meek fractional skin micrografting (2:1 ratio) (Figures 2(a) and 2(b)), at approximately 4 months (Figures 2(c) and 2(d)). The growth of the skin graft was 80% after a single application and did not require repeat grafting. This allowed epithelial coverage of the residual defect while limiting donor-site burden. Furthermore, the thinner and separated honeycomb sections may be advantageous in low perfusion and heterogeneous anatomical locations such as the foot, although there are no randomized control trial data yet. In the setting of ischemic limb salvage, if areas of the graft fail, it is easier to salvage neighboring sections as they are not connected and, after debridement, can be regrafted or allow adjacent areas to proliferate and achieve wound bed coverage. 4 This staged transition from perfusion restoration to source control, dermal-matrix support, and then fractional autologous coverage represents a practical reconstructive pathway for patients in whom immediate standard grafting would be less favorable.Figure 2.(a) Meek template for fractional skin grafting. Outcome and follow-up

The wound progressed to complete healing (full epithelialization) over approximately 7 months. Patients with venous arterialization require more prolonged and staged reconstruction versus typical conventional peripheral arterial disease patients. By follow-up, the patient had preserved the limb and had regained the ability to ambulate with cane support. Postoperatively, the patient underwent range-of-motion physical therapy and remained non-weight bearing until complete epithelialization of the wound was achieved. Once epithelialized, he was transitioned to protected ambulation using a custom shoe insert to accommodate the transmetatarsal amputation. This functional outcome is clinically meaningful, as limb preservation without restoration of mobility does not fully capture the success of a salvage pathway in a patient population at high risk for disability.

Postoperative care requires close coordination of offloading, rehabilitation, and home wound management, including negative-pressure wound therapy when used. In patients who have undergone venous arterialization, dressing configuration and pressure settings should be individualized to minimize external compression of the arterialized venous circuit. In this case, our team used a low-pressure NPWT strategy, within device-permitted settings, with the goal of balancing wound management with preservation of circuit perfusion. Further study is needed to define optimal NPWT parameters and develop broader postoperative wound-care guidelines for patients undergoing venous arterialization.5,6

Discussion

This case illustrates a central principle in no-option CLTI: venous arterialization should be understood as a perfusion-enabling step within a broader multidisciplinary reconstruction strategy. The recent case reports on hybrid deep venous arterialization 2 and hybrid superficial venous arterialization 1 support the idea that venous arterialization can offer limb-salvage potential in patients who otherwise face major amputation. Our present case extends that reconstructive perspective by emphasizing staged tissue management after transmetatarsal amputation, Integra-assisted granulation augmentation, and subsequent Meek fractional skin micrografting.

This reconstructive sequence is particularly important in diabetic patients with small artery disease (distal tibial and inframalleolar disease). Even when inflow has been re-established through an unconventional route, the wound bed often remains biologically fragile and structurally unfavorable for immediate closure. Serial debridement, pressure offloading, infection control, and temporary wound-bed support together create the conditions necessary for advanced tissue reconstruction. Patients with venous arterialization increase their TcPO2 levels close to 5- 6 weeks to healing capacity (TcPO2 >40 mmHg) and after that can undergo amputations and reconstructive work. 7 In our venous arterialization patients, we strive to delay any further resection/amputation until we see this TcPO2 rise. In cases of infection source control, as in our presented case above, the patient required trans-metatarsal amputation for gangrene control, and approximately 1 week after that, venous arterialization and staged reconstruction. The amputation level is guided by the anatomical location of wounds and infection. After venous arterialization, our strategy is to do the minimum necessary resection, debridement, and tissue buildup using dermal matrix, guided by tissue survival and granulation development over the trabecular bones. In our presented case, after the trans-metatarsal amputation, the patient required several debridement events, dermal matrix, and wound vac applications until granulation over the patient’s muscles and trabecular bones, approximately after 4 months (his TcPO2 had risen to over 40 mmHg at approximately 8 weeks). At 4 1/2 months, we performed Meek split-thickness skin grafting. Subsequently, at approximately 7 months, his wound was fully epithelialized. The venous arterialization circuit remained patent at that time, without any interim interventions. Unfortunately, we do not have TcPO2 measurements at 7 months. In this context, Integra functioned as a dermal matrix scaffold that supports granulation and neodermis formation, while Meek grafting provides a fractional autologous coverage strategy that is attractive when donor-site economy and staged closure are priorities. Outcomes in such cases should not be reported solely as technical patency or limb retention; functional recovery, ambulatory status, wound durability, and the time course of staged healing are equally important endpoints. The patient was able to ambulate with cane support after healing in our case.

The evidence supporting split-thickness skin grafting (STSG) in the diabetic foot is substantial. A retrospective series of 83 diabetic foot and ankle wounds reported that 65% healed after STSG, 28% required regrafting, and all patients ultimately achieved wound closure at final follow-up. 8 A separate retrospective series of 107 diabetic patients reported a mean healing time of 5.1 weeks following STSG for nonhealing foot and leg ulcers, with an overall complication rate of 2.8%. 9 In a TMA (transmetatarsal amputation)-specific cohort, patients undergoing STSG after open TMA achieved 86% healing with a median time to healing of 75 days, compared with 165 days in patients managed with continued negative pressure wound therapy alone. 10 For fractional grafting techniques specifically, a prospective noninferiority study of 50 chronic ulcers (26 minced skin grafts versus 24 conventional 1:3 mesh grafts) found all minced-skin wounds healed by 60 days, with a mean expansion ratio of 9.1-fold versus 3-fold, substantially reducing donor skin requirements. 11 The residual fractional skin clusters remaining after micrografting can be reapplied to the donor site to accelerate its healing, though this observation warrants further evaluation.

Dermal regeneration templates carry comparable supporting evidence. A recent review concluded that dermal regeneration templates are an established option for burns and complex wounds, and that their use in diabetic foot ulcers is associated with high rates of complete healing (full epithelialization) and low rates of adverse events. 12 In a prospective series of 42 patients with complex wounds involving exposed tendon, all wounds healed, with a mean STSG take of 92.5% and preserved postoperative range of motion when Integra was used as a staged scaffold prior to grafting. 13 Matrix scaffolds are a particularly reasonable option when flap reconstruction is not feasible, as is typically the case in no-option patients with small artery disease (distal tibial and inframalleolar disease) whose perfusion cannot reliably support a flap. 14 Matrix scaffolds offer the additional advantage of covering and protecting exposed deep structures (trabecular bone, tendon, and muscle) to generate granulation tissue adequate for subsequent meshed STSG or fractional micrografting.15–18

The evolving evidence base supports venous arterialization as an emerging limb-salvage strategy for carefully selected no-option CLTI patients but also emphasizes that “no-option” status should be defined systematically rather than used as an informal descriptor. The Global Vascular Guidelines recommend threatened-limb staging using WIfI and revascularization planning based on Patient risk, Limb severity, and ANatomic complexity (PLAN), thereby placing anatomic feasibility, limb threat, and systemic risk within a structured decision framework. 19 More recently, the International Cooperative Vascular Consortium (ICVC) proposed a consensus-based multidomain definition of “no-option” and “poor-option” CLTI using the ABRFC framework: arterial anatomy, biology, risk, function, and context. 20 In that modified Delphi study of 164 vascular specialists from 30 countries, the ABRFC framework reached 83% consensus; “desert foot” was defined by the absence of distal arterial revascularization targets on advanced imaging and digital subtraction angiography, with at least one failed endovascular revascularization attempt, while inadequate autogenous bypass conduit was defined as absence of usable autologous vein across all four limbs. 20 This framework is directly relevant to the present case, in which the patient had severe distal tibial and inframalleolar disease, no patent distal tibial or pedal target, no pedal arch, and tissue loss requiring urgent source control, thereby supporting the rationale for venous arterialization as the perfusion-enabling component of a staged limb-salvage plan.

Prospective and comparative data further support the clinical plausibility of this approach while underscoring the need for surveillance and staged reconstruction. PROMISE I demonstrated early feasibility of percutaneous deep vein arterialization with the LimFlow system in no-option CLTI: 31 of 32 patients were treated successfully, amputation-free survival was 91% at 30 days, 74% at 6 months, and 70% at 12 months, and wounds were healed or healing in 75% of patients at 12 months, although 52% required reintervention. 21 PROMISE II subsequently evaluated transcatheter arterialization of the deep veins in 105 no-option CLTI patients and reported high procedural success and clinically meaningful limb salvage. 22 The one-year PROMISE II analysis reported 54.2% amputation-free survival, 68.7% limb salvage, and 79.0% survival; pooled PROMISE I, II, and UK data showed 66.1% one-year amputation-free survival and 74.4% limb salvage.22,23 In comparative evidence, propensity-adjusted PROMISE II versus CLariTI analysis showed higher 6-month amputation-free survival after transcatheter arterialization than standard care/no-option registry controls, with an absolute adjusted risk difference of 29% and a 45% relative event-rate reduction. 22 Nevertheless, systematic evidence remains limited; a 2024 systematic review and meta-analysis reported pooled technical success of 96.2%, limb salvage of 77.2%, one-year amputation-free survival of 65.6%, complete wound healing of 53.4%, and reintervention of 46.7%, concluding that pDVA (percutaneous deep venous arterialization) is feasible but supported by low-certainty evidence from small observational cohorts. 24 These data align with the present case by reinforcing that venous arterialization is not simply a patency endpoint; it creates a biologic and hemodynamic opportunity that must be converted into durable limb salvage through infection control, staged debridement, wound-bed optimization, offloading, postoperative surveillance, and definitive reconstruction.

The present report also extends our group’s prior work across the spectrum of venous arterialization strategies. In a previously reported hybrid deep venous arterialization case, long-term limb salvage was maintained at 5 years in a no-option CLTI patient, with absence of ischemic symptoms and unsupported ambulation at follow-up. 2 In a separate “full metal jacket” peripheral arterial stenting case, hybrid superficial venous arterialization provided a technical alternative for a patient who was not an appropriate EVVA (endovascular venous arterialization) candidate, resulting in wound healing within 5 weeks and preservation of ambulatory status. 1 Together with the ICVC consensus framework and the PROMISE evidence base, these reports support a broader concept: no-option CLTI is heterogeneous, and the optimal salvage pathway may require individualized selection among deep, superficial, hybrid, and custom/off-label venous-arterialization strategies. The present case adds a reconstructive dimension to that literature by showing that, after restoration of perfusion through endovascular venous arterialization, definitive limb salvage depended on a staged pathway of transmetatarsal source control, Integra-assisted granulation, Meek fractional autologous coverage, and delayed protected ambulation.

The limitations of a single case report are acknowledged. Patients with no-option small artery disease represent a heterogeneous population in which anatomy is often uniquely complex, and reconstruction pathways must be highly individualized. Nonetheless, our report offers an instructive example of how advanced revascularization and biologic tissue reconstruction can be integrated into a no-option, microvascular disease limb-salvage setting.

Conclusion

In a patient in his late 70s with diabetes, small artery disease, and no conventional revascularization option, endovascular venous arterialization created a pathway for limb salvage that was ultimately realized through staged wound reconstruction. Open transmetatarsal amputation for source control, Integra for granulation augmentation, and subsequent Meek fractional skin grafting together enabled healing by 7 months with recovery of ambulation. This case supports the concept that durable success in no-option CLTI often depends on integrating unconventional revascularization with deliberate reconstructive staging.
