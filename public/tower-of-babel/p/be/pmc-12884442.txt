Adropin modulates pancreatic cell proliferation and glutathione levels in an animal model of type 1 diabetes mellitus
Authors: Ifrah I. Ali, Crystal D'Souza, Abderrahim Nemmar, Shreesh Ojha, Ernest A. Adeghate
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundDiabetes mellitus (DM), a metabolic disorder that leads to chronic hyperglycemia, is one of the topmost global public health concerns according to the International Diabetes Federation. Adropin is a peptide hormone that is primarily involved in energy homeostasis, but its involvement in other biological activities such as lowering hyperlipidemia, and diminishing insulin resistance has also been reported. In this study, we aimed to explore additional effects of adropin on oxidative stress, inflammation, and cell proliferation in an animal model of type 1 DM.MethodsTo achieve our aim, normal and diabetic Wistar rats were treated with adropin (2.1 μg/kg/day) for a period of 10 days. Pancreatic tissue samples were collected for histomorphological analysis and inflammation assay, while blood was collected for oxidative stress assay.ResultsOur results showed that diabetes induction stimulated cell proliferation in both exocrine and endocrine pancreas, and adropin dramatically attenuated this effect in pancreatic exocrine tissue, but not in the islet of Langerhans. In addition, adropin significantly increased glutathione reductase expression in pancreatic tissue, and augmented serum total glutathione in the diabetic rats compared to diabetic untreated rats.ConclusionOur study indicates the potential role of adropin in alleviating oxidative stress in DM.

INTRODUCTION

Diabetes mellitus (DM) is currently one of the major global public health concerns. According to the 2025 report of the International Diabetes Federation, around 11.1% of adults aged 20–79 years have DM. It was estimated that 1 in 8 adults, or 853 million people, will have diabetes by 2050, which is a 46% rise. 1 DM is a metabolic disorder that develops due to a defect in pancreatic insulin secretion and/or impairment of insulin action in insulin‐sensitive tissues, leading to chronic hyperglycemia. 2 The main diabetes types are type 1 diabetes mellitus and type 2 diabetes mellitus. 2 Type 1 and type 2 diabetes affect approximately 10% and 90%, respectively, of all individuals with diabetes. 1 Adropin, which is a peptide hormone that takes part in energy homeostasis, has been shown to play a pivotal role in regulating glucose and lipid metabolism. This peptide is primarily secreted by the liver and brain; however, it is expressed in other tissues such as the pancreas. 3 , 4 , 5

Interestingly, it has been shown that adropin contributes to several biological activities, including lowering hyperlipidemia, diminishing insulin resistance and reducing inflammation. 6 , 7 The expression of adropin and its receptor, GPR19, in pancreatic islet cells and their function in lowering glucagon release was demonstrated in our earlier study. 8 Further, several studies have shown that the blood concentration of adropin correlates directly with various disorders like cardiovascular diseases, 9 obesity 10 and diabetes complications. 11 , 12

During inflammatory responses, adropin has been shown to help protect tissues from disease pathogenesis by modulating cytokine levels. For instance, Sato et al. 13 demonstrated that adropin mitigated the chronic inflammation in atherosclerosis by suppressing TNFα and reducing expression of endothelial cells' adhesion molecules. Furthermore, it has been shown that adropin deficiency is implicated in the development and progression of several disorders such as inflammatory bowel disease, kidney related disorders and lung injury. 6 , 14 , 15

Adropin has been reported to have therapeutic potential and could play a protective role against oxidative stress. In mice with radiation‐induced myocardial damage, adropin therapy successfully enhanced cardiac function, prevented oxidative stress, decreased cardiomyocyte apoptosis, and encouraged micro‐angiogenesis via the VEGFR2/PI3K/AKT pathway. 16 Furthermore, adropin administration increased endothelial nitric oxide synthase (eNOS) expression in cardiomyocytes while decreasing the expression of transforming growth factor β1 (TGF‐β1), NOX4, and caspase 3. 16 In another study, adropin successfully decreased oxidative stress in the corpus luteum gland of adult mice, showing its potential role in regulating ovarian function. 17 Adropin enhanced nuclear factor erythroid‐derived 2‐like 2, also known as NRF‐2, in corpus luteum, thereby boosting superoxide dismutase (SOD) and catalase activity.

Over and above that, adropin can regulate cell division and proliferation in testicular cells in ways that go well beyond its traditional activity. Adropin can cause endothelial cells to proliferate and form capillary‐like tubes, which promote angiogenesis, as demonstrated by Lovren et al. 18 In cancer research, adropin has been shown to have a protective effect against breast cancer, forcing tumor cells to reach early stages of apoptosis, 19 while a different study demonstrated that GPR19 activation by adropin promoted the metastasis and proliferation of breast cancer cells, by facilitating the mesenchyme‐epithelium switch through the MAPK/ERK1/2 pathway. 20 In pancreatic ductal adenocarcinoma (PDA), adropin overexpression was found in the tissue of patients, and PDA cell lines supplemented with adropin demonstrated an increase in Ki67, p‐VEGFR2, Ki67, MMP2 and cyclin D1 expression, as well as accelerated tumor growth and stimulated angiogenesis in the tumor tissue microenvironment. 21 Overall, adropin has demonstrated a unique ability to modulate cell proliferation; thus, more research is necessary to fully understand how adropin functions in both cancer biology and cell proliferation.

Based on the previously mentioned results, we hypothesized in this study that adropin treatment would attenuate inflammation and oxidative stress in DM. We aimed to study whether adropin can regulate the proliferation of islet cells in the pancreas, specifically α and β cells. Oxidative overload and inflammatory conditions can induce DM and its related complications. Therefore, we investigated whether adropin can reduce oxidative distress in the pancreata of STZ‐induced diabetic rats by evaluating the levels of endogenous protective enzymes, such as superoxide dismutase, catalase, and glutathione reductase. Also, we examined the effect of adropin treatment on inflammation biomarkers such as TNFα, IL‐6 and NF‐κB in pancreas.

MATERIALS AND METHODS

Animals and experiment design

Male Wistar rats, weighing between 200 and 250 g were utilized for this research. The rats were sourced from the Animal Facility at the College of Medicine and Health Sciences, UAEU, and were housed in a Specific Pathogen Free (SPF) environment, maintained at round about 25°C with a 12‐h light/dark cycle. A total of 24 Wistar rats were given standard chow and had unrestricted access to water. The study adhered to the 3Rs principle in animal research, minimizing the number of rats needed by employing statistical techniques to ensure the minimum number required for findings that are valid from a scientific standpoint. To reduce pain and distress, the least painful sacrifice technique was applied, along with suitable anesthesia. Initially, two groups of rats were randomly selected, a normal group and a to‐be‐diabetic group (n = 12 each). To induce DM, a single intraperitoneal injection of 60 mg/kg of streptozotocin (STZ; Sigma Life Science, Burlington, MA, USA) was administered, and only those rats developing fasting blood glucose levels > 250 mg/dL were classified as diabetic. Both the normal and diabetic rat groups were divided into control and adropin treatment groups two weeks after the induction of diabetes, as outlined in Table 1. The treated groups received intraperitoneal injections of adropin peptide (Phoenix Pharmaceuticals, Burlingame, CA, USA) at a dose of 2.1 μg/kg/day for 10 days, a dosage established in prior reports in the literature. 22

Tissue and blood collection

A cocktail of xylazine (5 mg/kg) and ketamine (75 mg/kg) was used to anesthetize animals before sacrifice. After euthanasia, blood samples were expeditiously taken from the isolated IVC (inferior vena cava) and placed in special tubes (BD vacutainer). The BD vacutainer tubes were placed in a centrifuge and spun at 906 rcf for a quarter of an hour in order to separate the serum. For subsequent analysis, the supernatant (serum) was transferred to freshly labeled Eppendorf tubes and kept in a deep freezer (−80°C). Pancreatic tissue samples were collected from animals in each group and preserved in Zamboni fixative for immunofluorescence staining.

Histological analysis and immunofluorescence staining of paraffin sections

For light microscopy analysis, the harvested pancreas was preserved for 48 h in Zamboni fixative, rinsed with phosphate buffered saline (PBS), and subsequently kept in 70% ethyl alcohol. Water was removed from the tissue samples by rinsing them in a succession of ethyl alcohol solutions of increasing strength (from 70% to 95% to 100%) before being embedded in liquid paraffin to create paraffin blocks, which were divided into 5 μm sections using a microtome. Each segment was then put on a glass slide for immunological staining.

The slides were heated to 60°C for 10 min to melt the wax. The sections were then treated with xylene I and II for 5 min each. Following this, they were rehydrated using a sequence of descending ethyl alcohol levels (from 100% to 95% to 70% and to 50%), and then placed in distilled water for 5 min. A buffered sodium citrate solution (10 mmol/L of sodium citrate, 0.05% Tween 20, set at pH 6.0) was used for antigen retrieval. The slides were placed in the solution and heated in a microwave for 2 min at high power (P10), then for twenty minutes at low power (P1), before being cooled to room temperature (20°C–23°C). Following three PBS washes lasting 3 min each, the tissue sections were rinsed for an hour at 25°C using a protein block buffer containing 1% bovine serum albumin (BSA) and 0.05% Tween 20. Following protein blocking, the tissue sections were placed overnight in the relevant primary antibodies (Table 2). The next day, the sections were washed three times with PBS for 3 min each and then incubated with secondary antibodies (Jackson ImmunoResearch, UK) for 1 h at room temperature. After another PBS rinse, the sections were incubated with DAPI (4–6 diamidino 2 phenylindole) to stain the nuclei of pancreatic cells and subsequently mounted in the medium. Finally, the sections were examined using a Nikon ECLEPS Ni fluorescent microscope (Nikon, Japan) and analyzed with NIS‐Element D software according to previously published reports. 23 , 24

Oxidative stress assays

The effect of adropin peptide on antioxidative agents in normal and experimental diabetic rats was investigated by quantifying the levels of the endogenously produced antioxidants catalase, superoxide dismutase, and glutathione in the serum using specific colorimetric assay kits.

To measure catalase enzyme levels, the Catalase Assay Kit from Cayman Chemical (USA) was utilized. First catalase standards, samples, and a positive control were added to the assay wells. The addition of hydrogen peroxide started the reaction, after which the mixture was incubated at 25°C for 20 min. To stop the reaction, potassium hydroxide was added, and the color was developed by introducing catalase purpald, a chromogen, to each of the wells for 10 min. Subsequently, the samples were incubated with catalase and potassium periodate for 5 min. Using the Emax Plus® microplate reader, absorbance was recorded at 562 nm.

Superoxide dismutase levels in serum were measured using the Superoxide Dismutase Assay Kit from Cayman Chemical (USA). The procedure commenced with the addition of a radical detector to all wells, followed by loading the standards and test samples. Xanthine oxidase was added to start the reaction, and the samples were then incubated at room temperature for 30 min. Measurement of absorbance at 450 nm was carried out using the Emax Plus® microplate reader.

To evaluate glutathione (GSH) activity in serum, the Glutathione Assay Kit (Sigma, USA) was employed. The samples were first deproteinized with 5% sulfosalicylic acid (SSA). Next, the test samples and standards were added to designated wells, along with a working mixture. After a 5‐min incubation, NADPH was introduced to all wells, and glutathione activity was measured by taking five readings every minute at 410 nm.

Inflammation assays

The concentrations of the proinflammatory cytokines TNFα, IL‐6, and IL‐1β in pancreas tissue homogenate were measured using the Rat DuoSet ELISA kit (R&D Systems, USA), which is specific for each cytokine. Initially, the plates were coated overnight with a capture antibody for each cytokine. The following day, a one‐hour blocking step was carried out with 1% BSA in PBS to minimize nonspecific binding. The wells were then filled with standards and samples of pancreatic tissue homogenate, which were then allowed to sit at room temperature for 2 h. Following a wash with PBS containing 0.05% Tween 20, each well received a cytokine‐specific detection antibody and was incubated for 2 h at 25°C. After another wash, the wells were treated with Streptavidin‐HRP for 1 h. Finally, TMP substrate was added and allowed to react until the desired color developed; the reaction was then halted using 2 N HCl, and an Emax Plus® microplate reader was used to measure absorbance at 450 nm.

Quantification of cellular labeling in images

For counting cells in the histological images, ImageJ (2.1.0) software was used. The software's Cell Counter plugin was utilized to determine the number of labeled cells with specific markers for Ki67, catalase, superoxide dismutase, or glutathione reductase in order to quantify the level of pancreatic immunostaining. Initially, all cells in pancreatic islets, acini or ducts were counted; nuclei stained with DAPI and cells immunofluorescently stained with specific markers were subsequently counted. The percentage of cells labeled with a particular immunofluorescence secondary antibody was subsequently determined, as a proportion of the total number of cells.

For the triple labelling of pancreatic islets with Ki67, insulin and glucagon, the percentage of Ki67 labeled cells in either α‐ or β‐ cells was calculated.

Statistical analysis

Statistical analysis was conducted using GraphPad Prism (9.4.1) software to compare the various groups. The data were evaluated using an ANOVA, and Tukey's post‐hoc test was used to assess differences between groups. Results were reported as mean ± SEM, and differences with p values below 0.05 were considered significant.

DISCUSSION

Effect of adropin on cell proliferation in the exocrine and endocrine pancreas

Here, we examined the proliferation of pancreatic cells by detecting specific cell cycle antigen Ki67 in normal and diabetic rats treated with adropin. Our morphological analysis showed that in islets of Langerhans, cell proliferation was higher in diabetic rats compared to nondiabetics. A modest increase in the proportion of Ki67‐positive cells within the islets was observed in adropin‐treated diabetic rats compared to untreated diabetic rats. Interestingly, we observed a large rise in the number of proliferating cells in the acini and ducts of the pancreas after diabetes induction; however, this increase was dramatically reduced with adropin treatment.

Ki67 is a nuclear antigen and proliferation marker expressed in both the interphase and mitosis stage of the cell cycle. It plays a role in heterochromatin maintenance during the interphase, while during mitosis it is responsible for peri‐chromosomal layer formation. Ki67 undergoes degradation during the resting phase (G0) of the cell cycle. 23

DM is characterized by a reduction in β‐cell mass due to increased apoptosis. 24 Impairment in β‐cell proliferation is also associated with the disease pathogenicity. Studies have indicated that decreased β‐cell proliferation due to reduced serum placental growth factor levels could develop into gestational diabetes mellitus. 25 , 26

It has been proposed that the adult duct epithelium, specifically pancreatic duct glands, serve as a potential adult pancreatic stem cell niche. Generally, the pancreas is dynamic tissue, where the exocrine and endocrine cells undergo replication, neogenesis, differentiation and apoptosis. 27

The peptide adropin has shown to play a potential role in affecting the proliferation of different cells. For instance, adropin promoted the proliferation of rat primary preadipocytes and 3T3‐L1 fibroblast cells, 28 as well as endothelial cells. 18 However, when Billert et al. examined the effect of adropin on β‐cell proliferation in INS‐1E cells, no response in cell mitogenicity was reported. 29 In our study, we showed a general increase in islet cell proliferation with adropin treatment in diabetic rats. By closely examining α‐ and β‐cell proliferation in the diabetic treated rats, a very slight decrease in α‐cell proliferation was demonstrated, while β‐cell replication remained the same in comparison with the untreated diabetic rats. It is worth mentioning that we detected Ki67 labeled cells in the endocrine pancreas, other that α‐ and β‐cells, assuming these are somatostatin and/or pancreatic polypeptide secreting cells.

It is well known that the microenvironment has an impact on the functional expression of genes. Adropin plays an anticancer role in many cancer types by acting on different downstream molecules or being affected by distinct molecules and pathways. A specific mechanism cannot be elucidated as there are not yet many relevant research findings. Future studies should be carried out to investigate additional possible uses for adropin.

Effect of adropin on oxidative stress (OS) in diabetes mellitus

Here we demonstrated that the relative distribution of catalase, glutathione reductase, and superoxide dismutase in the pancreas was generally reduced in rats with diabetes in comparison with the nondiabetic controls. Pancreatic β‐cell expression of catalase, superoxide dismutase, and glutathione reductase decreased with diabetes induction; however, pancreatic α‐cell content of all the previously mentioned antioxidants enzymes remained the same. Adropin administration did not change catalase, superoxide dismutase or glutathione reductase expression in the endocrine cells in adropin‐treated rats compared to the untreated diabetic group, while in serum, glutathione contents were significantly increased in adropin‐treated diabetic rats compared to diabetic (untreated) control rats.

OS has been linked to the development and progressive course of diabetes in several studies, with effects including β‐cell dysfunction and impairment of insulin action as well as the elevation of incidence of diabetic complications. 30 , 31 Excessive ROS production, comprising species like superoxide anion (O•−2), H2O2, and OH•, and the imbalance between the production and scavenging of these species leads to an imbalance in redox state, commonly known as oxidative stress, and causes cellular damage. 32 Here lies the important role of free radical scavengers like vitamin C and E, flavonoids and antioxidant enzymes such as catalase, superoxide dismutase, glutathione, and others in scavenging free radicals. Superoxide dismutase, which is considered the primary initial defense agent against reactive oxygen species, catalyzes superoxide into oxygen and H2O2. While catalase neutralizes H2O2 by converting it into oxygen and H2O. 33 Glutathione (GSH) and its catalyzing enzymes, including glutathione peroxidase and glutathione reductase are involved in removing hydrogen and lipid peroxides, protecting biomolecules from oxidation and regulating intracellular redox homeostasis. 34 Patel et al. 35 illustrated that hyperglycemia elevates cellular generation of ROS including H2O2 and downregulates gene expression of catalase and superoxide dismutase in HUVEC endothelial cells.

Interestingly, several researchers have demonstrated the impact of adropin on oxidative stress in an animal model of DM. Yu et al. introduced adropin into the kidney of diabetic mice via ROS‐responsive nanocapsules to study renal oxidative stress in diabetic kidney disease. The results showed significant amelioration of the high ROS amounts in the kidney produced by diabetes induction, elevated superoxide dismutase levels and improved mitochondrial damage in renal tissue of adropin‐treated diabetic mice. 36 The possible impact of adropin on lung injury and OS biomarkers in diabetic rats have also been investigated. 37 An elevation in MDA levels and a dramatic decrease in GSH and superoxide dismutase levels in the lung tissue of diabetic rats. Adropin treatment attenuated the MDA content and elevated GSH and superoxide dismutase levels in the diabetic group, suggesting an important role of adropin in enhancing the anti‐oxidative response to lung injury induced by diabetes.

In contrast, we demonstrated that adropin did not increase the expression of glutathione reductase, superoxide dismutase, or catalase in the diabetic rat pancreas. The reason for this could be due to the short period of adropin administration in this study. However, the total glutathione level in the serum of the diabetic treated group was significantly augmented compared to the untreated diabetic group.

Effect of adropin on inflammation in normal and diabetic rats

We evaluated adropin's effect on inflammation by measuring proinflammatory biomarker levels of TNF‐α, IL‐6, and IL‐1β in the pancreas of diabetic and nondiabetic rats. We observed a considerable augmentation in TNF‐α and a moderate elevation in IL‐6 and IL‐1β as diabetes was induced in rats, in comparison to the normal controls. Adropin treatment did not significantly reduce inflammatory biomarkers in rats.

In DM, inflammation has a detrimental role in the disease pathophysiology as well as in developing diabetic complications. In T1DM, the pancreas, specifically pancreatic β‐cells are inflamed due to autoimmunity. 24 The cytokines TNF‐α and IL‐1β secreted from immune cells such as macrophages are primarily involved in promoting islet inflammation. Interferon gamma (IFN‐γ) is another cytokine that is implicated in inducing β‐cell inflammation in T1DM. 38 In T2DM, chronic inflammation due to hyperglycemia, excessive free fatty acids and reactive oxygen species in the body affect the disease pathogenesis and development. 30 Obesity and its consequences—adipose tissue inflammation and fat accumulation on insulin sensitive tissues such as liver, as well as other tissues including pancreas—not only cause insulin resistance, but also lead to pancreatic β‐cell dysfunction. 39 White adipose tissue is responsible for producing various proinflammatory cytokines and adipokines in T2D patients. 38

Several researchers have conducted anti‐inflammatory trials to study their effectiveness in fighting DM. Mastrandrea et al. 40 demonstrated that Etanercept medication, which is a TNF blocker, has shown promising results in increasing endogenous insulin release and decreasing HbA1C in T1D pediatric patients. Moreover, T2D patients received anakinra, an IL‐1R antagonist, for 13 weeks exhibited enhanced insulin secretion and reduced levels of the inflammatory marker c‐reactive protein compared to placebo receiving patients. 41 Other than the medical drugs, the potential role of peptides such as adropin as anti‐inflammatory agents against DM and other metabolic disorders has been investigated. According to reports, adropin deficiency has been linked to elevated TNF‐α, strong expression of NF‐kappa B (Nuclear Factor‐kappa B) and loss of Treg. 42 Researchers have studied the application of adropin as a therapeutic agent against inflammatory responses in various diseases, including diabetes. Previous studies have found that adropin can effectively downregulate TNF, IL‐6 and IL‐1β genes in NASH mice hepatocytes, 43 decrease macrophage infiltration in adipose tissue by reducing preadipocyte maturation and lipid accumulation, 28 and diminish the high mRNA expression of TNF and IL‐6 genes in the pancreatic tissue of diabetic rats. 22 All these data suggest that adropin could play a pivotal role in regulating inflammation via reducing pro‐inflammatory factors, consequently reducing insulin resistance, obesity, and diabetes progression.

CONCLUSION

The present study investigated the involvement of adropin in the regulation of pancreatic cell proliferation, oxidative stress and inflammatory status in the murine model of diabetes. A significant decrease in cell proliferation in the exocrine pancreas (ducts and acini) was detected. Further, adropin showed antioxidant activities by significantly increasing glutathione reductase expression in pancreatic islets and raising serum levels of total glutathione in diabetic animals. However, the pancreatic concentrations of cytokines capable of promoting inflammation (IL‐6, TNF‐α and IL‐1β) were unaffected by adropin administration in diabetic rats compared with untreated diabetic group. In summary, our research suggests that adropin could potentially reduce oxidative stress in DM by increasing glutathione reductase as well as total glutathione.
