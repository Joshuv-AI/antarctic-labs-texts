Real‐world determinants of time to initiation of anti‐amyloid treatments: comparative data from private and academic practice settings
Authors: Varun Mehta, Divya Singh, Jordyn Kelman, Saanvi Nimma, Helen Lutz, Andrea Schnee, David Weisman, Michael H. Rosenbloom
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractINTRODUCTIONEarlier anti‐amyloid therapy (AAT) initiation results in improved clinical outcomes in Alzheimer's disease (AD). We sought to identify demographic, clinical, and disease‐related factors impacting door‐to‐treatment times (DTTs).METHODSA retrospective review of AAT data was conducted at private and academic neurological practices.RESULTSA total of 329 patients (53.2% from private versus 46.8% academic practices), mean age 73.5 ± 7.2 (55.3% female), with most being non‐Hispanic White were treated with lecanemab (81.2%) or donanemab (18.2%). DTTs were significantly reduced (p < 0.05) in both practices when diagnosis was confirmed with blood‐based biomarkers (BBBs) as well as with greater clinic experience dosing AATs. DTT increased in patients with non‐Medicare insurance and was not impacted by diagnosis, referral source, apolipoprotein E genotype status, or clinic distance.DISCUSSIONPatients with private insurance may experience greater delay in DTT, whereas BBBs may reduce DTT relative to traditional confirmatory biomarkers. DTT improves with clinic experience.

INTRODUCTION

Alzheimer's disease (AD) is the most common cause of dementia, affecting an estimated 7.2 million Americans aged 65 and older. 1 In addition to dementia, approximately eight million Americans have mild cognitive impairment (MCI). 2

Lecanemab (Leqembi) was the first anti‐amyloid therapy (AAT) to receive traditional approval by the US Food and Drug Administration FDA in July 2023. 3 Donanemab (Kisunla) was subsequently approved in July 2024 for early‐stage AD. 4 Lecanemab specifically targets soluble protofibrils and amyloid plaques and is administered every 2 weeks, 3 whereas donanemab is a humanized IgG1 antibody directed at the N‐terminal pyroglutamate Aβ epitope of fibrillar amyloid plaques and is administered on a monthly basis. 4

Both AATs demonstrated efficacy in phase III clinical trials, slowing cognitive and functional decline as measured by the Clinical Dementia Rating–Sum of Boxes (CDR‐SB) by 27% to 29% over 18 months and were associated with amyloid‐related imaging abnormality (ARIA) rates ranging from 21.5% to 36%. 3 , 4

While the pivotal CLARITY‐AD and TRAILBLAZER‐ALZ 2 trials demonstrated clinical efficacy, 3 , 4 increasing evidence suggests that earlier initiation of AATs may improve long‐term outcomes relative to delayed treatment. Long‐term extension (LTE) data from CLARITY‐AD demonstrated that an 18‐month delay in initiation may diminish cumulative clinical benefit. 5 Similarly, donanemab efficacy in TRAILBLAZER‐ALZ 2 showed an inverse relationship with increasing cerebral tau burden as detected by tau positron emission tomography (PET). 4

Thus, current pivotal AAT clinical trials along with LTE data provide evidence supporting prompt diagnosis of early‐stage AD and the creation of standardized pathways for rapid initiation of AATs. 3 , 4 , 5

Previously, we reported that the mean delay between diagnosis and AAT initiation was 97 days in a private practice and 127 days in an academic healthcare system, 6 prompting further investigation into operational and clinical determinants of door‐to‐treatment time (DTT). Similar to thrombolytic guidelines for DTT established by the American Stroke Association for acute stroke management, such goals for AAT initiation in dementia may serve as a quality measure that not only ensures optimal treatment outcomes but also provides data distinguishing clinical centers of excellence.

Internal clinical operational discussions identified potential contributing factors related to practice (private vs academic), clinical experience with AATs, referral type (internal vs external), disease severity (MCI vs dementia due to AD), geographical distance (<50 miles [∼80.5 km] vs ≥ 50 miles from clinic), patient genetics (ApoE ε4 homozygotic versus non‐ε4 homozygotic), confirmatory biomarkers (cerebrospinal fluid [CSF]/amyloid PET versus blood‐based), case complexity (requiring adjudication by an AAT panel versus adjudication by a single clinician), and healthcare insurance (Medicare versus non‐Medicare/private insurance) to delays in DTT. We have leveraged the combined data from a private practice and an academic practice to better understand whether these factors delay or promote AAT initiation in the real world. Furthermore, we hypothesized that utilization of blood‐based biomarkers (BBBs) as confirmatory tests might reduce the DTT compared to using traditional confirmatory imaging or fluid biomarkers.

METHODS

Settings

University of Washington (UW) Medicine Memory and Brain Wellness Center (MBWC) is an academic tertiary referral center located at the Harborview Medical Center (HMC) Hospital in Seattle, Washington, receiving 1000 new patients and 4000 total visits per year. The clinic is staffed by a multidisciplinary team of behavioral neurologists, neuropsychiatrists, geriatric psychiatrists, geriatricians, palliative care specialists, neuropsychologists, and social workers. HMC is an urban community‐facing county hospital that provides care to all patients regardless of their ability to afford care.

Abington Neurological Associates (ANA), based outside Philadelphia in Abington, Pennsylvania, is one of the largest private neurology practices in Montgomery and Bucks County, Pennsylvania, serving 8096 general neurology patients per year. The practice has developed an AD Clinical Trials Center affiliated with the Alzheimer's Treatment Research Institute (ATRI) and recruits AD subjects enrolling subjects into phase 1b to phase 4 clinical trials. Two neurologists oversee the initiation of lecanemab and donanemab therapies at ANA.

Eligibility criteria

Patients diagnosed with early‐stage AD were selected for AAT treatment (lecanemab or donanemab) based on Appropriate Use Criteria. 7 , 8 Patients needed to demonstrate capacity to appreciate the benefits and risk of the intervention prior to initiating therapy. ApoE4 homozygotic carriers were eligible to receive AAT treatment; however, patients receiving anticoagulation therapies were excluded. Prior to receiving treatment, all patients were administered a Montreal Cognitive Assessment (MoCA) at MBWC versus a Mini‐Mental State Examination (MMSE) at ANA. To ensure consistency in data, all MMSE scores were converted to MoCA equivalents using a validated conversion scale. 9

At MBWC, but not at ANA, a monthly AAT approval board consisting of all prescribing clinicians, a neuropsychologist, a neuroradiologist, a stroke specialist, and a nuclear medicine radiologist adjudicated cases deviating from Appropriate Use Criteria. In cases where Appropriate Use Criteria were met, clinicians had a peer‐to‐peer discussion with another prescribing MD and a neuroradiologist before initiating treatment. Patients were monitored for adverse effects and therapeutic responses through regular follow‐up visits, scheduled surveillance MRI, and standardized assessment tools. Baseline and surveillance imaging at MBWC was performed on a 3T MRI scanner using susceptibility‐weighted imaging (SWI) to evaluate ARIA. At ANA, imaging was performed across various clinical settings on 1.5T MRI scanner using gradient echo (GRE) sequences. Both practices utilized a combination of CSF, amyloid PET, and phosphorylated tau at threonine 217 (p‐tau217 [Mayo Clinic, Lumipulse] for confirmation of the AD diagnosis.

Data acquisition

A clinic coordinator was responsible for entering information into an Excel spreadsheet that included but was not limited to demographics, diagnosis date, treatment initiation data, AD biomarkers, APOE genetics, MoCA/MMSE score, Functional Assessment Questionnaire (FAQ) scores, use of anticoagulants, and ARIA incidence. Relevant demographic data collected included age, distance from clinic, gender, race, ethnicity, referral type, and insurance type. Of note, we divided insurance by Medicare and non‐Medicare, referring to private insurance plans, most frequently used by patients younger than 65. Any patient with Medicare Advantage plans were classified as “Medicare,” as opposed to “non‐Medicare,” even when created by private insurers. Geographical distance was calculated as the shortest driving distance between the patient's residential ZIP code and the infusion center, for both MBWC and ANA data. Records were kept for all patients requiring case review by the UW AAT approval board.

Data analysis

A retrospective analysis was performed on the aforementioned data variables in all patients treated with AATs at MBWC and ANA, between December 1, 2023 and January 31, 2026. All cases processed by the MBWC AAT approval board were also reviewed. For patient demographics, statistics with mean and standard deviation were calculated for continuous variables, whereas frequencies were calculated for categorical outcomes.

Due to the potential for patients with a historical AD diagnosis (e.g., occurring years before AAT availability) or prior outside dementia evaluations to distort the primary outcome, DTT was defined as the interval between the date of a patient's confirmatory biomarker result (amyloid PET, CSF, or BBB) and the initiation of AAT treatment. A two‐sample two‐sided Welch's t‐test was used to compare door‐to‐treatment outcomes between individual clinics (ANA vs MBWC) and evaluate the impact of the following factors on DTT: clinic setting, ApoE genotype, treatment year, insurance type, drug type, patient distance from clinic, referral type, and diagnosis (MCI vs dementia due to AD). A one‐sample two‐sided t‐test was used to compare individual variables’ effects on DTT in the overall sample that included both clinics.

An analysis was performed to compare DTT in patients having AD confirmed with BBB versus CSF/amyloid PET. Since the previously described technique for calculating DTT could not be used to effectively compare the impact of amyloid PET/CSF versus blood‐based AD biomarkers (i.e., fails to capture delays in access to biomarker), a separate method was applied. DTT was defined in these cases as the time from the initial AAT evaluation visit to the initial infusion. Patients receiving a diagnosis or undergoing their initial evaluations more than 6 months prior to AAT availability were excluded from the analysis. Lecanemab was initially available at MBWC in December 2023, whereas donanemab use started in April 2025 (e.g., June 2023 and November 2024 cutoff, respectively). At ANA, lecanemab became available in February 2023 and donanemab in September 2024. Patients were also reviewed on a case‐by‐case basis and excluded based on situations where AAT initiation pre‐dated the initial clinic evaluation, biomarkers were performed >6 months prior to AAT availability, and biomarkers were performed outside of MBWC/ANA systems. A two‐sided Welch's t‐test was used to compare outcomes between confirmatory biomarkers.

A multivariable linear regression to examine factors associated with DTT while controlling for demographic factors (age, sex, and ethnicity). Each outcome was modeled separately, measured in days. Candidate predictors included APOE ε4 carrier status, biomarker confirmation year (2023/2024 vs 2025), insurance type, anti‐amyloid agent, distance from clinic (<50 vs ≥50 miles), referral type, diagnosis severity, and biomarker modality (PET/CSF vs blood p‐tau217). All covariates were entered simultaneously in a single adjusted model rather than selected by a stepwise procedure. We report adjusted beta coefficients with 95% confidence intervals (CIs) and two‐sided p values, with statistical significance set at p < 0.05.

All data analysis and figure production were conducted in RStudio Environment (Posit Software, Boston, MA, USA) using the ggplot, gt, and gtsummary packages. A multivariable regression was performed for age, race, and ethnicity.

RESULTS

Demographics

A total of 329 patients (53.2% from private vs 46.8% academic neurological practice), mean age 73.5 ± 7.2 (55.3% female), with the majority being non‐Hispanic White, were included in the analysis (Table 1). The diagnosis of MCI versus dementia due to AD was split evenly within the cohort, with an average MoCA score of 19.1 ± 4.2. The distribution of ApoE4 carrier status was as expected for a community sample, consisting of 59.8% and 13.4% ApoE4 heterozygotic and homozygotic patients, respectively. As a result of the earlier approval of lecanemab, the majority of patients were taking this agent (81.2%), with a smaller number treated with donanemab (18.2%) (two patients had been on both drugs). Most referrals to the two practices originated from external sources (79.5% external vs 20.5% internal at academic center; all private practice referrals were external). Most patients had Medicare‐based insurance (84.8%) compared to private insurance (15.2%). CSF was the most frequently used confirmatory biomarker (44.4%), closely followed by amyloid PET (38.9%) and plasma BBBs (15.2%), with the latter being used mainly in the academic practice. A multivariate regression showed that those patients <65 of age had a longer DTT compared to those ≥65 but otherwise revealed no impact of race or ethnicity on DTT.

Factors impacting door to treatment: private practice versus academic practice

There was a significant difference in time to treatment of 108 ± 82 versus 145 ± 96 days (p < 0.001) between private and academic practices, respectively (Table 2). This difference remained significant regardless of each institution's experience with AATs (2023 to 2025), the disease severity (MCI vs dementia due to AD), and geographic distance from the clinic (<50 vs ≥50 miles). The private practice started lecanemab significantly sooner in patients (110 ± 85 days) than did the academic center (155 ± 101 days) (p < 0.001), but no significant differences for DTTs were found for donanemab or ApoE4 homozygotic patients between practices. In addition, DTTs were significantly in the private practice compared to the academic practice for Medicare‐insured patients, but the difference was no longer detectable when looking at the privately insured individuals.

Within the academic center only, DTT improved significantly over the years; those diagnosed in 2025 had a much faster time to treatment compared to 2023 and 2024 (p = 0.009), whereas there was no difference in door to treatment in the private practice over time. Furthermore, patients treated with donanemab were started significantly earlier on this AAT following diagnosis compared to those started on lecanemab (p = 0.01). External referrals were correlated with a significantly increased time to treat compared to internal referrals (p = 0.004).

Academic practice adjudication panel

The academic center workflow included a monthly multidisciplinary AAT approval board as previously described for the complex patients failing to meet AUC, a step that has the potential to delay door‐to‐treatment intervals. 10 However, the adjudicated population mean time to treatment of 177 ± 136 days was not significantly different (p = 0.054) from the non‐adjudicated patients’ mean DTT of 133 ± 73.5 days.

Factors impacting door to treatment: combined data from private and academic practices

Between both practices, the average time from biomarker diagnosis to first infusion was 125.4 ± 90.3 days (Figure 1). Patients who received a biomarker diagnosis in 2025 had a significantly faster time to treatment compared to those diagnosed in 2023/2024. Additionally, patients with non‐Medicare or private insurance experienced significant delays to treatment compared to patients with Medicare. Between the two practices, there were no significant differences in DTT based on ApoE4 status, AAT choice, distance from clinic, referral type, or diagnosis. With a multivariable linear regression adjusting for age, sex, and ethnicity, the association between biomarker year and DTT remained significant (p = 0.015), whereas the difference in DTT between Medicare and non‐Medicare patients was no longer significant. This attenuation should be interpreted cautiously, however, because Medicare eligibility is closely tied to age. Adjusting for age removes much of the variance shared with insurance status, so the Medicare estimate may be over‐adjusted rather than truly null.

The impact of BBBs on DTT relative to CSF or amyloid PET resulted in shorter times when combining both clinic populations (Figure 2). DISCUSSION

Our investigation used real‐world clinical evidence to determine factors that improved and worsened DTT for AAT initiation. First, we found that DTT was dependent on the practice type: The private practice started AATs on average 37 days earlier than the academic practice. The operations of a private practice may offer certain advantages over larger integrated academic healthcare systems due to the increased flexibility and extended hours for dedicated clinical care. Such findings suggest that those private practices who have yet to adopt these treatments into clinical care may have certain advantages over larger academic systems in terms of this quality measure. Of note, no significant difference between the two practices was found for DTT in ApoE4 homozygotic patients, suggesting that this population required that both institutions take additional precautional measures. In addition, donanemab DTTs did not vary between healthcare systems, which can be explained by the fact that both institutions had greater experience with AATs before adopting this new therapeutic agent.

Disease severity, patient complexity (or those requiring review by the AAT approval board), referral type, and patient distance from clinic, although all potential delaying factors, were not found to significantly increase DTT, suggesting that these issues do not critically impact treatment initiation. The major deterrent to timely initiation of AATs was insurance type. Those patients with private insurance waited longer to start therapy relative to those with Medicare insurance. It can be assumed that most of these patients would be aged <65, where it is expected that individuals would experience the entire continuum of AD in their lifetime. Diagnosis in young‐onset AD is already delayed by 5.5 years, 11 and PET studies have indicated that disease progression may be more rapid than in late‐onset AD due to accelerated cerebral tau deposition. 12 Thus, private insurance and presumed processing delays add additional barriers to a population that has the greatest need for these treatments.

One of the important lessons from our analysis was the significant shortening of DTT for AATs when employing BBBs for AD diagnosis confirmation. The application of confirmatory plasma p‐tau217 was mostly used in the academic practice due to the high pre‐test probability of AD at a tertiary referral center. Results from the private practice should be interpreted with caution as the test was performed in 14 patients and likely, unmeasured confounders may undermine the strength of this association. Furthermore, the clinical application of BBBs may be limited due to out‐of‐pocket test costs, potential for insurance failing to cover the AAT without a traditional confirmatory biomarker (our experience in Washington state has shown consistent reimbursement in the case of patients with Medicare insurance), and lack of established role in monitoring longitudinal clinical changes (as opposed to using pre‐ vs post‐treatment amyloid PET). Ultimately, our findings have demonstrated the potential for BBBs to expedite AAT management, while further investigation is necessary to determine their role as a primary confirmatory rather than a screening confirmatory test for AD.

The strengths of this investigation include the combined data of 329 early‐stage AD patients from both an academic as well as a private neurological practice to address delays in AAT initiation, a highly relevant topic in real‐world clinical practice. The results further reflect outcomes in patients treated with donanemab as well as lecanemab. In addition, we had sufficient data to evaluate how the inclusion of a confirmatory blood‐based biomarker such as p‐tau217 may expedite the DTT.

The limitations of this study include the challenges associated with determining the initial start point to measure as patients may have been followed for years with an AD diagnosis before initiating an AAT. Furthermore, we used two separate methods to calculate DTT when comparing biomarkers versus the other clinical/demographic variables. Confounders for BBB times may be their use in more obvious cases and increased use over time, both of which contribute to reduced times to treatment. Furthermore, our findings indicated no significant impact of distance from clinical care on DTT, but we highly suspect that this finding resulted from a selection bias for highly motivated patients willing to travel long distances for AAT management. Finally, the ideal assessment for capturing the impact of BBBs would measure referral to treatment time, but neither clinic had access to primary care clinics willing to order p‐tau217 during the initial work‐up of cognitive impairment.

Emerging evidence from long‐term extension trials have provided a rationale for creating a quality measure for DTT for AATs. While pivotal trials have indicated that delays of 18 months may have a significant impact on long‐term outcomes, extensive investigation is still necessary to determine whether, for instance, a delay of 3 versus 6 versus 12 months within the treatment pathway should be acceptable. Meanwhile, efforts should continue to identify and intervene upon modifiable clinical factors that may shorten DTT for AATs in early‐stage AD.
