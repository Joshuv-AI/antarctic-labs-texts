Independent predictive role of nutritional markers in kidney function decline and mortality in diabetes
Authors: Tomohito Gohda, Nozomu Kamei, Marenao Tanaka, Masato Furuhashi, Tatsuya Sato, Mitsunobu Kubota, Michiyoshi Sanuki, Risako Mikami, Koji Mizutani, Yusuke Suzuki, Maki Murakoshi
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundMalnutrition and chronic inflammation are common in chronic kidney disease (CKD) and contribute to disease progression and mortality. While the prognostic nutritional index (PNI), geriatric nutritional risk index (GNRI), and controlling nutritional status (CONUT) scores assess nutritional status, their predictive values for CKD progression and mortality in individuals with diabetes, particularly independent of tumor necrosis factor receptor 2 (TNFR2), remains unclear. This study aimed to evaluate whether these markers predict outcomes beyond TNFR2.Subjects/methodsWe analyzed 640 individuals with diabetes, stratified by PNI quartiles (Q1 vs. Q2–4). Serum TNFR2 was measured using enzyme-linked immunosorbent assay. Nutritional status was assessed using PNI, GNRI, and CONUT scores. Cox proportional hazards models adjusted for covariates including TNFR2 examined associations between nutritional markers and a kidney event (≥30 % decline in estimated glomerular filtration rate), mortality, and a composite outcome.ResultsThe mean age was 65 years; 53.9 % were male. Over median follow-ups of 5.3 and 5.4-years, 75 (11.7 %) experienced a kidney event and 44 (6.9 %) died. A total of 112 (17.5 %) experienced the composite outcome. All three markers were independently associated with a kidney event (PNI: hazard ratio [HR], 1.84; 95 % confidence interval [CI], 1.13–3.02) and a composite outcome (PNI: HR, 1.94; 95 % CI, 1.30–2.89). GNRI was the only marker independently associated with mortality (HR, 2.90; 95 % CI, 1.56–5.37).ConclusionsPNI, GNRI, and CONUT scores strongly predict adverse outcomes in diabetes, emphasizing the importance of nutritional evaluation. Targeted nutritional interventions may improve prognosis.Keywords: Chronic kidney disease, Diabetes, Nutrition

Introduction

Malnutrition and chronic inflammation, which are common in individuals with chronic kidney disease (CKD), are significant risk factors for CKD progression, heart failure, and mortality [[1], [2], [3]]. Screening for malnutrition is crucial because at-risk individuals may benefit from targeted clinical nutritional therapies. The prognostic nutritional index (PNI), geriatric nutritional risk index (GNRI), and controlling nutritional status (CONUT) scores are practical and relatively user-friendly tools for identifying malnutrition in clinical settings. These markers have prognostic value in various conditions including cancer [4], stroke [5], heart failure [6], and end-stage kidney disease [7] as well as in the general population [8].

In diabetic kidney disease (DKD), proinflammatory cytokines secreted by renal cells or macrophages infiltrating the kidney play a pivotal role in disease pathophysiology [9,10]. Among these, tumor necrosis factor-α (TNF-α) is a key pro-inflammatory mediator that exerts its effects by binding to two high-affinity receptors [11]. Moreover, soluble forms of TNF receptors (TNFRs: TNFR1 and TNFR2) are strong predictors of adverse clinical outcomes in individuals with DKD [12,13].

Malnutrition and inflammation are bidirectionally related [14]. However, studies evaluating the role of multiple nutritional markers, such as PNI, GNRI, and CONUT scores, in predicting CKD progression and mortality, particularly in people with diabetes, while adjusting for established inflammatory markers of kidney prognosis, remain scarce. Therefore, we hypothesized that this approach is important because malnutrition and inflammation often coexist, and it remains unclear whether nutritional markers independently predict adverse outcomes or merely reflect underlying inflammatory burden. As TNFR2 is a well-established biomarker of chronic inflammation and tubular injury in DKD, adjusting for it allows us to evaluate the prognostic significance of nutritional status beyond the effects of inflammation alone.

Subjects and methods

Study design and participants

Participants with diabetes were recruited from the Kure Medical Center and Chugoku Cancer Center (Hiroshima, Japan) between July 1, 2014 and February 28, 2021 to observe the natural progression of DKD. Of the 738 participants initially enrolled, 98 were excluded because of a baseline estimated glomerular filtration rate (eGFR) of <30 mL/min/1.73 m2 or a follow-up period of <6 months. All participants had either type 1 or type 2 diabetes, diagnosed based on the American Diabetes Association (ADA) criteria: glycated hemoglobin (HbA1c) ≥ 6.5 %, fasting plasma glucose ≥126 mg/dL, or current use of antidiabetic medications. Participants with other types of diabetes were excluded. The final study population comprised 640 participants (Supplementary Fig. 1). The study was approved by the Ethics Committees of the Kure Medical Center and Chugoku Cancer Center (approval number: 26-06) and was conducted in accordance with the principles of the Declaration of Helsinki and the Ethical Guidelines on Clinical Studies established by the Ministry of Health, Labour, and Welfare of Japan. Written informed consent was obtained from all the participants.

Sample collection and laboratory measurements

Blood samples were collected at registration and stored at −80 °C until further analysis. The eGFR was calculated using formulas specific to the Japanese population following the guidelines of the Japanese Society of Nephrology [15]. Urinary albumin and creatinine levels were quantified by immunonephelometry (N-assay TIA Micro Alb; Nittobo Medical Co., Ltd., Fukushima, Japan) and enzymatic methods, respectively. The urinary albumin-to-creatinine ratio (UACR) is expressed as milligrams of albumin per gram of creatinine. Serum TNFR2 levels were measured using enzyme-linked immunosorbent assay (cat. No. DRT200; R&D Systems, Minneapolis, MN, USA) [16].

PNI, GNRI, and CONUT scores

Nutritional status at baseline was assessed using three validated indices: PNI, GNRI, and CONUT, calculated using standard published formulas. The PNI was calculated as follows: PNI = 10 × serum albumin level (g/dL) + 0.005 × lymphocyte count (/mm3) [17]. The GNRI was determined based on the serum albumin level and baseline body weight using the formula: GNRI = 14.89 × albumin level (g/dL) + 41.7 × usual weight (kg)/ideal weight (kg). The ideal weight was calculated using the Lorentz equation: for males, ideal weight (kg) = height (cm) – 100 – ((height – 150)/4); for females, ideal weight (kg) = height – 100 – ((height – 150)/2.5) [18]. The CONUT score was calculated using the albumin concentration, total peripheral lymphocyte count, and total cholesterol concentration. The CONUT score ranged from 0 (normal nutritional) to 12 (severe malnutrition) [19].

Adverse outcomes (kidney event, mortality, and composite outcomes)

A kidney event was defined as a 30 % decline in the eGFR from baseline, confirmed by two consecutive measurements at least one month apart. While some participants progressed to end-stage kidney disease (ESKD) or initiated dialysis during follow-up, all of them had already met the 30 % decline threshold prior to those events. Therefore, dialysis initiation or ESKD status was not treated as a separate outcome in our analysis. The composite outcome was defined as either a kidney event (a 30 % decline in eGFR) or mortality.

Statistical analysis

Continuous variables are expressed as means ± standard deviations (SDs) or medians (25th–75th percentiles), whereas categorical variables are presented as numbers and percentages. Participants were stratified into two subgroups based on their baseline PNI levels as follows: quartiles 1 (Q1) and 2–4 (Q2–4). Group characteristics are described as means ± SDs, medians (interquartile ranges), or frequencies, as appropriate. Groups were compared using the Mann–Whitney U test for continuous variables and the χ2 test or Fisher's exact test for categorical variables.

Spearman's correlation analysis was conducted to evaluate the relationships among the three nutritional markers (PNI, GNRI, and CONUT), kidney function markers (UACR and eGFR), and inflammatory markers (C-reactive protein [CRP] and TNFR2).

For logistic regression analyses, the primary objective was to evaluate whether low nutritional status—defined as being in the lowest quartile (Q1) of each nutritional index—was independently associated with TNFR2 levels, even after adjusting for clinical variables related to malnutrition. Covariates were selected based on two criteria: (1) variables that showed statistically significant differences between Q1 and Q2–4 in univariate comparisons (as shown in Table 1), and (2) clinical relevance to nutritional status or CKD based on prior literature. The following covariates were included in the models:

•PNI model: Age, sex, systolic and diastolic blood pressure, HbA1c, eGFR, UACR, high-density lipoprotein cholesterol (HDL-C), non-HDL-C, CRP, and body mass index (BMI).

•GNRI model: The same variables as the PNI model, with the exception of BMI, which was replaced by white blood cell (WBC) count.

This modification was necessary because BMI is a component of the GNRI calculation, and the PNI formula includes lymphocyte count, which is closely related to WBC count.

Kaplan–Meier survival curves were constructed to estimate the cumulative incidence of a kidney event, mortality, or composite outcome across groups defined by nutritional status. Differences between survival curves were evaluated using the log-rank test. We used a Cox proportional hazards model to investigate the prognostic impact of nutritional markers on adverse outcomes (a kidney event, mortality, and a composite outcome). Due to the limited number of kidney events (n = 70), we restricted the number of covariates to avoid overfitting. Our primary model included only essential clinical variables known to predict CKD progression: age, sex, eGFR, UACR, HbA1c, and TNFR2. As a sensitivity analysis, we constructed an additional Cox proportional hazards model. This model incorporated two additional covariates—diabetes duration and the presence of diabetic retinopathy—to assess the robustness of the association between nutritional indices and adverse outcomes after accounting for diabetes-related microvascular complications and disease duration. We did not include SGLT2 inhibitor use as a covariate in the primary analyses, as only 11 patients (1.7 %) were receiving this medication at baseline.

The PNI and GNRI were treated as both continuous and categorical variables (based on quartiles). In contrast, the CONUT score was analyzed only as a continuous variable and was not categorized. To evaluate the potential additive prognostic value of combined nutritional impairment, we performed an exploratory stratified analysis using both PNI and GNRI scores. Participants were classified into three groups: LL (both low PNI and low GNRI), LH (either low PNI or low GNRI), and HH (neither low PNI nor low GNRI). This stratification was based on the hypothesis that coexisting abnormalities in both nutritional indices would identify a subgroup at particularly high risk of adverse outcomes, beyond the predictive value of either index alone.

Biomarker discriminative performance was evaluated using C-statistics (analogous to the area under the receiver operating characteristic curve [AUC]), net reclassification improvement (NRI), and integrated discrimination improvement (IDI). All statistical analyses were performed using SAS version 9.4 (SAS Institute Inc., Cary, NC, USA) and R version 4.4.2 (The R Foundation for Statistical Computing, Vienna, Austria). Statistical significance was set at a two-tailed p < 0.05.

Results

Baseline characteristics

The participants were stratified into two groups based on their PNI values: Q1 and Q2–4. Their clinical characteristics are summarized in Table 1. Compared to the Q2–4 group, participants in the lowest PNI quartile (Q1) were older and had a higher UACR, longer diabetes duration, and a higher prevalence of diabetic retinopathy. Conversely, they showed a lower prevalence of type 2 diabetes and lower values for BMI, diastolic blood pressure, serum albumin, hemoglobin, non-HDL-C, and eGFR. Regarding inflammatory and nutritional markers, individuals with lower PNI values exhibited decreased GNRI values and white blood cell and lymphocyte counts, along with elevated CONUT scores and TNFR2 levels than their counterparts. However, CRP levels did not differ between the two groups. The clinical characteristics according to baseline GNRI values are summarized in Supplementary Table 1.

Correlation between nutritional and inflammatory markers and kidney function markers

As shown in Supplementary Table 2, Spearman's correlation analysis indicated weak to moderate correlations among the three nutritional markers, with correlation coefficients (r-values) ranging from −0.33 (GNRI and CONUT) to 0.57 (GNRI and PNI). Weak correlations were observed between nutritional markers, TNFR2, and eGFR. No correlation was identified between any nutritional marker and UACR. Although CRP levels demonstrated weak correlations with the GNRI and CONUT scores, they were no correlation with PNI.

Association between TNFR2 and PNI or GNRI in logistic regression analysis

As presented in Table 2, the multiple logistic regression analysis demonstrated that high TNFR2 levels were significantly associated with increased odds ratios (ORs) for low PNI (Q1) and GNRI (Q1) across models 1–4. This association remained significant even in the fully adjusted model (model 4) (OR: 2.25 for PNI; OR: 1.34 for GNRI).

Univariate analysis for determining the association of PNI or GNRI with clinical outcomes

Over median follow-ups of 5.3 and 5.4-years, 75 (11.7 %) experienced a kidney event and 44 (6.9 %) died. A total of 112 (17.5 %) experienced the composite outcome during median 5.3-year follow-up. To investigate the relationship between PNI and the temporal patterns of adverse outcomes (a kidney event, mortality, and a composite outcome), we plotted the cumulative risk of these outcomes over time according to PNI quartiles. Among individuals in the lowest PNI quartile, the cumulative risk of adverse outcomes steadily increased from the beginning of the observation period (Fig. 1). By contrast, individuals in the three higher quartiles experienced fewer events, which occurred later during follow-up. Similar trends were observed for the GNRI quartiles (Fig. 1). The differences in cumulative incidence among the PNI quartiles were statistically significant for all outcomes (log-rank test, p < 0.05 for a kidney event, mortality, and a composite outcome). Similarly, significant differences were observed among the GNRI quartiles (log-rank test, p < 0.05 for all outcomes).

Cumulative risk of a kidney event, mortality, and a composite outcome according to baseline PNI and GNRI quartiles, analyzed using a multivariate Cox proportional hazards model. P-values for the log-rank test were <0.05 for all outcomes in both GNRI and PNI analyses.

Multivariate analysis for determining the association of three nutritional markers with clinical outcomes

As shown in Table 3, individuals in the lowest PNI Q1 exhibited higher adjusted hazard ratios (HRs) than those in Q2–4. Specifically, the HRs were 1.84 (95 % confidence interval [CI]: 1.13–3.02) for a kidney event and 1.94 (95 % CI: 1.30–2.89) for a composite outcome. However, PNI was not significantly associated with mortality after adjusting for TNFR2. Additionally, a one SD increase in the log-transformed PNI was associated with a 30 % reduction in the risk of a kidney event (HR: 0.70) and a composite outcome (HR: 0.70).

For the AUC, the addition of biomarkers to the basic model did not significantly improve the prediction of adverse outcomes including a kidney event, mortality, or a composite outcome. However, the IDI and NRI demonstrated significant enhancements in predictive accuracy for a kidney event when each biomarker was included in the model as follows: TNFR2 (IDI: 0.023, p = 0.004; NRI: 0.480, p = 0.00006), PNI (IDI: 0.030, p = 0.005; NRI: 0.349, p = 0.004), and GNRI (IDI: 0.021, p = 0.04).

Similar trends were observed for a composite outcome, with significant improvements in both IDI and NRI as follows: TNFR2 (IDI: 0.032, p = 0.0004; NRI: 0.432, p = 0.00002), PNI (IDI: 0.035, p = 0.0009; NRI: 0.307, p = 0.003), GNRI (IDI: 0.032, p = 0.002; NRI: 0.285, p = 0.006), and CONUT (IDI: 0.015, p = 0.04).

In the case of mortality, only the GNRI significantly improved prediction when added to the basic model, as indicated by both the IDI (0.024, p = 0.04) and NRI (0.355, p = 0.02).

Sensitivity analysis (Supplementary Table 3) supported these findings. When the PNI or GNRI was added to the extended model, which included age, sex, HbA1c, UACR, eGFR, and TNFR2, the IDI showed a significant improvement in predicting a kidney event (PNI [IDI: 0.016, p = 0.004]; GNRI [IDI: 0.018, p = 0.004]) and a composite outcome (PNI [IDI: 0.020, p = 0.005]; GNRI [IDI: 0.026, p = 0.004]).

Discussion

In this study, we investigated the association between nutritional status—assessed using three simple indices: PNI, GNRI, and CONUT—and the risk of a kidney event, mortality, and a composite outcome in 640 Japanese individuals with diabetes. Lower baseline PNI and GNRI values were significantly associated with an increased risk of a kidney event (defined as a 30 % decline in eGFR from baseline), mortality, and a composite outcome comprising both a kidney event and mortality. These associations remained significant, even after adjusting for clinical covariates. After adjusting for TNFR2, a well-established predictor of CKD progression, both the PNI and GNRI predicted a kidney event and a composite outcome, whereas GNRI alone predicted mortality. Furthermore, baseline CONUT scores were significantly associated with a kidney event and a composite outcome but not with mortality.

Current CKD management emphasizes a holistic approach including dietary interventions to delay the progression to ESKD and prevent cardiovascular diseases [20]. Restricting protein intake beyond standard recommendations (0.8 g/kg/day) may suppress glomerular hyperfiltration and slow CKD progression [21]. However, a Cochrane systematic review has reported minimal to no impact on mortality or kidney failure when comparing very low-protein diets (0.3–0.4 g/kg/day) with low-protein diets (0.5–0.6 g/kg/day) or standard-protein diets (≥0.8 g/kg/day) over a 12-month period [22,23]. Additionally, restricting protein intake to <0.8 g/kg/day–especially when combined with limitations on carbohydrates and fats–can significantly reduce calorie intake. A meta-analysis of low-protein dietary therapy has indicated that despite maintaining serum albumin levels, BMI could decrease, particularly with long-term therapy and in older adults, where both albumin levels and BMI may decrease [24]. As a low GNRI score is associated with a higher risk of sarcopenia in individuals with and without type 2 diabetes [25,26], a flexible approach tailored to factors including age, physical activity, nutritional status, advanced cancer, and CKD severity is recommended, rather than uniformly applying strict protein restrictions [27].

Several studies have highlighted the association between lower PNI and GNRI values and adverse outcomes, such as CKD progression, cardiovascular diseases, and mortality in individuals with and without diabetes. For instance, Kim et al. [28] have reported that a low GNRI was significantly associated with CKD progression in individuals with type 2 diabetes, although their analysis was limited to logistic regression. In the National Health and Nutrition Examination Survey cohort, low GNRI and high CONUT scores were associated with increased all-cause mortality risk, whereas a high PNI was associated with reduced mortality risk in individuals with CKD; however, kidney outcomes were not evaluated in these studies [26,[29], [30], [31], [32], [33]]. Fujiwara et al. [34] have reported that a lower GNRI score was significantly associated with a higher risk of kidney outcomes and mortality in Japanese individuals with type 2 diabetes. Similarly, Xing et al. [35] examined multiple nutritional markers including the PNI and GNRI in biopsy-confirmed DKD cases and reported associations with kidney outcomes and all-cause mortality. Although previous studies have adjusted for proteinuria or urinary albumin as key kidney prognostic markers, these markers have not been quantitatively evaluated. Moreover, inflammatory markers, such as CRP or TNFR2, have rarely been adjusted for. By contrast, our study accounted for both urinary albumin and TNFR2 levels to more comprehensively evaluate the predictive value of nutritional markers for adverse outcomes using robust statistical methods. Since TNFR2 reflects chronic inflammation and tubular damage, adjusting for it enabled us to distinguish whether nutritional markers contribute to prognosis independently of systemic inflammation. Our findings showed that both the PNI and GNRI remained significant predictors of a kidney event and a composite outcome after TNFR2 adjustment, and significantly improved IDI and NRI metrics. These results suggest that poor nutritional status may capture additional risk domains not fully explained by inflammatory pathways alone.

In our exploratory subgroup analysis based on combined PNI and GNRI status (LL, LH, HH), we found a clear gradient in the risk of a kidney event and a composite outcome, with the LL group having the highest risk. This finding supports the notion that PNI and GNRI, although both reflecting nutritional status, capture distinct pathophysiological domains—immune-nutritional balance and protein-energy wasting, respectively. Their combined use may enhance prognostic discrimination. Clinically, this approach underscores the value of multidimensional nutritional assessment in identifying high-risk individuals with diabetes and CKD who may benefit from more intensive management.

Our multivariate logistic regression analysis revealed that the odds ratios for the association between nutritional markers and TNFR2 remained stable, indicating that these relationships were not confounded by clinical factors, such as kidney function. Additionally, weak correlations between nutritional and inflammatory markers (Supplementary Table 2) indicated that malnutrition and inflammation may independently contribute to adverse outcomes in this population, contrary to our initial expectations. Importantly, even after incorporating TNFR2 into the basic model, both the PNI and GNRI significantly improved the IDI for a kidney event and a composite outcome.

However, this study has several limitations. First, its observational design precludes causal inferences. Second, the study was conducted at a single center and involved only Japanese individuals with diabetes, which may limit the generalizability of the findings. Third, nutritional markers were assessed using single baseline measurements and temporal changes during follow-up could not be evaluated. Finally, the possibility of residual confounding from unmeasured factors cannot be excluded. In addition, despite a median follow-up of 5.3 years, the limited number of kidney events (n = 75) and deaths (n = 44) in our cohort of 640 participants suggests a need for larger, multi-center studies. These future studies would be essential to validate the generalizability and robustness of our findings.

In conclusion, poor nutritional status, as assessed based on GNRI and PNI values, could independently predict adverse outcomes including a kidney event and a composite outcome in individuals with type 2 diabetes, even after accounting for inflammatory markers, such as CRP and TNFR2. Tailored nutritional management strategies based on these markers are essential to improve outcomes in this population. Further research should determine whether improving the GNRI and PNI values can delay CKD progression and reduce mortality risk.
