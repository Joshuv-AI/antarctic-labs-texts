Diagnostic Performance of Direct PCR Assay for Candida auris Detection: A Comparison of Liquid Amies Transport Medium and Fungal Enrichment Broth
Authors: Jung‐ah Kim, Yu Kyung Jeon, Eun Jeong Won, Heungsup Sung, Mi‐Na Kim
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTBackground Candida auris has rapidly spread worldwide, representing a significant global health threat. Rapid diagnostic testing is essential for C. auris infection control. This study aimed to assess the diagnostic performance of a direct polymerase chain reaction (PCR) assay compared with culture. The liquid Amies transport medium (Copan ESwab; Copan, Italy) and fungal enrichment broth were compared to determine the optimal media for the direct PCR assay.MethodsThe diagnostic performance of the cobas omni Utility Channel Reagent Kit (Roche Diagnostics, Germany)‐based direct PCR assay and culture was assessed against clinical outcomes. Sixty skin swab specimens were prospectively collected from intensive care unit patients at a university hospital in Korea. Each sample was tested in parallel using the Copan ESwab and Sabouraud dextrose (SD) broth.ResultsThe direct PCR assay showed a sensitivity of 92.0%–96.0% and specificity of 85.7%–97.1% compared with a sensitivity of 76.0%–80.0% and specificity of 100% for culture across both media. For the direct PCR assay, the Copan ESwab showed a higher specificity, positive predictive value, and kappa value but lower sensitivity and negative predictive value than the SD broth. The discordant direct PCR assay‐positive/reference standard‐negative and PCR assay‐positive/culture‐negative cases showed significantly higher cycle threshold values than the concordant cases (p < 0.05).ConclusionThe direct PCR assay using the Copan ESwab showed high sensitivity for the detection of C. auris in clinical skin swab specimens. This approach may be useful for screening, although further validation in larger multicenter studies is needed.

Introduction

Candida auris (currently designated as Candidozyma auris, C. auris ) is an emerging multidrug‐resistant yeast that was initially reported in 2009 from the ear cultures of one Japanese patient and 15 Korean patients and was subsequently isolated from the blood cultures of three patients in Korea [1, 2, 3]. Since then, C. auris has spread rapidly across healthcare facilities in Asia, the United States (US), South Africa, and Europe, which has become a serious global health concern, based on the surveillance data from the World Health Organization (WHO) and other public health agencies [4, 5, 6, 7]. Its transmission dynamics and epidemiological distribution within healthcare environments have exceeded those of most other clinically relevant fungal pathogens [8], resulting in repeated outbreaks, particularly in long‐term care facilities and intensive care units (ICUs) [9, 10, 11, 12, 13]. Owing to its extensive antifungal resistance [14, 15, 16], high mortality rates (30%–60%) [17, 18], and rapid transmissibility, the WHO designated C. auris as a “critical human fungal pathogen” [19], and the US Centers for Disease Control and Prevention (CDC) categorized it as an “urgent threat” [20] in 2019.

The rapid identification and containment of C. auris are essential to prevent healthcare‐associated transmission. However, conventional laboratory workflows tend to involve fungal culture followed by a species‐level identification via matrix‐assisted laser desorption/ionization time‐of‐flight mass spectrometry (MALDI‐TOF MS), which requires approximately 3–7 days to obtain the results [21, 22]. Thus, reducing the time required for species identification is a critical step for improving infection prevention and control. Direct polymerase chain reaction (PCR) assays have been proposed as an effective alternative for the rapid detection and management of C. auris . It has been shown that implementing direct PCR assays significantly reduces the diagnostic turnaround time, delivering results within 3–24 h compared with several days required for culture [21, 22]. The cobas omni Utility Channel Reagent Kit, a high‐throughput, fully automated molecular diagnostic assay, can process 96 samples and generate results in approximately 5 h and 45 min on the cobas 5800 platform and about 3 h on the cobas 6800/8800 platforms. Moreover, the cobas 5800/6800/8800 systems provide end‐to‐end automation, incorporating direct specimen loading and on‐board nucleic acid extraction [23]. This integrated workflow helps minimize manual preprocessing, reduce contamination and nucleic acid loss, and improve overall laboratory efficiency.

The primary objective of this study was to assess the diagnostic performance of a direct PCR assay for detecting C. auris in clinical samples in comparison to conventional culture followed by MALDI‐TOF MS identification. Furthermore, to address practical considerations in clinical laboratory workflows, the liquid Amies transport medium (Copan ESwab) and fungal enrichment broth (Sabouraud dextrose broth, SD broth) were compared. The secondary objective of this study was to determine the most appropriate transport medium for direct C. auris PCR assay in routine screening settings.

Materials and Methods

Study Design and Clinical Specimen Collection

Figure 1 shows the overall study design. The skin swab specimens from the ICU patients undergoing C. auris screening were collected into two liquid media (Copan ESwab and SD broth) and tested using both direct PCR assay and culture followed by MALDI‐TOF MS. The concordance between the diagnostic methods was assessed using the clinical outcomes as a reference standard. In the present study, the clinical outcomes were defined as a composite clinical reference standard, comprehensively determined by the patient's documented colonization status and all available microbiological data other than the index tests, including previous and follow‐up culture results, and infection control records. This composite approach was applied because a single test alone is generally considered insufficient to accurately determine the true colonization status in a routine screening setting. In the present study, a total of 60 skin swab specimens were obtained on June 30, 2025, and July 7, 2025 (30 samples per day) from the ICU patients at Soonchunhyang University Seoul Hospital, Seoul, Korea. Swabbing targeted both the axillae and groin sites (four sites per patient) in accordance with the CDC guidelines [24]. Two Copan ESwabs per patient were simultaneously sampled at all four sites; one went into the Copan ESwab (Copan Diagnostics, Brescia, Italy) and the other into SD broth (Asan Pharmaceutical, Seoul, Korea) before sealing. Within 1 h, the samples reached the laboratory. Furthermore, the media were streaked for culture, and residual material was stored at a temperature of −70°C or below until direct PCR assays.

The performances of direct PCR assay and culture were assessed against clinical outcomes (reference standard). Direct PCR Assay

The direct PCR assay for C. auris was performed using the Copan ESwab and SD broth samples. The preformulated assay‐specific C. auris TaqMan primer and probe sets were obtained from Integrated DNA Technology (catalog #10017949; Integrated DNA Technologies Inc. Coralville, IA). The samples stored at a temperature of −80°C or below were thawed at room temperature before processing. Nucleic acid extraction, purification, and amplification were performed using the cobas omni Utility Channel Reagent Kit (Roche Diagnostics, Mannheim, Germany) on the cobas 6800 System (Roche Diagnostics, Basel, Switzerland) in accordance with the manufacturer's instructions. As previously described [23], 600 μL of the prepared oligopool was added to 10 mL of the supplied Master Mix R2 from the UC kit. This was mixed in a user‐supplied 50‐mL capped tube and then added to the UC cassette in accordance with the manufacturer's instructions. The cycling conditions included a three‐step pre‐PCR cycle of 55°C for 120 s, followed by 60°C for 360 s and 65°C for 240 s. The first measurement (5 cycles) included a two‐step PCR cycle at a temperature of 95°C for 5 s and 55°C for 30s. The second measurement (45 cycles) included a two‐step PCR cycle at a temperature of 91°C for 5 s and 58°C for 25 s. The cycle threshold (Ct) and relative fluorescence increase (RFI) values were defined for the C. auris target (Channel 3) (Ct of ≤ 38, RFI of ≥ 2.5) and the internal control (channel 5) (Ct of ≤ 50, RFI of ≥ 2) to report valid and positive sample results. Furthermore, the UC negative control cassette (catalog #7001185190, Roche) was used with a Ct value of ≥ 38 and an RFI value of ≤ 2.5 cutoffs to validate each run.

Culture Followed by MALDI‐TOF MS

The Copan ESwab and SD broth samples were each streaked onto CHROMagar Candida plates (CHROMagar, Paris, France) and incubated aerobically at a temperature of 35°C–37°C for 24–72 h using a BD 720 incubator (Binder, Tuttlingen, Germany). Visible colonies underwent MALDI‐TOF MS (Bruker Daltonics, Bremen, Germany) identification using the Bruker MALDI Biotyper database (version 12.0). The identification scores of ≥ 2.0 confirmed species‐level identification [25]. The isolates yielding scores of < 2.0 were sub‐cultured from CHROMagar Candida plates onto blood agar plates (Asan Pharmaceutical, Seoul, Korea), incubated at a temperature of 35°C–37°C for 24–48 h, and reanalyzed via MALDI‐TOF MS for the final identification.

Data Analysis

The concordance between the direct PCR assay and culture results ( C. auris detected or not detected) was assessed against clinical outcomes as the reference standard. The diagnostic performance characteristics—sensitivity, specificity, positive predictive value (PPV), negative predictive value (NPV), and Cohen's kappa—were measured for both diagnostic methods. The Ct value distributions (semiquantitative PCR results) across reference standard‐positive/−negative (Ref+/Ref−) and culture‐positive/−negative (Culture+/Culture−) groups were analyzed to assess direct PCR assay clinical validity. The Ct value distributions for the groups with n ≥ 5 were compared using the Mann–Whitney U test. For the comparison of the subgroups with n < 5 (specifically the Copan ESwab PCR+/Ref − group, n = 1), the single observed Ct value was compared against the mean and standard deviation of the larger reference group using a one‐sample Z‐test. This approach was chosen because nonparametric tests are generally inapplicable for a single observation, whereas the larger group (n ≥ 20) showed stable distribution parameters. Considering the extremely small subgroup size, these specific comparisons should be interpreted with caution. The results of the group with n = 1 were presented as an exploratory rather than a confirmatory finding. Furthermore, statistical significance was set at a p value of < 0.05. All analyses were performed using the Rex software, version 5.1 (Rexsoft, Seoul, Korea).

Results

The direct PCR assay performance by transport medium showed that the Copan ESwab yielded a sensitivity of 92.0% (95% confidence interval [CI] 75.0–97.8), specificity of 97.1% (95% CI 85.5–99.5), PPV of 95.8% (95% CI 79.8–99.3), NPV of 94.4% (95% CI 81.9–98.5), and kappa value of 0.897 (95% CI 0.784–1.000). The SD broth showed a sensitivity of 96.0% (95% CI 75.0–97.8), specificity of 85.7% (95% CI 70.6–93.7), PPV of 82.8% (95% CI 65.5–92.4), NPV of 96.8% (95% CI 83.8–99.4), and kappa value of 0.799 (95% CI 0.650–0.947). Compared with the SD broth, the Copan ESwab showed a lower sensitivity and NPV but higher specificity, PPV, and kappa value.

Culture performance by transport medium showed that the Copan ESwab showed a sensitivity of 76.0% (95% CI 56.6–88.5), specificity of 100% (95% CI 90.1–100), PPV of 100% (95% CI 83.2–100), NPV of 85.4% (95% CI 71.6–93.1), and kappa value of 0.787 (95% CI 0.630–0.944). The SD broth showed a sensitivity of 80.0% (95% CI 60.9–91.1), specificity of 100% (95% CI 90.1–100), PPV of 100% (95% CI 83.9–100), NPV of 87.5% (95% CI 73.9–94.5), and kappa value of 0.824 (95% CI 0.679–0.968). Compared with the SD broth, the Copan ESwab culture showed a lower sensitivity, NPV, and kappa value but identical specificity and PPV (100%).

Ct Value Distributions

Figure 2 shows the Ct value distributions for the PCR‐positive specimens only (semiquantitative assay) by concordance or discordance with the other diagnostic methods. Reference standard and culture results served as the comparison methods. The reference standard comparisons (Figure 2A) showed that the concordant results (PCR+/Ref+) had lower Ct values than discordant results (PCR+/Ref−) across both media, with statistically significant differences between the PCR+/Ref + and PCR+/Ref − groups (p < 0.05). For the Copan ESwab, the PCR+/Ref + group (n = 23) showed a Ct value range of 12.3–35.5 (median 20.3), and the PCR+/Ref − group (n = 1) showed a Ct value of 37.0 (p = 0.0046) (one‐sample Z‐test). Although statistical significance was achieved, this result was presented as an exploratory finding due to the presence of only a single observation in the comparative group. In the SD broth, the PCR+/Ref + group (n = 24) showed a Ct value range of 9.7–34.3 (median 15.0), while the PCR+/Ref − group (n = 5) showed a Ct value range of 29.7–36.3 (median 31.4) (p = 0.0061) (Mann–Whitney U test). Moreover, the Copan ESwab showed a narrower Ct value range and a higher median than the SD broth.

The culture comparisons (Figure 2B) showed that the concordant results (PCR+/Culture+) had lower Ct values than the discordant results (PCR+/Culture−) across both media, with statistically significant differences between the PCR+/Culture+ and PCR+/Culture− groups (p < 0.05). In the Copan ESwab, the PCR+/Culture+ group (n = 19) showed a Ct value range of 12.3–31.1 (median 19.6), while the PCR+/Culture− group (n = 5) showed a Ct value range of 18.4–37.0 (median 32.2) (p = 0.0192) (Mann–Whitney U test). In the SD broth, the PCR+/Culture+ group (n = 20) showed a Ct value range of 9.7–32.0 (median 14.6), while the PCR+/Culture− group (n = 9) showed a Ct value range of 24.0–36.3 (median 32.0) (p = 0.0001) (Mann–Whitney U test). Furthermore, the Copan ESwab showed a wider Ct value range and a higher median than the SD broth.

Discussion

This study showed that direct PCR assay using Copan ESwab is more sensitive and rapid for the detection of C. auris in clinical skin swab specimens than conventional culture. Moreover, the use of either the Copan ESwab or SD broth was comparable to determine the optimal medium for direct specimen application in the C. auris PCR assay.

First, when comparing the performances of the direct PCR assay and culture, the direct PCR assay showed a higher sensitivity and NPV but lower specificity and PPV than culture, regardless of the medium. The discrepancy observed—specifically, higher sensitivity but lower specificity of the direct PCR relative to culture—likely stems from the low fungal load typical of skin colonization, which may fall below the detection limit of culture [26, 27, 28]. Using the same system, Alexander et al. [23] observed a sensitivity of 99.0% (95% CI 93.7–99.8) and specificity of 87.8% (95% CI 81.3–95.2), whereas Lui et al. [29] observed a sensitivity of 91.7%, which is consistent with our results in terms of sensitivity and specificity. Studies using different assays based on direct PCR with the Copan ESwab also yielded comparable results. The SYBR Green‐Based Quantitative PCR Assay Kit (Bio‐Rad Laboratories, California, USA) showed a sensitivity of 93.0% and specificity of 96.0% [30], while the Luna Probe One‐Step RT‐qPCR Kit (New England Biolabs, Massachusetts, USA) showed a sensitivity of 100% (95% CI 90.7–100) and specificity of 100% (95% CI 83.2–100) at 100 CFU/mL and a sensitivity of 82.1% (95% CI 66.5–92.5) and specificity of 100% (95% CI 80.5–100) at 10 CFU/mL [21]. These findings suggest similar sensitivity compared with the results of our study but generally higher specificity. Moreover, the Luna Probe One‐Step RT‐qPCR Kit study confirmed high diagnostic performance even at low fungal concentrations.

When comparing the Copan ESwab and SD broth as media for direct PCR assay, the Copan ESwab showed a lower sensitivity and NPV but higher specificity and PPV than SD broth. Komorowski et al. [21] assessed the performance of C. auris diagnosis using the Luna Probe One‐Step RT‐qPCR Kit with the Copan ESwab and SD broth, reporting detection limits of 27.1. CFU/mL for the Copan eSwab and 2.8 CFU/mL for the SD broth and higher Ct values for true‐positive results using the Copan ESwab compared with the SD broth. Although that study concluded that the SD broth had superior analytical sensitivity compared with the Copan ESwab, a key difference can be observed in the enrichment duration. Their study involved 3 days of broth enrichment before the PCR assay, whereas the present study used SD broth samples frozen at a temperature of −70°C within 1 day of collection and thawed before the PCR assay. Dehesa‐Garcia et al. [31] assessed the C. auris diagnostic performance using the VIASURE C. auris Real‐Time PCR Detection Kit (Certest Biotec, Zaragoza, Spain) with only SD broth and approximately 2 h of enrichment, reporting a sensitivity of 98.0% (95% CI 90.0–100), specificity of 100% (95% CI 99.0–100), PPV of 98.0% (95% CI 90.0–100), and NPV of 100% (95% CI 99.0–100)—superior performance across all metrics compared with our study. Although the enrichment times were similar between that study and the present study, performance differences were observed. While the SD broth can increase fungal concentration to improve analytical sensitivity, making it suitable as a PCR assay medium, variations in postinoculation enrichment conditions (e.g., temperature and duration) can differentially affect the fungal concentrations, complicating the standardization for sensitivity and specificity evaluation. Moreover, cocultured fungi from the same specimen may result in enrichment contamination. Komorowski et al. [21] argued that SD broth is inefficient for routine diagnostics due to prolonged culture times, considering enrichment duration, workflow complexity, and higher costs, making it unsuitable as a routine PCR assay medium. Beyond diagnostic accuracy, our findings highlight the practical advantages of the Copan ESwab for direct C. auris PCR assay within routine clinical laboratory workflows. Since it bypasses the need for additional enrichment steps, it minimizes workflow complexity and allows for a better standardization of the screening process, which is highly beneficial for routine clinical laboratories.

Considering the importance of rapidity and accuracy in C. auris screening, our findings suggest that direct PCR assay, used in conjunction with confirmatory culture, may be a practical approach for C. auris screening in this setting. With regard to the turnaround time, direct PCR assay requires approximately 3 h per sample from collection to identification, whereas culture takes 3–7 days [21, 23]. In terms of diagnostic accuracy, the high sensitivity and NPV of the direct PCR assay make it suitable for early detection screen testing. However, due to its lower specificity and PPV compared with culture, confirmatory testing with culture is recommended. In our study population, the Copan ESwab showed a higher specificity and PPV than SD broth and may be a more suitable medium for direct PCR assay under the assessed conditions. The identification methods comparable with the direct PCR assay include culture as well as conventional phenotypic and biochemical assays [18, 23, 32, 33, 34]. However, these phenotypic and biochemical assays frequently misidentified C. auris as closely related species, including Candida haemulonii, Candida duobushaemulonii, Candida pseudohaemulonii, Candida guilliermondii , Candida lusitaniae , Candida famata , and Candida parapsilosis [34]. Considering these limitations, the CDC recommends that biochemical test results should always be interpreted with caution and in conjunction with confirmatory diagnostics, such as MALDI‐TOF MS or DNA sequencing [35]. Although next‐generation sequencing and Sanger sequencing provide improved accuracy [36, 37], their high cost, infrastructure demands, and requirement for specialized personnel compromise their routine application [38]. Moreover, our data confirmed the potential usefulness of direct PCR assay using the Copan ESwab as a screening method for C. auris, although the number of samples included in the present study was small. Thus, further validation from larger multicenter studies is needed to assess its generalizability, clinical impact, and cost‐effectiveness.

The results of direct PCR assay in relation to the discrepancy with clinical outcomes should be carefully interpreted. Among the direct PCR assay‐clinical outcome discrepancies in the present study, the direct PCR assay‐positive and clinical outcome‐negative (PCR+/Ref−; false‐positive) cases occurred once with the Copan ESwab (Ct value 37.0; negative with the SD broth) and five cases with the SD broth (Ct values of 29.7, 31.2, 31.4, 35.4, and 36.3; all negative with the Copan ESwab). The PCR+/Ref − group showed substantially higher Ct values than PCR+/Ref + (Figure 2A). Kordalewska et al. [28] suggested that microorganisms on the skin of the patient may cause false positives in the PCR assays. Dehesa‐Garcia et al. [31] and Alexander et al. [23] assessed the cross‐reactivity of C. auris PCR assays using closely related Candida species but observed no false positives. Conversely, clinical outcome‐positive and direct PCR assay‐negative (PCR−/Ref+; false‐negative) cases occurred twice with the Copan ESwab (Ct values of 24.0 and 37.0; both positive with the SD broth) and once with the SD broth (Ct value of 35.5; positive with the Copan ESwab). The most common cause of PCR assay false negatives is sparse fungal load from C. auris skin colonization. The PCR assay limit of detection in the skin swab matrix was reported to be 10.5 CFU/mL and 14.8 CFU/mL for the non‐aggregative and aggregative C. auris strains, respectively [26]. The axilla or groin colonization was estimated at < 101–102 CFU [28], so variations in the sampling area or pressure may yield false negatives. Moreover, thick C. auris biofilms may impair DNA release, resulting in false negatives. Strategies to reduce PCR assay false negatives include 3‐point sampling (adding nose) [24, 32] or repeat PCR on resampled specimens [27, 37]. The PCR inhibitors from skin swabs (sebum, antiseptics, blood contamination, etc.) may also hinder extraction, prevent amplification, or increase the Ct values [39].

Of the direct PCR assay‐culture discrepancies, the direct PCR assay‐positive and culture‐negative (PCR+/Culture−) cases numbered five with the Copan ESwab (four true positives, one false positive by clinical outcome) and nine with the SD broth (four true positives, five false positives). Of these, 50%–70% are attributed to residual nucleic acids from nonviable organisms [39], with discrepancies also possible at low viable burdens [39, 40, 41]. In the present study, the median Ct values were significantly higher in PCR+/Culture− (32.0–32.2) than in PCR+/Culture+ (14.6–19.6), which is consistent with the findings of Dehesa‐García et al. [31] who reported a Ct value of 40.42 in PCR+/Culture−. Contamination [42] or culture failures due to technical factors (incubation temperature, duration, etc.) [43] may also cause PCR+/Culture−. Furthermore, no culture‐positive and direct PCR assay‐negative (PCR−/Culture+) cases were observed in the present study.

However, this study has several limitations that should be addressed. The sample size was limited, and the samples were obtained solely at a single center over a brief interval, thereby restricting external validity. All tested samples were skin swabs from the axilla and groin, so diagnostic accuracy may not extend to other clinically relevant specimen types, such as the blood, sputum, or urine. Thus, future multicenter studies assessing direct PCR assay performance across diverse specimens are needed for the generalizability of the C. auris identification method performance comparisons. Moreover, clinical metadata were not included, precluding the analysis of the patient‐specific factor impacts on the results. Despite these limitations, this study is significant for not only analyzing direct PCR assay performance but also comparing media‐specific performance between liquid Amies transport media and fungal enrichment broth.

In conclusion, this study confirmed the superior sensitivity of direct PCR assay for C. auris identification compared with culture, with a specificity comparable to culture in clinical skin swab specimens. Furthermore, the Copan ESwab showed a more favorable performance profile—including a higher specificity and PPV—along with streamlined workflow advantages compared with SD broth as a direct PCR assay medium. Thus, direct PCR assay is expected to be a valuable tool for a rapid and accurate C. auris screening. However, considering the single‐center design and limited sample size of the present study, these findings should be carefully interpreted and validated in larger multicenter studies.
