Letter to the editor regarding “social determinants of health and hospitalization risk in heart failure: A retrospective analysis”
Authors: Amit Jaiswal, Manish Arya, Dharmendra B. Sharma
Published: 2026

Dear Editor,

We read with great interest the recent article by Crump et al. examining the association between social determinants of health (SDOH) and hospitalization risk among patients with heart failure (HF) [1]. The authors should be commended for addressing an increasingly important yet under-integrated dimension of HF care. However, several considerations merit further discussion.

First, while the study convincingly demonstrates that food insecurity, financial resource strain, and transportation barriers are independently associated with increased hospitalization risk, the analysis treats these determinants largely as isolated exposures [1]. This approach may underestimate the true burden of social vulnerability, as prior evidence suggests that SDOH frequently cluster and interact synergistically, amplifying their impact on cardiovascular outcomes [2]. A more integrative modeling strategy capturing cumulative or intersecting social risk could further strengthen the predictive and clinical relevance of these findings.

Second, although the authors highlight statistically significant associations, the clinical translation of these findings remains insufficiently developed. For instance, the observed magnitude of risk increase raises an important question: how should these social factors be operationalized within existing HF risk stratification tools? Prior literature indicates that traditional models often fail to account for social complexity, thereby underestimating risk in vulnerable populations [3]. Without clear integration pathways, the applicability of these findings to real-world clinical decision-making may remain limited.

Third, the reliance on self-reported SDOH measures introduces potential misclassification and underreporting bias, particularly given the stigma associated with financial hardship and food insecurity [1]. This limitation may lead to conservative estimates of effect size and highlights the need for more robust, standardized approaches to capturing social risk within electronic health records.

Despite these limitations, the study makes an important contribution to the growing body of literature emphasizing that SDOH are not peripheral but fundamental determinants of HF outcomes. Notably, the authors quantify the independent impact of specific, modifiable social risks on future hospitalization, thereby advancing the field beyond descriptive associations toward actionable risk stratification. These findings complement prior evidence linking food insecurity to cardiovascular disease and healthcare utilization [4], [5], and socioeconomic stress to adverse biological and clinical outcomes [2], while uniquely demonstrating their predictive significance in HF hospitalization risk.

The implications for clinical practice and health systems are substantial. Incorporating structured SDOH assessment into HF management frameworks, alongside targeted interventions addressing identified social needs, may represent a critical opportunity to reduce preventable hospitalizations and improve equity in cardiovascular care. However, future research should prioritize longitudinal and multi-level approaches to better capture the dynamic and interconnected nature of social risk.

In conclusion, Crump et al. provide timely and clinically relevant evidence supporting the integration of SDOH into HF risk assessment. Addressing these modifiable social determinants is essential to advancing comprehensive, patient-centered care and improving outcomes in this high-risk population.
