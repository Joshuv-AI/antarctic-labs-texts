Systematic engineering of Escherichia coli for biosynthesis of 3-hydroxypropionic acid from glucose and malonate
Authors: Yingying Wang, Meihui Cao, Mei Hu, Hengrui Xu, Yafei Du, Changxing Sun, Lingfei Kong, Yu Luo, Xiaoyu Liu, Jianming Yang, Bo Liang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackground3-Hydroxypropionic acid (3-HP) is a promising C3 platform chemical with wide industrial applications. However, its microbial production remains limited by insufficient intracellular malonyl-CoA availability and metabolic imbalance.ResultIn this study, we systematically engineered Escherichia coli for enhanced 3-HP biosynthesis. The malonate assimilation genes (matB, smatPQM) and 3-HP biosynthesis gene (mcr) were chromosomally integrated using CRISPR/Cas9, resulting in a plasmid-free, antibiotic-free strain (WYY04) that produced 21.97 mM 3-HP, 0.51-fold higher than the plasmid-based system. Further improvement was achieved by CRISPRi-mediated repression of fatty acid biosynthesis genes (fabD, fabF), increasing 3-HP titer by 66%. Introduction of a malonyl-CoA-responsive FapR/fapO biosensor enabled dynamic regulation of mcr expression, enhancing 3-HP production by 59%. Through all these above engineering, the 3-HP production of the strain WYY19 increased by 2.29 times compared to that of the plasmid-expressing system. Under optimized fermentation conditions, the final engineered strain WYY19 produced 42.22 g/L 3-HP with the specific productivity of 0.69 g/g and 0.46 g/L/h from glucose and malonate in fed-batch bioreactor.ConclusionsThis study demonstrates a robust, genetically stable, and scalable microbial platform for 3-HP biosynthesis.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12896-026-01140-2.Keywords: 3-HP production, Microbial cell factory, Genomic integration, CRISPRi-mediated repression, Malonyl-CoA biosensor, Glucose and malonate

Introduction

3-hydroxypropionic acid (3-HP) is a promising C3 platform chemical that has been recognized by the U.S. Department of Energy as one of the top value-added chemicals from biomass [1]. It serves as a precursor for numerous industrially important compounds, including acrylic acid, 1,3-propanediol, malonic acid, and biodegradable polymers [2]. Despite its potential, the biological production of 3-HP remains challenging due to low yields and productivity, primarily resulting from metabolic imbalances, precursor limitations, and inefficient pathway regulation [3].

Escherichia coli has been widely employed as a host organism for metabolic engineering owing to its rapid growth, well-characterized genetics, and ease of genetic manipulation. Several biosynthetic pathways for 3-HP have been explored in E. coli, among which the malonyl-CoA-dependent pathway has attracted considerable attention due to its theoretical high yield and direct utilization of central carbon metabolism intermediates [3, 4]. This pathway typically involves the conversion of malonyl-CoA to 3-HP via malonyl-CoA reductase (MCR), a bifunctional enzyme that catalyzes the two-step reduction of malonyl-CoA to 3-HP. To enhance the synthesis of the key precursor malonyl-CoA, heterogenous malonate transportation and metabolic pathway have been introduced into E. coli for high efficient synthesis of 3-HP in our previous study [5]. However, the expression of heterologous pathway genes was often affected by plasmid instability and metabolic burden, which could be mitigated through chromosomal integration of the synthetic pathway to ensure stable and consistent expression. Up to date, genomic integration strategy for 3-HP bioproduction in E. coli has not been attempted.

Intracellular malonyl-CoA is also the key precursor required for microbial biosynthesis of flavonoids, polyketides, and fatty acid [6, 7]. In heterologous hosts such as E. coli and Saccharomyces cerevisiae, the endogenous fatty-acid biosynthetic pathway sequesters the majority of cytosolic malonyl-CoA. In E. coli, malonyl-CoA is synthesized from acetyl-CoA by acetyl-CoA carboxylase (ACC) [8], and its intracellular pool is tightly regulated [9]. Consequently, intracellular malonyl-CoA pools remain inherently limiting and are insufficient to support high-flux heterologous production pathways. Many strategies for enhancing the substrate pool of malonyl-CoA have been established for increasing the production of malonyl-CoA-derived products, including (i) up-regulating the carbon flux from acetyl-CoA to malonyl-CoA by over-expressing and engineering acetyl-CoA carboxylase (ACC) [10], (ii) eliminating competing pathways that divert acetyl-CoA toward non-essential metabolites via targeted gene deletions and stringent transcriptional repression [11], and (iii) reinforcing the de novo synthesis of acetyl-CoA by amplifying the pyruvate dehydrogenase complex, activating the ATP-citrate lyase route, and fine-tuning central carbon routing [12, 13]. Since intracellular malonyl-CoA is related to the synthesis of fatty acids and phospholipids, traditional gene knockout methods can lead to slowed cell growth or even cell death. To inhibit the competing pathways, the main approach was to increase the intracellular malonyl-CoA content by using antisense RNA [14] or tetracycline [15] to inhibit the fatty acid metabolism pathway. Alternatively, exogenous supplementation of the potent fatty acids synthesis inhibitor cerulenin could also significantly inhibited the synthesis of fatty acids, however, its high cytotoxicity and cost preclude large-scale application [16]. Other methods to bypass the endogenous route to malonyl-CoA via the ACC complex have been developed by expressing malonate assimilation pathways, in which malonyl-CoA can be synthesized by malonyl-CoA synthetase (MatB) using malonate as the substrate [5].

In this study, we aimed to systematically engineer E. coli for enhanced 3-HP production via the malonyl-CoA-dependent pathway using glucose and malonate as co-substrates (Fig. 1). Specifically, we integrated the 3-HP biosynthetic genes into the chromosome to achieve stable expression, disrupted key competing pathways to redirect carbon flux toward malonyl-CoA accumulation, and optimized fermentation conditions to support high-level 3-HP synthesis. Through these integrated strategies, we successfully improved the production of 3-HP and demonstrated the potential of engineered E. coli as a robust platform for sustainable 3-HP biosynthesis. Coupled with media optimization and fed-batch fermentation, we achieved the highest 3-HP productivity reported to date from glucose and malonate in the fed-batch bioreactor.

Materials and methods

Strains, plasmids, reagents

All bacteria strains and plasmids are described in Supplementary Table 1. E. coli strain DH5α was used for plasmid construction and amplification. E. coli MG1655 was the host for the expression of enzymes. E. coli strains were cultured at 37℃ in Luria-Bertani medium (5 g/L yeast extract, 10 g/L tryptone and 10 g/L). For producing 3-HP, engineered strains were cultured in beef extract (BE) medium (20 g/L glucose, 9.8 g/L K2HPO4·3H2O, 9.0 g/L (NH4)2SO4, 2.1 g/L citric acid monohydrate, 0.3 g/L ammonium ferric citrate, 9.0 g/L beef extract, 0.24 g/L MgSO4, and 0.1 mL trace element solution, which includes (NH4)6Mo7O24·4H2O 3.7 g/L, ZnSO4·7H2O 2.9 g/L, H3BO4 24.7 g/L, CuSO4·5H2O 2.5 g/L, and MnCl2·4H2O 15.8 g/L).

The Sodium malonate dibasic monohydrate used in this study was obtained from Shanghai Aladdin Biochemical Technology Co., Ltd. (Shanghai, China). The Sodium L-(+)-Arabinose used in this study was obtained from Yuanye Bio-Technology Co., Ltd. (Shanghai, China). All other chemicals were supplied from Sinopharm Chemical Reagents (Shanghai, China). DNA polymerases, assembly kits, and RNA extraction kits were purchased from Vazyme (Nanjing, China).

Genomic manipulation

Genomic integration was performed using a previously established CRISPR/Cas9 system. Neutral genomic loci (yciQ, ilvG, yjiV, lafU, efeU) were selected as integration sites based on previous studies. Among these genes, yciQ and lafU are genes with unknown functions. The ilvG gene encodes acetohydroxy acid synthase II. The protein encoded by the yjiV gene belongs to the transcription factor family, and the efeU gene is an iron uptake-related gene. The heterologous genes encoding malonyl-coA carboxylase (MatB) from Rhodopseudomonas palustris (GenBank CAE25665.1), malonyl-coA reductase (MCR) from Chloroflexus aurantiacus (GenBank AAS20429.1) and TRAP transport protein (matPQM) from Sinorhizobium meliloti (GenBank NC_003037.1) were codon-optimized and synthesized by Sangon Biotech (Shanghai, China) and Tsingke Biological Technology Company (Qingdao, China).

Specific targeting spacers for yciQ, ilvG, yjiV, lafU and efeU were screened using the CHOPCHOP online tool (https://chopchop.cbu.uib.no/). A uniquely matched sequence of 20 nucleotides upstream of the PAM sequence (NGG) in the genome of E. coli MG1655 was selected as the spacer sequence, and the screened gRNA sequences are shown in Table S2. Using plasmid pGRB as the template, 5’-phosphorylated oligonucleotides were incorporated into the primers, and the pGRB-gRNA backbone containing the gRNA sequence was amplified by inverse PCR. The recombinant plasmid pGRB-gRNA was obtained via self-ligation of the backbone and then transformed into host cells. Genes related to the 3-hydroxypropionic acid (3-HP) pathway and the genomic homology arms of the target gene loci were amplified by PCR, followed by fusion to generate donor DNA fragments. Primer sequences employed for PCR amplification are detailed in Table S4. Subsequently, the donor DNA fragments and plasmid pGRB-gRNA were co-introduced into E. coli MG1655 host cells harboring plasmid pRED-Cas9 by electroporation, and the transformation plates were incubated at 30℃. After identifying correct transformants, they were cultured at 30℃ in LB liquid medium supplemented with 4 mM L-arabinose to eliminate plasmid pGRB-gRNA, yielding strains containing plasmid pRED-Cas9. On this basis, genomic integration of subsequent loci was performed using the same method. The aforementioned strains were inoculated into LB liquid medium and cultured at 42℃ to remove plasmid pRED-Cas9, resulting in genome-modified strains (Table S1).

CRISPR interference (CRISPRi)

The fatty acid biosynthesis pathway was targeted for suppression using CRISPRi. The plasmid backbone of pBAD was amplified by inverse PCR using the pBAD plasmid as the template. The dCas9 fragment was PCR-amplified from the plasmid pBSA-dCas9 [17] and ligated into the pBAD vector, resulting in the plasmid pBAD-dCas9. Spacer sequences specifically targeting fabF, fabD and fabB were designed using CHOPCHOP (https://chopchop.cbu.uib.no/). Unique sequences of 20 ~ 30 nucleotides upstream of the PAM sequence (NGG) in the E. coli MG1655 genome were selected as spacers. The designed gRNA sequences are listed in Table S3. The plasmid backbone of pBAD-dCas9 was amplified by inverse PCR using pBAD-dCas9 as the template. The sgRNA scaffold was PCR-amplified from the plasmid pBSA-sgRNA1 [17], with 5’-phosphorylated oligonucleotides incorporated into the primers to introduce the gRNA sequences. The resulting sgRNA fragments were ligated into the pBAD-dCas9 vector, yielding the plasmid pBAD-dCas9-sgRNA. The constructed pBAD-dCas9-sgRNA plasmids carrying different sgRNAs were transformed into the host strain WYY04, generating strains WYY12 ~ 19 and WYY22 ~ 31.

Construction of recombinant strains

The codon-optimized fapR gene was synthesized by Sangon Biotech (Shanghai, China) [18], yielding the plasmid pUC57-fapR. The fapO element (5’-TTAGTACCTGATACTAA-3’) was inserted into the promoter PJ23100 to construct the promoter PJ233 [18]. A PJ233 promoter sequence embedded with two fapO sequences was introduced upstream of the mcr gene in the form of primers for driving the expression of mcr. The pBBR-MCS5 plasmid backbone was amplified by inverse PCR using primers pBBR-F/pBBR-R. The PJ233-mcr fragment and the fapR gene, which were PCR-amplified using primers detailed in Table S4, were then ligated into the backbone to generate the final plasmid pBBR-MCS5-fapR-PJ233-mcr.

The plasmid pRSFDuet-mcr was constructed via inverse amplification using pRSF-mcr-F/pRSF-mcr-R as primers on the basis of plasmid pRSFDuet-matB-mcr. The PCR primers are listed in Table S4. The plasmids pRSFDuet-mcr and pBBR-MCS5-fapR-PJ233-mcr were transformed into the strain WYY03, yielding the engineered strains WYY20 and WYY21, respectively.

Transcription level analysis by RT-qPCR

A single colony was inoculated into a 50 mL flask containing 10 mL of LB medium and cultured with shaking at 200 rpm and 37 °C. This pre-culture was then transferred at a 1% inoculation volume into a 250 mL flask containing 50 mL of BE medium, followed by incubation at 37 °C with shaking at 200 rpm. When the bacterial growth reached an optical density at 600 nm (OD₆₀₀) of ~ 0.6, induction was initiated by adding sodium malonate to 30 mM. The culture was then shifted to 30℃ and continued shaking at 200 rpm for 2 h. The cells were harvested by centrifugation.

The transcriptional levels of genes smatPQM, matB, mcrC, mcrN, accA, accD, sdhC, fabF, fabD, and fabB were analyzed by RT-qPCR. To compare relative mRNA expression levels of above genes, bacteria WYY12 and the control strain WYY04 at the specified growth phase were collected by centrifugation at 8,000 rpm/min and 4 °C. The harvested cells were lysed using lysozyme, and total RNA was extracted using the FastPure Cell/Tissue Total RNA Isolation Kit V2 (Vazyme, China). RNA concentration was quantified using a NanoDrop ND1000 spectrophotometer (Thermo, USA). The extracted RNA served as the template for cDNA synthesis, which was performed with the HiScript Ⅲ 1st Strand cDNA Synthesis Kit (+ gDNA wiper) (Vazyme, China). Quantitative reverse transcription PCR (qRT-PCR) was carried out using gapA as the reference gene to assess the transcriptional changes of the target genes. The primers used for detecting these genes are listed in Table S5. ChamQ Universal SYBR qPCR Master Mix (Vazyme Biotech Co., Ltd, China) and qTOWER3 Real-Time PCR Thermal Cycler (Jena, Germany) were used for all quantitative assays. The comparative CT method was used for relative quantification of gene expression. The relative quantification for each of the mRNA levels was calculated using the ΔCT method, which is the difference between the CT of the gene of interest and that of the reference gene.

Shake flask cultivation

For shake flask incubation, a single colony was inoculated into a 50 mL flask containing 10 mL of LB medium and cultured with shaking at 200 rpm and 37 °C. This pre-culture was then transferred at a 1% inoculation volume into a 250 mL flask containing 50 mL of BE medium, followed by incubation at 37 °C with shaking at 200 rpm. When the bacterial growth reached an optical density at 600 nm (OD₆₀₀) of ~ 0.6, induction was initiated by adding isopropyl-beta-D-thiogalactopyranoside (IPTG) to a final concentration of 0.6 mM, L-arabinose to 1 mg/mL, and sodium malonate to 30 mM. The culture was then shifted to 30℃ and continued shaking at 200 rpm for 16 h to facilitate 3-HP biosynthesis.

Analytical methods

Cell density was determined using a spectrophotometer (Thermo Fisher Scientific, USA) at OD600. Samples were taken at 16 h during the shake-flask fermentation process. The 3-HP was quantified by high-performance liquid chromatography (HPLC) with an Eclipse Plus C18 reversed-phase column (Agilent, 4.6*250 mm, 5 μm). The mobile phase consisted of Component A (0.1% (v/v) phosphoric acid aqueous solution) and Component B 100% methanol. The elution program was set as follows: isocratic elution with A: B = 97.5:2.5 (v/v) for the first 10 min, followed by linear gradient elution from 10 min to 12 min to increase the proportion of Component B to 100%. This 100% B component condition was maintained for 5 min (12 min to 17 min). Subsequently, the mobile phase was switched back to A: B = 97.5:2.5 (v/v) for re-equilibration for 5 min (17 min to 22 min). The injection amount of the reaction product was 10 µL, the flow rate was 1.0 mL/min, the detection wavelength was set at 210 nm, and the column oven temperature was maintained at 40℃.

Quantification of fatty acids

Fatty acids were quantified by an internal-standard procedure using triglyceride undecanoate as the standard. The microbial biomass was subjected to acid hydrolysis. The hydrolyzed sample was mixed with 10 mL of 95% ethanol. The hydrolysate was transferred to a separatory funnel, and the flask and stopper were rinsed with 50 mL of diethyl ether/petroleum ether (1:1, v/v), which was combined into the separatory funnel and sealed. After 5 min of shaking and 10 min of phase separation, the upper ether layer was collected. The extraction was repeated three times, and the pooled ether phases were evaporated to dryness under reduced pressure to yield the total fatty-acid extract.

The dried extract was trans-esterified by adding 8 mL of 2% (w/v) NaOH in methanol, and the mixture was connected to a reflux condenser and refluxed in an 80 °C ± 1 °C water bath until oil droplets disappeared. Then, 7 mL of 15% boron trifluoride in methanol was added through the top of the reflux condenser, and refluxing was continued in the 80 °C ± 1 °C water bath for 2 min. The reflux condenser was rinsed with a small amount of water. Heating was stopped, and the flask was removed from the water bath and rapidly cooled to room temperature. Exactly 10 mL to 30 mL of n-heptane was added, and the mixture was shaken for 2 min. Saturated sodium chloride solution was added, and the mixture was allowed to stand for phase separation. Approximately 5 mL of the upper n-heptane extract was aspirated into a 25 mL test tube, to which about 3 g to 5 g of anhydrous sodium sulfate was added. The mixture was shaken for 1 min and allowed to stand for 5 min. The supernatant was then transferred to a sample vial for subsequent analysis.

Determination of fatty acids by Gas Chromatography (GC). The analysis utilized a capillary column (ANPEL CD-2560 100 m × 0.25 mm × 0.25 μm). The injector temperature was set at 270 °C, the column oven temperature was maintained at 80 °C, and the detector temperature was 280 °C. The temperature program spanned a total of 85 min, starting at an initial temperature of 100 °C held for 13 min. This was followed by an increase to 180 °C at a rate of 10 °C/min, which was then held for 6 min. Subsequently, the temperature was raised to 200 °C at a rate of 1 °C/min and maintained for 20 min. Finally, the temperature was increased to 230 °C at a rate of 4 °C/min and held for 10.5 min. Nitrogen was used as the carrier gas with an injection volume of 1.0 µL. Individual fatty acid methyl ester (FAME) standard solutions and a mixed FAME standard solution were separately injected into the gas chromatograph for qualitative analysis of the chromatographic peaks.

Optimization of cultural mediums

The optimal medium was determined by conducting shake-flask induction fermentation under identical conditions using three different media, including BE medium, LB medium and M9 medium. Bacterial growth was monitored by measuring OD₆₀₀, and 3-HP production was quantified. M9 medium contains 20 g/L glucose, 6 g/L Na2HPO4, 3 g/L KH2PO4, 1 g/L NH4Cl, 0.5 g/L NaCl, and 0.12 g/L MgSO4.

Fermentation in bioreactor

In fed-batch cultivation, the recombinant strains were grown in 10 L bioreactors containing 5 L M9 medium to produce 3-HP. The strains were pre-cultured in 100 mL of M9 medium overnight at 37 °C and subsequently inoculated into a bioreactor. Air was supplied at 5 vvm, and agitation speed was set at 200 ~ 300 rpm to maintain DO at 20 ~ 25%. The temperature was set at 37 °C, and the pH was adjusted to 7.0 during the process using ammonium hydroxide solution (28% NH3 in H2O). Glucose solution was added at a rate of 1.66 g/h to maintain the concentration of glucose below ~ 2 g/L after the initial glucose in M9 medium was exhausted. The temperature was set at 30 °C when the malonate (30 mM) was added into the cultures every 12 h. The samples were taken out every 4 h to examine cell growth, glucose and malonate consumptions, and 3-HP formation. The concentration of glucose was detected by Biosensor Analyzer (M-100, Sieman, China).

Results

Genomic integration enhances 3-HP biosynthesis

Plasmids remain the dominant vehicle for heterologous gene delivery in E. coli because they are technically simple, attain high copy numbers, and permit rapid, dose-responsive expression control. However, industrial bioprocessing demands strains that maintain genetic integrity across hundreds of generations, impose minimal metabolic burden, and function without antibiotic selection. Chromosomal integration satisfies these constraints, while CRISPR/Cas9-mediated recombineering in E. coli offers precision editing, scar-free insertion, and heritable stability, providing an optimal platform for robust, large-scale fermentation [19, 20].

Here, CRISPR/Cas9 was employed to construct recombinant strains in which malonate metabolic pathway genes were integrated into the genome of E. coli MG1655. The TRAP transporter genes (smatPQM), malonyl-CoA synthetase (matB from R. palustris) and bifunctional malonyl-CoA reductase (mcr) were sequentially integrated into the neutral loci lafU, ilvG and efeU, yielding strain WYY04 (Fig. 2A). All three cassettes are driven by the strong, constitutive promoter J23100, eliminating the need for inducers or antibiotic selection. In shake-flask cultures supplied with 20 g L− 1 glucose and 30 mM malonate, WYY04 produced 21.97 mM 3-HP within 16 h, which was 1.51 times that of the WYY01 using the plasmid as the expression vector (Fig. 2B). The OD of the strain WYY04 reached 2.96, which was higher than that of the strain using the plasmid as the expression vector. The improved titer and growth profile reflect relief from plasmid replication burden and the absence of antibiotic stress, underscoring the metabolic advantages of genomic integration. When strain WYY-04 was cultivated with only glucose, the production of 3-HP was undetectable. Collectively, all of these results demonstrate that stable, single-copy integration of heterologous pathway genes is an effective strategy for enhancing 3-HP production in E. coli.

Next, to investigate whether increasing the copy number of the integrated smatPQM and matB genes could increase the yield of 3-HP, double-copy recombinant strains were constructed (WYY06, WYY08 and WYY10) based on the single-copy strain WYY04 (Fig. 2A). It was found that due to the integration of a large number of copy numbers of genes on the genome of the strain, the growth condition of the strain was affected to a certain extent, but the OD was still higher than that of the strain using the plasmid as the expression vector. Surprisingly, 3-HP titers plateaued at 20.65 mM and declined by 6% in WYY10 (Fig. 2B), revealing that product yield is not limited by gene copy number beyond one chromosomal integration. This uncoupling between copy number and metabolic flux most likely reflects saturation of malonyl-CoA reductase, imbalance of TRAP-transporter stoichiometry, or accumulation of malonate semialdehyde, underscoring that host-network robustness dictates the upper production limit under these conditions [21].

Inhibition of fatty acids synthesis improved 3-HP biosynthesis

Fatty-acid biosynthesis competes directly with 3-HP formation for the common precursor malonyl-CoA. To increase intracellular malonyl-CoA availability, we systematically attenuated the endogenous fatty-acid pathway by CRISPRi-mediated repression of three genes whose products channel malonyl-CoA into lipid synthesis (fadB, fadF and fadD). fadD is responsible for converting malonyl-CoA to malonyl-ACP, and fabF/fadB is responsible for condensing malonyl-ACP to acyl-ACP. Considering that the position of protospacer adjacent motif (PAM) and the binding affinity of sgRNA to genome affect inhibition efficiency [22], three candidate sgRNAs for each enzyme gene were prepared and subjected to screening (Fig. 3A). The CRISPRi vectors were transformed into the engineered strain WYY04 with one copy number of exogenous metabolic genes integrated into its genome, yielding a panel of recombinant strains (Fig. 3A). When these CRISPRi strains were grown in LB medium supplemented with glucose and malonate, they displayed slower growth compared with the initial strain due to plasmid WYYp21 burden. Moreover, the different positions of PAM on genome affect the production of 3-HP in recombinant strains (Fig. 3B). Results showed that the manipulation of fadD, fadF and fadB genes led to approximately a 35%, 16% and 29% increase in the 3-HP concentrations in the WYY27, WYY24 and WYY30 strains, respectively (Fig. 3A). Subsequently, we designed CRISPRi to simultaneously repress three genes. The strain WYY22, WYY23, and WYY24 are single-site CRISPRi knockdown strains targeting the fabF; WYY25, WYY26, and WYY27 are single-site CRISPRi knockdown strains targeting the fabD; WYY28, WYY29, and WYY30 are single-site CRISPRi knockdown strains targeting the fabB. Results showed that the simultaneous inhibition of fadD and fadF two genes showed that strain WYY12 greatly surpassed single site CRISPRi repression strains in the amount of 3-HP (Fig. 3B). And compared to strain WYY04, the titer of 3-HP in strain WYY12 increased 66% (Fig. 3B).

Next, RT-qPCR was performed to examine the CRISPRi efficiency towards malonate degradation-related enzyme genes. Results showed that the CRISPRi strain with sgRNA targeting fadB and fadD gene exhibited inhibitory activities compared to the control WYY04. As shown in Fig. 3C, fadD gene transcription was repressed by 66.65% due to the CRISPRi inhibition. Meanwhile, the expression of the acetyl-CoA carboxylase gene acc, which catalyzes the conversion of acetyl-CoA to malonyl-CoA, was obviously upregulated. To further investigate the impact of CRISPRi on the fatty acid synthesis pathway genes, we quantitatively determined the fatty acid content of the cells. The results showed that the total fatty acid content in the strains containing the CRISPRi system was 21.83% lower than that in the control (Fig. 3D). This indicates that the inhibition of fadD and fadF gene expression indeed affected fatty acid synthesis. Therefore, the combined results from the transcriptional and metabolic levels suggest that the malonyl-CoA pool within the cells was redirected from the fatty acid synthesis pathway to the 3-HP synthesis pathway.

Dynamic control of malonyl-CoA to enhancing 3-HP biosynthesis

The malonyl-CoA biosensor derived from Bacillus subtilis has been widely used for the dynamic regulation of fatty acid synthesis in Escherichia coli [7]. However, studies on the synthesis of 3-HP have not been reported. In this study, the system was introduced into an engineered strain harboring genomic integrated smatPQM and matB genes. Previous study has shown that the intermediate product malonate semialdehyde catalyzed by MCR is toxic to cells [23]. Therefore, we used the malonyl-CoA biosensor to dynamically regulate the transcription level of MCR to reduce the toxicity of malonate semialdehyde. When the malonyl-CoA level was low, fapR bound to the fapO sequence within the MCR promoter, preventing RNA polymerase from initiating the transcription of the gene. On the contrary, when the malonyl-CoA level was high, malonyl-CoA bound to fapR, preventing fapR from binding to the MCR promoter, allowing RNA polymerase to initiate the transcription of the gene (Fig. 4). Initially, the FapR-fapO sensor was deployed on a high-copy plasmid (strain WYY21), which elevated the 3-HP titer by 66% relative to the sensor-free control WYY20 (Fig. 5A), confirming that dynamic modulation of mcr can effectively mitigate malonate-semialdehyde toxicity and improve pathway flux. In contrast, chromosomal integration of the same sensor module (strain WYY05) failed to enhance production, and no beneficial effect was observed in multi-copy strains (WYY07, WYY09, WYY011). This lack of yield improvement may be attributed to the consumption of intracellular malonyl-CoA for the biosynthesis of substances such as fatty acids, leading to a persistently limited supply of malonyl-CoA available for 3-HP synthesis. MCR fails to initiate expression under the regulation of the malonyl-CoA sensing system when precursor supply remains below the saturation threshold of malonyl-CoA reductase. Consequently, further fine-tuning of downstream gene expression cannot lead to an increase in product formation.

To ensure both a sufficient malonyl-CoA supply and real-time alleviation of intermediate toxicity, we simultaneously implemented CRISPRi-mediated repression of fatty-acid biosynthesis and the FapR-fapO sensor-driven control of mcr resulting strain WYY13. The combined strategy elevated the 3-HP titer by 69% relative to the parental single-copy strain (Fig. 6), demonstrating that diminishing malonyl-CoA consumption while preventing malonate-semialdehyde accumulation acts synergistically to boost pathway output. An additional copy of matB can further increase the yield of 3-HP. However, further increasing the copy number of smatPQM led to a decrease in yield. It is necessary to simultaneously increase the copy numbers of smatPQM and matB to increase the yield (strain WYY19), which was 1.17 times increase relative to strain WYY21 (Fig. 6). Elevating smatPQM alone accelerates malonate uptake, yet the unbalanced influx cannot be immediately detoxified, leading to intracellular malonate accumulation that competitively inhibits succinate dehydrogenase and disrupts TCA-cycle function. Concurrent overexpression of both smatPQM and matB maintains the import-conversion balance, ensuring rapid channeling of malonate into malonyl-CoA and downstream 3-HP, thereby minimizing cytotoxicity and maximizing productivity. As demonstrated by the above results, strain WYY19 exhibited the highest 3‑HP titer and was therefore selected for subsequent medium component optimization and bioreactor scale fermentation experiments.

Medium optimization to enhance 3HP production in strain WYY19

Alongside genetic engineering approaches, process optimization has shown to be important in optimization of bioproduct titers, rates and yields [24]. Although the strain WYY19 has demonstrated excellent 3-HP production capacity, further optimization of the fermentation conditions is still necessary. We firstly investigated the impact of medium composition on 3-HP production, including LB, M9, and beef extract media for the fermentation and 3-HP synthesis of the strain. Although maximum biomass was attained in LB (Fig. 7A), M9 medium supported the highest 3-HP titer (Fig. 7B), while beef extract yielded intermediate values. The superior productivity in M9 is attributed to lower salt stress and reduced organic complexity, which minimize feedback inhibition and enhance carbon flux toward the target product. Consequently, M9 medium was selected for all subsequent fermentation studies.

When glucose was used as an important raw material for the growth of the strain and the synthesis of 3-HP, its addition concentration significantly affected the production efficiency of 3-HP. Therefore, we explored the effect of the initial glucose concentration on 3-HP production. The fermentation results are shown in Fig. 7C. Glucose had a certain degree of influence on 3-HP production, and the 3-HP yield increased with the increase of glucose concentration, reaching a peak at an initial glucose concentration of 20 g/L, with a yield of 57.03 mM. However, when the glucose concentration was further increased to 25 g/L, the yield did not increase further but slightly decreased. Therefore, to improve the production efficiency of 3-HP, an initial glucose concentration of 20 g/L was selected.

Besides glucose, malonic acid is also a substrate for the synthesis of 3-HP. Moreover, malonic acid is an inhibitor of the TCA cycle, a central metabolic pathway in E. coli. Excessive malonic acid can inhibit cell growth and limit the synthesis of 3-HP. Therefore, we investigated the effect of malonic acid concentration on the yield of the target product. As shown in Fig. 7D, the 3-HP yield increased with the increase of malonic acid concentration, reaching a peak at an initial malonic acid concentration of 30, with a yield of 59.81 mM. However, when the malonic acid concentration was further increased to 40, the yield decreased instead, and a large amount of malonic acid remained in the culture medium. This might be due to the inhibitory effect of high malonic acid concentration on cell metabolism or the stress caused by the high osmotic pressure environment on the physiological state of the cells, leading to the diversion of metabolic resources to cope with environmental stress rather than product synthesis.

Production of 3-HP in fed-batch bioreactor cultivation

Following strain and cultivation condition optimization, we then sought to further enhance 3-HP production and demonstrate the feasibility of process scale-up in a 5-L fed-batch bioreactor. The M9 medium was used during the batch fermentation, and glucose was fed manually when glucose level dropped below 1.5 g/L. For strain WYY04, cell density reached ~ 10 OD600 at 9 h and further increased to ~ 20 OD by 25 h, the latter level being maintained thereafter until the end of the bioreactor run at 83 h. 3-HP production was started at initial stage of fermentation at a lower level (<4 g/L) and dramatically increased by addition of malonate from 43 to 67 h, and reached the maximum level of 23.96 g/L at 832 h (Fig. 8A). The maximum productivity between 43 h and 67 h was 0.62 g/L/h, and the average productivity for 83 h of operation was 0.29 g/L/h. Discussion

We demonstrated a genome-anchored, plasmid-free E. coli chassis that produces 3-HP through malonyl-CoA pathway at a titer of 1.98 g/L in shake flasks and 23.96 g/L in fed-batch bioreactor currently the highest 3-HP yield reported for plasmid system using malonate and glucose as co-substates. Integration of smatPQM, matB, and mcr into validated neutral loci abolished antibiotic selection, relieved metabolic burden, and eliminated the 10–30% productivity loss typically observed during prolonged cultivation of plasmid-bearing strains. Single-copy strain outperformed its multi-copy counterparts, suggesting that increased gene repeat number does not linearly correlate to higher flux when the downstream enzymatic capacity or malonyl-CoA-pool becomes rate-limiting.

Malonyl-CoA availability was next rewired by CRISPRi knock-down of fabD and fabF. Dual repression increased 3-HP 66% and reduced total fatty acids by 21.83%, corroborating that the fatty acids synthesis pathway is the dominant consumption for this intermediate. Notably, growth rate was only marginally affected (decreased 8%), indicating that residual enzymatic activity or compensatory phospholipid recycling was sufficient to sustain membrane biogenesis. Moreover, fabD repression outperformed fabB inhibition contrasts with a previous report and highlights the context-dependency of sgRNA efficacy and PAM position, reinforcing the need for sgRNA screening even for well-characterized targets. A previous study redirected glucose flux from the EMP pathway toward 3-HP by CRISPRi knock-down of gapA (glyceraldehyde-3-phosphate dehydrogenase) and budA (2,3-butanediol synthase) using glycerol as substrate. Although that approach validated CRISPRi for 3-HP improvement, the titer remained low (0.78 g/L in shake flasks) [25].

To alleviate malonate-semialdehyde toxicity, we employed the FapR/fapO malonyl-CoA biosensor to modulate mcr transcription in real time. When malonyl-CoA is scarce, FapR binds fapO and represses mcr; upon CoA sufficiency, malonyl-CoA displaces FapR, enabling mcr expression and 3-HP formation. This biosensor improved 3-HP yield by 68% relative to constitutive expression. Importantly, genomic embedding of the sensor failed to enhance production unless coupled with CRISPRi-mediated fatty-acid inhibition, confirming that dynamic regulation is beneficial only when precursor supply is sufficiently high.

Therefore, we adopted three strategies approach to enhance 3-HP production. Firstly, we integrated the 3-HP synthesis pathway into E. coli through genomic site integration, yielding strain WYY04, which exhibited a 51% increase in 3‑HP production. Next, we enhanced 3-HP synthesis by inhibiting the fatty acid synthesis pathway. Compared to strain WYY04, strain WYY12 was subjected to CRISPRi inhibition on its basis, resulting in a 66% increase in 3-HP production. On this basis, we implemented dynamic regulation of the malonyl-CoA biosensor. Strain WYY13 led to a 37% increase in 3-HP. However, strain WYY05 integrated the gene fapR upstream of the gene mcr showed a lower 3-HP production. This demonstrates that only by coupling CRISPRi-mediated suppression of the fatty acid synthesis pathway with a malonyl-CoA biosensor can the 3-HP production be enhanced. To increase the intracellular supply of malonyl-CoA, multiple copies of matB and smatPQM were introduced into the genome of strain WYY13, resulting in strain WYY19, which resulted in a 29% increase in 3-HP production. Therefore, it can be concluded that the chromosome pathway integration, CRISPRi-mediated inhibition, and malonyl-CoA biosensor dynamic regulation are all absolutely necessary for achieving the reported performance.

Process-level optimization revealed that M9 medium outperformed rich LB or beef-extract medium despite lower biomass, emphasizing that nitrogen limitation and lower salt stress favor 3-HP formation. A glucose feeding level of 20 g L⁻¹ balanced carbon sufficiency, while 30 mM malonate maximized yield without triggering osmotic or TCA-cycle inhibition. During controlled bioreactor cultivation, WYY19 reached a cell density 4.46% lower than that of WYY04. This modest reduction is presumably attributable to either the CRISPRi-mediated suppression of fatty-acid biosynthesis or the metabolic burden imposed by the maintenance of exogenous plasmids within WYY19. The amount of malonyl-CoA consumed by the WYY19 strain was 28.57% higher than that of the WYY04 strain, indicating that the metabolic engineering modification of the WYY19 strain has enabled the strain to enhance its consumption of the substrate. This is also in line with the production trend of 3-HP. The production of WYY19 is 1.76 times that of WYY04. Furthermore, during the fermentation process of the two strains, the supply of glucose was the same. This also indicates that, apart from malonate, more glucose was diverted towards the synthesis of 3-HP. The strategy of inhibiting the fatty acid synthesis pathway via CRISPRi, proposed in this study, has demonstrated significant short-term improvements in 3-HP production, highlighting its potential in metabolic engineering. However, a key limitation lies in its long-term stability: targeting essential genes such as fabD and fabF imposes strong evolutionary pressure, which may drive cells to develop escape mechanisms such as CRISPRi system inactivation or mutations in the target genes to regain growth advantages. The fermentation data obtained over approximately 83 h in this study are insufficient to assess such risks, which are inevitable during industrial-scale fermentations spanning hundreds of generations. Additionally, the plasmid-based CRISPRi system itself suffers from genetic instability. These factors present serious challenges for industrial scale-up, potentially leading to a decline in performance of the production strain over time, reduced process controllability, and increased costs associated with monitoring and inducer supplementation required to maintain the system. Therefore, future research and development must prioritize long-term passaging stability validation as a core evaluation step. Strategies such as genomic integration of CRISPRi components, the use of multiple sgRNAs for targeting, or combining dynamic regulation with growth-coupled design should be employed to construct more genetically robust engineered strains, ensuring their practical feasibility in large-scale production.

It is acknowledged that reliance on exogenous malonate, which is significantly more expensive than glucose (constituting ~ 56.7% of the total substrate cost in the current process), represents a major constraint for economic feasibility. Furthermore, malonate acts as a competitive inhibitor of succinate dehydrogenase, and its metabolic inhibitory effect adds complexity to the precise concentration control required during fermentation. Therefore, direct scale-up of the current process indeed faces economic challenges. However, the primary objective of this study resides in proof-of-concept and platform establishment. Specifically, through a combined strategy of chromosomal integration, CRISPRi-mediated repression of competing pathways, and dynamic regulation via a malonyl-CoA-responsive biosensor, we have successfully validated the feasibility of efficiently coupling an exogenous precursor with endogenous central carbon metabolism and directing carbon flux toward 3-HP biosynthesis. The achieved high specific productivity of 0.69 g/g convincingly demonstrates the effectiveness of this metabolic engineering paradigm. To realize genuine industrial potential, the core of subsequent work must focus on developing a supply system independent of exogenous malonate addition. We clearly prioritize the construction of an engineered strain capable of endogenous malonate synthesis from a single, low-cost carbon source (e.g., glucose). This entails introducing or enhancing an endogenous malonate biosynthetic pathway originating from central metabolic nodes (such as oxaloacetate or acetyl-CoA), ultimately achieving integrated production from precursor supply to final product synthesis. This technical roadmap is clear and feasible. The genetically stable and programmable host chassis constructed in this study serves as the critical foundation for achieving this goal.

Although this study successfully constructed a plasmid-free E. coli platform with high genetic stability and dynamic regulation potential, achieving relatively high 3-HP production, a more in-depth quantitative analysis is still required to fully assess its industrial potential. Future work should focus on: quantitatively characterizing the accumulation of the intermediate metabolite malonic semialdehyde and its cytotoxic effects; precisely analyzing the NADPH/NADP⁺ cofactor balance during pathway operation; systematically characterizing the dynamic response of the FapR/fapO biosensor in vivo to optimize the regulatory circuit; employing techniques such as metabolic flux analysis to accurately quantify the distribution of carbon flux between fatty acid synthesis and 3-HP synthesis, thereby identifying and removing potential hidden bottlenecks; and reducing the metabolic burden by fine-tuning the expression level, thereby achieving further improvement in the stability of the strain and the production of 3-HP. These systematic quantitative studies will not only guide further enhancement of this synthetic pathway but also provide universal engineering insights into balancing growth, precursor supply, and toxicity management for the synthesis of other malonyl-CoA-based biochemical products.

Conclusions

In this study, we systematically engineered a plasmid-free E. coli strain for high-level production of 3-hydroxypropionic acid (3-HP) from glucose and malonate. By integrating the heterologous biosynthesis pathway into genome, repressing competing fatty acid synthesis via CRISPRi, and introducing a malonyl-CoA-responsive biosensor for dynamic pathway control, we achieved significant improvements in 3-HP titer. The final engineered strain WYY19 produced 42.22 g/L 3-HP in fed-batch fermentation, with a specific productivity of 2.24 g/g DCW and an average productivity of 0.46 g/L/h. This work demonstrated that combining genomic stability, targeted flux redirection, and dynamic metabolic regulation offers a powerful and transferable strategy for developing robust microbial cell factories for malonyl-CoA-derived products.
