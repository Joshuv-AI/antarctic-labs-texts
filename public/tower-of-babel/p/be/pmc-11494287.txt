Dramatic droplet deformation through interfacial particles jamming
Authors: Luoran Shang, Ke Xu, Peter J. Lu, Alireza Abbaspourrad, Yuanjin Zhao, David A. Weitz
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
SignificanceColloidal particles can adsorb nearly irreversibly at a fluid interface and stabilize fluid drops. When particle-stabilized drops flow in a channel, viscous forces cause these particles to jam and form an elastic shell on the trailing side of the drop, albeit only under compression and not tension. The size of the elastic region depends on the balance of shear forces that drive the particles and diffusion that randomizes them. The flow causes the drops to deform and the elastic shell induces different shapes, which can wrinkle and invaginate, ultimately forming core–shell structures. This behavior highlights the unexpected consequences of asymmetry in the surface-particle forces and provides a unique method to controllably create droplets with a vast array of shapes.

Emulsion droplets of one fluid in a continuous phase of a different fluid can serve as containers for efficient encapsulation, mixing, separation, and reaction of fluids (1–6). Each emulsion droplet is typically spherical due to the interfacial tension between the droplet phase and the continuous fluid. However, the shape of a droplet can be altered by distorting forces that compete with the surface tension. For example, droplet distortion can be induced by geometric packing, buoyancy, hydrodynamic shear, or an electric field (7–11). In almost all cases, the interface of each droplet is covered with surfactant. The major role of these surfactants is to stabilize the droplets (12); however, they also typically reduce the interfacial tension, making it easier to deform the shape of the droplet (13). In addition, if the droplet is subjected to flow, the viscous forces on its interface can cause the surfactant to flow, inducing a gradient in the surfactant concentration (9). This results in a corresponding gradient in the interfacial tension, leading to Marangoni forces that compete with the viscous forces; this can also lead to large deformations of the droplet shape as it flows (14). For example, when a surfactant-stabilized droplet flows in a constrained channel, the interfacial flow of the droplet causes redistribution of the surfactant, generating a surface tension gradient leading to significant modification of the droplet shape (9, 15).

An alternative method of stabilizing droplets is through the use of a layer of colloidal particles adsorbed on its surface, resulting in what is known as a Pickering emulsion (16–20). Particles stabilize the droplets, yet the mechanics of particle-loaded interfaces can be intrinsically different from those stabilized with molecular surfactants (21, 22). Unlike surfactant molecules, particles rarely desorb from the interface because the energy that binds them to the surface is typically many orders of magnitude greater than kBT, the thermal energy, where kB is Boltzmann’s constant and T is the temperature (23). In addition, if the particles are sufficiently highly packed, they can jam together; this makes the interface elastic, albeit only under compression and not tension (24–27). This asymmetry in the surface properties can potentially alter the shape of the droplet, leading to arrested deformations including stretched slender droplets, droplet doublets, aggregates of droplets, and chains of droplets (24, 28, 29). Moreover, if the droplets are flowing, there can be a gradient of particle concentration. Because the stabilization behavior of colloidal particles is so different than that of molecular surfactants, the consequences of this concentration gradient can be significantly different. However, the effect of fluid flow on particle-stabilized droplets has not been explored, and the types of structures that can form under these conditions are not known. Of particular interest would be the effects of solidification of the interface in regions with high particle concentration, and the consequences of the asymmetric elastic properties of the particles under compression as compared to tension. This can potentially lead to the formation of very different structures and shapes as the droplets are deformed under flow. However, the range of possible shapes of droplets due to such asymmetric surface property has not been investigated.

In this paper, we investigate the consequences of particle stabilization on the structure and properties of droplets subjected to flow. We show that particle-stabilized droplets flowing through a constricted channel undergo an array of dramatic shape deformations. The shear-induced convection and redistribution of the particles cause the particles to accumulate at the rear of the droplet interface. This leads to a partial jamming transition with increased particle density and local solidification of the droplet interface. The external fluid causes the elastic rear region of the droplet to wrinkle and eventually to invaginate. Consequently, the droplet evolves through a series of different shape deformations depending on the magnitude of the flow. This leads to surprising transformations of a simple liquid droplet with a particle-stabilized interface, resulting in a series of unexpected deformations. These deformations explain the behavior of particle-stabilized droplets under flow and provide an alternate means of modifying droplet shape and creating different types of structure.

Results

In this work, the pressure-driven flow of droplets through a suspending fluid in a constricted channel with a circular cross-section is studied. We conduct our experiments in a microcapillary device that creates drops of one fluid in a second (5). The device consists of nested glass capillaries (30–32). The injection capillary is cylindrical and is tapered to a tip with an inner diameter of 40 μm. It injects the inner fluid to form the drops. The collection capillary is also cylindrical, with an inner diameter of 200 μm; its length is 10 cm, which serves as the region where the flow is constricted. The drops are imaged in this long collection tube. Coaxial alignment of the two capillaries is enabled by inserting each of them in a square capillary with an inner dimension of 1.05 mm and an outer dimension of 1.50 mm. The inner dimension of the square capillary matches the outer diameter of each of the two cylindrical capillaries, which is 1 mm. This allows the two capillaries to be concentrically aligned with the tip of the inner capillary partially inserted into the outer capillary to control the drop making, as shown in Fig. 1A.

The axisymmetric geometry of the device allows the inner and outer fluids to flow through the inner and outer tubes, respectively, to produce drops in a coflow regime. The inner fluid is an aqueous solution of poly (ethylene glycol) diacrylate (PEGDA, Mn = 700) with a concentration of 40% (v/v). The outer fluid is a pure monomer of trimethylolpropane ethoxylate triacrylate (ETPTA, Mn = 428). The densities of the inner and outer fluids are 1.0852 g/ml and 1.11 g/mL, and their viscosities at room temperature are 0.01 PaS and 0.07 PaS, respectively. The interfacial tension between the two liquids is 2.625 mN/m.

As we flow the solutions through the tubes, micrometer-sized droplets are continuously generated at the tip of the inner tube and then flow in the outer fluid through the restricted tube formed by the collection capillary. We study the shape of the droplets as a function of the flow rate of the outer fluid, F2, while keeping the flow rate of the inner fluid, F1, constant at 0.25 ml/h. At the beginning of the experiment when F2 = 0.2 ml/h, the droplets appear approximately ellipsoidal in shape with an avergage major axis length of 306 μm with a coefficient of variation (C.V.) of 2.8% (n = 100). As we increase F2, the shape of the droplets begins to change, featuring sharper front and flatter back surfaces, relative to the flow direction (SI Appendix, Fig. S1). Due to the micrometer-scale dimension of the channel, the effects of inertia and buoyancy can be neglected. Thus, deformation of a droplet depends mainly on the competing effects of the hydrodynamic forces which distort the shape of the drop, and the effect of the interfacial tension which provides a restoring force. As a benchmark, we first investigate the shapes of the drops in the absence of any surfactant whatsoever. As F2 increases, a stronger pressure gradient is established along the outer tube, and the droplet deforms with different curvatures across its surface, which generates a Laplace pressure gradient that is directed toward the back of the droplet and balances the pressure drop across the drop due to hydrodynamic effects. Eventually, the droplet takes on a bullet-like shape with a hemispherical back to support the maximum pressure difference, as shown in SI Appendix, Fig. S1. We expect that an even higher F2 would cause the droplet to be ruptured and even collapse, as has been predicted when a droplet experiences an extremely large shear force (33–35). However, by further increasing F2, we find the size of the droplets decreases until a threshold flow rate value of 2.5 ml/h is reached, resulting in the inner fluid undergoing a jetting transition that narrows and forms a thin stream, which eventually breaks into tiny little droplets.

To explore the deformations of the flowing drops when they are stabilized by colloidal particles, we add silica particles to the outer fluid at a concentration of 17.73% v/v. The silica particles have a diameter of 250 nm and a density of 1.8 g/ml, and the density of the suspension is 1.1788 g/ml. The suspension exhibits shear thinning, with the viscosity, η, initially decreasing from 0.14 Pa s and then reaching a plateau value of 0.08 Pa s as the shear rate, γ˙, increases from 100 to 103 s−1 (SI Appendix, Fig. S2). Silicon hydroxyl groups on the particle surfaces result in a zeta potential of −24.2 mV in water.

We maintain the flow rate of the inner fluid constant at F1 = 0.25 ml/hr and track the shape of the droplets as they flow down the confining capillary as we vary the flow rate of the outer fluid. We observe a series of dramatic deformations. They all begin by the invagination of the back of the flowing droplet, with the interface becoming concave in shape. When the outer flow rate is F2 = 1.0 ml/h, the droplets undergo an unusual “self-swallowing” deformation that begins with an axial elongation of the droplet followed by a change from an ellipsoid into a bullet shape. The droplet then shows a surprising tendency for inward curling, with an indentation formed at its rear surface. The indentation soon grows into a more pronounced cavity or invagination, as shown in the schematics and the corresponding time-lapse images in Fig. 1B. The whole process is shown in Movie S1.

When the outer flow rate is increased further, we observe different kinds of droplet deformation. At F2 = 1.2 ml/h, the cavity at the rear extends deeper into the droplet, with the outer fluid appearing to form a thin jet which penetrates into the droplet along its axis; as a result, the droplet becomes flatter at the leading edge, making the droplet appear as a plunger (Fig. 1C and Movie S2). Surprisingly, this deformation nearly doubles the surface area of the droplet. At an even higher outer fluid flow rate of F2 = 1.35 ml/h, the center of the inner core becomes thinner as the drop advances, and eventually pinches off, leaving an internal cusp at the back and a smaller droplet of the outer fluid at the front tip, where a double emulsion is formed, creating a drop within a drop (Fig. 1D and Movie S3). This is highly unusual as the pinch-off process normally results from a liquid stream breaking into separate droplets due to the Rayleigh–Plateau instability (36), as is the case at high flow rates in the absence of colloidal particles. However, here the pinch-off occurs internally, which results in the formation of a double emulsion.

To determine the extent to which the droplet deformations are induced by their flow, we block the outlet of the microfluidic channel to stop the flow of both the inner and outer fluids and follow the response of a single, deformed droplet. After the flow is blocked, the droplet slows down, and the shape of the droplet continues to evolve to form different structures, based on the flow-induced distortions. For the “self-swallowing” droplet (Fig. 1B), the concave rear surface bounces back and the cavity decreases until it ultimately vanishes completely. As a result, the droplet morphs into a bullet-like shape, then an ellipsoidal shape, and, eventually, a very nearly spherical shape, as shown in Fig. 1E and Movie S4. Interestingly, by fitting the final form of the drop with a circle, we find a distinct deviation from a perfect sphere, as shown in Fig. 1E. For the “bulging-plunger” droplet (Fig. 1C), an even larger distortion occurs as the flow stops; the leading edge of the penetrating jet merges with the outer flow and the droplet becomes fully perforated along the transverse axis. The front rim shrinks and moves back relative to the droplet, forming an asymmetric “toroidal” shape (Fig. 1F and Movie S5). For the “internal-pinch-off” droplet (Fig. 1D), the smaller internal drop snaps off from the fluid that form the invagination and seems to initially from a separate drop right at the leading edge of the droplet. However, rather than snapping off from the droplet, the internal drop instead moves from the tip inward, toward the back of the original drop where it stays (Fig. 1G and Movie S6).

These distortions of the drops occur only when the outer fluid contains the silica particles, suggesting that these particles are essential in driving the pronounced shape distortions observed. We hypothesize that the mechanism causing the invagination is the flow-induced redistribution of the particles at the back surface of the droplet, which leads to “jamming” of the particles, as is displayed schematically in Fig. 2. Upon generation of the droplets at the outlet of the tapered capillary, the interface immediately begins to be covered with colloidal particles, as shown in Fig. 2A. Due to the high energy barrier for desorption, the particles remain trapped at the droplet interface. Initially, they do not completely cover the interface and can therefore flow along it. Because the droplet is confined in the collection capillary, shear-induced convection drives the particles toward the back of the droplet, where they begin to accumulate and become partially jammed at the back, as shown in Fig. 2B. This causes the interface to become elastic in the region where the particles jam, reminiscent of the jamming fronts observed in arrested coalescence of Pickering emulsions and dense colloidal suspensions under impact (37, 38). The detailed microstructure of the particle packing at the droplet surface can be influenced by the local curvature (37, 39). As more particles continue to be forced into the jammed region, its size grows. Moreover, the force due to the fluid flow induces a compressive force on the elastic cap on the back of the drop, where the particles have jammed. This compressive force induces wrinkling of the droplet surface, leading to the invagination of the drop, as shown in Fig. 2C. This invagination increases the total surface area of the drop (Fig. 2, iv) and reduces the particle density; as a result, the particles are no longer jammed; instead, the invagination grows, further increasing the total surface area of the drop and further increasing the flow of the particles (Fig. 2, v).

To confirm the flow-induced particle motion on the droplet surface, we probe the inner fluid flow of a single droplet and the outer fluid flow surrounding it at the invagination transition with the aid of tracer beads (9.9 μm diameter). By tracking the displacement of individual tracers over time, we visualize the direction of the inner and outer flow relative to the moving droplet. Our results indicate that the droplet moves faster than the outer liquid in the vicinity of the interface. As a result, the motion of the tracers in the outer fluid effectively move from the front of the drop to the rear in steady flow lines (Movie S7). The fluid inside the drop moves from the front to the rear near the interface, but to retain continuity, it circulates and moves from the rear to the front in the middle of the drop. This sets up a vortex-like flow within the drop as shown in Fig. 2B and in Movie S8.

To further elucidate the flow, we measure the trajectories of a single tracer inside the droplet before, upon, and after the invagination transition of the interface. Before the transition, the path of the tracer forms a closed loop following the circulatory fluid flow within the droplet (Fig. 2 C, i and SI Appendix, Fig. S3). At the transition, a single tracer near the rear also follows the circulation path but no longer returns to its starting point, and instead begins to circulate somewhat before reaching the rear of the droplet (Fig. 2 C, ii and SI Appendix, Fig. S4). After the transition, the circulatory motion is restricted to the front of the droplet, while the tracers near the rear are immobilized (Fig. 2 C, iii and SI Appendix, Fig. S5). These results suggest that the invagination transition is accompanied by a loss of continuity in the internal fluid flow in the rear of the droplet due to the formation of the stagnant particle shell at the back surface.

We speculate that stopping the flow at the back surface of the droplet results from the jamming of particles in this region. If this is the case, an elastic “shell” would form at the back surface of the droplet and remain stationary, in the same location, even if the flow stops completely. To test this hypothesis, we examine the relaxation of the shape of the “self-swallowing” droplets when the flows of the inner and outer fluids completely stop. The droplet returns to a spherical shape as expected for a fluid surface, as shown in Fig. 3A and highlighted by the red outline in the lower figure. However, there is also a faint image of a small “cap” that remains stationary at the rear of the droplet, as seen at the back of the droplet in the upper figure of Fig. 3A and highlighted by the white detail in the lower figure. This observation suggests that the particles at the back surface of the droplet are jammed as a result of their nonuniform distribution.

The inset in (B) is a cross-sectional view that shows the crevice in the front part of the solidified droplet, and the inset in (C) is a top view of the crevice in the back, concave region of the solidified droplet. To further test this hypothesis, we characterize the particle distribution at the droplet surface after the invagination transition. Since both the inner and outer fluid phases are acrylate-based monomers, we add a photoinitiator to each phase and irradiate with UV light to initiate in situ polymerization of the two phases. We then remove the solidified liquids from the capillary and cut them lengthwise, or perpendicular to the solid column, to look at the droplet surface using by imaging with a scanning electron microscope (SEM). The inner volume of the droplets shrinks, causing them to delaminate from the particle shell, as shown in Fig. 3B. We therefore image the particles on the inner surface of the continuous fluid. The particles at the front of the droplet are much more sparsely packed than at the back where they exhibit a multilayer packing with a much higher density as seen by comparing Fig. 3B (front) and Fig. 3C (back). These results confirm the existence of a flow-induced accumulation of the particles at the rear surface of the droplet.

The particle motion is driven by shear-induced convection, which transports particles from the front to the back surface of the droplets, generating a concentration gradient. By contrast, Brownian diffusion drives particles from higher to lower concentrations, which would have the effect of dissipating any concentration gradient. Thus, the redistribution of the particles should depend on the balance of these two counteracting effects, which can be compared through the Péclet number Pe. For spherical, noninteracting Brownian particles, Pe = 6πη γ˙ r3/kBT, where kB is the Boltzmann energy, γ˙ is the shear rate, r is the particle radius, and η is the viscosity. This expression is modified slightly for particles at an interface (40), with the viscosity being that of the more viscous, outer fluid, and the numerical coefficients varying slightly. If this balance of effects controls the behavior of the particles, the droplet invagination transition should be a function of both the shear rate and the particle size as expressed in the Peclet number. To elucidate this functional relationship, we generate droplets using silica particles of different sizes (100 nm, 250 nm, 500 nm, and 1,000 nm in diameter) but with the same concentration. We find that the invagination transition of the droplets occurs in all cases (Movie S9). We then characterize the invagination transition by a state diagram that is a function of γ˙ and r, as shown in Fig. 4. Given the “no-slip” assumption and the continuity of velocity along the droplet surface, γ˙ is measured as the velocity of the droplet divided by the gap between the droplet and the inner wall of the glass capillary. The viscosity, η, is measured by a rheometer (SI Appendix, Fig. S6). For each particle size, we find that transition from the state lacking invagination (black squares) to the invagination state (blue triangles) occurs at a critical value of γ˙, denoted by the red arrows in Fig. 4.

The state diagram of the invagination transition of the droplets as a function of γ˙ and r. The red arrows depict the critical value of γ˙ at the invagination transition of the droplet for each particle size. The black square and blue triangle represent the noninvagination and invagination states, respectively.

We measure Pe at the invagination transition as a function of r and plot the data in a log–log plot. The values measured experimentally for Pe (black squares) are well described by a linear behavior as shown by the line in Fig. 5. The slope of the fit in the logarithmic plot is three, indicating that the measured Pe varies with the cube of the particle radius in excellent accord with our expectation. Since the particles are negatively charged, we further investigate whether the particle motion is also affected by electrostatic interactions between their surface charges. We add sodium chloride to the inner fluid to shield electrostatic interactions in the particle-stabilized droplets. However, regardless of the salt concentration, we observe the same invagination transition behavior of the droplets (Movie S10). Moreover, the values of Pe at the invagination transition vary with the cube of the particle radius independent of salt concentration, as shown in Fig. 5. This confirms the absence of electrostatic interactions between the particles. These results support our hypothesis that the invagination of the drops is controlled by a balance of shear forces that drive the particles to the back of the drop and Brownian motion which resists this. For droplets covered with 500 m particles, wrinkles are formed at the rear surface under relatively high γ˙, and these wrinkles are not reversed even when the flow stops (SI Appendix, Fig. S7 and Movie S11), suggesting the formation of an elastic layer.

Log–log plot of the Peclet number, Pe, at the invagination transition of the droplet as function of radius, r. The black line is a fit to the data, yielding a slope of three, as expected for the Peclet number.

Discussion

In this study, we find that droplets of a simple liquid with a particle-stabilized surface exhibit a variety of unique deformations in response to a surrounding hydrodynamic flow. All these deformations start with an invagination transition at the back surface of the droplet. We show that this transition results from the migration of the surface particles due to the hydrodynamic shear on the flowing droplets; this leads to a particle jamming transition and the formation of an elastic shell at the back of the droplet. We confirm the shear-induced redistribution of the particles by SEM and characterize the invagination transition of the droplet by a state diagram where the shear rate is plotted against particle size. The particle motion is characterized by the Péclet number, comparing shear-induced convection to diffusion. This study shows that flow in a constrained geometry leads to significant effects for a particle-stabilized droplet, including immobilizing regions of the droplet’s surface, changing the liquid–liquid boundary condition, and modifying the droplet’s internal flow pattern. These effects contribute to very large distortions of the droplet that have not been previously observed. We anticipate these findings will inspire technical innovations in microreaction, separation, and encapsulation applications using similar droplets, the fabrication of double Pickering emulsions and microparticles with unusual morphologies, as well as other industrial processes involving colloid particle migration, such as ultrafiltration and enrichment.

Materials and Methods

Materials.

Poly (ethylene glycol) diacrylate (Mw 700, PEGDA), Poly (ethylene glycol) diacrylate (Mw 428, ETPTA), 2-hydroxy-2-methylpropiophenone (97%), and Sodium chloride (≥99.9%, NaCl) are purchased from Sigma-Aldrich and used as received. Silica nanoparticles (ÅngströmSphere™ silica spheres, 100 nm, 250 nm, 500 nm, and 1 μm diameter) are purchased from Fiber Optic Center, Inc. and used as received. Tracer beads (polystyrene beads, 9.9 μm diameter) are purchased from Duke Scientific Corporation and used as received.

Microfluidic Device Fabrication.

A microfluidic device consisting of nested glass capillaries (Cat. No. 1B100-6; World Precision Instruments, Inc.) is assembled on a glass slide. The left cylindrical capillary has an inner diameter of 580 μm and is tapered using a micropipette puller (P-97; Sutter Instrument, Inc.) with an inner diameter of the tip of 40 μm. The right cylindrical capillary has an inner diameter of 200 μm and is 10 cm long. Coaxial alignment of the two capillaries is ensured by inserting each of them in a square capillary with an inner dimension of 1.05 mm and an outer dimension of 1.50 mm (Atlantic International Technologies Inc). The inner dimension of the square capillary matches the outer diameter of each of the two cylindrical capillaries, which is 1 mm. Needles (Type 304; McMaster Carr) are attached to the capillaries to serve as fluid inlets. A two-component epoxy glue (Devcon 5 min Epoxy) is applied to seal all capillary junctions and to fix the capillaries onto a glass slide.

Droplet Generation and Imaging of Droplet Deformation.

The left capillary of the microfluidic device serves as the injection capillary, injecting the inner fluid. The right capillary serves as the collection capillary and the region with the constricted flow. To form colloid-free droplets, the inner fluid is an aqueous solution of PEGDA (40% v/v) and the outer fluid is ETPTA. To form colloid-stabilized droplets, silica colloids are added to the outer fluid at a concentration of 17.73% v/v. We use two syringes (1 ml, SGE Analytical Science) to load the inner and outer fluids and connect them to the dispensing needles with polyethylene tubing (0.86 mm inner diameter, Scientific Commodities, Inc.). The syringes are mounted on syringe pumps (Harvard Apparatus). As we operate the pumps to flow the solutions through the tubes, micrometer-sized droplets are continuously generated at the tip of the inner tube and then flow through the outer fluid through the restricted tube formed by the collection capillary. The flow rates of the inner and outer fluids are controlled by the syringe pumps. The drops are imaged with a fast camera (Phantom V9.0) using a Leica inverted optical microscope. To generate droplets containing salts, sodium chloride is added at various concentrations. To generate photopolymerizable droplets, 2-hydroxy-2-methylpropiophenone is added as an initiator to the inner fluid at a concentration of 1% v/v.

Fluid Flow Tracking.

Tracer beads are added to the inner or outer fluid at a concentration of 1 wt%. The tracers move along with the droplets flowing in the microfluidic channel and are imaged with the fast camera. The displacement of individual tracers over a certain time is determined by overlapping two images. The direction of the inner and outer flow relative to the moving droplet is determined by subtracting the displacement of the droplet.

Scanning Electron Microcopy Characterization.

We use a field emission scanning electron microscope (FESEM, Zeiss Ultra Plus) to characterize the surface and internal structures of the solidified droplets. The samples are washed 3 times using DI water and then deposited on a silicon wafer. After that, the samples are freeze-dried using a lyophilizer (FD-1D-50, Biocool) for 12 h. Before SEM imaging, the samples are coated with 5-nm-thick gold using a sputter coating machine. After that, we operate the SEM instrument to image the solidified drops.
