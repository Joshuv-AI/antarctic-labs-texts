Malaria in pregnancy: a holistic exploration of synergistic association through environmental, sociocultural, and socioeconomic lenses in Pru east municipality, Ghana
Authors: Dennis Bardoe, Daniel Hayford, Robert Bagngmen Bio, Ernest Osei
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
BackgroundMalaria in pregnancy (MiP) is responsible for several maternal and neonatal complications. Despite the renewed dedication towards elimination and eradication, it is still endemic in Ghana. Although previous studies have examined MiP from clinical or epidemiological standpoints, gaps remain in understanding the interactions between determinants of transmission. This study examined the synergistic association between environmental, sociocultural, and socioeconomic determinants of MiP.MethodsA cross-sectional study was conducted among 456 pregnant women in the Pru East Municipality from April to August 2024. Data were collected using serological screening and a structured, closed-ended questionnaire and were analysed with STATA 17 and IBM SPSS AMOS 29. Descriptive statistics, chi-square tests, logistic regression, confirmatory factor analysis (CFA) and structural equation modelling (SEM) were employed as part of statistical analyses. CFA and SEM were employed to validate multidimensional latent constructs and assess their interdependent and synergistic association within a single analytical framework. A significant level was set at (p < 0.05) at a 95% confidence interval (CI).ResultsOverall, the prevalence of MiP was 21.5% (95% CI 17.7–25.3), with Plasmodium falciparum (14.7%) being the most prevalent species. A co-infection by Plasmodium falciparum and Plasmodium malariae was also found at 3.5%. High Plasmodium parasitaemia was observed in 6.4% of infected women. Moreover, all factor loadings for the three determinants showed significance (p < 0.001) and a high internal consistency. The SEM also yielded good fitness (NFI 0.998, GFI 0.994, AGFI 0.996, CFI 0.984, RMSEA 0.048). Key environmental (proximity to stagnant water, refuse sites, Volta Lake, livestock shelters), sociocultural (spiritual attribution, illness confusion, plant repellents, outdoor sleeping), and socioeconomic (rural residence, delayed ANC initiation, limited media exposure, LLIN use, IPTp-SP uptake, health insurance) factors were strongly associated with MiP. Importantly, the structural model showed that these three domains were moderately to strongly correlated, confirming their synergistic influence on MiP.ConclusionMalaria control strategies should go beyond biomedical interventions to incorporate community-based environmental management, sociocultural engagement, and socioeconomic empowerment. Such a multidimensional approach is essential to achieving sustainable reductions in malaria burden among vulnerable populations and improving health outcomes.

Background

Malaria in pregnancy (MiP) persists as a significant global public health problem caused by the protozoan of the Plasmodium genus [1]. Despite renewed commitments and interventions for malaria elimination in recent years, the disease continues to cause morbidity, mortality, and substantial economic losses [2]. Five species, Plasmodium falciparum, Plasmodium vivax, Plasmodium ovale, Plasmodium malariae, and Plasmodium knowlesi, are considered genuine human parasites due to their exclusive dependence on humans as natural intermediate hosts [3]. Among them, P. falciparum is the most infectious and predominant species in sub-Saharan Africa [4]. During pregnancy, infected erythrocytes bind to chondroitin sulphate A (CSA) on placental syncytiotrophoblasts, causing inflammation and disrupting nutrient and oxygen transfer to the fetus [5]. The pathogenesis of MiP involves both maternal immune modulation and parasite sequestration, resulting in placental malaria with monocyte accumulation, syncytial knotting, and fibrin deposition [6]. These changes impair placental function and lead to maternal and neonatal complications, including maternal anaemia, intrauterine growth restriction (IUGR), miscarriage, stillbirth, preterm delivery, low birth weight (LBW), and neonatal mortality [7]. Primigravidae are particularly vulnerable due to a lack of immunity against CSA-binding parasite variants [8].

Globally, malaria remains endemic in 85 countries, with an estimated 263 million cases and 597,000 deaths in 2023 [4]. The incidence in 2023 was higher than in 2022, when 58.6 cases per 1000 population at risk were reported [4]. In the WHO African Region, 12.4 million of 36 million pregnant women in 33 moderate-to-high-transmission countries were infected [4]. To reduce the burden of MiP, WHO recommends three core interventions, including the use of long-lasting insecticidal nets (LLINs), intermittent preventive treatment with sulfadoxine-pyrimethamine (IPTp-SP), and effective case management [9]. Ghana has adopted these strategies as part of routine antenatal care (ANC). By 2021, LLIN distribution had exceeded the 80% target, reaching over 90% of pregnant women and young children [10]. Also, about 80% of pregnant women had received at least two doses of IPTp-SP, though only 61.6% achieved the optimal 3 + recommended doses [11]. Despite these efforts, coverage and compliance remain suboptimal in rural areas due to persistent barriers [12]. Consequently, recent evidence points to the increasing prevalence of MiP, contributing to adverse maternal and neonatal outcomes [12, 13].

Although previous studies have explored MiP from clinical and epidemiological perspectives, limited evidence exists on how environmental, sociocultural, and socioeconomic factors interact to influence its transmission in Ghana. This gap is particularly critical in rural settings, where community-level behaviours, cultural beliefs, and structural barriers strongly shape preventive practices and health-seeking behaviours. To address this, the present study integrates environmental, sociocultural, and socioeconomic determinants within a single analytical framework to assess their synergistic association with MiP transmission in the Pru East Municipality. Beyond informing the local epidemiological profile, this study explores under-investigated predictors of malaria and provides practical insights to guide policymakers, public health stakeholders, and researchers in designing context-specific interventions for rural Ghana.

Methods

Study area and context

The study was conducted in the Pru East Municipality, Ghana (Fig. 1), which lies between 0°30′W and 10°26′W and latitudes 7°50′N and 8°22′N [14]. The municipality has a population of 101,545, comprising 51,087 males and 50,458 females [14]. Its topography is generally plain and undulating, with elevations ranging from 60 to over 300 m above sea level [15]. The area has a dendritic drainage pattern, with the Volta Lake, the Pru River, and several tributaries flowing through its western parts [15]. The climate is tropical continental (interior savanna), with a major rainy season from July to October and a minor one from April to June [15]. Annual rainfall ranges between 1400 and 1800 mm, while mean temperatures vary from 24 °C to 30 °C, peaking at 40 °C in March and April [15]. Atmospheric humidity fluctuates between 70% in the dry season and 80% in the rainy months [15]. These environmental conditions promote the formation of stagnant water bodies, which serve as sites for sporogony and mosquito breeding [16]. Combined with limited healthcare access and prevailing sociocultural and socioeconomic challenges, they make this rural municipality a critical context for examining the determinants of malaria in pregnancy (MiP).Fig. 1Bono East Regional Map showing the study area

Design, population, and duration of the study

A cross-sectional study was conducted among pregnant women in the Pru East Municipality from April to August 2024.

Eligibility criteria

Inclusion

The study included consenting pregnant women who lived in the Pru East Municipality at the time of the study and received ANC services at St. Matthias Catholic Hospital, Yeji.

Exclusion

The study excluded pregnant women who were not dwellers of the Pru East Municipality and those who did not attend antenatal care (ANC) services at St. Matthias Catholic Hospital, Yeji. Likewise, it excluded those who consented to participate but later opted out due to discomfort, occupational commitments, and time constraints.

Sampling procedure

A multistage sampling method was used. As part of selecting sampling points, the Bono East region was clustered into eleven (11) and stratified into seven (6) districts and five (5) municipalities. Pru East Municipality was selected from the municipality strata. Furthermore, St. Matthias Catholic Hospital is located in Yeji; the municipality’s capital was purposely selected. Thus, it remains the primary health facility that provides essential antenatal care (ANC) services to registered pregnant women within the municipality and beyond. The second sampling stage involved the serial selection of participants. Consented pregnant women were randomly selected until their numbers were proportional to the estimated sample size.

Enrolment of participants

After an initial examination to determine eligibility, those who consented were approached to discuss the study’s objectives, risks, protection against discomfort, and the right to withdraw from the study. Afterwards, those WHO agreed to participate in the study signed or thumb-printed a written consent form to affirm their engagement. Of the 475 estimated pregnant women, only 456 consented to participate in the study. Nineteen (19) of the pregnant women did not participate for various reasons, including discomfort, time constraints, and occupational commitment.

Variables of interest

Outcome variable: The outcome variable was the malaria status of pregnant women at the time of data collection.

Main explanatory variables: The main explanatory variables were the environmental, sociocultural, and socioeconomic determinants of MiP.

Data collection methods

Data for this study were collected using serological screening and a structured, closed-ended questionnaire.

Questionnaire validity and reliability

The close-ended questionnaire was based on the model questionnaires of the Ghana Demographic and Health Survey 2022, adapted to suit the population and health issues relevant to the study area. A thorough validation process was performed to ensure the reliability and validity of the questionnaire. The first draft of the questionnaire was submitted to experts in epidemiological studies, WHO assessed each question for appropriateness. Feedback from the experts was used as a guide to refine the questionnaire. In addition, the questionnaire was cognitively pre-tested with a small group of 10 pregnant women from Kintampo South District Hospital, Jema, who provided feedback for any unclear or difficult questions they encountered. To verify the relationship among interrelated variables, a reliability test using Cronbach’s alpha was used. Cronbach’s alpha [α] was 0.814, which also confirmed good reliability for all variables.

Serological screening, identification, and estimation of parasitaemia of Plasmodium species

In this study, malaria diagnosis was conducted exclusively by microscopy, which remains the gold standard for identification and quantification of Plasmodium species [13]. At the laboratory, the participant’s finger was cleansed with an alcohol-moistened swab and was punctured with a disposable blood lancet. Thick blood films were prepared on a glass slide using 10 μL of blood and evenly spread to cover an area of 15 \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\times$$\end{document}× 15 mm. Each slide was properly labelled to indicate the corresponding sampled individual. The smears were air-dried and fixed in 70% methanol for 30 s. The smear was stained with 10% Giemsa for 15 min and then examined under oil immersion (X100) using a binocular light microscope. Asexual parasite densities were estimated by counting the number of parasites per 200 white blood cells (WBCs) in the thick film. A sample was considered negative if no parasite was counted after 200 high-power fields were read [18]. If there were inconsistencies in the reading of the slide (positive or negative, or a 50% or more difference in parasite density), the senior microscopist’s reading was accepted as the true report. To uphold ethical standards, pregnant women who tested positive for malaria were promptly referred to the attending clinicians for immediate treatment following Ghana Health Service (GHS) and World Health Organization (WHO) guidelines for malaria in pregnancy. This ensured that no participant left the facility without appropriate management.

Data management and statistical analysis

As part of management, the dataset was rigorously checked for completeness before being analysed using STATA 17 (StataCorp, College Station, TX, USA) and IBM SPSS AMOS 29 (IBM, USA). Descriptive statistics were performed to obtain summaries of frequencies, percentages, and means with standard deviations for continuous variables. Pearson’s chi-Square tests for homogeneity of proportions were computed to ascertain significant differences in proportions among the categorical variables of interest. In addition, logistic regression analyses (bivariate and multivariate) were performed to identify variables significantly associated with malaria infection at 0.05 [95% Confidence Interval (CI)]. To avoid premature exclusion of potential confounders and effect modifiers, all variables with a p-value ≤ 0.25 in the bivariate logistic regression (Model I) were included in the multivariate logistic regression (Model II). The results are presented in the form of odds ratios (ORs), along with their 95% confidence intervals (CIs) and p-values, which indicate both the magnitude and statistical significance of the associations.

Structural equation modelling (SEM) was employed to examine the interrelationships among environmental, sociocultural, and socioeconomic factors influencing MiP. Latent constructs for environmental, sociocultural, and socioeconomic domains were developed from multiple observed indicators and modelled to predict the malaria infection status. The model also included covariance and correlation pathways to assess potential synergistic interactions between the constructs. Estimation was performed using maximum likelihood, and model fit was evaluated using standard indices, including the adjusted goodness of fit index (AGFI), Comparative Fit Index (CFI), Goodness of Fit Index (GFI), Normed Fit Index (NFI), and Root Mean Square Error of Approximation (RMSEA). This approach allowed us to assess direct, indirect, and interacting effects across the three domains to identify complex pathways influencing MiP.

Results

Sociodemographic and obstetric characteristics of pregnant women

Table 1 presents the sociodemographic and obstetric profiles of the 456 pregnant women WHO participated in the study. The mean age was 28.3 ± 3.53 (95% CI 27.98–28.63) years, and a significant majority (77.9%) were between 26 and 30 years. Most pregnant women were married (82.7%) and had received formal education (59.2%). Christianity was the predominant religious affiliation (64.9%), and a significant proportion of pregnant women were employed (91.2%). The most common occupations were farming (32.9%), trading (31.4%), and fish mongering (27%). Regarding income levels, 40.6% of participants earned above Ghȼ 1000 monthly. In terms of residence, most pregnant women resided in rural areas (66.4%). Extended family structures were more common (65.8%). Regarding obstetric history, 33.3% of pregnant women were primigravida, 81.4% were multiparous, and 52% were in their second trimester. Access to maternal healthcare services and media exposure

Maternal health service utilization and related factors were also examined (Table 1). The average number of ANC visits was 2.35 ± 1.6 (95% CI 2.26–2.55). Most pregnant women (45.6%) reported attending ANC between 4 and 7 times during their pregnancy. In addition, more than half (52%) began ANC visits during the second trimester. In terms of media exposure, 38.4% of them reported they accessed media at least once a week. Regarding IPTp-SP uptake, 55% of women had received it, whereas 39.9% were not due for uptake at the time of the study. Among those WHO had taken IPTp-SP, 43.6% had received two or fewer doses, whereas only 11.4% had taken the recommended three or more doses. Regarding LLIN ownership, 66.7% of pregnant women owned at least one net. Among those WHO owned the LLIN, 26.8% used the nets infrequently or improperly. Furthermore, a significant majority (67.1%) of the pregnant women perceived distance to health facilities as a notable barrier to accessing ANC services. Finally, 81.4% of the women reported having health insurance.

Prevalence of malaria in pregnancy (MiP), parasitic density, and Plasmodium species

The prevalence of malaria in pregnancy (MiP) was 21.5% (95% CI 17.7–25.3) (Table 2). Among those WHO were infected, 9.9% (95% CI 7.1–12.6) had high Plasmodium parasitaemia, 5.3% (95% CI 3.2–7.3) had moderate parasitaemia, and 6.4% (95% CI 4.1–8.6) had low parasitaemia. There was a statistically significant difference in the proportions of parasitaemia densities across the three categories (χ2 = 7.4, DF = 2, p = 0.025). The predominant Plasmodium species was P. falciparum [14.7% (95% CI 11.4–18.0)], followed by P.malariae [3.3% (95% CI 1.6–4.9)]. A co-infection of P.falciparum and P. malariae was also recorded [3.5% (95% CI 1.8–5.2)]. Environmental factors

The model fitting summary of the multivariate analysis (Model II) for the nine (9) explanatory variables [LR Chi2 (9) = 371.7, Prob > Chi2 = 0.000, Pseudo R2 = 0.7832] showed that the model was statistically significant (χ2 (9) = 371.7, p < 0.001). Similarly, the Pseudo R2 of 0.7832 also showed that the model explains approximately 78% of the variance in the outcome variable, highlighting a very good model fit. As presented in Table 3, pregnant women in close proximity to stagnant water had significantly higher odds of being infected with malaria compared to those who were farther from stagnant water (AOR 4.96, 95% CI 1.51–16.31, p = 0.008). Similarly, those who resided near such sites had more than eight times the odds of being infected with malaria than those who were farther from refuse dumping sites (AOR 8.24, 95% CI 2.43–13.91, p < 0.001). Furthermore, pregnant women WHO lived closer to Volta Lake had 53.76 times the odds of malaria compared to those living farther away (AOR 53.76, 95% CI 12.61–57.10, p < 0.001). Likewise, pregnant women who resided near drainage tunnels had significantly higher odds of being infected with malaria compared with those whose households were farther from drainage tunnels (AOR 74.43, 95% CI 17.39–81.00, p < 0.001). In addition, the odds of being infected with malaria were substantially higher among pregnant women who lived closer to livestock shelters compared to those whose households were farther from these shelters (AOR 51.05, 95% CI 30.85–57.66, p < 0.001). Also, pregnant women who resided in areas with poor road access to health facilities had approximately four times the odds of being infected with malaria compared to those who lived in areas with better access to roads (AOR 3.61, 95% CI 1.28–5.88, p = 0.007). Finally, households with windows that fit perfectly into walls had an 89% protective effect against MiP compared to those whose windows had poorly fitting windows (AOR 0.11, 95% CI 0.03–0.45, p = 0.002).

Sociocultural factors

In addition to environmental factors, some sociocultural determinants were found to be significantly associated with the risk of MiP (Table 3). The model fitting of the multivariate logistic regression assessing these sociocultural factors demonstrated a good fit [LR Chi2 (12) = 294.97, Prob > Chi2 = 0.000, Pseudo R2 = 0.6215] and was also statistically significant (χ2 (12) = 294.97, p < 0.001). Again, 62.2% of the variability in MiP was explained by sociocultural factors (Pseudo R2 = 0.6215). In model II, pregnant women WHO confused malaria with other febrile illnesses had 14 times the odds of MiP compared with those who correctly distinguished malaria from other illnesses (AOR 13.90, 95% CI 5.48–15.23, p < 0.001). In addition, pregnant women who perceived that pregnancy inherently makes them vulnerable to spiritual attacks had more than five times the odds of being infected with malaria compared to those who did not share this belief (AOR 5.14, 95% CI 1.64–7.15, p = 0.005). Moreover, those who believed that taking medications during pregnancy is harmful to the unborn child had higher odds of being infected with malaria relative to those who had no such concerns (AOR 1.18, 95% CI 1.13–2.42, p < 0.001).

Furthermore, pregnant women who stored water in media without proper seals or covers had more than twice the odds of being infected with malaria compared to those who adhered to best practices of water storage (AOR 2.66, 95% CI 1.17–4.01, p = 0.019). Moreover, the odds of being infected with malaria were significantly higher among pregnant women who slept outdoors or in poorly protected structures compared to those who slept indoors or in protected shelters (AOR 6.08, 95% CI 2.57–8.34, p < 0.001). Again, the odds of malaria were more than 15 times greater among pregnant women who shared sleeping rooms with other relatives compared to those who did not share living space with other persons (AOR 15.60, 95% CI 6.32–18.47, p < 0.001). Consequently, pregnant women who feared being stigmatized due to certain illnesses had nearly three times the odds of being infected with malaria compared to those who did not share such sentiment (AOR 2.77, 95% CI 1.13–3.87, p = 0.025). Finally, pregnant women who used local plants as mosquito repellents had more than eight times the odds of being infected with malaria compared with those who did not rely on local plant-based mosquito repellents (AOR 8.31, 95% CI 3.54–11.47, p < 0.001).

Socioeconomic factors

The logistic regression model assessing socioeconomic factors associated with MiP was statistically significant and demonstrated a good fit (χ2 (12) = 135.1, p < 0.001). The model also yielded a Pseudo R2 of 0.7315, signifying that approximately 73.2% of the variance in MiP was explained by socioeconomic factors. From Table 3, compared with pregnant women living in urban areas, those residing in rural settings had 1.63 times higher odds of being infected with malaria (AOR 1.63, 95% CI 1.34–2.19, p = 0.019). Furthermore, the odds of malaria infection were nearly two times higher among pregnant women who had begun antenatal care (ANC) in the second (AOR 1.95, 95% CI 1.15–3.12, p = 0.012) and third trimesters (AOR 1.74, 95% CI 1.23–2.68, p = 0.014) compared with those WHO initiated ANC in the first trimester. In addition, pregnant women who were exposed to media almost every day had 88% lower odds of malaria infection than those who were exposed to media less than once a week (AOR 0.12; 95% CI 0.10–0.43; p = 0.009). Similarly, those WHO were exposed to media at least once a week had 81% lower odds of being infected with malaria compared to those who were exposed less than once a week (AOR 0.19; 95% CI 0.13–0.57; p = 0.011). Moreover, pregnant women who considered distance from their households to the health facility as a major problem had significantly higher odds of MiP than those who did not consider distance to a health facility as a major problem for seeking ANC services (AOR 1.82; 95% CI 1.24–2.78; p = 0.005).

It was also revealed that pregnant women WHO owned LLINs had 67% lower odds of malaria infection compared with those who did not own LLINs (AOR 0.33; 95% CI 0.13–0.64; p < 0.001). Nevertheless, among pregnant women who owned LLINs, those who used them improperly or infrequently had almost two times the odds of malaria infection (AOR 1.99; 95% CI 1.19–3.47; p = 0.028). Similarly, those who did not own LLINs at all had nearly three times higher odds of MiP compared with those who owned and adhered to proper usage guidelines (AOR 2.86; 95% CI 1.83–4.21; p = 0.036). Besides, pregnant women WHO had not yet received any dose of IPTp-SP had 3.54 times the odds of being infected with malaria compared with those who adhered to the uptake (AOR 3.54; 95% CI 2.14–6.85; p = 0.021). Similarly, compared with those who adhered to the recommended optimal uptake of IPTp-SP (≥ 3), pregnant women who received less than or equal to two doses (≤ 2) had 1.27 times higher odds of MiP (AOR 1.27; 95% CI 1.14–2.56; p = 0.012). Finally, pregnant women with insurance were 75% less likely to be infected with malaria compared with those without health insurance (AOR 0.25; 95% CI 0.13–0.46; p < 0.001).

Model fit, covariance, and correlation among latent constructs

To further validate the fitness of the structural model (Fig. 2), model fit indices, covariances, and correlations between latent constructs were also examined (Table 5). First, the model fit indices NFI 0.998, GFI 0.994, AGFI 0.996, CFI 0.984, and RMSEA 0.048 (90% CI 0.036–0.074) all exceeded their respective threshold criteria (Table 5), yielding strong indications of a good model fit. Except for these, significant correlations (p < 0.001) were found when the covariance of latent factors was considered. The highest covariances occurred between socioeconomic and sociocultural groups (estimate = 1.372, C.R. = 10.311, p < 0.001), socioeconomic and environmental variables (estimate = 0.852, C.R. = 7.041, p < 0.001), and sociocultural and environmental variables (estimate = 0.806, C.R. = 8.167, p < 0.001). Furthermore, the correlation estimates revealed moderate to strong positive relationships among the three latent variables. Among all the constructs, the strongest correlation (r = 0.642) was found between socioeconomic and sociocultural determinants of MiP. This observation is contrary to the weak positive correlations found between sociocultural and environmental determinants (r = 0.459) and between socioeconomic and environmental determinants (r = 0.375). These indicate that changes in one domain are meaningfully related to variations in other domains, reinforcing the study’s premise of a synergistic association. Regarding the structural path to MiP, the model (Fig. 2) revealed that all three latent constructs showed statistically significant paths to MiP, although their strengths of association differed. In particular, environmental factors showed the strongest direct effect on MiP (β = 0.81), followed by sociocultural factors (β = 0.15). In contrast, the socioeconomic determinants exhibited a significantly weaker effect on MiP (β = 0.01). In a nutshell, the structural model (Fig. 2) revealed that there is a significant synergistic association between environmental, sociocultural, and socioeconomic determinants and also a positive influence on MiP. Discussion

Prevalence of malaria in pregnancy (MiP), parasitic density, and Plasmodium species

This study assessed the prevalence of malaria in pregnancy (MiP) and associated parasitaemia densities among pregnant women. It revealed a 21.5% prevalence of MiP, which is markedly higher than the national threshold of 8.6% [25]. Among the infected pregnant women, 9.9% (95% CI 7.1–12.6) had high Plasmodium parasitaemia. Plasmodium falciparum [14.7% (95% CI 11.4–18.0)] was also revealed as the predominant species among the infected pregnant women. A co-infection with P. falciparum and P. malariae was also observed among 3.5% of the infected pregnant women. Building on existing literature, the findings of this study are consistent with previous studies that reported a high prevalence of MiP ranging from 10.8% to 76% [13, 17, 26, 27]. The convergence of these findings with the present study underlines the persistent public health burden of MiP, despite the implementation of control strategies. More importantly, the elevated prevalence in this study suggests that current preventive interventions are either inconsistently implemented, insufficiently accessed, or suboptimally adhered to by pregnant women. Several contextual factors may explain the high prevalence observed in this study. Firstly, entomological and parasitological evidence in most settings within Ghana indicates the continued presence of competent Anopheles species capable of sustaining malaria transmission [28]. However, biological susceptibility alone does not fully account for the burden. Similar contextual factors such as coverage and compliance with key preventive interventions, including long-lasting insecticidal nets (LLINs) and intermittent preventive treatment with sulfadoxine-pyrimethamine (IPTp-SP), remain inconsistent across Ghana [12]. These challenges are further compounded by systemic barriers, including poor implementation of national malaria control guidelines and recurrent stockouts of essential drugs and commodities, which collectively weaken the overall impact of malaria prevention strategies [13]. In addition, the high parasitaemia density is in line with a previous study [29] and could be explained by immune modulation during pregnancy, which compromises maternal resistance to malaria parasites [13]. Similarly, P. falciparum revealed as the predominant species mirrors a known dominance of this specific species in most of sub-Saharan Africa (SSA) [26, 30], which could be due to its strong affinity for the placenta [31]. When considered as a whole, these findings not only reaffirm the persistent burden of MiP in Ghana but also highlight critical gaps in the delivery and uptake of recommended preventive strategies. Consequently, the relatively high prevalence of infection and parasitaemia density, particularly the predominance of P. falciparum, signals an urgent need to strengthen ANC-based malaria interventions, improve adherence to IPTp-SP, ensure uninterrupted LLIN supply, and address systemic challenges in program implementation.

Determinants of malaria in pregnancy (MiP)

Environmental determinants

The study further revealed that certain environmental and infrastructural conditions were significantly associated with increased odds of malaria in pregnancy (MiP). Specifically, living close to stagnant water bodies, refuse dumping sites, the Volta Lake, drainage tunnels, livestock shelters, and residing in areas with poor road access to health facilities were significantly associated with higher odds of MiP, and are in line with some earlier reports [13, 27, 32]. The underlying mechanisms for these observations are multifaceted and may be due to the role of these determinants in promoting mosquito breeding and increasing human-vector contacts [13]. For instance, areas around large water bodies such as the Volta Lake often have shallow and stagnant pools that serve as ideal breeding sites for Anopheles mosquitoes [13]. Similarly, refuse dump sites and poorly maintained drainage systems block drains and harbour different types of water-holding media, which may create microhabitats for the growth of mosquito larvae [13]. In addition, livestock shelters often provide a conducive resting and breeding ground for mosquitoes, especially when there is an accumulation of excretes and water [33]. Beyond breeding sites, infrastructural barriers such as poor road networks may indirectly heighten risk by limiting access to antenatal care (ANC), malaria testing, and preventive interventions, thereby compounding vulnerability to MiP [9]. Collectively, these conditions do not only increase mosquito densities, but support mosquito proliferation, intensify human-vector contact, amplifying the risk of infection. Conversely, the study found that pregnant women living in households with well-fitted windows had significantly lower odds of MiP. This protective effect is supported by earlier evidence [13, 34], suggesting that reducing entry points for mosquitoes helps lower indoor mosquito density and, by extension, the risk of malaria transmission [13].

Sociocultural determinants

Contrary to reports from previous studies whose focus was mainly on general cultural experiences [17, 35], the findings of this study revealed a higher odd of infection among pregnant women who confused malaria with other febrile illnesses, those who perceived pregnancy as a state of spiritual vulnerability, and those who feared being stigmatized due to certain illnesses during pregnancy, those who perceived that taking medications during pregnancy is harmful to the unborn child. The inability to distinguish malaria from other febrile illnesses may reflect gaps in health education, leading to poor health literacy and misattribution of common symptoms such as fever, chills, and body aches to less severe conditions. Consequently, delays in timely recognition of malaria can result in missed opportunities for early diagnosis and treatment [12]. Moreover, the perception that pregnant women are at risk of spiritual attacks is likely to shape how healthcare is sought. This perception may lead to social withdrawal or reduced interaction with formal health care and limit engagement with antenatal care (ANC) services. Instead, these pregnant women may prioritize spiritual alternatives or traditional healing, which may inadvertently undermine malaria control efforts and consequently result in higher odds of infection among those who hold such perceptions [36].

Furthermore, the fear of social stigma associated with certain illnesses, together with the belief that medications in pregnancy are harmful, may contribute to secrecy, self-medication, late ANC initiation, and non-compliance with prescribed interventions. These behaviours collectively foster delays in diagnosis and treatment, ultimately raising the odds of malaria infection [37].

Beyond cultural perceptions, this study also revealed increased odds of malaria among women who engaged in risky household practices such as storing water in uncovered containers, sleeping outdoors or in poorly protected structures, sharing sleeping rooms with others, and relying on local plants as mosquito repellents. These findings are consistent with previous studies linking such practices to higher malaria risk [13, 38, 39]. Poor water storage significantly contributes to the proliferation of mosquito larvae. This practice is mostly prevalent in peri-urban settings and could lead to an increased density of Anopheles mosquitoes, thereby increasing the transmission of malaria [38]. Likewise, sleeping in areas without adequate protection from mosquitoes exposes pregnant women to peak mosquito-biting hours [13], while, sharing living space may inherently lead to overcrowding [40]. These factors may, in turn, limit the proper use of LLINs, hinder the optimal installation of nets, or increase the local mosquito density indoors, thereby reducing the coverage and effectiveness of malaria control efforts [13]. In addition, traditional plant-based repellents are typically used in malaria-endemic areas. However, their efficacy is limited and poorly standardized [41]. This is because the variability in the preparation of plant-based repellents, their application, and the active ingredients may reduce their reliability [41]. This observation is consistent with a previous study showing that plant-based repellents provide a short-term knock-off effect on mosquitoes [41]. Therefore, pregnant women who rely solely on these repellents may not receive adequate protection during peak seasons of malaria transmission.

Socioeconomic determinants

In addition to the environmental and sociocultural determinants, study also identified several socioeconomic actors that significantly influenced the odds of MiP. Notably, pregnant women residing in rural areas were more likely to be infected compared to their urban counterparts, a finding consistent with reports from Northwest Ethiopia and Rwanda [27, 42]. This association may be explained by limited access to healthcare services, financial constraints, inadequate access to preventive interventions, educational disparities, and weak infrastructural conditions in rural settings [27, 42, 43]. Collectively, these barriers negatively influence health-seeking behaviour, thereby increasing the risk of malaria among pregnant women [13]. Another important determinant was the timing of ANC. Late initiation of ANC was associated with higher odds of malaria infection, corroborating earlier studies [44, 45]. Early initiation of ANC offers sufficient time for timely diagnosis and the completion of the recommended doses of IPTp-SP [44]. By contrast, delayed attendance limits access to essential interventions such as IPTp-SP, LLIN distribution, and targeted health education [45], which in turn elevates the risk of infection.

Moreover, exposure to mass media emerged as a protective factor. Women with frequent exposure to media had lower odds of malaria infection, a finding supported by previous studies [46]. This protective association may be linked to accessibility to a wealth of health information and resources related to malaria, its risk factors, complications, and intervention schedules [46, 47]. These are likely to reinforce pregnant women’s awareness, enhance their knowledge, and empower them to take proactive steps towards shaping social norms and addressing common misconceptions or fears about interventions [46]. Similarly, frequent exposure to mass media, especially the Internet, may provide pregnant women with an avenue to form an online peer group community with healthcare providers where preventive healthcare services during pregnancy are shared [48]. This could trigger their cues for actions needed for behaviour modification towards good health-seeking practices.

In addition, distance from the household to the health facility as a perceived barrier to seeking maternal healthcare services was associated with higher odds of malaria, which is also consistent with a previous report [49]. The aforementioned association could also be attributed to pregnant women’s drive to be proactive in health-seeking behaviour. These pregnant women may not prioritize maternal and neonatal safety during the limited opportunities they have to visit a health facility [50], thereby increasing the risk of infections.

Also, ownership of LLINs and health insurance coverage were associated with decreased odds of malaria infection. These observations are in line with reports from some existing literature [51, 52]. LLINs serve as a physical barrier against mosquitoes and reduce their frequent bites [12], thereby lowering the risk of malaria infection. In addition, health insurance coverage has been associated with improved access to maternal healthcare services [51]. Thus, pregnant women with health insurance are more likely to seek timely and appropriate care for illnesses, including malaria, thereby lowering the risk of infection.

Besides, non-ownership of LLINs, infrequent or improper usage of LLINs, non-uptake of IPTp-SP, and not adhering to optimal uptake of IPTp-SP were associated with higher odds of malaria infection. These findings are consistent with a series of previous studies that have also associated higher odds of MiP with these determinants [12, 52–54]. Non-ownership and improper usage have been reported to diminish the protective benefits of LLINs [53], increasing the frequency of mosquito bites and ultimately increasing the risk of infection. Again, as a preventive chemoprophylaxis, IPTp-SP protects pregnant women against malaria by clearing existing asymptomatic infections and preventing new infections [54]. Non-uptake or sub-optimal uptake could compromise the continuous protective effect of this prophylaxis, making these women susceptible to malaria infection.

Synergistic association between environmental, sociocultural, and socioeconomic determinants of malaria in pregnancy (MiP)

This study examined the relative contributions of environmental, sociocultural, and socioeconomic factors in predicting MiP. Among the three constructs, environmental factors emerged as the most significant predictor of MiP, with a standardized regression weight (β = 0.81). This finding is consistent with previous studies in sub-Saharan Africa, which reported that environmental factors in most rural and peri-urban areas such as stagnant water, poor drainage, and inadequate housing serve as primary drivers of malaria transmission [55, 56]. Moreover, the sociocultural determinants also showed a substantial influence on MiP (β = 0.15). This observation corroborates earlier reports linking cultural practices, beliefs, and behavioural norms to malaria risk during pregnancy [57, 58]. For instance, practices such as favouring outdoor nighttime activities or reluctance to use insecticide-treated nets could interact with environmental exposure to heighten vulnerability. By contrast, socioeconomic factors exhibited only a weak direct effect on MiP (β = 0.01), which is consistent with previous literature that has associated socioeconomic factors as drivers of MiP [43, 59]. Although this may suggest a limited role, the finding does not diminish the relevance of socioeconomic conditions in shaping malaria outcomes. Rather, their influence appears to operate indirectly by mediating or amplifying the effects of other domains. For example, poverty, limited education, and unemployment may not directly cause malaria infection but are likely to restrict access to preventive measures, hinder adherence to ANC schedules, or reinforce sociocultural misconceptions. This indirect role helps explain the relatively weaker standardized regression weight. Similar trends have been reported in other studies, where socioeconomic disadvantages exacerbated environmental exposures and cultural practices, thereby compounding vulnerability [43, 59]. Furthermore, the relative homogeneity of the study population, largely composed of low-income, rural households may have reduced variability in socioeconomic characteristics, leading to an underestimation of their contribution [60].

Beyond their individual effects, this study also revealed important interrelationships among the three domains. The positive correlation between sociocultural and environmental factors (r = 0.459) points to a feedback loop, where, for instance, pregnant women who practice unsafe waste disposal or prolonged outdoor activities may simultaneously be more exposed to conducive environmental conditions for mosquito breeding. This finding calls for behavioural change communication strategies that are sensitive to local norms and beliefs but still promote practices that reduce vector exposure. The strongest correlation was observed between socioeconomic and sociocultural factors (r = 0.642). This relationship suggests that economic factors indirectly fuel risky cultural or behavioural practices. This finding also echoes a broader public health foundation, namely, that poverty is not just a background factor but a structural driver of poor health outcomes, including malaria transmission [43]. Even modest improvements in socioeconomic conditions could yield meaningful benefits for MiP prevention by enhancing awareness, access, and agency among vulnerable populations.

In addition to the contributions of each domain, this study sought to uncover synergistic relationships among the three latent constructs. The significant covariances among the factors showed that these domains (environmental, socioeconomic, and sociocultural) are not isolated. Rather, they form a web of interdependence that sustains malaria vulnerability in pregnancy. For instance, distributing LLINs may be ineffective without simultaneous efforts to address cultural reluctance or lack of media-based health advocacy. These findings draw attention to the need for a comprehensive approach grounded in environmental, sociocultural, and socioeconomic underpinnings to improve maternal and neonatal health.

Recommendations for policy and practice

To contribute meaningfully to maternal and neonatal health, as well as to public health policy, practice, and research within and beyond the Pru East Municipality, the findings were translated into actionable recommendations. It is recommended that health stakeholders integrate behaviour change communication campaigns with existing environmental malaria management strategies. This is likely to address cultural barriers and societal misconceptions that may hinder malaria prevention efforts. Health policymakers and local health systems could develop culturally sensitive malaria education campaigns that explicitly acknowledge and respectfully address spiritual interpretations of illness. Embedding malaria prevention messages into religious and community platforms could increase acceptance of IPTp-SP, LLIN use, and timely ANC attendance. Such strategies may not only reduce misconceptions but also foster trust, making biomedical interventions more compatible with local sociocultural realities.

Again, consistent health advocacy through mass media and digital platforms is also likely to positively reinforce pregnant women’s health-seeking behaviour. Similarly, the Central Government, the Ministry of Health, the Ghana Health Service, and other stakeholders in health should enact, implement, or strengthen policies that address issues of non-access to maternal healthcare through health insurance, transportation support, and infrastructure development. This is likely to reduce socioeconomic barriers, compromising access to healthcare and adherence to preventive interventions. Finally, to sustain these recommendations, there is a need to foster multisectoral collaboration among all the stakeholders of health, sanitation, and education.

Study’s strengths and limitations

This study demonstrates several notable strengths that distinguish it from much of the existing literature on malaria in pregnancy (MiP). Unlike most prior studies that have focused primarily on clinical or epidemiological perspectives, the present analysis integrated environmental, sociocultural, and socioeconomic determinants within a single analytical framework to examine their synergistic association with MiP transmission. Theoretically, the study contributes to the existing body of knowledge by not only informing the local epidemiological profile of MiP but also highlighting under-investigated predictors of malaria transmission. Methodologically, the application of structural equation modelling (SEM) and confirmatory factor analysis (CFA) provided a robust assessment of latent constructs and their synergistic associations, an approach that is often overlooked in conventional regression-based analyses.

Despite these strengths, several limitations should be acknowledged. Although SEM and CFA were employed to provide a rigorous framework for understanding composite determinants of MiP, the cross-sectional design limits the ability to draw causal inferences. In addition, reliance on self-reported data introduces the possibility of response bias. Another limitation relates to the regression modelling strategy. Separate multivariate logistic regression models were performed for the three determinant domains to highlight their explanatory power in relation to MiP. While this stratified approach offered domain-specific clarity, it did not permit mutual adjustment across categories, which may have biased some effect estimates upward. Although SEM accounted for cross-domain overlaps by examining interrelationships, a combined logistic regression model could have identified the strongest independent predictors more directly.

In addition, the possibility of interviewer bias and social desirability bias cannot be ruled out, particularly for sensitive variables such as ownership and usage of long-lasting insecticidal nets (LLINs), where respondents may have overstated adherence to recommended practices. Furthermore, the study did not include entomological assessments to identify the local Anopheles species responsible for malaria transmission in the Pru East Municipality. Future research that integrates vector species profiling with community-level determinants would provide a more comprehensive understanding of malaria transmission dynamics in this setting. Finally, the findings are contextually grounded in the Pru East Municipality, which may limit their generalisability to other areas with different ecological and health system characteristics.

Conclusion

This study revealed 21.5% prevalence of malaria in pregnancy (MiP), which is substantially higher than the national prevalence of 8.6%. Plasmodium falciparum emerged as the predominant species, and most of the infected pregnant women presented with high parasitaemia. These findings reaffirm the persistent biological risk of malaria during pregnancy, highlighting the continued dominance of P. falciparum in sub-Saharan Africa. Beyond biological foundations, the study further identified several determinants of MiP. Environmental factors, such as proximity to mosquito breeding sites and poor housing conditions, were found to increase vulnerability. Sociocultural influences including misconceptions about malaria, fear of stigma, and reliance on traditional health beliefs also shaped health-seeking behaviours. In addition, socioeconomic barriers such as rural residence, financial hardship, and limited access to healthcare services contributed significantly to the risk of infection. Importantly, the findings point to a strong synergistic association among environmental, sociocultural, and socioeconomic determinants. Rather than operating in isolation, these factors interact to create a web of interdependence that sustains the vulnerability of pregnant women to malaria. This interconnectedness suggests that malaria control strategies targeting only one dimension are unlikely to yield optimal results. Given this complexity, there is a pressing need for a paradigm shift in malaria control efforts from isolated interventions to integrated, multisectoral approaches. Effective prevention of MiP must simultaneously address environmental, socioeconomic, and sociocultural drivers. The broader policy implications are equally important, as effort to reduce the burden of MiP should move beyond clinical interventions to encompass structural and behavioural change. An inclusive approach that integrates environmental management, community education, and social protection measures may be vital for improving maternal health. This perspective aligns with Ghana’s National Malaria Elimination Strategic Plan (NMESP) 2024–2028, which emphasizes integrated, community-based, and multisectoral strategies. Ultimately, translating these insights into action requires coordinated investments across health, housing, education, sanitation, and social protection sectors. By embedding MiP prevention within broader development policies, Ghana could not only accelerate progress toward malaria elimination but also strengthen maternal and neonatal health outcomes. In this way, tackling MiP becomes not merely a biomedical challenge but a development priority, demanding sustained political commitment and community-level engagement.
