Healthcare professionals’ understanding and current practice in sarcopenia screening and diagnosis in the UK: a qualitative study
Authors: Isobel Jacob, Theocharis Ispoglou, James Woodall
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Abstract AbstractObjectivesTo explore healthcare professionals’ (HCPs’) understanding and current practice in the screening and diagnosis of sarcopenia to help with the development of a standardised sarcopenia screening tool that can be implemented within the National Health Service (NHS) of the UK.DesignA qualitative study using semistructured interviews that were analysed using Braun and Clarke’s reflexive thematic analysis steps.SettingThe interviews were conducted online.ParticipantsTen HCPs (consultants, physiotherapists, community matrons, dieticians and community falls specialists) within acute or community NHS settings based in the West Yorkshire area. No prior knowledge of sarcopenia was necessary.ResultsFive key themes were identified including (1) sarcopenia as an unseen condition; (2) current practice for sarcopenia screening; (3) challenges of measurement; (4) knowledge gaps and awareness and (5) patient trust and engagement. Interviews revealed a lack of consistency of sarcopenia awareness and understanding across HCPs, no formal screening of sarcopenia and several barriers including time constraints, resource limitations, lack of access to equipment and insufficient education on sarcopenia and its clinical consequences.ConclusionsThere is a need for a standardised screening tool to be implemented within NHS care pathways to allow for the early assessment and early management of sarcopenia. The future screening tool should embed a clear process of the measurements to be taken (including identifying which HCPs are responsible), defined management pathways for patients identified as sarcopenic, and integrated education for HCPs to promote cross-skilling of HCPs and alleviate pressures across the NHS system.Keywords: Aging, Health, Health Education, GERIATRIC MEDICINE, PUBLIC HEALTH

Introduction

Sarcopenia is a musculoskeletal disease characterised by low muscle strength, reduced muscle mass and impaired physical performance.1 Despite growing recognition of its clinical importance, sarcopenia is inconsistently identified in UK healthcare practice.2 3 Contributing factors include variation in definitions and diagnostic criteria, the wide range of tools used to assess sarcopenia, limited knowledge and awareness of sarcopenia within the healthcare workforce, and variable access to diagnostic equipment.4 5 A recent scoping review highlighted limited knowledge and awareness of sarcopenia across patients, healthcare professionals (HCPs) and the public globally, and identified a ‘know-do’ gap in clinical practice, defined as the disconnect between knowledge of sarcopenia and its translation into routine assessment and management.4 Addressing these gaps is essential to improve the uptake of sarcopenia screening and diagnosis within the UK National Health Service (NHS), where care pathways and resource constraints differ from other healthcare systems.

Previous UK surveys indicate progress in sarcopenia assessment. In 2018, few organisations within the NHS reported using formal diagnostic algorithms, whereas more recent data demonstrate increased adoption of recognised screening and diagnostic algorithms, indicating progress towards greater standardisation in sarcopenia assessment.2 3 These surveys focused on HCPs providing care for older adults who are at risk of sarcopenia or living with sarcopenia. This focus is appropriate given that primary sarcopenia is closely associated with ageing.1 With approximately 11 million people aged 65 years and older in England,6 there is increasing emphasis on preventing and managing age-related conditions such as sarcopenia. This emphasis is justified given the condition’s association with serious adverse outcomes, including falls, hospitalisation, reduced quality of life and increased mortality.7 8

It is important to recognise that sarcopenia is not solely age-related; secondary sarcopenia can result from factors including physical inactivity, systemic disease and inadequate nutrition.1 In hospital settings, inadequate nutritional intake and protein–energy malnutrition are recognised contributors to muscle loss, particularly during periods of illness or reduced mobility.9 This is further supported by audit data from the NHS, which identified high rates of malnutrition risk and variable implementation of nutritional interventions in older patients undergoing emergency surgery, despite routine screening.10 Acute sarcopenia, defined as the rapid decline in muscle mass and function following injury or illness,11 has been associated with poorer recovery and increased risk of long-term functional decline.11 12 Separate hospital-based studies have also linked sarcopenia with prolonged hospitalisation and higher readmission rates.13 14 Given these implications, it is essential to examine HCPs’ current practices in screening and diagnosing sarcopenia across diverse professional roles and care settings within the NHS.

Significant differences in funding, resources and staffing exist between acute, community and outpatient settings. However, it remains unclear how frequently sarcopenia is screened and diagnosed across these environments, or whether barriers to identification differ by setting or clinical role. Existing survey-based evidence provides limited insight into how sarcopenia is understood and addressed in day-to-day practice, particularly across diverse healthcare contexts. Qualitative exploration of HCPs’ perspectives is therefore required to understand current practices, perceived barriers and opportunities for improvement across care pathways. Accordingly, the aim of this study was to explore HCPs’ understanding and current practice in the screening and diagnosis of sarcopenia across acute and community NHS settings. These insights are intended to inform the development of screening approaches that are feasible, acceptable and responsive to the practical realities of clinical care, including time constraints, staffing pressures and patient complexity.

Methods

Study design

This qualitative study employed semistructured interviews to explore HCPs’ understanding and current practices regarding sarcopenia screening and diagnosis within the Leeds area. Reporting of the study adhered to the Consolidated criteria for Reporting Qualitative research (COREQ) guidelines,15 a completed COREQ checklist is provided as online supplemental material.

Patient and public involvement

It was not appropriate or possible to involve patients or the public in the design, or conduct, or reporting, or dissemination plans of our research.

Recruitment

Participants were recruited via gatekeeper emails from a large teaching hospital trust and a community trust in West Yorkshire. Inclusion criteria were broad to capture diverse perspectives; no prior knowledge of sarcopenia was required. These inclusion criteria allowed for a diverse range of views to be considered and included, reflecting real-world practice and allowed for knowledge gaps to be identified, potentially impacted by the clinical roles and opportunities provided throughout HCPs careers. Participants were purposively sampled to capture a range of HCP roles across acute and community settings where sarcopenia may be encountered in practice. Recruitment was conducted over 4 months and continued until data saturation was achieved; this was determined when no new themes were identified from successive interviews. Data saturation was agreed within the research team based on comparing the initial themes from earlier interviews compared with the latter interviews. This was determined in the initial coding and initial theme development stage of the analysis. HCPs who expressed an interest in taking part were sent a participant information sheet. Following this, those who were willing to be interviewed signed a consent form. All participants’ personal data were stored securely in line with GDPR guidelines, and each participant was allocated a unique ID for anonymity purposes.

Data collection

Interviews were conducted online via MS Teams and lasted up to 1 hour. All participants agreed for the interviews to be recorded for data analysis purposes. Interviews took place at a time that suited the working patterns of the HCPs, ensuring that no patient care was affected.

All interviews were conducted by the principal investigator (PI) to ensure consistency in delivery. To ensure rigour throughout the data collection process, the research team met to discuss the data collection process, including the development of the semistructured interview guide. There was no set list of standardised questions; instead, interviews were semistructured around three key themes: current understanding of sarcopenia diagnosis; current practice for sarcopenia screening and diagnosis; and barriers to implementing sarcopenia diagnosis in practice. The interview topic guide (please see online supplemental file 2) was informed by existing literature and refined through discussion within the research team, drawing on both clinical and qualitative research expertise. This approach allowed the investigator to add additional questions and explore themes as they arose during the interviews. Additionally, working as a research team on the data collection process ensured that any potential bias from the PIs’ knowledge of sarcopenia was minimised. The flexible design enabled exploration of individual experiences and opinions and facilitated the collection of open-ended data, allowing the researcher to probe and adapt questions where appropriate.16

Data analysis

All interviews were transcribed using the software on MS Teams. Transcripts were reviewed, and any personal data were anonymised. The data analysis and representation of the findings was undertaken by the PI and JW who has comprehensive experience of qualitative data analysis. The PI completed all parts of the analysis processes including the initial coding and theme development process; JW contributed to the reviewing and refinement process of the themes relating to the codes, and the defining of the themes. Working as a team ensured rigour of the analysis and interpretation process, minimising the risk of bias.

An inductive, semantic reflexive thematic analysis was used to allow for reflexivity and the researchers’ knowledge and expertise being used as a central tool for rich, reflexive and interpretative analysis, rather than attempting to eliminate it as bias.17 The steps outlined by Braun and Clarke’s Reflexive Thematic Analysis17 process were undertaken, this involved: (1) familiarisation with the data to identify patterns and trends; (2) generating initial codes and applying these to the transcripts; (3) constructing themes by grouping related codes; (4) reviewing and refining themes in relation to the coded data and study aim and (5) defining and naming themes. During analysis, the researcher actively considered how their own background, experience, and assumptions might influence interpretation of the data, researcher knowledge is seen as a key strength of reflexive thematic analysis, allowing for nuances in language and terminology to be accurately interpreted. However, the team were mindful of potential bias, and therefore, the PI worked on the analysis with JW who has extensive expertise in qualitative data analysis but does not have extensive subject knowledge of sarcopenia. When analysing the data, the inclusion of HCPs with no prior awareness of sarcopenia was considered to ensure that themes were not derived as an outcome of the inclusion strategy instead of real-world system-wide findings.

COREQ compliance

The study followed COREQ recommendations for transparency in reporting qualitative research. Details regarding interviewer characteristics, sampling, setting, data collection and analysis are documented in the online supplemental material COREQ checklist.

Results

Participants

Due to the distribution of emails via gatekeepers across the NHS locations, it was not possible to ascertain how many HCPs were approached about the study. In total, 10 HCPs were interviewed as part of this study. Participants were recruited from a large teaching hospital trust and a community trust in West Yorkshire. The HCPs’ roles included: one colorectal surgeon, two elderly medicine consultants, one community matron (physiotherapist), one senior dietician, one clinical falls specialist, one falls pathway lead, two neighbourhood physiotherapists and one musculoskeletal qutpatient physiotherapist. No participants dropped out of the study.

Theme 1: sarcopenia as an unseen condition

HCPs’ understanding of sarcopenia varied and was often contingent on professional role. Geriatric consultants, for example, had a strong understanding, commensurate with their role, whereas HCPs working in community settings demonstrated less specific insight into the presentation of the condition. Participant 9 stated:

From a sarcopenia perspective, I think that it’s multifactorial. I think I’d be looking at things like muscle mass, grip strength, weight, I think maybe divide it into two sort of areas. So physically I’ll be looking at like physical parameters of sarcopenia. And then also probably be looking at biochemical parameters of sarcopenia. So things like albumin for example play a central role within sort of my surgical patients.

This was supported by participant 8 stating:

So I’d say sarcopenia, like the geriatrician is well aware of it but we’re not necessarily, it’s not necessarily at the forefront of our mind going oh, this patient has sarcopenia, this patient doesn’t.

It was acknowledged that understanding and confidence in relation to sarcopenia was premised on personal interest rather than being routinely embedded within practice:

I have more of an interest because of this issue that this partner practice which deals with people with problems with their small intestine, so firstly, they’ve frequently been in hospital for a long time and secondly, they will have lost nutrition so it’s an area which I am more familiar with, I would say, than many of my colleagues. [Participant 1]

Participants reported that there is no formal screening process or requirement within the NHS for sarcopenia and suggested that this may contribute to variation in understanding across HCPs:

There’s nothing structured that we do, and we should. And so just down to the individual medics to give a brief outline of how you go about things… [Participant 1]

One HCP stated the following when describing sarcopenia:

I refer to someone having reduced strength, like deconditioning, but I don’t so much say that I would often use that word in my practice. [Participant 6]

Frailty was described as being conceptually and practically easier for HCPs to discuss, with various tools available to screen patients, including the Rockwood scale used in community settings and the Electronic Frailty Index used in primary care. Frailty and sarcopenia were often confused and conflated; some, but not all, participants were able to differentiate between the two conditions:

They think they’ve got frail patients, which may be true, but possibly they’ve got sarcopenic patients who aren’t traditionally frail, and the evidence base of frailty and younger age isn’t particularly proven as far as I’m aware. You know, it’s frailty of old age. The scoring system is evidence-based when you’re over 65. [Participant 8]

Some HCPs recognised that the two conditions are often confused, stating:

I imagine it probably just gets a bit lost in that like umbrella term of frailty because there’s so much, there’s so many kinds of aspects to it that aren’t just kind of from my perspective like the mobility and falls and things. [Participant 4]

HCPs may miss patients who are at risk of, or are living with, sarcopenia if assessments rely on visual impressions of frailty. This was linked to confidence in differentiating between the two conditions. If HCPs are not confident in screening for sarcopenia, this may lead to misidentification of sarcopenia as not all ‘sarcopenic adults’ have obvious muscle mass atrophy, for example, adults living with obesity and post-menopausal women. Inconsistent, inaccurate methods may lead to variations in sarcopenia screening and management.

So frailty, people used to eyeball it, and I think as geriatricians we’re better now at not doing that, but it doesn’t mean everybody else isn’t doing that. [Participant 8]

Theme 2: current practice for sarcopenia screening

None of the HCPs reported using a formal diagnostic algorithm, such as the one proposed by the European Working Group on Sarcopenia in Older Persons (EWGSOP), to screen for or diagnose sarcopenia. For those HCPs who stated that they did screen for sarcopenia, measures of muscle strength and physical function were the most commonly cited tools. However, these measures were often taken as part of existing assessment, such as falls assessments, rather than as part of sarcopenia-specific assessment.

One participant suggested that sarcopenia may be identified indirectly through their current holistic and comprehensive assessment process.

So I guess we’d pick up when we’re assessing their strength again it’s in terms of sarcopenia that way as well, if there’s been like a deterioration in their normal activities. [Participant 6]

In clinical contexts, a myriad of assessment tools were available; however, the practicalities and feasibility of measuring strength and function in home or ward environments influenced the methods used:

You know, so obviously things like, so mobility, gait speed. I use gait speed very, just very simply, you know. And so the timed up and go test for example, I use that. We don’t, obviously you know within a clinical practice we don’t have things like grip strength etcetera, but I would test sort of muscle power for example, upper limb, lower limb, muscle strength, muscle power. [Participant 9]

It was acknowledged that some strength measures, such as the Oxford Scale, are subjective but were considered a useful starting point for further observation, especially in settings with limited resources.

It’s incredibly subjective, isn’t it? So I think probably one person, if somebody followed in your patient after they might grade them as something different. And I know that when we have students with us sometimes they’ll say oh, they’re so and so and you think well are they? And then some people use plus or minus and other people don’t use plus or minus. But it gives you an indication, doesn’t it, that this person could either resist me or not resist me. [Participant 10]

No formal measures of muscle mass were reported by participants either as part of current routine care or sarcopenia-specific screening:

I wouldn’t ever measure somebody’s muscle mass, like I would never do like an [routine] arm measurement or a thigh measurement or anything as part of normal [routine practice] [Participant 10]

Other HCPs referred to monitoring patients’ weight as part of sarcopenia screening and diagnosis, this was reported to be due to resource availability and weight being perceived as an ‘easy’ measure with limited equipment necessary and using medical history, as noted by one participant:

Weight is easy because if they came in a year ago, they maybe they’re, you know, 85 kilos and then now they’ve come in, they’re 60 kilos, so you know they’ve lost weight. [Participant 9]

Theme 3: challenges of measurement

The interviews revealed several factors that may act as barriers to the future implementation of sarcopenia screening within the NHS, with time constraints being reported as a major concern. The HCPs are already pressed for time within their current ward or community visits, and adding extra measurements would add to that limited time. This pressure may result in a balancing act for HCPs between what care is the most effective versus the most efficient, with one HCP stating:

Sadly, time is limited so we’ve got to kind of think about clinical efficiency just as much as clinical effectiveness. [Participant 7]

Additional barriers included the burden placed on patients and carers, particularly where assessments would require travel between departments:

Getting them to go for another test where they’ve got to, you know, go from home to a department and then back to home again is, is a bit more challenging. [Participant 1]

Access to appropriate equipment was also raised as a barrier, with participants highlighting issues relating to cost, availability, number of pieces of equipment required, portability, infection control and maintenance:

We need at least ten. How much do they cost? Who’s going to fund it? What do we do when they break? What do we do when so and so’s in isolation, you know, how are we going to share it or clean it? [Participant 8]

Human resources required to take the measurements was also highlighted as a barrier, with one HCP stating:

[measurements] involve a human being doing that and that is what we don’t have so many of. [Participant 1]

The lack of confidence and time may be a contributing factor to the previous themes regarding HCPs’ knowledge and current practice, especially if they have limited time to undertake additional measures and undertake further education/training around sarcopenia screening/diagnosis.

Measurements of muscle mass were consistently identified as the most challenging aspect of completing a full sarcopenia diagnosis. When the potential use of portable ultrasound was discussed, concerns were raised regarding training and confidence:

[strength and function measures] They’re much more easier things to incorporate into our assessment than the ultra, ultrasound I would say. And like you say, then there’s other factors that come with the ultrasound like the training and everything like that. I probably haven’t used an ultrasound machine for like 20 years. [Participant 6]

Participants emphasised that any future screening tool would need to be practical, accessible and easy to use.

It’s got to be something practical that’s easy for the clinician to do or to identify these patients. [Participant 8]

Theme 4: knowledge gaps and awareness

Participants reported limited confidence in screening for or diagnosing sarcopenia, which was attributed to several factors, including lack of prior education and the paucity of opportunities for professional development in this area:

So lack of knowledge is the first one. People just aren’t trained, at medical school at least, in knowledge about sarcopenia. It’s a relatively new concept, although unwell, frail, weak people have been recognised a long time by the medical profession. The actual making a diagnosis-, calling something a sarcopenic diagnosis or making that diagnosis isn’t something that will be taught at medical school even now. [Participant 1]

More fundamentally sarcopenia was not perceived to be a core part of participants’ training courses or qualifications which was, arguably, due to a greater focus on frailty rather than sarcopenia per se. Some participants suggested that attitudinal change was necessary to establish sarcopenia as a condition necessitating discreet focus and training for professionals:

It’s something about changing perception and what people think about sarcopenia and frailty and what it is. Because these perceived biases are so entrenched that it’s quite difficult to shake [Participant 9]

Theme 5: patient trust and engagement

Participants reported that patients would be receptive and willing to undergo additional measurements as part of the sarcopenia screening. This may be due to longer assessments making patients feel valued and respected, with one HCP stating:

[patients] quite often say oh, that was very thorough and things like that afterwards. In they say it in a positive way, as in they’re grateful that they’ve had such a thorough assessment sort of thing. So yeah, I’m sure most of them would be open to those extra bits [for sarcopenia screening]. [Participant 6]

Another reason for patients being receptive to additional testing may be due to patients trusting the HCPs due to their medical expertise, particularly in hospitals, with one HCP stating

If you say you’re going for a scan, then they go [patients]. You know, blood test. Yeah, OK, here’s my arm yet again. So people are pretty willing in hospital. [Participant 1]

It was also highlighted that patient interaction and motivation to treatment may improve if HCPs could clearly screen and diagnose for sarcopenia, as this would allow for them to educate the patients on causes of sarcopenia, which may help with patient understanding and ultimately adherence to the treatment.

People like a diagnosis, don’t they? You’ve got this, this means your muscles are really weak. But we can improve it by doing XYZ. But people like to know you’ve got this and but we can make it better by doing XYZ. So, I think there is some benefit from that point of view as well [Participant 10]

Discussion

Principal findings

This qualitative study explored HCPs’ understanding and current practices regarding sarcopenia screening and diagnosis. Findings revealed considerable variation in awareness and confidence among HCPs. No participants reported using formal diagnostic algorithms such as EWGSOP, and screening was often embedded within broader assessments (eg, falls pathways). Importantly, the findings suggest that sarcopenia is not consistently named or prioritised in routine clinical care, contributing to its relative diagnostic invisibility within everyday practice. Barriers included time constraints, resource limitations, lack of access to equipment and insufficient education on sarcopenia and its clinical consequences. Identifying these barriers and facilitators is essential for developing and implementing a future sarcopenia screening tool that reflects both evidence-based practice and the experiences of HCPs and patients. Finally, our findings revealed that not only is sarcopenia under-screened but it is also conceptually marginalised in practice and displaced by frailty frameworks; HCPs were more confident discussing frailty as a condition and the various frameworks available for frailty screening. This was in stark contrast to the HCPs’ current knowledge and practice of sarcopenia screening.

Comparison with existing literature

Our findings align with previous UK surveys2 3 which reported limited adoption of standardised diagnostic algorithms across NHS Trusts. Despite some progress reported in 2025,2 only 17 of 32 organisations reported using a recognised algorithm, suggesting that routine sarcopenia screening remains uncommon nationally. Both this study and Hurst et al’s work may be subject to response bias as participants likely had prior awareness or interest in sarcopenia. Consequently, current practice across England remains unclear, though it can be assumed that routine screening is uncommon. Similar to previous evidence,2 this study highlights a persistent ‘know-do’ gap, where awareness of sarcopenia does not consistently translate into clinical practice. The conflation of sarcopenia with frailty observed in our interviews echoes findings from Offord et al3 and Beaudart et al,8 underscoring the need for clearer differentiation in education and care pathways. By moving beyond survey-based approaches, this qualitative study illustrates how sarcopenia is understood and acted on in day-to-day clinical work, rather than solely whether guidelines are known.

The barriers identified in this study are not unique to the UK. Research from the Netherlands,18 Australia and New Zealand5 also report that a lack of knowledge among HCPs, limited availability of equipment and time constraints all negatively impact sarcopenia diagnosis. A survey conducted in the USA revealed that although many of the clinicians reported familiarity with the term sarcopenia, very few either used the term in their practice or used a formalised algorithm to diagnose sarcopenia.19 The researchers proposed that the presence of several sarcopenia diagnostic algorithms, alongside the absence of universal diagnostic criteria, may contribute to limited knowledge in HCPs and low uptake of formal diagnostic approaches.19

It is important to acknowledge that several widely used sarcopenia screening tools were developed primarily in older adults. Consequently, some measurements and threshold cut-off values may be less appropriate or sensitive in other clinical contexts, such as acute illness, perioperative care or populations with secondary sarcopenia. For example, evidence from hospital-based studies of acute sarcopenia demonstrates that clinically meaningful declines in muscle mass can occur during short hospital stays, even when measures such as handgrip strength or functional tests show little or no change.12 This does not indicate that current tools are flawed, but rather they were designed for different populations and clinical contexts than those encountered in contemporary practice. This misalignment between tool design and clinical reality may limit sensitivity in secondary sarcopenia contexts and contribute to under recognition in patients who do not fit traditional frailty-based profiles. Furthermore, threshold cut-off values, such as hand grip strength, may be less informative for certain populations, including adults living with sarcopenic obesity and postoperative patients. Relying solely on fixed thresholds, rather than also considering the magnitude of change at the individual level, may increase the risk of sarcopenia underdiagnosis in these groups.

Implications for practice and policy

The findings align with the principles outlined in NHS Fit for the Future,20 which emphasise prevention, integrated care and workforce flexibility. Embedding a standardised sarcopenia screening tool within community pathways supports these priorities by enabling early identification and management of sarcopenia and has the potential to reduce hospital admissions and improve patient outcomes. Importantly, the findings suggest that HCPs already adapt existing assessments pragmatically under constraint, indicating that future screening approaches should build on current practice rather than introduce entirely new processes. The approach promotes cross-skilling within neighbourhood teams, allowing a broader range of HCPs (including physiotherapists, nursing associates and pharmacists) to undertake screening. This flexibility may help alleviate workforce pressures and support more equitable access to screening across diverse settings.

The evidence from this study highlights variation in sarcopenia awareness and practice, with some key barriers identified. One potential strategy to improve sarcopenia awareness and screening could be the development of a standardised, practical approach embedded within NHS care pathways. A clear sequence of measurements (muscle strength, muscle mass, functional ability) including identifying which HCPs should lead the screening process.

Embedding these elements may improve implementation outcomes such as fidelity, adoption and acceptability.21 Additionally, appropriate resource planning is essential to address equipment availability and staffing constraints.

Embedding training and educational resources within a sarcopenia screening tool may help HCPs understand the importance of early detection and management. Evidence shows that sarcopenia is associated with increased hospital admissions,22 longer hospital stays, higher readmission rates, comorbidity development,23 reduced quality of life7 and poorer mental health outcomes.24 Robust evidence also supports the role of strength-based exercise25 26 and nutritional interventions25 27 28 in the prevention and management of sarcopenia. Positioning patient education as part of screening may also enhance engagement and adherence, reflecting the importance of trust identified in the findings. Equipping HCPs with this knowledge may increase confidence and acceptance of screening interventions, and improve the likelihood of successful implementation within NHS care pathways. Integrated education should be relevant to a wide range of HCPs, including healthcare assistants, physiotherapists, nursing associates and pharmacists, supporting cross-skilling within NHS neighbourhood teams and helping to alleviate pressures across services.

The findings also have implications for the NHS Long Term Plan.20 Sarcopenia screening tools should enable assessment to be undertaken by community-based HCPs, including neighbourhood teams and pharmacies. This approach supports the shift from hospital to community care and may reduce pressure on acute services. Early intervention is considered cost-effective, given the association of sarcopenia with increased length of stay and readmissions.8 Therefore, introducing sarcopenia screening within community settings, where there is a broader range of HCPs and wider reach to individuals, may increase opportunities for identification and assessment of sarcopenia earlier in the care pathway, prior to hospital admission or periods of illness associated with accelerated muscle loss.

Aligned with the plan’s prevention focus, sarcopenia screening should identify individuals at risk and provide education on nutrition and strength-based exercise to support prevention. Preventive strategies may further reduce hospital admissions, length of stay and associated health and social care costs, enabling individuals to maintain independence and return to daily activities. Embedding screening within community settings and promoting cross-skilling of HCPs may increase opportunities for early identification and patient education across a wider population.

Strengths and limitations

A key strength of this study is the qualitative exploration of HCPs’ perspectives across acute and community settings, providing insights into practical barriers and facilitators. To our knowledge, this is the first UK-based qualitative study to examine HCPs understanding and current practice of sarcopenia screening within the NHS, extending survey-based evidence. Although participants were drawn from a single geographical area, the study included a range of professional roles across acute and community settings and varying levels of clinical experience. The findings reinforce the need for more standardised approaches to sarcopenia screening within the NHS. Future research should focus on developing and testing screening tools and evaluating their cost-effectiveness as limited evidence currently exists regarding the economic impact of sarcopenia screening and management within NHS care pathways.

There are some limitations that should be acknowledged. Findings may be subject to response bias, as participants with prior interest in sarcopenia may have been more likely to engage in interviews and may have contributed towards the findings. However, perspectives were gathered from a breadth of HCP roles, some with prior knowledge and others with no prior interest/knowledge of sarcopenia. The recruitment strategy may have increased the risk of selection bias due to the investigator emailing gatekeepers and HCPs regarding the study. That said, none of the HCPs were known to the investigator or wider researcher team. To minimise the risk of selection bias, the investigator emailed gatekeepers across the NHS sites to distribute the study information instead of the investigator directly emailing HCPs. Additionally, for all interviews, the interview guide (online supplemental file 2) was followed. Furthermore, the study was conducted in a single geographical location, and practices may differ elsewhere in the UK. Further research should examine national trends and evaluate interventions designed to improve screening uptake. Due to the relatively small sample size, HCP roles and experiences were not revealed; it would be worthwhile to conduct a larger-scale version of this study with varying HCPs and experiences to explore if there are any knowledge gap trends.

Conclusions

This study identified considerable variation in awareness and understanding of sarcopenia among HCPs interviewed in this study. To address these variations in sarcopenia screening and diagnosis practice, further research should be conducted to determine if the development of a screening tool would improve HCPs’ knowledge and overall patient outcomes. Initial findings suggest that in order to develop a feasible tool that can be implemented within the NHS care pathways, the screening tools need to be practical, portable and cost-effective to address the resource, time, and financial barriers identified in this study. Future research should therefore focus on developing and testing the feasibility of integrated sarcopenia screening tools across acute and community NHS settings and across diverse clinical populations.
