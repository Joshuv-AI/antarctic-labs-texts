Intratympanic dexamethasone microcrystals/lidocaine‐loaded PLGA non‐spherical microparticles for local drug delivery to the inner ear
Authors: Dongcheng Wang, Xin Zhang, Zhen Wang, Cheng Ran, Jia Hao, Xiaobo Ma, Minghui Wang, Zhimin Zhou
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundSudden sensorineural hearing loss (SSNHL), often associated with tinnitus, significantly impacts individuals' quality of life. Current treatments, such as free drugs via intravenous or intratympanic (IT) administration of dexamethasone (DEX) and lidocaine, face limitations like low bioavailability and rapid drug clearance. To address these challenges, we developed a local co‐delivery system combining DEX microcrystals (DEX MCs) and lidocaine‐loaded poly(lactic‐co‐glycolic acid) (PLGA) non‐spherical microparticles (LPNMs) for sustained drug release in the inner ear.MethodsDEX MCs and LPNMs were prepared using the traditional precipitation technique and double emulsion‐solvent evaporation, respectively. After characterizing physicochemical properties and drug release kinetics, they were dispersed in sodium hyaluronate solution for IT injection, then in vivo pharmacokinetics and biocompatibility in guinea pigs were studied.ResultsDEX MCs exhibited stable dissolution, while LPNMs provided sustained lidocaine release, reducing potential side effects. In vivo studies in guinea pigs demonstrated prolonged drug retention in the perilymph and improved pharmacokinetics. Histological evaluation confirmed the good biocompatibility of this combined delivery system, with no significant inner ear damage observed.ConclusionThis co‐delivery system can be used as a depot for delivering both DEX and lidocaine to the inner ear and offers a promising approach for the synergistic treatment of SSNHL associated with tinnitus.Keywords: dexamethasone, inner ear delivery, lidocaine, microcrystals, non‐spherical particles

INTRODUCTION

Hearing loss has a serious impact on people's physical and mental health. Among various hearing problems, sudden sensorineural hearing loss (SSNHL) is growing in young populations and causes anxiety in sufferers, which seriously influences their professional performance. 1 , 2 Tinnitus and vertigo are often associated with SSNHL, further aggravating the patients' mental health and quality of life. 1 , 3 , 4 Currently, high‐dose corticosteroids such as dexamethasone (DEX), administered orally, are generally prescribed clinically for the treatment of SSNHL. 1 , 2 , 3 , 4 Intratympanic (IT) injection of DEX has also been proposed to alleviate subjective idiopathic tinnitus (SIT) or chronic tinnitus associated with sensorineural hearing loss. Nevertheless, local or systemic administration of lidocaine, an anesthetic and analgesic drug, is still most widely used for tinnitus treatment in the clinic. 4 , 5 , 6 , 7 , 8 , 9 More importantly, combined DEX and lidocaine, administrated by different routes, has been shown to prolong axillary brachial plexus blockade and also shows better therapeutic efficacy in the treatment of SSNHL associated with tinnitus or sole SIT. 10 , 11 , 12 However, most of the above‐mentioned cases are affected by the limitations of administering free drugs via intravenous or IT administration, such as repeated injections, low bioavailability and Eustachian tube drainage. 2 Therefore, local drug delivery systems have been developed which have the advantages of bypassing the blood‐labyrinth barrier and offering sustained release in the treatment of inner ear diseases. 13 , 14 , 15

To reduce the administration frequency and enhance the drug concentration in the inner ear, various drug delivery formats have been created including polymeric microspheres, microcrystals, microrobots, nanoparticles, hydrogel, implantable controlled release devices and traditional ventilation tubes. 9 , 15 , 16 , 17 , 18 , 19 Among them, DEX microcrystals (DEX MCs) have attracted extensive interest in the field of inner ear delivery due to the distinct advantages they offer compared with the raw counterparts and free drugs, such as easy surface modification and superior pharmacokinetic parameters. 2 , 15 In one study, lidocaine‐loaded poly(lactic‐co‐glycolic acid) (PLGA) microspheres were prepared by single emulsion‐solvent evaporation technique, and sustained delivery of lidocaine into the cochlea was observed, indicating potential application for the attenuation of peripheral tinnitus. 17 Nowadays, it is recognized that drug delivery systems based on non‐spherical particles exhibit distinct advantages in comparison with spherical counterparts, including escaping phagocytosis, specific intracellular transportation and long retention on the round window membrane (RWM). 2 , 20 , 21 Compared to anisotropic inorganic particles or drug crystal synthesis, it is not easy to prepare biodegradable and non‐spherical polymeric particles for biomedical purposes. 22 Previously, we developed a methodology based on the traditional emulsion‐solvent evaporation technique to prepare polylactide and PLGA non‐spherical particles assisted by small molecules such as epirubicin, glycerol, sodium tripolyphosphate or ammonium bicarbonate. 21 , 23 , 24 However, there are no reports of lidocaine encapsulated in biodegradable polymeric non‐spherical particles for tinnitus treatment through IT administration to the inner ear. Thus, based on current treatments involving IT injection of free DEX and lidocaine in the clinic, we aimed to construct a local co‐delivery system based on DEX microcrystals and lidocaine‐loaded PLGA non‐spherical microparticles to offer improved therapeutic efficacy and patient compliance in the treatment of SSNHL associated with tinnitus.

Currently, combined drug delivery systems required by clinic practice for treating various diseases have attracted great attention, although single drug formulations are still the mainstream method for systemic administration or local delivery. 15 , 18 , 25 In our previous reports, DEX and lipoic acid microcrystals were mixed together in aqueous solution or silk hydrogel to exert a synergistic anti‐inflammatory and antioxidant role in treating cisplatin‐induced inner ear injury. 15 , 26 This simple process can easily be adapted for use in the clinic. SSNHL associated with tinnitus differs from hearing loss alone, and so requires a co‐delivery system containing both DEX and lidocaine for simultaneous hearing recovery and tinnitus alleviation. 2 , 3 , 11 , 17 , 21 Thus, based on our previous studies, a combined local drug delivery system constructed from DEX MCs and lidocaine‐loaded PLGA non‐spherical particles (LPNMs) was proposed to investigate the potential synergistic effect for SSNHL associated with tinnitus. 21 , 26

Herein, we prepared optimized DEX MCs and LPNMs using the traditional precipitation technique and double emulsion‐solvent evaporation, respectively, according to our previous reports. 2 , 21 Subsequently, DEX MCs and LPNMs were mixed with sodium hyaluronate solution to obtain a co‐delivery system for the distinctive formulation properties and a longer duration time in the tympanic cavity assisted by sodium hyaluronate. 27 , 28 A series of physicochemical characterizations were then carried out. Finally, after IT administration in guinea pigs, the pharmacokinetics and biocompatibility of the co‐delivery system were investigated.

MATERIALS AND METHODS

Materials

Raw DEX was provided by Tianjin Tianyao Pharmaceutical Company (Tianjin, China). Poly(lactic‐co‐glycolic acid) (PLGA) (Mw. 50 000, 50:50) was purchased from Jinan Daigang Biomaterial Co., Ltd. (Shandong, P. R. China). Lidocaine and lidocaine hydrochloride were purchased from Shanghai Aladdin Biochemical Technology Co., Ltd. (Shanghai, China). Polyvinyl alcohol (PVA) (degree of hydrolysis 88%, degree of polymerization 500) was purchased from Sinopec Sichuan Vinylon Works (P.R. China). All other chemicals were analytical grade and purchased from commercial sources.

Preparation of DEX MCs and LPNMs

DEX MCs were prepared by a precipitation technique according to our previous report. 2 Briefly, raw DEX was dissolved in ethanol (10%, w/v), then the DEX solution was injected dropwise into 100 mL of a PVA aqueous solution (0.1%, w/v) under stirring at 10 800 rpm. Finally, the suspension was left to stand overnight to allow ethanol evaporation. The DEX MCs produced were collected by centrifugation and washed three times using ultrapure water for further use.

LPNMs were prepared by a modified double emulsion‐solvent evaporation technique. 21 Briefly, a specified amount of lidocaine and 200 mg of PLGA were dissolved in 8 mL of dichloromethane. Then 3 mL of NH4HCO3 solution (2%, w/v) was added to the solution, which was homogenized for 2 min to form the W1/O emulsion. After that, the primary emulsion was immediately poured into 100 mL of PVA aqueous solution (1%, w/v) containing the desired amount of lidocaine to re‐emulsify and the solution was stirred using a magnetic stirrer for 16 h for dichloromethane evaporation. Finally, the prepared LPNMs were collected by centrifugation after washing three times using ultrapure water, and then lyophilized. PLGA non‐spherical particles (PNMs) were prepared using a similar method as described above, except that lidocaine was not added. In order to improve the stability and injectability of DEX MCs and LPNMs, sodium hyaluronate solution (0.2%, w/v) instead of pure water was used as the dispersing medium. 2 , 27

In vitro drug release kinetics

Samples (0.3 mL) of sodium hyaluronate solution containing DEX MCs (5 mg/mL), LPNMs (10 mg/mL), raw lidocaine (2.15 mg/mL), or DEX MCs + LPNMs were placed into a dialysis bag (MWCO 8–14 k) with 2 mL of PBS release medium (pH = 7.4) containing 0.5% (w/v) SDS. Then the dialysis bag was suspended in 28 mL of the same medium. Afterwards, the samples were placed in a shaker and incubated at 100 rpm at 37℃ for 36 h. At 0.5, 1, 2, 4, 8, 12, 24, and 36 h, 1 mL of release medium was withdrawn from each sample and replenished with an equal amount of medium. The release of DEX and lidocaine was detected by HPLC (Waters e2695, USA) at 230 nm. The mobile phase consisted of 20 mmol/L disodium phosphate (pH = 7.45, adjusted with phosphoric acid) and acetonitrile (50: 50, v/v) at a flow rate of 1.0 mL/min. A standard curve was generated with solutions containing both DEX and lidocaine and used to calculate the concentrations of both in the samples. Good linearity was observed in the range of 1–200 μg/mL for DEX (R 2 = 0.9999) and 0.2–40 μg/mL for lidocaine (R 2 = 0.9990).

In vivo experiments

Animals and surgery

Healthy guinea pigs, 6–8 weeks old, weighing 250–350 g, were purchased from the Laboratory Animal Center, Institute of Radiation Medicine, Chinese Academy of Medical Sciences (Tianjin, China). All animal experiments were approved by the Animal Ethics and Welfare Committee of the Laboratory Animal Center, Institute of Radiation Medicine, Chinese Academy of Medical Sciences (approval number: IRM/2‐IACUC‐2411‐024). After anesthesia, a postauricular incision was made, and a 1‐mm‐diameter hole was drilled in the wall of the temporal bone. After injecting the samples with a syringe, the bone defect was closed with bone wax, then the incision was closed with sutures.

Distribution of DEX MCs and LPNMs on RWM

In order to observe the distribution of the samples on the RWM, the guinea pigs were euthanized and the bullae were removed 1 h after IT injection of DEX MCs and the other DEX MCs + LPNMs mixtures, and the bullae were vacuum‐dried for one week and then observed by SEM.

Pharmacokinetics study

IT injections (0.15 mL) of each of lidocaine hydrochloride (2%, w/v), DEX MCs (5 mg/mL), and DEX MCs (5 mg/mL) + LPNMs (10 mg/mL) were given to guinea pigs (totally 54 animals, 108 ears). At 0.5 h, 1 h, 2 h, 4 h, 8 h, 12 h, 1 d, 3 d, 5 d, an overdose of anesthesia was injected into the guinea pigs, and then the heads were quickly severed and the bullae were removed. The cochleae were gently wiped using a sterile cotton ball after exposing the cochleae to avoid contamination. Then, a 5 μL sample of perilymph was extracted from the cochlea for HPLC analysis, and stored at −20℃ if necessary. The chromatographic conditions were consistent with the in vitro release experiments. Good linearity was observed in the range of 0.5–100 μg/mL for DEX (R 2 = 0.9995) and 0.5–200 μg/mL for lidocaine (R 2 = 0.9999).

Biocompatibility assay

Five days after IT injection, the bullae of the samples were collected and immersed in 10% formalin. To visualize the cochlear hair cells by SEM, the cochleae were exposed and dehydrated with ethanol and isoamylacetate, then dried in a critical point dryer. The cochleae were decalcified in 5% HCl for 5 h, then dehydrated in ethanol, embedded in paraffin and sectioned for Hematoxylin–Eosin (HE) staining.

Statistical analysis

All statistical calculations were performed with IBM SPSS Statistics for Windows, version 27.0 (IBM Corp., Armonk, N.Y., USA). Data are presented as mean ± standard deviation (SD). Differences were analyzed by one‐way ANOVA, with a p‐value ≤0.05 considered significant.

RESULTS

Preparation of DEX MCs and LPNMs

Figure 1A shows the typical diamond‐shaped DEX MCs prepared by the precipitation method. The shape of the MCs was regular with side lengths of about 4.1–7.5 μm and thicknesses was about 0.4–0.6 μm, which is consistent with our previous reports. 2 , 15 Meanwhile, we prepared PLGA non‐spherical microparticles (PNMs) using a double emulsion‐solvent evaporation technique (Figure 1B). The size of PNMs was in the range of 112.9–224.7 μm and the surface was coarse, which is in accordance with our previous report. 21 After lidocaine loading, the size (98.4–231.7 μm) and the thickness (19.7–35.4 μm) of LPNMs were bigger than DEX MCs (Figure 1A,C). In addition, we observed that the surface of LPNMs was still coarse in comparison with PNMs (Figure 1B,D). DEX MCs adhered solidly to both the front and side of LPNMs (Figure 1E,F). In order to obtain a combined drug delivery system, we mixed LPNMs and DEX MCs in sodium hyaluronate solution and verified the stability of the suspension (Figure S1).

SEM images of (A) DEX MCs; (B) PNMs; (C, D) LPNMs; (E, F) DEX MCs + LPNMs. Arrows point to DEX MCs.

We further investigated the drug loading and encapsulation efficiency of LPNMs. Based on our previous report on the encapsulation of simvastatin in PNMs, 21 we first used the same procedures to prepare LPNMs in this study. The drug loading was in the range of 3.7% ± 0.1% to 5.6% ± 0.1% (Figure 2A). In addition, the encapsulation efficiency was from 12.6% ± 0.1% to 17.7% ± 0.3% (Figure 2B). By adding the desired amount of lidocaine into the PVA aqueous solution to reduce the concentration gradient between the oil phase and external aqueous phase, the drug loading and encapsulation efficiency were increased to 22.0% ± 0.9% and 83.8% ± 4.0%, respectively. 29 , 30

Drug loading (A) and encapsulation efficiency (B) (average ± standard deviation, n = 3) of different LPNMs with varying lidocaine concentrations (■, without lidocaine in PVA solution; □, with lidocaine in PVA solution).

FTIR was used to investigate the physical state of LPNMs and DEX MCs. As shown in Figure 3A, several characteristic bands of lidocaine were located at 3252 cm−1 (N—H stretch), 764 cm−1 (N—H bend), and 2804 cm−1 (C—H stretch), in accordance with previous literature. 31 The characteristic bands of dexamethasone were located at 3406 cm−1 (O—H stretch), 1708 cm−1 (C20=O stretch), and 891 cm−1 (Δ1,4‐4‐diene‐3‐ketone), which were in agreement with previous reports for DEX. 32 The typical absorption bands of PLGA were located at 2804 cm−1–3001 cm−1 (C—H stretch), 1756 cm−1 (C=O stretch), 1090, 1132, 1182 and 1273 cm−1 (=C—O stretch). 21 All of the above characteristic peaks appeared in the mixed group of DEX MCs + LPNMs, which indicated that the substance is composed from PLGA, DEX and lidocaine.

(A) IR spectra of (1) DEX MCs + LPNMs, (2) LPNMs, (3) PNMs, (4) DEX MCs, (5) Lidocaine; (B) XRD patterns of (1) DEX MCs + LPNMs, (2) DEX MCs, (3) Lidocaine, (4) LPNMs, (5) PLGA; (C) DSC thermograms of (1) PLGA, (2) LPNMs (with 5.1% Lidocaine), (3) LPNMs (with 22.0% Lidocaine); (D) Dissolution profiles (n = 3) of DEX MCs, DEX MCs in DEX MCs + LPNMs, raw lidocaine, lidocaine in LPNMs, and lidocaine in DEX MCs + LPNMs.

As shown in Figure 3B, the XRD pattern of DEX MCs shows the typical diffraction peaks at 7.54°, 14.28°, 15.18°, 15.74°, 16.96°, and 18.64°, which indicated it was Merck's B crystal form of DEX. 2 In addition, there are obvious diffraction peaks of raw lidocaine at 8.04°, 10.16°, 12.52°, 14.98°, and 16.1°; however, the LPNMs did not have any distinct diffraction peaks, similar to blank PLGA counterparts. In the case of DEX MCs + LPNMs, only the DEX diffraction peaks appeared, which confirmed the surface adsorption of DEX MCs to LPNMs (Figures 1E,F and 3B). DSC was employed to investigate the thermal properties of PNMs and LPNMs. As shown in Figure 3C, the glass transition temperature (T g) of PNMs was 46.9℃, and the T g values of LPNMs were 42.4℃ (drug loading: 5.6%) and 25.2℃ (drug loading: 22.0%), respectively.

In vitro release kinetics

As shown in Figure 3D, the dissolution profiles of DEX in both the DEX MCs group and the combined DEX MCs + LPNMs group showed a similar trend, with a rapid dissolution in the first 4 h and an almost completed dissolution at 24 h. On the other hand, raw lidocaine demonstrated a rapid dissolution profile over the first 8 h followed by a sustained rate until 36 h. The amount of lidocaine released in the LPNMs group and the DEX MCs + LPNMs combination group reached only 45.2% and 53.0% over 36 h, respectively. Through fitting the release kinetics to several models (Zero‐order, First‐order, Higuchi and Weibull), we found that the Weibull equations were the best fit for all five groups (Table S1). To further investigate the release mechanism, lidocaine release data were fitted to the Ritger‐Peppas model. As shown in Table S1, the release exponent (n) was calculated to be 0.2128 and 0.1999, indicating that lidocaine release in LPNMs was controlled by Fickian diffusion. 21

Distribution of drugs on the RWM

As shown in Figure 4A, we observed that DEX MCs were distributed uniformly and widely on the RWM without any agglomeration. In the case of the DEX MCs + LPNMs group, both LPNMs and MCs were also deposited on the round window niche. Although a small amount of MCs was scattered on the RWM indicated by white arrows, most of them were still on the surface of LPNMs after IT injection (Figure 4B).

Optical and SEM images of the distribution of DEX MCs (A) and DEX MCs + LPNMs (B) on the RWM at 1 h after IT injection. Arrows point to DEX MCs.

In vivo DEX and lidocaine concentrations in the perilymph

Figure 5 shows the pharmacokinetic profiles after IT injection of DEX MCs, free lidocaine hydrochloride, and DEX MCs + LPNMs. The pharmacokinetic parameters are summarized in Table S2. In both the DEX MCs and the combined groups (Figure 5A,D), the DEX concentration in the perilymph reached the highest level at 0.5 h, 175.4 ± 67.5 and 125.7 ± 43.8 μg/mL, and then gradually decreased, with the lowest concentration detected at 24 h (10.3 ± 6.4 μg/mL) and 120 h (1.2 ± 0.1 μg/mL), respectively. In the lidocaine hydrochloride group (Figure 5B), the lidocaine concentration in the perilymph at 0.5 h was 301.1 ± 166.0 μg/mL, then a sharp peak of 910.6 ± 142.2 μg/mL, about three times greater than the 0.5 h level, was observed at 1 h. The lowest concentration was detected at 8 h (114.9 ± 58.2 μg/mL). Though the lidocaine level in the combined group also reached a peak of 111.2 ± 49.8 μg/mL at 1 h, the change in concentration between 0.5 h (83.6 ± 35.6 μg/mL) and 1 h was apparently less abrupt (Figure 5C,E), and lidocaine was still detectable at 12 h in the combined group (4.4 ± 1.1 μg/mL).

DEX (A) and lidocaine (B, C) concentrations in perilymph after IT injection, and the corresponding histograms (D, E). Bars express mean ± SD, n = 4, ***p < 0.001.

Biocompatibility evaluation

Histological analysis showed that the stria vascularis (SV), organ of Corti (OC), and spiral ganglion cells (SGCs) maintained good morphology in all experimental groups, and no significant inflammatory response was observed (Figure 6). Cross‐sections of SV demonstrated well‐defined nuclei and dense striations, and there was no cell swelling into the endolymphatic space. The tunnels of Corti were clear and no degeneration or myelin shedding of the SGCs was observed. Moreover, the hair cells were observed by SEM, and both inner and outer hair cells were intact and not detached in all groups. The stereocilia bundles were ordered regularly (Figure 7). The results showed that DEX MCs and LPNMs did not cause significant histological changes in the inner ear and showed good biocompatibility. 2 , 15 , 26

DISCUSSION

Non‐spherical particle‐based drug delivery systems, including MCs and biodegradable polymeric particles, show great potential for inner ear delivery due to advantages such as feasible surface functionalization and superior pharmacokinetics. 2 , 20 In this study, we prepared diamond‐shaped DEX MCs and disc‐like LPNMs (Figure 1A,D), then mixed them in sodium hyaluronate solution to construct a combined drug delivery system. The non‐spherical morphology of LPNMs further verified our synthetic methodology for geometry control of polymeric particles via emulsion‐solvent evaporation in the presence of various small molecules. 21 , 23 , 24 The coarse surface of LPNMs is also conducive to adsorbing smaller particles and reducing the likelihood of detachment, helping to retain the DEX MCs by physical insertion and obstruction. This ensures excellent deposition of DEX MCs and enhances the combination of DEX MCs and LPNMs. As shown in Figure S1, sodium hyaluronate significantly improved the stability of the suspensions compared to pure water. In pure water, DEX MCs, LPNMs and DEX MCs + LPNMs showed noticeable precipitation after 45, 5 and 45 min, respectively, whereas in sodium hyaluronate solution, precipitation was delayed until 9 h, 45 min and 90 min. This enhanced stability is likely due to the increased viscosity of the sodium hyaluronate solution, 33 which helps to prevent microparticles from aggregating and precipitating, while promoting more uniform dispersion and interaction between DEX MCs and LPNMs. Thus DEX MCs + LPNMs system suspended in sodium hyaluronate solution demonstrates good dispersion and stability. This combination strategy successfully integrates two drug carriers, MCs and polymeric particles, in one system and provides a distinctive platform for the co‐delivery of DEX and lidocaine.

Considering the limited space within the tympanic cavity and the size of the LPNMs, it was necessary to optimize the drug loading of the LPNMs for IT injection. Using our previous procedures to load lidocaine instead of simvastatin, the drug loading was lower owing to the different physicochemical properties of both drugs, which may not be able to accommodate IT injections requiring large doses of medication. 21 Taking into account the different molecular structure of simvastatin and lidocaine, we noticed that the solubility of lidocaine can be improved in the presence of the surfactant PVA. 34 Thus, a large amount of lidocaine could escape from the oil phase to the external aqueous phase of PVA solution by diffusion during the emulsion formation and solidification processes. Based on this, we adjusted the procedure, adding an appropriate amount of lidocaine to the PVA solution in order to reduce diffusion from the oil to the aqueous phase, further optimizing the drug loading.

As the physicochemical characterizations showed, the obvious decrease in T g and the XRD pattern suggested that lidocaine acts as plasticizer dispersed in PLGA matrix molecularly. 21 , 35 Especially, the lower T g, as low as 25.2℃, of LPNMs in high drug loading (22.0%) indicated that LPNMs are in an easily deformable and highly elastic state at guinea pig or human body temperatures, which suggests further exploration in other applications based on shape‐switchable particles, in addition to the field of inner ear drug delivery. 2 , 36

The in vitro release profiles of DEX MCs were in accordance with our previous report, which indicated that DEX MCs exhibited a stable dissolution kinetic beyond any additional excipient including sodium hyaluronate, LPNMs or lipoic acid MCs. 26 The release profiles of lidocaine in LPNMs and DEX MCs + LPNMs combination group were slowed down significantly in comparison with raw lidocaine. Taking into account the dissolution release of DEX, lidocaine and lipoic acid from the crystal format or polymer encapsulation, we consider that DEX MCs exhibited more stable features than other drugs in drug delivery systems; however, DEX MCs can promote or suppress the dissolution or release profiles according to the basic physicochemical properties of different drugs. 10 , 26

Based on the above results, it was shown that the LPNMs have ideal drug loading, physicochemical properties and achieve sustained release in vitro. By carrying DEX MCs on the surface of LPNMs, like an aircraft carrier, we were able to construct a combined drug delivery system in which DEX was stably dissolved on the surface and lidocaine was slowly released from the interior, laying the foundation for subsequent in vivo experiments.

The RWM is a semipermeable membrane that separates the inner ear from the middle ear, and is in direct contact with the perilymph in the inner ear. It has been demonstrated that drugs can go into the perilymph via the RWM, so the distribution of the drug on the RWM directly affects the efficiency with which it enters the perilymph, which in turn affects the pharmacokinetics. Generally, free drugs are easily and rapidly cleared through the Eustachian tube along with the fluid, and obviously solid particle‐based drugs like drug MCs are more likely to stay on the RWM and its surrounding area. 2 , 13 In this study, the LPNMs we prepared have a disc‐like shape and a rough surface, which is favorable for drug MCs adhesion and RWM residence. In our previous report, the uniform distribution and prolonged residence on the RWM of DEX MCs were ascribed to the mucoadhesive property of the silk fibroin coating and the particles' diamond shape. 2 Similar to silk fibroin, sodium hyaluronate, a hydrophilic formulation agent, also was helpful in preventing DEX MC aggregation in addition to enabling convenient injectability. 27 The consistent appearance of MCs on the surface of non‐spherical particles suggested good stability of the DEX MCs + LPNMs group for inner ear delivery by IT injection (Figures 1E,F and 4B). Since the size of both DEX MCs and LPNMs was bigger than the permeability pores of RWM, this combined delivery system could serve as a drug depot for sustained release from the side of middle ear to the lymphatic system of inner ear. 37 , 38 Overall, the advantage of this proposed geometry‐controlled microcrystal/microparticle drug delivery system is that the prolonged residence of both DEX MCs and LPNMs on the RWM can greatly reduce administration frequency and drug loss through the Eustachian tube.

After IT injection into guinea pigs, we observed that DEX concentrations were detected up to 120 h in the combined DEX MCs + LPNMs group whereas in the DEX MCs group DEX concentrations were only detected for a short time – 24 h (Figure 5A,D). The prolonged drug delivery from the tympanic cavity to the inner ear can thus be attributed to the MCs on the surface of non‐spherical particles hindering the interaction between most of the MCs and RWM directly in comparison with DEX MCs alone. This affects drug dissolution and subsequent permeability into the perilymph through RWM (Figure 4). To our surprise, the highest concentration of DEX in this study was approximately 43 times higher than that of our previous report (4.1 ± 2.5 μg/mL) although administered at the same concentration of 5 mg/mL. 2 Such a high drug concentration in the perilymph may be attributed to the increased permeability of the RWM assisted by sodium hyaluronate solution, which facilitated the absorption of DEX further. 28 Regarding the lidocaine concentration (Figure 5B,C,E), there was an abrupt rise in the lidocaine hydrochloride group between 0.5 and 1 h, and it was suddenly undetectable after 8 h, indicating rapid clearance through the Eustachian tube. It was more moderate in the combined group; the lidocaine concentration gradually decreased until it was undetectable after 12 h, indicating that lidocaine in LPNMs was released slowly and continuously. In addition, the absorption C max/AUC of the combined group (0.2) was smaller than that of lidocaine hydrochloride group (0.3), implying that the absorption rate of lidocaine in LPNMs was lower than that of lidocaine hydrochloride (Table S2). Thus, compared to the rapid and sudden clearance in the free lidocaine hydrochloride group, LPNMs appeared to have an obvious sustained release profile due to the depot effect. Although the prolonged release of lidocaine from LPNMs was not significant in comparison with free lidocaine hydrochloride, the sustained delivery is helpful in avoiding the potential side effects on inner ear tissues induced by a sharp and high peak concentration in the perilymph.

In summary, we developed a co‐delivery system containing DEX MCs, LPNMs and sodium hyaluronate solution, which can be uniformly distributed and stably retained on the RWM of guinea pigs, and possesses sustained‐release properties in vitro and in vivo. This novel system can be used as a depot for delivering DEX and lidocaine from the tympanic cavity to the inner ear, providing an optional modality to synergistically treat SSNHL associated with tinnitus. Nevertheless, pharmacodynamic examinations are still needed to further determine the synergistic therapeutic effect of DEX and lidocaine. In addition, we still face many potential challenges in adjusting the system for clinical applications, such as reproducibility, the feasibility of large‐scale production, and the long‐term safety, which need to be addressed in future research.

CONCLUSION

DEX MCs and LPNMs were prepared using the traditional precipitation technique and the double emulsion‐solvent evaporation method, respectively. The involvement of sodium hyaluronate improved the injectability of the system and the permeability of the RWM. Compared to lidocaine hydrochloride alone, LPNMs may help to avoid potential side effects through their sustained release behavior. More importantly, this system could extend the duration of DEX in the perilymph of the inner ear, achieving a synergistic pharmaceutical effect in the combined delivery group. This co‐delivery system offers a potential method of application of dexamethasone and lidocaine in the synergistic treatment of SSNHL associated with tinnitus. Further studies will focus on in vivo pharmacodynamic examination.
