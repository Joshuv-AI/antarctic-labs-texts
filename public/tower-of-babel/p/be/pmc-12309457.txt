On the role of stems and prefixes in reading complex nonwords: Evidence from individuals with and without acquired dyslexia
Authors: Elisabeth Beyersmann, Tara Arrow, Ali Behzadnia, Simon Fischer-Baum
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTThe role of stems and prefixes in complex nonword reading was investigated in unimpaired readers and five individuals with acquired dyslexia. All participants completed a reading aloud task (and the reading impaired individuals also completed a repetition task) with four different types of nonwords: prefix + stem (refront), non-prefix + stem (tefront), prefix + non-stem (refrint), non-prefix + non-stem (tefrint); and prefixed and non-prefixed filler words. The unimpaired readers responded fastest to nonwords containing two morphemes (prefix + stem), slower to nonwords with one morpheme (non-prefix + stem; prefix + non-stem), and slowest in the non-morphemic control condition (non-prefix + non-stem), providing evidence for the added benefit of prefixes and stems during reading. The five reading impaired individuals showed facilitatory morpheme effects across both tasks, but stem-effects were more robust than affix-effects. There was no difference between the prefixed and non-prefixed words in any of the data. The impact of morphological structure on nonword reading and repetition points to the important role of morphemes across different modalities..

The aim of the current reading aloud study was to address one of the key challenges that current theories of reading face: the question of how readers extract morphemic information from print. Despite several decades of research into the role of morphemes in word production, the mechanisms by which morphologically complex words are processed in reading are still widely debated (Stevens & Plaut, 2022). While computational models of reading successfully capture patterns of performance for reading aloud mono-morphemic words, including models of mono-syllabic (Coltheart et al., 2001; Perry et al., 2007) and poly-syllabic reading (Mousikou et al., 2017; Perry et al., 2010), computational implementations of complex word reading are still sparse (Baayen et al., 2011). Embedding morphological processes into these reading models is not straightforward, and there are a number of open questions that need to be addressed, which include the stage of reading process at which letter sequences are segmented into morphemes, how this segmentation takes place, how different types of morphemes (e.g., stems vs. affixes) are processed, and how they are used by different reading processes to generate a spoken response. This study focuses on the question of how morphemic knowledge is used when reading aloud nonwords, with a particular focus on one type of morphemic structure – combinations of prefixes and stems.

The past decades of research into the morphology of reading have shown that, despite major differences in theoretical viewpoints, the predictions that arise from different models are often overlapping (Stevens & Plaut, 2022). One key challenge continues to be the localization of morpheme effects within the reading system. An ongoing debate in that regard has been between distributional and decompositional theories of morphological processing. Distributional theories are based on the assumption that morpheme effects are captured in the links between orthography and semantics, and as such, morphemes do not exist as units of representation (e.g., Gonnerman et al., 2007; Plaut & Gonnerman, 2000; Rueckl, 2010). Although this approach offers a great deal of flexibility and has found support in data derived from artificial neural networks, the presumed absence of morpheme representations makes it difficult to derive specific predictions regarding the localization of morpheme effects and to pinpoint mechanisms of morphological processing. In contrast to distributional morphology, decompositional accounts (beginning with the early works by Taft & Forster, 1975) have argued that explicit localist representations of morpheme units exist at an intermediate level between orthography and semantics. As summarized in one of Taft’s (2023) recent chapters, “Even if these mediating units are in reality distributed or can be statistically described, I believe it is more informative to refer to them in localist terms in order to envisage how the system works. […] A localist account allows us to trace through the steps involved in recognizing particular types of words, and make predictions about the relative difficulty in recognizing them based on their characteristics or their manner of presentation.” Following a similar kind of thinking, our goal was to use the predictions of current localist (i.e., decompositional) theories to further tease apart the cognitive mechanisms of complex word processing. Although we did not aim to adjudicate between distributional and decompositional frameworks, there are some aspects of our case-study data, such as morpheme substitutions, that are challenging to explain within distributional approaches, which we will further discuss below. Our primary goal was to build on a theoretical foundation that offered specific insights into the locus of morpheme effects in reading, by investigating complex nonword processing in a group of unimpaired and impaired readers of English.

Amongst the existing theories in the field, decompositional theories are the ones that have been most specific with respect to how and when morpheme-segmentation effects arise during reading, yet many aspects continue to be a matter of debate. They offer a window into the functional modularity of the reading system, which Coltheart (2001) described as one of the key assumptions of cognitive neuropsychology (for further discussion, see Rapp, 2001). The argument is that readers not only have access to the lexical representations of whole words, but also to the representations of morphemes, which enable the decomposition of complex words into morphemes, as well as the creation of novel complex words. There is growing consensus that access to whole words and morphemes tends to occur in parallel (e.g., Baayen et al., 1997; Beyersmann et al., 2012; Grainger & Beyersmann, 2017; Grainger & Ziegler, 2011; Schreuder & Baayen, 1995), where the relative importance of the whole word route compared to constituent-based processing has been shown to vary as a function of word frequency and word length (e.g., Baayen et al., 1997; Bertram et al., 2000; Beyersmann et al., 2018; Schreuder & Baayen, 1995). However, several major questions still exist, which are, as the current investigation will show, difficult to test and resolve within existing theories of morphological processing.

Does morphological decomposition occur at the level of the orthographic input, in the lexical-semantic systems, and/or at the level of phonological output? We will use cognitive neuropsychological data from five individuals with acquired dyslexia and two different tasks (reading aloud and repetition) to test if morpheme effects “survive” when certain reading processes are impaired. However, as we will discuss further below, most results, even the ones predicted by specific theoretical models, continue to be ambiguous in terms of the locus of morphological processing. Previous studies examining visual word recognition using lexical decision have shown that morpheme effects arise within the orthographic input (e.g., Beyersmann et al., 2016; Longtin et al., 2003; Longtin & Meunier, 2005; Rastle et al., 2004; Taft & Nguyen-Hoan, 2010), but this does not imply that morphological parsing is restricted to the orthographic system alone. Other studies examining auditory word recognition (e.g., Beyersmann et al., 2019; Marslen-Wilson et al., 1994) and spoken word production (e.g., Biedermann et al., 2013; Biedermann et al., 2017; Lorenz et al., 2014; Lorenz et al., 2018; Rapp et al., 2015) have shown that morpheme effects arise within phonological input and phonological output, but this does not imply that morphological parsing is restricted to the phonological system alone. Our goal was to narrow down the different possibilities by using a repetition task, tapping into the phonological system only, alongside the reading aloud task. Specifically, we asked if and how individuals with impaired nonword reading and intact word reading skills are able to recover morphemic information from morphologically complex nonword letter strings.

While the reading of complex real words has been widely investigated in individuals with acquired dyslexia conditions (e.g., Badecker & Caramazza, 1987, 1991; Castles et al., 1996; Hamilton & Coslett, 2008; Jaichenco & Wilson, 2012; Miozzo, 2003; Rastle et al., 2006; Ullman et al., 2005), comparatively little work has been done to examine the mechanisms, and their potential impairment, underlying complex nonword reading. The investigation of complex nonwords is particularly revealing, as participants cannot fall back onto a whole-word reading strategy and it therefore provides a tool to investigate sub-lexical mechanisms of reading (e.g., Burani et al., 1999; Longtin & Meunier, 2005). In complex nonwords, stems and affixes can be separately manipulated, thereby offering insights into the independent contribution of stems and affixes in reading (e.g., Beyersmann et al., 2015; Beyersmann et al., 2021). Despite this obvious advantage, there has been relatively little investigation of complex nonword reading in individuals with acquired dyslexia. An early study tested complex nonword reading with an Italian individual, LB, diagnosed with acquired dyslexia (Caramazza et al., 1985). LB had intact word reading (∼100%) but impaired nonword reading skills (∼70%). In addition, the authors reported that LB’s reading was more accurate for nonwords containing real stems compared to non-stems, suggesting that the presence of stems facilitated reading in this individual. The study included a mix of suffix and non-suffix endings that were not systematically investigated and therefore provide no clear evidence regarding the role of suffixes in LB’s reading. Nevertheless, the study provides some initial evidence regarding the relevance of morphological decomposition and thus (potentially) the existence of orthographic morphemes.

Building on these initial findings, Beyersmann et al. (2022) conducted a more systematic complex nonword reading study in five English-speaking individuals with acquired dyslexia. The patients selected for the study had comparatively more accurate word reading skills (∼80% accurate) than nonword reading skills (∼50% accurate) and were generally characterized as having an impairment consistent with phonological dyslexia, whereby their sight word reading was intact but their phonemic decoding skills were impaired. The study’s focus was on four types of complex nonwords, including stem + suffix (e.g., farmity), stem + non-suffix (e.g., farmald), non-stem + suffix (e.g., falmity), and non-stem + non-suffix nonwords (e.g., falmald). Based on the presence and absence of stems and suffixes, the study was able to clearly dissociate the independent role of stems (compared to non-stems) and suffixes (compared to non-suffixes) in complex nonword reading. Logistic regression analyses on each individual data set revealed a significant stem-effect in all five individuals, with better reading performance for stimuli with embedded stems than without, as had been previously reported in the Caramazza et al. (1985) case study, supporting a core role for embedded word activation in reading aloud, even for nonword stimuli. Benefits of having an embedded suffix was less robust across participants, with the suffix-effect only present in two out of five individuals. This finding is consistent with the idea that stems play a more important role than suffixes in reading. However, there was a potential confound in the previous study, which was that all of the suffixes occurred at the right edges of the orthographic input letter strings and were therefore always pronounced last. It is therefore less clear if the absence of a suffix-effect in the three individuals, who showed no benefit of embedded suffixes, is a reflection of a selective impairment of suffix processing, or the result of a left-to-right processing bias in word production. Thus, questions remain regarding the generalisability of these findings to a context where affixes precede the stem morpheme (i.e., prefix + stem concatenations).

The aim of the current study was to use prefixed nonwords to further resolve questions around whether or not affixes are units of orthographic representation. Prior studies have reported notable differences in the processing of suffixes and prefixes across a variety of different reading tasks (e.g., Beauvillain, 1996; Bergman et al., 1988; Beyersmann et al., 2015; Colé et al., 1989; Feldman & Larabee, 2001; Grainger et al., 1991; Kim et al., 2015; Meunier & Segui, 2002). Prefixes and suffixes have been argued to differ in status, with prefixes having a more “quasi-lexical” standing in the reading system (Giraudo & Grainger, 2003) for a variety of reasons. One possibility is that reading relies on an obligatory left-to-right processing bias, whereby prefixes are always encountered first. Other possible reasons are that prefixes, as opposed to suffixes, are always derivational morphemes in English, and therefore have an exclusively semantic function, and that prefixes do not tend to modify the orthographic form of stems, whereas suffixes can (e.g., time vs. timing). Our goal was to use prefixed nonwords, such as disnight, to test if the robustness of the previously observed stem-effect with suffixed nonwords replicates in a situation where stems are tentatively disadvantaged by being moved into the string-final position. Moreover, we aimed to directly compare stem- and prefix-effects within the same participants to shed further light onto the predicted differences in the processing of stems and affixes. To address this goal, we carried out a reading aloud task in a group of one-hundred healthy readers (Experiment 1). In addition, five individuals with acquired dyslexia participated in the same reading aloud task (Experiment 2), as well as a repetition task (Experiment 3).

The study’s design involved a set of prefixed and non-prefixed real words (e.g., disagree vs. diamond) and four types of complex nonwords. The complex nonwords formed the focus of the current investigation and were divided into prefix + stem (e.g., disnight), prefix + non-stem (e.g., disnisht), non-prefix + stem (e.g., pranight), and non-prefix + non-stem nonwords (e.g., pranisht). This design allowed us to independently test for the role of the stem (by comparing the two stem conditions against the two non-stem conditions) and the role of the prefix (by comparing the two prefix conditions against the two non-prefix conditions). Based on previous findings, we predicted a morpheme facilitation effect (i.e., faster reading of nonwords including existing morphemes compared to nonwords excluding existing morphemes). If it is true that stems facilitate complex nonword reading even when placed in string-final position, as has been previously shown to be the case in lexical decision tasks (e.g., Beyersmann et al., 2016; Crepaldi et al., 2013; Duñabeitia et al., 2009; Heathcote et al., 2018), then the reading of nonwords including stems should be faster and more accurate than the reading of nonwords including non-stems. Similarly, if readers benefit from the presence of prefixes in their reading, this should result in faster and more accurate responses to nonwords including prefixes compared to nonwords including non-prefixes. In addition, the investigation of a stem-by-prefix interaction serves as a test of whether complex nonword reading is additionally facilitated whenever a letter string is exhaustively decomposable into morphemes, as was the case in the prefix + stem condition (e.g., dis + night). The corresponding hypotheses and analyses plan were pre-registered (https://aspredicted.org/KX6_917).

We note that the hypotheses regarding the expected reading pattern in our five individuals with acquired dyslexia were less clearly defined and not pre-registered. Based on prior evidence (Beyersmann et al., 2022; Caramazza et al., 1985), we predicted a facilitation effect arising from the presence of stems and prefixes. In addition, we hypothesized that if stem representations do indeed play a more prominent role in reading, we would predict more robust stem- compared to prefix-effects. Two out of the five reading impaired participants, JS and RF, had taken part in the preceding suffixed nonword reading study (Beyersmann et al., 2022) and we therefore had a particular interest in comparing their prefixed nonword reading data to their prior suffixed nonword reading data. RF previously stood out for their particularly robust evidence for an embedded stem effect (more accurate reading of nonwords including stems than non-stems), a suffix effect (more accurate reading of nonwords including suffixes than non-suffixes), and was the only individual to show a significant stem-by-suffix interaction, with a larger suffix effect in nonwords containing stems than non-stems, thereby providing the clearest form of evidence for the benefit of morphological structure in reading. The current study aimed to further examine RF’s nonword parsing skills by testing whether the previously observed morpheme-parsing advantage in suffixed nonwords would extend to prefixed nonwords.

Experiment 1: Healthy individuals

Method

Participants

A pre-registered sample of 100 Macquarie University students participated as healthy controls in this online study (25 participants per list). They participated to receive credit for their first- or second-year Psychology subjects. Participants were monolingual and native English speakers without any reading disorders. The participants identified as female (81%), male (18%), and non-binary (1%), and their ages ranged from 16 to 59 (M = 23.63, SD = 8.93). The most common highest level of education completed was high school or equivalent (85%), followed by a bachelor’s degree (8%), other (6%), and a master’s degree (1%).

Materials

The materials included 60 words and 240 nonwords (see Table 1). The study’s primary focus was on the analyses of four nonword conditions containing 60 items each: prefix + stem (e.g., disnight), prefix + non-stem (e.g., disnisht), non-prefix + stem (e.g., pranight), and non-prefix + non-stem (e.g., pranisht). The nonword conditions were matched on the number of letters, number of phonemes, and number of syllables. The nonwords were separated into four counterbalanced lists, each containing 60 words and 60 nonwords, so that no participant saw the same stem or non-stem twice. Procedure

Data were collected online using Gorilla Experiment Builder (www.gorilla.sc; Anwyl-Irvine et al., 2019). Data collection occurred between the 7th of July and the 24th of August 2021. Participants were instructed to have a laboratory-like environment with minimal distractions. They were guided through a microphone check to ensure that their voice recording was clear. They were instructed to read aloud words and nonwords as quickly and accurately as possible, using a loud and clear voice. Each participant saw six practice items in random order. They were then randomly allocated to one of four experimental lists, containing 60 nonwords and 60 words presented in random order. The experimental lists were designed to ensure that no participant saw the same stem or prefix more than once. Participants had 3000 ms to read aloud the individually presented items. Each item was displayed in the centre of the screen in 30 pixels, Arial font, black text, and on a white background.

Results

Reading aloud responses for each participant were carefully screened and phonetically transcribed using the International Phonetic Alphabet (IPA). All data, including the original recordings and phonetic transcriptions, were uploaded into the study’s Open Science Framework online repository (https://osf.io/hs5rx/?view_only=f45eea61689446508d33c83a313e4526).

Reaction time processing

Reaction times (RTs) were manually marked by one of the co-authors using the computer programme Praat (Boersma & Weenink, 2022). Each RT was scored in milliseconds, starting from the moment the participant initiated their first full attempt at reading aloud an item. Partial responses or stuttering were not counted when marking RTs (e.g., if the response was [dɪs] … [dɪsfɪlt], the RT was marked from the beginning of [dɪsfɪlt]). To account for discrepancies in item presentation timing (e.g., due to differing Internet speeds), RTs were adjusted based on the timestamps collected by Gorilla Experimenter Builder (www.gorilla.sc; Anwyl-Irvine et al., 2019), which indicated when the audio recording started for each item and when the item was presented on the screen. The difference between the audio recording commencement timestamp and the item presentation timestamp was subtracted from the Praat-derived RTs to enhance measurement accuracy.

Error coding

Response accuracy was coded by marking the responses of each participant as either correct or incorrect. To account for high pronunciation variability (e.g., Coltheart & Ulicheva, 2018; Mousikou et al., 2017), we adopted lenient marking criteria, by which responses were only considered errors if they included clear mispronunciations (i.e., implausible grapheme-to-phoneme mappings). Responses were marked as correct when they included different plausible phonemic variations (e.g., [prəmaʊs] and [prəmuːs] for pramouse). Repair that simplified complex coda clusters, either through phoneme omission (e.g., [prənɪʃ] instead of [prənɪʃt] for pranisht, or [ri:wɜː(r)t] instead of [ri:wɜː(r)lt] for reworlt), or epenthesis (e.g., [təwɒlɪp] instead of [təwɒlp] for tewolp), were not considered errors. Error rates were overall low (below 2% in the word trials; below 4% in the nonword trials). In line with the pre-registered analysis plan, words and nonwords were analysed separately.

Nonword analyses

Incorrect responses were removed from the reaction time (RT) analysis (3.7% of the data). RTs reflected the time participants took to initiate a response. Naming latencies were inverse transformed to normalize residuals and residual outlier trimming was applied (Baayen, 2008), which led to the removal of 1.7% of the data. Analyses were performed using generalized linear mixed-effects models (Baayen et al., 2008) as implemented in the lme4 package (Version 1.1.27.1; Bates et al., 2015) in the statistical software R (Version 4.2.2, 2022-10-31, “Innocent and Trusting”, RCoreTeam, 2022). The significance of the fixed effects was determined with type III model comparisons using the Anova function in the car package (Version 3.0.12; Fox & Weisberg, 2019). The model included two effect-coded independent variables (Stem [stem, non-stem] and Prefix [prefix, non-prefix]), their interaction, and random slopes and random intercepts for items and participants. As per Barr and colleagues (2013), models were computed with the maximal random effects structure (Baayen et al., 2008). Next, the random intercepts model was computed and random slopes were added incrementally. The highest converging nonsingular models are reported.

The analysis of the naming latencies revealed a significant main effect of Stem (χ2(2) = 82.26, p < .001), suggesting that nonwords with embedded stems were read aloud faster than nonwords including non-stems. There was also a significant main effect of Prefix (χ2(1) = 39.87, p < .001), showing that prefixed nonwords were initiated faster than non-prefixed nonwords. The interaction between Stem and Prefix was significant (χ2(1) = 4.07, p = .044), suggesting that there was a larger Stem-Effect for prefixed (t = 7.96, p < .001) than non-prefixed nonwords (t = 5.25, p < .001).

Although error rates in the healthy controls were generally low (< 4%), mean error rates varied across conditions. There was a significant main effect of Stem (χ2(2) = 39.82, p < .001), suggesting that nonwords with embedded stems were read more accurately than nonwords including non-stems. There was also a significant main effect of Prefix (χ2(1) = 5.43, p = .020), showing that prefixed nonwords were read more accurately than non-prefixed nonwords. The interaction between Stem and Prefix was not significant (χ2(1) = 1.35, p = .245). All subtypes of errors that were analysed as part of the impaired speaker data below (see Table 6 for a summary) were also witnessed in the healthy control data, but only sporadically; the proportion of each error type was below 1.2%, and therefore, they were not further analyzed.

Word analyses

Incorrect responses were removed from the RT analysis (1.1% of the data). Naming latencies were inverse transformed to normalize residuals and residual outlier trimming was applied (Baayen, 2008), which led to the removal of 2.6% of the data. RTs and ERs were analysed for each participant. A (generalized) linear mixed-effects model was fitted including the fixed effect of condition (prefixed, non-prefixed), by-participant random slopes for the effect of condition, and random intercepts for items and participants. The effect of condition was not significant, neither in the RT nor in the error analyses, showing that participants responded similarly to prefixed (mean RT: 690 ms [SD = 144]; mean error rate: 1.03% [SD = 10.33]) and non-prefixed words (mean RT: 703 ms [SD = 171]; mean error rate: 1.06% [SD = 10.27]).

Discussion

The nonword data of Experiment 1 revealed that healthy participants strongly relied on morphemes in their reading. First, a robust embedded stem-effect was observed in both response time and accuracy analyses, showing that nonwords including stems were initiated faster and more accurately than nonwords including non-stems. Second, a significant prefix-effect was found in both response time and accuracy analyses, showing that prefixed nonwords were initiated faster and more accurately than non-prefixed nonwords. The prefix-effect did not reach significance in the word analyses, although the mean RTs showed that participants responded slightly faster to prefixed compared to non-prefixed words (690 vs. 703 ms). This lack of significant difference is consistent with prior findings showing that skilled readers benefit from the presence of morphemes only when they were present in newly encountered words (i.e., nonwords; Burani et al., 2002) and in low-frequency words (e.g., Carlisle & Stone, 2005; Deacon et al., 2011), but not in mid-to-high-frequency affixed words, whereas children with dyslexia have been reported to benefit from the presence of morphemes when reading both novel words and words of various frequencies, including high-frequency words (e.g., Burani et al., 2008; Marcolini et al., 2011). These findings point to a particularly important role for morphological parsing in the reading of novel words and nonwords, which found support in the current nonword analyses. Embedded stems and prefixes facilitated nonword reading in our healthy participant sample, which is consistent with prior evidence from suffixed nonwords (Mousikou et al., 2020), and further supports the notion that stems and affixes are used as islands of regularity between form and meaning (e.g., Ulicheva et al., 2020; Ulicheva et al., 2020). Third, a significant stem-by-prefix interaction in the response time analyses showed that the prefix-effect was larger for nonwords including stems than non-stems. One possibility is that the reading system is sensitive to the decomposability of complex letter strings, and recognizes whenever an input letter string is fully parsable. Such a mechanism has been proposed in the form of “affix-stripping”, by which the reading system conducts a search for affixes on any given letter string (e.g., Kingma, 2013; Schreuder & Baayen, 1994; Stockall et al., 2019; Taft, 1981, 2004; Taft & Forster, 1975). Whenever an affix is detected, it is stripped-off, which in turn allows the isolation of the stem morpheme. While affix-stripping provides an explanation for the larger effect in the prefix + stem condition, it does not as easily account for the difference between the non-prefix + stem and non-prefix + non-stem nonwords. The finding that embedded stems were activated independently of the presence or absence of a prefix is difficult to reconcile with the idea that embedded stem activation follows prefix-stripping. Another, simpler possibility is that the interaction was based on the combined presence of prefixes and stems, leading to faster and more accurate responses in the prefix + stem condition compared to the two conditions where only one morpheme was present. In sum, the data of Experiment 1 provide a robust baseline for the facilitatory role of morpheme processing in the reading-unimpaired population. Nevertheless, a point worth acknowledging is that the locus of the reported morpheme facilitation effects cannot be clearly pinned to the orthographic, lexical-semantic, or phonological system. As we will discuss in more detail below, localizing morpheme effects within the reading system is difficult. While our data from reading impaired individuals (Experiments 2 and 3) have the potential to further narrow down the locus of morphological processing in reading, the here reported data are not unambiguous and continue to pose important challenges to research in complex word reading.

Experiment 2: Impaired speakers – reading

Building on the results of Experiment 1, Experiment 2 aimed to investigate the robustness and potential failures of morpheme processing in five individuals with acquired dyslexia, to shed further light onto the independent morphemic component processing in reading. Here, case series research was used as a tool to evaluate typical cognitive processes involved in reading (Nickels et al., 2022). As opposed to the group of healthy controls in Experiment 1, we were not able to collect reaction times with our impaired speaker group, because speech was generally slow and disrupted. Instead, a rich set of error subtype analyses were conducted on the impaired data to disentangle the underlying component processes. We were particularly interested in comparing the robustness of the stem and prefix effects in participants who have been reported to have comparatively more intact word reading skills compared to nonword reading skills. Prior evidence from suffixed nonword reading had shown a reduced ability to process suffixes in 3/5 participants, whereas stem-effects were absent in only 1/5 participants, indicating that the identification of embedded stems may be more robust because the identification of stems may be recovered via the whole-word lexical pathway (Beyersmann et al., 2022). In the present study, the investigation was extended to prefixed nonwords to shed further light onto the different component processes involved in the retrieval of stems and affixes in reading.

Across the board, all participants scored 80% correct or higher on this task (CY: 180/216, 83%; JS: 203/216, 94%; RF: 216/216, 100%; RV: 209/216, 97%; TP: 204/216, 93%). Phonological processing was further tested with a single-word, single-picture matching task (Martin et al., 1999). In this task, a word was spoken and a picture was presented that was either the same as the target (e.g., “dog” – DOG), a phonological foil, (e.g., “dog” – LOG), a semantic foil, (e.g., “dog” – CAT), or an unrelated foil (e.g., “dog” – CAR), and the participant had to indicate whether they were the same or different. The same 54 spoken words were used in each condition, and the experiment was carried out over four separate testing sessions. All participants were 100% accurate when the foil was unrelated to the target and >95% correct for the matching trials. Critically, all of the participants, except for CY, rarely made errors on the phonological foil trials. JS and RV were 100% correct on those trials, and RF made a single error and TP made two errors. CY, on the other hand, scored only 46/54 correct with the phonological foils. Paired with the minimal pair discrimination test described above, it is likely that CY has difficulties in mapping from speech sounds onto meaning. However, as we will see below, given that word and nonword reading and picture naming were all quite strong with this participant, it is unlikely that this impairment is impacting her reading or her language production.

All five individuals showed impaired performance with the semantic foils (CY: 48/54, 89%; JS: 44/54, 81%; RF: 42/54, 78%; RV: 43/54, 80%; TP: 49/54, 91%). This difficulty could indicate a problem in mapping from phonological forms onto meaning, or impairments of semantic knowledge. In the absence of control data, these results were interpreted considering performance on tasks designed to specifically evaluate semantic performance for which normative data is available. To assess semantic knowledge, two tasks were administered: spoken vocabulary, as measured by the Peabody Picture Vocabulary Test (PPVT; Dunn & Dunn, 2007), and the Pyramids and Palm Trees Test (Howard & Patterson, 1992), a nonverbal test of semantics in which a participant had to select one of two competitor pictures that more closely matched a target picture. The PPVT is a standardized, normed test of vocabulary, and all five participants fell well within the distribution of neurotypical performance for age-matched adults (CY: 84th percentile, JS: 73rd percentile, RF: 45th percentile, RV: 58th percentile, TP: 77th percentile). The control range for the 52-item Pyramids and Palm Trees Test is 50–52 correct. RF (51/52), RV (52/52), and TP (50/52) were all within the control range, and JS (49/52) and CY (48/52) fell just below the cutoff. Therefore, it appears that all five participants had relatively intact semantic systems.

Given that the current study focused on reading aloud, which engages aspects of the spoken production system, all participants were also administered the Philadelphia Naming Test, a 175 item confrontation naming task with simple black and white line drawings (Roach et al., 1996). RV (161/175; 92%) and TP (162/175; 93%) performed similarly to controls on this task (control mean: 96%, SD = 6.9%). JS (133/175; 76%) and RF (112/175; 64%) were impaired in confrontation naming, while CY (153/175; 87%) was borderline. Given their good performance on tasks of semantics, their difficulty emerged in either lexical-semantic mapping or phonological encoding. JS’s errors were mostly semantically related to the target (23/42 errors) or were no responses (10/42), indicating a difficulty in lexical retrieval from semantics in speech production. A similar pattern was observed with CY, who made mostly semantic errors (14/22 errors). RF’s errors were more distributed, with the most common categories being semantically related errors (18/63 errors), no-responses (13/63 errors), and also phonologically related nonword responses (12/63 errors), indicating additional difficulties in phonological encoding.

Finally, two standardized tests of reading aloud were administered from Psycholinguistic Assessments of Language Processing in Aphasia (PALPA; Kay et al., 1992). PALPA 35 includes 30 words with regular spellings and a matched set of 30 words with exceptional spellings. Greater accuracy with regular words than exception words is indicative of surface dyslexia. PALPA 36 includes 24 short nonwords between 3 and 6 letters long, with highly consistent pronunciations in a normative sample. Worse performance on nonword reading than word reading is indicative of phonological dyslexia. JS, RV, and TP all performed well on the word reading task (all scored at least 56/60 correct; 93% accuracy) and none showed effects of regularity (JS’s accuracy was 28/30 for both word types, TP’s accuracy was 29/30 for both word types, and RV’s accuracy was 29/30 for regular words and 27/30 for exception words [χ2(1) = 0.27, p = .60]). CY made a few more errors in reading aloud words, reading 87% (52/60) of the words correctly, but did not show a regularity effect (regular words: 27/30 correct, exception words: 25/30 correct; χ2(1) = 0.14, p = .70). RF only correctly read aloud 48% (29/60) of them, but also did not show an effect of regularity (regular words: 13/60 correct; exception words: 18/60 correct; χ2(1) = 1.07, p = .30). From this, we concluded that none of the participants in the sample had a pattern consistent with acquired surface dyslexia. In terms of nonword reading, JS (14/24; 58% accuracy), TP (2/24; 8% accuracy), and RF (4/24; 17% accuracy) were all poor at this task, and performed worse than their word reading (JS: χ2(1) = 12.71, p = .0004; TP: χ2(1) = 61.29, p < .0001; RF: χ2(1) = 5.94, p = .0148), indicating a pattern consistent with phonological dyslexia. An inspection of the nonword error types showed that participants typically made lexicalization errors (e.g., reading squate as “square”) or errors in which they were able to sound out the first few letters but not the whole word (e.g., reading dusp as “duh, duh, duhrnm”) and occasionally other nonword errors (e.g., reading grest as “gretch”). These error patterns are consistent with deficits in grapheme-to-phoneme conversion, though it is possible that they could be emerging at the level of phonological output. However, of note is that all three of these participants showed strikingly poor nonword reading despite relatively good phonological processing, contra the hypothesis that poor nonword reading and wider phonological impairments go hand-in-hand (see Tree, 2008; Tree & Kay, 2006; Vliet et al., 2004, for a similar result). CY’s reading of these short nonwords was much better than these other participants (20/24; 83% accuracy), and RV’s performance was even better (22/24; 92% accuracy). There was no significant difference in word versus nonword reading in CY (χ2(1) = 0.002, p = .96) and RV (χ2(1) = 0.072, p = .79), though, as will be shown below, their nonword reading starts to break down with longer strings.

Materials

The materials were identical to Experiment 1.

Procedure

Each participant completed the experiment separately over Zoom with an experimenter guiding the process. All 300 items from the four counterbalanced lists (60 words and 240 nonwords) were presented individually in the centre of the screen via a slideshow. The items were in black text on a white background, 115 in size, and Calibri Light font. The items were randomized within each list and each participant saw the same randomized lists. Participants were given as long as they needed to read aloud each item. They attended one-hour Zoom sessions once a week until they attempted all 300 items. Their responses were audio recorded, written down by the present experimenter, and later transcribed into IPA by another experimenter.

Results

Responses of each participant were labelled as either correct or incorrect. Error coding was based on the first full response (i.e., false starts and stutters were ignored until the first full response). As in Experiment 1, we adopted lenient marking criteria, by which responses were only considered errors if they deviated from the range of responses given by the healthy controls and if they included clear mispronunciations (i.e., implausible grapheme-to-phoneme mappings). Then, the incorrect responses were scored for a range of error subtypes (see Table 6). The labelling of errors was completed by two of the authors (TA and EB). Nonword analyses

The data of each reading impaired individual were analysed separately. Analyses were performed using generalized linear models (Dobson, 1990; Venables & Ripley, 2002) using the glm function as implemented in the statistical software R (Version 4.1.2, 2021-11-01, “Bird Hippie”, RCoreTeam, 2022). The generalized linear models included two effect-coded independent variables (Stem [stem, non-stem] and Prefix [prefix, non-prefix]), their interaction, and a binomial link function. The dependent variables varied (e.g., first component errors were included as a dependent variable to analyse first component errors, second component errors were included as a dependent variable to analyse second component errors, etc.). In addition, to reduce the probability of making a Type I error, Bonferroni corrections were applied throughout the analyses to account for multiple comparisons (i.e., the level of statistical significance was adjusted to p < .01). The full model outputs can be viewed within the following Open Science Framework repository: https://osf.io/hs5rx/?view_only=f45eea61689446508d33c83a313e4526.

In addition to the first and second component error analyses, we analysed the proportion of prefix substitutions within each participant (see Figure 6). Prefix substitutions formed a subset of first component errors and represented substitutions of the first component (prefix or non-prefix) with another prefix. Prefix substitutions were interpreted as evidence for the activation and segmentation of bound prefix-morphemes, and participants’ reliance on affix morphemes in their reading. Prefix substitutions were prevalent in RF and TP but almost entirely absent in CY, JS, and RV. RF showed that the proportion of prefix substitutions was significantly higher in prefixed nonwords (e.g., target: intralig; response: interlig) than non-prefixed nonwords (target: nisand; response: unsand; z = 2.22, p = .026). The majority of RF’s prefix substitutions were due to the substitution of the prefix intra- with inter-, which sometimes also involved a final position stem substitution (e.g., targets: intraholl, intrafierd, intraflish; responses: interbeen, interfield, interfleece). A re-analysis of RF’s overall error rates, following the exclusion of all inter/intra substitutions, revealed a significant main effect of Stem (z = 2.74, p = .006), and most importantly, a now significant stem-by-prefix interaction (z = 2.54, p = .011). The main effect of Prefix (i.e., the difference in prefix substitutions between prefixed and non-prefixed nonwords) was no longer significant (z = 0.22, p = .823). The overall accuracy analyses showed that three of the participants (CY, JS, and RV) had excellent word reading skills of over 90% accuracy, whereas the other two (RF and TP) had moderate word reading skills of over 70% accuracy (see Figure 8). Stem first readings

RF stood out, because this individual frequently made a first attempt to read the stem, and then the prefix. This was the case for 35% of the nonwords (e.g., target: inwall, response: wall … inwall; target: inpoint, response: point … inpoint), and 8.3% of the words (e.g., target: overcook, response: cook … overcook; target: unstable, response: stable … unstable).

Discussion

The results of Experiment 2 shed light onto the underlying cognitive mechanisms of nonword reading in individuals with acquired dyslexia. All five individuals were shown to be more severely impaired in the nonword reading compared to the word reading, which indicates that all individuals in the current sample broadly classify as readers with phonological dyslexia. The present data suggest that individuals with acquired dyslexia can use morphological knowledge to compensate for their nonword reading deficits. One of the most notable findings is the observed difference in stem- and prefix-effects in the current data. While a robust stem-effect was evidenced in all five individuals, the prefix-effect was significant in only two out of five participants (CY and TP). This result replicates and extends findings from suffixed nonwords (Beyersmann et al., 2022) to prefixed nonwords, and suggests that stem-effects were more robust than affix-effects, despite stems being embedded in the string-final position. It is worth noting that although the prefix-effect was significant in CY and TP, the first component error analyses (which were based on errors that were specifically due to an erroneous pronunciation of the prefix or non-prefix, and therefore provide a cleaner prefix-type analysis) show that this effect was only truly present in TP.

Of the five participants, only JS and RF also completed the suffix task in Beyersmann et al. (2022), where JS showed remarkably similar patterns of performance, including a clear boost in nonword reading when the complex form contained a real stem, but no impact of whether the complex form contains a real affix. The one participant who showed the most unique reading pattern across both studies was RF. In the preceding suffixed nonword reading study (Beyersmann et al., 2022), RF had provided the clearest form of evidence for the facilitatory role of stems and suffixes in nonword reading, and was the only individual to provide evidence for a stem-by-suffix interaction, suggesting that RF’s reading was at best when nonwords were exhaustively decomposable into morphemic subunits (e.g., farm + ity). However, RF’s prefixed nonword reading only revealed a main effect of stem, but the prefix-effect was absent. Moreover, for 35% of the nonwords, RF attempted to read the stem first, then the prefix (e.g., night … disnight), suggesting that the stem served as an important segmentation cue. This finding is particularly noteworthy, given that the stems of the prefixed nonwords were identical (besides moot being changed to mool) to the stems used in the preceding suffixed nonword study. If serial processing was applied, preference would have been given to the left-aligned prefixes, which clearly was not the case in RF’s data.

A point that adds another layer of complexity to the interpretation of RF’s data is that she was one out of two individuals (besides TP) to produce a large proportion of prefix substitutions (∼20%). In contrast to TP, the results of RF are of particular interest, as she was the only person to show a significantly higher proportion of prefix substitutions in prefixed compared to non-prefixed nonwords. We had hypothesized earlier that prefix substitutions may be used to substitute non-prefixes more often than prefixes, but RF’s results go in the opposite direction with prefix substitutions being higher in the prefixed than non-prefixed condition. A closer inspection of RF’s prefix substitutions shows that these were primarily due to the fact that she replaced the prefix intra- with the prefix inter-. RF’s prefix substitutions therefore have to be interpreted with caution. Given the obvious orthographic and phonological similarity between intra- and inter-, questions remain whether the observed pattern was indeed a pure form of prefix substitution, or simply a form of retrieval of an orthographically related and potentially more familiar letter sequence. Indeed, if we ignore the inter-/intra- substitution errors, the observed difference in prefix substitutions between the prefixed and non-prefixed conditions is no longer significant, and RF’s overall pattern of performance on this task is closer to their performance on the suffix nonword reading task reported previously, with a now significant stem-by-prefix interaction, suggesting near perfect performance for the fully-decomposable nonwords, and worse performance for the other three conditions. These findings suggest that RF’s reading performance was more accurate when prefixed nonwords were exhaustively decomposable into morphemes (e.g., dis + night), which is exactly the kind of pattern that was observed earlier in RF’s suffixed nonword reading (e.g., night + ness; Beyersmann et al., 2022).

While the results of Experiments 1 and 2 clearly point to the important role of stems and affixes in complex nonword reading, questions remain regarding the locus of morpheme parsing within the reading system. One possibility is that morpheme effects arise within the orthographic system (at the level of the orthographic input lexicon, or during sub-lexical processing - i.e., in preparation for orthographic input), as has been previously argued by studies examining visual word recognition using lexical decision tasks (e.g., Beyersmann et al., 2016; Rastle et al., 2004). Another possibility is that morphological processing takes place within the phonological system, within the phonological lexicon or in preparation for output (e.g., Biedermann et al., 2013; Biedermann et al., 2017; Lorenz et al., 2014; Rapp et al., 2015); or both within the orthographic and the phonological systems. Hence, to specifically identify the locus of morphological processing, and the involvement of the phonological system, we carried out a third experiment using a repetition task.

Experiment 3: Impaired speakers – repetition

A repetition task was administered to the same five individuals from Experiment 2. Given that prefixed nonword repetition data were not available for these individuals, the reported data are from a suffixed nonword repetition task instead. The experiment was based on the following four conditions: stem + suffix (e.g., nightness), stem + non-suffix (e.g., nightlude), non-stem + suffix (e.g., nishtness), and non-stem + non-suffix (e.g., nishtlude). We hypothesized that if the observed morpheme effects in the reading aloud tasks of Experiments 1 and 2 were solely due to the segmentation of the orthographic input, no differences between conditions should arise in the repetition task of Experiment 3. However, if morphemic constituents of complex nonwords are indeed activated and retrieved within the phonological system, then we would expect to observe a similar stepwise pattern as in the first two experiments, i.e., faster and more accurate responses in the stem + suffix condition, compared to the stem + non-suffix and non-stem + suffix conditions, and the slowest and least accurate responses in the non-stem + non-suffix condition.

Method

Participants

The participants were the same five individuals with acquired dyslexia who participated in Experiment 2.

Materials

The materials included a list of 240 nonwords, adapted from Beyersmann et al. (2022), including the following four conditions: stem + suffix, stem + non-suffix, non-stem + suffix and non-stem + non-suffix. The stem and non-stem stimuli of Experiments 2 and 3 were identical, and paired with a list of suffixes and non-suffixes. The 2 × 2 experimental design was identical to Experiments 1 and 2, including the following four conditions: stem + suffix (e.g., nightness), stem + non-suffix (e.g., nightlude), non-stem + suffix (e.g., nishtness), and non-stem + non-suffix (e.g., nishtlude). The full list of items is available in Appendix B.

Procedure

An experimenter spoke each target item to the participant and asked them verbally repeat them. Participants came in for a two-hour session once a week to repeat the stimuli until the task was finished.

Results

The repetition data of each reading impaired individual were analysed separately, using generalized linear models (Dobson, 1990; Venables & Ripley, 2002) and the glm function as implemented in the statistical software R (Version 4.1.2, 2021-11-01, “Bird Hippie”, RCoreTeam, 2022). The generalized linear models included two effect-coded independent variables (Stem [stem, non-stem] and Suffix [suffix, non-suffix]), their interaction, and a binomial link function.

Overall error rates per participant showed that individuals differed in their level of nonword repetition impairment (CY: 92.9%; JS: 54.6%; RF: 21.7%; RV: 15.8%; TP: 40.0%). CY stood out with the severest form of impairment, which was likely due to difficulties in perceiving spoken language at a phonological level (as also evidenced by the poor performance in the minimal pair discrimination task, see Table 5). The error rates for each participant are displayed in Figure 9. There was a main effect of Stem which was significant in four out of the five individuals (JS: z = 2.97, p = .003; RF: z = 3.35, p < .001, RV: z = 2.18, p < .030, TP: z = 2.12, p = .034), showing that error rates were lower for stems than non-stems. The main effect of Suffix was significant for three out of five individuals, including CY (z = 2.09, p = .036), RF (z = 3.09, p = .002), and TP (z = 2.12, p = .034). The interaction between Stem and Suffix was not significant for any of the participants. Discussion

Experiment 3 explored complex nonword repetition in five individuals with acquired dyslexia. Each participant was asked to repeat four types of complex nonwords: stem + suffix (e.g., nightness), stem + non-suffix (e.g., nightlude), non-stem + suffix (e.g., nishtness), and non-stem + non-suffix nonwords (e.g., nishtlude). The results revealed a robust stem-effect in four of the five individuals. CY was the only individual who did not show a significant stem-effect, which was likely due to the severity of an underlying speech perception impairment. The affix-effect was present in three of the five individuals, which shows, once again, that the retrieval of embedded stems was overall more prevalent than affix retrieval in complex nonword processing.

General discussion

Cognitive mechanisms underpinning the reading of morphologically complex nonwords were investigated in a group of one-hundred healthy readers (Experiment 1) and five individuals with acquired dyslexia (Experiments 2 and 3). Participants were asked to read (Experiments 1 and 2) and repeat (Experiment 3) four types of nonwords, including an affix + stem (e.g., disnight), a non-affix + stem (e.g., pranight), an affix + non-stem (e.g., disnisht), and a non-affix + non-stem condition (e.g., pranisht). The analyses primarily focused on the nonwords to better understand the mechanisms by which nonwords are read. In the healthy control group, the main dependent variables were response times and error rates. In the reading impaired individuals, the analyses were centred around error rates, and a fine-tuned breakdown into error subtypes of the reading aloud data was provided. The results revealed several key findings, which are summarized below.

Evidence for the representation of morphemes

The study’s findings provide robust support for the representation of morphemes within reading, as evidenced by a facilitatory stem-effect across all participants, including our five reading-impaired individuals. In addition, the healthy controls showed a significant main effect of prefix, where nonwords including prefixes were initiated faster and more accurately than nonwords including non-prefixes. However, the prefix-effect was only significant in two of five impaired readers (CY and TP), reflecting a reduction in error rates when morphemes were present. This trend was also mirrored in the results of the repetition task, where there was a significant stem-effect in four out of the five individuals (JS, RF, RV, and TP), and an affix-effect in three out of the five individuals (CY, RF, and TP). Overall, JS and RV showed the clearest form of dissociation between the presence of stem-effects in the absence of affix-effects, both in the reading and repetition data. Our findings fit with a pattern described in the results of Badecker and Caramazza (1991) for patient SJD who produced 388 morphological errors (where the target stem was preserved, and not the affix), but only 15 stem errors (where the target affix is preserved, and not the stem). A possible explanation for the absence of an affix-effect is that JS and RV suffered from an additional impairment that left the retrieval of stem morphemes intact while impairing the retrieval of affixes.

Theoretical implications

One must thus ask how these data can be reconciled with current theories of morphological processing. A first point to note is that our findings challenge distributional theories of morphological processing that claim that morphology is entirely captured in the links between orthography and semantics (e.g., Gonnerman et al., 2007; Plaut & Gonnerman, 2000). It is difficult to account for morpheme-effects that arise from complex nonwords when there is no existing underlying lexical whole-word representation and where there is no semantic relationship between the constituents and the whole letter string. In addition, distributional theories predict that stems and affixes undergo the same kind of triangular mapping mechanisms between orthography, semantics, and phonology, which makes it more difficult to account for differences between the here reported stem- and affix-effects (but see Stevens & Plaut, 2022, who outline the many similarities between distributional and decompositional accounts of morphological processing). A further challenge for such accounts lies in the here reported affix substitutions (in TP and RF), which provide the clearest form of evidence for a deficit in affix processing. As already discussed by Badecker and Caramazza (1987), affix substitutions are difficult to reconcile with distributional theories that are based on a single, non-morphological mapping mechanism, and these theories are therefore not sufficiently explicit to differentiate the specific deficits of TP and RF.

Instead, our data need to be explained within a framework that can account for the observed differences in stem- and affix-processing. At least within the reading world, this is not a new idea. For example, Grainger and Ziegler’s (2011) dual route framework of orthographic processing proposes that fine-grained orthographic processing applies to affixes, involving precise letter order encoding, whereas coarse-grained orthographic processing applies to stems, which tolerates greater levels of orthographic imprecision. It has further been proposed that embedded stems are retrieved just like any free-standing word by mapping the input onto existing representations in the lexicon (e.g., Beyersmann & Grainger, 2023; Grainger & Beyersmann, 2017), whereas affix activation is handled by a morpheme-specific parsing mechanism that is more constrained with regards to edge-alignedness (i.e., suffixes are only recognized in the final position, and prefixes only in the initial position; Spencer et al., 2024) and letter position specificity (i.e., the recognition of affixes requires more precise, or fine-grained, letter position information as opposed to the more coarse-grained letter position encoding of embedded stems; Grainger & Ziegler, 2011).

Within the word and affix model (Beyersmann & Grainger, 2023), embedded word activation is an entirely non-morphological process by which the reading system recognizes not only whole words (e.g., farmer, corner, cashew), but also embedded words (e.g., farm, corn, cash). It is successful even if the embedded word is not a real morphemic stem (as the corn in corner) and even if the entire letter string is not exhaustively decomposable into morphemes (as in cashew, where the final letters ew do not form an affix). As such, embedded word activation represents the mapping of orthographic input onto existing lexical representations, including whole words, embedded words, and stem morphemes. Affix activation, on the other hand, is a morpheme-specific parsing mechanism by which the reading system recognizes prefixes and suffixes. Affix activation is more constrained than embedded stem activation, for a variety of reasons (Beyersmann & Grainger, 2023, p. 27), and only successful whenever a suffix is found in the word-final position or a prefix in the word-initial position. In addition, the model incorporates the principle of morpho-orthographic full decomposition, which is a parsing mechanism exclusively applicable to exhaustively decomposable morphologically complex words. Within this framework, it is therefore possible to assume a selective impairment of affix processing, which explains the pattern observed in JS and RV.

Challenges in localizing morpheme effects in reading

An ongoing challenge in the field of complex word reading continues to be the question of where morpheme-effects arise, and whether or not there might be multiple places for morphology within the reading system. One obvious limitation of theories of morphological processing in visual word recognition (e.g., Beyersmann & Grainger, 2023; Crepaldi et al., 2010; Feldman et al., 2015; Taft, 2003) is that they cannot fully capture our findings from the reading aloud and repetition modalities. Our repetition data show that the activation of embedded words and affixes is not restricted to tasks involving written input. Previous work from (masked primed) lexical decision shows that readers decompose complex letter strings into their morphemic sub-units (e.g., Beyersmann et al., 2015; Beyersmann et al., 2016; Diependaele et al., 2009; Rastle et al., 2004), which provides strong support for the idea that the orthographic input lexicon does not just store whole words, but also the orthographic forms of morphemic stems and affixes (Beyersmann & Grainger, 2023). However, morphemic representations at the level of the orthographic lexicon are not enough to account for the observed stem and affix effects within the repetition data of Experiment 3. One option is that the morphemic information is also retrieved at the level of the phonological output lexicon, in preparation for phonological output. However, research from auditory lexical decision suggests that morphological structure is already analysed at the point of phonological input (e.g., Beyersmann et al., 2019; Marslen-Wilson et al., 1994). It therefore seems likely that embedded word activation and affix activation operate over both orthographic and acoustic input (see Figure 10). The word and affix forms that are stored and activated within the phonological input lexicon (via speech) and within the orthographic input lexicon (via print) then in turn activate the word and affix representations at the level of the phonological output lexicon (e.g., Badecker & Caramazza, 1991), as well as at the level of the orthographic output lexicon (as shown previously by Hillis & Caramazza, 1991; Rapp et al., 2015). The data of JS and RV showed the clearest form of dissociation between stems and affixes: both benefited from the presence of stems, but not affixes, when reading and repeating complex nonwords. Given that word reading ability in these individuals was superior to their nonword reading ability, it is possible that embedded stems were “recovered” via the lexical (embedded) word recognition pathway. The account depicted in Figure 10, which incorporates aspects of the Dual-Route Cascaded (DRC) Model – in the form of parallel lexical and non-lexical routes (Coltheart et al., 2001) – along with aspects of the word and affix model (Beyersmann & Grainger, 2023), thus offers an explanation for the stem- and affix-effects observed in both the reading aloud and repetition data. The model incorporates aspects of the Dual-Route Cascaded (DRC) Model, in the form of parallel lexical and non-lexical routes (Coltheart et al., 2001), and the word and affix model, based on parallel access to words and affixes (Beyersmann & Grainger, 2023).

The model described in Figure 10 is based on the idea that there are multiple loci for morphological processing, including the phonological input lexicon, the orthographic input lexicon, the phonological output lexicon, and – based on earlier research – also at the level of semantics. It is a widely replicated finding that semantics modulate morphological processing under certain conditions (e.g., Diependaele et al., 2009; Feldman et al., 2004; Rastle et al., 2000). Although the current nonword reading data do not directly support the idea that complex nonword reading is modulated by semantics, it is nevertheless possible that the observed morpheme-effects were, perhaps at least partially, due to the activation of word and affix meanings at the level of semantics. A more direct test of this possibility would be a complex nonword reading study that systematically varied the degree of semantic interpretability of the letter strings. This would allow us to see to which extent the reading of complex nonwords is modulated by the semantics of its sub-units. The comparison between semantically interpretable and semantically non-interpretable complex nonwords thus represents a valuable extension of the current work for future investigations. Yet another possibility is that the results that arise at the level of morpheme representations are not semantic, per se, but rather amodal in their representation, and not tied specifically to orthographic or phonological processes. One possible locus would be a level of lemma representation – an amodal representation of word forms that is tied to syntactic properties but not to specifics as form – a level that has been frequently debated in the psycholinguistics literature (e.g., Caramazza, 1997; Levelt et al., 1999) Marelli et al. (2012) reported a case study of an individual with acquired dyslexia who was better at reading aloud monomorphemic nouns than verbs. When asked to read aloud compound words, performance was impacted by the part of speech of the component word, mirroring the noun/verb dissociation in monomorphemic word reading. This pattern indicates impairment at a lemma level of representation in reading.

Future research will also need to carefully consider the conditions under which morphological processing applies, such as the role of morpheme frequencies and frequency differences between whole words and their embedded morphemes, as well as the syntactic properties of the stems and affixes, which have been previously shown to affect word production in unimpaired and impaired speakers with aphasia (e.g., Baayen et al., 1997; Bertram et al., 2000; Beyersmann et al., 2015; Beyersmann et al., 2018; Biedermann et al., 2013; Biedermann et al., 2017). Moreover, a broader question for future research is whether other tasks may be better suited to investigate the locus of morpheme effects in reading. One of the challenges that the current study faced was that participants completed an oral reading task involving phonological output. One option to build on our findings could be to use a lexical decision task with similar items. While lexical decision tasks are commonly used to investigate morphological processing within the healthy population, the task largely relies on the recording of reaction times. Moreover, the dichotomous nature of the lexical decisions means that the task is more limited in providing a window into different error subtypes, which explains why it is less commonly used within reading impaired participants. Nevertheless, since lexical decision is less susceptible to concerns about a phonological output locus, the task may provide a new avenue for future research into the cognitive neuropsychology of complex word reading.

Conclusions

In sum, the current findings suggest that embedded words – the stems of morphologically complex words and nonwords – are reliably identified during nonword reading, whether or not the embedded word is at the left edge (in the stem + suffix experiment) or the right edge (in the prefix + stem experiment). Affix activation is less prominent during nonword reading, even if embedded in the initial string position (as in the prefix + stem experiment). In this way, we have been able to disentangle effects of stem and affix from effects of position within the word, more clearly demonstrating the distinct roles that different kinds of morphemes play in reading.
