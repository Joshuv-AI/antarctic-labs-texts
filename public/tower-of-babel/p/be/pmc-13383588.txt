A novel microvascular arterio-neural pathway for intraneural ganglion cyst formation secondary to cystic adventitial disease: illustrative case
Authors: Nontaphon Piyawattanametha, Tomas Marek, Godard C. W. de Ruiter, Miguel A. Reina, Kimberly K. Amrami, Manju Kalra, Robert J. Spinner
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BACKGROUNDCystic adventitial disease (CAD) and intraneural ganglion cysts (IGCs) are rare mucinous lesions typically linked to an articular origin via macroscopic conduits. While secondary propagation between vascular and neural systems may occur through shared fascial sheaths, a direct microvascular route has not been previously described.OBSERVATIONSA 60-year-old man presented with progressive left tibial neuropathy. High-resolution 7T MRI and surgery revealed a large popliteal CAD lesion connecting to the knee joint via the middle genicular artery, alongside a secondary tibial IGC lacking an independent articular nerve connection. Targeted articular branch disconnection and cyst decompression yielded significant clinical improvement, with no recurrence on 7-month follow-up 7T MRI.LESSONSThis case expands the unifying articular theory, demonstrating that synovial fluid propagation can exploit a microvascular arterio-neural pathway rather than strictly macroscopic fascial planes. High-resolution 7T MRI is essential for visualizing these subtle connections. Two rare but historically distinct cystic lesions can occur in the popliteal fossa: intraneural ganglion cysts (IGCs) and cystic adventitial disease (CAD).1–3 CAD is characterized by mucinous cyst accumulation within the arterial adventitia that leads to luminal narrowing and intermittent calf claudication; it most commonly occurs in the popliteal artery.4–6 Although traditionally considered separate pathologies, contemporary literature has increasingly linked both conditions to a shared pathogenetic origin rooted in the adjacent joint.1

The unifying articular (synovial) theory has been well established as the primary pathogenetic mechanism for IGCs, most extensively documented in the common peroneal nerve arising from the superior tibiofibular joint.1,7–9 According to this paradigm, synovial fluid from a pathological joint extends outward along an articular nerve branch, following the path of least resistance into the parent nerve and influenced by pressures and pressure fluxes.8,10 Similarly, the formation of CAD has been attributed to an articular origin, wherein joint fluid tracks along capsular vascular branches, such as the middle genicular artery, to accumulate within the arterial adventitia.2,11 In both entities, a direct articular branch, whether neural or vascular, is classically required to serve as the macroscopic anatomical conduit connecting the joint to the affected structure.2,11,12

Because these articular branches share a proximate neurovascular bundle at the joint capsular level, CAD lesions and IGCs have occasionally been shown to coexist as combined cysts.1,13

While secondary cross-system propagation from an adventitial cyst to a nerve has been theorized to occur macroscopically via a shared common neurovascular fascial sheath,12,14 the possibility of a direct microvascular communication has never been proposed.

We present a rare case of coexisting CAD lesion of the popliteal artery and a small IGC of the tibial nerve within the popliteal fossa. Importantly, while the CAD lesion demonstrated a clear connection to the knee joint via the middle genicular artery, the IGC lacked any independent articular nerve branch cystic connection or cystic extension into the tibial nerve. We propose and substantiate a novel mechanism in which the adventitial cyst acted as the primary lesion. Driven by high intracystic pressure, the mucin extended secondarily from the arterial wall along a small connecting vascular branch, ultimately infiltrating the nerve via its microvascular supply to form the IGC. This proposed microvascular arterio-neural pathway represents an indirect route for IGC formation that expands the current boundaries of the articular theory.

Illustrative Case

A 60-year-old active male farmer presented with a progressively worsening left foot neuropathy. His symptoms began abruptly 18 months earlier with an episode of excruciating, shooting pain extending from the left heel to the posterior knee, followed by persistent, painful dysesthesias, numbness, and hypersensitivity on the plantar aspect of the foot. His past medical history was notable for congenital club feet treated remotely, although he had maintained a highly active lifestyle prior to symptom onset.

Neurological examination revealed weakness in left toe and plantar flexion, decreased sensation in the tibial nerve distribution, and marked hyperalgesia of the plantar forefoot. He was unable to perform a single calf rise. Electromyography demonstrated reduced tibial conductions and denervation in the tibial-innervated distal leg muscles, localizing the lesion proximally to the tarsal tunnel but distal to the gastrocnemius innervation.

Preoperative Imaging

Noninvasive vascular studies revealed a popliteal mass compressing the tibial nerve and popliteal vein without baseline fixed arterial stenosis. An initial outside MRI study demonstrated a large popliteal cystic mass causing compression on the neurovascular elements. A high-resolution 7T MRI study was subsequently performed at our institution to better delineate the relationship of the cyst to the adjacent neurovascular structures. The imaging revealed a complex, multiloculated adventitial cyst measuring 6.2 × 2.1 × 1.9 cm surrounding the popliteal artery and extending to circumferentially encase a high-origin anterior tibial artery that connected to the posterior knee joint (Fig. 1A–C). Proximally, a 1.7 × 1.3–cm cyst was noted near the tibial nerve appearing to communicate with the primary cyst via a traversing oblique arterial branch (Fig. 1D and E), and there was some increased hyperintensity proximal to the cyst, presumably related to mechanical irritation of the cyst on the tibial nerve. Advanced chondromalacia involving the three compartments of the knee was seen along with a tear of the medial meniscus. Operative Intervention

The patient underwent surgical exploration via a posterior approach to the popliteal fossa. Distal to the sciatic nerve bifurcation, the tibial nerve was identified and found to be flattened, compressed, and adherent to the underlying complex multilobulated cyst. To our surprise, at the onset, a nerve branch from the tibial nerve at the popliteal fossa was found to have a small cystic component (Fig. 2A, in blue vasoloops). Control of the anterior tibial artery was then obtained more distally as was the popliteal artery more proximally (Fig. 2B, red vasoloops). The larger adventitial component involved the arterial wall of the popliteal and anterior tibial arteries. During the dissection of the popliteal artery, an oblique arterial branch arising from it (Fig. 2B, in red vasoloops) was noted with cyst that could be traced proximally (previously placed in blue vasoloops) directly to the tibial nerve. The red and blue vasoloops distally and proximally on this oblique branch (arterio-nervous connection) in Fig. 2 reflect our convention of choosing a red loop for a vessel (here a branch from the popliteal artery) and a blue loop for a nerve (here a branch from the tibial nerve, which was first thought to be a neural branch). A genicular arterial branch was ligated (Fig. 2C). Then, the middle genicular artery and accompanying structures connecting this cyst to the posterior knee joint were identified, ligated, and disconnected. Intraoperative exploration, supplemented by indocyanine green fluorescence imaging via an operative microscope, confirmed this joint connection. Thorough exploration confirmed no other cyst extensions or distinct articular neural branches communicating with joint space. The cysts were opened, yielding a characteristic jelly-like mucinous substance. A partial excision and marsupialization of the adventitial cyst walls was performed while leaving the adherent arterial portions intact, followed by meticulous neurolysis and resection of the intraneural cyst component.

Retrospective Reinterpretation of MRI

Reinterpretation of the 7T MRI noted that the adventitial cyst appeared to track from the popliteal artery along a traversing small arterial branch vessel toward the tibial nerve. A small amount of cyst could be seen in the paraneurium of the tibial nerve more proximally without extension (Fig. 3). While the primary adventitial cyst had a definitive connection through a small arterial branch to the posterior knee joint, there was no independent cystic articular nerve branch connecting the IGC directly to the joint or cystic extension into the tibial nerve. Postoperative Course

The patient’s immediate postoperative recovery was uncomplicated, with preservation of baseline tibial nerve function. At the 3.5-month follow-up, he reported a 50% improvement in his neuropathic symptoms. Follow-up 7T MRI at 7 months postsurgery demonstrated expected postoperative changes with successful resection of both the intraneural and adventitial cysts and no evidence of recurrence within the nerve, further supporting the efficacy of targeted disconnection (Fig. 4). Discussion

The coexistence of CAD lesions and IGCs is a rare phenomenon that provides critical insights into their shared pathogenesis.1 Historically considered separate entities, similarities between these two cysts were first mentioned by Parkes and Richards.15,16 Their shared formation and propagation can now be understood through the lens of the unifying articular (synovial) theory, which posits that synovial fluid from a degenerative joint escapes through a capsular defect and propagates along the path of least resistance driven by dynamic pressure fluxes.7,10,17 In previously reported cases of coexisting CAD lesions and IGCs, the connection to the joint is typically explained by simultaneous propagation; fluid tracks from the joint along both the capsular blood vessels (forming CAD) and the adjacent articular nerve branch (forming IGC) within the same macroscopic neurovascular bundle.1,13 To explain disproportionate or secondary cyst formation, previous authors have proposed a sequential spread theory via macroscopic structures, wherein an adventitial cyst descends along a vascular sheath and subsequently bursts into a shared common neurovascular fascial sheath (the paraneurium) to bathe and engulf the adjacent nerve.14

Observations

Our case presents a unique anatomical observation that challenges and expands on existing macroscopic theories. We observed a primary CAD lesion with a clear connection to the knee joint, accompanied by a small secondary IGC of the tibial nerve that entirely lacked an independent articular nerve branch connection or cystic propagation from a joint along the articular branch to the tibial nerve. Based on our findings, we propose a novel microvascular arterio-neural route for IGC formation. Rather than spreading through a direct articular nerve branch or a macroscopic fascial sheath, we explain how mucinous fluid, driven by high intracystic pressure from the primary CAD lesion, extended secondarily from the arterial wall along a traversing nutrient artery, ultimately invading the nerve via its microvascular supply (such as the vasa nervorum).

This hypothesis is directly supported by our high-resolution preoperative 7T MRI study, which successfully demonstrated the small connecting vascular branch between the adventitial cyst and the nerve. The arterio-nervous connection was demonstrated by meticulous intraoperative dissection. Although direct histopathological analysis of this specific microscopic tract in our patient was not done to preserve neurovascular integrity, normal anatomical specimens robustly support this proposed mechanism18 (Fig. 5). These reference samples clearly demonstrate a shared vascular branch supplying the nerve within the same neurovascular bundle, confirming previous observations from Sunderland that the tibial nerve is often perforated by a large arterial vessel,19 which can serve as a conduit for secondary cyst propagation. While we cannot definitively exclude the possibility that the CAD lesion and IGC formed primarily from the same joint of origin (the knee), given the lack of cyst in the articular branch and cyst extension along it into the tibial nerve on MRI, we believe this explanation is unlikely. The implications of this case are profound for the continued evolution of the unifying articular theory to support the formation and propagation for CAD but also for IGCs.7 As demonstrated in recent literature, joint connections are frequently missed with standard imaging, leading to the misclassification of cysts as de novo or lacking an articular origin.20,21 This case demonstrates that synovial fluid propagation first extends along the articular branch to the parent nerve or vessel. From there, it may propagate further, using other networks as paths of least resistance. For CAD, this has been shown to involve other genicular arteries.4 Herein, we believe that synovial fluid tracked along an artery supplying the tibial nerve. The existence of this perforating artery has been demonstrated previously19 and was confirmed by histological analysis in this study. Our group has previously shown that vessels can enter nerves through fenestrations,18 and these openings can explain extreme subparaneurial distribution from the subepineurial to subparaneurial compartments, through a burst mechanism, which we have compared to a geyser.22 The present case interestingly shows that cyst can also travel the other way around using the same fenestration following the artery perforating the nerve. As expected, this does not lead to extreme circumferential distribution, which we observed previously for IGCs,23 as in this case of CAD, the cyst travels against an increasing pressure gradient.

Because of this subtle distribution and propagation along a relatively small perforating artery, the identification of this pathway relies heavily on advanced high-resolution imaging.20 Advanced modalities like 7T MRI are crucial for resolving these fine anatomical details, allowing us to visualize nearly invisible cysts and microvascular connections that would otherwise remain occult.24,25

Lessons

Understanding this novel pathway underlying the complex, interconnected cysts in this patient is critical for surgical management. Extensive literature highlights that the failure to identify and disconnect the articular connection is the primary risk factor for cyst recurrence for both CAD and IGC.21,26,27 Surgeons must be highly vigilant during both preoperative imaging review and intraoperative exploration.21,24 Finding an IGC or CAD lesion should immediately prompt a meticulous search for the primary joint connection, as well as any subtle, secondary interconnections between vascular and neural structures.27 Recognizing that cysts can propagate through microscopic traversing vessels emphasizes the need for careful, high-magnification dissection to completely isolate the cyst from its source and prevent the high recurrence rates historically associated with simple cyst decompression.27,28
