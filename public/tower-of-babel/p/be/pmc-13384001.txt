Effects of early life adversity on acute metabolic outcomes during neonatal development
Authors: Hannah E. Lapp, Melissa G. Salazar, Evelyn Deveraux, Pawel K. Misztal, Frances A. Champagne
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Early life adversity has lasting effects on neuroendocrine function and increases risk for psychopathology and metabolic disease. Stress and metabolism are closely intertwined, with critical periods for early life stress overlapping those for metabolic programming. Early life adversity models in rodents have the potential to affect development through multiple pathways, including nutrition, parental behavior, and direct physical stress. In the current study, we investigate how a limited bedding and nesting material (LBN) postnatal environment affects maternal behavior, milk composition, and acute metabolic outcomes in rat offspring. We examined the impact of LBN exposure during two postnatal windows (Early: P0–6 and/or Late: P6–13) and effects of key elements of early experiences (maternal behavior and nutrition) on acute metabolic outcomes, with assessment of sex-specific effects in pups. LBN shifted maternal behavioral phenotype, increased dam milk corticosterone and triglyceride concentration, affected serum metabolic and pituitary hormones in pups, and increased pup estimated daily energy expenditure. Differential gene expression analyses of the liver and arcuate nucleus of the hypothalamus showed timing and sex-specific effects of LBN. LBN predicted expression of genes related to amino acid biosynthesis, protein assembly, and developmental transitions. These effects were associated with individual milk components, pup sex, and pup weight. Collectively, these findings demonstrate that early life adversity alters acute metabolic outcomes through multiple, interacting pathways that may contribute to increased vulnerability to endocrine and metabolic dysfunction later in life.

Introduction

Adverse early life experiences confer heightened risk for a wide range of poor health outcomes across the lifespan and earlier all-cause mortality (Felitti et al., 1998; Kelly-Irving et al., 2013). There is remarkable overlap in the effects of early adverse experiences related to nutritional extremes (over and under nutrition) and other stressors such as poverty, abuse and neglect, and maternal stress on long-term health outcomes (Boersma et al., 2014). When these stressors are experienced during developmentally sensitive periods, they increase vulnerability to poor metabolic outcomes including obesity, diabetes, cardiovascular disease, and metabolic syndrome as well as psychiatric conditions such as schizophrenia and mood disorders (Mcmillen and Robinson, 2005; Seal and Turner, 2021). The overlapping effects of early life nutrition and early life stress on mental and physical health reflect the bidirectional interactions between stress physiology and metabolic regulation (Morris et al., 2015).

Activation of the hypothalamic-pituitary-adrenal (HPA) axis in response to stress initiates a hormonal cascade beginning in the paraventricular nucleus (PVN) of the hypothalamus and ultimately leading to glucocorticoid (GC) release by the adrenal glands. Circulating GCs mobilize energy reserves by promoting glucose availability with chronic GC elevation affecting appetite, feeding, and visceral adipose deposition (Lee et al., 2014; Sominsky and Spencer, 2014). Metabolic homeostasis, including feeding and appetite, are regulated by an array of hormones that act on the brain and periphery with a central role of the arcuate nucleus of the hypothalamus, lateral hypothalamus, and PVN. Leptin, an anorexigenic cytokine hormone secreted primarily by adipose tissue, regulates energy balance and facilitates energy expenditure by acting on the arcuate nucleus (Mainardi et al., 2013; Morton, 2007). GCs and leptin co-regulate each other: leptin acts on several levels of the HPA axis to suppress GC production, while GCs increase leptin expression (Paul and Buchholz, 2023). GCs may also interact with other metabolic hormones, including ghrelin and insulin, to affect food intake and energy balance (Janssen, 2022; Spencer et al., 2012). Collectively, these interactions can account for the significant associations between stress and metabolic dysregulation.

The HPA axis and metabolic systems are immature at birth and undergo substantial developmental changes in the early postnatal period. In rodents, the stress-hyporesponsive period (SHRP) refers to the period from birth through about postnatal day 14 (P14) when the HPA system is actively suppressed to keep adrenocorticotropic hormone (ACTH) and GC levels low in response to stimuli that would typically elicit a stress response (Levine, 1970; Levine et al., 1967; Schmidt, 2019). Maternal care plays a critical role in maintaining the SHRP and exerts life-long effects on HPA function by affecting GC receptor levels in the brain (Weaver et al., 2004), and pup leptin levels also contribute to the SHRP by lowering GCs and regulating HPA response (Paul and Buchholz, 2023; Salzmann et al., 2004). Hypothalamic circuitry regulating energy balance is similarly immature at birth, with arcuate nucleus projections to feeding-related hypothalamic nuclei emerging around P5 in rats (Matsumoto and Arai, 1976; Sominsky et al., 2018). Leptin, although anorexigenic in adulthood, does not suppress feeding in early development and instead promotes hypothalamic neurocircuitry development (Bouret et al., 2004; Proulx et al., 2002), while ghrelin levels increase progressively over the first two postnatal weeks to reach adult levels by weaning (Sominsky et al., 2018). The liver also undergoes a profound shift at birth, transitioning from its prenatal role as the primary hematopoietic source to a central metabolic regulator through fatty acid synthesis and gluconeogenesis (Böhme et al., 1983; Herrera and Amusquivar, 2000; Innis, 2011).

The developmental trajectory of the HPA axis and metabolic systems is subject to influence by postnatal experience. Prolonged maternal separation induces long-term dysregulation of HPA function, affects metabolic hormones including leptin, ghrelin, and insulin, reduces body weight, and alters neuropeptide expression in the arcuate nucleus in rodents (Paul and Buchholz, 2023; Schmidt et al., 2006; Viveros et al., 2010), with both loss of food intake and loss of maternal tactile care contributing to these effects (Oers et al., 1998; Rosenfeld et al., 1993). Other models of chronic early life stress similarly alter leptin, ghrelin, insulin, adipose tissue, and hypothalamic fiber density (Yam et al., 2017a; Yam et al., 2017b). Beyond the direct effects of parental care, early adverse environments may also affect offspring nutrient intake through changes in milk composition. For example, maternal stress can increase milk GC concentration and affect milk lipid content and immune factors (Wijenayake et al., 2023). Milk composition has long-term effects on offspring emotionality, HPA function, body weight, and hypothalamic feeding circuits (Kirk et al., 2009; Langley-Evans et al., 1996; Vogt et al., 2014), and postnatal diet can affect peripheral metabolic development including liver gene expression (Nakagaki et al., 2018). Corticosterone and leptin transferred through maternal milk may further shape pup stress and metabolic systems during early-life sensitive periods (Casabiell et al., 1997; Stead et al., 2022), suggesting that early adversity affects offspring development through both behavioral and nutritional channels.

The present study used the limited bedding and nesting material (LBN) postnatal environmental manipulation during sensitive periods of HPA and metabolic development to examine effects of early adversity on acute metabolic outcomes in rat pups. The LBN model alters the pup microenvironment by increasing thermal stress, changing pup peer interactions in the litter, and changing maternal behavior, although there is considerable variability in maternal response to LBN in rats (Lapp, Bartlett, et al., 2020a, 2020b; Lapp et al., 2024). The goals of this study were: 1) determine the acute effects of LBN on metabolic outcomes in offspring, 2) determine how these effects vary with timing of LBN, 3) identify key elements of early experience that affect metabolic outcomes, and 4) examine sex differences in metabolic outcomes. Pup outcomes include serum metabolic and pituitary hormones, energy expenditure, and transcriptomic analysis of the liver and arcuate nucleus of the hypothalamus. We used maternal behavioral phenotype measures and measures of milk macronutrients and hormones to identify key elements of pup experience responsible for metabolic changes.

Methods

Animals

All animal protocols were approved by the Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee (IACUC) at the University of Texas at Austin and were performed in accordance with IACUC guidelines and regulations (protocol # AUP-2022-00239). Sample sizes were based on previous studies using this model (Lapp et al., 2020a; Moriceau et al., 2009) rather than a formal power calculation. Animals were housed in polycarbonate cages (48 cm × 26.5 cm × 20 cm) with standard wire tops and were kept on a reverse 12:12 h light cycle (lights off at 11am, lights on 11pm). All dams were provided with only Aspen shavings (Nepco) for bedding and nesting material, which can be manipulated to construct nests, with no other enrichment provided. All animals were provided with standard chow (Lab diet 5LL2) and water ad libitum. Two cohorts of 30 adult (P60) Long-Evans female rats and 15 adult (P60) Long-Evans male rats were purchased from Charles River Labs and acclimated to the vivarium in housing groups of three animals for at least two weeks before breeding. During breeding, one receptive female was housed with a breeder male overnight. All dams were socially housed throughout pregnancy until they were singly housed starting on gestational day 18-21 before giving birth. Six females did not become pregnant and were excluded from the study.

Experiment timeline

On P0 (day of birth), pups were culled to five males and five females per litter using anogenital distance to determine sex. Litters were excluded if they had fewer than 5 pups of either sex (n = 10) or were born on a day when no other litters were born, precluding cross-fostering on P6 (n = 5). Litters included in the study were assigned to Control (225 g of bedding) and LBN (50 g of bedding) conditions in order of birth using a pre-generated random sequence, such that each successive litter born within a cohort was assigned the next condition in the sequence (adapted from Moriceau et al., 2009, Fig. 1A). Sequences were stratified by cohort to ensure balanced group sizes within each experimental group. On P6, milk was collected from dams (5:00am-8:00am) and CO2 emissions were measured from pups (1:00pm-4:00pm). This resulted in brief maternal separations for up to 1 h during milk collection (dam removed from the home cage) and 5 min during CO2 emissions testing (1 pup removed at a time). After pup testing on P6 (4:00pm-5:00pm), entire litters were fostered to a new dam in a clean cage in the same or opposite condition resulting in four experimental litter groups reflecting pup exposure to LBN during two exposure periods: LBN-LBN (n = 10), LBN-Control (n = 10), Control-LBN (n = 9), and Control-Control (n = 10). Foster dam assignment was not random and was determined by the conditions of the available litters born on the same day such that only litters with P6 pups were fostered, with the goal of achieving balanced representation across the four experimental groups (LBN-LBN, LBN-Control, Control-LBN, Control-Control). P0-6 LBN exposure occurred prior to the development of axonal projections from the arcuate nucleus to other hypothalamic nuclei involved in feeding regulation, whereas P6-13 LBN exposure occurred during this period of arcuate development, so these exposure periods reflect distinct developmental stages of metabolic development. Dams remained in the same condition for the duration of the experiment. Milk collection and CO2 emission measurements were repeated on P12. On P13, offspring were euthanized via rapid decapitation and brain tissue, liver tissue, and trunk blood were collected from one male and one female pup in each litter (4:00pm to 9:00pm). Male and female pups used for CO2 testing and tissue collection were selected by taking the first male and female pup selected from the nest in the home cage. Due to limited equipment availability, a subset of pups were not able to undergo CO2 testing (P6 litters: Control = 3, LBN = 3; P12 litters: Control-Control = 1, Control-LBN = 2, LBN-Control = 1, LBN-LBN = 1), resulting in a smaller sample size for that measure. Because experimental condition was readily apparent from the cage appearance, the researcher selecting the pup was not blind to condition. Pups were then transported to a separate area for CO2 measurement and tissue collection, where the researcher performing the procedure was blind to condition. Because neonatal pups did not have permanent identification, it was not possible to determine whether pups tested for CO2 emissions on P6, tested for CO2 on P12, and tissue collection on P13 were the same individuals. Sample processing and CO2 measurement calculations were conducted by researchers blind to experimental groups. Home cage recordings

Home cage behavior was recorded and analyzed as reported in Lapp et al. (2024). Briefly, Raspberry Pi 3B + minicomputers equipped with Raspberry Pi Module 1 NoIR cameras were used to record home cage behavior for 24 h (greyscale, 2 fps) on P1, P3, P7, and P9 (https://github.com/lapphe/raspberry_rat). These recording days provided data on maternal behavior in the beginning and middle of the Early LBN and Late LBN phases while avoiding cage change and testing disruptions (P0, P6, and P12). Videos were screened to ensure the camera was placed at the nest end of the cage and data were excluded if the dam moved the nest to the opposite end (P1 = 7 videos, P3 = 7 videos, P7 = 6 videos, P9 = 10 videos). An additional 9 videos were excluded due to technical failures (e.g. lighting failure; P1 = 3 videos, P3 = 5 videos, P7 = 1 video, P9 = 0 videos) that prevented accurate pose estimation, resulting in a total of 38 excluded videos (24%; Supplemental Table 1 and Supplemental Table 2). Videos were automatically scored using the AMBER pipeline (Lapp et al., 2023, 2024). AMBER produced measurements of total behavior durations, mean bout durations, and mean bout intervals for nursing (active nursing: crouched above pups with dam supporting her own weight; and passive nursing: dam on her side and not supporting herself with her limbs while pups are seen attached to nipples), pup-directed licking and grooming (licking any part of a pup's body with contact between the dam's nose and mouth and pup), nest attendance (a portion of the dam's body is in the nest and in contact with pups) for each 24 h video recording. Average nursing latency, defined as the average time from the onset of nest attendance to the onset of nursing for each nest attendance bout in each video, was also calculated for each recording. Because nursing is initiated in response to pup cues during nest attendance bouts, nursing latency reflects maternal responsiveness to pup stimulation within the home cage context, with shorter latencies indicating faster responses to pup solicitation. Key settings and parameters for the AMBER pipeline are described in the Supplemental Methods.

Milk collection

Milk was collected from dams on P6 and P12 to assess milk composition as described in Paul et al. (2015). Milk let down can be induced by oxytocin but is inhibited by stress, so dams were anesthetized with isoflurane (5% induction) prior to the oxytocin injection to collect milk (Lau, 1992). A nose cone was used to maintain anesthesia at 2-3% isoflurane with total anesthesia and recovery time less than 60 min. Once anesthetized, dams were injected with 2IU oxytocin (intraperitoneal). Dam's nipple regions were cleaned with alcohol wipes and warm water. Milk was collected by squeezing the base of the teat and manually expelling the milk to collect it in capillary tubes. After milk collection, dams were returned to their home cage with their pups once fully conscious. The pharmacokinetic profile of isoflurane suggests that the drug is not absorbed by offspring and that nursing can resume immediately after anesthesia (Lee and Rubin, 1993). The researcher conducting the milk collection was not blind to condition.

Enzyme-linked immunosorbent assay (ELISA)

Concentration of lactose (Sigma-Aldrich # MAK017, 1:500 sample dilution), triglycerides (Abcam #AB65336, 1:1000 sample dilution), total protein (Pierce BCA Protein Assay # 23225, 1:50 sample dilution), corticosterone (Enzo Life Sciences ADI-900-097, 1:40 sample dilution), and leptin (Millipore # EXRL-83K, no dilution) in dam milk were measured using ELISA according to the manufacturer's instructions.

CO2 emissions

CO2 exhaled by pups was measured from one male and one female (randomly selected) from each litter to estimate pup total daily energy expenditure. During testing, one pup was placed in a 500 ml flow-through jar for 5 min. Prior to pup measurements, empty jars were sampled to provide background measurements. A flow rate of 800 standard cubic centimeters per minute of filtered sweep air from a hydrocarbon trap (VICI Metronics) was used for the duration of the measurement. The last 15 s of the third minute were used for analysis to allow ample time for the volume to be flushed of any lab air. The CO2 Analyzer (Model LI-850; LI-COR Environmental, 2018) measured CO2 concentrations from flow-through effluent at 1 Hz.

Blood and tissue collection

Following rapid decapitation, 1 mL of pup trunk blood was collected in tubes containing 1:100 protease cocktail inhibitor (Sigma P2714), 1:100 Pefabloc SC (Sigma 11429868001), and DPP inhibitor (Millipore # Dpp4-101). Pup whole livers and brains were extracted and placed in hexanes on dry ice. Blood was allowed to sit for a minimum of 30 min on ice before being spun down at 1000 x g at 4 degrees C for 10 min and serum was aliquoted for metabolic and pituitary assay panels. Tissue and serum were stored at −80 Celsius (C) until processing.

Blood panels

Serum samples were processed using the Milliplex Rat Pituitary (Millipore RPTMAG-86K) and Metabolic Hormone (Millipore RMHMAG-84K) Magnetic Bead panels by the Bioanalytics and Single-Cell Core at UT-Health San Antonio.

Tissue processing

Liver extraction

Liver tissue was processed as previously described (Lee et al., 2022). Approximately 30 mg of liver tissue was extracted into reinforced microvials containing zirconia/silica beads (11079110z, BioSpec) and homogenized in 500ul of lysis buffer (Thermo Fisher Scientific #A27828) with 0.7% beta-mercaptoethanol using a beadbeater (607, Bio Spec). 100ul aliquots of homogenates were used for RNA extraction on the KingFisher Flex (Thermo Fisher #5400630) according to the manufacturer's protocol. RNA quality was determined in a subset of samples using the RNA 6000 Nano Assay with BioAnalyzer (Agilent Technologies) and RNA concentration for all samples was determined with Quant-it RNA High Sensitivity assay kit (Thermo Fisher Scientific #Q33140). Two samples (one male Control-Control and one female Control-Control) had insufficient RNA quality (RIN <5) and were not sent for sequencing. Remaining RNA samples were normalized to 100ng/ul and stored at −80C until sequencing.

Arcuate nucleus sectioning and RNA extraction

Brains were cut into 70 μm sections on a cryostat set at −20C. The arcuate nucleus was dissected using a 0.35 mm core sampling tool (Electron Microscopy Sciences, #NC1310088) from brain slices ranging from bregma −1.8 to −3.12 (Paxinos and Watson, 1998). 120ul of lysis buffer containing 0.7% beta-mercaptoethanol was immediately added to tube containing the tissue and vortexed until homogenized. Samples were frozen at −80C until RNA extraction. RNA was extracted from tissue samples using the MagMax Total RNA isolation kit on Kingfisher Flex system according to manufacturer instructions (MagMax #A27828). RNA quality was determined using RNA 6000 Nano Assay and RNA concentration was determined with Quant-it RNA High Sensitivity assay kit. Samples with a RIN <5 or RNA concentration <10 ng/ul were not sent for sequencing (females: Control-Control = 2, Control-LBN = 1, LBN-Control = 2, LBN-LBN = 1, males: Control-Control = 2, Control-LBN = 1, LBN-Control = 3, LBN-LBN = 2). Remaining RNA samples were normalized to 10ng/ul and stored at −80C until sequencing.

Transcriptomic processing

Liver and arcuate nucleus samples were Tag-sequenced at the Genome Sequence and Analysis Facility at the University of Texas at Austin. Tag-sequencing is designed to measure polyadenylated transcripts yielding data for differential gene expression analysis in well annotated genomes (Lohman et al., 2016; Meyer et al., 2011). Reads were sequenced on the NovaSeq 6000 SR100 with minimum reads of four million and target reads of five million. Raw data were preprocessed to remove adapter sequences, PCR duplicates, and low-quality reads using cutadapt v. 2.8 (Martin, 2011) and FastQC v. 0.11.9 was used to check quality control of the sample data (Andrews, 2010). All samples passed QC checks and were used in the analysis. Preprocessed reads were then aligned to the rat genome (rnor6) using STAR (Dobin et al., 2013) and raw counts were obtained.

Data analysis

Maternal behavior PCA

Principal component analysis (PCA) was conducted on home cage maternal behavior received by pups during the early (P1 and P3) and late (P7 and P9) recording days as a dimension reduction technique. Maternal behavior measures were first averaged for P1 and P3 (early maternal behavior) and P7 and P9 (late maternal behavior) recordings, resulting in 20 maternal behavior measurements. The majority of dams/litter had 3 or 4 videos with useable data (Supplemental Table 1) and Fisher's exact tests with simulated p-values (Monte Carlo, 10,000 replicates) confirmed that missing videos did not differ significantly by Early LBN exposure (p = .880), Late LBN exposure (p = .149), or postnatal day (p = .607) indicating that missingness was balanced across conditions and recording days (Supplemental Table 2). Missing measurements due to missing videos (10-15% per measure) were imputed using random forest imputation with the missForest function of the missForest package in R. Although imputation may subtly influence the covariance structure underlying the PCA, the low proportion of missing values per measure and the balanced pattern of missingness across conditions minimizes the risk of systematic bias. Prior to PCA, data were z-scored and assumption checks were conducted. Bartlett's test of sphericity confirmed sufficient correlation structure to justify dimension reduction (χ2(190) = 716.11, p < .001). No multivariate outliers were detected based on Mahalanobis distance, and no variables were removed due to multicollinearity. PCA was run using the prcomp function in R without rotation and PCA loadings are reported as eigenvectors extracted from the component coefficient matrix. Given the diffuse loading structure characteristic of behavioral data, variables with absolute loadings greater than 0.25 were considered meaningful contributors to each component for interpretation purposes.

Energy expenditure analysis

CO2 emission rates were calculated from the flow rate of the sweep air and from measured volume mixing ratios which were converted to mass concentrations assuming constant temperature and pressure. Incoming CO2 concentrations were subtracted using background measurements. Exhalation flow rates of CO2 in units of milliliters per minute were calculated using the following equation:V˙CO2=ER×1ρCO2÷60minhourWhere ER is the calculated mass emission rate of CO2 in milligrams per hour and ρCO2 is the density of CO2 at room temperature. Daily energy expenditure (DEE) in units of kilocalories was estimated using the following equation:DEE=∑(D×V˙CO2E×R)i×1,440minday÷4.184kJkcal

Where D is the percentage of total macronutrient content of the milk consumed by each pup, E is the volume of O2 consumed per kilojoule produced, and R is the ratio of CO2 produced to O2 consumed for each of i macronutrients (carbohydrates, protein, and lipids; Even and Nadkarni, 2012). Sampling was limited to 5-min measurement periods which were then extrapolated to estimate the total daily energy expenditure for each pup.

Differential gene expression analysis

Differentially expressed gene (DEG) analysis was conducted using the limma package in R in male and female datasets separately (Smyth, 2005). First, genes with less than 10 counts across all samples were removed. DEG analysis with voom transformation was used to identify DEGs for three comparisons: LBN-LBN vs. Control-Control, LBN-Control vs. Control-Control, and Control-LBN vs. Control-Control. Raw p-values were adjusted using empirical false discovery rate (eFDR; Storey and Tibshirani, 2003) by permutating sample labels 5000 times to obtain a null distribution. Exploratory DEGS (unadjusted p value < 0.05) and significant DEGs (eFDR <0.05) are reported as indicated in the results. Gene ontology (GO) enrichment analysis was used to explore biological processes related to DEGs using Enrichr with the full gene list as the background (Chen et al., 2013; Kuleshov et al., 2016; Xie et al., 2021, Fig. 1B).

Candidate gene analysis

A list of 78 candidate genes was compiled a priori to examine expression of genes related to neuropeptide and neurotransmitter signaling, synaptic plasticity, axon growth, and neurotrophic factors in the arcuate nucleus (Bedont et al., 2015; Bouret, 2022; Burbridge et al., 2016; Molnár et al., 2016; Tucker et al., 2007; Vernes et al., 2011; Zhang et al., 2024). Only 57 candidate genes were present in the data after filtering low counts.

Weighted gene co-expression network analysis (WGCNA)

WGCNA was performed in R using the WGCNA package (Langfelder and Horvath, 2008) with limma-normalized male and female data (Fig. 1B). For both the liver and arcuate nucleus, the softpower threshold was set to 7 based on the scale-free topology criterion (Zhang and Horvath, 2005) and signed hybrid mode with a minimum model size set of 30 was used to construct modules. Regression models were run in male and females separately to predict module eigengene expression from LBN exposure, maternal behavior, and milk components with batch included as a covariate to determine modules of interest. Two-way ANOVAs (sex and Late LBN) were run as post-hoc tests for modules with LBN exposure as a significant predictor. Module membership was determined and intramodular connectivity was determined for each module of interest. The top 5 genes with the highest intramodular connectivity score are reported as hub genes. GO analysis was run on genes in modules of interest using Enrichr.

Statistical analysis

Statistical analyses were conducted using R 4.3.3. For all mixed models, normality of residuals and homoscedasticity were verified by visual inspection of Q-Q plots and residuals versus fitted value plots, and normality was confirmed using Shapiro-Wilk tests prior to analysis. Litter was considered the unit of analysis for data collected from pups, with data from only one female and one male pup per litter included for each analysis. Mixed effects models on pup data that included data from both sexes had litter ID nested within cohort included as a random factor to account for non-independence of littermates and cohort variation. Mixed effects models stratified by pup sex (serum hormones, transcriptomic expression) or for dam data (milk measures) included cohort as a random factor. Early LBN, Late LBN, and Early X Late LBN effects on all PCs accounting for at least 5% for variance were evaluated using linear mixed effects models with cohort included as a random factor. Satterthwaite's method was used to estimate degrees of freedom for linear mixed effects models. Model selection was performed using sample-size corrected AIC (AICc) where noted, calculated using the MuMIn package (Burnham and Anderson, 2002). Models differing by less than 2 AICc units were considered equivalent, in which case the simpler additive model was retained. Statistical significance was set at α = .05 for all analyses. Error bars in figures are reported as mean ± SEM. Results are exploratory and were not corrected for multiple comparisons. Therefore, they should be interpreted with caution.

Dam P12 milk corticosterone and P12 triglyceride data violated the assumption of normality (p < .05), so log-transformations were applied prior to analysis. Linear mixed model analysis with the assay plate as a random factor was used to assess the effects of dam LBN exposure on milk macronutrient and hormone concentrations separately on P6 and P12 measures. For several models, the plate random effect variance was estimated at zero; these models were retained with the random effect structure intact. Pearson correlations were run to examine relationships between different milk components and between milk components and Early caregiving attenuation, Late caregiving attenuation, and Bout brevity PCs.

Group differences in pup daily energy expenditure and CO2 emissions were analyzed using separate linear mixed effects models for P6 and P12 data. For P6 outcomes, a single additive model was fit including Early LBN exposure, pup sex, and pup body weight as fixed effects. For P12 outcomes, two models were compared: an additive model including Early LBN exposure, Late LBN exposure, pup sex, and pup body weight, and an interaction model that additionally included an Early × Late LBN exposure interaction term. Effects of Early caregiving attenuation, Late caregiving attenuation, Bout brevity PCs, and milk hormones were evaluated in separate P6 and P12 linear mixed models with a gaussian distribution with pup sex and pup body weight as fixed effects. Relationships between energy expenditure and milk macronutrients were not explored because energy expenditure estimates are derived from macronutrient measures.

Serum hormones with more than 50% of samples below the limit of detection were not analyzed (ACTH, FSH, LH, TNF-α, prolactin). Hormone concentrations were modeled using generalized linear mixed models (GLMMs) with a gamma distribution and log link, to account for the positive, right-skewed distributions of hormone data. Pup serum hormone concentrations were analyzed in two stages. First, the effects of early and late LBN exposure on each hormone were examined. Each model included Early LBN exposure, Late LBN exposure, pup sex, and pup body weight as fixed effects. For each hormone, an additive model was compared to an interaction model including Early × Late LBN exposure. Second, to examine whether maternal behavior, milk composition, and metabolic variables predicted pup hormone concentrations, a series of GLMMs was run separately for males and females due to pronounced sex differences identified in the first analysis. Each model included a single predictor of interest and pup body weight as a covariate. Predictors included Early caregiving attenuation, Late caregiving attenuation, Bout brevity, milk corticosterone, leptin, lactose, protein, and triglyceride concentrations at P6 and P12, and pup energy expenditure at P6 and P12. Models were fit for each combination of predictor and hormone outcome.

To assess effects of predictor variables (behavior PCs, LBN exposure, energy expenditure, and milk measures) on module eigengene expression for the liver and arcuate nucleus transcriptomic data and arcuate candidate genes, linear mixed models with a gaussian distribution were run with a single predictor variable in separate models stratified by sex. For modules showing significant effects of LBN exposure, 2x2 ANOVAs (Sex x LBN exposure) were used as follow-up tests for sex differences and LBN effects. For arcuate candidate gene analysis, results were filtered to only significant relationships and the top 10 genes were selected based on largest absolute estimates.

To examine whether the effects of early-life experience and maternal factors on liver and arcuate gene module expression were direct or mediated through maternal behavior variables, we used path analysis (structural equation modeling) with the lavaan and piecewiseSEM packages (Lefcheck, 2016; Rosseel, 2012). Piecewise SEM estimates each component of the causal model as a separate regression equation, allowing the use of mixed models to account for the nested data structure. Continuous predictors were standardized prior to analysis to allow comparison of effect sizes across predictors on different scales. LBN exposure variables were dummy coded as “0” for Control and “1” for LBN since piecewiseSEM was not designed for categorical variables (Lefcheck, 2024). For each module of interest, we specified a set of a priori candidate models representing alternative causal structures based on prior work suggesting LBN effects on pups are through effects on maternal behavior. Specifically, the liver pink and purple modules, which were both predicted by LBN exposure and a maternal behavior measure, were tested to determine whether the effects of Late LBN on eigengene expression were direct, mediated through Late caregiving attenuation, or both. We first assessed whether each model exhibited adequate global fit using the χ2 statistic (p > .05) or Fisher's C statistic if submodels failed to converge and the χ2 statistic was not reported. Acceptable models were then compared using AICc. Tests of directed separation were used to evaluate whether excluded paths were justified.

Results

Maternal behavior principal component analysis and relationship to LBN exposure

The effects of LBN on specific maternal behaviors (total duration, bout intervals, and bout durations) have been previously reported (Lapp et al., 2024) and raw data are presented in Supplemental Fig. 1. LBN increased pup-directed maternal behaviors (nest attendance, nursing, licking and grooming), reduced bout intervals, and increased nursing latency on P3 and reduced nursing latency on P6. PCA of maternal behavior received by pups showed seven PCs that each explained at least 5% of the variance (Supplemental Fig. 2A). Principal component 1 accounted for 27.23% of the variance with top loadings that included positive loadings of maternal behavior total durations and negative loadings of bout intervals on P1 and P3 (Fig. 2A–B, Supplemental Fig. 2B). We refer to this PC as “Early caregiving attenuation”, where higher values reflect less pup-directed behavior on P1 and P3. Condition analysis of Early caregiving attenuation scores showed a strong significant effect of Early LBN exposure (Fig. 2C; β = −3.662, SE = 0.751, t(35.00) = −4.876, p < 0.001). Likewise, principal component 2, which accounted for 16.51% of the variance, had top loadings that included positive loadings of maternal behavior total durations and negative loadings of bout intervals for P7 and P9 pup-directed behaviors and was significantly affected by Late LBN exposure (Fig. 2D; Supplemental Fig. 2B; β = −1.882, SE = 0.746, t(35.00) = −2.523, p = .016). We refer to this PC as “Late caregiving attenuation”, where higher values reflect less pup-directed behavior on P7 and P9. There were no other statistically significant main effects or interactions of Early or Late LBN on PCs1-7 (all p > .05). Principal component 4, which accounted for 10.36% of variance, had top loadings primarily comprised of negative loadings of mean bout durations and will be referred to as “Bout brevity”, where high values of Bout brevity correspond to shorter bouts of pup-directed behaviors. Although Early and Late LBN exposure did not significantly affect Bout brevity (Fig. 2E; p > .05), shorter bout durations have been proposed as a key mechanism linking fragmented maternal care to adverse offspring outcomes in the LBN model (Molet et al., 2016). Effects of LBN timing on the liver transcriptome

Gene expression in the male and female liver varied depending on timing of LBN exposure (Fig. 5C and D), with the majority of DEGs unique to each exposure condition and similar number of DEGS for each type of exposure in males and females. Only 9-15% of DEGS were differentially expressed in all LBN conditions relative to the same-sex Control-Control pups. Cfhr1 (females logFC = 1.03, eFDR <0.001; males logFC = 1.01, eFDR <0.001) and Aass (females logFC = 0.88, eFDR <0.001; males logFC = 0.85, eFDR <0.001) were higher in male and female pups in Control-LBN compared to same-sex Control-Control pups. Atp9a (females logFC = −0.38, eFDR <0.001; males logFC = −0.40, eFDR <0.001) was lower and Acsl4 (females logFC = 0.49, eFDR <0.001; males logFC = 0.52, eFDR <0.001) was higher in male and female pups in both LBN-Control and LBN-LBN and in Control-LBN and LBN-LBN respectively. The top three GO terms for overlapping and LBN-timing specific DEGs are shown in Supplemental Fig. 5B (females) and 5C (males). GO terms were largely sex-specific, except for Negative regulation of phosphate metabolic process which was a top term for DEGS overlapping in all three LBN exposures in both males and females.

Discussion

Stressful experiences occurring during early life require substantial energy expenditure to maintain homeostasis, potentially diverting resources away from critical growth and maturation processes during developmentally sensitive periods. In the current study, we found that LBN during the first two postnatal weeks exerted acute effects on rat pup metabolic outcomes, including estimated energy expenditure, circulating metabolic and pituitary hormones, and transcriptomic profiles of the liver and arcuate nucleus. Many of these effects were specific to timing of LBN, with peripheral measures impacted by exposure occurring both early and later within the postnatal period and the hypothalamus more heavily impacted by the later LBN exposure. Limited bedding had multifaceted effects on dams and pups including changes to dam milk composition, and here we identify several key elements of early experience contributing to the development of metabolic endpoints. Finally, we observed sex differences across many of these LBN-associated outcomes. Sex differences were particularly pronounced in the arcuate nucleus transcriptome, where gene modules in females were more susceptible to influence of early experiences.

Acute effects of LBN on pup metabolism

Exposure to LBN during P0–13 altered pup estimated energy expenditure, circulating hormone levels, and transcriptomic profiles in both the liver and arcuate nucleus. Estimated daily energy expenditure was elevated when measured proximal to the stressor (Early LBN at P6; Late LBN at P12) but was reduced at later time points relative to LBN exposure (Early LBN at P12). This pattern suggests distinct acute versus delayed responses to the stressor, where initial energy expenditure increases to mobilize resources to restore homeostasis (Seal and Turner, 2021). Maternal corticosterone, transferred via milk, may contribute to this effect since corticosterone increases blood glucose through actions in the liver, pancreas, smooth muscle, and white adipose tissue to increase energy supply during acute stress (Seal and Turner, 2021). However, this early increase in energetic output may incur a cost, leading to subsequent energy depression as reserves are depleted. A similar effect was previously observed where a marker of brown adipose tissue (BAT) activation, an energetically expensive thermoregulatory mechanism, was initially increased in LBN females relative to controls, consistent with mild cold stress induction by LBN. Later measures of BAT activation were reduced despite continued exposure to the LBN thermal environment and reduction in core body temperature until approximately P9 (Lapp et al., 2024; Lapp et al., 2020a). Reduced energy expenditure at later stages could indicate a lower metabolic rate, potentially freeing resources previously devoted to immediate stress responses (Pettersen et al., 2016). This idea is corroborated by a previous study showing decreased P10 blood glucose levels in male and female rat pups exposed to LBN with a wire mesh platform on P2-10 (Moussaoui et al., 2016). This adaptive reallocation of energetic resources may help sustain growth and development under chronic stress conditions. The observed changes in serum hormones and transcriptional profiles of LBN-exposed pups are consistent with this interpretation and analogous work in adult animals demonstrates long-term alterations in hormonal regulation, glucose metabolism, and mitochondrial dysfunction that contribute to heightened risk for metabolic syndrome following chronic stress exposure (Nirupama et al., 2012; van der Kooij, 2020).

After birth, the liver undergoes a functional transformation to become the central regulator of glucose homeostasis, coinciding with the dietary shift to energy derived from maternal milk (Girard, 1990). The onset of gluconeogenesis is critical to maintaining blood glucose levels in the switch to the new relatively low-carbohydrate diet by producing glucose from non-carbohydrate precursors during intervals between feedings (Li et al., 2023). LBN affected expression of genes related to essential processes associated with gluconeogenesis including amino acid biosynthesis, which provides critical non-essential amino acid substrates such as glutamine (Zhang et al., 2018). Expression of genes related to regulation of phosphate metabolism and macromolecule glycosylation, processes which contribute to foundational liver functions critical for health and maintaining energy balance, were also disrupted in both sexes (Supplemental Fig. 5A; Blomme et al., 2009; Yasuda et al., 2023). These transcriptional changes could suggest early impairments in liver-mediated glucose homeostasis that are similar to chronic stress-induced dysregulation of gluconeogenesis that leads to hyperglycemia and insulin insensitivity in adult animals (Nirupama et al., 2012). Because uncontrolled gluconeogenesis is a major contributor to type 2 diabetes, these transcriptional signatures may represent early markers of increased susceptibility to metabolic syndrome later in life (Petersen & Shulman, 2018). Additionally, alterations in processes such as the regulation of neutrophil migration suggest that LBN disrupts the concurrent postnatal maturation of the liver as an immune-regulatory organ.

LBN also altered the arcuate nucleus transcriptome, although to a lesser extent than in the liver. Generally, LBN impacted transcription of genes contributing to biological processes involved in developmental maturation (i.e. proximal/distal pattern formation, astrocyte differentiation, positive regulation of podosome assembly, negative regulation of microtubule polymerization) as well as those involved in gene regulation (i.e. protein sumoylation, chromatin looping; Supplemental Fig. 6B and 6C). These transcriptional changes may be mediated by LBN-induced alterations in circulating hormones, as arcuate nucleus development is highly sensitive to leptin and ghrelin signaling, which were respectively reduced and elevated following LBN exposure (Bouret et al., 2004; Sominsky et al., 2018; Steculorum et al., 2015). Candidate gene analysis further revealed effects of LBN on genes associated with neuropeptide signaling (Ntsr1, Sst, Calca, Nts, Pdyn, Glp1r, Ramp3) and axonal growth (Tenm3). In males, expression of the arcuate “brown” module eigengene, which was enriched for cilia-related genes, was predicted by P6 corticosterone levels. Primary cilia in the arcuate play key roles in nutrient sensing and hormonal responsiveness, and their developmental disruption has been linked to adult obesity (Lee et al., 2020; Leon-Mercado et al., 2017; Yang et al., 2021). Although these effects were specific to LBN timing and pup sex, they nevertheless demonstrate that LBN-induced transcriptional changes for genes critical to energy homeostasis are detectable before arcuate nucleus projections and circuits supporting metabolic homeostasis have matured (Bouret and Simerly, 2007).

Effects on metabolic measures depend on timing of LBN exposure

Few previous studies have compared the effects of different LBN exposure periods, and none have specifically focused on LBN timing effects on pup metabolic outcomes. After controlling for pup body weight, we found that concentrations of four circulating metabolic and pituitary hormones measured on P13 were more strongly affected by Early LBN than by Late LBN exposure (Table 1). Another study with mice subject to P2-9 LBN with wire mesh found no effect of LBN on active (acetylated) ghrelin and LBN-induced increases in insulin on P10, which were reduced by Early LBN and unaffected by LBN respectively in our study (Yam et al., 2017b). This disagreement in findings may be attributed to methodology differences including species, use of wire mesh platform, and LBN timing. In adults, serum hormone levels are sensitive to nutrient intake, and the observed changes in milk corticosterone and triglyceride levels in LBN dams on P6 (but not P12) may contribute to the greater hormonal effects of Early LBN (Lu et al., 2021; Vella and Camilleri, 2017). Notably, Atp9a, a gene involved in protein trafficking and differentially expressed in the liver following Early LBN in both sexes, has been implicated in Neurodevelopmental Disorder with Poor Growth and Behavioral Abnormalities, in which affected individuals display failure to thrive and poor overall growth (Vogt et al., 2022). Consistent with this, Pardo et al. (2025) reported that LBN exposure on P2-4, but not P5-6 or P7-9, reduced litter body weight in Sprague-Dawley rats using a wire mesh floor variation of the LBN paradigm. Effects of LBN on maternal behavior also appear to be weaker during later postnatal days compared to the early postpartum period (Lapp et al., 2024; Pardo et al., 2025). Similarly, the thermal effects of LBN are more pronounced in younger pups, as many physiological thermoregulatory mechanisms do not fully emerge until after P6 (Lapp et al., 2024; Shelton and Alberts, 2018). Together with our findings, this suggests that early postnatal LBN exposure exerts stronger effects on peripheral metabolic systems undergoing rapid developmental transitions. These systems lack fully developed mechanisms to cope with adverse experiences at a time when maternal behavior and maternal milk are also more susceptible to environmental influence.

Linear mixed model analysis of WGCNA modules in the liver and arcuate nucleus revealed that Late, but not Early, LBN exposure predicted eigengene expression of select modules (liver pink and purple; arcuate greenyellow and grey60). The influence of Late LBN on the arcuate transcriptome may reflect the maturation of arcuate hypothalamic projections during this developmental window, consistent with the regulatory and developmental GO terms enriched in the affected modules (Matsumoto and Arai, 1976; Sominsky et al., 2018). Notably, these effects were observed in only females, suggesting that females may be more susceptible than males to the effects of environmental adversity on neural circuitry underlying metabolic homeostasis during this period. At the level of individual genes, some arcuate candidates were selectively affected by either Early or Late LBN in a sex-specific manner, suggesting that the timing of LBN exposure exerts distinct, non-overlapping effects on transcriptional regulation. A prior investigation of P2-9 LBN effect on P9 hypothalamic expression of 14 genes related to fatty acid metabolism, hormone receptors, and neuropeptides in mice found higher expression of Lepr with LBN in both sexes (Ruigrok et al., 2021). In contrast, Lepr expression in the arcuate nucleus was unaffected by Early or Late LBN in our study (Supplemental Table 7). Our hormonal and transcriptomic measures were collected shortly after Late LBN exposure and it remains unclear whether Early LBN would have produced stronger effects on these outcomes had parallel measures been obtained at P6.

Individual variability in LBN effects on maternal behavior and dam milk composition

LBN litters had lower scores on both Early and Late caregiving attenuation, indicating that they received more pup-directed behaviors from dams with shorter intervals between bouts. This is consistent with previously reported LBN-induced increases in nursing, nest attendance, and licking and grooming, and suggests that the stress of limited bedding increased overall quantity of pup-directed care (Eck et al., 2020; Fuentes et al., 2014; Lapp et al., 2020b; Lapp et al., 2024; Shupe and Clinton, 2021). This increase in caregiving quantity may reflect compensatory maternal responses to changes in pup signals and underscores the importance of examining individual variability and specific dimensions of maternal behavior rather than relying solely on group-level comparisons.

The PCA approach builds on a tradition of using PCA to reduce correlated behavioral measures into interpretable components that predict biologically meaningful outcomes across animal species (Budaev, 2010; Tamin et al., 2023; Vannoni et al., 2014). While LBN clearly altered maternal behavior, maternal behavior phenotypes showed considerable overlap between LBN and Control dams. Reducing the dimensionality of correlated maternal behavior measures into orthogonal components allowed us to capture the full spectrum of individual variation in maternal behavior and to identify which aspects of that variation (early versus late caregiving quantity and bout length) were most predictive of pup outcomes. The sensitivity of this approach to account for individual variability is further illustrated by the relationship between maternal behavior and milk corticosterone: although Bout brevity did not differ at the group level, shorter mean bout length was significantly associated with higher milk corticosterone on P12, consistent with expected effect of LBN-induced maternal stress on dam hormones and behavior and demonstrating that individual variability can obscure group-level effects. Notably, we only investigated three maternal behavior PCs that collectively accounted for ∼54% of the total variance in maternal behavior, leaving more than half unaccounted for in our analyses. The remaining PCs may capture ethologically meaningful dimensions of maternal care organization, including temporal contrasts in care across lactation, that were beyond the scope of the present study but represent important directions for future work examining how the fine-grained temporal structure of maternal behavior shapes offspring development.

LBN also altered triglyceride and corticosterone levels in dam milk on P6, implicating maternally derived hormones and macronutrients as additional potential mediators of LBN effects on pups alongside changes in maternal care. Maternal corticosterone can elevate corticosterone levels in serum and the brains (which contain GR) of pups at a developmental stage when endogenous corticosterone is typically low (Brummelte et al., 2010; Rosenfeld et al., 1988). Tight regulation of endogenous GC concentration during the SHRP is thought to protect the developing brain from GC effects on neurogenesis and neural growth; thus, increased exposure via milk may disrupt brain development in regions containing GR, including the hypothalamus (Schmidt, 2019). Fatty acids, in addition to serving as the most energy-dense macronutrient, play essential roles in neural growth, synaptic signaling, and cell differentiation (Uauy and Dangour, 2006). Accordingly, the elevated triglyceride levels in LBN dams may have broad developmental consequences for pups. Future studies should examine whether environmental adversity alters the composition of specific fatty acids, micronutrients, the milk microbiome, and bioactive factors such as milk-derived extracellular vesicles, which could be additional pathways of maternal transmission (Wijenayake et al., 2023).

Key elements of early experience contributing to metabolic outcomes

By examining maternal behavior and milk measures, we were able to disentangle specific effects of key components of early influential experiences on acute metabolic outcomes. Although LBN-induced changes in maternal behavior are often considered the primary driver of LBN effects on pups, maternal behavior in this study was associated with a minority of outcomes, and these relationships were largely dependent on developmental timing and sex. This more limited influence may reflect our focus on metabolic outcomes, which are likely to be shaped more strongly by other aspects of early experience, such as nutrient intake. Accordingly, milk composition exerted broader effects than maternal behavior on many of our endpoints. The distinct relationships between P6 and P12 milk components and transcriptional profiles in the liver versus arcuate nucleus align with the developmental timing of these tissues: the liver undergoes substantial maturation immediately after birth, making it highly sensitive to nutrient input across the entire developmental period examined here (Patel and Srinivasan, 2006), whereas the arcuate nucleus projections develop starting about a week later, rendering it more likely to be influenced by later milk content or influenced by early milk indirectly through secondary pathways (Cottrell et al., 2009). Milk corticosterone was a significant predictor of eigengene expression of a few arcuate modules, potentially affecting processes including translational regulation, protein synthesis, and ciliary assembly and function in a sex-specific manner. These predictive relationships suggest a potential role for elevated corticosterone exposure, as opposed to variation in maternal care, during the SHRP in shaping some LBN-related outcomes, though mechanistic studies would be required to establish causality. Because corticosterone increases gluconeogenesis and lipogenesis, excess exposure during a period when endogenous levels are typically low could prime metabolic systems for long-term dysfunction (Seal and Turner, 2021). Body weight had especially large effects on arcuate and liver eigengene expression. Effects of body weight likely reflect not only differences in overall size but also in body composition, particularly reduced white adipose tissue following LBN, which plays a central role in glucose and lipid metabolism as well as metabolic hormone secretion (Speakman, 2005; Yam et al., 2017a). These findings underscore the potential for early adversity to shape metabolism via hormonal and nutrition exposure through maternal milk and open opportunities for intervention through maternal diet. Indeed, prior studies have successfully mitigated some effects of early adversity by manipulating maternal intake of polyunsaturated fatty acids, methionine, and B vitamins (Bernardi et al., 2013; Naninck et al., 2017; Yam et al., 2019).

Path analysis was used to test whether the effects of LBN exposure on module eigengene expression were direct or mediated through maternal behavior variables. For the liver pink module, path analysis revealed that Late LBN had a strong direct effect on eigengene expression that was independent of its effect on Late caregiving attenuation, suggesting that the programming of hepatic amino acid metabolism genes occurs through a pathway other than altered caregiving quality. In contrast, for the liver purple module, the effect of Late LBN on eigengene expression was fully mediated by Late caregiving attenuation, indicating that the influence of Late LBN on genes related to mRNA splicing and gene regulation is contingent on changes in maternal behavior rather than a direct effect of the LBN condition itself. Together, these results demonstrate that LBN exposure programs liver gene expression through at least two distinct mechanisms: one that operates independently of maternal behavior and one that is transmitted through the caregiving environment.

Sex differences in metabolic outcomes

There are prevalent sex differences in energy balance, energy intake, and metabolic disease susceptibility, so it is not surprising that we observed sex differences in several of our outcomes (Tramunt et al., 2020). Seven metabolic and pituitary hormones in serum were lower in males than females, consistent with previous reports (e.g. Matsuda et al., 1997). Males, however, had higher levels of growth hormone, which is regulated by testosterone and acts on the liver to regulate transcription and contribute to sex differences in primary liver functions (Ramirez et al., 2012). Although there was high agreement among differentially expressed genes by LBN groups between sexes, there were also several DEGs that were sex-specific and differences in predictors of module eigengene expression, which is consistent with studies showing sex differences in liver response to nutritional stress (Maggi, 2022). Late caregiving attenuation predicted eigengene expression of multiple liver modules exclusively in females, while Early caregiving attenuation predicted expression of the same modules exclusively in males, suggesting there may be sex-specific temporal dissociation in the relationship between maternal behavior quantity and hepatic gene expression such that female offspring liver transcriptome is more sensitive to late lactation caregiving whereas male offspring liver transcriptome is more sensitive to early lactation caregiving. Similarly, the effects of LBN on the arcuate nucleus transcriptome were largely sex-specific, with little overlap in the top candidate genes except for Tenm3, a gene that is important for establishing synaptic connections. Predictors of arcuate module eigengene expression were entirely sex-dependent, with more associations in females. The arcuate nucleus has testosterone-dependent ER-alpha expression in the postnatal period and synapse formation has been shown to be modified by neonatal estrogen, which contribute to sex differences in arcuate function and may be related to sex differences in developmental programming of obesity (Arai and Matsumoto, 1978; Cortes et al., 2021; MacKay et al., 2022). Our previous work has shown that LBN exposure increases circulating testosterone in females, which could contribute to the shift in the female LBN eigengene expression of the greenyellow module, which was associated with key neural developmental processes, towards more male-like expression levels (Lapp et al., 2024).

Strengths and limitations

By assessing a wide range of metabolic, hormonal, and transcriptomic endpoints, we built a multidimensional picture of how early adversity shapes both peripheral and central processes underlying energy balance. Importantly, we examined two key components of early experience, maternal behavior and milk composition, using approaches that accounted for individual variability between dams. Multiple, complementary measures of metabolic state allowed us to assess relationships between outcomes, and the use of path analyses helped clarify direct and indirect effects on pup physiology. A further strength was the ability to examine the timing of adversity, distinguishing between early and late exposure periods, which aligned with distinct developmental windows.

These strengths are balanced by several methodological limitations. While the maternal behavior principal components included loadings for nursing duration, the time a dam spends in a nursing posture does not necessarily reflect milk transfer, especially given evidence that stress can inhibit milk production (Negoro et al., 1987). Future studies will need to disentangle the relative influence of nursing behavior, milk intake, and total pup-directed care on developmental outcomes. Furthermore, because P13 sampling was more temporally distant from Early LBN than from Late LBN, the observed effects may have differed had Early LBN data also been collected on P6. Milk collection and behavioral testing on P6 and P12 required separation of the dam and pups, so it should be noted that pups were exposed to a form of maternal separation in addition to LBN that may affect the outcomes measured, although this separation applied to all groups. Additionally, although we treated the relationship between pup weight and energy expenditure as unidirectional (weight predicting expenditure) for interpretability, this relationship is likely bidirectional, as energy expenditure also influences weight trajectories over time (Franczak et al., 2024). Our method for estimating daily energy expenditure has inherent constraints: it does not account for the thermic effect of feeding, was based on indirect estimates of oxygen consumption, and did not include pup activity during measurement (Speakman et al., 2013). Finally, while the high-dimensional methods we used are well-suited for generating hypotheses and identifying candidate pathways, the relatively small sample size may limit statistical power, model stability, and generalizability. Specifically, PCA loadings and WGCNA module composition may be unstable at this sample size and should be interpreted as exploratory rather than confirmatory. Independent replication in a larger cohort will be necessary to validate the specific modules, pathways, and mediating relationships identified here. Additionally, because analyses were not corrected for multiple comparisons given the exploratory nature of the study, the probability of false positives is elevated, and all findings should be interpreted with appropriate caution pending replication. These considerations highlight the need for future studies to further parse the complex mechanisms linking early adversity to metabolic development.

Conclusions and future directions

This study provides evidence that early adversity exerts acute and timing-specific effects on metabolic function, gene expression, and hormones critical for metabolic signaling. Our findings highlight the complex interplay between the environment and foundational elements of mother-offspring interactions. These data suggest that the liver transcriptome is shaped by early stress experiences across both the first and second postnatal weeks, while the arcuate nucleus of the hypothalamus is primarily affected by later experiences (particularly in females), mapping on to the respective periods of maturation for these tissues. These findings underscore the need to consider temporal dynamics and sex-specific nature of developmental sensitivity to environmental stressors. Moreover, we were able to uncover otherwise hidden outcomes by considering individual variability of the litter's early experience rather than solely relying on LBN group differences. Future work should aim to disentangle contributions of specific components of maternal care, milk, and other facets of LBN and understand synergistic effects on offspring outcomes including long-term effects on metabolism, stress response, and emotionality. Understanding these causal mechanisms will inform strategies to mitigate long-term consequences of early life adversity.
