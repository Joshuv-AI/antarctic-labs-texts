Hospital Course and Outcomes After Major Bleeding in Patients on Factor Xa Inhibitors in Japan: A Nationwide Observational Study
Authors: Shun Kohsaka, Masahiro Yasaka, Mitsuru Hoshino, Ryotaro Ide, Fumitaka Wada, Naru Morita
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Aim: To evaluate the clinical characteristics, management, and in-hospital outcomes of patients hospitalized with major bleeding while receiving direct factor Xa inhibitors (FXa-I) using a large-scale, nationwide administrative claims database. Methods: This retrospective cohort study analyzed an administrative hospital claims database to identify patients hospitalized with major bleeding while receiving FXa-I between August 2011 and May 2022 (before the launch of andexanet alfa), resulting in 8011 patients (median age, 80.0 years) being eligible for the analysis. The data collected encompassed patient demographics, hospitalization details, bleeding sites, and outcomes of interest, such as in-hospital mortality and the resumption of OACs. Results: The most common bleeding sites were upper gastrointestinal bleeding (GIB; 30.3%), intracranial hemorrhage (ICH; 17.6%), lower GIB (11.7%), and trauma-related bleeding (9.1%). Patients had a median hospital stay of 11.0 days (range, 1–911 days). The overall in-hospital mortality rate was 7.1% (95% CI: 6.6–7.7%). In-hospital mortality rates by bleeding site were 17.8% for ICH, 5.1% for upper GIB, 1.4% for lower GIB, 10.2% for trauma-related bleeding, and 4.1% for other bleeding sites. The most common cause of death was hemorrhage-related (70.1%). OACs were resumed in 67.9% of patients, with a median time to resumption of 3.0 days. Conclusion: In this nationwide study, the in-hospital mortality rate for patients hospitalized for FXa-I-related major bleeding was approximately 7%. Although most patients resumed oral anticoagulant therapy during hospitalization, the significant in-hospital mortality rates observed, especially in cases of ICH, highlight the need for strategies to further improve the outcomes in patients experiencing FXa-I-related complications.

Introduction

Anticoagulant therapy plays an essential role in the prevention and management of thrombosis. In the current era, direct oral anticoagulants (DOACs) have become increasingly common for preventing ischemic stroke and systemic embolism in patients with nonvalvular atrial fibrillation, as well as for treating and preventing the recurrence of deep vein thrombosis and pulmonary embolism 1 , 2) . For example, in the United States, prescriptions for DOACs increased significantly from 7.4% in 2011 to 66.8% in 2019 2) . Similar trends have been observed in the United Kingdom 3) , and more recently, in Asian countries such as China and Japan. For example, in Japan, the proportion of patients receiving DOACs in antithrombotic therapy increased substantially from 2% in 2011 to 52% in 2021 1) .

Although direct-acting factor Xa inhibitors (FXa-I) among DOACs are associated with a lower risk of cerebral or major bleeding compared with older vitamin K antagonists, the risk of major bleeding events remains substantial 4 - 6) – Numerous clinical trials and database studies have reported the incidence of bleeding in patients using DOACs. However, few studies have investigated the subsequent outcomes after bleeding events, such as the prognosis and resumption of oral anticoagulation (OAC) following the event. While several reports have examined the prognosis following major bleeding in patients using FXa-I 7 , 8) , these were mostly small-scale, single-institution studies that could not provide detailed, stratified information based on the site of bleeding.

Aim

This study aimed to describe the patient characteristics, hospital course, in-hospital prognosis, and resumption of oral anticoagulant therapy among the patients hospitalized for major bleeding while taking FXa-I, stratified by bleeding site. By providing detailed insights into these aspects, we aimed to inform clinical decision-making and highlight areas where strategies are needed to improve the outcomes in patients experiencing FXa-I-related complications.

Methods

Study Design and Participants

This retrospective observational cohort study used an existing Japanese hospital claims database (Medical Data Vision Co., Ltd. [MDV], Tokyo, Japan), which includes individual patient data from administrative claims and Diagnosis Procedure Combination (DPC) data. The data were obtained from hospitalizations and outpatient visits of patients who attended hospitals participating in the DPC Per-Diem Payment System, providing acute inpatient care. The study period was from August 1, 2010, to August 30, 2023, and the index period was from August 1, 2011, to May 24, 2022. Patient-level data from individuals who experienced major bleeding during FXa-I therapy were included. FXa-I approved for anticoagulation in Japan were apixaban, rivaroxaban, and edoxaban during the study period.

The index date was defined as the date of hospitalization for the first major bleeding event occurring after the prescription of FXa-I during the index period. Major bleeding events were identified using the International Classification of Diseases, Tenth Revision (ICD-10) codes related to bleeding and were defined as any bleeding diagnosis requiring hospitalization. FXa-I prescriptions were identified using Anatomical Therapeutic Chemical (ATC) codes.

Baseline demographic and clinical characteristics were captured for 6 months before and on the index date (“look-back period”; Supplementary Table 1 ). The follow-up period extended from the index date until hospital discharge. Consequently, the patient data from 6 months before the index date through the end of the follow-up period were used in the analysis.

Patients

Patients were considered eligible for inclusion if they were 18 years of age or older at the index date and hospitalized due to a bleeding event at the index date. A bleeding-related hospitalization was defined based on administrative records documenting the primary and associated conditions during hospitalization. To qualify as a bleeding-related hospitalization, patients had to have a bleeding diagnosis recorded as either the primary condition, a related condition, or as the event that required the most medical resources during their hospital stay ( Supplementary Table 2 ) . Additionally, eligible patients had to have claims records for at least 6 months prior to the index date and receive FXa-I, rivaroxaban, apixaban, or edoxaban (ATC code: B01AF) at the index date (considered the “exposure”). The bleeding sites at index date were defined by ICD-10 codes and standard disease code ( Supplementary Table 3 ) . If multiple bleeding sites were recorded for one patient, they were counted as duplicates.

Bleeding site at index date with ICD-10 code and standard disease code

Patients were excluded if they met any of the following criteria: (i) hospitalization due to any bleeding during the look-back period of 6 months, (ii) first prescription date of FXa-I and index date were the same, (iii) records of continuous hemodialysis (ICD10-code: Z49 or procedure code: J038, C102 recorded two or more times in a month), estimated GFR ＜15 mL/min/1.73 m2, renal replacement therapy (ICD10-code: Z940 or claim code: 150324810, 150196310, 150338610), ICD-10 code of end-stage kidney disease or chronic kidney disease stage 5 (ICD10-code: N185) during the look-back period, and (iv) records of pregnancy (ICD10-code: O00-O99) and FXa-I prescription simultaneously during the study period.

Variables and Outcomes

Emergency room (ER) and intensive care unit (ICU) utilization during hospitalization were defined using procedure codes A205 and A300, and A301, respectively. Blood transfusion, including fresh frozen plasma (FFP), during hospitalization was defined using procedure code K920. The primary endpoints were the proportion of patients with the following during index hospitalization: (i) composite of thromboembolic events, consisting of acute myocardial infarction (acute MI; ICD-10 codes: I210, I211, I212, I214), stroke (ICD-10 codes: I63, I65-I66, G45), unstable angina pectoris (UAP; ICD-10 code: I200), venous sinus thrombosis (VST; ICD-10 codes: I676, G08), systematic embolism (SE; ICD-10 codes: I740-I745, I748, I749), venous thromboembolism (VTE; ICD-10 code: I802, I269), acute limb ischemia (ALI; standard disease code: 8844374), and, as a secondary analysis, (ii) in-hospital mortality. The cause of death was defined as the most resource-input disease in hospitalization, and the outcome at discharge was death. Therefore, each death was attributed to one cause exclusively.

Secondary endpoints were the proportion of patients with the following events during index hospitalization: (i) bleeding events and (ii) each component of thromboembolic events (acute MI, stroke, UAP, VST, SE, VTE, and ALI). The ad hoc endpoints were the proportion of patients with the following events during index hospitalization: (i) a composite of thromboembolic events (acute MI, stroke, UAP, VST, SE, VTE, and ALI) by bleeding site, (ii) bleeding events by bleeding site, (iii) in-hospital mortality by bleeding site, and (iv) resumption of OACs by bleeding site.

Statistics

Descriptive statistics were used to summarize the patient characteristics and outcomes. Continuous variables are presented as medians with ranges or interquartile ranges, and categorical variables are expressed as frequencies and percentages. For the primary, secondary, and ad hoc endpoints, the proportions of patients with TE events, rebleeding events, and in-hospital mortality during the index hospitalization were calculated by dividing the number of patients who had events during index hospitalization by the number of patients in the target population, and the exact 95% confidence interval (CI) was calculated using the negative binomial distribution (Clopper–Pearson method). The outcomes were stratified by bleeding site for comparative purposes.

The time to resumption of OAC therapy was calculated from the date of admission to the date of the first OAC prescription (ATC codes: B01AA, B01AE, B01AF) during index hospitalization. In terms of data handling, missing values were not imputed and, when data were missing in category counting, they were counted as “missing.”

SAS® version 9.3 and 9.4 (SAS Institute Inc., Cary, NC, USA) were used for all statistical analyses.

Outcomes

Regarding the primary endpoints, the overall in-hospital mortality rate during index hospitalization was 7.1% (95% CI, 6.6%–7.7%) ( Table 3 ) . In-hospital mortality rates by bleeding site were as follows: 17.8% (95% CI, 15.9%–19.9%) for ICH, 5.1% (95% CI, 4.2%–6.0%) for upper GI bleeding, 1.4% (95% CI, 0.7%–2.4%) for lower GI bleeding, 10.2% (95% CI, 8.1%–12.6%) for trauma-related bleeding, and 4.1% (95% CI, 3.4%–4.9%) for other bleeding sites. The most common causes of in-hospital death were hemorrhage (70.1%), followed by malignancies (8.3%), ischemic stroke/systemic embolism (4.4%), and infections (4.4%) ( Fig.2 ) . The proportions of cause-of-death categories related to hemorrhage were as follows: 40.6% for head, 14.9% for GI, 10.4% for trauma, and 4.2% for other causes ( Fig.2 , Supplementary Table 4 for details).

OACs were resumed in 67.9% of patients with a median time to resumption of 3.0 (range, 1–216) days ( Table 2 ) . The proportion of OAC resumption by bleeding site was 60.7% for ICH, 69.6% for upper GI bleeding, 71.3% for lower GI bleeding, 68.5% for trauma-related bleeding, and 68.6% for other bleeding sites. Discussion

Since their introduction, FXa-I has played a significant role in the prevention and treatment of various thromboembolic diseases. Consequently, their use has increased worldwide. However, despite a better safety profile than that of warfarin, this has led to an increase in FXa-I-associated bleeding events and the need to understand the real-world situation of FXa-I-associated bleeding. This large-scale observational database study, the largest and most comprehensive assessment in Japan, provides detailed data on the characteristics and outcomes of patients hospitalized for FXa-I-related bleeding. Notably, GI bleeding was the most common site, accounting for approximately 42% of bleeding events (upper GI: 30.3%; lower GI: 11.7%). However, the highest in-hospital mortality rate was observed in patients with ICH at 17.8%, with hemorrhage being the leading cause of death. Furthermore, OACs were resumed in nearly 70% of survivors, with a median time to resumption of 3.0 days.

The finding that GI bleeding was the most common bleeding site in FXa-I-associated bleeding is consistent with previous reports 8 - 10) . This may be explained by the fact that DOACs have been shown to exhibit adverse local effects in the GI tract, potentially due to incomplete absorption, direct caustic effects, or inhibition of mucosal healing 11) . However, GI bleeding related to DOACs is reported to be less severe than that related to warfarin 12) , with a lower in-hospital mortality rate than that associated with ICH 13 - 16) . Although this tendency was consistent with our findings, the number of GI bleeding events suggests the need to consider appropriate management of GI bleeding during FXa-I use, including the use of neutralizing agents.

The in-hospital mortality rate due to ICH was 17.8% in this study, with hemorrhage-related causes accounting for 70.1% of deaths, of which cerebral hemorrhage represented 40.6%. Previous clinical data on patients with atrial fibrillation and major bleeding during FXa-I use reported an ICH mortality rate of 14.0% 9) , which is consistent with the findings of this study. Although DOACs have demonstrated a lower incidence of ICH and fatal bleeding compared to warfarin 17 - 19) , after ICH, there was no significant difference in the outcome in patients taking warfarin compared with those taking DOACs 20) . Importantly, this study included data prior to the launch of andexanet alfa in Japan and therefore did not include patients prescribed this reversal agent. A randomized controlled trial (ANNEXA-I) in patients with cerebral hemorrhage receiving FXa-I demonstrated that andexanet alfa reduced hematoma growth more effectively than standard care, which primarily involved prothrombin complex concentrate 21) . Retrospective observational studies have also suggested that andexanet alfa reduces the mortality rate compared with four-factor prothrombin complex concentrate (4F-PCC) 22) . In Japan, the efficacy of 4F-PCC for major bleeding associated with FXa-I remains unestablished, and the effect of andexanet alfa on ICH prognosis requires independent validation.

Regarding the resumption of OACs, 67.9% of the patients resumed OACs during hospitalization with a median resumption time of 3 days after the index date, potentially resulting in a lower rate of thromboembolic events. This timing aligns with previous reports from Japan, where 80% of patients resumed OACs within 7 days of admission due to major bleeding 8) . The proportion of OAC resumption and the time to resumption by bleeding site were generally similar, although ICH had a slightly lower percentage of resumption and required more time to resumption than the other bleeding sites, which may result in a high incidence of thromboembolic events in the ICH. In contrast, the lower percentage of resumption in ICH reflects the physician’s concern that the patients may have amyloid angiopathy, which makes anticoagulant therapy infeasible, or taking into account the patient’s age and poorly controlled blood pressure that could cause a recurrence of fatal cerebral hemorrhage associated with anticoagulant therapy. A meta-analysis of the significance of anticoagulant resumption after intracranial hemorrhage, which compared resumption with non-resumption, showed that the risk of thromboembolic events was significantly lower in the resumption group (pooled relative risk, 0.34) 23) . It was reported in the ANNEXA-4 trial that the incidence of thrombotic events was low after the resumption of OACs 24) . Several western cardiovascular guidelines specify the timing for the resumption of anticoagulants. The NOAC Practical Guide of the European Heart Rhythm Association recommends restarting anticoagulants as soon as clinically possible after intracranial hemorrhage, considering factors that may warrant discontinuation 25) . Similarly, the ACC/AHA/ACCP/HRS guidelines recommend resuming anticoagulants 1-2 weeks after intracranial hemorrhage in patients at very high risk of atrial fibrillation and thromboembolism 26) . In Japan, the 2024 Japanese Circulation Society / Japanese Heart Rhythm Society Guidelines for the Treatment of Arrhythmias and the Japan Stroke Society Guideline 2021 for the Treatment of Stroke also state the need for the early resumption of antithrombotic therapy after ICH associated with antithrombotic therapy, considering rebleeding risk 27 , 28) . Moreover, the American College of Gastroenterology guidelines strongly recommend the resumption of anticoagulation after hemostasis of GI bleeding 29) . Regarding the resumption of anticoagulation after GI bleeding, one cohort study of 4602 patients compared the resumption group with the non-resumption group and showed that both the mortality and thromboembolism rates were lower in the resumption group data 30) , thus supporting the guideline. However, in a previous report, the optimal timing of resumption has not been specified. This study clarified the actual circumstances of OAC resumption in Japan, and further verification of its effectiveness and safety is warranted. It should also be noted that the timing and decision to resume OACs after major bleeding may vary between institutions and regions, influenced by differences in hospital resources, clinical protocols, and physician experience. Such practice variations may have impacted the observed patterns in this study and highlight the need for further investigation to standardize and optimize care for patients on FXa-I therapy.

Limitations

First, as this study included patients with a look-back period of 6 months before the index date, some selection bias cannot be excluded. Second, although major bleeding can be treated in primary medical institutions, MDV mainly includes data from secondary medical institutions. Consequently, some selection bias may affect the applicability of these results to the general population. Third, the MDV database has several limitations. For example, the MDV database can only collect monthly and yearly event timing information. In this study, the number of events, such as thromboembolic and rebleeding events, may be underestimated, especially since event dates other than death during the index hospitalization were recorded as the last day of the hospitalization month. Additionally, it includes only limited information on systolic blood pressure, laboratory data such as hemoglobin, and lifestyle-related data such as alcohol intake. This made it difficult to perform risk stratification for bleeding using the Glasgow Blatchford Score or the modified HAS-BLED score. Moreover, because the exact event date is not available in the MDV database, it was difficult to determine whether rebleeding occurred before or after OAC was restarted in the analysis stratified by OAC resumption. More robust studies are needed to clarify the predictors and timing of rebleeding in patients treated with FXa-I. Finally, the MDV cannot link patient data from multiple institutions. Therefore, it may not reflect accurate data if there is a transfer of institutions during hospitalization.

Conclusions

In this large, comprehensive assessment of FXa-I-associated bleeding events, we found a high overall in-hospital mortality rate of approximately 7%, with 70.1% of in-hospital deaths directly related to hemorrhage. The highest in-hospital mortality rate (17.8%) occurred among the patients hospitalized with ICH. These findings have significant clinical implications, emphasizing the need for strategic approaches to mitigate bleeding events. Consistent with contemporary guideline recommendations, the resumption of OACs was both feasible and common, occurring in approximately 68% of patients after major bleeding events. These results highlight the importance of an acute management plan aimed at ensuring clinical stabilization and facilitating the safe and timely resumption of OACs when appropriate to improve the outcomes in this highly vulnerable population.
