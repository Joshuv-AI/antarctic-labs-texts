Identification of novel high-risk genes in gastric cancer through single-cell RNA sequencing, eQTL Mendelian randomization, and in vitro validation
Authors: Li Qi, Haoyu Chen, Xinyu Hao, Tianyu Gao, Pingping Zhou, Wenbo Li, Chen Wang, Kunfeng Li, Shaowei Liu, Yuhua Wang, Xuetong Ren, Haiyan Bai, Ningning Ren, Yangang Wang
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundCurrent targeted therapies for gastric cancer have limited efficacy, and recently discovered markers have not significantly improved survival rates in patients with gastric cancer. Therefore, it is imperative to identify more specific genes associated with the occurrence and progression of gastric cancer to achieve prevention and treatment. The aim of this study is to discover high-risk genes for gastric cancer by integrating single-cell transcriptomics and Mendelian randomization (MR) analysis.MethodsThis study integrates gastric cancer genome-wide association study (GWAS) data, single-cell transcriptomics (sc-RNA-seq), and expression quantitative trait loci (eQTL) data for analysis, and employs two-sample MR to elucidate the causal relationships between genes and gastric cancer, thereby identifying high-risk genes for gastric cancer. Subsequently, in vitro cellular experiments are conducted to validate the transcriptional expression levels of these genes.ResultsAfter quality control of the sc-RNA-seq data, we identified 2463 markers for gastric cancer cell subtypes. Subsequently, we utilized eQTL data and GWAS data for gastric cancer to perform MR analysis, yielding 149 genes with a causal relationship with gastric cancer. By applying log2FC filtering, we ultimately identified 5 high-risk gastric cancer genes: SORBS3, RMND5A, FBXO6, LPGAT1, and EPHB4. Finally, in vitro validation confirmed the differential expression of these 5 high-risk genes between normal gastric epithelial cell lines and gastric cancer cell lines.ConclusionsOur study reveals previously unattended high-risk gastric cancer genes, potentially offering new directions and evidence for the molecular diagnosis and treatment of gastric cancer.Keywords: Mendelian randomization, Gastric cancer, Sc-RNA-seq

Introduction

Gastric cancer, a malignant neoplasm arising from the epithelial cells of the gastric mucosa, persists as a prevalent malignancy worldwide [1]. With an estimated one million new cases annually, it ranks fifth in global cancer incidence and mortality [2], [3]. Regional disparities exist, with the highest rates in East Asia, notably China, which accounts for 50 % of global gastric cancer incidence and mortality [4], [5]. Despite a decline in incidence in many countries, the aging population is expected to lead to an increase in cases [1]. Studies indicate a rising trend in gastric cancer incidence among younger individuals, even in low-risk countries [6]. Moreover, survival rates remain dismal, with 5-year survival rates for cardia and non-cardia gastric adenocarcinomas at only 18 % and 16 %, respectively, even in developed nations [7]. Notably, due to a lack of clear clinical indicators, most patients are diagnosed at advanced stages, leading to poor prognoses [8]. Therefore, identifying effective strategies to reduce gastric cancer incidence is of paramount importance.

The etiology of gastric cancer is complex and multifactorial, associated with Helicobacter pylori infection [9], inflammation [10], genetic predispositions [11], and lifestyle factors such as alcohol consumption and smoking [12]. Genetic variations in DNA are considered key factors in the susceptibility to most complex traits, particularly cancer. Meta-analysis results have shown that genetic variants such as MUC1 rs2070803 and MTX1 rs2075570 are associated with diffuse gastric cancer, while PSCA rs2294008 and PRKAA1 rs13361707 are related to non-cardia gastric cancer [13]. Genetic analysis of sporadic gastric cancer patients reveals that over 10 % of cases harbor pathogenic mutations [14]. A Chinese research cohort has demonstrated that genetic variations in lncRNA SNHG5 polymorphisms are associated with the susceptibility and prognosis of gastric cancer [15]. A European cohort study indicates that polygenic risk scores constructed from functional SNPs are associated with the occurrence of gastric cancer, serving as an indicator of genetic risk for gastric cancer in European populations [16]. With the maturation of sequencing technologies and the continuous deepening of research, an increasing number of novel genetic variations have been discovered. However, these new markers have not significantly improved the survival rates of gastric cancer patients, as targeted therapies have shown only limited efficacy [8]. Therefore, there is a need to identify more specific genes associated with the occurrence and progression of gastric cancer to achieve prevention and treatment of the disease.

Mendelian randomization (MR) is a robust analytical approach for evaluating the causal relationships between exposures or risk factors and clinically relevant outcomes [17], [18]. This method diminishes the impact of unmeasured confounders and reverse causality by leveraging Mendelian inheritance patterns [19]. Furthermore, single-cell transcriptomics (sc-RNA-Seq) has become an indispensable tool for elucidating disease mechanisms at the single-cell level, offering insights into cellular heterogeneity and disease pathogenesis [20]. Unlike bulk RNA-seq, which provides aggregate gene expression data, sc-RNA-Seq technology delivers gene expression profiles from individual cells, enabling the identification of changes within specific subpopulations and a more nuanced understanding of disease processes [21].

Currently, numerous MR analyses and sc-RNA-Seq studies on gastric cancer have identified risk factors and characteristic genes associated with disease-related cellular subpopulations [22], [23], [24], [25]. This study integrates MR analysis with sc-RNA-Seq research to conduct a comprehensive examination of gastric cancer, aiming to uncover differentially expressed genes within cancer cells that influence the disease. Utilizing MR analysis with expression quantitative trait loci (eQTL) data, we aim to establish causal links between these genes and gastric cancer (Fig. 1). Through this research, we aim to elucidate the pathological mechanisms of gastric cancer, providing novel insights and strategies for its study and treatment.

Materials and methods

Summary of data sources

Genome-wide association study (GWAS) data for gastric cancer were sourced from BioBank Japan [26], which conducted 220 deep phenotyping GWAS. In addition to the target disease status, this study collected in-depth phenotypic data, such as medical history and medication prescription records, through text mining of electronic health records. The dataset comprised 167,122 samples, including 7921 gastric cancer cases, with 12,453,108 SNPs.

Whole-blood eQTL data were sourced from the eQTLGen Consortium (www.eQTLgen.org) [27], [28]. eQTL data have become a common tool for elucidating the regulatory mechanisms of variants identified in GWAS. The eQTLGen Consortium comprises a total of 25,482 whole-blood samples from 37 datasets, predominantly of European ancestry. Gene expression levels in these samples are analyzed through gene expression microarrays and RNA-seq data, thereby enabling the identification of genetic variants associated with gene expression levels in blood samples using eQTL data.

The sc-RNA-seq data were derived from the GSE183904 dataset [29] in the Gene Expression Omnibus (GEO) public database. This dataset comprises 40 tissue samples subjected to droplet-based sc-RNA-seq (10x Genomics), including 29 gastric cancer lesion samples and 11 normal tissue samples, encompassing a total of 158,500 cells. For subsequent analysis, samples were categorized into "Primary_Normal" and "Primary_Tumor" groups.

Analysis of single-cell RNA-Seq data

For the single-cell RNA-seq data, we utilized the Seurat R package to integrate data from 29 gastric cancer lesion samples and 11 normal tissue samples. Cells were filtered based on the original study's criteria [29], selecting those with expression features ranging from 500 to 6000 genes and mitochondrial RNA gene content less than 20 %, resulting in a total of 158,500 cells for further analysis. We employed Seurat's NormalizeData, FindVariableFeatures, and ScaleData functions to obtain a low-dimensional representation in UMAP space. The functions FindAllMarkers and FindMarkers were used to identify markers for each cellular subpopulation and differentially expressed genes (DEGs) between specified subpopulation pairs, preparing for subsequent functional analysis.

Instrumental variable selection and data harmonization

Based on previous studies, we applied two-sample Mendelian randomization (MR) to assess the causal impact of DEGs from sc-RNA-seq data on gastric cancer, utilizing SNPs data from eQTL and GWAS as instrumental variables (IVs), with a selection criterion of p-values less than 1 × 10^-8 for the IVs [30], [31]. These SNPs (linkage disequilibrium [LD] r2 threshold <.1 within 500 kb distance) were trimmed using the clump function in the R package TwoSampleMR [32], where LD r2 was a control calculated according to the 1000 Genomes Project as a reference plate. For CKD, the data were screened and analyzed to enhance the reliability of the results (p < 5 × 10−8) by referring to multiple publications and developing stricter criteria [33], [34]. Meanwhile, we calculated the proportion of phenotypic variation explained and F-statistics for each IV to assess IV strength and avoid weak instrument bias.

Two-sample bidirectional MR analysis

The analyses were conducted utilizing R software, specifically version 4.3.2, which can be accessed at the Comprehensive R Archive Network website (https://cran.r-project.org/). Primarily, we utilized the "TwoSampleMR" package [32] to execute a suite of methodological approaches including inverse-variance weighting (IVW) [18], weighted median-based [35], MR-Egger [36], and mode-based analyses [37]. The MR-Egger regression algorithm was implemented to mitigate the potential biases introduced by horizontal pleiotropy [36]. The presence of a significant intercept term in the MR-Egger regression would indicate the existence of horizontal pleiotropy. To further refine our analysis and exclude any outliers that could significantly skew the estimation outcomes, we applied the MR pleiotropy residual sum and outliers (MR-PRESSO) technique using the MR-PRESSO package [38]. To visualize our findings, we generated scatter plots and forest plots for a clear and comprehensive representation of the data.

Cell culture

The GES-1, AGS, BGC-823, and SGC-7901 cell lines were procured from Pricella Life Sciences Co., Ltd. (Pricella, China). All cells were cultured in RPMI-1640 medium (Gibco, USA), supplemented with 10 % fetal bovine serum (Gibco, USA) and 1 % penicillin-streptomycin (Gibco, USA), at 37℃ in a humidified atmosphere containing 5 % CO2. The cells were passed every 2–3 days and experiments were conducted between passages 3 and 10.

Detection of transcript levels

Real-time Quantitative Polymerase Chain Reaction (RT-qPCR) was utilized to evaluate the transcriptional levels of genes. Total RNA was isolated from gastric tissues or cultured cells employing the Total RNA Extraction Kit (Solarbio, China). Reverse transcription of mRNA to cDNA was carried out using the Super plus qPCR RT kit with gDNA remover Kit (Mei5bio, China), which also facilitated the removal of genomic DNA (gDNA). Quantitative PCR was then conducted with the HiPer SYBR Premix EsTaq Kit (Mei5bio, China). The relative quantification of mRNA expression levels across different groups was calculated using the 2^-ΔΔCt method. The housekeeping gene β-actin served as an endogenous control for normalization of target gene expression. The primers detailed in Table 1 were employed for amplification.

Statistical Analysis

The data from all experiments within this study were obtained from a minimum of three independent replicates and are expressed as the mean ± standard deviation (SD). To compare the variances among groups, we employed one-way analysis of variance (ANOVA) using GraphPad Prism software, supplemented by Tukey's multiple comparisons test. Statistical significance was determined at the threshold of p < 0.05 for all pairwise comparisons.

Results

Annotation of gastric cancer cell subsets

Following quality control, we performed clustering and annotation on a total of 158,500 single cells from the gastric cancer dataset, ultimately classifying them into 11 distinct cell subsets based on canonical biomarkers (Fig. 2A). These subsets include natural killer cells marked by KLRD1, T cells by CD3D, plasma cells by TNFRSF17, epithelial cells by CDH1, fibroblasts by LUM, endothelial cells by ENG, macrophages by CD163, B cells by MS4A1, stromal cells by FN1, mast cells by KIT, and pericytes by RGS5 and NOTCH3 (Fig. 2B). Subsequently, we employed the FindAllMarkers algorithm to identify the top five most significant markers for each subset (Fig. 2C). Given the study's focus on epithelial cells, we utilized FindMarkers to detect differentially expressed genes in epithelial cells between Primary Tumor and Primary Normal states and performed GSEA enrichment analysis on KEGG pathways. The results indicated that, compared to the Primary Normal phase, the Primary Tumor phase exhibited upregulation of DNA replication, cell cycle, natural killer cell-mediated cytotoxicity, cytokine receptor interaction, viral protein interaction with cytokines, TNF, and Toll-like receptor signaling pathways, while downregulation of protein digestion and absorption, gastric acid secretion, and PI3K-AKT signaling pathways (Fig. 2D). These findings align with previous studies, suggesting that DNA replication and cell cycle processes in gastric cancer cells may be more active than in normal cells [39], [40]. Similarly, the activation of immune-related pathways is associated with gastric cancer cells' evasion of immune surveillance, promotion of inflammatory responses, and the formation of the tumor microenvironment [41], [42]. Furthermore, the suppression of gastric acid secretion and protein digestion and absorption may underlie the biological basis for symptoms such as indigestion observed in gastric cancer patients [43].

Annotation to identify gastric cancer subtypes within epithelial cells

To precisely identify gastric cancer cell subtypes, we excluded other cell types and categorized gastric epithelial cells into five subgroups based on clustering results (Fig. 3A). The mucous pit cells were marked by GKN1 and GKN2, intestinal-type cells by REG4 and TFF3, chief cells by LIPF and PGA3, enteroendocrine cells by CHGB, and cancer cells by CEACAM5 and CEACAM6 (Fig. 3B). Subsequently, we employed the FindAllMarkers algorithm to identify the top five most significant markers for each subgroup (Fig. 3C). Additionally, KEGG enrichment analysis based on the markers of each cell subgroup revealed differences in pathways such as cell cycle, HIF-1, carbon metabolism, and amino acid biosynthesis within gastric cancer cell subtypes (Fig. 3D). Finally, we exported the 2463 markers identified for gastric cancer cell subtypes from the FindAllMarkers algorithm results for further downstream analysis (Supplementary Table 1).

Determining causality between markers and gastric cancer through mendelian randomization

To establish the causal relationships between markers of gastric cancer cell subtypes and GC, we conducted an MR analysis. After quality control filtering, we identified 15,695 eQTL genes for MR analysis. Following the IVW algorithm, 149 genes were selected that have a causal relationship with the risk of gastric cancer. To further identify genes, we filtered marker genes with an avg_log2FC greater than 1 and genes whose expression values are in the same direction as the beta values. Ultimately, we selected the following five genes: SORBS3 (p-value=0.011, OR=1.339, 95 % CI=1.070–1.676), RMND5A (p-value=0.017, OR=1.317, 95 % CI=1.050–1.653), FBXO6 (p-value=0.005, OR=1.152, 95 % CI=1.043–1.274), LPGAT1 (p-value=0.002, OR=1.141, 95 % CI=1.049–1.240), and EPHB4 (p-value=0.024, OR=1.055, 95 % CI=1.007–1.106) (Fig. 4 and Supplementary Table 2). Scatter plots and sensitivity analyses demonstrate the reliability of Mendelian randomization results (Supplementary Figure 1 and Supplementary Table 3).

Examination of high-risk markers in gastric epithelial cells

We utilized sc-RNA-seq data to observe the distribution of high-risk markers in gastric epithelial cells. Violin plots revealed that SORBS3, RMND5A, FBXO6, LPGAT1, and EPHB4, which are five high-risk gastric cancer markers, are specifically expressed in gastric cancer cell subtypes, indicating the reliability of our MR-selected high-risk markers (Fig. 5A). Subsequently, density plots were constructed to examine the distribution of these five high-risk markers in gastric epithelial cells. The results showed that these five high-risk markers are predominantly distributed in gastric cancer cell subtypes, with RMND5A, FBXO6, LPGAT1, and EPHB4 also present in intestinal-type cells (Fig. 5B). This suggests that these four high-risk markers may be overexpressed in the premalignant stages of gastric cancer, as intestinal-type cells are a key feature of this stage. Collectively, these findings underscore the reliability of the five high-risk markers for gastric cancer.

In vitro validation of gastric cancer high-risk markers

To further validate our results, we conducted in vitro experiments to assess the expression of the five gastric cancer high-risk markers—SORBS3, RMND5A, FBXO6, LPGAT1, and EPHB4—in the normal gastric epithelial cell line GES-1 and three gastric cancer cell lines: AGC, BGC-823, and SGC-9701. According to RT-qPCR results, we found that SORBS3, FBXO6, LPGAT1, and EPHB4 were differentially expressed in gastric cancer cell lines, while RMND5A showed differential expression in the SGC-9701 cell line but not in AGC and BGC-823 cell lines (Fig. 6 A-E). The in vitro experimental results confirmed the differential expression of SORBS3, RMND5A, FBXO6, LPGAT1, and EPHB4 in gastric cancer.

Discussion

In recent years, the emergence and advancement of various molecular sequencing technologies have unveiled potential targets for a range of tumors, enabling precision medicine to utilize these novel molecular targets for diagnosis and to implement more effective and safer targeted therapies. Gastric cancer continues to impose a significant global burden, with one million new cases annually, ranking fifth in global cancer incidence and mortality rates. Therefore, the discovery of more effective and specific molecular biomarkers associated with the occurrence and progression of gastric cancer could facilitate the prevention and treatment of the disease. This study initially employed sc-RNA-seq data to mine markers for gastric cancer cell subtypes, followed by eQTL-based MR to select markers associated with gastric cancer risk, and finally confirmed the differential expression of the high-risk markers SORBS3, RMND5A, FBXO6, LPGAT1, and EPHB4 through in vitro experiments.

SORBS3 is a scaffold protein known to interact with proteins involved in cell adhesion, the cytoskeleton, and various signaling pathways, participating in multiple intracellular signal transduction processes. It has been implicated in neural development [44], brain aging [45], atherosclerosis [46], and has been shown in studies to be associated with uveal melanoma [47], lung adenocarcinoma [48], hepatocellular carcinoma [49], and other cancers [50]. However, its specific role in cancer, particularly gastric cancer, remains unclear. Current research indicates that increased expression of SORBS3 can lead to a decrease in autophagy levels by affecting the YAP1-WWTR1/TAZ signaling pathway [51] and can also cause the activation of the epidermal growth factor ERK2 [52], both of which may influence the occurrence and progression of gastric cancer [53], [54]. Although there are no reports on the relationship between SORBS3 and gastric cancer, our results suggest that elevated SORBS3 expression increases the risk of gastric cancer.

RMND5A has not been extensively studied in relation to gastric cancer, although several studies have linked its expression to various types of cancer. In oral cancer, RMND5A can interact with oral cancer cells and endothelial cells, influencing angiogenesis [55]. The expression of RMND5A correlates with the overall survival of patients with pancreatic cancer, and miR-590–5p can target RMND5A, promoting the migration of pancreatic cancer cell lines [56]. Additionally, research has found that miR-138 can significantly inhibit the migration of HeLa cells by targeting RMND5A [57]. RMND5A may also mediate the ubiquitin-proteasome system-related degradation of the oncogenic peptide MBOP, affecting cell migration and proliferation in colorectal cancer [58]. Our study suggests that RMND5A is differentially expressed in gastric cancer cells and is associated with the occurrence of gastric cancer.

FBXO6 is a member of the F-box protein family, which plays a role in various biological processes, including cell cycle regulation, signal transduction, and ubiquitin-dependent protein degradation [59]. Current research has identified a correlation between FBXO6 and gastric cancer. Studies have shown that FBXO6 interacts specifically with RIOK1 methylated at K411 through its FBA domain, inducing RIOK1 ubiquitination, which in turn regulates the progression of colorectal and gastric cancer [60]. Additionally, FBXO6 has been shown to promote the growth and proliferation of gastric cancer cells and normal gastric cells, while also maintaining the malignant phenotype of tumor cells. However, it negatively impacts apoptosis or invasive capabilities in gastric cancer cells [59]. Our study not only demonstrates the specific expression of FBXO6 in gastric cancer cells but also indicates that it is a high-risk factor for gastric cancer.

LPGAT1 is an enzyme involved in phospholipid metabolism, specifically regulating the biosynthesis of triacylglycerols, which is crucial for maintaining phospholipid homeostasis and modulating the structural integrity of mitochondrial membranes [61]. It has been gaining attention due to its role in various cancers. Studies have shown that LPGAT1 is overexpressed in lung adenocarcinoma and can promote proliferation while inhibiting apoptosis, with knockdown of LPGAT1 abolishing tumor growth and proliferation in both in vivo and in vitro models [62]. Our research indicates that LPGAT1 is highly expressed in gastric cancer cells and may be positively correlated with the occurrence of gastric cancer. This could be related to its function in regulating mitochondrial membrane, as the stability of mitochondrial function is closely associated with the development of gastric cancer. Multiple studies have shown that modulating mitochondrial function can induce apoptosis and affect the cell cycle in gastric cancer cells [63], [64].

EPHB4, a member of the Eph receptor family, is implicated in tumor angiogenesis, growth, and metastasis, and has recently garnered significant attention for its role in gastric cancer. Studies utilizing tumor genomic profiling have identified EPHB4-associated gene networks, suggesting its potential as a biomarker for tumor invasiveness and a therapeutic target in gastroesophageal cancer [65]. Research has also shown that the long noncoding RNA BC005927 upregulates EPHB4, promoting gastric cancer metastasis under hypoxia [66]. Additionally, EPHB4 is localized to the cytoplasm of gastric cancer cells, and its overexpression is significantly correlated with tumor size [67]. Our study corroborates these findings, demonstrating high expression of EPHB4 in gastric cancer cells and a positive correlation with gastric cancer risk.

In summary, the combination of sc-RNA-seq and MR is a cutting-edge strategy for pinpointing important genes within cellular subpopulations. Our study leveraged this approach to identify SORBS3, RMND5A, FBXO6, LPGAT1, and EPHB4 as high-risk genes for gastric cancer, and we validated the expression of these five genes through in vitro experiments. However, our study has some limitations. First, the data involved in this study were derived from Japanese and European individuals, and further research is needed to determine the applicability of these results to other ethnicities. Second, the diagnostic model was built on publicly available datasets with limited diversity in gastric-cancer sample types; additional samples from multiple tissue origins are required to validate the expression levels of the high-risk genes. Moreover, prospective cohort studies are required to investigate the impact of these five high-risk genes on gastric cancer. And our analysis focused on eQTL-level associations; protein-level validation will require further experimental work. Additionally, further basic experiments are needed to comprehensively understand the mechanisms of these high-risk genes. We hope that future studies will provide further insights into our research through evidence at other levels.

Conclusion

In conclusion, this study integrated multiple datasets from sc-RNA-seq, GWAS, and eQTL to explore biomarkers in gastric cancer cells and to identify genes associated with gastric cancer risk. Additionally, we conducted in vitro experiments to validate the expression levels of these five genes. Our findings reveal high-risk gastric cancer genes that have not been previously focused on, potentially providing new directions and evidence for the molecular diagnosis and treatment of gastric cancer.
