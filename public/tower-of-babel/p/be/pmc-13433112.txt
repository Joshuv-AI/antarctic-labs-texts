Evidence summary of nursing care of arterial drug administration and catheter management for HAIC in hepatocellular carcinoma
Authors: Zuxin Shi, Linqiu Han, Tian He, Mingyan Shen
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Background:This study aimed to summarize and grade the existing evidence on continuous hepatic artery infusion chemotherapy (HAIC) arterial drug administration and catheter care, providing evidence-based guidance for clinical nursing decisions.Methods:Using the “6S” pyramid model, We searched 10 electronic resources (UpToDate, Joanna Briggs Institute [JBI], Cochrane Library, Web of Science, Embase, PubMed, EBSCOhost Research Platform, China National Knowledge Infrastructure, Wanfang, and SinoMed) and 5 professional organization websites (National Comprehensive Cancer Network, the American Society of Clinical Oncology, the Japan Society of Clinical Oncology, the Oncology Nursing Society, and the Multinational Association of Supportive Care in Cancer/European Society for Medical Oncology) from inception to March 31, 2024. Quality appraisal tools were selected by evidence type: Appraisal of Guidelines for Research and Evaluation II for guidelines, JBI critical appraisal tools for systematic reviews/meta-analyses and JBI standards for expert consensus, and the Critical Appraisal for Summaries of Evidence tool for evidence summaries. Evidence was graded using the JBI levels of evidence (2014).Results:We identified 769 records and included 12 publications (1 guideline, 2 systematic reviews, 4 meta-analyses, 2 evidence summaries, and 3 expert consensus documents). A total of 36 evidence statements were organized into 4 domains: HAIC puncture care (8), medication care (15), complication prevention and management (10), and catheter removal care (3). Evidence levels ranged from 1b to 5b.Conclusion:This evidence summary offers clear, graded recommendations to guide continuous arterial drug administration and catheter care in HAIC. The evidence can be used to develop or update local protocols and to support bedside decisions on HAIC drug delivery and catheter management. Implementation should be tailored to local healthcare settings by considering workflow, device availability, staffing capacity, and patient risk profiles to optimize safety and feasibility.

What we investigated and why

Hepatic artery infusion chemotherapy has become a critical treatment option for patients with unresectable advanced liver cancer, helping to extend survival and create opportunities for surgery. This study focuses on drug administration and catheter care, using an evidence-based approach to develop best practices. These practices offer clinical nursing teams and patients scientific, practical, and cost-effective management strategies to ensure safe medication use and reduce adverse events.

How we conducted the study

This study followed the Evidence Summary Reporting Standards of Fudan University’s Evidence-Based Nursing Center. The report was developed based on the Joanna Briggs Institute’s evidence summary methodology, involving processes such as problem formulation, literature search, screening, quality assessment, evidence extraction, and classification to generate the final evidence entries.

1. Introduction

Primary liver cancer is a malignancy with a high incidence rate worldwide, ranking 3rd among the leading causes of cancer-related deaths globally.[1] According to Global Cancer Observatory 2022, liver cancer accounted for approximately 866,000 new cases and 759,000 deaths worldwide, with the majority of cases occurring in Asia.[2] China contributes a substantial proportion of the global burden.[3] Although surgical resection remains the only curative treatment for liver cancer, most patients with advanced-stage liver cancer lose the opportunity for surgery by the time they are diagnosed.[4] Consequently, interventional therapies have become a crucial treatment option for patients with unresectable advanced liver cancer.

Hepatic arterial infusion chemotherapy (HAIC) involves delivering chemotherapeutic agents directly into the liver’s target lesion area via a hepatic arterial port or an indwelling arterial catheter. Regarding clinical practice guidelines, the Barcelona Clinic of Liver Cancer group and the National Comprehensive Cancer Network recommend systemic therapies as the primary approach for extensive disease.[5,6] While Asian groups, including those in Korea, Japan, and China, suggest systemic therapies and HAIC as potential treatment options for these patients.[7,8]

Both transarterial chemoembolization (TACE) and HAIC have demonstrated efficacy in advanced or unresectable HCC.[9] Unlike TACE, HAIC emphasizes continuous arterial drug administration. This sustained delivery is critical for time-dependent drugs like 5-fluorouracil, which require prolonged exposure for optimal cytotoxicity. It can also increase intrahepatic exposure to concentration-dependent agents such as oxaliplatin through local arterial delivery.[10] The infusion typically lasts 24 to 48 hours and uses portable infusion pumps.[11] Comparative studies suggest that HAIC may offer a superior objective response rate, disease control rate, and overall survival compared to TACE in patients with unresectable HCC.[12,13]

However, the unique administration mode of HAIC presents significant challenges. The requirement for prolonged continuous infusion via an indwelling arterial catheter imposes distinct technical and safety demands on nursing staff. These demands include the management of catheter displacement, arterial thrombosis, and pump malfunction.[14] Despite the established clinical consensus on medical regimens, a critical knowledge gap remains regarding nursing practice. Current guidelines primarily focus on therapeutic efficacy and patient selection, while nursing-specific recommendations for arterial catheter care and infusion safety are often fragmented or insufficiently operationalized for bedside decision-making. Consequently, there is a lack of a unified, evidence-based protocol to guide nurses in managing these complex procedures.

This study aims to evaluate and integrate the evidence on continuous chemotherapy infusion care for patients with indwelling hepatic arterial catheters, using an evidence-based approach to summarize the best available evidence, thereby providing clinical nurses with evidence-based recommendations for scientific and standardized drug administration and arterial catheter care.

We retrieved evidence using the 6S hierarchy of evidence, which recommends starting at the highest available level (systems/summaries) and moving downward only when higher-level evidence is insufficient for the clinical question.[16] Accordingly, we 1st searched UpToDate, Joanna Briggs Institute (JBI), and The Cochrane Library. Comprehensive databases searched included Web of Science, Embase, PubMed, EBSCOhost Research Platform, China Biomedical Literature Database (Sinomed), China National Knowledge Infrastructure, and Wanfang. The search timeframe extended up to March 31, 2024. The search terms included:

2.4. Quality assessment of literature

Different quality assessment tools were used based on the type of literature. Conflicts of interest and funding disclosures of included publications were not formally extracted or appraised. For all evaluations of guidelines, the ICC values were >0.750, indicating the high consistency and good reliability between the evaluators.

Guidelines were evaluated using the Appraisal of Guidelines for Research and Evaluation II instrument,[17] which covers 6 domains and 23 items. Each item was rated from 1 (strongly disagree) to 7 (strongly agree), and scores for each domain were standardized into percentages. Guidelines with scores >60% in all domains were rated as grade A, while those with ≥3 domains scoring between 30% and 60% were rated grade B, and those with ≥3 domains scoring <30% were rated grade C.

Four reviewers trained in evidence-based nursing independently appraised the included guidelines using the Appraisal of Guidelines for Research and Evaluation II instrument, and the scores were cross-checked. Any discrepancies were resolved through group discussion and reexamination of the guideline text; if disagreement persisted, the group leader made the final decision. For other types of literature, 2 reviewers independently conducted appraisal using the corresponding tools, with cross-checking. Disagreements were resolved by reexamining the original sources and discussion; if consensus could not be reached, the group leader acted as a 3rd reviewer to adjudicate and make the final decision.

2.5. Evidence synthesis and grading

Two researchers (SZX and HLQ) independently extracted evidence using a structured extraction form that included evidence topic, description, source, reference, and level. To derive the final set of evidence, raw extractions were synthesized following 3 specific principles: for overlapping evidence, concise and clear recommendations were prioritized; complementary evidence addressing the same theme was logically integrated; and conflicting evidence was resolved by prioritizing newer publications and higher-quality evidence. Disputes were resolved by reexamining source documents, with final adjudication by the group leader as 3rd therapy specialist. Through this process, the initial extractions were consolidated into 36 final pieces of evidence. We used the JBI Levels of Evidence system (2014 version)[21] to classify evidence into 5 levels based on study design, with level 1 (experimental designs) being the highest and level 5 (expert opinion) the lowest. For clinical guidelines, original sources were traced to ensure accurate grading. In this study, no major conflicting evidence requiring parallel comparison was identified among the included studies.

2.6. Systematic review registration

This evidence summary study utilized the standard of evidence summary report from Fudan University Center for evidence-based Nursing, which includes problem establishment, evidence retrieval, literature screening, quality evaluation of the literature, and the summary and grading of evidence.[22,23] The registration no. is ES20244006.

Studies focused on clinical outcomes were retained when they contained extractable data on symptom management or adverse event monitoring that could directly inform nursing protocols for patient safety. 4. Discussion

4.1. Optimizing HAIC catheter placement and catheter care

4.1.1. Strategic catheter selection and access site choice

Successful HAIC administration requires strategic catheter placement decisions that balance procedural efficacy with patient safety. The current HAIC protocol, involving oxaliplatin, calcium folinate, and fluorouracil administered tri-weekly over a minimum of 4 cycles,[33,36] presents unique challenges due to the necessity for repeated catheter repositioning at each session.

Single-use arterial catheterization has emerged as the preferred approach over hepatic arterial port installation, primarily due to its procedural adaptability and reduced risk of port-related complications.[11,37] This approach aligns with the Japanese Society of Interventional Radiology clinical practice guidelines for hepatic arterial infusion chemotherapy. This preference introduces the challenge of selecting optimal vascular access for each treatment session. The choice between femoral and radial arterial access should be individualized based on patient-specific conditions and procedural requirements.

4.1.2. Pre-procedural vascular and dermatological assessment

For radial access candidates, pre-procedural evaluation using the Allen test provides a straightforward and effective assessment of radial and ulnar artery patency and collateral circulation.[38] The reliability of this bedside test depends on rigorous adherence to standardized protocols, with supplementary diagnostics such as Doppler ultrasound or arteriography recommended when clinical uncertainty exists.[36]

Beyond vascular evaluation, a thorough assessment of pericatheter skin conditions is essential to minimize infection and complication risks. This assessment should systematically evaluate skin color, temperature, integrity, and signs of infection to ensure puncture site suitability. The integration of vascular and dermatological assessments reflects a holistic approach to patient selection and risk stratification.

4.1.3. Insertion technique and stabilization strategies

The Seldinger technique is recommended as the standard insertion method, offering advantages in simplicity, minimal invasiveness, and high success rates while reducing both immediate surgical risks and subsequent catheter-related complications.[36] Post-insertion stabilization represents a critical but often underemphasized aspect of catheter care. Radial artery catheterization carries a specific risk of arterial spasm due to smooth muscle stimulation during catheter manipulation. Radial artery spasm manifests as arterial narrowing and reduced lumen size, complicating blood flow and instrument passage.[39] The use of specialized radial artery catheter fixation sleeves addresses this challenge through dual mechanisms: effective catheter stabilization and provision of thermal comfort, both contributing to reduced spasm incidence and enhanced patient tolerance.

4.1.4. Catheter identification and safety marking

Given the high-risk nature of arterial catheters, proper identification marking serves as a fundamental safety measure. Clear catheter labeling enables healthcare personnel to rapidly recognize catheter type, location, and intended purpose, thereby reducing mishandling risks and enhancing overall patient safety. This seemingly simple intervention reflects broader principles of high-reliability healthcare systems, where clear communication and visual cues prevent adverse events.

4.2. Ensuring patient safety and comfort during continuous HAIC administration

4.2.1. Process standardization and monitoring

As HAIC involves high-risk procedures and chemotherapy administration, safeguarding patient safety through process standardization is critical.[40] A double-check of personnel and equipment should occur before each session.[41] Continuous monitoring includes vital signs, abdominal indicators, catheter position, and equipment functionality. Multi-parameter surveillance enables rapid intervention when complications arise.

4.2.2. Patient positioning and systemic toxicity management

Positioning strategy depends on the access site. Femoral catheter patients should restrict major leg movements to prevent displacement. Radial access patients should have the forearm elevated on a cushion with the wrist flexed 10° to 15° to facilitate venous return.[42] HAIC causes systemic complications, including fever, rash, pruritus, nausea, vomiting, diarrhea, neutropenia, anemia, and hepatic dysfunction.[43] Routine monitoring of complete blood counts, liver and kidney function, and coagulation profiles enables early toxicity detection and treatment adjustment. Laboratory surveillance should follow standardized intervals to establish trends.

4.2.3. Prevention of contrast-induced nephropathy

Contrast-induced nephropathy ranks as the 3rd leading cause of hospital-acquired acute kidney injury.[44] Prevention requires meticulous hydration: fluid intake ≥3000 mL/d and urine output >2000 mL/d during the 1st 48 hours post-catheterization. Evidence-based protocols recommend intravenous 0.9% normal saline at 1 mL/kg/h for 6 to 12 hours pre-procedure, continuing post-procedure.[45] Hydration targets may require adjustment based on cardiac function, baseline renal status, and fluid tolerance.

4.2.4. Patient education and psychological support

Patient comfort and psychological preparation significantly impact adherence. Active listening to patient experiences provides insights complementing objective monitoring. Structured education with information brochures provided pre-procedure significantly reduces procedural anxiety and fear.[46] Education regarding expected sensations, warning signs, and positioning rationale enhances cooperation and reduces anxiety. This communication improves treatment adherence across multi-cycle regimens.[42] Comprehensive documentation and shift handovers ensure treatment continuity, enable pattern recognition, and prevent information loss leading to errors.[44]

4.3. Enhancing arterial catheter care to minimize complication risks

4.3.1. Monitoring for mechanical and infectious complications

Effective management of catheter-related complications directly impacts treatment completion rates and patient outcomes during HAIC administration.

Three primary complications require systematic monitoring during HAIC: catheter displacement, blockage, and infection. Displacement compromises drug delivery precision and may require repositioning. Blockage interrupts infusion and risks incomplete dosing. Infection remains a concern despite lower rates with single-use systems compared to implanted ports. Early recognition through regular assessment of catheter function, flow rates, and insertion site appearance enables timely intervention.

4.3.2. Management of radial artery spasm and unplanned removal risks

Radial artery spasm occurs more frequently than femoral spasm due to smaller vessel size. Management emphasizes non-pharmacological approaches: active limb movement and gentle forearm massage promote vasodilation. Compression adjustment, guided by oxygen saturation monitoring, balances hemostasis against radial artery occlusion risk without increasing bleeding. Unplanned removal causes treatment interruption and necessitates re-catheterization with associated risks. High-risk patients can be identified through assessment of consciousness, mental status, pain control, fever, pruritus, and prior removal history. Recognition enables targeted interventions, including enhanced education, increased monitoring, and family involvement.[47]

4.3.3. Post-removal protocols and discharge care

When unplanned removal occurs, immediate assessment must verify catheter integrity and evaluate the puncture site. Re-catheterization decisions should weigh treatment urgency against complication risks. Post-removal protocols differ by access site. Femoral access requires 6 to 12 hours of assisted ambulation following sheath removal due to larger vessel size and deeper location. Radial access permits immediate ambulation due to superficial location, enabling direct compression.

Regardless of site, patients should avoid re-puncture or blood pressure measurement on the affected limb, manipulation of the puncture site for 1 week, and heavy lifting for 4 weeks.

4.4. Implications for nursing practice and research

This synthesis of 36 evidence-based recommendations provides a framework for HAIC nursing care spanning the full procedural continuum.

4.4.1. Standardization and protocol implementation

Translating these recommendations into consistent bedside practice requires adaptation to diverse clinical contexts. Standardized protocols offer particular value in institutions with moderate HAIC procedure volumes, where consistent protocols maintain competency across distributed provider experience. Successful implementation demands alignment with existing workflows, electronic health record systems, and nursing staffing models.

4.4.2. Educational requirements and resource allocation

The technical complexity of HAIC procedures necessitates structured nursing education addressing vascular assessment skills, early complication recognition, risk stratification, and patient communication. Beyond education, institutions must consider resource allocation for equipment, simulation-based training, and potentially specialized credentialing processes.

4.6. Limitations

This review has several limitations. First, the search was restricted to Chinese and English databases, potentially overlooking relevant studies in other languages. Second, the methodological quality of the included evidence was heterogeneous. While systematic reviews provided strong evidence for medication management, specific nursing procedures relied heavily on expert consensus (level 5 evidence). This reliance on expert opinion implies a lower certainty of evidence for procedural recommendations. Finally, the specific nursing protocols varied across the included guidelines, reflecting a lack of global standardization.

5. Conclusion

This evidence summary synthesizes 36 recommendations for HAIC nursing care and provides an evidence-informed framework to support standardized bedside practice. The study suggests that safe HAIC nursing care requires standardized vascular assessment and catheter placement and fixation. It also requires continuous monitoring during infusion and timely prevention and management of catheter-related events such as displacement, occlusion, or thrombosis, and infection. Implementation should be adapted with local validation, and future studies should compare catheter care bundles in multicenter prospective designs or pragmatic RCTs.
