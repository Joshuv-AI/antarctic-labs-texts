Powers of magnetic graph matrix: Fourier spectrum, walk compression, and applications
Authors: Yinan Huang, David F. Gleich, Pan Li
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
SignificanceMagnetic graph matrices are powerful tools for modeling quantum systems and directed networks, but their application in network analysis has been limited by a lack of combinatorial understanding. We present a combinatorial interpretation that fundamentally reveals how these matrices encode local network structure. We further show that this structure information is highly compressible in real-world networks, enabling accurate approximations from a small number of magnetic potentials. This fresh foundation also unlocks further applications, like identifying crucial network motifs (e.g., frustrated directed cycles such as feed-forward loops) and enhancing directed link prediction.

Magnetic graphs are complex-weighted graphs in which each edge is assigned a complex phase. Originally developed to model the effects of external magnetic fields on electrons in lattice systems (1–6), magnetic graphs have recently proven to be powerful tools for analyzing complex systems with asymmetric interactions (7–10). These graphs encode edge directionality through complex phases in their matrix representations, such as adjacency or Laplacian matrix. For instance, the spectrum of magnetic graph Laplacians has been used to reveal community structures in directed cycles (11) and to enhance visualization of directed networks (12, 13). More recently, magnetic graphs have inspired novel signal processing and machine learning models tailored to directed graphs (14–16). Additionally, magnetic graph Laplacians can be interpreted as connection graph Laplacians associated with the SO(2) group (17), leading to applications in angular synchronization (18, 19) and high-dimensional data analysis via diffusion maps (20), as well as algorithms for sparsifying and solving linear systems in connection Laplacians (21).

Previous studies on the magnetic graph matrix have primarily focused on how their eigenvalues and eigenvectors encode the topological properties of directed graphs (11–13, 22, 23). Such spectral information is widely recognized for capturing global and stationary characteristics of graphs (24). In contrast, modeling local or nonequilibrium behaviors often requires the analysis of random walks, quantum walks, or diffusion processes (24–26), which are often characterized by the powers of the graph matrix. For undirected graphs, these processes have been extensively studied and proven effective for detecting local communities and node clusters (27–30), quantifying node centrality [e.g., PageRank (31, 32), Katz index (33)] and measuring node proximity (34, 35). However, their counterparts on magnetic graphs remain relatively underexplored. This stems not only from the complex-valued nature of the magnetic matrix, whose powers resist straightforward application of standard Markov process theory (11), but also critically, from the lack of a clear combinatorial interpretation that links the matrix power to the underlying directed network topology.

In this article, we investigate the powers of the magnetic graph matrix to uncover properties of local network behaviors encoded in directed graph topologies. Our primary finding is that the m-th power of the magnetic graph adjacency matrix with magnetic potential q naturally corresponds to the Fourier transform of a bidirectional walk statistic, which we term the Walk Profile. Specifically, the m-step walk profile consists of bidirectional walk counts of length m, indexed by the number of edge reversals. These ideas also give probabilistic interpretations when using the normalized adjacency matrix, in which case, these walk counts become the corresponding bidirectional random walk landing probabilities. This result is significant as it reveals a fundamental connection between the behaviors on magnetic graphs captured by powers of the magnetic matrix and the combinatorial structure of the underlying directed graphs, described by walk profiles. It also offers a physical interpretation of the magnetic potential q as the Fourier transform frequency. Importantly, this connection implies that by evaluating magnetic matrix powers at a finite set of discrete potentials, one can reconstruct the entire m-step bidirectional walk profiles via inverse Fourier transforms, thereby enabling the expression of magnetic matrix powers at arbitrary values of potential q.

In theory, to exactly reconstruct the m-step walk profiles, evaluating magnetic matrix powers at ⌊m/2⌋+1 distinct frequencies q is required. However, for most random and real-world graphs, we demonstrate that significantly fewer frequencies often provide an accurate approximate reconstruction, as the energy of the walk profiles predominantly concentrates in the low-frequency region. The accuracy of this compression depends on the spectral radius of the magnetic graph matrix, which is related to the smallest eigenvalue of the magnetic graph Laplacian and its associated frustration index (18, 22). This relationship enables us to characterize directed graphs whose walk profiles are either easily compressible or resistant to compression, by analyzing the eigenvalues of their corresponding magnetic graph matrix.

Regarding practical applications, we further explore the feasibility of capturing diverse directed motifs using magnetic matrix powers with a varying number of potentials. In particular, we demonstrate that these representations can detect minimally frustrated directed cycles, i.e., directed cycles perturbed by a single reversed edge, which extend beyond purely directed cycles emphasized in previous spectral analyses of magnetic Laplacians (11). The minimally frustrated directed cycles include feedforward loops, a prominent three-node motif prevalent in control systems and biological networks (36). Finally, we employ magnetic matrix powers as structural features for directed graphs in link prediction tasks, aimed at inferring missing directed edges in incomplete networks. The strong performance of these features highlights their effectiveness in encoding local structural information of directed graphs.

Results

Magnetic Graph Matrix and Walk Profiles.

We first define Walk Profiles that captures bidirectional walk patterns of a directed graph, and we will then show how powers of the magnetic matrix are inherently connected to walk profiles (Fig. 1). Let G be a directed graph with asymmetric adjacency matrix A, where Au,v=1 if u connects to v, denoted by u→v and otherwise Au,v=0. If G is a weighted directed graph, then Au,v is real-valued and represents the edge weight. A bidirectional walk refers to a sequence of nodes u1,u2,...,um, where each transition step can be either be forward edge ui→ui+1 or a backward edge ui←ui+1. Walk profiles, denoted by Φu,v(m,k), are defined by the number of m-step bidirectional walks from node u to node v, with exact k forward edges and m−k backward edges. An example is illustrated in Fig. 1A. These can be computed via a general iterative matrix expression (SI Appendix, Eq. S2), for instance, Φu,v(2,2)=[A2]u,v,Φu,v(2,1)=[AA⊤+A⊤A]u,v,Φu,v(2,0)=[(A⊤)2]u,v.

The graph is given in the Left figure and the three repetitions show the three different paths counted in Φ1,4(3,2). (B) The collection of walk profiles Φu,v(3,k),k=0,...,3 on the same graph, and how its Fourier transform relates to the powers of the magnetic adjacency matrix.

The directed graph G induces a magnetic graph with potential q, defined via a complex-valued, Hermitian adjacency matrix Aq=Aei2πq+A⊤e−i2πq called magnetic adjacency matrix (8). A forward edge u→v induces a positive-phase edge weight [Aq]u,v=ei2πq and a backward edge u←v induces a negative-phase edge weight [Aq]u,v=e−i2πq, so that the phases encode the direction of edges in G. We focus on the powers of magnetic adjacency matrix Aqm that naturally characterizes how information propagates on the magnetic graph.

One of our key findings is that by expanding the powers of matrix power [Aqm]u,v we can rewrite it as a Fourier transform of walk profiles [Φu,v(m,m),...,Φu,v(m,0)] at frequency 2q:[1][Aqm]u,v=[(Aei2πq+A⊤e−i2πq)m]u,v[2]=e−i2πqm∑k=0mei4πqkΦu,v(m,k).

Intuitively, each potential q plays the role of a Fourier frequency that separates the contributions of the edges forward and backward in the aggregated walk signal Aqm. For example if q=0, Aq=0m=(A+A⊤)m is the simple sum ∑k=0mΦ(m,k), which accounts for walks in all possible directions. It is convenient to represent walk profiles in a vector form: Φ(m,·)=(Φ(m,m),Φ(m,m−1),...,Φ(m,0)). We can rewrite Eq. 2 as Aqm=e−i2πqm·F2q(Φ(m,·)), i.e., Aqm is the discrete Fourier transform of a signal Φ(m,·) at ordinary frequency 2q. See SI Appendix, section S.II for the full details of derivation.

Magnetic Graph Matrix with Multiple Potentials.

The Fourier transform interpretation implies that Aqm only carries partial information about Φ(m,·) and it suggests that we consider a full set of potentials q→=(q0,...,qQ−1) so that Aq→m=(Aq0m,...,AqQ−1m) can express full information in Φ(m,·). This is important because once Φ(m,·) is reconstructed from Aq→m, then Aqm for arbitrary q can be calculated by Eq. 2. In other words, there exists a finite set of potentials q→ that capture all possible information magnetic adjacency matrix can express. Note that Fourier transform Eq. 2 with Q many potentials q→ can be written as a Q×(m+1) linear system:[3]ei2πq0m·Aq0mei2πq1m·Aq1m⋯ei2πqQ−1m·AqQ−1m=Fq→,m+1Φ(m,m)Φ(m,m−1)⋯Φ(m,0),

where [Fq→,Q]j,k=ei4πqjk is a Q×(m+1) Fourier matrix. Since Φ(m,·) is real, we can compare real parts and imaginary parts of the linear system and construct a 2Q×(m+1) equivalent linear system of real matrices. A canonical choice to solve a Fourier system is to let q→=(q0,...,q⌊m/2⌋+1) with qj≜j2(m+1), which leads to the inverse Fourier transform:[4]Φ(m,k)=1m+1Aq0m+2·∑j=1⌊m/2⌋Re(eiπj(m−2k)m+1·Aqjm)

Therefore, using potentials with qj=j2(m+1), j=0,1,...,⌊m/2⌋ allows us to fully capture walk profiles. More generally, choosing any arbitrary ⌊m/2⌋+1 distinct potentials in the range [0,14] can also solve the linear system, although an analytical solution like Eq. 4 may not exist.

Note that all the results we present so far are not exclusively for Aq, but also can be translated to other types of magnetic graph matrix, such as random walk magnetic matrix D−1Aq and normalized magnetic adjacency matrix D−1/2AqD−1/2 (D=Diag(d1,d2,...) is the diagonal degree matrix, counting both in-edges and out-edges). For example, let us denote A^q≜D−1Aq and Φ^(m,k) as the corresponding random-walk version. Eqs. 2–4 still hold if we replace all Φ(m,k) by Φ^(m,k) and all Aqm by A^qm. Particularly, when we consider the random-walk version of walk profiles, [Φ^(m,k)]u,v can be interpreted as the transition probability of m-step bidirectional random walks from node u to v, whose random walk sequences contain exact k forward edges and m−k backward edges. The probabilistic interpretation implies the normalization property ∑k=0m∑v[Φ^(m,k)]u,v=1.

Spectral Sparsity and Compressibility of Walk Profiles.

Having established the walk profile as the Fourier transform of the magnetic matrix, a natural question arises: To what extent do all magnetic potentials (frequencies) contribute meaningfully to walk profiles? Many biological and physical systems exhibit sloppiness, meaning their behavior is largely governed by a few stiff combinations of parameters, while being relatively insensitive to variations in the remaining, sloppy directions (37, 38). Similarly, in classical signal processing, many natural signals can be efficiently approximated using only a small number of dominant frequencies (39–42). These motivate us to ask whether walk profiles admit a similar kind of compressibility.

Here we explore the spectral density of random-walk walk profiles Φ^u,v(m,·), i.e., the magnitude |[A^qm]u,v| with respect to different potential q’s (formal definitions in Materials and Methods). We analyze Erdős-Renyi random graphs and 26 real-world directed networks from 8 different domains, ranging in size from N≈102 to N≈106 (Materials and Methods and SI Appendix, section S.I). We mainly adopt walk length m=50, while our findings and conclusions are consistent for different walk lengths (SI Appendix, section S.III). Fig. 2 shows that on most networks, the energy in walk profiles tends to be largely concentrated on the low-frequency region.

Spectral density of walk profiles. The average relative spectral density of walk profiles Φ(50,·), i.e., ⟨|Aq50|2⟩ with respect to potential q, often shows a concentration in the low-frequency regime. The average ⟨·⟩ is taken over all node pairs in the network.

This strongly implies that walk profiles, the combinatorial structure of bidirectional walks, are compressible: Although ⌊m/2⌋+1 many potentials are required to precisely express walk profiles Φ^(m,·) in the frequency space, the top-Q smallest potentials already carry the essential information of walk profiles and should be able to approximate walk profiles with neglectable reconstruction errors. To verify this, we choose the first Q smallest potentials q→=(02(m+1),12(m+1),...,Q−12(m+1)) and reconstruct normalized walk profiles Φ^(m,·) from A^q→m by solving the underdetermined linear system (Eq. 3). Fig. 3 shows how reconstruction error (the relative error between true Φ^(m,·) and the estimated one, averaged over node pairs) varies with different number of potentials Q. As expected, as we increases Q, the reconstruction error gradually decreases, and goes to zero when Q reaches ⌊m/2⌋+1. Notably, for many real-world networks, adopting less than m/5 number of potentials is sufficient to yield a low reconstruction error of 5%. This finding, however, is far from universal and the data show substantial variation in reconstruction. For instance, computational and program graphs require more potentials than social and information networks. We study this behavior next.

Spectral Sparsity from Decayed Spectral Radius.

We have found that networks differ in how strongly their energy concentrates in the low-frequency region, which in turn affects the compressibility of their walk profiles. Here, we argue that this spectral sparsity phenomenon stems from and is governed by the global structure of directed networks, specifically, the spectral radius of random-walk magnetic matrix A^q. Intuitively, the magnitude |[A^qm]u,v|2 is asymptotically governed by the spectral radius ρ(A^q). Thus, we expect that if ρ(A^q)<ρ(A^q′), then |[A^qm]u,v|≪|[A^q′m]u,v| as m goes large. Note that the spectral radius of A^q is closely related to the smallest eigenvalue of magnetic Laplacian L^q=I−A^q, which is known to describe how “consistent” the magnetic graph is, measured by the maximal accumulated phases over any cycles in the directed graph (12). For instance, the smallest eigenvalue of the magnetic Laplacian of a directed tree, i.e., a directed graph whose underlying undirected graph is a tree, is zero, because the accumulated phase along any round trip between two nodes on the magnetic graph is always zero (43).

The Left subfigures of Fig. 4 illustrate how the spectral radius ρ(A^q) varies with q (more results in SI Appendix, section S.III). Interestingly, we consistently observe a decayed pattern of ρ(A^q) as q grows, which corresponds to the low-frequency structure of walk profiles. To further validate the connection between the spectral radius and walk profile compressibility, we compare the reconstruction errors of using Q potentials vs. the spectral radius of A^qQ with qQ=Q2(m+1) (here we choose m=50 and Q=10). If the spectral radius of A^qQ is small, it should yield a stronger energy concentration effect and thus a better compressibility. Indeed, a clear positive correlation between these two is observed (Pearson correlation coefficient r=0.95 with 95% CI [0.9,0.98]), as shown in the Right subfigure in Fig. 4. This also explains why the compressibility is poor for program graphs, since they are directed trees with ρ(A^q)≡1, leading to the uniform spectral density of their walk profiles. SI Appendix, section S.III further supports the correlation between compressibility and spectral radius, showing that compression becomes progressively easier for graphs with a monotonically decreasing spectral radius.

Spectral radius of A^q characterizes walk profiles compressibility. Left four subfigures: the decay phenomenon of spectral radius of A^q, w.r.t. the potential q on some representative networks. Right: The decay rate of spectral radius (measured by spectral radius of A^q10 with q10=102(50+1)=5/51) is strongly correlated with the compressibility of walk profiles Φ^(50,·), with Pearson correlation coefficient r=0.95 and 95% CI (0.90,0.98) estimated by Fisher transformation.

Directed Network Motif Detection.

We now explore the ability to detect motifs in directed networks using the powers of magnetic adjacency matrix and walk profiles. Network motifs are substructures (subgraphs) that serve as the fundamental building blocks of complex networks, which can be used to uncover a network’s functional abilities, e.g., for biological networks(44–46). Particularly, we are interested in direction-aware cycles, i.e., a sequence of unrepeated nodes (u1,u2,...,um,u1) that can be traversed with either forward edges or backward edges. Given a cycle length, there are different nonisomorphic classes of cycles, categorized by the order of the edge orientations. For instance, 4-cycles have four classes: fully oriented u1→u2→u3→u4→u1, partially oriented with one backward edge u1←u2→u3→u4→u1, partially oriented with two successive backward edges u1←u2←u3→u4→u1, partially oriented with two nonsuccessive backward edges u1←u2→u3←u4→u1.

We are interested to see whether the powers of the magnetic adjacency matrix ([Aq0m]u,u,...,[AqQ−1m]u,u) can identify if a node u is part of a cycle of length m. While walk-based methods generally cannot guarantee the detection of cycles, because walks permit repeated nodes whereas cycles do not, they are frequently adopted as a proxy for cycle detection for two main reasons: a) Walk counts correlate well with the presence or absence of cycles. For instance, a walk count of zero for a specific length m at node u indicates the absence of m-cycles involving u. Moreover, a walk that does not revisit any intermediate nodes is, in fact, a cycle; b) Calculating walks is computationally efficient, primarily requiring standard matrix multiplication.

Remarkably, through an analysis of their theoretical capacity (SI Appendix, section S.IV), we can prove that even assuming walks have no repeated nodes, it is impossible for walk profiles or magnetic adjacency matrix powers to detect cycles containing more than one backward edge. This fundamental limitation arises because walk profiles only account for the number of backward edges, not their specific ordering. Consequently, walk profiles cannot differentiate between nonisomorphic cycles that possess the same quantity of backward edges but differ in the arrangement of these edges. This inability to distinguish extends to the powers of magnetic adjacency matrix, as they convey the same informational content as walk profiles, a point substantiated by Eq. 2.

On the other hand, walk profiles (and powers of magnetic adjacency matrices) can detect fully oriented cycles and, importantly, one-backward-edge cycles, assuming the absence of repeated node counting in the walks. This is because when there is at most one backward edge in the cycles, the position of the single backward edge does not affect the detection of such substructures, due to the rotational symmetry of cycles. The class of one-backward-edge cycles include feed-forward loops prominent in biological systems (36), which cannot be detected by unidirected walks. Notably, this capacity goes beyond prior spectral analyses of magnetic Laplacians (11), which primarily focused on purely directed cycles (i.e., cycles with all edges aligned to the same direction).

To validate our argument above, we train and test a linear classifier (logistic regression) to predict if a node u is involved in a class of length-m cycle, using Φu,u(m,·) or [Aq0m]u,u,...,[AqQ−1m]u,u as input features. We also compare to undirected walks, i.e., (A+A⊤)m, and unidirected walks, i.e., Am and (A⊤)m. The detailed descriptions of the experimental setup are in Materials and Methods. Fig. 5 shows that walk profiles can effectively capture cycles involving a single backward edge. Meanwhile, the performance of the magnetic adjacency matrix, with an increasing number of potentials, progressively approaches the performance of walk profiles, as anticipated from their theoretical connection.

Directed Network Link Prediction.

We next explore the application of magnetic matrix to link prediction tasks, by leveraging their ability to capture directed structural patterns that can help the inference of missing or future edges. We train and test a linear classifier to predict if a directed edge exists from u to v. We use link features [Aq01]u,v,...,[AqQ−11]u,v,...,[Aq0m]u,v,...,[AqQ−1m]u,v representing the walk information within an m-hop subgraph. For comparison, we evaluate several baseline methods commonly used for link prediction (refer to Materials and Methods). It is worth noticing that the baseline methods are mostly only capable of capturing unidirectional information, i.e., none of them can express bidirectional patterns such as feed-forward loops (3-cycles with one backward edge). Fig. 6 shows the AUCROC score on the training set and the test set using different link features mentioned. Compared to other baseline methods, both magnetic adjacency matrix and walk profiles consistently achieve a better test prediction performance, highlighting their effectiveness in capturing crucial substructures and predicting missing edges. Remarkably, the powers of magnetic adjacency matrix with only a single potential achieve competitive results to walk profiles, beating all baseline methods. This again suggests the empirical compressibility of walk profiles in the context of inference of missing directed links.

Discussion

The issue of directed networks for modeling complex systems with asymmetric interactions has a rich history. Despite its breadth of applications, directed networks have posed theoretical and algorithmic challenges, particularly in spectral and walk-based analyses. This arises due to the tension between the asymmetry of the interaction with the desire for symmetry for node-level properties, along with the inherent nonreversibility of the common associated random walk processes. These asymmetries hinder the direct application of the powerful spectral theory and algorithmic frameworks developed for undirected graphs. To address this, researchers have proposed various symmetric matrix representations for directed graphs, including straightforward symmetrization via A+A⊤, bipartite graph representations (47–49), bibliometric symmetrization AA⊤+A⊤A (50), asymmetric Laplacians (51, 52), directed graph Laplacian based on circulations (53), and motif-based symmetrizations (54). The magnetic Laplacian we study emerged from this study as a flexible approach to “symmetric” modeling of directed networks. It preserves the “symmetry” of problems through the complex-valued edge weights encoding of edge orientation, yielding a Hermitian matrix representation of the directed networks. Moreover, it admits a natural physical interpretation as modeling the phase acquired by a charged particle (e.g., an electron) in a magnetic field. These properties make the magnetic matrix a compelling framework for understanding the geometry and dynamics of directed networks.

At the same time, there has been a long-standing and fruitful exchange of ideas among physics, combinatorics, algorithms, and algebra in the study of undirected networks. Foundational concepts such as the graph Laplacian, graph cuts, random walks, and the heat (diffusion) equation are deeply interconnected, offering a unified lens for both theoretical analysis and practical algorithms (24). While the magnetic matrix provides a clear physical interpretation for directed networks, its connection to combinatorial structure remains less clear. This paper resolves that issue by uncovering a combinatorial foundation for the magnetic matrix via the concept of walk profiles. Walk counts are a fundamental concept in network science and these formulations could enable broader relationships with heat kernels and matrix resolvents (55). They also generalize to non-backtracking random walks (56, 57), which we leave for future studies. This connection offers a perspective to study complex systems with directional interactions, and thus opens exciting avenues for theory and applications of complex networks.

For future work, beyond the motif detection and link prediction tasks explored here, the walk-based feature representation can be adapted to a broader range of applications. For example, a natural extension is generalizing PageRank, heat kernels, and other walk-based graph kernels by incorporating the concept of walk profiles. This would yield features derived from bidirectional walk patterns, directly benefiting tasks such as community detection, graph clustering (58, 59), and graph classification (60) for directed networks.

Materials and Methods

Network Datasets.

The list of all datasets and their statistics are provided in SI Appendix, section S.I.

The synthetic datasets in our paper include Erdős-R’enyi random graphs (61) (ER graphs) and scale-free random directed graphs (62). For ER graphs, we set number of nodes N=5,000 and each node pair (u,v) can be a directed edge independently with probability p=d/N, with varying d. For scale-free random directed graphs, it starts with a directed 3-cycle and sequentially add nodes and edges until it reaches the desired number of nodes N. Each step it can: 1) with probability α, add a new node connected to an existing node chosen randomly according to the in-degree distribution; 2) with probability β, add an edge between two existing nodes. One existing node is chosen randomly according to the in-degree distribution and the other chosen randomly according to the out-degree distribution; 3) with probability 1−α−β, add a new node connected to an existing node chosen randomly according to the out-degree distribution. We set number of nodes N=3,000 and α=0.75,β=0.2.

The real-world datasets covers 26 networks from 8 different domains, ranging in size from N≈102 to N≈106. It contains widely adopted domains such as a) Biological networks [C. elegans (63), Figeys (64), Drug–drug (65), Drug–target (66), Bio-functions (67), and Protein–protein (68)]; b) Citation networks [Cora_ML (69), Citeseer (70), ogbn-arxiv (71), and Arxiv HEP-TH (72)]; c) Social networks [Adolescent health (73), High-school (74), Epinion (75), and Wikipedia vote (76)]; d) Information networks [Political blogs (77) and Email (78)]; and e) Transportation networks [Air traffic control and US airports (79)]. We also explore two emerging domains of interest in directed network analysis: program graphs [ogbg-code2 (71)] and TPU computational graphs (AlexNet, ResNet, Transformers, Mask-RCNN, BERT) (80).

Spectral Density of Walk Profiles.

The spectral density of walk profiles Φu,v(m,·), by Eq. 2, is |[Aqm]u,v|2. As we choose discrete frequencies qj=j2(m+1), we further define the relative spectral density[5]Su,v(qj)≜|[Aqjm]u,v|2∑ℓ=1⌊m/2⌋+1|[Aqℓm]u,v|2.

For each network dataset, we report the average relative spectral density over a subset of node pairs. We first randomly sample N0<N source nodes (denoted by V0⊆V) from the node set, and then estimate over N0·N node pairs where the first node belongs to this subset V0,[6]⟨S(qj)⟩≜1N0·N∑u∈V0,v∈VSu,v(qj).

This avoids the lengthy calculation over N2 node pairs (u,v) on large graphs. We found that N0=2,000 is sufficient for a good estimate in all networks we adopt, since further increasing N0 does not alter the value of ⟨S(qj)⟩ within our chosen precision (four decimal places).

Walk Profile Reconstruction Algorithm.

The reconstruction of walk profiles Φu,v(m,·) from the powers of magnetic matrix is via solving the linear system Eq. 3. In our empirical study, as the walk profiles are always real, we can further expand the linear system in real domain by comparing the real and imaginary parts on both sides:[7]Re(ei2πq0m·Aq0m)⋯Re(ei2πqQ−1m·AqQ−1m)Im(ei2πq0m·Aq0m)⋯Im(ei2πqQ−1m·AqQm)=Re(Fq→,m+1)Im(Fq→,m+1)Φ(m,m)Φ(m,m−1)⋯Φ(m,0),

To solve this equation, we leverage the Moore–Penrose inverse (·)+ of the expanded Fourier matrix (which is of size 2Q by m+1) to solve Φ(m,·):[8]Φrecon(m,·)=Re(Fq→,m+1)Im(Fq→,m+1)+Re(ei2πq0m·Aq0m)⋯Re(ei2πqQ−1m·AqQ−1m)Im(ei2πq0m·Aq0m)⋯Im(ei2πqQ−1m·AqQ−1m).

We measure the reconstruction errors by the relative L2 distance defined by [9]error=1N·N0∑u∈V0,v∈VΦ^u,v(m,·)−Φ^u,vrecon(m,·)max{Φ^u,v(m,·),Φ^u,vrecon(m,·)},

Again, here we estimate the average error on the same subset of nodes as those in the estimation of the average spectral density.

Motif Detection Method.

The motif detection is a node-level binary classification task to determine if a node is involved in a target motif. We consider the minimally frustrated cycles as the target motif, i.e., directed cycles with one backward edge. Note that we are only interested in whether a node is in such a motif, without considering the possibly different roles of nodes in a motif. For example, consider a feed-forward loop that consists of u1→u2→u3 and u1→u3, then nodes u1,u2,u3 are all considered in such a motif. We use the open-sourced library for directed motif searching (81) to acquire the ground-truth labels.

Given a directed network, we randomly partition the node set into a training set, a validation set, and a test set. We train a logistic regression on the training set, evaluate the ROCAUC on the validation set, and report the ROCAUC on the test set. We use sklearn.linear_model.LogisticRegression as the optimizer.

To detect a target cycle of length m at node u, the input features to the logistic regression will be one of the four: 1) wp: walk profiles of length m(Φu,u(m,0),...,Φu,u(m,m)); 2) mw: m-th powers of magnetic matrix ([Aq0]u,um,...,[AqQ−1]u,um) with qj=j2(m+1); 3) rw: undirected walks of length m(A+A⊤)u,um; 4) rw+: unidirected walks of length m(Au,um,(A⊤)u,um); 5) di-sim (49): Originally proposed for graph clustering, this method uses the left and right singular vectors XL,XR of the regularized asymmetric Laplacian. To adapt it for motif detection, we use row vectors (XL)u,:,(XR)u,: as the node features; and 6) Hermitian (23): Also proposed for graph clustering, this method leverages the eigenvectors X of the Hermitian matrix H:=i(A−A⊤), which corresponds to a specific Magnetic matrix with potential q=1/4. For motif detection, we use (X)u as the node feature. For both di-sim and Hermitian, we use the top-K singular/eigenvectors corresponding to the largest singular/eigenvalues, with K∈{8,16,32,64,128} chosen for best performance.

For powers of magnetic matrix, we first apply the reconstruction mapping (Eq. 8) to obtain Φu,urecon(m,·) for improving numerical stability. These features are calculated on the whole graph, and each dimension of the input features is normalized by subtracting the mean and dividing by the SD on the training set. We implement both the adjacency-matrix version (i.e., each A here is the adjacency matrix) and the random-walk version (i.e., each A here is replaced by the random walk matrix D−1A), and for each target motif detection we report the one with better performance.

Link Prediction Method.

The link prediction task is to predict unobserved edges in an incomplete network. We follow the standard setting of link prediction, randomly partitioning the edge set E of a directed network into training set Etr, validation set Eval and test set Ete. The observed true edges are called positive edges. To perform logistic regression for link prediction, we also need negative samples that are nonedges. To this end, we introduce two types of negative edges: For each (u,v)∈Etr (and Eval,Ete correspondingly), we add 1) reverse negatives: If (v,u)∉E, then add (v,u) as a negative edge; 2) structured negatives: For each (u,v)∈E, find (u,w)∉E and (w′,v)∉E with some node w,w′, and add both of them as negative edges. The final negative edges E¯tr for training is formed by randomly choosing negative edges from the selected negative edges described above such that it has the same number of positive edges and the ratio between reverse negatives and two types of structured negatives are 1:1:1. After constructing positive edges Etr,Eval,Ete and negative edges E¯tr,E¯val,E¯te, we perform logistic regression trained on dataset Etr and E¯tr, where (u,v)∈Etr has label 1 and label 0 otherwise. Again we use sklearn.linear_model.LogisticRegression as the optimizer.

We consider various baselines as the input features to the logistic regression. To predict if (u,v) is an edge, we use one of the followings: 1) wp: walk profiles up to length m(Φu,v(1,0),Φu,v(1,1),Φu,v(2,0),...,Φu,u(m,m)); 2) mw: 1,2,...,m-th powers of magnetic matrix ([Aq1]u,v,[Aq2]u,v,...,[AqQ]u,vm), with qℓ=ℓ2(m+1); 3) rw: undirected walks up to length m(A+A⊤)u,um; 4) rw+: unidirected walks up to length m(Au,um,(A⊤)u,um); 5) degree: node total in-degree + out-degree (du,dv); 6) degree+: node in-degree and out-degree (duin,duout,dvin,dvout); 7) cn: undirected common neighbor: N(u)∩N(v) (N(u) means the in-neighbor and out-neighbor of u); 8) cn+: directed common neighbor: (Nin(u)∩Nin(v),Nout(u)∩Nin(v),Nin(u)∩Nout(v),Nout(u)∩Nout(v)), where Nin(u),Nout(u) stands for the in-neighbor {w:(w,w)∈E} and out-neighbor {w:(u,w)∈E} respectively; 9) ppr: undirected-version Personalized PageRank: [(1−γ)(1−γD−1(A+A⊤))−1]u,v; 10) ppr+: directed-version Personalized PageRank: ([(1−γ)(1−γDout−1A)−1]u,v,[(1−γ)(1−γDin−1A⊤)−1]u,v). We use γ=0.15 for PageRank throughout our experiments; 11) di-sim (49): To adapt it for link prediction, we construct four link features from the inner products (XL)u⊤(XL)v,(XL)u⊤(XR)v,(XR)u⊤(XL)v,(XR)u⊤(XR)v of left and right singular vectors as the link features; and 12) Hermitian (23): We use the inner product (X)u⊤(X)v of eigenvectors as the link feature. For both di-sim and Hermitian, we use the top-K singular/eigenvectors corresponding to the largest singular/eigenvalues, with K∈{8,16,32,64,128} chosen for best performance.

Note that the input features to the classifier are calculated differently for different data splits: 1) During training and validation, the input features to predict the link (u,v) are calculated on the graph induced by Etr\{(u,v)}; and 2) During testing, the input features are instead calculated on the graph induced by Etr∪Eval\{(u,v)}.
