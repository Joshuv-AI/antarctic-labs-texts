Targeting ZMIZ1 induces differentiation in acute myeloid leukemia via chromatin remodeling
Authors: Huimin Li, Yabin Liu, Jiachen Cui, Ziyu Fu, Yiyang Xu, Sizhe Yang, Kai Zhu, Yuhao Xue, Yi Zhang, Yiheng Zhao, Guiqiyang Xiang, Xiaoguang Xu, Fulun Jiao, Shuting Yu, Qiang Wang, Yuting Dai, Qianqian Zhang, Jianfeng Li, Ruihong Zhang, Fangyi Dong, Shanhe Yu, Shengyue Wang, Saijuan Chen, Bo Jiao, Shuo Chen, Yun Tan
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractAcute myeloid leukemia (AML) is an aggressive hematologic malignancy characterized by profound transcriptional dysregulation and impaired hematopoietic differentiation. While differentiation therapy has revolutionized treatment in acute promyelocytic leukemia (APL), analogous strategies in non-APL AML remain elusive. Here, we identify ZMIZ1 (Zinc finger MIZ domain-containing protein 1) as a previously unrecognized transcriptional co-regulator that enforces differentiation blockade in AML from a targeted CRISPR-Cas9 screen. ZMIZ1 expression is elevated in AML patients and correlates with adverse outcomes. Genetic ablation of ZMIZ1 in leukemic cells induces terminal differentiation, reduces leukemia cell stemness, restores anti-leukemic immune responses, and significantly prolongs survival in murine AML models. Mechanistically, ZMIZ1 forms phase-separated nuclear condensates and maintains the super-enhancer architecture of genes critical for hematopoietic identity and immune evasion. Integrated Hi-C and ChIP-seq analyses reveal that ZMIZ1 cooperates with MEF2D (Myocyte-specific enhancer factor 2D) to promote enhancer-promoter looping and transcriptional output. Finally, we report the development of small-compounds potentially targeting ZMIZ1 with high binding affinity, selective on-target activity, and potent in vivo efficacy in both the murine AML model and AML organoids. Collectively, these findings uncover ZMIZ1 as a targetable epigenetic vulnerability in AML, underscoring its potential as a promising therapeutic target for differentiation-based treatment strategies.Subject terms: Haematological cancer, Haematological cancer, Target validation, Cancer genetics

Introduction

Acute myeloid leukemia (AML) is one of the most prevalent and aggressive hematologic malignancies, with approximately over 140,000 new cases diagnosed annually worldwide.1 Despite advances in molecular classification and the introduction of targeted therapies, outcomes remain poor, particularly in older adults, with 5-year survival rates below 10% and limited tolerability to intensive chemotherapy regimens.2 Conventional induction chemotherapy, typically based on cytarabine and anthracycline combinations, has remained largely unchanged for decades and is associated with significant toxicity, high relapse rates, and limited efficacy in achieving durable remission.3 Although inhibitors targeting FLT3 (Fms Related Receptor Tyrosine Kinase 3), IDH1/2 (Isocitrate Dehydrogenase 1/2), BCL2 (BCL2 Apoptosis Regulator), and Menin have shown clinical benefit in selected genetic subtypes,2,4 these responses are often partial, transient, and ineffective in eradicating leukemic stem cells. Leukemic stem cells exhibit intrinsic resistance to many therapeutic agents through mechanisms such as enhanced quiescence, altered metabolic states, and protective interactions with the bone marrow microenvironment, thereby sustaining disease propagation and relapse.5

A defining pathogenic feature of AML is the blockade of myeloid differentiation,6 resulting in the accumulation of immature blasts and impaired hematopoiesis. This differentiation arrest reflects a fundamental disruption of the transcriptional and epigenetic programs that normally govern lineage commitment and maturation within the hematopoietic hierarchy, leading to the persistence of progenitor-like states with enhanced self-renewal capacity.7 Restoring differentiation has the potential not only to reduce leukemic burden but also to reprogram malignant cells into a more benign or terminally differentiated state, thereby circumventing the limitations of cytotoxic therapies that primarily target proliferating cells. The success of differentiation therapy in acute promyelocytic leukemia (APL), through targeted modulation and degradation of PML-RARα using all-trans retinoic acid (ATRA) and arsenic trioxide, has established a powerful proof-of-concept, achieving remission and cure in over 95% patients.8 Targeted disruption of oncogenic transcriptional complexes can effectively release the differentiation block, reactivating endogenous gene expression programs required for myeloid maturation. However, this approach has not translated to non-APL AML, where the molecular underpinnings of differentiation blockade are less well understood, and clinically effective strategies remain lacking. Non-APL AML comprises a highly heterogeneous group of diseases characterized by diverse genetic lesions and convergent yet poorly defined epigenetic dysregulation.9 Emerging evidence suggests that aberrant chromatin organization, dysregulated enhancer landscapes, and altered higher-order genome architecture collectively contribute to the maintenance of the differentiation block.10 Consequently, there is a critical need to identify unifying regulatory principles and actionable vulnerabilities that sustain the undifferentiated state in AML, to develop broadly applicable differentiation-based therapeutic strategies.

Recent efforts have focused on targeting epigenetic regulators to overcome differentiation arrest in AML. Considerable attention has been directed toward chromatin modifiers and transcriptional cofactors as therapeutic entry points to restore normal hematopoietic differentiation.11 Inhibition of the histone demethylase LSD1 (Lysine-specific histone demethylase 1 A), a key modulator of LSC self-renewal,12 has been shown to induce myeloid differentiation in preclinical models.13 Nonetheless, clinical trials of LSD1 inhibitors have yielded limited efficacy,14 hindered by toxicity and incomplete responses, highlighting the challenges of epigenetic modulation in AML therapy. These findings suggest that targeting individual epigenetic enzymes may be insufficient to fully reverse the complex and multilayered regulatory networks sustaining the undifferentiated leukemic state.

Similarly, differentiation-promoting effects have occasionally been observed in patients treated with Menin inhibitor (Revumenib),15,16 IDH1/2 inhibitors (Ivosidenib, Olutasidenib, and Enasidenib)17–19 or FLT3 inhibitors (Gilteritinib, and Quizartinib),20,21 yet these responses remain context-specific and have not been broadly effective across AML subtypes. However, such effects are typically restricted to genetically defined subsets of AML and are often accompanied by transient responses and the development of resistance. Collectively, these findings underscore the complexity of transcriptional and epigenetic regulation in AML and highlight the need for more effective strategies that can target core regulatory mechanisms underlying differentiation blockade across diverse AML contexts. Targeting such integrative regulatory nodes may enable coordinated reactivation of lineage-specific gene expression programs and overcome the limitations of mutation-specific therapies.

Collectively, these limitations highlight an urgent need for identifying novel regulatory targets capable of broadly reactivating differentiation in AML. In this regard, approaches that can simultaneously remodel chromatin accessibility and transcriptional circuitry may offer a more robust and durable strategy to overcome differentiation blockade. In particular, transcriptional co-regulators that modulate chromatin structure and enhancer-driven transcription represent an attractive but largely unexplored category of differentiation targets, representing a distinct class of regulatory vulnerabilities beyond canonical enzymatic targets.

Here, we identify ZMIZ1 (Zinc finger MIZ domain-containing protein 1), a previously uncharacterized transcriptional co-regulator, as a critical epigenetic mediator enforcing differentiation blockade in AML, thus providing a new mechanistic and therapeutic avenue for differentiation-based therapies in non-APL AML.

Results

ZMIZ1 is a critical regulator of differentiation blockade in AML

To systematically identify regulators that participate in the differentiation block in AML, we performed a focused CRISPR-Cas9 loss-of-function screening targeting 512 genes (covering key hematopoietic transcription factors and epigenetic regulators) in AML cell lines (MOLM13 and OCI-AML3). Following sgRNA library transduction, we isolated cell populations with high expression of the myeloid differentiation markers CD11b or CD14 (Fig. 1a). As expected, sgRNAs targeting known regulators such as KMT2A and CEBPA were enriched (Fig. 1b and Supplementary Table 1). Notably, sgRNAs against ZMIZ1 (Zinc finger MIZ-type containing protein 1), a previously uncharacterized transcriptional co-activator in AML, were consistently enriched in both CD11b⁺ and CD14⁺ populations (Fig. 1b, c and Supplementary Tables 1–2).

To validate these findings, we silenced ZMIZ1 using shRNA in MOLM13 and OCI-AML3 cells (Supplementary Fig. 1a). ZMIZ1 knockdown led to a marked upregulation of CD11b and CD14 expression (Fig. 1d, e), and Wright-Giemsa staining revealed morphological changes consistent with terminal myeloid differentiation, including the emergence of segmented nuclei (Fig. 1f). Additionally, ZMIZ1 suppression impaired AML cell growth and increased apoptosis (Fig. 1g, h), phenotypes commonly associated with enforced differentiation in vitro.

To assess the role of Zmiz1 in leukemic self-renewal, we generated conditional knockout murine bone marrow cells (Mx1-Cre⁺ Zmiz1Δ/Δ) and transformed them with MLL-AF9. Zmiz1 deletion significantly reduced colony-forming capacity in methylcellulose assays (Fig. 1i), and serial replating revealed a marked reduction in colony numbers across passages compared to Zmiz1fl/fl controls (Fig. 1j), indicating impaired self-renewal and loss of leukemic clonogenicity.

To assess the clinical relevance of ZMIZ1 in AML, we analyzed the expression of ZMIZ1 in our clinical proteomics cohort (374 AML blasts and 6 CD34+ cells isolated from the bone marrow of healthy donors). We found that, compared to the control CD34+ cells, ZMIZ1 expression was significantly upregulated (Supplementary Fig. 1b–d). In addition, patients with high ZMIZ1 expression exhibited significantly shorter overall survival compared to those with low expression in our AML transcriptomics cohort (997 AML cases) and the TCGA cohort (142 AML cases) (Fig. 1k).

Targeting ZMIZ1 delays disease onset and enhances the immune response in murine AML model

Next, we evaluated the effect of Zmiz1 downregulation in a murine AML model (Fig. 2a). We found Zmiz1-deficient mice (Zmiz1Δ/Δ) exhibited significantly lower peripheral white blood cell (WBC) counts compared to controls (MLL-AF9 Zmiz1fl/fl) following MLL-AF9 transduction, with differences reaching statistical significance on days 42, 56, and 70 post-transplantation (Fig. 2b). Consistently, Zmiz1 deletion significantly extended overall survival in primary transplant recipients (Fig. 2c) and markedly reduced leukemia burden, as indicated by decreased spleen weight and attenuated leukemic infiltration in both the liver and spleen (Fig. 2d, e). Notably, residual leukemic infiltration remained detectable at experimental endpoints, indicating incomplete disease eradication despite overall disease attenuation.

Flow cytometric analysis further revealed a substantial decrease in c-Kit+ leukemic stem/progenitor populations in the bone marrow and spleen of Zmiz1Δ/Δ mice (Fig. 2f), supporting a functional requirement for Zmiz1 in maintaining leukemia-initiating cell compartments. Moreover, in secondary transplantation assays, Zmiz1Δ/Δ leukemic cells failed to efficiently engraft and propagate disease in naïve recipients (Fig. 2g), further demonstrating the essential role of Zmiz1 in sustaining leukemia cell stemness and disease propagation.

To define how Zmiz1 constrains leukemic cell fate in vivo, we performed single-cell RNA-seq on bone-marrow cells from MLL-AF9 AML mice carrying Zmiz1fl/fl or Zmiz1Δ/Δ alleles (Fig. 2h, i, Supplementary Figs. 2 and 3a–d and Supplementary Table 3–5). Unsupervised embedding resolved major compartments, including leukemic stem-like blasts and lineage-biased blast states (granulocytic and monocytic), as well as T/NK and B-cell populations (Fig. 2h). Zmiz1 deletion markedly shifted population structure: stem-like blasts contracted, while compartments of granulocytic and monocytic precursors expanded, indicating a global move toward differentiation (Fig. 2h, right panel). Computational inference of ligand-receptor signaling implied a potential remodeling of the immune microenvironment (Fig. 2i). In T/NK and B-cell compartments, we observed increased predicted activity of antigen-presentation and adhesion-related signaling programs (for example, MHC-I and PECAM1 modules, selectin pathways).

We further sorted the MLL-AF9 expressing cells by flow cytometry and analyzed these cells by scRNA-seq (Fig. 2j–m and Supplementary Figs. 3e–h). RNA velocity showed directional flow from the stem-like region toward both granulocytic and monocytic termini after Zmiz1 loss (Fig. 2j). Stratification of blast subclusters (C0-C6) by marker genes demonstrated a redistribution away from proliferative/stem-like programs toward inflammatory/monocytic modules in Zmiz1Δ/Δ marrow (Fig. 2k). Consistently, pseudotime mapping indicated progression from stem-like to lineage-biased endpoints, with arrows tracing bifurcating routes toward granulocytic and monocytic fates (Fig. 2l, left panel). Quantitatively, pie charts summarizing blast states recapitulated the depletion of stem-like blasts and reciprocal accumulation of lineage-biased populations in Zmiz1Δ/Δ mice (Fig. 2l, right panel). Finally, differentiation potency scoring revealed a genotype-wide reduction in developmental potential following Zmiz1 loss: UMAP coloring showed lower potency across blasts, and bar plots documented an increase in differentiated/unipotent states with a corresponding decrease in oligopotent cells (Fig. 2m). Together, these data indicate that Zmiz1 ablation drives leukemic blasts out of a self-renewing state, promotes bifurcating myeloid maturation trajectories, and reconfigures immune signaling within the bone-marrow niche.

ZMIZ1 forms nuclear condensates and regulates AML-essential genes

Next, we investigated the mechanism underlying ZMIZ1-induced AML differentiation. We first examined its nuclear distribution in AML cell lines and primary AML cells using immunofluorescence staining. Notably, we found that ZMIZ1 predominantly localizes to the nucleus and exhibits a punctate pattern in both AML cell lines and primary AML cells (Fig. 3a).

To further investigate this punctate distribution, we analyzed the intrinsic disorder region (IDR) characteristics of ZMIZ1 using three models (PONDR, IUPRED, and ANCHOR). Two regions, 102-567 (IDR1) and 829-1067 (IDR2), were found to significantly exceed the threshold (Fig. 3b). Across PIAS family members and other SUMO E3 ligases, IDRs were likewise detected; however, ZMIZ1 harbors relatively larger IDRs with strong concordance across predictors (Supplementary Fig. 4a). Subsequently, we constructed ZMIZ1-GFP fusion vectors, along with ZMIZ1-IDR and IDR-deleted variants and examined the location of these constructs. Remarkably, we found that ZMIZ1 protein formed punctate structures within the cells, and in some cells, they even formed large punctate droplets (Fig. 3c and Supplementary Figs. 4b, c). Deletion or isolation analyses revealed a functional separation between these properties: IDR2 was required for nuclear localization but did not support puncta formation, whereas IDR1 was sufficient to drive puncta formation but localized predominantly in the cytoplasm (Fig. 3c). We next performed fluorescence recovery after photobleaching (FRAP) assays using purified ZMIZ1-GFP fusion protein and observed recovery of fluorescence within punctate structures (Supplementary Fig. 4d), indicating that ZMIZ1 has the capacity for dynamic phase separation. Time-lapse live imaging further revealed dynamic behavior of ZMIZ1 condensates, with individual puncta undergoing fusion/coalescence to form larger assemblies (Fig. 3d).

We then purified ZMIZ1-GFP proteins and analyzed their ability in forming condensates. We observed that ZMIZ1-GFP formed highly defined droplets, which were disrupted upon treatment with high salt or 1,6-hexanediol, a well-known disruptor of phase separation (Fig. 3e). These findings indicate that ZMIZ1 exhibits phase-separation properties. Consistently, depletion of ZMIZ1 by independent shRNAs markedly reduced discrete nuclear ZMIZ1 puncta in OCI-AML3 cells (Supplementary Fig. 4e).

To further investigate the potential functions of ZMIZ1 in regulating transcription, we examined the genome-wide chromatin occupancy of ZMIZ1 in AML cells using ChIP-seq analysis across five AML cell lines (HL-60, MOLM13, MV4-11, NB4, OCI-AML3) and two primary AML patient samples (P03, P38) (Supplementary Table 6–7). UpSet plot analysis revealed extensive conservation of ZMIZ1 binding sites among these samples, with a substantial number of peaks consistently identified across all five cell lines, accompanied by additional peaks specific to subsets of lines (Fig. 3f and Supplementary Table 7). The majority of ZMIZ1 peaks localized near gene promoters (±3 kb from transcription start sites) and to intronic regions (Fig. 3g). Heatmap visualization of ChIP-seq signal intensity within ±3 kb of the 3,567 conserved peak summits (defined as peaks present in at least three cell lines) demonstrated robust and highly consistent ZMIZ1 binding enrichment across all AML cell lines and primary AML blasts (Fig. 3h). A representative example of this conserved ZMIZ1 targeting is illustrated at the RUNX1 locus (Fig. 3i and Supplementary Figs. 5a, b). We then performed Gene Ontology (GO) enrichment analysis and found that ZMIZ1 bound to the regulatory regions of genes related to hematopoietic differentiation (e.g., “hematopoietic cell lineage” and “myeloid leukocyte differentiation”) (Fig. 3j).

ZMIZ1 is involved in the regulation of super-enhancers in AML

To investigate the proteins associated with ZMIZ1 condensates, we performed TurboID-based proximity labeling assays in two AML cell lines (MOLM13 and OCI-AML3) to identify proteins interacting with ZMIZ1. We found 185 high-confidence ZMIZ1 interactors, 94 of which were common to both cell lines. Functional annotation of these interacting proteins highlighted a pronounced enrichment in transcription-related activities, including nucleic acid binding, chromatin interactions, and transcriptional co-regulation (Fig. 4a and Supplementary Fig. 6a). Consistent with its role as a transcriptional co-activator, the ZMIZ1 interactome included multiple core components and regulators of super-enhancers (Fig. 4b). Specifically, we identified robust enrichment for histone acetyltransferases (p300/CBP), several Mediator complex subunits (notably MED1), BRD4, components of the SWI/SNF chromatin remodeling complex, and nuclear receptor co-repressor NCOR2/SMRT, all of which are central to enhancer-mediated transcriptional regulation.

We further analyzed the relationship between ZMIZ1 condensates and the super-enhancer key regulator BRD4 condensates. We found that a significant portion of ZMIZ1 condensates overlapped with BRD4 condensates in both AML cell lines and primary AML cells (Fig. 4c and Supplementary 6b). To directly examine whether ZMIZ1 associates with super-enhancer regions in AML, we performed ChIP-seq for the active enhancer mark H3K27ac in these cell lines and analyzed their super-enhancer landscapes (Supplementary Table 6). Genome-wide binding analysis across five AML cell lines (HL-60, MOLM13, MV4-11, NB4, OCI-AML3) revealed a striking preference for ZMIZ1 binding at super-enhancers (SEs) compared to typical enhancers (TEs) (Fig. 4d). Visualization of representative genomic loci confirmed co-localization of ZMIZ1 peaks with broad H3K27ac domains characteristic of active super-enhancers, such as those near the RUNX1 locus and the ZMIZ1 locus itself (Fig. 4e). Notably, ZMIZ1 itself was also regulated by super-enhancers in AML cells (Supplementary Figs. 6c–e).

Notably, ZMIZ1 depletion selectively reduced histone acetylation at super-enhancer sites, whereas TEs were largely unaffected. Quantitatively, mean H3K27ac signals at SEs decreased by approximately 40-45 reads per million (RPM) in MOLM13 cells and 20-35 RPM in OCI-AML3 cells upon ZMIZ1 knockdown, compared with minimal changes at TEs (Fig. 4f). Genome browser visualization of representative loci such as the MEF2D super-enhancer further illustrated marked loss of H3K27ac enrichment following ZMIZ1 depletion, underscoring the specificity of ZMIZ1 function for SE regulation (Fig. 4g and Supplementary Figs. 7a, b).

RNA-seq analysis in MOLM13 cells following ZMIZ1 knockdown revealed a broad downregulation of genes driven by super-enhancers (Supplementary Tables 8–10). Among the most significantly downregulated transcripts were known super-enhancer target genes critical to AML biology, including MEF2D, TWIST1, FLI1, and BCL2 (Fig. 4h and Supplementary Figs. 7c, d). Notably, MEF2D expression markedly decreased upon ZMIZ1 depletion, in line with the loss of its upstream super-enhancer and prior reports establishing MEF2D as an SE-driven oncogenic factor in AML.22 To quantitatively confirm the selective repression of SE-associated genes, we performed gene set enrichment analysis (GSEA). This analysis demonstrated a highly significant negative enrichment of SE-associated gene signatures in ZMIZ1-depleted cells (Fig. 4i).

ZMIZ1 remodels chromatin structure via MEF2D

In addition to super-enhancer related transcription co-regulators, several key transcription factors were also selectively enriched within the ZMIZ1 proximity proteome, most notably MEF2D (Fig. 4b). MEF2D is of particular interest, as core regulatory circuitry (CRC) analysis predicted MEF2D to be an active transcriptional regulator in approximately 67% of AML samples in this cohort, and prior studies have demonstrated an essential role for MEF2D-driven transcriptional programs in hematologic malignancies.22 (Fig. 5a and Supplementary Table 11). Such CRC-associated transcription factors typically occupy SEs and sustain oncogenic gene expression programs in leukemia. Motif enrichment analysis of ZMIZ1-bound chromatin regions revealed strong enrichment for DNA sequence motifs recognized by the MEF2 transcription factor family, further implicating MEF2D as a direct ZMIZ1 chromatin partner (Fig. 5b).

The interaction between ZMIZ1 and MEF2D was detected by co-immunoprecipitation (Supplementary Fig. 8a). To validate this relationship at the genomic level, we performed ChIP-seq for MEF2D in both MOLM13 and OCI-AML3 cell lines, as well as in primary AML patient blasts (AML #P03 and AML #P38). Notably, the genome-wide binding profiles of MEF2D and ZMIZ1 exhibited remarkable overlap, with many peaks of co-occupancy by both ZMIZ1 and MEF2D (Fig. 5c and Supplementary Fig. 8b). Importantly, these shared genomic binding regions encompassed critical regulatory elements associated with AML cell identity, including the prominent RUNX1 SE region, which displayed robust co-occupying patterns by ZMIZ1 and MEF2D (Fig. 5d and Supplementary Table 12).

To directly establish that ZMIZ1 and MEF2D concurrently bind to the same genomic regions, we conducted sequential ChIP (Re-ChIP-seq) experiments. Chromatin immunoprecipitated first with anti-ZMIZ1 antibodies from MOLM13 cells was then subjected to a second round of immunoprecipitation with anti-MEF2D antibodies. The resulting Re-ChIP-seq analysis demonstrated extensive and reproducible overlap between ZMIZ1 and MEF2D chromatin occupancy (Fig. 5e and Supplementary Fig. 8c), confirming simultaneous and cooperative binding of these factors rather than coincidental co-localization within heterogeneous cell populations.

We next assessed the functional dependency of MEF2D chromatin binding on ZMIZ1 expression. Upon shRNA-mediated knockdown of ZMIZ1, MEF2D occupancy on chromatin markedly decreased across shared genome-wide target sites in AML cells (Fig. 5f, g). Consistent with the loss of chromatin binding, MEF2D protein levels were also significantly reduced upon ZMIZ1 depletion (Fig. 5h). Collectively, these findings suggest a mutual regulatory relationship wherein ZMIZ1 stabilizes MEF2D on chromatin and sustains its protein expression, while MEF2D in turn may facilitate ZMIZ1 recruitment or retention at critical enhancer regions. Disruption of ZMIZ1 thus dismantles this regulatory circuit, compromising MEF2D stability and chromatin occupancy, and subsequently reducing the expression of MEF2D-driven genes.

Finally, we explored the impact of ZMIZ1 on higher-order chromatin architecture using Hi-C analysis. ZMIZ1 depletion led to a pronounced reduction in enhancer-promoter interactions (Supplementary Figs. 9a, b and Supplementary Tables 13–14), especially at genomic loci co-bound by ZMIZ1 and MEF2D (Fig. 5i). For example, in control AML cells, the MEF2D super-enhancer locus exhibited robust long-range chromatin loops with its promoter, indicative of active transcriptional engagement. In ZMIZ1-depleted cells, however, these enhancer-promoter contacts were substantially weakened or abolished, demonstrating a loss of critical chromatin interactions (Fig. 5i). Notably, this phenomenon was observed at multiple ZMIZ1-MEF2D co-bound SEs including RUNX1, BCL2, and HOXA9 (Supplementary Fig. 9c), highlighting a generalized role for ZMIZ1 in maintaining chromatin topology conducive to transcriptional activation. Thus, our results illustrate that ZMIZ1 is essential not only for biochemical enhancer function, but also for sustaining the three-dimensional chromatin structure necessary for efficient enhancer-promoter communication and oncogenic transcriptional activity in AML.

SIH-001 and SIH-002 induce differentiation in AML cells and delay AML onset

Next, we explored small-molecule inhibitors of ZMIZ1. Using the crystal structure of an N-terminal ZMIZ1 fragment23 together with an AlphaFold model, we conducted a structure-guided screening of a diverse compound library. From ~50,000 candidates (Schrödinger Glide XP docking), we prioritized 40 compounds for biophysical testing (Fig. 6a). Sixteen compounds displayed measurable interaction with ZMIZ1 (Supplementary Fig. 10). Single-cycle kinetic binding analyses then identified two leads with high affinity, Compound 21 (hereafter SIH-001) and Compound 24 (SIH-002), which bound purified ZMIZ1 with sub-micromolar K_D values (Fig. 6b and Supplementary Figs. 11a, b).

We employed cellular thermal shift assays (CETSA) to evaluate the effects of SIH-001 on ZMIZ1 and other members of the ZMIZ family. SIH-001 selectively reduced the intracellular stability of ZMIZ1, while exerting no detectable effects on other ZMIZ family proteins (Fig. 6c). We next performed molecular modeling to analyze the interaction between SIH-001 and ZMIZ1, which revealed that SIH-001 is positioned near residues Y75, R105, and V288 (Fig. 6d). To further validate this interaction, we individually mutated Y75, R105, and V288 to alanine. Notably, mutation of these residues markedly attenuated SIH-001-mediated ZMIZ1 degradation. Consistently, CETSA performed in cells expressing wild-type or point-mutant ZMIZ1 showed that the Y75A and V288A mutations significantly reduced binding to SIH-001, further supporting that SIH-001 engages ZMIZ1 through this region (Fig. 6e, f).

To directly assess the impact of SIH-001 on ZMIZ1 chromatin occupancy, OCI-AML3 and MOLM-13 cells were treated with SIH-001 and subjected to ZMIZ1 ChIP-seq analysis. We observed a pronounced genome-wide reduction in ZMIZ1 binding upon SIH-001 treatment (Fig. 6g), implying effective suppression of ZMIZ1 chromatin association.

We next treated MOLM13 and OCI-AML3 cells, as well as primary AML blasts, with SIH-001 and assessed ZMIZ1 abundance and differentiation status. Both compounds induced myeloid differentiation and/or apoptosis, as evidenced by cytomorphology and canonical markers (Fig. 6h–j). Concurrently, we observed that SIH-001 markedly potentiated AML cell differentiation and/or apoptosis induced by the menin inhibitor ziftomenib and the BCL2 inhibitor venetoclax, respectively (Fig. 6i, j). RNA-seq (Supplementary Table 8) showed concordant transcriptional reprogramming: SIH-001 and SIH-002 downregulated DNA-replication and stemness-related programs and upregulated hematopoietic and antigen-presentation/surface-receptor signaling pathways (Supplementary Fig. 11c–e and Supplementary Tables 15–18). Moreover, these transcriptional effects showed strong concordance with ZMIZ1 knockdown, demonstrating that SIH-001 and SIH-002 phenocopy key features of ZMIZ1 loss (Supplementary Fig. 11c–e).

We further evaluated the effects of SIH-001, Ziftomenib, Venetoclax, and their combinations using a human iPSC-derived bone marrow organoid model with AML engraftment. Flow cytometric analysis revealed that SIH-001 in combination with Venetoclax selectively suppressed AML blast proliferation within the AML organoids, and that SIH-001 exhibited synergistic effects with both Venetoclax and Ziftomenib (Supplementary Fig. 12a). We subsequently performed single-cell RNA sequencing of these samples. Consistent with the flow cytometry results, SIH-001 together with venetoclax reduced the abundance of AML blasts, with clear evidence of synergistic activity between the two agents (Supplementary Fig. 12b–e). Furthermore, analysis of cellular stemness demonstrated that SIH-001 significantly diminished the stem-like properties of AML blasts (Fig. 6k and Supplementary Fig. 12f, g).

To benchmark pharmacologic ZMIZ1 targeting in vivo, mice bearing MLL-AF9 AML blasts were treated with SIH-001, Venetoclax, or vehicle and then subjected to single-cell RNA-seq (scRNA-seq) (Fig. 6k and Supplementary Table 3). SIH-001 significantly prolonged the survival of AML-bearing mice, although the effect was moderate (Fig. 6l). Notably, combination treatment with venetoclax further extended overall survival. Unsupervised analysis resolved C0-C7 clusters spanning immature/proliferative blasts, lineage-biased blast states, and immune/stromal compartments (Fig. 6m and Supplementary Fig. 13a–c and Supplementary Table 19). Mapping treatment conditions onto a shared embedding revealed that SIH-001 redistributed cells away from immature territories and toward lineage-biased/differentiated regions, whereas Venetoclax produced more modest shifts (Fig. 6n and Supplementary Fig. 13b). Cluster composition likewise favored differentiated/lineage-biased states under SIH-001 (Supplementary Fig. 13d). RNA-velocity vectors flowed from high-potency regions toward differentiated endpoints, most prominently with SIH-001 (Supplementary Fig. 13d). CytoTRACE2 potency scores were reduced by SIH-001 relative to DMSO and Venetoclax, indicating enforced maturation (Fig. 6o). In parallel, SIH-001 increased expression of MHC-I pathway genes (e.g., H2-K1, Tap1; Fig. 6o and Supplementary Fig. 13e) and elevated an MHC-I module score at single-cell resolution (Fig. 6m), consistent with restoration of antigen-presentation machinery in formerly undifferentiated blasts.

Collectively, these data show that structure-guided ZMIZ1 inhibitors directly bind ZMIZ1, lower ZMIZ1 protein abundance, and reprogram leukemic transcription toward myeloid differentiation and immune activation in vitro, while driving state transitions in vivo that reduce developmental potency and restore MHC-I antigen presentation within the leukemic marrow.

Discussion

Acute myeloid leukemia (AML) is a hematological malignancy characterized by a pathological block in myeloid differentiation. The curative success of all‑trans retinoic acid plus arsenic trioxide in acute promyelocytic leukemia (APL) established the modern paradigm of differentiation therapy, yet the approach has not generalized to non‑APL AML.24 Here we identify the transcriptional co‑regulator ZMIZ1 as a central enforcer of differentiation arrest, and we connect its condensate‑forming capacity to SE maintenance, enhancer-promoter communication, and self‑renewal of leukemic blasts. We show that genetic ablation or small‑molecule inhibition of ZMIZ1 dissolves disease‑critical nuclear hubs, collapses SE‑driven transcription, restores myeloid maturation, and re‑engages antigen‑presentation pathways.

ZMIZ1 is a PIAS-like SUMO E3 that also functions as a transcriptional cofactor.23,25 Previous work has shown that ZMIZ1 binds and co-activates multiple transcription factors, including the androgen receptor (AR), p53, SMAD3/4, and NOTCH1 in a SUMO-dependent manner.23,25–27 Given these roles, ZMIZ1 stands out as a modulator of transcription factor networks through SUMOylation. Consistent with its role in transcriptional control, ZMIZ1 has emerged as a player in T-lymphocytic leukemia and development. It is frequently overexpressed and genetically altered in T-cell leukemias.23,28 ZMIZ1 also cooperates with NOTCH1 in T-ALL, converging on targets like c-MYC.25,26 Here, we identified a new function of ZMIZ1 in super-enhancer regulation, and particularly its role in condensate formation. SEs are dense regulatory hubs that sustain lineage‑defining and disease programs by concentrating master transcription factors and co‑activators.29,30 Their preferential vulnerability can be exploited pharmacologically, as shown by selective suppression of SE‑driven oncogenes upon BET perturbation.31 Biophysical work further demonstrated that co‑activators and transcription factors form liquid‑like condensates at SEs with hallmark behaviors linking phase separation to transcriptional control.32–34 ZMIZ1 harbors extensive IDRs, assembles into nuclear puncta that co‑localize with BRD4 and broad H3K27ac, and is required to maintain SE acetylation and enhancer-promoter looping at AML‑essential loci.

Moreover, we found ZMIZ1 physically associates with MEF2D, co‑occupies MEF2D motifs genome‑wide, and is required to stabilize MEF2D protein and chromatin binding. Although MEF2D has primarily been implicated in lymphoid leukemogenesis, emerging evidence demonstrates that it also plays a pivotal role in acute myeloid leukemia.22,35–37 Specifically, MEF2D is essential for the maintenance of MLL-rearranged AML, where it enforces differentiation blockade and preserves leukemic self-renewal by repressing the lineage determinant CEBPE, thereby establishing a pathogenic MEF2D-CEBPE axis. In line with our core regulatory circuitry analysis, recent studies have further identified MEF2D, together with IRF1, ELF1, and ETV6, as cooperative regulators sustaining oncogenic transcriptional programs in AML. Additionally, Hi‑C demonstrates that ZMIZ1 depletion diminishes long‑range contacts at shared targets (for example, the RUNX1, BCL2, MEF2D, and HOXA9), consistent with a model in which ZMIZ1 forms nuclear condensates that scaffold enhancer-promoter communication while MEF2D confers locus specificity. This cooperative mechanism explains how ZMIZ1 simultaneously sustains stemness‑linked programs and suppresses terminal myeloid differentiation.

ZMIZ1 knockdown or inhibition induces canonical differentiation markers and morphology, depletes clonogenic growth, and shifts transcription toward hematopoiesis and antigen‑presentation/surface‑receptor signaling. Notably, ZMIZ1 is dispensable for steady-state adult hematopoiesis. Using the same inducible Mx1-Cre-mediated deletion model employed in this study, Pinnell et al. reported preserved hematopoietic stem and progenitor cell numbers following Zmiz1 ablation, with no evidence of sustained myeloproliferation or bone marrow failure during long-term follow-up.25 ZMIZ1 exerts stage- and lineage-restricted functions, particularly in lymphoid development, rather than serving as a global regulator of adult hematopoiesis.23 Thus, there is a potential therapeutic window in which ZMIZ1 inhibition may be tolerated without causing overt disruption of normal hematopoietic homeostasis. Although survival extension upon Zmiz1 deletion was moderate under the fixed transplantation conditions used, Zmiz1 loss consistently reduced leukemia burden and impaired leukaemia-propagating capacity. Moreover, in vivo, scRNA‑seq reveals redistribution of blasts away from immature territories toward granulocytic‑ and monocytic‑oriented states, with RNA‑velocity and CytoTRACE2 indicating directional maturation and reduced developmental potency. Notably, ZMIZ1 inhibition restores MHC‑I machinery (for example, H2‑K1 and Tap1) and elevates an MHC‑I module score in formerly undifferentiated blasts, suggesting enhanced immune visibility of leukemia cells after differentiation. These results extend and complement clinical efforts to harness differentiation in genetically defined AML subsets.38 IDH1/2 inhibition induces maturation and produces durable remissions in subsets of patients. 39,40 Menin inhibition down‑shifts HOX/MEIS programs and elicits differentiation phenotypes in KMT2A‑rearranged or NPM1‑mutant leukemia.16 Targeting FLT3 can also yield differentiation‑like changes in selected contexts.41 Parallel epigenetic strategies established that inhibiting LSD1 reactivates differentiation pathways in AML, via demethylase‑dependent and scaffolding mechanisms involving the GFI1 complex, and can be potentiated by WNT pathway modulation.42–45 Within this broader landscape, ZMIZ1 represents a distinct class of target, an SE‑associated condensate organizer, whose inhibition collapses enhancer topology upstream of multiple oncogenic circuits and thereby offers a mechanistically orthogonal route to differentiation.

From a translational standpoint, potential small‑molecule inhibitors (SIH‑001/002) provide chemical proof‑of‑concept. These compounds bind ZMIZ1 directly, lower ZMIZ1 protein levels, phenocopy genetic loss by inducing differentiation in AML lines and primary blasts, reduce leukemic burden in vivo, and restore antigen‑presentation programs. The condensate‑centric nature of the target suggests a therapeutic index, that cancer cells are disproportionately dependent on SE‑associated condensates, whereas normal progenitors operate closer to the threshold of such phase‑separated assemblies.29,31

Nevertheless, this study has several limitations. SIH-001 represent first-generation tool compounds that have not yet undergone comprehensive medicinal chemistry optimization, extensive selectivity profiling, or full pharmacokinetic and pharmacodynamic characterization. Moreover, their in vivo efficacy was primarily evaluated in an aggressive MLL-AF9 leukemia model, in which overall survival extension remained modest despite clear evidence of cytoreduction and induction of myeloid differentiation. While our data demonstrate that SIH-001 directly engages recombinant ZMIZ1 in vitro and promotes differentiation of AML cells, the precise causal relationship between ZMIZ1 engagement and the downstream differentiation phenotype has not yet been fully delineated and will require further mechanistic investigation. While multi-omic data support a condensate-centric mechanism, direct, dynamic evidence of condensate dissolution and enhancer-promoter loop attenuation in vivo remains to be expanded. Single-cell analyses were performed in murine marrow; broader genetic contexts and microenvironmental diversity were not exhaustively surveyed. These caveats motivate further chemistry, target-engagement biomarkers, and combination studies across additional AML genotypes.

In summary, our study identifies ZMIZ1 as a key mechanistic driver of the AML differentiation blockade and as a promising therapeutic target in non-APL AML. By potentially converting an acute leukemia into a chronic, differentiated condition or even eliminating it entirely through maturation and senescence, ZMIZ1-targeted therapy could complement existing treatments and improve outcomes. We envision that integrating differentiation-based approaches, targeting ZMIZ1 or similar regulatory nodes, with current cytotoxic, epigenetic, or immunotherapeutic regimens may usher in a new era of AML treatment, one in which leukemic cell self-renewal is disarmed and the patient’s normal hematopoietic developmental program is harnessed to combat the disease.

Methods

Ethics approval

All studies involving human samples were reviewed and approved by the Ethics Committee of Ruijin Hospital (approval IDs: 2025-717 and 2026-199), Shanghai Jiao Tong University School of Medicine. Informed consent was obtained from all participants prior to sample collection. Animal experiments were performed in accordance with guidelines approved by the Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee (IACUC) of Ruijin Hospital (approval ID: RJ2023044), Shanghai Jiao Tong University School of Medicine.

Cell lines

MOLM13, OCI-AML3, Kasumi-1, HL-60, MV4-11, NB4, and HEK-293T cells were authenticated. All AML cell lines were cultured in RPMI-1640 (L200KJ, BasalMedia Technologies) supplemented with 10% of FBS (F102-01 Vazyme Biotech). HEK-293T cells were cultured in DMEM (L110KJ, BasalMedia Technologies) containing 10% FBS. All cell lines were incubated at 37 °C in a humidified atmosphere with 5% CO2. Cell lines were authenticated by RNA-seq. Mycoplasma contamination was routinely examined with the MycoBlue Mycoplasma Detector Kit (D101-01, Vazyme Biotech).

Antibodies

The anti-ZMIZ1 (89500S, Cell Signaling Technology), anti-MEF2D (14353-1-AP, Proteintech), anti-GAPDH (60004-1-Ig, Proteintech), anti-Histone H3K27ac (39085, Active Motif), anti-ZMIZ2 (A17301, ABclonal), anti-PIAS1 (23395, Proteintech), anti-PIAS2 (16074, Proteintech), anti PIAS3 (13486, Proteintech), anti PIAS4 (14242, Proteintech) antibodies were used in Western blot assays and/or ChIP assays. The anti-CD11b-APC (S0065, Vdobiotech), anti-CD11b-PE (301306, Biolegend), anti-CD14-APC (S0079, Vdobiotech), anti-CD14-APC (301808, Biolegend), anti-Annexin V-APC (640930, Biolegend) were used in flow cytometry experiments.

Knockdown experiments

shRNA oligos targeting ZMIZ1 (NM_020338.4) were designed with flanking restriction sites, annealed (95 °C to 25 °C gradient cooling), and ligated into the linearized PLVX-shRNA2 vector. Lentiviral particles were generated by co-transfecting HEK-293T cells with 10 μg pLVX-shRNA2 recombinant plasmid, 3.75 μg psPAX2 packaging plasmid and 1.25 μg pMD2.G envelope plasmid using 40 µg 40-kDa linear polyethylenimine (PEI, 919012, Sigma) in a 10-cm culture dish. Viral supernatants were harvested 48 h post-transfection, filtered through 0.45-μm PVDF (IPVH00010, Merck) membranes, and concentrated via ultracentrifugation. Cells were infected using the lentivirus with 8 μg/ml Polybrene (S2267, Sigma) by centrifuging directly at 1200 × g for 90 min at 32 °C. Primer sequences for shRNA plasmid were listed in Supplementary Table 20.

RNA extraction, reverse transcription and RT-qPCR

Total RNA was extracted using the RNA Isolator Total RNA Extraction Reagent (R401-01, Vazyme Biotech Co., Ltd) according to the manufacturer’s instructions. Then 1 µg of total RNA was aliquoted and reverse-transcribed into cDNA using HiScript II Q RT SuperMix for qPCR (+gDNA wiper) (R223-01, Vazyme Biotech). Using the ChamQ Universal SYBR qPCR Master Mix kit (Q711-02, Vazyme Biotech) for Real-Time qPCR detection. The relative expression level of each gene was calculated as 2−△△Ct, and the experiment was independently repeated three times.

Colony formation assay

The MLL-AF9 fusion gene was transfected into bone marrow cells from Polyinosinic-polycytidylic acid (Poly (I:C), T23171, TargetMol) treated Zmiz1flox/flox; Mx1-Cre, or Zmiz1+/+; Mx1-Cre C57BL/6 mice. Cells were seeded in 48-well plates at a density of 2000 cells/well using MethoCult™ semi-solid medium (M3434; STEMCELL Technologies) following the manufacturer’s protocol. The cells were cultured under standard conditions for 7 days, after which colony formation was assessed and the number of colonies was counted.

Co-immunoprecipitation (Co-IP)

About 1 × 107 MOLM13 and OCI-AML3 cells were washed with PBS, lysed in lysis buffer (RIPA with 100 μM PMSF (A100754, Sangon Biotech) and 1 × protease inhibitor cocktail (C0001, TargetMol)) and clarified by centrifugation. Incubate the supernatant overnight at 4 °C with indicated antibodies (5 µg), followed by 2–4 h of capture with DynabeadsTM Protein G (1004D, Invitrogen). After incubation, Protein G beads were washed thoroughly with lysis buffer. Then elute bound proteins by boiling 10 min in 1 × SDS loading buffer (LT103, Epizyme Biotech). Immunoprecipitates and interacting partners were detected by Western blot experiments. Include species-matched IgG as a negative control. Maintain samples at 4°C throughout non-denaturing steps to preserve protein interactions.

Chromatin immunoprecipitation (ChIP) and Re-ChIP assays

Chromatin immunoprecipitation (ChIP) assays were performed as previously reported46 with modification. In brief, MOLM13 and OCI-AML3 cells were cross-linked with 1% formaldehyde (F8775, Sigma) in RPMI-1640 for 10 min at room temperature. Reactions were quenched with glycine (0.125 M final concentration), followed by sequential washes in PBS and ice-cold lysis buffer (10 mM Tris-HCl, pH 7.5, 10 mM NaCl, 3 mM MgCl₂, 0.5% NP-40, 1 mM PMSF, and 1 × cocktail). Cell pellets were resuspended in ChIP buffer (20 mM Tris-HCl, pH 8.0, 150 mM NaCl, 2 mM EDTA, 1% TritonX-100, 0.15% SDS, 1 mM PMSF, and 1 × cocktail). Chromatin fragmentation was performed using a Bioruptor Pico ultrasonicator (B01080010, Diagenode) with 30 cycles of 30 s ON/OFF pulses (high power setting). Sheared chromatin was immunoprecipitated overnight at 4 °C with target-specific antibodies. Immune complexes were captured by incubation with DynabeadsTM Protein G (1004D, Invitrogen) for 4 h at 4 °C, followed by four washes with 900 μL Wash Buffer (20 mM Tris-HCl, pH 8.0, 150 mM NaCl, 2 mM EDTA, 1% TritonX-100). Elution was performed using 50 μL Elution Buffer (25 mM Tris-HCl pH 7.5, 10 mM EDTA, 0.5% SDS) for 15 min at 65 °C. Crosslink reversal was achieved by adding Proteinase K (20 mg/mL, DC102, Vazyme Biotech) and incubating at 65 °C overnight.

Input and ChIP DNA were purified using VAHTS DNA Clean Beads (N411, Vazyme Biotech). ChIP-seq libraries were prepared with VAHTS Universal Pro DNA Library Prep Kit (NDM608, Vazyme). Library quality was assessed via Qubit 4 Fluorometer (Q33238, Thermo Fisher). Paired-end sequencing was performed on an Illumina HiSeq platform (Illumina), MGISEQ-2000 (BGI), or SURFSeq 5000 (Genemind).

Turbo-ID analysis

TurboID-based proximity labeling was performed as previously describedwith minor modifications.47 Briefly, MigR1-Flag-ZMIZ1-TurboID constructs were transiently transfected into MOLM13 and OCI-AML3 cells, and cells were harvested 48 h post-transfection. For proximity labeling, cells were incubated with D-biotin (50 μM) at 37 °C for 30 min, based on prior optimization. Labeling was terminated by placing cells on ice, followed by extensive washing with ice-cold PBS.

Cells were lysed in RIPA buffer supplemented with protease inhibitors, and clarified lysates were obtained after sonication and centrifugation. Biotinylated proteins were enriched using streptavidin-conjugated magnetic beads by overnight incubation at 4 °C. Beads were subjected to stringent sequential washes to minimize non-specific binding, including two washes with RIPA lysis buffer, one wash with 1 M KCl, one wash with 0.1 M Na₂CO₃, one wash with 2 M urea in 10 mM Tris-HCl (pH 8.0), followed by two additional washes with RIPA lysis buffer. Bound proteins were eluted by boiling in SDS sample buffer.

Proximity labeling efficiency was initially assessed by streptavidin-HRP Western blotting, and enriched proteins were subsequently identified by liquid chromatography-tandem mass spectrometry (LC-MS/MS).

Recombinant protein expression and purification

The human ZMIZ1 cDNA sequence was cloned into the pCAG expression vector, incorporating an N-terminal 8 × His tag and a C-terminal maltose-binding protein (MBP) tag linked via a TEV protease cleavage site. HEK 293 F cells were cultured in SMM 293-TII medium (M293TII, Sino Biological Inc., China) at 37 °C under 5% CO₂ with orbital shaking (120 rpm; MQD-B3 incubator, MINQUAN, China). For transient transfection, cells were harvested at a density of 2 × 106 cells/mL. Per liter of culture, 1.5 mg plasmid DNA was mixed with 40 µg 40-kDa linear polyethylenimine (PEI, Sigma) and 30 mL fresh medium, incubated for 30 min, and added to the cells. Post-transfection (72 hr), cells were centrifuged (500 × g, 10 min), washed with PBS, and resuspended in lysis buffer (50 mM Tris-HCl, pH 8.0, 50 mM NaCl, 10% glycerol, 1 mM DTT (A300862, Thermo Scientific), protease inhibitor cocktail (A32965, Thermo Scientific, USA). Cell disruption was performed using a high-pressure homogenizer (300 bar), followed by centrifugation (16,000 × g, 30 min) and 0.22 µm filtration. The clarified lysate was loaded onto an MBPTrap HP column (28918778, Cytiva, Sweden) equilibrated with 50 mM Tris-HCl, pH 8.0, 400 mM NaCl, 5% glycerol, and 0.5 mM TCEP. After washing, bound proteins were eluted with 25 mM HEPES pH 8.0, 50 mM NaCl, 0.5 mM TCEP, and 10 mM D-maltose (A604950, BBI). The eluate was incubated with TEV protease (4 °C, overnight), concentrated using a 50-kDa cutoff Centricon device (Millipore), and further purified by size-exclusion chromatography on a Superose-6 Increase column (29091596, Cytiva). Peak fractions were collected for SPR analysis.

Surface plasmon resonance (SPR) analysis

SPR binding assays were conducted on a Biacore 8 K system (Cytiva) at 25 °C using HBS-EP+ buffer (10 mM HEPES pH 7.4, 150 mM NaCl, 0.05% v/v Tween 20, 5% v/v DMSO). MBP-tagged ZMIZ1 was immobilized on a CM7 sensor chip via amine coupling. A small molecule was injected as the analyte over the functionalized surface. The equilibrium dissociation constant (KD) was determined using single-cycle kinetics in accordance with the manufacturer’s protocol. Data were analyzed processed and fitted to determine kinetics parameters using Biacore Insight Evaluation Software (Cytiva).

IDR prediction

The intrinsic disorder regions (IDRs) of ZMIZ1 were predicted using three online tools: PONDR, IUPRED, and ANCHOR. PONDR-VSL2 predictions were performed using the PONDR website (https://pondr.com/), while IUPRED and ANCHOR analyses were conducted using the IUPRED2A web tool (https://iupred2a.elte.hu/). The query used for all predictions was Q9ULJ6, and default parameters were applied.

Droplet formation assay

Protein solutions were prepared at concentrations of 0.5, 1, 2, and 4 μM in a droplet formation buffer consisting of 150 mM NaCl, 25 mM HEPES (pH 8.0), 3% glycerol, and 50 mM D-biotin (B20656, Thermo Fisher Scientific). To recapitulate molecular crowding, 8% PEG-8000 (ST483, Beyotime) was included as a crowding agent. Recombinant protein was incorporated to a fixed final concentration of 4 μM. The final NaCl concentrations were systematically varied to 25, 100, 200, 500, 1000, and 2000 mM. Additionally, 1,6-hexanediol was introduced at final concentrations of 4% or 8%, while maintaining PEG-8000 was maintained constant at 8%. Immediately after preparation, 5 μL aliquots were pipetted onto a glass slide, covered with a coverslip, and incubated at 4 °C in the dark for 10 min. Images were acquired promptly using a 60× objective on a Leica confocal microscope.

Immunofluorescence

Cells were washed once in DPBS (B210KJ, BasalMedia Technologies), resuspended in 100 µL DPBS, and centrifuged at 800 rpm for 5 min. Cell pellets were applied onto glass slides and air-dried at room temperature for 15 min. Samples were fixed in 4% (w/v) paraformaldehyde (252549, Sigma) for 15 min at 25 °C to preserve cellular architecture and antigen integrity. Following fixation, slides were washed three times with PBS. Permeabilization was carried out using 0.25% Triton X-100 (T8787, Sigma) for 8 min at 25 °C, followed by three additional washes with PBS. Then, sections were blocked with 2% BSA (ST023, Beyotime) prepared in PBST for 45 min at room temperature. The primary antibody, diluted in blocking buffer, was applied to the samples and incubated at 4 °C for 12 h. Excess primary antibody was removed by washing three times with PBST. For fluorescence detection, samples were incubated with fluorochrome-conjugated secondary antibody diluted in blocking buffer for 1 h at room temperature in the dark, followed by three washes with PBST. Nuclei were counterstained with Hoechst (H33258, ThermoFisher) for 5 min at room temperature protected from light, and washed once with PBS. Coverslips were mounted using anti-fade mounting medium (G1401, Servicebio) and sealed with clear nail polish. All slides were stored at 4 °C in the dark until image acquisition. Confocal imaging was performed on a Leica spinning-disk confocal microscope using a 100× oil immersion objective and LAS X software.

Cellular thermal shift assay (CETSA)

Cellular thermal shift assay (CETSA) was performed to evaluate target engagement of SIH001 in MOLM-13 cells. MOLM-13 cells were treated with SIH001 or DMSO for 12–16 h prior to thermal challenge. After treatment, washed with PBS twice and resuspended at 1 × 10⁷ cells/mL in PBS containing protease inhibitors. Aliquots (50 μL) of the cell suspension were transferred into PCR tubes and heated at the indicated temperatures (42, 46, 48, 50, 52, 54, and 58 °C) for 5 min using a thermal cycler, followed by equilibration at 25 °C for 3 min, and then immediately placed on ice. Cells were then subjected to three freeze-thaw cycles in liquid nitrogen to ensure complete lysis. Lysates were centrifuged at 20,000 g for 20 min at 4 °C to remove aggregated proteins. The supernatants were collected and analyzed by Western blotting to detect the soluble fraction of the target protein.

Survival analysis

Transcriptomic data and clinical survival records were obtained from two independent AML patient cohorts. The discovery cohort (Cohort-RJ) included RNA-seq data (quantified as Transcripts Per Million, TPM) from patients treated at Ruijin Hospital, as previously described by Li et al.48 The validation cohort (Cohort-TCGA) consisted of data retrieved from The Cancer Genome Atlas (TCGA) LAML dataset.49 To determine the optimal cutoff values for stratifying patients into ZMIZ1-high and ZMIZ1-low expression groups, we utilized the maximally selected rank statistics method. This analysis was performed using the surv_cutpoint function within the survminer R package (v0.5.0), which identifies the threshold that yields the most significant difference in survival outcomes. Survival differences between these two groups were subsequently assessed using the Log-Rank test, and visualized with Kaplan-Meier survival curves. All statistical analyses were conducted using R software.

Mouse studies

C57BL/6 mice (Cat. No. SM-001) and Zmiz1flox/flox mice (Cat. No. NM-CKO-2101744) were purchased from Shanghai Model Organisms Center, Inc. B6. Cg-Tg(Mx1-cre)1Cgn/J mice (Strain #: 003556) were obtained from The Jackson Laboratory. Bone-marrow (BM) cells were isolated from Zmiz1flox/flox; Mx1-Cre, or Zmiz1+/+; Mx1-Cre C57BL/6 mice and transduced with retroviral vectors expressing the MLL-AF9 oncogene via spinoculation. Potential Mx1-Cre-related effects were considered as a limitation of the study. GFP-positive ratios were analyzed by flow cytometry, and equal numbers of GFP-positive cells were transplanted into recipient mice. Transduced BM cells were mixed at a 1:1 ratio with un-transfected BM cells and intravenously transplanted into lethally irradiated (6.5 Gy X-ray) syngeneic recipient mice. Fourteen days post-transplantation, Zmiz1 deletion was induced by intraperitoneal injection of polyinosinic-polycytidylic acid (poly(I:C)), 10 mg/kg (24939-03-5, TargetMol, Shanghai, China), every 48 h for a total of three doses. Disease monitoring comprised weekly automated peripheral blood cell enumeration (Sysmex XN-1000), Kaplan-Meier survival curve analysis with log-rank statistical testing, and hematoxylin & eosin (H&E) staining and immunohistochemical (IHC) analysis to quantify leukemic infiltration.

For secondary transplantation, BM cells were isolated from moribund primary leukemic mice, both ZMIZ1-knockout and control. These cells were then transplanted intravenously into sub-lethally irradiated (4.5 Gy, X-RAD 320) secondary C57BL/6 recipient mice. The survival analysis was performed using the Log-rank (Mantel-Cox) test.

For in vivo efficacy studies, mice were randomly assigned to four treatment groups (n = 8 per group): (1) vehicle control group, (2) SIH-001 monotherapy group, (3) Venetoclax monotherapy group, and (4) SIH-001 plus Venetoclax combination group. SIH-001 and Venetoclax were dissolved in a vehicle solution containing PEG300 and Tween 80. Compounds were administered by oral gavage at a dose of 20 mg/kg once daily for 21 consecutive days. The vehicle control group received the same volume of vehicle solution. Mice were monitored daily for survival and clinical signs. Overall survival was analyzed using Kaplan-Meier survival curves, and statistical significance between groups was determined using the log-rank (Mantel-Cox) test.

Generation of human induced pluripotent stem cell (iPSC)-derived bone marrow organoid (BMO)

Human bone marrow organoids (BMOs) were generated from a homemade human iPSC line (Shanghai Amplicon Gene) following a modified protocol based on the previously published studies.50,51 Briefly, on the day before mesodermal induction, iPSCs were digested with ReLeSR (StemCell, 100–0483) and seeded in ultra-low attachment (ULA) 6-well plates (Corning, 3471) with mTeSR Plus medium (StemCell, 100–0276) containing 10 μM Y27632 (StemCell, 72302) for embryoid body (EB) formation. Then, mesoderm differentiation was initiated on Day 0 (D0) by replacing with D0 medium (STEMdiff APEL2 medium (StemCell, 05275) with 50 ng/mL BMP4 (Peprotech, 120-05), FGF2 (StemCell, 78134.1), VEGFA (StemCell, 78159.1) and 3 μM CHIR99021 (Merck, SML1046)) for 3 days (Day 0–3). On Day 3, the medium was refreshed to D3 medium (APEL2 medium with 50 ng/mL BMP4, FGF2, VEGFA; 25 ng/mL SCF (StemCell, 78062) and FLT3L (StemCell, 78009) for 2 days (Day 3–5). On Day 5, cell aggregates collected by gravitation were embedded in a two-layer hydrogel with D5 medium (APEL2 medium with 5% (vol/vol) KnockOut (KO) serum replacement (ThermoFisher, 10828028) and 5 U/mL heparin (Merck, H3149); 20 ng/mL IL3 (StemCell, 78194.1) and IL6 (StemCell, 78050); 50 ng/mL VEGFC, FGF2, SCF, FLT3L, TPO (StemCell, 78210.1), EPO (StemCell, 78007), BMP4, and G-CSF (StemCell, 78012); 100 ng/mL VEGFA) in 12-well plates (Coring, 10253041). The hydrogels were composed of 60% (vol/vol) human collagen mixture (3 mg/mL Type I (Advanced Biomatrix, 5008) and 1 mg/mL Type IV (Advanced Biomatrix, 5022)) and 40% (vol/vol) Matrigel (Coring, 354230). The culture media were refreshed every 72 h with D7 medium (APEL2 medium with 5% KO serum, and 5 U/mL heparin; 10 ng/mL IL3 and IL6; 25 ng/mL VEGFC, FGF2, SCF, FLT3L, TPO, EPO, BMP4, and G-CSF; 50 ng/mL VEGFA) on Day 7 and D10 medium (APEL2 medium with 5% KO serum, and 5 U/mL heparin; 10 ng/mL IL3 and IL6; 25 ng/mL VEGFC, FGF2, SCF, FLT3L, TPO and EPO; 50 ng/mL VEGFA) on Day 10, respectively. On Day 13, formed bone marrow organoids were transferred to ULA 96-well plates (Corning, 7007). Each BMO spheroid was seeded in one well with 200 μL D13 medium (StemPro-34 SFM (ThermoFisher, 10639011) with 2% KO serum, 2% (vol/vol) Chemically Defined Lipids (ThermoFisher, 11905031); 10 ng/mL IL3, IL6, EPO and TPO; 25 ng/mL VEGFC, FGF2, SCF, FLT3L, and VEGFA), which was refreshed every 3 days with BMO maintenance medium (StemPro-34 SFM with 2% KO serum, 2% Chemically Defined Lipids; 5 ng/mL EPO and 1 ng/mL TPO).

Engraftment of AML patient sample and ex vivo drug treatment

The AML patient was written informed consent in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki, and the BM sample at diagnosis was collected under the protocol that was approved by Ruijin Hospital Ethics Committee (No. 384 of 2024). For AML engraftment, on Day 14 of iPSC induction, every mature BMO was inoculated with 1 × 10⁵ BM cells from an MDS-related AML patient carrying KMT2A-PTD, DNMT3A-R882H and NRAS-G13D mutations. To distinguish the exogenous AML cells from BMO cells, AML cells were fluorescently stained with CellTrace Yellow reagent (ThermoFisher, C34573) before engraftment. For drug treatment, on Day 21, co-cultures of AML and BMO were treated with 0.1/1 μM ZMIZ1 inhibitor, 0.1 μM Menin inhibitor Ziftomenib (MENi), 0.1 μM BCL2 inhibitor Venetoclax (VEN), their combinations, or DMSO (control) for 7 days. Samples across all groups were collected on Day 28 for the endpoint assays, such as whole-mount imaging, FACS and scRNA-seq analyses. In brief, BMOs for whole-mount imaging were fixed in 4% PFA, blocked (5% BSA/0.3% Triton X-100 in PBS, 4 °C overnight, and subsequently immunostained with the following fluorescence-conjugated antibodies: anti-human CD33-FITC (Biolegend, 366620), anti-CD45-APC-cy7 (Biolegend, 368515), and nuclear fluorescent DAPI (Biolegend, 422801). Stained BMOs were embedded in double-layer 0.5% low-melting agarose (Yeasen, 10214ES08), dehydrated with gradient ethanol (pH 9.0), cleared with ethyl cinnamate (Targetmol, TN1624), and imaged by using a 3D high-content microscopy (Opera Phenix, Revvity). Z-stack images were rendered with Harmony software (Revvity). For FACS assays, single-cell suspensions of BMOs were digested and prepared by collagenase Type II (Merck, C2-22-1G). 1×10⁵ Cells were stained anti-human CD45-APC-Cy7 (Biolegend, 368515); anti-human CD33-FITC (Biolegend, 366620), anti-CD271-APC (Biolegend, 345107), and dead cell marker Zombie UV reagent (Biolegend, 423107). Data were analyzed via FlowJo v10 software, with debris/doublets excluded by gating; live cells (Zombie UV ⁻), CD45⁺ hematopoietic subsets, and CellTrace+ / CD33+ subsets were identified as exogenous cells from the AML patient. The scRNA-seq experiment was conducted by using the remaining single-cell suspensions from 3 BMOs per group.

CRISPR-Cas9 screening data analysis

CRISPR-Cas9 screening data were analyzed using the MAGeCK pipeline (v0.5.9.5).52 After transduction of Cas9-expressing MOLM13 and OCI-AML3 cells with a custom sgRNA library (512 target genes with 6 sgRNAs per gene, plus 300 non-targeting control sgRNAs), cells were sorted by flow cytometry into differentiated fractions defined by high expression of CD11b or CD14, alongside unsorted input controls. Genomic DNA was extracted, sgRNA cassettes were PCR-amplified, and deep sequencing was performed to quantify sgRNA representation. Sequencing reads were mapped to the library reference and counted with MAGeCK count, and normalization was performed using the 300 non-targeting sgRNAs to adjust for library size and technical bias. Differential enrichment was then assessed with the MAGeCK robust rank aggregation (RRA) algorithm, which aggregates information across sgRNAs to derive gene-level scores, p-values, and FDR-adjusted significance. Genes with sgRNAs enriched in the CD11bhi/CD14hi fractions relative to input were interpreted as relieving the differentiation block, whereas depletion indicated maintenance of the undifferentiated state. Analyses were performed independently for MOLM13 and OCI-AML3 cell lines, and results were compared across differentiation markers to prioritize reproducible regulators.

RNA-seq data processing

Raw sequencing reads were first assessed and trimmed to remove adapters and low-quality bases using Fastp (v0.23.2).53 The resulting high-quality reads were then aligned to the human reference genome (GRCh38/hg38) using HISAT2 (v2.2.1).54 Gene-level quantification was performed with featureCounts (v2.0.1),55 using GENCODE gene annotations. Expression values were normalized to transcripts per million (TPM) to allow for cross-sample comparisons.

Differential expression analysis between ZMIZ1 knockdown and control (shZMIZ1 vs. SCR) groups was conducted using the DESeq2 package (v1.46.0)56 in R, which models count data using the negative binomial distribution. Genes with an absolute log2 fold change (|log2FC | ) ≥ log2(1.5) and nominal p value < 0.05 were considered differentially expressed.

Functional enrichment analysis was performed on the differentially expressed genes. Gene Ontology (GO) Biological Process (BP) enrichment was conducted using the clusterProfiler package (v4.14.6)57 in R, identifying overrepresented biological processes among up- and downregulated genes. For gene set enrichment analysis (GSEA), a custom gene set was constructed based on putative ZMIZ1 target genes identified from ZMIZ1 ChIP-seq data. Genes were ranked by log2 fold change, and GSEA was performed using the GSEA function in clusterProfiler, with default parameters for enrichment score and significance estimation.

ChIP-seq and Re-ChIP-seq data analysis

ChIP-seq reads were aligned to the human reference genome (hg38) using Bowtie2 (v2.3.5.1)58 with default parameters. Duplicate reads were marked and removed using Picard MarkDuplicates (v2.25.0). To ensure comparability across samples, aligned reads were downsampled to the same library size using the randsample function in MACS2 with the parameters --number 1e-7 and --seed 614.

Peak calling was performed using MACS2.59 For transcription factor ChIP-seq datasets, peaks were called using a relaxed q-value threshold of 0.01 to enhance sensitivity. In contrast, for histone modification ChIP-seq data, a more stringent p-value cutoff of 1e-9 was applied to improve specificity.

Normalized signal tracks were generated from aligned ChIP-seq data using deepTools (v3.5.6)60 and were used for both global visualization and quantitative signal profiling. Signal intensity around defined genomic regions was assessed using reference-point heatmaps, allowing comparison across different sample groups. For gene-level visualization, signal tracks were displayed using the trackViewer (v1.42.0)61 package in R, enabling direct inspection of ChIP-seq signal enrichment at selected genomic loci.

Collection of ChIP-seq data

Publicly available ChIP-seq datasets were retrieved from GEO and SRA. For H3K27ac, raw data were collected for NB4 (SRR5413503), MV-4-11 (SRR5413510), OCI-AML3 (SRR5413517),62 HL60 (GSM2836486),63 and MOLM13 (GSM1652920).64 MED1 ChIP-seq data in MOLM14 were obtained from GSM1587903.65 BRD4 ChIP-seq data were retrieved for MOLM14 (GSM1587905),65 MV-4-11 (GSM340895),66 and OCI-AML3 (GSM2807116).67

Identification of master TFs based on CRC analysis

To identify master transcription factors (TFs) in AML, we constructed core regulatory circuitry (CRC) models based on super-enhancer landscapes in 21 primary AML samples. Super-enhancers (SEs) were defined using the Rank Ordering of Super-Enhancers (ROSE) algorithm31 based on H3K27ac ChIP-seq data. A stitching distance of 12.5 kb was applied uniformly across all samples to facilitate inter-sample comparison, with other parameters set to default values. The enhancer-gene assignment was based on the ENHANCER_TO_TOP_GENE.txt output from ROSE, and these gene annotations were used to define SE-associated target genes.

We next employed the CRC mapper algorithm68 to construct CRC models for each of the 21 AML samples using the identified SEs. Genome annotation was performed using the UCSC hg38 refGene.txt, and transcription factor motif scanning was conducted with FIMO, using motif position weight matrices obtained from the JASPAR database. Transcription factors appearing in more than 25% of CRC models were considered as recurrent regulators and candidate master TFs. Among these, MEF2D was highly recurrent, present in 67% of samples, suggesting it may serve as a key regulatory hub in AML. Other frequently identified TFs included SPI1, RUNX1, MYB, and IRF1, consistent with their known roles in hematopoietic development and leukemogenesis.

ZMIZ1 motif analysis

To identify ZMIZ1-associated DNA binding motifs in AML, motif enrichment analysis was performed on genomic regions co-bound by ZMIZ1 in five different AML cell lines. These co-bound regions were derived from ZMIZ1 ChIP-seq datasets and were used as input for de novo motif discovery using the findMotifsGenome.pl tool from the HOMER suite (v5.1).69 Enriched motifs were identified relative to genomic background, and the most significantly enriched motif was designated as the putative binding motif of ZMIZ1 in AML.

Hi-C data analysis

Hi-C libraries were generated from MOLM13 and OCI-AML3 cell lines, each under scrambled shRNA control (SCR) and ZMIZ1 knockdown (shZMIZ1) conditions, resulting in four total samples. Raw paired-end sequencing data were aligned to the human reference genome (hg38) using BWA-MEM (v 0.7.19-r1273)70 with the -SP flag to allow for split alignments, and alignment results were converted to BAM format using samtools (v1.21).71 Hi-C read pairs were processed using the pairtools suite (v1.1.3),72 which parses, sorts, and deduplicates valid chromatin contacts; basic contact statistics were also computed at this step.

The resulting contact pairs were used to construct .cool and .mcool matrices at 10 kb resolution using the cooler toolkit (v0.10.3).73 For each sample, genomic bins were defined from the hg38 chromosome size file, and contact matrices were loaded using cooler cload pairs, followed by iterative correction with cooler balance. Multi-resolution matrices were generated via Cooler Zoomify, enabling analyses at multiple scales.

Quality control metrics, including coverage and matrix sparsity at 10 kb and 100 kb resolutions, were computed using cooltools (v0.8.1)74 and custom shell scripts. A/B compartment analysis was performed at 100 kb resolution by computing the first eigenvector of the observed/expected cis contact matrix using cooltools eigs-cis, with GC content tracks as the phasing reference. To identify topologically associating domains (TADs), insulation scores were computed across a range of window sizes, and TAD boundaries were extracted using a custom script based on insulation score minima.

To identify differential chromatin loops between ZMIZ1 knockdown and scrambled control groups within each cell line, contact matrices at 10 kb resolution were extracted and compared using the diff_mustache.py module of the Mustache package (v1.3.3).75 Additionally, to robustly detect and visualize differential chromatin interactions, we utilized the Selfish tool.76 Differential contact maps were generated using Selfish to explicitly visualize statistically significant changes in interaction frequencies between ZMIZ1-shRNA and scrambled control samples. Candidate differential loops were converted to BEDPE format for downstream analysis.

To visualize chromatin architecture differences between treatment groups, especially at loci of interest, the coolbox toolkit (v0.3.0)77 was used. Coolbox sessions were configured to display Hi-C contact maps from .mcool files alongside tracks for loop anchors, compartment signals, and gene annotations. By overlaying the scrambled control and ZMIZ1 knockdown samples from each cell line, we identified treatment-specific structural changes, such as weakened loops, compartment switches, or altered TAD boundaries, highlighting the impact of ZMIZ1 depletion on 3D genome organization.

scRNA-seq data processing and analysis

Single-cell RNA sequencing (scRNA-seq) was performed using the Chromium Next GEM Single Cell 3’ Kit v3.1 (10x Genomics, PN-1000268) or the MobiCube Single-Cell 3’ RNA-seq Kits (Mobidrop), following the manufacturers’ protocols. Raw sequencing data were processed using Cell Ranger (v9.0.1) with the GRCm39 reference genome (release 2024-A) to generate gene-cell count matrices. Subsequent quality control and downstream analysis were performed in Seurat (v5.0).78 Low-quality cells were filtered out by retaining only those with 200 < nFeature_RNA < 8000, 500 < nCount_RNA < 50,000, and mitochondrial gene percentage < 12.5%. Doublets were identified and removed using scDblFinder (v1.20.2).79

To mitigate batch effects, expression data were normalized and variance-stabilized using SCTransform,80 followed by dimensionality reduction with PCA (30 components). Integration of multiple datasets was performed using the Reciprocal PCA (RPCA) integration method implemented in Seurat, with SCT-normalized data as input. Graph-based clustering was conducted using the FindNeighbors and FindClusters functions, and the data were visualized with UMAP based on the first 30 PCs from the integrated reduction.

Cluster-specific differentially expressed genes (DEGs) were identified using FindAllMarkers, with cutoffs of |log2FC | > 0.5 and adjusted p < 0.05. Functional annotation was conducted by KEGG pathway enrichment using the clusterProfiler R package (v4.14.6).57 Cell identities were assigned using SingleR(v2.0.2)81 with the ImmGen reference dataset from the celldex R package (v1.16.0). Predicted labels were cross validated with canonical marker gene expression and complemented by scType82 annotation to ensure robustness.

Single-cell functional inference and immune regulation

Developmental potential was assessed using CytoTRACE2 (v1.1.0),83 which estimates stemness scores based on transcriptional entropy at the single-cell level. To reconstruct differentiation trajectories, we performed RNA velocity analysis with scVelo (v0.2.5),84 leveraging spliced/unspliced RNA dynamics. In addition, Monocle2 (v2.34.0)85 was applied to generate pseudotime trajectories using the DDRTree algorithm, enabling visualization of lineage bifurcations and temporal ordering of cells along myeloid differentiation paths.

To evaluate lineage bias, we curated canonical gene sets representing distinct hematopoietic fates. The monocyte-biased program included Mafb, Irf8, Klf4, Runx1, Csf1r, Fcgr3, Cx3cr1, and Irf5, which encode transcription factors and receptors critical for monocyte/macrophage specification and immune effector function. The granulocyte-biased program comprised Mpo, Elane, Prtn3, Camp, Ltf, S100a8, S100a9, and Cebpe, encompassing neutrophil-defining proteases, antimicrobial peptides, inflammatory mediators, and the granulocyte lineage-specific transcription factor Cebpe. Module scores were calculated in Seurat with AddModuleScore, generating MonoScore and GranScore as quantitative indicators of lineage commitment.

Cell-cell communication networks were inferred using CellChat (v2.2.0),86 based on curated ligand-receptor interactions from CellChatDB, identifying major sending and receiving populations. Transcriptional regulatory networks were reconstructed using pySCENIC (v0.12.1),87 integrating co-expression with cis-regulatory motif enrichment to reveal key transcription factors driving lineage transitions.

Finally, immune functional activity was assessed by constructing an MHC-I signature (H2-K1, H2-D1, H2-Q7, B2m, Tap1, Tap2), representing structural MHC-I components and peptide-loading machinery. Module scores derived from these genes were used as a proxy for antigen presentation capacity across single cells.

Single-cell RNA-seq analysis of the human BMO co-culture model

Single-cell RNA sequencing data from the human bone marrow organoid (BMO) model, including a BMO-only control and BMO co-cultured with AML blasts under drug perturbations, were analyzed in Seurat (v5.0). Standard preprocessing steps, including quality control, SCTransform-based normalization, cross-sample integration, graph-based clustering, and UMAP visualization, were performed as described above. AML blast cells were operationally defined by comparative profiling across conditions, with cell populations that were present or strongly enriched in the BMO + AML blast co-culture but not detected, or detected at negligible frequency, in the BMO-only condition designated as blasts and used for downstream blast-focused analyses. Myeloid transcriptional programs were quantified on a per-cell basis using Seurat AddModuleScore with a curated myeloid gene set (LYZ, S100A8, S100A9, LGALS3, CTSS, FCN1, FCER1G, LST1, TYMP, AIF1, MNDA, VCAN, MS4A7, CTSD, CST3, SPI1). Cellular developmental potential was estimated using CytoTRACE2, and CytoTRACE2(v1.1.0) scores were summarized across conditions, including within the blast compartment. Differentiation trajectories of AML blast cells were reconstructed using Monocle3(v1.4.26) by learning a trajectory graph and ordering cells in pseudotime, and pseudotime distributions were visualized with treatment labels to compare blast state progression across perturbations.
