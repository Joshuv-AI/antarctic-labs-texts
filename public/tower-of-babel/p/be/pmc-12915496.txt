Co-treatment of ticagrelor and anti-PD-1 immunotherapy in tumor-associated macrophages reduces pancreatic cancer cell growth and migration through the TGF-β1/Smad2 pathway
Authors: Ying Kang, Emmanuel Boadi Amoafo, Philomena Entsie, Buddhadev Layek, Paul M Grandgenett, Michael A Hollingsworth, Marina Pasca di Magliano, Elisabetta Liverani
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractPancreatic ductal adenocarcinoma (PDAC) is the third primary cause of cancer-related mortality in the US. PDAC is associated with an immunosuppressive tumor microenvironment (TME) that restricts the effectiveness of immunotherapies. Immunosuppressive tumor-associated macrophages (TAMs), the most abundant cell type in TME, express immune checkpoint ligands, including programmed cell death protein 1 (PD-1) ligand 1 (PD-L1). Targeting PD-1/PD-L1 alone has no effect in PDAC, highlighting the need for combination approaches. P2Y12, an ADP receptor, is expressed by macrophages, and P2Y12 antagonists promote phagocytosis, in turn, is linked to anti-tumor activity. Here, we tested the combined blockade of PD-1/PD-L1 and P2Y12 on TAMs in pancreatic cancer. For this purpose, we set up co-cultures of pancreatic cancer cells and TAMs and treated them with inhibitor BMS-1, which blocks PD-1/PD-L1 interaction, cemiplimab to block PD-1, and ticagrelor to block P2Y12. We observed that the co-treatment of ticagrelor and cemiplimab promoted TAM phagocytic ability and inhibited the TAM-induced growth and migration of cancer cells. The combination of ticagrelor and cemiplimab inhibited TGF-β1 release from TAMs and Smad2 phosphorylation in cancer cells. Our data suggest that co-treatment of ticagrelor and cemiplimab in TAMs inhibits pancreatic cancer cell growth and migration by suppressing the TGF-β1/Smad2 signaling pathway, providing an emerging strategy aimed at re–educating or depleting TAMs to enhance the efficacy of immunotherapy in pancreatic cancer.Keywords: PDAC, TME, Tumor-associated macrophages, PD-1, PD-L1, P2Y12

Introduction

Pancreatic ductal adenocarcinoma (PDAC) is one of the most aggressive and lethal solid malignancies. Unfortunately, it is rarely diagnosed before spreading to other organs [1]. Surgery followed by adjuvant therapy is the main curative option. Nevertheless, it is effective only for non-metastatic patients, and unfortunately, patients will often have tumor recurrence and develop metastasis post-surgery [2]. Conventional cytotoxic treatments (such as chemotherapy and radiotherapy) and targeted therapies are available options, especially for metastatic cancer [2]. However, they have achieved disappointing therapeutic results due to the heterogeneous nature of pancreatic cancer and the complexity of the crosstalk between tumor cells, immune cells, and stromal cells in the tumor microenvironment (TME).

The cellular and soluble components of the immunosuppressive TME support the immune escape of malignant cells and accelerate tumor growth. The immunosuppressive cells include myeloid-derived suppressive cells (MDSCs), tumor-associated macrophages (TAMs), and regulatory T cells (Tregs) [3]. The cancer cells or immunosuppressive cells secrete molecules like interleukin-10 (IL-10), transforming growth factor-beta (TGF-β), and vascular endothelial growth factor (VEGF) that negatively regulate the anti-tumor immune response [3]. To evade immune identification, cancer cells also upregulate the expression of immunological checkpoints on immunosuppressive cells or decrease the expression of neoantigens and antigen presentation molecules, such as MHC-I [4]. Treatments have been designed to enhance the anti-tumor capacity of the immune system. The interaction between tumor-specific T cells and cancer cells occurs through programmed death 1 (PD-1) on the T cells and programmed death 1 ligand 1 (PD-L1) on the cancer cells. This interaction leads to the suppression, exhaustion, and apoptosis of T cells, ultimately allowing cancer to evade the immune response. Thus, antibodies blocking PD-1 or PD-L1 should restore T cell activity and enable the clearance of cancer cells [5]. Indeed, targeting PD-1 or PD-L1 is associated with a significant clinical response in patients with different tumors [6,7]. Unfortunately, PD1 blockade has been largely unsuccessful in PDAC, with the possible exception of patients with DNA mismatch repair mutations and consequent high mutation burden [8]. Therefore, a deeper understanding of the PD-1/PD-L1 axis-mediated immune resistance mechanisms is essential to sensitize pancreatic cancer to immune checkpoint blockade.

TAMs, as the most abundant immune cells (~50 %) in the PDAC TME, are immunosuppressive cells that induce drug resistance to PD-1/PD-L1 therapy [9,10]. Circulating monocytes are recruited into tumor tissues through tumor-derived environmental factors and differentiated into anti-inflammatory TAMs [11]. The density of TAMs correlates negatively with PDAC’s overall survival rate [12]. TAMs promote cancer progression by secreting cytokines and growth factors (e.g., IL-10 and TGF-β) that regulate cancer cell proliferation, stemness, angiogenesis, migration, invasion, and immune suppression [13]. In pancreatic cancer, TAMs are a source of epidermal growth factor receptor (EGFR) ligands and thus directly promote cancer cell proliferation [14]. In addition, both PD-1 and PD-L1 are expressed on TAMs. PD-L1 on TAMs interacts with PD-1 on T cells, while PD-1 on TAMs interacts with PD-L1 on cancer cells [11]. The interaction between PD-L1 on TAMs and PD-1 on T cells suppresses T cell functions. PD-1 expression influences TAM polarization and favors M2 polarization, indicated by impaired phagocytosis through interaction with PD-L1 on tumor cells and disordered cytokine profiles [11,15,16]. Considering their central roles between cancer cells and the immune system, TAMs are promising targets for decreasing immune suppression. Re-education of TAMs from immunosuppressive into immunostimulatory phenotype can be achieved by inhibiting anti-inflammatory cytokine release and blockade of ligand-receptor pairs that inhibit phagocytosis [17]. A study has shown that blockade of PD-1/PD-L1 in vivo increases macrophage phagocytosis, reduces tumor growth, and improves mice’s survival in mouse cancer models independent of T cell presence [18]. Therefore, due to the relationship between macrophage polarization and the PD-1/PD-L1 axis, the alternative strategy focuses on TAM-related mechanisms that contribute to resistance against PD-1/PD-L1 blockade and enhancing the efficacy of PD-1/PD-L1 blockade by reprogramming or depleting TAMs [11].

The P2Y12 receptor is a G protein-coupled receptor for adenosine diphosphate (ADP). It is expressed on platelets and is the pharmacologic target for anti-thrombotic agents [19]. P2Y12 is also present in PDAC cell lines such as AsPC-1, BxPC-3, MiaPaCa-2, and PANC-1, where it is 5-fold greater than in normal pancreatic cells (hTERT-HPNE) [20]. In the TME, platelets and tumor cells release large amounts of ADP, suggesting that the P2Y12 signaling pathway is activated [21]. Indeed, the P2Y12 antagonist, ticagrelor, was able to reduce PDAC cell growth and migration in vitro [20,22]. Blocking P2Y12 in vivo reduced tumor growth in mouse models of pancreatic cancer [20,23]. Lastly, Pavlović et. al. reported that macrophages express P2Y12, and ticagrelor promotes a pro-inflammatory phenotype and enhances phagocytosis of tumor cells by macrophages [24]. These data suggest that ticagrelor has the potential as a therapeutic option in PDAC [20].

Given the important roles of both the PD-1/PD-L1 axis and the P2Y12 signaling pathway in macrophage polarization, we investigated whether P2Y12 antagonism and anti-PD-1/PD-L1 co-treatments on TAMs can prevent pancreatic cancer cell growth and migration. We investigated TAM phagocytosis and cancer cell proliferation and migration in the TAM-cancer cell co-culture when blocking PD-1/PD-L1 and P2Y12. To determine the roles of PD-1/PD-L1 axis in the TAM-cancer cell interaction, we selected two compounds: cemiplimab and BMS-1. While cemiplimab blocks only PD-1 on TAMs [25], BMS-1 blocks the interaction between PD-1 and PD-L1 by binding to PD-L1 (on either TAMs or cancer cells) [26]. Our data shows that co-treatment of ticagrelor and the anti-PD-1 antibody, cemiplimab, increases TAM’s phagocytic ability while diminishing the TAM-induced growth and migration of cancer cells. Moreover, treating TAM with ticagrelor and cemiplimab diminished their TGF-β1 release and subsequently prevented Smad2 phosphorylation in cancer cells. Overall, our findings suggest that ticagrelor and cemiplimab inhibit pancreatic cancer cell growth and migration through the TGF-β1/Smad2 signaling pathway.

Materials and methods

Phobol 12-tetradecanoate 13-acetate (PMA) and ticagrelor were obtained from Sigma-Aldrich (St. Louis, MO). Cemiplimab and Bristol-Myers Squibb (BMS)-1 were obtained from Selleckchem (Houston, TX). RIPA buffer was obtained from Abcam (Waltham, MA). The anti-human antibodies were purchased from Thermo Fisher Scientific including Fc Receptor Binding Inhibitor Polyclonal Antibody, CD163 Polyclonal Antibody, PD-1 Monoclonal Antibody (DHIC2–4A10), P2Y12 Recombinant Rabbit Monoclonal Antibody (4H5L19), CD206 (MMR) Monoclonal Antibody (19.2), PE, CD163 Monoclonal Antibody (eBioGHI/61 (GHI/61)), PE, CD279 (PD-1) Monoclonal Antibody (J43), FITC, Goat anti-Mouse IgG (H+L) Cross-Adsorbed Secondary Antibody, Alexa Fluor™ 488, Goat anti-Rabbit IgG (H+L) Cross-Adsorbed Secondary Antibody, Alexa Fluor™ 568, Phospho-SMAD2 (Ser465, Ser467) Polyclonal Antibody and beta Actin Loading Control Monoclonal Antibody (BA3R). Patients and tissue samples

Human pancreatic tissue samples from 5 non-cancer donors (2 females and 3 males) and 5 deceased pancreatic cancer patients (3 females and 2 males) were received from the Rapid Autopsy Program (RAP) for Pancreas Cancer and the Normal Organ Recovery (NORs) Program at the University of Nebraska Medical Center (UNMC) in compliance with IRB 091–01. Non-cancer samples were collected at UNMC in collaboration with Live On Nebraska.

Immunohistochemistry

Paraffin-embedded pancreatic tissue slides were deparaffinized in xylene and a hydration series (100 % ethanol, 95 % ethanol, 70 % ethanol, H2O). Antigen retrieval was performed by a pressure cooker for 40 min in 10 mM sodium citrate buffer (pH 6.0). The specimens were permeabilized in 0.1 % Triton X-100 for 15 min and blocked with 1 % bovine serum albumin in PBS for 1 h. Specimens were incubated with antibodies against human CD163, PD-1, and PD-L1 (1:200) overnight at 4 °C, followed by Alexa Fluor™ conjugate secondary antibody (1:500) for 1 h at room temperature. Tissue sections were mounted using Antifade mounting medium with 4’, 6-diamidino-2-phenylindole (DAPI). Images for 5 fields of view per section were collected by a confocal microscope (LSM 900, ZEISS, White Plains, NY) at 40x magnification.

Pancreatic cancer cell lines

Cells were maintained for passaging in cell culture tissue flasks at 37 °C, 5 % CO2 in a humidified tissue culture incubator. PANC-1 (CRL-1469, ATCC) cells were cultured in DMEM medium supplemented with 10 % FBS and 1 % penicillin/streptomycin/amphotericin. BxPC-3 (CRL-1687, ATCC) cells were cultured in RPMI 1640 medium supplemented with 10 % FBS and 1 % penicillin/streptomycin/amphotericin. Patient-derived human pancreatic cancer cells (HPCCs, 77007–07, Celprogen) were cultured in a human pancreatic cancer cell line complete medium.

Tumor-associated macrophage (TAM) differentiation

Cancer cells (PANC-1, BxPC-3, or HPCCs) grew to 90 % of confluence and were incubated with fresh media for 24 h. The supernatants were collected and filtered at 0.20 μm as tumor-conditioned media. Human monocytic THP-1 cells (TIB-202, ATCC) were cultured in RPMI 1640 medium supplemented with 10 % FBS and 1 % penicillin/streptomycin/amphotericin. Cells were stimulated with PMA (10 ng/mL) for 24 h to differentiate into macrophages (M0). Following a 24-hour resting period, M0 was cultured with 30 % of tumor-conditioned media in RPMI 1640 complete media for 48 h to generate TAMs [27,28]. As a result, we obtained P-TAMs, B-TAMs, or H-TAMs, respectively.

Flow cytometry

TAMs were resuspended in 100 μL 5 % FBS containing PBS at 106 cells/mL, incubated with human Fc receptor binding inhibitor (1:100) for 15 min on ice, followed by PE-labeled anti-CD206 and anti-CD163, and FITC-labeled anti-PD-1 (1:100) for 20 min at room temperature. The pancreatic cancer cells were resuspended and incubated with PE-labeled anti-PD-L1. To detect arginase 1 (Arg1), TAMs were fixed and permeabilized using the eBioscience™ Intracellular Fixation & Permeabilization Buffer Set, according to the manufacturer’s instructions. TAMs were then incubated with APC-labeled anti-Arg1 (1:100) for 20 min at room temperature. The samples were then analyzed using a flow cytometer (CytoFLEX S Flow Cytometer, Beckman Coulter, Brea, CA). Data analysis was performed using CytExpert software (version 2.6, Beckman Coulter)

Immunofluorescence

THP-1 cells were seeded in a 96-well plate (5000 cells/well) and differentiated into TAMs. Then cells were washed, fixed in 4 % Paraformaldehyde for 20 min, and permeabilized with 0.1 % Triton X-100 for 15 min. After 1 h of blocking with 1 % bovine serum albumin (BSA) in PBS, cells were incubated with antibodies against human CD163 and CD206 (1:200) overnight at 4 °C, followed by Alexa Fluor™ conjugate secondary antibody (1:500) for 1 h at room temperature. Nuclei were counterstained with DAPI. Images for 5 fields of view per well were collected by a fluorescent microscope (DMi8, Leica Microsystems, Deerfield, IL) at 20x magnification.

Colony formation assay

For the colony formation assay, cancer cells were seeded (500 cells/well) in 6-well plates and co-cultured with TAMs (167 cells/well) [29]. Cells were treated with 2 μM ticagrelor, 2 μM BMS-1, 2 μM cemiplimab, 2 μM ticagrelor and 2 μM BMS-1, 2 μM ticagrelor and 2 μM cemiplimab. After 14 days, cells were fixed with 4 % paraformaldehyde and stained with 0.5 % crystal violet. Images of wells were photographed with a 5x objective using a light microscope (Leica Microsystems). Colonies were counted via ImageJ software (Bethesda, Maryland).

Wound healing assay

Cancer cells were seeded in a wound-healing insert with TAMs at a cell number ratio of 3:1. Cell confluency was obtained the next day. After the insert was removed, a wound was made, and cells were exposed to 2 μM ticagrelor, 2 μM BMS-1, 2 μM cemiplimab, 2 μM ticagrelor and 2 μM BMS-1, or 2 μM ticagrelor and 2 μM cemiplimab in serum-free medium. Migrating cells at the wound front were photographed by a light microscope at 4x magnification after 48 h. We measured the surface area of the wound with ImageJ software.

Transwell growth assay

Cell growth assays were performed using 12-well Transwells (0.4 μm pore size, Corning, Glendale, Arizona). Cancer cells were seeded at 0.25 × 105/well in 1.5 mL culture medium in wells, while 1 × 105 TAMs in 500 μL were placed in the upper inserts [30]. The TAMs were treated with 2 μM ticagrelor, 2 μM BMS-1, 2 μM cemiplimab, 2 μM ticagrelor and 2 μM BMS-1, or 2 μM ticagrelor and 2 μM cemiplimab. After 72 h of incubation, the cells in the lower chamber were fixed in 4 % paraformaldehyde and stained with 0.5 % crystal violet. Stains were dissolved in 30 % acetic acid, and absorbance was measured at 560 nm with a plate reader (SpectraMax M5, Molecular Devices, San Jose, CA).

Transwell migration assay

Cell migration assays were performed using 24-well Transwells (8 μm pore size, Corning). Cancer cells were seeded 1 × 105/well in 100 μL serum-free medium in the upper inserts, while 1 × 105 TAMs in 600 μL were placed in the lower wells. The TAMs were treated with 2 μM ticagrelor, 2 μM BMS-1, 2 μM cemiplimab, 2 μM ticagrelor and 2 μM BMS-1, or 2 μM ticagrelor and 2 μM cemiplimab. After 48 h of incubation, the cancer cells remaining in the upper chamber were removed using cotton swabs. Cancer cells on the lower surface of the membrane were fixed in 4 % paraformaldehyde and stained with 0.5 % crystal violet in a clean well plate. A light microscope was used to capture images of 5 fields of view per well at 4 × magnification. The migrated cells were counted using ImageJ software. The supernatant of TAMs induced by HPCCs was stored at −20 °C for TGF-β1 analysis. For the recovery assay, a Transwell migration assay was performed for HPCCs as described above. One group received exogenous TGF-β1 (5 ng/mL), [31,32] which was added to the co-treatment of ticagrelor and cemiplimab.

Phagocytosis assay

THP-1 cells were seeded in 48-well plates (3 ×104 cells/well) and differentiated into TAMs. TAMs were incubated with CellTracker Red CMTPX dye (C34552). Cancer cells were incubated with CellTracker Green CMFDA dye (C7025) and added to the TAMs at a concentration of 30,000 cells per well. Simultaneously, 2 μM ticagrelor, 2 μM BMS-1, 2 μM cemiplimab, 2 μM ticagrelor and 2 μM BMS-1, or 2 μM ticagrelor and 2 μM cemiplimab were added to the co-culture, followed by 30-minute incubation at 37 °C to allow cancer cells to reach the macrophage monolayer [24]. TGF-β1 analysis

To measure TGF-β1 in the plasma samples from the healthy donors and cancer patients, plasma samples were diluted 60 times and sent to Eve Technologies Corporation (Calgary, Alberta, Canada) to perform Multi-Species TGF-β 3-Plex Discovery Assay®. We used an ELISA kit (BMS249–4) to determine the TGF-β1 level in the supernatant of HPCC-induced TAMs from the Transwell migration assay. The assay was performed according to the manufacturer’s instructions, and absorption was measured using a plate reader. Briefly, the diluted samples (1:10 with assay buffer) were treated with 1 N HCl for 1 h and then neutralized with 1 N NaOH. The TGF-β1 standard curve was prepared in wells over 31–2000 pg/mL. The samples were added to the appropriate wells and incubated for 2 h. After washing, the wells were incubated with biotin-conjugate for 1 h and then Streptavidin-HRP for 1 h. TMB (tetramethyl-benzidine) substrate solution was added and incubated for 30 min in the dark. Phosphoric acid (1 M) was used to stop the reaction, and the absorbance was read at 450 nm.

Western blot

The H-TAM cells were treated with 2 μM ticagrelor, 2 μM cemiplimab, or 2 μM ticagrelor and 2 μM cemiplimab for 48 h. The supernatant of H-TAMs was collected and filtered to remove cell debris as H-TAM-conditioned media. HPCCs were plated in a 6-well plate and left to grow for 48 h. HPCCs were cultured with 20 % H-TAM-conditioned media in HPCC serum-free media for 48 h. As a control, an unconditioned HPCC serum-free medium was used. After 48 h, HPCCs were lysed in RIPA buffer, including a protease inhibitor cocktail. Fifty micrograms of protein from cell lysates were loaded per lane. The proteins were separated by SDS-PAGE gels and transferred to PVDF membranes. After blocking with 5 % BSA in TBS (Tris Buffered Saline), the membranes were incubated with the primary antibody (1:1000) at 4°C overnight. The membranes were washed and incubated with IRDye-conjugated secondary antibody (1:10,000) for 1 h at room temperature in the dark. Proteins were detected using an imaging system (Odyssey DLx, Licor Biosciences). The intensity of bands was quantified using Image Studio software (version 5.2, Licor Biosciences). Pancreatic tissue samples from healthy donors and cancer patients were lysed in RIPA buffer, including a protease inhibitor cocktail, and analyzed using western blotting as described above.

Statistics analysis

Results are expressed as mean values ± standard error of each group’s mean (S.E.M.). An unpaired t-test with Welch’s correction was performed to calculate the statistical significance between the two groups. Statistical comparison between several groups was performed with a one-way analysis of variance (ANOVA), followed by Tukey’s multiple comparison procedure, which followed the significance of the ANOVA. For each independent experiment, untreated cancer cells or untreated co-culture were considered 100 % (control), and each treatment was normalized to the control. Robust and unbiased results were determined by collecting and analyzing samples with a blinded experimenter. Each treatment group included three or more experiments (n ≥ 3) based on power calculations and effect sizes used in the calculations. P < 0.05 was considered to be significant.

Results

TAMs in the PDAC TME express PD-1

We analyzed pancreatic tissues from healthy donors and cancer patients to determine the expression of PD-1 and PD-L1 in the PDAC TME. As expected, the level of PD-L1 in pancreatic tumors was higher than in the healthy pancreas (Fig. 1A) [33]. To determine whether PD-1 was expressed in the TAMs, we labeled tissues from pancreatic patients and healthy donors with anti-PD-1 and anti-CD163 (a TAM marker). The level of PD-1 in pancreatic tumors was higher than in the healthy pancreas. CD163 was only detected in the TME but not in the healthy pancreas (Fig. 1B). Furthermore, PD-1 was co-localized with CD163 (Fig. 1B), suggesting that PD-1 is expressed in TAMs. PD-1 in TAMs and PD-L1 in pancreatic tumors indicate a communication pathway between TAMs and cancer cells via the PD-1/PD-L1 interaction in the TME of pancreatic cancer patients.

The pancreatic tissue slides from healthy and pancreatic cancer patients were immunostained with (A) anti-PD-L1 and (B) anti-PD-1 and anti-CD163 (a marker for TAMs). In human pancreatic cancer specimens, PD-L1 was expressed, and PD-1 was co-localized with CD163. The images are representative of three independent experiments. Scale bar: 20μm. 40 × magnification.

TAMs were differentiated from THP-1 monocytes in vitro.

To study the interactions between pancreatic cancer cells and TAMs, PDAC-conditioned TAMs were differentiated in vitro. We used conditioned media from the PDAC cell lines PANC-1 (P-TAMs) or BxPC-3 (B-TAMs). Differentiation was first determined by examining changes in cell morphology under an optical microscope. Exposure to either conditioned medium was able to induce morphological modifications of macrophages. (Fig. 2A). As shown in Fig. 2A, TAMs (middle and right panel) show a polygonal shape with extended pseudopodia, as compared with a round shape noted in macrophages (left). No difference was noted between P-TAMs and B-TAMs (data not shown). Next, we used flow cytometry and immunofluorescence to determine the expression of the TAM markers, CD206 and CD163 (Fig. 2B and C). We analyzed both P-TAMs and B-TAMs. Flow cytometry showed that both P-TAMs and B-TAMs expressed CD206 and CD163 (Fig. 2B). The immunofluorescence images confirmed the expression of CD206 and CD163 in TAMs (Fig. 2C). Altogether, our results showed that PDAC-conditioned TAMs were differentiated in vitro.

PD-1, PD-L1, and P2Y12 are expressed in TAMs and PDAC cells

To determine if the selected cancer cell lines (PANC-1 and BxPC-3) express PD-L1 and the TAMs express PD-1, we analyzed these receptors using flow cytometry. Both P-TAMs and B-TAMs expressed PD-1 (~99 %, Fig. 3A), and both PANC-1 and BxPC-3 expressed PD-L1 (~99 %, Fig. 3B). These data are in line with what we observed in pancreatic cancer patients (Fig. 1). Next, we measured P2Y12 expression in TAMs and cancer cells using immunofluorescent microscopy and western blotting, respectively. TAMs expressed the P2Y12 receptor, as shown by immunofluorescence (Fig. 3C). P2Y12 expression could be detected in PANC-1 and BxPC-3 using western blot analysis (Fig. 3D&E).

Co-treatment of ticagrelor and cemiplimab increases TAMs’ phagocytic ability

Cancer cell exposure impairs the phagocytic ability of TAMs. Therefore, we assessed whether P2Y12 antagonism or PD-1/PD-L1 blockers (alone and in combination) could restore the phagocytic ability of TAMs. To achieve this aim, we checked the phagocytosis of cancer cells by TAMs in a co-culture. We used the P2Y12 receptor antagonist, ticagrelor, the PD-1/PD-L1 blocker, BMS-1, and the anti-PD-1 antibody, cemiplimab. A concentration of 2 μM for all these drugs was selected based on previous in vitro studies that investigated the effects of cemiplimab [34] or BMS-1 [35] on peripheral blood mononuclear cells (PBMCs), as well as the effects of ticagrelor on macrophages [24] and viability assays (Fig. S1). TAMs were labeled in red, and cancer cells in green. The overlay of both green and red staining within the same cell was used to identify TAMs that had phagocytosed cancer cells [24]. As shown in Fig. 4A&C, P-TAMs or B-TAMs could phagocytose cancer cells. The percentage of cancer cells that P-TAMs phagocytosed did not significantly change when cells were treated with ticagrelor, BMS-1, or cemiplimabalone alone (Fig. 4B). Co-treatment of ticagrelor and BMS-1 or ticagrelor and cemiplimab also did not alter P-TAM phagocytic abilities as compared with the untreated co-culture (Fig. 4B). However, the co-treatment of ticagrelor and cemiplimab significantly increased the percentage of cancer cells phagocytosed by P-TAMs compared to cemiplimab alone (Fig. 4B). Similarly, B-TAMs’ phagocytosis did not change when cells were treated with ticagrelor, BMS-1, or cemiplimab alone (Fig. 4D). Co-treatment of ticagrelor and BMS-1 also did not alter B-TAM phagocytic abilities as compared with the untreated co-culture (Fig. 4D). However, the co-treatment of ticagrelor and cemiplimab significantly increased the percentage of cancer cells phagocytosed by B-TAMs compared to the untreated co-culture and cemiplimab alone (Fig. 4D). These results indicate that the co-treatment of ticagrelor and cemiplimab enhances the phagocytic ability of both P- and B-TAMs.

To assess cell proliferation, we performed a colony-forming assay (Fig. 5A&B). The co-culture of TAM with cancer cells at a ratio of 3:1 resulted in an increased number of colonies for PANC-1 compared to when cancer cells were cultured alone (Fig. 5C). The P-TAM-induced proliferation of PANC-1 was decreased by single treatment with BMS-1 or cemiplimab, but not ticagrelor, as compared with untreated co-culture (Fig. 5C). Interestingly, the co-treatment of ticagrelor and BMS-1 significantly reduced TAM-induced proliferation compared to the untreated co-culture and the single treatment with ticagrelor (Fig. 5C). However, this reduction was not observed when compared to BMS-1 alone. Similarly, the co-treatment of ticagrelor and cemiplimab significantly decreased TAM-induced proliferation when compared to the untreated co-culture and the single treatment with ticagrelor, but not in comparison to cemiplimab alone (Fig. 5C). In addition, TAM-cancer cell co-culture (at a ratio of 3:1) resulted in an increased colony number for BxPC-3 compared to the cancer cells alone (Fig. 5D). The B-TAM-induced proliferation of BxPC-3 was decreased by single treatment with BMS-1 or cemiplimab but not affected by ticagrelor when compared to the untreated co-culture (Fig. 5D). Interestingly, the co-treatments of ticagrelor with either BMS-1 or cemiplimab significantly decreased TAM-induced proliferation compared to the untreated co-culture and the single treatment with ticagrelor (Fig. 5D).

To assess cancer cell migration, we conducted a wound-healing assay (Fig. 6A&B). When cancer cells were co-cultured with TAMs at a ratio of 3:1, we noted a faster closure of the wound compared to the cancer cells cultured alone (Fig. 6C&D). Similar observations were noted for PANC-1 and BxPC-3 cell lines (Fig. 6C&D). When assessing the effect of various treatments, we found that the P-TAM-induced migration of PANC-1 cells was significantly reduced with single treatments of either ticagrelor or BMS-1, but not with cemiplimab (Fig. 6C). Co-treatment with ticagrelor and cemiplimab significantly decreased wound healing closure induced by P-TAMs, whereas co-treatment with ticagrelor and BMS-1 did not have this effect (Fig. 6C). However, there was no significant difference in wound closure between the co-treatment of ticagrelor and cemiplimab compared to their individual treatments (Fig. 6C). On the other hand, B-TAM-induced migration of BxPC-3 was attenuated by all the treatments when applied alone (Fig. 6D). Co-treatment with ticagrelor and cemiplimab or ticagrelor and BMS-1 also significantly decreased wound healing closure induced by B-TAMs. However, the co-treatment of ticagrelor and cemiplimab or ticagrelor and BMS-1 did not exhibit a further decrease compared to the single treatments (Fig. 6D). Taken together, our results show that blocking the P2Y12 and the PD-1/PD-L1 axis using ticagrelor and cemiplimab is the most effective strategy for preventing TAM-induced proliferation and wound closure of cancer cells.

Next, we investigated whether the P2Y12 antagonist and PD-1/PD-L1 blockers can inhibit cancer cell proliferation and migration without direct cell-cell contact between TAMs and cancer cells. To achieve this, we used a transwell system where cancer cells were cultured in the bottom chamber and TAMs in the upper chamber. This system prevents physical contact between the cell types but allows for the free diffusion of soluble molecules (Fig. 7A). The proliferation of cancer cells in the bottom chamber was measured 72 h after co-culturing with TAM. The proliferation of cancer cells increased when co-cultured with P-TAMs compared to when they were cultured alone (Fig. 7B). While the single treatment with cemiplimab reduced the proliferation induced by P-TAMs, neither ticagrelor nor BMS-1 alone had an effect (Fig. 7B). However, the co-treatment of ticagrelor and BMS-1 or ticagrelor and cemiplimab, significantly decreased P-TAM-induced proliferation. Notably, the co-treatment of ticagrelor and cemiplimab inhibited the growth of PANC-1 cells compared to ticagrelor alone (Fig. 7B). In contrast, while ticagrelor alone reduced B-TAM-induced proliferation of BxPC-3 cells, neither BMS-1 nor cemiplimab had a significant effect when used alone (Fig. 7C). The co-treatments of ticagrelor with either BMS-1 or cemiplimab decreased B-TAM-induced proliferation, with the co-treatment of ticagrelor and cemiplimab showing a greater inhibitory effect on BxPC-3 growth compared to cemiplimab alone (Fig. 7C).

Next, we aimed to determine whether these treatments alter cancer cell migration without direct cell-to-cell contact. The cells were co-cultured in a transwell system where the membrane allowed free diffusion of soluble molecules and transmigration of cancer cells (Fig. 8A). The TAMs (in bottom chamber) were treated with 2 μM ticagrelor, 2 μM BMS-1, 2 μM cemiplimab, 2 μM ticagrelor and 2 μM BMS-1, or 2 μM ticagrelor and 2 μM cemiplimab. Similarly, the B-TAM-induced migration of BxPC-3 cells was not decreased by single treatments (Fig. 8E). These findings also suggest that co-treatments could alter the secretion profile of TAMs.

Co-treatment of ticagrelor and cemiplimab synergistically increases TAM’s phagocytic ability and decreases the migration of patient-derived human pancreatic cancer cells

Next, we investigated whether the treatments produced similar results in a cell line derived from human pancreatic cancer tissue, human pancreatic cancer cells (HPCCs). For these assays, TAMs were differentiated using media collected from HPCCs (H-TAMs). To verify whether THP-1 was successfully differentiated into H-TAMs, we first measured the expression of specific TAM surface markers, such as CD206, CD163, and PD-1, using flow cytometry. Our data shows that H-TAMs express CD206, CD163, and PD-1 (Fig. 9A). To further characterize these cells, we analyzed Arg1, an intracellular marker for TAMs [36], and found that H-TAMs also express Arg1 (Fig. 9A). Since TAMs in patient tumor tissues were double-positive for CD163 and PD-1 (Fig. 1B), we next determined whether H-TAMs share this phenotype. We observed that H-TAMs were CD163+PD-1+ (Fig. 9A), consistent with the CD163+PD-1+ TAMs observed in patient tissues. The co-treatment of ticagrelor and cemiplimab at the concentration of 2 μM was able to synergistically increase TAM phagocytic ability (Fig. 4) while decreasing TAM-induced proliferation (Figs. 5 and 7) and migration (Figs. 6 and 8). We first analyzed the phagocytosis of cancer cells by H-TAMs in a co-culture. In this setup, TAMs were labeled red and cancer cells green. The overlay of both green and red staining within the same cell was used to identify TAMs that had phagocytosed cancer cells [37]. As shown in Fig. 9C, both ticagrelor and cemiplimab significantly increased the phagocytic ability of TAMs when used as single treatments. However, the co-treatment of ticagrelor and cemiplimab exhibited a synergistic effect compared to the single treatments and the untreated co-culture (Fig. 9C).

TGF-β1/Smad2 contributes to the indirect interaction between TAMs and cancer cells

TGF-β is a key molecule in TAM-cancer cells communication, as it is not only secreted by TAMs to support cancer growth [38] but it is also secreted by tumor cells to recruit macrophages into the TME and polarize macrophages into the TAM phenotype [39]. Among the three types of TGF-β, we selected TGF-β1 because it is one of the TGF-β isoforms most frequently secreted by tumor cells [40]. First, we analyzed TGF-β1 in plasma samples from healthy donors and cancer patients (Fig. 10A). As expected, the level of TGF-β1 was higher in cancer patients’ plasma compared to that in healthy donors. The TGF-β signaling pathway is mediated by the phosphorylation of Smad2 [40]. To determine whether TGF-β signaling pathway was activated, we analyzed Smad2 phosphorylation using western blot (Fig. 10B). The data showed that the level of the phosphorylated Smad2 was significantly higher in pancreatic cancer tissues compared to that in the healthy pancreas (Fig. 10C), suggesting that TGF-β1/Smad2 signaling pathway is activated in pancreatic cancer. Based on these observations, we determined whether the combination of ticagrelor and cemiplimab act synergistically on the TAM-cancer cell co-culture by altering the TGF-β1/Smad2 signaling pathway in vitro. First, we measured the levels of TGF-β1 in the H-TAM supernatant collected from the transwell co-culture system (Fig. 9D) by performing an ELISA assay. The results showed that TGF-β1 levels were increased in the media from co-cultured H-TAMs with HPCCs compared to those from HPCCs alone (Fig. 11A). We did not observe any changes when ticagrelor or cemiplimab were added as a single treatment (Fig. 11A). However, the co-treatment of ticagrelor and cemiplimab significantly decreased the level of TGF-β1 in the supernatant collected from the TAM-cancer cell co-culture (Fig. 11A). To further explore immunosuppressive cytokines specific to TAMs, we checked IL-10 [13] in the H-TAM supernatant collected from the transwell assay. However, no significant differences were observed among the groups, suggesting that IL-10 may not contribute to the inhibitory effects of the co-treatment (Fig. S3). Next, we analyzed whether the TGF-β1 signaling pathway was activated using the western blot assay. HPCCs were exposed to conditioned media from H-TAMs for 48 h. We found that exposure to H-TAM-conditioned media increased the level of phosphorylated Smad2 in HPCCs compared to untreated HPCCs (Fig. 11C). The H-TAM-conditioned media treated with ticagrelor or cemiplimab alone did not change the level of phosphorylated Smad2 in HPCCs compared to the untreated co-culture (Fig. 11C). However, the co-treatment with ticagrelor and cemiplimab significantly reduced the level of phosphorylated Smad2 in HPCCs compared to the untreated co-culture and the single treatment with ticagrelor, although no significant change was noted with cemiplimab alone (Fig. 11C).

Exogenous TGF-β1 could reverse the effects of the co-treatments

To verify whether TGF-β1 played a role in the TAM-cancer cell crosstalk, we added exogenous TGF-β1 (5 ng/mL) to H-TAMs in the transwell migration assay. The inhibitory effect of the co-treatment of ticagrelor and cemiplimab on TAM-induced migration of cancer cells was reversed by TGF-β1 (Fig. 12). Taken together, our data suggest that the co-treatment of ticagrelor and cemiplimab on TAMs inhibits the release of TGF-β1 and thereby decreasing phosphorylation of Smad2 in cancer cells.

Discussion

PDAC accounts for 90 % of pancreatic malignancies and is the third primary cause of cancer-related mortality globally [41]. Existing treatment strategies are limited, making it compelling to investigate new options. Tumor cells evade immunosurveillance by suppressing the immune response. Indeed, the induction of an antitumor immune response has proven to be successful in various advanced-stage cancers, including non-small-cell lung cancer, melanoma, and renal-cell cancer [8]. However, immunotherapy alone has shown limited efficacy in PDAC [8]. Therefore, co-treatments may be more effective in cancer treatment when different pathways are targeted simultaneously.

We have identified PD-1/PD-L1 and ADP/P2Y12 axes that are activated in the TME and contribute to PDAC proliferation and migration. The co-treatment blocking PD-1/PD-L1 and ADP/P2Y12 targets crosstalk between the immune system and cancer cells while targeting single cells at the same time. To investigate whether blocking both pathways affects tumor growth, we used an in vitro TAM-pancreatic cancer cell co-culture as a model of the TME. Our data show that the PD-1 blocker, cemiplimab, combined with the P2Y12 blocker, ticagrelor, increases TAM phagocytic abilities and decreases TAM-induced growth and migration of cancer cells. The co-treatment decreased TGF-β1 release by TAMs and Smad2 activation in cancer cells. Our study provides a promising strategy that can enhance the efficacy of immunotherapy.

The recruitment of TAMs in the TME is a key step for cancer growth. TAMs support cancer growth and contribute to immune suppression through direct contact and the secretion of cytokines [3]. Both the depletion of TAMs and the re-education of TAMs from immunosuppressive into immunostimulatory phenotype can be valuable options. Hence, understanding how TAMs communicate with cancer cells can provide essential information to identify new drug therapies. We and other groups [12] identified TAMs in the PDAC tissues from patients’ pancreas specimens. While previous studies have reported that TAMs express PD-1 in colon cancer tissue [16] and T cell lymphoma [18,42], we have now shown that TAMs in pancreatic tumors express PD-1. Interestingly, no PD-1 expression has been identified in circulating monocytes or splenic macrophages [18]. Therefore, PD-1 expression appears to be closely related to the TAM phenotype. On the other hand, the aberrant expression of PD-L1 is exploited by tumor cells to evade immune monitoring [43], which we also show. These findings indicate that the PD-1/PD-L1 axis plays a significant role in the crosstalk between TAMs and cancer cells in pancreatic cancer. This finding has extended our understanding of PD-1 expression in T cells and identified TAMs as a novel target for counteracting anti-PD-1 resistance.

P2Y12 is highly expressed in platelets, T lymphocytes, and dendritic cells. Macrophages also express P2Y12, with higher levels found in M2 macrophages than in M1 macrophages [44]. We have shown that the P2Y12 receptor is expressed in TAMs. Blocking this receptor could increase TAM phagocytic abilities and decrease TAM-induced migration of pancreatic cancer cells. Moreover, P2Y12 is also present in pancreatic cancer cells. Our work (Fig. S2) and the work of others have shown that blocking P2Y12 using ticagrelor or clopidogrel alone reduced cancer cell migration in vitro or metastasis in vivo [22]. Hence, targeting P2Y12 could target pancreatic cancer cells directly. Ticagrelor could enhance the efficiency of chemotherapeutic treatment in a mouse model of pancreatic cancer [20,22]. In another study, the bioengineered platelets (PTNPs) with surface-anchored ticagrelor-loaded gelatin nanoparticles could enhance anti-PD-L1 therapy response by releasing ticagrelor in TME and inhibiting platelet-facilitated immunosuppression [45]. In the TICONC oncology study, patients receiving ticagrelor showed reduced platelet aggregation and activation in metastatic cancer compared with untreated patients [46]. No clinical data were collected to show whether ticagrelor could diminish tumor growth. However, ticagrelor has the potential to decrease the risk of cancer-associated thrombosis. Based on these data, we selected ticagrelor as a co-adjuvant treatment option in PDAC [47].

Given that PD-1 and P2Y12 are expressed in TAMs, blocking PD-1/PD-L1 and P2Y12 might have an effect on the antitumor immunity of TAMs for pancreatic cancer cells. To block PD-1/PD-L1, we used two drugs such as cemiplimab [25] and BMS-1 [26]. Cemiplimab is a humanized IgG4 monoclonal antibody intended to target PD-1 and approved by the FDA to treat several cancers, including cutaneous squamous cell carcinoma, non-small cell lung cancer, and basal cell carcinoma [25]. A Phase II study is currently underway to investigate the combination of plerixafor and cemiplimab in metastatic pancreatic cancer (NCT04177810). BMS-1 is a structure-based small-molecule inhibitor that binds to human PD-L1 and disrupts the interaction between PD-1 and PD-L1 by targeting a relatively flat and highly hydrophobic interaction surface [26]. Previous studies show that BMS-derived compounds alleviate the PD-L1-induced exhaustion of T cells and inhibit proliferation in PD-L1-positive cells [26,48]. Cemiplimab is more effective than BMS-1 when combined with ticagrelor in blocking cancer cells and TAM crosstalk, as shown in the phagocytosis and Transwell proliferation assay. It could be that inhibiting PD-1 on TAMs is more effective than the targeting PD-1/PD-L1 interaction by binding to PD-L1. Notably, our experiments are the first to test cemiplimab on TAMs, since this drug has not been extensively investigated in vitro. In fact, most of the previous studies have been performed in vivo [49] or in clinical settings [25]. On the other hand, BMS-1 is a research compound that has not yet been used in clinical settings. BMS-1 potentially binds to the PD-L1 on cancer cells as well and possibly involves off-target effects as a small-molecule inhibitor [50,51].

Co-treatments can be more effective as a therapeutic strategy against cancer. The co-treatment of ticagrelor and cemiplimab increases the phagocytic ability of TAMs compared to cemiplimab or ticagrelor alone. In other studies, ticagrelor could increase macrophages’ ability to phagocytose cancer cells in vitro [24]. Previous in vivo studies have shown that PD-1− TAMs phagocytosed tumor cells at a higher rate than PD-1 + TAMs [18]. Our data suggests that co-treatment could reprogram macrophages from TAMs to an M1-like phagocytic phenotype, thereby increasing their ability to phagocytose cancer cells. Investigating changes in surface receptor expression and cytokine secretion following the co-treatment with cemiplimab and ticagrelor can elucidate this mechanism in future studies. In the presence of TAMs, the growth and migration of cancer cells increase for both PANC-1 and BxPC-3. This is in line with the study by Wei et al. on colorectal cancer cell-induced TAMs [29]. Blocking PD-1/PD-L1, but not P2Y12, could decrease the TAM-induced colony formation of both PANC-1 and BxPC-3. However, TAM-induced wound healing is decreased by blocking P2Y12 and PD-1/PD-L1 alone or in combination. Interestingly, blocking P2Y12 and PD-1/PD-L1 decreases TAM-induced proliferation and migration even when cancer cells and TAMs are not directly exposed to each other. Therefore, the co-treatment may directly alter cell secretion rather than changing receptor expression.

Blocking PD-1/PD-L1 by BMS-1 or cemiplimab had a higher impact on BxPC-3 cells than PANC-1 cells. This could be due to their different genotypes and phenotypes associated with the stage of tumor development. For instance, BxPC-3 cells possess greater epithelial characteristics, while PANC-1 displays more mesenchymal and metastatic traits [52]. BxPC-3 with wild-type KRAS has a slow proliferation rate and is sensitive to gemcitabine, while PANC-1 with mutant KRAS and a fast proliferation rate is resistant to gemcitabine [52]. Among the treatment groups, the co-treatment of ticagrelor and cemiplimab shows the most consistent and highest efficiency for both PANC-1 and BxPC-3 in all the assays. This suggests that PD-1 plays a more important role in the antitumor effects of TAMs rather than PD-L1. Overall, our research shows for the first time that blocking PD-1/PD-L1 and P2Y12 on TAM can enhance their antitumor activities. These data provide a solid foundation for testing this co-treatment in an in vivo setting.

TGF-β1 is an anti-inflammatory cytokine secreted by immune cells, tumor cells, and stromal cells, and it is abundant in TME. Both TAMs and cancer cells secrete TGF-β to maintain immunosuppression. Indeed, we observed an increase in TGF-β in the plasma of pancreatic cancer patients. Our data show that co-treatment of ticagrelor and cemiplimab decreases TGF-β1 release by TAMs as compared with single treatments. Cemiplimab alone showed a decreasing trend, but the co-treatment was significantly effective. TGF-β1 binds to the TGF-β receptor, leading to the phosphorylation of Smad2/3 that binds to Smad4. In previous investigations, Smad4-negative PDAC exhibited elevated phospho-Smad2 levels, and phospho-Smad2 levels have been related to aggressive cancers with a worse prognosis [53]. Indeed, our data show that Smad2 is highly phosphorylated in the PDAC of patients. When we exposed pancreatic cancer cells to TAM-conditioned media, we observed increased Smad2. However, when the TAMs were co-treated with ticagrelor and cemiplimab, Smad2 phosphorylation in cancer cells was significantly reduced. Adding exogenous TGF-β1 could reverse the inhibitory effect of the co-treatment of ticagrelor and cemiplimab on TAM-induced migration of cancer cells. These data strongly suggest that the co-treatment alters TAM-cancer cell crosstalk through TGF-β1/Smad2 signaling. Since we specifically target TAMs, we can infer that co-treatment decreases cancer growth by reducing TAM secretion.

In the TME, other cells can be targeted by the co-treatment suggested in this paper. For instance, P2Y12 is expressed in platelets, and its activation is responsible for the secretion of immune mediators, including TGF-β, by platelets. As a result, we can speculate that the co-treatment could have further effects on altering the TME milieu. Moreover, T lymphocytes are also present in the TME, where PD-L1 is expressed on platelets and cancer cells, maintaining an immunosuppressive state. Once the PD-1/PD/L1 is blocked, T cells may get activated. Overall, there is promise that the co-treatment could shift the immune state of the TME, targeting multiple cells. The ability of the co-treatment to reduce tumor growth, metastasis, and overall modulation of TME need to be tested in an animal model of pancreatic cancer.

Conclusion

TAMs play roles in almost every step of tumor metastasis, which includes promoting epithelial-mesenchymal transition (EMT) in tumor cells, providing chemotactic signals that guide tumor cells toward blood vessels, and preparing sites for tumor cells by establishing pre-metastatic niches [54,55]. Therefore, our treatment strategy targeting TAMs could be important for metastatic PDAC, which occurs in around 50 % of diagnosed patients [2].

We have identified PD-1/PD-L1 immunotherapy and ticagrelor as a promising combination strategy that can inhibit TAM-induced pancreatic cancer cell growth and migration via the TGF-β1/Smad2 pathway. Our study provides new ideas for enhancing the efficacy of PD-1/PD-L1 immunotherapy that are ready to be tested in vivo.
