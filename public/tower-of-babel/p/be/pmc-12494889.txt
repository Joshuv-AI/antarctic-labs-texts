Impact of the October 7 Gaza war on post-traumatic stress symptoms and quality of life in Palestinian nursing
Authors: Mohammad Qtait, Nesreen Alqaissi, Fuad Farajalla, Yousef Jaradat, Khalaf Awwad
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractThe October 7, 2023, Gaza War imposed profound psychological and occupational burdens on Palestinian nurses, including those in the West Bank who were indirectly affected by the conflict. This study assessed the prevalence of post-traumatic stress symptoms (PTSS) and quality of life (QoL) outcomes among nurses working in the West Bank. A descriptive cross-sectional study was conducted from January to March 2025 with 350 registered nurses recruited through stratified random sampling. Data were collected using the Impact of Event Scale–Revised (IES-R) to measure PTSS and the World Health Organization Quality of Life–BREF (WHOQOL-BREF) to assess QoL. Statistical analyses included descriptive statistics, t-tests, and one-way ANOVA, with standardized effect sizes (Cohen’s d for t-tests and eta-squared, η², for ANOVA) reported to indicate practical significance. Nurses reported high PTSS across intrusion (mean = 1.94), avoidance (mean = 1.79), and hyperarousal (mean = 1.78) subscales. The total PTSS score (mean = 5.51 out of a possible 12) indicated a substantial symptom burden. Nearly half (49.2%) experienced violence at Israeli checkpoints, which was associated with significantly higher PTSS (mean = 6.09 vs. 4.90; p < .001; Cohen’s d = 0.49) and lower QoL (mean = 3.13 vs. 3.34; p = .001; Cohen’s d = 0.36). QoL scores were poorest in physical health (55.25%), psychological well-being (56.5%), and environmental conditions (48.25%), but higher in social relationships (63.9%). The Gaza War has had a marked psychological impact on nurses in the West Bank, particularly for those exposed to checkpoint violence. Introduction

Armed conflicts exert profound impacts on the mental health and well-being of affected populations, particularly those working in frontline healthcare services. The Gaza Strip has experienced recurrent cycles of violence over the past decades, but the October 7, 2023, war represents a significant escalation in both the intensity and scope of destruction. Palestinian nurses working in Gaza during this period have been exposed to extreme levels of physical danger, psychological trauma, and moral distress. These conditions make them highly susceptible to post-traumatic stress symptoms and substantial declines in quality of life (QoL)1.

Post-traumatic stress disorder (PTSD) is a well-documented consequence of exposure to war-related trauma. It encompasses a constellation of symptoms, including intrusive thoughts, avoidance behaviors, hyperarousal, and negative alterations in mood and cognition2. Nurses in conflict zones are especially vulnerable to developing PTSD due to their direct exposure to life-threatening situations, the high volume of casualties they care for, and the emotional toll of treating injured civilians, including children and colleagues3. Studies from previous conflicts in Palestine, including the 2014 Gaza War, revealed that a substantial proportion of nurses reported high levels of PTSD symptoms months after the conflict had ended4. The situation following the October 7 war appears even more severe, with increased casualties, infrastructure collapse, and prolonged siege conditions further exacerbating the psychological burden on healthcare workers5.

Quality of life is a multidimensional construct that includes physical health, psychological well-being, social relationships, and environmental conditions6. The World Health Organization emphasizes the importance of QoL as an essential outcome in evaluating the impact of both health conditions and sociopolitical crises7. In Gaza, the destruction of healthcare infrastructure, shortages of medical supplies, displacement, electricity outages, and the loss of family members have critically undermined the living and working conditions of nurses8. In such environments, it becomes exceedingly difficult for nurses to maintain their own health while caring for others, leading to a deterioration in both personal and professional domains of QoL.

Palestinian nurses also face moral and ethical challenges during wartime, including making rapid triage decisions, treating severely injured patients with limited resources, and sometimes being unable to save lives due to systemic constraints9. These experiences can result in what is termed “moral injury,” a form of psychological distress distinct from PTSD that arises from perpetrating, witnessing, or failing to prevent actions that violate one’s moral beliefs10. Moral injury, when combined with PTSD symptoms, further complicates the psychological aftermath for nurses and impairs long-term recovery and functioning11.

Cultural and contextual factors also play a role in shaping how Palestinian nurses experience and report psychological symptoms. Social stigma surrounding mental health, fear of professional repercussions, and limited access to psychosocial support services often prevent healthcare workers in Gaza from seeking help12. Moreover, under-resourced healthcare systems in conflict settings are typically ill-equipped to provide adequate mental health support to their own staff. This contributes to a cycle in which untreated trauma among nurses leads to burnout, decreased job performance, and eventually, workforce attrition, which further strains already fragile healthcare systems13.

Despite these challenges, there is a scarcity of empirical studies that specifically assess the psychological and quality-of-life outcomes among nurses following the October 7 war. Most available data come from humanitarian reports and anecdotal evidence, underscoring a critical gap in the academic literature14. Given the central role that nurses play in sustaining healthcare delivery during crises, investigating the psychological toll of war on this group is essential for informing policy, resource allocation, and support interventions.

Healthcare professionals in conflict zones face significant psychological challenges, including heightened risk of post-traumatic stress disorder (PTSD), anxiety, and burnout13. Exposure to ongoing violence and instability disrupts not only their mental health but also impacts their professional functioning and quality of life (QoL) (6.7.8).

Also, the killing of healthcare workers and the shortages caused by the escape or forced displacement of many others have severely undermined the provision of care. According to WHO, the ongoing hostilities have pushed Gaza’s health system to the brink of collapse, with attacks on medical personnel, destruction of facilities, and the loss of skilled staff critically limiting healthcare delivery (World Health Organization, 2025). Similar reports have documented that the targeted killing and emigration of healthcare professionals during conflict create long-term gaps in service capacity, further exacerbating public health crises (Healthcare Workers Watch.).

The theoretical framework for this study integrates Conflict Exposure, PTSD Symptoms, and Quality of Life among healthcare workers, particularly nurses, in the West Bank. Conflict exposure acts as a chronic stressor that elevates PTSD symptoms, which mediate the relationship between exposure and quality of life outcomes. QoL here encompasses physical health, psychological well-being, social relationships, and environmental factors.

This framework draws on the Stress-Exposure Model and Transactional Model of Stress and Coping to explain how conflict-related stressors translate into psychological symptoms and subsequently impair QoL. Understanding these pathways is crucial for developing targeted interventions as in Fig. 1 .

This study aims to assess the impact of the October 7 Gaza War on post-traumatic stress symptoms and quality of life among Palestinian nurses. By exploring both mental health outcomes and subjective well-being in this unique context, the study contributes to a broader understanding of how war affects frontline healthcare workers and highlights the urgent need for systemic support structures to protect and promote their well-being during and after conflict periods.

Methodology

Study design

This study employed a descriptive cross-sectional design to assess the impact of the October 7, 2023, Gaza war on post-traumatic stress symptoms and quality of life (QoL) among Palestinian nurses. The research was conducted between January and March 2025 across multiple healthcare institutions in the Gaza Strip, including public and private hospitals as well as primary healthcare centers. This design was selected to capture the psychological and quality-of-life outcomes during the post-war recovery phase.

Sample and setting

A total of 350 registered nurses working in the West Bank were recruited using stratified random sampling to ensure representation across different hospital departments (e.g., emergency, intensive care, medical-surgical, and maternity wards). Eligibility criteria included being currently employed in a healthcare facility in the West Bank during and after the October 7 war, having at least six months of professional experience, and providing informed consent. Nurses who were on extended leave or outside the West Bank during the period of conflict were excluded.

Instruments

Data collection was conducted using a self-administered structured questionnaire comprising three sections:

The first impact of event scale-revised (IES-R)

To assess post-traumatic stress symptoms, the Impact of Event Scale-Revised (IES-R) was used. This instrument includes 22 items distributed across three subscales: intrusion, avoidance, and hyperarousal. Participants rated the frequency of each symptom experienced in the past seven days using a 5-point Likert scale, ranging from 0 (“not at all”) to 4 (“extremely”). The total score ranges from 0 to 88, with higher scores indicating greater PTSD symptom severity. The IES-R is aligned with DSM-IV diagnostic criteria and has demonstrated high internal consistency, with reported Cronbach’s alpha values between 0.79 and 0.9215.

The second part included the Arabic version of the World Health Organization Quality of Life-BREF (WHOQOL-BREF), a standardized instrument designed to assess QoL15. The WHOQOL-BREF is composed 26 items designed for measuring QoL of overall level (2 items) and four main domains, including environment (8 items), social interactions (3 items), psychological health (6 items), and physical health (7 items). Each item is scored on a 5-point Likert scale, ranging from 1 (very poor/very dissatisfied/ never/none) to 5 (very good/very satisfied/always/extremely). A single item addressing sex life within the social domain was omitted in the current study due to cultural sensitivity, making it a 25-item tool. According to the WHO guidelines, raw domain scores4–19 were standardized on a 0-100 scale. Standardization allows comparison and interpretation across domains16. To classify QoL, a 60% cutoff was used for all domain scores in the (0-100) scale. A converted score of ≥ 60% represented good quality of life, and scores < 60% represented poor quality of life17.

The third demographic and background information

The questionnaire also included a demographic section developed by the researchers to capture background variables potentially associated with psychological outcomes and quality of life. These included age, gender, marital status, years of nursing experience, department of work, place of residence, economic status, and exposure to violence at military checkpoints during or after the conflict. These variables were included to enable stratified analyses and to identify potential risk factors associated with poor mental health or quality of life outcomes.

Validity and reliability

The instruments used in this study demonstrated strong psychometric properties. Internal consistency was confirmed through Cronbach’s alpha coefficients: 0.89 for the WHOQOL-BREF and 0.921 for the IES-R. Construct validity was supported by statistically significant Pearson correlation coefficients between the subscales and total scores (p < .001), indicating that each instrument measured its intended construct reliably.

Ethical considerations

This study was conducted in accordance with the ethical principles outlined in the Declaration of Helsinki. Ethical approval was obtained from the Research and Ethics Committee at Palestine Polytechnic University (approval code: RCK/2024/35). Prior to participation, all nurses were provided with detailed information about the study’s purpose, procedures, potential risks, and benefits. Participation was entirely voluntary, and nurses were informed of their right to decline or withdraw at any stage without penalty. Written informed consent was obtained from all participants before data collection. To ensure confidentiality and anonymity, no identifying information was recorded, and all responses were stored securely with access limited to the research team.

Data collection procedure

Data collection was carried out through in-person distribution of questionnaires at participating healthcare facilities. After obtaining official approval from hospital administrations and ethical clearance from the relevant institutional review board, trained research assistants approached eligible nurses, explained the purpose of the study, and secured informed consent. Participation was voluntary, and confidentiality was strictly maintained. Questionnaires were completed anonymously and returned to sealed drop boxes located in nursing stations.

Data analysis

Quantitative data were coded and entered into SPSS version 26 for statistical analysis. Descriptive statistics, including means, standard deviations, and frequencies, were used to summarize demographic characteristics and scores on the IES-R and WHOQOL-BREF. Inferential statistics such as independent-samples t-tests, one-way ANOVA, and Pearson correlation coefficients were applied to examine associations between PTSD symptoms, quality of life, and demographic variables. A significance level of P < .05 was used for all statistical tests.

To enhance interpretability, standardized effect sizes were reported alongside p-values. Cohen’s d was calculated for t-tests, and eta-squared (η²) was computed for ANOVA tests, providing an indication of the magnitude and practical significance of observed difference.

The total PTSD score of 5.51 represents the combined mean of the three subscale scores (avoidance, intrusion, hyperarousal) on a 0–4 scale, yielding a possible total mean range of 0–12. Higher scores indicate greater symptom severity.

Discussion

The findings of this study reveal a significant psychological toll on Palestinian nurses following the October 7 Gaza War. The elevated levels of post-traumatic stress symptoms across all three subscales—avoidance, intrusion, and hyperarousal—underscore the intense psychological impact of operating in a conflict zone. These results are consistent with earlier studies in similar contexts. Nurses exposed to war-related trauma frequently experience PTSD symptoms due to their constant exposure to mass casualties, moral dilemmas, and personal risk4,17.

The data show high mean scores for PTSD symptoms, especially within the intrusion subscale (M = 1.94), suggesting that traumatic memories and reminders are pervasive and disruptive. Comparable findings were reported in a study of nurses during the Syrian conflict, where persistent intrusive recollections and emotional numbing were common8. Avoidance behaviors (M = 1.79) and hyperarousal symptoms (M = 1.78), such as irritability and sleep disturbances, further highlight the enduring nature of psychological trauma in this population. These findings are aligned with the symptom clusters outlined in DSM-5 for PTSD2 and affirm the utility of the IES-R tool in assessing trauma among healthcare professionals.

Notably, nearly half of the respondents (49.2%) reported exposure to violence at Israeli military checkpoints—either verbal, physical, or psychological. Those exposed demonstrated significantly higher PTSD scores (M = 6.09 vs. 4.90) and lower quality of life scores (M = 3.13 vs. 3.34), with statistically significant differences (p < .001 and p = .001, respectively). This reinforces the growing body of literature linking political violence and militarized environments with adverse psychological outcomes in Palestinian civilians and healthcare workers12,18,19. In particular, checkpoint violence has been shown to contribute to chronic stress and a sense of powerlessness, compounding the effects of trauma sustained in clinical settings20.

The findings regarding quality of life were also concerning. While participants rated their general QoL and self-satisfaction as “good,” domain-specific scores in physical health (55.25%), psychological well-being (56.5%), and environmental factors (48.25%) were all below the 60% threshold, classifying them as poor. These scores are consistent with prior research in conflict zones, where continuous exposure to insecurity and systemic collapse undermines nurses’ physical functioning, mental clarity, and working conditions5,6,21. Energy, sleep, and the opportunity for leisure—essential components of QoL—were rated particularly poorly, indicating fatigue and burnout among the nursing workforce.

Interestingly, the social relationships domain showed relatively high scores (63.9%), highlighting the importance of interpersonal and community support. This is in line with studies emphasizing the buffering effect of social support networks on trauma and stress10,22. Palestinian culture places a strong emphasis on familial and communal ties, which may have helped maintain emotional stability despite widespread adversity.

Another key finding relates to demographic influences. Gender and marital status were not significantly associated with PTSD or QoL, suggesting that the impact of conflict may cut across these variables, as reported in other war-affected nursing populations23. However, place of residence was significantly associated with QoL (p = .004), with city residents reporting better outcomes. This may reflect urban-rural disparities in access to resources, safety, and infrastructure—a pattern also observed in conflict-affected regions of Lebanon and Iraq24,25.

Despite the severe psychological burden identified, the self-reported rate of psychological illness was low (5.4%), likely indicating underreporting due to stigma. Previous studies among Palestinian nurses have shown a reluctance to seek mental health care due to cultural beliefs, fear of judgment, and limited access to services (12,27.28). This highlights the urgent need for systemic efforts to destigmatize mental health care within the healthcare community and to ensure accessible, confidential support services for affected staff.

In addition to psychological interventions, strengthening nursing leadership is critical for mitigating the adverse effects of war-related trauma. Leadership in nursing has been shown to play a pivotal role in fostering workplace resilience, supporting team cohesion, and promoting emotional well-being among healthcare staff during crises. A systematic review by28.emphasized that effective nurse leadership contributes not only to improved clinical outcomes but also to staff retention, morale, and the psychological safety of nurses working under extreme stress conditions29.

Overall, the findings emphasize the urgent need for targeted mental health interventions. These should include trauma-focused therapies, peer support networks, and staff resilience programs, ideally embedded within hospital systems. Policymakers must also address the broader socio-political determinants of health—including occupation, violence, and infrastructure collapse—which continue to threaten the well-being of nurses and undermine the stability of the Palestinian healthcare system. Further insights may be drawn from studies in similar conflict contexts, such as research on war-related trauma and PTSD prevalence among Syrian university students, which highlights comparable psychological patterns and the need for culturally adapted interventions in volatile environments.

Strength of the study

This study offers timely insights into the psychological toll of the Gaza War on Palestinian nurses, using a robust sample and validated tools (IES-R, WHOQOL-BREF). High internal consistency and ethical rigor enhance its reliability. By linking PTSD symptoms and reduced quality of life to conflict exposure, the findings provide critical evidence to inform mental health interventions and policy reforms in conflict settings.

Limitation of the study

This study is limited by its cross-sectional design, which restricts causal inferences. Self-reported data may introduce recall and social desirability biases. The low rate of reported psychological illness suggests possible underreporting due to stigma. Additionally, unmeasured variables and the focus on nurses in Gaza may limit the generalizability of findings to other populations and settings.

This study is limited by its cross-sectional design, which restricts causal inferences. Self-reported data may introduce recall and social desirability biases. The low rate of reported psychological illness suggests possible underreporting due to stigma. Additionally, unmeasured variables and the focus on nurses in Gaza may limit the generalizability of findings to other populations and settings. Furthermore, due to the volatile security situation in the region, full access to all eligible nurses was not always possible. Checkpoint restrictions, movement limitations, and safety concerns posed significant challenges to reaching the entire target population, which may have introduced selection bias.

Conclusion

The October 7 Gaza War had a profound psychological and occupational impact on Palestinian nurses, with high levels of post-traumatic stress symptoms (PTSD) and poor quality of life (QoL), particularly among those exposed to checkpoint violence. While social support was a protective factor, deficiencies in physical health, psychological well-being, and environmental conditions remain critical concerns.

To address these challenges, healthcare policymakers should implement structured, trauma-informed mental health services within hospitals, provide resilience and peer-support programs, and reduce occupational stress through improved working conditions. At the policy level, urgent reforms are needed to ensure nurses’ safety, address violence at checkpoints, and strengthen healthcare infrastructure in conflict zones.
