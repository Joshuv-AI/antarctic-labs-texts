Is There Any Dose of Benzodiazepines Safe for Delirium in the PICU? A Secondary Analysis of the PICU Up! Pilot Trial Dataset
Authors: Isabella Blackman, Rachel De Armas, Sofia Padilla, Sarah Goldberg, Samer Abu-Sultaneh, Michelle Piole, Megan Geno, Mary-Jeanne Manning, Kate Madden, Erik Madsen, Deanna Behrens, Danielle Maue, Kelechi Iheagwara, Colleen Mennie, Sukaina Furniturewala, Ronke Awojoodu, Elizabeth Colantuoni, Dale Needham, Sapna R. Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
IMPORTANCE:A better understanding of the relationship between sedative dose and PICU delirium is crucial for delirium prevention.OBJECTIVES:To identify risk factors for delirium in critically ill children and characterize the dose-response relationship between benzodiazepine administration and next-day delirium in the PICU.DESIGN, SETTING, AND PARTICIPANTS:A secondary analysis of 10,644 patient-days from prospectively collected data, including sedative and analgesic use and delirium screening from the multicenter, randomized PICU Up! pilot trial, including seven PICUs and 1,006 patients.MAIN OUTCOMES AND MEASURES:Delirium odds were evaluated using a multivariable logistic regression model adjusted for known delirium risk factors.RESULTS:Benzodiazepines, opioids, and dexmedetomidine were commonly used in the PICU. A higher previous-day benzodiazepine dose was associated with increased odds of delirium on a given day with an adjusted odds ratio (aOR) of 1.12 per midazolam milligram equivalent per kilogram (95% CI, 1.02–1.25; p = 0.020). The most pronounced increase in delirium risk occurred at the lowest doses, between 0 and 0.01 mg/kg (p = 0.010), with risk continuing to rise at higher doses. Previous-day opioid dose was not associated with changes in the odds of delirium (aOR, 1.00; 95% CI, 1.00–1.00; p = 0.892). Any previous-day dexmedetomidine administration was associated with increased odds of next-day delirium (aOR, 1.26; 95% CI, 1.04–1.52; p = 0.019). Additionally, same-day physical restraint use was associated with increased odds of delirium (aOR, 1.72; 95% CI, 1.27–2.31; p < 0.001).CONCLUSIONS AND RELEVANCE:No safe benzodiazepine threshold was identified in critically ill children, with delirium risk increased even at minimal doses. The greatest increase in odds occurred at the lowest doses. Hence, regardless of dose, benzodiazepine use should be minimized or eliminated whenever possible. Physical restraints and dexmedetomidine use were also associated with increased risk of delirium; thus, a tailored approach for each patient with an emphasis on judicious use is critical.

KEY POINTS

Question: Is there a benzodiazepine dose threshold below which PICU delirium risk remains unaffected?

Findings: Among 10,644 patient-days, any previous-day benzodiazepine exposure increased delirium odds (adjusted odds ratio [aOR], 1.12 per mg/kg midazolam equivalents; p = 0.020), with the largest risk increment occurring at minimal doses (0–0.01 mg/kg; p = 0.010). Physical restraints (aOR, 1.72; p < 0.001) and dexmedetomidine (aOR, 1.26; p = 0.019) also increased delirium risk, while opioids showed no association.

Meaning: No safe benzodiazepine threshold exists; avoidance strategies should replace dose-minimization approaches in PICU sedation protocols.

Delirium is common in the PICU and is associated with increased mortality, longer mechanical ventilation, and longer ICU length of stay (1–5). In observational studies, pediatric delirium has been associated with younger age, severity of illness, baseline developmental disability, physical restraints, and mechanical ventilation (1–3, 5, 6).

The role of medications in the development of delirium is of particular interest for prevention. Sedative medications, especially benzodiazepines, are consistently linked to delirium, including increased risk of subsequent day delirium (1, 2, 7, 8). However, the precise dose-response relationship remains unclear, and whether there exists a threshold dose below which clinicians can safely use benzodiazepines without increasing delirium risk has not been established. The role of opioids is also uncertain: some studies have reported an association, while others have not (3, 6). Dexmedetomidine has been proposed as a safer alternative for sedation, although evidence remains limited and recent work found an increased prevalence of delirium among patients who received dexmedetomidine, suggesting that it may carry greater risk than previously believed (9–11).

While recent practice is focused on minimizing benzodiazepine exposure, benzodiazepines are needed in some children, and it is not known whether there is a lower limit of benzodiazepine dose below which there is not increased delirium risk. Therefore, we aimed to characterize the association between benzodiazepine dose and subsequent risk of developing delirium in PICU patients, with focus on defining the dose-response relationship across the therapeutic range. Our secondary objective was to assess the impact of other sedative agents on delirium risk.

MATERIALS AND METHODS

We conducted a secondary analysis of prospectively collected data from seven PICUs in the United States participating in the intervention phase of the PICU Up! stepped-wedge, cluster randomized mobility pilot trial (NCT03860168). All patients included were receiving the PICU Up! Early Mobility Intervention, which includes activity goals, systematic delirium screening, protocolized sedation, analgesia, and extubation readiness testing, family engagement, and sleep promotion (12). De-identified data were collected from the electronic health record using a HIPAA-compliant database. All sites obtained institutional review board (IRB) approval or acknowledgment with a waiver of informed consent (“PICU Up!: A pilot stepped-wedge quality improvement trial of a multicomponent early mobilization program,” Johns Hopkins School of Medicine: IRB00199373 approved February 25, 2019, Indiana University: 1908398592, August 13, 2019, Saint Louis University: 30371, July 14, 2019, Franciscan Missionaries of Our Lady University: 2019-020, February 1, 2019, Advocate Health Care: 1377686-1, Boston Children’s Hospital: IRB review not required March 4, 2020). This study was carried out in accordance with principles outlined in the Helsinki Declaration and reported in accordance with Strengthening the Reporting of Observational Studies in Epidemiology guidelines (Supplemental Table 1, https://links.lww.com/CCX/B614) (13, 14). Initial analysis of this data was presented in abstract form at the Society for Critical Care Medicine 2024 Critical Care Congress (15).

Patient Selection

As part of the pilot trial, all PICU patients at participating sites were screened and data were collected on children admitted for at least 3 days in 2019 and 2020. Patients were excluded if they were older than 21 years, had active or anticipated withdrawal of life support within 48 hours, an open chest or abdomen, or were receiving extracorporeal membrane oxygenation. Data from one site were excluded from analysis due to systematic differences in data collection procedures. Only patients from the intervention phase of the pilot trial were included.

Definitions

Our primary outcome was ICU delirium, defined as a score of 9 or greater on the Cornell Assessment of Pediatric Delirium or a positive result on the Pediatric Confusion Assessment Method for the ICU or Preschool Confusion Assessment Method for the ICU (16–18). Patient-days were ineligible for delirium screening if delirium could not be assessed due to sedation status, defined by any State Behavioral Scale score less than or equal to –2, or if the patient received neuromuscular blocking agents on that day.

We collected data on the administration of continuous IV infusions of opioids, benzodiazepines, ketamine, dexmedetomidine, pentobarbital, and propofol. Cumulative daily doses were recorded for opioids and benzodiazepines. The pilot trial did not collect data on doses of dexmedetomidine. Daily doses of opioids and benzodiazepines were converted to morphine milligram equivalents and midazolam milligram equivalents per kilogram, respectively (19, 20).

Data Analysis and Statistical Methods

Baseline patient characteristics were expressed as percentages for categorical variables and medians (interquartile range) for continuous variables. Age was grouped into 0–2, 3–6, 7–12, 13–17, and older than 18 years.

We developed a multivariable logistic regression model using generalized estimating equations to account for clustering within sites and repeated measures within patients. Participating PICUs were modeled as fixed effects in the regression model. Potential correlations among outcomes from multiple patient-days over time within the same patient were addressed using a working correlation matrix and the robust sandwich estimators. Odds ratios (ORs) with 95% CIs were estimated. Evaluation for multicollinearity was performed. Covariates included in the model were selected based on clinical relevance and previously published literature. We included Pediatric Risk of Mortality (PRISM) IV score in order to account for illness severity and baseline Pediatric Cerebral Performance Category (PCPC) to incorporate baseline cognitive comorbidity. Other covariates included age, gender, site, current level of respiratory support, current physical restraint use, previous-day delirium status, previous-day dexmedetomidine administration, previous-day benzodiazepine dose, and previous-day opioid dose. Although dosage data for dexmedetomidine was not available, we included previous-day dexmedetomidine administration as a covariate given our clinical interest in sedation, the high prevalence of dexmedetomidine administration within our cohort, and recent reports of association of dexmedetomidine with delirium (9).

To characterize the dose-response relationship between previous-day benzodiazepines and delirium, we first used marginal effects to predict the probability of delirium across the dose range. Second, we performed threshold regression analysis to evaluate whether the relationship between dose and delirium risk departed from linearity. We tested potential threshold points between 0 and 1 mg/kg (in 0.01 mg/kg increments), dividing the dose at each threshold into two components and testing whether the dose-delirium relationship differed. We specifically tested whether any dose maintained the same delirium risk as zero exposure. Third, we calculated derivatives of the dose-response curve to quantify the instantaneous rate of change in delirium probability at key dose percentiles. No imputation was performed for missing data. Statistical significance was set at p value of less than 0.05, and all analyses were performed using Stata 18 (StataCorp, LLC, College Station, TX) (21).

Among intervention-phase patients, 10,904 ICU days and 1,034 children were screened for inclusion, with 10,644 ICU days and 1,006 children included in the analysis (Supplemental Fig. 1, https://links.lww.com/CCX/B614). The reason for ICU admission was medical, rather than surgical or trauma-related, for 67.3% of patients. Median ICU length of stay was 7 days.

Sedative and Analgesic Use

The most common analgesic and sedative medications were opioids and dexmedetomidine (Fig. 1). The percentage of patients receiving continuous IV infusions of opioids, dexmedetomidine, and benzodiazepines trended upward over the length of the ICU stay. After ICU day 7, the mean benzodiazepine dose of the cohort remained stable or increased (Fig. 2). Delirium prevalence increased over the first 10 days of data collection and then plateaued (Supplemental Fig. 2, https://links.lww.com/CCX/B614).

Benzodiazepine Dose-Response Relationship

Previous-day benzodiazepine continuous infusion exposure was independently associated with delirium in a dose-dependent fashion (adjusted OR [aOR], 1.12 per mg/kg midazolam equivalent; 95% CI, 1.02–1.25; p = 0.020; Fig. 3). Threshold analysis testing at multiple cut points revealed significant nonlinearity (p = 0.010), with the most pronounced change occurring below 0.01 mg/kg. The risk increased continuously across doses. The risk of subsequent-day delirium increased from 34% with no exposure to a predicted delirium probability of 43% (95% CI, 0.42–0.44%) at very low doses (0.1 mg/kg) and 46% (95% CI, 0.43–0.48%) at the median dose (1.37 mg/kg) (Fig. 4) . No benzodiazepine dose level demonstrated equivalent delirium risk to zero. Above the smallest doses, the rate of change in predicted risk of subsequent day delirium was nearly identical (Supplemental Table 2, https://links.lww.com/CCX/B614).

Risk Factor Multivariable Regression Analysis

Opioid dose showed no association with delirium on the subsequent day (aOR, 1.00; 95% CI, 1.00–1.00; p = 0.892; Fig. 3). Although we did not have data for dexmedetomidine dose, any previous-day dexmedetomidine use (aOR, 1.26; 95% CI, 1.04–1.52; p = 0.019; Fig. 3) was independently associated with increased delirium odds.

Additional factors associated with increased delirium were previous-day delirium (aOR, 3.51; 95% CI, 2.92–4.23; p < 0.001), previous-day inability to assess for delirium (aOR, 3.34; 95% CI, 2.61–4.29; p < 0.001), physical restraints (aOR, 1.72; 95% CI, 1.27–2.31; p < 0.001), and higher PRISM score (aOR, 1.02 per 1% increase; 95% CI, 1.00–1.03; p = 0.009; Fig. 3). Baseline cognitive deficits as categorized on the PCPC, as well as current invasive and noninvasive mechanical ventilation, were all significantly associated with increased odds of delirium (Fig. 3). Older age was a protective factor (Fig. 3). ORs and p values for the multivariable regression are further described in Supplemental Table 3 (https://links.lww.com/CCX/B614). These associations remained similar after the addition of reason for ICU admission as a covariate (Supplemental Table 4, https://links.lww.com/CCX/B614).

DISCUSSION

Our secondary analysis from this multicenter trial demonstrates that no benzodiazepine continuous infusion dose maintains the same delirium risk as zero exposure in critically ill children. The dose-response relationship is nonlinear, with the steepest risk increase occurring at very low doses.

Our analysis provides evidence for clinicians that the transition from no benzodiazepine exposure to any benzodiazepine dose represents the single greatest increase in delirium risk. Doses clinicians might consider “minimal” may carry the greatest risk escalation. Our finding extends the work done by Smith et al (1) and Mody et al (8), who found an independent relationship between benzodiazepine dose and risk of delirium, by specifically demonstrating that no dose threshold exists below which risk remains equivalent to no exposure.

Our findings are particularly concerning given that in our cohort both benzodiazepine infusion usage and mean daily benzodiazepine dose increased with prolonged PICU stays. PICU clinicians should use minimum necessary doses of benzodiazepines and should reevaluate patients frequently for opportunities for infusion discontinuation, even for patients receiving very low doses.

In our cohort, physical restraints were cross-sectionally associated with delirium; suggesting that restraints should be used for the minimum time required. Our study also reinforces that not only invasive mechanical ventilation but also noninvasive respiratory support, such as continuous positive airway pressure or bilevel positive airway pressure, are risk factors for delirium in the PICU and clinicians should monitor these patients carefully for delirium development.

Our analysis is unique in that all patients were already receiving an interventional mobility, sleep promotion, and family engagement bundle, which are recommended for delirium prevention by the Society of Critical Care Medicine 2022 clinical practice guidelines (22). Despite these protective measures, sedative medication-associated delirium risk persisted. Our data underscores that while these nonpharmacologic interventions are important components of risk reduction strategies, they cannot fully mitigate the deliriogenic effects of benzodiazepines.

Previous work evaluating opioids as a risk factor for delirium has shown mixed results (3, 6). Despite increased attention to analgesia as part of the PICU Up! bundle, opioid administration was neither positively nor negatively associated with delirium risk. This suggests that concerns about delirium should not limit appropriate pain management in critically ill children and reassures clinicians of the safety of judicious opioid use for analgesia.

Our findings regarding dexmedetomidine warrant careful interpretation. While adult studies suggest dexmedetomidine may offer a lower risk of delirium compared with other sedatives (23, 24), we found that previous-day dexmedetomidine administration was associated with increased odds of delirium, even after controlling for preexisting delirium and benzodiazepines. This aligns with recent work by Christian et al (9), who reported an increased prevalence of delirium among pediatric patients who received dexmedetomidine. However, this association should be interpreted cautiously as dexmedetomidine is sometimes used to manage hyperactive delirium in PICUs, creating potential confounding by indication. In addition, we did not have data about dexmedetomidine dose. We recommend additional studies including more granular dosing data and randomized controlled trials to help clarify whether dexmedetomidine is independently deliriogenic or if our results merely reflect its widespread use in managing delirium.

Our study is limited by our observational design. It is possible that another confounding factor we were unable to identify both prompts PICU clinicians to prescribe sedating medications and also increases risk of delirium. To mitigate this, we included numerous delirium risk factors in our analysis, although randomized trials will be required for causal inference. Additionally, our study focused exclusively on total daily doses of continuous IV infusions of analgesic and sedative medications; however, future work should evaluate the impact of as-needed single doses of benzodiazepines, as well as pharmacokinetic factors, including drug route, dosing schedule, absorption, and metabolism, which may attenuate risk. Finally, days when delirium data were not collected are likely reflective of screening practices, which we have characterized previously, with significant intercenter variability in missing days, and it is possible that incomplete data biased our results (25).

CONCLUSIONS

In this multicenter study of 10,644 ICU days, there is no threshold dose of benzodiazepine infusion under which increasing the dose does not increase the risk for delirium among PICU patients. The greatest increase in risk for delirium occurred with very low doses. Therefore, PICU clinicians should reduce or eliminate continuous benzodiazepine infusions whenever possible, in addition to minimizing other iatrogenic risk factors. Further research should be performed to clarify the relationship between dexmedetomidine and delirium risk. Opioid administration was not associated with increased odds of delirium, providing further evidence that pain can be treated without increasing risk.
