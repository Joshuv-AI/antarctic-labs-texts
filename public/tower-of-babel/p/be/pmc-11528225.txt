The association between education level and overactive bladder: Evidence from a U.S. population-based study
Authors: Wentao Yao, Yang Zong, Feng Xu, Hongzhi Wang, Chendi Yang, Mingya Lu, Wenbin Jiang, Yuan Zhao, Haizhou Yang, Yinyi Zhou
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
Graphical abstractKeywords: Education level, Socioeconomic status, Overactive bladder, American adults, NHANES

Introduction

The International Continence Society describes overactive bladder (OAB) as a storage phase syndrome mainly marked by urgency, which might or might not be accompanied by urge urinary incontinence (UUI). There are no apparent clinical abnormalities, such as urinary tract infections, but symptoms like increased daytime frequency and nocturia are common (Abrams et al., 2002). An epidemiological study in China indicated a general OAB prevalence of approximately 6.0 % (Wang et al., 2011). In the United States, the prevalence rates among adults are 16 % for men and 16.9 % for women (Stewart et al., 2003). Projections by Irwin et al. suggested that the global prevalence might reach 20 % by 2018 (Irwin et al., 2011). The prevalence of OAB increases with age and significantly impairs the quality of life, sleep, and mental health of affected individuals. The limitations of current treatments, their side effects, and the increasing burden of OAB due to an aging population, underscore the significant healthcare challenges posed by this condition. In the United States, the financial burden of OAB on healthcare systems amounts to billions annually (Ganz et al., 2010), with the medical costs for individuals with OAB being more than 2.5 times those without the condition (Durden et al., 2018), thus constituting a significant public health issue. Nonetheless, the risk factors and mechanisms underlying OAB remain not fully understood.

Previous research has indicated a positive correlation between health status and educational level (Moor et al., 2018). Moreover, chronic conditions such as hypertension (Lopez-Lopez et al., 2022) and type 2 diabetes (Zhang et al., 2022) are frequently associated with lower levels of education. Recent studies have linked socioeconomic status (SES) with OAB (Lin et al., 2024). A cross-sectional analysis from Portugal revealed a reduction in the prevalence of urinary incontinence with increased educational attainment (Manso et al., 2023). Subsequent investigations have confirmed that elevated educational levels are associated with a lower risk of urinary incontinence (Zhang et al., 2023). Despite these findings, studies that directly examine the relationship between educational level and OAB remain limited. To bridge this gap, enhance OAB research, and improve intervention effectiveness, we utilized data from the National Health and Nutrition Examination Survey (NHANES) spanning 2005 to 2020 to scrutinize the association between educational attainment and OAB.

Methods

Study participants

NHANES, administered by the National Center for Health Statistics (NCHS), assesses nutritional status and health metrics across the U.S. populace (Yao et al., 2024). The analysis incorporated data from eight consecutive survey cycles spanning 2005 to 2020, since NHANES lacks survey data on nocturia prior to 2005, which is crucial for obtaining the OAB symptom score (OABSS) necessary for diagnosing OAB. NHANES is an anonymized, publicly accessible dataset approved by the NCHS Research Ethics Review Board, with written informed consent collected from all participants. Therefore, no additional ethical review was required for this study. Initially, exclusions removed 39,040 individuals lacking OAB data and 33 participants with missing education level information. After these criteria, 37,423 participants aged 20 years and older were included in the subsequent analysis (Fig. 1).

Participant selection flowchart: NHANES 2005–2020 for U.S. adults.

Exposure and outcome definitions

Education level, the exposure variable in this study, was ascertained through a structured questionnaire querying participants about their highest completed educational grade or degree. Responses were categorized into five levels: (1) less than 9th grade, (2) 9th to 11th grade, (3) high school graduate/GED or equivalent, (4) some college or an AA degree, and (5) college graduate or higher. Entries labeled “Refused” or “Don’t Know,” along with those lacking data, were omitted from the analysis. In this cross-sectional study, educational level was analyzed as both a categorical and a continuous variable. The outcome variable, OAB, was defined by an overactive micturition reflex manifesting as UUI and nocturia, based on questionnaire responses. OAB identification was established by a total OABSS of 3 or higher, consistent with prior implementations of NHANES data (Zhu et al., 2023). Detailed scoring protocols are provided in Table S1.

Covariates

Adjustments were made to mitigate the influence of potential confounding variables such as age, gender, race, poverty, living status, tobacco use, alcohol intake history, hypertension, diabetes, body mass index (BMI), and levels of low-density (LDL-C) and high-density lipoprotein cholesterol (HDL-C). Detailed covariate information is available on the NHANES website. For racial categorization, the segments included Mexican American, Other Races (including other Hispanic and multiracial categories), non-Hispanic White, and non-Hispanic Black. Economic status was evaluated using the poverty income ratio (PIR), with households below a PIR of 1 classified as below the poverty line. Living status differentiated between individuals living alone and those cohabiting with partners. Tobacco use was divided into non-smokers (less than 100 cigarettes lifetime), former smokers (more than 100 cigarettes historically, but not currently smoking), and current smokers. Alcohol consumption history was determined from responses to the questions: “Have you had at least 12 alcoholic drinks in one year?” and “Have you ever consumed any type of alcoholic beverage?” Hypertension identification involved participants’ self-reports, antihypertensive medication use, or an average systolic blood pressure ≥ 130 mmHg and/or diastolic blood pressure ≥ 80 mmHg from four separate measurements (Whelton et al., 2018). Diabetes was determined by self-reported medical history, use of antidiabetic medications or insulin, a glycohemoglobin level ≥ 6.5 %, or a fasting glucose level ≥ 7.0 mmol/L (Menke et al., 2015).

Statistical analysis

Baseline continuous metrics were expressed as means ± standard deviation, and categorical metrics as percentages. Depending on their distribution, based on normality assumptions, differences among continuous variables were assessed using either Student’s t-tests or Kruskal-Wallis tests. For categorical data, we used Chi-square tests. The correlation between education level and OAB was investigated using multifactorial logistic regression models across three frameworks: Model 1, without covariate adjustments; Model 2, adjusted for age, gender, and race; and Model 3, further accounting for poverty, living status, smoking status, alcohol drinking history, hypertension, diabetes, BMI, LDL-C, and HDL-C. The flexibility of generalized additive model (GAM) captures complex patterns, providing a more accurate analysis than traditional linear models by allowing data-driven relationship modeling. Non-linear correlations between education level and OAB were outlined in model 3 using a GAM with smooth curve fitting. Statistical analyses were carried out using R and EmpowerStats, both versions 4.2, with significance set at P < 0.05.

Results

Demographic characteristics

The study analyzed data from 37,423 American individuals aged 20 and older, comprising 50.87 % females and 49.13 % males, with 21.47 % diagnosed with OAB. Participant characteristics were stratified by education level (Table 1). OAB prevalence varied across educational levels: 32.81 % among those with less than a 9th-grade education, 28.27 % for 9th to 11th graders, 22.73 % for high school graduates or GED holders, 19.65 % for some college or AA degree holders, and 13.55 % for college graduates or those with advanced degrees. Individuals with higher educational levels were generally younger, predominantly female, white, economically non-deprived, non-smokers, and alcohol consumers. They were also more likely to cohabit with partners and showed a lower likelihood of developing hypertension and diabetes. Additionally, these individuals demonstrated lower BMI, waist circumference, triglyceride levels, total cholesterol levels, and higher HDL-C levels.

Association between education level and OAB

Table 2 showed that the prevalence of OAB was strongly inversely related to education level, as revealed using multifactorial logistic regression analysis. Model 1 (odds ratio [OR] = 0.76, 95 % confidence interval [CI] = 0.75–0.78, P < 0.001) and Model 2 (OR = 0.80, 95 % CI = 0.78–0.81, P < 0.001) both maintained this connection. The inverse connection remained significant (OR = 0.86, 95 % CI = 0.83–0.89, P < 0.001) even after adjusting for all potential variables in model 3. This analysis suggested each incremental advance in educational attainment correlated with a 14 % decrease in the likelihood of developing OAB.

The analysis revealed that, compared to individuals with less than a 9th-grade education, those with a college degree or higher had a lower likelihood of developing OAB: 32 % (OR = 0.32, 95 % CI = 0.29–0.35, P < 0.001), 40 % (OR = 0.40, 95 % CI = 0.36–0.45, P < 0.001), and 56 % (OR = 0.56, 95 % CI = 0.47–0.66, P < 0.001) in models 1, 2, and 3 respectively (Table 2). Discussion

OAB significantly impacts human health and burdens the healthcare system and economy. In this cross-sectional study, we noted a robust association between education level and OAB among American adults. Univariate and multivariable logistic regression analyses demonstrated that higher educational level correlated inversely with OAB prevalence. Our categorical analysis revealed that OAB prevalence was significantly reduced in individuals possessing at least a college degree.

Numerous prior epidemiological studies have identified lower educational attainment as a potential risk factor for OAB. Wang et al. identified a strong association between education level and OAB in their analysis of 14,844 Chinese individuals aged 18 and above (Wang et al., 2011). A cross-sectional study conducted by Jo et al. of 926 Koreans also indicated that lower educational attainment was significantly associated with increased OAB prevalence in both men and women aged 40 and above (Jo et al., 2012). Our study’s diagnosis of OAB was based on two questionnaires: UUI and nocturia. Interestingly, a cross-sectional study involving 10,465 Portuguese women aged 18 and above found that urinary incontinence prevalence decreased with higher educational attainment, with 28.2 % in uneducated women compared to 2.2 % in women with more than 12 years of education (Manso et al., 2023). Similarly, Kim et al. found that lower educational attainment was significantly associated with nocturia in adult Korean men (Kim et al., 2017). Our study also found an inverse correlation between education level and OAB. Notably, this analysis was based on a large, nationally representative cohort of 37,423 samples collected between 2005 and 2020, enabling a thorough examination. Nonetheless, some findings contradict ours. A preceding meta-analysis revealed no substantial educational disparities between OAB patients and controls (Zhu et al., 2019). Additionally, a cross-sectional study involving 9,637 Chinese individuals aged 40 and above found no correlation between nocturia and educational level (Wen et al., 2015). These differences may be attributed to population, racial, and environmental factors, requiring further research for confirmation.

A recent analysis also utilizing NHANES data spanning 2005–2020 corroborated that education level was independently and inversely related to OAB prevalence. However, this prior study examined prevalence trends among males and grouped educational attainment into three levels: below high school, high school graduates, and above high school (Cheng et al., 2024). In contrast, our study conducted a more detailed analysis across a gender-inclusive cohort, categorizing educational attainment into five levels: below 9th grade, 9th to 11th grade, high school graduate/GED or equivalent, some college or AA degree, and college graduate or above.

Education level is a crucial component of SES (Ye et al., 2023). SES represents the opportunities to acquire material, human, and social capital, significantly impacting an individual’s overall health. Moreover, SES directly affects not only the quality and accessibility of healthcare services (Yong and Yang, 2021) but also significantly shapes an individual’s lifestyle and surrounding environment (Adler and Newman, 2002), both associated with several adverse health outcomes, including OAB (McGrother et al., 2012, Zhang et al., 2024). Tousoulis and colleagues believed that education, rather than wealth, was the most significant socioeconomic factor in mitigating cardiovascular disease risks (Tousoulis et al., 2020). Numerous studies have investigated the nexus between educational achievement and health. A longitudinal cohort study in Japan revealed that individuals with lower educational levels experienced heightened overall mortality risks, increasing by 16 % among men and 26 % among women (Fujino et al., 2005). Similarly, research by Woodward et al., involving nearly 90,000 participants from Australia and New Zealand, indicated that those with only primary education or none faced a 20 % increased risk of mortality from cardiovascular diseases and a 14 % elevation in overall mortality rates compared to those with tertiary education (Woodward et al., 2015).

Educational attainment can influence health through multiple mechanisms. Education shapes future career opportunities and income potential, and imparts knowledge and skills that enable individuals to better access information and resources necessary for promoting health. According to Hahn et al., increasing educational attainment can improve health by enhancing personal agency, self-efficacy, and problem-solving abilities, promoting healthy lifestyles (Hahn and Truman, 2015). Compared to other SES indicators such as occupation or income, education level tends to remain stable throughout life and is less likely to be affected by health issues in adulthood (Kagamimori et al., 2009). However, the precise factors linking educational attainment to OAB prevalence remain uncertain and multifactorial. Evidence indicated an inverse relationship between educational level and smoking rate (Giovino, 2002). In 2000, the smoking rate among U.S. adults was 28.6 % for those with a secondary education of 12 years or less, 29.5 % for high school graduates, 22.6 % for those with some college education, and 11.7 % for those with a bachelor’s degree or above (Giovino, 2002). Research by Dallosso et al. in a prospective cohort study pinpointed smoking as a considerable risk factor for OAB. The study demonstrated that obesity and carbonated drink consumption were notable risk factors for OAB, whereas intake of vegetables, bread, and chicken was associated with a reduced risk (Dallosso et al., 2003). These findings suggested that the impact of education on OAB can be partly explained by health behaviors. Moreover, a comprehensive analysis involving 4,229,617 OAB patients showed that individuals without a bachelor’s degree were 3 % less likely to receive advanced therapeutic interventions for OAB, such as onabotulinumtoxinA, sacral nerve stimulation, and percutaneous tibial nerve stimulation, compared to those with a bachelor’s degree or higher (Syan et al., 2020). Education appears to significantly affect OAB outcomes, likely because those with higher educational levels are more knowledgeable about OAB treatment options, more compliant with prescribed therapies, and more adaptable to treatment regimens.

OAB is a complex syndrome influenced by various underlying mechanisms, making it a multifactorial disease. Due to the interplay among various known and unknown disease factors, environmental influences, socioeconomic conditions, and genetic predispositions, it is challenging to elucidate all factors associated with OAB. Therefore, studying the prevalence and characteristics of OAB is imperative to identify potential evidence that could enhance strategies for reducing its incidence. Verified through longitudinal prospective studies, accessible education level is anticipated to be integrated into future OAB management strategies. For instance, clinicians should prioritize targeted screening for OAB in individuals with lower educational levels, as these patients may not recognize symptoms or seek care due to limited awareness. Treatment plans should include patient education by explaining non-pharmacological interventions, such as bladder training and lifestyle modifications, in simpler terms to enhance compliance. Public health strategies should raise OAB awareness using easy-to-understand materials, community outreach, and partnerships with local organizations, emphasizing healthy lifestyles such as smoking cessation and weight management. To address healthcare access barriers, interventions like free or subsidized OAB screening in community health centers or mobile clinics are essential. Additionally, increasing healthcare provider awareness about the higher prevalence of OAB in this demographic can lead to more proactive care. Improving health literacy through simplified communication, visual aids, and dedicated educational sessions can also enhance outcomes.

The methodology employed by NHANES underpins the robustness of this analysis. To obtain a sample representative of the U.S. population, NHANES employs a complex, multi-stage, probability-based sampling process. To improve the accuracy of our statistical analysis, we made thorough adjustments for variables. However, our study had some limitations. Firstly, the cross-sectional nature of the study poses challenges in establishing causality or capturing temporal dynamics. While education typically precedes OAB onset, OAB symptoms might impact academic performance. Future research could conduct prospective cohort studies to track education level and OAB onset, providing a clearer examination of the potential causal relationship. Secondly, despite using a modified and simplified OABSS scale to diagnose OAB, NHANES data is limited by its failure to assess diurnal frequency and urgency symptoms, lacks detailed clinical evaluations, and relies on self-reports, which may introduce recall bias and constrain diagnostic precision. Thirdly, overcoming unmeasured confounding factors remains problematic due to inherent database restrictions. Lastly, the absence of OAB treatment information within NHANES database may result in an underestimation of OAB prevalence, particularly among higher-educated individuals with better healthcare access. Nonetheless, our research has uncovered a significant link between education level and OAB, a relationship that has not been thoroughly explored in extensive U.S. cohorts previously. This research is anticipated to contribute meaningfully to the management and prevention of OAB.

Conclusion

Our study suggested that in the United States, individuals with higher education levels were less likely to develop OAB. It is recommended that strategies for managing and preventing OAB be tailored according to education level to ensure interventions are accessible and effective across different educational backgrounds. Further prospective and experimental studies are needed to validate these findings and explore the underlying mechanisms.
