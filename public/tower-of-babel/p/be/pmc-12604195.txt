Multidrug antibiotic resistance index and antimicrobial resistance patterns of Escherichia coli in intensive pig farms in Kenya
Authors: Christine Inguyesi, Moses Olum, Peter Ndirangu, Nathan Langat, Ascah Jesang, Ednah Masila, Esther Wachuka, Ruth Onywera, Dishon Muloi, Linnet Ochieng, Victor Yamo, Kelvin Momanyi, Patrick Muinde, John M. Maingi, Monicah Maichomo
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
Antibiotic resistance is a significant global public health threat, rendering treating human and animal infections difficult, longer and expensive. This study was conducted to determine the antibiotic resistance profiles of Escherichia coli isolated from pig farming environments in selected pig farms around Kenya. Wastewater, the associated sludge, and ground surface boot sock samples were collected from preselected intensive pig farms. A total of 80 samples were collected from 16 intensive pig farms. The samples were cultured, and 112 E. coli isolates were identified using standard microbiological procedures and confirmed by Matrix-Assisted Laser Desorption/Ionization Time-of-Flight Mass Spectroscopy (MALDI-TOF MS). Antibiotic susceptibility testing was performed using the Kirby–Bauer disc diffusion method against ampicillin, amoxicillin-clavulanic acid, gentamicin, trimethoprim-sulfamethoxazole, chloramphenicol, enrofloxacin and cefotaxime. The highest resistance was recorded against ampicillin at 27.7% and the lowest for cefotaxime at 7.1%. Multidrug-resistance was observed for 25.9% of the isolates. There was no significant difference between resistances from the sampling locations. Fisher’s exact test conducted to determine the difference between resistance rates of different sample types and the antibiotics tested showed that trimethoprim-sulphamethoxazole showed a statistically significant difference (p = 0.003). The isolates had varied multidrug antibiotic resistance (MAR) indices, but the average index was 0.33. The mean MAR index was highest in boot socks samples (0.3 ± 0.3). Kruskal-Wallis test did not find a statistically significant difference between sample type and MAR index. The results suggest that there is potentially high antibiotic exposure in the intensive pig farms that may increase the selection pressure leading to the development and dissemination of antibiotic-resistant E. coli among pig populations. These findings highlight the presence of multidrug-resistant E. coli in intensive pig farm environments, posing a potential risk to humans and the wider environment, which may further compound the public health threat.

Background

The rising demand for livestock products in Kenya and worldwide, driven by population growth and urbanization, has led to the widespread adoption of intensive livestock production methods to meet this demand [1] efficiently. However, in large-scale pig production systems, diseases can significantly decrease productivity and increase mortality rates, mainly due to poor hygiene, low welfare standards, and production stress commonly seen in intensive operations [2].

To address health and productivity issues in pigs, antibiotics are frequently used, sometimes in sub-therapeutic doses, as feed additives or treatments to protect livestock and investments [3]. In Kenya, veterinarians are legally mandated to prescribe and supervise antimicrobial use; however, recent evidence shows widespread gaps between policy and practice. A survey revealed that while many farmers are aware of antimicrobials, knowledge of AMR is limited, prescriptions are not always sought from veterinarians and antimicrobials are frequently obtained directly from agroveterinary shops or reused from leftovers [4]. The Directorate of Veterinary Sciences (DVS) has developed two documents to streamline this. The National Action Plan on Prevention and Containment of AMR targeting all the sections of health and Guidelines on Prudent Use of Antimicrobials in Animals [5, 6]. Due to selective pressure, the indiscriminate use of antibiotics on farms has been identified as a major contributor to the emergence of antibiotic-resistant bacteria in pigs and their environments [7]. This resistance threatens the effective treatment of bacterial infections in humans and animals, leading to increased morbidity, mortality, and rising healthcare and veterinary costs [8].

The selection process for bacterial resistance does not end with the cessation of antibiotic treatment or animal feeding. Instead, it continues as waste from livestock farms-containing antibiotic residues, resistant bacteria, and resistance genes-is released into the environment through farmland application, wastewater collection points, or water bodies [9]. Research indicates that the repeated use of pig manure and wastewater from pig farms increases the selection of resistant bacterial populations in the soil due to the presence of sub-optimal concentrations of antibiotics, with effects reaching the atmosphere, surface, and groundwater, and even contaminating irrigated crops and nearby community water sources [9]. A recent study in Kenya demonstrated that antimicrobial use on pig farms is positively associated with higher concentrations of antibiotic residues in manure-fertilized soils, thereby increasing the risk of resistance emergence and dissemination [10].

The livestock farming environment thus serves as both a reservoir and a pathway for the emergence and spread of antibiotic resistance, significantly increasing the risk of transmission through the food chain, particularly in Low- and Middle-Income Countries (LMICs) [11]. There are limited measures to control the spread of resistant bacteria from livestock manure across humans, animals, and the environment, unlike the more stringent policies for human waste management.

Evidence shows that birds and rodents can contribute significantly to the spread of AMR. Birds may acquire resistant bacteria from contaminated farm environments and spread them across long distances through their droppings. Similarly, rodents frequently inhabit farm structures, come into direct contact with animal waste, feed, and water sources, and can act as reservoirs and vectors of resistant bacteria [12]. Horizontal gene transfer within the environment can play a key role in the exchange and acquisition of antibiotic resistance genes, significantly contributing to the evolution of microbial communities. Containing the spread of antimicrobial-resistant (AMR) bacteria is crucial for protecting the broader environment from related health hazards [13].

Escherichia coli is a normal part of the intestinal flora in animals and humans. However, in immunosuppressed individuals, it can cause disease, including urinary tract infections and life-threatening bloodstream infections [14]. In animals, E. coli infections can lead to significant economic losses due to increased mortality and morbidity. The World Health Organization (WHO) has categorized E. coli as a priority pathogen because it develops multidrug-resistance [15]. E. coli can act as a reservoir for resistant genes, facilitating their transmission, which poses a risk to both animal and human health [16].

Studies have shown a direct relationship between antimicrobial use and antimicrobial resistance in E. coli isolates from pigs, poultry and cattle. For instance, the emergence of resistance to critically important antimicrobials such as third-generation cephalosporins and fluoroquinolones has been reported in food-producing animals in Sub-Saharan Africa, including pigs, and is linked to antimicrobial usage patterns on farms [3].

Despite the growing recognition of the role of livestock and waste from livestock in spreading resistant bacteria, there is limited data on the resistance profiles of bacteria in piggery environments in Kenya. Generating such evidence is essential for understanding environmental AMR risks and for informing surveillance and control strategies under a One Health framework.

This study evaluated the dynamics of antimicrobial resistance in intensive pig farming in Kenya, focusing on E. coli.

Methods

Study design and sample size

This cross-sectional survey was conducted in 12 pig-producing counties in Kenya (Fig. 1) where intensive pig rearing is widely practiced [17].

A map of Kenya showing the study sites

The farms were purposively sampled based on herd size (≥ 15 sows) to include medium to largescale farms while at the same time excluding backyard farms. The role of the selected pig farms in supplying pigs to large retail bodies in Kenya was also considered to capture principal intensive pig production systems with high production and biosecurity levels. The sampling was carried out from February to April 2022, and the farms were identified through alphabetical codes. A total of 27 farmers were initially contacted for recruitment into the study; however, only 16 consented and were included.

A total of 80 environmental samples were collected in triplicate from 16 farms. Sample types included upstream and downstream water and sediment samples and pooled boot sock samples. The exploratory study aimed to generate baseline data on antimicrobial resistance in intensive pig farming environments in Kenya. Therefore, sample selection was based on feasibility, farm accessibility, and the willingness of farmers to participate. All participating farmers provided informed consent prior to sample collection.

Sample collection

Water and soil samples collection

These were collected aseptically upstream and downstream in triplicate from the piggeries. Fifty milliliters of water and a scoop of sediment were collected into sterile centrifuge tubes at two locations along the primary effluent exiting the piggery. The samples at the point closest to the shelter were designated upstream water and sediment samples, while the corresponding downstream samples were collected at the furthest possible location. Each sample was collected in triplicate. Following collection, the tubes were securely capped, wiped externally with 70% ethanol and appropriately labeled. Subsequently, the samples were stored in a well-insulated cool box with icepacks.

Boot socks sample collection

Two boot sock samples were collected from the windward direction of the piggeries and pooled. The boot socks were soaked beforehand in sodium chloride (0.9% NaCl) while being consistently gently massaged. Freshly gloved hands were used to put on sterile plastic shoe covers over the gumboots, followed by wearing the boot socks over the shoe covers. The individual conducting the sampling proceeded alongside the pig shelter, following the typical direction of the prevailing wind. The boot socks were removed and placed into pre-dispensed Tryptic Soy Broth within a zip-lock sampling bag using gloved sterile hands. After sealing the bag, it was gently massaged until adequately soaked in the broth, then appropriately labeled and positioned upright on ice within the cool box. All samples were promptly transported to the laboratory, where culturing began within 24 h of collection.

Laboratory methods

Culturing of the samples

This was conducted at the KALRO - Veterinary Science Research Institute (VSRI). The triplicate samples were pooled to form a composite sample. The water samples were vortexed for 30 s and were then diluted in sodium chloride (0.9% NaCl) at a ratio of 1:10. Approximately 5 g of sediment was diluted in 10 ml of sodium chloride (0.9% NaCl). The boot sock samples were gently massaged for a minute, after which 1 ml was taken into a sterile tube. A loopful was streaked for each sample on MacConkey Agar (Himedia, Mumbai, India). Culturing was performed in duplicates, and the cultures were incubated overnight at 370 °C. After 18-24 h, colony morphology was observed, followed by Catalase, Oxidase, and Indole tests, and subsequently Gram staining and microscopic observation. This was carried out for the preliminary identification of bacteria, showing pink colonies surrounded by a red region of precipitated bile salts on MacConkey agar that appeared as Gram-negative rods under the microscope. Matrix-assisted laser desorption/ionization time-of-flight mass spectroscopy (MALDI-TOF MS) was used to confirm the identity of the isolates, which were then preserved as 10% glycerol stocks at -200 °C for further analysis [18].

Antibiotic susceptibility testing (AST)

Antibiotic susceptibility testing was performed using the Kirby–Bauer disk diffusion method as recommended by the European Committee on Antimicrobial Susceptibility Testing [19], on Mueller-Hinton Agar (Himedia, Mumbai, India) [20]. Seven antibiotics belonging to six antibiotic classes were tested. The antibiotics tested were ampicillin 10ug, amoxicillin-clavulanic acid 20-10ug, gentamicin 10ug, trimethoprim-sulfamethoxazole 1.25-23.75ug, chloramphenicol 30ug, enrofloxacin 5ug and cefotaxime 5ug (Basingstoke, Hampshire, United Kingdom). E. coli ATCC® 25,922 was used for quality control during the antibiotic sensitivity testing. The diameters of the inhibition zones were measured and interpreted according to the EUCAST, 2024 breakpoints.

Data analysis

All the data was recorded using Microsoft Excel. The zones of inhibition obtained after conducting antibiotic sensitivity testing were interpreted according to EUCAST [19]. The multiple antibiotic resistance (MAR) index was calculated as follows: A/B, where ‘A’ was taken as the number of antibiotics an isolate was resistant to, and ‘B’ was taken as the total number of antibiotics tested. Multidrug-resistance (MDR) patterns were determined from the isolates exhibiting resistance to at least one antibiotic agent in different antibiotic classes [21]. The data was presented with the aid of Microsoft Excel.

These isolates were tested for their susceptibility to seven antibiotics (Table 1).

Antibiograms of E. coli isolates

In this study, 46.4% of the isolates were susceptible to all the antimicrobials tested, while 1.8% were resistant to all the antibiotics tested. Twenty-four resistance patterns to the tested antibiotics (antibiograms) were recorded (Table 2). The most dominant resistance was against AMP, registered in 15 out of 24 patterns. Interestingly, only 0.9% of the isolates were resistant to AMP alone. The isolates exhibited a total of 18 patterns that were designated as multidrug-resistant (MDR) since they exhibited resistance to more than one antibiotic agent in different antibiotic classes [21]. Among the isolates, 25.9% of the E. coli isolates were shown to be MDR. AMP-AMC and AMP-AMC-SXT were the most common resistance patterns at 4.5% each. Rates of “susceptible increased dosed” were low and hence were classified as resistant [22].

The highest calculated MAR was 1, exhibited by 1.8% of the isolates. The isolates had varied MAR indices, but the average index was 0.33 (Fig. 4).

Discussion

The present study sought to identify antibiotic resistance in and around intensive pig farms in Kenya by isolating E. coli and characterizing their antibiotic resistance profiles. Closely related studies conducted in other countries have reported varied percentages of resistance in E. coli against the antibiotics tested.

A study in Uganda in the pig holding environments in abattoirs across the country reported lower resistance rates against trimethoprim-sulfamethoxazole at 23.1% compared to 25.9% reported from farm environments in the present study. Furthermore, the study reports no resistance against ciprofloxacin in the environment of pig abattoirs compared to 11.6% observed in the present study. However, in the same study, 44.8% of E.coli isolates from pig feaces were resistant to trimethoprim-sulfamethoxazole and 2.3% resistant to ciprofloxacin [2]. These differences could be explained by the fact that at the abattoir, the pigs are held for a short time awaiting slaughter, compared to the pig farms, which consistently introduce resistant bacteria from the feces and urine of the pigs. Cleaning and disinfection of the holding areas at the abattoir may also reduce the persistence of bacteria in this environment, unlike the case on a pig farm.

A study conducted in Tanzania on pigs reported higher resistance rates against ciprofloxacin at 28.6%, chloramphenicol at 27.3%, gentamycin at 26%, ampicillin at 46.4%, trimethoprim-sulfamethoxazole at 47.7% and cefotaxime at 29.5% compared to 11.6%, 11.6%, 12.5%, 27.7%, 25.9% and 7.1% respectively identified in the present study [23]. The Tanzanian study sampled pig feaces, which could represent recent exposure to antibiotics administered to pigs. In contrast, the present study sampled the environment of the pig farm, which may comprise bacteria from multiple sources in addition to those from pigs. It may be subject to factors like competition with other bacteria and environmental survival, limiting the survival of some resistant bacteria.

Multidrug-resistance was observed to be 25.9% in the present study, as compared to a South African study, which recorded a high percentage of resistance in pig farm isolates, and the highest prevalence of MDR was found on the pig farm at 74.6%. Similar to our study, the most common MDR pattern included AMP-SXT-CHL, found in 4.7% of the isolates, similar to what is reported in the current study at 4.5% [24]. In the study, similar to the current one, E. coli showed the highest resistance to chloramphenicol, trimethoprim-sulfamethoxazole and ampicillin. This is because these antibiotics have been used for a long time in food-producing animals. In their 2019 Annual Report on Antimicrobial Agents Intended for Use in Animals, WOAH documented that the most antibiotics used for animal production were tetracyclines, macrolides and ampicillin [25].

Although the use of chloramphenicol in animal-producing animals was banned in 2010 in Kenya [26], E. coli isolates resistant to chloramphenicol were recovered from pig farms at a rate of 11.6%. This may possibly be attributed to horizontal gene transfer of chloramphenicol resistance genes and co-selection of resistance due to the use of other antibiotics [27]. For instance, reports have it that the resistance to tetracycline, sulfamethoxazole and kanamycin, which are accepted for animal use is often transferred together with chloramphenicol resistance [28].

Cephalosporins and fluoroquinolones exhibited a relatively low resistance rate compared to other antibiotics in this study. However, they still need monitoring due to their clinical importance since the World Health Organization classified both as critically important antibiotics (CIAs) for human medicine [29]. The E. coli isolates in the current study were highly sensitive to cephalosporins and fluoroquinolones, with resistance rates of 7.1% against cefotaxime and 8.9% against enrofloxacin. However, this serves to caution farmers that increased use of drugs in these classes should be regulated to minimize the risk of the development of resistance to them.

The increasing occurrence of MDR E. coli poses a public health challenge since E. coli occupies multiple niches, in animals, the environment and humans and, can acquire or transmit antimicrobial resistance genes vertically and horizontally. The prevalent MDR pattern reported in the current study included resistance to antibiotics commonly used for treating diseases in human and veterinary medicine. This indicates the possibility of the resistance genes being transmitted to other pigs, the environment, and consequently to humans.

The multiple antibiotic resistance (MAR) index is commonly applied in determining health risk associated with the dissemination of resistance in any given location [24]. A MAR index higher than 0.2 suggests that the bacteria under study were exposed to environments with high antibiotic use [24]. The current study recorded a mean MAR index of 0.33 in the pig farming environments, suggesting potential exposure to high antibiotic usage in pig rearing in Kenya and the possible high selective pressure created in these environments.

Isolates from bootsock samples collected from areas surrounding the pig houses exhibited the highest proportion of SXT-resistant isolates (66.7%, 95% CI: 34.9–90.1%). This finding indicates that SXT-resistant bacteria are not only confined to the inside of the pig houses but are present in the surrounding farm environment, suggesting potential spillover via wind, runoff, or farm activities like using the pig waste on the nearby agricultural lands. However, the wide confidence interval for bootsocks isolates reflects the small isolate number (n = 12) and the resulting uncertainty in the exact proportion. Nevertheless, these descriptive results support the earlier observation of a statistically significant difference in SXT resistance among sample types, with environmental bootsocks contributing most to the observed variation. A potential confounder to this finding could be endemic resistance in the soil. Future studies should expand the environmental sampling around the farm in addition to bootsock samples to provide a clearer picture of potential spillover pathways of resistant bacteria into the wider far environment.

Resistance was highest for older, commonly used drugs (ampicillin, sulfamethoxazole-trimethoprim) in the counties sampled, consistent with their widespread use in livestock. Though localized hotspots were noted, gentamicin, enrofloxacin, and cefotaxime had relatively low resistance. Variability between sites may reflect differences in antimicrobial use, farm practices, and environmental factors. Low isolate numbers at some locations brought about by sampling as low as one farm per county limit direct comparisons, but highlight the importance of future broader surveillance.

The present study had limitations, including the lack of antibiotic use history and information on the number of pigs in the sampled farms. Additionally, the resistance was assessed phenotypically; as a result, the underlying determinants of resistance were not identified. Future research should incorporate molecular assays to identify the genetic basis of antibiotic resistance, strengthen the understanding of the spread of resistance within intensive pig farms, and inform targeted interventions. In the present study, no negative control was tested alongside the samples; hence, it was challenging to identify whether resistance originated from pigs or was initially present in the environment before the study. Nonetheless, this study provides valuable baseline data on antimicrobial resistance in pig farm environments in Kenya, which can guide future surveillance and intervention strategies.

Conclusions

To the best of our knowledge, this study is among the first in Kenya to investigate antibiotic resistance in E. coli in intensive pig farming environments. The widespread use of antibiotics in food-producing animals, particularly in pig rearing, is intricately linked to the development and dissemination of the antibiotic resistance crisis. The high antibiotic use in swine farming in Kenya and worldwide promotes the proliferation of resistant bacteria in pigs that spill over into the pig-rearing environments, posing a significant public health threat. One notable route through which humans can be affected by these resistant bacteria is environmental contamination, greatly exacerbating the global public health challenge. E. coli has been documented to have a greater capacity to accumulate antibiotic-resistant genes, primarily through horizontal gene transfer. E. coli might colonize the human intestine and act as reservoirs or opportunistic pathogens later in life. Our findings present that E. coli around intensive pig farms in this study showed resistance against some critically important antibiotics, leading to serious concerns for public health. Compared to isolates from pigs in neighboring countries, resistance in the environmental isolates was generally lower. Measures should be implemented to continue having lower resistance rates and further studies should try to reduce the resistance to those with higher resistance prevalence.

To meet the increasing demand for pork, the number, size and population of pigs in pig farms will have to increase tremendously. This implies that even greater amounts of antibiotics are likely to be applied to support the increase in swine production, increasing the threat even further. Enhanced monitoring and surveillance in pig farms is essential to gain a more comprehensive understanding of the situation. This data will offer extensive insights to deepen the understanding of antibiotic resistance in pig farming, potentially informing the formulation and improvement of governmental policies aimed at mitigating AMR within the Kenyan pig industry.

A limitation of this study is that the resistance was assessed phenotypically; as a result, the underlying determinants of resistance were not identified. Future research should incorporate molecular assays to identify the genetic basis of antibiotic resistance, strengthen the understanding of the spread of resistance within intensive pig farms, and inform targeted interventions. In the present study, no negative control was tested alongside the samples; hence, it was difficult to identify if resistance originated from pigs or was initially present in the environment before the study.
