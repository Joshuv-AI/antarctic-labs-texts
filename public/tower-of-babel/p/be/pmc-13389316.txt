The CARDIOLIPID Plan: A Cardiovascular Risk-centred Implementation Framework for the Diagnosis and Management of Dyslipidaemia: Experts’ Proposal
Authors: Stanisław Surma, Marcin Barylski, Roland E Schmieder, Claudio Borghi, Giuseppe Mancia, Gregory YH Lip, Krzysztof J Filipiak
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Dyslipidaemia remains a major contributor to cardiovascular morbidity and mortality, despite the availability of effective lipid-lowering therapies and well-established European Society of Cardiology/European Atherosclerosis Society guidelines. In clinical practice, the implementation of these recommendations is often suboptimal, with persistent therapeutic inertia and fragmented decision-making processes. The CARDIOLIPID Plan is a proposed cardiovascular risk-centred implementation framework designed to support the practical application of guideline-based care. The model integrates two key components of clinical decision-making: comprehensive cardiovascular risk assessment (CARDIO) and personalised lipid-lowering intervention (LIPID), structured into a sequential and clinically applicable pathway. While the individual elements of the CARDIOLIPID Plan are derived from current evidence and guideline recommendations, their organisation within this framework reflects an interpretation by experts aimed at improving clarity, usability and clinical implementation. The model emphasises early and appropriate treatment intensification, reduction of therapeutic inertia and a holistic, patient-centred approach to cardiovascular risk management. The CARDIOLIPID Plan may serve as a practical tool to enhance the translation of evidence into clinical practice. However, as a conceptual and implementation-oriented framework, it requires validation in real-world settings to assess its impact on treatment outcomes and cardiovascular risk reduction.

Despite robust evidence and clear guideline recommendations, effective implementation of lipid-lowering therapy (LLT) remains suboptimal, largely due to therapeutic inertia and the complexity of cardiovascular risk stratification.1

The aim of this narrative review is to discuss the proposal of the CARDIOLIPID Plan. This approach is not intended to duplicate existing European Society of Cardiology/European Atherosclerosis Society (ESC/EAS) recommendations, but to organise them and translate them into a practical, sequential clinical management pathway. The proposed model is based on current guidelines and available scientific data that has been interpreted and integrated in a way that corresponds to the realities of everyday clinical practice. Its innovation lies in integrating two key aspects of dyslipidaemia care – comprehensive cardiovascular risk assessment (CARDIO) and the selection and intensification of LLT (LIPID) – into a single, operational management model that covers the patient journey from diagnosis of dyslipidaemia to long-term care and cardiovascular risk optimisation. The CARDIOLIPID Plan is consistent with current strategies aimed at reducing therapeutic inertia and improving guideline implementation, which, among other things, emphasise the need for simple management pathways that would ideally be published as one-page guidelines.2,3

A good example is the MASTER Plan, which organises the management of arterial hypertension into several steps in accordance with the European Society of Hypertension guidelines (ESH 2023).4 Another good example is the CC To ABC approach dedicated to the management of AF.5 To date, there has been no similar pathway for dyslipidaemia management. Therefore, the CARDIOLIPID Plan should be considered an implementation tool that shows not only what to do, based on the interpretation of guidelines and scientific evidence, but also how to translate recommendations into coherent and practical clinical decisions. It should be emphasised that the CARDIOLIPID Plan does not replace the need for a thorough reading of current international and national guidelines on dyslipidaemia management.

Why Do We Need a Cardiovascular Risk-centred Pathway for Dyslipidaemia?

Atherosclerotic cardiovascular disease (ASCVD), including coronary artery disease (CAD), ischaemic stroke and peripheral arterial disease (PAD), remains the leading cause of morbidity and mortality worldwide, despite advances in diagnostics, pharmacotherapy and preventive methods.6,7 Among the risk factors for ASCVD, elevated LDL cholesterol (LDL-C) levels have been the most prominent for decades.8

Epidemiological forecasts indicate that between 2025 and 2050, elevated LDL-C will be the second most common cardiovascular (CV) risk factor worldwide after arterial hypertension.9 A meta-analysis covering more than 2.5 million participants from 104 countries showed that the prevalence of dyslipidaemia is more than 34%.10 The analysis of data from the Global Burden of Disease Study showed that elevated LDL-C levels were responsible for approximately 3.63 million deaths in 2023, compared to 2.61 million in 1990. They were also responsible for 90.7 million disability-adjusted life years (DALYs), which constituted nearly 21% of the total global cardiovascular disease (CVD) burden, with approximately every fifth CVD-related DALY being due to lipid disorders.11

Lipid-lowering Treatment and Achieving Therapeutic Goals

Interventions aimed at lowering LDL-C levels have been shown to have cardiopreventive and life-extending effects.6,7 LLT has been shown to significantly improve CV outcomes in both primary and secondary prevention patients (Figure 1).12–17

Despite clear scientific evidence demonstrating the efficacy and safety of LLT and the availability of these therapies, LDL-C control remains unsatisfactory. For example, the SANTORINI study indicates that only slightly over 30% of patients at high or very high CV risk achieve the target LDL-C concentration (31.0% and 30.9%, respectively).18 This situation results from many factors, but therapeutic inertia plays a particularly important role, with procrastination when deciding to intensify LLT being a big part of this. In the SANTORINI study, only 37.9% of patients used combination therapy, while the EUROASPIRE V study, which included patients undergoing secondary prevention after a coronary event, demonstrated that nearly 21% of them had de-escalated or discontinued LLT within 6 months of hospitalisation and only 32.3% of men and 23.1% of women achieved their LDL-C targets.18,19 In the study by Seo et al., which included 204,708 participants after percutaneous coronary intervention (PCI), it was shown that therapeutic inertia regarding LLT occurred in over 64% of the study population.20 The results of these studies indicate that strategies to increase the level of implementation of the current European Society of Cardiology/European Atherosclerosis Society (ESC/EAS) guidelines are necessary.

It is also worth emphasising that dyslipidaemia rarely occurs in isolation from other risk factors. In a study of more than 12,000 patients at high CV risk, over 91% had dyslipidaemia, of whom over 43% also had arterial hypertension and overweight/obesity simultaneously.21 The EUROASPIRE V study found that CV risk factors very often coexisted and remained inadequately controlled.19 The coexistence of these risk factors further increases the risk of ASCVD.22 Therefore, in a patient with dyslipidaemia, one should be ‘CV-centric’ rather than ‘LDL-centric’.

The EAS/ESC guidelines provide detailed recommendations for CV risk assessment and lipid management, but their implementation in everyday clinical practice can be difficult.23–25 The complexity of the documents, the multitude of possible treatment paths and the rapid development of new therapies can lead to therapeutic inertia, thus delaying treatment intensification and failing to tailor therapeutic strategies to the individual patient’s risk profile.25 Consequently, there is a need for tools that transparently and intuitively integrate global CV risk assessment with targeted lipid-lowering interventions that are compliant with the current ESC/EAS guidelines.

In response to these challenges, we propose the CARDIOLIPID Plan – a patient-oriented management plan for lipid disorders based on ESC/EAS guidelines, comprising 11 consecutive steps focused on two sections: diagnosis and assessment of CV risk (CARDIO); and personalised intervention (LIPID) (Figure 2). This model integrates evidence-based elements with their clinical interpretation, enabling a smoother transition from risk assessment to therapeutic decision-making with dyslipidaemia patients and reducing therapeutic inertia in daily clinical practice.

C – Category of Cardiovascular Risk

The 2025 update of the ESC/EAS guidelines emphasises that CV risk assessment should use new calculators: SCORE2, SCORE2-OP and SCORE2-Diabetes (class Ib recommendation).23,24 SCORE2 calculators calibrated for the CV risk categories in a given European region should be used to determine risk category as low, moderate, high or very high. This is important because the new calculators enable a more comprehensive assessment of a patient’s CV risk.26 For example, the classic SCORE calculator included the following parameters: sex, age (40–70 years), smoking status, systolic blood pressure and total cholesterol. The SCORE2 calculator, however, includes sex, age (40–89 years), smoking status, systolic blood pressure and non-high density lipoprotein-cholesterol (non-HDL-C). Including non-HDL-C, compared to total cholesterol or even LDL-C, significantly increases the accuracy of a CV risk assessment.24 The use of SCORE2 versus SCORE was associated with significant reclassification of CV risk, for example, in men aged 40–50 years, SCORE classified 97.7% as low-moderate risk, while SCORE2 gave only 32.4% of them the same classification.27 In patients with diabetes, the dedicated SCORE2-Diabetes scale is recommended.26 In patients with pre-existing CVD (former secondary prevention), chronic kidney disease (CKD), genetic/rare lipid disorders or hypertension, CV risk tables (Table 1) should be used.24,26

A – Atherogenic Burden

A thorough lipid profile analysis should not focus solely on LDL-C level. Determining the total burden of atherogenic lipoproteins is important. The atherogenic index of plasma (AIP), a novel logarithmic index that combines fasting triglyceride (TG) and HDL-C concentrations, is worth mentioning. Elevated AIP has been shown to be strongly associated with increased CAD risk, greater severity and poorer prognosis in populations with and without established CAD.28

LDL-C is the primary fraction of atherogenic lipoproteins. The association between LDL-C concentration and ASCVD risk depends on the degree of LDL-C elevation and the duration of exposure to this elevated concentration (cholesterol years and lipaemic years).29 The theoretical cumulative threshold of LDL-C exposure at which acute coronary syndrome (ACS) is more likely to occur has been established at around 8,000 mg/dl-years; this threshold is reached at different ages depending on individual LDL-C levels and duration of exposure.30 For example, the estimated age of onset of ACS in a patient with LDL-C levels of 200, 125 and 80 mg/dl is approximately 30, 60 and 100 years, respectively.30

Therefore, in a patient with untreated heterozygous familial hypercholesterolaemia (HeFH) or lifestyle-related hypercholesterolaemia, the mean age of onset of ACS is 40–45 and 55–60 years, respectively. In the general population it is 70–75 years.31 Unlike LDL-C, determining non-HDL-C and apolipoprotein B (apoB) levels allows for more accurate CV risk stratification. It was shown that apoB was the most potent marker of CV risk (RRR 1.43; 95% CI [1.35–1.51]), LDL-C was the least (RRR 1.25; 95% CI [1.18–1.33]) and non-HDL-C was intermediate (RRR 1.34; 95% CI [1.24–1.44]).32

This is due to the fact that both apoB and non-HDL-C reflect the load of other atherogenic fractions – LDL, very LDL (VLDL) remnants.32 Atherogenic lipoproteins contain one apoB molecule each, hence apoB measurement reflects the true atherogenic burden. It is worth mentioning that a patient with an LDL-C concentration of 70 mg/dl may have small dense LDL (sdLDL), which will consequently be associated with a higher apoB count and greater atherogenicity than would be suggested by the LDL-C level alone.33

This situation often occurs in patients with atherogenic dyslipidaemia as LDL-C levels may not be elevated, but sdLDL are present and the risk of ASCVD remains elevated.33 Non-HDL-C correlates very well with apoB, which can be calculated using the formula: non-HDL-C = total cholesterol minus HDL-C.34 The presence of sdLDL significantly increases the risk of ASCVD, even when the LDL-C concentration is apparently within the normal range.33 The most atherogenic particle is lipoprotein(a) (Lp(a)), the concentration of which is genetically determined in ≥90% of cases.35,36 Lp(a) concentration should be measured in every person at least once in their lifetime.24 Approximately 20% of the population has elevated levels of this highly atherogenic lipoprotein (≥50 mg/dl).35,36 Lp(a) is five times more atherogenic than LDL-C and increases the risk of ASCVD independently of LDL-C.35,36 The 2025 update to the ESC/EAS guidelines indicates that a concentration of Lp(a) ≥50 mg/dl (≥105 nmol/l) warrants a reclassification of CV risk one level higher (class IIb recommendation).24

When analysing the lipid profile, attention should also be paid to HDL-C. Higher HDL-C levels are not necessarily beneficial – the notion that HDL-C is ‘good cholesterol’ should be abandoned, as everything depends on the clinical context.37,38 It is not the level of HDL-C itself, but its functionality that determines the impact on ASCVD risk.37,38 HDL-C most often becomes dysfunctional where chronic inflammation, oxidative stress and metabolic disorders predominate, such as in patients with conditions such as type 2 diabetes, visceral obesity, metabolic syndrome, chronic kidney disease and autoimmune diseases.37,38 Dysfunctional HDL-C loses its anti-atherosclerotic properties and can even become an atherogenic lipoprotein.

Therefore, in individuals with diseases associated with chronic inflammation, high HDL-C levels may not protect against ASCVD risk.37,38 To determine the likelihood of HDL-C dysfunction, the following factors should be considered: elevated TG concentration, TG:HDL-C ratio >2-3, high-sensitivity C-reactive protein >2 mg/l, low apo-AI with normal HDL-C and elevated apoB:non-HDL-C ratio >0.75-0.85.37,38 Considering TGs in the lipid profile analysis is also important, as hypertriglyceridaemia (HTG) increases the risk of ASCVD regardless of the levels of LDL-C, non-HDL-C and HDL-C or the intensity of LLT.39

Step A Summary

Lipid profile analysis should be comprehensive and include all lipid fractions, not just LDL-C.

The diagnostic criteria for familial hypercholesterolaemia (FH) include total cholesterol ≥310 mg/dl and/or LDL-C ≥190 mg/dl, and a history in the patient or their family of premature CVD (<55 years in men and <60 years in women), sudden cardiac death and tendon xanthomas. The Dutch Lipid Clinic Network scale is used to diagnose FH (scoring 3–5 points = possible FH; 6–7 = probable FH; >8 = definite FH).40,41 The Simon Broome Register Group Criteria can also be used to diagnose FH.41 Recent registries underscored the prevalence of FH as 1 in 200–250, translating to almost 1,500 million people worldwide. However, only a minority (approximately 10%) of FH patients are identified early and effectively treated. In most cases, it is mutations in the LDLR gene that cause FH, with mutations in the APOB, PCSK9 and the LDLRAP1 genes causing fewer cases.42 Compared with the general population, the likelihood of FH in patients with CAD, premature CAD or severe hypercholesterolaemia is 10-, 20- and 23-fold higher, respectively.43 In young adults, FH occurs in 1 in 10 patients with ACS; 2 in 10 patients with ACS and a family history of premature CAD; 4 in 10 with ACS and LDL-C ≥160 mg/dl and 6 in 10 patients with ACS, a family history of premature CAD and LDL-C ≥160 mg/dl.43 If FH is suspected, the patient should be referred to a centre specialising in the treatment of lipid disorders.41 It is important to consider cascade screening of close and extended family members of FH patients. This strategy allows for the identification of undiagnosed individuals with this disease and may reduce the risk of ASCVD and death through early diagnosis and implementation of pharmacological intervention.41

Step R Summary

The possibility of FH should be kept in mind when diagnosing lipid disorders and their causes. This is aimed at identifying individuals at significantly higher risk of ASCVD compared to those with ‘classic’ dyslipidaemia.

The 2025 update of the ESC/EAS guidelines emphasises identifying risk factors that warrant reclassification of CV risk one level higher (Box 1).24 This is significant because it allows for a more accurate determination of the actual CV risk burden in a given patient. It is important to identify conditions causing chronic, low-grade inflammation, which often fall outside the scope of routine cardiological assessment. Among these conditions, it is worth remembering less obvious ones, such as periodontitis or recurrent/chronic rhinosinusitis.44–47 When assessing CV risk, it is also worth taking into account the occurrence of cancer, which significantly increases it.48

Secondary causes should always be sought in patients with dyslipidaemia. Comprehensive screening should include dietary habits and physical activity levels, medical conditions, such as obesity, diabetes, Cushing’s syndrome, chronic kidney disease (CKD), autoimmune diseases, hypothyroidism, liver disease, cholestasis (lipoprotein X) and nephrotic syndrome; medications, such as diuretics, steroid hormones, dermatological medications, immunosuppressive medications, anticancer medications, antipsychotics, antivirals and anticonvulsants; and pregnancy (especially the third trimester).49

Step D Summary

It is not enough to simply determine the presence of dyslipidaemia during diagnostics; screening should also be expanded to include secondary causes both diagnosed (which can be discovered by reviewing the patient’s history), and undiagnosed, which, for example, could be discovered by assessing thyroid function.

I – Imaging Trigger

The 2025 update of the ESC/EAS guidelines emphasises the importance of detecting subclinical atherosclerosis. The guidelines indicate that the presence of subclinical coronary atherosclerosis on imaging or an elevated coronary artery calcium score (CACS) on CT should be considered risk modifiers in individuals at intermediate risk or near the treatment threshold (the grey zone) to improve risk classification (class IIa level b recommendation).24 In individuals at intermediate risk or near the treatment threshold, the use of risk modifiers should be considered to clarify their risk classification (class IIa level b recommendation).24

Screening for subclinical atherosclerosis reflects a shift in understanding of its progression. This recognition of atherosclerosis as a continuous and measurable process is seeing the abandonment of the rigid division into primary and secondary prevention. The atherosclerotic process can begin in early childhood and the rate of its development depends on genetic, environmental and behavioural factors.50 The PESA study, for example, which included 4,184 participants aged 41–54 years without documented CVD, showed that 63% had subclinical atherosclerosis (presence of plaque or CACS ≥1), most commonly in the iliofemoral arteries, carotid arteries, abdominal aorta and coronary arteries.51 The presence of subclinical atherosclerosis is independently associated with a higher risk of all-cause mortality.52 Identification of subclinical atherosclerosis and implementation of pharmacological intervention allows for the regression of lesions and reduction of CV risk.6,53

Step I Summary

Subclinical atherosclerosis should be sought in patients to identify those who will benefit most from early therapeutic intervention.

O – Overall CV Risk Integration

Accurate diagnosis and risk factor identification aim to establish the most complete picture of a patient’s CV risk burden. At this stage, clinical, laboratory and imaging data must be integrated and summarised.

The MESA study clearly demonstrated that including both classic and non-classic risk factors in ASCVD risk stratification allows for significantly more accurate CV risk stratification, which clearly translates into more appropriate interventions.54,55

Contemporary assessment of overall CV risk forms the basis for appropriate CVD prevention and treatment. CV risk is estimated for four primary reasons: to determine the intensity of the necessary intervention, for example lifestyle intervention; to decide on the initiation of pharmacotherapy, for example in a patient with dyslipidaemia or hypertension, and determining necessary drug doses; to establish therapeutic goals; and patient education.7,23,24

Step O Summary

The patient’s overall CV risk should be determined as precisely as possible.

LIPID – Personalised Intervention

Within the CARDIOLIPID Plan, the LIPID section represents a therapeutic extension of the assessment conducted in the CARDIO section. Recommendations regarding treatment goals and non-pharmacological and pharmacological strategies are based on current ESC/EAS guidelines and clinical trial results. However, within the CARDIOLIPID Plan, it is crucial to organise them in a way that allows for a direct transition from risk assessment to therapeutic decision-making. This structure, interpreted by the authors, aims to reduce treatment delays and increase consistency in daily clinical practice.

When planning an intervention, the focus should obviously not only focus on LLT but also on optimising (and targeting) other CV risk factors identified in the first part of the plan (CARDIO) and, if necessary, referring the patient for necessary specialist consultations.7 Among other things, it is necessary to maintain a normal body weight (BMI 20–25 kg/m2 and waist circumference <94 cm in men and <80 cm in women), blood pressure <140/90 mmHg (optimally <130/80 mmHg if well tolerated) and normal glycaemia (HbA1c <7%).23

Step L Summary

Therapeutic goals should be established and the type of intervention needed – non-pharmacological and/or pharmacological – should be planned.

I – Improved Lifestyle

Lifestyle improvement is the basis of all therapeutic interventions, not only in lipid disorders, and should be introduced at the earliest possible stage for the best results.7 Cigarette smoking in any form should be discontinued, dietary habits should be improved and the intake of products rich in saturated fats should be reduced in favour of wholegrain products, vegetables, fruits and fish. Moderately vigorous physical activity should be introduced for 3.5–7 hours a week or 30–60 minutes most days.23 Diets recommended for patients with dyslipidaemia include: Mediterranean, Dietary Approaches to Stop Hypertension (DASH), vegetarian and vegan.56 The diet should avoid: saturated fats and trans fats (e.g. animal and tropical oils), processed meat, refined grains and added sugars, highly processed foods with a high salt content, alcohol and large amounts of unfiltered coffee in favour of filtered coffee in moderate amounts.56–59 Due to the presence of elevated Lp(a) as the only indication, a ketogenic diet should not be used as it may lead to exacerbation of dyslipidaemia, an increased risk of diabetes and CV death.60

In the context of physical activity, the simplest forms, such as walking, should be encouraged. It has been clearly demonstrated that every 1,000-step increase taken daily can lead to a 15% reduction in the risk of death, while every 500-step increase can lead to a 7% reduction in the risk of CV death.61 Increasing physical activity contributes to better control of lipid profile, blood pressure, body weight and a reduction in major adverse CV events (MACE).62

As part of a healthy lifestyle, patients often use various dietary supplements. According to the updated ESC/EAS 2025 guidelines, the use of dietary supplements or vitamins without documented safety and significant efficacy in lowering LDL-C levels is not recommended to reduce CV risk (class IIIb recommendation).24 Supplementing healthy eating habits with bergamot or red yeast rice supplements may contribute to an additional reduction in LDL-C.63

It should be remembered that implementing simple, and realistic recommendations for lifestyle modifications for the patient (as opposed to complex lifestyle interventions) increases the likelihood of their implementation and long-term maintenance.64

Step I Summary

Lifestyle improvement should be implemented through simple actions (‘the easier, the better’), to enhance the chances of the patient making changes that continue into the long term.

P – Pharmacological Approaches

The essence of pharmacological intervention is to minimise lifetime exposure to elevated LDL-C levels. To achieve this, LLT should be conducted according to three principles: ‘the earlier, the better’, ‘the lower, the better’ and ‘the longer, the better’.7

The first principle stems from the fact that individuals genetically predisposed to lower LDL-C levels have an approximately 50–55% lower lifetime risk of CAD. Pharmacological treatment of adults with hypercholesterolaemia, in turn, is associated with a reduction in CAD risk by approximately 22%. This means that maintaining low LDL-C levels earlier and for longer will yield significantly greater cumulative CV benefits.65 It is worth recalling the results of a study by Lee et al., which demonstrated that delaying the initiation of LLT by 3 years in individuals with incidentally detected hypercholesterolaemia resulted in a 61% higher risk of MI (HR 1.61; 95% CI [1.37–1.89]).66 Procrastination should be strictly avoided when making the decision to start LLT.6,7 LLT will always provide benefits in the form of CV risk reduction, but the longer it is delayed, the smaller these benefits will be.67

The second principle closely relates to the fact that there is no plateau effect in the relationship between LDL-C reduction and CV risk reduction. This suggests that there is no clear threshold below which further benefits of LDL-C lowering are no longer observed.68 A good example is the study by Patti et al., which demonstrated that intensively treated patients, who were achieving an LDL-C concentration of <40 mg/dl, demonstrated a further reduction in CV risk (by 18%; OR = 0.82; 95% CI [0.72–0.94]), accompanied by a good safety profile.16 The FOURIER study demonstrated a continuous linear relationship between LDL-C level and the risk of CV events with no safety threshold, even at LDL-C <20 mg/dl.69 Clinical trials have shown that intensive LDL-C reduction is safe, with a positive balance of benefits.70 Regardless of whether a lower LDL-C concentration is genetically determined or as a result of intervention, a 1 mmol/l reduction in LDL-C will translate into a 20–25% reduction in the risk of MACE.71

The first and second principles are reflected in the results of the study by Patti et al. It showed that the implementation of a personalised ‘strike early and strong’ lipid-lowering strategy after ACS significantly increased achievement of the LDL-C target concentration and reduced 1-year MACE compared with a stepwise approach, with the greatest benefit observed in patients achieving early and sustained LDL reduction.72

The third principle concerns long-term maintenance of the target LDL-C concentration. Each 1 mmol/l reduction in serum LDL-C concentration was associated with a 12% (95% CI [8–16%]) reduction in the risk of MACE in the first year, 20% (95% CI [16–24%]) in the third year, 23% (95% CI [18–27%]) in the fifth year and 29% (95% CI [14–42%]) in the seventh year of LLT.73

The results ofthe study by Mahzar et al., which included 20,490 patients with CAD, demonstrated that each improvement in adherence to LLT (calculated for each 10% increase in adherence) translated into a 6% reduction in the risk of MACE (HR 0.94; 95% CI [0.92–0.96]), while each increase in treatment intensity (calculated for each 10% increase in intensity) was associated with an 8% reduction in the risk of MACE (HR 0.92; 95% CI [0.90–0.94]). The cumulative effect led to a 9% reduction in the risk of MACE (HR 0.91; 95% CI [0.89–0.93]). It should be emphasised that discontinuing therapy for ≥1 year resulted in a 66% increase in the risk of MACE (HR 1.66; 95% CI [1.52–1.81]). Hence, in the context of CV prevention, only long-term, consistent adherence to LLT is effective.74

Pharmacological treatment is an integral part of the treatment of lipid disorders.7,23,24 Before starting LLT, creatine kinase and alanine aminotransferase activity should be measured and carbohydrate metabolism (HbA1c; fasting blood glucose) should be considered.7

Pharmacological treatment follows a stepwise strategy. High-intensity statin therapy, administered at the maximally tolerated dose, remains the first-line treatment (mean reduction in baseline LDL-C: 50%). If LDL-C targets are not achieved (after 4–6 weeks), combination therapy is recommended, initially with the addition of ezetimibe (mean reduction in baseline LDL-C: 60%), followed by a proprotein convertase subtilisin/kexin type 9 inhibitor (PCSK9i; mean reduction in baseline LDL-C: 80%).23,24 Bempedoic acid, an ATP-citrate lyase inhibitor, can also be used to intensify therapy as it reduces endogenous cholesterol production only in the liver and it does not affect this process in muscles.75 Its addition to the maximally tolerated dose of statin therapy, with or without ezetimibe, should be considered in patients at high or very high CV risk to achieve the recommended LDL-C targets (class IIa level c recommendation).24 Bempedoic acid, in combination with a high-intensity statin and/or ezetimibe, reduces LDL-C by 60% and 70%, respectively.75

Upfront initiation of combination therapy with a statin and ezetimibe should be considered in patients hospitalised for ACS who have not previously received LLT and in whom attainment of LDL-C targets with statin monotherapy is unlikely (class IIa level b recommendation).24 The SWEDEHEART study showed that in patients with ACS, early initiation of combination therapy with statins and ezetimibe should be standard care, because delaying the use of combination LLT or using high-intensity statin monotherapy is associated with avoidable harm.76

In patients with documented ASCVD, LLT should be initiated with a combination therapy of a high-intensity statin at the maximum tolerated dose with ezetimibe in the form of a single-pill combination (SPC), regardless of the baseline LDL-C concentration.6,7,29 In patients at extreme risk, it is recommended to initiate triple combination therapy (high-intensity statin at the maximum tolerated dose + ezetimibe + PCSK9i) as soon as possible.29,77 Ideally, combination therapy should be based on an SPC formula. It has been shown that the use of an SPC was associated with improved compliance, adherence and persistence, which in turn contributed to greater optimisation of CV risk.78

Step P Summary

Step P is based on establishing therapeutic goals and careful planning of pharmacological treatment.

I – Individualisation of Lipid-lowering Therapy

Statin Intolerance

In patients with confirmed statin intolerance – most often in the form of statin-associated muscle symptoms (SAMS) – it is recommended to use the SAMS-CI scale. Before deciding to discontinue treatment, the following should be done: reduce the statin dose (for example, rosuvastatin 20 mg instead of 40 mg; switch the statin (for example from rosuvastatin to atorvastatin); and recommend taking the statin every other day.7,23,24,29 If the patient knows a priori that reducing the statin dose will lead to suboptimal LDL-C control, SPC statin at a moderate/low dose with ezetimibe may be considered. It has been shown that an SPC of a moderate/low-intensity statin with ezetimibe versus high-intensity statin monotherapy can lead to greater LDL-C reduction with the therapeutic target achieved more often, better adherence and persistence and a significantly lower likelihood of descaling or discontinuing treatment.29 To reduce the likelihood of nocebo effects (symptoms due to negative expectations) and drucebo effects (symptoms due to the medication itself), before starting LLT, the patient should be educated about the benefits and possible side effects of these medications and any questions regarding the treatment should be addressed.29

The 2025 update of the ESC/EAS guidelines emphasises the role of non-statin LLTs with proven CV benefit in patients with statin intolerance and in clinical scenarios requiring further LDL-C reduction. Bempedoic acid is recommended for patients unable to tolerate statins (class Ib recommendation).24 Combination therapy of bempedoic acid with ezetimibe or ezetimibe with PCSK9i or bempedoic acid with ezetimibe and PCSK9i allows for LDL-C reduction by 40%, 70% and 75%, respectively.7,29

In patients with statin intolerance, pitavastatin can be used, which is characterised by a lower risk of muscle symptoms.79,80

Familial Hypercholesterolaemia

Evinacumab, a recombinant human monoclonal antibody that inhibits angiopoietin-like protein 3, should be considered in patients with HoFH from the age of 5 years who fail to achieve LDL-C target levels despite treatment with maximal doses of other lipid-lowering agents.24

Hypertriglyceridaemia

In the management of HTG, optimisation of LDL-C-related risk reduction through statin therapy and lifestyle modification remains fundamental. In patients at high or very high CV risk with TG concentrations of 135–499 mg/dl (1.52–5.63 mmol/l), the addition of eicosapentaenoic acid to statin therapy should be considered to reduce CV risk. Ifthis therapeutic approach remains insufficient, fibrate therapy should be added.24 Severe HTG remains the only clinical indication for which fibrates constitute first-line therapy.24 Volanesorsen, an antisense oligonucleotide that inhibits apolipoprotein C-III synthesis, should be considered in patients with severe HTG (>750 mg/dl or >8.5 mmol/l), due to familial chylomicronaemia syndrome, to reduce TG levels and the risk of acute pancreatitis.24

In the context of HTG treatment, it is worth remembering that adding fenofibrate to a statin in patients with diabetes and atherogenic dyslipidaemia in the ACCORDION study led to a 27% reduction in the risk of CV events (HR 0.73; 95% CI [0.56–0.95]).81 The TRIUMPH study, including 70,000 patients with no history of ASCVD, showed that fenofibrate use was associated with a 24% lower ASCVD risk with elevated triglyceride-rich lipoprotein cholesterol (total cholesterol minus HDL-C minus LDL-C ≥33.8 mg/dl) despite statin therapy.82 Calculating triglyceride-rich lipoprotein cholesterol may allow for the identification of patients who may benefit from the addition of fenofibrate to therapy. Moreover, in patients with metabolic syndrome, the use of fenofibrate versus low doses of Ω3 fatty acids was associated with a greater reduction in the risk of MACE by 21% (HR 0.79; 95% CI [0.74–0.83]).83 This is important because the use of high doses of Ω3 fatty acids (recommended 4 g/day) may increase the risk of new cases of AF by up to 25%.84

Special Groups of Patients

The ESC/EAS guidelines also recommend statin therapy for primary prevention in individuals living with HIV aged ≥40 years, irrespective of estimated CV risk and baseline LDL-C levels, to reduce the incidence of CV events. Statin selection in this population should be guided by the potential for drug–drug interactions and pitavastatin is preferred.24,80 Further, the updated guidelines recommend consideration of statin therapy in oncology patients at high risk of cardiotoxicity, particularly in those receiving anthracycline-based chemotherapy.24

Chronic Kidney Disease

Lipid abnormalities in patients with CKD are primarily qualitative, not quantitative.85 The primary medications used to treat hypercholesterolaemia in patients with CKD stages 1–5 not undergoing dialysis are statins combined with ezetimibe. This therapy should not be initiated in patients with CKD stage 5 undergoing dialysis, since no benefit was found in the AURORA study, most likely because it was too late to start therapy. If this therapy was used before dialysis was initiated, it can be continued. Statins and/or ezetimibe can be used in patients who have had kidney transplantation.85 When prescribing statins to patients with CKD, atorvastatin may be preferred over rosuvastatin due to the results of the PLANET study that found it had greater nephroprotective benefits.86 Fibrates improve the lipid profile and reduce CV risk in CKD patients, but may increase serum creatinine levels. Due to the risk of muscle damage in CKD patients, fibrates should not be combined with statins.85

Pregnancy

Statins, which are the cornerstone of therapy for FH, are generally discontinued during preconception planning, pregnancy and breastfeeding due to longstanding concerns about potential teratogenic effects. Although subsequent research has alleviated some of these concerns, routine statin use during pregnancy is still not advised. Exceptions may be made in situations of extreme CV risk, such as HoFH or advanced ASCVD, and even then treatment should only be considered after the first trimester. Discontinuation of statin therapy is recommended 1 to 3 months prior to conception.87 Ezetimibe should likewise be avoided during pregnancy, as sufficient safety data in this population are lacking.87

PCSK9i, which are monoclonal antibodies including evolocumab and alirocumab, are capable of crossing the placental barrier. Due to their potential impact on fetal development, particularly neural tube formation, these agents are not recommended during pregnancy or lactation and should be stopped before conception.87

Bile acid sequestrants are considered a safer lipid-lowering option in pregnancy because they are not systemically absorbed. However, their use may interfere with the absorption of fat-soluble vitamins and folic acid, necessitating supplementation in some cases. Additionally, these agents can worsen HTG, which may pose additional risks during pregnancy.87

For women at very high risk – such as those with severe hypercholesterolaemia, HoFH or established ASCVD – lipoprotein apheresis may be considered. This approach is regarded as both safe and effective for reducing LDL-C and Lp(a) levels during pregnancy, although its availability is limited in many settings.87

Inclisiran, a small interfering RNA (siRNA)-based PCSK9i, represents a theoretical alternative, as it is rapidly cleared from circulation while producing sustained LDL-C reductions lasting up to 6 months. Nevertheless, its use during pregnancy has not been studied and further research is required before it can be recommended in this context.87

In cases of severe dyslipidaemia, initiation of bile acid sequestrant therapy may be appropriate. Although statin treatment – particularly with pravastatin – may be considered after the second trimester if LDL-C levels remain markedly elevated, such decisions should follow a careful assessment of risks and benefits. Ideally, management should be coordinated between the patient’s obstetrician and a lipid specialist. This approach should be reserved exclusively for women with very high CV risk, specifically those with FH and/or ASCVD.87

Other Groups

Some patients may be concerned about high doses of high-intensity statins due to the potential increased risk of new-onset diabetes (NODM). However, it is worth emphasising that the risk of NODM is very small and does not outweigh the CV benefits.79,80 In such patients, moderate doses of high-intensity statins, pitavastatin or bempedoic acid, may be considered.79 Pitavastatin has been shown to lower the risk of NODM compared to atorvastatin and rosuvastatin, while bempedoic acid may even reduce the risk of NODM.79,80

Bempedoic acid has the strongest anti-inflammatory effect among other lipid-lowering drugs (20% reduction in C-reactive protein level), which may further optimise the residual risk resulting from low-grade inflammation.88

Step I Summary

LLT should be individualised for the patient wherever possible.

D – Durable Integrated Risk Management

Achieving target LDL-C levels should not be the endpoint in the management of patients with dyslipidaemia. It is important to remember that achieving the target non-HDL-C level is equally important. It has been shown that patients who achieved the target LDL-C level but did not achieve the non-HDL-C goal had a higher CV risk.89 As shown in the PAMELA study, CV risk factors are closely correlated, therefore, they should be actively sought in patients with dyslipidaemia.90 The better the level of control of overall risk factors, the greater the CV risk reduction.16 Lipid profile and the degree of control of other CV risk factors should be monitored annually. CV risk should also be reassessed as age, risk factors and comorbidities change.7,23,24,26 A high percentage of patients with dyslipidaemia are not persistent in therapy and after initiating LLT, the percentage of patients using it gradually decreases. To improve persistence, education should be provided on the nature of the disease and the benefits of treatment, therapy regimens should be simplified as much as possible (in the case of combination therapy, SPC is preferable), reported adverse events should be addressed and necessary therapy modifications implemented, and patients should be regularly followed up.91

To reduce the degree of therapeutic inertia, it is necessary to: conduct regular training; integrate clinical decision support tools; use treatment pathways, such as the CARDIOLIPID Plan; involve pharmacists, nurses, health educators and psychologists in the diagnostic and therapeutic process; regularly perform clinical assessments of the patient; increase the patient’s knowledge and involvement in the self-management process and simplify treatment regimens, avoiding polypharmacy where possible.3

Step D Summary

Long-term care of patients with dyslipidaemia should be ensured, compliance, adherence and persistence should be maximally increased, and therapeutic inertia should be reduced as much as possible.

Practical Implementation of the CARDIOLIPID Plan

The CARDIOLIPID Plan can be used from various entry points, such as newly diagnosed dyslipidaemia, failure to achieve the target LDL-C level, the presence of multiple CV risk factors or a previous CV event. This reflects real-world clinical conditions, where the diagnostic and therapeutic process is rarely linear.

In practice, the CARDIO section can be considered a risk assessment module, encompassing risk category estimation, atherogenic burden assessment, risk modifier identification and, where appropriate, the use of imaging methods.

While these elements are derived from the guidelines, their arrangement within the CARDIOLIPID Plan reflects the authors’ interpretation, aimed at increasing clarity and usefulness in the clinical setting.

A key feature of the proposed model is the direct link between risk assessment and therapeutic decisions. After integrating data from the CARDIO section, the physician moves on to the LIPID section, which includes defining treatment goals, implementing lifestyle interventions, initiating LLT and further intensifying it. This approach supports the early use of appropriately intensive therapy, including combination therapy, in high- and very high-risk patients, which can help reduce therapeutic inertia. At the same time, the CARDIOLIPID Plan should be considered a dynamic process, enabling iterative risk assessment and treatment modifications throughout the care process.

This model can be applied in various clinical settings, including outpatient and inpatient care, as well as in multidisciplinary care. It can also serve as a foundation for developing local management pathways and integrating with digital tools that support clinical decisions.

Clinical Implications, Potential Barriers and Limitations of the CARDIOLIPID Plan

Current guidelines provide broad, evidence-based recommendations for both CV risk assessment and LLT.23,24 However, their practical application may be hampered by the complexity, dispersion of information (information is scattered throughout the text and across tables and figures, making it difficult to quickly find information, thus reducing its usefulness in everyday medical practice) and the need to simultaneously consider multiple decision-making pathways.1,25 The complexity of guidelines is one of the most important causes of diagnostic and therapeutic inertia, which is a common phenomenon in dyslipidaemia management, despite worsening patient prognosis.1–3,25,29,92–94 In this context, the CARDIOLIPID Plan proposes integrating these elements into a single, coherent and transparent management pathway, which may facilitate dyslipidaemia management and contribute to reducing diagnostic and therapeutic inertia.

Clearly linking risk stratification to therapeutic decisions promotes earlier treatment implementation and faster intensification in patients who do not achieve lipid goals. Creating algorithms and diagnostic and therapeutic pathways is one of the key methods for improving guideline implementation and reducing diagnostic and therapeutic inertia.1–3,25,92,93

Another important element of the CARDIOLIPID Plan is its focus on overall CV risk, rather than solely on individual lipid parameters. This approach allows for better consideration of comorbidities, risk modifiers and the dynamics ofthe atherosclerotic process. This supports a holistic approach to preventive cardiology.

The CARDIOLIPID Plan can be used in multidisciplinary care and serves as a foundation for developing local management pathways. It can serve as a starting point for integration with digital tools such as clinical decision support systems or electronic medical records, which may further enhance its use in clinical practice in the future.

It should be reiterated that the CARDIOLIPID Plan should be interpreted as a conceptual and implementation framework, not as a formally validated clinical algorithm. The proposed model is based on the current ESC/EAS guidelines and available scientific evidence, but has not yet been prospectively validated in clinical trials.23,24 Therefore, its impact on achieving lipid targets, reducing diagnostic and therapeutic inertia and improving cardiovascular outcomes requires further confirmation in prospective studies and real-world analyses.

It should also be emphasised that some elements of the CARDIOLIPID Plan reflect the authors’ interpretation of the diagnostic and therapeutic process, rather than direct recommendations derived from the guidelines. Therefore, this model should be considered a supplement to the guidelines, not an alternative.

Organisational factors, including limited time in outpatient settings, workload and differences in local healthcare systems, may limit implementation of the CARDIOLIPID Plan in clinical practice. The availability of some diagnostic methods, such as imaging of subclinical atherosclerosis or apoB and Lp(a) testing, may be limited, which affects the ability to fully assess risk in all patients.29 Access to therapy is also a significant limitation.29 Modern LLTs, including PCSK9i and inclisiran, may be limited by cost, reimbursement policies and differences between healthcare systems.29 These factors can significantly impact the ability to achieve therapeutic goals, despite a well-planned approach. Despite these limitations, the CARDIOLIPID Plan can be a useful tool supporting the implementation of recommendations in clinical practice and a starting point for further validation studies and the development of clinical decision support tools.

Conclusion

The CARDIOLIPID Plan represents a comprehensive, CV-risk-centred approach to the diagnosis and management of dyslipidaemia, translating contemporary ESC/EAS guidelines into a structured and clinically intuitive pathway. By integrating global CV risk assessment, advanced lipid profiling, risk modifiers, imaging triggers and personalised therapeutic strategies, it moves beyond an LDL-centric paradigm towards truly CV-centric prevention.

The stepwise CARDIOLIPID framework facilitates early risk identification, reduces therapeutic inertia and supports timely initiation and intensification of LLT, including upfront combination treatment where appropriate. Importantly, it promotes long-term, durable risk management through individualisation of therapy, optimisation of adherence and continuous reassessment of CV risk over time.

By offering a pragmatic, evidence-based and patient-oriented tool, the CARDIOLIPID Plan has the potential to improve implementation of guideline-recommended care, enhance lipid target attainment and ultimately reduce the burden of atherosclerotic CV disease.

The CARDIOLIPID Plan is an implementation framework based on current guidelines and scientific evidence, enriched with the authors’ interpretation of the diagnostic and therapeutic process. Its value lies in integrating cardiovascular risk assessment with therapeutic decisions in a clear, sequential pathway.

The proposed model can help reduce therapeutic inertia, improve the achievement of lipid targets and increase consistency in daily clinical practice. Although it requires further prospective validation, it could be a useful tool supporting the implementation of recommendations in real-world clinical settings.
