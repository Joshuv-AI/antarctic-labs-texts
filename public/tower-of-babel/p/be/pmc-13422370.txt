Aspiration to architecture: multi-omics, AI, digital twins, and blockchain for P4 medicine
Authors: Alex E Mohr, Anil Bajnath, Gail King, Thomas E Ichim, Paniz Jasbi
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractTwo decades after its formalization, P4 medicine (predictive, preventive, personalized, participatory) remains more framework than practice. Most implementations stall at single-omics prediction and fail to close the loop across all four dimensions. In this Perspective, we argue that the P4 framework becomes actionable only when each pillar is anchored to a specific, implementable digital technology: multi-omics for prediction, artificial intelligence for prevention, digital twinning for personalization, and blockchain for participation. We propose a tiered multi-omics classification (Tier 1: genomics, measured once; Tier 2: epigenomics/proteomics, periodic; Tier 3: metabolomics/wearables, frequent) and present preliminary metabolomic aging data from 2,072 individuals identifying nine metabolites with linear age associations. We offer a computational definition of personalization requiring baseline state estimation, trajectory prediction, and counterfactual intervention simulation via stochastic digital twin engines. For the participatory pillar, we describe a blockchain architecture enabling patient-controlled data sovereignty and a health data marketplace. These four technologies form a reinforcing flywheel, where longitudinal patient participation enriches upstream data layers. We discuss validation challenges, regulatory gaps, equity concerns, and privacy risks that must be addressed before clinical deployment.

Introduction

Two decades after Hood and colleagues formalized the vision of P4 medicine (predictive, preventive, personalized, and participatory), the framework remains more aspiration than operation [1, 2]. The core thesis is straightforward: molecular profiling should predict disease before symptoms appear, computational tools should guide prevention, treatment should be tailored to the individual, and patients should be active stakeholders in their own health data. Yet translating these principles into scalable clinical workflows has proven elusive. Most implementations stall at the first P (prediction via single-omics panels) and rarely address the systemic architecture required to close the loop across all four dimensions [3]. The Pioneer 100 / Arivale program, the most ambitious early P4 implementation combining whole-genome sequencing, clinical labs, gut microbiome, metabolomics, and wearable data with health coaching, ultimately ceased operations, illustrating that scientific ambition without sustainable digital infrastructure cannot scale [4].

Recent perspectives have attempted to bridge this gap. Emmert-Streib and colleagues proposed digital twins as a paradigm for P4 medicine, mapping each pillar onto a conceptual twin architecture [5]. Their contribution clarifies the theoretical alignment between digital twins and P4 but provides no implementation evidence: no cohort data, no simulation outputs, no validation framework. The distance between such conceptual mappings and deployable systems remains the central bottleneck in the field.

In this Perspective, we argue that the P4 framework becomes actionable only when each pillar is anchored to a specific, implementable digital technology: multi-omics for prediction, artificial intelligence for prevention, digital twinning for personalization, and blockchain for participation (Fig. 1). We draw on preliminary metabolomic data from a cross-sectional cohort of 2,072 individuals, emerging AI pipelines for biomarker discovery, the architecture of stochastic digital twin engines, and a Health Insurance Portability and Accountability Act (HIPAA)-compliant blockchain proof-of-concept for patient-controlled data exchange. Our aim is not to review these fields exhaustively but to demonstrate, with concrete examples, how the four technologies interlock to form a closed-loop P4 system that moves beyond aspiration to architecture.

Predictive: Multi-omics as the molecular foundation

The term “multi-omics” is used loosely in the literature, sometimes applied to studies combining two data types with modest sample sizes [6]. We propose a minimum standard: multi-omics requires the simultaneous integration of at least three molecular layers measured from the same biological sample or individual, with analytical validation sufficient for clinical interpretation. This threshold reflects the principle that cross-compartment interactions, from genome through proteome to metabolome, require a minimum of three measurement layers to capture regulatory cascades that dual-omics designs inherently miss [7]. This definition excludes studies that merely concatenate published datasets across different cohorts.

A useful operational framework stratifies omics by measurement frequency and temporal dynamics [7, 8] (Table 1). Tier 1 (measure once) encompasses whole-genome sequencing, the stable genetic blueprint providing risk architecture and pharmacogenomic context [9]. Tier 2 (measure periodically) includes epigenomics, transcriptomics, and proteomics, which shift over months to years and track disease trajectories and biological aging [10, 11]. Tier 3 (measure frequently) comprises metabolomics, clinical laboratories, wearable sensor data, and the microbiome, rapidly fluctuating layers reflecting acute physiological state, diet, and lifestyle [4, 12]. This tiering directly informs clinical implementation: genomics is a one-time foundational investment, whereas metabolomics panels can serve as recurring health monitors analogous to blood work but with far greater molecular depth. Population-level metabolomic reference maps now establish baseline distributions against which individual deviations become clinically interpretable [13]. At population scale, the UK Biobank has released nuclear magnetic resonance (NMR) metabolomic profiles for approximately 500,000 participants alongside proteomic and genomic data, enabling multi-omic disease prediction models that outperform single-layer approaches [14]. The NIH All of Us Research Program is assembling multi-omic data, including whole-genome sequencing for over 245,000 diverse participants, demonstrating population-scale feasibility of integrated molecular profiling [15].

Multi-omics tier classification for clinical implementation

Metabolomics occupies a distinctive position in this hierarchy. As the most downstream omics layer, it integrates the effects of genome, transcriptome, proteome, microbiome, diet, and environment into a single measurable readout [12]. As a proof-of-concept, we present preliminary data from a targeted liquid chromatography–tandem mass spectrometry (LC-MS/MS) panel quantifying calibrated metabolites from dried blood spots collected as research-use-only samples in a cross-sectional cohort of 2,072 individuals (aged 18–85 years; 57% female; excluding users of glucose-lowering, lipid-modifying, immunosuppressive, and antipsychotic medications). In this nascent dataset, nine metabolites exhibited approximately linear associations with chronological age across adults [16] (Fig. 2). These nine aging axes span mitochondrial redox, one-carbon metabolism, and branched-chain amino acid catabolism, providing mechanistic anchors rather than opaque risk scores. A composite metabolic age derived from these axes yields a residual, metabolic age minus chronological age, that defines metabolic resilience: the capacity of a metabolic system to withstand perturbation and maintain function [8]. We present these results as an illustration of the type of metabolomic aging signature that Tier 3 profiling can generate, not as a validated clinical biomarker panel. Several limitations of this proof-of-concept warrant explicit acknowledgement. First, the cross-sectional design precludes inference about within-individual rates of change; longitudinal resampling will be required to confirm that the identified aging axes track metabolic change within persons rather than merely differing across age strata, and to distinguish age-related drift from birth-cohort effects. Second, the cohort was profiled on a single targeted LC-MS/MS platform and has not yet been replicated in an independent, external cohort, so generalizability of effect sizes and the stability of the composite metabolic age estimator across analytical platforms, sample matrices, and source populations remain to be established. Third, demographic representation is limited and ancestry-stratified analyses have not yet been performed; the medication-exclusion criteria, while reducing pharmacologic confounding, may have selected a healthier-than-average subpopulation, and the dried-blood-spot research-use-only matrix has not been benchmarked against plasma or serum on the same metabolite panel. These constraints reinforce that the nine-metabolite signature is presented here as a methodological illustration of Tier 3 profiling, not as a clinically actionable biomarker set, and they define the immediate next steps for analytical and biological validation.

This metabolomic aging signature complements epigenetic clocks such as GrimAge [17] and DunedinPACE [18], which quantify biological age and its pace of change through DNA methylation. However, methylation clocks reflect cumulative epigenetic drift, while metabolomic markers capture the functional state of metabolism in real time, a distinction critical for monitoring intervention response at Tier 3 frequency. The integration of multiple clock types (epigenetic, proteomic, metabolomic) improves prediction of healthspan outcomes beyond any single layer [19]. Multi-omics prediction, therefore, is not about choosing the best single layer but about integrating the stable architecture of Tier 1 with the dynamic functional readout of Tier 3 through the trajectory-tracking capacity of Tier 2.

Preventive: AI for actionable risk stratification

Prediction without actionable prevention is surveillance, not medicine. The preventive dimension of P4 requires computational models that translate multi-omic signals into intervention decisions, and this is where AI earns its clinical utility, but also where the greatest risks of misapplication emerge.

The AI methods most suited to multi-omic preventive medicine are not the most fashionable. Gradient-boosted trees (XGBoost) remain among the highest-performing algorithms for tabular biomarker data, consistently matching or exceeding deep learning in structured clinical datasets [20], while offering native feature importance ranking that is directly interpretable by clinicians. Elastic net regularization provides principled feature selection when the number of analytes exceeds the number of subjects, balancing sparsity with grouped variable retention [21]. SHAP (SHapley Additive exPlanations) values offer post hoc interpretability, decomposing each prediction into per-feature contributions and enabling clinicians to understand why a model flags a particular patient [22]. Directed acyclic graphs (DAGs) and Bayesian network approaches move beyond correlation to encode causal structure, a prerequisite for designing interventions rather than merely predicting outcomes [23].

These methods are well-validated in controlled research settings. The critical gap is deployment governance. The United States Food and Drug Administration has cleared over 800 AI/ML-enabled medical devices, yet the majority received 510(k) clearance without prospective validation in diverse populations [24, 25]. Radiology dominates (> 70% of approvals); multi-omic preventive health applications remain scarce. The EU Artificial Intelligence Act classifies medical AI as high-risk, requiring conformity assessments, human oversight, and robustness testing before market placement [26]. Minimum reporting standards, including CONSORT-AI for randomized trials [27], SPIRIT-AI for trial protocols [28], and the forthcoming TRIPOD-AI for prediction model studies [29], provide the structural framework for transparent clinical AI evaluation. These reporting guidelines represent the floor, not the ceiling, of responsible deployment.

A less discussed but equally urgent concern is the misuse of AI by practitioners without adequate training in model development, validation, and interpretation. Off-the-shelf machine learning libraries have dramatically lowered the barrier to building predictive models, creating a proliferation of poorly validated clinical algorithms deployed without external validation, calibration assessment, or fairness auditing. The consequences are not hypothetical: a widely used healthcare risk algorithm exhibited racial bias affecting approximately 70 million patients annually because it used healthcare cost as a proxy for health need [30]. Biological age calculators built from small convenience samples, proprietary “health scores” derived from single-omics panels without disclosed methodology, and large language models generating clinical recommendations with fabricated references [31] all represent failure modes of undisciplined AI deployment.

The concept of health trajectories, modeling an individual’s multi-omic profile as a time series and predicting future states, represents the frontier of AI-driven prevention [9]. Deep learning on electronic health records has demonstrated prediction of in-hospital mortality (area under the receiver operating characteristic curve [AUROC] 0.95), readmission, and disease onset across hundreds of thousands of patients [32]. Risk stratification identifies who needs intervention; the next question is what intervention, tailored to whom. The preventive pillar of P4 medicine demands not only the right algorithms but enforceable standards for who deploys them, how they are validated, and under what regulatory framework they operate.

Personalized: Digital twinning as computational individuation

Personalization is perhaps the most overused and least defined term in precision medicine. We propose a computational definition: personalization requires three components: (1) estimation of the individual’s current biological state, (2) prediction of their health trajectory under no intervention, and (3) simulation of counterfactual outcomes under candidate interventions. Any system that delivers only population-average recommendations, regardless of how many biomarkers it measures, is not personalized in this operational sense.

Digital twinning provides the computational architecture to meet this definition. A medical digital twin is a patient-specific computational model that is continuously calibrated against incoming data and used for in silico testing of therapeutic strategies [33, 34]. The most mature implementations exist in cardiology, where imaging-derived patient-specific heart models simulate arrhythmia risk and guide catheter ablation [35, 36]. Oncology digital twins, though less advanced, are being developed to simulate tumor growth, treatment response, and pharmacokinetics at the individual level [37].

For systemic health and aging, the domain most relevant to P4 medicine, digital twinning requires a different approach than organ-specific biophysical models. An ideal implementation would operationalize personalization as follows. A patient’s multi-omic baseline (Tier 1–3 data including a comprehensive metabolomics panel) defines the initial state vector. A stochastic simulation engine generates on the order of millions of health trajectories by Monte Carlo sampling across parameter distributions derived from a sufficiently powered reference cohort [38]. This order of magnitude reflects convergence requirements for stable quantile estimation in high-dimensional parameter spaces; in virtual population generation for quantitative systems pharmacology models, 105–106 samples are typically necessary before population-level summary statistics and tail-quantile estimates stabilize [39]. These trajectories represent the space of plausible futures under no intervention, visualized as a probability cloud with quantile ribbons (5th-95th percentile). A personalized protocol, selected algorithmically to maximize expected improvement while satisfying coverage constraints across impaired biological subdomains (with protocol complexity explicitly constrained to minimize adherence decay, consistent with evidence that regimen burden is inversely associated with long-term compliance [40]), is then overlaid as a single trajectory showing predicted response [41].

This approach addresses several limitations of existing frameworks. First, it quantifies uncertainty: rather than a single deterministic prediction, the patient sees the full distribution of possible outcomes, building appropriate calibration of expectations. Second, the personalized protocol is not a generic recommendation list but a constrained optimization output, where each recommendation is ranked by importance, adjusted for adherence burden, and designed to cover every subdomain classified as impaired or in early disruption. Third, the system is designed for longitudinal recalibration: at retest, new multi-omic data update the twin, and the trajectory cloud narrows as individual response data replace population priors.

A thermodynamic framing reinforces this architecture conceptually. Biological aging can be modeled as progressive entropy increase at the molecular level, including disordered metabolic networks, declining pathway coherence, and loss of homeostatic resilience [42, 43]. The digital twin’s objective function can be formulated as identifying interventions that minimize the rate of entropy production, shifting the system toward a lower-entropy, more youthful configuration. While this remains a conceptual anchor rather than a literal Gibbs free energy calculation, it provides theoretical grounding for why multi-omic trajectory simulation is the right computational framework for aging-focused personalization.

Clinical validation of such a digital twin system requires a staged framework rather than any single endpoint study, and we outline four sequential stages that together address the validation paradox inherent to individualized prediction. Stage 1 (technical and analytical validation) establishes the credibility of each model component before any patient-facing claim is made: deterministic reproducibility of the simulation engine across seeds and compute environments, calibration of stochastic outputs against held-out reference cohorts, formal sensitivity analyses across the parameter distributions used for trajectory sampling, and explicit conformity with the U.S. Food and Drug Administration’s framework for assessing the credibility of computational modeling and simulation in medical device submissions [44]. Stage 2 (prospective single-arm validation) then compares twin-predicted trajectories with observed multi-omic and clinical outcomes in a defined patient cohort followed for at least 12 to 24 months, quantifying calibration (for example, Brier score, integrated calibration index) and discrimination (for example, time-dependent area under the receiver operating characteristic curve) for each pre-specified endpoint, with pre-registration of prediction targets to prevent post hoc redefinition. Stage 3 (n-of-1 crossover validation) allows each patient to serve as their own control, alternating between twin-recommended and standard-of-care protocols with appropriate washout periods and protocol-specified non-inferiority margins, enabling individual-level efficacy estimation that respects the absence of a population-level ground truth for personalized predictions [45, 46]. Stage 4 (randomized comparative validation) evaluates twin-guided care against standard of care on hard clinical endpoints, including hospitalizations, incident disease, and healthspan markers, with trial design and reporting aligned to CONSORT-AI25 and SPIRIT-AI26. Each stage should incorporate prospective fairness audits across demographic subgroups, transparent reporting of model updates between stages, and an explicit decision rule for advancing from one stage to the next; collectively, this staged framework defines what it would mean to say that a digital twin has been validated clinically, rather than merely demonstrated computationally.

Participatory: Blockchain for data sovereignty and economic empowerment

Even a well-personalized intervention protocol is limited if the patient cannot access, control, or contribute the data that drives it. The participatory pillar is the most neglected dimension of P4 medicine, typically reduced to patient portals and shared decision-making [1]. We argue for a stronger definition: participation means patients exercise sovereign control over their health data, including the right to share, restrict, sell, or withdraw that data at any time, with full transparency into how it is used.

Blockchain technology provides the infrastructure for this vision. The consensus architecture for healthcare blockchain is a permissioned (private or consortium) chain, not a public chain like Ethereum or Bitcoin, with health data stored off-chain in encrypted form and the blockchain managing access control, audit trails, and consent [47, 48]. This design satisfies HIPAA technical safeguards: access controls are enforced by smart contracts, integrity is guaranteed by cryptographic hashing, audit trails are immutable, and transmission security is maintained through encryption [49]. MedRec, developed at MIT, demonstrated this architecture using Ethereum smart contracts for medical record access management, establishing the foundational pattern of on-chain permissions with off-chain data [50]. Estonia’s deployment of Guardtime’s Keyless Signature Infrastructure secures the health records of over one million citizens, representing the only national-scale blockchain health data system in operation [51].

Smart contracts, self-executing code with predefined rules, automate consent management at a granularity impossible with traditional systems. A patient can define access rules specifying which data elements, which requestors, and for how long, enforced automatically without a central authority [52]. This enables a fundamentally new economic model: the health data marketplace. Nebula Genomics demonstrated this concept for genomic data, where individuals receive sequencing at reduced cost by contributing anonymized data to a token-based marketplace accessible to pharmaceutical companies and researchers [53].

We extend this model to longitudinal multi-omic data. Consider a patient who completes quarterly metabolomics testing over two years, accumulating eight time points of metabolite profiles alongside genomic, epigenomic, and microbiome data. This longitudinal, deeply phenotyped dataset holds significant commercial value for pharmaceutical companies, insurers, and academic researchers. In a blockchain-mediated data marketplace enabling trustless brokering, the patient can tokenize their de-identified data and license access to multiple buyers simultaneously [54, 55]. Each transaction is recorded immutably, the patient retains ownership, and revenue flows directly to the data originator.

The economic implications merit careful consideration, though they remain largely theoretical. Notably, the Nebula model offered reduced-cost sequencing rather than direct revenue to data contributors. If a sufficiently large cohort contributes longitudinal multi-omic data to such a marketplace, the aggregate dataset may become commercially attractive enough to partially offset testing costs for participants, though the per-patient economics remain unproven [56]. The infrastructure components (permissioned blockchain, smart contract consent, token economics) have been individually demonstrated, though no integrated system yet operates at scale, and the commercial viability of patient-driven multi-omic data marketplaces remains to be established. As an initial step, our group has developed a proof-of-concept blockchain network housing 50 patient records designed to meet HIPAA technical safeguards, though independent compliance auditing remains to be completed.

Integration: The P4 flywheel

The four pillars are not independent modules but components of a reinforcing cycle (Fig. 1). Multi-omic profiling generates the data substrate (prediction). AI models transform this data into risk stratification and intervention targets (prevention). The digital twin consumes both the raw data and the AI-derived risk estimates to simulate personalized trajectories and optimize treatment protocols (personalization). Blockchain secures the data infrastructure, enables patient-controlled sharing, and creates economic incentives for longitudinal data contribution (participation), which in turn feeds richer data back into the multi-omic and AI layers, tightening the flywheel.

This closed-loop architecture addresses a persistent failure mode of precision medicine initiatives: they generate data but fail to close the loop from data to decision to outcome to updated data [7]. The Pioneer 100 / Arivale program demonstrated the promise and limitations of early P4 implementations, combining multi-omics with health coaching but lacking the computational infrastructure (digital twins, blockchain) to scale beyond a research cohort [19]. Our framework builds on this foundation by specifying the technical stack required to operationalize each pillar at clinical scale. Critically, this flywheel is only as durable as the sustained patient engagement that feeds it and the data standardization infrastructure that connects its components; without ongoing participation and interoperable data formats, the reinforcing cycle stalls.

Challenges and the path forward

Candor about limitations is essential. Validation remains the hardest problem: digital twin predictions must be tested against longitudinal patient outcomes, and the validation paradox, that each patient is unique and there is no population-level ground truth for individual predictions [45], requires creative solutions including n-of-1 trial designs [46] and the FDA’s emerging framework for computational model credibility [44]. Regulatory pathways for integrated P4 systems do not yet exist; current frameworks evaluate individual components (a single AI model, a single diagnostic) rather than interconnected systems. Equity concerns are real: multi-omic testing is expensive, blockchain participation requires digital literacy, and AI models trained on unrepresentative cohorts perpetuate disparities [57]. Privacy and re-identification present a fundamental tension: longitudinal multi-omic datasets are resistant to true de-identification, as demonstrated by recovery of individual identities from supposedly anonymous genomic data [58], and adding temporal layers only increases re-identification surface area. Any patient-controlled data marketplace must therefore incorporate differential privacy or federated analytics to mitigate this risk.

Data standardization across omics types remains technically challenging. The FAIR principles (Findable, Accessible, Interoperable, Reusable) provide guidance [59], and Health Level Seven Fast Healthcare Interoperability Resources (HL7 FHIR) is emerging as the interoperability standard for clinical genomics [60], but no unified framework yet exists for integrating metabolomic, proteomic, and microbiome data into clinical workflows at the point of care.

Finally, clinical adoption requires demonstrating that the P4 flywheel produces measurably better outcomes, including fewer hospitalizations, earlier disease interception, and reduced healthcare costs, not merely more data and more dashboards. Prospective trials comparing P4-guided care with standard of care are the necessary next step, and they must be designed with the rigor that CONSORT-AI and SPIRIT-AI demand.

The core tools exist in prototype or early deployment. The challenge is no longer technological imagination but engineering discipline: building integrated systems that are validated, regulated, equitable, and, above all, useful to the patients and clinicians they are designed to serve.
