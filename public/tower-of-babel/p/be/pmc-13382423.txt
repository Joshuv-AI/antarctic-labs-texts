Case Report: Successful R0 resection following conversion immunochemotherapy in a patient with biliary tract cancer and multiple metastases discovered after laparoscopic gallstone surgery
Authors: Yan Wang, Shulong Zhang, Ningjia Shen, Jiahui Chen, Jinyan Zhao, Chang Xu, Xiaojian Ni, Jiamin Lu, Ying Zhou, Sheng Shen, Bin Li, Jun Zhou, Yonghua Xu, Yuan Ji, Houbao Liu
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Gallbladder cancer is usually discovered as an incidental finding during or after cholecystectomy for gallstones. Most patients have advanced gallbladder cancer at diagnosis and have lost the opportunity for radical surgery, leading to dismal prognosis. Standard conversion therapy regimen is still in exploration. We reported a patient with unresectable biliary tract cancer (most likely gallbladder cancer) and multiple metastases (TxN2M1) discovered one month after laparoscopic gallstone surgery. The patient received margin-free resection after 9 cycles of immunotherapy (camrelizumab) combined with chemotherapy, followed by adjuvant immunochemotherapy. Pathological complete response was achieved and no disease recurrence occurred 5 months after surgery. Decreased neutrophil count was the only grade 3–4 treatment-related adverse event during the 9 cycles of conversion therapy. This case provides some reference for the conversion therapy of advanced gallbladder cancer or other biliary tract cancers.

Introduction

Gallbladder is the most common site where biliary tract cancer (BTC) occurs. In 2022, there were 122,462 new gallbladder cancer cases and 89,031 related deaths worldwide (1). The majority of patients have asymptomatic advanced gallbladder cancer, which is usually discovered as an incidental finding during or after cholecystectomy for gallstones (2, 3). Owing to difficulty in early diagnosis, patients often lose the opportunity for radical surgery, leading to dismal prognosis (2, 3).

Based on the phase 3 TOPAZ-1 and KEYNOTE-966 trials, immune checkpoint inhibitors (ICIs; durvalumab or pembrolizumab) combined with gemcitabine and cisplatin have become the preferred first-line treatment for advanced BTC. However, the addition of ICIs to chemotherapy only prolonged the median overall survival (OS) from 10.9-11.3 months to 12.7-12.9 months (4, 5). The improvement in survival benefit was limited in the gallbladder cancer subgroup, with a hazard ratio of 0.90-0.96 (4, 5). More effective treatment strategies are needed for this population.

Conversion therapy enables the initially unresectable disease to become resectable, which has been explored in several solid tumors (6–8). For patients with incidental gallbladder cancer found during gallstone surgery or those with BTC discovered after surgery, systemic therapies are needed to control the metastatic sites and strive for surgical opportunity. However, the evidence on conversion therapy, especially ICI-based regimen, for either BTC or gallbladder cancer subtype is scarce. Standard conversion therapy has not yet been well-established.

Here we reported a patient who underwent surgery following conversion immunochemotherapy for BTC (most likely gallbladder cancer) with multiple metastases discovered one month after laparoscopic gallstone surgery.

Case description

A 61-year-old woman went to the local hospital due to repeated upper right abdominal distension and discomfort for 1 year which aggravated for 3 days. Ultrasound indicated gallbladder stones and cholecystitis, while computed tomography (CT) and magnetic resonance imaging were not performed. Blood routine and liver function were normal. The patient had no diabetes mellitus, hypertension, coronary heart disease, family history of tumors, or drinking or smoking history. Following laparoscopic cholecystectomy, the pathological examination showed chronic cholecystitis with cholelithiasis, which were confirmed by re-evaluation. However, abdominal pain was not relieved after surgery. Nausea and vomiting appeared, and the skin and sclera turned yellow.

One month after cholecystectomy, the patient was admitted to our hospital. She had tenderness in the upper right abdomen, an Eastern Cooperative Oncology Group performance status of 1, and elevated total bilirubin (102 μmol/L), direct bilirubin (79.5 μmol/L), alanine aminotransferase (178 U/L), CA50 (829.0 IU/mL), CA199 (1123.3 U/mL), and CA242 (92.9 IU/mL) levels. Other physical examinations and laboratory tests were unremarkable. Contrast-enhanced CT indicated multiple implantation masses at hepatic hilum, right peritoneum and right abdominal wall, with intrahepatic bile duct dilation and duodenum and colon involvement (Figure 1). The 18F-fluorodeoxyglucose positron emission tomography/CT (18F-FDG PET/CT) showed a perihilar mass encasing the perihilar bile confluence with obscure boundary involving adjacent gastric antral wall. Two FDG-avid low-density nodules were found at the segment IV of the left liver lobe. Multiple FDG-avid nodules and/or masses were identified at the gallbladder fossa, upper right abdomen and right abdominal wall. Some of them showed obscure boundary with adjacent liver and bowel. A mass with rim-sign uptake of 18F-FDG was observed at the right paracolic sulcus (Figure 2). Ultrasound-guided biopsy of the abdominal wall mass showed poorly differentiated adenocarcinoma of biliary tract origin (Figure 3). Immunohistochemistry results were as follows: AFP (–); ARG-1(+); CK7(+); CK19(+); HNF-1β(+); CD34 (vascular+); Ki-67 (80%+); MLH1(+); PMS2(+); MSH2(+); MSH6(+); HER2 (–); C-Met (80%+); Claudin18.2 (40%+); IDH1 (–); NTRK (–); CDX2 (–); SATB2 (partial+); SYN (–); CgA (–); P40 (individual+); PD-L1 (tumor proportion score of 30% and combined positive score of 35). Finally, the patient was diagnosed as metastatic BTC (TxN2M1 stage), with gallbladder cancer being the most likely primary tumor (not excluding other BTCs). Analgesic therapy was administered, and percutaneous transhepatic cholangial drainage (PTCD) was performed to reduce jaundice.

With the consent of the patient, she was enrolled into a clinical trial (ChiCTR2100049404) and received camrelizumab (200 mg, day 1) plus albumin-abound paclitaxel (100 mg/m2, days 1 and 8), gemcitabine (800 mg/m2, days 1 and 8), and cisplatin (25 mg/m2, days 1 and 8; CAGC regimen) in each 3-week cycle. Analgesic therapy was discontinued when abdominal pain was relieved after 2 cycles of immunochemotherapy. On the eighth day of cycle 7, cisplatin was discontinued due to adverse events (pruritus, maculopapular rash, and palpitations, all grade 3), and the dosages of albumin-abound paclitaxel (140 mg/m2, days 1 and 8) and gemcitabine (1100 mg/m2, days 1 and 8) were adjusted. After 9 cycles, the contrast-enhanced CT indicated that the lesions at hepatic hilum and right abdominal wall markedly regressed (Figure 1). The 18F-FDG PET/CT showed remarkable reductions in the size of the perihilar mass (from 3.9×2.9 cm to 1.2×1.1 cm) and maximum standard uptake value (SUVmax; from 20.7 to 1.8) compared to baseline. Two FDG-avid low-density nodules at the segment IV of the left liver lobe and multiple nodules and/or masses at the gallbladder fossa and right abdominal wall completely disappeared. The mass at the right paracolic sulcus regressed with decreased 18F-FDG uptake. No new FDG-avid lesions were identified (Figure 2). The patient achieved a partial response per Response Evaluation Criteria in Solid Tumors version 1.1 and a complete metabolic response per Positron Emission Tomography Response Criteria in Solid Tumors version 1.0. Tumor stage was downgraded to TxN0M0. The main treatment-related adverse events were grade 3 decreased neutrophil count, grade 2 electrolyte imbalance, grade 2 anemia, and grade 1 hypoalbuminemia.

The patient successfully underwent surgery (resection of the segments IVb and V of the liver, extrahepatic bile duct, regional lymph nodes, and abdominal tumor, followed by cholangioenteric anastomosis) immediately after the completion of 9 cycles of conversion therapy. Margin-free (R0) resection was confirmed and no viable tumor cells were found in postoperative pathological examinations (Figure 3), indicating a pathological complete response. No surgical complications occurred. The PTCD tube was removed one month after surgery, and she began to receive adjuvant therapy with camrelizumab plus albumin-paclitaxel and gemcitabine. The study protocol of the clinical trial did not prespecify the adjuvant therapy regimen, which was left to the discretion of the treating physician. After 2 cycles, no lesions were identified by contrast-enhanced CT (Figure 1), and the adjuvant therapy was continued with camrelizumab monotherapy. By the last follow-up (8 months after R0 resection), the disease-free status was still maintained.

Discussion

Despite the great development of immunotherapy-based regimens, the current standard first-line ICIs combined with gemcitabine and cisplatin only resulted in a median OS of 12.7-12.9 months in patients with unresectable advanced BTC (4, 5). Radical surgery remains the optimal option to achieve long-term survival benefit for this population (9). Thus, the application of conversion therapy has attracted growing interest. In the present case report, the patient with BTC (most likely gallbladder cancer) and multiple metastases successfully underwent R0 resection and had achieved a progression-free survival (PFS) of at least 16 months since the initiation of conversion therapy with the CAGC regimen, and the final OS is quite worth looking forward to.

Zhang et al. reported a patient with unresectable advanced gallbladder cancer who received durvalumab plus gemcitabine and cisplatin for 3 cycles and successfully underwent conversion surgery with a pathological complete response (10). However, this patient refused adjuvant therapy and developed disease recurrence at abdominal lymph nodes 6 months after surgery (10). In another case report, a patient received durvalumab plus gemcitabine and cisplatin for 8 cycles and successfully underwent R0 resection (11). Adjuvant therapy with durvalumab monotherapy was given and the recurrence-free survival was at least 8 months since surgery (11). We speculated that the difference in PFS from the initiation of conversion therapy between these two cases might be attributed to the number of conversion therapy cycles and the presence or absence of adjuvant therapy. Our patient also received long-course conversion therapy (9 cycles) and adjuvant therapy. In addition, albumin-abound paclitaxel was added as the third chemotherapeutic agent for treating our patient. The phase 3 SWOG S1815 trial failed to prove the OS benefit with the addition of albumin-abound paclitaxel to gemcitabine-cisplatin regimen in patients with newly diagnosed advanced BTC. However, the gallbladder cancer subgroup analysis showed that both the median PFS (9.3 months vs. 4.1 months) and OS (17.0 months vs. 9.3 months) were numerically doubled with albumin-abound paclitaxel plus gemcitabine and cisplatin compared to gemcitabine plus cisplatin (12). Thus, we used this alternative recommended combination as chemotherapy partner to enhance anti-tumor activity. Nevertheless, these one-to-one comparisons requires cautious interpretation. The differences among the three cases also indicate that more data are warranted to determine the optimal conversion and adjuvant therapy regimens as well as the timing of surgery. On the other hand, comprehensive assessment by a multidisciplinary team before surgery, a quite experienced surgeon, and post-surgery active monitoring are also crucial for these patients to achieve favorable prognosis in clinical practice.

In a retrospective cohort study, 34 patients with initially unresectable BTC who met the conversion surgery criteria after responding to ICI-based combination therapy were enrolled. Thirteen of them underwent conversion surgery while 21 patients continued systemic therapy (13). The results indicated that ICI-based systemic therapy followed by conversion surgery might lead to better PFS and OS compared to ICI-based systemic therapy alone (13). Recently, the phase 2 ZSAB-TransGOLP trial investigated the triplet combination of chemotherapy, anti-angiogenic agent and ICI with four-drug GOLP regimen (gemcitabine plus oxaliplatin, lenvatinib, and tislelizumab) as conversion therapy in 41 patients with unresectable locally advanced BTC. Following 3–6 cycles of GOLP regimen, the surgical conversion rate was 68%, the R0 resection rate was 63% (100% for three patients with gallbladder cancer), and the median OS was 30.8 months (14). The post-hoc analysis suggested better median OS in the surgery subgroup than in the non-surgery subgroup (30.8 months vs. 13.4 months) (14). The conversion therapy strategy was also different between our case and this trial. When we obtain the mature data from our clinical trial (ChiCTR2100049404), we will perform a comprehensive comparison between studies, which may provide more valuable information for clinical practice.

In the era of precision medicine, biomarkers have become crucial for screening target population, predicting prognosis, and decision-making. The immunohistochemistry results indicated that the present patient had HER2-negative, PD-L1-positive disease, which might benefit from immunotherapy. However, the ZSAB-TransGOLP trial showed that the R0 resection rate (67% vs. 61%) and median OS (not reached vs. 30.8 months) did not differ between PD-L1-positive and PD-L1-negative patients (14). The appropriate predictive biomarkers need further explorations.

In conclusion, this paper reported a patient with unresectable advanced BTC (most likely gallbladder cancer) and multiple metastases who successfully underwent R0 resection after conversion therapy with the CAGC regimen. Active systemic therapy under the comprehensive judgement of clinicians may provide opportunity for further surgery. The final results of our clinical trial (ChiCTR2100049404) are awaited to provide more supporting evidence.
