Comorbidities in spinal muscular atrophy and their impact on the course of the underlying disease: a real-life observational study
Authors: Małgorzata Błauciak, Jakub Ubysz, Małgorzata Wieczorek, Magdalena Koszewicz
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
BackgroundThe lack of the SMN protein in SMA affects different tissues and organs, not only alpha motoneurons; this creates a broad spectrum of potential concomitant diseases, making SMA a multidisciplinary problem. This study focuses on an analysis of comorbidities, and their frequence and severity in SMA.MethodsThe study group consisted of 55 patients, 25 women and 30 men, with genetically confirmed SMA types 2, 3a, and 3b. A medical history focused on comorbidities and a neurological examination with the evaluation on the extended Hammersmith Functional Motor Scale (HFMSE). The prevalence and severity of comorbidities were assessed using the Cumulative Illness Rating Scale (CIRS).ResultsSMA type 2 was associated with the highest mean number of comorbidities and CIRS scores. All patients with type 2 had scoliosis. When scoliosis was not included in the analyses, there were no significant differences in the prevalence of comorbidities and CIRS scale scores between the SMA groups. CIRS scale scores with and without scoliosis were significantly higher in patients with low HFSME scores. Male patients had a greater tendency to develop lithiasis.ConclusionsScoliosis is a major factor that has an influence on the clinical course of SMA and on patients’ overall condition. Other comorbidities are similar in all types of SMA. Comorbidities are more frequent and have a greater impact on the general condition in patients with more severe disabilities. The current difference between the sexes is the higher incidence of lithiasis and overweight in men.

Introduction

Proximal spinal muscular atrophy (SMA) is the most common disease among spinal muscular atrophies, a group of heterogenic neuromuscular disorders with progressive history and associated with loss of alpha motoneurons in the spinal cord and brainstem. These disorders.

present a varied clinical picture, including motor symptoms and often other non-motor symptoms. They are differ in the molecular biology, inheritance pattern and severity of clinical course. The cause of SMA is a mutation in survival motor neuron 1 (SMN1) gene, localized in chromosome 5q13, which leads to the deficiency of an SMN protein, an essential factor for the existence and function of lower motor neurons. A variety of phenotypes of the disease can be distinguished, from the most severe congenital forms to clinically benign adult types. This variety is thought to be closely linked with the number of valid SMA2 gene copies that leads to different amounts of SMN protein, but, although individuals with the same genetic subtype can present different clinical courses, and some borderline cases are difficult in their classification [1, 2].

There is evidence that SMA is not an isolated alpha-motoneuron disease. Symptoms from different central nervous system structures and from other organs, especially in clinically severe forms, are also reported. The lack of the SMN protein affects both alpha motoneurons and the rest of the central nervous system cells, as well as the peripheral and autonomic nervous system [3, 4]. Other tissue cells are also affected, and this allows SMA to be considered a multi-system disease [5, 6].

Lack of the SMN protein changes cell metabolism, leading to multidirectional malfunctions that can be measurable in laboratory tests and may present as clinical symptoms [7]. In large-scale studies [8, 9], ⁠ it has been shown that in SMA patients the prevalence of additional diseases is significantly higher, and the onset of symptoms is earlier than in the general population. In view of the above facts, there is growing interest in comorbidities in patients with SMA. In the literature, it has been demonstrated that the cardiovascular system is the one of most commonly involved among SMA patients and can be affected both structurally (heart and great vessel defects) and functionally (arrythmias, ECG changes, hypertension) [10–12]. Weakening of the axial muscles and limbs, leading to skeletal deformation, in turn leads to movement and breathing difficulties. Hypotonia of pharyngeal muscles affecting swallowing and coughing leads to nutritional impairment and susceptibility to infections. The abnormality of the digestive system organs (liver, pancreas, intestines) causes metabolic and nutritional aberration [13, 14]. ⁠ This can promote problems of being both underweight and overweight in SMA patients, as well as diabetes and other metabolic (e.g. fatty acids, nucleic acids) malfunctions [2]. ⁠ Bone metabolism disorders with increased osteoclast activity, combined with limited physical activity, and mechanical stimulation of the bones increase the risk of bone fractures, contribute to poor posture and spinal deformities. Studies show that SMA patients, especially in clinically severe forms, have a very high risk of scoliosis development and a significant percentage of them will require a surgical correction of a spine deformity within their lifetime [15].

Metabolic abnormalities also contribute to the development of lithiasis, e.g. increased plasma calcium levels are a significant risk factor for nephrolithiasis or urolithiasis [16]. It appears that in the absence of SMN protein, a vicious circle of pathological processes develops in various organs and tissues [17]. Landfeld at al. [18] Wohnrade et al. [19] and other authors [20, 21] have shown in their studies that SMA patients’ quality of life is strongly correlated with their disability. Additional elements that make patients dependent on other people and increase the need for medical care (additional drugs, surgeries, hospitalizations, rehabilitation) inevitably reduce quality of life.

Given the rapid development of therapeutic options and the increasing life expectancy of individuals with SMA, it is essential to approach SMA as a multi-system disease and ensure diagnosis and treatment of associated systemic complications [16]. ⁠ Accordingly, we decided to gather and analyze data about systemic manifestations of SMA among our patients before starting treatment. Then, using standardized scale (e.g. Cumulative Illness Rating Scale, CIRS) we attempted to identify a correlation between SMA type, the number of SMN gene copies, SMA severity, and the presence of comorbidities.

Materials and methods

The authors have received approval for their research from the Ethics Committee of Wroclaw Medical University, Poland. The authors confirm that the study was conducted in compatibility with principles of Good Clinical Practice. All patients agreed to their participation in the study and signed the informed consent form.

Our study includes data from 55 adult patients (25 women and 30 men). Each patient had a diagnosis of SMA 5q confirmed by genetic testing with copy number assessment of the SMN2 gene. All examinations, i.e. physical and clinical examinations, including the identification of comorbidities, were performed prior to the start of treatment, i.e. in untreated patients.

According to the European Neuro Muscular Center (ENMC) International Workshop guidelines [22–25], the cohort was divided into SMA types 2 and 3, and type 3 patients were divided into subtypes 3a and 3b. Our study group consisted of 14 SMA type 2 patients (F = 10, M = 4), 24 SMA type 3a patients (F = 8, M = 16) and 17 SMA type 3b patients (F = 7, M = 10).

Each patient underwent a general clinical and neurological examination together with assessment using the Hammersmith Functional Motor Scale – Expanded (HFMSE) [26–29].

Medical history was assessed carefully together with available medical records in order to establish all comorbidities. Table 3 shows the types of comorbidities in each type of SMA. Among the conditions identified as ‘Other’, psoriasis, bronchial asthma, hyperlipidemia, tetany, depression, anxiety disorders were noted in individual patients.

We used the Cumulative Illness Rating Scale (CIRS) (Table 2) [30, 31] to assess both the number and severity of comorbidities. The scale allows assessment of 14 aspects, each of which can be scored on a five-point scale:

0—no dysfunction.

1—mild dysfunction/past dysfunction in medical history—no interference with normal activity.

2—moderate dysfunction—interference with normal activity; treatment is needed.

3—severe dysfunction/chronic with non-controlled disease—disabling; urgent treatment is needed.

4—extremely severe—life-threating; without treatment, it leads to death.

In the analysis of the number of comorbidities and the CIRS scale, we performed the calculations twice. The first analysis included the presence of scoliosis as a comorbidity (“k” scale score)—referred to as the whole CIRS–WCIRS. In the second analysis, scoliosis and past surgery were excluded from the calculation as a natural complication of the underlying disease; [23, 32] the corrected CIRS (CCIRS) was calculated. All further statistical analyses were performed separately for these two calculations.

We analyzed the correlations between patients’ sex, number of SMN2 gene copies, SMA type, onset of symptoms, motor function assessed on the basis of HMFSE, and the type of comorbidities and their severity.

All quantitative data was assessed using the Shapiro–Wilk Normality test—none of these (excluding patients age) featured a normal distribution. The correlation analysis was conducted with the nonparametric U Mann–Whitney test. For comparison of quantity for the subgroups, a Chi square test and a Chi square test with Yates continuity correction were used.

Results

Demographic data

The demographic analysis of the study group showed, similar to the literature [33–35], that the onset of symptoms strongly correlated with the SMA type and was the earliest in type 2 SMA; in SMA type 3a, the onset of symptoms occurred in early childhood, but the mean age was significantly higher (2.29 versus 1.11 years, p = 0.04); SMA type 3b was diagnosed mainly in early adolescence (mean age = 10.65, Table 1). The average age of inclusion for patients with different SMA types to the study followed the same pattern (SMA type 2–30.14 ± 7.93, SMA type 3a − 36.08 ± 11.70, SMA type 3b − 40.24 ± 10.54 years), and the age differences in the respective SMA types were statistically significant (p < 0.00001). Mean HMFSE rates were significantly lower in SMA type 2 in the comparison to SMA type 3a and SMA type 3b (p < 0.00001). Three copies of the SMN2 gene was definitely the most frequent variant in all SMA types (Table 1).

General data for comorbidities

The comorbidities found in our patients are shown in Table 3. The mean number of comorbidities was 2.3 in all SMA patients. After correction excluding scoliosis and scoliosis surgery, the average was 1.2. Half of the patients had no more than two comorbidities. The maximum was eight co-occurring conditions (one patient with SMA type 2), but there were also individuals without any comorbidities (two with SMA type 3a and three with SMA type 3b).

Comorbidities in relation to SMA types

The number of comorbidities differed significantly between groups: highest in the SMA type 2 group (type 2 versus type 3a p = 0.043, versus type 3b p = 0.003) when scoliosis was included. The results did not differ between the groups when scoliosis was excluded (Table 4).

Individuals with SMA type 2 had the highest number of comorbidities, with all presenting at least one. All of them had significant scoliosis, and 50% had undergone scoliosis surgery. Nearly ½ of SMA type 2 patients (43%) has respiratory failure, 50% were underweight, only one patient was overweight. Of note is the rare occurrence of fractures—7% of patients; similarly in group SMA 3a, this was 8% as opposed to group 3b where it was 24% of patients.

Patients with SMA type 3a were more likely to develop significant scoliosis (six patients, 54%) than patients with type 3b (three patients, 18%) and half of them (25%) had undergone surgery for this reason. These patients were more commonly overweight (21%) than underweight (13%). While in patients with SMA type 3b, only two individuals (12%) were overweight and two individuals (12%) were underweight. Other individual different conditions mentioned above were noted in 25% of patients.

Individuals with type 3b SMA had the highest incidence of coexisting bone fractures, allergies and other individual conditions, mentioned above (24% each). Only three patients had significant scoliosis and two of them had undergone surgery for this reason. The same proportion of individuals with SMA type 3b were underweight and overweight (12% each).

Nephrolithiasis and cholelithiasis were more common in individuals with the more severe SMA, e.g. with types 2 and 3a. Heart disease and hypertension were generally rare comorbidities in all patient groups. The former was more common in severe forms of SMA, while hypertension was more common in milder forms. Respiratory failure was mainly present in patients with type 2 SMA and was not reported at all in type 3b.

Comorbidities in relation to gender

Women and men did not differ in age (36.84 ± 10.82 v. 35.03 ± 10.88 years, p = 0.549) and HFSME scores (18.32 ± 21.30 v. 17.20 ± 17.70 points, p = 0.431). Disease onset was earlier in the female group (3.58 ± 3.87 v. 5.40 ± 4.94, p = 0.044), which was most likely due to the fact that it was mainly women in the study group that had type 2 SMA (10 women, 4 men). A comparison of comorbidities in the group of men and women with SMA showed a higher prevalence in the male group for nephrolithiasis (four men, one woman) and cholelithiasis (four to two). In the group of patients with type 3b SMA, up to 68% of the men had significant scoliosis, compared to only 38% of the women. Overweight was more common in the male group (seven men and two women, 23.3% versus 8%).

Comorbidities in relation to SMA gene copy number

There were no significant differences in comorbidities between patients with three and four copies of the SMN2 gene. One patient with two copies of this gene was excluded from the calculation. This person was 22 years old woman with SMA type 2. She had undergone surgery for severe scoliosis in childhood, used non-invasive ventilation for about 6 h a day, was obese, had diabetes type 2 and cholelithiasis. She scored 2 points on the HFSME scale, 18 on the WCIRS scale, and 8 on the CCIRS scale.

Comorbidities in relation to HFSME score

In the statistical analysis, we divided the patient group into two subgroups in respect to HFSME score: equal to or below and above 10 points [28]. None of the patients with higher HFSME scores had more than three comorbidities. All patients without comorbidities belonged to the group with higher HFSME scores. (Fig. 1).

WCIRS and CCIRS in relation to SMA types, HFSME score and gender

The WCIRS analysis showed that the scale score depended on the type of SMA and had significantly higher values in more severe forms of the disease. On the other hand, the CCIRS analysis did not show such a relationship – scores were comparable in all types of SMA (Table 4).

A comparison of both WCIRS and CCIRS in groups of patients with an HFSME score of 10 or less and in the group of patients with a score above 10 showed statistically significant worse results in the group of patients with low HFSME scores. They had higher numbers of all comorbidities, also after exclusion of severe scoliosis (Table 5).

There were no statistically significant differences in WCIRS and CCIRS scores between male and female SMA groups (p = 0.402 and p = 0.203, respectively).

Discussion

The approach in which SMA is viewed solely as a disorder of alpha motor neurons has been abandoned in light of the results of many studies. Many symptoms involving other organs have been described in studies on large groups of patients [8, 33, 34, 36]. In milder forms of the disease, abnormal function of structures other than the alpha-motoneurons, e.g. myocardium or testes, may cause symptoms that appear before the neurological symptoms typical of advanced forms of SMA [35, 36].

The aspect of comorbidities is further complicated by the broad clinical spectrum of SMA itself. There are differences in comorbidities and their severity in the different forms of SMA. In severe forms of SMA, the number and clinical severity of comorbidities appear to be greater than in patients with milder forms of the disease [1, 17, 35]. Similarly, in our research individuals with SMA type 2 had by far the highest average number of comorbidities; there were no patients without additional conditions in this group. In the SMA type 3a group there were two individuals free from comorbidities in SMA type 3b—three.

In the statistical analysis, the presence of scoliosis was the determining factor for the discrepancies between the mean number of comorbidities in the different SMA types. Taking scoliosis into account, patients with SMA type 2 had the highest mean number of comorbidities (Table 4), which is in line with the literature data [8, 36]. Whitney et al. [8] showed that the prevalence of comorbidities is correlated with early onset and is the highest among young adults and decreases in older groups. When scoliosis was excluded as a comorbidity, no differences were found between SMA types. An analogous pattern was observed in the analysis between CIRS scores and SMA type, where, after excluding spinal deformity, the corrected CIRS score (CCIRS) showed no important differences between SMA types. These observations suggest that scoliosis is the most important additional health factor affecting patients with SMA. However, it is closely related to the underlying disease and is a direct complication of it, especially in more severe forms of SMA. Therefore, including scoliosis in the analysis of comorbidities in SMA clearly inflates the statistics, particularly to the detriment of patients with SMA type 2/3a.

Respiratory failure, considered one of the most serious comorbidities in SMA and associated with high mortality, was common in our group of individuals with SMA type 2 (43%, 5 women and 1 man). According to literature data, subtype 2 SMA has no significant relationship between respiratory failure and gender. There is such a relationship in milder forms; women were at greater risk, partly due to anatomical conditions (lower thoracic capacity and less strength of the diaphragm and other respiratory muscles) [17, 37]. ⁠ Due to the high heterogeneity of the phenotypes of SMA and the relatively small group of patients studied in our analysis and in the analyses of other authors, it is not possible to clearly determine sex-specific differences.

The HFSME score was negatively correlated with the number of comorbidities. All patients with SMA without comorbidities belonged to the group with higher HFSME scores. The exclusion of scoliosis led to the removal of differences between SMA types. In contrast, in the analysis between HMFSE score and CIRS score, the presence of spinal deformity did not affect this relationship; both WCIRS and CCIRS were significantly lower in the group of patients with low HFSME scores. These observations, like those described above, are consistent with the literature, and suggest that individuals with severe disability are more burdened with comorbidities [8, 36, 38].

A well-known phenomenon in various neurological diseases is the clinical differences between the sexes, i.e. women are more likely to develop Alzheimer’s disease and other dementia-related conditions, while men are more prone to Parkinson’s disease and amyotrophic lateral sclerosis [39–42]. There are no significant differences between the sexes in neuromuscular disorders, with the exception of X-chromosome-related Duchenne muscular dystrophy and Becker muscular dystrophy [26]. The literature does not report a significant correlation between gender and the incidence of severe SMA; in milder forms of SMA, a higher incidence and a three-year earlier onset of the first symptoms have been reported in males [43]. ⁠ In our study: the onset of the disease was earlier in women, but this may be due to the small study population and the fact that women predominated among the most severe types of SMA (72%).

Previous studies have highlighted the role of X-linked factors in the pathogenesis of SMA, some of which may play an important role in modifying the clinical course [44–46]. ⁠ Mitochondrial dysfunction has been demonstrated in SMA animal models, which is inherited through the maternal line [47, 48]. ⁠ Deficiency of the SMN protein may induce sex-specific effects in specific cells [42]. ⁠ Impaired testicular development, hypogonadism and cryptorchidism are well documented, and male infertility may be one of the earliest symptoms of mild SMA; in women with the same disease, reproductive problems are not significantly emphasized [49]. In analyses of treatment effects in humans, there is no clear evidence of their dependence on sex, but in some experimental studies based on animal models, treatment effects were better in females [50]. ⁠ In our analysis, we only observed a greater tendency for lithiasis (urolithiasis and cholelithiasis) to occur in men. While in the general population men also have a higher prevalence of urolithiasis [51], ⁠ cholelithiasis is more common in women [52].

SMN metabolism and endocrine alterations can lead to weight disturbances. It is well documented that differences in metabolic disturbances are related to SMA type and age, both among patients and in animal models [2, 53–58]. While malnutrition is characteristic of severe forms of SMA, overweight can occur in milder subtypes, mainly in type 3b. In our results, being overweight was most common in patients with SMA type 3a, but only in men. Being underweight was mostly seen in the type 2 SMA patients what this is generally in line with reports from the literature [57]. In animal models, a trend towards weight loss has only been observed only in females [17, 59], while we observed underweight equally in women and men.

Increased activity of osteoclasts relative to osteoblasts, abnormalities in bone structure with reduced bone density, and growth disturbances due to a lack of loading create favorable conditions for bone fractures in SMA patients [60, 61]. ⁠ In our study group, fractures occurred in seven of the 55 patients (five men, two women), mainly in individuals with SMA type 3b. Fractures of the femur predominated, with only one patient having a fracture of the humerus. Four patients sustained fractures twice, two with SMA type 3a, one patient each with type 3b and 2 SMA. We did not show a clear predominance of fractures in patients with specific types of SMA. The majority of our patients suffered fractures due to a fall from a wheelchair. The data obtained are in agreement with those available in the literature [60].

The main limitation of our study is the small, single-centre cohort. Future studies will aim to include participants from multiple Polish centres to improve generalisability. It is also necessary to combine these studies with quality-of-life assessments based on various questionnaires. We published an article on this topic, but also based on a small group of patients, in 2024 [21].

Conclusions

The results of our study lead to the conclusion that the presence of scoliosis and the need for its treatment is an important element affecting the overall condition of SMA patients. Analysis of the number of comorbidities and their severity, as measured by the CIRS scale, showed that when spinal deformity was not taken into account, differences in terms of the presence of comorbidities and their severity were significantly smaller and similar in all types of SMA. The CIRS scale, whether or not it takes scoliosis into account, shows significantly worse results in patients with a high degree of motor disability. In the analysis of gender differences, an interesting finding is the greater predilection among males for lithiasis, and overweight.
