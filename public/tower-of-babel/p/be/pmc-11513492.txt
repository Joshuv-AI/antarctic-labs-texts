miR-10a regulates cell death and inflammation in adipose tissue of male mice with diet-induced obesity
Authors: Sumin Lee, Yoon Keun Cho, Heeseong Kim, Cheoljun Choi, Sangseob Kim, Yun-Hee Lee
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
AbstractObjectiveAdipose tissue remodeling plays a critical role in obesity-induced metabolic dysfunction, but the underlying molecular mechanisms remain incompletely understood. This study investigates the role of miR-10a-5p in adipose tissue inflammation and metabolic dysfunction induced by a high-fat diet (HFD).MethodsMale miR-10a knockout (KO) mice were fed a HFD to induce obesity for up to 16 weeks. RNA sequencing (RNA-seq) analysis was performed to profile mRNA expression and assess the effects of miR-10a-5p KO in gonadal white adipose tissue (gWAT). Additional analyses included immunoblotting, qPCR, histological examination, and validation of the miR-10a-5p target sequence using a dual-luciferase reporter assay.ResultsmiR-10a-5p was highly expressed in gWAT but decreased after 8 weeks of HFD feeding. Over the 16-week HFD period, miR-10a KO mice exhibited greater weight gain and reduced energy expenditure compared to wild-type (WT) controls. gWAT of miR-10a KO mice on a HFD showed an increased population of proinflammatory macrophages, elevated inflammation, and increased cell death, characterized by upregulated apoptosis and necrosis markers. This was also associated with increased triglyceride accumulation in liver. Mechanistically, the proapoptotic gene Bcl2l11 was identified as a direct target of miR-10a-5p. Loss of miR-10a-5p led to BIM-mediated adipocyte death and inflammation, contributing to mitochondrial metabolic dysregulation, increased fibrosis marker expression, and the onset of inflammation in adipose tissue.ConclusionsThis study demonstrates the significant role of miR-10a-5p and its downstream target BIM in regulating adipocyte death during diet-induced obesity. This signaling pathway presents a potential therapeutic target for modulating obesity-induced inflammation and cell death in adipose tissue.Keywords: Obesity, microRNA, Adipose tissue, Cell death, Inflammation

Introduction

The rapidly growing prevalence of obesity has become a worldwide concern, as it is linked to various pathologies, including type 2 diabetes, cardiovascular diseases, and certain types of cancer [1]. A heightened turnover rate is a hallmark of adipose tissue in obesity, and the significance of adipocyte death in the context of adipose tissue remodeling has garnered increasing attention [2]. Maintenance of adipose tissue homeostasis normally involves a tightly regulated balance between adipocyte death and adipogenesis. Besides its contribution to adipose tissue dysfunction, the imbalance between newly differentiated adipocytes and apoptotic or necrotic death of adipocytes influences the secretion of various adipokines and cytokines, with subsequent effects on systemic inflammation and insulin sensitivity [3]. This complex interplay between adipocyte death, inflammation, and lipid metabolism underscores the complexity of adipose tissue remodeling and its implications for metabolic health [3].

MicroRNAs (miRNAs) are small non-coding RNA molecules that play pivotal roles in post-transcriptional gene regulation. Approximately 30% of genes expressed in the human genome are regulated by miRNAs, and the orchestration of miRNA-mediated regulation is crucial for various biological processes [4]. To generate mature miRNA, long primary transcript (pri-miRNA) is transcribed by RNA polymerase II, forming a hairpin structure, and processed into pre-miRNA [5]. Pre-miRNA is then transported to the cytoplasm, where RNase III Dicer cleaves it into the ∼22 nt miRNA duplex, consisting of 5p and 3p strands [6]. The miRNA duplex is loaded onto the AGO protein within the RNA-induced silencing complex (RISC), where it is subsequently unwound into a single strand [7]. The retained strand is the guide strand, functioning as the biologically active miRNA [8]. Among 5p and 3p strands, the strand with thermodynamically less stable 5' end is favored for survival, such as having an A or U as the terminal nucleotide [7,9]. This miRNA acts by base pairing with corresponding mRNA targets, leading to translational repression or mRNA cleavage [5,7,10].

Dysregulation of miRNAs in adipose tissue has been implicated in the pathogenesis of insulin resistance and metabolic diseases [[11], [12], [13]]. For instance, miR-155 from adipocytes has been reported as a predictive biomarker for the development of type 2 diabetes in obese patients [14,15]. Adipocyte-specific Dicer KO mouse study further supported critical roles of circulating miRNAs from adipose tissue in regulation of energy metabolism [16].

We have previously identified miR-10a-5p originating from adipose tissue macrophages (ATMs), which promote adipogenesis through exosomal transfer by targeting Rora in adipose-derived stem cells (ASCs) [17]. Furthermore, miR-10a-5p regulates macrophage polarization by suppressing M1 polarization [17].

Here, we further investigated the regulatory role of miR-10a-5p in adipose tissue remodeling in obesity, using a whole-body genetic deletion mouse model. Specifically, we investigated the effect of miR-10a-5p on adipocyte death and inflammation, and subsequent effects on liver. We aimed to uncover novel insights into the molecular mechanisms underpinning adipocyte death, which are essential prerequisites for the homeostatic remodeling of adipose tissue in situations of energy excess [18]. We integrated transcriptomic and functional analyses to shed light on the complex interplay between miRNA regulation and adipose tissue dynamics, offering a deeper understanding of the pathophysiological processes that underlie metabolic diseases.

Materials and methods

Animals

All the protocols of animal studies were approved by Institutional Animal Care and Use Committees of Seoul National University (SNU-210119-3-1). In vivo experiments were conducted at the Animal Center for Pharmaceutical Research at Seoul National University, where mice were housed under controlled conditions at 22 ± 1 °C, 50% ± 5 humidity, and a 12-hour light/12-hour dark cycle. The mice had unrestricted access to both food and water. miR-10a WT and KO mice (B6.129-Mir10a<tm1.1Ahl>/Kctt strain) were procured from Karolinska Institutet (Karolinska Center for Transgene Technologies). Male WT and miR-10a KO mice were used for in vivo experiments and were sacrificed between 8 and 24 weeks of age. To establish the diet-induced obesity model, mice at 8 weeks of age were provided with a high-fat diet (HFD) containing 60% fat (Research Diets, cat#D12492) for a duration of 8, 12, or 16 weeks. A normal chow diet (NCD), represented by a standardized rodent pellet diet (Purina, cat#38057), was used as the control diet. Energy expenditure was estimated through indirect calorimetry using the PhenoMaster system (TSE Systems), while body composition was assessed using nuclear magnetic resonance (NMR) scanning with the EchoMRI-700 (Echo Medical Systems).

Cell culture

We acquired HEK293T cells from the American Type Culture Collection (ATCC, CRL-3216) and cultured them in Dulbecco's Modified Eagle Medium (DMEM, Welgene, cat#LM001-07) supplemented with 10% fetal bovine serum (FBS, Gibco, cat#16000044) and 1% penicillin/streptomycin (P/S, Welgene, cat#LS202-02).

C3H10T1/2 cells (ATCC, CCL-226) were cultured in growth medium (DMEM supplemented with 10% FBS and 1% P/S) until confluency, and then exposed to bone morphogenetic protein 4 (R&D systems, 20 ng/mL, cat#314-BP-050) for 2 days. The cells were then cultured in differentiation medium (growth medium containing IBMX (Sigma, 0.5 mM, cat#I5879-1G), dexamethasone (Cayman, 1 μM, cat#11015), insulin (Sigma, 10 μg/mL, cat#I9278), indomethacin (Cayman, 0.125 mM, cat#70270) and triiodothyronine (T3, Cayman, 1 nM, cat#16028)) for 3 days, followed by exposure to maintenance medium (growth medium with insulin (10 μg/mL) and T3 (1 nM)) for an additional 3 days.

For miRNA overexpression, cultured primary adipocytes or C3H10T1/2 adipocytes were transfected with 20 nM syn-mmu-miR-10a-5p and syn-mmu-miR-10a-3p miScript miRNA mimics or a negative control miRNA (Qiagen) using INTERFERin (Polyplus Transfection, cat#409-10), according to the manufacturer's instruction. 24 h after transfection, the media were replaced with growth medium containing palmitic acid (Sigma, 400 μM, cat#P5585) for 48 h, and the resulting apoptotic adipocytes were used for analysis. Regarding the sequences of mature miRNA, mature miR-10a-5p is 5’-UACCCUGUAGAUCCGAAUUUGUG-3’, and mature miR-10a-3p is 5’- CAAAUUCGUAUCUAGGGGAAUA-3’.

Isolation of mature adipocytes and SVF from mouse adipose tissue

Adipose tissue was isolated, washed, minced, and digested with collagenase type I (Gibco, 2 mg/mL, cat#17100-017) in KRBB buffer containing 3% bovine serum albumin (BSA) at 37 °C. Dissociated preparations were then passed through a 100 μm cell strainer (Corning) and centrifuged at 300 × g for 5 min. Floating adipocytes were collected for further analysis. Red blood cells (RBC) were lysed with RBC lysis buffer (15.5 mM NH4Cl, 1.2 mM NaHCO3 and 50 mM EDTA, pH 7.4) and pellets were then washed with KRBB buffer containing 3% BSA for two times. Stromal vascular fraction (SVF) was then used for magnetic-activated cell separation (MACS) or primary cultures. For MACS, SVF was incubated with anti-F4/80-MicroBeads (Miltenyl Biotec, cat#130-110-443) for 15 min at 4 °C, and then magnetically separated. F4/80+ and F4/80- cells were centrifuged at 500 × g for 10 min and the pellets were used for RNA extraction.

mRNA expression analysis by real-time quantitative PCR (RT-qPCR)

We extracted total RNA from tissues and cells, using the TRIzol reagent following the manufacturer's instructions. Subsequently, we performed cDNA synthesis using a High-Capacity cDNA Reverse Transcription kit (Applied Biosystems, cat#4368814), followed by qPCR analysis using iQ SYBR Green Supermix (Bio-Rad, cat#170–8884) in the Bio-Rad CFX Connect Real Time PCR Detection system. The collected data were further processed using Maestro 1.1 software (version 4.1.2433.1219). Relative expression levels of each gene were calculated using the 2−ΔΔCt method, with the reference housekeeping gene as follows: Glyceraldehyde-3-phosphate dehydrogenase (Gapdh) for whole tissues, Peptidylprolyl isomerase A (Ppia) for cellular fractions, and Beta-actin for F4/80+ cells. The primer sequences used for qPCR are listed in the Supplementary Table (Table S1).

miRNA expression analysis by real-time quantitative PCR (miRNA RT-qPCR)

We used Mir-X™ miRNA First Strand Synthesis Kit and miRNA RT-qPCR TB Green Kit (Takara, cat#638315) for miRNA cDNA synthesis and quantification, following the manufacturer's instructions. The RT-qPCR analysis was conducted using the Bio-Rad CFX96 real-time PCR detection system. The relative miRNA expression levels were normalized using the internal control U6 expression level, determined by the 2−ΔΔCt method [19]. The primer sequences used for qPCR are listed in the Supplementary Table (Table S2).

Flow cytometry analysis

SVFs were obtained from gWAT of mice as described above, and then stained with antibodies or control IgG for 20 min at room temperature. The following antibodies were used: CD45 (Biolegend, 30-F11, cat#103116), CD64 (Biolegend, FcγRI, X54-5/7.1, cat#139311), CD11b (Biolegend, M1/70, cat#101206), CD11c (Biolegend, N418, cat#117310) and CD206 (Biolegend, MMR; C068C2, cat#141717)

The LSRFortessa X-20 Flow Cytometer (BD Biosciences) was used for sample analysis, with data acquisition performed using BD FACSDiva 8.0 software and subsequent analysis carried out with Flowjo software (version 10.5.3; TreeStar).

RNA sequencing (RNA-seq)

Tissue total RNA extraction was performed using TRIzol reagent (Thermo Fisher Scientific, cat#15596018) following the manufacturer's instruction. Subsequently, the integrity of RNA was assessed by determining the RNA integrity number (RIN), rRNA ratio, and sample concentration using an Agilent Technologies 2100 Bioanalyzer with a DNA 1000 chip. For RNA-seq analysis, cDNA libraries were generated utilizing the TruSeq mRNA Library Kit, using 1 μg of total RNA. The resulting total RNA was subjected to sequencing using the NovaSeq 6000 System (Macrogen). The RNA-seq data were deposited in Gene Expression Omnibus [20] (accession number GSE260771). Heatmaps were generated using Morpheus, a matrix visualization and analysis software provided by the Broad Institute (https://software.broadinstitute.org/morpheus).

Western blotting

We used tacoPrep Bead Beater homogenization system to homogenize adipose tissue of WT and miR-10a KO mice. This was done with PRO-PREP Protein Extraction Solution (iNtRON Biotechnology, cat#17081), which contained SIGMAFAST Protease Inhibitor Cocktail (Sigma, cat#S8820) and PhoSTOP phosphatase inhibitors (Roche, cat#4906845001). After homogenization, we quantified the protein concentration in the extracts using a BCA assay, measuring at 562 nm using a spectrophotometer (MultiSkan GO, Thermo Fisher Scientific) with SkanIt software ver 5.0.

The proteins were then denatured in SDS sample buffer (ELPISbio, cat#EBA-1052) at 95 °C for 5 min, separated on an SDS-PAGE gel, and transferred onto a PVDF membrane (Bio-Rad). To prevent non-specific binding, the membrane was blocked with 5% non-fat dry milk or BSA in TBST (Tris-buffered saline with 0.1% Tween 20). Subsequently, it was incubated with primary antibodies at 4 °C overnight, followed by incubation with horseradish peroxidase-conjugated secondary antibodies at room temperature for 1 h. Antibodies used for immunoblot analysis are listed in Table S3.

To visualize protein expression, we used the Fusion Solo chemiluminescence imaging system (Vilber Lourmat) and analyzed the results with EvolutionCapt software (version 17.03). Immunoblots were quantified using the National Institutes of Health ImageJ software (version 1.52a).

Measurement of liver TG content

To measure hepatic triglyceride (TG) content, 100 mg of liver was overnight digested in an ethanolic KOH solution (EtOH: 30% KOH = 2:1) at 55 °C [21]. After complete digestion, 50% EtOH was added to tissue, bringing the total volume to 1,000 μL, followed by centrifugation at 1,000 rpm for 5 min. The resulting supernatants were transferred to a new tube, and an additional 50% EtOH was added to reach a final volume of 1,200 μL. Subsequently, 200 μL of this solution was transferred to another tube and mixed with 215 μL of 1M MgCl2. This mixture was incubated on ice for 10 min. After centrifugation at 1,000 rpm for 5 min, the supernatant was transferred to a new tube. Triolein was determined using glycerol reagent (Sigma, cat#F6428), following the manufacturer's instruction. Hepatic TG content was then calculated based on its equivalents, triolein.

Histology and immunohistochemistry

The gWAT and liver were isolated and fixed in 10% formalin (Sigma, cat#HT50128) for 24 h at 4 °C, followed by paraffin embedding. 5 μm paraffin sections were used for histological analysis. For Hematoxylin and Eosin (H&E) staining, the deparaffinized sections were stained with ClearView Staining Hematoxylin (BBC Biochemical, cat#MA010081) and Eosin Y Alcoholic solution (BBC Biochemical, cat#3610). Picro Sirius Red Stain kit (Abcam, cat#ab150681) was used to assess fibrosis [22]. Images were captured using Nikon Elements (NIS BR Analysis ver 5.10.00). We utilized Adiposoft software [23] for adipocyte size measurement in histological sections of gWAT. Only complete adipocytes in the field of view were considered. Individual adipocytes were manually demarcated, and their sizes were calculated.

For immunofluorescence staining, we performed blocking with PBS containing 3% BSA for 30 min at room temperature. The primary antibody was diluted in 3% BSA in PBS and applied to the slides for overnight incubation in a humidified chamber. The primary antibodies used: anti-F4/80 antibody (Cat#30325S, 1:100) and anti-BIM antibody (Cat#2933S, 1:100), purchased from Cell Signaling Technology. Subsequently, the slides were washed three times and incubated with goat anti-rabbit IgG cross-adsorbed secondary antibody, Alexa Fluor 488 (Invitrogen, 1:500, cat#A11008), for 1 h at room temperature. Finally, the slides were mounted using Anti-Fade Fluorescence Mounting Medium (Abcam, cat# ab104135) with DAPI.

Images were acquired using a Leica confocal microscope (TCS8) and analyzed with Leica Application Suite X 3.6.

Dual luciferase reporter assay

We generated two luciferase reporter vectors with a wide-type or mutant 3′-untranslated region (UTR) fragment of Bcl2l11 inserted downstream of the stop codon of the hRluc (Renilla luciferase) gene in the psiCHECK2 vector (Promega, cat#C8021). Putative miRNA target sequences used for plasmids construction for the dual luciferase assay are indicated in Supplementary Table (Table S4). HEK293T cells were seeded in a 24-well plate and co-transfected with 0.25 μg of wild-type or mutant plasmid and 100 nM miR-10a-5p mimics (syn-mmu-miR-10a-5p miScript miRNA mimic (Qiagen)) or negative control mimics (Qiagen) using jetPRIME transfection reagent (Polyplus, cat#114-15). After 48 h of transfection, the activities of firefly and Renilla luciferases in cell lysates were determined with a dual-luciferase assay system (Promega, cat#E1910), according to the manufacturer's instruction.

Transmission electron microscopy (TEM)

For TEM imaging, small 1–2 mm3 pieces of minced gWAT were immersed in a solution containing 2% paraformaldehyde and 2.5% glutaraldehyde in 0.1 M sodium cacodylate buffer (pH 7.2). The tissues were then further fixed in 1% osmium tetroxide (OsO4) for 2 h. Following fixation, a graded ethanol dehydration process was employed, and the samples were subsequently embedded in Spurr's Resin (Electron Microscopy Sciences). Sections of 60 nm thickness were obtained using an Ultramicrotome (Leica) and transferred onto nickel grids. Imaging was performed using a Talos L120C cryo-TEM (FEI) transmission electron microscope, at the Nanobio Imaging Center, Seoul National University.

Micro-computer tomography (Micro-CT)

The abdominal areas of the mice were examined using the Quantum GX2 micro-CT imaging system (PerkinElmer). We followed a previously established method with some modifications [24]. In brief, mice were first anesthetized with isoflurane within a chamber and then positioned on the CT bed, which was connected to an inhalation anesthesia machine. The X-ray tube was operated at a voltage of 90 kV and a current of 88 μA, with a field of view measuring 45 mm and a pixel size of 90 μm. The scan mode was configured for a 4-minute high-resolution scan. We determined the volumes of abdominal fat regions located between the proximal end of the L1 vertebra and the distal end of the L5 vertebra. The abdominal muscular wall served as the boundary between the visceral adipose depot inside the abdominal cavity and the subcutaneous adipose depot outside the abdominal wall [25]. Volume determination was conducted using AnalyzeDirect software (ver. 12.0).

Glucose tolerance test (GTT)

After 12 h of fasting, blood glucose levels were measured, and D-(+)-glucose (Sigma, 2 g/kg body weight, cat#49139) was injected intraperitoneally. Blood glucose levels were then measured at the indicated times using a Gluco Doctor Top meter (Allmedicus, cat#AGM-4100) with glucose indicator strips.

Serum TG and FFA quantification

Serum TG levels were quantified using Fujifilm Dri-Chem 3500s and TG-P Ⅲ Fujifilm Dri-Chem slides (Fujifilm, Kanagawa, Japan) according to the manufacturer's instructions.

Serum FFA levels were quantified using HR Series NEFA-HR (2) (Color Reagent A (999–34691), Solvent A (995–34791), Color Reagent B (991–34891), Solvent B (993–35191), and NEFA Standard Solution (276–76491) (FUJIFILM Wako Pure Chemicals, Osaka, Japan), following the manufacturer's instruction as previously described [26].

Statistical analysis

Statistical analyses were performed using GraphPad Prism 9 software. Data are presented as mean ± standard errors of the means (SEMs). Unpaired two-tailed t-tests were used for pairwise comparisons. Statistical significance between multiple groups was performed using a two-way ANOVA with Bonferroni post hoc tests to determine p values. The statistical parameters used for the specific sets of data are described in the figure legends.

Results

miR-10a-5p is downregulated in gWAT of HFD-fed mice

In our previous study, we identified miR-10a-5p as a key regulator of adipose tissue metabolism [17]. Using RT-qPCR, we analyzed its expression across adipose depots, including brown adipose tissue (BAT), inguinal white adipose tissue (iWAT), and gWAT, as well as in liver of WT mice. Compared to its expression in liver, miR-10a-5p was highly expressed in adipose tissues, with the highest expression observed in the gWAT of NCD-fed lean mice (Figure 1A). Conversely, miR-10a-3p levels were approximately 2,000-fold lower than miR-10a-5p levels in adipose tissues when normalized to U6 expression (Figure 1B). This suggests that miR-10a-5p is the predominant biologically functional variant of the miR-10a species in adipose tissues. It is plausible that mature miR-10a-5p, which has uracil (U) at the 5' end, may be favored for loading onto the AGO protein. This preference could potentially explain the dominance of miR-10a-5p over miR-10a-3p.

We observed a substantial decrease in miR-10a-5p levels in the gWAT of WT mice after 8 weeks of HFD feeding (Figure 1A). miR-10a-5p exhibits significant evolutionary conservation across diverse species, including humans and mice [27]. Analysis of publicly available transcriptome-profiling data (GEO: GSE87379) revealed decreased miR-10a-5p expression in the adipose tissue of insulin-resistant obese individuals compared to lean subjects (Figure 1C), underscoring its potential clinical relevance and the obesity-associated reduction of miR-10a-5p in human adipose tissue.

Following 8 weeks of HFD, miR-10a-5p expression was significantly reduced in adipocyte fractions, with a less pronounced but still significant decrease observed in the stromal vascular fraction (SVF) (Figure 1D, Fig. S1).

miR-10a deficiency accelerates HFD-induced weight gain of mice

To elucidate the effects of miR-10a-5p on adipose tissue metabolism in the HFD-induced obesity mouse model, we utilized male miR-10a whole-body KO mice with a 70-nucleotide deletion in the pre-miRNA sequence (Fig. S2). We confirmed the loss of miR-10a in white adipose tissues (iWAT and gWAT) through RT-qPCR analysis (Figure 2A). miR-10a KO mice exhibited a significantly higher weight gain than their WT counterparts under HFD conditions, while weight remained comparable in the NCD-fed lean group (Figure 2B). Body fat content significantly increased in obese miR-10a KO mice (Figure 2C). Consistent with increased body weight in both WT and miR-10a KO mice on HFD, miR-10a KO mice exhibited significant tissue mass expansion in gWAT, iWAT, and liver at 8 weeks of HFD. (Figure 2D). However, gWAT mass was markedly reduced in miR-10a KO mice compared to obese WT controls by 16 weeks of HFD (Figure 2E,F).

Expansion of visceral adipose tissue is more closely associated with insulin resistance than expansion of subcutaneous adipose tissue [[28], [29], [30]]. To distinguish between subcutaneous and visceral adipose depots, we used micro-CT imaging. The abdominal region was defined from the L1 vertebra to the distal end of the L5 vertebra, with the abdominal muscular wall serving as the boundary between the visceral adipose depot within the abdominal wall and the subcutaneous adipose depot outside it, as previously described [25]. Abdominal micro-CT imaging demonstrated a higher volume of visceral fat in miR-10a KO mice after 8 weeks of HFD feeding (Figure 2G). However, after 16 weeks of HFD feeding, miR-10a KO mice showed a reduced volume of visceral adipose tissue compared to WT mice (Figure 2H). This observation is consistent with the finding that the major visceral adipose tissue depot, gWAT exhibited a reduced volume and yellowish discoloration compared to the WT mice after 16 weeks of HFD feeding (Figure 2E,F).

Loss of miR-10a induces adipocyte death and inflammation in gWAT during HFD-induced obesity

Despite the overall increase in body weight, gWAT mass and abdominal visceral adipose volume decreased significantly in miR-10a KO mice after 16 weeks of HFD feeding. While increased visceral adipose tissue mass is often linked to insulin resistance, it is essential to differentiate between healthy and unhealthy adipose tissue expansion by examining cellular and molecular features rather than solely relying on mass. Previous studies examining adipose tissue remodeling over 20 weeks of HFD feeding in mice reported adipose tissue expansion up to 12 weeks, followed by a peak in adipocyte death and fibrosis with subsequent gWAT mass reduction by 16 weeks [18]. Based on these findings, we hypothesized that miR-10a KO accelerated pathological adipose tissue remodeling, leading to enhanced adipocyte death, inflammation, and fibrosis [[31], [32], [33]], which contributes to adipose tissue dysfunction and insulin resistance [34,35]. To gain insights into the molecular underpinnings of the observed phenotype, we performed mRNA profiling by RNA-seq analysis of gWAT from WT and miR-10a KO mice after 16 weeks on either an NCD or HFD. The heatmap of differentially expressed genes revealed a clear segregation of gene expression profiles based on diet (NCD vs HFD) and genotypes (WT vs. KO) (Figure 3A). Gene Ontology (GO) functional enrichment analysis showed that genes with significantly upregulated expression (genes satisfying | fold change (FC) | > 1 and p < 0.05) were linked to immune system process and inflammatory response, while those with downregulated expression were associated with lipid and mitochondrial metabolism (Figure 3B). A heatmap visualizing gene expression associated with the GO terms (immune system process and inflammatory response) is presented (Figure 3C). Kyoto Encyclopedia of Genes and Genomes (KEGG) pathway analysis of upregulated genes in gWAT of obese miR-10a KO mice identified the immune system, lysosomes, and phagosomes (Figs. S3A–D).

Consistent with the RNA-seq results, a notable increase of crown-like structures was observed in the gWAT of obese miR-10a KO mice (Figure 3D), and F4/80 immunofluorescence staining verified macrophage recruitment (Figure 3E). Flow cytometric analysis showed a substantial increase in the number of M1-like macrophages (identified as CD45+CD11b+CD64+CD11C+ cells) and a decrease in the population of M2-like macrophages (identified as CD45+CD11b+CD64+CD206+ cells) in the gWAT of miR-10a KO mice after a 12-week HFD feeding (Figure 3F, Fig. S4). The heightened expression of proinflammatory genes in the gWAT of miR-10a KO mice also supported KO-induced inflammatory responses in gWAT (Fig. S5). In addition, western blotting analysis of gWAT and iWAT revealed elevated levels of F4/80, Cleaved Caspase 3, and RIP3 in obese male miR-10a KO mice, indicating increased accumulation of dead adipocytes and inflammation (Figure 3G, Fig. S6A).

miR-10a deficiency in obesity exacerbates fibrosis in inflamed tissue

In healthy adipose tissues, a highly flexible extracellular matrix (ECM) accommodates fluctuations in adipocyte size [36]. Conversely, adipose tissue inflammation is strongly associated with the development of a fibrous and stiff ECM, hindering tissue remodeling and limiting the metabolic functions of adipocytes [36]. Transmission electron micrographs of gWAT from miR-10a KO mice on HFD for 16 weeks revealed increased disorganized collagen fiber deposition in the interstitial space, indicating pathological fibrosis (Figure 4A). These findings are consistent with the histological evaluation of gWAT sections stained with picrosirius red (Figure 4B). Analysis of the adipocyte area in gWAT indicated restricted hypertrophy of adipocytes in the absence of miR-10a (Figure 4C,D). The expression of genes associated with fibrosis (Col1a1, Col3a1, Tgfb1, Tgfb2, Acta2 and Timp1) were upregulated in the gWAT of HFD-fed miR-10a KO mice (Figure 4E), with a corresponding increase in COL1A1, TGF-β2, α-SMA and TIMP1 proteins (Figure 4F). Collectively, these results suggest that miR-10a deficiency leads to heightened adipocyte death and inflammation in gWAT, ultimately resulting in pathological fibrosis and limited adipocyte plasticity and metabolic function.

Loss of miR-10a impairs lipid metabolism in gWAT and drives ectopic fat accumulation in liver during obesity

We then investigated the pathways with downregulated activity in the gWAT of obese miR-10a KO mice using KEGG analysis. Numerous genes with downregulated expression were associated with metabolic pathways, oxidative phosphorylation, fatty acid metabolism, and peroxisome (Figure 5A); a heatmap shows the genes linked to these pathways (Figure 5B). RT-qPCR analysis further demonstrated decreased expression of genes involved in oxidative phosphorylation (Atp5b, Ndufa3, Cox8a, and Uqcrh), fatty acid metabolism (Elovl3), and peroxisome (Acox1) in the gWAT of HFD-fed miR-10a KO mice (Figure 5C). In line with the observed transcriptional changes, protein levels of mitochondrial enzymes, medium-chain acyl-coenzyme A dehydrogenase (MCAD) and cytochrome c oxidase subunit 4I1(COXIV) were reduced in the gWAT of obese male miR-10a KO mice (Figure 5D).

Interestingly, while male miR-10a KO mice gained weight during 16 weeks of HFD feeding, a paradoxical reduction in gWAT mass was observed alongside the expected increase in iWAT mass. The compromised HFD-induced expansion of gWAT is likely due to fibrosis and a proinflammatory environment that impaired metabolic function and fat storage capacity of gWAT. Based on these observations, it is hypothesized that the impaired ability of adipose tissue to effectively store lipids could result in lipid redistribution to other organs, including liver and skeletal muscle [18,30,37]. Additionally, obesity-associated inflammation within adipose tissue has been implicated in the pathogenesis of liver disease in obesity, with inflammatory mediators derived from omental fat contributing to non-alcoholic steatohepatitis (NASH) progression [18,38]. Histological analysis of liver (Figure 5E) and assessment of hepatic TG content (Figure 5F) revealed elevated TG accumulation in liver of HFD-fed miR-10a KO mice. Moreover, glucose tolerance test and fasting glucose levels indicated severe insulin resistance in miR-10a KO mice, while serum lipid levels were comparable to WT controls (Fig. S7).

The increased apoptotic and necrotic death of adipocytes and inflammation likely triggered mitochondrial dysfunction in adipose tissue of miR-10a KO mice. miR-10a deficiency led to decreased mitochondrial protein levels in both gWAT and iWAT, accompanied by reduced UCP1 expression in BAT. These changes may have contributed to the reduced energy expenditure measured by indirect calorimetry (Figures 8 and 9).

Cell death mediator Bcl2l11 is a putative target of miR-10a-5p

miRNAs regulate gene expression post-transcriptionally by binding to complementary sequences in the 3' UTR of target gene mRNAs, inducing translational repression or mRNA degradation. We focused on identifying downstream targets of miR-10a-5p involved in HFD-induced adipose tissue remodeling. Considering the reduced levels of miR-10a-5p in the gWAT of HFD-fed WT mice, we anticipated that the expression of downstream targets would be upregulated. Thus, potential target genes of miR-10a-5p were identified by intersecting the genes upregulated by a HFD in the RNAseq dataset [39] (GEO: GSE182930; FC > 2 and p value < 0.05; gWAT of mice fed a HFD for 8 weeks) with those predicted by miRNA target prediction tools, TargetScanMouse8.0 and miRDB. Four genes—Bcl2l11, E2f7, Prrx1, and Tiam1—were identified (Figure 6A, Fig. S10).

Of these genes, Bcl2l11 (gene encoding BIM), a key player in initiating apoptosis and mediating cell death [20], emerged as a highly reliable target with one highly and one poorly conserved putative target sequence in its 3' UTR (Figure 6B). Dual luciferase reporter assay confirmed the direct binding between miR-10a-5p and Bcl2l11 3' UTR. The procedure involved cloning a fragment of the WT Bcl2l11 3' UTR sequence and corresponding mutant sequence into a luciferase reporter vector. The presence of miR-10a-5p led to suppression of the reporter gene expression in constructs containing the WT Bcl2l11 3' UTR, whereas it had no effect on those with the mutant Bcl2l11 3' UTR (Figure 6C). This result was validated by RT-qPCR (Figure 6D), RNA-seq (Figure 6E), immunohistochemical analysis (Figure 6F), and BIM immunoblotting (Figure 6G) in the gWAT of obese miR-10a KO mice.

We aimed to identify specific adipose tissue cell types exhibiting changes in Bcl2l11 expression as a result of miR-10a KO. To address this, Bcl2l11 and miR-10a expression levels were assessed in isolated adipocytes and the SVF from gWAT. Bcl2l11 expression was increased in the adipocytes, while this effect was not observed in the SVF fraction (Figure 6H, Fig. S11). Palmitic acid-induced apoptosis is associated with increased Bcl2l11 levels and downregulation of miR-10a in C3H10T1/2 adipocytes (Fig. S12). Additionally, miR-10a-5p mimics suppressed palmitic acid-induced BIM expression, whereas miR-10a-3p had no such effect (Figure 6I, Fig. S13). These findings suggest that miR-10a-5p is involved in suppression of BIM expression in adipocytes. Both hsa-miR-10a-5p and mmu-miR-10a-5p were predicted to target 59 common genes, including BCL2L11 (Bcl2l11). This highlights the potential clinical relevance of our findings, as BCL2L11 is shared target gene of miR-10a-5p in human adipose tissue (Figure 14 and Table S5).

cGAS-STING signaling pathway is upregulated in gWAT of obese miR-10a KO mice

Considering that adipocyte death in miR-10a deficiency contributes to the release of damage-associated molecular patterns (DAMPs), we hypothesized that the pathway of cyclic GMP–AMP synthase–stimulator of interferon genes (cGAS-STING) would be activated in gWAT; the process may lead to macrophage M1-like polarization through proinflammatory gene expression. Immunoblotting analysis of gWAT revealed elevated levels of phosphorylated STING and interferon regulatory factor 3 (IRF3) in obese miR-10a KO mice (Figure 7A). To investigate whether changes occurred within macrophages, we used MACS-identified F4/80+ cells isolated from the gWAT of WT and miR-10a KO mice fed a HFD for 16 weeks. mRNA expression of representative cGAS-STING downstream genes such as Tnf-α, Il6, Ifnb1, and Nlrp3 increased significantly in the absence of miR-10a (Figure 7B). In line with this finding, RNA-seq results confirmed increased NFκB-downstream gene expression (Figure 7C), indicating cGAS-STING pathway activation in the gWAT of obese miR-10a KO mice.

Discussion

We investigated the regulatory functions of miR-10a-5p, with a focus on adipose tissue metabolism. In the context of HFD-induced obesity, we observed that miR-10a-5p expression in gWAT was notably downregulated, and the effect was accompanied by a significant increase in the translation of the target gene Bcl2l11, particularly in gWAT. BIM-mediated adipocyte death led to the recruitment of proinflammatory macrophages, exacerbating the pathological adipose tissue remodeling. Consequently, adipose tissue of miR-10a KO mice lost its capacity for lipid storage, resulting in ectopic lipid accumulation in liver. These findings highlight the molecular mechanisms underlying the metabolic phenotype induced by miR-10a-5p deficiency.

Most research on miR-10a-5p in adipose tissue has focused on its role in adipocyte differentiation. A previous study revealed that miR-10a-5p can inhibit adipocyte differentiation by targeting Map2k6 and Fasn [40]. However, interspecies results are conflicting. In pigs, miR-10a-5p enhances adipocyte differentiation by regulating KLF11 [41], while in goats, it inhibits the differentiation of intramuscular preadipocytes by targeting KLF8 [42]. While we concluded that miR-10a-5p suppresses BIM expression and reduces adipocyte cell death in diet-induced obesity model, further investigation is needed to fully understand the roles of miR-10a-5p in adipose tissue, particularly in the context of specific diseases.

In our previous study, we discovered that mice treated with the β3 adrenergic receptor agonist CL316,243 showed elevated miR-10a-5p expression in ATMs, stimulating beige adipogenesis through exosomal communication with ASCs by targeting Rora [17,43]. Additionally, overexpression of miR-10a-5p in vitro reduced the proinflammatory characteristics of RAW264.7 cells and promoted the expression of brown/beige adipocyte markers in differentiated C3H10T1/2 cells [17]. Additionally, the miR-10a-5p mimetic administration improved HFD-induced inflammation and insulin resistance. Together with the present study, we have demonstrated the therapeutic potential of miR-10a-5p acting in both ATMs and adipocytes, treating obesity-induced metabolic dysfunction.

A primary limitation of this study is the use of miR-10a whole-body KO mice. To address this, we examined miR-10a and its target gene expression in adipocytes and SVF isolated from mouse adipose tissue. HFD-induced downregulation of miR-10a-5p, accompanied by upregulated Bcl2l11 expression, was observed primarily in adipocytes, with a less pronounced effect in SVF. In vitro experiments using cultured adipocytes confirmed that palmitic acid-induced apoptosis recapitulated HFD-induced adipocyte death, characterized by reduced miR-10a-5p expression and increased BIM levels, further supporting our in vivo findings. These results suggest that the adipocyte-specific downregulation of miR-10a-5p and the corresponding upregulation of BIM play significant roles in HFD-induced adipocyte death and inflammation. While we identified the role of miR-10a-5p in isolated adipocytes, the phenotypic features observed in gWAT within the context of the whole-body KO system may have arisen from the complex interplay of miR-10a-5p actions across various cell types, including macrophages, adipocytes, and adipocytes progenitors. To gain a more comprehensive understanding, future investigations should explore cell type-specific miR-10a KO models.

Regarding the interaction between ATMs and adipocytes, these cells play crucial roles in maintaining adipose tissue homeostasis. With obesity, hypoxia, lipotoxicity, and adipocyte death alter ATM phenotypes and functions [3]. We observed altered proinflammatory macrophage characteristics in miR-10a KO mice during HFD-induced obesity, using classical markers of M1-and M2-like phenotypes [44]. We speculated that the increased DAMPs release resulting from apoptotic and necrotic adipocyte death activated STING signaling in gWAT, inducing a proinflammatory phenotype. This sets off a vicious cycle, in which heightened inflammation further accelerates adipocyte death.

Subcutaneous and visceral adipose tissue responded differently to the loss of miR-10a in terms of HFD-induced fat mass gain. We hypothesized that the reduction of miR-10a under HFD conditions upregulates the BIM-mediated cell death pathway, leading to inflammation and fibrosis in gWAT. While miR-10a KO mice exhibited increased visceral abdominal fat during the early stages of obesity (8 weeks of HFD), this trend reversed during the chronic diet-induced obesity (16 weeks of HFD), with KO mice displaying lower visceral fat despite higher overall body weight. Visceral WAT harbored approximately twice as many macrophages compared to subcutaneous WAT, contributing to a more pronounced proinflammatory response and severe metabolic dysfunction in gWAT [45,46]. Excessive ECM accumulation in gWAT of HFD-fed KO mice, driven by inflammation, is expected to hinder adipocyte expansion [32] ultimately reducing lipid storage capacity and contributing to a lipodystrophic phenotype.

The cellular functions of BIM are regulated at multiple levels, including via transcriptional control, mRNA stability, post-translational modification, proteasomal degradation, and cellular localization [47]. Numerous studies have illuminated the role of BIM as a regulator of cell death, particularly in the context of apoptosis mediated by TNFα cytokines in various cell types [48,49]. Regarding adipose tissue metabolism, Wang et al. proposed a mechanism wherein TNF-α and IL-1β, released by obese adipose tissue, activate YAP/TAZ in adipocytes, suppressing proapoptotic BIM and safeguarding adipocytes from cell death. This highlights the pivotal role of BIM as a regulator of cell death in obesity [50]. Although our study did not clarify the steps preceding the transcriptional activation of Bcl2l11, numerous studies have explored the effect of BIM overexpression on cell death. For example, Bcl2l11-induced toxicity in HEK293T cells has been reported, with approximately 90% of Bcl2l11-expressing cells succumbing to death within three days. Notably, the degree of cell death appeared to correlate with the amount of transfected Bcl2l11 DNA [20]. This finding highlights the cytotoxic potential of BIM overexpression and its relevance in the regulation of cell death.

In summary, our results demonstrate that miR-10a-5p plays a crucial role in gWAT by influencing adipocyte apoptosis, death, and the ensuing inflammatory response. These findings suggest that miR-10a-5p can be a prospective therapeutic target for enhancing the healthy remodeling of adipose tissue and addressing metabolic dysfunctions in adipose tissue, along with associated metabolic diseases.
