Boron, Titanium, and Calcium Doped Graphene Monolayers for Alzheimer’s Disease Biomarker Detection: A Density Functional Theory Study
Authors: Leon Zhou, Xuan Luo
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
Alzheimer’s disease remains a critical public health concern, with early detection hindered by the limited sensitivity and invasiveness of conventional diagnostic techniques. Two-dimensional monolayer materials offer a promising alternative by enabling label-free, real-time, and noninvasive detection of disease-specific biomarkers. In this study, we analyzed the effectiveness of doped graphene monolayers as biosensing platforms for two emerging biomarkers of Alzheimer’s disease: glycine and d-serine. Using density functional theory, we investigated the adsorption behavior of these biomarkers on pristine graphene as well as boron-, titanium-, and calcium-doped graphene monolayers. Our results showed that calcium-doped graphene exhibits the most favorable sensing characteristics across multiple criteria. Glycine adsorption on calcium-doped graphene resulted in a recovery time of 0.170 s, indicating a rapid sensor response and high reversibility. Both biomarkers induced a semiconductor-to-metal transition, significantly enhancing electrical conductivity. Charge density and band structure analyses further revealed strong orbital interactions and substantial electronic modulation upon adsorption. These findings demonstrate that calcium-doped graphene is a promising candidate for the development of highly sensitive, reversible, and real-time biosensors for the early stage detection of Alzheimer’s disease.

Introduction

Alzheimer’s disease (AD) is a progressive neurodegenerative disorder currently affecting more than 55 million individuals worldwide, with projections estimating a 3-fold increase by 2050 due to population aging. As the most prevalent form of dementia, AD is characterized by cognitive deterioration, memory loss, and behavioral impairment. , Early detection is critical, as it enables timely clinical intervention, lifestyle adjustments, and therapeutic planning that can significantly delay disease progression and improve patient quality of life. − However, existing diagnostic methods, such as neuroimaging, cerebrospinal fluid (CSF) analysis, and neuropsychological assessment, typically identify the disease only after considerable neurodegeneration has occurred, thus narrowing the window for early therapeutic intervention. −

Biosensors have emerged as a promising alternative for the early detection of AD, offering rapid, noninvasive, and highly sensitive diagnostics by targeting disease-specific biomarkers. − These devices combine biological recognition elements (e.g., antibodies, peptides) with physicochemical transducers capable of converting molecular interactions into measurable signals. Early development in this field has primarily focused on canonical biomarkers such as amyloid-beta­(Aβ) peptides and tau­(τ) proteins. For instance, Yu et al. developed an electrochemical biosensor using a horseradish peroxidase-gold-gelsolin nanohybrid to selectively bind soluble Aβ1–40/1–42 peptides in CSF and brain tissue samples of AD model rats. Similarly, Špringer et al. developed a surface plasmon resonance (SPR) biosensor incorporating a sandwich-type assay with functionalized gold nanoparticles to detect τ–Aβ complexes in CSF. Despite the predominance of Aβ peptides and τ proteins in AD diagnostics, several underexplored biomarkers, such as glycine and d-serine, have demonstrated strong diagnostic potential. −

These molecules were selected in this study due to emerging evidence of their elevated relevance in early stage AD. Glycine, an inhibitory neurotransmitter, has shown over 90% diagnostic accuracy when normalized to creatinine in the urine samples of AD patients. d-serine, a coagonist of NMDA receptors, shows elevated concentrations in AD-affected brain regions and CSF and has been experimentally proven to be an accurate indicator of early stage AD. , While these biomarkers offer promising diagnostic value, their use in monolayer-based biosensing platforms remains largely unexplored. Given their chemical diversity and early stage association with AD pathology, these biomarkers represent ideal candidates for assessing the selective sensing capabilities of biosensor platforms.

Two-dimensional (2D) monolayers have demonstrated significant potential in biosensing due to their high surface-to-volume ratios, electronic tunability, and chemical versatility. Materials such as boron nitride (BN), MXenes, and silicene have been investigated for biosensing applications due to their favorable biocompatibility and surface functionality. Prasongkit et al. demonstrated the adsorption behavior of DNA nucleobases on silicene using first-principles calculations, highlighting its potential in label-free DNA sensing. BN-based biosensors have also demonstrated strong sensing capabilities. , For example, Li et al. synthesized a BN monolayer-based sensor for dopamine detection with excellent selectivity in complex serum environments. Sharma et al. developed an h-BN monolayer based biosensor for label-free detection of carcinoembryonic antigen­(CEA), achieving a remarkably low detection limit of 22.5 pg/mL with high sensitivity and biocompatibility. MXenes, particularly Ti3C2, have garnered significant attention due to their exceptional conductivity, high surface area, and ease of functionalization. , Kumar et al. demonstrated that functionalizing Ti3C2 with aminosilanes enabled effective antibody attachment for the label-free detection of CEA, proving its viability and accuracy in label-free biosensing applications.

Among 2D monolayers, graphene remains the most widely studied due to its exceptional conductivity, mechanical strength, and high surface area. − However, pristine graphene often exhibits limited sensitivity and selectivity toward neutral biomolecules. To overcome these limitations, heteroatom doping has been employed to tailor graphene’s electronic properties and enhance its reactivity with target analytes. − Previous studies have explored codoping graphene with multiple heteroatoms, citing improvements in its sensitivity and increases in binding energy with target molecules. Deji et al. examined the adsorption chemistry of codoped armchair graphene nanoribbons toward carbon-based gases, which showed enhanced adsorption energies, significant variations in the band gap, and improved selectivity. Other studies have evaluated the doping of graphene with a single heteroatom, citing similar improvements in adsorption chemistry and sensitivity toward biomolecules. Deji et al. also evaluated the adsorption of NO gas on alkali- and transition-metal-doped nanoribbons, observing a monumental increase in adsorption energy compared to pristine graphene nanoribbons and a semiconductor to metal transition following the adsorption of NO. Wang et al. demonstrated that metal-doped graphene significantly improves the adsorption of serine due to enhanced charge transfer. Zhu and Luo reported the effective use of transition metal-doped graphene for detecting COVID-19 biomarkers, while Ren et al. demonstrated its potential in the detection of cancer-related biomarkers. Cazorla demonstrated that calcium-doped graphene enhanced the adsorption of collagen-related amino acids, providing improved sensitivity compared to pristine graphene. Additional studies have reported that boron doping induces localized positive charge centers, facilitating interactions with electron-rich biomolecules. In contrast, titanium doping enhances the adsorption of oxygen-containing groups, which are common to many biological molecules.

Despite significant advancements in graphene-based biosensing, no studies have evaluated the performance of doped graphene systems as platforms for detecting emerging AD biomarkers. Prior work has demonstrated the viability of single-heteroatom-doped graphene for biomedical sensing applications. However, no studies have yet explored the use of doped graphene for the reversible biosensing of these two promising AD biomarkers.

In this study, we evaluated pristine graphene, as well as boron-, titanium-, and calcium-doped graphene, as potential biosensor platforms for detecting glycine and d-serine. These dopants were selected based on their electronic diversity and prior evidence of enhancing molecular adsorption, especially toward molecules with oxygen-containing functional groupsfeatures present on both glycine and d-serine. Using first-principles density functional theory (DFT) calculations, we analyzed adsorption energies, recovery times, charge transfer characteristics, and modifications to the electronic structure. The results provide comparative insights into the reactivity of doped graphene systems and support the rational design of graphene-based biosensors for early stage, noninvasive AD diagnostics.

Methods

Computational Details

All first-principles calculations were conducted using DFT with the generalized gradient approximation (GGA) and the Perdew–Burke–Ernzerhof (PBE) exchange–correlation functional implemented in the ABINIT software package. The projected augmented wave (PAW) method was utilized to generate pseudopotentials with the AtomPAW code. During total energy calculations, the self-consistent field (SCF) cycle is considered converged when the change in total energy falls below 1.0 × 10–10 Ha for two consecutive iterations. Additionally, convergence tests were performed for the kinetic energy cutoff, k-point mesh, and vacuum layer thickness. These parameters were deemed converged when the difference in total energy between two successive configurations is less than 0.0001 Ha (0.003 eV). Structural optimizations were performed using the Broyden–Fletcher–Goldfarb–Shanno (BFGS) minimization algorithm, with a maximum force convergence tolerance of 2.0 × 10–4 Ha/Bohr (0.01 eV/Å).

Adsorption Energy

The adsorption energies of the biomarker-graphene complexes were calculated by placing the molecule above pristine or doped graphene in various orientations. The adsorption energy, designated as E ad, was calculated using 1 Ead=Esurf+mol−(Esurf+Emol) where E surf+mol, E surf, and E mol are the total energies of the biomarker-adsorbed graphene complex, the pristine or doped graphene monolayer, and the isolated biomarker, respectively.

The band structures of pristine and doped graphene were calculated before and after adsorption of the selected biomarkers using high-symmetry k-points: Γ (0, 0, 0), K (2/3, 1/3, 0), and M (1/2, 0, 0). The charge transfer (Δρ) between the biomarkers and graphene monolayer was calculated using the formula 2 Δρ=ρsurf+mol−(ρsurf+ρmol) where ρsurf+mol, ρsurf, and ρmol are the charge densities of the biomarker-adsorbed graphene complex, the pristine or doped graphene monolayer, and the isolated biomarker, respectively.

Results and Discussion

In this section, the adsorption properties of glycine and d-serine on pristine graphene were studied. To enhance the sensitivity of graphene toward the biomarkers, boron and titanium were substitutionally doped into the graphene sheet, while calcium was interstitially doped. The doped graphene systems (BDG, TiDG, and CaDG) were then analyzed following adsorption. The adsorption energies, band structures, sensitivities, charge transfer plots, and recovery times of each biomarker-graphene system were calculated and compared to determine the most effective sensor for the selected biomarkers of AD.

Boron-Doped Graphene as a Biosensor for Glycine and d-Serine

The adsorption behavior of glycine and d-serine on boron-doped graphene (BDG) was investigated to assess its potential as a reversible biosensor. To construct the boron-doped graphene monolayer, a single carbon atom in the 4 × 4 graphene supercell was substituted with a boron atom. Following structural optimization, the optimized lattice constant, B–C bond length, and band gap of BDG are presented in Table . The calculated lattice constant and B–C bond length of BDG are similar to those of pristine graphene. However, BDG possesses a direct band gap of 0.189 eV, in contrast to the zero band gap of pristine graphene. The optimized atomic structure of BDG is illustrated in Figure b. Initial adsorption geometries were chosen by aligning the oxygen-rich functional groups of each biomarker directly above the boron dopant, leveraging boron’s electron-deficient nature. The optimized structures of the biomarker-adsorbed systems are displayed in Figures a and a. Adsorption energies and distances are summarized in Table . BDG exhibited stronger adsorption compared to pristine graphene for both glycine and d-serine, with adsorption energies of −0.149 eV and −0.293 eV, respectively. The corresponding adsorption distances were calculated to be 2.96 Å for glycine and 3.30 Å for d-serine. Both distances are shorter than those observed on pristine graphene, further confirming enhanced binding on the B-doped substrate. Charge transfer plots for each biomarker-BDG system are displayed in Figures g and g. In all cases, the plots exhibit spatially limited charge redistribution near the adsorption site, with minimal overlap between electron accumulation (red) and depletion (cyan) regions. This behavior is characteristic of weak adsorption and is consistent with the moderate binding energies observed. The influence of adsorption on the electronic structure of BDG was determined through band structure analysis. Prior to adsorption, the band structure of the BDG monolayer was calculated and displayed in Figure f, showing a direct band gap of 0.189 eV. Then, following adsorption of glycine and d-serine, band structures were calculated and plotted for each biomarker-BDG system and are shown in Figures d and d. Adsorption of glycine induced a significant increase in the band gap of BDG from 0.189 to 0.240 eV, while d-serine induced a slight reduction from 0.189 to 0.185 eV. Notably, because the glycine-BDG system exhibited the most significant band gap shift, it creates a stronger modulation of conductivity. Given the correlation between conductivity and sensitivity, shown in eqs and , the larger band gap modulation induced by glycine suggests that BDG is more sensitive to glycine than to d-serine. Recovery times of the BDG sensor, estimated using eq , are presented in Table . Both systems exhibited recovery times on the subnanosecond to nanosecond scale in visible light and at room temperature, with values of 3.21 × 10–10 s and 8.43 × 10–8 s for glycine and d-serine, respectively. The calculated recovery times remain too short for stable molecular retention under ambient conditions, diminishing the accuracy and reliability of BDG as a reversible biosensor for glycine and d-serine. In conclusion, boron doping enhances the adsorption strength and induces moderate changes in the band gap upon biomarker adsorption. However, the low charge transfer and short recovery times limit the viability of BDG for practical biosensing of AD biomarkers. These findings emphasize the need for alternative dopants that promote stronger molecule–substrate interactions, resulting in longer recovery times and improved adsorption.

Titanium-Doped Graphene as a Biosensor for Glycine and d-Serine

To enhance the adsorption strength of pristine graphene toward glycine and d-serine, titanium doping was implemented to modify the graphene sheet. A single carbon atom in the graphene lattice was substituted with a titanium atom, forming a Ti-doped graphene (TiDG) monolayer. The optimized structure of TiDG is displayed in Figure c. Given titanium’s known affinity for oxygen-rich functional groups, , each biomarker was initially positioned with its carboxyl group oriented toward the Ti dopant. The fully relaxed structures of the biomarker-TiDG systems are displayed in Figures b and b. Adsorption energies and distances for the optimized complexes are summarized in Table . Glycine and d-serine exhibited highly negative adsorption energies of −6.03 eV and −6.16 eV, respectively. These large adsorption energies reflect strong chemisorption between the titanium dopant and the oxygen-containing functional groups of the biomarkers. Additionally, short binding distances of 2.14 Å and 2.21 Å for glycine and d-serine, respectively, were observed due to the formation of chemical bonds between the oxygen atoms of the functional groups and the Ti dopant. Charge transfer plots provide further insight into the adsorption properties of glycine and d-serine on TiDG. As shown in Figure h and h, the systems exhibit pronounced overlap between the electron accumulation (red) and depletion (cyan) regions near the Ti–O bonding interface. This overlap confirms significant electron redistribution and supports the presence of strong chemical bonding. To evaluate changes in the electronic structure, the band structures of TiDG were computed before and after adsorption. The preadsorption band structure of TiDG is shown in Figure g, while the postadsorption band structures are shown in Figures e and e. Glycine adsorption led to a moderate decrease in band gap from 0.443 to 0.414 eV, while d-serine adsorption caused a more pronounced shift from a direct to indirect band gap and a reduction from 0.443 to 0.321 eV. According to eq , these electronic modulations suggest improved sensitivity compared to pristine graphene, particularly for d-serine. Despite the presence of changes in electronic properties, the recovery times were calculated using eq . They were found to be 2.32 × 1089 s and 3.07 × 1091 s for glycine and d-serine, respectively. These extremely long recovery times signify permanent adsorption, rendering TiDG unsuitable for reversible biosensing under ambient conditions. In summary, titanium-doped graphene demonstrates strong chemical binding and measurable electronic modulation in response to the adsorption of glycine and d-serine. While this makes TiDG promising for irreversible or one-time-use biosensors, its lack of reversibility poses limitations for reversible sensing applications.

Calcium-Doped Graphene as a Biosensor for Glycine and d-Serine

To achieve a balance between adsorption strength and recovery potential, calcium-doped graphene (CaDG) was constructed by placing a calcium atom above the hollow site at the center of a graphene hexagon. This configuration is known to facilitate moderate interactions with oxygen-rich functional groups, making it suitable for detecting biomarkers such as glycine and d-serine, which both contain oxygen-rich functional groups. The optimized atomic structure of CaDG is shown in Figure d, and its key structural parameters are listed in Table . The optimized adsorption geometries of the CaDG-biomarker complexes are shown in Figures c and c. Following adsorption, the binding distances were reduced to approximately 2.19 Å and 2.18 Å for glycine and d-serine, respectively, indicating stronger interactions than those observed for boron-doped graphene, but weaker than the titanium-doped systems. Adsorption energies were calculated to be −0.669 eV and −1.63 eV for glycine and d-serine respectively, reflecting moderate binding strength. These values are summarized in Table . Charge transfer analysis further confirmed the formation of stable complexes. As illustrated in Figures i and i, significant overlap was observed between electron accumulation (red) and depletion (cyan) regions, particularly near the Ca–O bonding interface. These interactions indicate moderate to strong adsorption between CaDG and the biomarkers. To assess the effect of molecular adsorption on the electronic structure, the band structures of CaDG were computed before and after adsorption. Prior to biomarker adsorption, CaDG exhibited an indirect band gap of 0.057 eV, displayed in Figure h and listed in Table . The adsorption of glycine and d-serine resulted in the complete closure of the band gap, leading to a transition to metallic behavior. The postadsorption band structures are displayed in Figures f and f, with both exhibiting a closed band gap. This transition results in a significant change in the band gap, which, according to eq , corresponds to the most significant change in conductivity. Therefore, according to eq , CaDG exhibits the greatest sensitivity toward glycine and d-serine. Recovery times, displayed in Table , were calculated to be 0.170 s for glycine, indicating rapid desorption. In contrast, d-serine exhibited a significantly longer recovery time of 2.48 × 1015 s, suggesting slow desorption and potential irreversibility under ambient conditions. These findings indicate that CaDG could serve as an effective reversible biosensor for glycine, but may be less suitable for reversible sensing of d-serine due to its slow desorption. Overall, calcium-doped graphene offers an optimal balance between adsorption strength, electronic modulation, and recovery time. The semiconductor-to-metal transition upon biomarker binding, along with short recovery times for most targets, indicates that CaDG is a promising candidate for the practical, label-free biosensing of biomarkers associated with early stage Alzheimer’s disease.

Conclusion

In conclusion, we investigated the adsorption behavior of two biomarkers of Alzhiemer’s diseaseglycine and d-serineon pristine and doped graphene using DFT calculations. The electronic properties of the doped graphene systems underwent significant changes upon biomarker adsorption, in contrast to the minuscule changes observed in pristine graphene. The calculated adsorption energies, charge transfer, and electronic band structures indicate that BDG, TiDG, and CaDG all enhance biomarker adsorption. Among the doped systems, B-doped graphene exhibited moderate enhancements in d-serine adsorption compared to pristine graphene, but displayed subnanosecond recovery times, thereby diminishing its retention capability. Ti-doped graphene exhibited the strongest adsorption among the doped systems; however, its exceedingly long recovery times suggested irreversible binding, rendering it unsuitable for reversible biosensing. Finally, Ca-doped graphene exhibited a favorable combination of moderate-to-strong adsorption energies, significant changes in conductivity, high sensitivity, and rapid recovery times. Based on these results, B-, Ti-, and Ca-doped graphene are all viable candidates for the detection of AD biomarkers, with Ca-doped graphene emerging as the most promising option for reversible biosensing applications. Future work should prioritize experimental validation of these computational results and the evaluation of other novel biomarkers of Alzheimer’s disease to expand diagnostic relevance. Further efforts could also involve the testing of different computational methods, including hybrid functionals like B3LYP or many-body methods like GW-BSE, laying the groundwork for the development of next-generation, noninvasive biosensors for early stage detection of Alzheimer’s disease.
