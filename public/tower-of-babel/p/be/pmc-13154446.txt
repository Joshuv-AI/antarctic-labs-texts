Biomechanical analysis of interradicular and infrazygomatic miniscrew anchorage during two-step and en-masse retraction: A finite element study
Authors: Ruşen Erdem, Nihat Kılıç
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundThe aim of this study was to evaluate the effects of interradicular and infrazygomatic miniscrew anchorages on tooth movement and von Mises stress distribution during two-step and en-masse retraction using three-dimensional finite element analysis.MethodsEight finite element models were constructed combining two retraction mechanics (en-masse and two-step), two anchorage types (infrazygomatic and interradicular miniscrews), and two power arm heights (3 and 6 mm). A 150 g retraction force was applied along the line connecting the miniscrew and power arm. Static structural analyses were performed in ANSYS to calculate tooth displacement (x-, y-, and z-axes) and von Mises stress distribution.ResultsStress was mainly concentrated in the cortical bone around miniscrew insertion areas, as well as on the lateral incisors and canines. Increasing the power arm height from 3 mm to 6 mm reduced uncontrolled tipping and extrusion, improving biomechanical control. Infrazygomatic anchorage produced less tipping and extrusion than interradicular anchorage in both mechanics. Two-step retraction generally showed slightly more controlled tooth movement compared with en-masse retraction.ConclusionsThis finite element simulation showed that the two-step retraction approach with infrazygomatic anchorage and a 6-mm power arm resulted in comparatively reduced tipping and a more distributed stress pattern under the conditions of the present model. However, complete translational movement was not achieved in any model. As finite element models cannot fully replicate clinical conditions, these findings should be interpreted with caution and validated through clinical studies.

Background

Extraction of the maxillary first premolars is a widely used treatment approach in the management of conditions requiring anterior tooth retraction, such as severe crowding, bimaxillary dentoalveolar protrusion, Class II malocclusion, convex facial profile, and borderline skeletal relationships [1]. In these patients, the primary objective of orthodontic treatment is to reduce dental protrusion and improve facial convexity through the controlled retraction of the anterior teeth under maximum anchorage following premolar extraction. Therefore, effective and well-controlled closure of the extraction spaces constitutes a critical stage of orthodontic treatment planning [2].

Closure of the extraction spaces is a critical stage in achieving esthetic and functional treatment goals and is commonly performed using two different approaches: two-step retraction, in which the canines are retracted first followed by the four incisors, and en-masse retraction, in which the six anterior teeth are retracted simultaneously as a single unit [2]. The effectiveness of these retraction mechanics depends heavily on maintaining adequate anchorage throughout treatment. Anchorage control has always played a pivotal role in achieving successful orthodontic outcomes. Traditionally, anchorage was reinforced by engaging more teeth, using musculature, or applying extraoral appliances [3]. However, these methods have limitations related to patient cooperation, the risk of allergic or iatrogenic complications, and unwanted reactive tooth movements [4]. These limitations led to the development of temporary skeletal anchorage devices (TSADs), which provide stable bone-borne anchorage with minimal patient cooperation [4, 5]. TSADs, particularly miniscrews, are an effective option for maxillary distalization because they provide absolute anchorage independent of patient compliance and are easy to place [6].

Contemporary orthodontic techniques require an understanding of dentoalveolar biomechanical responses, increasing the need for advanced analytical methods. Research over the past five decades has introduced several experimental techniques, including photoelastic analysis, strain measurement, and laser holography, to evaluate mechanical changes in biological systems [7–9]. Among numerical approaches, the finite element method (FEM) enables the simulation of complex structures and material properties, providing quantitative insights into the biomechanical responses of dentoalveolar tissues under orthodontic forces. In addition, finite element analysis (FEA) helps eliminate the influence of confounding variables and offers a cost-effective method for evaluating the biomechanical effects of a single influencing factor [10].

In the literature, the biomechanical effects of en-masse retraction on anterior teeth have been investigated using FE models evaluating different biomechanical strategies and anchorage control approaches during orthodontic space closure [11–13]. However, information comparing en-masse retraction and two-step retraction using different miniscrew anchorage sites, such as infrazygomatic and interradicular regions, remains limited. Moreover, few studies have simultaneously evaluated the combined effects of anchorage location, retraction protocol, and power arm height on tooth displacement patterns and stress distribution using a three-dimensional (3D) finite element (FE) model. Therefore, the purpose of this study was to compare, through FEA, the biomechanical effects of infrazygomatic and interradicular miniscrew anchorage on the anterior teeth during en-masse and two-step retraction protocols.

Methods

This study was approved by the Ethics Committee of the Faculty of Dentistry, Atatürk University (decision no. 08, dated January 3, 2022) and supported by the Atatürk University Scientific Research Projects Coordination Unit under project number TDK-2022-10331.

Cone-beam computed tomography (CBCT) data were obtained from a 17-year-old male patient with balanced skeletal measurements, normal root development, no missing teeth, and no periodontal bone loss. The scans were acquired using a NewTom VGi evo (QR Verona, Verona, Italy) unit (NNT Viewer; amorphous silicon flat panel; field of view 15 × 12 cm; voxel size x–y: 0.478 mm, z: 0.30 mm; ~194 µSv; upright position; 512 × 512 matrix; 628 slices) and exported in Digital Imaging and Communications in Medicine (DICOM) format. The data were imported into Materialise Interactive Medical Image Control System (MIMICS v21; Materialise, Leuven, Belgium). Hounsfield unit (HU) thresholds were set to exclude soft tissues (e.g., muscles, ligaments, and vessels) and to emphasise denser structures (e.g., teeth and cortical bone). Segmentation masks were created for different density ranges, and 3D models of the maxilla (cortical and trabecular) and teeth were reconstructed and exported in Standard Tessellation Language (STL) format.

The periodontal ligament (PDL) was modelled in MIMICS by enlarging the tooth geometry by 0.25 mm using the “rescale” command and subtracting it from the original tooth via the “Boolean” function, thereby defining a 0.25-mm-thick linear elastic film surrounding the root surfaces with reference to the cortical bone [11, 12]. 3D modelling of orthodontic components was performed in SolidWorks (Dassault Systèmes, Vélizy-Villacoublay, France) using a digital caliper. The modelled components included interradicular miniscrews (1.6 × 8 mm), infrazygomatic miniscrews (2 × 12 mm), power arms (3 mm and 6 mm), closed-coil springs, a 0.017 × 0.022-inch stainless-steel archwire, and 0.018 × 0.025-inch slot Roth brackets. Power arms were attached to the archwire between the canine and lateral incisor, while the stainless-steel archwire was positioned at the center of the bracket slot. The interradicular miniscrew was placed between the second premolar and first molar, 6 mm superior to the alveolar crest, and inserted perpendicular to the cortical bone surface relative to the occlusal plane [14, 15]. The infrazygomatic miniscrew was inserted approximately 15 mm superior to the posterior occlusal plane, with a 60–70° gingival inclination relative to the occlusal plane and approximately 30° distal angulation [16].

A total of eight FE models were created, representing combinations of two retraction mechanics (en-masse and two-step retraction), two anchorage types (infrazygomatic and interradicular), and two power arm heights (3 mm and 6 mm). These included: En-masse retraction with interradicular miniscrew and 3-mm power arm (Model A), En-masse retraction with interradicular miniscrew and 6-mm power arm (Model B), En-masse retraction with infrazygomatic miniscrew and 3-mm power arm (Model C), En-masse retraction with infrazygomatic miniscrew and 6-mm power arm (Model D), Two-step retraction with interradicular miniscrew and 3-mm power arm (Model E), Two-step retraction with interradicular miniscrew and 6-mm power arm (Model F), Two-step retraction with infrazygomatic miniscrew and 3-mm power arm (Model G), and Two-step retraction with infrazygomatic miniscrew and 6-mm power arm (Model H) (Fig. 1). The SolidWorks (Dassault Systèmes, Vélizy-Villacoublay, France) models were imported into ANSYS (ANSYS Inc., Canonsburg, PA, USA) for static structural analysis. All materials were assumed to be homogeneous, isotropic, and linearly elastic, and their Young’s moduli and Poisson’s ratios are listed in Table 1 [17–22].

The superior part of the maxillary bone (except the palatal bone) was fixed in all directions (x, y, and z axes) to simulate the anatomical constraint provided by the circummaxillary sutures [23–25]. A retraction force of 150 g was applied [12, 26–29] via closed-coil springs along the line connecting the power arm and the miniscrew, with direction and attachment points arranged according to each retraction protocol. Frictional contacts were defined between the archwire and brackets (coefficient of friction µ: 0.2) [30], while bonded contacts were assigned between the miniscrew and bone, bracket and tooth, and power arm and archwire. The bonded contact between the miniscrew and bone was assumed to simulate the primary stability of the miniscrew immediately after insertion, representing an ideal initial anchorage condition in the FE model [31]. The 3D geometric models were meshed with 10-node tetrahedral SOLID186/187 elements to convert them into numerical models (Table 2). Static structural analyses were then performed to evaluate tooth displacements and stress distributions under the applied retraction forces.

Results

The results presented below reflect the initial mechanical response of the modeled dentoalveolar structures under the defined loading conditions within the FE simulation. The anatomical model was constructed from the CBCT data of a single 17-year-old individual; therefore, the findings should be interpreted in this specific anatomical configuration and under the assumptions of the present FE model.

In this study, data from the right maxillary segment were analyzed. A single global coordinate system was used for all measurements. In this system, the X-axis represented the transverse direction (left–right), the Y-axis represented the anteroposterior direction, and the Z-axis represented the vertical direction (superior–inferior). Tooth displacements were calculated according to this global coordinate system for all teeth. Negative values indicated movement in the opposite directions. Tooth displacement and von Mises stresses were calculated and visualized using color maps with a standardized color scale across the models. The minimum and maximum values, together with their corresponding units, were displayed on the color scale to allow direct visual comparison of stress distributions between the simulations. Displacement was assessed along the X (Fig. 2), Y (Fig. 3), and Z (Fig. 4) axes in millimeters (mm) (Table 3), and von Mises stresses were expressed in megapascals (MPa = N/mm²).

Examination of the von Mises stress distributions showed that stress was predominantly concentrated in the cortical bone, with minimal transmission to the trabecular bone. The highest stress values in both bone types were concentrated around the miniscrew insertion areas (Figs. 5 and 6). On the anterior teeth, stress was primarily concentrated on the lateral incisors and canines (Fig. 7).

An increase in power arm height generally tended to reduce uncontrolled tipping and extrusion across all retraction mechanics and anchorage types. In the en-masse retraction models (A, B, C, D), a higher hook position caused a shift in anterior tooth movement from uncontrolled tipping to controlled tipping or near-translation. In the “two-step retraction” models (E, F, G, H), increasing hook height generally reduced tipping tendencies in all teeth, and it was associated with a reduction in extrusion, particularly in the central incisors and canines. Overall, increasing the power arm height improved biomechanical control during space closure by moving the line of action closer to the anterior segment’s centre of resistance.

In the en-masse retraction group, Model D, which used infrazygomatic miniscrew anchorage and a 6-mm power arm, exhibited less tipping than the other models. Similarly, in the two-step retraction group, Model H, which used infrazygomatic miniscrew anchorage and a 6-mm power arm, showed less tipping than the other models. Among all models, the least amount of tipping was observed in Model H. In the intergroup comparison, the amount of tipping observed in the X and Y axes for all teeth except the lateral incisor, as well as the amount of extrusion in the Z axis for most teeth, was lower in the two-step retraction group. Except for minor variations in the lateral incisor, the overall tipping of teeth was lower in the two-step retraction group compared with the en-masse group; however, this difference was minimal and may not represent a meaningful biomechanical difference.

When the effects of infrazygomatic and interradicular miniscrew anchorage on tooth movement were compared, infrazygomatic anchorage generally showed a tendency to reduce tipping and extrusion in both “en-masse” and “two-step” retraction groups. In en-masse retraction models, infrazygomatic miniscrews with 3-mm and 6-mm power arms provided more controlled tooth movement than interradicular miniscrews. Depending on tooth position, slight variations were observed in the intrusion–extrusion direction, particularly in the lateral incisor. Similarly, in the two-step retraction models, infrazygomatic anchorage tended to reduce tipping in almost all teeth and to decrease extrusion.

Discussion

In patients with Class II Division 1 malocclusion, the extraction of the maxillary first premolars is a conventional treatment approach to correct excessive overjet by retracting the anterior teeth into the extraction spaces. This procedure contributes to improved soft tissue profile and overall facial esthetics [32]. A variety of orthodontic techniques have been proposed for space closure, including sliding mechanics [33], frictionless loop mechanics [34], and labial and lingual appliance systems [35]. FEM provides a powerful computational tool for simulating these biomechanical interactions, allowing precise evaluation of stress distribution and tooth displacement under well-controlled force vectors. FEA eliminates biological variability, thereby providing a more standardized framework for analyzing orthodontic force systems [36].

The findings of the present study indicate that increasing the height of the power arm generally improved control of anterior tooth movement by reducing tipping tendencies under simulated conditions. This effect can be explained by the biomechanical relationship between the applied force vector and the center of resistance of the anterior dental segment. Increasing the height of the power arm shifts the line of action of the retraction force closer to the center of resistance, thereby reducing the moment responsible for uncontrolled tipping. Similar biomechanical effects have been reported in recent FE studies investigating miniscrew-assisted retraction mechanics, which demonstrated that increasing the vertical level of force application can improve control of anterior tooth movement by modifying the force vector relative to the center of resistance [1, 6, 37] In line with these findings, Oğuz and Özden [37], who evaluated IZC-supported maxillary arch distalization using FEA, reported that increasing the vertical level of force application shifted the force vector closer to the center of resistance, resulting in more controlled sagittal tooth movement with reduced crown tipping and relatively greater root displacement. Hedayati and Shomali [12] and Kim et al. [38] observed nearly translational tooth movement with 8–9 mm power arms, whereas Sung et al. [11] noted that even an 8-mm arm failed to produce full bodily retraction. Tominaga et al. [39] further indicated that power arms shorter than 9.1 mm prevent translation, and El Khoury et al. [23] emphasized that some degree of tipping is inevitable during miniscrew–assisted retraction.

The changes observed along the Z-axis can be explained biomechanically in relation to the vertical component of the force system generated by the miniscrew–power arm configuration. Increasing the height of the power arm alters the vertical position of the force vector relative to the center of resistance and may therefore influence intrusive or extrusive tendencies of the anterior teeth. In the present study, increasing the power arm height generally reduced the extrusion tendency of the anterior teeth. Similarly, Parashar et al. [40] reported anterior extrusion when the force vector was positioned below the center of resistance. Furthermore, Schwertner et al. [25] reported that shorter power arms produced greater extrusion of the incisors, whereas increasing the power arm height reduced the extrusion tendency. These findings support the biomechanical concept that vertical control during anterior retraction is strongly influenced by the geometry of the force system and the relative position of the force vector. Overall, increasing the power arm length reduced tipping and extrusion tendencies and promoted more controlled anterior retraction; however, complete bodily translation was not achieved. Considering that such movement patterns may occur, additional torque control may be considered before retraction, for example through the use of high-torque brackets or pre-torqued archwires.

When the en-masse and two-step retraction protocols were compared, the two-step configurations (e.g., Model H compared with Model D) exhibited slightly less tipping and extrusion; however, the observed differences were relatively small within the limitations of the FE model. These findings are consistent with results from prospective clinical studies. Felemban et al. [41], and Schneider et al. [42] reported comparable treatment outcomes for both approaches. In contrast, Rizk et al. [43], and Al-Sibaie and Hajeer [44] demonstrated that miniscrew-anchored en-masse retraction offers advantages in terms of treatment efficiency and anchorage stability. They found that in cases of severe protrusion, space closure was achieved more rapidly and with less anchorage loss. Therefore, while the two-step approach may provide more refined control in complex malocclusions, miniscrew assisted en-masse retraction can be regarded as a more time efficient alternative with stable anchorage.

In both retraction groups (en-masse and two-step retraction), infrazygomatic anchorage generally produced less tipping and extrusion than interradicular anchorage, apart from minor individual variations. Infrazygomatic miniscrews are placed in extra-alveolar cortical bone, which allows the force vector to be applied from a more superior and posterior position compared with interradicular miniscrews. This configuration may reduce the moment acting on the anterior segment and improve biomechanical control during retraction. Although direct FE comparisons between these two anchorage sites during anterior retraction remain limited, previous studies have suggested that infrazygomatic crest miniscrews can provide stable skeletal anchorage and improve anchorage control in orthodontic mechanics [45, 46]. Therefore, when combined with a properly designed anterior retraction hook, infrazygomatic crest mini-implants may provide improved anchorage control in both the sagittal and vertical dimensions.

Vertical control is particularly critical in clinical situations such as deep-bite or high-angle Class II camouflage cases with increased overjet [44, 47]. El Khoury et al. [23] reported inward transverse displacement of the canines across all models, regardless of power arm height. Similarly, in the present study, transverse constriction was observed in the canines and adjacent teeth in all models. The more controlled nature of this movement in the infrazygomatic anchorage models may be explained by the broader transverse vector influence associated with this anchorage site.

Analysis of the von Mises stress distribution across the models revealed that stress was predominantly concentrated within the cortical bone, with only a minor portion transmitted to the trabecular bone. The highest stress values were observed in the cortical regions surrounding the miniscrews, which can be explained by cortical bone’s higher Young’s modulus and greater resistance to deformation. These findings are consistent with previous studies reporting higher stress levels in cortical bone compared with trabecular bone [20]. Examination of the anterior teeth showed that, in the en-masse retraction group, stress was primarily concentrated around the lateral incisors and canines, whereas in the two-step retraction group, stress was more pronounced in the lateral incisors. This localized accumulation of stress likely reflects the initiation of tipping movements in these teeth during the early stages of retraction. Additionally, stress levels were higher in the coronal regions of anterior teeth than in their apical areas. These results corroborate the findings of Ali et al. [48] who reported similar stress distribution patterns in their FE models. Furthermore, variation in power arm height did not significantly affect the overall stress distribution within the teeth and alveolar bone. However, it should be noted that the positions of the power arms and miniscrews in previous studies do not exactly correspond to those in the present models; therefore, direct comparisons of stress magnitudes across different configurations may not be entirely valid.

Limitations

In this FEA, tooth movements were modeled within the defined PDL limits, representing the initial biomechanical displacement. Therefore, the results reflect the immediate mechanical response rather than the long-term biological adaptations that occur during clinical tooth movement. For analytical simplicity, the modeled structures were assumed to be homogeneous and isotropic.

Another limitation of this study is that the FE model was constructed using the CBCT data of a single individual. Therefore, the results represent the biomechanical response under this specific anatomical configuration and may not fully reflect anatomical variations among different patients. Moreover, although the PDL exhibits variable thickness under physiological conditions, it was modeled with a constant thickness in this study, which may not fully represent the complex biomechanical behavior of the PDL in vivo. In addition, sensitivity analyses evaluating the potential influence of boundary condition assumptions and friction parameters were not performed in the present study.

These simplifications may partially limit the reflection of individual variations in tissue properties under clinical conditions. Therefore, the findings should be interpreted with caution and validated through future experimental and clinical studies.

Conclusions

The two-step retraction configuration demonstrated a slightly lower tipping tendency than the en-masse retraction. However, the magnitude of this difference was relatively small within the limits of the FE model.Infrazygomatic miniscrew anchorage produced less tipping and extrusion compared with the interradicular anchorage.Increasing the power arm height from 3 mm to 6 mm generally reduced tipping along the X- and Y-axes and decreased extrusion along the Z-axis; however, complete translational movement was not achieved in any model.In the en-masse retraction group, the configuration using infrazygomatic anchorage with a 6-mm power arm (Model D), and in the two-step retraction group the same configuration (Model H), showed lower tipping values compared with the other configurations. Within the assumptions of this FE model, among all models, Model H demonstrated comparatively less tipping and extrusion under the stated conditions.The von Mises stress analysis showed that stress was concentrated around the miniscrew insertion areas and within the cortical bone, with only a small portion transmitted to the trabecular bone; in the anterior teeth, it was particularly concentrated on the lateral incisors and canines.
