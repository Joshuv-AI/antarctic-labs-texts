Sustainable fabrication of TIF@ZIF-8 nanocomposites: characterization, antioxidant potential, and bioactive applications
Authors: Raju Potnuru, Gopi Mamidi, Tirumala Santhoshkumar Saripalli, Mary Shamya Arokiarajan, John Christopher, Achintya Kumar Mandal, Jameel Mohammad
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractThe exploration of advanced delivery systems for natural antioxidants is crucial for enhancing their bioavailability and therapeutic efficacy. Metal–organic frameworks, particularly zeolitic imidazolate frameworks (ZIFs), offer promising solutions due to their tunable pore structures, robust chemical stability, and customizable surface functionalities, making them ideal candidates for drug delivery and the encapsulation of bioactive compounds. This study aimed to encapsulate Tamarindus indica L. (TIF) flower extract into ZIF-8 using a Nano-precipitation method to develop the TIF@ZIF-8 nanocomposite with enhanced antioxidant and antimicrobial properties. The TIF@ZIF-8 nanocomposite was synthesized via Nano-precipitation. Antioxidant activity was evaluated using DPPH radical scavenging and lipid peroxidation inhibition assays. The phytochemical composition, physicochemical properties were performed following standard protocols. A qualitative fingerprint of TIF was analyzed using high-performance thin layer chromatography. The key phytoconstituents were identified using gas chromatography–mass spectrometry (GC–MS). Structural and morphological characterizations of the nanocomposite were performed using powder X-ray diffraction, scanning electron microscopy, transmission electron microscopy, and thermal gravimetric analysis. The TIF@ZIF-8 nanocomposite demonstrated significant antioxidant activity with a DPPH scavenging rate of 72.54% and lipid peroxidation inhibition of 69.14%, outperforming Vitamin C and BHT. The IC50 values for DPPH and lipid peroxidation inhibition were 64.14 µg/mL and 57.74 µg/mL, respectively. GC–MS identified key bioactive compounds, including Isobutylene epoxide and Ethyl leucinate. This study demonstrates the potential of ZIF-8 as a carrier for bioactive plant compounds. Introduction

Tamarindus indica L., commonly known as tamarind, is a tropical evergreen tree with significant medicinal and industrial applications [1]. It has been widely used in traditional medicine for centuries due to its diverse therapeutic properties, including antioxidant, antimicrobial, anti-inflammatory, and antidiabetic effects [2]. Various parts of the tamarind tree, including its pulp, seeds, and flowers, contain essential nutrients, bioactive compounds, and phytochemicals that contribute to its medicinal value. Given its wide-ranging benefits, tamarind has been extensively studied for its potential in pharmaceutical and nutraceutical applications [3].

Numerous cultivars have previously been acknowledged in many nations across Asia and Africa. Cultivars are differentiated by tree morphology, flowering patterns, pod attributes, pulp qualities, and seed count per pod. T. indica possesses remarkable therapeutic qualities. Numerous individuals utilize various components of this species for traditional remedies of diverse diseases. Various pharmaceuticals have been developed utilizing distinct components of this species. It serves as a crucial element in cardiac and glycemic-lowering pharmaceuticals. Tamarind possesses potential for the manufacture of various industrial goods, including tamarind pulp powder, tamarind juice concentrate, tamarind kernel powder, tartaric acid, tartrates, pectin, and alcohol. Throughout the years, every component of the Tamarindus tree has been traditionally utilized in the Ayurvedic medicinal system for its nutritional benefits [4].

Flowers are bisexual, measuring 2.5 cm in width, including five petals, arranged in tiny racemes, and exhibiting a yellow hue with orange or red streaks. The buds exhibit a pink hue, with four sepals and five petals. The flowers of Tamarindus indica L. are a rich source of bioactive compounds, including phenolics, flavonoids, triterpenes, and organic acids, which contribute to their strong antioxidant and antimicrobial properties. Previous studies have explored the chemical composition and pharmacological potential of tamarind fruit and seeds; however, limited research has been conducted on the bioactivity of its flowers [5]. This study aims to bridge this gap by investigating the phytochemical profile and bioactive potential of tamarind flower extract (TIF) and enhancing its efficacy through nanotechnology-based encapsulation .

Metal organic frameworks (MOFs) have garnered significant attention due to their highly adjustable pore structures, impressive chemical stability, and diverse surface functionalities [6], which make them ideal candidates for a range of applications, including energy storage [7], chemical separation [8], catalysis [9], and drug delivery [10]. Among MOFs, Zeolitic Imidazolate Frameworks (ZIFs) stand out for their high surface area, porosity, and biocompatibility, making them promising candidates for applications in catalysis, drug delivery, and chemical separation. ZIF-8, in particular, has shown remarkable stability under physiological conditions, preventing premature drug release and improving bioavailability [11–15]. Despite these advantages, the potential of ZIFs as carriers for plant-derived bioactive compounds remains underexplored.

To address this research gap, we developed a novel nanoscale TIF@ZIF-8 nanocomposite using a simple nano-precipitation method. This nanocomposite effectively encapsulates tamarind flower extract, enhancing its bioavailability and stability while maintaining its potent antioxidant and antimicrobial properties [16–17]. The goal is to encapsulate TIF extract within ZIF-8 using a simple nano-precipitation method, and to examine the potential synergistic effects of the bioactivity of TIF with the catalytic and drug delivery properties of ZIF-8. By investigating this novel composite, the research will address the existing knowledge gap surrounding the catalytic applications of ZIFs and their potential for enhancing the bioactivity of MOF-based nanocomposites, providing a new direction for future applications in drug delivery and catalysis. Our study demonstrates that TIF@ZIF-8 exhibits a superior DPPH radical scavenging rate and lipid peroxidation inhibition compared to standard antioxidants like Vitamin C and BHT. Additionally, the nanocomposite exhibits significant antibacterial properties, highlighting its potential as a multifunctional therapeutic agent.

The synthesis and characterization of the TIF@ZIF-8 nanocomposite were conducted using advanced analytical techniques, including powder X-ray diffraction (PXRD) which was employed to confirm the crystallinity and phase composition of the nanocomposite, ensuring the successful formation of the ZIF-8 structure, transmission electron microscopy (TEM) is allowed for the visualization of the nanoscale morphology and particle size, providing information on the uniformity and dispersion of the nanocomposite, scanning electron microscopy (SEM) was used to examine the surface morphology and texture, offering a closer look at the overall structure and particle distribution, and thermal gravimetric analysis (TGA) helped assess the thermal stability and the presence of the TIF extract in the composite by monitoring weight loss at different temperatures [18–22]. This study not only presents an eco-friendly and cost-effective method for MOF-based nanocomposite synthesis but also opens new avenues for the application of plant-derived bioactive compounds in biomedical and pharmaceutical fields.

In this paper, we discuss the synthesis process, structural characterization, and bioactive properties of the TIF@ZIF-8 nanocomposite, emphasizing its potential for antioxidant and antibacterial applications. This work highlights the role of ZIF-8 as an effective carrier for natural bioactive compounds and its promising implications in drug delivery, therapeutic formulations and biomedical applications.

Methods and material

Plant material preparation

Collection and authentication of the Tamarindus indica L. flower

Tamarindus indica L. flowers were collected at Regional Research Institute of Unani Medicine in the campus, Chennai (Tamilnadu, India) in the peroid of June to August (Springs & Summer) shown in Fig. 1 and authenticated/varified by Dr. Venkatesan (Botanist) of Regional Research Institute of Unani Medicine (RRIUM), Chennai, meanwhile a Sample submitted in the Museum of DSRU (Drug Standardization Research Unit)-RRIUM Chennai-600,013 for validation purpose.

Natural flower of Tamarindus indica L

Drying and powdering of flowers

The plant material was shade-dried and ground into a coarse powder using an electric grinder, yielding 25 g of dry flower material (Fig. 2).

Dried & coarse powdered Tamarindus indica L. flower

Alcoholic extraction of Tamarindus indica L. flower

Tamarindus indica L. Ethanol solvent flower extract was procured according to the methodology outlined by UPI 2016. The powdered 25 g of Tamarindus indica L. (Fig. 2) flowers were subjected to a Soxhlet device containing 150 mL of ethanol solvent at 40 °C for 20 min, repeated three times for thorough extraction. Upon completion, the extracts were filtered using Whatman filter paper and stored in sealed glass vials for analysis.

Phytochemical and quality analysis

Qualitative phytochemical analysis

The content of amino acids, steroids, carbohydrates, reducing sugars, cardiac glycosides, phenols, tannins, terpenoids, alkaloids, flavonoids, saponins, and anthrones was assessed in several solvent extracts of Tamarindus indica L. (flower) [23].

Physicochemical analysis

A quality control research was conducted by evaluating physicochemical parameters, including the extractive values of ethanol, hexane, and water extracts. The total ash value, acid-insoluble ash, moisture content, loss on drying, pH values (5% solution), volatile oils, and heavy metals were assessed according to recognized methodologies [24, 25].

High-performance thin layer chromatography (HPTLC)

The ethanolic extract of Tamarindus indica L. flowers was subjected to HPTLC analysis. The analysis was conducted using a CAMAG HPTLC system (Switzerland) with a pre-coated silica gel 60F254 HPTLC plate (5 × 10 cm, Merck, Germany). Five microliter (µL) of the sample were applied to the plate using a CAMAG ATS4 sample applicator. The mobile phase consisted of Toluene: Ethyl acetate: Formic acid (7:3:0.01). The plates were developed to a height of 8 cm at ambient temperature in a CAMAG twin-through glass chamber (10 × 10 cm) with a 20-min saturation time. After development, the plates were air-dried at room temperature. The chromatographic bands were visualized using the CAMAG UV Visualizer 2 at 254 nm and 366 nm. The chromatograms were scanned using a CAMAG TLC scanner, and the Rf values were documented with WINCATS planar chromatography manager software [26].

Gas chromatography–mass spectrometry

The ethanolic extract of Tamarindus indica L. flower was further analyzed using GC–MS to identify bioactive compounds. The analysis was conducted on a Perkin Elmer Clarus 680 GC–MS system (Waltham, MA, USA) equipped with an Elite-5 MS capillary column (30 m × 0.25 mm ID × 250 μm film thickness). Helium was used as the carrier gas at a constant flow rate of 1 mL/min. The oven temperature was programmed to increase at 6 °C/min until reaching 150 °C. The injector temperature was set at 260 °C. A 1 µL sample was injected with a split ratio of 1:10. Ionization was achieved in electron ionization (EI) mode with an energy of 70 eV. The transfer line and ion source temperatures were maintained at 220 °C. Data acquisition was performed in the range of 40–600 Da with a scan time of 0.2 s and an interval of 0.1 s. The resulting mass spectra were compared with those in the NIST Library database to identify the chemical constituents of the extract [26].

Nanoparticle synthesis

Experimental procedure for synthesis of TIF@ZIF-8 nanoparticle

Solid 2-methylimidazole (H-Meim) (3.3 g, 2 mmol) was dissolved in 50 mL of methanol and added slowly to an RB flask. Slowly, solid zinc nitrate hexahydrate (1.5 g, 2 mmol) was dissolved in 50 mL of methanol, and the extract of TIF (10 mL) was added slowly, drop by drop. The mixture was stirred for one hour with the support of a magnetic stirrer at 400 rpm. The residue is separated by centrifuge (at 4000 rpm for 15 min), and the residues with water and methanol. The sample is dried; 0.258 g of colorless polyhedral solid is obtained. The crystalline and phase structure of the synthesized ZIF-8 was characterised by powder-XRD, TEM, and SEM, respectively.

Characterization techniques for TIF@ZIF-8

Various analytical approaches were employed to determine the crystalline structure of TIF@ZIF-8. X-ray diffraction (XRD) observations were conducted across a 2θ range of 0 to 90° utilizing a Bruker Kappa Apex II equipment. The apparatus was utilized with CuKα radiation (λ = 1.54060 Å) at parameters of 40 kV, 35 mA, and a scanning rate of 0.2 s. The surface topography and elemental content of TIF@ZIF-8 were analyzed using scanning electron microscopy (SEM). The specific apparatus utilized was the JEOL 6390LA/OXFORD XMX N, which incorporated energy-dispersive X-ray (EDX) spectroscopy. SEM pictures were captured at an acceleration voltage of 20 kV. High-resolution transmission electron microscopy (HRTEM) was utilized for an in-depth examination of the microstructure and particle size. The JEOL/JEM 2100 instrument operated at a voltage of 200 kV. Carbon-copper grids were employed for sample preparation, allowing for the acquisition of micrographs that enabled particle size measurement. Additionally, selected area electron diffraction (SAED) pictures were obtained using the HRTEM apparatus.

Bioactivity and safety assays

Microbial load determination

The microbiological harmony of commercially available and handmade herbal remedies was evaluated using (UPI, 2016) and the World Health Organization (WHO guidelines, 2007). Experiments were carried out to ascertain the aerobic proliferation of bacteria and fungus in a 1 gram specimen. The samples were mixed and added to a peptone broth. They were subsequently diluted many times until the appropriate concentration was attained. The microbiological tests were performed three times, using the pour plate technique to evaluate the survival of bacteria and determine the types of fungi present. The CFU/g count was assessed and compared to the guidelines established by the World Health Organization (WHO). Any samples with a bacterial count over 105 CFU per gram of medical product were considered unsuitable for evaluation.

Identification of bacteria

In order to differentiate and confirm the identity of bacteria, the samples were diluted in peptone water and subsequently agitated rigorously to achieve uniformity. The culture mediums utilized for examining Escherichia coli, Salmonella spp., Pseudomonas aeruginosa, and Staphylococcus aureus were MacConkey agar, cetrimide agar, EMB agar, and Mannitol salt agar. Following the incubation period, the pathogenic bacteria were initially identified by observing the morphology of the colonies, employing Gram staining, and conducting biochemical assays to assess oxidase, gas, and catalase production.

Aflatoxin test using afla-test fluorometer

Aflatoxins are toxic secondary metabolites produced primarily by Aspergillus flavus and Aspergillus parasiticus, posing serious health risks through food contamination. AflaTest is a quantitative method used to detect various aflatoxin types (B1, B2, G1, G2, M1, and M2). In this procedure, 1 g of TIF@ZIF-8 is mixed with 0.4 g sodium chloride and extracted using methanol, 2% Tween 20, or phosphate buffer. The mixture is filtered and diluted with 20 mL of distilled water. The diluted extract is passed through an AflaTest WB column, then washed sequentially with 2% Tween 20, filtered water, and HPLC-grade methanol. The eluate is mixed with AflaTest Developer in a sterile VICAM cuvette and analyzed using a VICAM Series-4EX fluorometer. Aflatoxin concentration is measured after 60 s.

Antioxidant activity

DPPH (diphenyl-1-picrylhydrazyl)/radical scavenging activity

The free radical scavenging capacity of TIF@ZIF-8 was measured using a modified method [27]. The mixture of DPPH solution in methanol and TIF@ZIF-8 solution was mixed in different concentrations (25, 50, 75 and 100 µg/ml), and absorbance was measured at 517 nm utilizing a UV-Vis-spectrophotometer [28]. The inhibition percentage was calculated using a formula.

A0 = The Absorbance of control reaction and A1 = The Absorbance in presence of test or standard sample. IC₅₀ values were calculated using dose-response curves to determine the concentration required to inhibit 50% of the radical activity.

ABTS radical scavenging assay

The ABTS radical cation decolorization test was employed to assess the antioxidant capacity of TIF@ZIF-8. The ABTS + cation radical was generated by combining 7 mM ABTS in water with 2.45 mM potassium persulfate. The measurement of absorbance was conducted at a wavelength of 734 nm subsequent to the addition of TIF@ZIF-8. The percentage inhibition of absorbance was determined by applying the formula: ABTS + scavenging effect (%) = ((AB −A)/ AB) ×100, where AB represents the absorbance of the control sample and AA represents the absorbance of the sample being tested. Trolox was used as the reference drug [29].

Powder XRD of TIF@ZIF-8

X-ray diffraction (XRD) analysis was conducted to evaluate the crystalline structure of Tamarindus indica flower extract (TIF), pure ZIF-8, and the synthesized TIF@ZIF-8 nanocomposite. The XRD pattern of ZIF-8 displayed distinct and sharp peaks at 2θ values of 8.46°, 10.42°, 12.76°, 16.44°, and 18.02°, which correspond to the crystallographic planes (110), (200), (211), (013), and (222), respectively. These peak positions are in good agreement with previously reported patterns for ZIF-8, confirming the successful synthesis of the framework and its high degree of crystallinity [39, 40]. In the case of TIF@ZIF-8, the XRD pattern retained the same characteristic peaks as pure ZIF-8, indicating that the encapsulation of the Tamarindus indica flower extract did not alter the crystal structure of the metal–organic framework. The preservation of these peaks suggests that the ZIF-8 framework remains intact and stable after the loading of the natural extract, thus verifying the formation of a pure-phase nanocomposite. These findings provide strong evidence that the green synthesis approach used in this study effectively incorporated bioactive plant compounds into the ZIF-8 structure without compromising its integrity, making it a promising material for further biological and pharmaceutical applications [41] (Fig. 9).

TGA of TIF@ZIF-8 nanocomposite

The TGA of ZIF-8 and TIF@ZIF-8 under nitrogen flow (see Fig. 10) shows extended plateaus in the temperature range of 25–900 °C for both ZIF-8 and TIF@ZIF-8. ZIF-8 demonstrates the capability to endure temperatures reaching as high as 500 °C. The material ZIF-8 exhibits stability with no weight loss observed up to 500 °C, followed by a pronounced weight loss occurring after this temperature threshold. The ZIF-8 curve exhibited a 5% weight reduction within the temperature range of 500 to 540 °C. A weight loss of 25% was observed up to 650 °C, attributed to the decomposition of ZIF-8 as the framework structure collapsed. A reduction of 35% was observed at 800 °C. The decomposition of 2-methylimidazole occurred within the temperature range of 800 to 900 °C, accompanied by a further weight loss of 45% attributed to the collapse of its crystal structure. The ultimate weight loss indicates that ZIF-8 has undergone full conversion to zinc oxide within an oxidative setting [42]. No weight loss was observed with TIF@ZIF-8 up to 400 °C. The TGA curve of TIF@ZIF-8 exhibits a gradual weight loss starting from 400 °C, which is associated with the elimination of TIF. The TGA curve for TIF@ZIF-8 exhibited a minor weight loss of 5% in the temperature range of 400 to 450 °C, attributed to the loss of water molecules. The decomposition of organic molecules from TIF@ZIF-8 resulted in a weight loss of 10% observed between 450 and 650 °C. Additionally, a reduction in weight of 20% was noted within the temperature range of 650 to 700 °C. At temperatures ranging from 700 to 900 °C, a significant weight loss of 40% was recorded, alongside the decomposition of 2-methylimidazole. This phenomenon is attributed to the collapse and disintegration of the crystal structure, resulting from the partial degradation of intermolecular bonds within ZIF-8. The observed progressive weight losses confirm the effective integration of TIF into ZIF-8.

SEM of TIF@ZIF-8 nanocomposite

The morphological characteristics of the synthesized TIF@ZIF-8 nanocomposite were examined and compared with those of pure ZIF-8 using Scanning Electron Microscopy (SEM) and High-Resolution Transmission Electron Microscopy (HRTEM). The SEM images (Fig. 11a and b) clearly illustrate that both ZIF-8 and TIF@ZIF-8 nanocomposite possess a uniform, well-defined truncated rhombic dodecahedron shape, which is a hallmark of ZIF-8 crystals. These morphologies are characterized by smooth surface textures and sharp edges, confirming the high degree of crystallinity and uniform particle growth. The TIF@ZIF-8 nanocomposite retained the same geometric morphology as pure ZIF-8, indicating that the encapsulation of Tamarindus indica flower extract did not disrupt or distort the structural integrity of the original framework. This observation is consistent with previously reported morphological features of ZIF-8 crystals in the literature [43, 44], supporting the conclusion that the green synthesis approach used in this study successfully preserves the native architecture of the metal–organic framework. Additionally, HRTEM imaging further confirmed the nanoscale dimensions and crystalline nature of the particles, displaying well-resolved lattice fringes that validate the internal order of the ZIF-8 framework even after loading with plant-derived bioactive compounds. The consistent morphology and structural stability of the nanocomposite highlight its suitability for applications in targeted drug delivery, antioxidant therapy, and antimicrobial treatment, where both structural robustness and bioactivity are essential.

In-vitro antioxidant activity for TIF@ZIF-8 nanocomposite

In this study, the antioxidant potential of the synthesized TIF@ZIF-8 nanocomposite was thoroughly assessed using two widely recognized in vitro assays: the DPPH (2,2-diphenyl-1-picrylhydrazyl) radical scavenging assay and the ABTS (2,2’-azino-bis(3-ethylbenzothiazoline-6-sulfonic acid)) assay. These methods are standard techniques for evaluating the ability of compounds to neutralize free radicals, a crucial property for therapeutic agents targeting oxidative stress-related disorders. The DPPH assay measures the capacity of antioxidants to donate hydrogen atoms or electrons to stabilize the DPPH free radical, causing a color change from purple to yellow, which is quantified spectrophotometrically. The TIF@ZIF-8 nanocomposite showed dose-dependent scavenging activity, with increasing concentrations leading to higher inhibition percentages in Table 6. At a concentration of 100 mg/mL, the nanocomposite achieved a maximum DPPH radical inhibition of 72.54%, indicating a strong antioxidant effect. The IC₅₀ value (the concentration required to inhibit 50% of the DPPH radicals) was found to be 64.14 mg/mL. For comparison, the synthetic antioxidant L-ascorbic acid was used as the standard, highlighting the effectiveness of the natural TIF extract within the nanocomposite system.

The comparatively low IC₅₀ values indicate that the nanocomposite is an effective antioxidant agent. These findings support the potential application of TIF@ZIF-8 in pharmaceutical or nutraceutical formulations designed to combat oxidative stress and related diseases. Conclusion

In this study, we successfully developed a novel nano-antioxidant system, TIF@ZIF-8, using a simple, one-step green synthesis approach. The process involved the encapsulation of bioactive compounds derived from the ethanolic extract of Tamarindus indica L. flowers into a zeolitic imidazolate framework-8 (ZIF-8) structure. This method not only ensured efficient loading of phytoconstituents but also maintained the integrity and porous structure of ZIF-8, as confirmed through multiple analytical techniques. In Phytochemical and Quality Analysis the ethanolic extract of Tamarindus indica flowers (TIF) was found to be rich in phytochemicals including alkaloids, flavonoids, phenols, lignins, and amino acids. Physicochemical parameters and quality control assessments (such as microbial load testing and aflatoxin analysis) verified that the extract was of high quality, free from adulterants and within safe biological limits, making it suitable for further medicinal use. HPTLC fingerprinting and GC–MS profiling revealed a number of secondary metabolites with notable bioactivities such as antioxidant, anti-inflammatory, and antimicrobial potential. Though only a limited number of bioactive compounds were identified, these profiles lay a strong foundation for further isolation and characterization of active constituents. To characterize the synthesized TIF@ZIF-8 nanocomposite (Nanocomposite), various advanced techniques were employed: Powder X-Ray Diffraction (PXRD) confirmed the formation of a crystalline ZIF-8 framework, with no significant structural disruption upon integration of TIF, thereby ensuring the structural stability of the nanocomposite. Scanning Electron Microscopy (SEM) revealed the particles’ truncated rhombic dodecahedron morphology—a hallmark of well-formed ZIF-8 particles—with smooth surfaces and good dispersion. High-Resolution Transmission Electron Microscopy (HRTEM) provided further evidence of morphological consistency and thermal stability, supporting the sustainable performance of the nanocomposite under varying conditions. The microbial analysis of TIF@ZIF-8 Nanocomposite showed no contamination by harmful bacteria or fungi, ensuring biosafety for potential oral or topical applications. The antioxidant potential was demonstrated through DPPH and ABTS radical scavenging assays, with significant free radical neutralization activity observed at higher concentrations. The IC₅₀ values derived from both assays suggest that the TIF@ZIF-8 Nanocomposite can act as efficient natural antioxidant agents. Overall, the TIF@ZIF-8 nanocomposite system developed in this study offers several advantages: Eco-friendly synthesis, Cost-effectiveness, Short reaction time, High reproducibility and Physicochemical and biological stability. This work opens new pathways for the sustainable development of plant-based nanomaterials with therapeutic potential, encouraging future research in bio-nanotechnology, green synthesis, and phytopharmaceutical formulations.
