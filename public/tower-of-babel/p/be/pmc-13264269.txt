Regulating surimi particle size promotes ice-crystal alignment and anisotropic lamellar structuring in vacuum-compressed surimi-based products: Protein conformation and microstructural insights
Authors: Zhiwen Shen, Yaping Fu, Yuxin Ding, Yanshun Xu
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractThis study aims to investigate the influence of surimi particle size on anisotropic lamellar structure formation in restructured fish products and the mechanisms underlying the evolution of associated microstructures and protein conformations. Silver carp surimi with varying disruption levels was prepared by controlling chopping time, followed by vacuum processing, compressing, freezing, and frying to obtain the final products. Moderate chopping (4 min) produced a relatively uniform matrix with intermediate particle size and protein solubilization, which tended to promote the formation of well-oriented ice crystals during freezing and resulted in relatively distinct lamellar structures after heating. These samples exhibited a maximum measured anisotropy index of 2.64, moderate hardness and chewiness, improved water distribution, and relatively favorable sensory characteristics. Moreover, moderate chopping induced partial protein unfolding, accompanied by increased exposure of free sulfhydryl and hydrophobic groups and interconversion between α-helix and β-sheet structures, thereby establishing a balanced protein continuous phase.Keywords: Surimi, Ice crystal, Lamellar structure, Microstructure, Protein conformation

Introduction

In recent years, the development of restructured fish products has attracted considerable attention in the field of aquatic product processing. Freshwater fish such as silver carp are widely available and cost-effective, yet their intramuscular bones limit direct processing and high-value utilization. Surimi processing offers an efficient approach to overcome these limitations by producing reconstituted surimi-based products with distinct sensory attributes (Xu et al., 2026; Zhang et al., 2026). Conventional surimi-based products, including fish balls, fish sausages, and fish tofu, rely primarily on homogeneous gel structures. While these products efficiently utilize aquatic resources and largely retain the nutritional value of fish, their uniform gel-like texture often results in a monotonous mouthfeel and lacks the characteristic qualities of real meat products (Yu et al., 2025; Zhou et al., 2026). This may reduce consumer sensory satisfaction and limit product added value. Developing restructured fish products with anisotropic structures, such as fibrous or lamellar structures, represents a promising strategy to address these limitations, but studies on this topic remain scarce. Products with muscle-like texture and appearance could potentially offer improved competitiveness compared with traditional surimi-based products (Gräfenhahn & Beyrer, 2026; Xu et al., 2023). Previous attempts to construct anisotropic structures have mainly employed high-moisture extrusion, mechanical shearing, 3D printing, or cell culture techniques (Chen et al., 2026; Ur-Mora et al., 2025; Yen et al., 2023). These approaches can partially achieve protein alignment or phase separation to mimic muscle structure (Baeghbali et al., 2026; Choi et al., 2026; Deng et al., 2026). However, most research has focused on plant protein-based meat analogs, and the theoretical understanding of anisotropic structure formation using fish proteins remains limited. Fish myofibrillar proteins also exhibit the potential to form anisotropic networks, and their molecular conformational characteristics differ from those of plant proteins, which may lead to distinct structuring mechanisms. Studies have demonstrated that incorporating surimi proteins into soy protein matrices creates phase-separated systems, in which surimi proteins serve as the dispersed phase within continuous plant protein networks to reinforce fibrous structures through filling effects and interfacial interactions (Hou et al., 2023). Nevertheless, these studies primarily rely on twin-screw extrusion and tend to produce fibrous structures resembling livestock meat, which differ from the typical lamellar organization of natural fish muscle. Furthermore, existing techniques are often complex, costly, and may compromise the preservation of juiciness and structural characteristics of the final product (Garina et al., 2026; Guan et al., 2026; Vargas et al., 2025). Notably, these mainstream extrusion-based strategies primarily simulate the fibrous texture of livestock muscle and are generally unsuitable for constructing unique lamellar structures resembling natural fish muscle. The role of the protein continuous phase in guiding anisotropic structure formation has also received limited attention, leaving both theoretical and practical gaps in producing high-quality lamellar structures from lower-value fish raw materials.

Current studies on surimi mainly focus on the improvement of gel texture and the development of antifreeze protection strategies, yet most of them are limited to isotropic gel systems. In contrast to these traditional research directions, the present study focuses on the construction of lamellar structures based on surimi protein. Our previous work proposed a combined vacuum-compression-freezing process to produce restructured fish products with lamellar structures, revealing the contributions of vacuum and compression treatments and highlighting the importance of freezing process (Shen et al., 2026a). The formation of lamellar structures in this system primarily depends on the templating effect of ice crystals during freezing, while vacuum removal of entrapped air and compression-induced compaction facilitate more uniform and ordered ice crystal growth (Shen et al., 2026b). Previous studies have also demonstrated that the degree of vacuum and compression intensity can regulate the development and refinement of lamellar structures (Fu & Xu, 2024; Fu & Xu, 2025). However, the influence of intrinsic surimi characteristics, such as changes in particle size and corresponding molecular conformation, on lamellar structure formation remains unclear. In this study, we introduce a strategy to regulate surimi particle size and systematically investigate its effect on lamellar structure formation. Physicochemical properties of raw surimi, ice crystal growth within the surimi system during freezing, and the quality and microstructure of the final product after heating were comprehensively evaluated. The novelty of this work lies in the integration of particle-scale regulation with freezing-driven structuring to elucidate the effect of continuous phase changes on lamellar structure formation. This multiscale approach provides a deeper mechanistic understanding of the way surimi particle size contributes to lamellar structure formation and offers a low-cost, practical framework for enhancing the value of fish with numerous interosseous bones.

Materials and methods

Materials

Silver carp (Hypophthalmichthys molitrix) with an average weight of approximately 2 kg were purchased from a local market in Wuxi, China. After preliminary processing of the fish by the supplier, the dorsal white fish meat was excised and used as the raw material. The collected fish meat was packed in crushed ice and transported to the laboratory within 1 h for subsequent processing. All chemical reagents used in this study were of analytical grade and purchased from Sinopharm Chemical Reagent Co., Ltd. (Shanghai, China).

Preparation of restructured surimi products

The fish meat was cut into small pieces for subsequent chopping. To obtain surimi samples with different degrees of fragmentation, the fish meat pieces were pre-chopped using a household meat grinder (Joyoung, China) for 0, 1, 2, 3, and 4 min. Subsequently, NaCl was added at a level of 0.5% (w/w), followed by an additional 2 min of chopping, resulting in total chopping times of 2, 3, 4, 5, and 6 min, respectively. The 2-min group served as the control group. Referring to the method reported by Fu and Xu (2024), the prepared surimi was transferred into polyethylene bags and vacuum-treated for 25 s using a vacuum sealing machine (Kewo, China). The vacuumed surimi was then placed into cylindrical molds (6.5 cm in diameter and 2 cm in height) and subjected to compression molding under a 3 kg weight for 1 min, corresponding to an applied pressure of approximately 9 kPa. After compression, the samples were frozen at −20 °C for 24 h to obtain frozen samples. The frozen samples were subsequently coated with batter and breadcrumbs, and then fried at 170 °C for 8 min to produce final restructured surimi-based products. A flowchart of the sample preparation process is shown in Fig. 1S (Supplementary Material). The compressed raw surimi samples, frozen samples, and heated samples by frying were subsequently subjected to analytical testing.

Characterization of the degree of fragmentation of raw surimi samples

Particle size distribution

The particle size distribution of raw surimi was determined according to Guo et al. (2026) with minor modifications. Briefly, surimi (5 g) was dispersed in deionized water (45 mL) by high-speed homogenization at 8000 rpm for 30 s to obtain a homogeneous suspension. Particle size analysis was performed using a laser diffraction particle size analyzer (S3500, Microtrac, USA), with deionized water used for background calibration. The refractive indices of the dispersant and surimi particles were set to 1.333 and 1.520, respectively. During measurement, the obscuration was maintained between 10% and 20%, and continuous stirring at 200 r/min was applied to prevent sedimentation. The pretreatment and measurement parameters were adapted to the characteristics of surimi (Zhang et al., 2026). The volume-weighted mean diameter D[4,3] and surface-weighted mean diameter D[3,2] were recorded. All tests were conducted in triplicate.

Ultrastructural observation by transmission electron microscopy (TEM)

The ultrastructure of raw surimi was observed following the method of Wang et al. (2023). Surimi samples were fixed in 2.5% glutaraldehyde (0.1 mol/L phosphate buffer, pH 7.2) at 4 °C for 4 h and post-fixed in 1% osmium tetroxide for 2 h. Samples were dehydrated through a graded ethanol series, embedded in epoxy resin (Epon 812), and polymerized. Ultrathin sections (60–80 nm) were prepared using an ultramicrotome (EM UC7, Leica, Germany), mounted on copper grids, and stained with uranyl acetate and lead citrate. Observations were carried out using a TEM (2100PLUS, JEOL, Japan) operated at 80 kV, and images were acquired at magnifications of ×5000 and ×10,000.

Determination of myofibrillar fragmentation index (MFI)

The MFI of raw surimi was measured according to Wan et al. (2023). Surimi (2 g) was homogenized with 20 mL of ice-cold extraction buffer (0.1 mol/L KCl, 20 mmol/L Tris-HCl, pH 7.0, containing 0.5 mmol/L EDTA) and centrifuged at 8000 r/min for 10 min at 4 °C. The precipitate was washed twice using the same buffer and resuspended. Protein concentration was determined using the Coomassie Brilliant Blue method and adjusted to 0.5 mg/mL. Absorbance was measured at 540 nm (UV-2700i, Shimadzu, Japan), and MFI was calculated as absorbance multiplied by 200.

Determination of protein solubility

Protein solubility of raw surimi was determined according to Liu et al. (2026) using the Coomassie Brilliant Blue assay. Surimi (2.00 g) was homogenized with 18 mL of Tris-HCl buffer (20 mmol/L, pH 7.2). The homogenate was incubated at 4 °C for 1 h and centrifuged at 9500 r/min for 15 min at 4 °C. Protein concentration in the supernatant was determined at 595 nm using bovine serum albumin as the standard. Protein solubility was expressed as milligrams of soluble protein per gram of surimi.

Determination of physicochemical properties of raw surimi

Rheological properties

The rheological properties of raw surimi were measured using a rotational rheometer (DHR-3, Waters, USA) equipped with a 40 mm parallel-plate geometry, following the method of Guo et al. (2026). Approximately 2 g of sample was loaded onto the lower plate, and measurements were conducted with a plate gap of 2 mm. Storage modulus (G′) and loss modulus (G″) were recorded. Temperature sweep tests were performed from 25 to 95 °C at a heating rate of 2 °C/min, with a strain of 1% and a frequency of 0.1 Hz. To minimize moisture loss during heating, the sample edge was sealed with a thin layer of edible oil. After the temperature sweep, frequency sweep tests were conducted at 25 °C under a constant strain of 1%, with the angular frequency ranging from 0.1 to 100 rad/s. Flow behavior was evaluated at 25 °C using the same geometry, with the shear rate increased from 0.1 to 100 s−1. Apparent viscosity was recorded as a function of shear rate.

Thermal properties

Thermal properties of raw surimi were analyzed using a differential scanning calorimeter (DSC 3+, Mettler-Toledo, Switzerland) to evaluate protein thermal stability and denaturation behavior, following the method of Lyu et al. (2026). Approximately 10 mg of sample was sealed in an aluminum pan, with an empty pan used as the reference. Nitrogen was purged throughout the measurement at a flow rate of 50 mL/min.

The DSC analysis consisted of two temperature programs. For the low-temperature program, the samples were cooled from ambient temperature to −30 °C at a rate of 10 °C/min and then reheated to ambient temperature. For the high-temperature program, the samples were heated from ambient temperature to 200 °C at a rate of 10 °C/min.

Determination of protein molecular structure and chemical interactions in samples

Raman spectroscopy and secondary structure analysis

Raman spectra of frozen and heated surimi samples were collected according to the method of Zhang et al. (2025) with minor modifications, using a confocal micro-Raman spectrometer (LabRAM HR Evolution, HORIBA Jobin Yvon S.A.S., France). Spectral acquisition was performed with an excitation wavelength of 532 nm over a wavenumber range of 400–4000 cm−1, an acquisition time of 8 s, and two accumulations. The raw spectra were pre-processed using Labspec software, and spectral deconvolution and curve fitting were conducted with PeakFit software to analyze changes in protein secondary structure.

Intrinsic fluorescence spectroscopy

Intrinsic fluorescence measurements were conducted following the method described by Ren et al. (2025). Briefly, the frozen samples were freeze-dried, and 10 mg of freeze-dried powder was dissolved in 10 mL of phosphate buffer (0.01 mol/L, pH 7.0) and centrifuged at 6000 r/min for 10 min. The supernatant was collected for analysis. Intrinsic fluorescence spectra were recorded using a fluorescence spectrophotometer (F-2500, Hitachi, Japan) to evaluate changes in protein tertiary structure. The excitation wavelength was set at 280 nm with a slit width of 5 nm, and the emission spectra were scanned from 300 to 400 nm.

Ultraviolet absorption spectroscopy

The supernatant obtained in Section 2.5.2 was further analyzed using a UV–visible spectrophotometer. Absorption spectra were recorded over a wavelength range of 200–400 nm with a slit width of 1 nm at an appropriate scanning speed (Zhang et al., 2026). The second-derivative spectra were calculated from the original spectra, and the r value was determined through spectral analysis to characterize changes in molecular structure and intermolecular interactions in the surimi protein system.

Surface hydrophobicity

Surface hydrophobicity was determined using the 8-anilino-1-naphthalenesulfonic acid (ANS) fluorescent probe method. The supernatant obtained in Section 2.5.2 was used as the test solution. Fluorescence measurements were conducted with an excitation wavelength of 374 nm, a slit width of 1 nm, and an emission scanning range of 350–400 nm. All samples were diluted to a series of identical protein concentration gradients. Within the linear range common to all samples, fluorescence intensity was plotted against protein concentration. Surface hydrophobicity was expressed as the initial slope of the linear regression curve, as described by Baeghbali et al. (2026). Only regression results with a coefficient of determination (R2) greater than 0.99 were considered valid.

Determination of free sulfhydryl (SH) and disulfide bond (S—S) content

The contents of free sulfhydryl groups and total sulfhydryl groups were determined according to Choi et al. (2026) with minor modifications. Freeze-dried powder (1 g) obtained in Section 2.5.2 was dispersed in urea–Tris–glycine buffer (8 mol/L urea) and reacted with 5,5′-dithiobis-(2-nitrobenzoic acid) (DTNB) at 25 °C for 30 min. After centrifugation (12,000 r/min, 5 min), the absorbance of the supernatant was measured at 412 nm to determine free sulfhydryl content. For total sulfhydryl determination, disulfide bonds were first fully reduced by treating the sample with β-mercaptoethanol at room temperature for 1 h. Proteins were then precipitated using 12% (w/v) trichloroacetic acid, redissolved in a buffer containing 2% sodium dodecyl sulfate, 8 mol/L urea, and Tris–glycine, and reacted with DTNB. Absorbance was measured at 412 nm. Disulfide bond content was calculated from the difference between total and free sulfhydryl contents according to the corresponding equations.

Observation of macroscopic lamellar structure and ice crystal morphology

Observation of lamellar structure

Heated samples were cut along the central plane, and the cross-sections were visually examined to evaluate the macroscopic lamellar structure. The degree of lamellar separation was further assessed by gently peeling the lamellar structure manually. Images of the samples were captured using a digital single-lens reflex camera (Sony, Japan) under standardized background and natural lighting conditions to ensure comparability among samples.

Observation of ice crystal morphology

Frozen samples were embedded in an optimal cutting temperature compound (Tissue-Tek® OCT, Sakura Finetek, USA) and sectioned using a cryostat microtome (CM1950, Leica, Germany) to clearly visualize the internal ice crystal structures. The obtained sections were photographed using a digital single-lens reflex camera (Sony, Japan) under consistent background and illumination conditions to ensure uniform observation conditions across different samples.

Determination of texture properties and anisotropy index

Heated samples were trimmed to remove the crust, and the inner portion was cut into cubes (20 mm × 20 mm × 20 mm). Texture profile analysis (TPA) was conducted using a texture analyzer (TA-XT Plus, Stable Micro Systems, UK) equipped with a P/35 cylindrical probe. Samples were compressed to 50% of their original height at a test speed of 2 mm/s, and hardness and chewiness were recorded (Lyu et al., 2026). Directional shear properties were measured using an A/MORS blade at a deformation level of 50% and a test speed of 2 mm/s. Shear force was determined in the transverse direction (perpendicular to the lamellar orientation) and the longitudinal direction (parallel to the lamellar orientation). The anisotropy index was calculated as the ratio of transverse to longitudinal shear force according to Qi et al. (2025).

Determination of water-holding capacity (WHC) and water distribution

WHC

The crust of the heated samples was removed, and the inner portion was cut into cubes of 20 mm × 20 mm × 20 mm. Each sample was tightly wrapped with double-layer filter paper and placed into a centrifuge tube. Centrifugation was performed using a high-speed centrifuge (4 K-15, Sigma, Germany) at 3600 ×g for 3 min. The sample mass before and after centrifugation were recorded, and WHC was calculated according to the method described by Yu et al. (2025) based on the mass retention after centrifugation.

Where M1 and M2 refer to the masses before and after centrifugation, respectively.

Water distribution

Water distribution was characterized using a low-field nuclear magnetic resonance (LF-NMR) analyzer (MesoMR23-060 V-I, Niumag, China). The crust-removed heated samples were cut into cubes of 15 mm × 15 mm × 15 mm and placed into 25 mm NMR tubes. Prior to measurement, sample mass was normalized to minimize the influence of mass differences on the results, following the method of Xu et al. (2026). The transverse relaxation time (T₂) was measured using the Carr–Purcell–Meiboom–Gill (CPMG) pulse sequence. The measurement parameters were set as follows: spectrometer frequency 18 MHz, pulse width 8 μs, spectral width 100 kHz, waiting time 2500 ms, number of scans 8, and number of echoes 4000.

Sensory evaluation

Sensory evaluation was conducted using a 10-point scale (where 10 = maximum desirability and 1 = minimum desirability) by a trained panel consisting of 20 experienced assessors (aged 18–60) following the method of Sun et al. (2025). Individual heated samples were served in dedicated sensory evaluation booths, labeled with randomized three-digit numerical codes, and assessed under ambient temperature conditions (ISO 8589, 2007). The samples were evaluated for lamellar structure state, smell, mouthfeel, juiciness, texture, and acceptability. Detailed sensory evaluation criteria for each attribute were provided in Table S1 (Supplementary Material). Panelists were instructed to rinse their mouths with water before evaluating each sample. All participants provided informed consent prior to the sensory evaluation.

Microscopic observation of lamellar structure

Light microscopy observation

Heated samples were trimmed and cut into blocks approximately 10 mm thick. Samples were fixed in 4% paraformaldehyde at 4 °C for 24 h, dehydrated through a graded ethanol series, and embedded in paraffin. Sections (5–8 μm) were prepared using the cryostat microtome mentioned earlier and stained with hematoxylin and eosin (HE) following Zhang et al. (2026). Microscopic observation was performed at ×40 magnification, and images were analyzed using ImageJ to quantify the number of lamellar structures per unit length as an indicator of lamellar density.

Scanning electron microscopy observation

The crust-removed heated samples were cut into thin slices with a thickness of approximately 2 mm and rapidly frozen in liquid nitrogen for 2 min to preserve the microstructure. The samples were then freeze-dried for 48 h to remove moisture. After sputter-coating with gold to improve electrical conductivity, the samples were observed using a field-emission scanning electron microscope (SU8100, Hitachi, Japan) at magnifications of ×500 and ×1000. The observation and sample preparation procedures followed the method reported by Fang et al. (2026).

Three-dimensional structural analysis by X-ray micro-computed tomography

Heated samples were trimmed and cut into cubes (10 × 10 × 10 mm) and then subjected to freeze-drying. Three-dimensional microstructural imaging was performed using a nano-scale X-ray micro-CT system (Xradia 515 Versa, Carl Zeiss, Germany) following Sickel et al. (2025). Scanning parameters were: accelerating voltage 60 kV, exposure time 1.5 s, scanning angle 180°, and spatial resolution 2 μm. Images were reconstructed and analyzed using Dragonfly software. Density-based segmentation was applied to distinguish lamellar structures from air regions, and three-dimensional thickness distribution and total porosity of the lamellae were quantified.

Statistical analysis

Unless otherwise stated, all experiments were performed in triplicate, and results were presented as mean values. Statistical analysis was conducted using SPSS (SPSS Inc., Chicago, IL, USA) with one-way ANOVA, and significant differences among means were determined by Duncan's multiple range test at p < 0.05. Correlation heatmaps were generated using Origin 2019 (OriginLab, Northampton, MA, USA) based on Pearson correlation coefficients.

Results and discussion

Effect of chopping time on the myofibrillar fragmentation index (MFI) and protein solubility of raw surimi

As shown in Fig. 1A, the MFI increased progressively with chopping time, with the 6 min sample exhibiting a significantly higher value than the 2 min sample (p < 0.05). Since MFI is commonly used to reflect the integrity of myofibrillar structures, this trend indicates a gradual transition from limited structural damage to more extensive myofibrillar disruption as chopping proceeded (Fang et al., 2026). Notably, the increase in MFI is unlikely to result solely from mechanical breakage of myofibrils. Partial disassembly and dissolution of fragmented myofibrillar structures and associated protein assemblies might also contribute to the measured values (Li et al., 2025; Ren et al., 2025). Therefore, the observed increase in MFI likely reflected the combined effects of mechanical shear and progressive release of myofibrillar subunits, rather than pure physical fragmentation (Wang et al., 2023).

Fish muscle proteins can be broadly classified into water-soluble, salt-soluble, and insoluble fractions, among which salt-soluble myofibrillar proteins play a central role in determining the rheological behavior and structural development of surimi systems (Yu et al., 2024). Protein dissolution was therefore assessed to evaluate the extent of soluble protein release during chopping. As shown in Fig. 1B, protein solubility remained relatively stable when the chopping time did not exceed 5 min, with no significant differences observed among these samples. In contrast, a significant increase in protein solubility was detected when chopping time was extended from 5 to 6 min (p < 0.05). This suggested that, at shorter chopping durations, mechanical action primarily induces structural fragmentation of muscle fibers, whereas more intensive chopping promotes the liberation of myofibrillar proteins from the muscle matrix (Lee et al., 2026; Wang et al., 2023). An increase in soluble protein content may elevate the effective macromolecular concentration in the system, potentially strengthening interparticle interactions (Liu et al., 2026). However, the increase in solubility should not be interpreted as a simple indication of improved dispersion. Notably, due to the low salt content (0.5%), the increase in protein solubility remained relatively limited with prolonged chopping time. Moderate fragmentation accompanied by limited protein release might favor a dispersed yet structured system, whereas excessive chopping may simultaneously enhance protein release and induce partial reaggregation of fine fragments through protein-mediated interactions (Guo et al., 2026; Sun et al., 2025). These differences in the initial matrix were expected to influence subsequent particle size distribution and microstructural organization, which are further examined in the following section.

Effect of chopping time on particle size distribution and ultrastructure of raw surimi

As shown in Fig. 1(C—D), particle size decreased significantly with increasing chopping time (p < 0.05), reflecting the progressive breakdown of muscle tissue from larger structural units into finer particles. This reduction generally indicates enhanced dispersion and improved structural uniformity within the surimi system (Zhang et al., 2026). However, when chopping time was extended from 5 to 6 min, a slight increase in the volume-weighted mean diameter D[4,3] was observed. Therefore, the 6-min sample represented an over-chopped state in which fragmentation was accompanied by limited reaggregation. This change suggested that under intensive mechanical shear combined with moderate protein solubilization induced by low salt content, some fine particles might undergo limited reaggregation. At this stage, particle size evolution appeared to be governed not only by shear-induced fragmentation but also by protein-mediated interactions among dispersed particles (Reddy et al., 2025; Vargas et al., 2025). Thus, the inconsistent trend between the elevated protein solubility in Fig. 1B and the partial protein reaggregation in Fig. 1C might be associated with the dual characteristics of dissociated myofibrillar proteins. These partially dissociated proteins could increase the proportion of soluble components while also facilitating interparticle adhesion and molecular bridging among fragmented protein structures (Guo et al., 2026; Wan et al., 2023).

Ultrastructural observations further corroborated the particle size results. Myofibrils are composed of serially arranged sarcomeres delineated by Z-discs, with thick and thin filaments orderly organized within each sarcomere (Wang et al., 2023). As shown in Fig. 1E, increasing chopping time led to progressive disruption of Z-disc structures, as evidenced by reduced continuity and structural integrity. In samples chopped for 6 min, localized disappearance or blurring of Z-disc regions was observed, indicating an advanced degree of myofibrillar disintegration. These observations suggested that prolonged chopping compromised the hierarchical organization of muscle fibers, consistent with the observed reduction in particle size and changes in dispersion state (Reddy et al., 2025; Zhang et al., 2025).

Taken together, the results in 3.1, 3.2 indicate that chopping time regulated the structural state of raw surimi through a coupled process involving myofibrillar fragmentation, protein dissolution, and particle size redistribution. Moderate chopping (3–5 min) resulted in fragmented yet discernible structural units with limited protein release, whereas excessive chopping (6 min) did not simply produce a finer and more homogeneous system. Instead, it tended to produce a protein-rich and partially continuous matrix, where fine fragments coexisted with locally reaggregated structures (Shen et al., 2025). Such differences in the initial microstructural organization might influence the behavior of water and protein phases during subsequent freezing and thermal processing, thereby providing a basis for the development of lamellar structure and the final quality attributes of restructured surimi products, which are discussed in the following sections.

Effect of chopping time on rheological properties of raw surimi

As shown in Fig. 2, temperature sweep tests (Fig. 2A–B) indicated that storage modulus (G′) and loss modulus (G″) followed similar temperature-dependent trends across all samples, suggesting comparable thermal response patterns. However, both moduli increased gradually with longer chopping time, with values converging at 5–6 min. This reflected enhanced viscoelastic resistance during heating as chopping promoted protein dispersion and increased the contribution of the protein continuous phase (Fang et al., 2026; Hu et al., 2026). Differences among samples became more pronounced at elevated temperatures, particularly above 50 °C, where protein denaturation and intermolecular interactions dominated the rheological response. Under these conditions, variations in protein dispersion arising from different chopping intensities were amplified, leading to distinct gelation behaviors (Xu et al., 2026; Zhang et al., 2026). Frequency sweep results (Fig. 2C–D) showed that both G′ and G″ generally increased with chopping time, indicating reinforcement of elastic and viscous components (Chen et al., 2026). However, the 6-min sample exhibited slightly lower moduli than the 5-min sample, suggesting that excessive chopping might compromise network stability under dynamic deformation, consistent with partial over-disruption of protein structures (Deng et al., 2026; Shen et al., 2024).

As shown in Fig. 2E, all samples exhibited shear-thinning behavior, as expected for surimi systems. Apparent viscosity increased with chopping time, reflecting enhanced interparticle interactions, while a slight decrease at 6 min further indicated that excessive chopping might reduce multiscale structural support and promote a more homogeneous protein-dominated system (Gräfenhahn & Beyrer, 2026; Guo et al., 2026). Chopping time primarily regulated the viscoelastic and flow properties of raw surimi by modulating protein dispersion and continuous phase characteristics. These rheological parameters characterized the deformation and thermal response of the surimi matrix and served as intrinsic indicators of its structural organization prior to further processing.

Effect of chopping time on thermal properties of raw surimi

As shown in Fig. 2F, all samples exhibited typical exothermic peaks during cooling and corresponding endothermic peaks during reheating, associated with water crystallization and ice melting, respectively (Xu et al., 2026). With increasing chopping time, the onset temperature of crystallization gradually decreased, which might reflect changes in water distribution and binding states. Meanwhile, the crystallization temperature range became narrower for samples chopped for longer times. This phenomenon might be related to the altered water mobility and distribution state in the surimi matrix under different chopping conditions, which could also be modulated by the structural rearrangement and aggregation behavior of myofibrillar proteins induced by mechanical chopping intensity (Yang et al., 2025). In contrast, insufficient chopping retained heterogeneous structures, resulting in a broader crystallization interval due to sequential freezing of different water fractions (Ding et al., 2026).

As shown in Fig. 2G, all samples exhibited clear endothermic transitions corresponding to protein thermal denaturation. Increasing chopping time shifted the onset of denaturation to lower temperatures, implying that protein structures became more susceptible to thermal unfolding (Lyu et al., 2026). However, the end temperatures of denaturation remained relatively consistent, indicating that the overall stability of protein structural units was not fundamentally altered (Vargas et al., 2025). Fig. 2H shows that longer chopping reduced the enthalpy change value, consistent with partial disruption of ordered protein structures prior to heating, while slight increases observed at extended chopping times might be associated with enhanced intermolecular interactions (Ye et al., 2025). For the 6-min sample, such thermal behaviors could be attributed to the counterbalance between two structural variations: the enhancement of protein solubilization and water-binding capacity, and the formation of local particle associations induced by prolonged chopping. Therefore, the DSC data could only reflect macroscopic characteristics of water crystallization and protein denaturation, and might not fully represent the uniformity of microscopic structure. These thermal responses provided macroscopic indicators of the internal physicochemical state of surimi, which could be interpreted in conjunction with molecular-level analyses.

Effect of chopping time on protein secondary structure

Fig. 3A shows the Raman spectra of surimi samples before and after frying, highlighting the water region (3000–3500 cm−1), the amide I region (1600–1700 cm−1), and the amide II region (1500–1600 cm−1). The amide I band was used to quantify protein secondary structure, including α-helix, β-sheet, and random coil contents (Zhang et al., 2025; Zhou et al., 2026).

Before frying, the α-helix content initially increased and then decreased with longer chopping times, whereas the β-sheet content showed the opposite trend, with inflection points around 4 min (Fig. 3B). This pattern aligned with changes in intrinsic fluorescence and water-holding capacity, suggesting that chopping modulated protein conformational states and might influence subsequent structural evolution during freezing and thermal processing (Xu et al., 2026). Mild chopping (within 4 min) might disrupt certain structural constraints within myofibrils, such as Z-disc-associated organizational structures, thereby allowing protein chains to adopt more ordered α-helical conformations (Yu et al., 2024). With further chopping, increased mechanical disruption likely promoted partial unfolding of proteins, leading to a reduction in α-helix content and a concomitant increase in β-sheet structures associated with intermolecular interactions (Ur-Mora et al., 2025). After frying, prolonged chopping resulted in a significant reduction of α-helix and random coil contents, accompanied by an increase in β-sheet structures (p < 0.05). This shift reflected protein thermal denaturation, aggregation, and gel network formation, consistent with structural rearrangement in the cooked product (Ren et al., 2025).

Effect of chopping time on intrinsic fluorescence, UV absorption, and surface hydrophobicity

As shown in Fig. 3C, fluorescence intensity initially decreased and then partially recovered with increasing chopping time, with the largest change occurring between 3 and 4 min. This pattern indicated that early mechanical shearing and appropriate salt-induced solubilization partially unfolded proteins, exposing buried tryptophan residues to the polar environment (Qi et al., 2025). At longer chopping times, the observed fluorescence recovery might reflect a redistribution of protein conformations, where unfolding and intermolecular association could occur simultaneously, leading to partial shielding of some residues (Ren et al., 2025; Sun et al., 2025). UV absorption spectra, quantified by the r value (Fig. 3D), showed a similar trend: an initial increase followed by a decline after 5 min, reflecting exposure and subsequent partial shielding of aromatic residues (Wan et al., 2023). The increase in UV absorption suggested enhanced interaction between aromatic amino acid residues and the surrounding solvent due to protein unfolding, whereas the subsequent decrease might indicate restricted chromophore accessibility caused by molecular rearrangement or weak aggregation (Ye et al., 2025). Thus, the fluorescence and UV results were better interpreted as evidence of local residue exposure followed by partial shielding. Notably, the relatively small amplitude of r value variation implied that chopping primarily affected the local microenvironment of aromatic residues rather than inducing extensive disruption of the protein backbone (Zhang et al., 2026). These changes aligned with observed fluorescence results.

Surface hydrophobicity (Fig. 3E) increased overall with chopping time, indicating that mechanical shear exposed hydrophobic groups, enhancing protein-protein and protein-solvent interactions (Choi et al., 2026). Slight decreases at 5–6 min likely resulted from partial masking of hydrophobic patches due to protein–protein association or spatial rearrangement. This suggested that, at extended chopping times, protein systems might exhibit coexistence of unfolded structures and intermolecular associations (Baeghbali et al., 2026). From a molecular perspective, moderate chopping enhanced protein dispersion and increased the exposure of reactive groups, whereas excessive chopping might promote premature association among protein molecules, partially limiting further conformational rearrangement (Ding et al., 2026). In summary, chopping induced a dynamic balance between protein unfolding, residue exposure, and partial molecular reorganization in raw surimi (Fang et al., 2026; Li et al., 2025). These spectroscopic responses characterized changes in the molecular environment and interaction potential of surimi proteins, providing complementary insight into the physicochemical state of the system prior to subsequent processing.

Effect of chopping time on free sulfhydryl and disulfide bond content

Protein cross-linking and aggregation are associated with sulfhydryl exposure and disulfide bond rearrangement (Yu et al., 2025). As shown in Fig. 3F, the content of active sulfhydryl groups initially increased and then decreased with prolonged chopping, reaching a maximum at 4 min. This suggested that early-stage mechanical shearing partially depolymerized myofibrillar structures, exposing buried sulfhydryl groups (Wan et al., 2023). With further chopping, the decrease in sulfhydryl content might be associated with multiple concurrent processes, including limited oxidation, intermolecular interactions, or conformational rearrangements that reduce the accessibility of reactive groups (Liu et al., 2026; Lyu et al., 2026). Correspondingly, the disulfide bond content exhibited an opposite trend, initially decreasing and then increasing. This indicated that early chopping predominantly caused structural disruption and bond cleavage, whereas later stages were dominated by a combination of structural reorganization and possible formation of intermolecular linkages (Lee et al., 2026; Tan et al., 2026). These changes reflected a dynamic balance between protein depolymerization and subsequent reorganization/cross-linking during chopping (Sun et al., 2025).

Taken together with other molecular-level indicators, moderate chopping primarily promoted partial unfolding and dispersion of protein aggregates, whereas extended chopping might lead to a coexistence of unfolded conformations and intermolecular associations (Ren et al., 2025; Wang et al., 2026). These processes might occur concurrently, contributing to the differences in spectroscopic responses, solubility, and reactive group accessibility (Choi et al., 2026). These initial conformational states therefore characterized distinct molecular organizations of raw surimi generated under different chopping intensities.

Macroscopic lamellar structure and ice crystal growth behavior

Appearance of the lamellar structure

Fig. 4(A-B) presents the cross-sectional morphology of the samples and their internal structures after gentle manual separation. The results showed that chopping time markedly influenced the formation and stability of lamellar structures in restructured surimi products. At a chopping time of 2 min, both the cross sections and the separated interiors appeared loose and disordered. Only limited regions exhibited incipient layering, and fracture paths propagated in multiple directions, indicating low structural continuity and weak alignment. This suggested that insufficient chopping resulted in the uneven distribution of coarse structural units, which restricted effective directional rearrangement during subsequent compression and freezing.

When chopping time was increased to 3–4 min, the overall uniformity of the surimi system improved noticeably. Distinct lamellar structures were clearly observed at the macroscopic scale, characterized by more regular layer boundaries and improved structural order. The lamellar structures could be readily separated while maintaining continuity, indicating a balanced structural state in which tissue fragmentation and protein continuity were appropriately matched. Such a configuration appeared favorable for the formation of stable lamellar structures. With further extension of chopping time to 5–6 min, both lamellar thickness and interlayer spacing decreased, and the overall structure became increasingly compact. Lamellar separation became more difficult, particularly in the 6-min samples, whose macroscopic appearance gradually approached that of a homogeneous surimi gel. This transition suggested that excessive chopping increased the proportion of fine particles and solubilized proteins, strengthening the protein continuous phase and diminishing the structural independence of individual lamellar structures.

Ice crystal growth characteristics

As shown in Fig. 4C, differences in ice crystal growth behavior provided further insight into the macroscopic structural variations observed after heating. In the 2-min samples, ice crystals exhibited poor directional alignment, with only localized alignment evident. This behavior was consistent with the disordered lamellar structure and suggested that large, heterogeneously distributed particles interfered with cooperative and directional ice crystal growth, thereby limiting the development of continuous lamellar ice frameworks.

As chopping time increased, the surimi matrix became more uniform, and the directionality of ice crystal growth was correspondingly enhanced. In the 3–4 min samples, ice crystals were relatively coarse and exhibited clearer alignment. These lamellar ice crystals effectively partitioned the surimi matrix during freezing, creating spatial separation that was subsequently reflected as wider interlayer gaps after heating and contributed to improved lamellar structure separability in the final products. At higher chopping intensities, although the overall directionality of ice crystals was largely preserved, their size decreased substantially. Under these conditions, matrix partitioning during freezing was less pronounced, which may have facilitated stronger interactions and fusion between adjacent lamellar structures during subsequent thermal processing (Yang et al., 2026). As a result, the lamellar structure became less distinct and gradually transitioned toward a more continuous, gel-like network. Overall, chopping time regulated the uniformity and continuity of the surimi matrix, thereby influencing ice crystal size, directionality, and partitioning behavior during freezing. The combined effects of matrix structure and ice crystal growth might play an important role in determining the clarity, compactness, and separability of macroscopic lamellar structures in restructured surimi products, providing a structural basis for the subsequent analysis of texture and water retention.

Effect of chopping time on textural properties and anisotropy index

As shown in Fig. 5A, hardness decreased gradually with prolonged chopping, declining from 3222.1 g to 3055.7 g. In contrast, chewiness initially increased and then decreased, with a relative maximum observed at 3 min. This behavior suggested that moderate chopping reduced excessive firmness while maintaining sufficient structural cohesion (Lyu et al., 2026). This trend was consistent with the physicochemical and molecular characteristics of raw surimi, where moderate chopping enhanced protein dispersion and exposure of reactive groups without inducing extensive aggregation, thereby supporting effective stress transfer within the heated gel matrix (Yu et al., 2025; Zhang et al., 2025). With further extension of chopping time, the increasing dominance of the protein continuous phase and greater structural homogeneity might limit cooperative deformation between lamellar structures, leading to a reduction in chewiness (Baeghbali et al., 2026; Ur-Mora et al., 2025).

Directional shear measurements further revealed the anisotropic mechanical behavior introduced by lamellar structures (Tan et al., 2026), as shown in Fig. 5B. When loaded perpendicular to the layer alignment (transverse direction), shear strength was less affected by layer interfaces and followed a trend similar to that of hardness, reflecting the overall integrity of the gel network. In contrast, samples loaded parallel to the layer alignment (longitudinal direction) were more sensitive to interlayer boundaries, as sliding or separation between layers could occur under stress (Sun et al., 2025; Vargas et al., 2025). Longitudinal shear strength decreased initially and then increased, reaching a minimum at 3–4 min. This indicated that lamellae formed within this chopping range possessed relatively high separability. With further chopping, the lamellar structures became thinner and partially fused, resulting in increased longitudinal shear strength as the structure evolved toward a more homogeneous network. The anisotropy index, defined as the ratio of transverse to longitudinal shear strength, was used to quantify directional mechanical differences. This index increased initially and then declined, reaching a maximum value of 2.64 at 3 min. The 4-min sample also exhibited a relatively high index (>2). In contrast, the 6-min sample showed an anisotropy index close to 1, suggesting that mechanical responses in different directions were nearly equivalent and the lamellar structure was largely diminished. These results indicated that moderate chopping promoted directionally dependent mechanical behavior and muscle-like texture.

Effect of chopping time on WHC and water distribution

Changes in WHC

As shown in Fig. 5C, WHC exhibited an initial decrease followed by an increase with prolonged chopping, with a turning point observed at 4 min (p < 0.05). This variation in WHC could be interpreted in conjunction with the chopping-induced changes in protein dispersion, thermal response, and molecular interaction potential of raw surimi, which collectively influence water–protein interactions during heating. At relatively low chopping intensities, residual muscle structures, including partially intact myofibrils and connective tissue, might provide additional sites for water retention. As chopping intensity increased, enhanced protein unfolding might temporarily reduce the availability of stable water-binding sites due to disruption of the original muscle framework, leading to a decrease in WHC. From a structural standpoint, lamellar structures formed under moderate chopping, together with the interlamellar spaces generated by ice crystal partitioning during freezing, might further influence water retention. These ordered yet discontinuous structural features could facilitate physical entrapment of water within the gel matrix, thereby contributing to the recovery of WHC at higher chopping times.

Changes in water distribution

Low-field nuclear magnetic resonance (LF-NMR) was used to characterize moisture distribution by distinguishing water populations with different proton relaxation times (Zhou et al., 2026). Based on increasing relaxation times, water can be classified as bound water, physically immobilized water, and free water, corresponding to different degrees of interaction with the protein matrix (Shen et al., 2024). As shown in Fig. 5D, with increasing chopping time, the proportion of physically immobilized water (P₂₂) first decreased and then increased, whereas the proportion of free water (P₂₃) exhibited an opposite trend (p < 0.05). This indicated that limited chopping facilitated the release of water previously constrained within myofibrillar structures, increasing water mobility, while more intensive chopping promoted the reincorporation of water into a reorganized protein network, shifting it toward a less mobile state (Qi et al., 2025; Zhang et al., 2026). Notably, prolonged chopping might enhance the overall water-binding capacity, which helped explain the change in the crystallization onset temperature observed in the DSC results.

These changes in proton relaxation behavior likely arose from the combined effects of chopping-induced microstructural disruption and subsequent network reformation during freezing and heating. Under moderate chopping conditions, a relatively uniform protein continuous phase and well-defined lamellar structures might facilitate more homogeneous ice crystal formation during freezing, thereby reducing localized water migration or accumulation (Jiang et al., 2024; Zhang et al., 2026). In contrast, insufficient chopping could increase structural heterogeneity, which might amplify fluctuations in water mobility during freezing and thermal processing, as reflected by the observed variations in LF-NMR water populations and WHC.

Effect of chopping time on sensory evaluation

Sensory evaluation was conducted to assess the appearance, structural organization, and texture of the restructured surimi products. As shown in Fig. 5E, both lamellar structure state and texture scores exhibited an initial increase followed by a decrease with prolonged chopping, reaching their highest values at 3 min. This trend suggested that moderate chopping produced structural and textural attributes that were more readily perceived and preferred by panelists. The variation in lamellar structure state scores was generally consistent with the observed macroscopic appearance characteristics. At moderate chopping times, the lamellar structures exhibited appropriate thickness and continuity, which might enhance their visual clarity and perceptibility during mastication. In contrast, insufficient chopping resulted in poorly defined and disordered layering, whereas excessive chopping led to thinner, more compact, and less distinguishable lamellar structures, both of which reduced sensory acceptance. The texture scores indicated that limited chopping restricted structural rearrangement, yielding a relatively firm and less deformable texture. Conversely, excessive chopping promoted a more homogeneous matrix that weakened muscle-like textural characteristics. Moderate chopping appeared to provide a balance between tenderness and structural definition, resulting in a texture that was evaluated more favorably. The sensory results indicated that chopping time played an important role in shaping consumer-perceivable structural and textural attributes.

Effect of chopping time on microstructure

Light microscopy (LM) and scanning electron microscopy (SEM) were employed to characterize the microstructural evolution of restructured surimi products under different chopping times. LM enabled visualization of anisotropic features such as lamellar structure continuity, alignment, and spacing, whereas SEM provided higher-resolution information on internal morphology and myofibrillar organization. As shown in Fig. 6A, samples prepared with 2 min of chopping exhibited only a weak tendency toward layering, with poorly defined boundaries and low overall structural order and poor compactness. This observation was consistent with the macroscopic appearance results. With increasing chopping time, internal lamellar structures became progressively clearer and more regularly arranged. Quantitative analysis using ImageJ (Fig. 5F) showed that lamellar structure density increased with chopping time and gradually approached a plateau, with the 4 min sample exhibiting a significantly higher lamellar structure density than the 2 min sample.

SEM observations (Fig. 6B) further supported these trends. At shorter chopping times, relatively large and partially intact myofibrillar bundles might be retained, forming coarse structural units that generated pronounced spatial constraints within the matrix (Lee et al., 2026; Shen et al., 2026a). Moderate chopping reduced particle size and improved myofibril dispersion while preserving overall structural continuity, resulting in a matrix that was sufficiently flexible to undergo directional reorganization and conducive to the formation of clearer and more regular lamellar structures (Hoxha et al., 2026). Besides, in the incompletely chopped samples, structural heterogeneity and localized spatial constraints could restrict ice nucleation and growth, leading to uneven crystal sizes and disrupting the development of continuous lamellar ice frameworks (Yang et al., 2026). In contrast, moderate chopping produced a more uniform protein continuous phase, providing a relatively consistent spatial environment for directional ice crystal growth. These lamellar ice crystals, although melting during subsequent heating, might act as transient structural guides that support lamellar structure separation and alignment during thermal gelation. It is worth noting that high-temperature frying at 170 °C may trigger rapid water evaporation and oil uptake in surimi products. Such processing-related variations could remodel or mask the initial ice-templated lamellar microstructure. In this study, the acquired microscopic data might not fully distinguish the original structural features derived from ice templating from the secondary structural modifications induced by frying.

Effect of chopping time on three-dimensional structure of lamellar structures

Micro-computed tomography (micro-CT) was employed to examine the three-dimensional internal structures of freeze-dried restructured surimi products, providing non-destructive visualization of their microscale structural features (Sickel et al., 2025). As shown in Fig. 7A, air was labeled in blue, while the higher-density lamellar structure matrix was labeled in red. Samples prepared with 2 min of chopping exhibited interwoven distributions of components with different densities. Low-density regions were discontinuous and irregularly distributed, and clear lamellar interfaces were not observed, suggesting limited three-dimensional spatial organization. With chopping times extended to 3–4 min, low-density components became more distinctly separated from the surrounding matrix and formed continuous, well-arranged lamellar structures with clearer boundaries. These three-dimensional lamellar structures exhibited improved spatial continuity and alignment, which might reflect enhanced matrix uniformity and deformation adaptability. Such conditions might be favorable for directional rearrangement of components during compression, thereby potentially promoting the development of well-defined lamellar structures in three-dimensional space (Choi et al., 2026; Rolandelli & Eeckhout, 2026; Shen et al., 2026b). When chopping time was further increased to 5 min, the continuity of lamellar structures decreased. Low-density regions became less clearly delineated, and localized irregular distributions were observed, suggesting that excessive chopping did not necessarily further improve phase separation.

Quantitative analysis of thickness distribution based on three-dimensional reconstruction (Fig. 7B-C) revealed that structures thinner than 250 μm accounted for a large proportion of the matrix, which likely corresponded to fine interlayer or cross-linked regions. The proportion of thicker structures (>250 μm) decreased with increasing chopping time, indicating that reduced particle size might favor the formation of thinner lamellar structures. In parallel, porosity decreased progressively with chopping time (Fig. 7D), suggesting that mild chopping tended to preserve larger voids or interlayer gaps, whereas excessive chopping might result in a denser structure, potentially due to enhanced interlayer interactions that reduce effective lamellar structure separation (Gräfenhahn & Beyrer, 2026; Tan et al., 2026). From a structural perspective, moderate chopping yielded a more uniform matrix with interconnected low-density spaces, which might facilitate directional ice crystal growth during freezing (Guo et al., 2026). Overall, chopping time modulated particle size, matrix continuity, and ice crystal growth-induced phase separation behavior, thereby influencing both the formation efficiency and spatial density of lamellar structures in restructured surimi products.

Correlation analysis

As shown in Fig. 8A, lamellar density was positively correlated with the myofibril fragmentation index and negatively correlated with D[3,2] and α-helix content (p < 0.05). This suggested that increased surimi disruption and reduced particle size tended to coincide with refined myofibrillar structures, which might be linked to the formation of denser lamellar structures. Sensory scores of muscle texture were positively correlated with α-helix content and negatively correlated with β-sheet content (p < 0.05). A decrease in α-helix proportion and an increase in β-sheet content are generally considered to reflect deeper protein unfolding and enhanced intermolecular aggregation, which may facilitate gelation. These associations might be related to reduced lamellar thickness and increased lamellar density, although excessive aggregation might be associated with a slight decline in the perception of natural muscle fibers. WHC was positively correlated with protein solubility and negatively correlated with total sulfhydryl content (p < 0.05), indicating that these variables varied in a coordinated manner within the studied system. This suggested that protein solubilization and sulfhydryl oxidation/cross-linking might be jointly associated with moisture retention. In addition, the results showed that D[3,2] was negatively correlated with surface hydrophobicity and positively correlated with anisotropy index, while decreasing particle size was associated with increasingly ordered ice crystal orientation. These relationships further supported the coordinated variation among particle size, interfacial properties, and structural anisotropy. Overall, the correlation analysis indicated that mechanical disruption was associated with changes in surimi particle size, protein conformation, and cross-linking behavior, which might collectively establish a dynamic balance for the development of the lamellar structure. Controlling chopping time around 3–4 min might help improve the natural muscle-like characteristics and overall quality of restructured fish products.

Mechanistic discussion

This study highlighted the potential role of surimi particle disruption in regulating the formation of lamellar structures in restructured fish products, as schematically illustrated in Fig. 8B. Insufficient chopping results in larger particles, relatively intact myofibrillar structures, and relatively low apparent viscosity with limited structural continuity. Such a system might exhibit non-uniform water distribution, which might be associated with heterogeneous nucleation and uneven ice crystal growth during freezing, leading to poorly defined lamellar structures. Moderate chopping reduced particle size, partially disrupted myofibrils, and increased protein solubilization, resulting in an intermediate rheological state with improved matrix deformability. These conditions might favor the phase separation and directional alignment of low-density regions and the continuous protein matrix. During freezing, more uniformly distributed water phases and a relatively continuous protein network might provide more consistent spatial constraints, which could promote more ordered, layered ice crystal growth and the subsequent formation of well-defined lamellar pores after heating. Excessive chopping further minimized the surimi particle size and enhanced protein cross-linking, which might contribute to blurred lamellar boundaries. Overall, the formation of lamellar structures appeared to result from the synergistic interaction between the microstructural state of the surimi and ice crystal dynamics. The continuous protein matrix and ice crystal templating effect are likely to play important roles in modulating ice crystal growth and helping to stabilize the lamellar structure.

Conclusions

This study demonstrated that surimi particle size is a critical intrinsic factor influencing lamellar structure formation in restructured fish products. By systematically regulating chopping time, we revealed that moderate disruption (4 min) established a favorable balance between particle refinement and preservation of structural continuity, which is essential for protein matrix rearrangement and directional ice crystal growth. Unlike conventional homogeneous surimi gels, lamellar structures emerged from the synergistic interaction among matrix uniformity, the segregation effect induced by ice crystal growth, and subsequent protein gelation. Insufficient disruption resulted in larger and uneven particles within the surimi system, hindering the formation of well-ordered ice crystals and ultimately leading to a poorly organized lamellar structure. Excessive disruption resulted in a smaller particle size, which increased the protein network density but concurrently impaired the independence of the lamellar structure and reduced its anisotropic properties.

From an application perspective, these findings provide a practical strategy to tailor muscle-like texture in restructured fish products using low-cost mechanical processing, without relying on complex extrusion or novel additives. This work contributes to the theoretical understanding of anisotropic structure formation in fish protein systems and supports the development of biomimetic products from low-value aquatic resources. Future studies may further explore the effects of freezing parameters and exogenous additives on lamellar stability, as well as extend this framework to other protein matrices to broaden its industrial applicability.
