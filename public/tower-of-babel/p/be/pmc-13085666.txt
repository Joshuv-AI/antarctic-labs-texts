Acceptable outcomes after anterior subcutaneous pelvic Internal Fixation (INFIX) for Fragility Fractures of the Pelvis (FFP) type III in elderly patients: a retrospective study
Authors: Shaozheng Yang, Qiwang Yang, Li Yang, Yihui He, Weicheng Li, Yongqiang Liu, Hua Zhong, Hongfen Chen
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackground and purposeFor elderly with fragility fractures of the pelvis (FFP) type III, minimally invasive surgery may reduce perioperative morbidity and facilitate mobilization, but evidence guiding implant selection remains limited. We evaluated postoperative hip function, recovery of independence in activities of daily living, and safety of the anterior subcutaneous pelvic internal fixator (INFIX) in elderly patients with FFP type III fractures, including surgery-related complications and fixation failure.MethodsWe retrospectively reviewed all elderly patients (≥ 65 years) with FFP type III fractures treated with INFIX between 2020 and 2023. Data collected included demographics, comorbidities, general and surgery-related complications, length of hospital stay, visual analogue scale (VAS), Majeed score, Barthel Index, and mortality. Minimum follow-up was 12 months.ResultsForty-seven patients were included, with a mean follow-up of 28.2 ± 13.1 months. The mean Majeed score was 82.9 ± 10.1. 91.5% (n = 43) achieved a cumulative walking distance > 1,000 m at follow-up. The mean Barthel Index was 84.3 ± 8.9. Surgery-related complications included lateral femoral cutaneous nerve irritation/injury in 21.3% (n = 10), wound infection in 6.4% (n = 3), and heterotopic ossification in 10.6% (n = 5). Screw loosening occurred in 12.8% (n = 6) but did not require reoperation.ConclusionINFIX for elderly patients with FFP type III fractures was associated with acceptable functional outcomes despite a relatively high rate of surgery-related complications, and most patients regained independence in activities of daily living during follow-up. Therefore, for Type III FFP, the INFIX technique maybe a viable option. Keywords: Elderly, Pelvic fracture, INFIX, Minimally invasive surgery

Introduction

With population aging, osteoporotic fractures in the elderly are increasing, and fragility fractures of the pelvis (FFP) are expected to rise disproportionately [1, 2]. Unlike high-energy pelvic trauma in younger patients, FFP typically results from low-energy mechanisms. More than 60% of elderly patients with FFP have comorbidities or functional impairments, which are associated with higher disability and mortality after fracture [3, 4].

Rommens introduced the FFP classification and recommended surgical indications according to fracture type. He stated that in FFP type III lesions, both the anterior and posterior pelvic ring are involved, with posterior ring instability, so anterior fixation must always be combined with posterior fixation, and vice versa [5]. FFP type III and IV fractures are considered unstable pelvic ring injuries, and minimally invasive stabilization is preferred in elderly patients [6–8]. However, the optimal strategy to balance surgical invasiveness with adequate stabilization remains uncertain [9, 10]. Current treatment concepts prioritize posterior ring stabilization with percutaneous screw fixation. A commonly used technique is fluoroscopy-guided percutaneous S1 sacroiliac (SI) screw fixation; however, it is technically demanding and is associated with risks such as screw malposition and loosening in osteoporotic bone [11–14].

The anterior subcutaneous pelvic internal fixator (INFIX) is a minimally invasive technique for anterior ring stabilization. Biomechanical studies suggest that INFIX alone can provide stability in unstable pelvic ring injuries [15], and clinical studies have reported similar outcomes after INFIX with or without additional SI screw fixation in selected cohorts [16, 17]. Nevertheless, evidence remains limited regarding whether INFIX alone is appropriate for elderly patients with FFP type III fractures, given the biomechanical heterogeneity among subtypes IIIa–IIIc and the complication profile in osteoporotic bone. Therefore, we conducted a retrospective study to provide preliminary clinical data on (1) fixation strategy selection across FFP type IIIa–IIIc, (2) postoperative hip function and independence in activities of daily living, and (3) surgery-related complications and fixation failure after INFIX for FFP type III fractures in elderly patients.

Patients and methods

Patients

We conducted a retrospective case series of all pelvic fracture cases treated at our trauma center from January 2020 to December 2023. This study was approved by the Institutional Medical Ethics Committee, and written informed consent was obtained from all participants before the study began. Based on radiographs and CT scans, pelvic fractures were classified according to the FFP classification [5]. Patients were screened according to the following inclusion and exclusion criteria (Fig. 1). Inclusion criteria were: (1) FFP type III fractures; (2) surgical treatment using INFIX; and (3) age ≥ 65 years. Exclusion criteria were: (1) concomitant acetabular fractures, hip fractures, or pathological fractures; (2) pelvic fractures caused by high-energy trauma with an Injury Severity Score (ISS) ≥ 16 [7]; (3) plate fixation; and (4) loss to follow-up. The follow-up period was at least 1 year postoperatively. Demographic characteristics, body mass index, and preoperative comorbidities are summarized in Table 1.

A total of 58 geriatric patients with FFP type III injuries were identified, of whom 52 underwent INFIX fixation. Three patients were lost to follow-up and two patients died within the first postoperative year, leaving 47 patients in the final study cohort

Methods

After spinal anesthesia, the patient was placed supine on a radiolucent operating table. Approximately 3-cm oblique incisions were made bilaterally over the anterior superior iliac spine (ASIS). The interval between the tensor fasciae latae and sartorius muscles was bluntly developed. Using a pedicle screw probe, screws were inserted between the inner and outer cortices of the ilium under fluoroscopic guidance, ensuring that the cortex was not breached. A 6.5 × 70 mm polyaxially pedicle screw was placed on each side. A 6-mm titanium rod was contoured to the required length, passed subcutaneously through a soft-tissue tunnel, and secured to the bilateral pedicle screws. Based on preoperative pelvic CT, additional SI screw fixation was performed when any of the following were present: (1) marked SI joint widening or diastasis/dislocation; (2) gapping along a vertical sacral fracture line; or (3) vertical displacement of the sacral fracture.

All patients received prophylactic antibiotics within 24 h postoperatively and low-molecular-weight heparin for prevention of deep vein thrombosis. After recovery from anesthesia, patients were encouraged to begin isometric quadriceps exercises and ankle dorsiflexion/plantarflexion. At 1 week postoperatively, patients were encouraged to ambulate with assistive devices using device-assisted weight bearing, as tolerated by pain. At 2–3 months, patients were allowed to progress to full weight bearing without assistive devices.

Patients were scheduled for follow-up pelvic radiographs (anteroposterior view) or CT at 1, 2, 3, 6, and 12 months after surgery to monitor fracture healing.

Methods of assessment

At the final follow-up, hip function was assessed using the Majeed score [18]. Activities of daily living (ADL) were evaluated using the Barthel Index, and pain was assessed using the visual analogue scale (VAS). Because many patients lived in remote areas and were unwilling to return for an in-person assessment at the final follow-up, these outcomes were collected by telephone interview. Respondents included the patient, a cohabiting family member, or a caregiver. Surgical safety was assessed through the incidence of postoperative lateral femoral cutaneous nerve (LFCN) injury, wound infection, heterotopic ossification, and implant loosening. LFCN injury was defined as numbness over the lateral thigh on postoperative physical examination. Heterotopic ossification and screw loosening were assessed on the final anteroposterior pelvic radiograph; heterotopic ossification was defined as a radiodense shadow at the pedicle screw entry site, and screw loosening as widening of the screw tract with a radiolucent halo around the screw. General perioperative data included surgical duration, intraoperative blood loss, and length of hospital stay. Destination at discharge, mobility, and mortality at 1 and 2 years postoperatively were also recorded.

Statistical analyses

Descriptive statistics were performed using SPSS (version 27.0; IBM Corp., Armonk, NY, USA), including means, standard deviations, and percentages. Because subgroup sample sizes were small and statistical power was limited, we did not perform between-group comparisons to avoid potentially biased or misleading results. Three patients were lost to follow-up and were therefore excluded from the final analysis. Two patients died within the first postoperative year; neither death was attributable to direct surgical complications. Based on the available information, we considered the missingness to be approximately random, as the reasons for loss to follow-up/death were not systematically related to the primary outcomes. Accordingly, analyses were restricted to patients with complete data.

Results

General data

A total of 52 patients were eligible. Two patients died within the first postoperative year. One patient (female, 89 years) with a history of cardiovascular disease died at home 8 months after surgery without presentation to a medical facility; the cause of death was presumed to be cardiac-related. The other patient (male, 80 years) with a history of lung malignancy died in a medical facility 10 months after surgery due to organ failure. Three patients were lost to follow-up because their contact information changed and they could not be reached by other means. Thus, 47 patients were included in the final analysis. Fractures resulted from a fall while walking in 95.7% (n = 45) and from a fall from a sitting position in 4.3% (n = 2). Mean age was 75.2 ± 6.3 years; 40.4% were male (n = 19) and 59.6% female (n = 28). Comorbidities were present in 80.9% (n = 38), and 44.7% (n = 21) had ≥ 2 comorbidities. Fracture subtypes were FFP type IIIa in 36.2% (n = 17), type IIIb in 6.4% (n = 3), and type IIIc in 57.4% (n = 27). The time from injury to surgery was 6.0 ± 4.7 days. Additional SI screw fixation was performed in 17.0% (n = 8). Mean surgical duration was 74.8 ± 26.5 min, intraoperative blood loss was 45.5 ± 15.4 mL, and length of hospital stay was 11.9 ± 3.1 days. The destination at discharge was home in 80.8% (n = 38) (Table 2).

Clinical outcomes

No patients died during hospitalization. Mean postoperative follow-up was 28.2 ± 13.1 months. Mortality was 4.3% (n = 2) at 1 year and 8.5% (n = 4) at 2 years postoperatively. The mean time to full weight bearing was 2.4 ± 0.6 months. Mean fracture healing time was 2.9 ± 0.7 months. Two patients developed nonunion of the pubic ramus fracture with progressive displacement; however, both were asymptomatic, had no limitation in daily activities, and did not undergo further treatment. The mean Majeed score was 82.9 ± 10.1, and the mean VAS score was 0.7 ± 1.1. Regarding mobility, 91.5% (n = 43) were able to walk a cumulative distance > 1,000 m at follow-up; 14.6% (n = 6) required a walker. The mean Barthel Index was 84.3 ± 8.9. All but one patient regained independence in ADL (97.9%, n = 46) (Tables 3 and 4). INFIX removal was requested in 70.2% (n = 33), at a mean of 7.5 ± 5.0 months after surgery. Two representative cases are presented in Figs. 2, 3, 4, 5, 6 and 7: Figures 2, 3 and 4 show Case 1 (FFP type IIIb), and Figs. 5, 6 and 7 show Case 2 (FFP type IIIc).

Complications

Surgery-related complications included LFCN injury or irritation in 21.3% (n = 10) and wound infection in 6.4% (n = 3). Among the infected cases, 2 patients achieved wound healing after multiple debridements. One patient with deep infection underwent INFIX removal after fracture healing at 2 months postoperatively, followed by successful wound closure after debridement. Heterotopic ossification occurred in 10.6% (n = 5); however, none had clinical symptoms and no intervention was required. INFIX screw loosening was observed in 12.8% (n = 6), and no patient required revision surgery. In-hospital complications included pulmonary infection in 8.5% (n = 4), deep vein thrombosis (DVT) in 4.3% (n = 2), and gastrointestinal bleeding in 4.3% (n = 2) (Table 5).

Discussion

FFP have traditionally been treated nonoperatively, with generally favorable outcomes reported mainly for FFP types I and II. In contrast, Rommens and colleagues classified FFP type III as an unstable pelvic ring injury and provided clear surgical recommendations. They also emphasized that larger surgical exposures are associated with higher complication rates and less favorable postoperative physical and mental health scores; therefore, minimally invasive approaches are advocated whenever possible in elderly patients, with the primary goals of survival and achieving sufficient stability rather than extensive reconstruction [5, 19]. Moreover, the aim of surgery for FFP type III fractures is to restore function and stability, which is considered more important than achieving anatomic reduction [5, 20]. Consistent with this concept, anatomic reduction was not pursued in our series.

Most published studies on the treatment of FFP type III emphasize posterior ring stabilization, typically using percutaneous screw fixation. A systematic review reported that the majority of surgeons treating elderly pelvic fractures use percutaneous screws for posterior fixation alone [21]. However, percutaneous screw fixation in fragility fractures is associated with a relatively high rate of screw loosening. Eckardt et al. reported that, after CT-guided percutaneous SI screw fixation for FFP, 18% of patients required reoperation due to screw loosening [22]. Wagner et al. reported that after trans-sacral bar (TB) osteosynthesis for FFP, 15.3% of patients required revision surgery due to TB-related surgical complications, with screw loosening accounting for 4.7% [23]. Although cement augmentation has been used to reduce the risk of screw loosening, it carries a substantial risk of cement leakage, the biomechanical benefit remains uncertain, and it may hinder fracture healing [24, 25]. Hartensuer et al. reported a 22% rate of cement-related complications after cement-augmented SI screw fixation for FFP, including intraspinal leakage, foraminal leakage, and cement extravasation involving neurovascular structures [26]. In addition, the most widely used minimally invasive technique is fluoroscopy-guided placement of an S1 SI screw, which is technically demanding. In elderly patients, osteoporosis and obesity can reduce fluoroscopic image quality, resulting in indistinct key landmarks (e.g., the sacral body and sacral foramina) and thereby increasing the difficulty and risk of freehand screw placement. Previous reports have shown screw malposition rates of up to 3%–17% and neurological injury rates of 0%–8% with percutaneous screw placement [27–29].

Xia et al. [30] used finite element analysis and showed that elastic intramedullary nailing of the anterior pelvic ring can also provide favorable biomechanical stability. Li et al. [31] reported that both plate fixation and cannulated screw fixation of the pubic ramus achieved good stability in Tile B2 pelvic fractures. Zou et al. [32] compared INFIX with retrograde cannulated screw fixation for anterior ring injuries in elderly patients with LC1 fragility pelvic fractures and found that both approaches were associated with earlier satisfactory recovery of function and independence in activities of daily living. However, compared with INFIX, retrograde cannulated screw fixation was used only for pubic ramus fractures with no or minimal displacement. Regarding complications, in the cannulated screw group, 9.5% (2/21) sustained vascular injury and 9.5% (2/21) experienced fixation failure, whereas no heterotopic ossification or LFCN irritation was observed; by contrast, the rates of heterotopic ossification and LFCN irritation after INFIX were 25% and 6.3%, respectively. Shea et al. [33] reported a 4.9% failure rate after retrograde screw fixation for pubic ramus fractures, and that advanced age and higher BMI were associated with an increased risk of fixation failure; furthermore, the indication was limited to patients with pubic ramus fractures without obvious displacement.

IINFIX has been widely adopted because it is technically straightforward, requires shorter operative time, is minimally invasive with limited blood loss, and may reduce some procedure-related risks commonly associated with open reduction and internal fixation [34]. Several studies have suggested that INFIX can provide favorable biomechanical stability and achieve satisfactory clinical outcomes even in fragility pelvic fractures [32, 35, 36]. Vigdorchik et al. reported that INFIX alone, without posterior fixation, can provide adequate stability for unstable pelvic ring injuries [15]. Ansari et al. found no significant difference in clinical outcomes between INFIX with and without additional SI screw fixation in unstable pelvic fractures [16]. In our series, 83.0% (n = 39) of patients—including all FFP type IIIa fractures, type IIIb fractures without marked SI joint widening, and type IIIc fractures without substantial fracture gapping or lateral compression—were treated with INFIX alone for anterior ring stabilization. Additional SI screw fixation was performed in 17.0% (n = 8) of patients due to marked SI joint widening/diastasis or dislocation, or severely displaced sacral fractures. This approach was associated with low pain levels at follow-up (mean VAS score 0.8). The mean Majeed score was 81.9 ± 10.6, consistent with the findings of Zou and Deng et al. [32, 37]. Regarding mobility, 91.5% (n = 43) were able to walk a cumulative distance > 1,000 m at follow-up, and 14.6% (n = 6) required a walker. The mean Barthel Index was 84.3 ± 8.9, and 97.9% (n = 46) regained independence in ADL, comparable to results reported by Dalos and Tolosano et al. [8, 38]. We also presented clinical outcomes separately for patients treated with INFIX alone and those treated with INFIX plus SI screw fixation (Table 3). Outcomes tended to be better in the combined-fixation subgroup; however, the results also suggest that INFIX alone yielded acceptable outcomes in selected patients. Notably, the mean age in our cohort was 75.2 ± 6.3 years, which is lower than that reported in many previous studies and may partly explain the favorable functional results.

The most common complication associated with INFIX was LFCN injury. According to several reports, the incidence of LFCN injury ranges from 23% to 34% [39, 40]. However, symptoms related to LFCN injury often alleviate or resolve after implant removal and generally do not affect hip function. Other complications include heterotopic ossification and wound infection, although most cases of heterotopic ossification are asymptomatic and do not require treatment. In our study, LFCN injury occurred in 21.3% (n = 10) of patients, wound infection in 6.4% (n = 3), and heterotopic ossification in 10.6% (n = 5). Additionally, because the INFIX device is located subcutaneously, patients may experience lower abdominal discomfort postoperatively, often necessitating a second procedure for implant removal. In our series, 70.2% of patients (n = 33) eventually requested INFIX removal. Although screw loosening occurred in 12.8% (n = 6), no patient required reoperation because of screw loosening. This rate was substantially lower than previously reported reoperation rates of 6%–18% due to screw loosening [22, 23].

In summary, there is still no consensus on the treatment of osteoporotic pelvic fractures. Standardizing treatment protocols, raising awareness, investigating new minimally invasive techniques, and reducing the high disability and mortality rates remain significant challenges. In our case series, INFIX alone for FFP type III fractures was associated with acceptable clinical outcomes; however, given the retrospective design, small sample size, and lack of a comparator group, the strength of evidence remains limited. Further biomechanical studies and larger comparative clinical datasets are needed.

Limitations

First, this was a single-center retrospective study with a relatively small sample size, which may limit the generalizability of our findings. Second, functional outcomes were assessed by telephone interview. Although telephone-based follow-up is commonly used and practical in orthopedic research, we acknowledge that it may introduce recall bias or information bias. Therefore, results derived from these assessments should be interpreted with caution, particularly when differences are marginal. In addition, key studies cited in support of INFIX-only fixation (e.g., Vigdorchik et al., Ansari et al.) were conducted in high-energy trauma populations with non-osteoporotic bone and therefore cannot be directly extrapolated to elderly patients with FFP type III fractures. Finally, we did not evaluate long-term outcomes, such as sustained functional recovery and health-related quality of life. In this field, many treatment recommendations are still based on expert opinion or small retrospective studies, underscoring the need for more robust evidence.

Conclusions

INFIX fixation was associated with acceptable outcomes in selected patients with FFP type III fractures. However, surgery-related complications were relatively common, and most patients required a second procedure for implant removal. Given that satisfactory functional recovery may also occur with nonoperative management—particularly in low-energy osteoporotic pelvic fractures—careful patient selection is essential when considering this technique. Moreover, because most published evidence still supports prioritizing posterior ring stabilization, the role of INFIX as a standalone anterior fixation strategy in FFP type III fractures requires further clinical and biomechanical investigate.
