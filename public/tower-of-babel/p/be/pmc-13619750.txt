Efficacy and Safety of Statins in Patients with Liver Cirrhosis: A Systematic Review and Meta-Analysis of Randomized Controlled Trials with Trial Sequential Analysis
Authors: Han Deng, Ran Wang, Yuhang Yin, Rui Zhang, Xiangbo Xu, Xiaohui Fang, Xingshun Qi
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
IntroductionLiver cirrhosis is a global health burden with high morbidity and mortality. Recent evidence suggests the benefits of statins in liver cirrhosis due to its pleiotropic effects. This systematic review and meta-analysis was conducted to evaluate the efficacy and safety of statins in patients with liver cirrhosis.MethodsPubMed, EMBASE, and Cochrane Library databases were systematically searched to identify all relevant randomized controlled trials (RCTs) regarding use of statins in cirrhosis. Risk ratios (RRs) or mean differences (MDs) with their 95% confidence intervals (CIs) were pooled for the outcomes, including overall mortality, hepatic venous pressure gradient (HVPG), cirrhosis-related complications, and adverse events. Heterogeneity was calculated. Trial sequential analysis (TSA) was performed.ResultsThirteen RCTs were included. Statins significantly decreased the overall mortality (RR 0.63, 95% CI 0.47–0.84, I2 = 0%), reduced HVPG (MD 0.96, 95% CI 0.35–1.57, I2 = 19%), and increased the proportion of patients who achieved HVPG response (RR 1.88, 95% CI 1.04–3.38, I2 = 62%). Statins were not associated with the incidence of acute-on-chronic liver failure, variceal bleeding, new or worsening ascites, hepatic encephalopathy, or acute kidney injury, but significantly reduced the incidence of spontaneous bacterial peritonitis (RR 0.35, 95% CI 0.16–0.75, I2 = 0%). There was no significant difference in the incidence of any adverse events or serious adverse events between patients who received statins and those who did not. TSA suggested that further studies should be necessary to validate the efficacy of statins.ConclusionsStatins may reduce the overall mortality and HVPG in patients with liver cirrhosis. Additionally, statins can prevent from spontaneous bacterial peritonitis, but not other cirrhosis-related complications.Clinical RegistrationThe protocol was prospectively registered on the PROSPERO database (CRD420251000903).Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at https://doi.org/10.1007/s12325-026-03682-5.

Introduction

Liver cirrhosis, the end-stage of chronic hepatic injury, represents a global health burden with high morbidity and mortality [1]. It is characterized by extensive fibrosis, parenchymal nodule regeneration, and architectural distortion, ultimately leading to life-threatening complications, including variceal bleeding, ascites, spontaneous bacterial peritonitis (SBP), and hepatic encephalopathy, etc. [2, 3]. Despite recent advances in the management of cirrhosis-related complications, effective treatment options remain very limited, underscoring the urgent need for novel strategies.

Statins inhibit 3-hydroxy-3-methylglutaryl-coenzyme A reductase, a rate-limiting enzyme in cholesterol biosynthesis. Beyond their lipid-modifying effects, statins exert pleiotropic actions, including antioxidant, anti-inflammatory, anti-fibrotic, and vasoprotective properties [4–6]. It has been shown that statins may reduce portal pressure, delay fibrosis progression, and decrease the incidence of hepatocellular carcinoma in patients with cirrhosis [6, 7]. More recently, two large-scale randomized controlled trials (RCTs) evaluating the effectiveness of statins for liver cirrhosis have been published, but their conclusions were not entirely consistent [8, 9]. Rana et al. [8] reported that simvastatin treatment improved the survival and reduced the incidence of new onset/refractory ascites and SBP. In contrast, Pose et al. [9] found that adding simvastatin plus rifaximin to standard therapy did not reduce the risk of mortality or cirrhosis-related complications. Although several previous meta-analyses [10–12] have synthesized the available evidence on this topic, the inconsistent findings from the two large-scale RCTs have not been reconciled in updated meta-analyses.

We therefore performed an updated systematic review and meta-analysis of all currently available RCTs to comprehensively evaluate the clinical efficacy and safety of statin therapy in patients with cirrhosis. Furthermore, we performed trial sequential analysis (TSA) to quantify the robustness of the pooled evidence and determine whether the current findings are sufficiently conclusive to guide the clinical decision.

Methods

This systematic review and meta-analysis was performed according to the Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Reviews and Meta-analyses (PRISMA) statement [13]. The study selection, data extraction, and study quality assessment were carried out independently by two investigators (HD and RW). Disagreement between them was resolved by the discussion with another investigator (XQ).

This article is based on previously conducted studies and does not contain any new studies with human participants or animals performed by any of the authors.

Registration

This study was registered on the international prospective register of systematic reviews (PROSPERO) database (registration number CRD420251000903).

Literature Search

All papers were searched via the PubMed, EMBASE, and Cochrane Library databases. The last search was conducted on March 17, 2026. The search items were as follows: ((hydroxymethylglutaryl CoA reductase inhibitor) OR (statin) OR (atorvastatin) OR (fluvastatin) OR (lovastatin) OR (pitavastatin) OR (rosuvastatin) OR (simvastatin) OR (pravastatin)) AND ((cirrhosis) OR (cirrhotic)). Additional studies were also identified through manual search by reading the reference list.

Eligibility Criteria

Eligible studies should investigate the efficacy and/or safety of statins in patients with liver cirrhosis. Included studies were not restricted by the publication language. If the same population was included in two or more studies, only the most recent and comprehensive data were included. Exclusion criteria were as follows: 1) duplicates; 2) commentaries, notes, and letters; 3) reviews, guidelines, and meta-analyses; 4) protocols and clinical trial registrations; 5) case reports and case series; 6) non-RCTs; 7) lack of relevant data; 8) inaccurate data; and 9) the treatment duration of less than 1 day to evaluate the change of acute hemodynamics.

Data Extraction

The following data were extracted: 1) the first author, publication year, country, type, dose, and frequency of statins, interventions in the control groups, total number of patients, mean or median age, sex distribution, etiology of cirrhosis, baseline model for end-stage liver disease (MELD) and Child–Pugh score, and follow-up duration; 2) outcome data, including hepatic venous pressure gradient (HVPG) reduction, number of patients who reached hemodynamic response defined as HVPG decrease > 20% from baseline or HVPG < 12 mmHg, incidence of mortality, acute-on-chronic liver failure (ACLF), variceal bleeding, new or worsening ascites, SBP, hepatic encephalopathy, and acute kidney injury; and 3) safety data, including type and frequency of any adverse events (AEs) and serious AEs.

Study Quality Assessment

The revised Cochrane Risk of Bias tool (ROB 2) was used to assess the quality of RCTs in five domains, namely randomization process, deviations from intended interventions, missing outcome data, measurement of the outcome, and selection of the reported results. Each item has five possible answers: “yes”, “probably yes”, “no”, “probably no”, and “no information”. The overall bias risk is classified as “low risk”, “high risk”, or “some concerns” [14].

Statistical Analyses

Review Manager software (version 5.4, Cochrane Collaboration, the Nordic Cochrane Centre, Copenhagen, Denmark), Stata software (version 17.0, Stata Corp, College Station, USA), and TSA software (version 0.9.5.10, CTU, Centre for Clinical Intervention Research, Copenhagen, Denmark) were employed to perform meta-analyses. Only a random-effects model was employed. Dichotomous variables were expressed as risk ratios (RRs) with 95% confidence intervals (CIs). Continuous outcomes were expressed as mean differences (MDs) with 95% CIs. χ2 test and the I2 statistics were employed to assess the heterogeneity among studies, and P < 0.1 or I2 > 50% was considered as statistically significant heterogeneity. Leave-one-out sensitivity analyses were performed by omitting each study. Subgroup analyses were performed according to the type of statins, dose of statins (low, simvastatin 20 mg/day or atorvastatin 10 mg/day; moderate, simvastatin 40 mg/day or atorvastatin 20 mg/day), follow-up duration (≤ 3 and > 3 months), study design (open-label and blinded), mean MELD score (≤ 13 and > 13), combination with non-selective beta-blockers (NSBBs) (yes and no), proportion of patients with a history of variceal bleeding (0–10%, 10–50%, and 100%), and proportion of patients with decompensated cirrhosis (< 50%, 50–80%, and 100%). If the proportion of patients with decompensated cirrhosis was not reported, it would be estimated by using the proportion of patients with ascites or variceal bleeding from the baseline characteristics. Publication bias was evaluated solely through visual inspection of funnel plots because of the limited number of eligible studies. TSA was conducted to assess the reliability and stability of cumulative evidence by minimizing type I error and random error. The required information size (RIS) was calculated on the basis of a two-sided 5% risk of a type I error and 20% risk of a type II error (power of 80%). The trial sequential monitoring boundaries were computed using the O’Brien-Fleming approach. Relative risk reduction was set as 20%.

In studies where the means and standard deviations (SDs) of HVPG reduction were not reported, the pre- and post-intervention values were extracted, thereby estimating the change-from-baseline SDs by using the following equation reported in Chapter 6.5.2.8 of the Cochrane Handbook for Systematic Reviews of Interventions [15]:\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\mathrm{SD}_{\mathrm{change}} = \sqrt{\mathrm{SD}_{\mathrm{baseline}}^{2}+\mathrm{SD}_{\mathrm{final}}^{2}-\left(2\times\mathrm{corr}\times\mathrm{SD}_{\mathrm{baseline}}\times\mathrm{SD}_{\mathrm{final}}\right)}.$$\end{document}SDchange=SDbaseline2+SDfinal2-2×corr×SDbaseline×SDfinal.

A correlation coefficient of 0.50 was applied for this calculation. To evaluate the robustness of our estimation, we conducted sensitivity analyses with two alternative correlation coefficient values (0.2 and 0.8), and all tests produced consistent results. In studies where HVPG reduction was reported as the median with interquartile range, the corresponding means and SDs were estimated on the basis of the conversion method described by Wan et al. [16]

Study Quality

Study quality was shown in Supplementary Fig. 1. Seven studies [9, 17–19, 24–27] were evaluated as “low risk”, and six [8, 20–23, 25] as “some concerns”.

Overall Mortality

Based on the available data obtained from eight studies [8, 9, 17, 19, 20, 22, 25, 26], meta-analysis demonstrated that the overall mortality was significantly lower in patients who received statins than those who did not (RR 0.63, 95% CI 0.47–0.84, P = 0.002) without significant heterogeneity (I2 = 0%, P = 0.61) (Fig. 2). In subgroup analyses of patients treated with simvastatin (RR 0.62, 95% CI 0.46–0.83, I2 = 0%), patients treated at a moderate dose of statins (RR 0.50, 95% CI 0.30–0.85, I2 = 0%), patients followed for a duration of > 3 months (RR 0.65, 95% CI 0.46–0.92, I2 = 17%), open-label studies (RR 0.56, 95% CI 0.38–0.81, I2 = 0%), patients with a mean MELD score > 13 (RR 0.66, 95% CI 0.48–0.91, I2 = 0%), patients treated with a combination of NSBBs (RR 0.56, 95% CI 0.38–0.81, I2 = 0%), studies where 100% of patients had a history of variceal bleeding (RR 0.54, 95% CI 0.38–0.76, I2 = 0%), and studies where 100% of patients had decompensated cirrhosis (RR 0.62, 95% CI 0.44–0.89, I2 = 20%), the effect of statins on overall mortality remained statistically significant (Supplementary Fig. 2). In sensitivity analyses, the statistical significance was altered after omitting the study by Rana et al. (RR 0.67, 95% CI 0.45–1.01, I2 = 0%) (Supplementary Table 1). In TSA, the cumulative Z-curves neither crossed the TSA monitoring boundary nor surpassed the RIS (n = 3545), indicating that more evidence is needed to conclude a significant association of statins with a decreased risk of overall mortality (Supplementary Fig. 3). HVPG Reduction

Based on the available data obtained from seven studies [17, 18, 20, 23, 25–27], meta-analysis demonstrated that HVPG was significantly reduced in patients who received statins compared with those who did not (MD 0.96, 95% CI 0.35–1.57, P = 0.002) without significant heterogeneity (I2 = 19%, P = 0.28) (Fig. 3a). In subgroup analyses of patients treated with simvastatin (MD 0.98, 95% CI 0.34–1.61, I2 = 11%), patients treated at a moderate dose of statins (MD 1.04, 95% CI 0.34–1.74, I2 = 28%), patients followed for a duration of ≤ 3 months (MD 1.20, 95% CI 0.49–1.92, I2 = 22%), blinded studies (MD 0.98, 95% CI 0.31–1.65 I2 = 14%), patients with a mean MELD score ≤ 13 (MD 1.24, 95% CI 0.36-2.12, I² = 42%), patients treated with a combination of NSBBs (MD 0.92, 95% CI 0.04–1.81, I2 = 27%), studies where 10–50% of patients had a history of variceal bleeding (MD 1.95, 95% CI 0.98–2.92, I2 = 0%), and studies where < 50% of patients had decompensated cirrhosis (MD 0.96, 95% CI 0.20–1.71, I2 = 33%), the effect of statins on HVPG reduction remained statistically significant (Supplementary Fig. 5). Leave-one-out sensitivity analyses showed that no single study influenced the overall result (Supplementary Table 2). In TSA, the cumulative Z-curves neither crossed the TSA monitoring boundary nor surpassed the RIS (n = 1440), indicating that more evidence is needed to conclude a significant association of statins with HVPG reduction (Supplementary Fig. 6). HVPG Response

Based on the available data obtained from five studies [17, 18, 20, 25, 27], meta-analysis demonstrated that patients who received statins achieved a significantly higher proportion of HVPG response than those who did not (RR 1.88, 95% CI 1.04–3.38, P = 0.04) with significant heterogeneity (I2 = 62%, P = 0.03) (Fig. 3b). In subgroup analyses of blinded studies (RR 2.93, 95% CI 1.50–5.72, I2 = 0%), patients with a mean MELD score ≤ 13 (RR 2.42, 95% CI 1.12–5.20, I2 = 44%), and studies where 50–80% of patients had decompensated cirrhosis (RR 1.99, 95% CI 1.17–3.40, I2 = 0%), the effect of statins on HVPG response remained statistically significant (Supplementary Fig. 7). In sensitivity analyses, the heterogeneity was not statistically significant after omitting the study by Vijayaraghavan et al. (RR 2.35, 95% CI 1.38–4.00, I2 = 18%), indicating that it might be a contributor to the heterogeneity (Supplementary Table 3). TSA was not performed due to insufficient available data. There was moderate publication bias in the funnel plot (Supplementary Fig. 4C).

New or Worsening Ascites

Based on the available data obtained from ten studies [8, 9, 17, 19–22, 24–26], meta-analysis demonstrated that statins did not significantly change the incidence of new or worsening ascites (RR 0.82, 95% CI 0.66–1.02, P = 0.07) without significant heterogeneity (I2 = 0%, P = 0.71) (Fig. 4). In subgroup analyses of open-label studies (RR 0.71, 95% CI 0.53–0.96, I2 = 0%) and patients treated with a combination of NSBBs (RR 0.72, 95% CI 0.53–0.98, I2 = 0%), the effect of statins on new or worsening ascites became statistically significant (Supplementary Fig. 13). Based on the available data obtained from six studies [8, 19, 20, 22, 24, 26], meta-analysis demonstrated that statins significantly reduced the incidence of SBP (RR 0.35, 95% CI 0.16–0.75, P = 0.007) without significant heterogeneity (I2 = 0%, P = 0.93) (Fig. 4). Any AEs

Based on the available data obtained from eight studies [8, 9, 17–19, 24, 25, 27], meta-analysis demonstrated that the incidence of any AEs were not significantly different between statins and control groups (RR 1.06, 95% CI 0.94–1.19, P = 0.37), and the heterogeneity was not significant (I2 = 33%, P = 0.17) (Fig. 5a). In subgroup analyses of studies where 0–10% of patients had a history of variceal bleeding (RR 1.40, 95% CI 1.07–1.83, I2 = 0%) and studies where < 50% of patients had decompensated cirrhosis (RR 1.41, 95% CI 1.08–1.84, I2 = 0%), the effect of statins on any AEs became statistically significant (Supplementary Fig. 21). Discussion

The major finding of our study is that the use of statins is associated with a reduction in overall mortality among patients with liver cirrhosis. Furthermore, the results of our subgroup analyses might be useful for identifying some groups of patients who can obtain survival benefits from the use of statins. First, there was a significant positive trend in patients with cirrhosis treated with a higher dosage of simvastatin. But this finding was restricted by a very limited number of studies. Second, four studies [8, 9, 19, 22] had a follow-up duration lasting for 1 or 2 years, of which three [8, 19, 22] showed the survival benefits of statins. By comparison, among the studies with a follow-up duration of less than 3 months [17, 20, 25], the survival benefits of statins seem to be inadequate. Third, the survival benefit of statins is significant in patients with a MELD score > 13, but not those with a MELD score ≤ 13. Fourth, it seemed that additional use of statins could achieve survival benefits among patients with a history of variceal bleeding, those with decompensated cirrhosis, and those treated with a combination therapy with NSBBs.

Another finding of our study is that statins could decrease HVPG among patients with cirrhosis. Indeed, the Baveno VII consensus recommends the use of statins in patients with cirrhosis, if the indications for statins are established [29]. This recommendation is primarily due to its benefit on decreasing portal pressure and improving overall survival. These clinical benefits may be attributed to the fact that statins ameliorate liver fibrosis, endothelial dysfunction, and portal hypertension via the upregulation of Kruppel-like factor 2 expression, inhibition of RhoA/Rho-kinase signalling, and activation of endothelial nitric oxide synthase [4–6]. Notably, sensitivity analyses on HVPG response showed that the heterogeneity was not statistically significant after omitting the study by Vijayaraghavan et al. [25]. This discrepancy may be attributed to the variations in baseline HVPG, etiology of cirrhosis, liver functional reserve, standard therapy, and follow-up duration among studies. Additionally, our subgroup analyses found that continued simvastatin therapy for 1–3 months (MD 1.20, 95% CI 0.49–1.92) might achieve a greater HVPG reduction than those for 3 months or longer (MD 0.20, 95% CI − 0.95 to 1.35). This can be explained by the fact that simvastatin can rapidly and temporarily increase the hepatosplanchnic output of nitric oxide products and decrease hepatic resistance in patients with cirrhosis [7], but the long-term effect of simvastatin on hemodynamics is probably offset by the progression of cirrhosis itself. In future, high-quality RCTs are needed to identify the target population who can gain benefits from long-term use of statins. Regardless, the removal or suppression of the primary etiology, use of NSBBs, preferably carvedilol [29], endoscopic therapy, and transjugular intrahepatic portosystemic shunt remain the current cornerstone for the management of portal hypertension.

Our study found that statins did not reduce the incidence of major cirrhosis-related complications, such as ACLF, variceal bleeding, new or worsening ascites, hepatic encephalopathy, and acute kidney injury, but did reduce the occurrence of SBP. This is consistent with previous findings that the use of statins may potentially reduce the risk of infections among patients with cirrhosis [30].

We also found no significant differences in the incidence of any AEs and serious AEs between statins users and non-statins users. TSA indicated that the statistical result was firm. Notably, 10 (3.5%) patients who had deteriorated liver function at baseline developed rhabdomyolysis in four studies [9, 19, 24, 25]. Three of those patients received simvastatin at a dose of 20 mg/day [9], and seven at a dose of 40 mg/day [19, 24, 25]. Thus, a lower dose of statins should be considered in patients with cirrhosis and Child–Pugh classes B and C [29]. Other side effects of statins included muscle weakness, myalgias, elevated transaminase levels, pruritus, dizziness, and epistaxis, but they were often mild. Collectively, muscle-related signs and liver toxicity should be closely monitored in patients with cirrhosis.

Compared with two recently published meta-analyses [11, 12], our current meta-analysis has some unique features. First, the two prior meta-analyses only evaluated the efficacy of statins in patients with cirrhosis, while ours simultaneously assessed the efficacy of statins on multiple clinical outcomes as well as their safety. Second, our meta-analysis included four additional RCTs [8, 9, 23, 24], especially two large-scale RCTs published in 2025 [8, 9]. One [9] of them was excluded from the meta-analysis by Moraes et al. [12], because it also used rifaximin in the intervention group, but placebo in the control group. Notably, it showed that adding simvastatin plus rifaximin to standard therapy did not reduce the risk of mortality nor cirrhosis-related complications. However, considering that rifaximin is a guideline-recommended therapy for reducing the risk of hepatic encephalopathy recurrence and modulating gut microbiota in patients with cirrhosis [2, 31], rifaximin co-administration would not compromise the theoretical benefit of statins. Therefore, this trial was not excluded from our meta-analysis, as doing so could overestimate the positive conclusion. Third, we conducted comprehensive subgroup analyses to explore which subgroups of patients with cirrhosis are the most likely to derive clinical benefit from statins, providing a direction for future research. Fourth, TSA was performed to further demonstrate that more evidence is needed to validate the benefits of statins on overall mortality, HVPG, and cirrhosis-related complications. Fifth, in the meta-analysis by Moraes et al. [12] observational studies were also included. However, it should be noted that the heterogeneity was high in the subgroup meta-analyses of observational studies. By comparison, our meta-analyses included only RCTs, and the heterogeneity was not significant or very low in most of our meta-analyses, suggesting that our results are more reliable.

There were some limitations in our study. First, the quality of included studies was moderate. Second, a relatively short follow-up duration among a proportion of included studies weakens the robustness of effectiveness evidence for certain outcomes. Third, as a result of the limited number of eligible studies, the results of subgroup analyses should be further confirmed, and meta-regression analyses could not be performed to explore the potential sources of between-study heterogeneity. Fourth, as a result of the diverse characteristics of patients among the included studies, the group of patients with cirrhosis who benefit most from statin therapy should be further identified.

Conclusions

Statins may improve the overall survival of patients with cirrhosis, especially those with a MELD score > 13. Statins can be beneficial for reducing HVPG, but this effect may be acute and transient. Statins may decrease the incidence of SBP, but not ACLF, variceal bleeding, new or worsening ascites, hepatic encephalopathy, or acute kidney injury. Despite the overall safety profile, clinicians should closely monitor the AEs of statins in cirrhosis. Future studies remain essential to explain the mechanisms underlying the survival benefits of statins in cirrhosis, and large-scale RCTs should also identify who will benefit from statins.
