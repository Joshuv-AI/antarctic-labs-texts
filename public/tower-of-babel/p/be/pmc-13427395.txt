Prescribing patterns and determinants of long-term proton pump inhibitor use in Malaysian public hospitals
Authors: Hayati Alwani Yahaya, Nor Asyikin Mohd Tahir, Mohd Makmor-Bakry, Farida Hanim Islahudin, Lim Ming Chiang, Subramaniam Thanimalai, Nur Asyikin Adzmi, Razanah Zainuddin
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ObjectivesThis study aimed to evaluate the prescribing patterns and determinants of long-term proton pump inhibitor (PPI) therapy across four public hospitals in Malaysia.MethodsAdult inpatients aged ≥18 years old and who received PPIs during medical ward admission in 2023 were recruited as respondents, whereas adult inpatients who were pregnant, prescribed PPIs for Zollinger–Ellison syndrome, Barrett's esophagus, or Helicobacter pylori eradication, or with incomplete data were excluded.ResultsIn total, 392 adult inpatients with PPI prescriptions in 2023 were included. The mean age was 66.7 years, with most patients aged ≥60 years (76.5%) and predominantly male (75%). Pantoprazole was the most frequently prescribed (83.7%), and only 29.6% had documented indications. Long-term PPI therapy over 12 weeks was observed in 87.5% of patients and 71.4% received continuous therapy. Laboratory analyses also found significant reductions in serum magnesium and hemoglobin post-PPI therapy (p < 0.001), whereas changes in pre- and post-corrected calcium levels (p = 0.115) were not significant. In addition, multivariate logistic regression analysis identified the number of received medications as the only independent predictor of long-term PPI use over 12 weeks (odds ratio = 1.913, 95% confidence interval: 1.525–2.398, p < 0.001).ConclusionsLong-term PPI therapy beyond 12 weeks was highly prevalent in Malaysian public hospitals, and predominantly driven by the number of medications received by patients. These findings highlight the need to rationalize PPI prescribing and implement strategies to optimize patient safety.

Introduction

Proton pump inhibitors (PPIs) are among the most widely prescribed medications for the management of acid-related gastrointestinal disorders. Since the introduction of PPIs over two decades ago, they have largely substituted histamine-2 receptor antagonists due to their superior efficacy in suppressing gastric acid secretion and favorable short-term safety profile.1,2 Common indications include gastroesophageal reflux disease, peptic ulcer disease, Zollinger–Ellison syndrome, eradication regimens for Helicobacter pylori, and prevention of nonsteroidal anti-inflammatory drugs (NSAIDs)-related ulcer complications.3,4 Despite the effectiveness of PPIs, concerns have emerged regarding the frequent overuse and prolonged duration of PPI therapy beyond recommended treatment courses as clinical guidelines generally recommend 4–8 weeks of therapy for most acid-related conditions, with longer treatment reserved for selected conditions, such as severe erosive esophagitis and Zollinger–Ellison syndrome.5,6 Nevertheless, in practice, numerous patients have remained on PPIs for months or years without an appropriate indication or regular review of therapy.7

Throughout the world, it is estimated that 25–70% of patients have been prescribed PPIs without justifiable indications.7 A previous literature review also found that over one-third of older adult patients received PPIs, and 79% of patients received PPIs for a longer period than recommended.8 Moreover, a study in Thailand showed that PPIs were inappropriately utilized among ∼50% of patients, and 79.1% of these resulted from invalid indications.9 Long-term usage of PPIs has also been linked to potentially adverse outcomes, including hypomagnesemia, vitamin B12 and iron deficiency, reduced calcium absorption, increased fracture risk, and gastrointestinal infections.10 In particular, elderly patients, who frequently have multiple comorbidities and polypharmacy, are more vulnerable to these risks. By recognizing safety concerns, multiple international societies, including the American College of Gastroenterology and American Gastroenterological Association, have emphasized the importance of appropriate prescribing and deprescribing strategies.3,4,6,10, 11, 12 Moreover, data are lacking on PPI prescribing patterns and factors that might influence prolonged PPI therapy in Malaysia. At present, Malaysian guidance on the rationalization of PPI therapy remains limited. Thus, understanding relevant trends is essential for optimizing safe prescribing practices, especially among older adults with multimorbidity. Accordingly, the present study aimed to assess the prescribing patterns, biochemical changes associated with prolonged PPI use, and determinants of long-term PPI therapy in Malaysian public hospitals.

Materials and Methods

Study design and setting

A retrospective cross-sectional multicenter study was conducted in four major public hospitals across Malaysia, which represented different geographical regions, i.e., Hospital Putrajaya (Central), Hospital Sultanah Bahiyah (Northern), Hospital Pakar Sultanah Fatimah (Southern), and Hospital Sultan Haji Ahmad Shah (Eastern). These four hospitals functioned as referral centers within the respective regions.

Study population

All patients admitted to medical wards between January and December 2023 were eligible for inclusion in the current study. Eligible patients were selected from electronic admission records through systematic random sampling. The sampling interval (k) was determined for each participating site, and the first patient was randomly selected, followed by every k-th patient thereafter. The inclusion criteria comprised patients aged ≥18 years and prescribed PPIs during admission to the medical wards of public hospitals under the Ministry of Health (MOH) Malaysia. Patients were excluded if pregnant or the indication for PPI use was aimed at managing Zollinger–Ellison syndrome, Barrett's esophagus, or eradication of H. pylori infection. Patients with incomplete or missing medical records were excluded. The sample size was estimated using two approaches. The first approach was Cochran's formula, which indicated a requirement for ∼384 patients.13 The second approach was the Peduzzi rule of 10 outcome events per predictor variable, which was employed for logistic regression analysis and suggested a minimum sample size of 120 patients.14 The final sample size justification was based on a larger sample size to ensure adequate statistical power for both analyses. Data extraction was independently verified by two investigators to guarantee satisfactory levels of accuracy and consistency.

Data collection

Data were extracted from electronic medical records (EMR) at the participating hospitals through a standardized collection form, which encompassed demographic characteristics (age, ethnicity, and gender), medical history (comorbidities and number of hospital admissions), medication history (number of concomitant medications or medications received), and PPI-specific information (type of PPI, dosage, duration of therapy, and indication and course of therapy). In the present study, long-term PPI use was defined as therapy lasting over 12 weeks. This threshold was selected to align with the local prescribing practices, where patients would be typically reviewed at 3-month intervals. Nevertheless, we acknowledged that international definitions of long-term PPI use vary. The threshold for defining long-term PPI use varies from >2 weeks to >7 years.15 Therefore, the 12-week point represented a practical clinical option to review the indication, safety, and continuing need for PPI therapy. International definitions of long-term PPI use vary according to indication, where some guidelines consider treatment beyond 4–8 weeks as prolonged, but the 12-week threshold was used as a conservative and locally relevant operational definition in this study.

Studies of adverse effects might require a tailored definition depending on the necessary exposure time.15 In particular, courses of therapy in the current study were divided into two methods, i.e., intermittent and continuous therapy. Intermittent PPI therapy was defined as administration provided whenever necessary or in non-continuous courses with treatment interruptions, whereas continuous therapy referred to uninterrupted daily administration of PPIs throughout the prescribed treatment period. Laboratory parameters comprised serum magnesium, hemoglobin, and corrected calcium levels, which were recorded both before and following initiation of PPI therapy. Baseline laboratory values were defined as the most recent results obtained before commencing PPI therapy, whereas post-treatment values were the last available readings during or at the end of PPI therapy. This approach ensured that the post-treatment results more accurately reflected the patient's most recent biochemical status while remaining under PPI exposure, depending on the patient's length of therapy and availability of the data. Other parameters, such as serum vitamin B12, were not appraised because these parameters were not routinely measured in the participating centers, which would have resulted in incomplete and non-comparable data. Only patients with both pre- and post-treatment results available were included in the laboratory analysis subset. Laboratory reference ranges were defined as follows: magnesium, 0.70–1.00 mmol/L; hemoglobin, 12–16 g/dL; and corrected calcium, 2.10–2.55 mmol/L. All values were interpreted according to local clinically validated reference ranges to guarantee consistency and comparability of results. Changes in laboratory values were categorized as “below normal range after PPI therapy.”

Statistical analysis

Data were analyzed using IBM SPSS statistics version 29.0 software (IBM, Armonk, NY. USA). Descriptive statistics were presented as means with standard deviations (SDs) for continuous variables and frequencies with percentages for categorical variables. Normality of continuous data was assessed using the Shapiro–Wilk test. The laboratory parameters were not normally distributed, so the Wilcoxon signed-rank test was utilized to compare pre- and post-treatment laboratory values. Multivariable logistic regression was performed to identify factors associated with long-term PPI use, defined as therapy exceeding 12 weeks. The independent variables entered into the model were gender, age, number of comorbidities, number of hospital admissions, and number of medications received. Odds ratios (ORs), 95% confidence intervals (CIs), and p-values were calculated. Model calibration was assessed using the Hosmer–Lemeshow goodness-of-fit test and discriminative ability using the c-statistic.

Results

In total, 392 patients were included in the present study and their demographic characteristics are summarized in Table 1. Most were male (75.0%) and older adults, with 76.5% aged ≥60 years old, and the mean age was 66.7 ± 11.2 years. Most patients were Malay (71.7%), followed by Chinese (18.6%) and Indian (9.7%). The overall burden of chronic illness was substantial, where the mean number of comorbidities was 3.3 ± 1.6, with hypertension (64.0%) and diabetes mellitus (62.2%) as the most prevalent conditions. On average, patients were prescribed 5.4 ± 2.7 concomitant medications. In terms of PPI use (see Table 2), pantoprazole was the most frequently prescribed (83.7%), followed by omeprazole (9.2%) and esomeprazole (7.1%). Most patients (87%) had been on PPIs for over 12 weeks, and 71.4% received continuous therapy. Notably, the indication for PPI was not documented in 70.4% of cases. Moreover, esophagogastroduodenoscopy (EGD) had been performed in only 17.0% of patients. Laboratory investigations also revealed trends pre- and post-PPI therapy (see Table 3), where only patients with available paired pre- and post-PPI laboratory values were included for each respective parameter in the laboratory analyses. Therefore, the sample size varied across magnesium, hemoglobin, and corrected calcium analyses according to the availability of routine clinical laboratory data. Serum magnesium levels decreased statistically significantly following PPI therapy, and a significant proportion of patients developed hypomagnesemia (p < 0.001). Hemoglobin levels also decreased significantly after treatment (p < 0.001), suggesting a possible link between prolonged PPI use and anemia risk. By contrast, the corrected serum calcium levels only decreased by minor amounts and not significantly (p = 0.115). In summary, these findings highlight notable biochemical changes in the magnesium and hemoglobin levels associated with PPI exposure, whereas the calcium levels remained stable. According to the multivariate logistic regression model (see Table 4), the number of received medications was identified as the only significant independent predictor of long-term use of PPI (over 12 weeks). Each additional medication increased the odds of extended PPI therapy by approximately twofold (OR = 1.913, 95% CI = 1.525–2.398, p < 0.001). Discussion

This study identified several key concerns regarding the prescribing and monitoring of PPI therapy, especially given that the majority of patients were older adults aged ≥60 years with multiple comorbidities and polypharmacy. The predominance of pantoprazole use might reflect institutional preferences or formulary restrictions. In most public hospitals, formulary inclusion is influenced by cost considerations, and pantoprazole is frequently available as a more affordable generic option. In addition, national or institutional procurement contracts, tender processes, and stock availability frequently determine the PPI that is preferentially supplied. Other than procurement and logistical factors, the preference for pantoprazole is driven by clinical and pharmacotherapeutic considerations. In contrast to omeprazole and esomeprazole, which are both potent inhibitors of CYP2C19, pantoprazole exhibits minimal CYP2C19 inhibition and results in fewer clinically significant interactions with medications. This characteristic makes pantoprazole more preferable for use in populations that are commonly exposed to polypharmacy, particularly older adults with multiple comorbidities. The pharmacological advantages and availability of cost-effective generic formulations might collectively influence prescribing patterns in different clinical settings.16,17 In addition, the current study identified notable gaps in the appropriateness and documentation of PPI therapy because most patients were prescribed PPIs without a clearly stated indication. The low rate of documented PPI indications may reflect several system-level factors, including incomplete documentation in medical records, continuation of therapy initiated during previous admissions, limited medication review during transitions of care, prescribing inertia once therapy has been started, and the absence of standardized prompts requiring clinicians to document the indication and intended duration of therapy. Similarly, previous studies found that ∼30% of prescribed PPIs had no clear indication and emphasized the need for proper documentation of evidence-based indications in medical records.18,19 These findings suggest that poor documentation is not simply an administrative issue but a patient safety concern because it limits assessment of prescribing appropriateness, contributes to unnecessary long-term exposure, and reduces opportunities for timely deprescribing. Numerous patients also remained on prolonged or continuous therapy. Therefore, these findings are consistent with those obtained in several previous studies, where PPIs were frequently prescribed without appropriate indications or continued beyond recommended durations. These patterns contribute to unnecessary drug exposure and increase the risk of adverse effects and medication cascades, which exacerbate the clinical and economic burden.7,20, 21, 22 Inappropriate long-term use is particularly concerning among older adults, who are more susceptible to adverse drug effects due to age-related physiological changes, multimorbidity, and polypharmacy.10,23

Long-term PPI use has consistently been associated with adverse outcomes, such as hypomagnesemia, anemia, and hypocalcemia, which were also observed in the present study.24, 25, 26, 27 The incidence of decreased magnesium and calcium levels was relatively low in the present study, but the results indicated the need for regular monitoring, especially in high-risk populations. Moreover, the current results indicated a statistically significant reduction in serum magnesium levels following PPI therapy, with hypomagnesemia occurring among 14.1% of patients. A systematic review and meta-analysis by Cheungpasitporn et al.28 also showed that PPI use was associated with a 43% increased risk of hypomagnesemia (OR = 1.43, 95% CI = 1.08–1.88). The proposed mechanism of PPI-associated hypomagnesemia involves interference with intestinal magnesium absorption, particularly through disrupting active transcellular magnesium transport mediated by TRPM6/7 channels. Reduced intestinal magnesium uptake may lead to clinically relevant hypomagnesemia, especially in patients with prolonged exposure or additional risk factors. This interference will lead to disruption of mucosal Mg2+ entry into the small intestinal epithelium and inhibit transcellular Mg2+ absorption.29 Thus, the clinical significance of PPI-induced hypomagnesemia extends beyond laboratory abnormalities because magnesium deficiency can precipitate cardiac arrhythmias, neuromuscular irritability, secondary hypocalcemia, and hypokalemia, which might be refractory to supplementation until magnesium stores have been fully replenished. Similarly, anemia in older adults has been associated with frailty, impaired quality of life, and increased mortality. The observed decrease in hemoglobin levels affecting 21.8% of patients reflected a clinically meaningful finding with multiple potential etiologies. A recent meta-analysis by Ali30 showed that PPI users had a higher risk (OR = 2.56, 95% CI = 1.43–4.61) of developing iron deficiency anemia compared with non-users, which could be attributed to impaired iron absorption in the alkaline gastric environment created by acid suppression.31 Nevertheless, other potential contributors, such as chronic kidney disease, acute illness, nutritional deficiency, and underlying comorbidities, cannot be fully excluded in this retrospective study. The reductions in both magnesium and hemoglobin were statistically significant, but their clinical relevance should be interpreted based on the magnitude of change and the proportion of patients who developed values below the reference range. In this study, reduced magnesium and hemoglobin levels were observed in 14.1% and 21.8% of patients after PPI therapy, respectively. The average reductions were modest, but these findings suggest that a clinically relevant subgroup developed biochemical abnormalities that may warrant monitoring, particularly among older adults with multimorbidity and polypharmacy. The trend toward decreasing calcium levels warrants closer attention because of the established association between long-term PPI use and fracture risk, and the mechanism involves impaired calcium absorption due to reduced gastric acidity, which is vital for calcium dissolution and absorption. Similarly, a comprehensive meta-analysis of 11 international studies by Yu et al.32 found an increased fracture risk associated with PPI use, particularly for hip fractures (OR = 1.30, 95% CI = 1.19–1.43). Moreover, in the present study, multivariate regression analysis showed that the number of medications received was the only independent predictor of long-term PPI therapy, whereas demographic factors, such as age and gender, were not significantly associated. However, this finding should be interpreted with caution as the model exhibited suboptimal calibration. Patients with multiple comorbidities and medications will be constantly prescribed PPIs for gastroprotection due to the chronic use of NSAIDs, antiplatelets, or corticosteroids, and continuation will frequently be driven by the phenomenon of prescribing or clinical inertia.18,33 Polypharmacy will further reinforce the pattern as complex regimens increase the gastrointestinal risk and frequently lead to defensive or cascade prescribing of PPIs.34,35 Similar observations were made in a previous study, which identified high medication counts as a significant predictor of long-term or inappropriate PPI use.36 Through prescribing cascades or drug sequencing, PPIs may also be started to prevent or manage gastrointestinal adverse effects associated with other medications. Once initiated, they may be continued during subsequent admissions or outpatient follow-up without reassessment of indication or duration, thereby contributing to unnecessary long-term exposure. Older age and multimorbidity are commonly associated with long-term or inappropriate PPI use, but these variables were not statistically significant predictors in our regression model, partly due to the characteristics of the study population, which was predominantly older, male, and multimorbid. Indeed, 76.5% of patients were aged ≥60 years, so the limited age variability may have reduced the ability to detect an independent association between age and long-term PPI use. Similarly, the high burden of comorbidities across the cohort may have reduced between-patient variation, limiting the ability to identify comorbidity count as an independent predictor. A study conducted by Rodrigues et al.8 found that although inappropriate PPI use was highly prevalent among Portuguese older adults, multivariate analysis did not find any specific pattern of predictors for prolonged use. The divergence might reflect differences in healthcare settings, prescribing practices, or covariate selection, demonstrating that the determinants of long-term PPI exposure may be multifactorial and context dependent.

The findings presented above have crucial implications for clinical practice and healthcare policy in Malaysia. Implementing standardized stewardship programs and deprescribing initiatives could help guarantee that PPIs will only be prescribed when clinically indicated and for appropriate durations. In addition, mandating proper documentation and promoting regular therapy reviews might assist in reducing unnecessary long-term use. These measures would enhance patient safety by mitigating preventable adverse effects but also help optimize resource allocation within the public healthcare system, where medication expenditure constitutes a significant operational burden. Hence, strengthening PPI stewardship is a pragmatic and impactful strategy for improving the quality of care and minimizing avoidable healthcare costs in Malaysia. Furthermore, the current study had several strengths. To the best of our knowledge, this is the first multicenter Malaysian study to evaluate long-term inpatient PPI therapy alongside measurable biochemical changes. By including four public hospitals across different geographical regions, this study captured real-world prescribing patterns across various clinical settings to provide locally relevant evidence and support safer PPI prescribing, routine therapy review, and future stewardship or deprescribing initiatives in Malaysian hospitals. The sample size in the current study also fulfilled the calculated requirement, ensuring that the statistical power of the analyses was adequate. In addition, the inclusion of pre- and post-treatment laboratory data, particularly serum magnesium, calcium, and hemoglobin, provided objective biochemical evidence of potential adverse effects associated with long-term PPI therapy. By evaluating prescribing patterns and the documentation of indications, this study also provided more meaningful real-world insights that could help support targeted interventions for safer prescribing and deprescribing practices, especially among older adults with polypharmacy and comorbidities.

The present study also had several limitations. The retrospective design limited the ability to establish definitive causal relationships between PPI use and laboratory changes. The data were collected from a hospital-based sample, so potential selection bias might exist because prescribing practices in tertiary care might vary compared with those in primary care, which could limit the generalizability of the findings. The study population was predominantly older and male, which may limit the generalizability of the findings to younger patients and female patients. In addition, dietary intake was not assessed, although dietary variation may influence magnesium, calcium, and hemoglobin levels, and could have confounded the observed biochemical outcomes. The use of a threshold >12 weeks as the operational definition of long-term PPI use may affect comparability with international studies that applied different duration thresholds. Thus, future studies should consider stratifying PPI exposure duration into clinically relevant categories to allow more precise comparisons across guideline definitions and healthcare settings. The limited number of EGD procedures conducted also restricted the capability to confirm the appropriateness of PPI indications and the duration of treatment because multiple prescriptions were based on presumptive diagnoses or empirical treatment rather than endoscopic validation. Nevertheless, the results obtained in the present study provide real-world evidence of potentially inappropriate long-term PPI use and support the call for deprescribing initiatives, particularly in elderly and multimorbid patients. Future studies can focus on prospective designs to more effectively quantify clinical outcomes associated with PPI-related biochemical changes, age- and gender-stratified analyses, and dietary assessment to better contextualize the relationship between PPI exposure in Malaysia's diverse population, assess the cost effectiveness of stewardship programs, and evaluate the impacts of deprescribing interventions on safety and healthcare resource utilization.

Conclusions

This study provides crucial real-world evidence of suboptimal PPI prescribing practices in multicenter Malaysian public hospitals. Beyond safety, the overuse of PPIs led to additional clinical and economic implications. Long-term therapy contributed to unnecessary healthcare expenditure in terms of avoidable drug costs and managing associated adverse events. Practical solutions may include pharmacist-led medication reviews, implementation of deprescribing protocols, mandatory documentation of PPI indication and intended duration, EMR-based alerts for prolonged therapy, and routine reassessment of PPI necessity during ward rounds and discharge medication reconciliation. These interventions may help reduce unnecessary long-term PPI exposure, improve patient safety, and optimize medication-related healthcare costs.
