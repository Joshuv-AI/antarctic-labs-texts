Machine learning–enhanced preoperative assessment of spontaneous ventilation risk in diverse video-assisted thoracoscopic surgeries
Authors: Xiaohan Ren, Fengfa Yang, Jiayu Li, Shujun Wu, Xinyuan Zhu, Fuli Feng, Xinting Hu, Lan Lan
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractObjectivesDespite its widespread use, spontaneous ventilation can lead to intraoperative hypoxemia or hypercapnia. In this retrospective study, we developed and validated a preoperative clinical data-based model to predict intraoperative hypoxemia or hypercapnia risk during spontaneous ventilation video-assisted thoracoscopic surgery.MethodsWe enrolled patients who underwent spontaneous ventilation video-assisted thoracoscopic surgery at the National Respiratory Medicine Center of First Affiliated Hospital of Guangzhou Medical University between 2011 and 2023. Four machine learning algorithms—light gradient boosting machine, category boosting, extreme gradient boosting, and random forest—were trained and combined into a weighted ensemble to predict intraoperative hypoxemia (peripheral oxygen saturation <94%) or hypercapnia (partial pressure of end-tidal carbon dioxide >80 mm Hg).ResultsAmong 6590 included patients, 12.6% and 3.8% developed intraoperative hypoxemia and severe hypercapnia, respectively. The final risk assessment model achieved an area under the receiver operating characteristic curve of 0.72 (95% CI, 0.68-0.76) and an area under the precision-recall curve of 0.35 (95% CI, 0.29-0.42). The statistical analysis of the data and the risk interpretation from the ensemble machine learning algorithm consistently identified body mass index (23.6 [21.6-25.5] vs 22.2 [20.4-24.0] kg/m2) and age (55 [46-62] vs 50 [41-58] years) as the strongest patient-related predictors positively correlated with intraoperative hypoxemia and severe hypercapnia. Segmentectomy (22.4% vs 14.7%), lobectomy (21.9% vs 17.2%), and surgical location in the right lung (86.4% vs 73.8%) increased risk of intraoperative hypoxia and hypercapnia. Analysis of postoperative hospital stay (3.67 vs 3.49 days), chest tube drainage (1.59 vs 1.47 days), complications (88.1 vs 91%), and incidence of intensive care unit (8.2 vs 7.6%) demonstrated that intraoperative hypoxia and hypercapnia can adversely affect patient postoperative recovery (P < .05).ConclusionsThe predictive model based on preoperative clinical characteristics effectively assessed intraoperative hypoxemia and severe hypercapnia risk in diverse spontaneous ventilation video-assisted thoracoscopic surgery procedures. This model can systematically guide preoperative evaluation to select appropriate patients and improve the intraoperative safety and surgical outcomes of spontaneous ventilation video-assisted thoracoscopic surgery.Key Words: machine learning, retrospective study, spontaneous ventilation-video-assisted thoracoscopic surgery

We developed a powerful risk model for SV-VATS based on large-scale clinical data.

Central Message

The predictive model based on preoperative clinical characteristics effectively assessed intraoperative hypoxemia and severe hypercapnia risk in diverse SV-VATS procedures and classified patients into 4 different levels of risk for SV-VATS.

Perspective

Despite its widespread use, spontaneous ventilation can lead to intraoperative hypoxemia or hypercapnia. The model generated in this study can be used to systematically guide preoperative evaluation to include appropriate patients, thus improving the intraoperative safety and surgical outcomes of SV-VATS.

Although video-assisted thoracoscopic surgery (VATS) under general anesthesia with mechanical ventilation remains a standard for thoracic procedures, risks, including airway injury, operative-side atelectasis, and neuromuscular complications, still exist.1, 2, 3 To mitigate these limitations, spontaneous ventilation (SV)-VATS has emerged as an alternative approach that reduces reliance on muscle relaxants and large opioid doses while potentially lowering hospitalization costs and improving recovery outcomes.4, 5, 6, 7 However, SV-VATS introduces unique physiological challenges. For instance, maintaining spontaneous respiration in 1 lung while inducing iatrogenic pneumothorax in the contralateral lung can create pressure imbalances that risk mediastinal shift and carbon dioxide reinhalation, potentially causing intraoperative hypoxemia and hypercapnia, both direct manifestations of intraoperative complications.8, 9, 10, 11 Currently, patient selection for SV-VATS primarily relies on surgical team consensus,12,13 a resource-intensive process lacking standardized risk assessment frameworks. Thus, data-driven predictive models are needed to complement clinical expertise in preoperative decision-making to optimize SV-VATS outcomes. Although previous studies have addressed patient selection for SV-VATS using predictive models, notable limitations persist. Some models focus exclusively on non–small cell lung cancer, limiting their applicability to other thoracic procedures.14 Others primarily assess postoperative outcomes while overlooking intraoperative respiratory risks, despite their direct impact on surgical safety.15 Additionally, conventional machine learning (ML) models such as logistic regression (LR)16 and support vector machines (SVMs)17 have demonstrated suboptimal predictive performance because they cannot effectively capture complex interactions among patient-specific variables.

To address these challenges, we developed an ML-based predictive model for preoperative risk assessment in SV-VATS, designed to estimate the likelihood of intraoperative hypoxemia or severe hypercapnia. We then used SHapley Additive exPlanations (SHAP)18 to elucidate the model's decision-making process.

Material and Methods

Patient Inclusion and Exclusion Criteria

This retrospective, single-center study included patients who underwent SV-VATS between January 1, 2011, and December 31, 2023, at the National Respiratory Medicine Center of First Affiliated Hospital of Guangzhou Medical University. This study was approved by the Medical Ethics Committee of the National Center for Respiratory Medicine of the First Affiliated Hospital of Guangzhou Medical University (Approval No. ES-2024-K111-01; date of approval: August 21, 2024), which waived the requirement for informed patient consent due to the retrospective analysis. This study was conducted according to the tenets of the Declaration of Helsinki (revised in 2013).

The exclusion criteria were (1) age less than 18 years; (2) nonpulmonary procedures such as mediastinal thymectomy, tracheal tumor resection, chest wall tumor resection, or bilateral sympathectomy; (3) intraoperative emergencies including anesthetic or colloid allergy, massive hemorrhage, cardiopulmonary resuscitation, or transfer tracheal intubation; (4) severe concomitant disease of left ventricular ejection fraction less than 50%, predicted forced expiratory volume in 1 second (FEV1%) less than 50%, and severe decrease in glomerular filtration rate (15-29 mL/min); (5) patients with a history of radiotherapy or chemotherapy requiring endotracheal intubation or robotic thoracic surgery; (6) concurrent participation in other clinical trials or any other conditions deemed inappropriate for inclusion by investigators; and (7) incomplete perioperative data.

Clinical Data Collection

Anesthesia and surgical procedures are performed according to the thoracoscopic procedure under spontaneous breathing.12,19 The anesthesia induction was achieved by target controlled infusion of propofol (2-3 μg/mL), intravenous injection of sufentanil (0.1-0.3 μg/kg), and midazolam (0.05 mg/kg). After anesthesia induction, a double-lumen laryngeal mask was inserted to achieve airway isolation. Before positioning the patient laterally, a gastric tube was placed and connected to continuous suction to reduce the risk of aspiration. The maintenance was through intravenous injection of dexmedetomidine (0.3-1 μg/kg/h) and remifentanil (0.03-0.1 μg/kg/min). To minimize interference with the surgical field, the anesthesiologist modulates the patient's respiratory effort and tidal volume by adjusting the infusion rates of the sedatives propofol and dexmedetomidine, as well as the analgesic remifentanil. All patients were placed in the lateral decubitus position, and a 3- to 5-cm utility incision was created at the fifth intercostal space between the anterior and middle axillary lines, protected by a wound protector sleeve. The patients underwent intercostal nerve block with 10 mL of 1% lidocaine and 10 mL of 0.375% ropivacaine after entering the chest cavity, and performed cervical vagus nerve block under direct visualization with 5 mL of 2% lidocaine to reduce the impact of intraoperative coughing on surgical procedures. In addition, lymph node dissection was performed using a sampling technique, and the Commission on Cancer criteria were applied to ensure standardized pathological evaluation. Lymph node sampling was not a mandatory component of every wedge resection. It was typically undertaken when there was a specific indication, such as suspicious lymph nodes identified on imaging or intraoperatively, in accordance with the Commission on Cancer criteria for staging.

The dataset included various features encompassing patient characteristics such as body mass index (BMI), age, and sex; preoperative pulmonary functions including the percentage of predicted forced vital capacity (FVC%) and predicted FEV1%; comorbidities; American Society of Anesthesiologists and Revised Cardiac Risk Index levels; and surgical information such as surgical procedure, surgical site, surgical position, TNM stage, and the times of surgery and anesthesia. The dataset also included intraoperative values of the lowest pulse oxygen saturation (SpO2) and highest partial pressure of end-tidal carbon dioxide (PETCO2) during SV-VATS.

Study Outcomes

The primary clinical outcomes were intraoperative hypoxemia and severe hypercapnia. Hypoxemia was indicated by lowest SpO2 level less than 94%,20 and severe hypercapnia was defined as highest PETCO2 level greater than 80 mm Hg21 both lasting more than 5 minutes. We compared the clinical features of the positive (hypoxemia and severe hypercapnia) and negative (nonhypoxemia and severe hypercapnia) groups and developed an ML-enhanced model to predict intraoperative hypoxemia and severe hypercapnia in SV-VATS. All data were cross-checked by at least 2 clinical physicians and researchers.

Model Establishment and Evaluation

All experiments were conducted using Python version 3.10 (Python Software Foundation). This study adhered to the reporting guidelines of Transparent Reporting of a Multivariable Prediction Model for Individual Prognosis or Diagnosis. To develop a predictive model for the occurrence of intraoperative hypoxemia or severe hypercapnia, 4 advanced ML models—light gradient boosting machine (LightGBM),22 categorical boosting (CatBoost),23 extreme gradient boosting (XGBoost),24 and random forest25—were selected for their strengths in handling complex clinical data. For instance, LightGBM is efficient with large datasets, CatBoost excels with categorical features, XGBoost demonstrates robust performance and scalability, and random forest helps reduce overfitting by applying a bagging approach.22, 23, 24, 25 Given the intrinsic ability of tree-based models to capture nonlinear relationships, all preoperative variables were included without statistical prescreening. Variables related to intraoperative outcomes (eg, degree of hypoxemia/hypercapnia) were strictly excluded from the input features to prevent data leakage. The training objective was to predict the likelihood of intraoperative hypoxemia or severe hypercapnia based on patient preoperative characteristics, with the ground truth defined by the occurrence of hypoxemia or hypercapnia during surgery. Because ensemble modeling techniques can effectively reduce model variance and enhance generalizability,26 we combined these 4 base models into an ensemble model using the weighted ensemble approach. The weights for each sub-model were determined by minimizing binary cross-entropy loss in the validation set.27,28

The model's performance was assessed in the test cohort using the following metrics: area under the receiver operating characteristic curve (AUROC), area under the precision-recall curve (AUPRC), accuracy, and F1 score. Traditional LR and SVM were used as baseline models for comparison. The performance metrics for each model were compared using bootstrapping techniques and 95% CIs were computed for all evaluation metrics.

Statistical Analyses

All analyses were performed using Python 3.10, leveraging SciPy 1.14.1 and NumPy 1.26.4. Continuous variables, such as age, BMI, height, weight, FVC-predicted, FEV1-predicted, FEV1/FVC-predicted, and left ventricular ejection fraction, were assessed for normality using D'Agostino's K2 test. Because all these variables were found to be non-normally distributed, their values are presented as medians with interquartile ranges (IQRs). The Mann–Whitney U test was used to compare the distributions of continuous variables between the positive and negative groups. For comparing categorical variable distributions, chi-square tests were used.

Results

Cohorts

The study cohort was randomly partitioned in a 4:1:1 ratio into the training, validation, and test sets, consisting of 4392, 1109, and 1109 patients, respectively. Overall, 15.3% of patients experienced hypoxemia or severe hypercapnia (positive group, n = 1010), whereas 84.7% experienced no hypoxemia or severe hypercapnia (negative group, n = 5580) (Figure 1).

Model Performance

We computed various metrics, including AUROC, AUPRC, accuracy, and F1 score, to assess the effectiveness of the models in predicting intraoperative hypoxemia and hypercapnia. LR and SVM were used as baseline models for comparison. Because of the significant class imbalance in the training set, where negative samples markedly outnumbered positive ones, the models exhibited skewed probability distributions. Because the default classification threshold was 0.5, some models classified nearly all samples as negative, which may have distorted some performance metrics. To mitigate this issue, we evaluated the model performance under multiple thresholds (0.1, 0.3, and 0.5), focusing especially on threshold-sensitive metrics such as accuracy and F1 score (Table 2). Given the complexity of factors influencing surgery, predicting intraoperative complications from preoperative patient features is a challenging task, a fact reflected in our model's performance. Although the absolute scores are not exceptionally high, our risk prediction model demonstrates a significant advantage over traditional methods, and we believe it can provide a valuable reference for clinical risk assessment.

Model Performance

AUROC, Area under the receiver operating characteristic curve; AUPRC, Area under the precision-recall curve; LR, logistic regression; SVM, support vector machine.

Following model development, the ensemble model, which integrated 4 tree-based learners (LightGBM, CatBoost, XGBoost, and random forest), demonstrated superior predictive performance compared with traditional LR and SVM classifiers (Figure 1). The hyperparameter settings for each sub-model are provided in Table E1. The corresponding ensemble weights are listed in Table E2. The ensemble model achieved an AUROC of 0.723 (95% CI, 0.679-0.764), significantly higher than that for LR (0.647 [95% CI, 0.599-0.689]; P < .001) and SVM (0.556 [95% CI, 0.503-0.605]; P < .001). Likewise, in AUPRC, the ensemble model outperformed both baselines (0.353 [95% CI, 0.291-0.425] vs 0.258 [95% CI, 0.213-0.316] for LR and 0.224 [95% CI, 0.179-0.282] for SVM; P < .001 for both). The F1 score at a threshold of 0.1, which reflected the best-performing setting across all models, was 0.345 (95% CI, 0.304-0.383) for the ensemble model, again surpassing that of LR (0.287 [95% CI, 0.251-0.323]; P < .001) and SVM (0.275 [95% CI, 0.240-0.305]; P < .001). All models achieved similar accuracies at a threshold of 0.5 (0.842 [95% CI, 0.821-0.864] for the ensemble model, 0.839 [95% CI, 0.818-0.862] for LR, and 0.841 [95% CI, 0.820-0.864] for SVM) (Table 2).

Risk Stratification for Clinical Application

To facilitate clinical application, we developed a simple risk stratification system based on the model's predicted probabilities (showed in Video 1). Specifically, patients were categorized into 4 tiers: low-risk, low-intermediate risk, high-intermediate risk, and high-risk, using probability thresholds of 0.08, 0.12, and 0.2. Analysis of the test set revealed that the incidence of intraoperative hypoxemia or hypercapnia was approximately 5.79% in the low-risk group, 8.39% in the low-intermediate risk group, 18.09% in the high-intermediate risk group, and 30.77% in the high-risk group. This provides a meaningful reference for clinical use and, to some extent, validates the effectiveness of our risk prediction model. Risk Factor Analysis

To better understand the factors contributing to intraoperative hypoxemia or hypercapnia, we performed SHAP analysis to quantify the impact of individual features on model predictions. The SHAP explanations for the final risk assessment and ensemble models were shown in Figure 3, whereas the SHAP summary plots for each sub-model were presented in Figure E3. The results indicated that BMI and age, as fundamental patient characteristics, were positively correlated with surgical risk. Additionally, the contribution of BMI to the model output varied with the status of left lung surgical site, and the contribution of age varied with BMI. These findings offer valuable insights into the effects of patient characteristics on intraoperative outcomes of SV-VATS.

Figure 3, A illustrates the relationships between the feature values and corresponding SHAP values in the test set. Both BMI and age showed strong positive associations between the feature values and their SHAP contributions, consistent with the clinical observations and the input data distribution. Among surgery types, the SHAP values were higher for segmentectomy and lobectomy than for wedge resection. Regarding surgical sites, the SHAP values were higher in the right lung than in the left lung. Beyond its direct association with surgical risk, BMI also showed an interaction with left lung surgical site relative to intraoperative risk. As shown in Figure 3, B, the results of the SHAP dependency analysis demonstrated that the left lung surgical site significantly influenced the SHAP value of BMI and that this effect varied across the BMI range. Specifically, for BMI values of 22.5 kg/m2 or greater, its contribution to the predicted risk was higher in patients whose surgeries did not involve the left lung (eg, right or bilateral lung procedures) than in those with surgeries involving the left lung (median [IQR], 0.063 [0.034-0.095] vs 0.037 [0.022-0.068]; P < .001). In contrast, for BMI values of less than 22.5 kg/m2, the SHAP value distribution was reversed (median [IQR], −0.012 [−0.014 to −0.010] vs −0.009 [−0.010 to −0.007]; P < .001), which may reflect a compensatory effect of the surgical site/left lung feature on risk prediction in patients with lower BMIs. Figure 3, C illustrated the dependence of SHAP value for age on BMI. Among patients aged 50 years or more, SHAP values and BMI showed a significant positive correlation (correlation coefficient = 0.20; P < .001). In contrast, among patients aged less than 50 years, the SHAP value of age showed a modest negative correlation with BMI (correlation coefficient = −0.07; P = .10), possibly reflecting a redistribution of feature contributions, in which lower BMI-related risk is offset by slightly higher attribution to age to maintain consistent overall risk estimates.

Discussion

In this study, we applied ML techniques to establish and validate a clinical prediction model for intraoperative hypoxemia and severe hypercapnia during SV-VATS. Our 2 major findings were as follows: First, the ensemble model integrating ML basic algorithms showed potential to predict hypoxemia and severe hypercapnia during SV-VATS surgery and was superior to any single ML algorithm or traditional inclusion criteria to help decrease intraoperative safety risks in preoperative assessment. Second, the ML ensemble model and logistic regression analysis with positive condition selection identified age and BMI as basic positive correlation factors for intraoperative hypoxemia and severe hypercapnia in SV-VATS. In addition, segmentectomy, lobectomy, and surgical location on the right lung were surgical risks, which resulted in the redistribution of feature contributions with age and BMI.

A total of 15.3% of patients experienced hypoxemia or severe hypercapnia in our study. Intraoperative hypoxia and severe hypercapnia can easily lead to hemodynamic instability29 and even require surgical suspension for assisted ventilation to restore oxygenation and eliminate CO2, all of which affect the surgical progress. Our findings advance intraoperative risk prediction in SV-VATS by addressing the key limitations of prior models. Existing frameworks primarily focus on surgical approach selection or postoperative complications and lack systematic quantification of intraoperative hypoxemia and hypercapnia risks. Models such as those by Wang and colleagues and Lan and colleagues incorporate clinical parameters such as BMI and smoking history but do not account for diagnostic information and surgical site details.14,15 Based on preoperative clinical information from a diverse cohort of patients undergoing various thoracic surgeries and diagnostic categories, the ensemble model integrates predictions from 4 tree-based learners (LightGBM, CatBoost, XGBoost, and random forest), enabling more generalizable risk estimation across SV-VATS indications. This data-driven approach showed significantly improved predictive performance over traditional LR and SVM, offering a more precise tool for preoperative risk stratification and intraoperative management in SV-VATS.

Beyond methodological advancements, our study findings contribute clinical insight by applying SHAP analysis to interpret the risk assessment model. Advanced age and BMI emerged as key risk factors, with obesity amplifying age-related vulnerability, consistent with clinical observations. Overall, our approach provides a more systematic, data-driven solution for preoperative assessment, ultimately enhancing patient safety and surgical outcomes of various SV-VATS procedures. Age-related declines in pulmonary function and comorbidities such as chronic obstructive pulmonary disease increase susceptibility to respiratory events,30, 31, 32, 33 whereas higher BMI may impair diaphragmatic motion and ventilation, promoting hypoxemia and carbon dioxide retention.34, 35, 36 These effects are intensified under spontaneous ventilation.37 Many doctors practicing in obesity prone areas may not be able to personally compare the conventional ventilation (single lung isolation) and spontaneous ventilation of VATS. However, this model can compensate for this deficiency and avoid intraoperative complications of hypoxia and hypercapnia. Surgical factors also contribute. Segmentectomy and lobectomy carry higher risks than wedge resection due to greater tissue disruption, and right-sided procedures are associated with increased risk due to higher perfusion, less favorable ventilation-perfusion matching, and anatomic complexity.38 Notably, the surgical site modifies the influence of BMI, and patients with a BMI of more than 22.5 kg/m2 undergoing right or bilateral lung procedures face elevated risks. Although the location (center vs periphery) and size of the tumor are not included, the surgical site (left or right), surgical location (upper, middle, and lower lobe), TNM stage, surgical procedure, and surgical time are sufficient to reflect the complexity of the surgery. Moreover, age and BMI were significantly positively correlated with intraoperative hypoxemia and severe hypercapnia in patients aged more than 50 years undergoing SV-VATS. However, in patients aged less than 50 years, their younger age can offset the risk of intraoperative hypoxemia and severe hypercapnia caused by increased BMI. These findings highlight the importance of preoperative optimization and tailored intraoperative management in patients at high risk, because the intraoperative hypoxemia and severe hypercapnia may defer the postoperative recovery.

The incidence of intraoperative hypoxia or severe hypercapnia in patients predicted by the model to be low risk or low intermediate risk is 5.79% and 8.39%, respectively. These patients are suitable for SV-VATS, and the incidence of intraoperative hypoxia or severe hypercapnia is low and mild. As for high/intermediate-risk patients, it needs to balance the benefits of SV-VATS over the risks. For example, to avoid difficulty in postoperative extubation, patients with pulmonary dysfunction can apply SV-VATS.39 However, perioperative lung rehabilitation exercise, aerosol antiasthmatic treatment, improved surgical procedure, and more blood gas analysis should be carefully scheduled. For high-risk patients with 30.77% of intraoperative hypoxia or severe hypercapnia, SV-VATS should be carefully selected. Intraoperative long-term hypoxia and unstable hemodynamics may encounter, whereas manual control or synchronized intermittent mandatory ventilation and vasoactive drugs are required to maintain gas exchange and maintain stable hemodynamics, which may significantly affects the surgical process. For these patients, thoracoscopy with endotracheal intubation is the preferred option. The risk factors of this model were similar to those of non–SV-VATS, but the scope was more limited, such as BMI more than 22.5 kg/m2 and age more than 50 years. Moreover, lobectomy and segmentectomy were the risk factors for intraoperative hypoxia and hypercapnia.

Diffusing capacity for carbon monoxide is not a routine examination in our institution. It is only required for elderly patients, those who have smoked for more than 20 years, and those with COPD who have undergone immunotherapy. Patients with lower preoperative FEV1 combined with hypertension, diabetes, and a history of thoracic surgery had higher incidence rates of intraoperative hypoxemia (SpO2 <94%) and severe hypercapnia (PETCO2 >80 mm Hg). Whether SV-VATS should exclude patients with poor preoperative lung function and multiple comorbidities depends on the experience of the center. However, performing SV-VATS in patients with moderate-to-severe ventilatory dysfunction can help avoid postoperative mechanical ventilation and achieve early extubation.39,40

Although 15.3% of patients experienced hypoxemia and 85% of patients experienced hypercapnia, only 4% developed moderate hypoxia (SPO2 <90%) and 3.8% experienced severe hypercapnia (PETCO2 >80 mm Hg) among 6590 cases, supporting the overall safety and feasibility of SV-VATS for most thoracic patients. Moreover, a laryngeal mask airway is often used for intraoperative ventilation-assisted management to ensure improved intraoperative oxygenation and ventilation. Most patients undergoing SV-VATS have early-stage lung cancer with a tumor stage of T1N0M0, whereas patients with longer surgery and anesthesia duration may have a higher incidence of intraoperative hypoxia or hypercapnia. These results suggest that SV for early-stage lung cancer is a simple surgical procedure that does not require dissection and separation of lung tissue and can shorten the surgery and anesthesia time as well as the occurrence of intraoperative hypoxia or hypercapnia. Therefore, SV-VATS is suitable for thoracic patients with early-stage lung cancer.

Strengths and Limitations

With a large-scale dataset, we used mechanical learning to a comprehensive risk assessment model for predicting high-risk patients with spontaneous ventilation undergoing diverse thoracic surgical procedures. The model demonstrated significantly improved predictive performance compared with traditional approaches such as LR and SVM. SHAP analysis was then applied to enable interpretable evidence-based decision-making. Subsequently, an application mini program software can be developed based on this model to turn the prediction model into a practical tool, and a demonstration of the mini program operation is provided in Video 1.

This study also has certain limitations. First, data heterogeneity among centers prevented external validation and limited the ability to assess the model's generalizability across institutions. Second, although the model outperformed conventional methods, predictive accuracy could still be improved. Third, although our dataset included various surgical procedures and diagnostic categories, small sample sizes in certain subgroups (complex vs simple segments) may have restricted the model's capacity to learn. Future studies should increase the sample sizes for rare procedures and categories, incorporate additional perioperative risk variables, and conduct multicenter external validation to evaluate the generalizability of the model. Fourth, the exclusion criteria in this study, such as intraoperative emergencies (eg, allergy, hemorrhage) and a history of chemotherapy or immunotherapy, although necessary to reduce confounding factors, may limit the generalizability of our findings to patients with these complex clinical conditions. Future studies focused on external validation and potential model adaptation in populations including patients with prior cardiotoxic or pulmonotoxic therapies are warranted. Furthermore, the inclusion and exclusion criteria designed to maintain cohort homogeneity in this study, such as the exclusion of patients undergoing robotic thoracic surgery, open surgery, and the lack of subcategorization of respiratory diseases, may limit the generalizability of the findings across all minimally invasive platforms and different respiratory disease subtypes, which warrants further validation in future studies. Fifth, we have identified that a key direction for future research is to design prospective studies to further investigate the impact of factors such as the complexity of segmentectomy and adherence to the Enhanced Recovery After Surgery pathway on patient outcomes. Sixth, the fact that multiple hypoxemic or hypercapnic episodes each lasting less than 5 minutes were not considered events may fail to capture the full picture of intraoperative instability. This is because repeated 4-minute episodes could have physiological consequences that are comparable to, or even exceed, those of a single 5-minute event. Therefore, regarding this specific limitation of the event definition, we believe that future studies could analyze the cumulative impact of multiple shorter episodes.

Conclusions

The ensemble ML algorithm identified BMI and age as basic characteristics positively correlated with intraoperative hypoxia and severe hypercapnia in SV-VATS. Segmentectomy and lobectomy procedures and surgical location in the right lung were associated with surgical risk. The calibration curve showed that intraoperative hypoxia and severe hypercapnia predicted by the model fit reasonably well with the actual clinical evidence. This model can be used to systematically guide preoperative evaluation to include appropriate patients, thus improving the intraoperative safety and surgical outcomes of SV-VATS.

Video 1

This study enrolled 6590 patients who underwent spontaneous ventilation video-assisted thoracic surgery (SV-VATS) at the National Respiratory Medicine Center of First Affiliated Hospital of Guangzhou Medical University between January 1, 2011 and December 31, 2023. Four advanced machine learning algorithms—Light Gradient Boosting Machine (LightGBM), Categorical Boosting (CatBoost), Extreme Gradient Boosting (XGBoost), and Random Forest—were selected. A weighted ensemble approach was used to integrate these base models into an ensemble model. This ensemble model was developed to predict the risk of intraoperative hypoxemia (peripheral oxygen saturation [SpO2] <94%) and/or hypercapnia (partial pressure of end-tidal carbon dioxide [PETCO2] >80 mm Hg) in patients selected for SV-VATS. Subsequently, this predictive model was implemented into a mini-program (WeChat Mini-Program) designed to provide clinicians with reference guidance on the suitability of SV-VATS for individual patients. Users, such as thoracic surgeons or anesthesiologists, can access the “SV-VATS Risk Prediction” mini-program to input or select all evaluation variables included in this study. The process involves the following steps: (1) Input basic patient information: Age, height, weight, and sex. The body mass index is automatically calculated from the height and weight; (2) Input preoperative metrics: Forced vital capacity (FVC) and forced expiratory volume in 1 second (FEV1). The FEV1/FVC ratio is automatically calculated. Left ventricular ejection fraction (LVEF) is also entered; (3) Select disease diagnosis; (4) Select surgical information: Surgical procedure, surgical site, and specific location; and (5) Select supplementary patient information: Comorbidities, American Society of Anesthesiologists levels, and Revised Cardiac Risk Index. After completing all steps, users can click “Submit” to obtain the evaluation result. The mini-program then automatically computes a risk score based on the model’s algorithm and provides a corresponding risk stratification suggestion. The predictive model utilizes probability thresholds of 0.08, 0.12, and 0.2 to categorize patients into 4 tiers: low risk, intermediate low risk, intermediate high risk, and high risk. Analysis of the test set revealed the following incidence rates of intraoperative hypoxemia/hypercapnia: 5.79% in the low-risk group, 8.39% in the intermediate-low-risk group, 18.09% in the intermediate-high-risk group, and 30.77% in the high-risk group. Based on this risk stratification, our study suggests that SV-VATS is suitable for low and low-intermediate risk patients and requires benefit outweighing risk for high-intermediate risk patients but should be carefully selected for high-risk patients. After obtaining the SV-VATS risk prediction result from the mini-program, clinicians can integrate this information with the patient's overall condition and preferences to make a final decision regarding surgical approach. The development and application of this predictive program further validate the effectiveness of our model and provide practical guidance for clinical practice.
