Botulinum neurotoxins: A distinct subclass of SNARE-Cleaving endopeptidases
Authors: Sabine Pellett
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBotulinum neurotoxins (BoNTs) are bacterial protein toxins that cause the severe and potentially fatal neuroparalytic disease botulism in humans and vertebrate animals. Together with tetanus toxin (TeNT), BoNTs comprise the large family of clostridial neurotoxins (CNTs). Structurally, CNTs share conserved features: a 50 kDa zinc-dependent light chain endopeptidase with an HExxH motif linked via a disulfide bond to a 100 kDa heavy chain with distinct domains for translocation and receptor binding that mediate neuronal specificity. During the last decade, genomic analyses have uncovered bont-like genes in non-clostridial species including as Weissella oryzae, Enterococcus faecium, Paeniclostridium ghonii, Chryseobacterium piperi, Paraclostridium bifermentans, and Bacillus toyonensis. The BoNT-like proteins encoded by these genes retain SNARE-cleaving activity and share structural motifs with BoNTs, yet studies during the last decade have shown they exhibit minimal toxicity in vertebrate systems and form phylogenetically distinct clades, suggesting alternative ecological roles. Growing evidence suggests insects as the target of BoNT-like proteins. The expanding repertoire of BoNT-like proteins with conserved architecture and enzymatic function supports the classification of BoNTs as a distinct sub-family within a broader super-family of SNARE-cleaving endopeptidases. We propose the designation SNACLEs (SNARE-Cleaving-Endopeptidases) for this protein super-family, with CNTs and BoNT-like proteins as a distinct families.Keywords: Botulinum neurotoxin, BoNT, BoNT-like, SNACLE, SNARE cleaving endopeptidase, Homologs

Introduction

Botulinum neurotoxins (BoNTs), produced by several species within the anaerobic genus Clostridium, are the causative agents of botulism—a severe and long-lasting neuroparalytic disease in vertebrates [1-5]. Together with the closely related tetanus toxin (TeNT), BoNTs form the clostridial neurotoxin (CNT) family [5]. Historically, CNTs and their producing organisms were identified through their association with devastating diseases in humans and other vertebrates.

Over the past two decades, advances in genomics, molecular biology, and bioinformatics have enabled the discovery of numerous microbial species and their potential virulence factors directly from environmental samples. In the last decade, these efforts have uncovered several distantly related homologs of CNTs based on bioinformatic analyses, without any link to disease [6-16]. The nomenclature for these putative neurotoxins has been inconsistent—some homologs are designated as BoNT/followed by a species identifier, while others are given entirely new names based on the producing organism.

Recent studies on BoNT-like proteins have demonstrated enzymatic activity similar to CNTs, including cleavage of soluble N-ethylmaleimide-sensitive factor attachment protein receptor (SNARE) proteins, but with little or no neurotoxicity in vertebrates or human neuronal cell cultures ([6,11,13,17-19]. Recognizing the genomic, structural, and functional relatedness between CNTs and these more distant homologs—yet acknowledging their distinct evolutionary lineages and lack of or weak vertebrate neurotoxicity—we propose a unified and descriptive nomenclature. Specifically, we define this expanding group as a superfamily of SNARE-Cleaving Endopeptidases (SNACLEs), encompassing at least two major amilies: (i) CNTs, as highly potent vertebrate neurotoxins, and (ii) BoNT-like proteins, which exhibit SNARE-cleaving activity but minimal or no vertebrate neurotoxicity.

History of BoNT nomenclature

The term botulinum originates from the Latin word botulus, meaning “sausage,” reflecting its association with “sausage poisoning” described by Justinus Kerner following several major outbreaks of foodborne botulism linked to blood sausage consumption in the late 18th and early 19th centuries [20-22]. Although relatively rare compared to diseases such as dysentery or influenza, these early outbreaks drew considerable attention due to their severity: mortality rates approached 50%, and survivors often experienced prolonged flaccid paralysis, requiring weeks to months for recovery. Today, naturally occurring botulism is even less common, with approximately 200 cases reported annually in the United States. Advances in medical care have dramatically reduced mortality to below 5%. Nevertheless, BoNTs remain a significant concern because of their extraordinary potency, absence of a definitive cure, and relative ease of production [23]. Consequently, BoNTs are classified as Tier 1 Category A Select Agents in the United States and are considered potential bioweapons. Thus, distinguishing BoNTs from more distantly related homologs that lack comparable neurotoxicity is critical for risk recognition and regulatory oversight.

The large family of clostridial neurotoxins

TeNTs and BoNTs together comprise the family of CNTs. While few variants of subtypes of TeNT have been observed, and TeNT is produced by one species of clostridia –Clostridium tetani, BoNTs comprise seven immunologically distinct serotypes with multiple subtypes within the serotypes [9,10,24]. Morover, multiple species of clostridia produce BoNTs, including Group I-IV C. botulinum as well as a few strains of otherwise non-toxic species including C. sporogenes, C. butyricum, and C. baratii [1-3,25]. The genes encoding BoNTs are located within the bont gene cluster alongside ntnh and either ha or orfX genes, and the gene clusters are located either on the chromosome, on a large plasmid, or a phage [2,26,27]. This indicates horizontal gene transfer and diversification of bonts, which is also reflected in the diversity and large number of BoNT sero- and subtypes and the discovery of several chimeric BoNTs containing subunit combinations of different sero-or subtypes [1,26]. All BoNTs cause botulism in vertebrates including humans, although naturally occurring botulism in humans is usually caused by BoNT serotypes A, B, E, and F, whereas serotypes C and D commonly cause botulism in wildlife and birds [3,4,28]. All CNTs share similar structural elements. CNTs are classic AB-type toxins, which are two-compartment exotoxins consisting of an enzymatic A unit and a cell-binding B unit. BoNTs consist of a heavy chain (HC) that selectively recognizes and binds vertebrate neuronal cells leading to endocytosis, and a light chain (LC), which is the enzymatic portion of the toxins that cleaves neuronal SNARE proteins inside the neuronal cell cytosol. TeNT and each of the seven known BoNT serotypes target distinct SNARE substrates and cleavage sites [29]. The structure of CNTs can be described as a tripartite structure of LC, translocations domain (HCN) and receptor binding domain (HCC). While TeNT is encoded as a single gene on a large plasmid in C. tetani, BoNTs are synthesized as part of a complex with several non-toxic proteins [2,27]. The enzymatic LC domain of CNTs contains a conserved and characteristic HExxH motif, which is responsible for coordination of a zinc molecule in the LC, which is required for enzymatic function. The receptor binding domain of CNTs contains structural loops that form the binding interface for specific binding to gangliosides or synaptic vesicle proteins on the surface of vertebrate neurons [30-38]. Through this dual neuronal targeting by both HC and LC functions of CNTs, these toxins have achieved remarkable neuronal selectivity and potency, which is the hallmark of CNTs.

BoNT-like proteins

The more distantly related BoNT-like proteins share some key structural and functional properties with CNTs but have overall lower primary amino acid sequence similarity (Fig. 1). The first BoNT-like protein was discovered in 2015 by bioinformatic analysis of the genome of Weissella oryzae SG25 alongside an ntnh, and the LC was quickly shown to have catalytic activity against the neuronal SNARE protein VAMP2 [6,12]. The protein was termed BoNT/Wo to indicate its relatedness to BoNTs, but no toxicity of this BoNT-like protein in mice or rodent or human cultured neurons was observed [39]. This discovery was followed by bioinformatic identification of a BoNT homolog, BoNT/En, in the genome of Enterococcus faecium sp. 3G1_DIV0629, isolated from cattle feces in South Carolina, in 2017 and 2018 [11,40,41]. Like BoNT/Wo, the recombinant En-LC was identified as an active zinc protease capable of cleaving neuronal VAMP1, VAMP2, VAMP3, and SNAP-25, as well as non-neuronal SNAP-23 [11,40,42]. However, sortase-ligated holotoxin was not active in cultured neurons or in mice [11]. During the same time, BoNT/X was identified within the genome of a BoNT/B producing strain of C. botulinum and the LC was shown to cleave mammalian VAMP1-5 and Ykt6 with high potency, but BoNT/X holotoxin produced only mild paralysis in mouse hind limb models at concentrations as high as 1-2 μg and had weak activity in cultured human neurons and caused no mouse deaths at 50 μg doses [16,43]. This BoNT-like protein differs from other identified BoNT homologs in that it is produced by a C. botulinum strain that was isolated from an infant botulism case in Japan [16], but it is more similar to BoNT-like proteins both by structure and by functional characteristics [17]. In 2019, three potential BoNT homologs were found bioinformatically in Chryseobacterium piperi and termed CP1-3, although no functional characterizations have been conducted of these putative boNT-like proteins and their relatedness to other BoNT-like proteins remains unclear [9]. In the same year, PMP1 was identified as a further BoNT-like homolog produced by an insecticidal strain of Paraclostridium bifermentans and was found to specifically cleave syntaxin in Anopheles mosquitoes and to have insecticidal activity [13]. This finding for the first time raised the possibility that BoNT-like toxins may constitute a distinct clade to BoNTs that specifically target insect and invertebrate species. This was further strengthened by the discovery and characterization of the BoNT-likes PG1 and PG2, produced by strains NCTR 3900 and DSM15049 of Paeniclostridium ghonii and the discovery that the LCs of these proteins cleave insect but not human or rat SNAP-25 and the holotoxins had insecticidal activity [18]. The two latest additions to this growing protein family are BTNT produced by Bacillus toyonensis isolate CH177 [15], and furfuritoxin, produced by Furfurilactobacillus sp. OKN36 [14]. Neither of these BoNT-like proteins has been functionally characterized.

The superfamily of SNARE cleaving endopeptidases (SNACLEs)

Although functional characterization of BoNT-like proteins is still ongoing, phylogenetic analyses of their primary amino acid sequences reveal that these proteins form at least two distinct sub-lineages alongside clostridial neurotoxins (CNTs) within a broader superfamily of SNARE-cleaving endopeptidases (SNACLEs) (Fig. 2) [14]. This evolutionary framework underscores both the shared mechanistic feature—SNARE proteolysis—and the divergence in biological niches among these groups. Considering the exceptional significance of BoNTs as highly potent vertebrate neurotoxins, pathogens, and potential bioweapons, precise descriptive classification is critical. Accumulating evidence indicates that BoNT-like proteins exhibit little or no neurotoxicity in vertebrates or human neuronal cultures but instead may target insects or invertebrates rather than vertebrates.

We propose a clear and biologically meaningful nomenclature that distinguishes the phylogenetic major clades of the CNTs and the BoNT-like proteins within the SNACLE superfamily. Specifically, we define two major families - CNTs, encompassing the sub-families BoNTs and TeNT as highly potent vertebrate neurotoxins, and BoNT-like proteins, representing homologs with SNARE-cleaving activity but minimal or absent vertebrate neurotoxicity, likely encompassing two or more distinct clades that may have distinct biologic targets. Future research will no doubt lead to expansion of the members within this family by uncovering additional homologs, with the exciting prospect of revealing new insights into their functional roles and diverse ecological niches. By enhancing our understanding of the evolution of proteins within the SNACLE superfamily, this research will be important to inform risk assessment but also opens the doors for developments of novel therapeutics and potential applications in agriculture or biotechnology.
