Long-term outcomes of middle ear regenerative therapy using cell sheet transplantation combined with tympanoplasty
Authors: Kazuhisa Yamamoto, Tsunetaro Morino, Akira Baba, Yoshiyuki Kasai, Yuki Takizawa, Nobuki Fukuda, Shun Kikuchi, Yutaka Yamamoto, Masayuki Yamato, Hiromi Kojima
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
IntroductionRegenerative medicine using autologous mucosa-derived epithelial cell sheets has attracted increasing attention as a novel therapeutic strategy for reconstructing epithelial tissues. We have developed a new regenerative treatment for middle ear diseases, including cholesteatoma, using autologous nasal mucosal epithelial cell sheets, and conducted clinical studies demonstrating its potential to suppress postoperative recurrence. In this study, we evaluated the long-term clinical outcomes of patients treated with this approach.MethodsIn our clinical research project, six patients with acquired cholesteatoma underwent canal wall up tympanoplasty with mastoidectomy combined with autologous nasal mucosal epithelial cell sheet transplantation between 2014 and 2019. Four patients who were followed up for more than five years after transplantation were included in this long-term analysis. Postoperative outcomes were assessed based on mastoid cavity aeration on computed tomography (CT), hearing outcomes, recurrence of cholesteatoma, and transplantation-related adverse events.ResultsMean follow-up was 9.25 years (range: 6.5–11.5 years). All patients maintained successful mastoid aeration throughout the study, with mean CT areas of 216.08 ± 75.87 mm2 at 2 years and 193.80 ± 71.41 mm2 beyond 5 years postoperatively. No cholesteatoma recurrence or tympanic membrane retraction was observed. Hearing levels improved and remained stable during long-term follow-up. No serious complications, allergic reactions, or signs of malignant transformation were identified.ConclusionCanal wall up tympanoplasty with mastoidectomy combined with autologous nasal mucosal epithelial cell sheet transplantation is a safe and effective long-term regenerative treatment for middle ear diseases. This therapy contributes to sustained aeration and prevention of recurrence through regeneration of the middle ear mucosa and may represent the first regenerative medicine product for clinical application in otology.

Introduction

Acquired cholesteatoma is a chronic inflammatory disease characterized by the loss of normal middle ear mucosa and impaired gas exchange, and postoperative recurrence remains a major clinical challenge even after surgery. These unfavorable outcomes are closely associated with insufficient regeneration of the middle ear mucosa and impaired aeration of the middle ear cavity, including the mastoid cavity [1].

Adequate mastoid aeration contributes to stable ventilation of the middle ear, prevents the development of negative middle ear pressure, and reduces the risk of cholesteatoma recurrence and tympanic membrane retraction. Therefore, the presence of mastoid aeration is considered clinically important.

Among the surgical approaches for acquired cholesteatoma, canal wall up tympanoplasty with mastoidectomy is widely adopted in many institutions because it can achieve an ideal healing condition if postoperative aeration of the middle ear cavity is successfully maintained. However, previous studies have demonstrated that fewer than half of patients undergoing canal wall up tympanoplasty with mastoidectomy achieve stable postoperative aeration of the mastoid cavity, and patients who fail to obtain adequate aeration have a significantly higher risk of recurrent cholesteatoma compared with those who achieve sufficient aeration [1].

In conventional surgical procedures, including canal wall up tympanoplasty with mastoidectomy, mucosal defects remaining after the removal of cholesteatoma lesions are left to heal spontaneously. When regeneration of the middle ear mucosa is insufficient, progressive fibrotic scarring occurs, leading to loss of ventilation, disappearance of middle ear aeration, and an increased risk of postoperative recurrence [2]. Therefore, the active induction of mucosal regeneration from the early postoperative period is considered a key factor in preventing recurrence after surgery [3].

Cell sheet engineering is a tissue engineering technology that allows the harvest of contiguous cell layers with preserved intercellular junctions and extracellular matrix (ECM) using temperature-responsive culture dishes [[4], [5], [6]]. This technique enables scaffold-free transplantation of living cell sheets into host tissues. The clinical applications of cell sheet technology have been successfully reported in several fields with favorable outcomes [[7], [8], [9], [10]]. Based on this concept, we applied cell sheet engineering to the otologic field and established a novel regenerative therapy using autologous nasal mucosal epithelial cell sheets to promote regeneration of the middle ear mucosa and maintain postoperative aeration [2].

In the present study, we investigated the long-term safety and efficacy of this regenerative therapy in patients with acquired cholesteatoma who underwent nasal mucosal epithelial cell sheet transplantation and were followed up for more than five years after surgery.

Although several studies have reported the long-term outcomes and safety of cell sheet transplantation in other organ systems, such reports remain limited [11,12]. To our knowledge, nasal mucosal epithelial cell sheet transplantation represents the first clinical application involving the transplantation of cultured epithelial cells into the middle ear, and this study is the first to report long-term clinical outcomes following this novel regenerative treatment.

Materials and methods

Patients and study design

This study retrospectively analyzed data from a clinical research project conducted at Jikei University School of Medicine. In this clinical research project, six patients with acquired cholesteatoma underwent tympanoplasty combined with autologous nasal mucosal epithelial cell sheet transplantation between 2014 and 2019. Among these patients, four (two males and two females) were followed up for more than five years after transplantation and were included in the present long-term analysis. The mean age at the time of cell sheet transplantation was 42.7 years old.

In all cases, the extent of cholesteatoma was classified as stage II according to the EAONO/JOS classification system [13,14]. The postoperative follow-up period ranged from 6 years to 6 months to 11 years and 6 months, with a mean duration of 9 years and 3 months.

Preparation of nasal mucosal epithelial cell sheets

All epithelial cell sheets were produced under sterile conditions in a cell processing facility (CPF) that complied with good manufacturing practice (GMP) standards. Prior to tissue collection, written and oral informed consent was obtained from all patients. A nasal mucosal specimen measuring approximately 10 × 10 mm was harvested endoscopically from the inferior turbinate in an outpatient setting (Fig. 1A). The harvested nasal mucosa was initially subjected to primary explant culture to expand the epithelial cells (Fig. 1B). Subsequently, the expanded epithelial cells were seeded onto a temperature-responsive culture substrate to generate epithelial cell sheets (Fig. 1C). When the cells reached confluence, the culture temperature was reduced to 20 °C, allowing the intact epithelial cell layer to detach as a contiguous cell sheet. Using this method, an epithelial cell sheet with a diameter of approximately 20 mm was obtained (Fig. 1D) [3,15]. The entire fabrication process required approximately four weeks from tissue harvesting to completion of the cell sheets. In all the fabricated cell sheets, the proportion of keratin-positive cells exceeded 90% (Fig. 1E). Cell sheet transplantation

Cell sheet transplantation was performed as previously described [3]. In all cases, canal wall up tympanoplasty with mastoidectomy was performed. The transplanted cell sheets spontaneously adhered to the exposed bone surfaces without the need for additional fixation using biological adhesives or sutures. In cases of tympanic membrane adhesion, cell sheets were also transplanted onto the undersurface of the cartilage graft used for tympanic membrane reconstruction and onto the mucosal defect sites within the tympanic cavity.

All surgical procedures were performed by the same experienced otologic surgeon to ensure procedural consistency.

Outcome measures

Postoperative outcomes were evaluated based on mastoid cavity aeration assessed by computed tomography (CT) at 2 and beyond 5 years after surgery, tympanic membrane findings, hearing outcomes, recurrence of cholesteatoma, and safety parameters, including adverse events and postoperative complications. Safety was evaluated through structured long-term clinical follow-up, including periodic otoscopic examinations and serial temporal bone CT imaging specifically reviewed for any findings suggestive of neoplastic transformation. Postoperative CT images were carefully reviewed for any findings suggestive of malignant transformation, including abnormal soft-tissue masses, progressive soft-tissue density within the mastoid cavity, or bone destruction indicative of neoplastic changes at the transplantation site. Hearing levels were calculated according to the guidelines of the Committee on Hearing and Equilibrium of the American Academy of Otolaryngology-Head and Neck Surgery [16]. Postoperative hearing outcomes were evaluated using the most recent pure-tone audiogram results obtained more than 5 years after surgery.

Assessment of postoperative mastoid cavity aeration using CT

Postoperative aeration of the mastoid cavity was evaluated using a previously established imaging protocol [1]. Temporal bone CT scans were acquired under bone window settings with a slice thickness of 1 mm to determine the presence of air within the mastoid cavity after the surgery. For quantitative assessment, a region of interest (ROI) was defined on the axial CT slice in which the lateral semicircular canal was identified. The anterior boundary of the ROI was set as a horizontal line at the anterior margin of the cochlea. The posterior boundary was defined by a line drawn at an angle of 30° lateral to the posterior semicircular canal, extending to the lateral edge of the sigmoid sinus and posterior cranial fossa. The medial boundary corresponded to the lateral margin of the otic capsule, excluding the petrous apex, while the lateral boundary was defined by the inner cortical surface of the temporal bone (Supplementary Figure).

Consistent with previous reports [1], mastoid cavity aeration was considered present when the aerated area within the defined ROI exceeded 50 mm2 on the selected CT slice. All ROI measurements were performed at the Department of Radiology, Jikei University School of Medicine. The threshold of 50 mm2 was selected based on our previously established CT-based evaluation protocol, in which the cross-sectional area of the attic within the defined ROI was approximately 40 mm2. Therefore, a value exceeding this area was conservatively used to define the presence of mastoid aeration.

Furthermore, no recurrence of cholesteatoma was detected in any patient during the follow-up period of more than 5 years.

Discussion

The present study is the first to evaluate the long-term clinical outcomes of middle ear mucosal regeneration using autologous nasal mucosal epithelial cell sheet transplantation, with a postoperative follow-up ranging from more than 6 years to a maximum of 11 years. Traditionally, regeneration of the middle ear mucosa after middle ear surgery has been left to spontaneous healing; however, this process is often inconsistent and frequently results in fibrosis, adhesion formation, and impaired ventilation. The findings of this study demonstrate that cell sheet transplantation promotes regeneration of the middle ear mucosa and enables stable maintenance of aeration in the middle ear and mastoid cavity over the long term, providing clinically meaningful evidence to support the practical application of regenerative therapy in otology. This report presents the long-term outcomes of four patients with postoperative follow-up exceeding five years among a total of six patients treated within a single institutional clinical research cohort, with a specific focus on durability and safety beyond five years.

In cholesteatoma, one of the major causes of postoperative recurrence is the loss of the middle ear mucosa and its incomplete regeneration. In canal wall up tympanoplasty with mastoidectomy, while anatomical structures are preserved, postoperative aeration of the mastoid cavity is not always achieved, and insufficient aeration is known to serve as a nidus for cholesteatoma recurrence. Previous studies have reported that fewer than 40% of patients achieve postoperative mastoid cavity aeration following canal wall up tympanoplasty with mastoidectomy, and that patients without adequate aeration have a significantly higher risk of recurrence [1]. Therefore, inducing functional regeneration of the middle ear mucosa from the early postoperative period is a critical determinant of long-term surgical outcomes.

Previous studies have reported that postoperative mastoid aeration following conventional intact canal wall up tympanoplasty in patients with cholesteatoma is limited. Specifically, the aerated area of the mastoid cavity has been shown to increase only modestly, from approximately 25 mm2 in the preoperative state to approximately 35-40 mm2 one year after surgery [17]. In contrast, the present study demonstrated substantially larger aerated areas in all patients who underwent tympanoplasty combined with autologous nasal epithelial cell sheet transplantation, with a mean aerated area exceeding 200 mm2 during long-term follow-up. The CT-based evaluation method used in the present study followed the previously established imaging protocol reported in the cited study, including the definition of the region of interest (ROI) and the criteria for mastoid aeration. Although a direct comparison between these studies is not appropriate owing to differences in study design, evaluation timing, and follow-up duration, the magnitude and sustained nature of mastoid aeration observed in the present series suggest a qualitatively different postoperative course. These findings imply that epithelial cell sheet transplantation may promote not only early re-aeration but also long-term stabilization of the middle ear and mastoid cavity environment by facilitating functional mucosal regeneration instead of scar-based healing. In this context, the present results extend previous reports on postoperative aeration and support the concept that regenerative approaches targeting the middle ear mucosa can fundamentally alter postoperative aeration outcomes.

Cell sheet engineering enables the transplantation of contiguous cell layers while preserving the ECM using temperature-responsive culture substrates [[4], [5], [6]]. A key advantage of this technology is its ability to transplant living tissues without the use of scaffold materials. Clinical applications of cell sheet technology have been successfully reported in multiple fields, including corneal, esophageal, myocardial, and pulmonary regeneration [[7], [8], [9], [10]], where transplanted cell sheets function harmoniously with host tissues without eliciting foreign body reactions or excessive fibrosis. The present study extends the fundamental principles of cell sheet engineering to the middle ear, sharing a common theoretical basis with regenerative therapies in other organs. Specifically, ECM-preserved cell sheets rapidly adhere to the transplantation site, promote wound healing, and suppress excessive scar formation.

Nasal mucosal epithelial cells were selected as the donor source in this study because they are embryologically and functionally similar to the middle ear mucosa and possess ciliated epithelial characteristics, making them particularly suitable for middle ear regeneration [18,19]. Unlike skin-derived cells, the nasal mucosal epithelium is inherently adapted to a moist environment and plays a physiological role in gas exchange and clearance of foreign substances, suggesting favorable compatibility with the middle ear cavity. In addition, nasal mucosal epithelial cells can be harvested safely and minimally invasively in an outpatient setting, further supporting their clinical suitability as a donor source for middle ear mucosal regeneration.

Importantly, transplanted nasal mucosal epithelial cell sheets are not merely passive covering materials but have the potential to reconstruct the structure and function of the middle ear mucosa. In this study, aeration of the middle ear and mastoid cavity was achieved in the early postoperative period and maintained for more than six years in all cases. This finding suggests that the regenerated mucosa functions as a biologically active tissue contributing to ventilation and gas exchange rather than as a fibrotic scar.

Previous attempts to regenerate the middle ear mucosa, including the transplantation of mastoid mucosa, nasal mucosa grafts, or artificial honeycomb bone, have been reported [20,21]; however, none have demonstrated long-term functional reconstruction. One of the most critical aspects of regenerative therapy is its long-term safety. In the field of cell sheet transplantation, mid-to long-term safety and efficacy have been reported for esophageal and pulmonary applications [11,12]. Consistent with these findings, the present study observed no adverse events, including recurrence, tympanic membrane re-adhesion, chronic inflammation, or foreign body reactions, during a follow-up period of up to 11 years.

The middle ear is a confined anatomical space in which even minimal fibrosis or tissue thickening can result in ventilation impairment. The long-term preservation of mastoid cavity aeration observed in this study strongly suggests that cell sheet transplantation functions in a biologically harmonious manner in the middle ear environment. In addition, because the transplanted cell sheets are autologous, immunosuppressive therapy is unnecessary, and the risks of infection and immune rejection are extremely low.

Several mechanisms may contribute to the long-term maintenance of aeration observed in this study. First, ECM-preserved cell sheets function as immediate biological scaffolds after transplantation, rapidly covering exposed surfaces and suppressing excessive fibroblast infiltration. Second, early restoration of epithelial continuity prevents persistent exudation and inflammation. Third, the recovery of ciliary function in the regenerated mucosa may stabilize the middle ear environment and reduce the likelihood of ventilation pathway obstruction. These mechanisms collectively highlight a key feature of middle ear regenerative therapy: the restoration of functional mucosa, rather than simple tissue coverage. The results of this study indicate that cell sheet transplantation is not merely an adjunctive technique but represents a regenerative therapy capable of fundamentally improving surgical outcomes in middle ear surgery. In particular, the prevention of recurrence and long-term maintenance of aeration are outcomes that directly contribute to an improved patient quality of life.

Although direct assessments of ciliary function or gas exchange were not performed in this study, sustained long-term aeration and stable clinical findings suggest functional mucosal regeneration. Future studies incorporating direct physiological measurements will be necessary to confirm these mechanisms.

This study has several limitations. The small number of cases and absence of a control group limit the generalizability of the findings. Future studies involving multicenter collaborations and prospective comparative designs are necessary to further validate the efficacy of this therapy. Functional assessment of the regenerated mucosa remains an important topic for future investigations.

Overall, this study expands the application of cell sheet therapy to the middle ear and adds novel value to functional regeneration, specifically the long-term maintenance of aeration. These findings provide concrete clinical evidence supporting the potential of regenerative medicine in otology and establish a foundation for future therapeutic strategies targeting middle ear disease.

Conclusions

Canal wall up tympanoplasty with mastoidectomy combined with autologous nasal mucosal epithelial cell sheet transplantation enables long-term regeneration of the middle ear mucosa and achieves stable postoperative outcomes without cholesteatoma recurrence. The results of this study demonstrate that this approach is a safe and effective regenerative therapy and supports its potential clinical application as the world's first regenerative medicine product for middle ear diseases. This therapy represents a new treatment paradigm in otology and may significantly advance the future management strategies for middle ear disorders.
