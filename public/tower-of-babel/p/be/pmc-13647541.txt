Dual-targeting oral nanoparticle mucosal vaccine against brucella infection: co-targeting M cells and dendritic cells
Authors: Tingting Tian, Xinxin Qi, Juan Shi, Kaiyu Shang, Huidong Shi, Jiarui Luo, ZhengLong Chai, Chuang Li, Jianbing Ding, Yuejie Zhu, Fengbo Zhang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
This study designed and evaluated a chitosan nanoparticle-based mucosal vaccine (CS-CKS9-MEV-DCpep-NPs) targeting M cells and dendritic cells against Brucella. From four immunogenic outer membrane proteins (OMP10, Omp25, Omp31, BtpB), 41 specific epitope peptides were screened and optimized via bioinformatics and ELISPOT for high antigenicity and safety. The dual-targeting strategy uses CKS9 to enhance M cell-mediated antigen uptake and DCpep, together with CS-NPs, to activate DCs and promote their maturation. This coordinates cDC1, cDC2, and pDC subsets, inducing CD8⁺ and CD4⁺ T cell responses and strengthening Th1-biased immunity against intracellular bacteria. The vaccine activates mucosal (sIgA), humoral (IL-21, memory B cells), and cellular (Th1/CTL, central memory T cells) immunity. It significantly reduces bacterial load and tissue damage, offering comprehensive protection. Compared with conventional vaccines, this oral nanovaccine provides high safety, precise targeting, and multi-dimensional immune activation, representing a novel strategy for brucellosis control.Graphical Abstract Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at https://doi.org/10.1186/s12951-026-04802-7.

Introduction

Brucellosis, caused by the facultative intracellular Gram-negative bacterium Brucella spp., is a major global zoonotic disease with severe impacts on public health and the economy [1, 2]. In humans, the disease causes over 500,000 new cases annually worldwide, manifesting as non-specific influenza-like symptoms such as recurrent fever, fatigue, arthralgia, night sweats, and hepatosplenomegaly. Without treatment, it can lead to serious complications, including endocarditis, neurobrucellosis, osteoarticular infections, and chronic disability [3–5]. In animals, brucellosis causes abortion, infertility, and decreased milk yield, resulting in huge economic losses [6]. Transmission routes include direct contact with infected animals, consumption of contaminated products, or inhalation of aerosols [2]. The high infectivity of Brucella and its potential as a biological weapon underscore the urgent need for effective control measures.

Despite this pressing need, no vaccine is currently approved for human use. Existing veterinary live attenuated vaccines (e.g., Rev 1, RB51) have limitations such as residual virulence, zoonotic risk, and interference with serological diagnosis [7–9]. Therefore, innovative vaccine strategies are urgently needed. Given that natural Brucella infection primarily invades the host through mucosal routes (e.g., digestive and respiratory tracts) [10], mucosal vaccination represents a highly promising control strategy [11–13].This approach induces immune responses at the first line of defense against pathogen invasion, the mucosal surface, while also eliciting systemic immunity, thus closely matching the infection pattern of the pathogen [14–16].

A key challenge in mucosal vaccine development is achieving efficient antigen delivery. Nanoparticle technology offers a powerful tool to address this issue: it can protect antigens from enzymatic degradation, enhance mucosal penetration, and enable targeted delivery to immune cells [17–19]. Among these, chitosan nanoparticles (CS-NPs) are particularly attractive as carriers due to their biocompatibility, mucoadhesive properties, and intrinsic adjuvant activity, especially in promoting protective Th1-type immune responses [20–23]. Although preliminary studies have shown that Brucella antigen-loaded CS-NPs induce stronger immune responses than soluble antigens [22], their targeting and immunogenicity still need further improvement [24].

To enhance delivery efficiency while ensuring safety, recombinant peptide vaccines offer an alternative superior to live vaccines. They avoid residual virulence and diagnostic interference [25–27] and allow precise screening and combination of antigenic epitopes, thereby directing immune responses against protective antigens [28]. On this basis, effective mucosal immunity also requires precise antigen delivery to two key types of antigen-presenting cells: Microfold (M) cells, responsible for antigen transcytosis, and dendritic cells (DCs), responsible for T-cell activation [29–31]. Studies indicate that single targeting strategies are often insufficient to fully realize the potential of mucosal immunity; instead, a dual-targeting strategy that simultaneously targets M cells and DCs can synergistically enhance antigen capture, presentation, and subsequent immune activation.

Based on these concepts, we systematically screened 13 outer membrane proteins of Brucella melitensis in previous work and identified four immunogenic candidates (OMP10, Omp25, Omp31, BtpB) [32]. We then predicted T-cell and B-cell epitopes and selected 41 optimal epitopes to construct a multi-epitope vaccine (MEV). To further enhance its mucosal immunogenicity, we designed a novel vaccine delivery system: the multi-epitope vaccine was loaded onto CS-NPs, with the M-cell-targeting peptide CKS9 (CKSTHPLSC) [33] fused to the N-terminus and the DC-targeting peptide DCpep (FYPSYHSTPQRP) [34] fused to the C-terminus, thereby achieving dual active targeting of the nanoparticles. We hypothesize that this strategy will significantly enhance antigen uptake and presentation efficiency, induce robust mucosal and systemic Th1-biased immune responses, and thus provide superior protection against Brucella infection.

Materials and methods

Epitope screening

Peptide library construction

Based on previously identified dominant T-cell and B-cell epitopes [32], a peptide library comprising 41 candidate epitopes was constructed, including cytotoxic T lymphocyte (CTL) epitopes, helper T lymphocyte (HTL) epitopes, linear B-cell epitopes (LBE), and conformational B-cell epitopes (CBE). All peptides were synthesized via solid-phase synthesis by Xinjiang Youkang Biotechnology Co., Ltd., with purity > 95% as verified by HPLC and mass spectrometry. The library was stored as a lyophilized powder at -20 °C, protected from light, and reconstituted to 1 mg/mL in sterile PBS before use; repeated freeze-thaw cycles were avoided.

Functional epitope screening

Enzyme‑linked immunospot (ELISPOT) assays were performed. Heparin‑anticoagulated venous blood was collected from healthy donors using BD Vacutainer® tubes (362,788) and processed within 6 h. Human peripheral blood mononuclear cells (PBMCs) were isolated by density gradient centrifugation with Ficoll‑Paque™ PLUS (1.077 g/mL, Cytiva 17-1440-02) at 400 × g for 30 min with minimal braking. Viability was assessed by trypan blue exclusion (Gibco 15,250,061), and PBMCs were resuspended at 1 × 10⁶ cells/mL in RPMI‑1640 (Gibco 31,800,022) supplemented with 10% heat‑inactivated fetal bovine serum (HyClone H30072.03) and 1% penicillin‑streptomycin (Gibco 15,140,122).

For antigen‑specific stimulation, 41 HLA‑restricted epitope peptides (purity > 95%; Youkang Biotechnology) were dissolved in DMSO (Sigma D2650). PBMCs (2 × 105 cells/well, triplicate) were incubated with 10 μg/well of each peptide (final DMSO concentration < 0.1%) or with controls (positive control: 5 μg/mL PHA, Sigma L8902; solvent control: 0.1% DMSO; unstimulated control).

ELISPOT plates were coated overnight at 4 °C with capture antibodies: human IFN‑γ (5 μg/mL, Mabtech 3420‑3‑1000), human IL‑2 (2 μg/mL, Mabtech 3444‑3‑1000), human IgG (5 μg/mL, SouthernBiotech 1031‑01), mouse IFN‑γ (5 μg/mL, Mabtech ZMT‑3321‑3‑1000), mouse IL‑2 (2 μg/mL, Mabtech 3441‑3‑1000), or mouse IgG (5 μg/mL, Mabtech 3825‑2A). After blocking, cells were stimulated for different durations (IFN‑γ: 24 h; IL‑2: 48 h; IgG: 72 h). Biotinylated detection antibodies were then added: human (2 μg/mL, Mabtech 3420‑6‑250) or mouse (2 μg/mL, SouthernBiotech 1030‑06), followed by streptavidin‑HRP (Thermo 21,126, 1:1000). Spots were developed with TMB (Mabtech 3320‑9) for 10 min and counted using an ImmunoSpot® S6 Ultra analyzer. Positive spots were defined as those with a diameter > 50 μm and an optical density exceeding the mean background ± 3 standard deviations; a 500‑μm marginal zone was excluded from analysis.

Vaccine design

Based on the dominant epitopes identified by ELISPOT screening, a vaccine construct designated CKS9-MEV-DCpep was designed using a linker strategy: B-cell epitopes were linked with KK linkers, HTL epitopes with GPGPG linkers, and CTL epitopes with AAY linkers. To enhance targeting and immunogenicity, the M-cell-targeting peptide CKS9 (CKSTHPLSC) was fused to the N-terminus and the DC-targeting peptide DCpep (FYPSYHSTPQRP) to the C-terminus via a flexible linker (GGGSGGGS), successfully generating a dual-targeting vaccine.

Bioinformatics design and analysis

The molecular weight, theoretical isoelectric point, amino acid and atom composition, instability index, aliphatic index, and grand average of hydropathicity (GRAVY) of the target protein were evaluated using ProtParam 2.3.1 (http://web.expasy.org/protparam/), providing parameters for subsequent analyses [35]. Antigenicity of the vaccine components was predicted with VaxiJen 2.0 (http://www.ddg-pharmfac.net/vaxijen/VaxiJen/VaxiJen.html; bacterial antigen threshold 0.4) [36], allergenicity was screened using AllerTOP v3.0 (https://www.ddg-pharmfac.net/AllerTOP/) to exclude allergenic risks [36], and solubility of the vaccine protein in the E. coli expression system was predicted using the Protein-SOL platform (https://protein-sol.manchester.ac.uk) [27]. Immune response simulations were performed with C-ImmSim v10.3.0 (github.com/C-ImmSim), with parameters based on HLA haplotype frequencies in the Xinjiang region (HLA-A11:01 13.46%, HLA-A02:01 12.50%, HLA-A03:01 10.10%); the immunization schedule consisted of three doses at weeks 0, 2, and 4 using 1,000 host equivalent units, and simulations focused on germinal center reactions, evaluating vaccine immune responses within an 85% confidence interval [37]. The SOPMA (https://npsa.lyon.inserm.fr/cgi-bin/npsa_automat.pl?page=/NPSA/npsa_sopma.html/) was used to predict the secondary structure [36], and initial tertiary structure models were constructed with Robetta (robetta.bakerlab.org) [27]. Subsequently, interactions between the vaccine and Toll-like receptors 2, 4, and 9 were assessed using HDOCK v2.0 (http://hdock.phys.hust.edu.cn/) to predict binding modes and key residues. Finally, based on the docking results, classical molecular dynamics simulations were performed using GROMACS 2023.5 to validate the binding stability of the CKS9-MEV-DCpep vaccine protein with TLR-2, TLR-4, and TLR-9.

Expression and purification of recombinant proteins in Escherichia coli

Codon‑optimized sequences of MEV and CKS9‑MEV‑DCpep were cloned into the pET‑19b vector (Xinjiang Youkang Biotechnology Co., Ltd.). The resulting plasmids (50 ng/μL) were transformed into Escherichia (E.) coli BL21 pLysS competent cells (Sangon Biotech, C03012) by heat shock. Transformants were plated on LB agar (BD 244520) supplemented with ampicillin (100 μg/mL, Sangon Biotech A100487) and chloramphenicol (0.1% w/v, Sangon Biotech C100723) and incubated at 37 °C for 18 h. After verification by colony PCR (Thermo EP0701), protein expression was induced by adding 0.5 mM isopropyl β-D-1-thiogalactopyranoside (IPTG; Sigma I6758) when the OD₆₀₀ reached 0.6-0.8, followed by overnight incubation at 18 °C. Cells were harvested, resuspended in lysis buffer (20 mM Tris‑HCl pH 8.0, Sigma T6066; 300 mM NaCl, Absin 47,052,148; 10 mM imidazole, Absin I5513) supplemented with protease inhibitors (Solarbio P8340), and disrupted by sonication. The lysate was clarified by centrifugation at 12,000 × g for 30 min. The supernatant was subjected to Ni‑NTA affinity chromatography (Cytiva 17,524,802), washed with imidazole, and eluted with a linear gradient of 50-300 mM imidazole. The target protein eluted at approximately 200 mM imidazole, desalted, and stored at -80 °C.

Validation of the recombinant protein by western blot analysis

Protein concentrations were determined using a bicinchoninic acid (BCA) protein assay kit (Solarbio PC0001). Samples were separated by SDS‑PAGE (12% gel, Bio‑Rad 4,568,093) and then electrotransferred (50 V, 60 min) onto PVDF membranes (Merck Millipore IPVH00010). The membranes were blocked with 5% non‑fat dry milk (BD 232100) and subsequently incubated with a rabbit anti‑His tag primary antibody (STARTER S0B0006, 1:1000) overnight at 4 °C, followed by incubation with an HRP‑conjugated goat anti‑rabbit secondary antibody (ORIGENE ZB‑2301, 1:5000) at room temperature. After washing with TBST, signals were developed using enhanced chemiluminescence substrate (Cytiva RPN2106), and images were captured with an imaging system (Amersham Imager 600).

Preliminary specificity evaluation of the recombinant protein

Clinical sample selection and detection

A total of 204 patients with brucellosis (131 males, 73 females; age range 18-65 years, mean ± SD 48.3 ± 16.7 years) and 108 healthy controls (67 males, 41 females; 45.2 ± 13.8 years) were enrolled. All patients were confirmed by standard tube agglutination test (SAT), while healthy controls were verified as negative by both Rose Bengal plate agglutination test (RBPT) and SAT. Venous blood was centrifuged at 3,000 × g for 10 min to separate serum, which was stored at -80 °C. The study was approved by the ethics committees (approval numbers K202409-16, KY2023060171), and all participants provided written informed consent. For RBPT screening, 30 μL each of test serum, positive control, and negative control were placed on a clean glass slide, mixed with an equal volume of RBPT antigen to form a uniform film, and gently rocked at room temperature; results were read within 4 min. A clear agglutination (≥ 50%) was considered positive, while no agglutination was considered negative. Positive or suspicious samples were further confirmed by SAT. For SAT, serum samples were inactivated at 56 °C for 30 min, serially diluted twofold with normal saline, mixed with an equal volume of standard Brucella agglutination antigen (Qingdao Yibang Q8906), and incubated at 37 °C for 18-24 h. The agglutination titer was defined as the highest serum dilution showing “ + + ” agglutination (≥ 50% agglutination).

Preliminary specificity screening of the recombinant protein

The specificity of the recombinant protein CKS9-MEV-DCpep was preliminarily assessed by indirect ELISA. The optimal coating concentration was determined as 5 μg/mL by checkerboard titration. Briefly, 96-well plates (Corning 3590) were coated with either CKS9-MEV-DCpep or MEV protein (5 μg/mL) overnight at 4 °C. The plates were blocked with 5% non-fat dry milk. Serum samples were serially diluted twofold and incubated for 2 h at 25 °C, followed by addition of HRP-conjugated rabbit anti-human IgG (Elabscience E-AB-1006, 1:3000). Color was developed with TMB for 10 min at 37 °C in the dark, and the reaction was stopped with 2 M H₂SO₄. Absorbance was measured at 450 nm (reference wavelength 630 nm). The endpoint titer was defined as the highest serum dilution yielding an OD₄₅₀ ≥ 0.5.

Preparation and characterization of CS-NPs

Preparation of CS-NPs

Three types of CS-NPs were prepared using an ion gelation technique: blank CS-NPs, CS-MEV-NPs, and CS-CKS9-MEV-DCpep-NPs. Chitosan (1 mg/mL, Hengxing) was dissolved in 1% acetic acid, adjusted to pH 4.3, and then mixed with MEV or CKS9-MEV-DCpep protein solution (1 mg/mL) at a volume ratio of 4:1. An equal volume (1.25 mL) of sodium tripolyphosphate (1 mg/mL, Hengxing) was added dropwise under ice-bath conditions, followed by probe sonication for 1 h. The reaction mixture was centrifuged at 10,000 × g for 30 min, and the pellet was washed three times with PBS and then resuspended.

Characterization of nanovaccines

The average particle size, polydispersity index (PDI), and zeta potential were measured by dynamic light scattering (Malvern Zetasizer ZS90) at 25 °C. Morphology was observed using transmission electron microscopy (JEOL JEM-F200, Japan).

Encapsulation efficiency (EE%) was determined by BCA assay. A standard curve was established using a BCA kit (Solarbio, PC0001). After three centrifugation washes (10,000 × g, 30 min each) of the CS-NPs, the combined supernatants containing free MEV or CKS9‑MEV‑DCpep protein were quantified. EE% was calculated as follows: Encapsulation efficiency(EE) = [(Total Protein Amount - Free Protein Amount) / Total Protein Amount] × 100%; Antigen Loading Efficiency = [Mass of encapsulated protein vaccine / (Mass of chitosan carrier + Mass of encapsulated protein vaccine)] × 100%. Subsequently, the CS-NPs were resuspended in PBS to a final concentration of 350 μg/mL for oral immunization, optimizing delivery for subsequent biological evaluation.

Mucus penetration assay

Mucus penetration of CS‑NPs was evaluated using a Transwell system (Corning 353,493). The donor compartment was loaded with 100 μL of porcine mucus (Sigma M1778), and the receiver compartment with 800 μL of PBS (pH 7.4). The system was shaken at 37 °C for 30 min to form a mucus layer. Subsequently, 100 μL of fluorescently labeled NPs (FITC‑CS‑NPs, FITC‑CS-MEV-NPs, or FITC‑CS‑CKS9‑MEV‑DCpep‑NPs; 100 μg/mL; n = 3) was added to the donor compartment and incubated at 37 °C. At 0.5, 1, 2, 4, and 6 h, 200 μL aliquots were withdrawn from the receiver compartment and replaced with an equal volume of pre‑warmed PBS. Fluorescence intensity (Ex/Em 488/525 nm) was measured, and the penetrated FITC concentration was calculated from a standard curve. The apparent permeability coefficient (Papp) was calculated as: Papp = (dQ/dt) / (A × C₀), where dQ/dt is the flux to the receptor chamber, C₀ is the initial concentration in the donor compartment, and A is the membrane’s effective surface area.

In vitro simulated gastrointestinal release assay

The in vitro release behavior of the nanoparticles was evaluated using a sequential simulated release method. The nanoparticles (protein concentration 500 μg/mL) were successively incubated in simulated gastric fluid (SGF, pH 1.2, containing 0.32% pepsin, Sinopharm M0020) and simulated intestinal fluid (SIF, pH 6.8, containing 1% pancreatin, Aladdin P110505) at 37 °C with shaking at 100 rpm. All release media were supplemented with 0.02% (v/v) Tween‑80. At designated time points (0.5, 1, 2 h for the gastric phase; and 4, 6, 8, 12, 24, 48, 72 h for the intestinal phase), the samples were centrifuged (12,000 × g, 10 min), the entire supernatant was collected for protein quantification, and the pellet was resuspended in an equal volume of fresh PBS. The protein concentration in the supernatant was determined by the BCA method, and the cumulative release rate was calculated.

Animal immunization

Female BALB/c mice (SPF grade, 6-8 weeks old, body weight 18-22 g) were purchased from Xinjiang Medical University and housed under standard SPF conditions. All experimental procedures were approved by the Animal Care and Use Committees of the First Affiliated Hospital of Xinjiang Medical University and the People’s Hospital of Xinjiang Uygur Autonomous Region (approval numbers K202409-16 and KY2023060171).

At weeks 0, 2, and 4, female BALB/c mice (n = 6 per group) were immunized orally with 200 μL of the designated formulations. The four experimental groups were: PBS control group, phosphate buffered saline; CS-NPs group, blank CS-NPs; CS-MEV-NPs group, MEV-loaded CS-NPs (70 μg MEV per dose); and CS-CKS9-MEV-DCpep-NPs group, CS-CKS9-MEV-DCpep-conjugated CS-NPs (70 μg CKS9-MEV-DCpep per dose). All were endotoxin-depleted to a final concentration of < 10 EU/mL.

Animal sample collection

Blood samples were collected from BALB/c mice by retro-orbital venous plexus puncture under isoflurane anesthesia. After clotting at room temperature for 30 min, serum was separated by centrifugation at 2,000 × g for 15 min. Bronchoalveolar lavage was performed by lavaging the lungs three times with 1 mL of sterile PBS; the lavage fluids were pooled and centrifuged at 800 × g for 10 min at 4 °C. For intestinal lavage, the small intestinal lumen was flushed with 5 mL of sterile PBS, the effluent was collected, and centrifuged at 3,000 × g for 15 min. Vaginal/nasal lavage was collected with 1 mL of PBS, centrifuged at 2,000 × g for 10 min, and filtered through a 0.22 μm filter (Merck Millipore UFC30GV0PT). All samples were aliquoted and stored at -80 °C.

M‑cell targeting efficiency analysis

Intestinal transport of nanovaccines

Closed ileal loop model: Under sterile conditions, a midline laparotomy was performed to expose the distal ileum of BALB/c mice (Sect. "Animal immunization"), and closed ileal loops were constructed. Candidate vaccines (70 μg/mouse) or an equal volume of PBS (Servicebio G4202‑1L) were injected into the intestinal lumen through the intestinal wall. The tissues were maintained at 4 °C throughout the procedure. At 1 h post‑operation, the ileal loops were harvested and immersed in pre‑cooled 4% paraformaldehyde (Biosharp BL539A) for fixation at 4 °C for 4-6 h.

Tissue processing and sectioning: The tissues were dehydrated through a graded ethanol series, cleared with xylene, and embedded in paraffin (Leica 39,602,004). Sections of 4 μm thickness were cut using a microtome (Leica RM2235), deparaffinized, and processed for staining.

Immunohistochemistry: Sections were subjected to heat‑induced antigen retrieval in 10 mM citrate buffer (Sigma C7254‑500G) at 95 °C for 25 min, followed by blocking with 5% normal goat serum (ORIGENE ZSJQ‑100). The sections were incubated with anti‑His tag antibody (1:50) overnight at 4 °C in a humidified chamber, then with HRP‑conjugated goat anti‑rabbit IgG (ORIGENE ZB‑2301) for 1 h at room temperature in the dark. Color was developed using DAB (ORIGENE ZLI‑9018), and images were captured with a light microscope (Nikon Eclipse Ni‑E).

Immunofluorescence staining of tissue sections

Tissue sections from the closed ileal loop model were subjected to antigen retrieval and blocking, then incubated overnight at 4 °C with the primary antibody (rabbit anti‑MEV/CKS9‑MEV-DCpep polyclonal serum, 1:50). This antibody was generated by immunizing New Zealand white rabbits with MEV and CKS9‑MEV-DCpep antigens (Sino Biological) via subcutaneous injection (antigen concentration 500 μg/mL; first immunization with complete Freund’s adjuvant, booster injections on days 14 and 28 with incomplete Freund’s adjuvant, 100 μg per rabbit). After the ELISA titer reached ≥ 1:10,000, blood was collected, and the antibody was verified by Western blot showing bands at 42/46 kDa. After washing with PBS, sections were incubated with HRP‑conjugated goat anti‑rabbit IgG (ORIGENE ZB‑2301, 1:200) for 1 h at room temperature in the dark, and the vaccine was labeled using TSA® 700 (Absin abs50010). To eliminate epitope masking, heat‑induced antigen retrieval was repeated (95 °C, 10 mM citrate buffer, 25 min) followed by blocking with 5% normal goat serum. Subsequently, sections were incubated with rabbit anti‑mouse UEA‑1 antibody (Abcam ab24874, 1:200) for 1 h at room temperature, then with HRP‑conjugated goat anti‑rabbit IgG (1:200) for 1 h in the dark, followed by TSA® 620 dye (1:200) for 10 min. Nuclei were counterstained with DAPI for 5 min, and sections were mounted. Imaging revealed triple co‑localization of the vaccine (pink), M cells (green), and nuclei (blue).

Flow cytometric analysis

Samples were collected at 1 h and 7 d post-immunization. Mice were euthanized, and the spleen, lymph nodes (placed in 4 mL ice-cold PBS in a centrifuge tube, Corning 352,096), peripheral blood (sodium heparin anticoagulation tube, BD 366480), and intestinal tissues (flushed with PBS and cut into 1.5-2 cm segments) were harvested to prepare single-cell suspensions.

For spleen and lymph nodes, tissues were gently ground in PBS containing 10% FBS (Gibco 10,437,028) using a 70 μm cell strainer (Corning 352,350), filtered, and centrifuged at 500 × g for 5 min. Cells were resuspended in RPMI 1640 supplemented with 10% FBS, and viable cell density was determined by trypan blue (Sigma T8154) staining.

For intestinal lamina propria cells, intestinal segments were vortexed with PBS (30 s × 2), then sequentially incubated in dissociation solution 1 (containing DTT and HEPES) and dissociation solution 2 (containing EDTA and HEPES) at 37 °C with shaking at 250 rpm for 15 min, each repeated twice. Tissues were minced into pieces < 0.5 cm and digested in digestion buffer (RPMI 1640 + 10% FBS + 7 mg type IV collagenase + 7.5 μL DNase I) at 37 °C in 5% CO₂ for 50 min. After filtration and centrifugation, cells were subjected to 40%/80% Percoll (Cytiva 17‑0891‑01) density gradient centrifugation (1000 × g, 20 min), and the interphase layer was collected.

To evaluate intracellular localization of the vaccine, analysis was performed at 1 h post‑immunization. Before staining, Fc receptors were blocked with anti-CD16/CD32 (BD 553142). After surface marker staining, cells were fixed and permeabilized using Cytofix/Cytoperm (BD 554714), followed by intracellular staining. Dendritic cell targeting analysis

Immunofluorescence staining

Bone marrow‑derived dendritic cells (BMDCs) were seeded at 2 × 105 cells/well in 24‑well plates containing poly‑L‑lysine‑coated coverslips (Sigma P4707) and cultured overnight. Cells were then stimulated with 5 µg/mL of either CKS9‑MEV‑DCpep or MEV recombinant protein for 2, 4, 8, or 12 h. After stimulation, fixation was performed as follows: for preservation of surface antigens, cells were fixed with 4% paraformaldehyde (Sigma P6148) at 25 °C for 15 min; for detection of intracellular antigens, cells were fixed with pre‑cooled methanol (-20 °C) for 5 min. Samples for intracellular staining were permeabilized with 0.1% Triton X‑100 (Sigma T8787) at room temperature for 10 min. All samples were blocked with 5% BSA (Solarbio A8020) for 1 h and then incubated with rabbit anti‑His tag primary antibody (STARTER S0B0006, 1:200) at 37 °C for 1 h. Under dark conditions, FITC‑conjugated goat anti‑rabbit secondary antibody (Absin abs90007, 1:200) and the membrane dye DiI (Thermo Fisher C10608, 1:1000) were added and incubated for 50 min. Images were acquired using a confocal microscope (Nikon A1R, 60 × oil immersion objective, NA = 1.40) with Z‑stack intervals of 0.5 µm, and preprocessed with NIS‑Elements AR 5.21.

Flow cytometric analysis

BMDCs were stimulated with CKS9‑MEV‑DCpep or MEV recombinant protein for 8 h. Surface markers of mature dendritic cells, cDC1, cDC2, and pDC were then analyzed. ELISPOT

BMDCs, T cells (Yaji LM-1355), and B cells (Yaji YS2357C) were co-cultured at a ratio of 1:10:10 (i.e., 1 × 104: 1 × 105: 1 × 105) in a final volume of 200 µL. IFN-γ (2 μg/mL, Mabtech 3441–3-1000), IL-2 (5 μg/mL, Mabtech ZMT-3321–3-1000), and IgG (5 μg/mL, Mabtech 3825-2A) were detected following the same procedures as described in Sect. "Functional epitope screening".

Assessment of humoral immune responses

Indirect ELISA

Serum levels of IgG, IgG1, IgG2a, and IgM were determined by indirect ELISA as described in Sect. "Preliminary specificity screening of the recombinant protein". The HRP-conjugated secondary antibodies and their dilution ratios were: goat anti‑mouse IgG (BioLegend 405,306, 1:1000), IgG2a (SouthernBiotech 1081–05, 1:6000), IgG1 (SouthernBiotech 1071-05, 1:8000), and IgM (SouthernBiotech 1020-05, 1:10,000). The remaining steps were the same as in Sect. "Preliminary specificity screening of the recombinant protein".

Direct ELISA

Mouse serum IL-21 (Absin abs552954) and IL-12 (Absin abs552706), as well as sIgA in serum and lavage fluids (CUSABIO CSB-E08375M), were detected using corresponding commercial ELISA kits according to the manufacturers’ instructions.

Flow cytometric analysis

Assessment of protective immunity in animals

Challenge experiment

At two weeks after the final immunization, mice in each group were challenged orally with Brucella melitensis smooth strain 16 M (ATCC 23456) at a dose of 1010CFU. Mice were euthanized at 2 weeks (acute phase) and 8 weeks (chronic phase) post‑challenge. The liver, spleen, lungs, and lymph nodes were aseptically collected. The liver and spleen were weighed; the liver and lung tissues were fixed in paraformaldehyde for subsequent hematoxylin and eosin (H&E) staining and immunohistochemistry; the liver, spleen, lungs, and lymph nodes were separately homogenized for bacterial colony counting.

H&E staining

Sections were immersed in hematoxylin solution (Changsha SunHong, CAS: 517-28-2) for 2 min, differentiated in 1% hydrochloric acid in ethanol (Takara T9181) for 1 s, rinsed with PBS for blueing, and counterstained with eosin solution (ZK-Bioscience ZK-0109) for 30 s.

Immunohistochemistry was performed as described in Sect. Immunofluorescence staining of tissue sections using a rabbit anti-Brucella polyclonal antibody (Bioss bs-2229R, 1:200 dilution).

Colony-forming unit determination

Liver, spleen, lung, and lymph node tissues were individually homogenized in sterile PBS to prepare 10% (w/v) homogenates, followed by five‑fold serial dilutions. An equal volume of each dilution was evenly spread onto 5% sheep blood agar plates (BD 221780) and incubated at 37 °C in 5% CO₂ for 72 h. Colonies were counted using an automatic colony counter, and results were expressed as log₁₀ CFU/g ± SEM.

Statistical analysis

Data are presented as mean ± SD from at least three independent experiments. Normality was assessed using the Shapiro‑Wilk test, and homogeneity of variances was assessed using Levene’s test. For parametric data satisfying normality and homoscedasticity, multiple comparisons among groups were performed by one‑way ANOVA followed by Tukey’s post hoc test. For nonparametric data, the Kruskal‑Wallis test followed by Dunn’s post hoc test was used. Correlation analysis was performed using Pearson or Spearman test as appropriate. Statistical significance was set at P < 0.05. Significance levels in figures are indicated as *P < 0.05, **P < 0.01, ***P < 0.001; ns, not significant. Functional epitope screening

Based on previous findings [32], systematic epitope screening was performed using ELISPOT assays. For T‑cell epitopes, seven CTL epitopes (S113‑R122, T135‑K144, S116‑K125, R139‑L148, H230‑K239, K24‑R33, F65‑M74) were identified by IFN‑γ secretion analysis, showing strong IFN‑γ‑inducing capacity (Fig. 1A and D). Seven HTL epitopes (E84‑V98, A80‑E94, E116‑V130, K154‑K168, K199‑F213, G89‑E103, P27‑Q41) were identified based on IL‑2 levels, exhibiting markedly elevated IL‑2 secretion (Fig. 1B and E). Design and bioinformatics analysis of the dual-targeting peptide CKS9-MEV-DCpep vaccine

Based on the 21 screened epitopes (including 7 CTLs, 7 HTLs, 6 LBEs, and 1 CBE), the MEV and CKS9‑MEV‑DCpep vaccines were designed and constructed (information on the 21 epitopes is provided in Supplementary Table 2). During structural assembly, distinct linkers were employed to optimize antigen processing and presentation. The AAY linker was used to connect CTL epitopes [39], as it can be efficiently cleaved by the proteasome, thereby facilitating antigen presentation via the MHC‑I pathway [40]. The GPGPG linker was adopted to join HTL epitopes [39], which aids in the separation of HTL epitopes within endosomes and thus promotes MHC‑II pathway processing [41]. The KK linker was utilized for linking B‑cell epitopes (LBEs and CBE) [42]; its dibasic amino acid properties help enhance endosomal escape and cross‑presentation potential of the antigen [43]. To improve targeted delivery, the M‑cell‑specific targeting peptide CKS9 (sequence: CKSTHPLSC) was fused to the N‑terminus via a flexible linker (GGGSGGGS). Simultaneously, the DC‑targeting peptide DCpep (sequence: FYPSYHSTPQRP) was conjugated to the C‑terminus using the same flexible linker, generating the dual‑targeting CKS9‑MEV-DCpep vaccine (Fig. 2A).Fig. 2Design and structural analysis of the CKS9-MEV-DCpep vaccine. Bioinformatics analysis revealed that the CKS9‑MEV-DCpep vaccine possesses favorable physicochemical properties: a molecular weight of 46 kDa, molecular formula C₂₀₃₅H₃₀₈₅N₅₇₁O₆₀₇S₄, theoretical isoelectric point of 9.35, grand average of hydropathicity (GRAVY) of - 0.646, and no predicted transmembrane domains. Antigenicity assessment indicated strong immunogenicity (VaxiJen score 1.1374; threshold ≥ 0.5) and non‑allergenicity (AllerTop prediction). The vaccine exhibited excellent solubility (predicted to exceed the average level of endogenous E. coli proteins; SolPro score 0.550; Supplementary Fig. 2) and high stability (instability index 14.88; threshold < 40), supporting its potential as a vaccine candidate. Secondary structure composition was 20.47% α‑helix, 25.18% β‑strand, and 54.35% random coil (Fig. 2B), and tertiary structure modeling confirmed topological consistency (Fig. 2C1, C2).

To investigate whether CKS9‑MEV‑DCpep initiates innate immune responses by activating toll-like receptor-2 (TLR‑2), TLR‑4, and TLR‑9 (receptors that recognize Brucella lipopeptide, lipopolysaccharide, and CpG DNA, respectively, constituting the core innate immune recognition mechanism against Brucella infection [44]), molecular docking and molecular dynamics simulations were employed to evaluate the binding stability of the vaccine protein with these three TLRs. Molecular docking results showed binding free energies of CKS9‑MEV‑DCpep with TLR‑2 (UniProt Accession: O60603), TLR‑4 (UniProt Accession: O00206), and TLR‑9 (UniProt Accession: Q9NR96) of -493.2, -666.86, and -439.35 kcal/mol, respectively, with the lowest binding energy observed for TLR‑4, suggesting the strongest potential binding affinity for this receptor (Fig. 2D).

Molecular dynamics simulation results demonstrated that the CKS9-MEV-DCpep/TLR-4 complex exhibited superior conformational stability, structural compactness, and hydrogen bond network robustness compared to complexes with TLR-2 and TLR-9. Binding free energy calculations and residue‑wise energy decomposition further confirmed the strongest binding affinity for TLR-4 (detailed data are provided in Supplementary Fig. 3). Collectively, CKS9-MEV-DCpep can stably bind to TLR-2, TLR-4, and TLR-9 and possesses the potential to activate innate immune responses. A 350-day in silico immune simulation indicated that CKS9-MEV-DCpep can induce activation of B cells and T cell subsets, along with dynamic antibody and cytokine responses, and exhibited a marked enrichment trend toward Th1 cells (detailed data are provided in Supplementary Fig. 4).

To assess its diagnostic potential, the recombinant protein was used as a coating antigen in an indirect ELISA to detect specific IgG in sera from 108 healthy controls and 204 brucellosis patients. IgG levels were significantly higher in infected patients (Fig. 3C1). ROC curve analysis yielded (Fig. 3C2) a sensitivity of 62.96% (95% CI: 53.56%-71.48%), a specificity of 71.57% (95% CI: 65.02%-77.32%), and an AUC of 0.7425 (95% CI: 0.6863-0.7987), indicating that the recombinant antigen possesses specific recognition ability and can be recognized by a considerable proportion of infected sera, suggesting favorable preliminary antigenicity and population coverage potential.

CS-NPs loaded with vaccine proteins were prepared by ion gelation. Under acidic conditions, protonated chitosan amino groups enabled electrostatic antigen adsorption [45]; anionic tripolyphosphate formed a 3D network encapsulating the antigen (Fig. 3A) [46]. Electron microscopy showed spherical, uniform blank CS-NPs, CS-MEV-NPs, and CS-CKS9-MEV-DCpep-NPs with narrow size distribution (Fig. 3D) and mean diameters of 190 ± 35 nm, 205 ± 20 nm, and 210 ± 32 nm, respectively. DLS gave slightly larger hydrodynamic diameters. All formulations had PDI < 0.3 (Fig. 3E1), indicating good dispersity; the 200–300 nm size favors mucosal penetration and uptake (Fig. 3E2). Zeta potentials were strongly positive (Fig. 3F1): + 33.0 ± 1.0 mV (blank), + 31.4 ± 2.3 mV (CS-MEV-NPs), and + 32.2 ± 2.0 mV (CS‑CKS9‑MEV‑DCpep‑NPs) (Fig3F2), facilitating electrostatic adhesion to negatively charged mucosa. Encapsulation efficiencies (BCA assay) were 63.11 ± 2.51% (CS-MEV-NPs) and 64.65 ± 3.15% (CS-CKS9-MEV-DCpep-NPs) (Fig. 3G), with drug loadings of 23.98 ± 0.96% and 24.43 ± 1.19% (Fig. 3H), confirming effective antigen encapsulation.

Cumulative penetration at 6 h was 13.81 ± 0.13% (CS-MEV-NPs), 14.05 ± 0.10% (CS-CKS9-MEV-DCpep-NPs), and 13.27 ± 0.11% (blank) (Fig. 3I); corresponding Papp values were 1.92 × 10⁻⁶ cm/s, 1.95 × 10⁻⁶ cm/s, and 1.86 × 10⁻⁶ cm/s, with no significant differences (P > 0.05), indicating that protein loading did not enhance mucus penetration. Sequential simulated release in SGF (0-2 h) and SIF (2-72 h) showed rapid gastric release: at 2 h, cumulative release was 58-66% (non‑targeting) and 59-65% (targeting). Intestinal release slowed but continued, reaching 83-87% at 6 h, > 95% at 24 h, and a plateau of 98-99% after 48 h (Fig. 3J). No significant differences were observed between the two groups at any time point (P > 0.05), confirming that targeting modification did not alter the release profile in simulated gastrointestinal environment.

CKS9 peptide targeted modification enhances targeted delivery of MEV

CKS9 peptide mediates receptor‑dependent endocytosis through its unique “pocket” structure, thereby promoting efficient vaccine uptake by M cells and optimizing mucosal immune delivery via this dual mechanism [47]. IHC of the ligated ileal loop model (Fig. 4A) confirmed that both CS-MEV-NPs and CS‑CKS9‑MEV‑DCpep‑NPs effectively crossed the intestinal mucosal epithelium (Fig. 4B), a critical prerequisite for mucosal vaccine function. To verify the targeting functionality conferred by CKS9 modification, the ligated ileal loop model combined with immunofluorescence staining was employed (Fig. 4A). The regulatory effect of the vaccines on DC subset polarization was assessed after 8 h of co‑culture (Fig. 5A). Both soluble MEV and CKS9‑MEV‑DCpep specifically promoted cDC2 expansion (gating strategy, Supplementary Fig. 8; Fig. 5D3). Compared with HC, MEV increased cDC2 proportion by 14.71% ± 3.57% (P < 0.01), while CKS9‑MEV‑DCpep further raised it to 28.45% ± 3.28% (P < 0.001), a 19.20% increase over MEV (P < 0.01; Fig. 5D4). Thus, DCpep modification enhanced MEV‑induced cDC2 polarization, a subset central to initiating adaptive immunity. Encapsulation with CS-NPs enabled the vaccine to overcome subset‑specific limitations and achieve broad activation of multiple DC subsets (gating strategy, Supplementary Fig. 8; Fig. 5D1, D3, D5). Compared with HC group, CS‑CKS9‑MEV‑DCpep‑NPs significantly increased cDC1 (308% ± 12.2%), cDC2 (38.96% ± 5.98%), and pDC (469.3% ± 9.32%) (all P < 0.01; Fig. 5D2, D4, D6). This indicates that CS‑NP encapsulation, via its adjuvant effect, breaks polarization restriction and synergistically activates multiple DC subsets [48, 49].

DC maturation, essential for antigen presentation and T-cell activation, was evaluated (Fig. 5.A). Among soluble vaccines, CKS9-MEV-DCpep induced a DC maturation rate 3.56-fold higher than MEV (P < 0.01; gating strategy, Supplementary Fig. 9; Fig. 5E1, E2). After CS-NP encapsulation, CS-MEV-NPs increased the rate by 3.59-fold over unencapsulated MEV (P < 0.01). CS-CKS9-MEV-DCpep-NPs yielded the highest maturation frequency, 1.64-fold higher than that of soluble CKS9-MEV-DCpep (P < 0.01) and significantly above all control groups (P < 0.01; gating strategy, Supplementary Fig. 9; Fig. 5E1, E2). Thus, DCpep modification and chitosan carriers synergistically promote MEV-induced DC maturation.

To assess whether vaccine‑stimulated DCs effectively present antigen and activate downstream lymphocytes, an ELISPOT assay was performed. As demonstrated by the results (Fig. 5F1, F2), in the absence of DCs ( group B: T/B + MEV; group C: T/B + CKS9‑MEV‑DCpep), no significant IFN‑γ or IL‑2 responses were detected (P > 0.05), confirming that T cells cannot be directly activated by the vaccine alone. In contrast, inclusion of DCs (group D: DC/T/B + MEV) significantly increased IFN‑γ and IL‑2 spot‑forming cells (SFCs) compared with groups A and B (all P < 0.01), indicating that MEV‑stimulated DCs efficiently process and present antigen to activate T cells. Notably, CKS9‑MEV‑DCpep‑stimulated DCs (group E: DC/T/B + CKS9‑MEV‑DCpep) further enhanced the response, yielding 1.376‑fold (P < 0.01) and 1.344‑fold (P < 0.05) higher IFN‑γ and IL‑2 SFCs than group D, respectively, demonstrating superior antigen uptake, processing, and presentation. Similarly, for B‑cell antibody responses, only weak IgG spots were observed without DCs (groups B and C). With DCs present, group D showed significantly more IgG spots than groups A and B (all P < 0.01; groups A: DC/T/B + PBS), confirming a T‑cell‑dependent humoral response. Moreover, group E exhibited 1.517‑fold more IgG spots than group D (P < 0.01), suggesting that CKS9‑MEV‑DCpep, via more efficient DC antigen presentation, provides stronger activation signals to B cells and induces a more potent antibody response.

In summary, DCpep modification optimizes MEV antigen presentation by targeting cDC2 subset activation; chitosan nanocarriers, leveraging their adjuvant properties [23], significantly broaden the spectrum of DC subset activation mediated by MEV, including key subsets such as cDC1 and pDC. Their synergistic effect endows CS‑CKS9‑MEV‑DCpep‑NPs with significant advantages in promoting DC proliferation, antigen uptake, and maturation.

At two weeks post‑immunization, an early IgM peak was observed in all groups except HC, consistent with a primary immune response (Fig. 6C). Total IgG and subclass titers followed a clear hierarchy: CS‑CKS9‑MEV‑DCpep‑NPs > CS-MEV-NPs (P < 0.05) > CS‑NPs (P < 0.01) > HC (P < 0.01; Fig. 6D), indicating that targeted modification enhances antibody responses. IgG subclass analysis revealed that the CS‑NPs group had an IgG2a/IgG1 ratio of approximately 1.66 (Th1‑biased); the CS-MEV-NPs group showed a ratio of about 1.33 (balanced Th1/Th2); and the CS‑CKS9‑MEV‑DCpep‑NPs group exhibited a ratio of about 1.5, reflecting a stronger Th1 response than that of the CS-MEV-NPs group (Fig. 6E). These findings suggest that MEV modification reverses the Th1 polarization induced by CS‑NPs, and the incorporation of CKS9 and DCpep further potentiates the Th1‑type immune response.

Germinal center B cells (GC B) are the site of antibody affinity maturation and class switching, while follicular helper T cells (Tfh) regulate GC B function through IL‑21 secretion. To dissect the humoral immune mechanism, we measured GC B and Tfh frequencies. Two weeks after the final immunization, flow cytometry showed that the frequency of B220⁺ B cells in the spleen (gating strategy, Supplementary Fig. 10; Fig. 6G1) and lymph nodes (Supplementary Fig. 13) followed a gradient: CS‑CKS9‑MEV‑DCpep‑NPs > CS-MEV-NPs > CS‑NPs > HC (all P < 0.01), consistent with antibody titers. GC B frequency in the spleen (gating strategy, Supplementary Fig. 11; Fig. 6H1) followed the same gradient: CS‑CKS9‑MEV‑DCpep‑NPs > CS-MEV-NPs (P < 0.05) > CS‑NPs (P < 0.01) > HC (P < 0.01; Fig. 6H1). In lymph nodes (Supplementary Fig. 13), no significant difference was observed between the targeted and non‑targeted groups, but both were significantly higher than the CS‑NPs group (P < 0.01; Fig. 6 H2). These data indicate that the intensity of the splenic GC response correlates with vaccine complexity in a dose‑dependent manner.

Tfh frequency in the spleen and lymph nodes exhibited a gradient highly consistent with that of GC B (gating strategy, Supplementary Fig. 12). In the spleen (Fig. 6I1): CS‑CKS9‑MEV‑DCpep‑NPs > CS-MEV-NPs (P < 0.05) > CS‑NPs (P < 0.01) > HC (P < 0.01). In lymph nodes (Supplementary Fig. 13): CS‑CKS9‑MEV‑DCpep‑NPs > CS-MEV-NPs (P < 0.01) > CS‑NPs (P < 0.01) > HC (P < 0.01; Fig. 6I2). ELISA confirmed that IL‑21 levels in the spleen and lymph nodes followed the same order (Fig. 6F). The synchronized changes in Tfh, GC B, and IL‑21 confirm the central regulatory role of the Tfh-GC B axis: Tfh cells drive GC B proliferation, antibody class switching, and affinity maturation mainly through IL‑21 secretion, thereby shaping an effective adaptive humoral immune response.

Further evaluation of mucosal immune responses revealed that intestinal sIgA levels followed a clear gradient (Fig. 6J): CS‑CKS9‑MEV‑DCpep‑NPs > CS-MEV-NPs (P < 0.01) > CS‑NPs (P < 0.01) > HC (P < 0.01), likely attributable to CKS9 peptide‑mediated M‑cell targeting, which enhances antigen uptake by Peyer’s patches and efficiently activates local intestinal immunity. In distal mucosal sites (lung, nasal, and vaginal lavages), sIgA levels were not significantly different between the two vaccine groups (P > 0.05), but both were significantly higher than those in the CS‑NPs and HC groups (P < 0.01). This is consistent with lymphocyte homing properties: after oral immunization, activated B cells preferentially home to the intestinal lamina propria, while their homing to the respiratory and reproductive tracts is less efficient. Consequently, the additional benefit of targeted modification on distal mucosal sites is limited.

Mucosal IgG levels were low in all groups, indicating a predominantly mucosal response without significant inflammation. Across all lavage fluids (Fig. 6K), no significant difference in IgG levels was observed between the two vaccine groups (P > 0.05), but both were significantly higher than those in the CS‑NPs and HC groups (P < 0.05). This is likely because mucosal IgG mainly originates from passive serum exudation, and the systemic IgG difference between CS‑CKS9‑MEV‑DCpep‑NPs and CS-MEV-NPs groups is relatively small, leading to a similar difference at mucosal sites. Notably, in nasal lavage fluid, CS-MEV-NPs did not differ significantly from CS‑NPs (P > 0.05), whereas CS‑CKS9‑MEV‑DCpep‑NPs showed a significant increase over CS‑NPs (P < 0.05). This suggests that non‑targeted nanoparticles fail to activate nasal IgG responses through passive uptake, while targeted modification effectively induces such responses, possibly because the nasal mucosa requires more efficient antigen delivery to trigger IgG exudation.

In summary, the CS‑CKS9‑MEV‑DCpep‑NPs nanovaccine not only induces potent and durable systemic humoral immunity (high IgG titers, Th1‑biased polarization, strong GC B and Tfh responses) but also efficiently stimulates local intestinal sIgA responses and activates distal mucosal immune networks, without causing significant inflammation or elevated IgG levels, demonstrating considerable potential for defending against mucosally transmitted pathogens.

CD4⁺ TEM was inversely correlated with TCM: in the spleen, the targeted group had higher TEM than HC but lower than the non‑targeted group (P < 0.05); in lymph nodes and blood, TEM in the targeted group was higher than in HC but lower than in both the CS‑NPs and non‑targeted groups (P < 0.05; Fig. 10C2, C3).

For CD8⁺ TCM (Fig. 10C1), a compartmentalized distribution was observed: the targeted group had significantly higher levels in the spleen and peripheral blood than all other groups (P < 0.05; Fig. 10C3, C4), with no intergroup differences in lymph nodes (Supplementary Fig. 26). For CD8⁺ TEM: in the spleen and blood, the targeted group had higher levels than HC but lower than the non‑targeted group (P < 0.05); in lymph nodes, the targeted group reached the highest level, exhibiting a clear hierarchy of targeted group > non‑targeted group > CS‑NPs > HC (P < 0.01; Fig. 10C3, C4).

In summary, the targeted vaccine broadly enhanced CD4⁺ and CD8⁺ TCM reservoirs across multiple tissues, reduced TEM proportions in peripheral sites such as the spleen, and promoted T‑cell polarization toward the central memory phenotype. Moreover, the response was tissue‑microenvironment‑dependent: lymphoid organs supported the simultaneous expansion of both TCM and TEM, whereas peripheral sites preferentially established TCM reserves.

Bacterial loads (Log₁₀ CFU/g) were further measured to quantitatively assess the ability of the vaccines to inhibit bacterial colonization and promote clearance. At the acute phase (Fig. 11C1), the PBS group showed high bacterial loads: spleen 5.77 ± 1.22, liver 4.21 ± 0.63, lung 3.15 ± 0.39, and lymph nodes 3.58 ± 0.59; the CS-NPs group was not significantly different from the PBS group (Fig. 11C2). Compared with the PBS group, the CS-CKS9-MEV-DCpep-NPs group exhibited bacterial load reductions of 4.12 ± 1.60 (spleen), 3.63 ± 0.85 (liver), 1.99 ± 1.29 (lung), and 2.57 ± 0.67 (lymph nodes); the CS-MEV-NPs group showed reductions of 3.51 ± 1.45 (spleen), 2.90 ± 1.17 (liver), 1.98 ± 1.23 (lung), and 2.57 ± 1.40 (lymph nodes) (all P < 0.01). Notably, the CS-CKS9-MEV-DCpep-NPs group achieved significantly greater bacterial reductions in the spleen and lung than the CS-MEV-NPs group (P < 0.05). At the chronic phase (8 weeks, Supplementary Fig. 27), the PBS group still maintained high loads: spleen 3.73 ± 1.14, liver 2.04 ± 0.57, lung 1.85 ± 0.34, and lymph nodes CFU/g (Fig. 11C3). The CS-CKS9-MEV-DCpep-NPs showed reductions of 2.68 ± 1.26 (spleen), 1.53 ± 0.76 (liver), 1.32 ± 0.66 (lung), and 1.85 ± 1.03 (lymph nodes); the CS-MEV-NPs group showed reductions of 2.03 ± 1.32 (spleen), 1.03 ± 0.65 (liver), 1.15 ± 1.0 (lung), and 1.30 ± 0.75 (lymph nodes). The targeted group exhibited significantly higher clearance efficiency in the liver, lung, and lymph nodes than the non-targeted group (P < 0.05), indicating its ability to effectively block the progression of chronic infection.

Histopathological changes were evaluated by H&E to assess the protective effects of the vaccines against acute inflammatory injury and chronic tissue repair. At the acute phase, the PBS and CS-NPs groups showed extensive inflammatory foci, diffuse inflammatory cell infiltration, and granulomas in the liver, as well as alveolar septal thickening, peribronchial “cuff-like” lymphocyte infiltration, and granulomas in the lung (Fig. 11D1). After immunization, no significant histological changes or functional impairments were observed in the lungs and liver (Supplementary Fig. 28). Both vaccine groups exhibited significant improvement (P < 0.01): the CS-MEV-NPs group showed only a few aggregations of inflammatory cells and occasional small granulomas, while the targeted group displayed almost no inflammatory cells in the liver and nearly normal alveolar walls; however, no significant difference was observed between the two vaccine groups (P > 0.05; Fig. 11D2, D3). At the chronic phase (Supplementary Fig. 29), inflammation persisted in the control groups, with chronic granulomatous inflammation in the liver and residual chronic inflammatory traces in the lung. The non-targeted group showed reduced granulomas and thinner alveolar septa; the targeted group exhibited no inflammatory cells in the liver and complete recovery of alveolar structure (compared with control groups, P < 0.01), but again no significant difference was seen between the two vaccine groups (Fig. 11D2, D3).

Finally, immunohistochemistry was used to localize Brucella antigens, verifying the pathogen-clearing effect of the vaccines. After immunization, no significant Brucella colonization or antigen-positive signals were observed in the lungs or liver (Supplementary Fig. 30). At the acute phase (Fig. 11E1), abundant Brucella antigen signals were detected in the cytoplasm of macrophages and within inflammatory foci in the liver and lung of the control groups, whereas almost no signals were observed in the two vaccine groups (P < 0.01; Fig. 11E2). At the chronic phase (Supplementary Fig. 31), scattered positive signals remained in the control groups, while only minimal or no signals were detected in the vaccine groups (P < 0.01; Fig. 11E3). The distribution of bacterial antigens was highly consistent with the areas of pathological damage, confirming Brucella as the primary etiological agent. The vaccine groups simultaneously eliminated bacterial colonization and severe pathological lesions, demonstrating a synergistic protective effect.

In summary, both CS-MEV-NPs and CS-CKS9-MEV-DCpep-NPs significantly reduced acute organ damage, inhibited bacterial colonization, and promoted bacterial clearance and tissue repair during the chronic phase. Compared with CS-MEV-NPs, CS-CKS9-MEV-DCpep-NPs exhibited superior bacterial clearance efficiency and tissue repair in the chronic phase, indicating that CKS9-DCpep modification enhances anti-Brucella immune responses, whereas the CS-NPs carrier alone possessed no protective activity.

Discussion

This study successfully designed and systematically evaluated an oral CS-NPs-based mucosal vaccine (CS-CKS9-MEV-DCpep-NPs) that simultaneously targets M cells and DCs. Through the synergistic effects of dual-targeting modification and the nanocarrier delivery system, the vaccine effectively activated mucosal, humoral, and cellular immune responses, significantly reduced bacterial loads and histopathological damage after Brucella infection, and provided a safe and efficient new strategy for brucellosis control.

Regarding vaccine design, this study overcame the limitations of traditional whole-cell or single-protein vaccines. From 13 outer membrane proteins of Brucella melitensis, four highly immunogenic candidates (OMP10, Omp25, Omp31, BtpB) were selected. A total of 41 non-cross-reactive epitopes with high antigenicity indices were predicted, and 21 dominant epitopes were identified by three-dimensional ELISPOT (immunogenicity, specificity, effector function). Bioinformatics analysis confirmed favorable physicochemical properties, strong antigenicity, high safety and no allergenicity, meeting the core requirements for mucosal vaccine development.

The core innovation of this study lies in the dual-targeting synergistic mechanism: the CKS9 peptide enhanced M cell-specific antigen uptake and presentation and increased the number of M cells in the follicle-associated epithelium, thereby promoting mucosal immune activation [47]; the DCpep peptide targeted DCs [50]; and chitosan nanoparticles (CS-NPs) acted as a natural adjuvant [21]. These three components synergistically promoted the proliferation, uptake and maturation of BMDCs, with the CS-CKS9-MEV-DCpep-NPs group showing the most significant effect. This strategy simultaneously activated cDC1, cDC2 and pDC subsets: cDC1 activation improved CD8⁺ T cell cross-presentation; cDC2 expansion enhanced CD4⁺ T cell activation and antibody production; and pDCs secreted type I interferon to inhibit intracellular pathogen survival [51, 52]. The chitosan carrier further amplified Th1-type responses, establishing a comprehensive “uptake-presentation-effector” inhibitory mechanism against Brucella. Importantly, DCpep improved cDC2 targeting and optimized antigen presentation, while CS-NPs overcame the limitation of restricted DC subset polarization seen in traditional vaccines.

In terms of immune efficacy, the vaccine performed excellently in humoral, cellular and mucosal immunity. For humoral immunity, although it did not significantly increase peak antibody levels, it maintained a Th1 bias, promoted IL-21 secretion via the GC B-Tfh axis, maintained germinal center activity and memory B cell formation, thereby effectively inducing long-term humoral immune memory [53]. For cellular immunity, the vaccine induced a complete reaction chain from mucosal innate immunity to systemic adaptive immunity: proliferation of ILCs, ILC1s and DCs in the intestinal lamina propria confirmed effective mucosal antigen uptake; increased DC numbers in secondary lymphoid organs and blood (most pronounced in the targeted group) validated efficient antigen transport to lymphoid tissues. At the systemic level, the vaccine activated CD4⁺ and CD8⁺ T cells and upregulated IFN-γ, IL-4, TNF-α, perforin and granzyme B. The dominance of Th1-type cytokines (IFN-γ, TNF-α), elevated CTL effector molecules and CD8⁺ T cell expansion collectively demonstrated a potent anti-Brucella cellular response. The oral CS-NPs system enhanced by CKS9 and DCpep acted through three mechanisms (increasing mucosal uptake, activating DCs and optimizing antigen presentation), inducing a hierarchical response from early IFN-γ⁺ ILC1 activation to systemic T cell (especially CD8⁺ T cell) expansion, establishing a Th1/CTL-dominant immune response accompanied by early TEM and TCM formation, thereby enhancing long-term immune memory.

For mucosal immunity, the vaccine induced high levels of sIgA, building a first line of defense against Brucella invasion via the digestive and respiratory tracts. The inherent adjuvant effect of the chitosan carrier further strengthened the Th1-biased response, providing a new paradigm for developing hierarchically targeted anti-intracellular bacterial vaccines based on DC subsets. Notably, oral immunization not only increased intestinal sIgA but also significantly elevated sIgA in lung, nasal and vaginal lavage fluids. This is consistent with the “common mucosal immune system” theory: oral antigen activates B/T cells in Peyer’s patches, which express homing receptors (e.g., α4β7, CCR9) and migrate to distal respiratory and reproductive tract mucosae, where they differentiate into plasma cells secreting sIgA, thus generating cross-mucosal protection [54]. This study confirms that oral CS-CKS9-MEV-DCpep-NPs vaccine achieves cross-protection in the lung through the common mucosal immune network, which is of great significance for defense against respiratory pathogens.

Final protective efficacy analysis showed that, using Brucella melitensis as the infection model, the dynamic trends of bacterial load and organ changes in the control group (model group) were similar to those observed by A M Arenas‑Gambo et al. in the acute and chronic phase model groups [55]. Moreover, the developed vaccine significantly reduced Brucella loads in the liver, spleen, lung and lymph nodes, alleviated hepatosplenomegaly, and mitigated inflammatory infiltration and histopathological damage in target organs, fully confirming its comprehensive protective effect against Brucella infection.

Compared with current research, although targeting strategies against M cells [33, 47, 56] or DCs [34, 50, 57] have shown potential to enhance mucosal and systemic immunogenicity in mucosal vaccines against other pathogens, their application and mechanistic studies in Brucella mucosal vaccines remain limited. Existing Brucella vaccines (e.g., live attenuated vaccines) have limitations such as low safety, narrow protection spectrum and the need for injection [58, 59]. The CS-CKS9-MEV-DCpep-NPs vaccine, through oral administration, precise targeting modification and an excellent carrier, not only overcomes the application bottlenecks of traditional vaccines but also achieves important breakthroughs in immune memory remodeling and multi-dimensional immune activation, providing a solid theoretical and experimental basis for developing safe, efficient and scalable new tools for brucellosis control.

This study has several limitations: the precise molecular binding target of the CKS9 peptide to M cells remains unknown; long-term safety and protective efficacy in different animal models need further validation; and clinical translation will require optimization of manufacturing processes and pharmacodynamic evaluation. Future work will explore the applicability of this vaccine platform to other mucosal pathogens (e.g., Mycobacterium tuberculosis, Salmonella spp.), evaluate its immune efficacy and dose optimization in susceptible hosts such as cattle and sheep under natural infection conditions, and conduct long-term safety assessments to promote clinical translation.

Conclusion

In conclusion, through a systematic strategy of "rational epitope screening-precise targeting modification-excellent carrier selection," the CS-CKS9-MEV-DCpep-NPs vaccine achieves efficient immune defense against Brucella. Its unique ability to remodel immune memory and its advantages in multi-dimensional immune responses provide new insights for brucellosis prevention and establish a new paradigm for developing hierarchical anti-intracellular bacterial vaccines targeting DC subsets, holding significant theoretical innovation value and broad application prospects.
