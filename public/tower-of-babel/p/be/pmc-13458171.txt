Development and Evaluation of Artificial Intelligence-Based Two-Step Model for Automated Serum Quality Assessment in Clinical Laboratories
Authors: Weixin Chen, Yuxuan Xiong, Chenxi Zhang, Zhifang Sui, Fengning Yang, Zikun Ma, B.M., Qinglin Liu, Yuexin Cui, Ran Han
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundEnhancing pre-analytical QC in clinical laboratories and providing risk alerts is crucial for ensuring accurate test results for patients whose samples are affected by hemolysis, icterus, or lipemia. We evaluated a novel, self-developed artificial intelligence (AI) approach for assessing serum quality using a two-step deep learning YOLOv5–ResNet-50 architecture model and explored its performance under real-world laboratory conditions.MethodsThe performance of the two-step model prototype integrated into a Roche Cobas c 701 module (Roche Diagnostics, Shanghai, China) for preprocessing was assessed. By combining serum index testing with manual evaluation, we established a laboratory-specific model for automated sample quality identification. A primary dataset of 21,000 serum samples was partitioned into a training set (80%) and a validation set (20%) for model development and tuning. Subsequently, a separate, independent test dataset of 21,000 samples was used to conduct the final, unbiased evaluation of the model’s performance, with statistical analysis.ResultsThe AI serum model achieved recognition accuracies exceeding 97% for hemolysis and icterus and over 92% for lipemia. In human–machine comparisons, the AI model demonstrated higher consistency (72% exact agreement) than manual assessments (42%–68% exact agreement).ConclusionsThe AI-powered serum quality software demonstrated high accuracy in pre-analytical serum quality assessment, offering a performance that meets or exceeds that of both automated and manual measurements. This tool can enhance laboratory efficiency and reduce the error risks associated with subjective visual evaluations.

INTRODUCTION

In laboratory testing, laboratory analysis of serum samples is often hampered by hemolysis, icterus, and lipemia (HIL), which lead to erroneous results [1–3]. These interferences substantially affect biochemical assays, with combined HIL reducing their accuracy [4]; therefore, QC analysis of serum samples is essential in clinical laboratory practice.

Traditionally, serum quality has been assessed visually by laboratory personnel, a process that is highly subjective, prone to variability, and inconsistent, particularly for mildly abnormal samples [5]. Automated quantitative serum hemolysis-icterus-lipemia (HIL) index testing offers objective measurements; however, it is typically performed concurrently with clinical testing. This approach leads to delayed detection of abnormalities, resulting in sample waste and prolonged reporting cycles, which is inefficient and may impact patient care [6]. Furthermore, the reliance of automated testing on specific instruments adds cost and operational constraints [7]. These limitations highlight the need for an alternate objective and timely approach for pre-analytical serum quality assessment.

Artificial intelligence (AI), particularly deep learning, offers a promising approach to overcome these limitations [8, 9]. A key challenge for clinical applications, however, is the inherent imbalance in data distribution, such as the disproportionate occurrence of hemolyzed, icteric, and lipemic specimens in real-world datasets and their frequent pairwise or triple co-occurrence, which hinders the accurate identification of these phenomena and their respective severity gradations. Approaches that leverage the hierarchical feature extraction of convolutional neural networks (CNNs) and judicious data augmentation can help models learn from underrepresented instances and improve classification performance while maintaining clinical relevance [10]. This approach leverages the intrinsic strength of CNNs in identifying complex patterns directly from image data, while strategically amplifying the signal from critical but infrequent abnormalities.

We developed and evaluated an AI-based model, created using a two-step deep learning YOLOv5–ResNet-50 architecture, for automated assessment of serum quality and HIL detection in clinical samples and assessed the model’s ability to grade HIL severity to support timely, objective, and efficient pre-analytical workflows.

METHODS

Sample collection and HIL index detection

This retrospective study was reviewed and approved by the Ethics Committee of Dongfang Hospital Affiliated to Beijing University of Chinese Medicine (approval No.: JDF-IRB-2020041003), and informed consent was obtained. The study was conducted in the Laboratory Department of Dongfang Hospital Affiliated to Beijing University of Chinese Medicine (Beijing, China). Broad informed consent was obtained through a notification posted in the blood collection room, and no personal or sensitive patient information was used in this study. To ensure strict control of pre-analytical variables, we uniformly used VACUETTE® disposable vacuum blood-collection tubes (CAT clot activator, 3.5 mL, 13×75 mm; Greiner Bio-One GmbH, Chonburi, Thailand).

Two distinct datasets were collected for this study. The primary dataset comprised 21,000 serum samples collected between November 1, 2020, and June 30, 2022. The primary dataset was randomly divided into a training set (80%, N=16,800) and a validation set (20%, N=4,200). The training set was used to build the deep learning model, whereas the validation set was used during training to monitor the performance, finetune the hyperparameters, and prevent overfitting. To develop the deep learning model, balanced training subsets were created from the initial 21,000-sample pool to address severe data imbalance. For the hemolysis index, we used a curated training set of 293 samples. The model’s performance was continuously evaluated using the validation dataset during training to prevent overfitting and to select the best-performing model. To perform a final, unbiased evaluation of the trained model, we collected a second independent test dataset comprising 21,000 serum samples between November 11, 2020 and June 30, 2022. This dataset was not used during model training or tuning. For both datasets, samples were processed using the Roche Cobas c 701 module, with HIL indices measured and images captured after centrifugation at 4000 rpm for 10 min (3,130×g).

Serum HIL indices and sample parameters were determined simultaneously on the Roche system. The HIL grading and classification criteria were primarily based on two sources: the manufacturer’s standard instructions for the Roche analyzer and authoritative expert consensus literature in the field. This ensured that the classifications aligned with both clinical practice norms and instrument-specific guidelines (Table 1). Following index measurement and classification, trained physicians visually assessed the captured serum images to exclude those with image interference. A sample was deemed to have image interference if the visible portion of the serum in the blood-collection tube was covered by labels or handwriting, which could compromise subsequent AI model analysis or manual evaluation. To ensure reliable HIL index measurements and to minimize variability in index measurements, we implemented comprehensive QC procedures, including daily internal QC analysis for HIL detection, verification that all QC results met preset laboratory acceptance criteria, regular instrument calibration and maintenance per manufacturer recommendations, and adherence to standard operating procedures for sample handling.

Image processing

To ensure accuracy and consistency of the model input data, all captured sample images were resized to 224×224 pixels to meet the input dimensions required by the ResNet-50 classification model [11, 12]. Normalization was applied to adjust pixel intensity values, preparing the dataset for neural network input.

Deep learning-based system

Model selection and architecture

Given that serum images contain irrelevant information, precise focus on key regions within the images is crucial. The YOLOv5 algorithm was selected for regions-of-interest (ROI) detection owing to its superior performance in small-target detection and real-time processing, which are critical for efficiently localizing serum regions in high-throughput laboratory environments. To develop the detector, a representative subset of 800 images was manually annotated with bounding boxes identifying the serum ROI. This subset was then partitioned into a training set of 720 images and a testing set of 80 images.

Establishing and training the ResNet-50 model

Following ROI extraction, the pre-trained ResNet-50 model was employed for classification, using its deep residual network structure to capture complex spectral and morphological features associated with HIL abnormalities. To optimize the ResNet-50 model for specific serum image-classification tasks, the final fully connected layer was replaced with one tailored to our classification needs, ensuring that the model accurately captured subtle differences in serum images. The deep network structure of ResNet-50 comprises 50 carefully designed convolutional layers, batch-normalization layers, rectified linear unit activation functions, and crucial residual connections. During training, categorical cross-entropy was selected as the loss function, and computation was accelerated on a graphics processing unit (GPU), reducing the training time for large datasets. The ResNet-50 classification model was trained using a categorical cross-entropy loss function on an NVIDIA A100 GPU. Key hyperparameters for this classification training included a batch size of 32, an initial learning rate of 0.001 (reduced by a factor of 0.1 after 10 epochs), and 50 training epochs (Fig. 1).

The Roche preprocessing module automatically captures sample images. Utilizing CNNs and matrix transformation algorithms, the captured sample images undergo quality calibration. A deep learning system model is established, which, through extensive learning from numerous sample images, can automatically identify and extract serum regions within the images, classifying the samples based on their image characteristics.

Comparison of ROI detection methods

To determine the most effective method for identifying serum ROI, we compared the performance of our deep learning model against a traditional computer vision (CV) baseline. These methods were evaluated strictly on their ability to accurately localize serum regions; the traditional CV techniques served as a comparative benchmark and not as preprocessing steps for the final classification pipeline.

Prior to analysis, all images underwent quality calibration to ensure environmental consistency. The traditional CV approach utilized a sequence of established image-processing techniques, including histogram equalization for contrast normalization and edge-detection algorithms to delineate the serum layer boundaries within the test tubes. This method yielded a recall of 0.70 and an intersection over union (IOU) of 0.82, reflecting the limitations of manual feature engineering in handling the optical variability of serum samples.

The deep learning approach utilized the YOLOv5 model, trained on a manually annotated dataset to automatically identify and extract serum regions based on learned image characteristics. The YOLOv5 model outperformed the traditional CV method, achieving a recall of 1.00 and an IOU of 0.95. This substantial improvement in detection sensitivity and localization precision indicates that the deep learning system was more robust than traditional edge-based methods for automated serum ROI extraction. The performance of both methods is quantitatively compared in Supplemental Data Table 1.

Data augmentation and annotation

To address data imbalances, abnormal samples were augmented using random rotations within ±15°, horizontal and vertical flipping, contrast adjustments of ±20%, and illumination normalization to simulate diverse lighting conditions.

All images were independently annotated by three senior physicians, and annotation consistency was validated using Cohen’s Kappa coefficient (κ>0.8), ensuring high-quality ground-truth labels.

Human–AI classification comparison protocol

We employed random sampling to select 100 representative serum samples from the study population, including 50 normal and 50 abnormal samples (covering samples with different degrees of HIL). This sample size ensured that, while maintaining statistical power, the model’s performance in practical clinical applications was fully reflected. Nine physicians and all qualified in-service staff of the biochemical laboratory participated in the serum classification. All participants held a professional title of laboratory technician or higher, had completed standardized training on the visual interpretation of serum indices, and were employed at a grade A clinical laboratory in a tertiary hospital.

We established a standardized manual interpretation process and adopted a double-blind design, where interpreters were unaware of both the instrument test results and the interpretations of other personnel. All interpreters independently completed the assessment results in a unified environment within a fixed timeframe, using a standardized interpretation record form. During the classification process, 12 serum categories were distinguished to ensure the comprehensiveness and depth of the evaluation framework. Consistency assessments were conducted based on exact and fuzzy match counts calculated from the manual visual classification results.

Model evaluation

When assessing the classification performance of the deep learning system for both binary and multi-class classification tasks, efficacy was measured based on a confusion matrix and the percent accuracy, specificity, sensitivity, and precision. In addition, human–AI classification comparisons were employed using exact and fuzzy match metrics: an exact match indicated full agreement between the AI and human classifications; a fuzzy match allowed for differences of ≤1 severity level (e.g., mild hemolysis vs. normal).

Training dataset curation for model development

The IOU is a standard metric for evaluating how well a predicted bounding box (the AI’s estimate of the ROI) matches the ground-truth bounding box (the manually labeled ROI).

The IOU is calculated as:

IOU=area of overlap/area of union,

where the “area of overlap” (the intersection) is the area where the predicted box and the ground-truth box overlap, while the “area of union” is the total area covered by both boxes combined, counting the overlapping area only once.

The resulting score ranges from 0 to 1. An IOU value of 1 indicates a perfect match, with the boxes overlapping completely. An IOU value of 0 indicates no overlap.

RESULTS

Data collection

We collected 21,000 samples, including 19,597 normal samples. In addition, 1,254 samples showed either hemolysis, icterus, or lipemia; 148 samples showed two of these categories simultaneously; and one sample met all three HIL criteria (Fig. 2A).

For automated detection of the hemolysis index, 21,000 samples were collected, with 20,487 normal samples (H≤30) accounting for 97.6% of all samples, 265 mild-hemolysis samples (30<H<50) accounting for 1.3%, 94 moderate-hemolysis samples (50≤H<70) accounting for 0.5%, 67 severe-hemolysis samples (70≤H<100) accounting for 0.3%, and 87 rejected samples (H≥100) accounting for 0.4%. Hemolytic samples accounted for 2.4% of all samples (Fig. 2B).

For automated detection of the icterus index, 21,000 samples were collected, with 20,714 normal samples (I≤5) accounting for 98.6%, 72 icteric samples (5<I<8) accounting for 0.3%, and 214 severe-icterus samples (I≥8) accounting for 1.0%. Icteric samples accounted for 1.4% of the total (Fig. 2C).

For automated detection of the lipemia index, 21,000 samples were collected, with 20,246 normal samples (L≤25) accounting for 96.4%, 459 mild-lipemia samples (25<L<50) accounting for 2.2%, 207 moderate-lipemia samples (50≤L<130) accounting for 1.0%, 14 severe-lipemia samples (130≤L<150) accounting for 0.1% (which potentially affected the measurement and were recommended for reexamination), and 74 rejected samples (L≥150) accounting for 0.4%. Lipemia samples accounted for 3.6% of the total (Fig. 2D).

Serum image recognition

For ROI detection, we compared our deep learning model with a traditional CV baseline. This traditional method, which relies on algorithmic analysis of an image’s red, blue, and green channels to perform tasks (such as edge detection), achieved a recall of 0.70 and an IOU of 0.82 (Supplementary data table 1).

Model performance with the validation and independent test datasets

A validation dataset containing 4200 images was randomly selected, accounting for 20% of the 21,000-image dataset. The model demonstrated accuracies of 81%, 85%, and 88% in identifying samples with abnormal H>30, L>25, and I>5 indices, respectively. To assess generalizability, the final model was evaluated using an independent test dataset, where 15,517 images were selected to validate the performance of the hemolysis index, and 15,574 images were used to verify the performance of the lipemia index at a separate time point. The model demonstrated excellent performance with this newly generated data, achieving high binary classification accuracies of 97% (H>30), 97% (L>25), and 93% (I>5) (Table 2).

Fig. 3 shows confusion matrixes for a representative subset of the validation data, illustrating the classification performance for HIL. During development, the model’s performance was monitored using the validation dataset to guide hyperparameter tuning and prevent overfitting. The model showed strong performance, achieving overall multi-class classification accuracies of 78% for hemolysis, 86% for icterus, and 80% for lipemia. The multi-class classification was also highly accurate, with confusion matrixes showing overall accuracies of 97% for hemolysis and 92% for lipemia. This strong performance with an independent dataset confirmed the model’s stability and reliability for real-world applications (Fig. 3).

Comparing the consistency of human and AI results in terms of serum classification

The exact-match consistencies ranged from 42% to 68%, whereas fuzzy match consistencies were higher, ranging from 73% to 93%. Additionally, the specificity of the manual visual classification results ranged from 90% to 100%, and the sensitivity ranged from 47% to 88% (Table 3).

Consistency assessments were also performed for exact and fuzzy matches with the AI learning model. We observed 72% exact matches and 82% fuzzy matches. The specificity and sensitivity of the AI learning model’s classification results were 92% and 96%, respectively. The AI learning model substantially outperformed manual visual classification in terms of serum classification (Supplemental Data Table 2).

DISCUSSION

AI is becoming increasingly integral in modern clinical laboratories, enhancing processes such as managing test requests and automated sample handling, thereby improving both efficiency and safety. In the domain of sample QC analysis, AI has demonstrated robust capabilities for error detection and analysis, strongly enhancing diagnostic accuracy [9, 13]. Our current findings build upon these advancements by validating a novel two-step deep learning model for automated pre-analytical assessment of serum quality, which integrates a YOLOv5 algorithm for ROI detection and a ResNet-50 CNN model for multi-level classification. This deep learning model achieved high accuracy on a large, independent clinical dataset and outperformed traditional CV methods and manual visual inspection in terms of classification consistency, highlighting its potential to enhance laboratory efficiency and diagnostic accuracy.

The principal strength of this research lies in the model’s robust performance under conditions of severe class imbalance, a common challenge in medical datasets with infrequent pathological samples. The prevalences of hemolysis (2.4%), lipemia (3.6%), and icterus (1.4%) in our cohort were low, reflecting normal clinical distributions, albeit lower than some reported rates where HIL abnormalities constituted over 5% of samples [14]. By employing targeted data-augmentation strategies and ensuring data quality through professional annotation [15], the model effectively learned the distinguishing features of these rare but critical abnormalities. Accurate identification of these interferences is imperative, as underscored by their well-documented, specific impact on clinical assays [16–17].

During hemolysis, analytes such as AST, lactate dehydrogenase, and potassium ions, all of which are present at higher concentrations in erythrocytes, are released into the sample, markedly increasing their measured concentrations and leading to potential false-positive results [18–20]. Moreover, the unique spectral properties of bilirubin and biliverdin can directly affect photometric measurements, and bilirubin further interferes with various biochemical tests utilizing peroxidase-coupled reactions, including those for total cholesterol, triglycerides, and uric acid [21–24]. Furthermore, hyperlipidemia manifests with excess serum lipids and abnormal chylomicron contents, primarily interfering with light scattering and transmission detection by increasing absorbance, thereby affecting the accuracy of numerous assays [14, 25]. The two-step architecture, which first isolates the serum from confounding visual noise (e.g., tube labels and caps), represents an important methodological advantage, ensuring that the classifier analyzes only relevant biological material. The efficacy and scalability of the system were confirmed by deploying it with existing laboratory hardware, where it achieved a mean inference time of <100 msec/sample, which meets the demands of a high-throughput clinical workflow without requiring specialized equipment.

A key finding of our evaluation was the model’s high sensitivity (87%–100%) and specificity (93%–97%), accompanied by comparatively low precision (15%–23%). However, this performance profile is a result of a deliberate clinical design choice rather than a limitation. During the pre-analytical phase, minimizing false negatives is imperative, as processing compromised samples can lead to erroneous test results and adverse patient outcomes. Our design approach directly addresses this pre-analytical concern of issuance of erroneous reports due to undetected abnormal sample quality, which could lead to misdiagnosis and mistreatment. These potential consequences justify prioritizing sensitivity, even at the expense of lower precision, as a fundamental safety measure in the screening process [6]. Consequently, our model was optimized as a high-sensitivity screening tool to flag all potentially problematic samples for further review. This trade-off of accepting more false positives to achieve near-perfect detection of true positives constitutes a robust safety measure. The relatively low precision may have been associated with the inherent complexity of abnormal samples, especially in cases with ambiguous boundarie. This clinical rationale also informed our decision to use a classification framework, which directly mapped outputs to discrete, actionable decisions required in laboratory workflows (e.g., “accept,” “comment,” or “reject”), rather than a regression model predicting continuous index values.

The clinical utility of this AI system lies in its multi-classification capability, which enables a nuanced, automated workflow aligned with practical clinical needs. By providing a graded assessment of HIL severity, the model can support differentiated clinical decision-making. This model assesses interference severity which can not only aid in optimizing laboratory workflow and reducing operational costs but also support specific, differentiated clinical actions such as appending advisory notes for mild interference, recommending cautious interpretation for moderate cases, or suggesting sample rejection for severe cases. Furthermore, by accounting for the variable sensitivity of different test items to HIL, the system offers the potential for personalized prompts, systematically reducing the risk of misjudgments due to poor sample quality. This granular and nuanced functionality addresses key drawbacks of traditional approaches. It helps prevent unrestricted sample rejection and the consequent need of repeated blood collection (which increases patient discomfort, prolongs the reporting cycle, and raises costs) [6]. This functionality is particularly valuable for managing samples from high-acuity settings, such as intensive care units, where a patient’s condition may preclude re-collection. In these instances, the system provides laboratorians with precise data to make informed decisions regarding sample utility, thereby preserving diagnostic integrity while accommodating clinical constraints.

Despite the robust internal validation on a large, temporally distinct dataset, the primary limitation of this study was its single-center design. The model’s generalizability has not yet been confirmed by testing it on external data from other institutions, which could involve variations in patient demographics, pre-analytical procedures, or instrumentation. The design necessitates data security and patient privacy regulations. A prospective, multicenter validation study is essential to establish the model’s external validity before it can be recommended for widespread adoption. Future research must also focus on benchmarking our architecture against newer deep learning models and developing a complementary regression model for predicting continuous HIL index values to further enhancing the system’s clinical applicability and integration with laboratory information systems.
