Synergistic Enhancement of Zinc Electrowinning Performance by Ti2N Interlayer and CeMnOx Powder Modification
Authors: Wentao Wang, Nan Li, Lingjing Yang, Jinlong Wei, Yuantao Yang, Yi Luo, Ruidong Xu, Xuanbing Wang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
In zinc electrowinning, industrial Pb-Ag anodes have inherent limitations, including high oxygen evolution overpotential and rapid corrosion. This study constructs Ti-Ti2N-PbO2-CeMnOx composite anodes to overcome these shortcoming, Electrochemical characterization revealed enhanced performance with a reduced overpotential (725 mV 50 mA cm−2) and lower Tafel slope (102.92 mV dec−1) in the standard zinc electrowinning electrolyte, indicating faster oxygen evolution kinetics compared to commercial benchmarks. Analysis of the XPS test revealed an increase in the content of Mn3+, which helps enhance the OER catalytic activity of the electrode. The Ti/Ti2N/α/β-PbO2-CeMnOx (abbreviation: CMO) composite anode exhibited superior corrosion resistance with an extended service life of 53 h under accelerated polarization at 2 A cm−2. This durability enhancement is attributed to the combined effects of the Ti2N interlayer and CMO incorporation, which effectively mitigate anode degradation through passivation inhibition. The developed fabrication strategy enables the production of dimensionally stable anodes (DSAs) with balanced electrocatalytic activity and operational stability, showing promising potential for industrial zinc electrowinning applications.

1. Introduction

Metallic zinc serves as a critical foundational industrial metal with extensive applications in construction and chemical sectors [1,2,3]. Over the past five years, global zinc production has stabilized at an annual output exceeding 13 million metric tons, surpassing lead to secure the third position in worldwide non-ferrous metal production after aluminum and copper [4]. Zinc hydrometallurgy is the dominant production route for zinc smelting, while the Pb-Ag (0.5~1%) alloy is the most widely used anode material in this industry [5]. Nevertheless, this conventional anode exhibits significant limitations, including suboptimal oxygen evolution reaction (OER) performance and inadequate corrosion resistance [6,7]. Consequently, to reduce the costs of zinc hydrometallurgical processes, it is imperative to develop novel anode materials that integrate high OER catalytic activity with superior corrosion durability.

Based on this foundation, extensive research efforts have been directed toward the development of advanced anode materials. Among the various candidates, PbO2-coated anodes represent a promising class of composite anodes with significant potential [8]. These anodes can be categorized based on the substrate material used, with common types including lead-based [9], aluminum-based [10], stainless steel-based [11], carbon-based [12], and titanium-based substrates [13]. In particular, the titanium substrate has garnered considerable research interest owing to its excellent corrosion resistance [14], favorable electrical conductivity [15], and relatively low cost [16]. Despite these advantages, titanium-based PbO2 coated anodes still face significant challenges: the PbO2 coating tends to peel off from the substrate [17], which can substantially compromise the lifespan and stability of the composite anode [18,19]. To overcome this limitation, researchers often introduce an intermediate layer at the junction of the titanium substrate and the PbO2 coating to boost interfacial adhesion, which can significantly extend the operational lifetime of the composite anode [20,21]. Transition metal nitrides have gained attention in this context owing to their favorable chemical stability and electrical conductivity [22], with synthesis routes including ammonolysis [23], chemical vapor deposition (CVD) [24], and electrodeposition [25], among others [26]. In a typical preparation procedure, a titanium substrate is employed as the titanium source and subjected to thermal treatment in a tube furnace under an ammonia atmosphere. Ammonia not only serves as a nitrogen source but also provides a reducing environment that inhibits high-temperature oxidation of titanium. This process facilitates direct gas-phase nitridation, resulting in the formation of a Ti2N intermediate layer [27,28].

In zinc electrowinning, PbO2 coatings serve as critical anode materials and exist in two primary crystalline phases: α-PbO2 and β-PbO2 [29]. Although lead (Pb) is a toxic substance, it can form a protective layer in sulfuric acid solutions, greatly extending the service life of the anode, and therefore it is still widely used in the field of zinc electroplating. The α-PbO2 phase crystallizes in an orthorhombic structure, typically exhibiting a dense, fine-grained, or layered morphology with a relatively smooth surface. Owing to its favorable interfacial characteristics, α-PbO2 is widely adopted as an interlayer to enhance adhesion between the substrate and the outer active coating [30]. In contrast, β-PbO2 possesses a tetragonal (rutile-type) structure, offers superior corrosion resistance, and exhibits a relatively high overpotential for the oxygen evolution reaction (OER), thereby contributing to improved anode current efficiency during electrolysis [31]. As a result, β-PbO2 is commonly employed as the active outer layer in composite anodes [32]. Its characteristic dendritic or coarse-crystalline morphology provides a large specific surface area, which generally promotes the deposition and activity of catalytic particles. Therefore, during the electrodeposition of β-PbO2, active particles are commonly incorporated into the coating matrix to modify its electrochemical properties. These particles can effectively reduce the oxygen evolution overpotential during zinc electrowinning, thereby enhancing the process efficiency [33]. Based on their composition, active particles are typically categorized into noble metal oxides and non-noble metal oxides [34]. Noble metal oxides include IrO2 and RuO2, which have great advantages in reducing the oxygen evolution overpotential. However, due to production costs, non-noble metal oxides are more favored by researchers [35].

Non-noble metal oxides represent a broad category of electrocatalytic materials, with manganese oxides, cobalt oxides, nickel oxides, and iron oxides being notable examples that demonstrate suitability for the oxygen evolution reaction (OER) in acidic media [36,37]. Catalysts derived from these oxides generally exhibit favorable electrocatalytic activity. Among them, manganese-based oxides have garnered extensive research interest in OER catalysis owing to their multiple accessible oxidation states and structural diversity [38]. Specifically, Mn2O3, characterized by its unique crystallographic structure and abundant surface defects, exhibits distinct bifunctional electrocatalytic properties, making it a candidate material in relevant catalytic systems [39,40,41,42]. Furthermore, cerium oxide (CeO2), known for its rich active sites and exceptional oxygen storage capacity, has been shown to refine the grain structure of the PbO2 active layer when introduced as a dopant [43,44]. This modification contributes to a more favorable anode morphology and enhanced corrosion resistance during operation [45,46].

Building upon these previous studies, the Ti2N intermediate layer was synthesized in this study by the ammonia method, and a layer of α-PbO2 was electrodeposited on it to improve the bonding between β-PbO2 and the Ti2N intermediate layer. Subsequently, β-PbO2 and the prepared CeMnOx powder were co-deposited on Ti/Ti2N/α-PbO2, resulting in a Ti/Ti2N/α-PbO2/β-PbO2-CeMnOx composite anode. Owing to its superior electrochemical performance, the Ti/Ti2N/α-PbO2/β-PbO2-CeMnOx anode achieves a current density of 50 mA cm−2 at a low overpotential of only 725 mV. Furthermore, its accelerated service life tested at 2 A cm−2 is extended to 52 h, significantly longer than that of the Ti/β-PbO2 anode (11 h), manifesting considerably enhanced corrosion resistance.

2. Materials and Methods

2.1. Materials

The following materials were used in this work: Aluminum plate (Al, 20 × 20 × 1 mm3), Titanium plate (Ti, 20 × 10 × 1 mm3), Ammonia (NH3⋅H2O) manganese nitrate hydrate (MnN2O6·4H2O), Cerium nitrate hexahydrate (CeN3O9·6H2O), and zinc sulfate heptahydrate (ZnSO4·7H2O),1,2,4-triazole (C2H3N3), N,N-dimethylformamide (DMF),1,3,5-benzenetricarboxylic acid (C9H6O6), polyvinylpyrrolidone (PVP), methanol (CH3OH), deionized water (DI), Sodium hydroxide (NaOH), lead nitrate (Pb(NO3)2), yellow lead oxide (PbO). Analytical-grade chemical reagents were sourced from Tianjin Fengchuan Chemical Reagent (Tianjin, China) Technology Co., Ltd. (Tianjin, China) and used as received. Deionized water was employed for solution preparation.

2.2. Preparation Processes of Electrode

Preparation of Ti/Ti2N: Prior to formal preparation, the Ti substrate was pre-treated, with detailed procedures provided in the Supporting Information. The pretreated Ti plates were rinsed with absolute ethanol and deionized water, respectively, and then dried at 60 °C. Subsequently, the dried Ti plates were placed in a tube furnace, and a Ti2N interlayer was prepared via chemical vapor deposition (CVD): Ammonia gas (NH3) was introduced, and the system was maintained at 850 °C for 2 h to enable in situ growth of the Ti2N interlayer on the Ti plates.

Preparation of CeMnOx (CMO) Powder and Ti/Ti2N/α/β-CMO composite anode: Solution A was prepared by dissolving 1,3,5-benzenetricarboxylic acid (20 mmol), 1,2,4-triazole (60 mmol) and 3.3 g of polyvinylpyrrolidone (PVP) in a mixed solvent of 40 mL anhydrous methanol and 200 mL N,N-dimethylformamide (DMF). Separately, Solution B was obtained by dissolving Mn(NO3)2·6H2O (10 mmol) and Ce(NO3)3·6H2O (2 mmol) in a dl water-DMF mixture (1:1) Solution B was then slowly added into Solution A under continuous stirring at 50 °C for 20 min. The resulting mixture was sealed and heated at 95 °C for 6 h. After the mixture had cooled to room temperature, we collected the product and washed it three times by centrifugation with anhydrous ethanol and deionized water. It was then dried in an oven at 60 °C for 24 h. As the final step, the obtained powder was annealed at 600 °C for 3 h in a tube furnace to yield the desired material.

A thin α-PbO2 layer was first electrodeposited on the Ti/Ti2N anode (with stainless steel as the cathode) at a current density of 15–20 mA cm−2 under stirring at 40 °C to enhance interfacial adhesion. Subsequently, the β-PbO2-CMO active layer was obtained by electrodeposition at 25 mA cm−2 for 1 h at the same temperature (40 °C) using a mixture of CMO powder and β-PbO2 solution.

2.3. Physical Characterization and Electrochemical Tests

The prepared materials were characterized using various techniques. X-ray diffraction (XRD, D/MAX-2200, Rigaku Corporation, Tokyo, Japan) with monochromatic Cu-Kα radiation (λ ≈ 0.15 nm) was employed to analyze the crystal structure. Morphological and elemental analyses were conducted using field emission scanning electron microscopy (NanoSEM-450, FEI Company, Hillsboro, OR, USA) coupled with an energy dispersive spectrometer (EDS). The chemical states and composition were investigated by X-ray photoelectron spectroscopy (XPS, PHI500 and PHI 5500, Physical Electronics (PHI), Chanhassen, MN, USA) with X-ray photons (1–1.5 keV), while the microstructure of the powder was further examined using transmission electron microscopy (TEM, Tecnai G2 F30, FEI Company, Hillsboro, OR, USA).

All experiments were performed using a three-electrode system in an electrolyte containing 50 g L−1 Zn2+ and 150 g L−1 H2SO4 to investigate the electrochemical performance of the composite anode. All electrochemical measurements were conducted at 35 °C, with the fabricated composite anode as the working electrode (1 cm2), an aluminum plate (4 cm2) as the counter electrode, and a saturated calomel electrode (SCE) and Mercury/Mercurous Sulfate Electrode (MSE) as the reference electrode. Electrochemical characterization was performed using multiple techniques. Linear sweep voltammetry (LSV) and cyclic voltammetry (CV) measurements were conducted at a scan rate of 5 mV s−1. Electrochemical impedance spectroscopy (EIS) was carried out over a frequency range spanning from 105 to 0.1 Hz.

3.1. Physicochemical Characterization of the Ti/Ti2N Intermediate Layer

The crystal structure of the Ti2N deposit was examined by XRD, as shown in Figure 1a. The main diffraction peaks appeared at 25.45°, 34.66°, 36.30°, 39.28°, 40.77°, 51.06°, 52.28°, 61.03°, 67.30°, 73.12° and 76.01°, which were assigned to the (110), (101), (200), (111), (210), (211), (220), (002), (311), (202) and (212) planes of Ti2N (JCPDF#76-0198). These results demonstrate that the intermediate layer is Ti2N. Moreover, no characteristic peaks of metallic titanium were detected in the XRD, indicating that a uniform and dense Ti2N film was prepared on the surface of the titanium substrate.

Figure 1b presents the morphological characteristics of the pretreated titanium plates before and after high-temperature nitridation. As observed, the pretreated plates display a grooved, mountain-range-like surface topography. Previous studies have indicated that such a textured morphology can substantially improve the bonding force of the substrate. Following drying, the pretreated plates were subjected to calcination in a tube furnace at 850 °C under an ammonia atmosphere for several hours. After nitridation, the originally silver-colored surface exhibited a distinct golden-yellow hue, which aligns with reported color characteristics of Ti2N [47]. Although partial surface melting occurred due to the elevated temperature, the overall roughness and grooved topography were largely preserved. The elemental mapping (EDS) also confirmed a homogeneous distribution of titanium and nitrogen across the modified surface (Figure S1), with the proportions of Ti and N being 89.4% and 10.6%, respectively.

To gain further insight into its chemical composition and valence states, the Ti2N intermediate layer was subjected to XPS analysis. In Figure 1c, High-resolution Ti 2p spectra exhibit characteristic spin–orbit doublet structures. The spin–orbit splitting energy of Ti 2p3/2 and Ti 2p1/2 is approximately 5.7 eV, with an area ratio of 2:1. Figure 1d shows N 1s spectrum exhibits two distinct components. The dominant peak at 396.80 eV corresponds to nitrogen in a Ti–N bonding environment, characteristic of the Ti2N phase. The minor component at 399.22 eV is attributed to nitrogen atoms incorporated into an oxynitride structure, formed at the surface region. No significant signal from oxidized nitrogen species is observed, indicating minimal surface contamination [48]. The deconvolution of the Ti 2p spectrum reveals a chemically graded surface architecture characterized by a sequential variation in composition from the bulk to the outermost region. The coexistence of three distinct Ti chemical states indicates a non-uniform surface stratification comprising. This graded structure is commonly observed in transition metal nitrides exposed to ambient conditions and explains the golden-yellow appearance of the sample, where the color of the underlying layer Ti2N is visible through the thin oxide overlayer. The survey spectrum and the high-resolution O 1s spectrum of Ti2N are presented in Figure S2.

3.2. Physical Characterization of Powders

The SEM image in Figure 2a shows that the prepared CeMnOx powder exhibits a prismatic structure with pillar diameters of 200–300 nm. Further elemental mapping analysis revealed that the elements Ce, Mn, and O were evenly distributed, with respective proportions of 61%, 11.8%, and 27.2% (Figure S3). The 0.306 nm and 0.184 nm lattice spacings observed in Figure 2b correspond to the (311) planes of CeO2 and the (222) planes of Mn2O3, respectively. In addition, the SAED transformed by Fourier transform (FFT) (Figure 2c) confirms the polycrystalline nature of the powder. Based on the sizes of the polycrystalline diffraction rings, they can be assigned to the (311) planes of CeO2. These planes are consistent with the X-ray diffraction analysis results below.

Through XRD analysis of the powder (Figure 3d), The XRD pattern exhibits diffraction peaks at 2θ values of 28.55°, 33.01°, 47.48°, 56.33°, 69.4°, and 76.70° correspond to the crystal planes (111), (200), (220), (311), (400), and (331) of the CeO2 standard card (JCPDS 34-0394). The 2θ diffraction peaks at approximately 33.01° and 55.14° correspond to the crystal planes (222) and (044) of the Mn2O3 standard card (JCPDS 24-0508).

In addition, this work also conducted XPS spectrum analysis of CeMnOx powder, which is mainly composed of three elements: Ce, Mn, and O (Figure S4). In the high-resolution Mn 2p spectrum (Figure 2e), it consists of two standard spin–orbit peaks. In Mn 2p3/2, 642.07 eV and 640.62 eV correspond to the signature peaks of Mn3+ and Mn2+, respectively. In Mn 2p1/2, 653.63 eV and 652.09 eV correspond to the signature peaks of Mn3+ and Mn2+, respectively. The Ce 3d XPS spectrum clearly indicates the coexistence of Ce4+ and Ce3+ species (Figure 2f), with the characteristic peaks at positions 2, (2′), 4, (4′), 5, (5′) assigned to Ce4+, while those at 1, (1′), 3, (3′) are attributed to Ce3+. The presence of Ce3+ is closely associated with the formation of oxygen vacancies (OV) to maintain charge balance, which are expected to enhance the oxygen evolution reaction (OER) activity. This is further corroborated by the O 1s spectrum (Figure 2g), which can be deconvoluted into three components: the lattice oxygen (OL) at lower binding energy, the component associated with oxygen vacancies (OV) at intermediate energy, and the surface-adsorbed oxygen species (Oads) at higher energy. Notably, the relative content of the Oads component is the lowest, suggesting a limited surface concentration of active hydroxyl groups, which may correlate with a reduced capacity for generating hydroxyl radicals (*OH). Specifically, the peak located at 530.79 eV in the O 1s spectrum, corresponding to the OV component, provides direct evidence for the existence of oxygen vacancies (Table S2).

3.3. Characterization of PbO2 Layer

To gain insight into the morphological and microstructural features of the fabricated PbO2 anodes, SEM images of the pristine PbO2 layer and the composite PbO2-CeMnOx layer are presented in Figure 3a and Figure 3b, respectively. As shown in Figure 3a, the surface of the pure PbO2 electrode displays a compact, characteristic pyramidal morphology. The underlying Ti2N interlayer is not visible, confirming that a continuous PbO2 coating was successfully electrodeposited onto the substrate. In contrast, Figure 3b illustrates the co-deposited PbO2-CeMnOx composite, where CeMnOx particles are uniformly embedded within the PbO2 matrix while retaining their original particulate shape. By increasing the specific surface area and providing additional active sites on the electrode surface, the incorporation of CeMnOx is expected to promote the oxygen evolution reaction (OER). Furthermore, the modification with CeMnOx leads to a noticeable reduction in the size of PbO2 crystallites, likely due to a grain-refining effect induced by the introduced Ce species. This refined microstructure increases the contact area between the anode and electrolyte, leading to enhanced electrochemical performance.

Figure 3c displays the X-ray diffraction pattern of the PbO2 active layer. The diffraction peaks at 25.43°, 31.97°, 36.19°, 49.04°, and 62.45° correspond to the (110), (101), (200), (211), and (301) crystal planes of β-PbO2 (JCPDF41-1492). Figure 3d shows peaks of Pb 4f7/2 and Pb 4f5/2 at 136.9 eV and 142.5 eV, confirming that the electrode surface consists of β-PbO2 and CeMnOx.

3.4. Electrochemical Behavior of Composite Electrodes

LSV was utilized to assess the contribution of the Ti2N interlayer and CeMnOx powder to the electrocatalytic activity. The Ti/α/β-PbO2 anode without powder and interlayer modification had an overpotential of 1055 mV at 50 mA cm−2 (Figure S5), whereas the Ti/Ti2N/α/β-PbO2 anode with the Ti2N interlayer had an overpotential of 971 mV, a reduction of 84 mV. This indicates that Ti2N has good conductivity and can improve the electrocatalytic activity of the anode to some extent. Figure 4a shows the LSV curves of Ti/Ti2N/α/β-PbO2 anodes modified with different powders. Overall, the overpotential shows a decreasing trend. Notably, the overpotential at 50 mA cm-2 for Ti/Ti2N/α/β-PbO-CeO2 (951 mV) is comparable to that of Ti/Ti2N/α/β-PbO2 (971 mV). The overpotential of the Mn2O3-modified Ti/Ti2N/α/β-PbO2 anode (785 mV) is much lower than that of the Ti/Ti2N/α/β-PbO2 anode, demonstrating excellent electrocatalytic activity. In addition, the Ti/Ti2N/α/β-PbO2-CMO anode displayed the lowest overpotential of 725 mV at 50 mA cm−2. Notably, a significant difference in overpotential emerged among the electrodes when the current density reached 100 mA cm−2. At 100 mA cm−2, the Ti/Ti2N/α/β-PbO2-CMO anode still showed the lowest potential. Therefore, the CeMnOx composite powder catalyst demonstrated superior electrocatalytic activity compared to single-element catalysts.

To study the OER kinetics, the Tafel slopes were calculated, as illustrated in Figure 4b. The Tafel slopes for the Ti/α/β-PbO2, Ti/Ti2N/α/β-PbO2, Ti/Ti2N/α/β-PbO2-Mn2O3, and Ti/Ti2N/α/β-PbO2-CeMnOx anodes were 345.82 mV dec−1, 343.15 mV dec−1, 237.66 mV dec−1, and 102.92 mV dec−1, respectively. The Ti/Ti2N/α/β-PbO2-CMO electrode displayed the smallest Tafel slope (102.92 mV dec−1) among all electrodes, indicating its superior OER kinetics in acidic media.

The Nyquist plots shown in Figure 4c compare different types of electrodes. The resistance of the Ti/β-PbO2 electrode (3.78 Ω) is three times that of the Ti/Ti2N/α/β-PbO2 electrode (1.24 Ω), indicating that the introduction of the Ti2N intermediate layer not only enhances the adhesion of the anode but also reduces the electrode impedance. Furthermore, after modification with Mn2O3 and CeMnOx powder catalysts, the impedance decreased to 1.01 Ω and 0.54 Ω, respectively, indicating that the Ti/Ti2N/α/β-PbO2-CMO anode has lower charge transfer resistance and facilitates the oxygen evolution reaction (OER).

Beyond catalytic activity, stability is another essential metric for determining the suitability of an electrode. The corrosion resistance of the tested anodes was evaluated from the Tafel curves shown in Figure 4d, based on the corrosion current density (Icorr) derived from the equation. In Table S3, the Ti/Ti2N/α/β-PbO2-CMO anode has the lowest corrosion current (1.28 × 10−5 A cm−2), indicating that it has the best corrosion resistance. At the same time, open-circuit potential can also be used to evaluate the corrosion performance of materials. Electrodes without the Ti2N interlayer and powder modification have higher open-circuit potentials.

To justify the stability of the electrodes, a 24-h constant current test (50 mA cm−2) was conducted. Although the voltage of the Ti/Ti2N/α/β anode with the intermediate layer was higher than that of the Ti/β-PbO2 electrode, it remained more stable throughout the test, indicating that the fabrication of the Ti2N intermediate layer improved electrode stability. Upon the addition of CeMnOx powder, the modified electrode exhibited a slight decrease in voltage over time while maintaining a stable curve. Taken together with the aforementioned data, these results indicate that the incorporation of both the intermediate layer and the CeMnOx powder effectively lowered the oxygen evolution overpotential and improved the stability of the composite electrode.

Furthermore, Figure 4f also compares the electrochemical performance of zinc electrodeposition using different types of anodes. The results display that the Ti/Ti2N/α/β-PbO2-CeMnOx electrode synthesized in this work requires a lower overpotential (725 mV) and a lower Tafel slope (102.92 mV dec−1) to reach the benchmark current density at 50 mA cm−2. This phenomenon is ascribed to the presence of the CeMnOx catalyst, which promotes the OER catalytic activity of the electrode.

The cell voltages are shown in Figure 5a. The cell voltages of Ti/β-PbO2, Ti/Ti2N/α/β-PbO2, Ti/Ti2N/α/β-PbO2-Mn2O3, and Ti/Ti2N/α/β-PbO2-CMO are 3.21 V, 3.11 V, 2.98 V, and 2.90 V, respectively. In the initial stage of electrolysis, the cell voltage shows a decreasing trend, which may be due to the activation process occurring on the electrode surface. The cell voltage of Ti/β-PbO2 is about 3.21 V, indicating its poor electrochemical performance and high energy consumption. After introducing the Ti2N intermediate layer, the cell voltage decreased by about 0.1 V, suggesting that the introduction of the intermediate layer improves the electrocatalytic performance of the anode. The introduction of Mn2O3 active particles and CMO composite active particles further reduces the cell voltage. Among them, the cell voltage of the Ti/Ti2N/α/β-PbO2-CMO anode drops to 2.90 V, highlighting its excellent electrocatalytic activity.

Besides electrocatalytic activity, the lifespan of electrode is also a significant factor in evaluating zinc electro-deposited anodes. Given that a longer electrode lifespan ensures sustained production and reduced costs, the accelerated corrosion lifetime of the composite electrodes was investigated under harsh conditions. The test was conducted in a 150 g L−1 H2SO4 solution at 35 °C with an applied current density of 2 A cm−2. The voltage increased significantly until electrode failure (battery voltage exceeding 10 V). The accelerated corrosion lifetimes of four electrodes were 11 h, 21 h, 35 h, and 53 h, respectively. This test further demonstrates that the modification of the Ti2N intermediate layer and the CeMnOx powder can enhance the adhesion between the titanium substrate and the PbO2 layer, effectively improving the service life of the anode (Figure 5b).

4. Conclusions

In summary, this study prepared Ti/Ti2N electrodes with excellent conductivity and binding strength using the ammonolysis method. The Ti/Ti2N/α/β-PbO2 was modified with CeMnOx powder doped catalysts to reduce the overpotential and energy consumption of the electrode during zinc electrodeposition. The Ti/Ti2N/α/β-PbO2-CeMnOx exhibited favorable electrochemical performance at 50 mA cm−2 (725 mV and 102.92 mV dec−1), with a cell voltage (2.92 V) significantly lower than that of the Pb0.76%Ag anode (3.29 V). Furthermore, under harsh acidic conditions at a current density of 2 A cm−2, the accelerated service life of the Ti/Ti2N/α/β-PbO2-CeMnOx composite electrode reached 53 h. Therefore, this work provides a new method for the preparation of electrodes for acidic OER processes and zinc electrodeposition.
