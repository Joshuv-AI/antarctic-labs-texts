Physicians' Perceptions of Prescribing in Pregnancy and Package Inserts: A Survey of General and Hub Hospitals in Japan
Authors: Mikako Goto, Naho Yakuwa, Atsuko Murashima
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTThis study investigated how Japanese physicians make decisions about prescribing medications during pregnancy, based on safety information provided in drug package inserts. An online questionnaire survey targeted physicians working at pregnancy hub hospitals with expertise in drug treatment during pregnancy and physicians in general hospitals without such specialization. The findings revealed significant differences in prescribing behavior and perceptions of safety. Physicians at hub hospitals were more likely to view certain medications as safe for pregnant individuals when supported by epidemiological data, even if such drugs were contraindicated based on preclinical studies. In contrast, physicians in general hospitals followed the wording in the package inserts more strictly and often avoided prescribing. Physicians from both groups strongly needed more detailed descriptions of therapeutic benefits and epidemiological evidence in the pregnancy sections of package inserts. The results suggest that current drug labeling in Japan does not provide sufficient information for evidence‐based risk–benefit decision‐making in pregnancy care. Differences in prescribing behavior were attributed to disparities in training and access to specialized information. The study highlights the importance of revising Japanese package inserts to include more precise descriptions of clinical benefit and human data to improve the quality of medication decision‐making during pregnancy and reduce unnecessary contraindications. Improved access to training and updated information may help close the gap between general and specialized physicians and promote more consistent and appropriate care for pregnant individuals across healthcare settings.

Introduction

Ensuring the safety of medication use during pregnancy is a critical clinical challenge, yet significant gaps in safety data and clinical guidance persist worldwide. A study on drug use during pregnancy targeted approximately 100,000 pregnant women in Japan. The findings revealed that 78.4% of pregnant women reported using medication or supplements 1 year before pregnancy, 57.1% used medication from confirmation of pregnancy to 12 weeks gestation, and 68.8% used medication after 12 weeks gestation [1]. According to a questionnaire survey we previously conducted among Japanese physicians, the majority of physicians cited package inserts as their primary source of information when prescribing drugs to pregnant patients [2].

In Japan, as in many other countries, information about drug safety during pregnancy is based primarily on animal reproduction studies because pregnant women have historically been excluded from clinical trials to protect them from potential fetal risks [3]. However, there is a growing international consensus shifting from ‘protecting pregnant women from research’ to ‘protecting them through research’ by actively generating evidence‐based safety data [4], addressing the current lack of international harmonization in drug labeling [5]. Despite the publication of subsequent epidemiological studies on pregnant populations, the package inserts are not actively reviewed and updated with the new insights. Drug labels are under the jurisdiction of the Food and Drug Administration (FDA) in the United States and the European Medicines Agency (EMA) in Europe. While drug package inserts are derived from the same epidemiological studies, substantial differences exist in their labeling frameworks. The EMA, following the Guideline on Summary of Product Characteristics (SmPC), often uses direct, prescriptive expressions such as “avoid using” or “contraindication,” which can restrict drug use during pregnancy and lactation. In contrast, the FDA's Pregnancy and Lactation Labeling Rule (PLLR), implemented in 2015, replaced the old letter categories with narrative summaries. This terminology is designed to provide clinicians with detailed data on fetal risk and clinical considerations, enabling them to independently assess the benefits and risks for individual patients. In Japan, the Pharmaceuticals and Medical Devices Agency (PMDA) updated the descriptions in the pregnancy section of package inserts in 2019 to provide more detailed safety information; however, many descriptions still remain standardized compared to the FDA's narrative summaries. Approximately 20% or more of Japanese package inserts are contraindicated during pregnancy, with a higher percentage of contraindications in Japanese package inserts compared to those of the United States [6]. Currently, Japanese package inserts lack comprehensive information on epidemiological studies related to the efficacy and safety of treatments; however, epidemiological studies of risk information may be found randomly.

In this study, we aimed to examine the decision‐making process of physicians in Japan in relation to the pregnancy‐related information in package inserts, which remains a primary but often insufficient source of information due to vague or outdated content. We also sought to analyze the disparities in decision‐making among physicians who were trained on pregnancy medications and those who were not. Our aim was to evaluate the necessary information included in the pregnancy section of package inserts in Japan, with the intention of informing future improvements and potential applications. This approach allows us to evaluate the study findings within a pharmacological and regulatory framework, focusing on how risk–benefit communication can be improved in clinical practice.

The Japan Drug Information Institute in Pregnancy of the National Center for Child Health and Development (hereinafter referred to as the Center) has placed at least one hub medical facility per prefecture in all 47 prefectures in Japan [7], establishing a counseling system for drug treatment during pregnancy. The goal of the Center is to eliminate health disparities throughout Japan by sharing the safety information database of drug use during pregnancy and providing skill training and education required for counseling on a regular basis. In this study, we conducted an online questionnaire survey to elucidate awareness and information requirements regarding drug administration during pregnancy among physicians specialized in pregnancy and medication, as well as those who are not.

Materials and Methods

The survey for hub hospitals was conducted online from March 4 to March 18, 2024, by sending an email link to 210 physicians via the JDIIP mailing list using an email distribution system (Kohmu‐ra Co. Ltd., Aichi, Japan). For general physicians, the survey was administered between December 21 and December 25, 2023, through an online physician panel (MyVoice Communications Inc., Tokyo, Japan) targeting 400 responses. In both groups, recruitment was closed once the target numbers or deadlines were reached, and no reminders were sent to the participants. The questions consisted of approximately 20 items, covering physician demographics, clinical decision‐making processes, and requirements for safety information. Among those, five key items directly addressing the relationship between package insert descriptions and prescribing behavior were selected for this analysis.

We used the chi‐square test to verify significant differences in the distribution of answers between the two groups. The chi‐square test was conducted using the SciPy library (Python, version 3.13.1). The significance level was set to 0.05, and a p value < 0.05 was considered statistically significant. To account for the multiplicity of comparisons across the survey items, the significance level was adjusted using the Bonferroni correction. Fisher's exact test was used when there were cells in the table that had expected counts that were less than 5. To compare the decision‐making between the two groups (Table 3), the data were analyzed using univariate logistic regression with the statsmodels library to calculate crude odds ratios (ORs) and 95% confidence intervals (CIs). For this analysis, response categories collapsed into a binary outcome: “positive prescribing behavior” (combining “administer the drug” and “make a decision based on the needs”) and “cautious behavior” (combining “avoid administration as much as possible” and “not administer the drug”), with general hospitals as the reference category (OR = 1.0). Only complete responses were included (no missing data), and adjustments for multiple comparisons were made using the Bonferroni correction as described above. This study was approved by the Ethics Committee of the National Center for Child Health and Development (approval number: 2023–233), and informed consent was obtained from all participating physicians.

Results

Responses were obtained from 78 of the 210 invited physicians in the hub hospitals (response rate: 37.1%). The survey for general hospitals was closed exactly when the target of 400 responses was reached. In general hospitals, 48.8% (n = 195/400) of respondents were experienced physicians with over 30 years of experience, while 42.3% (n = 33/78) of those in hub hospitals were in their mid‐career stage with 10–19 years of experience (Table 1). Regarding specialties, 91.0% (n = 71/78) of hub hospital physicians were obstetrics and gynecology specialists, whereas internal medicine specialists were the largest group in general hospitals, accounting for 36.5% (n = 146/400). In both groups, the respondents were primarily staff physicians employed at university or general hospitals (100%, n = 78/78 in hub; 87.0%, n = 348/400 in general) (Table 1).

In terms of drugs with contraindications for pregnant individuals based on reproduction studies but no risk in epidemiological studies (despite contraindication on labeling), a notable disparity was observed between healthcare professionals at the hub hospital and those at the general hospital. Specifically, 61.5% of hub hospital doctors viewed these drugs as mostly safe, in contrast to only 22.8% (n = 91/400) of general hospital doctors who shared this perspective (p < 0.001, Figure 1). Regarding prescription for pregnant women requiring such medication, 53% of the hub hospital doctors answered, “I will make a decision based on the needs,” and 23.1% answered, “I will administer the drug,” but 24.5% of general hospital doctors answered “I will make a decision based on the needs” and 4.8% answered “I will administer the drug,” also showing a marked difference between the two groups (p < 0.001, 0.05 Figure 2).

Discussion

The results of this study indicated a substantial disparity in the awareness regarding drug administration during pregnancy among physicians in hub and general hospitals. The physicians in the hub hospitals actively considered drug administration for pregnant patients based on epidemiology research, whereas those in general hospitals tended to be more cautious not to overstep the instructions specified on package inserts. This difference between the two groups is attributed to the accumulation of specialized knowledge and experience in the hub hospitals and the well‐developed information system of the Center. Our findings are consistent with a previous study by Dude et al. [8], which investigated US physicians' perceptions of drug labeling. While US clinicians similarly reported challenges in interpreting fetal risk, the study highlighted that narrative summaries, such as those introduced by the FDA's PLLR, are preferred over categorical labeling because they facilitate a more nuanced risk–benefit assessment. In Japan, the heavy reliance on preclinical animal data often leads to “contraindicated” status in package inserts, even when human epidemiological data suggest safety. This discrepancy creates a significant barrier for general physicians, who, as shown in our results, tend to adhere strictly to the written contraindications to avoid liability. Furthermore, the disparity between hubs and general hospitals underscores the need for Shared Decision Making (SDM). As Dude et al. emphasized [8], detailed clinical considerations in labeling allow physicians to communicate more effectively with patients about uncertainty. By incorporating real‐world evidence and clearer descriptions of therapeutic benefits into Japanese package inserts, we can empower non‐specialized physicians to move beyond reflexive avoidance of medication and engage in informed SDM, ultimately ensuring that pregnant women are not denied necessary treatment due to outdated labeling. In Japan, there have been minimal revisions to package inserts for medications post‐market release. However, our study indicates a strong need for updated information to be included in package inserts, as evidenced by high demand from both groups. Following the American package inserts, adding information on therapeutic benefits and results of epidemiology research to the existing package inserts may enhance physicians' consideration of drug use during pregnancy by providing comprehensive, evidence‐based information. This in turn can facilitate informed decision‐making about the administration of medication to pregnant individuals based on risk–benefit assessment.

This study had some limitations. First, it is possible that among the 400 physicians from general hospitals who participated in our survey, some may have had training in drug treatment during pregnancy. This potential inclusion of physicians with this experience, due to the lack of specific questioning on their training background, could have influenced the study results. Second, we collected responses from 400 physicians from general hospitals; however, the response rate of the physicians in the hub hospitals was low. This potential confounding factor could impact the accuracy of the data.

In conclusion, there has been a marked increase in both gestational age and prevalence of chronic diseases among pregnant women. However, most internal medicine practitioners and specialists have limited opportunities to gain substantial expertise in the field of gynecology and obstetrics. A lack of understanding of an attending physician regarding the most recent developments in drug treatment during pregnancy may lead to difficulty in appropriate decision‐making regarding drug administration for pregnant women with chronic diseases. It is evident that physicians, regardless of their attributions, require additional information on epidemiology research and treatment benefits included in Japanese package inserts to enhance the decision‐making process and risk–benefit assessment.
