Prefrontal Hemodynamics Underlie the Synergistic Effect of Tai Chi Combined with Transcranial Direct Current Stimulation on Memory in Patients with MCI: An Exploratory fNIRS Study
Authors: Li Liufu, Mengyuan Wu, Peiling Zeng, Yuping Yang, Cong Chen, Jia Huang, Shufang Lin, Hong Liu, Ying Xu
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractObjectiveMemory dysfunction is a major concern in patients with mild cognitive impairment (MCI) and is closely associated with changes in prefrontal cortex (PFC) hemodynamics. Tai Chi (TC) combined with transcranial direct current stimulation (tDCS) has been shown to improve MCI-related memory deficits, but the underlying neural mechanisms remain unclear. This study aimed to investigate which hemodynamic changes are attributable to the individual effects of TC or tDCS, and which alterations arise from their synergistic interaction.MethodsIn this randomized clinical trial, 128 patients with MCI were assigned to one of four groups: TC combined with tDCS, TC alone, tDCS alone, or health education. Outcome measures included memory scale scores and prefrontal hemodynamic activity assessed using functional near-infrared spectroscopy (fNIRS), measured at baseline and after 12 weeks.ResultsThe combined TC and tDCS intervention produced a significant synergistic interaction effect on verbal memory (P = 0.032), as well as on changes in oxyhemoglobin (HbO2) concentrations across multiple prefrontal regions. These included the right orbitofrontal cortex during resting state (P = 0.026); the left dorsolateral prefrontal cortex (DLPFC) during the 0-back (P = 0.012), 1-back (P < 0.001), and 2-back tasks (P = 0.003); the left frontopolar cortex during the 0-back (P = 0.003), 1-back (P = 0.008), and 2-back tasks (P = 0.020); the right frontopolar cortex during the 1-back task (P = 0.017); and the left ventrolateral prefrontal cortex during the 1-back task (P = 0.041). Correlation analysis revealed a positive association between changes in HbO2 concentrations in the left DLPFC during the 1-back task and verbal memory performance.ConclusionsTC combined with tDCS may synergistically enhance verbal memory in patients with MCI through modulation of HbO2 concentrations in the left DLPFC.Trial registrationClinicalTrials.gov Identifier: ChiCTR2200059316Key words: Memory function, Mild cognitive impairment, Prefrontal Cortex, Tai Chi, Transcranial direct current stimulation, Functional near-infrared spectroscopy

Introduction

Mild cognitive impairment (MCI), characterized predominantly by memory decline, offers a critical therapeutic window within the Alzheimer's disease (AD) continuum. Globally, 15.5% of adults aged ≥50 years have MCI (Bai et al., 2022), and 10%–15% progress to dementia annually (Tahami Monfared et al., 2022). The World Alzheimer Report 2025 projects that the global number of people with dementia will rise from 55 million in 2019 to 139 million by 2050 (World Alzheimer Report 2025, 2025). Therefore, it is critically important to identify interventions that improve memory function in patients with MCI and to elucidate their underlying mechanisms.

The prefrontal cortex (PFC) is a critical brain region for regulating cognitive functions (Perus et al., 2022). Its hemodynamic activity is closely linked to memory performance (Karthikeyan et al., 2024). Studies have shown that during memory tasks, healthy older adults exhibit an increase in PFC oxyhemoglobin (HbO2) concentrations (Lapanan et al., 2023), whereas patients with MCI demonstrate abnormal activation patterns. Specifically, compared with healthy controls, patients with MCI show either significantly elevated or reduced HbO2 concentrations in the PFC, particularly in the left PFC (Yang et al., 2025). Furthermore, mean HbO2 concentrations in the left dorsolateral prefrontal cortex (DLPFC) decrease sequentially across the subjective cognitive decline (SCD), MCI, and AD groups (Zhang et al., 2024). This decline is accompanied by a parallel reduction in PFC functional connectivity, which correlates with cognitive decline (Pu et al., 2025). Therefore, abnormal hemodynamics in the PFC may represent a key factor contributing to memory deterioration in patients with MCI, and timely modulation of this region could help delay the progression of memory impairment.

Transcranial direct current stimulation (tDCS), is a non-invasive neuromodulation technique that modulates cortical excitability by targeting specific brain regions (Sudbrack-Oliveira et al., 2021). Studies have demonstrated that tDCS improves memory function in patients with MCI (Wu et al., 2025). For example, applying tDCS over the right DLPFC enhanced memory performance and increased right DLPFC HbO2 concentrations during memory tasks (M et al., 2023). However, interindividual variability in tDCS efficacy remains a major clinical challenge (Arar, 2025; Van Hoornweder, Stagg and Wischnewski, 2025). Recent research indicates that personalized dosing strategies improve tDCS consistency by enhancing cortical excitability (Bhattacharjee et al., 2025). Similarly, combining tDCS with other cognitive interventions may further improve consistency by optimizing the neural environment for tDCS effects.

Tai Chi (TC), a traditional Chinese Daoyin therapy, has also been shown to enhance memory function (Hong et al., 2024). Studies indicate that TC significantly increases HbO2 concentrations in the bilateral DLPFC and ventrolateral prefrontal cortex (VLPFC) (Qi et al., 2023). Furthermore, after nine weeks of TC practice, older adults exhibited elevated PFC HbO2 concentrations during cognitive tasks (Chen et al., 2025). Therefore, TC may serve as a complementary intervention to tDCS. By promoting synergistic hemodynamic regulation, TC could create a favorable neuroplastic environment for tDCS, thereby enhancing overall therapeutic efficacy.

In recent years, numerous studies have suggested that combined interventions may produce synergistic effects (Talar et al., 2022). Our preliminary research has provided initial evidence that TC combined with tDCS improves memory function in patients with MCI (Xu et al., 2023). However, the neural mechanisms underlying this synergistic effect remain unclear. Specifically, it is unknown which hemodynamic changes following the intervention are attributable to the main effects of TC or tDCS alone, and which alterations result from their synergistic interaction. To our knowledge, no relevant studies have been reported in this area.

Therefore, we employed a factorial randomized controlled trial design to investigate the effects of TC combined with tDCS on memory performance in patients with MCI. Using functional near-infrared spectroscopy (fNIRS) (Lee et al., 2024), we further analyzed the underlying hemodynamic regulatory mechanisms. Our aim was to clarify the distinct contributions of each intervention as well as their synergistic mechanisms, thereby providing scientific evidence for precise rehabilitation strategies for patients with MCI.

Method

Design Overview

We conducted a 2 × 2 factorial randomized controlled trial to investigate the memory-enhancing effects of TC, tDCS, or both in patients with MCI. Changes in HbO2 concentrations in PFC subregions were analyzed using functional near-infrared spectroscopy (fNIRS). The study protocol was approved by the Rehabilitation Hospital of Fujian University of Traditional Chinese Medicine (approval No. 2022KY-002-01), and all participants provided written informed consent. The trial was registered with the Chinese Clinical Trial Registry (ChiCTR2200059316).

Participants

MCI was diagnosed based on Petersen’s criteria, which required: 1) cognitive impairment reported by the patient, an informant, or a clinician; 2) deficits in at least one cognitive domain (memory, language, visuospatial or executive function); 3) essentially preserved activities of daily living; 4) the absence of dementia.

Inclusion criteria required participants to: 1) meet the MCI diagnostic criteria; 2) have a MoCA score between 18 and 26; 3) be aged 50-75 years; 4) have no history of regular exercise in the preceding 6 months.

Exclusion criteria were: 1) a score of ≥9 on the Geriatric Depression Scale (GDS-15); 2) uncontrolled hypertension (defined as systolic blood pressure >160 mm Hg or diastolic blood pressure >100 mm Hg despite medication); 3) conditions precluding exercise adherence, including motor dysfunction due to neurologic (stroke, Parkinson’s disease) or musculoskeletal disorders (lower limb arthritis, history of hip or knee replacement), severe organ disease or other exercise contraindications; 4) severe aphasia, audiovisual impairment, or illiteracy that would prevent completion of assessments; 5) concurrent participation in other conflicting research trials; 6) use of medications that could significantly alter cognitive function within the past 6 months.

Randomization and Masking

A total of 128 eligible participants were randomly allocated to one of four groups in a 1:1:1:1 ratio: TC combined with tDCS, TC, tDCS, and a health education (HE) group. The randomization sequence was generated by specialized statistical personnel using SPSS (version 24; IBM, Chicago, IL, USA) and concealed using opaque, sealed envelopes. A designated staff member, who was not involved in recruitment, outcome assessment, or data analysis, managed this allocation sequence. This was a single-blind trial in which only outcome assessors and statisticians were blinded to group assignments. The groups were labeled as A, B, C, and D. The blinding protocol, established via SPSS 24, involved password-protected files that were securely maintained by a custodian according to the random sequence. The custodian revealed the sequence only after the final statistical analysis was completed.

Interventions

All participants first received health education on the etiology and prevention of cognitive impairment (including MCI and AD) and the promotion of a healthy lifestyle. The 12-week intervention began immediately after baseline data collection.

TC combined with tDCS group Participants received concurrent TC and tDCS interventions three times per week for 12 weeks. tDCS was administered during the first 20 minutes of each TC session. The TC component used the 24-form style, led by two certified instructors (each with ≥5 years of experience), and included a 10-minute warm-up, 40 minutes of practice, and a 10-minute cool-down. The tDCS component used the Brain Stimulator v3.0 Deluxe Kit, with the anode and cathode positioned over the right DLPFC and left superior orbit, respectively, according to the international 10-20 system. A 2-mA current was applied for 20 minutes.

TC group Participants received only the TC intervention three times per week for 12 weeks. The protocol was identical to the TC component described for the combined group.

tDCS group Participants received only the tDCS intervention three times per week for 12 weeks. The equipment, electrode placement, parameters, and procedure were identical to the tDCS component described for the combined group (Liao et al., 2021).

Health Education group Participants received health education sessions once every 4 weeks over the 12-week study period.

Assessments and Outcomes

Outcome assessment This study employed a comprehensive assessment protocol to collect baseline characteristics, measure primary and secondary outcomes, and acquire fNIRS data. Baseline data included age, sex, educational level, and medical history. Outcome measures were assessed at baseline and immediately after the 12-week intervention. Memory function was evaluated using a battery of neuropsychological assessments, including the Chinese Wechsler Memory Scale-Revised (WMS-RC), the Auditory Verbal Learning Test (AVLT), and the Rey-Osterrieth Complex Figure Test (ROCF). Prefrontal hemodynamic activity was monitored via fNIRS at both assessment time points, under both resting-state and task-state conditions. All evaluations were conducted by trained assessors blinded to group assignment. The primary outcomes were defined as the change in memory test results from baseline to week 12, along with changes in β weights derived from fNIRS data. These β weights represent hemodynamic activity during both resting-state and task-state conditions, as estimated by a general linear model.

Primary outcome The WMS-RC is a widely adopted instrument for comprehensive memory assessment (Wan et al., 2020). Its ten subtests—evaluating personal and current information, orientation, digital sequence relationships, picture recall, visual recognition, visual reproduction, associated learning, tactile performance test, logical memory and digit span — collectively measure immediate, working, and long-term memory functions. The results are summarized as a Memory Quotient (MQ), with higher scores indicating superior memory performance.

Secondary outcomes Secondary outcomes were evaluated using the AVLT and the ROCF (Seo et al., 2018; Putcha et al., 2019). The AVLT assesses multiple memory domains, including immediate recall (IR), short-term delayed recall (SR), long-term delayed recall (LR), cue recall (CR) and recognition recall (REC). During the test, a list of 12 two-character Chinese words is presented at 1-second interval. Participants are instructed to recall the list immediately after the first presentation. This learning trial is repeated three times, and the average number of correct words across these trials represents the IR ( Liu et al., 2023). After a 5-minute non-verbal distractor task, the fourth recall assesses SR. A fifth recall, conducted after a 20-minute non-verbal task, is used to assess LR. Then, category cues are provided for the sixth recall to evaluate CR. Finally, REC is assessed using a 24-word recognition list, which includes the 12 target words and 12 category-related distractors. For all AVLT components, a higher number of correctly recalled words indicates better memory function. The ROCF is a widely used measure of visuospatial construction and visual memory. It comprises two sections: copying and delayed recall. Each drawing phase is limited to 10 minutes, with a 20–25 minute delay between the two phases. Performance is scored on 18 elements, each rated from 0 to 2 points, with higher total scores reflecting superior visuospatial and visual memory abilities.

fNIRS data acquisition

fNIRS data acquisition and preprocessing

fNIRS data were acquired using a LABNIRS system (Shimadzu, Japan) equipped with 40 photodetectors. The system employed integrated L-shaped fiber-optic probes in a bracketed configuration, featuring alternating rows of near-infrared emitters and detectors, with an inter-optode distance of 3 cm and a sampling rate of 3.71 Hz. A total of 18 sources and 18 detectors were arranged over the prefrontal region according to the International 10-20 system, forming a 57-channel measurement array. Channel 42 (CH42) was positioned at the frontal pole midpoint (Fpz). Prefrontal subregions—including the DLPFC, VLPFC, frontal pole (FPA), and orbitofrontal cortex (OFC)—were defined based on Brodmann areas using the NIRS-SPM toolbox and were further subdivided into left and right hemispheres. In the probe layout, red circles indicate light sources, blue circles represent detectors, and numbers between optodes denote channel identifiers (Fig. 1a). Following acquisition, fNIRS data were preprocessed using the NIRS-KIT toolbox in MATLAB. Raw optical intensity data were first converted into optical density. Subsequently, a data preview and quality check were performed to identify and mark channels with significant motion artifacts or poor signal quality for exclusion from subsequent statistical analysis. A first-order detrending algorithm was then applied to remove slow baseline drift, followed by motion artifact correction using the Temporal Derivative Distribution Repair (TDDR) method (Guan et al., 2024). A band-pass filter (0.01–0.08 Hz) was applied using a third-order infinite impulse response (IIR) filter to reduce high-frequency noise and physiological interference (Bulgarelli et al., 2020). Finally, the filtered optical density data were converted into changes in hemoglobin concentrations using the modified Beer-Lambert law. Subsequent analysis focused exclusively on HbO2 concentrations.

n-back task

During fNIRS data recording, participants performed an n-back task while minimizing movement (Meidenbauer et al., 2021). The task comprised nine blocks, each containing randomly sequenced 0-back, 1-back, and 2-back conditions. Each numeric stimulus was displayed for 1000 ms, followed by a 2000 ms response window. Participants pressed “1” for targets and “2” for non-targets, each condition included 14 trials (Fig. 1b). Accuracy and reaction times were recorded using E-Prime 3.0. The entire task lasted approximately 8 minutes.

Sample Size Calculation

Sample size was estimated based on the MQ of the WMS-RC. Using data from a prior pilot study and PASS 15 software, we estimated that 27 participants would be needed to detect an effect size of 0.275 with 80% power (1− β) at α = 0.05 (two-sided). After accounting for a 15% dropout rate, the final sample size was set at 128 participants (n = 32 per group). Details of this calculation are available in the published study protocol (Wu et al., 2023).

Statistical analysis

Behavioral measures

Predefined analyses were conducted using IBM SPSS Statistics (Version 24) in accordance with a pre-registered statistical analysis plan (Wu et al., 2023). The per-protocol (PP) population included all participants who completed the 12-week intervention and underwent both baseline and post-intervention fNIRS assessments. Baseline demographic characteristics were compared across groups. A 2 × 2 factorial general linear model(GLM) was performed to assess the main effects of TC (underwent TC intervention vs. did not undergo TC) and tDCS (underwent tDCS intervention vs. did not undergo tDCS), as well as their interaction. The primary test of intervention efficacy was the change in outcome metrics from baseline to post-intervention in participants randomized to receive each intervention compared with those not receiving that intervention, as computed with the appropriate contrast (e.g., TC vs. no TC; tDCS vs. no tDCS). Age, gender, and years of education were included as covariates. In addition, because significant baseline differences were observed in β weights for changes in HbO2 concentrations in the left VLPFC during the 1-back and 2-back tasks, the respective baseline values for these tasks were also included as covariates in the model. For significant main or interaction effects, the Dunn-Bonferroni method was applied to adjust for multiple comparisons. Results are reported as estimated marginal means, along with between-group differences and 95% confidence intervals. Statistical significance was defined as a two-sided P < 0.05.

fNIRS data analysis

For task-state fNIRS data, a GLM was applied at the individual level to assess task-related activation (von Lühmann et al., 2020). The hemodynamic response function (HRF) as implemented in SPM was convolved with the task paradigm to generate the regressor of interest. Model parameters were estimated for each channel to obtain β weights for each condition. These β weights were then exported for subsequent group-level analysis.

Correlation analysis

Spearman’s rank correlation was used to evaluate associations between changes in β weights and changes in neuropsychological assessment outcomes, with a two-tailed significance level was set at α = 0.05.

Results

Enrollment and Participant Characteristics

A total of 949 individuals were assessed for eligibility. Of these, 245 declined to participate, 253 did not meet the inclusion criteria, and 323 met the exclusion criteria. Accordingly, 128 eligible participants completed baseline assessments and provided written informed consent, after which they were randomly allocated to one of the four intervention groups. Overall, 114 participants completed the full 12-week intervention, and 113 underwent both pre- and post-intervention fNIRS assessments. These 113 participants were included in the per-protocol analysis (Fig. 2). The mean age of the total sample was 66.3 ± 4.8 years, with a mean of 9.5 ± 3.2 years of education. Of the 113 participants, 81 (71.7%) were female. Baseline demographic and clinical characteristics were well balanced across the four intervention groups. (Table 1).

Primary Outcome

The primary outcome was the change in MQ from baseline to 12 weeks (ΔMQ), with greater increases indicating clinically meaningful improvement in memory function. At 12 weeks, the raw changes in ΔMQ across the four intervention groups were as follows: TC combined with tDCS group: 24.929 ± 13.939; TC group: 16.786 ± 9.496; tDCS group: 8.038 ± 8.072; and health education group: 2.484 ± 6.613. Factorial analysis revealed a significant main effect of TC, with participants receiving TC showing greater improvement than those without TC (mean ΔMQ, 20.858 vs 5.261; mean difference, 15.596 [95% CI: 11.859 to 19.334]; P < 0.001) (Fig. 3a). Similarly, a significant main effect of tDCS was observed (mean ΔMQ, 16.501 vs 9.618; mean difference, 6.884 [95% CI: 3.147 to 10.620]; P < 0.001) (Fig. 3b).

Discussion

Our findings demonstrate that a 12-week intervention combining TC with tDCS may synergistically influence verbal memory in patients with MCI, an effect that appears to be mediated by regulation of HbO2 concentrations in the left DLPFC. Notably, the combined intervention synergistically modulated HbO2 concentrations across multiple prefrontal subregions. Of particular significance, the synergistic interaction observed in the left DLPFC during the 1-back task was specifically associated with verbal memory performance. To our knowledge, this is the first hemodynamic evidence supporting the synergistic benefits of TC combined with tDCS for memory function in this population. In contrast, when administered alone without TC, tDCS failed to produce significant alterations in prefrontal HbO2 concentrations. This limited efficacy of standalone tDCS may be attributable to either suboptimal stimulation parameters or the absence of complementary cognitive engagement, both of which could constrain its capacity to promote neural plasticity in patients with MCI (Brown & Brown, 2022; Prathum et al., 2025). Conversely, TC alone independently increased HbO2 concentrations in the right FPA, highlighting its role in modulating prefrontal hemodynamics even in the absence of tDCS.

The current findings are consistent with previous reports on combined interventions and suggest that the synergistic effect of TC combined with tDCS on verbal memory likely originates from their complementary mechanisms. TC may establish an optimized neural state that enables tDCS to exert its effects, while tDCS concurrently delivers targeted neuromodulation to memory-critical brain regions. This combined approach promotes a heightened global neural milieu while selectively activating memory-specific regions, thereby yielding a synergistic effect. This interpretation aligns with the view that tDCS efficacy is highly dependent on the prevailing brain state. Prior studies have confirmed that tDCS, whether targeting the left angular gyrus or the left DLPFC, produces superior memory improvements when paired with cognitive training compared with cognitive training alone (Lau et al., 2024; Tao et al., 2023). These earlier observations are consistent with our current results. Although specific intervention combinations and stimulation targets vary across studies, they share a common mechanistic framework: behavioral or cognitive interventions create optimized neural conditions that enhance the effects of tDCS. Notably, the memory benefits of combining TC with other interventions appear to depend critically on the mechanistic characteristics of the co-intervention. While TC combined with either right DLPFC-targeted repetitive transcranial magnetic stimulation or cognitive training has been shown to yield additional memory improvements in patients with MCI (Li et al., 2023; Liu et al., 2025). TC combined with music therapy failed to produce significant memory enhancement(Zhou, 2025). This discrepancy may be explained by the fact that music therapy primarily modulates global brain network activity rather than specifically reinforcing prefrontal or memory-related circuitry(Slade et al., 2025).

The hemodynamic changes observed in the PFC following the combined intervention may constitute a key mechanism underlying memory improvement in patients with MCI. Substantial evidence indicates that elevated PFC HbO2 concentrations contribute to enhanced memory function(Dhahbi et al., 2025). Specifically, our combined intervention produced synergistic increases in HbO2 concentrations across several prefrontal subregions, including the left DLPFC, bilateral FPA, left VLPFC, and right OFC. The left DLPFC serves as a critical hub for memory regulation, primarily supporting encoding processes(Orth et al., 2023), with its increased activation being linked to improved emotional memory performance(Wang et al., 2024). The FPA, as an essential component of the PFC, maintains normal memory function(Bludau et al., 2014); its involvement is further substantiated by fMRI studies demonstrating reduced right FPA activation in patients with MCI during visual oddball tasks(Kemik et al., 2024), along with fNIRS evidence showing bilateral FPA recruitment in older adults to support memory performance(Hyodo et al., 2024). The left VLPFC may contribute to memory through mechanisms involving emotion regulation and stress reduction (Roy et al., 2025), as well as through cognitive operations such as thought substitution(Qi et al., 2025). Evidence also suggests that specific OFC neuronal populations support stable memory trace formation, and that their inactivation during memory encoding disrupts subsequent retrieval and behavioral flexibility (Yount et al., 2025).

In normal aging, right PFC hyperactivation is generally interpreted as compensatory resource mobilization (Hyodo et al., 2024). However, under pathological conditions such as cognitive impairment, right PFC overactivation may indicate inefficient compensation for compromised left-hemisphere networks, resulting in suboptimal neural processing patterns (Liu et al., 2024). According to the Scaffolding Theory of Aging and Cognition (STAC), the aging brain builds supportive networks through recruitment of additional regions to maintain cognitive function (Park & Reuter-Lorenz, 2009). In our investigation, TC combined with right DLPFC-targeted tDCS may promote integration of bilateral prefrontal resources, thereby guiding neural scaffolding reorganization toward left-hemisphere optimization. Supporting this view, fNIRS data revealed that recipients of the combined intervention did not exhibit widespread compensatory increases in HbO2 concentrations in the right PFC during memory tasks, but instead showed elevated HbO2 concentrations in multiple left PFC subregions, suggesting a restoration of left DLPFC dominance in memory processing (Saikia et al., 2021).

In summary, during the combined TC and tDCS intervention, TC may establish a foundation for enhanced neuroplasticity through prefrontal hemodynamic regulation, thereby enabling tDCS to more effectively modulate prefrontal excitability and plasticity. This mechanism may account for the synergistic effects on verbal memory function observed in patients with MCI.

The present study has several limitations that should be considered when interpreting the findings. First, the non-double-blind design may have introduced detection bias; however, blinding of outcome assessors and statisticians was implemented to mitigate this potential bias and maintain trial quality. Second, the absence of long-term follow-up limits insight into the sustained effects of the intervention. Nevertheless, the 12-week intervention period was designed to reflect real-world clinical application and was sufficient to evaluate short-term efficacy. Third, fNIRS measurements were restricted to the PFC. Although tDCS primarily targets prefrontal regions and TC is known to induce whole-brain modulation, this regional focus may have constrained a more comprehensive exploration of the synergistic mechanisms underlying the combined intervention. Finally, the relatively small sample size may limit the generalizability of the results. Future studies should include larger cohorts and extend follow-up durations to validate and extend our findings.

Conclusion

In conclusion, TC combined with tDCS may synergistically enhance verbal memory in patients with MCI, an effect that appears to be mediated by regulation of HbO2 concentrations in the left DLPFC. Furthermore, the synergistic effect of the combined intervention may operate through modulation of hemodynamic activity across multiple prefrontal subregions, with a particular emphasis on enhancing left PFC function. These findings provide preliminary evidence supporting the potential application of this combined intervention in clinical settings.
