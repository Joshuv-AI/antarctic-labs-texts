Treatment decision support for esophageal cancer based on PET/CT data using deep learning
Authors: Qiuxiang Zheng, Fobao Lai, Zhiyong Chen
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
BackgroundMaking precise treatment decisions in esophageal cancer is essential for enhancing patient outcomes and avoiding overtreatment. Traditional approaches relying on special features or shallow learning models often fail to capture the complex, multi-scale patterns embedded in PET/CT imaging data. Recent advances in deep learning provide an opportunity to build more robust, data-driven systems for predictive modeling in oncology.MethodsWe propose a novel deep learning model that integrates convolutional and transformer-based components based on PET/CT data to support treatment decisions for esophageal cancer. The architecture introduces a Convolutional Feature Extractor with split-based residual blocks for efficient local feature capture, a Multi-scale Pooling module for spatial context aggregation, and an Multilayer Perceptron block for predicting. The model was evaluated using several performance metrics such as AUCROC, F1 score, Balanced Accuracy and benchmarked against state-of-the-art convolutional and transformer backbones such as ConvNeXt and Vision Transformer.ResultsThe proposed model achieved superior performance across all evaluation metrics, including an AUCROC of 0.9935 and a Balanced Accuracy of 0.9630, outperforming existing models. These results validate the effectiveness of combining local-global representation learning through custom-designed modules. In addition, we conducted ablation studies to further demonstrate the individual contributions and effectiveness of each component within the proposed architecture. By systematically removing or replacing specific modules such as the Convolutional Feature Extractor and Multi-scale Pooling, we observed consistent performance degradation, which highlights the necessity and complementary roles of these components in achieving optimal predictive accuracy.ConclusionsThis study presents a novel hybrid deep learning architecture that enhances treatment decision support for esophageal cancer by leveraging multi-scale spatial encoding. The empirical evidence demonstrates that tailored architectural innovations significantly improve predictive accuracy over existing methods.

Background

Esophageal cancer remains one of the most aggressive malignancies with poor prognosis and high treatment complexity. Accurate and timely treatment decision-making is critical to improve patient outcomes, yet remains challenging due to tumor heterogeneity and variability in response to therapy. Esophageal cancer is a malignant neoplasm that arises from the epithelial lining of the esophagus and is primarily characterized by two major histological subtypes: Esophageal Squamous Cell Carcinoma (ESCC) and Esophageal Adenocarcinoma (EAC) [1, 2]. These two main types of esophageal cancer arises through distinct etiological mechanisms, ESCC is closely associated with environmental and lifestyle risk factors, including tobacco smoking and alcohol consumption, which cause chronic irritation and lead to malignant transformation of the squamous epithelium [3]. In contrast, EAC is often related to gastroesophageal reflux disease, which can lead to Barrett’s esophagus that is a recognized precancerous lesion and is also associated with risk factors such as obesity [4]. These differences are of great significance for both screening strategies and treatment approaches, as they reflect variations in disease biology and progression.

Worldwide, cancer of the esophagus ranks as one of the most common cancers and a leading cause of cancer mortality. According to recent data, it ranks eighth among the most diagnosed cancers and sixth among the most diagnosed cancer deaths globally [3, 5]. Regional epidemiological studies reveal notable variations in incidence and mortality. For instance, in the high-risk areas of sub-Saharan Africa and Asia, most are dominated by ESCC, whereas the largest proportion of the cases comes from EAC in North America and some areas in Europe [6]. All these variations bear witness to the impacts environmental, genetic, and lifestyle elements have across populations. The key factors of esophageal cancer vary depending on histological subtype and geographical region. For example, in Western countries, heavy smoking and alcohol consumption are associated with the development of ESCC, while obesity and gastroesophageal reflux disease are closely linked to the rising incidence of EAC [7]. In contrast, in low-income regions, dietary factors such as exposure to nitrosamines and nutritional deficiencies are believed to contribute to the high burden of ESCC. Therefore, it is evident that the etiology of esophageal cancer is highly diverse and requires a nuanced approach to prevention and early detection.

The treatment of esophageal cancer involves different treatments, including the combination of traditional modalities with advanced technologies to address the inherent complexity of the disease. Current treatment options primarily include surgery (esophagectomy), radiation therapy, chemotherapy, and multimodal therapy that integrates these methods. Surgical resection remains a cornerstone treatment, especially in cases where the disease is still localized. However, it has been observed that even with advancements in surgical techniques, the prognosis remains suboptimal in many cases [8]. Radiation therapy, particularly with advanced techniques such as intensity-modulated radiation therapy and volumetric modulated arc therapy, offers effective local control while minimizing damage to healthy tissues [9]. Chemotherapy, often used in the neoadjuvant (preoperative) or adjuvant (postoperative) setting, has also been shown to improve survival outcomes in numerous clinical studies [10]. Moreover, multimodal therapy combining surgery, chemotherapy, and radiation has demonstrated additional benefits, particularly for patients with locally advanced disease [11].

A major challenge in making treatment decisions for esophageal cancer is the complexity of accurate staging. Classification systems has been widely used, however variations in staging criteria and the influence of patient-specific characteristics have led to heterogeneity in treatment outcomes [12]. This complexity is further compounded by the need to synthesize diverse data sources, including imaging studies, endoscopic evaluations (such as endoscopic ultrasound), and pathological assessments [13]. These factors, along with patient demographics such as age, comorbidities, and performance status, necessitate a more refined interpretation of tumor biology and aggressiveness [14]. In clinical practice, the variability in tumor response to standard treatments further complicates decision-making, as patients with the same disease stage may respond differently due to intrinsic biological characteristics and molecular profiles of the tumor.

In light of these challenges, there is a growing role and evident need for treatment decision support systems in esophageal cancer management. Such systems are designed to integrate and analyze heterogeneous data, ranging from imaging studies and laboratory tests to histopathological reports to aid physicians in selecting the optimal treatment regimen [15]. Recent developments in artificial intelligence and machine learning have demonstrated significant promise, as exemplified by predictive models that utilize multimodal features to forecast response to neoadjuvant therapies [14, 15]. These advanced systems support clinicians by enabling personalized therapy that accounts for individual patient profiles and by reducing the potential for medical errors, thereby ultimately improving overall survival and quality of life. In addition, multidisciplinary team meetings that incorporate these decision support systems have been shown to enhance staging accuracy and refine treatment strategies, contributing to more tailored and effective therapeutic interventions [16].

Literature reviews

Recent studies in medical informatics demonstrate a steady evolution in the application of Machine Learning (ML) and Deep Learning (DL) models to support treatment decision-making, particularly for complex diseases such as esophageal cancer. These models have advanced from traditional classifiers to sophisticated DL architectures capable of processing complex clinical data, such as PET/CT imaging, commonly encountered in medical care [17]. In the domain of esophageal cancer treatment, the use of ML/DL models with PET/CT data has shown significant potential to assist physicians in making more informed therapeutic decisions. Early studies in the 2010s primarily employed conventional ML algorithms like Support Vector Machines and Random Forests for tasks such as tumor classification and lesion detection in diagnostic imaging. These methods often relied on handcrafted features, which limited their ability to capture the inherent complexity of medical images.

The emergence of deep learning marked a significant turning point. Convolutional Neural Networks (CNNs) demonstrated superior performance in automatically extracting features and analyzing medical images more accurately and efficiently. The work of Tajbakhsh et al. (2016) [18] highlighted the effectiveness of CNNs in medical image analysis, paving the way for their integration into clinical decision support systems. By the early 2020s, research had expanded toward multimodal data integration to improve diagnostic precision and prognosis prediction, especially in oncology [19]. This period also saw rapid advances in clinical decision support systems leveraging DL for treatment planning and patient management [20]. With the rise of architectures such as CNNs and Vision Transformers [21], the ability to automatically analyze PET/CT images and integrate them with clinical variables for esophageal cancer decision-making has significantly improved [19]. These systems are not only used for image classification or segmentation but also offer quantitative insights to assist physicians in personalizing treatment strategies [22]. These models have been successfully applied across various clinical domains, including musculoskeletal rehabilitation and coronary artery disease diagnosis, underscoring their versatility and translational potential [23]. More importantly, the integration of radiomics with artificial intelligence has enabled the development of predictive models with higher accuracy, aiding in staging and prognosis in oncology [23]. This is particularly crucial in esophageal cancer, where accurate assessment of tumor progression and treatment response is essential for optimal intervention planning. Additionally, DL applications in analyzing diagnostic images for conditions such as fractures and leukemia have improved diagnostic precision, an essential factor for timely and effective clinical intervention [24, 25].

Despite these advancements and major limitations remain. One of the primary challenges is the lack of large, multi-center datasets, which restricts the generalizability of algorithms and leads to issues such as bias and overfitting when models are trained on homogeneous data. Moreover, deploying ML/DL models in clinical workflows still faces barriers, including requirements for real-time data processing, model interpretability, and consistent regulatory oversight [26]. These challenges call for continued research to ensure that ML/DL models are not only technically robust but also clinically acceptable, particularly when applied in the context of personalized treatment decision support for esophageal cancer using PET/CT imaging.

Dataset

The dataset used in this study consists of clinical and imaging data collected from three independent hospitals. The training dataset comprises patients enrolled at Guangdong Provincial People’s Hospital between January 1, 2012, and December 31, 2019, while the test dataset includes patients from Zhuhai People’s Hospital and The Fifth Affiliated Hospital of Sun Yat-Sen University, enrolled between January 1, 2017, and December 31, 2021. The patient cohort is divided into three main groups: Surgery Alone (SA), Surgery followed by POCT (SPOCT), and a Control Cohort consisting of patients with advanced-stage ESCC who could not receive surgery or chemotherapy due to physical limitations. The total number of patients in the training dataset is 651, with 219 patients in the SA cohort, 174 patients in the SPOCT cohort, and 258 patients in the control cohort. The test dataset consists of 186 patients, with 62 in the SA cohort, 57 in the SPOCT cohort, and 67 in the control cohort. Convolutional feature extraction

Basic Block Design: The basic building block consists of two convolutional layers: the first is a Conv2D \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$(3 \times 3)$$\end{document}, followed by a Conv2D \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$(1 \times 1)$$\end{document}. The final output of the block is obtained by adding the input of the first convolutional layer to the output of the second convolutional layer, forming a residual connection.

Building upon this foundation, we develop a CFE by stacking multiple such basic blocks. The design of the core convolutional layer block draws inspiration from the ConvNeXt [27] architecture, which has demonstrated strong performance in recent vision tasks by leveraging hierarchical feature representations and optimized convolutional designs. However, we introduce several crucial modifications to better capture local context and improve feature fusion efficiency. In particular, we incorporate a split mechanism in which the input feature map is evenly divided into two parallel branches. One of the branches is processed through two basic blocks to extract deeper local patterns, while the other branch retains the original features. The outputs of the two branches are then concatenated to form the final feature map, which is denoted as \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$ X \in \mathbb{R}^{C \times H \times W} $$\end{document}. This architecture allows the model to effectively integrate both transformed and original representations, enriching the feature space while maintaining computational efficiency.

Multilayer perceptron

In this paper, we setup MLP-based processing block applied to one of the branches within the grouped structure with a lightweight multilayer perceptron (MLP) block that consists of a normalization layer, a fully connected layer, and a sigmoid activation. The input feature map \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$ X \in \mathbb{R}^{N \times C} $$\end{document}, where \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$ N = H \times W $$\end{document}, is first normalized to stabilize the distribution of activations: 5\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$ \hat{X} = \text{Normalize}(X).$$\end{document}

The resulting compressed feature maps are then processed by a fully connected layer: 6\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$ Z = \text{Linear}(\hat{X}).$$\end{document}

Finally, a sigmoid activation function is applied to introduce non-linearity and bound the output values: 7\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$ \text{Output} = \text{Sigmoid}(Z).$$\end{document}

This MLP block serves as a lightweight layer, capturing local spatial patterns and scale-aware features through convolutional compression while enhancing the representational capacity with normalization and nonlinear transformation.

Experimental design

Computational infrastructure

All experiments were conducted on a high-performance computational server to ensure consistent and efficient training and evaluation of the deep learning models, comprising an Intel Core i9-12900K CPU (16 cores, 24 threads), an NVIDIA RTX 3090 GPU (24 GB VRAM), 32 GB DDR4 RAM, and a 1 TB SSD. The operating system was Ubuntu 22.04 LTS, and all models were implemented using PyTorch 2.1 with CUDA 12.2 acceleration. Training was implemented in a single GPU. Model hyperparameters, such as learning rate, batch size, and weight decay, were carefully tuned based on validation performance. Typically, a batch size of 32 per GPU and an initial learning rate of \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$10^{-4}$$\end{document} with cosine annealing schedule were employed. All experiments were managed and tracked using the Weights & Biases platform for reproducibility and efficient hyperparameter tuning. This computational setup allowed us to handle the high-resolution PET/CT imaging data, while ensuring robustness across repeated trials.

Limitation and future research

Although the proposed model achieved promising results, this study still has certain limitations. First, generalisability is constrained by data being collected from only three hospitals within one region, with a relatively small overall cohort and particularly small test set; this raises risks of overfitting, site-specific bias, and optimistic estimates of external performance. Second, the study relied solely on PET/CT imaging features, without integrating clinical variables, molecular biomarkers, or serum indicators that could add complementary signal and improve predictive accuracy. Third, the current model focused on classifying treatment groups (SA vs. SPOCT) rather than modelling long-term outcomes such as overall survival, progression-free survival, or recurrence, which are ultimately more actionable for clinical decision-making.

For future work, we will expand the feature space by integrating clinical variables, molecular biomarkers, and serum indicators, alongside immunological context and tumour-microenvironment cues [34–36]. To move beyond treatment-group classification, we plan to model radiosensitivity and treatment response directly from PET/CT, and to incorporate time-to-event objectives for survival and recurrence prediction [37]. To mitigate small-sample and site-shift issues, we will investigate cross-domain transfer learning, data harmonisation, and multimodal fusion strategies that have shown promise in other medical-AI settings [38, 39]. Finally, we aim to connect imaging-derived phenotypes to computational pipelines for therapeutic prioritisation, enabling hypothesis generation for candidate interventions informed by ensemble and evolutionary strategies [40].

Conclusion

In this work, we proposed a novel deep learning-based framework for treatment decision support in esophageal cancer, leveraging PET/CT imaging data. Through the use of split-based CFE, MSP, and MLP layer, the proposed method effectively combines local details and global dependencies to generate robust and discriminative features from multi-modal imaging inputs. Experimental results demonstrate significant improvements in predictive accuracy for treatment response classification, highlighting the effectiveness of multi-scale representation integration in complex medical image analysis tasks. The modular design also facilitates interpretability and scalability, making it a promising solution for real-world clinical decision support systems.
