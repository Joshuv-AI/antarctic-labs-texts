A Rare Case of Thoracic Spinal Dural Arteriovenous Fistula Emerging Gradually after Lumbar Decompression, Requiring Direct Transection
Authors: Takuya Nomura, Toshiki Okubo, Narihito Nagoshi, Takahito Iga, Kazuki Takeda, Masahiro Ozaki, Satoshi Suzuki, Kota Watanabe, Masaya Nakamura
Published: 2026

A 67-year-old man presented with progressive bilateral numbness in the lower extremities and intermittent claudication. Seven years prior, he had undergone L3/4 and L4/5 lumbar decompression for spinal canal stenosis. Although his initial postoperative recovery was favorable, sensory disturbances gradually re-emerged, predominantly affecting the left lower extremity, persisted even at rest, and proved refractory to conservative treatment. Subsequently, the patient developed urinary frequency and symptoms of incomplete bladder emptying. Lumbar magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) conducted 5 years after surgery demonstrated sustained decompression without restenosis; however, intramedullary signal changes and newly developed perimedullary flow voids were observed on sagittal T2-weighted images. These abnormalities progressed on MRI performed 7 years postoperatively, with more pronounced serpiginous perimedullary flow voids along the dorsal spinal cord (Figure 1A and B). Additional thoracic MRI revealed longitudinal spinal cord edema with dilated perimedullary vessels and flow voids (Figure 2A-C). Spinal angiography revealed a spinal dural arteriovenous fistula supplied by the right sixth intercostal artery (Figure 2D). The final preoperative neurological examination revealed cauda equina-type intermittent claudication, with a Japanese Orthopedic Association score of 15/29 points. Deep tendon reflexes were normal in all extremities before the initial lumbar surgery. However, at presentation for the current surgery, deep tendon hyperreflexia of the lower extremities and a positive Babinski sign were observed.

Spinal dural arteriovenous fistula (sdAVF) is the most common type of spinal arteriovenous shunt, predominantly affecting elderly men and showing a preference for the thoracic spine1,2). Its pathophysiology involves venous hypertension due to abnormal arteriovenous shunting, resulting in progressive spinal cord dysfunction. Typical MRI findings include perimedullary flow voids and longitudinal spinal cord edema; however, these features can be subtle or absent in the early stages of the disease3). Combined with the slow and insidious progression of symptoms, sdAVF is often misdiagnosed as degenerative, inflammatory, or ischemic spinal disease, leading to delayed diagnosis4,5).

In the present case, neurological deterioration gradually occurred after lumbar decompression, despite the absence of recurrent stenosis or other pathological findings at the surgical site. Notably, the sdAVF was located at the thoracic level, distant from the previous lumbar surgery. Previous reports have described sdAVF diagnosed after lumbar surgery at variable intervals ranging from several weeks to 8 years, and in many cases, the fistula was located at a different level from the lumbar operative site (Table 1)6-9). Compared to these reports, the present case represents a particularly long latency period and is notable for the thoracic location of the fistula, which is anatomically distant from the prior operative site. Lumbar decompression may alter spinal venous hemodynamics, potentially increasing the flow through a previously asymptomatic shunt and eventually leading to clinically significant venous congestion. However, this hypothesis remains speculative, and a causal relationship between lumbar surgery and the subsequent development or progression of sdAVF cannot be established based on the present case.

This case demonstrates that sdAVF should be included in the differential diagnosis of late-onset or recurrent neurological deterioration after lumbar surgery and that evaluation of the entire spinal axis is warranted when postoperative imaging at the surgical site is unremarkable. Because sdAVF can manifest clinically years after lumbar decompression and at a spinal level distant from the surgical site, a thorough evaluation of the entire spinal axis with careful attention to subtle vascular findings is required.
