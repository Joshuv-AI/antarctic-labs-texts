Drilling a safer path: Reconsidering the iliac wing as a reconstructive corridor
Authors: Apoorva Bhandari, Matthew R Smeds
Published: 2026

Hostile groin pathology remains one of the most formidable challenges in vascular surgery. Infection, radiation injury, tumor resection, and repeated operative failure converge to render conventional in-line repair unsafe, while traditional extra-anatomical options often trade surgical feasibility for compromised durability. In this context, Barillà et al1 revisit and refine an underused solution: the transiliac bone tunnel route for lower limb vascular reconstruction.

What distinguishes this report is not novelty alone, but clarity of indication. The authors present transiliac bone tunnel bypass (TIBB) not as a salvage curiosity, but as a deliberate, anatomically rational strategy when medial and inguinal corridors are irrevocably compromised. By lateralizing the graft trajectory through the iliac wing, the reconstruction bypasses infected or irradiated tissue while maintaining a short, direct conduit with favorable hemodynamics. This balance between the avoidance of contamination and preservation of flow efficiency has historically been difficult to achieve with axillofemoral or obturator routes.

The inclusion of both arterial and venous reconstructions is particularly instructive. Although prior reports have focused predominantly on arterial limb salvage, this article expands the conceptual boundaries of TIBB by demonstrating its applicability to complex venous oncovascular reconstruction, including popliteocaval bypass supported by temporary arteriovenous fistulas. This adaptation underscores an important principle: in extreme anatomical disruption, reconstruction should be guided by physiological objectives rather than adherence to conventional pathways.

Importantly, the authors' technical refinements reflect lessons learned from earlier transiliac experiences. The use of ring-reinforced polytetrafluoroethylene mitigates compression within the osseous tunnel, and the lateral subcutaneous routing facilitates postoperative surveillance, a critical advantage over deeply buried pelvic grafts. Prior case reports and small series have suggested acceptable patency and low reinfection rates with this approach, but outcomes have varied depending on conduit choice, tunnel geometry, and distal target selection.2,3 The present work consolidates these variables into a reproducible framework.

That said, the role of TIBB should be viewed as complementary rather than competitive. Obturator bypass, in situ femoral vein reconstruction, and endovascular temporization each retain value in carefully selected scenarios. The strength of this report lies in articulating when those options fail and when a transiliac route may succeed precisely because it abandons the diseased field altogether. Larger series suggest that, when appropriately selected, transiliac bypass achieves limb salvage and patency rates comparable with other extra-anatomical strategies, without added tunnel-related morbidity.4 For practicing vascular surgeons, TIBB should enter consideration when groin contamination, radiation injury, or oncological resection precludes safe medial reconstruction and when traditional extra-anatomical routes would impose excessive graft length or flow compromise. Widespread adoption will depend on surgeon comfort with retroperitoneal exposure and osseous tunneling, underscoring the importance of experience and careful preoperative planning.

Ultimately, Barillà et al1 remind us that durable reconstruction in hostile territory demands both anatomical creativity and technical discipline. The iliac wing, long regarded as a barrier, is reframed here as a protected conduit, one that deserves renewed consideration in modern vascular and oncovascular practice.
