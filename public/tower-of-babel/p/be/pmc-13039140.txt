Basketball participation improves sleep quality through psychological flexibility in adolescents
Authors: Bo Peng, Tong Liu, Yawei Ren, Mingmin Kong, Zhongen Yin
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
This study investigated the relationship between basketball participation and sleep quality among adolescents, focusing on the mediating role of psychological flexibility. The research aimed to clarify how participation in structured team sports contributes to daytime and nighttime aspects of sleep health, including daytime distress and insomnia symptoms. A total of 818 students (54.03% male) from Grades 5 to 9 participated in the study. Participants completed validated questionnaires assessing basketball participation, psychological flexibility, daytime distress, and insomnia symptoms. Reliability and validity analyses confirmed satisfactory psychometric properties for all scales. Multiple regression and structural equation modeling were conducted to examine the direct and indirect associations, and a bias-corrected bootstrapping approach (5,000 resamples) was used to test mediation effects. Basketball participation showed significant negative effects on both daytime distress (β = − 0.191, 95% CI [–0.265, − 0.113]) and insomnia symptoms (β = − 0.214, 95% CI [–0.173, − 0.095]). Psychological flexibility exerted significant negative effects on daytime distress (β = − 0.398) and insomnia symptoms (β = − 0.402), and it mediated the relationships between basketball participation and both outcomes. The indirect effects were significant for daytime distress (β = − 0.129, 95% CI [–0.170, − 0.095]) and insomnia symptoms (β = − 0.130, 95% CI [–0.292, − 0.134]), accounting for 40.31% and 37.79% of the total effects, respectively. Basketball participation was associated with lower levels of daytime distress and insomnia symptoms, both directly and indirectly through enhanced psychological flexibility. The results underscore the role of sport-based engagement in promoting psychological adaptability and sleep health among adolescents. Integrating physical activity with interventions targeting psychological flexibility may represent an effective strategy for improving emotional regulation and sleep quality in youth.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1038/s41598-026-46073-0.

Introduction

Physical activity plays an important role in promoting adolescents’ physical and mental health. Regular exercise is associated with improved cardiovascular and immune functioning, as well as reduced symptoms of anxiety and depression1. Among various forms of physical activity, basketball has become one of the most popular sports among adolescents and university students because of its strong team orientation and social interaction, and because it often combines intermittent high-intensity activity with peer engagement and enjoyment2,3. It should be noted that prior research has proposed several general mechanisms through which physical activity may improve sleep quality, such as the regulation of physiological rhythms, reductions in anxiety, and increased energy expenditure4. However, whether these mechanisms operate similarly in the context of basketball participation among student populations, and whether basketball’s distinctive features—such as its competitive, intermittent, and socially interactive nature—may introduce additional or basketball-specific pathways, remains insufficiently clarified.

A substantial body of evidence indicates that regular participation in physical activity is associated with better sleep quality5–7. From a physiological perspective, exercise may promote sleep by influencing circadian rhythms and autonomic regulation, by improving mood and thereby reducing pre-sleep arousal, and by increasing sleep drive through greater energy expenditure8. Nevertheless, much of the existing literature has focused on dose–response relationships, such as associations of exercise volume or intensity with sleep duration, sleep onset latency, and sleep efficiency9–11. In recent years, researchers have increasingly emphasized that the impact of physical activity on sleep is also shaped by psychological processes (e.g., stress, emotion regulation, and cognitive arousal), which may function as mediators or moderators. However, compared with the extensive evidence on physiological mechanisms, psychological mechanisms have been examined less systematically and less specifically across different activity contexts, particularly in team-sport settings. This gap is especially relevant to basketball participation: even at the student or recreational level, basketball involves not only substantial physical demands (e.g., repeated accelerations/decelerations, jumping, and short sprints)12 but also context-related psychological experiences, such as social evaluation, peer comparison, perceived competence, brief competitive tension, and demands for attention and emotion regulation during play13. These experiences may further influence sleep through changes in stress reactivity, affective states, and pre-sleep cognitive arousal. Therefore, understanding the relationship between basketball participation and sleep quality requires an integrative perspective that considers both physiological and psychological pathways.

Psychological Flexibility, a core concept in Acceptance and Commitment Therapy (ACT), refers to an individual’s ability to remain present and respond adaptively to internal experiences and external demands in ways that are consistent with personal values14. Higher Psychological Flexibility has been linked to more effective stress coping, fewer negative emotional reactions, and better sleep outcomes15,16, whereas psychological inflexibility is often associated with anxiety, insomnia, and related problems17. Although Psychological Flexibility has been widely examined and validated in clinical and health psychology18,19, evidence for its role in sport- and physical-activity contexts remains relatively limited. For students who participate in basketball, Psychological Flexibility may be particularly important because it may shape how individuals interpret and cope with everyday stressors arising during participation (e.g., performance concerns, peer interactions, and emotional fluctuations), thereby influencing sleep quality. Accordingly, empirical evidence is still scarce regarding whether Psychological Flexibility explains (i.e., mediates) or alters (i.e., moderates) the association between basketball participation and sleep quality.

Based on the above considerations, this study aims to examine the relationship between basketball participation and sleep quality and to further explore potential psychological mechanisms underlying this relationship. Specifically, we focus on the practical effects of basketball participation on sleep quality and incorporate key individual psychological characteristics to clarify their direction and magnitude of influence within this association. Using questionnaire surveys and structural equation modeling (SEM), this study seeks to address the following core questions: (1) Is basketball participation associated with higher levels of sleep quality? (2) Do individual psychological characteristics mediate or moderate this relationship? The findings are expected not only to deepen scientific understanding of the exercise–sleep relationship but also to provide empirical evidence to support sport-based interventions and mental health promotion.

Literature review and hypotheses

Current research on physical activity and sleep quality

Adequate and high-quality sleep is one of the key factors for maintaining physical and mental health, promoting exercise recovery, and enhancing learning performance. In recent years, numerous empirical studies have confirmed that regular physical activity can significantly improve sleep architecture and subjective sleep quality20. Moderate exercise has been shown to shorten sleep onset latency, extend deep sleep duration, and enhance sleep efficiency21,22. From a physiological perspective, exercise improves sleep by facilitating thermoregulatory rhythms, increasing energy expenditure, and enhancing melatonin secretion23. Moreover, exercise can reduce cortisol levels and alleviate sympathetic nervous system arousal, thereby mitigating pre-sleep tension24,25.

However, the relationship between exercise and sleep is not entirely linear. High-intensity or late-evening exercise may negatively impact sleep onset due to excessive sympathetic activation26. In addition, different types of exercise exhibit varying effects on sleep quality. Team-based competitive sports (e.g., basketball, football), while inducing physical fatigue, are also accompanied by substantial psychological load and social pressure, which may attenuate the improvements in sleep compared to moderate-intensity aerobic exercise27,28. Consequently, understanding this complex relationship—and the potential role of individual psychological differences—has become a key focus in recent research on exercise and sleep.

The relationship between psychological factors in exercise and sleep quality

Beyond physiological mechanisms, researchers have increasingly recognized that the impact of physical activity participation on sleep quality is also influenced by various psychological factors serving as mediators or moderators. For example, participation in sports is often associated with enhanced positive emotions, psychological resilience, self-efficacy, and adaptive coping strategies29,30. These psychological variables can indirectly improve sleep quality by reducing anxiety, depression, and stress responses31,32. Conversely, negative psychological experiences—such as perceived exercise stress, competition anxiety, or role conflict—may diminish the beneficial effects of exercise on sleep33,34.

In team sports, psychological factors play an even more prominent role. Basketball players, for instance, must manage not only physical fatigue but also various psychological stressors, including team coordination demands, performance pressure, and social evaluation35. When individuals lack effective psychological regulation abilities, prolonged psychological burden may lead to difficulties in falling asleep, lighter sleep, or inadequate recovery36. Therefore, exploring the psychological mechanisms that influence the relationship between physical activity and sleep—particularly those reflecting an individual’s capacity to cope with stress—holds substantial theoretical and practical importance.

The concept and applications of psychological flexibility

Psychological Flexibility is a core concept in Acceptance and Commitment Therapy (ACT), proposed by Hayes and colleagues37. It refers to an individual’s ability to maintain present-moment awareness and acceptance when encountering unpleasant experiences14. Psychological Flexibility comprises six dimensions: acceptance, cognitive defusion, being present, self-as-context, values clarification, and committed action (values-directed behavior). In contrast, psychological inflexibility is characterized by avoiding negative experiences and becoming entangled in rigid thinking patterns or emotional distress, thereby undermining behavioral regulation.

A substantial body of research has demonstrated the importance of Psychological Flexibility in the domain of mental health38,39. Higher Psychological Flexibility is significantly associated with lower levels of anxiety, depression, and stress18,40, and is positively related to greater life satisfaction, better work performance, and stronger interpersonal functioning41,42. In health psychology, Psychological Flexibility has also been shown to facilitate adaptive coping with illness, pain, and chronic stress contexts19. Moreover, Psychological Flexibility is significantly linked to sleep quality: individuals with higher Psychological Flexibility tend to maintain better emotional balance when dealing with sleep-related difficulties, experience less rumination and worry, and consequently report better subjective sleep quality16.

The potential role of psychological flexibility in the relationship between exercise and sleep

Although psychological flexibility has been extensively validated in clinical and health psychology research18,19, its application in sports contexts remains at an early stage. Preliminary evidence suggests that athletes’ psychological flexibility is closely related to their training engagement, management of competitive anxiety, and emotional recovery43,44. Greater psychological flexibility enables athletes to maintain focus under competitive pressure, accept uncertainty, and recover quickly from mistakes, thereby sustaining psychological stability45. These characteristics are highly consistent with the mental relaxation and cognitive disengagement processes required for high-quality sleep.

Nevertheless, empirical research examining the role of psychological flexibility in the exercise–sleep relationship remains scarce. Most existing studies have focused on clinical or general populations46,47, with limited attention to high-intensity, team-based, competitive sports such as basketball. The literature has yet to clarify whether basketball participation can promote the development of Psychological Flexibility, and whether psychological flexibility serves as a mediating or buffering factor in the relationship between exercise participation and sleep quality. Therefore, empirical research is needed to test this potential psychological mechanism, thereby enriching the theoretical framework of sport psychology and advancing the integration of exercise and health behavior research.

Theoretical model

Based on the literature review above, this study proposes the following hypotheses:

H1:Adolescent basketball participation is negatively associated with adolescents’ daytime distress.

H2:Adolescent basketball participation is negatively associated with adolescents’ insomnia symptoms.

H3: Psychological flexibility mediates the association between adolescent basketball participation and adolescents’ daytime distress.

H4: Psychological flexibility mediates the association between adolescent basketball participation and adolescents’ insomnia symptoms.

This study integrates the proposed hypotheses into a comprehensive theoretical framework (see Fig. 1).

Materials and methods

Participants and data

The participants in this study were students from 30 middle schools in Sichuan, Chongqing, and other regions of China, covering Grades 5 to 9 (aged 11–15 years). Data were collected from April 2, 2025, to September 31, 2025, and a total of 900 questionnaires were distributed.

To ensure that the study specifically focused on basketball participation rather than general physical activity, a screening item was included to confirm whether students had participated in basketball activities during the past three months (e.g., school basketball classes/training, extracurricular basketball activities, or informal basketball play). Only students who reported basketball participation were retained for analysis. During data screening, 82 questionnaires were excluded due to missing data, inconsistent responses, or fixed-pattern responding. Ultimately, 818 valid questionnaires were included, yielding a valid response rate of 90.89%.

According to an empirical rule commonly used in social science research, the required sample size is typically 10 to 15 times the total number of questionnaire items48. The measurement instruments used in this study comprised 35 items; therefore, the required sample size ranged from 350 to 525. The final valid sample (N = 818) exceeded this requirement.

Measurement tools

Sports participation: Basketball participation was assessed using the Sports Participation Scale developed by Zheng et al.49. Although this scale was originally developed and validated in an urban older-adult sample, its items primarily capture general participation-related facets (e.g., participation behaviors, attentional engagement, perceived ability, learning/behavioral involvement, and participation-related emotional experiences). To ensure alignment with the present study, all items were administered with basketball as the specific activity context (i.e., item stems referred to “when participating in basketball” or “during basketball participation”), thereby operationalizing the construct as basketball participation rather than general physical activity participation. The scale includes 20 items across seven first-order dimensions: Functional assessment (5 items), Attentional focus in sport (4 items), Athletic ability (2 items), Learning behavior (3 items), Exercise behavior (2 items), Exercise-related emotion (2 items), and Leisure-related emotion (2 items). Items were rated on a 5-point Likert scale (1 = strongly disagree, 5 = strongly agree), with higher scores indicating higher levels of basketball participation. In this study, the scale showed good internal consistency (Cronbach’s α = 0.897).

Psychological flexibility: This study used a scale developed by Cao to measure individual psychological flexibility50. The scale consists of 7 items and is designed to assess an individual’s psychological flexibility in physical activity. The scale uses a 5-point Likert scale for scoring (1 = “Strongly Disagree,” 5 = “Strongly Agree”), with higher scores indicating better psychological flexibility. Previous studies have shown that the scale has good cross-cultural applicability and internal consistency (Cronbach’s α > 0.80). In this study, the Cronbach’s α coefficient for the scale was 0.884, indicating high internal consistency.

Sleep quality: Sleep quality was measured using the Adolescent Sleep Quality Scale developed by Liu51, which has been widely applied in adolescent populations and validated across cultural contexts. The instrument comprises two dimensions: Daytime Distress (5 items) and Insomnia Symptoms (3 items), capturing both daytime and nighttime aspects of sleep-related functioning. Participants rated each item on a 5-point Likert scale (1 = Strongly disagree, 5 = Strongly agree), with higher scores reflecting greater levels of sleep-related problems. The scale has demonstrated strong psychometric properties in prior studies, with Cronbach’s α coefficients typically exceeding 0.80. In the present study, the internal consistency reliability was satisfactory, with Cronbach’s α = 0.860 for the Daytime Distress subscale and α = 0.734 for the Insomnia Symptoms subscale. These results indicate acceptable internal reliability for both dimensions and confirm the suitability of the instrument for assessing adolescents’ sleep quality within the current research context.

Data analysis

All statistical analyses were conducted using IBM SPSS Statistics 26.0 and AMOS 26.0. Prior to the main analyses, data screening procedures were performed to detect missing values, outliers, and violations of normality assumptions. All variables met the statistical assumptions required for parametric analyses.

Descriptive statistics, including means, standard deviations, and frequencies, were calculated to summarize participants’ demographic characteristics and main study variables. Internal consistency reliability for each construct was assessed using Cronbach’s α, with values above 0.70 considered acceptable52. To further evaluate construct reliability and convergent validity, composite reliability (CR) and average variance extracted (AVE) were computed. The thresholds for adequacy were set at CR > 0.70, AVE > 0.50, and standardized factor loadings > 0.6053.

Confirmatory factor analysis (CFA) was conducted to examine the construct validity of the four latent variables. Model fit was evaluated using multiple indices, including the chi-square to degrees of freedom ratio (χ²/df < 5), Comparative Fit Index (CFI > 0.90), Tucker–Lewis Index (TLI > 0.90), Standardized Root Mean Square Residual (SRMR < 0.08), and Root Mean Square Error of Approximation (RMSEA < 0.08) with its 90% confidence interval (CI)54.

Since all variables were measured through self-report questionnaires, the potential influence of common method bias (CMB) was tested using Harman’s single-factor test and a series of CFA-based comparisons among alternative measurement models (one-factor, two-factor, three-factor, and the hypothesized four-factor model)55. Additionally, an unmeasured latent method construct (ULMC) model was estimated by adding a latent factor loading on all observed indicators to control for potential method variance. CMB was considered negligible when the first unrotated factor explained less than 40% of the total variance and when the ULMC model did not significantly improve the model fit compared with the hypothesized model.

After confirming the reliability and validity of all measures, Pearson correlation analysis was performed to examine the bivariate relationships among the four main constructs. Subsequently, multiple regression analyses were conducted to assess the predictive effects of basketball participation and psychological flexibility on daytime distress and insomnia symptoms, controlling for gender, grade, and place of residence.

To test the hypothesized mediation model, structural equation modeling was performed in AMOS 26.0, specifying both direct and indirect pathways from basketball participation to the two indicators of sleep quality, with psychological flexibility serving as the mediator. Model fit was evaluated using the same indices as described above.

Finally, the bias-corrected percentile bootstrap method with 5,000 resamples was used to test the indirect effects. Mediation effects were considered statistically significant when the 95% confidence interval (CI) of the bootstrap estimates did not include zero, indicating a robust indirect effect of psychological flexibility in the relationship between basketball participation and sleep quality56.

Common method bias test

Given that all variables were measured using self-reported questionnaires, the potential influence of common method bias (CMB) was examined through both Harman’s single-factor test and confirmatory factor analyses of alternative measurement models. First, Harman’s single-factor test was conducted using unrotated exploratory factor analysis. The results indicated that five factors had eigenvalues greater than 1, jointly accounting for 62.36% of the total variance. The first factor accounted for 34.03% of the variance, which is below the commonly accepted threshold of 40%. This finding suggests that common method bias is unlikely to pose a substantial threat to the validity of the data.

To further verify this conclusion, a series of confirmatory factor analyses (CFA) were conducted to compare the fit of different measurement models, as shown in Table 3. The hypothesized four-factor model (Basketball Participation, Psychological Flexibility, Daytime Distress, and Insomnia Symptoms) exhibited excellent model fit (χ²/df = 2.479, CFI = 0.969, TLI = 0.965, SRMR = 0.025, RMSEA = 0.042 [90% CI = 0.037, 0.048]), substantially better than the one-factor, two-factor, and three-factor alternatives. Moreover, the addition of an unmeasured latent method construct (ULMC) to the four-factor model did not yield any significant improvement in model fit (χ²/df = 2.475, CFI = 0.970, TLI = 0.965, SRMR = 0.025, RMSEA = 0.042 [90% CI = 0.037, 0.048]). These results provide further evidence that common method bias is not a serious concern in this study, supporting the robustness of the self-reported measures.

Multiple regression analysis

To further examine the associations among basketball participation, psychological flexibility, and the two indicators of sleep quality, multiple regression analyses were conducted. The results are summarized in Table 5.

In Model 1, with daytime distress as the dependent variable, gender emerged as a significant control variable (β = 0.090, p < .01), whereas grade and place of residence were nonsignificant. After controlling for these demographic factors, basketball participation (β = − 0.188, p < .001) and psychological flexibility (β = − 0.355, p < .001) were both significant predictors of daytime distress. The model accounted for 20.3% of the total variance (R² = 0.203, F = 41.279, p < .001).

In Model 2, predicting insomnia symptoms, none of the control variables were statistically significant. Basketball participation (β = − 0.183, p < .001) and psychological flexibility (β = − 0.328, p < .001) were both negatively associated with insomnia symptoms. The model explained 18.5% of the variance (R² = 0.185, F = 36.770, p < .001).

Overall, these results indicate that both basketball participation and psychological flexibility are negatively related to daytime distress and insomnia symptoms, after controlling for demographic variables. The consistent pattern across models provides preliminary support for the hypothesized mediating role of psychological flexibility, which was further examined in the subsequent mediation analysis.

Structural equation modeling analysis

A structural equation model (SEM) was constructed to examine the direct and indirect associations among basketball participation, psychological flexibility, and two indicators of sleep quality: daytime distress and insomnia symptoms. The overall model demonstrated a satisfactory fit to the data, with χ²/df = 2.479, CFI = 0.969, TLI = 0.965, SRMR = 0.025, and RMSEA = 0.042 (90% CI [0.037, 0.048]). All fit indices met the recommended criteria (χ²/df < 5, CFI and TLI > 0.90, SRMR < 0.08, RMSEA < 0.08), indicating that the proposed model adequately represented the observed data.

As shown in Fig. 2, basketball participation had a significant positive effect on psychological flexibility (β = 0.324) and significant negative effects on both daytime distress (β = − 0.191) and insomnia symptoms (β = − 0.214). These findings indicate that higher levels of basketball participation are associated with reduced daytime distress and fewer insomnia symptoms, supporting the hypothesized H1 and H2. Psychological flexibility was found to have significant negative effects on daytime distress (β = − 0.398) and insomnia symptoms (β = − 0.402). These results suggest that individuals with higher psychological flexibility experienced lower levels of both forms of sleep-related problems. Moreover, the inclusion of psychological flexibility in the model reduced the direct effects of basketball participation on both outcome variables, indicating potential mediation effects to be further examined through indirect path analysis.

Bootstrapping mediation test

To further examine the mediating role of psychological flexibility in the relationships between basketball participation and the two indicators of sleep quality, a bias-corrected percentile bootstrapping procedure (with 5,000 resamples) was employed. Table 6 summarizes the total, direct, and indirect effects in the multiple mediator model.

For daytime distress, the total effect of basketball participation was significant (β = − 0.320, 95% CI [–0.388, − 0.247]). The direct effect remained significant after including the mediator (β = − 0.191, 95% CI [–0.265, − 0.113]), and the indirect effect via psychological flexibility was also significant (β = − 0.129, 95% CI [–0.170, − 0.095]). The indirect path accounted for 40.31% of the total effect, indicating that psychological flexibility partially mediated the relationship between basketball participation and daytime distress, supporting the hypothesized H3.

For insomnia symptoms, the total effect of basketball participation was similarly significant (β = − 0.344, 95% CI [–0.418, − 0.268]). The direct effect (β = − 0.214, 95% CI [–0.173, − 0.095]) and the indirect effect through psychological flexibility (β = − 0.130, 95% CI [–0.292, − 0.134]) were both significant, with the indirect path accounting for 37.79% of the total effect, supporting the hypothesized H4.

These findings indicate that psychological flexibility serves as a significant partial mediator in the associations between basketball participation and both indicators of sleep quality, thereby confirming the stability of the structural model established in the previous analysis.

Discussion

Overview of the findings

The present study examined the relationships among basketball participation, psychological flexibility, and sleep quality, focusing on two indicators of sleep-related functioning: daytime distress and insomnia symptoms. Using a large sample of adolescents (N = 818), the results revealed several consistent and statistically significant patterns across analyses.

First, descriptive and correlation analyses indicated that basketball participation was positively associated with psychological flexibility and negatively associated with both daytime distress and insomnia symptoms. These correlations provided initial evidence that higher engagement in basketball activities was linked to better psychological and sleep outcomes.

Second, the multiple regression analyses confirmed that basketball participation and psychological flexibility were both significant negative predictors of daytime distress and insomnia symptoms, after controlling for demographic variables. These findings were further validated by the structural equation modeling results, which demonstrated a good overall model fit and significant direct paths from basketball participation to both indicators of sleep quality.

Third, the bootstrapping mediation analysis revealed that psychological flexibility partially mediated the effects of basketball participation on daytime distress and insomnia symptoms. Specifically, individuals with higher levels of basketball participation tended to exhibit greater psychological flexibility, which in turn was associated with reduced sleep-related problems. The indirect effects accounted for approximately 40% of the total effects, indicating that psychological flexibility plays a substantial, though not exclusive, role in the relationship between sport participation and sleep quality.

Overall, the findings provide convergent empirical evidence for the beneficial role of basketball participation in promoting sleep-related health and for the psychological flexibility mechanism underlying this relationship. The robustness of the results across multiple analytic approaches supports the reliability and validity of the proposed model.

Interpretation of the relationships among key variables

Basketball participation and sleep quality

The results of this study show that basketball participation is significantly associated with lower levels of daytime distress and insomnia symptoms. This is consistent with a large body of research indicating that regular physical activity helps improve sleep quality and reduce sleep-related issues58,59. As a high-intensity sport with social interaction, basketball may improve sleep patterns by promoting physiological recovery, emotional regulation, and circadian rhythm stability.

From a physiological perspective, moderate-to-high intensity exercise improves sleep by facilitating thermoregulation and homeostatic processes25,60. The energy expenditure and subsequent body temperature decline resulting from exercise are associated with shorter sleep onset latency and deeper slow-wave sleep21. Basketball, characterized by high-intensity intermittent exercise and dynamic coordination, may promote restorative sleep through these mechanisms.

From a psychological perspective, participation in structured team sports like basketball provides individuals with opportunities for social interaction, goal-oriented motivation, and enhanced self-efficacy61. These experiences can reduce stress and negative emotions, which are significant psychological precursors to sleep disturbances. Therefore, the negative association between basketball participation and daytime distress and insomnia symptoms may reflect the “stress-buffering” effect of regular exercise.

Furthermore, this study extends this association to the adolescent population, which is a developmental stage where sleep problems and reduced physical activity are common. The results suggest that promoting adolescent participation in basketball may be a feasible behavioral pathway for alleviating sleep difficulties and improving daytime functioning.

Psychological flexibility as a mediating mechanism

The mediation analysis results show that psychological flexibility plays a significant partial mediating role between basketball participation and both daytime distress and insomnia symptoms. This finding reveals the pathway through which basketball participation improves sleep quality via psychological mechanisms. Psychological flexibility refers to the ability to face internal experiences openly while pursuing goal-directed behaviors that align with personal values14. Individuals with higher psychological flexibility can effectively regulate distressing thoughts or emotions, avoid excessive avoidance or rumination, and maintain adaptive functioning under stress38,62.

Basketball participation promotes psychological flexibility through various processes. First, the challenges and uncertainties of competitive sports require athletes to quickly adapt to changes, tolerate mistakes, and persist under pressure. These experiences cultivate acceptance of discomfort, cognitive reframing, and present-moment awareness—all key elements of psychological flexibility. Second, the social and cooperative aspects of basketball emphasize teamwork, communication, and emotional regulation, further strengthening individuals’ ability to flexibly manage emotional and cognitive responses.

In turn, higher psychological flexibility improves sleep quality by reducing maladaptive cognitive and emotional patterns that disrupt sleep. Empirical studies show that individuals with greater psychological flexibility experience fewer intrusive thoughts before sleep, lower physiological arousal levels, and lighter stress responses—all factors associated with insomnia and daytime fatigue16,63. Therefore, the mediating effect observed in this study reflects a self-regulation mechanism, where enhancing psychological flexibility through exercise participation indirectly improves sleep quality.

In conclusion, this finding integrates the theoretical perspective of Acceptance and Commitment Therapy (ACT) with the literature on exercise psychology, providing empirical support for the notion that participation in structured physical activity can enhance psychological flexibility, thereby promoting better emotional and physiological regulation, and contributing to healthy sleep64.

Integration with prior research

The findings of this study are largely consistent with previous research on the positive impact of physical activity on psychological and sleep functions8,65. Earlier studies have shown that adolescents and young adults who regularly engage in physical activity experience lower levels of sleep disorders, depressive symptoms, and fatigue65,66. In this study, the negative association between basketball participation and both daytime distress and insomnia symptoms confirms and extends these findings, applying them to a specific sport context characterized by high dynamic physical engagement and strong social interaction.

Moreover, the results of this study are consistent with emerging evidence that psychological flexibility is an important protective factor for sleep health15,16. Early research found that individuals with higher psychological flexibility tend to have lower levels of insomnia severity, less excessive cognitive attention to sleep, and better pre-sleep emotional regulation abilities15. This study empirically validates the mediating role of psychological flexibility between exercise participation and sleep outcomes, providing an integrated behavioral and psychological explanation for how structured physical activity promotes sleep quality.

It is noteworthy that the partial mediation effect found in this study suggests that, in addition to psychological flexibility, the positive impact of exercise participation on sleep may also be achieved through other mechanisms, such as physiological regulation, inflammation reduction, and social connectedness. Therefore, the results reinforce a multidimensional understanding of the exercise-sleep relationship, integrating both psychological and physiological aspects.

Unlike previous studies that emphasized general physical activity, this study focuses on basketball participation, thereby expanding the scope of the literature. The characteristics of team-based, skill-oriented sports like basketball—such as strategic cooperation, competition, and high-intensity engagement—may provide additional psychological benefits that are not fully captured by non-structured or individual exercise forms. Therefore, the results of this study contribute to further refining theoretical models on how different types of sport participation affect mental and behavioral health outcomes.

Theoretical and practical implications

Theoretical implications

This study deepens the theoretical understanding of the mechanism linking physical activity and sleep quality by identifying psychological flexibility as a key mediating variable. By integrating behavioral, emotional, and cognitive perspectives, the study provides a more comprehensive explanation of how structured physical activity promotes psychological and physiological health. Specifically, this study applies the psychological flexibility framework (originally developed within Acceptance and Commitment Therapy, ACT) to the field of exercise and sleep research67, expanding its theoretical scope.

The results indicate that participation in structured, skill-oriented team sports (such as basketball) may provide a natural context for enhancing psychological flexibility. This finding enriches the literature on the self-regulatory and value-driven functions of physical activity, which have often been overlooked in previous sleep studies. Additionally, the discovery of partial mediation suggests that psychological flexibility is one of several complementary mechanisms in the relationship between physical activity and sleep, supporting a multidimensional model that integrates psychological resilience, physiological regulation, and social adaptation.

By empirically validating this integrated model, this study contributes to the theoretical development in the fields of exercise and sport psychology, as well as behavioral sleep medicine. The research constructs a conceptual bridge between exercise-induced emotional adaptation and sleep-related cognitive-behavioral processes, enriching theoretical discussions on how behavioral activities enhance psychological and physiological health.

Practical implications

From an applied perspective, the findings offer meaningful implications for health promotion, educational practice, and preventive intervention. Encouraging regular basketball participation among adolescents may serve as an accessible and non-pharmacological strategy to improve sleep quality and reduce daytime distress. Given the social and cooperative nature of basketball, such activities can be integrated into school-based physical education programs to enhance not only physical fitness but also emotional well-being and psychological adaptability.

The mediating role of psychological flexibility further highlights the potential value of incorporating psychological skills training into sport and health programs. Interventions emphasizing mindfulness, acceptance, and value-driven behavior could be combined with physical activity initiatives to reinforce adaptive coping strategies and stress tolerance, thereby maximizing the overall benefits for sleep and emotional functioning.

Finally, the study underscores the importance of interdisciplinary collaboration between educators, sport psychologists, and sleep researchers in designing integrated interventions. Programs that combine structured physical activity with psychological flexibility training may yield synergistic benefits for youth health and well-being, contributing to more sustainable models of mental health promotion through sport.

Limitations and future directions

Several limitations should be acknowledged when interpreting the present findings. First, the study employed a cross-sectional design, which precludes definitive conclusions regarding causality among basketball participation, psychological flexibility, and sleep quality. Although the hypothesized model was theoretically grounded and statistically supported, longitudinal or experimental studies are needed to verify the temporal and causal relationships implied in the present model.

Second, the use of self-reported measures may introduce biases such as common method variance and social desirability effects. Although the statistical tests indicated that common method bias was not a major concern, future research could benefit from incorporating objective assessments—such as actigraphy, polysomnography, or physiological stress indicators—to provide a more precise evaluation of sleep quality and behavioral engagement in sport.

Third, the sample was composed of adolescent students from specific educational settings, which may limit the generalizability of the findings. Adolescence represents a developmental period characterized by unique biological and psychosocial changes that affect both sleep patterns and emotional regulation. Future studies should include more diverse age groups, cultural contexts, and types of sports to assess the broader applicability of the model.

Fourth, although the present study identified psychological flexibility as a key mediating variable, other potential mechanisms—such as emotional regulation, self-efficacy, and social connectedness—were not included. Subsequent research should adopt a multifactorial approach to examine how these psychological and social processes jointly influence the relationship between sport participation and sleep outcomes.

Finally, while basketball participation was treated as a form of structured physical activity, variations in intensity, frequency, and competitive level were not explicitly controlled. Future studies should distinguish between recreational and competitive forms of basketball participation, as well as consider individual differences in motivation, skill level, and training load, which may differentially affect psychological and sleep-related outcomes.

Conclusion

This study provides empirical support for a theoretically integrated model linking basketball participation, psychological flexibility, and sleep quality among adolescents. The results demonstrate that engagement in basketball, as a structured and socially interactive form of physical activity, contributes to lower levels of daytime distress and insomnia symptoms. Psychological flexibility emerged as a central mechanism through which this relationship operates, reflecting the capacity for adaptive self-regulation fostered through sport participation. By bridging perspectives from sport psychology, behavioral health, and sleep science, the present research advances understanding of how experiential and cognitive processes jointly shape sleep-related well-being. Beyond its theoretical contributions, the findings suggest that promoting basketball participation may represent a practical avenue for enhancing psychological resilience and sleep health in youth, highlighting the broader significance of integrating physical activity and psychological adaptability within health-promotion frameworks.
