Introduction

Cardiovascular disease (CVD) remains the world's leading cause of death, underscoring the importance of early detection through accessible, cost-effective tools like the electrocardiogram (ECG). Despite its widespread use, accurate ECG interpretation demands consideration of individual physiological factors, particularly sex- and ethnicity-specific variations in cardiac electrophysiology.

Decades of research have shown that males and females exhibit distinct ECG profiles. Women typically present with faster heart rates, shorter PR and QRS durations, and longer QT intervals, while men more commonly show features like J-point elevation, sometimes associated with arrhythmic syndromes such as Brugada syndrome. Ethnic background also plays a critical role, influencing parameters like ST-segment elevation, ventricular voltage patterns, and T-wave morphology.

Yet, current ECG reference ranges are largely derived from Western populations, leaving groups such as Southeast Asians—particularly Vietnamese individuals—underrepresented. This study addresses this gap by examining sex-based ECG differences in a large cohort of healthy Vietnamese adults. By establishing baseline reference values for key ECG parameters, this study aims to establish sex specified ECG reference values tailored to the Vietnamese population, supporting ethnically attuned diagnostic accuracy and risk stratification.

Materials and methods

Study design and participant

This cross-sectional study enrolled first-year Vietnamese university students as part of the annual pre-semester health screening program conducted by Phenikaa University, in collaboration with Phenikaa University Hospital in Hanoi, Vietnam.

Participants who fulfilled all inclusion criteria underwent a standard 12‑lead resting ECG. ECGs showing normal sinus rhythm, as defined by accepted diagnostic standards, were included for further analysis. In line with current clinical and research guidelines, ECGs demonstrating sinus arrhythmia, premature atrial contractions (PACs), or premature ventricular contractions (PVCs) were also retained. These findings are well-recognized as benign and physiologically normal in healthy young individuals.

ECG measurement and data collection

ECGs were recorded in the supine position using standardized 12‑lead placement and the MAC 5 Resting ECG system (GE Healthcare, USA; 500 Hz sampling rate). Trained nurses performed all recordings. ECGs were saved as anonymized PDFs and coded using the Minnesota system. Of 5391 recordings, 264 were excluded for technical issues; 5127 met quality criteria and were included in the analysis.

Core parameters—heart rate, PR interval, QRS duration, QTcB, and QRST angle—were automatically extracted via the GE Marquette™ 12SL™ algorithm. Additional features, including precordial QRS amplitude, J-point elevation, ST60, and T-wave amplitude (V1–V6), were manually measured relative to the PR segment. Ambiguous J-points were estimated using standard tangential methods.

All ECGs were reviewed independently by four trained physicians; discrepancies were adjudicated by a fifth cardiologist. Reviewers were blinded to the sex of the participants. Measurements were standardized and rounded to the nearest 0.5 mm (0.05 mV) to ensure consistency.

Statistical analysis

Statistical analyses were performed using STATA version 14 (StataCorp LP, College Station, TX, USA). Normality of continuous variables was assessed using the Kolmogorov–Smirnov test. Due to non-normal distributions, continuous ECG parameters were summarized as medians with interquartile ranges (25th–75th percentiles); means ± SD were also reported for comparison across studies. Between-group differences were evaluated using the Wilcoxon rank-sum test or independent t-test, as appropriate. Categorical variables were compared using the Pearson chi-square test. A two-sided p-value <0.05 was considered statistically significant. To characterize the distributional extremes and identify potential outliers, we additionally reported the 1st and 99th percentiles and calculated z-scores (standardized deviation from the mean) for age and all core ECG parameters. Reference ranges were defined by the 2nd and 98th percentiles.

Results

Baseline demographic

A total of 5127 healthy students were included (3002 females, 2125 males), with a mean age of 17.8 ± 0.6 years (range: 17–29). The cohort was heavily skewed toward younger ages, with 94.5 % aged 17–18 (23.8 % and 70.7 %, respectively). Only 5.5 % were older than 19 years, including two students aged up to 28 years (maximum z-score + 15.4). Percentile analysis confirmed this narrow distribution (1st–99th percentiles: 17–20 years). Older participants were retained given their rarity and absence of ECG abnormalities (Fig. 1, Table 1).

Basic ECG parameters

Age showed minimal variability, with a median and IQR of 18 years (17–18), and 94.5 % of participants clustered at 17–18 years. Heart rate had a median of 82 bpm with an IQR of 73–92 bpm, and outliers beyond ±3 SD were rare (Table 1). Conduction intervals were consistent with physiological norms: the median PR interval was 138 ms (IQR 128–150 ms), QRS duration 84 ms (IQR 78–92 ms), and QTcB interval 416 ms (IQR 401–430 ms). Dispersion was modest, with 1st–99th percentile boundaries spanning 104–188 ms for PR, 66–110 ms for QRS duration, and 362–466 ms for QTcB. The QRST angle, reflecting repolarization heterogeneity, had a median of 32° (IQR 21–46°), with the majority of values within ±2 SD, although rare extremes extended to +8 SD (Table 1).

Sex-based differences in core ECG parameters

Statistical comparison of ECG parameters between males and females using the Wilcoxon rank-sum test. Females had a higher resting HR than males, with a median of 83 bpm (IQR 75–93) versus 80 bpm (IQR 70–91), respectively (p < 0.001; z = 8.259). PR intervals were shorter in females [138 ms (IQR 128–150)] than in males [140 ms (IQR 130–152), p < 0.001; z = −6.010]. In contrast, males exhibited significantly longer QRSd [90 ms (IQR 84–96)] compared to females [80 ms (IQR 76–86); p < 0.001; z = −37.509].

The QTcB was notably prolonged in females [423 ms (IQR 409–437)] compared to males [406 ms (IQR 391–420)] (p < 0.001; z = 27.730). Similarly, the QRST angle was significantly wider in females [35° (IQR 23–50)] than in males [28° (IQR 18–41); p < 0.001; z = 12.209]. (Table 2).

Discussion

Ethnicity-specific core ECG parameter:

In our study, we observed a combined mean heart rate of 82.8 bpm (Table 1), notably higher than the values reported in several other ethnic cohorts. For instance, the Singaporean SAFE study by Sia et al. reported lower mean heart rates among Southeast Asian males, while Palhares et al. found a median of 65 bpm in Brazilian males aged 16 years and older, and Ahmadi et al. reported a mean of 70.6 bpm in the Tehran adult population [[1], [2], [3]] (Table 6).

The mean PR interval in our study was 139.9 ms, which is shorter than the median values reported in Chinese (149 ms, Wu et al.), Thai (148–164 ms, Khumrin et al.), and Brazilian (150–164 ms, Palhares et al.) studies. [[3], [4], [5]] This finding aligns more closely with the SAFE study, which found shorter PR intervals in young Southeast Asian males. [1]

Regarding QRSd, the mean value of 84.8 ms in our sample is comparable to the findings from Wu et al. and Palhares et al. (median 94 ms) and falls within the range reported by Khumrin et al. (76–142 ms). [2,4,5] It is slightly lower than the mean of 87.9 ms reported in Nigerian males (Katibi et al.) but consistent with other global populations [6].

Our QTcB interval averaged 415.5 ms, slightly higher than values reported in Mason et al. (403 ms), Palhares et al. (406 ms), and notably higher than the 394 ms found in a Swiss conscript study. [2,7,8]

Additionally, frontal plane axis measurements, such as mean R axis (70.7°), T axis (39.2°), and QRST angle (35.1°), are relatively understudied in other populations but appear to follow similar patterns of variation. Palhares et al. and Ahmadi et al. documented shifts in QRS and T axes with age and sex, suggesting our findings may contribute valuable normative data for regional diagnostic criteria in Southeast Asia. [2,3]

Sex - specific core ECG parameters

The higher resting heart rate in females, observed in our study (84.1 vs. 80.9 bpm), aligns with findings from the Tehran Cohort (Ahmadi et al.) and Brazilian studies (Palhares et al.), reinforcing the influence of autonomic regulation and hormonal differences. [2,3] Based on percentile analysis, we suggest reference ranges of 60–115 bpm in females and 57–115 bpm in males.

The shorter PR interval in Vietnamese females (138.7 ms vs. 141.6 ms in males) echoes similar sex gaps reported in Thai (Khumrin et al.) and Chinese (Wu et al.) populations, with Southeast Asian groups tending toward shorter overall PR durations. [4,5] For our cohort, we recommend limits of 106–178 ms for females and 110–181 ms for males.

Males in our cohort had longer QRS durations (90.7 ms vs. 80.7 ms), a pattern universally observed across studies in Nigeria, Iran, and Latin America, likely reflecting greater myocardial mass and conduction path length in males. [2,3,6] Based on centiles, we suggest reference ranges of 66–100 ms in females and 74–110 ms in males, consistent with AHA recommendations that values >110 ms in adults older than 16 years be considered prolonged [22].

Similarly, QTcB intervals were significantly prolonged in females (422.8 ms vs. 405.2 ms), a well-established finding with implications for sex-specific arrhythmia risk thresholds. The Thai study found much longer QTcB values across age groups (proposing 380–495 ms as “normal” in their population) [4], whereas our Vietnamese cohort shows narrower ranges, with reference limits of 378–464 ms in females and 362–446 ms in males.

Notably, the QRST angle, a less commonly reported parameter, was also significantly higher in Vietnamese females. While Palhares et al. suggested wider QRS–T variability in males [2], our findings support reference ranges of 3–90° for females and 2–78° for males in this Vietnamese student cohort.

When compared with elite athletes, our Vietnamese freshmen demonstrated higher heart rates (82.8 bpm vs. 54–57 bpm in MLS and WNBA athletes) and shorter PR intervals (139.9 ms vs. 164–174 ms) [Lander et al. 2024; Bryde et al. 2025; [19,20]], reflecting the absence of training-induced vagal adaptations such as sinus node remodeling and atrioventricular conduction slowing [17,18]. QRS durations were shorter than those of MLS men (96–100 ms) but comparable to WNBA women (∼90 ms), consistent with sex- and size-related differences in ventricular mass, with testosterone promoting greater hypertrophy in men and women maintaining smaller, more eccentric dimensions [17,18]. QTc intervals showed the expected sex difference, with Vietnamese women demonstrating longer QTc (422.8 ms) than men (405.2 ms), paralleling athlete and non-athlete cohorts and reflecting estrogen's prolonging effect on repolarization, compounded by lower hemoglobin and blood volume in women [17,18]. These sex effects persist despite training, with WNBA women showing longer QTc than MLS men, indicating that while training primarily modulates sinus rate and PR interval, sex hormones and cardiac size exert stronger influence on QRS duration and QTc across populations [25].

Vietnamese young adults' precordial ECG pattern

Gender-related differences in J-point amplitude and ST-segment morphology have been consistently reported across numerous studies. These distinctions form the basis of what is commonly referred to as the “male” and “female” repolarization patterns on the ECG, particularly in the precordial leads. [[9], [10], [11],24,27]

The male ECG pattern—characterized by higher J-point elevations, steeper ST segments, and greater early repolarization—is largely influenced by testosterone and emerges during puberty, peaking in young men aged 17–24 (up to 91 %), before declining with age to around 14 % in older men [26]. In contrast, the female pattern—defined by lower J-point and ST amplitudes with flatter repolarization—remains consistent across the lifespan, with a stable prevalence of ∼80 % from adolescence through old age. [10,12,24,27] (Fig. 2).

J point and ST-segment pattern in precordial leads

In our cohort, the 98th percentile ST60 in V2 reached 0.40 mV in males, exceeding the commonly cited 0.30 mV threshold for White males, and aligning more closely with values reported in Black male populations. [13,14] Among females, the ST60 amplitude peaked at 0.20 mV, consistent with the ULN for White women. In both sexes, lead V2 consistently demonstrated the highest ST elevations (Table 7).

Our findings parallel those of Surawicz and Parikh. In their cohort of young adults (ages 17–24), this pattern was observed in 91 % of males but only 13 % of females. [13] Similarly, Vietnamese males in our study showed mean J-point elevations ≥0.10 mV and ST60 values up to 0.40 mV in V2–V3, while females displayed significantly lower values—the mean ST60 in V2 was just 0.072 mV. [13,14]

A large study by Hiss et al. found that 91 % of 6014 healthy U.S. Air Force men (aged 16–58) exhibited 1–3 mm ST-segment elevation in at least one precordial lead, most commonly in V2. [15] The 0.40 mV (4 mm) ST60 amplitude observed in Vietnamese males at the 98th percentile in V2 is comparable in magnitude and lead distribution.

Importantly, the AHA/ACC/HRS recommendations emphasize that thresholds for abnormal J-point elevation should be ≥0.2 mV in men ≥40 years, ≥0.25 mV in men <40 years, and ≥ 0.15 mV in women in leads V2–V3, with ≥0.1 mV in all other leads considered abnormal. [16]. When our Vietnamese data are interpreted against these recommendations, the high J point amplitudes observed in males likely represent a normal population-specific variant analogous to the racial differences already recognized in Black versus White cohorts. In females, the values remain closer to established reference ranges for White and Black women.

Using the 98th-percentile cutoffs observed in our cohort, we recommend sex- and lead-specific ULNs for J-point and ST60 amplitude (e.g., male V2 ST60 ≤ 0.40 mV; female V2 ST60 ≤ 0.20 mV; J-point generally ≤0.10–0.25 mV depending on lead) (Table 4).

Precordial T-wave inversion (PTWI)

In our Vietnamese cohort (mean age 17.8 ± 0.6 years, with 94.5 % aged 17–18), PTWI was predominantly confined to V1, where it was strikingly common, especially in females (84.6 % vs. 48.1 % in males), consistent with a benign age- and sex-related pattern (see Table 5). The prevalence fell steeply in more lateral leads, occurring in only 2.5 % of females and 0.9 % of males in V2, 3.7 % and 0.1 % in V3, and ≤ 0.6 % in V4–V5, with complete absence in V6. These findings mirror those of Malhotra et al., who reported that in healthy White individuals aged 16–35, PTWI was largely confined to V1–V2 and more common in women [10]. While the “juvenile” pattern of anterior TWI beyond V2 is considered a normal variant only in adolescents ≤16 years or those who are prepubertal [Drezner et al. 2017], the AHA/ACCF/HRS statement notes that TWI in V1–V2 remains normal in young adults, particularly women [16,23]. Notably, the prevalence of V1 TWI in our Vietnamese females (84.6 %) is far higher than the 2.5 % reported in White athletes aged 14–15 years by Drezner et al., underscoring marked ethnic differences in repolarization patterns. In our cohort, inverted T waves were generally shallow, with amplitudes within expected sex-specific ranges, reaching up to 1.3 mV in V2–V3 in males [16]. Taken together, these results suggest that TWI confined to V1–V2 in Vietnamese adolescents should be interpreted conservatively as a benign finding, reducing the risk of overdiagnosis in clinical screening.

By contrast, anterior TWI extending beyond V2 was uncommon in our cohort (0.9 % of males and 2.5–3.7 % of females in V2–V3) and markedly less frequent than in professional athlete cohorts. For example, MLS athletes—particularly Black players—demonstrated anterior TWI in up to 8.6 % of cases [Bryde et al.], while African adolescent athletes showed TWI extending into lateral and inferior leads in 14 % of cases [Di Paolo et al.]. [20,21] Interestingly, the modest female predominance observed in our Vietnamese youth contrasts with the male-predominant distribution in African cohorts, highlighting how sex, ethnicity, and training status interact to shape repolarization patterns.

Limitations

This study has several limitations. First, because nearly all participants were 18-year-old first-year university students, the sample represents a narrow, academically selected subset of young Vietnamese adults, which may limit the generalizability of our findings to older individuals or non-academic populations. Second, although strict inclusion criteria were applied, lifestyle factors such as diet, smoking, alcohol consumption, stress, sleep patterns, and participants' anxiety levels during ECG recording were not systematically assessed. Third, we did not account for body composition or detailed physical fitness levels, which may confound ECG indices like heart rate and QT interval, especially in sex comparisons. Fourth, while hormonal therapies were excluded, serum hormone levels were not measured, limiting insights into endogenous hormonal effects on repolarization. Fifth, the absence of adjunctive diagnostic testing such as echocardiography, cardiac MRI, or genetic screening means that subclinical structural or inherited cardiac disease cannot be completely excluded. Sixth, the cross-sectional design precludes longitudinal assessment of how ECG features evolve with age or their prognostic significance. Finally, while percentile-based reference ranges were defined, further external validation in independent Vietnamese and Southeast Asian cohorts is necessary before clinical implementation.

Conclusion

This study represents the largest investigation to date of sex-specific electrocardiographic reference values in a healthy Vietnamese population aged 17–29 years. Our findings underscore clear and consistent sex-based differences across multiple ECG parameters, including HR, PR interval, QRSd, QTcB, and QRST angle, supporting the need for sex-specific interpretation in routine clinical practice. Notably, the mean QTc interval in Vietnamese females (423 ms) and the median QRS duration in males (90 ms) are higher than values commonly reported in Western cohorts, signaling the importance of regionally appropriate diagnostic thresholds.

We also provide the first detailed characterization of anterior repolarization patterns in Vietnamese youth. ST-segment elevation in V2 was highly prevalent among males, resembling early repolarization patterns traditionally reported in athletes or individuals of African descent, while T-wave inversion beyond V2 was exceedingly rare and strongly female-predominant. These findings indicate that both patterns likely represent benign, ethnicity-specific variants in this population.

Taken together, these results emphasize the clinical relevance of incorporating population-specific ECG norms into cardiovascular risk stratification and screening protocols. Although our cohort was limited to late adolescents and young adults in a university setting, the robust normative ranges presented here provide a critical foundation for future longitudinal and multicenter validation studies aimed at improving the accuracy and equity of ECG interpretation across Southeast Asia.
