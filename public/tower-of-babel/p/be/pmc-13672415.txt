Machine learning–based prediction of hospital-associated complications after tibial fracture surgery in older patients: a nationwide Japanese database study
Authors: Akio Shimizu, Ryota Sakamoto, Hirofumi Nagayama, Ryo Momosaki
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundOlder adults requiring emergency surgery for acute tibial fractures are vulnerable to hospital-associated complications (HACs), but admission-time risk stratification tools are lacking. We aimed to characterize HACs and develop both an ensemble prediction model and a simplified bedside risk score.MethodsThis retrospective cohort study used the Japanese Diagnosis Procedure Combination database provided by JMDC Inc. Patients aged ≥ 65 years emergently admitted for tibial fracture (ICD-10: S82, 2014–2025) who underwent surgery within 5 days, with stay > 5 days, were included. The primary outcome was composite HACs during index hospitalization. Missing data were handled with multiple imputation. A Super Learner ensemble was developed and evaluated on held-out test data, and a simplified scorecard was derived using analysis of variance (ANOVA)-based feature selection and Weight-of-Evidence transformation.ResultsAmong 53,186 admissions, 5,193 met eligibility criteria. HACs occurred in 851 patients (16.4%), most commonly delirium (7.5%) and falls/trauma (5.7%). The Super Learner achieved a test-set area under the receiver operating characteristic curve (AUC) of 0.740 (95% CI 0.707–0.772), higher than conventional linear logistic regression (0.724). The most influential predictors were Hospital Frailty Risk Score, dementia, Barthel Index, comorbidity burden, and days to surgery. The simplified 7-variable scorecard achieved a test-set AUC of 0.736 (95% CI 0.703–0.769), stratifying patients into five risk groups (HAC rate: 3.45%–38.64%).ConclusionsHAC risk after emergency tibial fracture surgery in older adults was driven primarily by geriatric vulnerability rather than fracture-specific factors. A simplified admission-time score may support targeted prevention, pending external validation.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at https://doi.org/10.1007/s40520-026-03460-x.Keywords: Tibial fracture, Hospital-associated complications, Older adults, Frailty, Machine learning

Introduction

Population aging has increased the number of fall-related fractures treated in emergency departments and the need for hospitalization among adults aged 65 years and older [1, 2]. Tibial fractures are clinically important injuries, with an incidence of 51.7 per 100,000 person-years [3], and their incidence increases with age, particularly among women [3, 4]. In older adults, tibial fractures often result from acute falls and may require urgent hospitalization [3] and operative management, especially when the proximal tibia or knee joint is involved [3, 5]. These injuries may substantially affect mobility, activities of daily living (ADL), and longer-term quality of life [6]. Furthermore, older adults with fractures are at increased risk of perioperative complications related to anesthesia, prolonged immobilization, and polypharmacy [7]. Therefore, improving perioperative care and early risk management in this population is an important clinical priority.

Hospital-associated complications (HACs) in older inpatients, including functional decline, delirium, incontinence, falls, and pressure injuries, are common geriatric syndromes precipitated by acute illness, immobilization, and medication burden [8–10]. Their incidence in older adults with tibial fractures requiring emergency hospitalization and surgical treatment remains unclear. In patients with hip fractures, delirium and other in-hospital complications are associated with higher mortality and poorer recovery [11, 12]. Proactive geriatric consultation and structured delirium prevention programs reduce delirium after hip fracture surgery [13–15]. Because these interventions depend on early identification of high-risk patients, timely risk stratification at admission is essential.

However, effective risk stratification for HACs in older adults undergoing emergency tibial fracture surgery remains challenging. The Hospital Frailty Risk Score (HFRS) [16] is a claims-based frailty measure derived from routinely collected ICD-10 codes and has been evaluated as a prognostic indicator in several orthopedic settings [17, 18]. In older surgical patients, frailty, comorbidity, and cognitive vulnerability often coexist and interact, suggesting that risk stratification based on a single measure may be insufficient. Ensemble machine learning approaches that capture such complex interactions may improve HAC risk stratification in this population [19].

In this study, using a Japanese nationwide claims database, we aimed to (1) describe the incidence and patterns of HACs in older adults hospitalized for emergency tibial fracture surgery, (2) develop an ensemble machine learning model to identify the geriatric risk domains most predictive of HACs, and (3) translate the model into a simplified point-based risk score for bedside use.

Methods

Study design and data source

This retrospective cohort study used the JMDC Hospital Database (JMDC Inc., Tokyo, Japan), a hospital-based administrative claims database that integrates claims and Diagnosis Procedure Combination (DPC) data from acute care hospitals across Japan. As of March 2025, the database included data from 1,136 medical institutions [20]. The database structure and available variables have been described previously [21]. The study is reported in accordance with the TRIPOD statement [22] and the TRIPOD + AI guidance [23] for machine learning–based prediction modeling.

Study population

We identified patients aged ≥ 65 years with a primary diagnosis of tibial fracture (ICD-10: S82) who were admitted between February 2014 and September 2025. The eligibility criteria were designed to approximate a cohort of community-dwelling older adults who sustained an acute tibial fracture and underwent early surgical management, thereby aligning the cohort definition with the study’s objective of admission-time risk stratification for non-fatal HACs. Specifically, we required: (1) emergency admission via ambulance from home, to restrict the cohort to community-dwelling individuals with acute injury; (2) surgical treatment of tibial fracture initiated within 5 days after admission, with the day of admission defined as day 0, to capture patients managed with an early operative strategy; (3) length of stay > 5 days, to ensure a sufficient observation window for HACs to manifest; and (4) exclusion of patients who died during hospitalization, to focus on non-fatal HACs and avoid incomplete ascertainment among decedents. The target surgery procedure codes used to identify the eligible cohort are detailed in Supplementary Table 1.

Ethical approval

The dataset was provided to researchers as de-identified and unlinkable data; therefore, the Ethics Committee of Mie University determined that formal ethical review and informed consent were not required.

Definition of HACs

The primary outcome was a composite of HACs, defined as the occurrence of one or more complications recorded as a post-admission diagnosis (i.e., not present on admission) during the index hospitalization. The selection of HAC domains was informed by the HAC of older people (HAC-OP) framework [9] and its adaptation for Japanese administrative data [10]. HACs comprised seven domains: delirium (identified by a recorded diagnosis of delirium combined with recorded prescriptions of drugs used to manage agitation, such as injections of haloperidol or other antipsychotic drugs), pressure injury, falls or trauma, respiratory complications (pneumonia, aspiration pneumonia, respiratory failure, and postprocedural respiratory disorder), urinary tract infection, cardiovascular complications (heart failure and deep vein thrombosis or pulmonary embolism), and gastrointestinal complications (bowel obstruction and dysphagia). These complications are frequently observed in older fracture patients [17, 24, 25]. Functional decline and hospital-associated incontinence, included in the original HAC-OP framework [9] and its Japanese adaptation [10], were not used as outcome domains because patients admitted emergently with tibial fractures typically present with acute ADL impairment at admission, making it difficult to distinguish hospital-associated functional decline from injury-related disability; instead, these variables were incorporated as predictors using Barthel Index (BI) [26] scores at admission. The specific ICD-10 codes used to define each domain are listed in Supplementary Table 2.

Clinical predictors

We prespecified 11 candidate predictors based on clinical relevance and availability in the DPC database. Predictor selection followed two principles: (1) variables had to be routinely available at admission without additional bedside assessment, and (2) variables were chosen to represent key geriatric domains that have been associated with postoperative outcomes in older surgical patients. Frailty was assessed using the HFRS [16], an ICD-10–derived cumulative frailty measure validated in multiple orthopedic surgical populations [17, 18, 24]. Functional status at admission was assessed using the BI [26], a 0–100 measure of basic ADL in which higher scores indicate greater independence. Cognitive status was represented by the criteria for determination of the daily life independence level of older adults with dementia (CDED) (none/I/II/III/IV/M), a government-mandated ordinal classification documented in administrative records, in which higher categories indicate greater dementia-related dependence [27]. Comorbidity burden was quantified using both the Charlson Comorbidity Index [28] and the total number of comorbid conditions, reflecting weighted and unweighted disease burden. Additional demographic and clinical predictors included age, sex, body mass index (BMI), days from admission to surgery, the Brinkman index as a marker of smoking-related respiratory risk, and use of general anesthesia. All predictors, except days from admission to surgery and use of general anesthesia, were obtained from information available at admission. Because this study aimed to stratify admission-time risk among older patients emergently admitted for tibial fracture surgery, these two perioperative variables were treated as proxies for the initial operative plan formulated at admission.

Statistical analysis

Missing predictor data (BMI, 17.5%; BI, 12.9%; dementia level, 0.1%) were handled using multiple imputation by chained equations with random forest imputation, generating 20 imputed datasets with 20 iterations each. The cohort was randomly split into training (70%) and test (30%) sets before imputation, and imputation was performed separately within each set to prevent data leakage. The outcome variable was not imputed. The cohort size was determined by the number of eligible admissions, and sample-size adequacy for model development was confirmed against established criteria (approximately 40 events per variable; minimum required size 1,265 by the method of Riley et al. [29]; Supplementary Table 3).

We developed a prediction model using a Super Learner ensemble framework [30], which combines multiple candidate algorithms through stratified 10-fold cross-validation. The base learner library comprised 23 algorithms, including linear and penalized regression, Bayesian generalized linear models, random forests, gradient boosting, multivariate adaptive regression splines, generalized additive models, and neural networks. Each algorithm was implemented with multiple hyperparameter configurations and selected to avoid assuming a single functional form a priori. Out-of-fold predictions from each learner were combined by a non-negative least-squares meta-learner into the final ensemble (full workflow, Supplementary Fig. 1). The Super Learner was trained separately on each of the 20 imputed datasets, and predicted probabilities were pooled by averaging across imputations. As pre-specified comparators using the same predictors and pooling, we also fitted a conventional (linear) logistic regression and a flexible logistic regression with natural splines on continuous predictors. Model performance was evaluated in the test set. Discrimination was assessed using the area under the receiver operating characteristic curve (AUC) with 95% confidence intervals (DeLong method). Calibration was evaluated using the calibration intercept, calibration slope, and Brier score, and examined visually using calibration plots. Variable importance was quantified using permutation-based AUC decrease, computed within each imputed dataset and summarized across imputations.

To facilitate bedside use, a simplified scorecard was constructed following a multi-step procedure. Starting from the Super Learner’s permutation variable-importance ranking, highly correlated features were removed (Pearson r > 0.7, retaining the more important feature of each pair), and incremental feature selection was performed using three ranking strategies (LASSO, ridge regression, analysis of variance (ANOVA) F-statistic); the ANOVA-based approach selected 7 variables with the highest test-set AUC (0.7251). Continuous variables were discretized via decision-tree binning, and Weight-of-Evidence (WoE) was calculated for each bin. A logistic regression model was fitted using WoE-transformed features. Risk scores were derived using a shifted, normalized linear transformation calibrated to a 0–100 scale (higher score = higher risk). Risk stratification was conducted using Kolmogorov–Smirnov curve quintiles into five groups. All analyses were performed using R version 4.5.2 with fixed random seeds.

Results

Of 53,186 admissions for tibial fracture (ICD-10: S82) among patients aged ≥ 65 years, 5,193 met all eligibility criteria (Supplementary Fig. 2) and were split into training (n = 3,635) and test (n = 1,558) sets. The mean age was 76.5 (SD 7.3) years, 60.5% were female, and the median HFRS was 1.8 (IQR 0.0–4.4) (Table 1). The composite HAC occurred in 851 patients (16.4%), most commonly delirium (7.5%) and falls/trauma (5.7%) (Table 2).

The Super Learner ensemble achieved a test-set AUC of 0.740 (95% CI 0.707–0.772; training AUC 0.850), with a Brier score of 0.1228 (Supplementary Fig. 3). The calibration slope was 1.129 and the calibration-in-the-large was − 0.002, indicating acceptable calibration; the Hosmer–Lemeshow test was non-significant (P = 0.641). Among the base learners, the generalized additive model contributed the largest mean ensemble weight, followed by random forest and XGBoost (Supplementary Table 4). Permutation importance identified HFRS, absence of dementia, BI, number of comorbidities, and days to surgery as the top five predictors, and the feature contributions and their direction are summarized in Fig. 1 (Supplementary Fig. 4).

The simplified scorecard retained 7 variables selected by ANOVA-based incremental feature selection (Fig. 2). Item-specific points were summed to generate a total risk score, with higher scores indicating a higher predicted risk of HACs (Supplementary Table 5). The scorecard achieved a test-set AUC of 0.736 (95% CI 0.703–0.769), with a calibration slope of 1.013 and a Brier score of 0.1226 (Supplementary Fig. 3). HFRS showed the highest information value (0.409; strong), followed by BI (0.210), age (0.165), and number of comorbidities (0.163), all of which showed moderate predictive strength (Supplementary Fig. 4). Based on predefined cut points of the total score (17, 27, 37, and 47), patients were classified into five risk groups: very low (0–17; n = 319, HAC rate 3.45%), low (18–27; n = 306, 9.15%), normal (28–37; n = 319, 12.54%), high (38–47; n = 306, 18.63%), and very high (≥ 48; n = 308, 38.64%) (Fig. 3).

A conventional linear logistic regression yielded a test-set AUC of 0.724 (95% CI 0.690–0.758), which was lower than that of the Super Learner (DeLong P = 0.049). The flexible spline regression yielded an AUC of 0.733 and the scorecard yielded an AUC of 0.736. Neither differed significantly from the Super Learner in DeLong tests (P = 0.193 for the spline regression and P = 0.597 for the scorecard; Supplementary Table 6).

Discussion

This study used a large Japanese DPC administrative database to characterize HACs in older adults admitted for tibial fracture surgery. We also developed both an ensemble prediction model and a simplified admission-time risk score. The Super Learner showed acceptable discrimination, with a test-set AUC of 0.740. The simplified scorecard achieved similar performance, with a test-set AUC of 0.736. The scorecard stratified patients into five risk groups, with HAC incidence ranging from 3.45% to 38.64%. Across models, the most influential predictors were HFRS, absence of dementia, functional status, comorbidity burden, and days to surgery.

The composite HAC incidence in our cohort was 16.4%, with delirium (7.5%) and falls/trauma (5.7%) as the most frequent domains. In hip fracture populations, postoperative delirium rates of approximately 20 to 40% have been reported [31]. The lower delirium rate observed in the present study should be interpreted cautiously because delirium may be under-recorded in administrative data. Differences in case mix and surgical invasiveness between tibial and hip fracture surgery may also contribute. Even so, delirium and falls/trauma were the most frequent domains and are key prevention targets in this population. Proactive geriatric consultation and structured delirium prevention programs have been shown to reduce delirium after hip fracture surgery [13–15]. Whether similar strategies are effective in older adults undergoing tibial fracture surgery warrants further investigation.

Among candidate predictors, HFRS, cognitive vulnerability, and functional status emerged as the leading determinants of HAC risk. Although HFRS was originally developed as an ICD-10-based frailty screening index [16] and has been evaluated in orthopedic populations [17, 24], it does not directly capture other important dimensions of vulnerability such as cognition and ADL. The present findings therefore suggest that HAC risk in older surgical patients is multidimensional and cannot be adequately characterized by frailty alone. The additional contributions of dementia-related functional classification and admission BI support the importance of multidimensional geriatric assessment and are also consistent with the HAC-OP framework, which conceptualizes preventable adverse events in older inpatients as a cluster of interacting geriatric syndromes [9, 10].

Although these predictors are well established in geriatric care, their application to older adults undergoing tibial fracture surgery remains clinically relevant. Unlike hip fracture, for which orthogeriatric co-management is now well established [7, 25], tibial fractures are commonly managed within orthopedic pathways without routine geriatric assessment. In this cohort, HACs occurred in 16.4% of patients, and the scorecard identified a wide risk gradient from 3.45% to 38.64%. As an admission-time screening tool based on routine data, the score may support targeted use of preventive strategies, including geriatric consultation, delirium prevention, and early mobilization. Rather than replacing assessment already in place, the score functions as an admission-time triage step, identifying which patients warrant the proactive geriatric assessment that this population currently receives only inconsistently.

The performance and calibration of both models were acceptable. The Super Learner showed good overall calibration, and the simplified scorecard also demonstrated close agreement between predicted and observed risk in the test set. However, before the score can be used for absolute risk estimation or clinical decision support, external validation in an independent cohort is essential [32, 33]. Validation across hospitals and healthcare settings will be particularly important given the potential variability in coding practices and case mix.

This study has several strengths. First, the ensemble and the simplified scorecard were not independent competing models, but sequential components of a single development pipeline. The Super Learner was used to search across flexible functional forms and to prioritize predictors, whereas the scorecard was derived from these predictors using WoE binning to retain non-linear risk gradients in a simplified integer format. Thus, the scorecard’s performance close to that of the ensemble should be interpreted as preservation of ensemble-derived risk information, rather than as independent validation of the scorecard. External validation is required to assess its transportability, calibration, and clinical usefulness in other settings. Second, the composite outcome, based on the HAC-OP framework [9, 10], was deliberate: several domains were individually too infrequent (e.g., cardiovascular 0.7%, pressure injury 0.8%) for stable single-event prediction, yet share common admission-time risk factors and an overlapping, bundle-based prevention strategy, making the composite the appropriate target for an admission-time screening tool. In particular, the delirium definition incorporated both diagnosis codes and antipsychotic prescriptions, which may have improved specificity compared with diagnosis codes alone. Third, model interpretability was enhanced through permutation importance and individual-level prediction visualizations, which may facilitate clinical understanding.

Several limitations apply. First, this was a derivation and internal-validation study in a single database; external and temporal validation have not been performed, and some optimism in the more complex ensemble cannot be excluded. Second, outcome ascertainment relied on administrative coding, so delirium in particular may be under-recorded and coding practice may vary across institutions. Third, clinically relevant variables (nutritional status, polypharmacy, formal cognitive testing, operative time) were unavailable in the DPC database, potentially limiting performance and the identification of modifiable factors. Fourth, residual bias cannot be excluded if the missing-at-random assumption does not fully hold. Fifth, subgroup performance (by age, sex, fracture location, institution) was not systematically examined, so prediction bias cannot be ruled out. Sixth, excluding in-hospital deaths and short stays may limit applicability to the most acutely ill. Finally, although suitable for screening, the composite outcome is pathophysiologically heterogeneous, and predicting individual complications would require domain-specific models.

Conclusion

Among older adults undergoing emergency tibial fracture surgery, HAC risk appeared to be driven more by geriatric vulnerability than by fracture- or procedure-specific factors. External validation is needed before clinical implementation.
