Combined ultrasound localization and bronchoscopic-guided percutaneous dilatational tracheostomy in a child with pharyngolaryngeal venous malformation: a case report
Authors: Yunlei Zan, Quande Li, Bin Zhang, Tingting Gao, Hui Liu, Lei Wang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundPharyngolaryngeal venous malformations in children are rare congenital lesions that can cause severe supraglottic narrowing and pose significant challenges for airway management during anesthesia. When conventional intubation is unsafe or infeasible, alternative strategies are required to establish a secure airway and facilitate repeated interventions.Case presentationWe report a 6-year-old girl with an extensive pharyngolaryngeal venous malformation causing marked upper-airway obstruction and requiring multiple sessions of sclerotherapy. Preoperative evaluation revealed severe supraglottic narrowing that limited visualization of the glottic structures. To ensure a safe and stable airway, a combined approach using ultrasound localization and bronchoscopic-guided percutaneous dilatational tracheostomy (PDT) was selected. Under general anesthesia with preserved spontaneous ventilation through a laryngeal mask airway, ultrasound was used to identify the tracheal anatomy and determine an optimal puncture site. Bronchoscopic guidance enabled continuous airway visualization during puncture, dilation, and tube placement. After successful PDT, the patient safely underwent four sessions of image-guided sclerotherapy over four months. No airway-related complications, tracheostomy tube intolerance, or perioperative adverse events were observed. The patient was successfully decannulated and demonstrated complete airway recovery at follow-up.ConclusionsThis case illustrates that combining ultrasound localization with bronchoscopic guidance may offer a feasible, minimally invasive approach to establishing a stable airway in children with complex supraglottic obstruction. In this patient, the technique enabled repeated sclerotherapy sessions while maintaining ventilation stability and minimizing airway trauma. It may be considered as a potential option in carefully selected older children with immediate surgical backup when conventional airway techniques are limited by severe supraglottic distortion.Keywords: Pharyngolaryngeal venous malformation, Pediatric anesthesia, Percutaneous dilatational tracheostomy, Bronchoscopic guidance, Case report

Introduction

Pharyngolaryngeal venous malformations are rare congenital vascular lesions in children that may involve the soft palate, supraglottic region, and pharynx. As these lesions enlarge, they can distort upper-airway anatomy and substantially narrow the airway lumen, creating significant challenges for anesthetic management [1]. Securing a stable airway is particularly difficult in such cases, as conventional endotracheal intubation may be unsafe or impossible when supraglottic structures are displaced, compressed, or obscured.

Percutaneous dilatational tracheostomy (PDT) is widely used in adults because it is minimally invasive, can be performed at the bedside, and may reduce tissue disruption compared with open surgical tracheostomy [2, 3]. However, pediatric application remains limited and controversial due to smaller anatomical dimensions, the compliance of pediatric airway structures, and concerns regarding procedure-related complications [4, 5]. Ultrasound enables real-time identification of tracheal landmarks and adjacent vascular structures, potentially improving puncture-site selection and reducing vascular injury [6]. Bronchoscopic guidance provides continuous intraluminal visualization during needle entry, dilation, and cannula placement, helping confirm correct positioning and reduce the risk of posterior wall injury or false passage [2, 7]. Evidence suggests that combining ultrasound with endoscopic guidance may further enhance procedural safety [8].

Despite these potential advantages, reports describing the combined use of ultrasound localization and bronchoscopic guidance for PDT in pediatric patients—particularly in the setting of complex supraglottic vascular malformations—are extremely limited [9, 10]. Evidence regarding feasibility and safety remains sparse, and no standardized approach has been widely adopted in pediatric practice. Here, we describe the successful use of combined ultrasound localization and bronchoscopic-guided PDT to secure a difficult airway in a child with an extensive pharyngolaryngeal venous malformation requiring repeated sclerotherapy [11, 12]. This case highlights how a dual-guidance strategy, together with strict safeguards and immediate surgical backup, may offer a potential option in carefully selected older children with severe supraglottic distortion when conventional airway techniques are limited [13, 14].

Patient information

A 6-year-old girl weighing 19.5 kg was admitted with a 5-day history of an oral mass. Five days before admission, her parents noticed a bluish-purple swelling on the hard palate and palatine arch with ill-defined borders, accompanied by worsening nocturnal snoring and sleep disturbance. The child remained in good general condition, with normal appetite, urination, and defecation, and no significant change in body weight. She had a history of nocturnal snoring since 6 months of age. There was no history of blood transfusion, infectious disease, surgery, or trauma. The patient denied any drug or food allergies, and her immunizations were up to date at the time of admission.

Physical examination

Physical examination showed an alert and responsive child with stable vital signs. Oral inspection revealed a bluish-purple, soft, and poorly demarcated mass involving the hard palate and palatine arch, without tenderness. There was no cyanosis or respiratory distress. Lung auscultation demonstrated coarse breath sounds bilaterally. The abdominal examination was unremarkable.

Nasopharyngolaryngoscopy

Endoscopic examination revealed hypertrophic, orange-peel–like tissue in the nasopharynx partially obstructing the posterior choanae. The soft palate appeared locally elevated. Diffuse reddish vascular lesions were observed on the ventricular bands, aryepiglottic folds, and pyriform fossae, obscuring visualization of the vocal cords and indicating a probable pharyngolaryngeal venous malformation.

Neck ultrasonography

Ultrasound demonstrated soft-tissue thickening from the oropharynx to the upper thyroid level (approximately 4.6 × 3.2 × 2.2 cm) with indistinct margins and blurred visualization of the glottic structures. Color Doppler imaging showed low-velocity venous flow, consistent with a venous malformation.

Magnetic resonance imaging (MRI):

MRI revealed patchy, linear, and nodular enhancing lesions involving the soft palate, supraglottic region, anterior cervical tissues, and the posterior aspect of the upper trachea. The vascular malformation caused substantial supraglottic narrowing (Fig. 1), confirming a high-risk difficult airway.

Axial MRI of the pharyngolaryngeal region. Axial MRI demonstrating patchy and nodular enhancing lesions involving the soft palate, supraglottic region, and cervical soft tissues, causing significant airway narrowing

Bronchoscopy

Bronchoscopy showed multiple purplish-red, bead-like vascular proliferations on the soft palate, epiglottic base, posterior commissure, aryepiglottic folds, and pyriform fossae, significantly obstructing the glottis (Fig. 2a). Vocal cord mobility was preserved, with partial involvement of the right cord. The subglottic airway and trachea appeared normal (Fig. 2b).

Diagnostic challenges

Severe supraglottic narrowing limited visualization of key structures during endoscopy. The extensive vascular lesions obscured anatomical landmarks and compromised the clarity of both ultrasound and MRI images. The patient’s young age and limited cooperation further constrained image acquisition, necessitating multimodal assessment to evaluate the full extent of airway involvement.

Anesthesia and surgical procedure

Preoperative planning

Image-guided percutaneous sclerotherapy was planned. Due to the high-risk features of the pharyngolaryngeal venous malformation—including significant airway narrowing, the patient’s young age, and low body weight—a multidisciplinary consultation concluded that a tracheostomy followed by image-guided sclerotherapy would provide the safest airway management strategy. Considering the anticipated need for repeated sclerotherapy sessions, the anesthesiology team opted for bronchoscopic-guided percutaneous dilatational tracheostomy (PDT) to establish a stable artificial airway and ensure both perioperative safety and long-term airway control.

Preoperative preparation and assessment

The patient’s guardians were fully informed regarding the diagnosis, procedural risks, alternative treatment options, and prognosis, and written informed consent was obtained. Preoperative assessment classified the patient as ASA III, reflecting a high-risk difficult airway due to supraglottic and glottic narrowing caused by the venous malformation.

A comprehensive difficult-airway cart was prepared, including rigid and flexible bronchoscopes, endotracheal tubes of various sizes, laryngeal masks, tracheostomy instruments, and emergency airway equipment. Blood products and cardiopulmonary resuscitation equipment were readily available. The patient fasted for 6 hours for solids and 2 hours for clear liquids. Routine laboratory investigations—including complete blood count, liver and kidney function, and coagulation profile—were within normal limits.

Additional preparation included tracheostomy tubes, guidewires, dilators, dressing pack, curved mosquito forceps, sterile gloves, sterile gown, gauze, cotton balls, uncuffed endotracheal tubes (#3.5, #4.0, #4.5), suction catheters, povidone-iodine, and paraffin oil. Ultrasound imaging was performed using a Philips CX50 system. Flexible bronchoscopy was conducted with an Olympus BF-XP260F (outer diameter 2.8 mm).

Anesthesia management

Upon arrival in the operating room, standard monitoring was applied, including electrocardiography, heart rate (HR), oxygen saturation (SpO₂), and mean arterial pressure (MAP).

Rationale for LMA selection and risk–benefit considerations. Awake fiberoptic intubation was considered but deemed impractical in this 6-year-old child because limited cooperation and airway topicalization could provoke coughing/straining and bleeding from friable supraglottic venous lesions. Asleep fiberoptic intubation (nasal/oral) was also considered; however, severe supraglottic narrowing and obscured glottic landmarks increased the risk of failed intubation, airway trauma, and airway loss during repeated instrumentation. Although laryngeal mask airway (LMA) placement may cause mucosal trauma and bleeding, we considered the net risk of transglottic endotracheal intubation to be higher in this anatomy because even minor bleeding could rapidly obscure visualization and compromise both intubation success and ventilation. Therefore, an LMA was selected as the initial conduit to secure oxygenation, permit controlled bronchoscopic assessment, and preserve spontaneous ventilation during induction and LMA placement. To mitigate LMA-related bleeding risk, insertion was performed gently after confirming adequate mask ventilation and depth (BIS 40–60), followed by immediate bronchoscopic inspection.

Airway contingency planning. A predefined rescue algorithm was in place, with emergency front-of-neck access equipment prepared and the surgical airway team immediately available.

Plan A: Optimize LMA ventilation (jaw thrust, adjust head/neck position, deepen anesthesia, recruitment/PEEP as needed).

Plan B: Convert to face-mask ventilation with an oral airway and reassess; consider LMA repositioning/exchange if appropriate.

Plan C: If ventilation remained unstable and the anatomy allowed, proceed to fiberoptic-guided endotracheal intubation as a bridge to secure the airway.

Plan D (stop-and-convert): Immediate conversion to open surgical tracheostomy if ventilation became unstable, bleeding was uncontrolled, or intraluminal position could not be clearly confirmed.

Induction and transition to controlled ventilation. Anesthesia was induced with propofol (medium- and long-chain formulation, 2 mg/kg), sufentanil (0.1 µg/kg), and sevoflurane (3%) in oxygen at 3 L/min. After spontaneous movements ceased and BIS values reached 40–60, adequate mask ventilation was confirmed. A size 2.0 disposable LMA was inserted under preserved spontaneous ventilation during the highest-risk phase to avoid airway collapse potentially associated with neuromuscular blockade. After connection to the ventilator circuit and confirmation of stable tidal volumes and airway pressures, cisatracurium (0.2 mg/kg) was administered to facilitate bronchoscopy-guided PDT under controlled ventilation.

Ventilation strategy during bronchoscopy through the LMA and physiologic parameters. A flexible bronchoscope was introduced through the LMA for airway evaluation and positioning. Mechanical ventilation was delivered in pressure-controlled mode with an inspiratory pressure setting of 15–20 cmH₂O, a respiratory rate of 30 breaths/min, an inspiratory-to-expiratory ratio of 1:1.5, and an FiO₂ of 0.50. The bronchoscope was advanced through a bronchoscopy swivel adapter to minimize circuit leak. Pressure-controlled ventilation was maintained to accommodate variable airway resistance during scope manipulation and to limit peak airway pressures; measured peak airway pressure (Ppeak) transiently increased during bronchoscopy but remained within 18–22 cmH₂O. FiO₂ was titrated to the lowest level that maintained adequate oxygenation. Continuous monitoring included SpO₂, end-tidal CO₂ (ETCO₂), airway pressure, HR, and MAP. During bronchoscopic guidance and PDT, the patient remained physiologically stable (SpO₂ ≥ 95%; ETCO₂ 40–45 mmHg; HR 90–120 bpm; MAP 60–75 mmHg) without clinically significant desaturation or hemodynamic instability.

Anesthesia was maintained with continuous infusions of remifentanil (0.5 µg·kg⁻¹·min⁻¹) and propofol (medium- and long-chain formulation, 10 mg·kg⁻¹·h⁻¹).

Surgical procedure

The patient was positioned supine with shoulder and neck elevation to optimize exposure of the anterior neck. Ultrasound was used to identify the thyroid isthmus, cricoid cartilage, and tracheal rings, confirm normal anatomy, and select the puncture site.

A flexible bronchoscope was inserted via the LMA for continuous airway visualization. The puncture site was determined by aligning the ultrasound-identified external landmark with the bronchoscopic light spot (Fig. 3a). A 0.5–1 cm transverse skin incision was made at the marked site. Under real-time ultrasound and bronchoscopic guidance, a needle attached to a 20-ml syringe was advanced into the tracheal lumen (Fig. 3b). Following successful puncture, a guidewire was inserted through the needle (Fig. 3c), followed by sequential dilation of the subcutaneous tissue, muscle layers, and anterior tracheal wall using dilators and forceps (Fig. 3d–e). A size 4.5 tracheostomy tube was then introduced over the guidewire into the trachea (Fig. 3f). Correct placement was confirmed bronchoscopically, after which the tracheostomy tube was secured and connected to the anesthesia circuit.

Tracheal puncture performed with a needle under direct bronchoscopic visualization. c. Guidewire introduced into the tracheal lumen through the puncture needle. d–e. Sequential dilation of the anterior neck tissues and tracheal wall using dilators and forceps. f. Insertion of the tracheostomy tube over the guidewire with bronchoscopic confirmation

Once vital signs stabilized, image-guided percutaneous sclerotherapy was performed via the tracheostomy tube under general anesthesia. Following sterile preparation, a #4.5 needle was introduced under digital subtraction angiography (DSA) guidance for multi-point, multi-angle puncture. Small volumes of contrast revealed irregular microcystic spaces and venous reflux. Sclerotherapy was completed under combined ultrasound and DSA visualization. The patient was subsequently transferred to the intensive care unit for postoperative monitoring.

This approach ensured continuous airway visualization and accurate intraluminal placement, with readiness to proceed to open surgical tracheostomy if needed.

Follow-up and outcomes

The patient tolerated all four sclerotherapy sessions without airway-related complications or tracheostomy tube intolerance. At the four-month follow-up, bronchoscopy showed near-complete resolution of the supraglottic vascular lesions and a well-healed tracheostomy stoma, permitting safe decannulation (Fig. 4a). By six months, the patient demonstrated normal breathing, swallowing, and phonation, with no evidence of granulation tissue or tracheal stenosis (Fig. 4b). Final bronchoscopy confirmed minimal residual lesions and complete healing of the tracheostomy site.

Discussion

What this case adds: This case illustrates the feasibility of combining ultrasound localization with bronchoscopic guidance to perform percutaneous dilatational tracheostomy (PDT) in a pediatric airway severely distorted by a pharyngolaryngeal venous malformation. It demonstrates a carefully selected dual-guidance strategy designed to maintain ventilatory continuity and limit supraglottic manipulation, thereby enabling multiple subsequent sclerotherapy sessions. Importantly, we present this approach as a potential option in highly selected older children with immediate surgical backup, rather than a routine alternative to open tracheostomy.

Pharyngolaryngeal venous malformations frequently distort supraglottic anatomy, leading to airway narrowing and making both evaluation and airway management hazardous. When supraglottic structures obscure the glottis or render conventional intubation unsafe, airway strategies must prioritize continuous oxygenation while minimizing trauma to fragile vascular lesions.

Open surgical tracheostomy remains the standard approach in most pediatric centers. Although generally safe in experienced hands, pediatric tracheostomy—regardless of technique—can be associated with perioperative complications, particularly in anatomically complex cases. Reported complications include pneumothorax, subcutaneous emphysema, bleeding from adjacent vascular structures, and injury to airway or neural structures, underscoring the need for careful technique selection and rigorous risk mitigation in difficult pediatric airways. Systematic reviews indicate that complication risk is not uniform across children and is influenced by age/size, disease complexity, and perioperative context, reinforcing the need for individualized decision-making and robust safeguards in challenging pediatric airways [15]. Therefore, any consideration of PDT in children should be highly individualized and accompanied by strict safeguards [16, 17].

We fully acknowledge that percutaneous techniques in children remain controversial because of smaller airway dimensions and the pliability of the pediatric tracheal wall. Accordingly, we do not advocate routine pediatric PDT. In the present case, the decision was individualized and driven by three considerations: (1) the clinical need for repeated sclerotherapy sessions requiring a secured airway; (2) a bleeding-prone supraglottic venous malformation in which traumatic intubation or repeated translaryngeal instrumentation could precipitate hemorrhage and airway obstruction; and (3) the availability of a dual-guidance strategy with a clearly defined conversion pathway to open surgical tracheostomy.

Recent pediatric series suggest that PDT may be feasible in carefully selected children when performed by an experienced multidisciplinary team with endoscopic and/or ultrasound guidance and careful anatomic screening, while emphasizing that patient selection and institutional expertise are critical determinants of safety [18]. Consistent with this, we suggest that pediatric PDT—if considered at all—should be reserved for highly selected cases and performed under dual guidance: ultrasound for vascular mapping and anatomic localization, and endoscopy for continuous intraluminal visualization. An explicit “stop-and-convert” threshold should be predefined; unstable ventilation, uncontrolled bleeding, or uncertain intraluminal position should prompt immediate conversion to open surgical tracheostomy, consistent with recommendations to mitigate major tracheostomy-related complications and ensure timely rescue when adverse events occur [19].

In this context, airway management should minimize manipulation across fragile supraglottic vascular tissue while maintaining continuous ventilation [20]. We therefore adopted a laryngeal mask airway (LMA)-based strategy as a lower-trauma conduit, with a clearly defined escalation and conversion pathway [21, 22]. This LMA strategy was not intended to replace endotracheal intubation universally; rather, it aimed to reduce the risk of hemorrhage-related airway loss in a highly selected anatomic scenario with immediate surgical backup. Ventilation was maintained through the LMA using pressure-controlled ventilation via a swivel adapter, with close monitoring of oxygenation, ventilation, and hemodynamics during bronchoscopic guidance and PDT.

Although PDT has gained wide acceptance in adults because of procedural efficiency and limited tissue disruption [23, 24], its pediatric application is constrained by small anatomic dimensions, compliant cartilaginous structures, and challenges in landmark identification. In this case, ultrasound provided real-time visualization of anterior neck anatomy and vascular mapping to guide puncture-site selection and reduce vascular injury risk [25]. Simultaneously, bronchoscopic guidance enabled continuous visualization of needle entry, dilation, and cannula placement, supporting accurate intraluminal positioning and reducing the risk of posterior wall injury.

Anesthetic management posed additional challenges because bronchoscopy and PDT required a shared airway, and loss of airway tone could exacerbate obstruction. Maintaining spontaneous ventilation during the highest-risk phase and using an LMA as the ventilation conduit provided a stable platform for bronchoscopic assessment and avoided transglottic tube passage, which could have triggered bleeding or airway obstruction in the presence of supraglottic vascular lesions. Together, preserved spontaneous breathing, LMA placement, ultrasound localization, and bronchoscopic guidance facilitated controlled establishment of the tracheostomy.

This dual-guidance technique subsequently enabled repeated sclerotherapy sessions by providing a reliable airway while minimizing repeated supraglottic instrumentation. The patient experienced no airway-related complications, and full airway recovery was achieved, supporting the feasibility of this approach in carefully selected cases with immediate surgical backup and predefined rescue thresholds.

Limitations

As a single-case report, our observations cannot be generalized to all pediatric airway lesions or age groups, particularly infants and younger children in whom airway dimensions and tracheal compliance may confer higher procedural risk. This report does not allow direct comparison with open surgical tracheostomy, and it cannot define relative complication rates, optimal patient-selection criteria, or institutional prerequisites for safely performing pediatric PDT. In addition, although the patient had an uneventful clinical course, larger series with standardized and longer-term follow-up are required to better characterize late complications (e.g., granulation, stenosis, or tracheal deformation) and to determine the reproducibility of a dual-guidance (ultrasound plus endoscopy) strategy across centers.

Take-home message

In children with a severely distorted upper airway due to supraglottic obstruction, ultrasound localization combined with bronchoscopic-guided percutaneous dilatational tracheostomy (PDT) may be feasible when conventional airway techniques are unsafe or unsuccessful. An LMA-based strategy that preserves spontaneous ventilation during the highest-risk phase, together with dual imaging guidance, may facilitate controlled tracheal access while minimizing repeated transglottic instrumentation. This approach should be reserved for carefully selected older children and performed by an experienced multidisciplinary team with predefined stop-and-convert criteria and immediate surgical backup; it should not be considered a routine alternative to open surgical tracheostomy.

Patient perspective

The patient’s parents reported that they were initially anxious about the airway risks and the need for tracheostomy. They stated that detailed explanations and perioperative planning by the multidisciplinary team helped alleviate their concerns. During follow-up, they reported satisfaction with the child’s recovery and expressed relief that subsequent sclerotherapy sessions could be completed safely via the established airway.
