Predictive processing in visual perception of actions: The modulation of short-term priors by the long-term prior
Authors: Gizem Özen, Burcu A. Urgen
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Predictive processing theories suggest that perception is shaped by prior knowledge, with long-term priors formed through lifelong experiences and short-term priors emerging from immediate contextual cues. Although prior research has explored the influence of expectations on action perception, the interaction between short-term and long-term priors remains understudied. This study examines how these priors interact in visual action perception by embedding animated human and robot agents within a probabilistic-cueing paradigm. Participants observed agents performing biological or mechanical motion, with cues providing either form-based or motion-based short-term priors. Behavioral results showed that long-term priors guided perceptual decisions across conditions when the target stimuli were congruent with them. When expectations were violated, performance patterns reflected a greater weighting of motion-based short-term cues, while form-based cues contributed minimally. These findings highlight the hierarchical nature of predictive processing in action perception, and the precision assigned to different priors is adjusted according to context, favoring motion-based information instead of form-based when resolving perceptual ambiguity.

Introduction

The influence of expectations on perception is a well-established area of research in cognitive science. Prior knowledge about the world has been shown to play a significant role in shaping visual perception, often leading to measurable behavioral effects. Specifically, stimuli that align with our expectations tend to be perceived more quickly and accurately than those that contradict them (De Lange, Heilbron, & Kok, 2018; Pinto, van Gaal, de Lange, Lamme, & Seth, 2015). The underlying mechanism of expectation and prediction has been conceptualized within the predictive processing framework (Clark, 2015; Friston, 2010; Heeger, 2017), which posits that incoming sensory information is continuously compared against prior knowledge at multiple levels of the brain's neural hierarchy. This framework has been instrumental in advancing both theoretical and empirical research, including studies on the visual perception of others’ actions.

From a theoretical standpoint, the action observation network as a Bayesian model is aligned with predictive processing principles (Kilner & Frith, 2007). This network represents the neural system involved in observing and interpreting others’ actions. Empirical studies have tested these theoretical predictions by incorporating both human and non-human (robot) actions to create scenarios that violate expectations (Cook, Saygin, Swain, & Blakemore, 2009; Gazzola, Rizzolatti, Wicker, & Keysers, 2007; Sarıgül & Urgen, 2023; Saygin, Chaminade, Ishiguro, Driver, & Frith, 2012; Urgen, Kutas, & Saygin, 2018; Urgen & Saygin, 2020). These studies have consistently revealed distinct behavioral and neural responses when participants observe robotic actions compared to human movements.

While considerable research has explored how expectations influence action and body movement perception, most studies have not distinguished between short-term and long-term priors. Short-term priors refer to expectations introduced within the context of an experimental task, such as stimulus-stimulus associations in probabilistic-cuing paradigms (Vogel, Arnett, Blais, & Brewer, 2023). In contrast, long-term priors are shaped by lifelong experiences (De Jong, Brascamp, Kemner, van Ee, & Verstraten, 2014; De Lange et al., 2018; Hsieh, Vul, & Kanwisher, 2010; Oliva & Torralba, 2007). Some paradigms have extended these investigations to action perception, examining short-term predictive mechanisms (Sarıgül & Urgen, 2023) and long-term priors (Urgen et al., 2018). However, to the best of our knowledge, no study has systematically examined the relationship between short-term and long-term priors in the visual perception of actions, a crucial aspect of everyday life.

The present study aims to investigate the short-term and long-term priors in action perception. To this end, we designed animated characters, a human and a robot, that moved either in line with or against our lifelong expectations (long-term priors). These stimuli were then embedded in a probabilistic-cueing paradigm, introducing two types of short-term priors: form-based and motion-based cues. Participants were given a task in which they needed to identify the movement patterns in noisy animation sequences of human and robot characters. By analyzing reaction times and accuracy, we examined how different types of priors were used in this perceptual task.

Methodology

Participants

Thirty adults (13 males, 17 females) from the university community participated in the study, with a mean age of 29.6 years (SD = 4.62). The study was conducted at Bilkent University and received approval from the Human Research Ethics Committee. Informed consent was obtained from all participants at the start of the study.

Stimuli

The stimuli set consisted of 80 target videos and two cue images. The cue images depicted a robot and a human character standing idle. The target videos featured four distinct actions, performed by two different agents (Figure 1), in two motion styles and across five levels of noise. The agents, a human and a robot, were obtained from the Unity Asset Store (2022) (https://assetstore.unity.com/). The four actions included kicking, handwaving, walking, and itching. These actions were selected based on categories known to activate the parietal cortex (Orban, Sepe, & Bonini, 2021), with the criterion that only a single actor appeared in each visual scene. Motion capture data for these actions were sourced from Carnegie Mellon Graphics Lab's motion capture archive (http://mocap.cs.cmu.edu/) and the Tele-Immersion Lab at Berkeley University (https://tele-immersion.citris-uc.org/) (2022).

Static frames from the videos of human and robot characters. (A, E) Hand waving; (B, F) kicking; (C, G) itching; (D, H) walking. These character-action pairs were animated with two motion trajectories (See Figure 2).

Each action was performed in two different motion styles: biological and mechanical. For biological motion, both human and robot characters were animated using motion capture data (Figure 2 shows an example of a biological motion trajectory). The mechanical motion was created by disrupting the biological trajectory, following a method in Blender Foundation (2021) that preserves the final position of each direction change but alters the movement path (Özen & Urgen, 2023). This technique has been shown to produce motion perceived as mechanical (Figure 2).

Sample trajectories of motion from the two methods used in the study. Orange dashed lines represent biological motion; a continuous purple line represents mechanical motion.

To introduce ambiguity, the videos were masked by using five levels of noise (90%, 92%, 95%, 97%, and 99%). This approach, inspired by Platonov and Orban (2016), involved swapping pixel positions within the videos. Such noise manipulation is commonly used to encourage participants to rely on cue information rather than long-term prior knowledge (De Lange et al., 2018).

Procedure

The experiment began with instructions informing participants that they would observe three-dimensional characters performing either biological or mechanical motion. Their task was to determine the motion type by pressing a key. Participants were warned that this decision would be challenging due to the presence of noise throughout the videos. However, a cue image would be presented before each video, which they could use to help their judgment.

Each trial started with the presentation of a cue image for 500 ms, followed by a 500 ms fixation cross. After this, a target video was displayed for 2000 ms, during which participants were required to respond by pressing “B” for biological motion or “M” for mechanical motion. Once they provided their response, accuracy feedback (correct, incorrect, or missed) was displayed for 500 ms. Reaction times and accuracy were recorded for each trial. (see Figure 3) Reaction time was measured as the interval between the onset of the target video and the participant's button press. Accuracy was measured based on the correspondence between the motion type identified by participants and the actual motion in the target video.

The task paradigm is demonstrated through the visually congruent and incongruent robot video conditions. The “a” is the sequence of form-based congruence trials, whereas “b” is the sequence of the incongruent ones. The cue can be either the picture of the human or the robot. The target is the masked video of the human or robot with biological or mechanical motion. The videos are masked via 90%, 92%, 95%, 97%, or 99% noise levels. During the presentations of the target videos, participants are expected to identify the motion. After their identification, they are given feedback as “wrong,” “right,” and “missed” depending on their answer.

The study included two independent variables. The first was the type of prior, which had three levels: short-term form-based prior, short-term motion-based prior, and long-term prior. The second was the congruency between form and motion, which had two levels: congruent and incongruent. Long-term prior was defined as the natural relationship between an agent's form and motion in the target videos. A human agent moving biologically or a robot agent moving mechanically reflected a congruent long-term prior, whereas a human agent moving mechanically or a robot agent moving biologically reflected an incongruent long-term prior (see Table 1, columns Target and Long-term prior). Short-term priors were defined based on the relationship between the cue and target videos. Short-term form-basedvisual prior referred to the relationship between the form of the agent in the cue and the form of the agent in the target video (see Table 1, columns Cue, Target, and Short-term form-based), whereas short-term motion-based prior was based on the relationship between the form of the agent in the cue and the motion of the agent in the target video (see Table 1, columns Cue, Target, and Short-term motion-based). These short-term priors acted as brief predictive cues since they were presented immediately before the target videos. The probability of form-based and motion-based congruency was 75% for each (75% congruent, 25% incongruent).

Results

Accuracy

A repeated measures 2 × 2 × 2 ANOVA was conducted to examine the effects of congruent and incongruent conditions of three prior types (long-term prior, form-based short-term prior, motion-based short-term prior) on accuracy scores. A significant main effect of the motion-based short-term prior (F(1, 30) = 33.463, p = 0.001, η2 = 0.322) and the long term prior (F(1, 30) = 69.318, p = 0.001, η2 = 0.222) was found. Post hoc results revealed that accuracy was significantly higher in the congruent condition of the long-term prior than in the incongruent condition (t = 8.326, p < 0.001). Similarly, the congruent condition of the motion-based short-term prior has higher accuracy results than the incongruent condition (t = 5.784, p < 0.001; see Figure 4).

The plot illustrates accuracy for congruent and incongruent conditions across different types of priors. To differentiate between these conditions, darker shades denote congruent conditions, and lighter shades represent incongruent conditions. The results indicate that there are significant differences between congruent and incongruent conditions for the long-term prior and motion-based short-term prior, whereas there was no significant difference for the form-based short-term prior.

Conversely, there was no significant main effect of the form-based short-term prior (F(1, 30) = 3.228, p = 0.082, see Figure 3). On the other hand, there was no significant interaction effect between motion-based short-term prior and form-based short-term prior (F(1, 30) = 0, p = 1.0), motion-based short-term prior and long-term prior (F(1, 30) = −6.354 × 10−14, p = 1.0), form-based short-term prior and long-term prior (F(1, 30) = −4.130 × 10−13, p = 1.0). Moreover, there was no three-way interaction between long-term prior, form-based short-term prior, and motion-based short-term prior (F(1, 30) = −4.130 × 10−14, p = 1.0).

Detailed accuracy

We also ran two separate repeated measures ANOVAs to investigate the effects of cue and target variables on accuracy by controlling the long-term prior knowledge. The first ANOVA was of 2 (Cue: human, robot) × 2 (target: robot moving mechanically, human moving biologically) type and run on the congruent long-term prior trials, and the second ANOVA was of 2 (Cue: human, robot) × 2 (target: robot moving biologically, human moving mechanically) type and ran on the incongruent long-term prior trials.

In the first ANOVA, the cue did not have a main effect (F(1, 30) = 0.338, p = 0.565) but target videos did have (F(1, 30) = 15.353, p < 0.001, η2 = 0.144) The interaction between them was also significant (F(1, 30) = 8.461, p = 0.007). Post hoc results showed that people gave more accurate answers when they encountered a human with biological motion compared to a robot with mechanical motion (t = 3.918, p < 0.001; see Figure 5).

The labels “HB” and “RM” indicate congruent target video conditions, with “HB” representing a human moving biologically, and “RM” reflecting a robot moving mechanically.

In the second ANOVA, the cue (F(1, 30) = 0.842, p = 0.036) did not have a main effect. On the other hand, target videos (F(1, 30) = 20.737, p < 0.001, η2 = 0.157) and the interaction between the cue and the target (F(1, 30) = 18.895, p < 0.001, η2 = 0.211) were significant. Post hoc results showed that a human with mechanical motion results in lower accuracy scores than a robot with biological motion (t = −4.554, p < 0.001; see Figure 6). Because the robot is the agent with a more familiar motion type (biological motion) in this case, motion seems to have more effect than the form on the accuracy results. On the other hand, when participants encountered the robot cue before the human with the mechanical motion video, accuracy was higher than when encountering the human cue (t = −3.582, p = 0.003). Moreover, the accuracy decreased when the human cue was presented before the human with mechanical motion compared to the robot with biological motion (t = −6.251, p < 0.001). This suggests that the motion-based congruence is effective in the motion discrimination task rather than the form-based congruence. Similarly, when people encountered human cues before the robot with biological motion, accuracy increased compared to encountering robot cues (t = 4.339, p < 0.001). This result also supports the effect of the motion-based short-term prior. Additionally, when participants encounter robot cues before the human with mechanical motion, accuracy decreases compared to encountering human cues before the robot with biological motion (t = −4.433, p < 0.001). This result is consistent with both the effect of the motion-based short-term prior in the general accuracy analysis and the expected lower accuracy in relatively unfamiliar stimuli.

Response time

The Shapiro-Wilk test indicated that the assumption of normality was violated for the congruent conditions of motion-based short-term prior (p = 0.008), form-based short-term prior (p = 0.015), long-term prior (p = 0.009) and incongruent conditions of form-based short-term prior (p = 0.010), and long-term prior (p = 0.035), whereas incongruent condition of motion based short-term prior did not significantly deviate from normality (p = 0.106). Because the response time data deviates from normality, a 2 × 2 × 2 ART ANOVA analysis was conducted to examine the effects of congruent and incongruent conditions of three prior types (long-term prior, form-based short-term prior, motion-based short-term prior) on response time scores.

There was a significant main effect of the long-term prior (F(1, 30) = 58.58, p < 0.001, η2 = 0.218), the motion-based short-term prior (F(1, 30) = 69.59, p < 0.001, η2 = 0.249) and the form-based short-term prior (F(1, 30) = 4.193, p < 0.05, η2 = 0.02, see Figure 7). Post-hoc tests were implemented by using t-tests on the aligned ranks using the ART-C procedure. The results indicated that response time was significantly higher in the incongruent condition compared to the congruent condition for long-term prior (t = −8.342, p < 0.00001), motion-based short-term prior (t = −7.654, p < 0.0001), and form-based short-term prior (t = −2.047, p < 0.05).

The graph describes response time differences between the congruent and incongruent conditions across prior types. Darker shades denote congruent conditions, and lighter shades represent incongruent conditions. The results show that all prior types have significantly different response time results in congruent and incongruent trials.

On the other hand, there was no significant interaction effect between motion-based short-term prior and form-based short-term prior (F(1, 30) = 0.037, p = 0.84), motion-based short-term prior and long-term prior (F(1, 30) = 0.12, p = 0.78), form-based short-term prior and long-term prior (F(1, 30) = 0.079, p = 0.78). Moreover, there was no three-way interaction between long-term prior, form-based short-term prior, and motion-based short-term prior (F(1, 30) = 0.001, p = 0.97).

Detailed response time

We also ran two separate ART ANOVA analyses to investigate the effects of cue and target variables on response times by controlling the long-term priors. The first ART ANOVA test was run on the congruent long-term prior trials with a 2 (Cue: human, robot) × 2 (target: robot moving mechanically, human moving biologically) design. The second ART ANOVA test was run on the incongruent long-term prior trials, again with a 2 (Cue: human, robot) × 2 (target: robot moving biologically, human moving mechanically) design.

The first test, which was done on the congruent trials of long-term prior data, showed that the main effect of the cue (F(1, 30) = 0.002, p = 0.96) and the interaction between the cue and the target (F(1, 30) = 0.04, p = 0.84) were not significant. However, the main effect of the target video was significant (F(1, 30) = 6.744, p = 0.014, η2 = 0.017). Post hoc comparisons by using the ART-C procedure showed that there is no significant difference between the response time after seeing the human cue and the robot cue (t = 0.039, p = 0.97). However, the response time of participants towards the human moving biologically was significantly higher than the robot moving mechanically (t = 2.993, p = 0.004, see Figure 8).

In the second ART ANOVA, which was applied to the incongruent trials of the long-term prior data, the main effect of the target (F(1, 30) = 27.383, p < 0.001, η2 = 0.057) was significant. The main effect of the cue (F(1, 30) = 4.032, p = 0.0537) and the interaction between the cue and the target (F(1, 30) = 0.019, p = 0.89) were not significant, although the main effect of the cue might be regarded as marginally significant. Post hoc tests by using the ART-C procedure revealed that participants have shorter response times watching a human with mechanical motion than a robot with biological motion (t = −5.815, p < 0.05). Moreover, when participants encountered a robot cue before the video of human moving mechanically, response time decreased compared to encountering a video of a robot moving biologically (t = 2.781, p = 0.0324, see Figure 9). These results imply the use of motion-based prior and are compatible with the detailed accuracy results. However, there was no significant difference between the videos of the human moving mechanically and the robot moving biologically when participants presented a human cue (t = −0.777, p = 1.0).

Discussion

In this study, we investigated the relationship between long-term and short-term priors in the visual perception of actions. Long-term priors were reflected in the relationship between form and motion information in the target videos (Oliva & Torralba, 2007), while short-term priors were captured through the association between cue images and target videos (Hsieh et al., 2010). Short-term priors consisted of two types: form-based and motion-based. Our findings indicate that long-term priors modulate the use of short-term priors. Specifically, when the target video is congruent with the long-term priors, participants rely primarily on target videos. In contrast, when it is incongruent with the long-term priors, the participants appear to use motion-based short-term information while disregarding the form-based short-term cues.

Modulatory influence of long-term priors on short-term priors

Consistent with previous research (De Lange et al., 2018), our results show that expected (congruent) trials were associated with shorter reaction times and higher accuracy compared to unexpected (incongruent) trials. These differences were statistically significant for both the long-term prior condition and the short-term motion-based prior condition. However, for the short-term form-based prior condition, accuracy did not significantly differ between expected and unexpected trials. This suggests that participants relied on their long-term prior knowledge and short-term motion-based priors, but not on short-term form-based priors, during the motion identification task.

A more detailed analysis examined the relationship between cue and target videos by analyzing the congruent and incongruent conditions of long-term prior separately. When the long-term prior knowledge was congruent, for instance, when participants watched a biologically moving human agent or a mechanically moving robot (both aligned with long-term expectations), the type of cue (human or robot) shown beforehand had no significant effect on response times. This suggests that short-term priors play a minimal role when the target itself aligns with long-term expectations and participants primarily rely on long-term information in such a context. This result is consistent with the account of the brain as a “stubborn scientist” proposed by Yon, de Lange, and Press (2019), which describes perceptual systems as assigning high precision to long-term priors, thereby claiming that they are resistant to revision from sensory evidence.

The context-dependent weighting of the short-term motion-based prior

Unlike in congruent trials, the type of cue became important when long-term prior knowledge was incongruent. For example, when participants observed a human moving mechanically, they responded faster and more accurately if the preceding cue was a robot rather than a human. This suggests that participants relied on short-term motion-based priors when the target was incongruent, highlighting a context-dependent weighting of short-term motion-based prior knowledge. A similar pattern was observed in the other incongruent condition, a robot moving biologically, where accuracy was affected by cue type, but response times remained unchanged, requiring further investigation.

This context-dependent modulation of short-term motion-based priors in accuracy results supports the hierarchical nature of predictive processing. Visual recognition benefits from probabilistic information that aligns with prior knowledge. Both long-term and short-term priors play crucial roles in this probabilistic recognition process. Previous studies have shown that short-term priors can bias the perception of ambiguous stimuli (Hsieh et al., 2010). However, when ambiguous stimuli align with long-term knowledge, such as a robot moving mechanically, short-term priors appear to have little influence on decision-making. In these cases, whether the cue accurately predicts the ambiguous video or misleads the participant, long-term knowledge alone is sufficient for making decisions. Conversely, when stimuli contradict long-term knowledge, people rely on motion-based short-term prior. This suggests that the use of short-term priors is not merely triggered by ambiguity but is also strongly influenced by the violation of long-term expectations regarding ambiguous stimuli.

The low weighting of form-based cues

Our findings distinguish how motion-based and form-based short-term priors influenced perception within our task. Specifically, although short-term motion-based priors significantly impacted accuracy and reaction times, short-term form-based priors had minimal effects. On the other hand, the detailed analysis of the relationship between cue and target emphasizes the use of motion-based cues instead of form-based cues when participants resolve perceptual ambiguity in action perception.

Even though our cue images were static and devoid of explicit motion information, participants utilized these cues primarily to infer motion-based rather than visual characteristics of the subsequent dynamic stimuli. This outcome suggests that observers naturally rely heavily on motion-related expectations when faced with ambiguous dynamic stimuli instead of form-based cues, despite the inherent visual similarity between cue and target.

From a predictive processing perspective (Clark, 2015; Friston, 2010; Heeger, 2017), these results indicate a hierarchical prioritization in perceptual processing, where motion information holds higher predictive value compared to form information in action perception tasks. This prioritization can be attributed to the intrinsic task demands, which explicitly emphasized motion discrimination. Thus, the task may have directed participants’ attentional resources predominantly towards motion features, enhancing the predictive relevance of motion-based information.

Furthermore, the probabilistic cueing paradigm used in our study inherently merges predictive expectation with attention. While predictive expectation biases interpretation based on prior probabilities, attention boosts the processing efficiency of relevant stimulus features (Kok, Jehee, & De Lange, 2012; Pearce & Hall, 1980; Summerfield & Egner, 2009; Summerfield et al., 2006). Our results support this distinction, showing that task relevance can modulate the effectiveness of short-term priors. Specifically, the emphasis on motion discrimination within our task appears to have amplified the effect of motion-based cues instead of form-based ones.

It remains unclear whether the dominance of motion-based priors stems solely from their task relevance or if it reflects a more general intrinsic preference for motion information in predictive hierarchies. Although experimental paradigms have been developed to differentiate task-relevant attention from expectation-based attention (De Lange, Kok, Rahnev, Jehee, & Lau, 2011), they are not designed to account for the predictive hierarchy among the stimuli, namely, the short-term motion-based and form-based cues in our context. To address the ambiguity about the foundation of attention, future research could directly compare motion and form discrimination tasks within a similar paradigm. Such studies would clarify whether the observed use of motion-based instead of form-based cues is a general principle of action perception or specifically a consequence of task demands.

To sum up, our findings clearly demonstrate the strong influence of the motion-based short-term prior instead of the form-based short-term prior in perceptual decision-making tasks involving action perception. These results emphasize the importance of considering both hierarchical predictive frameworks and task relevance when interpreting how short-term priors guide perception.

Conclusions

This study demonstrates that long-term prior knowledge has a strong influence on perceptual decisions in a motion discrimination task involving motion-based and form-based cues presented before target videos. When long-term prior knowledge was congruent with the target video, short-term priors, both form-based and motion-based, had minimal impact on response times and accuracy, suggesting that participants primarily relied on long-term expectations. However, when long-term priors were incongruent, the type of short-term cue became important. Participants responded faster and more accurately when the cue conveyed motion-based information consistent with the observed motion, indicating a context-dependent weighting between long-term and motion-based short-term priors. These findings indicate that short-term priors are not only utilized by the presence of ambiguous stimuli but also during violations of long-term expectations. Moreover, decision-making was strongly influenced by the motion-based short-term prior. This might lead to two separate explanations; one is that motion-based short-term priors have a higher prior likelihood than form-based ones, while the other one is that motion-based short-term is task-relevant.
