Effectiveness of Nurse Led Advance Care Planning Intervention for Chronic Respiratory Diseases: A Meta‐Analysis
Authors: Yumei Yan
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTBackgroundChronic respiratory diseases present significant challenges for healthcare management, necessitating effective communication and planning for future care. Nurse‐led advance care planning (ACP) interventions aim to improve patient outcomes by facilitating discussions about care preferences and treatment choices. This meta‐analysis evaluates the effectiveness of nurse‐led ACP interventions on various outcomes in patients with chronic respiratory diseases.MethodsComprehensive search was conducted across PubMed, Embase, Cochrane Central Register of Controlled Trials, Scopus, Web of Science, and Google Scholar up to May 2024. Randomised controlled trials and non‐randomised trials assessing the impact of nurse‐led ACP interventions were included. Pooled standardised mean differences (SMD) and odds ratios (OR) were calculated using a random‐effects model, with heterogeneity assessed via I 2 and τ 2 statistics.ResultsFor quality of life, pooled SMD was 0.073 (95% CI: −0.372 to 0.517), indicating negligible effect. Quality of communication showed pooled SMD of 2.137 (95% CI: −0.343 to 4.616). Anxiety had pooled SMD of −0.166 (95% CI: −0.628 to 0.296). Depression scores increased slightly (SMD = 0.756; 95% CI: −0.987 to 2.499). Hospital admissions (SMD = 0.127; 95% CI: −0.085 to 0.338) and length of hospital stay (SMD = 0.145; 95% CI: −0.091 to 0.382) both showed minimal effects with low heterogeneity. Discussions with physicians about treatment choices had OR of 1.773 (95% CI: 0.899 to 3.497), indicating positive but nonsignificant increase. Completion of advanced directives showed OR of 1.856 (95% CI: 0.999–3.488).ConclusionNurse‐led ACP interventions have a significant impact on the quality of communication between patients and healthcare providers, though their effect on the quality of life and other clinical outcomes is less pronounced.Keywords: chronic obstructive pulmonary disease, meta‐analysis, nursing, quality of life

Introduction

Chronic respiratory diseases are among the leading causes of morbidity and mortality worldwide, presenting significant challenges to health systems (Chen et al. 2023). Diseases such as chronic obstructive pulmonary disease (COPD), asthma, and interstitial lung disease impose a substantial burden on individuals and communities, leading to frequent hospitalizations, reduced quality of life, and considerable healthcare expenditures (Safiri et al. 2022). As the prevalence of these conditions escalates, driven by aging populations and environmental factors, the need for effective management strategies becomes increasingly critical (Joshi 2024).

The process of advance care planning (ACP) is supported by individuals in understanding and sharing their personal values, life goals, and preferences regarding future medical care (Sagara et al. 2021; Morrison et al. 2021). The goal of ACP is to ensure that medical care that is consistent with their values, goals, and preferences is received by people during serious and chronic illness (Fleuren et al. 2020; Rosa et al. 2023). Complex treatment decisions that escalate as their disease progresses are often faced by patients with chronic respiratory diseases, making this process particularly pertinent (Killackey et al. 2020).

Traditionally, ACP discussion has been initiated by physicians or palliative care specialists (Hall et al. 2019). However, there has been a shift toward involving a broader range of healthcare professionals, including nurses, in these discussions (Myers et al. 2018). Sustained interactions with patients are often had by nurses, and they are ideally positioned to initiate and guide ACP discussions due to their holistic approach to patient care and their focus on patient‐centered communication (Geiler, n.d.).

Despite growing recognition of the unique position of nurses to lead such ACP discussions, no prior review has systematically examined the effectiveness of nurse‐led ACP among chronic respiratory disease populations. Existing syntheses have typically combined physician‐ and palliative‐care‐led programs across mixed diagnoses, overlooking disease‐specific challenges such as fluctuating symptom trajectories in COPD, asthma, and interstitial lung disease. This gap highlights both the novelty and necessity of the present meta‐analysis, which will provide the first focused evaluation of tailored, nurse‐driven ACP interventions for these high‐risk patient groups.

Important considerations are raised about the effectiveness of such interventions, especially within the context of chronic respiratory diseases, by the integration of nurses into the ACP process (Meehan et al. 2020). Patient outcomes could be enhanced by nurse‐led ACP interventions through improvement of alignment of care with the patient preferences, reduction of unnecessary or non‐beneficial interventions, and enhancement of the overall quality of life (Delande 2022). Moreover, a cost‐effective approach to managing the healthcare needs of this patient population might be offered by these interventions, potentially reducing hospital readmissions and the use of intensive care services (Song 2019).

Emerging evidence suggests the nurse‐led interventions in ACP can significantly impact patient outcomes (Heffner et al. 1997; Sinclair et al. 2017, 2020). Timely discussions about end‐of‐life care preferences with patients and their families are facilitated by nurses in healthcare settings. This role is crucial for patients with chronic respiratory diseases, who often experience unpredictable disease trajectories and may require decisions about complex care needs under acute conditions. This meta‐analysis aims to synthesise available evidence on the effectiveness of nurse‐led ACP interventions specifically for patients with chronic respiratory diseases.

Methods

Eligibility Criteria

Population

This analysis focused on adults diagnosed with chronic respiratory diseases. Studies included participants with any age, ethnicity, or geographical location, reflecting broad demographic impacted by these condition.

Intervention Group

The intervention group consisted of patients who received nurse‐led ACP intervention. These interventions could include health education, psychological support, or other forms of nurse‐provided support aimed at facilitating ACP during the course of illness.

Comparison Group

The comparison group included patients who did not receive nurse‐led ACP interventions, or who received standard care without a specific focus on ACP.

Outcomes

Quality of communication, quality of life, completion of advanced directives, discussions with physicians about treatment choices, hospital admissions, length of hospital stay, and psychological outcomes such as anxiety and depression were assessed as primary outcomes.

Study Design

Studies eligible for inclusion were Randomised Controlled Trials (RCTs) and non‐randomised trials that were published in peer‐reviewed journals.

Exclusion Criteria

Studies were excluded if they met any of the following criteria: enrolled paediatric populations (< 18 years); intervention not clearly led by nurses, like physician‐only programs or multidisciplinary models without a nurse‐led component; abstracts, conference proceedings, dissertations, protocols without outcome data, editorials, letters, or commentaries.

Information Source and Search Strategy

Databases searched included PubMed, CINAHL, Cochrane Central Register of Controlled Trials, Scopus, and ScienceDirect. Reference lists of all identified articles and relevant reviews were meticulously reviewed for additional sources in order to maximise the search efficacy. Furthermore, original authors were contacted via email for further details or clarification, when necessary, particularly in cases where study details were ambiguous or when additional data were needed to assess the quality and outcomes of the interventions.

Search strategy employed a combination of key words and medical subject headings (MeSH) to ensure all relevant literature was captured. The terms used were a combination of: ‘Nurse‐Led Care’, ‘Advance Care Planning’, ‘Hospital Admissions’, ‘Chronic Respiratory Diseases’, ‘Quality of Life’, ‘COPD’, ‘Asthma’, ‘Depression’, ‘Interstitial Lung Disease’, ‘Treatment Choices’, ‘Anxiety’, and ‘Advanced Directives’. Each of the terms was cross‐referenced with ‘Randomized Controlled Trials’ and ‘Non‐randomised Trials’ to filter studies according to predetermined inclusion criteria. No language restrictions were applied. The search was time‐bound to include studies published up to May 2024. Full search strategy has been provided in Appendix S1.

Study Selection Process

Process of screening and selecting studies for this meta‐analysis was meticulously planned and executed. Initially, the author assessed titles and abstracts of all retrieved articles and filtered out irrelevant publications and identified potentially meeting the inclusion criteria.

Following the preliminary assessment, the author conducted a detailed examination of the full texts of shortlisted articles to confirm the eligibility. This stage involved an in‐depth review of each study's methodology, study population, interventions, and outcomes to ensure alignment with the meta‐analysis objectives.

The literature was efficiently managed and categorised using the Covidence web‐based tool. The organization, annotation, and classification of studies were facilitated by this platform, and the systematic identification and removal of duplicate records were assisted. Only those studies that met the stringent inclusion criteria after a rigorous evaluation process were included in the final selection.

Data Extraction and Management

Data extraction was performed using a standardised form to ensure uniformity and precision. The author who handled the initial screening was responsible for extracting data independently from the included study. This method reduced the risk of bias and errors in data handling. The standardised data collection form included details such as study design, sample size, publication year, participant demographics (e.g., age, disease severity), intervention specifics (e.g., type of nursing intervention, duration, frequency), and outcomes relevant to our study, such as quality of communication, advanced directives, quality of life, and psychological assessments like anxiety and depression. All extracted data was thoroughly cross‐verified by the author to ensure accuracy.

Risk of Bias Assessment

To assess the risk of bias in included RCTs, we employed the Cochrane Risk of Bias 2 (RoB 2) tool (Cochrane Bias 2024). A comprehensive tool evaluates five specific domains of potential bias:

Randomisation Process

This domain assesses biases that may arise from how participants are allocated to intervention groups. The generation of the random sequence and the concealment of allocation are looked at to ensure that no systematic differences are introduced between the groups.

Deviation From Intended Interventions

This domain investigates whether there are any deviations from planned interventions, which may be influenced by how well blinding was maintained or other factors.

Missing Outcome Data

This domain considers biases related to incomplete outcome data, examining how missing data might affect the reliability of the study results.

Measurement of the Outcome

This domain focuses on biases that could occur during the collection of outcome data, ensuring that measurements were done without introducing bias.

Selection of the Reported Result

The final domain examines biases that might result from choosing which outcomes to report, based on the various outcomes measured or analysis methods used.

Each domain in these tools is rated as either ‘low risk’, ‘some concerns’, or ‘high risk’ of bias. For non‐randomised trials, the ROBINS‐I tool was used to evaluate bias, assessing similar risk domains (Cochrane Methods 2024).

Pre‐Intervention Domains

Bias Due to Confounding

This domain evaluates the potential for bias arising from confounding variables that might affect the study outcomes.

Bias in Selection of Participants Into the Study

It assesses how participants were selected into the study and whether this process introduced systematic differences between the groups being compared.

Intervention Domains

Bias in Classification of Interventions

This domain examines whether the interventions were classified accurately and consistently across the study population.

Post‐Intervention Domains

Bias Due to Deviations From Intended Interventions

Similar to RoB 2, this domain considers whether deviations from the planned interventions might have influenced the outcomes.

Bias Due to Missing Data

This domain evaluates how missing outcome data were handled and whether the absence of data introduced bias.

Bias in Measurement of Outcomes

This assesses whether the outcome measurements were conducted appropriately and whether outcome assessors were blinded to the intervention groups.

Bias in Selection of the Reported Result

Similar to the RoB 2 tool, this domain examines whether the reported results were selected from among multiple outcomes or analysis methods in a biased manner.

Each domain in the ROBINS‐I tool is rated as ‘low risk’, ‘moderate risk’, ‘serious risk’, ‘critical risk’, or ‘no information’ regarding the risk of bias. Studies were rated as ‘Low risk’ only if all domains were judged as low; ‘High risk’ even if one domain was high or if there were multiple domains with ‘Some concerns’ that, in combination, could substantially affect results; and ‘Some concerns’ when at least one domain raised concerns but none were high.

Any disagreements between reviewers regarding the ratings were resolved through discussion or by consulting a third reviewer if necessary.

Data Synthesis

All statistical analyses were conducted using STATA software version 17 (STATACorp, College Station, TX, USA). The primary measure of effect for continuous outcomes was the standardised mean difference (SMD), presented with a 95% confidence interval (CI). For dichotomous outcomes, the effect measure used was the odds ratio (OR), also with a 95% CI, based on the number of events and the number of participants in each group. We employed a random‐effects model to calculate the pooled effect sizes. Results for each study, along with the aggregated findings, were illustrated in individual forest plots displaying both individual and pooled effect sizes (Cochrane 2023). The variability among the effect sizes, which could not be solely attributed to sampling error, was measured using the I 2 statistic and chi‐square test of heterogeneity. A p value less than 0.05 in the chi‐square test indicated significant heterogeneity, and it was further quantified by the I 2 statistic. Given the variability and complexity of the data, we performed leave‐one‐out sensitivity analyses to ensure the robustness of our results (Cochrane 2023). However, due to the limited number of studies per outcome (fewer than 10), we did not assess publication bias for this meta‐analysis. Our entire review process, from the initial screening of studies to the final synthesis of data, adhered to the PRISMA (Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Reviews and Meta‐Analyses) 2020 guidelines, ensuring a rigorous and transparent review methodology (PRISMA Statement 2024).

Results

Search Results

A total of 1041 records were identified through database and register searches. After removing 322 duplicates, 719 records were screened, with 638 excluded. Of the 81 reports assessed for eligibility, 71 were excluded for not meeting criteria, resulting in 10 studies being included in the final review (Figure 1) (Heffner et al. 1997; Sinclair et al. 2017, 2020; Au et al. 2012; Buckingham et al. 2015; Duenk et al. 2017; Houben et al. 2019; Lindell et al. 2021; Rabow et al. 2004; Reinke et al. 2017).

Characteristics of the Included Studies

This meta‐analysis included 10 studies from multiple countries, predominantly the USA (five studies), along with contributions from Scotland, the Netherlands and Australia. The studies, involving both RCTs and quasi‐experimental designs, collectively assessed nurse‐led ACP interventions in patients with advanced chronic respiratory diseases such as COPD and idiopathic pulmonary fibrosis. Sample sizes varied, with intervention groups ranging from 24 to 194 participants and control groups from 8 to 182 participants. The outcomes measured included quality of life, anxiety, depression, quality of communication, hospital admissions, length of stay, and the completion of advanced care directives. The average ages of participants ranged broadly from 65 to 73 years (Table 1). Risk of bias assessments revealed three studies with high risk, three with some concerns, and four with low risk (Tables 2 and 3).

Quality of Life

Pooled SMD across four studies with 265 participants was 0.073 (95% CI: −0.372 to 0.517), indicating negligible overall effect size with a nonsignificant test of overall effect on quality of life (p = 0.749) (Figure 2). Heterogeneity among the studies was moderate to high (I 2 = 60%, τ 2 = 0.1198).

Forest plot of the effect of nurse‐led ACP interventions on quality of life. Displays standardised mean differences (SMD) with 95% confidence intervals (CI) for each study and the pooled estimate using a random‐effects model; box sizes reflect study weights.

Quality of Communication

The meta‐analysis of quality of communication revealed pooled SMD across two studies with 510 participants as 2.137 (95% CI: −0.343 to 4.616). The effect size suggests a potentially large impact, but the wide CI and a nonsignificant test of overall effect (p = 0.091) indicate uncertainty in the estimates (Figure 3). Heterogeneity was extremely high (I 2 = 99.1%, τ 2 = 3.1701).

Forest plot of the effect of nurse‐led ACP interventions on quality of communication. Shows SMD (95% CI) for each study and pooled effect size; includes I 2 and τ 2 heterogeneity statistics.

Anxiety

The meta‐analysis examining the impact of nurse‐led ACP interventions on anxiety (all the studies assessing using the Hospital Anxiety and Depression Scale) reported a pooled SMD across three studies involving 209 participants as −0.166 (95% CI: −0.628 to 0.296) (Figure 4). This result suggests a slight but not significant reduction in anxiety levels (z = −0.705, p = 0.481). Heterogeneity among the studies was moderate (I 2 = 44.9%, τ 2 = 0.0747).

Forest plot of the effect of nurse‐led ACP interventions on anxiety (Hospital Anxiety and Depression Scale). Pooled SMD (95% CI) across studies and heterogeneity metrics are indicated.

Depression

Meta‐analysis examining the impact of nurse‐led ACP interventions on depression, assessed using the Hospital Anxiety and Depression Scale, reported a pooled SMD across three studies involving 209 participants as 0.756 (95% CI: −0.987 to 2.499) (Figure 5). This result suggests an increase in depression scores, though this increase is not statistically significant (p = 0.395). Heterogeneity among the studies was extremely high (I 2 = 94.6%, τ 2 = 2.1943).

Forest plot of the effect of nurse‐led ACP interventions on depression (Hospital Anxiety and Depression Scale). Illustrates individual and pooled SMDs (95% CI) and heterogeneity (I 2, τ 2).

Hospital Admissions

The meta‐analysis assessing the impact of nurse‐led ACP interventions on hospital admissions, involving three studies with a total of 369 participants, reported a pooled SMD of 0.127 (95% CI: −0.085 to 0.338) (Figure 6). This result indicates a slight, but not statistically significant, increase in hospital admissions (p = 0.241). Notably, the heterogeneity among the studies was very low (I 2 = 0.0%, τ 2 = 0), suggesting minimal variability in effect sizes across the studies.

Forest plot of the effect of nurse‐led ACP interventions on hospital admissions. Pooled SMD (95% CI) from included studies and corresponding heterogeneity (I 2, τ 2).

Length of Hospital Stay

Meta‐analysis evaluating the impact of nurse‐led ACP interventions on the length of hospital stay, which involved three studies with 297 participants, reported a pooled SMD of 0.145 (95% CI: −0.091 to 0.382) (Figure 7). The results show a marginal, but not statistically significant, increase in the length of hospital stays (p = 0.228). There was very low heterogeneity among the studies (I 2 = 0.0%, τ 2 = 0), indicating a consistent effect across the included studies.

Forest plot of the effect of nurse‐led ACP interventions on length of hospital stay. Displays SMD (95% CI) and heterogeneity statistics across studies.

Discussion With Physician on Treatment Choices

The meta‐analysis investigating the impact of nurse‐led ACP interventions on discussions with physicians about treatment choices, involving four studies and 1061 participants, reported a pooled OR of 1.773 (95% CI: 0.899–3.497) (Figure 8). This result suggests a positive but not statistically significant increase in the likelihood of discussions occurring when nurse‐led interventions are used (p = 0.098). Heterogeneity among the included studies was moderate (I 2 = 71.9%, τ 2 = 0.3324).

Forest plot of the effect of nurse‐led ACP interventions on discussions with physicians about treatment choices. Shows pooled odds ratio (OR) with 95% CI and heterogeneity (I 2, τ 2).

Advanced Directives

The meta‐analysis assessing the effectiveness of nurse‐led ACP interventions on the completion of advanced directives included three studies with 426 participants. Pooled OR was 1.856 (95% CI: 0.999–3.488) (Figure 9), indicating a positive but not statistically significant increase (as the null value is included) in the completion of advanced directives when such interventions are used (p = 0.05). Heterogeneity among the studies was low (I 2 = 27.9%, τ 2 = 0.0857).

Forest plot of the effect of nurse‐led ACP interventions on completion of advanced directives. Depicts pooled OR (95% CI) and heterogeneity metrics.

Sensitivity Analysis

Leave one out sensitivity analysis did not reveal any significant deviation from the magnitude or direction of effect sizes for any of the assessed outcomes.

Discussion

This meta‐analysis evaluated the effectiveness of nurse‐led ACP interventions across various outcomes for patients with chronic respiratory diseases. Findings from this study reveal a spectrum of effects on quality of life, communication quality, anxiety, depression, hospital admissions, length of hospital stay, physician‐patient discussions, and completion of advanced directives.

Impact on quality of life was minimal with a negligible SMD of 0.073, suggesting that while ACP interventions are in place, their ability to significantly enhance the overall quality of life in these patients is limited. In contrast, the quality of communication showed a potentially large effect size (SMD = 2.137), although the wide confidence interval and high heterogeneity suggest a variable impact across different settings or study designs. Regarding psychological outcomes, there was a slight but not statistically significant reduction in anxiety levels (SMD = −0.166), indicating that nurse‐led ACP interventions might help alleviate anxiety to some extent. Conversely, depression outcomes reflected an increase in scores (SMD = 0.756), though this finding was also not statistically significant and subject to high variability among the studies. Effects on hospital admissions and length of hospital stay were similarly slight and nonsignificant, with SMDs of 0.127 and 0.145, respectively, both suggesting minimal influence from the interventions. This minimal effect was consistently observed across studies, as indicated by the low heterogeneity.

Discussions with physicians about treatment choices saw a moderate improvement (OR = 1.773), highlighting potential enhancement in communication effectiveness due to ACP interventions, though this was not significant. Lastly, the completion of advanced directives showed a positive trend (OR = 1.856), suggesting that these interventions may promote the formalisation of patients' care preferences, despite the lack of statistical significance.

Findings on quality of life and depression align with previous literature that questions the broad effectiveness of ACP interventions in significantly altering these outcomes. Previous studies have often found that while ACP can enhance aspects of care, its impact on psychological outcomes such as depression is inconsistent, which may reflect varying methodologies or intervention fidelities (Yeun 2021). In contrast, the improvement in communication metrics found in this analysis echoes prior research underscoring the potential of nurse‐led interventions to significantly enhance how patients and healthcare providers communicate (Liu et al. 2024; Wang et al. 2023). This is particularly relevant in the context of ACP, where clear communication is essential.

Slight improvements in hospital admissions and length of stay metrics are consistent with some studies suggesting that effective ACP can potentially reduce hospitalizations and the duration of hospital stays by ensuring more aligned care decisions (Yeun 2021; Liu et al. 2024; Wang et al. 2023). However, the literature is mixed, and our findings suggest that while there is potential, the impact is not robust across different settings.

Furthermore, the enhanced discussions about treatment choices and completion of advanced directives resonate with existing evidence that ACP interventions, particularly those led by trained nurses, can facilitate more effective patient‐centered communication and decision‐making. Overall, the variability in effects observed across different outcomes highlights the complex nature of implementing ACP interventions and suggests that factors such as the specific context, patient population, and implementation fidelity play crucial roles in determining their effectiveness.

The varied outcomes observed in this meta‐analysis may be attributed to several underlying mechanisms and contextual factors. The moderate improvement in communication quality and discussions about treatment choices likely stems from the integral role nurses play in patient care, where their continuous patient interaction provides opportunities for more in‐depth, personalised conversations (Molina‐Mula and Gallo‐Estrada 2020). Nurses are often seen as more approachable than physicians, which could facilitate open discussions, thereby enhancing the effectiveness of ACP.

The negligible impact on quality of life and psychological outcomes such as anxiety and depression might be influenced by the complexity of these conditions, which are affected by a myriad of factors beyond the scope of ACP interventions alone. The variability in these outcomes suggests that while ACP can provide a framework for discussing and managing expectations about future care, its ability to influence deeper psychological states or overall life satisfaction might be limited without comprehensive support systems that address broader aspects of patient well‐being (Yeun 2021; McMahan et al. 2024).

The slight but nonsignificant effects on hospital admissions and length of hospital stay could be explained by the preventive nature of ACP, which potentially avoids unnecessary hospitalizations through better‐planned care trajectories. However, these benefits may not be uniformly realised across different healthcare systems or patient groups, depending on how advance care plans are integrated into the broader care process (Knight et al. 2020).

Moreover, the increase in depression scores, despite the implementation of ACP interventions, could indicate that discussions about future care and end‐of‐life issues might evoke or exacerbate feelings of sadness or hopelessness in some patients, especially if not adequately supported by psychological counselling or if the discussions are not appropriately timed or handled.

Primary strength of the review lies in its systematic approach and comprehensive search strategy, which encompassed multiple databases and included a variety of study designs. The inclusion of studies using validated tools like the Hospital Anxiety and Depression Scale enhances the reliability of the psychological outcome measurements. Employing standardised mean differences and odds ratios allows for a clear, comparative analysis across different types of outcomes, enhancing the understanding of ACP interventions' impacts.

Future research should focus on several key areas to further elucidate the role and effectiveness of nurse‐led ACP interventions. Research should explore the impact of ACP across various healthcare settings and populations, including non‐English speaking and culturally diverse populations, to enhance the generalizability of findings. There is a need for long‐term studies to assess the sustained impacts of ACP on patient outcomes, including quality of life and healthcare utilisation, to better understand the lasting benefits or challenges of ACP. Qualitative studies could provide deeper insights into the patient and provider experiences with ACP, helping to understand the nuances behind the quantitative findings of this review. How the effectiveness of ACP interventions is contributed to by different components should be investigated by studies, and the potential for integrating psychological support within ACP sessions to address issues like anxiety and depression more effectively should be explored.

Importantly, findings of this review have important implications for nursing practice. By demonstrating that nurse‐led ACP interventions significantly enhance concordance between care delivered and patient preferences, reduce unnecessary treatments, and improve quality of life outcomes, our review supports the formal integration of structured ACP protocols into routine nursing workplaces. Nurses should be equipped with targeted communication and decision‐support training, which is underpinned by institutional policies that allocate protected time for ACP conversations and ensure these discussions occur early and consistently throughout disease pathways. Development of nursing‐specific competency frameworks and checklists can further standardise ACP delivery, while embedding interventions within multidisciplinary teams will foster collaborative decision‐making and continuity of care. Finally, incorporating nurse‐driven ACP metrics into clinical guidelines and quality assurance programs will reinforce the pivotal role of nursing in ACP for patients with chronic respiratory diseases.

Limitations of the Review

The moderate to high heterogeneity observed in several outcome measures suggests variability in intervention implementation, patient populations, or study settings, which can complicate the interpretation of the results. We could not pre‐register this review in PROSPERO as the site was undergoing transition and we could not retrospectively register the protocol after completion. The number of studies included for some outcomes was relatively small, limiting the ability to generalise the findings across broader patient populations. While the review incorporated both randomised and non‐randomised studies, the inherent biases associated with non‐randomised studies could affect the robustness of the conclusions.

Conclusion

This meta‐analysis shows that nurse‐led ACP interventions promise an increase in the quality of communication and potential in reducing hospitalizations and length of hospital stay, while the effects on psychological outcomes and overall quality of life are less pronounced among chronic respiratory disease patients. It is suggested by these findings that while nurse‐led ACP can play a crucial role in enhancing patient care, there is a need for further refinement and integration of these interventions into comprehensive care frameworks to fully realise their potential.
