Initial placement of TEE probe by observing the position of esophageal opening in advance on improving the success rate of initial placement of TEE probe by observing the position of esophageal opening: a randomized controlled study
Authors: Gai-Jiao Liu, Li Xiao, Han Yang, Jing Liang, Xin-Ping Yang, Zhi-Heng Liu, Yu Chen, Xiao-E Wang
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractObjectiveThe objective of this study is to examine the success rate of transesophageal echocardiography (TEE) probe placement and the incidence of postoperative odynophagia based on pre-assessing the exposed position of the esophageal opening within the pharyngeal cavity.MethodsSixty-four patients scheduled for elective cardiac surgery under general anesthesia, with a requirement for transesophageal echocardiography (TEE) monitoring, were enrolled in this study. The patients were randomly assigned to either a pre-observation group or a conventional group. In the pre-observation group, the pharyngeal cavity was examined using a video laryngoscope during endotracheal intubation, and the TEE probe was inserted along the pre-observed esophageal opening. In the conventional group, the probe was inserted using the standard blind insertion technique. The number of insertion attempts and the time required for probe placement were recorded, with three unsuccessful attempts defined as a placement failure. Postoperative odynophagia was assessed in patients on postoperative days 1, 3, 5, and 7, with both the severity and duration of the condition being monitored.ResultsAll 64 patients successfully completed the study, with no statistically significant differences observed between the two groups in terms of basic demographic data, risk classification, or oropharyngeal grading. The pre-observation group demonstrated a significantly higher first-attempt success rate compared to the conventional group (78% vs. 22%; Odds Ratio [OR], 17.54; 95% CI, 3.86 to 83.33; P < 0.001). Additionally, the numeric rating scale (NRS) scores for odynophagia on postoperative days 1, 3, 5, and 7, as well as the duration of odynophagia, were significantly lower in the pre-observation group than in the conventional group (P < 0.05). The incidence of postoperative odynophagia was also significantly reduced in the pre-observation group compared to the conventional group (P < 0.001).ConclusionThe pre-observation group achieved a higher first-attempt success rate for probe placement compared to the conventional group, with patients experiencing less severe and shorter-lasting postoperative odynophagia, indicating significant advantages over the conventional blind insertion technique.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s13019-025-03589-2.Keywords: Esophageal opening, Odynophagia, Pre-observation, Probe placement, Transesophageal echocardiogram (TEE)

Introduction

Transesophageal echocardiography (TEE) is a key monitoring tool widely used in cardiac surgery. However, the blind insertion of TEE probes in patients who are anesthetized poses greater challenges compared to patients who are awake, primarily due to the obstruction caused by the endotracheal tube and the loss of pharyngeal muscle tone [1]. The risk of injury to the oropharyngeal mucosa and esophagus is a major contributing factor to the development of odynophagia. Under general anesthesia, where coordinated swallowing movements and pain feedback are absent, the likelihood of oral mucosal and esophageal injuries increases, leading to postoperative odynophagia. In recent years, various devices like video laryngoscopes and rigid laryngoscopes, have been known to enhance the efficiency of TEE probe placement and reduce oropharyngeal mucosal injury in patients under anesthesia [1–4]. However, in the authors’ opinion, after tracheal intubation, attempting to reinsert the probe using a video laryngoscope in such a confined space significantly raises the risk of damage to the patient’s teeth, oral mucosa, or the TEE probe itself, making it less favorable for routine clinical practice [5].

A recent cadaveric dissection study has highlighted anatomical variations in the positioning of the superior esophageal sphincter when compared to the laryngeal inlet, which differ from the conventional anatomical understanding that the esophagus lies directly behind the trachea [6]. The study indicated that when variations in the esophageal position when compared to the glottis occur, inserting the probe in a midline direction can meet with resistance. Consequently, it is hypothesized that pre-observing the position of the esophageal opening in the pharyngeal cavity during tracheal intubation and subsequently adjusting the direction of TEE probe insertion could enhance the success rate of blind probe insertion and reduce postoperative odynophagia in patients. The aim of this study is to verify that observing the position of the esophageal opening in advance and adjusting the orientation of the TEE probe can improve the success rate of the first TEE probe placement.

Materials and methods

Enrollment of study participants

The study adhered to the ethical guidelines established by the First Affiliated Hospital of Sun Yat-sen University and received approval from the hospital’s ethics committee (Reference number: [2021]069). Registration was completed with the Chinese Clinical Trial Registry under the registration number ChiCTR2000040448. The research was conducted at the Surgical Center of the First Affiliated Hospital of Sun Yat-sen University from February to September 2021. Inclusion criteria comprised of: patients > 18 years, classified as American Society of Anesthesiologists (ASA) class II or III; scheduled for cardiac surgery under general anesthesia; without oral malformations; requiring intraoperative TEE monitoring; and having provided signed informed consent. Exclusion criteria were: contraindications for TEE, cognitive dysfunction; preoperative assessments indicating difficult airways; preoperative sore throat; pharyngitis; oral ulcers; the glottis was not visible under videolaryngoscopy or shoulder and neck pain.

Totally 120 patients (60 ones in each group) were planned to enroll the study. However, during the mid-term analysis, it was found that there was a significant difference in the first-time success rate of TEE implantation between the control group and the pre-observation group, and this difference was statistically significant. Considering the actual clinical situation, in order to reduce patient injuries, the study was prematurely terminated. Thus, totally 70 patients provided informed consent to participate in the study; six were not enrolled due to surgery postponements. The remaining 64 patients were included and allocated into two groups: a pre-observation group and a conventional group, each consisting of 32 patients. Group assignment was based on whether the exposed position of the esophageal opening was observed in advance. The study was conducted using a fully randomized, double-blind design, with patient enrollment detailed in Fig. 1. Randomization was conducted using IBM SPSS Statistics 25.0 software to generate two sets of random number tables. Group assignments were sealed in opaque envelopes and disclosed by the anesthesiologist upon the arrival of the patient in the operating room.

Trial procedures

None of the patients received preoperative sedation or anticholinergic medications. Following the Mallampati classification assessment, peripheral intravenous access was established, and standard monitors were set up, including ECG, pulse oximeter, Narcotrend, and invasive blood pressure monitoring through the left radial artery. General anesthesia was induced with intravenous etomidate, administered at a dose of 0.2 to 0.3 mg/kg until the patient became unconscious, with Narcotrend values reaching between D2 and E0. This was followed by the administration of sufentanil at 0.5 to 1 µg/kg and cisatracurium at 0.2 to 0.4 mg/kg. Upon achieving satisfactory muscle relaxation, tracheal intubation was conducted using a video laryngoscope (inner diameter 7.5 mm for females and 8.0 mm for males). Cuff pressure was maintained at 28 to 30 cmH2O using a Cohui (Germany) balloon manometer, and the tracheal tube was secured at the right corner of the mouth. Anesthesia was maintained using a combined intravenous-inhalation approach, with sevoflurane inhalation set at 1.0–1.2.0.2% and propofol administered via target-controlled infusion (TCI) at a concentration of 1.0–2.0 µg/ml for sedation. Sufentanil was administered via effect-site TCI at 0.3–0.6 ng/ml for intraoperative analgesia, and cisatracurium was maintained at a rate of 2 µg/kg/min to ensure muscle relaxation.

The TEE probe (X8-2t, Philips Medical, USA) was lubricated before placement. All TEE probe insertions were conducted by the same experienced senior anesthesiologist, who had completed rigorous theoretical and simulator training. In Group C, the TEE probe was inserted along the midline of the posterior pharyngeal wall using the conventional blind insertion technique. The specific operation was as follows: Using the index finger of the left hand to gently lift and flex the mandible in the mouth, while keeping the head in a neutral position, the right hand gently inserted the TEE probe through the midline into the oral cavity, and inserted it at the center of the posterior pharyngeal wall. In Group P, the same anesthesiologist observed the pharyngeal cavity of the patient based on a video laryngoscope during endotracheal intubation to identify the relative position of the esophageal opening in relation to the glottis. The esophageal opening within the visual field was categorized into three positions: left, middle, and right (Fig. 2). The esophageal opening directly behind the glottis was designated as the middle position, with the positions to its left and right referred to as the left and right positions, respectively. The position of the esophageal opening (left, middle, or right) was precisely determined by temporarily inserting a gastric tube. The endotracheal tube was placed then, and the laryngoscope was withdrawn. Finally, the endotracheal tube was secured on the side opposite to the observed esophageal opening. After suctioning and removing the gastric tube, the probe was inserted lateral to the previously observed midline of the esophageal opening. For instance, if the esophageal opening was identified on the left, the endotracheal tube was fixed at the right corner of the oral cavity, and the probe was inserted along the left side of the posterior pharyngeal wall. Conversely, if the esophageal opening was in the middle position, the probe was placed along the midline (video in supplementary materials).

Successful probe placement was defined as advancing the TEE probe from the oral cavity to the esophagus to a depth of 30 cm. If strong resistance was encountered, the probe was withdrawn and recorded as one attempt, with subsequent adjustments in direction for additional attempts. A total of three unsuccessful attempts constituted a failure, necessitating the use of a video laryngoscope for insertion. The number of probe placement attempts was recorded. The duration of successful TEE probe placement was measured from the initial transoral insertion of the TEE probe until successful placement was completed, using a stopwatch. The number of intraoperative TEE examinations, total duration, and the time the probe remained in the esophagus were also recorded. Heart rate, mean arterial pressure (MAP), and central venous pressure (CVP) were documented before and after probe placement. Postoperative complications, including gastrointestinal bleeding and perforation, were noted. The degree of odynophagia was assessed using the Numeric Rating Scale (NRS) on postoperative days 1, 3, 5, and 7. The primary objective of this study was to compare the success rate of TEE probe placement between the conventional blind insertion technique and the pre-observation method in the context of general anesthesia cardiac surgery. The secondary objective was to assess the differences between the two methods concerning the incidence of odynophagia and other complications like gastrointestinal perforation and bleeding.

The sample size was calculated based on the primary endpoint of the study, which was the first-attempt success rate of TEE probe placement. In a pilot study involving 5 cases per group, the success rates were 80% in Group P and 40% in Group C, aligning with findings from previous research [1]. Statistical analysis was conducted using PASS 15 software, The original plan was 60 patients in each group, but the mid-term analysis of the experiment found that there was a significant difference between the pre-observation group and the conventional group, so the experiment was terminated. Finally, a total of 70 patients provided informed consent to participate in the study, six cases were not enrolled due to the delay of the surgery. Eventually, there were 32 cases in each group, and a total of 64 cases were included in the analysis.

Statistical analysis methods

Statistical analysis was conducted using IBM SPSS Statistics 25.0 software. The primary hypothesis was that the pre-observation method would result in a higher first-attempt success rate for TEE probe placement compared to the conventional blind insertion technique. The secondary hypothesis proposed that the pre-observation method would reduce the time required for TEE probe placement, reduce the severity and duration of postoperative odynophagia, and the incidence of other complications when compared to the conventional technique. Statistical significance was determined at P < 0.05.

Patient demographic data were reported as mean (standard deviation [SD]), while the outcomes of observed indicators were expressed as median (Inter-Quartile Range [IQR]) or the number of patients (%). The Shapiro-Wilk test was used to assess the normality of data distribution. Median differences were analyzed using the Mann-Whitney U test, with the 95% confidence interval (CI) calculated through the Hodges-Lehmann method. The difference in the first-attempt success rate of probe placement between the groups was compared using Fisher’s exact test, and the OR for first-attempt success between the groups was assessed through logistic regression analysis.

The differences in failure rates for probe insertion and the number of probe insertion attempts between the two groups were analyzed using the chi-square test. The time taken for probe placement and the NRS scores for odynophagia on postoperative days 1, 3, 5, and 7 were compared between the groups using the rank-sum test. Additionally, the differences in the proportion of patients experiencing moderate to severe odynophagia on postoperative days 1, 3, 5, and 7 were assessed using the chi-square test. The differences in the number and total duration of intraoperative TEE examinations between the groups were assessed with the Mann-Whitney U test for two independent samples. Finally, the difference in the length of time the probes remained in the esophagus between the two groups was compared using the two-sample t-test.

The relationship between the percentage of patients achieving successful probe placement over time in both groups was assessed using the Kaplan-Meier method, with differences analyzed through the log-rank test. The probability of successful probe placement in the pre-observation group compared to the conventional group was quantified through the hazard ratio (HR), estimated using Cox regression analysis. Similarly, the relationship between the percentage of patients experiencing postoperative odynophagia over time in both groups was analyzed using the Kaplan-Meier method, with the log-rank test applied to assess differences. The probability of postoperative odynophagia occurring in the pre-observation group when compared to the conventional group was also estimated using Cox regression to determine the HR.

Logistic regression analysis was used to assess the correlation between the development of moderate-to-severe swallowing pain on postoperative day 1 and the first-attempt success rate of TEE probe placement. Additionally, the association between the incidence of moderate-to-severe odynophagia lasting beyond one day postoperatively and the successful placement of the TEE probe on the first attempt was examined using logistic regression.

Results

All 64 patients successfully completed the study. Demographic data, including age, gender, height, weight, Body Mass Index (BMI), ASA class, New York Heart Association (NYHA) class, Mallampati class, and Cormack-Lehane class, did not differ significantly between the two groups. Additionally, no significant differences were observed between the groups in terms of heart rate, mean arterial pressure (MAP), and central venous pressure (CVP) before and after probe placement. Furthermore, there were no statistically significant differences between the two groups in the number of operations, total operation duration, or the length of time the probe remained in the esophagus (Table 1).

Pre-observation of the esophageal opening revealed that it was not always located directly behind the trachea. Specifically, in 11 cases, the esophageal opening was found behind the left posterior larynx, in 9 cases behind the right posterior larynx, and in 12 cases directly behind the larynx. All patients in the pre-observation group (Group P) experienced successful insertion of the TEE probe, whereas in the conventional group (Group C), probe insertion failed in 3 patients (the types of esophageal openings were as follows: 1 case was slightly to the left of the mid-line, and 2 cases were slightly to the right of the mid-line), necessitating the use of a video laryngoscope. Additionally, 3 patients in group C experienced oral and pharyngeal mucosal injuries. The pre-observation group exhibited a significantly higher first-attempt success rate, achieving 78% compared to 22% in Group C (OR: 17.54; 95% confidence interval [CI]: 3.86 to 83.33; P < 0.001). The number of TEE probe insertions required was significantly lower in Group P than in Group C (P < 0.001). Moreover, the total time required for successful TEE probe insertion was reduced in Group P, with a median duration of 11 s (IQR: 8–16 s), compared to 14.5 s (IQR: 10–25 s) in Group C. The median difference in placement duration was 3 s (95% CI: 1 to 7; P = 0.013) (Table 2). Kaplan-Meier analysis indicated that the probability of successful probe placement at any given time was significantly higher in Group P compared to Group C (HR: 2; 95% CI: 1.18–3.38; P = 0.010) (Fig. 3).

The occurrence of moderate to severe odynophagia (NRS > 3) on postoperative day 1 was significantly correlated with the first-attempt success of TEE probe insertion, with an OR of 0.35 (95% CI: 0.12–0.97; P = 0.044). This finding indicates that a higher first-attempt success rate is associated with a lower likelihood of experiencing moderate to severe odynophagia on the first postoperative day. Additionally, the prolonged presence of moderate to severe postoperative odynophagia lasting more than one day was also linked to the first-attempt success rate of TEE probe insertion, with an OR of 0.18 (95% CI: 0.05–0.65; P = 0.008). This indicates that a higher first-attempt success rate decreases the probability of experiencing moderate to severe odynophagia for more than one day postoperatively. A higher first-attempt success rate in TEE probe placement is associated with less intense odynophagia on postoperative day 1 and a shorter duration of postoperative odynophagia. The pre-observation group, which had a higher first-attempt success rate compared to the conventional group, experienced lower intensity of odynophagia on postoperative day 1, a shorter duration of postoperative odynophagia, and a reduced likelihood of developing moderate to severe odynophagia postoperatively.

All patients, including those who sustained throat injuries, were discharged without complications attributable to TEE probe insertion. Primarily, no severe injuries like esophageal rupture or gastric bleeding, were observed in either group.

Discussion

Studies have shown that video laryngoscope can help to insert the probe in patients under anesthesia and reduce the risk of mucosal injury and bleeding [1, 3, 4, 7–12]. However, the authors found it difficult to insert the probe after tracheal intubation in clinical practice. In this study, we enhanced the success rate of TEE probe placement and decreased the incidence and duration of postoperative odynophagia by pre-observing the position of the esophageal opening and adjusting the direction of TEE probe placement during tracheal intubation.

When compared to the conventional method, the pre-observation technique demonstrated a significantly higher first-attempt success rate for TEE probe placement, achieving 78% in Group P versus 22% in Group C (P < 0.001). A previous study reported a first-attempt success rate of 90.5% for TEE probe placement using a video laryngoscope, which is higher than the 78% observed in this study [3]. However, this does not necessarily indicate that video laryngoscopy is superior to the pre-observation method, as the first-attempt success rate of the conventional group in their study was 43.9%, which was also higher than the 22% observed here. This variation may be attributed to differences in the experience levels of the anesthesiologists performing the probe placements. Notably, the pre-observation method in our study enhanced the first-attempt success rate by a factor of 3.5 compared to the conventional technique, while the study using video laryngoscopy achieved a twofold increase. Previous research has indicated that the total duration for TEE probe placement with video laryngoscope assistance is comparable to that of conventional blind insertion techniques [3, 4, 13]. However, in this study, the total duration for successful TEE probe placement was shorter in Group P compared to Group C. The pre-observation approach, which involved fewer probe insertions and did not extend the time required for video laryngoscope insertion, led to a reduction in operational time compared to both conventional blind insertion techniques and the use of a video laryngoscope.

Dysphagia pain is reported as the most prevalent complication associated with TEE probe placement in patients who are anesthetized [12]. This study found that patients in the pre-observation group experienced less intense and shorter-lasting odynophagia compared to those in the conventional blind insertion group. It is important to note that odynophagia may not solely result from TEE probe insertion; for patients undergoing cardiovascular surgery, prolonged endotracheal intubation can also contribute to dysphagia pain, potentially leading to postoperative swallowing difficulties and an extended period before resuming oral intake [14]. Correlation analysis in this study demonstrated a significant relationship between the first-attempt success rate of TEE probe placement and both the intensity and duration of odynophagia.

In the study conducted by Nilgun et al., mucosal injury rates were reported at 17% for the conventional insertion technique and 2.4% for the video laryngoscope group. In contrast, this study observed a mucosal injury rate of 9.375% in the conventional group and 0% in the pre-observation group. The discrepancy in injury rates between this study and previous research may be attributed to differences in injury assessment methods or variations in the experience of the operators. Additionally, the mucosal injury rate associated with video laryngoscopy in the study conducted by Nilgun et al. was higher than that observed in this study (2.4% vs. 0%). This difference may reflect the inherent risk of mucosal damage associated with the use of video laryngoscopy.

This study provides new insights into the anatomical variations of the relationship between the esophageal opening and the laryngeal position during TEE probe insertion. Contrary to the conventional anatomical understanding that the esophageal opening is directly posterior to the trachea, our findings revealed that only 12 patients had the esophagus positioned directly posterior to the trachea [15]. In contrast, 11 patients had their esophagus located posterior to the left side of the larynx, and 9 patients had it posterior to the right side. This observation contrasts with the findings of Ahad et al., who reported that none of the nine cadavers had the esophagus positioned directly posterior to the trachea. Instead, their study found that six cadavers had the esophagus positioned posterior to the left of the trachea, one to the right, and two were nearly adjacent to the trachea. These discrepancies could be attributed to the small sample size of the cadaver study or potential alterations in anatomical structures due to cadaver preparation. Further research is needed to elucidate the specific anatomical relationship between the esophageal opening and the larynx during TEE probe placement.

This study has several limitations. First, the sample size may be inadequate for assessing rare complications associated with TEE probe placement. Second, the specific impact of gastric tube suction on TEE probe placement outcomes has not been thoroughly examined.

Though the jaw thrust maneuver has been revealed to facilitate TEE probe placement, it carries the risk of mandibular dislocation [7]. A study indicated that TEE probe placement is facilitated when the patient is positioned in the left lateral decubitus position while ventilated. However, this position may affect hemodynamics and is therefore not suitable for patients undergoing cardiac surgery. Pre-observing the position of the esophageal opening during tracheal intubation has been demonstrated to enhance the success rate of probe placement using the blind insertion technique and reduce postoperative odynophagia. Currently, TEE placement assisted by video laryngoscopy is not considered a standard procedure. Thus, the method of pre-observing the esophageal opening may be a preferable alternative.

Conclusion

Pre-observing the position of the esophageal opening significantly enhances the first-attempt success rate of TEE probe placement. It also reduces the number of attempts required, shortens the placement time, and diminishes both the intensity and duration of postoperative odynophagia. The incidence of moderate to severe odynophagia (NRS > 3) on postoperative day 1 was strongly correlated with the first-attempt success rate of TEE probe placement. Similarly, the persistence of moderate to severe odynophagia (NRS > 3) for more than one day postoperatively was related to the first-attempt success rate. A higher first-attempt success rate was associated with a lower probability of experiencing moderate to severe odynophagia on postoperative day 1 and a decreased likelihood of such pain lasting beyond one day. Therefore, the pre-observation method offers significant advantages over the conventional blind insertion technique for TEE probe placement.
