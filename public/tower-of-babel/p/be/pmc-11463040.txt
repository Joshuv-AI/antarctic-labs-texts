Clinical characteristics and toxicological spectrum analysis of 493 cases of acute poisoning in children
Authors: Chunling Xue, Jingxia Zeng, Wenjing Li
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
ObjectiveThis retrospective study aimed to analyze the clinical characteristics and toxicological spectrum of acute poisoning in children treated at our pediatric intensive care unit. The findings provide a basis for the treatment and prevention of acute poisoning in children.MethodsClinical data of hospitalized children aged 0 to14 years with acute poisoning, treated between January 2015 and December 2023, were collected and analyzed. The analysis included the types of poisoning, sex and age distribution of children, routes and causes of poisoning, time from poisoning to medical consultation, length of hospital stays, and treatment outcomes.ResultsAmong the 493 cases of acute poisoning in children, pesticide poisoning (n = 252, 51.1%) was the most common type, followed by medication poisoning (n = 182, 36.9%). Chemical poisoning accounted for 7.7% (n = 38), while food poisoning and inhalation of harmful gases accounted for 3.0% (n = 15) and 1.2% (n = 6), respectively. The main route of poisoning was oral ingestion (n = 477, 96.8%), while accidental ingestion accounted for a large proportion (n = 379, 79.4%), and adolescents aged 11 to 14 years accounted for 20.6% (n = 98).Apart from the predominance of girls attempting suicide by self-administering antipsychotic medications (n = 88, 75.9%) due to depression, there was no significant difference in sex distribution among other types of poisoning children. The highest incidence of poisoning except children who self-administer antipsychotic drugs occurred in children aged 1–3 years (n = 267, 54.2%) and 8–14 years (n = 135, 27.4%), with the highest proportion in the preschool age group. The average time from poisoning to medical consultation was 33.4 ± 54.9 h, and majority of children received treatment within 48 h after poisoning (n = 423, 85.8%). The overall effective treatment rate was 96.8%.ConclusionAcute poisoning in children has complex and diverse causes, with accidental ingestion being the main route. Prevention should be emphasized, including strengthening child safety education and improving parental supervision. Moreover, psychological health education is crucial for adolescents during their puberty. In the event of acute poisoning, prompt medical consultation and appropriate treatment are necessary to mitigate adverse outcomes.

Poisoning refers to the process in which toxic substances enter the human body in various forms and doses under certain conditions, interact with body fluids and tissues, and cause harmful biological changes or pathological reactions that disrupt normal physiological functions, leading to temporary or permanent pathological states or death [1]. Poisoning can be classified based on the course of the condition into acute poisoning and chronic poisoning. Chronic poisoning is more common in adults and is often related to occupational exposure, while acute poisoning of a single type is predominant in cases of poisoning in children, which is often associated with the surrounding environment [2].

Acute poisoning has a sudden onset, rapid changes in the condition, and clinical symptoms that lack obvious specificity. In severe cases, multiple organ dysfunction may occur, posing a life-threatening risk [3]. Children, as a special group, are at high risk for acute poisoning due to their lack of self-protection and safety awareness, and it can occur in various age groups [4, 5]. According to statistics, acute poisoning in children has become one of the common diseases in pediatric emergencies, accounting for 0.2–1% of the total number of children seen in emergency departments on a daily basis [2, 3], and 4.6% of poisoned children in outpatient settings require hospitalization and intensive care treatment [4].

Currently, there are limited number of epidemiological studies about acute poisoning cases in children [6–9]. In this study, a retrospective analysis was conducted to summarize and analyze the clinical data of 493 cases of acute poisoning in children admitted to our hospital from January 2015 to December 2023. The aim is to provide a basis for the clinical diagnosis, effective prevention, and treatment of acute poisoning in children.

Materials and methods

Clinical data

Study subjects

The hospitalization records of children aged 0 to 14 years with acute poisoning admitted to our hospital from January 2015 to December 2023 were retrieved. This study was approved by the Hospital Ethics Committee.

Our hospital is Children’s Hospital of Nanjing Medical University, a comprehensive tertiary Grade A children’s hospital integrating medical care, education, and research. We undertake the critical task of treating severely ill children from Jiangsu Province and surrounding areas, serving as a provincial-level emergency medical rescue base in pediatric medicine. Consequently, children with acute poisoning are urgently transferred to our hospital, with some patients initially receiving emergency treatments such as gastric lavage at nearby hospitals before being transferred to our hospital.

Data collection

Using the keywords “acute,” “poisoning,” and “ingestion” as search criteria, a case search was conducted in our hospital’s electronic health record system (HIS). Based on the inclusion and exclusion criteria, a total of 493 pediatric cases were selected. The clinical records of each of the 493 cases were reviewed, and information such as sex, age, diagnosis, type of poisoning, route of poisoning, time of poisoning at admission, symptomatic treatment methods, rescue procedures, relevant laboratory tests, prognosis, and length of hospital stay were recorded for retrospective statistical analysis. According to the recommended age groups in pediatric medicine, the patients were divided into the following age groups: ≤1 year, 1–3 years, 4–7 years, and 8–14 years.

Classification of poisonous substances

According to the ICD-10 coding, poisonous substances are classified as follows: medication (including traditional Chinese medicine), pesticides (such as insecticides, herbicides and rodenticides), inhalation of harmful gases (natural gas), food ( spoiled food and high levels of nitrite in food), mushrooms, chemicals (industrial solvents like caustic soda, potassium permanganate, sulfuric acid, household solvents like cosmetics, mosquito repellents, desiccants), alcohol, and others. Statistical analysis

Data processing was performed using SPSS 23.0 statistical software. Descriptive statistics, such as counts and proportions (%), were used for general categorical data. The Chi-square test or Fisher’s exact test was used for comparing categorical variables. Continuous data were expressed as mean ± standard deviation, and the t-test was used for comparisons. A significance level of P < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Distribution of poisoning routes

Among 493 cases of acute poisoning in children, the main route of poisoning was oral ingestion (96.8%). Among them, 379 cases (79.5%) were accidental ingestion, while intentional ingestion accounted for a large proportion, with 98 cases (20.6%) and all of which were adolescents aged 11 to 14 years. Apart from the route of poisoning through ingestion, the remaining 16 cases of acute poisoning in children were attributed to 10 cases of toxic substances contact and 6 cases of inhalation of harmful gases.

Distribution of poisoning types and toxic substances

Among the 493 cases of acute poisoning in children, pesticide poisoning (51.1%) was the most common type, followed by medication poisoning (36.9%). Chemical poisoning accounted for 7.7%, while food poisoning, mushroom poisoning and inhalation of harmful gases accounted for 1.8%, 1.2% and 1.2%, respectively. The distribution of different poisoning types and toxic substances by sex and age are analyzed and presented in Table 1.

Pesticide poisoning

A total of 252 cases of pesticide poisoning among children, accounting for the majority of acute poisoning cases. Among them, rodenticides poisoning was the most common (55.6%), followed by herbicides poisoning (31.4%), and insecticides poisoning accounting for 13.1%.

Rodenticides poisoning was primarily caused by bromadiolone, accounted for 83.6% of cases. There was no significant difference in the sex distribution of children with rodenticide poisoning (P > 0.05). In terms of age distribution, children aged 1 to 3 years accounted for 73.6%, followed by children aged 4 to 7 years at 22.9%. Children aged 8 to 14 years and those under 1 year old accounted for 2.1% and 1.4%, respectively, with smaller proportions.

Herbicides poisoning was mainly caused by paraquat, accounting for 69.6% of cases. There was no significant difference in the sex distribution of children with herbicides poisoning (P > 0.05). In terms of age distribution, children aged 1 to 3 years still represented the majority at 73.6%, followed by children aged 8 to 14 years, which differs from the overall age distribution of pesticide poisoning, as it includes children who intentionally ingested herbicides for self-harm.

There was no significant difference in the sex distribution of children with insecticides poisoning (P > 0.05). In contrast to the overall age distribution of pesticide poisoning, children aged 8 to 14 years were the majority in insecticide poisoning, followed by children aged 1 to 3 years. The reason for this difference is also due to the inclusion of children who intentionally ingested insecticides for self-harm.

Drug poisoning

There was a total of 182 cases of drug poisoning among children, involving 6 different types of drug poisoning. The most common type of drug poisoning was antipsychotic drug poisoning (63.7%), followed by other drug (15.4%), anti-diarrheal drug poisoning (9.3%), cardiovascular drug poisoning (8.2%), and Traditional Chinese Medicine poisoning (1.7%) and hypoglycemic drug poisoning (1.7%). Among children with drug poisoning, the number of female children is significantly higher than that of male children, and this sex difference is statistically significant (P < 0.05). This difference is primarily attributed to the sex disparity in cases of drug poisoning involving antipsychotic medications. Among children with antipsychotic drug poisoning, 88 cases involve female children, accounting for 75.9%. Most of these children had an underlying condition of depression, and the poisoning is a result of taking antidepressant medications. In terms of age distribution, adolescents aged 8 to 14 years, particularly noticeable after COVID-19, were the primary group affected. However, children poisoned by other types of drugs were still predominantly in the 1 to 3 age group.

Chemical poisoning

The number of children affected by industrial solvent poisoning is higher than those poisoned by household solvents, but there was no statistically significant difference (P > 0.05). There was no statistically significant difference in the sex distribution for children affected by either industrial solvent poisoning or household solvents poisoning (P > 0.05). In terms of age distribution, the majority of cases still involved children aged 1 to 3 years, regardless of whether it was industrial solvent poisoning or household solvents poisoning.

Food poisoning

Food poisoning primarily consists of 3 cases of food poisoning from spoiled food, 2 cases of ricin poisoning, 1 case of ginkgo nut poisoning, 1 case of nitrite poisoning and 2 cases of other food poisoning. Children with food poisoning showed no significant differences in terms of sex and age distribution.

Mushroom poisoning

All 6 cases of mushroom poisoning were caused by the accidental consumption of wild poisonous mushrooms. There were no significant differences in sex and age distribution among the affected children (P > 0.05).

Poisoning from inhalation of harmful gases

All 6 cases of poisoning from inhalation of harmful gases were due to natural gas inhalation. There were no significant differences in sex and age distribution among the affected children (P > 0.05).

The average time to medical treatment after poisoning among the 493 cases of acute poisoning was (33.4 ± 54.9) hours. Majority of patients (423, 85.8%) received medical treatment within 48 h after poisoning. Treatment and prognosis

Among the 493 cases, 371 cases (75.3%)underwent gastric lavage upon admission, and 165 cases received treatments such as blood purification, plasma exchange or blood perfusion. Except for cases of benzodiazepine poisoning treated with specific antidote flumazenil and cases of rodenticide poisoning treated with vitamin K1, all patients received symptomatic therapy, and achieved favorable therapeutic outcomes.

According to efficacy standards, 245 cases (49.7%) achieved complete recovery, 232 cases (47.1%) showed improvement, 10 cases (2.0%) did not recover, and 6 cases (1.2%) resulted in death. The overall effective rate of treatment was 96.8%. Among the 6 cases of deceased children, there were 4 cases of intentional poisoning (self-ingestion) and 2 cases of accidental poisoning. The 4 children with intentional poisoning were all in the 12 to 13 age group during adolescence, and 2 of them ingested diquat, 1 ingested paraquat, and 1 ingested organophosphorus pesticide. They were admitted to PICU due to symptoms such as dizziness, vomiting, and coma. After admission, gastric lavage, multiple sessions of blood purification, and symptomatic treatment were administered. Due to the substantial amount ingested, the child who ingested paraquat passed away 1 day after admission, while the two children who ingested diquat passed away 2 days and 4 days after admission, respectively. The child who ingested the organophosphorus pesticide passed away 7 days after admission.

Discussion

Sex and age characteristics of acute poisoning in children

In this study, a total of 493 cases of acute poisoning in children were analyzed, with a male-to-female ratio of 0.93:1. This discrepancy from previous literature reports, which indicated a higher incidence of poisoning in boys compared to girls [10, 11], is attributed to an increase in the number of adolescent girls attempting poisoning due to depression and suicidal tendencies after COVID-19. As a result, the overall incidence of poisoning among girls has surpassed that of boys. This issue also requires sufficient attention, particularly regarding mental health education for adolescents. Additionally, in other types of poisoning, the proportion of boys is consistently higher than that of girls. The analysis suggests that this could be attributed to the more active and curious nature of boys, leading to a greater tendency for adventurous activities and more frequent exposure to hazardous substances. Furthermore, social and cultural factors, family environment, and lifestyle may also contribute to sex differences in poisoning rates. Therefore, parents need to pay special attention to safety education for boys during their upbringing to prevent the occurrence of accidental poisoning incidents.

From the analysis of age distribution, acute poisoning primarily affects children in the age groups of 1–3 years (preschool age) and 8–14 years (school age) which is consistent with the results of a study of Germany [12]. The highest proportion of poisoning cases is observed in the preschool age group. The main causes of poisoning in this age group are accidental ingestion of pesticides including herbicides and rodenticides, medications, and others. Toddlers in this age range exhibit limited physical abilities and engage in a restricted range of activities. The primary cause of poisoning is self-induced, often resulting from placing objects in their mouths. However, in most cases, inadequate supervision by parents or caregivers is a contributing factor. Furthermore, from the toddler stage to early school age, children’s cognitive development gradually progresses. They become curious about their surroundings and tend to explore the world by putting things in their mouths. However, their understanding of hazards is limited, and they lack self-protective abilities. If caregivers are not vigilant, incidents such as accidental ingestion can occur. Therefore, effective supervision and monitoring of toddlers and children by parents are crucial.

In addition, among the cases of self-administered poisoning, there were 101 children involved. Out of these, 63 cases were due to excessive intake of antipsychotic medications, and 14 cases were a result of ingestion of diquat or paraquat. The majority of these cases occurred in children aged 8–14 years, especially 10–13 years during adolescence. Additionally, there were more girls affected than boys, especially after COVID-19, which aligns with the sex difference reported in the literature [13]. This could be related to the psychological development of children in this age group, as they are more susceptible to external influences. However, our study has not yet identified any patients admitted due to abuse, which contrasts with findings from studies conducted abroad [12]. The result of self-administered poisoning emphasize the need not only to strengthen poisoning education but also to prioritize mental health education, particularly for children, including those in their adolescence. It is essential to enhance communication, provide guidance, and pay attention to their psychological well-being by focusing on mental resilience education.

Analysis of types of acute poisoning in children

The types of acute poisoning among children vary in different countries and regions around the world. From 2009 to 2013, the top three types of poisoning among children in Egypt were corrosive substance poisoning, bacterial food poisoning, and pesticide poisoning [14]. Among them, pesticide poisoning, particularly from organophosphates and carbamate insecticides, was the most common. In Europe, household product poisoning is predominant, while in South America, pesticide poisoning is more common [13]. Domestic studies in China have shown that pesticide poisoning is the primary form of acute poisoning among children, with organophosphates and carbamate insecticides being the most commonly involved [15].

This study found that accidental ingestion is the primary route of acute poisoning in children, with pesticide poisoning being the most common including rodenticides, herbicides and insecticides poisoning, and the majority of these patients are from rural areas. Following pesticide ingestion, medication ingestion is the next most common cause. However, the proportion of poisonings caused by chemical exposure, food poisoning, and inhalation of harmful gases is relatively low. The use and proper placement of rodenticides and pesticides require the attention of parents, especially those with young children, to prevent accidental ingestion. Additionally, with the increasing awareness of public health, the increasing number of medications stored in households poses a significant risk of accidental ingestion for children. Therefore, it is important to promptly store rodenticides, pesticides, and medications in places inaccessible to children, consider using locks if necessary, and also provide safety education to children to prevent such incidents from occurring.

Clinical treatment and prognosis of acute poisoning in children

Children, especially infants and toddlers, often have inaccurate or incorrect expressions of symptoms and discomfort after experiencing acute poisoning. Without relevant evidence, caregivers can face significant challenges in diagnosing and treating acute poisoning cases. For children with known toxins that have specific antidotes, timely administration of the specific antidote is necessary. However, most toxins do not have specific antidotes, so effective supportive symptomatic treatment is crucial. Except for ingesting corrosive substances by mistake, for children primarily exhibiting gastrointestinal symptoms, gastric lavage, induced vomiting, and other measures are generally taken. Diuretics, laxatives, and other methods may be used to promote the excretion of intestinal toxins. In severe cases, blood purification treatment may be performed. Blood purification treatment is effective for cases of acute poisoning with unknown causes. It is important to note that in situations where symptoms are not obvious, the dosage is uncertain, or poisoning is uncertain, aggressive treatment is usually adopted. However, excessive medical intervention may potentially cause unnecessary harm.

Our hospital is a tertiary pediatric hospital of the highest level. Some patients are transferred to our hospital after receiving initial treatment at primary healthcare facilities. Our study indicated that 85.8% of patients sought medical attention within 48 h after poisoning. After the child is admitted to the PICU, proactive gastric lavage is administered, along with corresponding symptomatic treatments. Gastric lavage is one of the methods used to remove ingested toxins in cases of poisoning. Over the past decade, evidence-based medicine from abroad has shown that most patients with acute oral poisoning do not benefit from gastric lavage and, on the contrary, face an increased risk of complications. As a result, gastric lavage is not generally recommended. However, in China, gastric lavage remains a common method for clearing toxins from the stomach. Following the “Chinese expert consensus on diagnosis and treatment of acute poisoning” [16], we carefully assess the indications for gastric lavage based on factors such as the type of toxin and the time elapsed since ingestion. We actively administer gastric lavage to pediatric patients when appropriate, and after ruling out bowel obstruction, we also provide treatments like activated charcoal to absorb the toxins. Research has shown that gastric lavage can reduce the mortality rate in patients with acute (especially severe) poisoning, though further evidence-based studies are recommended to confirm these findings [17].

In cases of poisoning with specific antidotes, such as organophosphate pesticide poisoning, counteraction is initiated with iodine phosphate or chlorophos antidotes. Benzodiazepine poisoning is countered with flumazenil, while rodenticide poisoning is treated with vitamin K1. For other types of poisoning, activated charcoal is administered to absorb the toxic substances. Atropine is used to inhibit glandular secretion, and naloxone is employed to promote awakening in certain cases. For pediatric patients with severe conditions, treatment may involve blood perfusion, plasma exchange, and blood purification based on monitoring of blood drug concentrations including antipsychotic drugs and pesticides.

The overall effective treatment rate of 96.8%, and the majority of patients were discharged within 7 days of hospitalization. These favorable outcomes can be attributed to the timely medical intervention following poisoning, as well as the factors of a single toxic substance ingestion and relatively low dosage among most patients.

Prevention and treatment strategies for acute poisoning in children

The majority of cases of acute poisoning in children are accidental and preventable. Therefore, parents and caregivers need to raise their awareness of safety, especially in households with young children, by paying attention to the proper management of household medications and toxic substances. Pesticides and household stockpiles of medications should be securely stored and placed out of reach of children after use. In addition to regulating the storage and placement of toxins and medications, increasing caregivers’ awareness of safety, it is also important to familiarize oneself with common symptoms of medication poisoning. If a child is found to be poisoned, seeking prompt medical attention is crucial. Furthermore, as the pace of life accelerates, psychological stress among individuals, including children and adolescents, is increasing. Parents and teachers should pay special attention to students in their adolescence and prioritize providing psychological support and education to children and adolescents, which is imperative.

Limitation

This study had several important limitations. First, this study was conducted at a level III hospital in Nanjing, China; thus, our results may provide limited knowledge to other children’s hospital. Nonetheless, our hospital which is the core children’s hospital in the Jiangsu and Anhui provinces, provided emergency treatment to a large portion of acute poisoning patients in the region. Second, this was a retrospective observational study, and we may not have captured all the data of acute poisoning patients due to epidemic control during the COVID-19 period. Third, due to the large proportion of rural population in China, and the fact that many children suffering from pesticide poisoning come from rural areas, challenges such as transportation constraints or time limitations in rescuing poisoned children mean that some patients cannot promptly treated to our hospital. Therefore, the urban-rural disparity may lead to differences in the spectrum of toxins involved.

Conclusion

Acute poisoning in children varies in types and clinical manifestations depending on the type and dosage of the toxic substances involved. Severe cases may affect multiple organs and systems, causing irreversible damage to the body. The majority of acute poisonings, especially in children, are preventable, emphasizing the importance of prevention. Furthermore, parents and teachers should pay special attention to the mental health of adolescents. This study conducted a retrospective analysis of the clinical characteristics of acute poisoning in children in our hospital, providing valuable insights for the prevention of acute poisoning in children.
