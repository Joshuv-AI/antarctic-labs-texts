Spillover of SARS-CoV-2 to domestic dogs in COVID-19–positive households: A one health surveillance study
Authors: Santhamani Ramasamy, Felipe Bustamante, Lindsey C LaBella, Stephen D Cole, Jay Armstrong, David L Thompson, John M Hardham, Suresh V Kuchipudi, Deepanker Tewari
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
Highlights•Higher seroprevalence among the dogs exposed to COVID-19 positive individuals in the household.•Higher antibody titers detected for the SARS-CoV-2 Delta, Gamma and Alpha compared to Omicron variant.•Lateral flow assay developed at Zoetis for the SARS-CoV-2 antibody detection in dogs has acceptable sensitivity and specificity to use for surveillance.Keywords: SARS-CoV-2, Spillover, Pet dogs, Serology, Lateral flow assay, Surveillance

Introduction

Since the start of the coronavirus disease-2019 (COVID-19) pandemic, increasing evidence has shown that severe acute respiratory syndrome coronavirus-2 (SARS-CoV-2) can cross species barriers, raising questions about the role of animals in viral ecology and the importance of integrated human-animal surveillance systems. SARS-CoV-2 continues to circulate and evolve into new variants and subvariants (Chatterjee et al., 2023). Apart from humans, natural infections of SARS-CoV-2 are reported among several species of domestic and wild animals including cat, dog (Sit et al., 2020), ferret (Racnik et al., 2021), mink (Oreshkova et al., 2020), white-tailed deer (Hale et al., 2022; Kuchipudi et al., 2022), lion and tiger (Gallichotte et al., 2025). Due to household sharing and close contact between humans and pets, the risk of SARS-CoV-2 spillover from infected individuals to pets is high (Kannekens-Jager et al., 2022). Such interspecies transmission events underscore the interconnectedness of human and animal health, highlighting the need for a One Health approach to zoonotic disease surveillance. Previous studies showed a higher seroprevalence rate among the pets exposed to COVID-19 positive humans in the household. The seroprevalence of 53 % was reported in dogs exposed to COVID-19 positive individuals in the household compared to 0.2 % in dogs without known contact with COVID‐19‐positive persons (Fritz et al., 2021; Zhao et al., 2021). Human to dog spillovers of multiple SARS-CoV-2 variants have been documented (Jairak, Chamsai, et al., 2022, 2022; Lyoo et al., 2023). Monitoring these spillovers is critical not only for understanding animal exposure but also for assessing the broader ecological dynamics and potential viral adaptation events.

The SARS-CoV-2 infection in dogs and cats has been reported to cause respiratory and gastrointestinal illness in some animals while most dogs remain asymptomatic. An experimental study showed the transmission of SARS-CoV-2 from infected to in-contact dogs; but the window of virus presence in the nasal cavity was 6–8 days (Lyoo et al., 2023). Meanwhile, the antibodies induced by SARS-CoV-2 persist in dogs for up to 10 months (Decaro et al., 2022); therefore, the serological assays are promising tools for monitoring the SARS-CoV-2 spillover. Serological evidence of infection in dogs was determined using neutralization tests such as c-Pass surrogate virus neutralization test (sVNT), live virus neutralization test (VN), plaque reduction neutralization test (PRNT) and ELISA (Barua et al., 2021; Jairak, Chamsai, et al., 2022; Kannekens-Jager et al., 2022; Sit et al., 2020). Using these tests for routine surveillance of pets could be expensive and time consuming. Currently, there are no rapid tests available for the monitoring of SARS-CoV-2 infection in dogs. A field-deployable, affordable diagnostic tool for pets would enhance surveillance capacity and enable early detection of emerging variants in companion animals.

In this study, we have conducted SARS-CoV-2 serosurveillance in dogs (n = 196) living in Pennsylvania using sVNT and compared sVNT to pseudovirus neutralization test (pVNT) and evaluated a rapid and affordable lateral flow assay (LFA) for the detection of SARS-CoV-2 antibodies in dogs. Our analysis revealed an overall seropositivity of 12.24 % in Pennsylvania in 2021–2022, while the seropositivity was 68 % in dogs exposed to COVID-19 positive individuals in the household. LFA showed 85 % diagnostic sensitivity and specificity compared to the sVNT suggesting possible use for the SARS-CoV-2 monitoring in dogs.

Materials and methods

Serum samples

In total, we collected sera from 196 dogs. One set of serum samples (n = 171) were remnants of specimens collected from the dogs (Canis lupus familiaris) brought to the Veterinary Teaching Hospital at the University of Pennsylvania during November 2021 (Fig. 1). At the time of the clinic visit, the dogs did not show any symptoms of COVID-19-related illness, and they were brought to the clinic for other reasons. Owners sign an agreement upon admission that remnant specimens can be used for research purposes and use of remnant specimens is exempt from IACUC review at the University of Pennsylvania. A second set of dog serum samples (n = 35) were submitted to Pennsylvania Veterinary Laboratory as part of approved survey by Department of Health and Agriculture. Serum samples from 19 dogs were from households, where owners provided history of SARS-CoV-2 infection. Out of 19 dogs, 16 had exposure >30 days prior to the sampling, two had exposure from 10–30 days and one had exposure <10 days prior to the sampling. Two of the exposed dogs in one of the households had diarrhea and vomiting, while the other dogs sampled had no symptoms reported.

Surrogate virus neutralization assay (sVNT)

The serum samples were tested using cPass sVNT, which detects the binding of SARS-CoV-2-specific antibodies to SARS-CoV-2 RBD (B.1 lineage or Omicron) and measures the inhibition of SARS-CoV-2 RBD and human–ACE2 interaction (Tan et al., 2020). The assay was conducted as per manufacturer’s recommendations (GenScript, USA) using manufacturer’s provided antigens (Jung et al., 2021). Briefly, diluted serum samples (1:10) were incubated with a horseradish peroxidase conjugated receptor binding domain of SARS-CoV-2 for 30 min at 37 °C. Then the serum–RBD mixture was transferred to ACE2-coated 96-well plates, and the unbound RBDs were allowed to bind to the ACE2. The RBD–ACE2 interactions were measured by using a trimethyl benzidine substrate. The percentage of inhibition of RBD binding to ACE2 is proportional to the SARS-CoV-2-specific antibodies in the serum samples. The serum samples with percent inhibition above 30 % were declared positive. We have tested all the 196 serum samples using B.1 lineage RBD based sVNT for the determination of antibody status. A subset of samples (n = 28) was tested in Omicron BA.1 RBD based sVNT.

Pseudovirus neutralization test (pVNT)

The 3rd generation lentiviral transfer plasmid encoding luciferase and ZsGreen (BEI, NR-52,516), and packaging plasmids encoding Gag/pol (BEI, NR52517), helper plasmids and SARS-CoV-2 spike plasmids were co-transfected in HEK 293T cells using FuGene6 transfection reagent (Promega Cat. No. E2691). Pseudoviruses for B.1 lineage, Alpha, Beta, Gamma, Delta, or Omicron BA.1 variants of concern were produced using the spikes from respective variants (Ramasamy et al., 2023). The HEK 293T cells were propagated using Dulbecco’s Modified Eagle Medium (DMEM) with 10 % fetal bovine serum (FBS). The pseudovirus-containing supernatants were collected after 48 h of transfection and filtered through 0.45 µ membrane filters, and aliquots were stored at −80 °C until further use. The infectivity of pseudoviruses was tested in HEK 293T cells expressing human ACE2 (HEK-293T-hACE2). Briefly, 1.3 × 104 cells were infected with 10-fold serial dilutions of the pseudoviruses in a 96-well clear bottom white plate (ThermoScientific, Cat. No. 165,306). After 48–72 h, the Brightglo luciferase reagent (Promega, Cat. No. E2620) will be added to the wells (100 µL per well) and read using BioTek Cytation 5 Cell Imaging Multimode Reader (Agilent, USA), and relative luminescence units (RLUs) were recorded. The virus titer equivalent to 5 × 104 RLU were used for the neutralization assays. Twenty-two serum samples including 16 sVNT positives and 6 sVNT negatives were analyzed using pVNT for the estimation of NT50 of serum samples.

Antigenic cartography

The antigenic cartography was generated using the NT50 titers determined using pVNT as described by Smith et al. (Smith et al., 2004). Antigenic maps were optimized using Racmacs version 1.2.9(R) (Wilks, 2024) and the two-dimensional maps were created with 500 optimizations while the minimum column basis set to 0. The titer table with the NT50 of each serum against the SARS-CoV-2 variant was used to calculate the antigenic distance between each variant and serum samples. The table distance vs map distances was plotted, and antigenic maps were generated using the R language (RStudio 2024.12.1 + 563). We have included NT50 of serum samples from six cats collected from different locations for our previous study (Ramasamy et al., 2023) to enhance the robustness of the antigenic map.

Lateral flow assay

For comparative evaluation, an experimental lateral flow assay (LFA) developed by Zoetis for detecting the SARS-CoV-2-specific antibodies in serum samples from dogs was also used to assess seroreactivity (Ramasamy et al., 2023). The LFA kit components were equilibrated to room temperature prior to the sample testing and the LFA result was read visually. Briefly, the sample pad was instilled with a drop of undiluted serum followed by two drops of chase buffer, Zoetis (COVID-19 antibody assay chase buffer for canines). The reagents were allowed to flow horizontally on the work bench and incubated for 10 min. The results were considered positive if both test and control lines were visible, negative if only the control line was visible, or invalid if the control line were not visible. Eighty-seven serum samples with known sVNT positive or negative status were tested using LFA for the determination of diagnostic sensitivity and specificity of LFA.

Virus neutralization assay (VN)

The VN assay for the detection of SARS-CoV-2 antibodies was performed at the USDA National Veterinary Services Laboratory (NVSL) as previously described (Pauvolid-Corrêa et al., 2025). Briefly, Vero 76 cells (ATCC CRL-1587) were seeded onto 96-well plates for 18–24 h at 37 °C with 5 % CO2. Two-fold serially diluted serum samples (1:8 to 1:2048) were mixed with 100 TCID50/mL of SARS-CoV-2 (2019-nCoV/USA-WA1/2020; 126 ATCC NR-52,281) and incubated at 37 °C for one hour. The serum-virus mixtures were added to the monolayer of Vero cells and incubated for 72 h. The assay was performed in duplicates for each serum sample. Cytopathic effects were monitored under a light microscope and the reciprocal of the highest dilution of serum showing complete neutralization of the reference virus is considered as the neutralization titer of the serum. To verify the sVNT results, we used VN on 10 serum samples including 6 sVNT positives and 4 sVNT negatives.

Results

Out of 196 dog serum samples, 171 (87.2 %) were collected during November 2021 and the remaining were sampled during January-March 2022. We have tested the serum samples from 196 dogs using SARS-CoV-2 ancestral B.1 lineage RBD-based sVNT. Out of 196 serum samples, 22 were found positive for the antibodies (Fig. 2). We have further tested a subset of serum samples (n = 28) in SARS-CoV-2 Omicron RBD-based sVNT; which exhibited the presence of antibodies in eight samples. Out of eight positive dogs serologically, two dogs showed the presence of antibodies only to Omicron and the remaining six dogs showed the presence of antibodies to both B.1 lineage and Omicron, suggesting the exposure to both the strains. In total (B.1 lineage and Omicron sVNT), 24 dogs showed the SARS-CoV-2 specific antibodies indicating the seroprevalence of 12.24 %, and that included two dogs showing the presence of antibodies only for the Omicron variant (Fig. 2). In addition, out of all 22 positives for SARS-CoV-2 B1 lineage RBD-based sVNT, 18 samples were tested in Omicron sVNT. Ten of these were just positive for the B.1 lineage and negative for Omicron and as discussed only 2 dogs showed Omicron variant only response, remainder showed responses to both antigens (Table A.1 and Fig A.1).

We have categorized the dog sera into two groups; One group had sera from dogs (n = 19) with a prior history of SARS-CoV-2 exposure from the COVID-19 positive owners in the household. As per the owners’ note, exposure to sampling time ranged from <10 days to >30 days. Fifteen out of nineteen dogs were exposed to COVID-19 patients at least 30 days prior to the sampling; the serum was collected from remaining four dogs within a month of exposure to COVID-19. Another group represents the serum from dogs (n = 177) that were brought to the clinic for concerns other than for SARS-CoV-2 infection. To compare the seroreactivity between the two sera groups, we have analyzed the antibody data from B.1 lineage RBD-based sVNT (Table 1). The dogs exposed to COVID-19 positive individuals at home had a significantly higher seroprevalence rate (68.42 %) than the dogs with no known exposure status, which had 4.51 % seroprevalence (Fig. 2). Interestingly, the serum samples from two dogs that had vomiting and diarrhea, from a COVID-19-positive household, were tested positive in B.1 lineage RBD-based sVNT.

Discussion

SARS-CoV-2 continues to infect a broad range of hosts, including humans, domestic animals, zoological animals, and wildlife while novel variants continue to emerge (Hobbs and Reid, 2021; SARS-CoV-2 in animals, 2024; Tan et al., 2022). The emerging variants may have altered susceptibility to a given host (Halfmann et al., 2022). Continuous monitoring therefore of SARS-CoV-2 spillovers in susceptible hosts is important to build effective control measures. Given the close contact between pets and humans within households, it is imperative to monitor the spillover and viral evolution in pets. Dogs are the most popular pet in the US and 68 million households own a dog per American Pet Products Association (2025) (APPA, 2025). Dogs therefore could potentially serve as an important vector host for the SARS-CoV-2 evolution.

This study was performed to investigate the spillover of SARS-CoV-2 in dogs from November 2021 to March 2022, during which the Delta or Omicron variant was predominantly circulating. The gold standard sVNT demonstrated overall seropositivity of 12.24 % (24/196) that included two sets of dog samples. Further, we have analyzed the seroprevalence in the context of potential exposure to SARS-CoV-2 through COVID-19 positive individuals in the household. Dogs with known exposure to COVID-19 positive individuals in the household had a significantly higher seroprevalence (68.42 %, 14/19) compared to those with no recent infection history or known exposure, who were presented to the clinic for unrelated health issues (4.51 %, 8/177). Out of the dogs sampled from COVID-19 positive household, two dogs had a history of vomiting and diarrhea and tested positive for SARS-CoV-2 antibodies, suggesting the possibility of symptomatic illness due to SARS-CoV-2. These results indicate that exposure from humans in the household with infected individuals is common in dogs as they share the living space with humans as reported by other groups (Fritz et al., 2021; Kannekens-Jager et al., 2022; Pauvolid-Corrêa et al., 2025).

For the variant specific antibody analysis, we have used pseudoviruses containing spikes from SARS-CoV-2 B1 lineage, Alpha, Beta, Gamma, Delta and Omicron BA.1. The serum samples (n = 18) collected from January to March 2022 showed higher neutralization titer (NT50) to pre-Omicron variants such as Delta (n = 11), Gamma (n = 5) and Alpha (n = 2), implying the historic exposure to these variants, even though the Omicron variant was gaining prominence from February 2022. The pVNT results were consistent with sVNT results, except one Omicron sVNT positive serum was negative in pVNT; due to low antibody titer. Additionally, two of the sVNT negative serum were mildly positive in pVNT. It was also interesting to note that 2 sera samples were only positive for sVNT with Omicron antigen but not to the B.1 lineage antigen. These sera came from two dogs from the same household likely indicating exposure to Omicron variant. Omicron variant, also referred to as the escape mutant which harbor several unique mutations in the spike, can result in antibodies that show poor binding to other pre-Omicron variants (Willett et al., 2022). However, there is extensive cross reactivity among pre-Omicron strains, and it was evident from dog sera showing higher reactivity to Delta, Gamma and Alpha variants thus likely explaining exposure in such cases to other variants than Omicron. These 18 samples had significantly lower neutralization titers (NT50) to Omicron BA.1 and none of them showed antibodies to Omicron variant alone. The sVNT also showed some reactivity with Omicron but rather at a low level. Though, the Omicron was the commonly circulating variant at the time of sampling in humans, the Delta variants likely overlapped as it was being outcompeted by Omicron (Lundberg et al., 2022). Over 85 % of the sampling was achieved during November 2021; therefore, these dogs could be highly likely infected with SARS-CoV-2 Delta or other earlier variants. Human studies have shown that the Omicron reinfection of individuals immunized with B.1 lineage, did not induce higher levels of Omicron specific antibodies possibly due to antigenic imprinting (Pusnik et al., 2024). In retrospect, having the variant infection data from humans and dogs would have strengthened our observations and interpretations but was unavailable and undertaken.

Several studies including ours have documented the evidence of SARS-CoV-2 transmission from people to dogs. However, while human-to-dog transmission is well-established, the extent to which dogs contribute to onward transmission, whether to other dogs, pets, or even humans remains largely unknown (Fischer et al., 2023). One Health approach to investigate the epidemiology and evolution of SARS-CoV-2 at human-animal interface is critically needed to track the evolution of the virus. Our variant specific neutralization titer analysis showed the poor cross-neutralization of Omicron variant by the sera with very high neutralization titer to Delta (NT50: 22 to 6216; mean: 594), Gamma (NT50: 20 to 2374; mean: 466) and Alpha (25 to 1325; mean: 255). In household settings, there is the possibility that these antibody positive dogs would get infected with circulating SARS-CoV-2 variants (Aguero et al., 2024; Barua et al., 2025). The nationwide serosurveillance study in the US has shown the prevalence of antibodies to both Delta and Omicron variants (Barua et al., 2025). This sero-response poses immune pressure to the infecting variant and potentially contributes to the emergence of new variants and sub-variants (Raharinirina et al., 2025).

We have also evaluated the lateral flow assay developed at Zoetis for the serosurveillance of SARS-CoV-2 antibodies in dogs. The assay has over 85 % sensitivity and specificity for the detection of antibodies. The LFA could be an important adjunct tool for the SARS-CoV-2 spillover monitoring programs in dogs due to the ease of monitoring and to be used in clinic assays. These assays could also be used as vaccine efficacy monitoring tools in dogs.

Finally, our study demonstrates seroreactivity to SARS CoV-2 in dogs during the pandemic using multiple detection methods including LFA, sVNT, pVNT and VN. sVNT proved to be most efficient in monitoring seroreactivity and for antibody titration pVNT was most robust. We also showed that the exposure to infected individuals in the household increases chances for some pets in homes, including dogs to be infected, evidenced by seroreactivity in the dogs. As the SARS-CoV-2 continues to evolve, monitoring the pets in the wake of new infections and variants and sub-variants will play an essential role in the One-Health mission. As the SARS-CoV-2 infects wide variety of mammals, vaccination of humans and animals is important to consider as part of a One Health approach to pandemic mitigation.
