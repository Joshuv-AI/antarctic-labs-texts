Proton‐linked effects on the axial coordination in ferric and ferrous horse heart cytochrome c
Authors: Federico Sebastiani, Alessio Bocedi, Giovanna De Simone, Sara Notari, Giorgia Gambardella, Diego Sbardella, Giulietta Smulevich, Paolo Ascenzi, Massimo Coletta
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractThe effect of pH (between pH 7.0 and 0.0) on the spectroscopic properties of ferric and ferrous derivatives of horse heart cytochrome c (hhcytc‐Fe(III) and hhcytc‐Fe(II), respectively) in 1.0M phosphate buffer is reported. This study further deepens the investigation of spectroscopic properties of a heme‐protein to very low pH (i.e., pH ≈ 0) showing relevant pH‐dependent variations of the heme surroundings, involving the heme‐Fe atom coordination. For both hhcytc‐Fe(III) and hhcytc‐Fe(II) a severe weakening of the Fe‐Met80 has been detected with a similar pK a, ranging between 2.5 and 3.0, suggesting a very small effect on the free energy for the protonation of this bond from the oxidation state of the heme's iron. Therefore, in the presence of either CO or NO, it has been possible to detect the formation at acid pH of hhcytc‐Fe(II)‐CO and hhcytc‐Fe(II)‐NO forms. On the other hand, the free energy for the protonation of the His18‐Fe proximal bond seems dependent on the oxidation state of the heme's iron, since a pK a ≈ 0.2 is observed for its cleavage in the hhcytc‐Fe(III) species, while this bond appears still intact in hhcytc‐Fe(II) even at pH ≈ 0. Since during patho‐physiological conditions cytochrome c may experience dramatic pH changes of the surroundings, this information turns out of the utmost relevance to explore mechanistic insights of pathological processes, such as redox unbalance and mitochondrial dysfunction, linked to cytochrome c alterations (e.g., neurodegeneration), for which effective modulation of the cytochrome c properties may be of therapeutic relevance.Keywords: circular dichroism, electronic absorption spectroscopy, horse heart cytochrome c , resonance Raman spectroscopy

INTRODUCTION

Eukaryotic cytochrome c (cytc) is a class of heme‐proteins localized between the inner and the outer membrane of mitochondria, playing a crucial role in the electron transfer between complex III (UQH2‐cytc reductase) and complex IV (cytc oxidase) of the respiratory chain (Chan & Li, 1990). They are encoded by a nuclear gene and are usually constituted by a 13 kDa single polypeptide chain containing the heme c prosthetic group covalently bound to Cys14 and Cys17 of the polypeptide chain by thioether bonds. Further, the heme‐Fe atom is axially coordinated by His18 and Met80, forming a hexa‐coordinated His18‐Fe‐Met80 complex (Banci et al., 1997; Bushnell et al., 1990).

The main physiological roles of cytc are mitochondrial respiration and apoptosis (Ow et al., 2008; Zhou et al., 2024). Upon cellular stress, cytc can be released into the cytosol, where it interacts with apoptotic peptidase activator 1 to form the apoptosome, initiating caspase‐dependent apoptotic cell death (Liu et al., 1996). Furthermore, cytc can induce chromatin condensation and disrupt nucleosome assembly when translocated to the nucleus (Gonzàlez‐Arzola et al., 2021). Regulation of mitochondrial pathways and processes is crucial for the homeostasis of highly energy consuming post‐mitotic cells, such as neurons and retinal ganglion cells. Accordingly, alterations of cytc activity and biology have been linked to the onset and progression of neurodegenerative processes of the brain and the retina, including Alzheimer's and Parkinson's diseases, as well as glaucoma (Huang et al., 2005; Hung et al., 2021; Ju et al., 2009; Kim et al., 2014; Klettner, 2012; Kowluru, 2005; Lee et al., 2015; Li et al., 2021; Orrenius et al., 2007; Singh et al., 2024).

The role of cytc in apoptosis is closely related to the axial coordination of the heme‐Fe atom, since the cleavage (or the severe weakening) of the Fe‐Met80 bond triggers the release of cytc in the cytoplasm and its interaction with lipid membranes, such as cardiolipin (Ascenzi et al., 2015; Godoy et al., 2009). This event is also occurring upon pH lowering during proton translocation, connected to the cytc function in oxidative phosphorylation, as reported by others (Parui et al., 2019), leading to mitochondrial dysfunction and the severe pathological processes discussed above. Consequently, the investigation of the conformational changes occurring at low pH in cytc has been quite extensively explored, allowing characterization of a series of structural changes involving the stability of the Fe‐Met80 bond as well as the role of additional residues in the neighborhood of the metal center (Battistuzzi et al., 2012; Ciaccio et al., 2017; Droghetti et al., 2006; Fedurco et al., 2004; Fedurco et al., 2005; Goto et al., 1993; Hamada et al., 1996; Indiani et al., 2000; Kuroda et al., 1992; Marmorino et al., 1998; Varhač & Antalìk, 2004).

These investigations have been undertaken either on the ferric or the ferrous derivatives of cytc (cytc‐Fe(III) and cytc‐Fe(II), respectively) employing buffers of different ionic strength, which have been shown to affect dramatically the conformational changes and the stability of cytc (Indiani et al., 2000; Marmorino et al., 1998). In several cases the addition of urea induces the formation of alternative structural arrangements (Fedurco et al., 2004; Fedurco et al., 2005; Varhač & Antalìk, 2004).

To highlight pH‐dependent conformational changes of cytc, we have carried out a spectroscopic study by electronic absorption, circular dichroism (CD), and resonance Raman (RR) on both oxidation states of horse heart cytc (i.e., hhcytc‐Fe(III) and hhcytc‐Fe(II)) to determine pK as of various acid–base equilibria accompanying the acidification of the environment. The investigation is relevant also from the physiological viewpoint, since cytc may experience pH lowering of its environment down to pH 3.9 (Parui et al., 2019); thus, the extension to pH ≈ 0 indeed is demanded in order to try to fully characterize the proton‐linked transition. Two main pieces of information are coming out from this investigation, namely (i) a dramatic weakening of the Fe‐Met80 bond is observed upon pH lowering, which is characterized by a pK a ≈ 2.5 for hhcytc‐Fe(III) and ≈ 3.0 for hhcytc‐Fe(II), indicating that the oxidation state of the heme‐Fe atom scarcely affects this interaction, and (ii) the strength of the His18‐Fe proximal bond seems to depend on the oxidation state of the heme‐Fe atom, with a pK a ≈ 0.2 for its cleavage in the hhcytc‐Fe(III) species, while this bond appears still intact in hhcytc‐Fe(II) even at pH ≈ 0.

RESULTS AND DISCUSSION

Here, the effect of pH on the spectroscopic properties of hhcytc‐Fe(III) and hhcytc‐Fe(II) is reported; the investigation has been carried out in 1 M phosphate buffer at all pH values in order to limit ionic strength effects, which are known to increase the tertiary stability of hhcytc at the lowest pH values (Goto et al., 1993; Indiani et al., 2000; Kuroda et al., 1992). Electronic absorption spectroscopy, CD, and RR highlighted the proton‐linked variation of the heme surroundings and the heme‐Fe atom coordination. In the case of hhcytc‐Fe(II), a parallel investigation has been carried out in the presence of either CO or NO to detect their effect on the coordination of the trans ligand (i.e., Fe‐S of the distal Fe‐Met80 bond and Nε‐Fe of the proximal His18‐Fe bond).

Hhcytc‐Fe(III)

At pH 7.0, hhcytc‐Fe(III) displays an electronic absorption spectrum characterized by a Soret band centered at 410 nm and a maximum at 529 nm in the visible region, with a shoulder at about 558 nm. In addition, a charge transfer (CT) band is observable at 695 nm, being referable to the Fe(III)‐Met80 bond (Figure 1) (Schejter & Plotkin, 1988). Further, the CD spectrum in the Soret region shows a negative ellipticity with a peak at about 415 nm and a positive ellipticity at about 404 nm (Figure 2A). These spectra are typical of a 6‐coordinated (6c) low‐spin (LS) species with the Fe atom coordinated to Met80 and His18 (Pielak et al., 1986; Zheng et al., 2000).

Upon lowering the pH from 7.0 to 2.0, the electronic absorption spectroscopic transition can be detected both by (i) electronic absorption spectroscopy in the Soret region, characterized by a blue‐shift to 403 nm with a significant intensity reduction of the CT band at 695 nm (reflecting a marked weakening of the Fe‐Met80 bond) and the appearance of a new CT band at 623 nm (Figure 1), and (ii) CD, highlighted by the disappearance of the negative ellipticity at 415 nm and a slight blue shift to 402 nm of the positive ellipticity, which is also enhanced (Figure 2A).

Further pH lowering, down to pH 0.1, brings about the formation of an electronic absorption spectrum characterized by: (i) the blue‐shift of the Soret band to 399 nm, (ii) the formation of a second peak absorption in the visible region at 501 nm, and (iii) the enhancement of the CT band at 625 nm together with the almost complete disappearance of the CT band at 695 nm (Figure 1). Moreover, the rising of a positive ellipticity shoulder was observed in CD spectra at 415 nm, accompanied by a progressive decrease of positive ellipticity at 402 nm (Figure 2B). This spectroscopic transition is characterized by an isosbestic point at 409 nm, and it goes along with the blue‐shift to 399 nm, detected by absorption spectroscopy (Figures 1 and 2B). This indicates that below pH 2.0 the rigid chiral environment around the heme is progressively loosened, accompanied by a relevant decrease of ellipticity. These data are consistent with a previous investigation at low pH (Indiani et al., 2000), even though some differences for the ionic strength might be responsible for slight variations in the pH‐dependence of the transitions, as already reported by us and others (Goto et al., 1990; Goto et al., 1993; Indiani et al., 2000; Kuroda et al., 1992). An important contribution to the interpretation of the pH dependence comes from the RR spectra (Figure 4), which turned out to be very useful for the comprehension of different equilibria present in hhcytc‐Fe(III) (Indiani et al., 2000; Milazzo et al., 2018). Upon lowering the pH, a monotonic decrease of the native 6cLS form is observed. At pH 2.0, the spectrum is similar to that previously obtained at the same pH in the presence of 1.5M NaCl (Indiani et al., 2000; Jordan et al., 1995; Milazzo et al., 2018) with a mixture of 6cLS (with the ν 3 band at 1504 cm−1) and 6cHS (with the ν 3 band at 1481 cm−1), followed by the formation of a 5cHS species at pH 0.2 (with the ν 3 band at 1494 cm−1), which further increases at pH 0.1. Interestingly, at this very low pH, both the 6cLS and 6cHS species are still observed, although very weak.

Resonance Raman spectra in the low (left panel) and high (right panel) wavenumber regions of hhcytc‐Fe(III) at different pH values. RR experimental conditions: 404.8 nm excitation wavelength with a 3600 grooves/mm grating; average of six spectra with 30 min integration time.

The spectra in the low wavenumber region of hhcytc‐Fe(III) are particularly crowded since, in addition to the heme‐peripheral substituent modes, out‐of‐plane porphyrin deformation modes involving methine wagging, pyrrole folding, and pyrrole swiveling coordinate are activated because of the heme distortion (Hu et al., 1993). The wavenumbers of bands in this region provide information on the nature of the heme‐Fe ligands, as well as the conformation and orientation of the peripheral substituents (Hildebrandt, 1990; Milazzo et al., 2018). Between pH 7.0 and 2.0, changes are observed in both the wavenumbers and intensities of the bands between 300 and 450 cm−1. At pH 2.0, the spectrum is similar to that previously obtained at the same pH in the presence of 1.5M NaCl (Indiani et al., 2000; Jordan et al., 1995; Milazzo et al., 2018), as already observed in the high wavenumber region. In particular, the ν(Fe‐N) ν 8(A1g) and the δ(CβCaS) modes shift down to 344 and 396 cm−1, respectively, while the ν(Fe‐N) ν 50(Eu) shifts up to 364 cm−1. The δ(CβCcCd) propionyl bending modes result in a broad intense band centered at 385 cm−1, while the δ(CβCaCb) vibrations give rise to a broad band centered at 415 cm−1 as a consequence of the intensity decrease of the band at 419 cm−1. However, no further changes are observed at lower pH.

Based on spectroscopic results, the first proton‐linked process (characterized by the blue‐shift to 402 nm of the electronic absorption, the appearance of a CT band at 625 nm and the disappearance of the negative ellipticity in CD spectra) (Figures 1 and 2) is likely referable to the cleavage (or severe weakening) of the Fe‐Met80 bond. This transition is characterized by pK a = 2.35 ± 0.25, as for electronic absorption data, and by pK a = 2.72 ± 0.32, as for CD spectroscopy (Table 1), and it corresponds to a similar pH‐linked effect, reported by Varhač and Antalìk (2004), displaying a pK a = 2.38 ± 0.02. The interpretation of this phenomenon is supported by RR spectra (Figure 4) and is possibly related to the coexistence of a mixture of 6cHS and 6cLS species, containing the His18 and a water molecule as axial ligands. This partially folded state (A‐state (Goto et al., 1990) or state II (Milazzo et al., 2018)) may correspond to the molten globule conformation. The very weak CT band at 695 nm at pH 2.0 (Figure 1) also suggests the presence of a very minor contribution from the native of 6cLS species.

The more acidic process, showing pK a = 0.15 ± 0.04, as for electronic absorption, and pK a = 0.23 ± 0.05, as for CD spectroscopy (Figure 3 and Table 1) may correspond to the cleavage of the proximal His18‐Fe bond with the formation of a 5cHS planar heme (as indicated by the loss of the CD signal, see Figure 2B). As suggested by the relative intensity of the ν 3 band at 1494 cm−1 with respect to the ν 4 band at 1372 cm−1 (Figure 4, right panel), characteristic of a weak axial ligand, a water molecule is bound to the heme iron, in agreement with the previous conclusion (Indiani et al., 2000; Jordan et al., 1995). Therefore, even though a pure 4c species is not detected, indeed we very likely observed the cleavage of the His18‐Fe(III) proximal bond with a pK a ≈ 0.19 ± 0.06, envisaging a bond strength of about 36.9 kJ/mol (Section 3), with the formation of a heme‐Fe(III)‐H2O species.

HHcytc‐Fe(II), Fe(II)‐CO and Fe(II)‐NO

Unlike hhcytc‐Fe(III), the RR spectra of hhcytc‐Fe(II) show that the same coordination and spin state of the heme‐Fe(II) atom are maintained over the whole pH range down to pH = 0.1; the 6cLS species is characterized by core size marker bands (ν 4 = 1362 cm−1, ν 3 = 1491 cm−1, ν 11 = 1546 cm−1, ν 2 = 1592 cm−1, and ν 10 = 1621 cm−1). At pH 7.0 this form is assigned to a His18‐Fe(II)‐Met80 coordination, even though some differences are observed in the bending modes of the peripheral substituents at acidic pH. Upon excitation on the blue side of the Soret band (404.8 nm excitation; Figure 5B, left), in the low wavenumber region, an intensity decrease of the δ(CβCaCb) propio at 413 cm−1 is observed over the whole investigated pH range, as compared to pH 7.0. Upon excitation on the red side of the Soret band (441.8 nm excitation; Figure 5C), an overall weakening of the spectrum on going from neutral to very acid pH is observed. The same changes have been observed between native hhcytc‐Fe(II) in solution and its form adsorbed on a silver electrode at negative potentials by surface enhanced RR spectroscopy (Hildebrandt & Stockburher, 1989).

Therefore, although a pH‐dependent conformational change can be detected, no clear evidence for a cleavage of the Fe‐Met80 bond is observed. However, a change in the strength of the axial ligands at acid pH is clearly evidenced by CO binding to hhcytc‐Fe(II), while at neutral pH CO does not bind the heme, likely because of a strong Fe(II)‐Met80 bond. The spectrum of hhcytc‐Fe(II)‐CO is identical between pH 2.0 and 0.1, being characterized by a Soret band at 414 nm and a band at 532 nm in the visible region with a pronounced shoulder at about 559 nm (Figure 6, left). A residual contribution from hhcytc‐Fe(II) is still observed due to the presence of a small shoulder at 550 nm.

In the hhcytc‐Fe(II)‐CO RR spectra the intense new band observed at 495 cm−1 is assigned to the ν(FeC) stretching mode in the pH range from 0.1 to 2.0. The high photolability of the hhcytc‐Fe(II)‐CO species forced us to use a very low laser power (0.1 mW at the sample), thus we were unable to detect the corresponding ν(CO) stretching mode. This latter vibration could be in principle observed by infrared spectroscopy, but unfortunately standard CaF2 windows are not able to withstand pH values lower than 4. However, we note that the ν(FeC) wavenumber is very close to that observed at 491 cm−1 for hhcytc‐Fe(II)‐CO at neutral pH in the presence of cardiolipin (Milazzo et al., 2017). In that case, both the ν(FeC) and ν(CO) (1966 cm−1) were identified, and based on their wavenumbers it was concluded that CO probed an open heme pocket, characterized by an upright conformation with no polar distal interactions (Milazzo et al., 2017).

The CD spectra of hhcytc‐Fe(II) display a definite pH dependence over the pH 6.5–1.9 range (Figure 7A). At pH 6.5, a main positive ellipticity around 423 nm and a small additional “bump” at 409 nm are observed, a pattern corresponding to previously reported data in the literature (Fedurco et al., 2004; Myer, 1970). Lowering the pH is accompanied by the increase of positive ellipticity at both wavelengths, which is somewhat more marked at 409 nm than at 423 nm (Figure 7A). This enhancement of the positive ellipticity is completed at about pH 2.0; further pH lowering displays a marked decrease of the positive ellipticity at 423 nm with a slight blue shift of the peak ellipticity. The decrease is much less evident for the ellipticity at 409 nm, which becomes a shoulder at pH 0.6. At pH <0.5, only a broad low ellipticity with a blue shift to about 404 nm is observed (Figure 7B). This ellipticity is likely going to disappear at pH <0 (which is the lowest pH value measurable), suggesting no ellipticity for the final form.

Although at high ionic strength and in the presence of sodium dithionite, it is very difficult to record CD spectra in the near‐ultraviolet region between 190 and 280 nm, previous data indicate that hhcytc‐Fe(III) does not unfold under our conditions (i.e., 1.0 M phosphate buffer), keeping the α‐helical arrangement (Indiani et al., 2000). Therefore, these data suggest the occurrence of (at least) two pH‐dependent transitions, which can be described by the ellipticity variations as a function of pH at the two peak wavelengths, namely 423 and 409 nm (Figure 7). The pH‐dependence of the CD signal has been analyzed according to Equation (1), as for hhcytc‐Fe(III) (Figure 4). The analysis of data has been carried out freely, only imposing that the final ellipticity after the second pH‐dependent transition was 0 at both wavelengths (Figure 7C). Data analysis according to parameters reported in Table 2 indicates that pK a values of both transitions are essentially independent of the observation wavelengths within the error limits. Taking the average of values obtained at the two wavelengths, it comes out that the first proton‐linked process, characterized by an increase of ellipticity, displays pK a1 = 2.95 ± 0.45 and pK a2 = 0.45 ± 0.1 (Table 2).

The pK a1 value of hhcytc‐Fe(II) is closely similar to that of hhcytc‐Fe(III) (Tables 1 and 2), suggesting that the proton‐linked transitions could refer to the same process, which appears to be not significantly affected by the oxidation state of the heme‐Fe atom. As already hinted above, it likely refers to the severe weakening of the distal Fe‐Met80 bond, an interpretation which is strongly supported by the evidence that at pH 2.0 CO binds to hhcytc‐Fe(II). Since CO binding takes place as a consequence of weakening of the Fe(II)‐Met80 bond occurring at low pH, we may expect that upon lowering pH, the absorption spectra of the hhcytc‐Fe(II)‐CO species display the progressive formation of the ferrous carbonylated derivative with a pK a value similar to that of the pH‐dependent weakening of the Fe(II)‐Met80 bond. Figure 8A,B shows respectively: (i) absorption spectra of the hhcytc‐Fe(II)‐CO species in the visible region, and (ii) the pH‐dependence of the ratio for the absorption values at 550 nm (i.e., the peak wavelength of hhcytc‐Fe(II)‐Met80) and 521 nm (i.e., the peak wavelength of hhcytc‐Fe(II)‐CO). The pK a value of 3.32 ± 0.35, obtained from the analysis of data shown in Figure 8B, is indeed closely similar to that obtained by CD (Figure 7C and Table 2), being only slightly higher since CO facilitates the substitution of the Fe(II)‐Met80 bond.

A similar pH‐dependent behavior occurs in the presence of NO. Thus, while at pH 7.1 the CD spectra of hhcytc‐Fe(II) in the absence and in the presence of NO are closely similar (Figures 7A and 9A), lowering pH to 5.7 induces only a very small variation (Figure 9A). Further pH lowering to 3.9 (a pH at which the distal Fe‐Met80 bond is cleaved by less than 10%; Figure 7C) is enough to induce the formation of a large fraction of hhcytc‐Fe(II)‐NO with a peak ellipticity at 410 nm (Figure 9A). This suggests that the presence of NO, which competes with Met80 for the distal axial coordination site of the heme‐Fe(II) atom, brings about a shift of the equilibrium from the Fe(II)‐Met80 species (which is over 90% of the population in hhcytc‐Fe(II) in the absence of NO) to the Fe(II)‐NO derivative (which becomes >90% of population in the presence of NO), as also detected by EPR and electronic absorption spectroscopy over the same pH range (De Simone et al., 2023). This indicates that the weakening of the Fe‐Met80 bond in the hhcytc‐Fe(II)‐NO derivative starts at higher pH values and the apparent pK a depends on the relative affinity for the replacing ligand, NO binding being much stronger than that of CO. Further pH lowering is characterized by a progressive splitting of the ellipticity with a pH‐dependent decrease of ellipticity at 410 nm and rising of the ellipticity at 421 nm with an apparent isosbestic point at 417 nm (Figure 9B). In this respect, it is very important to remark that an environmental pH 3.9 can be detected physiologically under specific conditions (Parui et al., 2019); therefore, present data indicate that the formation of hhcytc‐Fe(II)‐NO is a likely event under these conditions with an almost complete displacement of the Fe‐Met80 bond.

Below pH 2.4 a progressive decrease of ellipticity of hhcytc‐Fe(II)‐NO takes place, being accompanied by a blue shift from 421 to 418 nm with a shoulder at about 400 nm (Figure 9C). The pH dependence of the ellipticity changes at 410 and 423 nm has been analyzed according to Equation (1) with i = 3; pK a values are given in Table 3 (Figure 9D). Three proton‐linked processes have been observed for hhcytc‐Fe(II)‐NO, namely (i) the first transition with pK a1 = 4.65 ± 0.58 is referable to the displacement of the Fe‐Met80 by NO as the distal axial ligand, (ii) the second process, characterized by pK a2 = 3.78 ± 0.52, with a negative ellipticity change at 410 nm and a positive ellipticity change at 423 nm (Figure 9B,D), possibly reflects the protonation of a residue in the immediate proximity of the heme, which affects the Fe‐NO geometry and the heme conformation, and (iii) the third event with pK a3 = 1.82 ± 0.27 is characterized by a progressive ellipticity reduction. The most acidic process likely corresponds to the protonation of the proximal His18‐Fe bond with the formation of a 5c heme‐Fe(II)‐NO species; in fact, the pK a3 value closely corresponds to that observed for the cleavage of the proximal bond heme‐Fe(II)‐NO of hhcytc‐Fe(II)‐NO by EPR spectroscopy (pK a = 1.8 ± 0.1) (De Simone et al., 2023).

CONCLUDING REMARKS

Hhcytc shows a remarkable overall stability of the tertiary structure under our conditions of high ionic strength (i.e., 1.0 M phosphate buffer), such that even at very low pH values it keeps an overall native‐like folding (Indiani et al., 2000). It guarantees that our proton‐linked events are referring to processes mostly concerning the axial coordination of the heme and/or protonation of residues in the immediate surroundings of the heme. This seems confirmed by the evidence that in our conditions all pH‐dependent processes are characterized by a single‐proton transition, unlike for cases where pH lowering was accompanied by protein (at least) partial denaturation (Goto et al., 1993; Kuroda et al., 1992).

However, structural changes take place as the pH is lowered below pH 4.5. The first less‐acidic process is characterized by a similar pK a value for both hhcytc‐Fe(III) (≈2.7) and hhcytc‐Fe(II) (≈2.9) species (Tables 1 and 2). Remarkably, this transition can be observed in hhcytc‐Fe(III) by electronic absorption, CD, and RR spectroscopy, while for hhcytc‐Fe(II) the same process shows a distinct signal only by CD spectroscopy. This proton‐linked structural change(s) is unraveled by electronic absorption and CD spectroscopy in either hhcytc‐Fe(II)‐CO or hhcytc‐Fe(II)‐NO species (Figures 8 and 9). This pH‐dependent transition probably refers to the cleavage or the severe weakening of the distal axial Fe‐Met80 coordination bond, as suggested by the occurrence of exogenous ligand binding to the heme‐Fe(II) atom at acidic pH; in contrast, heme‐Fe(II) ligation does not occur at neutral pH. Previous observations by ultrafast X‐ray spectroscopy suggest that this weakening is associated with a relevant alteration of the H‐bond network between the –OH group of Tyr 87 and residues Asn52 and Thr78, which stabilized the Fe‐Met80 (Mara et al., 2017). This interpretation is also supported by the evidence that partial unfolding of hhcytc‐Fe(II) leads to the appearance of the 5c species (with His18 as the unique axial ligand) without any misligated hexa‐coordination (Bhuyan & Udgaonkar, 2001; Droghetti et al., 2006; Fedurco et al., 2004). The relevance of the H‐bond network in controlling the stability of the Fe‐Met80 bond is also supported by the similarity of pK a values for hhcytc‐Fe(III) and hhcytc‐Fe(II). As a consequence, the overall conformation of the distal side of the heme pocket appears to control the redox properties of hhcytc and the stability of the Fe–S bond; thus, Fe(II) nitrosylation occurs much more efficiently when pH lowering loosens the H‐bond network, bringing about a weakening of the Fe–S bond (Figure 9A) (Ascenzi et al., 1994). Moreover, the large similarity observed between the RR spectra of hhcytc‐Fe(II) at acidic pH reported here and its adsorbed form obtained at negative potentials on electrodes, which may be regarded as a model to mimic the electrostatic interactions occurring in redox processes at cellular membranes, further supports the possible physiological role of these pH‐dependent conformations (Hildebrandt & Stockburher, 1989). Although no unequivocal structural interpretation is available at the moment for the second pH‐dependent transition, observed both in hhcytc‐Fe(III) (Figure 2A,B and Table 1) and hhcytc‐Fe(II)‐NO (Figure 9A,B and Table 3), this process may likely reflect a conformational change(s), such as the formation of a molten globule (Goto et al., 1990; Kuroda et al., 1992), which also affects the heme proximity, leading to the cleavage of the proximal His18‐Fe bond.

Conversely, the second proton‐linked process, observed by CD in hhcytc‐Fe(II), displaying a pK a2 = 0.45 ± 0.1 (Table 2), cannot be attributed to the cleavage of the His18‐Fe(II) bond, which appears still intact even at pH ≈ 0, since it turns out to be still mostly hexa‐coordinated, being possibly referable instead to a proton‐linked structural change that allows the heme coordination by a different ligand, as hinted by kinetic studies on the hhcytc folding at low pH (Chan et al., 1997; Takahashi et al., 1997). In any case, it suggests that in hhcytc‐Fe(II) the proximal bond energy is higher than 40 kJ/mol, and thus much stronger than in hhcytc‐Fe(III), where a value of 36.9 kJ/mol has been observed.

Thus, in the case of ferrous Aplysia limacina myoglobin, a pK a value of 2.98 has been established for the proximal bond cleavage, allowing us to calculate a free energy associated with the bond in the unliganded ferrous form of about 21.4 kJ/mol (Coletta et al., 1985) and about 11.7 kJ/mol for the ferric form; therefore, a pK a ≈ 4.7 is reported (Giacometti et al., 1981). Hence, in hhcytc‐Fe(III) the free energy of the proximal bond turns out to be about 25 kJ/mol higher than in ferric Aplysia limacina myoglobin, and also in the ferrous unliganded form, the free energy associated with the proximal His‐Fe bond is over 20 kJ/mol higher in hhcytc‐Fe(II) than in Aplysia limacina myoglobin. Likewise, in the hhcyt‐Fe(II)‐NO the proximal His18‐Fe bond is weaker than in the unliganded form, resulting of about 27.9 kJ/mol (De Simone et al., 2023), clearly indicating that NO bound at the distal trans position weakens the proximal His‐Fe(II) bond; a similar phenomenon is also reported in the literature for Aplysia limacina myoglobin with a pK a = 4.7, corresponding to the His‐Fe bond free energy of 11.7 kJ/mol (Ascenzi et al., 1981). Therefore, from this evaluation, it comes out that in the case of hhcytc‐Fe(II)‐NO the free energy of the proximal bond is about 16 kJ/mol higher than in NO‐bound Aplysia limacina myoglobin, confirming that the higher bond free energy of hhcytc is maintained also in the liganded form.

This feature underlies a dramatically different structure–function relationship between the oxidation state of the heme‐Fe atom and the free energy of the proximal bond in cytochromes and myoglobins (Gray & Winkler, 2010; Marcus & Sutin, 1985). This remarkable acid pH stability of the tertiary structure and of links between heme c and the protein moiety in hhcytc may have important consequences also on the functional role of cytc, especially considering the oxidative stress due to mitochondrial dysfunction observed in several neuropathological diseases, such as Alzheimer's disease, glaucoma, and retinal degeneration (Klettner, 2012; Kowluru, 2005; Orrenius et al., 2007). Conformational changes of cytc occurring after the exposure to large variations of proton concentration in the proximity of mitochondrial membranes (Parui et al., 2019) might represent an additional element in triggering the unbalance responsible for the mitochondrial malfunctioning, thus representing a relevant pathogenetic element. In this regard, although the extreme acidic conditions here tested might be unlikely to occur in living cells, it is worth pointing out that due to its intracellular localization cytc is involved in proton translocation, thus transiently experiencing microenvironments characterized by very acidic pH values, such as during apoptotic processes.

Therefore, while the extreme pH values here adopted have been experimentally set up to fully disclose the conformational changes of cytc, it cannot be aprioristically excluded that biochemical properties of cytc at very low pH values (i.e., pH ≤ 3) may be considered for a better comprehension at a molecular level of intracellular patho‐physiological processes.

In conclusion, this study represents a further step in understanding the role of pH variations in ligand binding, conformational stability, and physiological role of cytc. To achieve these results, we adopted a consolidated model system for studying electron transfer and protein folding processes along the last 50 years, suggesting that the diversity of the functional properties of cytochrome c is linked to its conformational plasticity (Schweitzer‐Stenner et al., 2009). Moreover, three‐dimensional structures and functional analysis, also by site‐directed mutagenesis, of key residues which contribute significantly to the structure, function, folding, and stability are reported for cytc from other eukaryotic species (Zaidi et al., 2014). Up to now, the hhcytc remains undoubtedly a unique case among cytc proteins that offers an optimal characterization at the molecular spectroscopy level and as a model for Fe‐heme ligand binding interactions.

MATERIALS AND METHODS

Sample preparation

Hhcytc‐Fe(III) was purchased from Merck (St. Louis, MO, USA) and used without further purification. A concentrated solution of hhcytc‐Fe(III) (usually 1 mM) was prepared by dissolving the heme‐protein in H2O and was then diluted to 1M potassium phosphate buffer at the desired pH value, adjusted with phosphoric acid. The final pH value was measured before and after the spectra collection. Hhcytc‐Fe(II) was obtained by adding a few grains of sodium dithionite to the concentrated solution of hhcytc‐Fe(III); then, the hhcytc‐Fe(II) solution was diluted into the desired buffer, previously deoxygenated either by an extensive N2 flux or by degassing it in a tonometer, to which an atmosphere of N2 was established. Hhcytc‐Fe(II)‐CO was prepared by mixing hhcytc‐Fe(II) in sodium dithionite with buffer at the desired pH value equilibrated with 1 atm of CO in a tonometer; the equilibration time was 10 min, and a check was undertaken at a longer time to ensure that no further spectroscopic changes were taking place. Hhcytc‐Fe(II)‐NO was prepared by mixing 3 mL of hhcytc‐Fe(II) in sodium dithionite at the desired pH value with 50 μL of a 100 mM sodium nitrite (final [NO] ≈ 1.0 mM); the equilibration time was 10 min, and a check was undertaken at a longer time to ensure that no further spectroscopic changes were taking place. For electronic absorption and CD observations, the cuvette, extensively flushed with N2, was filled without leaving a gas phase. For RR measurements, the ferric samples at the desired pH value were reduced with a freshly prepared sodium dithionite solution (10 mg/mL), after degassing with N2 or CO.

Electronic absorption measurements

The electronic absorption spectra were obtained using a Cary60 spectrophotometer (Agilent, Santa Clara, CA, USA) with a resolution of 1.5 nm and a 300 nm/min scan rate. The measurements were performed in a 1 cm cuvette or a 5 mm NMR tube at room temperature.

The second derivative spectra were calculated by applying the Savitzky–Golay method on 15 data points with a third‐order polynomial (LabCalc; Galactic Industries, Salem, NH). No changes in the wavelength or in the bandwidth were observed when the number of points was increased or decreased. All the spectra were normalized to the maximum intensity of the Soret band, and the intensity in the visible region was further magnified, as indicated in the figures for clear visualization.

Resonance Raman measurements

RR spectra were obtained at room temperature by exciting with the 404.8 nm line of a diode laser (MatchBox Series, Integrated Optics, Vilnius, Lithuania) and the 441.6 nm excitation of a He‐Cd laser (Kimmon IK4121R‐G, Tokyo, Japan); all RR spectra were obtained in a slowly rotating 5 mm NMR tube at room temperature. Backscattered light was focused into a triple spectrometer (ActonResearch, Acton, MA), consisting of two SpectraPro 2300i instruments that work in subtractive mode, and a SpectraPro 2500i instrument in the final stage with a 3600 grooves/mm or a 1800 grooves/mm grating with a nominal resolution of 1 or 4 cm−1, respectively. The spectrometer is equipped with a liquid nitrogen‐cooled CCD detector (Roper Scientific Princeton Instruments, Trenton, NJ, USA). Only for the Fe(II)‐CO adducts, a cylindrical lens was used to focus the laser beam on the sample to limit the photolysis.

The RR spectra wavenumbers were calibrated to an accuracy of 1 cm−1 for intense isolated bands using indene, carbon tetrachloride, DMSO, and acetonitrile as standards. The power on the sample was 10 mW using the 404.8 nm excitation wavelength for the hhcytc‐Fe(III) and hhcytc‐Fe(II) samples, 6 mW using the 441.6 nm excitation wavelength for the hhcytc‐Fe(II) samples, and 0.1 mW for the hhcytc‐Fe(II)‐CO adduct. To improve the signal‐to‐noise ratio, the RR spectra were repeated multiple times and averaged only when no spectral differences were observed. The RR spectra were baseline‐corrected and normalized in the high wavenumber region to the intensity of the ν 4 band at 1362–1372 cm−1.

Circular dichroism measurements

CD spectra of hhcytc‐Fe(III), hhcytc‐Fe(II), and hhcytc‐Fe(II)‐NO between pH 7.1 and 0.0 were performed in 1M phosphate buffer, adjusting the pH properly with NaOH, at 25°C, and in all cases with a protein concentration of 30 μM using a spectropolarimeter Jasco J‐1500 (Easton, MD, USA). The setting panel was: slit 2 nm, sensitivity 20 mdeg, resolution 0.5 nm, and range values according to the Soret region 370–450 nm using a quartz cuvette of 0.5 cm light path. The analyses of CD spectra were performed using MatLab software (Natick, MA, USA).
