Assessing the Role of Military Units in Epidemic Response: A Scoping Review of Key Issues
Authors: Ioannis Tsagarliotis, Nikolaos P Rachaniotis
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractIntroductionMilitary forces have long supported epidemic and pandemic response, offering rapid mobilization, logistics, and operational capacity in challenging environments. Their roles—from building treatment centers and transporting supplies to enforcing quarantines—were especially visible during the 2014-2016 Ebola outbreak and the COVID-19 pandemic. Despite abundant case-specific literature, no broad synthesis has examined operational contributions, ethical dilemmas, coordination mechanisms, and long-term effects on health systems.MethodsFollowing Reporting Items for Systematic Reviews and Meta-Analyses extension for Scoping Reviews (PRISMA-ScR) guidelines, we conducted a scoping review across PubMed, Scopus, Journal Storage (JSTOR), the WHO Library (WHOLIS), Military Health System publications and Google Scholar, including studies up to December 2024. Eligible sources described military involvement in epidemic or pandemic responses and included peer-reviewed articles, reports, and case studies. Two reviewers independently screened and selected studies, extracting data on context, epidemic type, military role, coordination, and outcomes. Thematic analysis grouped findings into 4 domains: logistics and rapid deployment, ethical considerations and human rights, civil–military coordination, and long-term implications for health systems and governance.ResultsOf 5,000 records identified, 100 met inclusion criteria. Most addressed Ebola or COVID-19, with geographic coverage spanning West Africa, Southeast Asia, and high-income countries. Militaries excelled in logistics—airlifting supplies, building facilities, and restoring disrupted supply chains. Ethical tensions arose in enforcement roles, where measures sometimes undermined public trust. Coordination quality varied, with integrated command structures improving efficiency. Long-term impacts were inconsistently reported, with benefits such as infrastructure transfer alongside risks of civilian dependency.Discussion and RecommendationsPre-established civil–military frameworks, ethical safeguards and community engagement are key to maximizing benefits and mitigating risks. Military roles should be framed as surge capacity with planned transition to civilian control.ConclusionsMilitary forces are valuable epidemic partners but require strategic integration, ethical oversight and focus on strengthening civilian systems. Standardized evaluation frameworks are needed to measure short- and long-term impacts.

INTRODUCTION

Military forces have consistently played a significant role in responding to epidemics and pandemics throughout history.1–11 Their ability to mobilize quickly, deploy personnel across challenging terrains, and establish temporary infrastructure has made them indispensable in many public health emergencies.12–22 From enforcing quarantines to transporting critical supplies and supporting medical interventions, military involvement has become an increasingly expected feature of national and international epidemic response plans.

Historically, this involvement can be traced as far back as the 14th century, when militaries were used to enforce cordon sanitaires during the Black Death in Europe. In the 20th century, military operations were deeply intertwined with the 1918 Spanish influenza pandemic,23–29 where troops in World War I contributed both to the spread and response to the disease. During that time, military hospitals were central to treatment efforts and knowledge of disease prevention within armed forces played a significant role in mitigating further mortality. However, lessons about overcrowding, inadequate sanitation, and lack of coordination with civilian sectors also emerged from that era.

In more recent decades, the role of military forces has evolved beyond traditional logistics30 and enforcement quarantine.31–33 During the 2014-2016 Ebola outbreak in West Africa, militaries—particularly from the United States, United Kingdom, and African Union—built treatment units, assisted with surveillance, and supported logistics in highly unstable regions.34–42 In the case of COVID-19, nearly every nation deployed military assets at some point to reinforce strained civilian capacities. Activities ranged from building temporary hospitals, deploying mobile testing units, and helping distribute vaccines to enforcing lockdowns and curfews.43 This scale and diversity of involvement highlight the importance of analyzing military engagement not just through a tactical lens, but also through ethical, governance, and public trust perspectives.44–46

Despite this growing reliance on military forces, there has been no comprehensive synthesis of the literature examining the range of their contributions, the recurring challenges in civil-military cooperation, and the long-term policy implications. Much of the existing evidence remains fragmented, focused on single case studies, or situated in disciplinary silos (military medicine, public health, disaster response). A scoping review can help bridge these gaps by systematically mapping the existing evidence, identifying recurring themes, and outlining future research needs. This review focuses on the operational, ethical, and strategic aspects of military involvement in epidemic response, drawing from multiple epidemic events and countries to provide a broad overview of current knowledge and policy lessons.

METHODS

Study Design

This review was designed and conducted as a scoping review, guided by the Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Reviews and Meta-Analyses extension for Scoping Reviews (PRISMA-ScR) framework.47 Scoping reviews are particularly suited to mapping the breadth of evidence in complex or interdisciplinary topics where heterogeneity in study types, outcomes, and methodologies is expected. In this review, our goal was to examine the scope, nature, and thematic trends in the literature on military involvement in epidemic and pandemic response, rather than to assess the quality of evidence or conduct a meta-analysis.

Research Questions

The review was structured around the following guiding questions: (1) What are the operational roles that military forces have played in epidemic and pandemic responses across different contexts? (2) What ethical, organizational and public trust issues have emerged from military involvement in epidemic response? (3) How has civil–military collaboration been managed and what barriers or enablers are commonly reported? (4) What are the documented or theorized long-term implications of military involvement in public health emergencies?

These questions reflect an effort to move beyond technical or tactical descriptions of military involvement and instead capture broader policy and system-level insights.

Eligibility Criteria

We included articles, reports and case studies that met the following criteria: Population: Military forces (national, international, or multinational) involved in epidemic or pandemic response; Concept: Contributions to epidemic management, including logistics, medical support, quarantine enforcement, coordination or planning; Context: Any geographic region or epidemic (e.g., Ebola, H1N1, COVID-19, SARS, Spanish Flu); Types of Sources: Peer-reviewed journal articles, official reports, organizational case studies and grey literature (e.g., WHO publications, military health policy documents); ­Language: English and Greek; Date Range: No restriction on publication year, up to December 2024.

We excluded news articles and documents that focused solely on military internal readiness without reference to public health collaboration or epidemic involvement.

Information Sources and Search Strategy

Searches were conducted across PubMed, Scopus, and JSTOR, as well as selected grey literature databases including the World Health Organization Library (WHOLIS), Military Health System (MHS) publications, and Google Scholar for government or non-governmental reports.

The search strategy used Boolean logic combining terms such as: “military” OR “armed forces” OR “defense” AND “epidemic” OR “pandemic” OR “outbreak” AND “public health” OR “response” OR “collaboration” OR “logistics” OR “quarantine.”

Additional filters were applied to exclude animal studies and unrelated disaster management topics (e.g., earthquakes, floods).

Selection of Sources

After de-duplication, all articles were screened by title and abstract. Two reviewers independently assessed the remaining articles for full-text eligibility. Disagreements were resolved through discussion or referral to a third reviewer. The screening process followed a structured flow outlined in the PRISMA-ScR diagram (see Figure 1).

Reporting items for systematic reviews and meta-analyses extension for scoping reviews flow diagram.

Of approximately 5,000 initial results, 100 sources were included after full-text review. Excluded studies were recorded with reasons, such as lack of focus on military engagement or irrelevance to public health.

Data Charting and Extraction

Data extraction was performed using a standardized charting form, capturing the following variables: Author(s) (year, country/region), Epidemic/pandemic type, Military role (logistics, enforcement, medical support, and coordination), Civil-military dynamics, Reported outcomes/limitations, and Ethical ­concerns/critiques (if present).

Synthesis of Results

Extracted data were coded using thematic analysis, allowing for the identification of recurring themes across diverse sources. These themes were inductively grouped into 4 major domains: (1) Logistics and rapid deployment, (2) Ethical considerations and human rights, (3) Civil–military coordination, and (4) Long-term implications for health systems and governance.

Thematic Domains

Logistics and Rapid Deployment

Across both Ebola34–42 and COVID-193,15,29,43,48–55 responses, militaries demonstrated a unique capacity to deliver rapid operational support. This included airlift of medical supplies, construction of treatment facilities and deployment of mobile laboratories and restoration of supply chains disrupted by the outbreak. The U.S. military’s rapid establishment of Ebola Treatment Units in Liberia and the Italian army’s conversion of exhibition halls into COVID-19 field hospitals, are illustrative examples of this capacity.

Ethical Considerations and Human Rights

Eighteen studies discussed ethical dilemmas56–65 surrounding military participation in epidemic control, particularly in enforcement roles such as lockdowns and quarantines. During the Ebola outbreak, the presence of armed personnel at health checkpoints in Sierra Leone and Liberia drew mixed reactions–some communities viewed it as necessary for safety, although others perceived it as coercive. Similar tensions arose during COVID-19, where military enforcement of movement restrictions sometimes fueled public resistance.

Civil–Military Coordination

Successful epidemic response was frequently linked to early integration of military and civilian health structures.66–74 In some cases, such as the UK’s military support to Sierra Leone during Ebola, coordination mechanisms were embedded within the national emergency operations center, enabling effective joint planning. Conversely, fragmented command structures in certain COVID-19 responses led to duplication of effort and inconsistent messaging between health and security sectors.

Long-Term Implications for Health Systems and Governance

Although many reports documented immediate operational successes, fewer addressed the sustainability of military contributions beyond the crisis. Only few of studies provided outcome metrics or follow-up assessments. The literature highlights both opportunities–such as infrastructure75,76 left for civilian use post-deployment–and risks, including over-reliance on military assets in fragile health systems, which may hinder long-term civilian capacity building.77,78

Evidence Gaps

There remains a lack of standardized reporting frameworks for evaluating military-led epidemic interventions.14,42,66 Data on cost-effectiveness, community perceptions over time and the impact of military participation on governance and health ­system resilience are particularly scarce.30,51,58

DISCUSSION AND RECOMMENDATIONS

Logistics and Rapid Deployment

The most consistently reported strength of military involvement lies in their capacity for rapid mobilization and operational scale.1,4,30 In Liberia during Ebola, military engineering units built treatment centers within days, complemented by airlift operations that sustained remote supply chains.43–45 Similarly, in COVID-19 responses across Europe and Asia, military units converted arenas and warehouses into functioning hospitals, distributed millions of test kits and deployed mobile laboratories.54,56,79 These contributions align with prior reviews that highlight military logistical advantages in time-critical operations, particularly where civilian capacity is insufficient.46,66 However, the literature also notes variability in efficiency depending on pre-existing civil–military agreements and resource availability.48

Ethical Considerations and Human Rights

The ethical dimensions of military engagement remain complex and context-dependent.57,59 Ebola-era interventions, such as armed checkpoints in West Africa, sometimes enhanced security for health workers but also risked alienating affected communities.41,45 In COVID-19, enforcement of lockdowns by armed personnel in certain countries sparked debates about proportionality, necessity, and the optics of militarizing public health.48,50,53 Few studies offered concrete ethical frameworks for balancing public health goals with civil liberties, suggesting a critical gap for policymakers and operational planners.57 The lack of such frameworks increases the risk of public mistrust, which can undermine epidemic control efforts.58

Civil–Military Coordination

Effective epidemic response depends on seamless integration between military and civilian systems.80–91 The Ebola response in Sierra Leone provides an example of effective alignment, where military medical teams worked under the direction of a unified national emergency operations center.34,38,39 Conversely, COVID-19 experiences in some settings revealed fragmented command structures and conflicting communication,48,50 which hampered efficiency. The literature indicates that pre-crisis joint planning, including simulation exercises92 and interoperable communication systems, is essential for mitigating these challenges. Without such integration, military and civilian actors risk working at cross purposes despite shared objectives.93–96

Long-Term Implications for Health Systems and Governance

Although short-term operational impacts are well documented, the long-term consequences of military involvement receive less attention.97–99 Infrastructure built during Ebola often remained in place for civilian use,42 but few studies assessed whether these assets improved long-term health system resilience. Conversely, there is evidence that over-reliance on military support can disincentivize investment in civilian capacity, especially in low-resource settings. Governance implications are similarly underexplored, particularly the potential normalization of military roles in civilian public health functions, which could alter institutional balance over time. Addressing these gaps will require longitudinal research and standardized evaluation metrics.

Value of the Scoping Review Approach

Although some operational lessons from Ebola and COVID-19 are widely known, this review’s contribution lies in its methodological mapping of the evidence base. By systematically charting the breadth of literature across contexts, we have identified not only recurring operational patterns but also significant evidence gaps. These include a paucity of empirical outcome data, limited ethical guidance, and underdeveloped governance analyses. Such findings provide a clear agenda for future research78 and can inform the design of both military and civilian preparedness frameworks for future health emergencies.

CONCLUSION

This scoping review maps the breadth of literature on the role of military forces in epidemic response, synthesizing evidence across 4 thematic domains: logistics and rapid deployment, ethical considerations and human rights, civil–military coordination, and long-term implications for health systems and governance. The findings reinforce the value of military capabilities in delivering rapid, large-scale operational support, particularly in contexts where civilian capacity is overwhelmed. However, they also highlight persistent challenges related to ethical legitimacy, coordination mechanisms, and the sustainability of military contributions.

From a policy perspective, 3 priority areas emerge:

Institutionalizing Civil–Military Coordination— Establish joint operational frameworks and regular intersectoral exercises before crises occur to ensure interoperability and reduce duplication during emergencies.

Future research should adopt standardized evaluation frameworks to assess both immediate and sustained impacts of military interventions, including cost-effectiveness and governance outcomes. Such work will be essential to translating operational experience into evidence-based doctrine for future public health emergencies.

In sum, although military involvement in epidemic response offers critical advantages, optimizing its contribution requires deliberate integration with civilian systems, proactive ethical planning, and a commitment to strengthening the broader health security architecture.
