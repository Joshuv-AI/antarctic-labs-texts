Genome-scale DNA methylome and transcriptome profiling of midgut of Bombyx mori infected with BmCPV
Authors: Qunnan Qiu, Zhe Liu, Yuqing Huang, Huilin Pang, Liuyang Li, Min Zhu, Xiaolong Hu, Chengliang Gong
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
DNA methylation, as well as histone modifications, is an important regulatory mechanism for altered gene expressions. Our previous study has shown that Bombyx mori cytoplasmic polyhedrosis virus (BmCPV) infection could change the level of trimethylation of lysine 9 of histone 3 (H3K9me3) and acetylation of lysine 9 of histone 3 (H3K9ac), thus regulating the mRNAs expressions in the midgut of silkworm, B. mori. However, the correlation between genome-scale DNA methylome and transcriptome remains underexplored. In this study, whole genome bisulfite sequencing (WGBS) was performed on the midgut of BmCPV-infected silkworms at 48 h and 96 h post infection, and corresponding midguts of uninfected silkworms. Above analysis will contribute to further understanding how BmCPV regulate gene expression through epigenetic modification at the genome-wide level.

Background & Summary

Bombyx mori, belonging to Lepidoptera, is one of the important economic insects. It has made important contributions to agriculture, textile and pharmaceutical industry. However, silkworm diseases cause damage to the growth and development of silkworm, resulting in huge economic losses. Among them, B. mori cytoplasmic polyhedosis virus (BmCPV) is one of the main pathogens. BmCPV is a double stranded RNA virus (dsRNA virus) belonging to the family Reoviridae and mainly infects silkworm midgut cells1,2.

With the development of high-throughput sequencing, some researchers have explored the effects of BmCPV infection on gene expression at the genome-wide level. The microarray analysis has found 285 differentially expressed genes (DEGs) in BmCPV-infected midgut3. And the number of up-regulated genes in the BmCPV-infected midgut increased with the severity of BmCPV infection4. There were significant differences in DEGs between samples with persistent infection and those with pathogenic infection5. Besides, silkworm varieties could have influence on gene expression patterns in midguts of BmCPV-infected silkworm. The number of up-regulated genes in sensitive variety LAN 5 was more than that in resistant variety Ou 17, while the results about down-regulated genes was opposite6. Moreover, BmCPV infection could alter the expression profiles of miRNA7, circRNAs8 and lncRNAs9.

The pioneering opening of chromatin and its accessible state set the stage for initial genome-wide expression in eukaryotes, a process meticulously orchestrated by epigenetic regulatory mechanisms10,11. DNA methylation, the first epigenetic modification discovered in eukaryote, was formed by covalent binding of the Methyl group to 5-carbon of cytosine12–14. DNA methylation usually play vital roles in competitive binding with transcription factors to promoter regions15–19, X chromosome inactivation in female animals20–23, gene reprogramming24–28, targeting repeats in genes29,30 and alternative splicing31,32. Previous studies have found that viral infection also could change DNA methylation levels in the hosts. Epstein-Barr virus (EBV)33, Severe acute respiratory syndrome coronavirus 2 (SARS-CoV-2)34 and hepatitis B virus (HBV)35 could increase DNA methylation levels in hosts’ genomes, further regulating gene transcript. As for silkworm pathogens, B. mori nucleopolyhedrovirus (BmNPV) and BmCPV were found to regulate DNA methylation. It was found that DNA methylation levels positively correlated with BmNPV proliferation36. Inactivation of DNA methyltransferase could induce apoptosis of BmNPV-infected cells, so as to inhibiting virus proliferation36. It was reported that the CpG methylation level in midguts were much higher than that in silk glands in the infected silkworm with BmCPV37. These differentially methylated genes (DMGs) might be involved in RNA transport, RNA degradation, nucleotide excision repair, DNA replication and so on37. However, the association between the DNA methylation level and the infection process of BmCPV remains unclear.

Also, Histone modifications are one of epigenetic modifications that alter gene expression patterns38,39. Our previous study has reported the correlations between the trimethylation of lysine 9 of histone 3 (H3K9me3) / acetylation of lysine 9 of histone 3 (H3K9ac) and gene expressions40. These intersection genes regulated by H3K9me3 were enriched in insect hormone biosynthesis and MAPK signaling, while the intersection genes regulated by H3K9ac were enriched in glycolysis/gluconeogenesis and TGF-beta signaling40. Given that DNA methylation is also closely associated with gene expressions, we aim to employ whole-genome bisulfite sequencing (WGBS) for BmCPV-infected silkworm midguts at 48 h post-infection(hpi) and 96 hpi (CPV48 and CPV96) and corresponding midguts of uninfected silkworms (GUT48 and GUT96) to identify DMGs (Fig. 1). With integration of WGBS and RNA-seq datasets, we screened DEGs regulated by DNA methylation (Fig. 1). Methods

Silkworm strain, BmCPV infection, and Sample collection

Detailed experimental design, BmCPV infection, and sample collection have been described in our previous study40,41. Briefly, silkworms (strain: Dazao) on the first day of the fifth instar were infected with BmCPV. At 48 hpi (Group CPV48) and 96 hpi (Group CPV96), midguts from 8 randomly selected silkworms were pooled, respectively. Midgut tissues from 8 age-matched healthy silkworms without viral infection served as controls (Group GUT48 and Group GUT96). Each group contained two biological replicates. Since BmCPV has specific tropism for the midgut of silkworm, and its infection causes B. mori cytoplasmic polyhedrosis, the midgut was selected for high-throughput sequencing in this study1,2.

WGBS library preparation

Genomic DNA was extracted by phenol-chloroform method, and degradation and contamination were assessed by agarose gels electrophoresis. DNA purity was checked using NanoPhotometer®spectrophotometer (IMPLEN, USA). DNA concentration was measured using Qubit® DNA Assay Kit in Qubit® 2.0 Flurometer (Life Technologies, USA).

After quality assurance, the genomic DNA was fragmented into 200–300 bp with Covaris S220 (30 s/30 s for 10 times). These short sequences were treated with bisulfite using EZ DNA Methylation-GoldTM Kit (Zymo Research, USA) to turn unmethylated cytosine (C) into Uridine (U) and remain methylated cytosine(C) unchanged. DNA libraries were constructed through Adaptase-mediated adapter ligation to single-stranded DNA (ssDNA) fragments, followed by PCR amplification. Library quality was initially assessed by quantification using a Qubit 2.0 at a dilution of 1 ng/μL. The insert size distribution was verified using an Agilent 2100 Bioanalyzer. DNA libraries that met the size requirements were subjected to precise quantification of effective concentration (>2 nM) by qPCR to ensure sequencing-ready quality.

WGBS data analysis

WGBS were performed on an Illumina HiSeq platform (Novogene, China). Read alignments were conducted using bismark software (version 0.16.3, https://www.bioinformatics.bbsrc.ac.uk /projects/bismark/). Then clean reads were obtained after trimming reads with low quality, including reads contaminated with adapters, reads with either end having a base quality score below 3 or containing N nucleotides, reads whose average base quality in the window falls below 15, reads shorter than 36 nucleotides (nt) and reads failing to form proper paired-end pairs.

After trimming, clean reads were mapped to B. mori genome (silkDB3.0, https://silkdb.bioinfotoolkits.net/main/species-info/-1). Uniquely aligned reads were cross-referenced with the native genomic sequence to systematically determine the methylation status of all cytosine loci. The average coverage depths of all cytosine loci across all samples exceeded 7.0 × (Table 1). Furthermore, methylation calling was performed via binomial distribution analysis incorporating three key parameters: methylated cytosine counts (mC), total coverage (mC + unmethylated cytosine umC), and bisulfite non-conversion rate (r). Genomic positions with statistically significant methylation (FDR-adjusted p < 0.05) were designated as methylated loci. For regional methylation quantification, the genome was partitioned into consecutive 10-kb bins using a sliding window approach, and methylation levels for each bin were calculated by quantifying the total number of methylated and unmethylated reads within each genomic interval, defined as follows:\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$${ML}({\rm{C}})=\frac{{reads}({\rm{mC}})\,}{{reads}({\rm{mC}})+{reads}({\rm{um}}{\rm{C}})}$$\end{document}ML(C)=reads(mC)reads(mC)+reads(umC)Where, ML(C) represented methylation level, mC represented the number of methylated C, umC represented the number of unmethylated C.

To Investigate potential effects of BmCPV infection on genome-wide DNA methylation in B. mori, we statistically analyzed context-specific methylation levels of cytosines across functional genomic regions43. As shown in Fig. 3, after merging biological replicates, methylation levels were computed across 20 equally sized bins within each functional region using the formula: mC / (mC + umC). The results were visualized, and no statistical tests were performed for the analyses presented in Fig. 3. Methylation levels in the CG contexts were ranked from highest to lowest as follows: 3′ untranslated region (3′ UTR), exons, 5′ UTR, and introns, while CpG islands (CGIs) and repeated regions displayed lower methylation levels (Fig. 3). Subsequently, differentially methylated regions (DMRs) were identified using the DSS software, which implements a dispersion shrinkage method for estimating dispersion parameter from Gamma-Poisson or Beta-Binomial distributions44–46. Based on the genomic distribution of DMRs, DMR-associated genes were defined as those satisfying either of the following criteria: (1) DMRs located within their transcriptional units (from transcription start site (TSS) to transcription end site (TES)), or (2) DMRs present in proximal promoter regions (defined as the region 2-kb upstream of TSS). A Wald test was performed at each cytosine site, and DMRs were identified based on the p-values. The analysis parameters in DSS software were set as follows: smoothing = TRUE, smoothing.span = 200, delta = 0, p.threshold = 1e-05, minlen = 50, minCG = 3, dis.merge = 100, and pct.sig = 0.5. Specifically, a genomic region was designated as a DMR only if it met all of the following criteria: (1) more than 50% of the CpG sites within the region had a p-value < 1e-05, (2) The region contained at least 3 CpG sites, and (3) the length of the region was at least 50 bp. Data Overview

DNA methylation usually plays vital roles in gene transcription regulation. To explore the relationship between the transcriptional level and DNA methylation levels, we conducted a correlation analysis between DMR-associated genes and DEGs. Correlated genes were defined as those with an absolute value of log2FoldChange of RNA-seq FPKM was greater than 1, and the absolute value of methylation difference of the corresponding DMR was greater than 0.2.

Technical Validation

Quality control

Raw sequencing data were processed to remove low-quality reads, yielding clean reads. FastQC (v0.11.5) was used to assess the quality of clean reads. Over 97% of bases had a Q-score ≥ 20 (sequencing error rate < 1%), and more than 90% of bases had a Q-score ≥ 30 (error rate < 0.1%) (Table 4).These metrics confirmed the high quality of the sequencing data. In addition, bisulfite (BS) conversion efficiency was estimated by calculating the non-conversion rate at unmethylated cytosines across the genome, using the formula: BS conversion = 1 – (Percentage of C Reads / Total Reads). BS conversion rates for cytosines in three sequence contexts (CG, CHG, and CHH) are summarized for each sample in Table 4. As shown, conversion rates exceeded 99% across all samples and contexts, consistent with the typically low genome-wide DNA methylation levels observed in insects. Furthermore, methylated cytosines (mCs) and unmethylated cytosines(umCs) were quantified and summarized in Table 5. The number of umCs is more than that of mCs (Table 5). Histograms of mCs distribution across all samples were generated (Supplementary Fig. 3), which showed that the percent of methylated cytosines at each level was low than 20%. Bismark software (version 0.16.3, https://www.bioinformatics.bbsrc.ac.uk /projects/bismark/) was then used with the parameter -X 700 --dovetail65 to align bisulfite-treated sequencing reads to the B. mori reference genome (SilkDB 3.0; https://silkdb.bioinfotoolkits.net/main/species-info/-1). Both of reference genome and clean reads were transformed into their bisulfite-converted version (C to T, G to A). Subsequently, bisulfite-converted clean reads were aligned to similarly converted reference genome. Uniquely aligned reads, determined by aligning positive and negative strands to the same regions in reference genomes, were used to infer the methylation status of all cytosine positions. Reads that aligned to the same regions were classified as duplicated reads. The proportion of uniquely aligned reads exceed 69% across all samples, with duplicate reads accounting for approximately 30% of the total aligned reads (Table 6), indicating that the data were adequate for downstream analyses66. After excluding duplicate reads, statistical analysis of sequencing depth and coverage was performed to ensure the rigor and reliability of methylation calling. For DMRs validation, BMSK0010150 in the GUT96 vs GUT48 group, BMSK0012101 in the CPV48 vs GUT48 group, BMSK0007114 in the CPV96 vs GUT96 group and BMSK0006886 in the CPV96 vs CPV48 group were randomly selected to be verified by MSP. Candidate promoter region were identified using Integrative Genomics Viewer (IGV), and CpG islands were predicted with stringent criteria (GC content > 50%, observed/expected CpG ratio > 0.6, and length > 100 bp) using MethPrimer software. Specific primers (Table 3) were designed to amplify the derived sequences (100–250 bp in length) from both methylated and unmethylated bisulfite-converted DNA. After treatment, extracted genomic DNA was bisulfite-treated, followed by PCR amplification and Sanger sequencing. As shown in Supplementary Fig. 4A-D, the the MSP validation results were consistent with the those obtained by WGBS.
