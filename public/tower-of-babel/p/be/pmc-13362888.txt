Pneumatosis Intestinalis: Identifying the Need for Surgery for Gas in the Gut Wall and Outcomes

ABSTRACT
AbstractIntroduction:Pneumatosis intestinalis (PI) is a rare (0.03%) pathology defined as the presence of gas within the small or large bowel wall. It was first described by Du Vernoi in autopsy studies in 1730. There are two types of PI: primary (15% cases) with benign and cystic intramural gas and secondary (85% cases) having a linear collection of gas reflecting an underlying pathology. It is usually diagnosed radiologically. Management of PI varies from conservative to emergency surgical intervention.Aims:We report this study to make the clinicians aware about this rare, but important differential diagnosis in patients who present with acute abdominal pain.Materials and Methods:This retrospective observational study includes 10 patients who presented with acute abdominal pain to the emergency department of a tertiary care hospital over a span of 24 months. Patients with radiological diagnosis of PI on contrast-enhanced computed tomography (CECT) abdomen were selected for the study.Results:All patients presented with severe abdominal pain; 60% had abdominal distension, and 40% showed signs of peritonitis and septic shock. Surgical management was needed in 60%, and the rest were treated conservatively with intravenous fluids, antibiotics and analgesics. About 20% patients could not survive despite surgery. All patients managed conservatively survived. Histopathological reports showed gangrenous changes of intestinal walls with vascular occlusion. Patients who have survived came for regular follow-up and does not show any complications.Conclusion:PI, though rare, must be recognized early to improve outcomes. Surgical intervention is necessary in cases with ischaemia on CECT abdomen. They have high mortality rates even after surgical intervention. Patients having long-term onset of symptoms with healthy bowels can be managed conservatively.

Introduction

Pneumatosis intestinalis (PI) is an infrequent clinical finding, primarily reported by Du Vernoi in 1730.[1] It accounts for approximately 0.0.3% cases in the general population.[2] It is characterized by the presence of gas within the intestinal wall, usually in the mucosa land submucosal layers of the anti-mesenteric border. It can be classified into two types: primary PI accounts for 15%–20% cases and secondary PI accounts for 80%–85%[3] of cases. Primary PI is typically a benign and idiopathic condition, whereas secondary forms are often linked to underlying disorders such as bowel ischaemia, necrotizing enterocolitis, chronic pulmonary diseases, inflammatory bowel disease, use of immunosuppressive medications, and connective tissue disorders.[3]

Several theories have been implicated in causation of PI, namely, mechanical, bacterial and pulmonary theory.[3] Mechanical theory states damage of the intestinal mucosa due to an increase in intraluminal pressure. Surgical trauma such as during endoscopies or blunt abdominal trauma may also cause shift of gas into the extraluminal area. The pulmonary theory proposes that alveolar rupture in lung diseases leads to the release of gas into the mesenteric vessels. Bacterial theory states extraluminal invasion of gas-forming bacteria such as Clostridium and Escherichia coli species.[4]

Clinical symptoms may vary; some patients may be asymptomatic, can have symptoms like abdominal pain, nausea, vomiting, diarrhoea, blood in stool, weight loss; or when complicated they may present with features of intestinal obstruction, pneumoperitoneum and peritonitis.[5] It is usually diagnosed radiologically.

The management of PI is determined by the severity of the condition and the presence of any underlying health issues. In some cases, it may present as a “benign pneumoperitoneum” that does not require surgical intervention, and conservative treatment may be sufficient.[6] However, in more severe cases, prompt exploratory laparotomy might be needed. Differentiating between benign and life-threatening forms is critical for proper management and is largely based on clinical evaluation and radiologic findings.

Patient demographic details, comorbidities, examination findings, investigations, treatment modalities and outcomes were entered in the Excel sheet. Data were summarized using descriptive statistics (mean and percentages) and correlations obtained among the variables. All patients were managed in the intensive care unit with intravenous fluids, higher antibiotics, antacids, nasogastric tube insertion and indwelling urinary catheter. Patients with signs and symptoms of peritonitis or radiological evidence of perforation and bowel ischaemia were immediately taken for surgical intervention. Those who were on conservative treatment were monitored 4-hourly in the form of vital parameters and abdominal examination for possible need of surgical intervention.

Patients with CT changes of bowel wall ischaemia, perforation, peritonitis or intramural gas in bowel wall or pneumoperitoneum underwent emergency exploratory laparotomy. Bowel resection and anastomosis or stoma were done on the basis of the viability of the remnant bowel.

In the postoperative period, the patients were monitored for vital parameters, abdominal examination and color of the stoma. Laboratory and radiological investigations were repeated as and when required.

The surviving patients were discharged after clinical and vital stabilization and were regularly followed-up in the outpatient department.

Discussion

PI, a very rare condition (0.03%[2]) characterized as gas filled bowel walls which may be benign or may be severe disease, may lead to mortality. It commonly affects the colon and small intestine.

It affects individuals usually in fourth to sixth decades and has equal preponderance to male and female gender as in our study where most of the patients were aged above 40 years, and males and females were both affected.

The etiology of PI is regarded in various theories. The mechanical theory suggests that increased endoluminal pressure, caused by factors such as intestinal obstruction, inflammatory bowel disease, ischemic bowel disease and bowel preparation for colonoscopy, leads to the migration of gas from the lumen into the gastrointestinal wall. Chronic lung diseases having air-filled bulla which might get ruptured lead to migration of this gas into intestinal walls and mesenteric vasculature. This explains the pulmonary theory implicated in PI. The bacterial theory is formations of cysts in the mucosa of intestine by the gas-producing intestinal bacteria. The nutrition theory states that malnutrition prevents the digestion of carbohydrates, causing an increase in bacterial gas production and consequent submucosal dissection.[7] One of our patients had infrarenal aortic occlusion leading to ischaemia and pneumatosis coli involving the whole of descending colon and a part of sigmoid colon.

Five (50%) patients in our study had COPD, 2 (20%) had previous surgical trauma and immunosuppressant use and 3 (30%) had a history of chronic steroid use.

PI may be asymptomatic in approximately 85%[8] of cases, or it may lead to a variety of symptoms ranging from vague symptoms of generalised abdominal pain, nausea, vomiting, diarrhoea, blood in stool or may present with features of severe disease such as intestinal obstruction, pneumoperitoneum and peritonitis. Four patients in our study exhibited signs of severe peritonitis accompanied by septic shock.

Need of surgery and high mortality rate were seen in such patients presenting with septic shock and peritonitis.

It is usually diagnosed by contrast-enhanced abdominal computed tomography scan (CECT abdomen), which remains the diagnostic modality of choice. CECT abdomen rules out other common pathologies like abdominal Koch’s, which may have similar presentation as PI.[9] CT scan shows intestinal air cysts within the submucosa or the subserosa of the intestine. Colonoscopy may also help diagnose the condition. It is done for milder cases having symptoms like recurrent diarrhoea or blood in stool. It shows grape-like or beaded subepithelial gas cysts.[7] Abdominal radiographs may show air pockets along the walls of intestine or pneumoperitoneum when it is complicated by intestinal perforation. Procalcitonin serves as a valuable biomarker in distinguishing bacterial sepsis in symptomatic patients of PI.[10]

Seven (70%) patients in our study had intramural air foci in the wall of the small intestine suggestive of small intestinal PI, and 3 (30%) had intramural air foci in the wall of the colon suggestive of pneumatosis coli [Figure 1]. Two (20%) patients had evident perforation on CECT abdomen, while three had pneumoperitoneum without confirmed perforation. Bowel ischaemia was present in 6 (60%) patients, while 4 (40%) exhibited thickening of the bowel wall. Features of mesenteric thrombosis were observed in 5 (50%) patients, and 3 (30%) patients showed signs signifying intestinal obstruction.

Complications of PI include intestinal perforation, obstruction, rupture of large air-filled cysts and necrotizing enterocolitis.

Asymptomatic disease does not need any treatment. When symptomatic, treatment options include medical therapy and surgical intervention on the basis of the patient's clinical condition. Conservative therapy includes intravenous fluids, intravenous antibiotics and supportive treatment. There is a theory stating inhalational oxygen or hyperbaric oxygen therapy can decrease the partial pressure of gases other than oxygen in the venous system, thus promoting their diffusion out of the bowel wall by acting as a toxin to anaerobic bacteria in the gut. 2.5 ATM of oxygen 90 min a day hyperbaric oxygen treatment continued till the disappearance of cysts and 2 days thereafter is used to reduce oxygen toxicity in patients and reduces the risk of recurrence.[11] In cases where a patient presents with peritonitis, sepsis, pneumoperitoneum or obstruction, emergency exploratory laparotomy should be performed, and bowel resection is necessary for removal of septic foci. PI has a high mortality rate of approximately 22%–27%.[12]

Four (40%) had features of peritonitis or septic shock at presentation. Six (60%) of the patients studied needed surgical intervention.

Mortality in our study was two out of 10 patients (20%), which is consistent with literature. Both the patients had presented with features of peritonitis and septic shock and had undergone surgery due to the presence of ischaemia and pneumoperitoneum [Figure 2].

Conclusion

PI is a rare condition and should be kept in mind as an important differential of acute abdominal pain. This condition mandates prompt diagnosis and management for favorable outcomes. Emergency exploratory laparotomy and resection of the ischemic bowel should be considered on the basis of the patients’ clinical profile. This condition may be managed conservatively when there is no evidence of bowel ischaemia or peritonitis. These patients should be closely monitored for possible development of complications. Even with prompt diagnosis and treatment, the mortality of PI remains high.
