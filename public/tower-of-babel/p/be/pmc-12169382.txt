Once in a blue moon: coronavirus disease complicates pregnancy in a patient with unknown pulmonary hypertension and subsequent left ventricular dysfunction
Authors: Santiago Giraldo-Ramírez, Jairo A. Gándara-Ricardo, Jesús A. Velasquez-Penagos, Juan P. Flórez-Muñoz, Edison Muñoz-Ortiz
Published: 2023

Introduction

COVID-19 is a viral disease of global distribution. This entity mainly involves the respiratory tract and the severity of the compromise fluctuates from upper respiratory tract infection to acute respiratory distress syndrome. Cardiovascular manifestations are not infrequently described and include asymptomatic biomarkers elevation (cardiac troponin), pulmonary embolism, myocarditis and arrhythmias. Pregnancy is a particular condition that could facilitate the viral infection.

Case report

A 28-year-old woman was admitted with dyspnea, uterine contractions, and clear vaginal discharge during the COVID-19 pandemic. She was 31-week pregnant and had no previous prenatal care. She reported a history of progressive effort dyspnea for 5 years that worsened during pregnancy; however, she had not consulted a physician before. A week before the hospital admission, she and her husband experienced nasal discharge, odynophagia, and general discomfort. The patient had no medical history except for a previously uncomplicated pregnancy 6 years ago.

The patient’s examination was remarkable for tachycardia, tachypnea, fixed split of the second heart sound, and oxygen saturation of 80%. Frequent spontaneous contractions were noted, with vaginal discharge of amniotic fluid. She required oxygen through a high flow nasal cannula and her PaO2/FiO2 was 161. Chest X-ray (CXR) revealed cardiomegaly, a prominent central pulmonary artery, and interstitial ground-glass lung opacities. In addition, the transthoracic echocardiogram revealed right chamber dilatation, right ventricular hypertrophy, and a high probability of pulmonary hypertension (Figs. 1A-C).

D: transesophageal echocardiogram with patent foramen oval, with right to left flow. E and F: magnetic resonance imaging with mild pericardial effusion, with severe dilation of right cavities with flattening of the interventricular septum and hypertrophy of the right ventricle.

Notably, nasopharyngeal swab test for SARS-CoV-2 was positive, and thus, the patient was diagnosed with severe COVID pneumonia. A computed tomography was not performed because of concerns of ionizing radiation effects. She was admitted in the intensive care unit, but mechanical ventilation was not required. Preterm premature rupture of membranes was diagnosed, and the patient started spontaneous labor. The cardio-obstetric unit decided to terminate the pregnancy through cesarean section owing to her condition at 32 weeks with previous use of antenatal steroids for fetal lung maturation. The neonate had an uneventful evolution and his nasal swab was negative for SARS-CoV-2.

The patient was begun on low doses of diuretics for pedal edema, and pulmonary vasodilator therapy with bosentan was initiated. BNP decreased from 1177 pg/mL to 318 pg/mL during the hospital follow-up. Transesophageal echocardiogram and cardiac magnetic resonance (CMR) ruled out significant intracardiac shunt (patent foramen ovale [PFO] was present) and congenital heart anomalies. These studies confirmed right ventricular dysfunction (RVD) with severe right ventricle dilatation (167 mL/m2, reference value 112 mL/m2) and reduced right ventricular ejection fraction (31%, reference value 50%) (Figs. 1D-F). Right cardiac catheterization confirmed pulmonary artery hypertension (PAH; mean pulmonary artery pressure of 43 mmHg, pulmonary capillary wedge pressure of 15 mmHg, and pulmonary vascular resistance of 6 wood units). Furthermore, ventilation-perfusion scan ruled out chronic pulmonary embolism, and oxygen therapy could be dismounted. The patient was discharged home on bosentan.

A month after hospital discharge, the patient presented to the emergency department with worsening dyspnea and orthopnea of 2-week duration. Repeat echocardiography and CMR revealed persistent RVD along with an unexpected finding of left ventricular dysfunction (LVD) with diffuse hypokinesis and left ventricular ejection fraction of 26%. Moreover, there was no late gadolinium enhancement (LGE) to suggest myocarditis, fibrosis, or infarction, and peripartum cardiomyopathy (PPCM) was deemed to be the most likely explanation. Pharmacological treatment was initiated for heart failure with reduced ejection fraction, and at 3-month follow-up, the patient was classified as NYHA class I, with the echocardiography revealing persistent RVD and LVD.

Discussion

To the best of our knowledge, this case is the first report of previous undiagnosed PAH in a pregnant patient with severe pneumonia because of SARS-CoV-2 infection. During the follow-up, she developed LVD probably secondary to PPCM. Despite the patient’s comorbidities and severe condition, she and her baby had an uneventful clinical course.

It is unknown whether pregnancy constitutes a risk factor for SARS-Cov-2 infection. An immunological change during pregnancy toward T helper type 2 immune response could enhance the host susceptibility to viral infections1. A greater predisposition to severe illness has been reported in other viral infections such as influenza, where episodes complicating pregnancy has been associated with higher rates of hospitalization, intensive care unit admission, and death2.

Our patient presented with upper respiratory tract symptoms, but the presence of unjustified severe hypoxemia and CXR infiltrates finally led us to the diagnosis of severe SARS-CoV-2 pneumonia. In a cohort of pregnant patients with COVID-19 from Wuhan, 8% of the cases were classified as severe and no maternal mortality was recorded3. The premature birth rate in this study was 24% and no cases of vertical transmission of SARS-CoV-2 was noted-similar to our report.

We believe that the viral infection could have worsened the pre-existing PAH. Hypoxemia, the inflammatory response caused by SARS-CoV-2, and high rates of pulmonary embolism (up to 24%) have been reported as the key factors causing RVD in patients with COVID-194. Argulian et al.5 found in a retrospective cohort of hospitalized patients with COVID-19, a 31% rate of right ventricle dilatation, and in this study, it was the only factor independently associated with mortality. Although the PFO could have contributed to desaturation, it is difficult to quantify the effect of the right-left shunt on the hypoxemia. We think it improbable for the PFO to be the sole reason for the patient’s deep desaturation and the normalized oxygen levels at discharge support this hypothesis.

The coexistence of pregnancy and PAH is unusual because typically patients with PAH are counseled to avoid pregnancy owing to the dismal prognosis. The estimated maternal mortality during pregnancy in patients with PAH is between 16% and 30%6. Furthermore, our patient unexpectedly developed postpartum LVD without LGE areas suggesting myocarditis or ischemia, and thus, PPCM was diagnosed. The absence of LGE makes SARS-CoV-2 myocarditis an unlikely explanation but we cannot completely rule out the possibility of the viral infection contributing to the LVD. Specially, studies using CMR with T1 and T2 mapping have shown myocardial involvement in 78% of patients recovered from COVID-19 and low rates of myocardial fibrosis (myocardial LGE detectable in only 32% of cases)7.

Conclusion

Pregnant patients are especially susceptible to SARS-Cov-2 infection due to their current exclusion from vaccination programs. In this report, we describe an unusual presentation of a pregnant patient with pre-existent pulmonary hypertension, severe SARS-COV-2 pneumonia, and unexpected postpartum LVD. Despite these adverse prognosis factors, our patient had a good clinical course. We highlight the relevance of cardio-obstetrics units to improve the care and the outcomes of pregnant patients with cardiovascular diseases.
