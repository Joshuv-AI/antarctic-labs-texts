Factors related to supplementation use and self-reported vitamin deficiency in the palestinian society
Authors: Nihal Natour, Sajeda hamadi, Wafaa Najar, Ahmed Abudawwas, Saja Elewi
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractObjectiveDespite the global increase in dietary supplement (DS) usage, no prior study has assessed the prevalence or associated factors of DS use in Palestine. This study aimed to determine the prevalence of DS use and its related variables among our (Palestinians) populations in the West Bank using an electronic survey which was distributed via social media platforms, collecting data on demographic characteristics, obesity, chronic disease history, and nutritional deficiencies.ResultsThe total sample was (n = 271). 39.9% used supplements. DS use is more common among older adults, particularly females, employed individuals, and those with chronic illnesses, obesity, or anemia. Red meat consumption was significantly higher among DS users compared to non-users (p = 0.008), while no significant differences were found in the consumption of other dietary types, except for fish. Furthermore, DS use was not significantly associated with place of residence, geographic region within the Palestine, weight loss intentions, weight satisfaction, education level, or malnutrition.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s13104-026-07759-w.Keywords: Dietary supplements, Vitamin deficiency, Palestinian population, Nutritional health, Socio-demographic factors

Introduction

There is no universally accepted definition of dietary supplements (DS) [1]. DS may contain a single nutrient or a combination of components, including vitamins, minerals, botanicals, amino acids, and other bioactive substances. Common examples include vitamins C, E, B₆, B₁₂, and A, as well as minerals such as magnesium and zinc [2]. Widely used DS also include fish oil, omega-3 fatty acids, and multivitamin–mineral formulations containing calcium, iron, and selenium [3].

Global sales of DS reached approximately USD 128 billion in 2007 [4], however, patterns of use vary considerably across countries. In developed nations, more than 60% of adults report using multivitamin or mineral supplements, with particularly high prevalence among individuals aged 60 years and older [5]. In 2018, consumer spending on vitamins and DS in the United States exceeded USD 30 billion [6] Among adolescents, studies indicate that approximately one-third use DS, and nearly half of these users consume them on a daily basis [7] In the United Kingdom, almost half of the population reports using DS [6] DS use is also common among young children; parents who purchase supplements for themselves often provide them to their infants and toddlers aged 4 to 24 months. Estimates suggest that approximately 19% of infants in the United States receive DS [8].

DS intake varies widely according to demographic and socioeconomic factors, including gender, age, educational level, income, place of residence, and ethnicity [9]. Patterns of use are also shaped by cultural practices, lifestyle behaviors, and dietary habits. Evidence consistently shows that women, older adults, and individuals with higher levels of education are more likely to use DS [10–13]. Supplement users generally exhibit healthier lifestyle and dietary patterns: they are more likely to consume diets rich in fruits and vegetables, engage in regular physical activity, and abstain from smoking [11, 14, 15]. In contrast, non-users of DS tend to follow diets higher in fat and lower in fiber, consume greater amounts of animal fats and alcohol, and have lower intakes of whole grains, fresh vegetables, and legumes [15, 16]. Poor dietary patterns may lead to inadequate vitamin and mineral status, increasing the risk of deficiency-related conditions such as scurvy, beriberi, pellagra, rickets, osteoporosis, and other micronutrient deficiencies [17]. Historically, DS were viewed as a means of correcting nutrient-deficient diets or facilitating the maintenance of overall health [5]. Many individuals use vitamin and mineral supplements to prevent or treat specific nutrient deficiencies [18]. Others consume supplements for purposes such as weight management, increasing energy levels, or compensating for nutritional inadequacies related to medication use or suboptimal dietary intake [19]. DS have also been proposed to reduce the risk of chronic diseases, including cardiovascular disease and cancer, and to support cognitive health [20]. Additional perceived benefits include improvements in skin health [21], as well as support for lung and muscle function [22]. Overall, supplements may help prevent nutrient inadequacies when dietary intake is insufficient [23]. During critical life stages, DS play a particularly important role; for example, pregnant women commonly use prenatal vitamins to reduce the risk of adverse maternal and fetal outcomes [22].

Despite ongoing debate regarding their health benefits, the use of DS continues to increase [24], particularly among students [25]. Studies indicate that the primary motivations for DS use include maintaining overall health [26], increasing energy levels, enhancing muscle mass, and improving physical performance [24]. Factors such as knowledge and awareness of DS, year of study [27], and gender [28] have been shown to influence patterns of use. In addition, students often adopt new dietary behaviors and lifestyle practices influenced by their peers [25] Some students also use dietary supplements to compensate for nutritional inadequacies or to address health concerns associated with poor dietary habits [25].

Despite their widespread use for improving nutrition, promoting health, and preventing diet-related illnesses, dietary supplement (DS) use has been rising globally. Alongside this increase, reports of misuse and adverse effects (AEs) associated with these products have also grown [18] Misuse often results from incorrect dosing, inappropriate duration of use, or manufacturing defects, which can lead to health problems ranging from mild gastrointestinal discomfort to severe, potentially life-threatening conditions [29] Because the effects of DS are dose-dependent and can interact with medications, accurate labeling is essential; however, consistency in labeling is often lacking [30]. Regulatory frameworks vary across countries, making oversight and monitoring a significant public health challenge. Ensuring product safety and transparent reporting of adverse events is therefore critical [31]. Historically, scientific research on dietary supplements was limited, leaving much of the public with insufficient knowledge about these products [32]. Over the past two decades, however, DS use has surged [33], attracting increasing consumer interest [34]. and scientific efforts to better understand their benefits and risks have expanded rapidly [1]. Data on DS use in the Arabian Gulf remain limited, with prevalence rates varying widely. In Qatar, nearly half of university students reported using health supplements (HS), while in the United Arab Emirates (UAE), reported use ranged from 38.7% to 39%, with most users citing maintenance of good health (58%) and ensuring adequate nutrition (43%) as their main reasons [30] In Saudi Arabia, approximately 63% of individuals reported past or current use of DS [35]. Internationally, about 52% of the U.S. population uses supplements [33], while prevalence in Poland is higher, at nearly 78% [36]. In some Asian countries, such as South Korea and Japan, regular DS use among adults is comparatively lower [37].

Research on dietary supplement (DS) use in Palestine remains limited. This study aims to determine the prevalence of DS use and identify factors associated with it, including age, lifestyle, and dietary habits. Although dietary supplements are widely used in many countries, few studies have focused on specific populations such as college students. These students often share similar characteristics and behaviors, which may influence their patterns of supplement use differently from the general population.[40] The study therefore seeks to assess the frequency of DS use in the West Bank, explore the factors associated with their consumption, and understand the motivations behind individuals’ use of dietary supplements.

Methodology

A cross-sectional study design was employed to examine the factors associated with dietary supplement (DS) use and self-reported vitamin deficiencies among the Palestinian population. This design is commonly used to assess the prevalence of health behaviors and their related factors at a specific point in time [38].

Data were collected using a self-administered electronic questionnaire, which was distributed online through various social media platforms, including Facebook groups representing students, professionals, and community organizations. The study population consisted of Palestinian adults residing in the West Bank.

Sampling

Because of corona restrictions and limited budget a convenient sampling method was employed to recruit participants from various regions, as it is practical for population-based online surveys [39]. A cross-sectional design was used to evaluate the trends of nutrition supplements among Palestinians through an electronic data collection tool which was distributed through different social methods that included facebook and professional, social and student facebook groups. The population consisted of all Palestinians living in the West Bank, Gaza, and in Israel. A convenient sampling method was adopted to reach the determined sample size of (n = 271, winter 2021).

Survey tool

The questionnaire which was published before [40] was developed following a literature review of similar studies and was modified to suit the local context [41]. The tool consisted of three main sections: (the questionnaire was added as supplementary file).

Demographic and socioeconomic characteristics, including age, gender, education level, employment status, income, place and region of residence.

Demographic and socioeconomic characteristics, including age, gender, education level, employment status, income, place and region of residence.

Medical and nutritional history, including self-reported data on the presence of chronic diseases, anemia, malnutrition, vitamin D and B12 deficiencies, obesity, and attempts to lose weight.

Medical and nutritional history, including self-reported data on the presence of chronic diseases, anemia, malnutrition, vitamin D and B12 deficiencies, obesity, and attempts to lose weight.

Supplementation practices, including whether the participant uses supplements, the type of supplements used (e.g., vitamins, minerals, omega-3), and frequency of use.

Supplementation practices, including whether the participant uses supplements, the type of supplements used (e.g., vitamins, minerals, omega-3), and frequency of use.

A brief food frequency questionnaire (FFQ) adapted from validated tools [42] was used to assess participants’ dietary intake over the past three days. The FFQ included items related to vegetables, fruits, whole grains, red and processed meats, fats, and fish. Responses were collected using a Likert-type scale and converted into scores representing the frequency of intake.

Ethical considerations

The study followed the ethical principles outlined in the Declaration of Helsinki. Ethical approval was obtained from the An-Najah National University Institutional Review Board (IRB), document number Med. Oct. 2025/29 wat. Participants provided informed consent by voluntarily completing the online questionnaire. The data was confidential, and only the principal investigator had access to this data.

Statistical analysis

Data were entered and analyzed using IBM SPSS version 21. Descriptive statistics (means, standard deviations, frequencies, and percentages) were calculated to summarize the characteristics of the study population.

To explore associations:

Chi-square tests were used for categorical variables (e.g., gender, employment, chronic disease).

Chi-square tests were used for categorical variables (e.g., gender, employment, chronic disease).

Independent sample t-tests were used to compare means (e.g., age, BMI) between supplement users and non-users or between vitamin-deficient and non-deficient groups.

Independent sample t-tests were used to compare means (e.g., age, BMI) between supplement users and non-users or between vitamin-deficient and non-deficient groups.

P-values less than 0.05 were considered statistically significant.

Supplement use and vitamin deficiencies (vitamin D and B12) were analyzed with demographic, clinical, and dietary factors. Graphs and tables were used to illustrate relevant associations and findings.

Discussion

This study provides a comprehensive overview of self-reported supplement use and vitamin deficiencies among Palestinians, taking into account and focusing on demographic, social, and health factors. The results of this study reveal varied and distinct patterns in reports of nutritional supplement use, which highlights the complex interaction between lifestyle, socioeconomic status, and health awareness in this context.

Supplement use patterns

The prevalence of dietary supplement (DS) use among participants in this study was 39.9%, which aligns with global estimates but is slightly lower than the rates reported in countries such as Germany and the United States, where adult prevalence can exceed 50% [43, 44]. In our study, supplement users were more likely to be older, female, employed, anemic, obese, or have chronic diseases. These findings are consistent with previous research, which has shown that women and individuals with current or past health conditions are more likely to use dietary supplements as part of strategies to prevent illness or maintain overall health [45, 46].

Most of this study participants were young and so age was not an important factor in determining use of supplements. Unlike elderly who use supplements because of physiological aging that is associated with decreased absorption and higher chronic disease risk [47]. Unlike youth who use supplements based on advice and for general wellbeing [48]. On the other hand use of supplements is more common among females as they are often more involved in health action due to their exposure to health issues like pregnancy, anemia, osteoporosis. Higher rates of anemia among females may have contributed to higher consumption of supplements [45].

Employment was important factor in supplements use, possibly because employment represents higher education level, better financial status which comes with more exposure to health information and access to resources and ability to use them [45].

Use of supplements among participants with chronic diseases due to higher interaction with health and professionals and concerns over health condition [49]. Use of obese for supplements could be to enhance metabolism, compensate for decrease in nutrients intake that associate lower intake attempts, whereas patients with anemia are more likely to consume supplements to treat their conditions [50].

Vitamin D deficiency determinants

The incidence of vitamin D deficiency was higher in urban areas compared to villages and refugee camps. Factors such as chronic diseases, employment, higher income, anemia, and urban residence were strongly associated with self-reported vitamin D deficiency. Urban lifestyle factors, including reduced sunlight exposure and limited outdoor activity, may contribute to the higher prevalence of deficiency in cities relative to rural areas and camps [51, 52]. Interestingly, higher income and education were also associated with greater reports of deficiency. This pattern has been observed in other studies, where individuals more concerned about their health are more likely to undergo medical testing and thus identify deficiencies [53]. Young adults may be particularly susceptible to vitamin D deficiency due to insufficient sunlight exposure and low intake of vitamin D–rich foods and beverages [54, 55]. The observed association between anemia and chronic diseases aligns with previous research indicating that individuals with digestive disorders, infections, or metabolic conditions often have lower vitamin D levels [56]. Moreover, the statistically significant relationship between vitamin D deficiency and higher consumption of undercooked meats combined with lower intake of whole grains suggests that the overall diet quality of individuals with deficiency may be poorer.

A previous study conducted in Palestine found that women are more susceptible to vitamin D deficiency than men due to various biological and lifestyle factors [57]. Similar findings have been reported in the region, including in Bahrain and Jordan, where women who wear body-covering clothing have reduced sunlight exposure, resulting in higher rates of vitamin D deficiency [58, 59].

Vitamin B12 deficiency determinants

The prevalence of vitamin B12 deficiency was significantly higher among employed individuals, which may reflect greater access to and investment in health screening, as well as among those with higher incomes, individuals who were overweight or obese, and those following specific diets. Similar to vitamin D, vitamin B12 deficiency was more common in urban areas, potentially due to testing biases and better access to healthcare services. These findings are consistent with regional and local studies showing that dietary changes and weight-loss practices, which often limit the intake of animal products, contribute to an increased risk of vitamin B12 deficiency [60, 61].

These dietary patterns are particularly relevant because vitamin B12 is primarily obtained from animal-based foods. The observed reduction in red meat and whole grain consumption, coupled with increased fish intake among individuals with vitamin B12 deficiency, has clinical implications. Although fish contains vitamin B12, some species provide lower amounts than red meat [62].

Furthermore, a study conducted in Gaza found that females with iron deficiency anemia had significantly lower mean serum vitamin B12 levels compared to healthy controls and individuals with microcytic anemia but normal ferritin levels [63].

Public health implications

Comparatively, the highest rates of micronutrients deficiencies is for vitamin D (more than 60% for pregnant women and children), iron (˃ 20% among females and children) and vitamin B12 (27%-82%), with modest deficiency for folate and low deficiency for vitamin A particularly among children and women [64]. It is important to note that in Palestine wheat is fortified by 8 vitamins and 2 minerals including vitamin A, vitamin D, vitamin B12 and iron but there was a call to change fortification policy to further tackle micronutrient deficiency [65].

The situation of micronutrients during the last two years could be different because of limited access to food in West Bank and Gaza due to war circumstances. Particularly in Gaza, limited nutrition and food adequacy have definitely contributed to low micronutrient intake [66]. Cases of malnutrition (including micronutrient deficiencies such as vitamin A, vitamin D, iron )particularly among children have escalated since 2023 due to Gaza war. The food and agricultural organization (FAO) pointed to that in 2023 that over 70% of Gaza’s population faced food insecurity, with many families relying on humanitarian.

aid for basic sustenance [67].

These findings reflect ongoing health transitions in several Middle Eastern countries, highlighting the dual burden of malnutrition and dietary supplement use. No significant differences were observed in food group intake between supplement users and non-users, except for red meat consumption. This discrepancy between supplement use and actual nutritional adequacy may suggest inappropriate or unhealthy supplement practices, or an overreliance on pills instead of improving overall dietary quality. Evidence-based nutrition education should therefore be prioritized in public health interventions, particularly for population groups requiring additional support, such as individuals with obesity, chronic diseases, or those attempting to lose weight.

Furthermore, the strong associations between vitamin D and vitamin B12 deficiencies and anemia underscore the urgent need for integrated nutritional screening within primary healthcare. Addressing these deficiencies requires targeted strategies that improve nutritional health while accounting for gender-specific behaviors and the differences between urban and rural populations in Palestine.

Conclusion

This study highlights significant patterns in DS use and self-reported vitamin deficiencies among some Palestinian groups. Micronutrient deficiencies in Palestine should be readdressed based on access to food and income based on political situation. Screening for micronutrient deficiencies should be provided to populations at risk and vulnerable communities for more reasonable prices or fully covered by international organizations, and groups of population at risk should be provided with supplementation rather than depending on diagnosis to prescribe them. Access to supplementation should be available in clinics and pharmacies beyond checkpoints and their provision should align with food fortification.

Strengths and limitations

This study is among the few to investigate dietary supplement use and self-reported nutrient deficiencies in a large and diverse Palestinian sample. However, several limitations should be noted. The cross-sectional design limits the ability to establish causal relationships, and the reliance on self-reported data may introduce recall or reporting bias. In addition, the accuracy of reported deficiency rates may be affected by the absence of biochemical confirmation.
