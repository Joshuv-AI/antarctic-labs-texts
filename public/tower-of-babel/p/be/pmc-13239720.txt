Identification of Patients for a Community Health Worker Program Using an Artificial Intelligence Algorithm
Authors: Samuel T Savitz, Brendan Broderick, Margaret M Paul, Timethia J Bonner, Jennifer L Ridgeway, Mikaela Kall, Jane W Njeru
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTIntroductionCommunity health workers (CHWs) help patients navigate community resources. CHW programs can improve health outcomes and reduce healthcare utilization, but identifying eligible patients is challenging. We developed an electronic health record (EHR)‐based algorithm trained to predict referrals to a CHW program. Our objective was to evaluate whether an algorithm trained on historical referrals identifies a subgroup of patients that differs from the subgroup identified through questionnaire‐based health‐related social needs (HRSN) screening alone.MethodsThis analysis used data from Mayo Clinic primary care patients in Southeast Minnesota. We developed a gradient boosted time‐to‐event model that incorporated demographics, diagnoses, HRSN questionnaire responses, and HRSNs derived from clinical notes using natural language processing. We compared the characteristics of algorithm‐identified patients with those identified through questionnaires and assessed the variable importance of model features.ResultsThe algorithm had AUC: 0.87 in the training and 0.92 in the hold out set. Patients flagged by the algorithm tended to use interpreter services more commonly (12.6% vs. 1.3%), have a non‐English preferred language (14.1% vs. 2.3%), and have more instances of health literacy documented (5.36 vs. 1.29) compared to patients identified by the HRSN questionnaire. Only 47.0% of patients flagged by the algorithm had responded to the HRSN questionnaire within 2 years. Variable importance was highest for health literacy and financial strain from unstructured notes.ConclusionsThe algorithm is a chart‐review prioritization tool for referral to the CHW program for patients that may overcome challenges to identifying patients with HRSNs, especially when questionnaire data is incomplete.Keywords: artificial intelligence, community health workers, electronic health records, social determinants of health

Introduction

Health systems are increasingly identifying patients with health‐related social needs (HRSNs) and offering navigation services that connect patients to community‐based resources to help address unmet HRSNs [1]. One such approach is community health workers (CHWs), who are members of the community that can help individuals with health education and referrals to government services and community‐based organizations (CBOs). CHWs often have relationships with health systems and receive referrals from them, but CHWs themselves are frequently not employees of the health system and operate independently [2, 3]. CHW programs have been linked to improved health outcomes and reductions in hospitalizations and emergency department visits [4, 5].

Mayo Clinic established a CHW program in 2013 and has found that the program was associated with reductions in healthcare utilization [6]. Identification of patients for referral to the CHW program, including the Mayo Clinic program, relies heavily on social needs questionnaires, but questionnaire data is often incomplete [7, 8]. Further, questionnaire data may be more likely to be missing for patients with a higher prevalence of HRSNs such as racial or ethnic minorities, patients requiring interpreter services, and patients with worse access to primary care [9, 10, 11]. As a result, key sub‐groups may be being missed by current processes, and the CHW program may be underutilized.

In this manuscript, we describe our use of an artificial intelligence (AI) algorithm to identify candidate patients for the CHW program at the Mayo Clinic. The AI algorithm was developed with the goal of identifying patients who may otherwise not be identified for referral to the program. Our objective for this analysis was to evaluate whether an algorithm trained on historical referrals identifies a subgroup of patients that differs from the subgroup identified through questionnaire‐based HRSN screening alone. The results have implications for the way health systems identify patients for navigation services and fully leverage these programs to connect patients to community‐based resources to help address HRSNs.

Methods

Study Design and Setting

This is a descriptive analysis of the development and evaluation of a predictive model developed using the Mayo Clinic electronic health record (EHR) data [12]. The model predictions change over time, and we selected the output as of August 19th, 2024 to illustrate the results. The study was deemed exempt from review by the Mayo Clinic Institutional Review Board. The data is available on reasonable request from the corresponding author.

Population

Patients included in model development were paneled to a primary care clinician at a Mayo Clinic primary care site in Rochester, Owatonna, Albert Lea, and Austin Minnesota between June 1st, 2019 to January 1st, 2023. All of the Family Medicine and Internal Medicine primary care sites are located in Southeast Minnesota. The Rochester clinics serve a higher proportion of urban patients than the other primary care sites. Mayo Clinic contracts with CHWs to receive referrals for primary care patients at these sites.

Outcome

The outcome for training the model was whether a patient had been referred to the CHW program. These prior referrals were generated through existing clinic workflows that relied primarily on needs identified through the Epic Social Drivers of Health questionnaire but could also incorporate needs identified during clinical encounters. The Epic Social Drivers of Health questionnaire includes a standard set of questions that are available for health systems using Epic as their EHR vendor. The outcome is known as a “silver standard” outcome because it is a proxy for the ideal (“gold standard”) outcome [13]. In this case, the “gold standard” outcome would have been whether a patient was a good candidate for the CHW program or had unmet HRSNs as determined by a chart review.

Model Features

The model features were built using two‐year lookbacks of features available directly from the EHR as well as clinical notes and portal messages from the prior 2 years. Details on how these variables were sourced from the EHR appear in Appendix 1. First, we identified responses from the HRSN screening questionnaire, which assesses food insecurity, housing instability, transportation needs, intimate partner violence, and financial insecurity. The questionnaire is assigned to patients annually although there was a high rate of noncompletion (26.9%). The screening questionnaire is only offered in English, and speaking a primary language other than Spanish or English, or using interpreter services, have both been linked to higher rates of missingness [9].

Second, we identified documentation of HRSNs (food insecurity, housing instability, transportation needs, financial insecurity, and health literacy) across all department clinical notes and portal messages from the prior 2 years. HRSNs were identified using rules‐based natural language processing (NLP) that searches clinical notes and portal messages for predefined regular expressions that indicate that a patient is experiencing a particular HRSN [12]. Specifically, we used the rule‐based ConText algorithm. The implementation was conducted using the open source MedTagger tool (see Appendix 2 for more information) from Open Health Natural Language Processing (https://github.com/OHNLP/MedTagger) [14]. Unlike the needs documented through the HRSN screening questionnaire, which is only available for patients who completed the screening, these features are available for all patients as long as they received any care in the two‐year lookback period. We believed it was important to include these HRSN features identified through NLP for the model to perform well at identifying patients who may be overlooked such as those who have not responded to the HRSN questionnaire.

Third, we identified demographics including age, gender, education, marital status, and the geographic area where the patient resides.

Fourth, we identified metadata from the clinical notes about the department for which the note was associated.

Fifth, we identified the presence of medical conditions determined from Hierarchical Condition Category codes [15] using ICD‐10 billing codes from clinical encounters.

Sixth, we identified use of tobacco and use of alcohol, which are also captured through questionnaires. For the documentation of HRSNs in the notes, the department from which the notes originated, and medical conditions we captured how many instances occurred during the lookback period as well as the time since the most recent occurrence. For the HRSN screening, demographics, and substance use domains we used the most recent available response.

Model Training

We created a training set that included cases that were referred to the CHW program and controls that were not referred to the program. We took all 1413 cases from the study period and matched them with a random sample of approximately 10 controls for each case (15 402). We used a case–control approach because it can help the performance of predictive models when there is a large imbalance in the proportion of cases and controls in the base data. Specifically, in our population, only 0.4% of eligible patients received a referral to the CHW program. When there is such a high class imbalance, models tend to predict that everyone belongs to the larger class because the accuracy would be extremely high (99.6% for this analysis) [16]. Additionally, because we were extracting a large amount of data including text from clinical notes we determined that it would be more computationally efficient to use a sub‐sample rather than training the data on the full set of 301 863 patients who met the inclusion and exclusion criteria. We also created a hold‐out validation set of 105 cases and 24 655 controls that represented the true prevalence of cases referred to the CHW in the population, which is important for calculating accurate positive predictive value (PPV) or negative predictive value (NPV). The hold‐out set was made up of cases and controls that were not included in the training data. Ensuring that the hold‐out set is distinct from the training set provides a more accurate assessment of how the model will perform in new or out‐of‐sample data [17].

The model was built using a gradient boosted model—a type of ensemble machine learning method that can automatically include interactions and higher order terms [18]. We modeled referral to the CHW program as a time‐to‐event outcome with time representing days since the patient was paneled in primary care. By modeling the outcome as a time‐to‐event outcome, we accounted for patients who we observed for shorter periods because they were paneled more recently, and we could also incorporate time varying predictors. In machine learning models like gradient boosted models, there are hyperparameters, which are parameters that affect how the model is trained to the data. Rather than arbitrarily selecting a single value for the hyperparameters ahead of time, better model performance can be achieved by learning from the data which values of the hyperparameters lead to better predictive performance [19]. For training the model, we used k‐fold cross‐validation, which involves the data being divided into “k” equally sized splits. The model was trained “k” times with a different split of the data being used as the validation set each time. The results were then averaged across each of the folds, which results in more efficient use of the data and more reliable estimates than doing a single split between a training and validation set [17]. We used nested k‐fold cross‐validation with an inner fold for hyperparameter tuning (Appendix 3) and an outer fold for assessing model performance. We then trained the final model using the full data using the hyperparameters selected through the cross‐validation. Using the final model, we evaluated the performance in the hold‐out set.

Model Evaluation

The significance of this work lies in its potential to identify patients who might otherwise be overlooked in standard questionnaire‐based screening methods. Therefore, in addition to traditional model performance statistics, we also evaluated the characteristics of patients identified by the model as compared to patients identified by the HRSN screening. We assessed model discrimination using the area under the receiver operating characteristic curve (AUC) and concordance. Both AUC and concordance typically range from 0.5 (no better than chance) to 1 (perfect discrimination). Model discrimination refers to the ability of the model to rank individuals with the outcome as having a higher risk than individuals without the outcome. This is important as a primary goal of predictive analytics is to accurately identify individuals with higher risk and to tailor services based on risk categories [20, 21]. To evaluate the characteristics of patients identified by the model, we produced predicted probabilities and categorized patients as having low (percentiles 0–49), medium (percentiles 50–89), high (percentiles 90–98), and very high (99th percentile) predicted probabilities of being a good candidate for the CHW program. We also categorized patients by whether they had a documented need identified in the HRSN questionnaire but were not identified by the algorithm, were identified as high or very high risk by the algorithm but not identified by the questionnaire or were identified by both. We considered the HRSN questionnaire to be the manual screening workflow for identifying patients. We then examined the differences in descriptive patient characteristics to assess whether there were meaningful differences by level of risk and source of identification. To characterize clinical complexity, we also summarized hospitalizations and emergency department visits in the year before August 19, 2024. We also analyzed variable importance, which is a metric for how much each variable contributes to the predictive performance. Specifically, we calculated variable importance as the average ‘gain’ or improvement in model accuracy by including the feature in the model. All analyses were conducted in R (version 4.2.2; R Core Team, 2022) [22] using the xgboost package (version 1.7.5.1) [23].

Results

Model Performance

The model achieved an AUC of 0.87 and concordance of 0.92 in cross‐validation and an AUC of 0.92 and concordance of 0.96 in the hold‐out set. Because historical CHW referrals were rare in the hold‐out set, PPV was low unless higher thresholds were used. At a threshold corresponding to a PPV of 0.05, sensitivity was 0.69 and specificity was 0.94.

HRSN Screening and Identification of Patients

A total of 178 973 patients were included in the analysis of patient characteristics categorized by the model (Figure 1), of whom 73.1% had completed an HRSN screening in the last 2 years. Of these patients, 9.1% had an HRSN documented but were not flagged by the algorithm, 4.3% were flagged by the algorithm but did not have a need documented in the screening, and 1.3% had both a need documented by the screening and were flagged by the algorithm. Among the 26.9% of patients who had not completed a screening in the last 2 years, 11.9% were flagged by the algorithm.

HRSN screening and identification by the screening and the algorithm. Patients were considered flagged by the algorithm if they were high (90–98th percentile) or very high (99th) in terms of their prediction from the model. The documented needs refer to the HRSN questionnaire.

Characteristics by Risk Level

We observed differences in patient characteristics by risk level as defined by the algorithm (Table 1). There was a consistent pattern of a lower proportion of White patients and a higher proportion across all other racial and ethnic categories going from low risk (88.0% White) to very high risk (65.3% White). There were also consistent patterns of an increasing percentage having a primary language other than English or Spanish (low risk 0.9%, very high risk: 17.3%). Patients with higher risk tended to have a lower Charlson comorbidity index 10‐year predicted survival (low risk: 78.8%, very high risk: 51.6%) and more health care utilization as measured by hospitalizations (low: 0.09, very high risk: 0.91) and ED visits (low: 0.22, very high: 2.12) in the prior year. Patients in the high and very high‐risk groups also had consistently higher values for HRSNs as measured by the questionnaire and from the notes.

Characteristics by Source of Identification

We also observed differences in patient characteristics according to how they were identified. Patients identified by the algorithm only were older (50.7 vs. 44.8), less likely to use the patient portal (76.7% vs. 92.8%), and had a lower predicted 10‐year survival (63.2% vs. 81.6%). Additionally, there were large differences for language‐related barriers—patients identified by the algorithm only were more likely to use interpreter services (12.6% vs. 1.3%) and speak a primary language other than English or Spanish (10.5% vs. 1.5%). Less than half of patients identified by the algorithm only (47.0%) had completed the HRSN questionnaire in the past 2 years. Patients identified by both sources had the most instances of HRSNs reported in the notes including health literacy (both: 10.14, questionnaire only: 1.29, algorithm only: 5.36) and the highest ED use in the prior year (both: 2.07, questionnaire only: 0.63, algorithm only: 0.80) (Table 1).

Variable Importance

Variable importance was highest for health literacy and financial strain documented in the clinical notes for both the count and the time since the last documented occurrence (Figure 2 shows variable importance for the top 20 features). After these features, there was a large decrease in importance (0.097 to 0.042) to the next most important feature. Other features that had relatively high importance included being seen in settings including case management, family medicine, community internal medicine, or the emergency department. The only variable in the top 10 of importance that did not come from the notes or the metadata was age.

Discussion

In summary, we found that an AI algorithm to identify patients for referral to a CHW program had excellent model discrimination. The patients identified by the model were a mostly distinct subset from those identified solely using the HRSN questionnaire. Patients identified by the algorithm tended to be older, have a lower predicted 10‐year survival, and were more likely to use interpreter services or speak a language other than English or Spanish. The factors that had the highest variable importance were health literacy and financial insecurity identified from the clinical notes.

The algorithm has started to be applied to identify candidates for the CHW program among select high‐risk populations. Specifically, the algorithm has been run for all patients in the senior services program. There are also efforts underway to apply the algorithm to heart failure patients as part of a larger initiative to improve the management of heart failure in a primary care setting. Although the PPV was low due to the large class imbalance, we have been able to identify cutoffs for the model predictions that achieve sufficiently high PPV. For example, we were able to maintain an adequate sensitivity (0.69) while achieving a PPV of 0.05. At this cutoff, an average of 20 charts would need to be reviewed to be expected to identify an individual who is a good candidate for the CHW program. While that is still a non‐trivial number of charts to review, it is much more efficient than reviewing a random selection of individuals from the entire population. Further, we can prioritize high‐risk populations for screening, such as those identified above.

There have been several previous efforts to use AI to inform social needs screening, referrals, and risk of adverse outcomes [24]. NLP and AI have been used in several studies to identify HRSNs from clinical notes [25, 26, 27]. HRSN data has also been used in algorithms to predict outcomes including in‐hospital mortality [28], health care expenditures [29], and avoidable utilization [30]. Algorithms have also been used in other contexts to predict appropriateness for intervention such as suitability for surgery [31], referral for eye care professionals [32], and specialist referral for low‐back pain [33]. While algorithms have not been extensively applied to prediction problems in the context of referrals to programs that address HRSNs, there is potential benefit from these algorithms addressing uncertainties around which patients are most likely to benefit from these programs. Our results extend this literature by focusing on referral prioritization for a CHW program.

The results of this analysis have implications for the collection of HRSN data and referral to services such as the Mayo Clinic CHW program. First, we found that patients who were flagged by the algorithm but not the screening were much more likely to use interpreter services or speak a language other than English. Currently, the Epic HRSN screening questionnaire is only offered to patients in English, and this is likely a barrier to completion. Patients with limited English proficiency tend to be more likely to have unmet HRSNs [34] and having less complete HRSN data from this population is a major concern. More work is needed to ensure that HRSN screenings are available in languages other than English. Importantly, the CHW program includes interpreters for common languages spoken in the local community and would therefore be well‐suited to addressing HRSNs among patients with language barriers. Second, the algorithm was able to identify patients who would otherwise not have been identified by relying on the HRSN questionnaires alone. Just 47% of the patients who were flagged by the algorithm only had completed the HRSN screening in the last 2 years. Completion of the HRSN screening is low in many health systems [7, 8] due to barriers such as patient privacy concerns and limited time for completing the questionnaires [7]. While more efforts are needed to overcome these barriers and increase screening completion, approaches such as this algorithm appear to work well in the presence of imperfect completion. Third, the algorithm was able to work successfully because it leveraged HRSN data from the clinical notes to improve predictive performance. While this was especially important for patients who did not complete the HRSN questionnaire, it also appeared to provide useful Supporting Information for patients. For example, health literacy is not captured in the questionnaire but was captured through the clinical notes. While not all health systems have CHW programs that they can refer patients to, many have other programs like internal navigator services or systems for physician referrals to CBOs [35].

The use of a proxy outcome has strengths and weaknesses. A key strength is that prior referrals to the CHW program were already being collected in the EHR. In contrast, we would have needed to perform thousands of chart reviews to identify the “gold standard” outcome. We did not have the resources to perform this many chart reviews to develop the model. Another advantage is that the outcome continues to be collected in the EHR. This enables the model to be retrained using updated data on new referrals to the program including those that are informed by previous iterations of the model without having to invest additional time to perform chart reviews. A major limitation of many predictive models is that they are trained on old data that may not reflect changes in health care delivery or how data is captured, which is a phenomenon known as model drift [36, 37]. Therefore, relying solely on data that is collected through the EHR supports the sustainability of the model over time. The main disadvantage of this approach is that it may replicate existing referral patterns and fail to identify patients who may in fact be good candidates for the CHW program, but who have systematically different characteristics. However, our hypothesis was that by including a broad array of model features including HRSNs identified from the clinic notes, that we could identify features that are predictive of past referrals but are also found among patients who had been overlooked for referrals in the past. This hypothesis was supported by the fact that only 0.4% of eligible patients were referred to the program, which suggests that the program was underutilized and that there would be a large group of patients who have similarities to the relatively small proportion of patients who were actually referred. The algorithm did identify many patients who would not have been identified through the HRSN questionnaire, which does suggest that it may identify patients who may have been overlooked through prior manual screening workflows that relied heavily on the HRSN questionnaire.

Generalizability is also a limitation. The model development required data on CHW program referrals, and therefore, the results may not translate to systems that lack similar programs and would not have referral history for model training. Likewise, the findings may not generalize to health systems with dissimilar workflows for HRSN screening and referrals. For example, health systems that have higher completion of the HRSN questionnaire may not see many additional patients identified by the algorithm. In contrast, systems with lower completion may see a much greater number of patients identified. Despite these limitations, the study contributes a practical example of how structured EHR data and note‐derived HRSN features can be combined to support identification of patients for resource‐intensive supportive programs. Future research could extend the model to other health system settings to compare the performance with what was achieved at Mayo Clinic and better characterize the generalizability. Additionally, it is important to evaluate the effect of model‐driven referrals to the CHW program on patient‐ and system‐level outcomes at Mayo Clinic to understand the full impact of the model.

Conclusions

An EHR‐based algorithm trained on historical CHW referral identified a subgroup of patients not well captured by questionnaire‐based HRSN screening alone, especially patients with language‐ or literacy‐related barriers. Patients identified by the algorithm were more likely to use interpreter services, have a non‐English preferred language, and have health literacy documented in the notes. These findings suggest that these patients may not be well served by relying on the HRSN questionnaire for making CHW referrals and that the algorithm is a chart‐review prioritization tool with value in identifying patients for referrals who may otherwise be overlooked.
