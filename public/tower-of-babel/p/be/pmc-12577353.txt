Exploring pleasure as a mediator of outcomes in music therapy
Authors: Lucy Elizabeth Bolger, Kirsten Bryony Hillman, Katrina Skewes McFerran
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
BackgroundThe experience of pleasure in music therapy has been underexplored. This paper examines the possibility that pleasure might mediate the effectiveness of music therapy interventions, particularly for individuals experiencing anhedonia (diminished ability to experience pleasure). We theorise this proposition by exploring music therapy processes and findings from two case studies involving people with anhedonia.MethodsAn interpretive case study approach was employed to generate explanations for observed outcomes. Following ethics approval, three participants meeting clinical criteria for anhedonia were recruited. Over four months, they engaged in tailored daily independent music activities focused on eliciting pleasure, supported by a music therapist. Multiple data sources were collected, including standardized anhedonia measures, daily ratings, interviews, and therapist observations.ResultsAll three participants showed improvements in anhedonia scores, with two moving into the normal range by study completion. Qualitative data revealed the importance of: 1) accessible and intentional pleasure-focused music activities; 2) therapeutic alliance providing guidance, psychoeducation and motivational support; and 3) participants' existing relationship with music as a historical source of pleasure. Specific therapeutic principles that facilitated outcomes are outlined.ConclusionsFindings suggest musical pleasure may be an important mediator of positive outcomes in music therapy, particularly for anhedonia. By overtly targeting pleasure through tailored music activities and therapeutic support, participants reconnected with musical pleasure, which generalised to improved hedonic capacity. Interacting dimensions of pleasure-focused musicking, the pleasure experience, and therapeutic scaffolding are proposed as key mechanisms. Implications for music therapy practice and future research are discussed.

The experience of pleasure in music therapy has potentially been underestimated. This paper examines the possibility that pleasure might be a key mediator that explains why music therapy helps, sometimes. We begin to theorise this proposition, abductively exploring the music therapy process and findings from two case studies of people who have anhedonia – the loss of pleasure in everyday experiences. By examining how a particular music therapy intervention can support the reestablishment of musical pleasure, we have the opportunity to critically consider perspectives from neuroscience and music psychology that focus on how music activates the limbic system. We also highlight the importance of the therapeutic relationship in both motivating and sustaining this rewarding possibility. By outlining the key principles that seem necessary to the successful outcomes, we highlight the interaction of music and relationship with a focus on attending to pleasurable experiences.

Background

Music is one of the great pleasures of life according to some studies of happiness [36]. For example, when asked to rate what bought a group of successful white Americans the most joy in their lives, music was consistently noted [16]. However, hedonistic experiences have been consistently under-valued and trivialised. Even in the field of positive psychology, Seligman was careful to depict flourishing as more than hedonism, emphasising meaning and engagement in equal measure, and almost apologising for associated experiences of joy [69]. This has been mirrored in the discipline of music therapy, where a focus on enhancing positive experiences has seldom been rated as important as reducing negative experiences, with some exceptions.

Music therapy and pleasure

The most explicit discussion of pleasure in music therapy was contributed by Brynjulf Stige [74] who searched the literature for reflections on pleasure, claiming that it was an underdeveloped notion. Although there were references to joy and pleasure as fundamental features of music therapy in the writings of early pioneers such as Juliette Alvin and Paul Nordoff and Clive Robbins, the references then reduced significantly, resulting in a ‘relatively meagre’ number of accounts ([74], p.43). This is despite historically significant emphases on pleasure, such as Epicurus (341–270 BC) and much later, Freud. Whilst Freud’s Pleasure Principle presents a dire view of pleasure as conflicting with the usual ‘state of things’ ([74], p.24), Abraham Maslow eventually argued for the value of pleasure, joy and happiness, seeing them as an inevitable part of the much sought after experience of self-actualisation. Ultimately, Stige argues for considering pleasure as a sensitising notion that might be a transformative aspect of music therapy processes ([74], p.50). We agree with this proposition, but suggest a more fundamental possibility, that it is a mediator between the music therapy experience and outcomes.

Despite Stige’s suggestion that references to pleasure are frequently buried when compared to working with pain and suffering, they do exist, and the Norwegian music therapy community have played an important role in this excavation of pleasure. Randi Rolvsjord’s Resource Oriented Music Therapy [59] intentionally focused on the pleasure that mental health consumers experience in their own music preferences, with subsequent studies also illuminating references to fun and how it feels good [60]. Hans Petter Solli has pursued this idea under her supervision and described music therapy as the ‘opposite of treatment’ [73], with participants describing music therapy as “fun, pure and simple” (p.77) with one contributor even suggesting that the psychosis became more pleasurable, and a young rapper describing inspiration and energy [73]. More recently, Guro Klyve [28] privileged the ‘importance of fun’ in her thesis on music therapy for children at an inpatient unit in mental health care. This is congruent with our own research, where fun has been a noteworthy facet in work with bereaved adolescents [40], after natural disasters [43], in school programs exploring gender [68] and in the context of psychological distress in early intervention [42], as well as in community based rehabilitation [9].

Although pleasure has not been referenced specifically, there are ample references to enjoyment in the music therapy literature that examine participant perspectives through analysis of interview data. For example, in dementia care where participants find the songwriting process enjoyable [5], or singing together is something they enjoy [32], both for the singing itself and the socialising that accompanies it [75]. Enjoyment is also referenced in a number of adult mental health studies, where people describe feeling good following music therapy [2] or loving playing instruments expressively [7] and having fun [13]. Some people describe this as feeling more alive and energized [33] and getting energy [34] while others use language such as feeling great [38, 71], heavenly [37], lifting their spirits [70], exciting [52], or being in a happy place [80]. The same types of ideas can be seen with adolescents who describe music therapy as playful and fun [30], or enjoyable [50] and feeling happy [45], with fun being a recurring theme. The choice of words vary, but fun and enjoyment are certainly present in the descriptions of music therapy consumers, even if they aren’t emphasised by practitioners.

Despite ongoing references to enjoyment, happiness and fun, there has not been specific research in music therapy that is focused on the transdiagnostic symptom of anhedonia, apart from our previous case study [10]. This is significant since anhedonia is associated with a wide range of chronic illnesses including chronic pain [22], Parkinson’s disease [3, 48], as well as mental illness [47, 57, 78, 82]. Some research exists that includes music-based interventions, for example in schizophrenia [46] and depression [11], and using musical stimulation [20], but these either do not focus specifically on anhedonia, or use music stimuli and processes that are not tailored to the individual. Music therapy utilises the therapeutic relationship to tailor interventions to meet specific needs of each individual in regard to music selection, but also the degree of scaffolding required to overcome motivational barriers, safety for recognising the other issues that may be related to the broader diagnosis, and psychoeducational support for understanding how music can be used as a resource beyond the end of treatment. Whilst there are a number of studies of musical anhedonia in music psychology [6], this is a very specific form of anhedonia that is not as broadly associated with chronic illness, and is not the focus of this study. We propose that the ability of the music therapy intervention below to address anhedonic symptoms is important for people with a range of chronic disorders, and additionally points to a new way of understanding mechanisms of action in music therapy, potentially positioning it as a mediator of successful outcomes. This research contributes to that argument, but further investigation is also needed.

Music psychology and pleasure

Beyond music therapy, the field of music psychology has been more persistent in investigating pleasure and less consumed with the task of reducing people’s anguish, such is the freedom of being an academic discipline that does not have to provide evidence of benefit for its existence. However, this has also led to challenges, such as Pinker’s [54] contention that music is an evolutionary equivalent of cheesecake, serving no function other than tickling the hedonic senses. This has been addressed with a raft of evolutionary arguments about the necessity of music for parent-infant bonding [19], being a powerful form of interpersonal affiliation [49] or arguing for its adaptive functions through the lens of biology [26]. Once again, challenges to pleasurable experiences with music lead to arguments for extrinsic psychological or biological benefits, rather than delving in to the intrinsic value of musical pleasure itself. This means that attention has skipped over the role of musical pleasure as a potential mediator, and focused further downstream, to the outputs in other arenas.

Considering the subjective nature of the phenomenon, it is perhaps surprisingly that a great deal of focus in research has been on the objectifiable dimensions of the phenomenon via brain studies, rather than holistic and ecological understandings [63]. For example, the capacity of musical pleasure to activate the limbic system has been well documented with a body of literature exploring emotional responses to music through brain studies following from the original discovery that peak emotional responses to music were associated with increased blood flow in the brain’s reward system [8]. Zatorre has been a strong advocate for understanding the neural interactions that give rise to musical pleasure [65] as well as trying to understand why some people do not seem to experience as much pleasure from music [83]. Subsequently, many music psychologists have tried to distinguish categories of musical pleasure, such as Reybrouck and Eeorla’s [56] classification of objective acoustic features, possible modulating factors, and real-time responses by the listener. Powell, Olsen and Thompson [55] distinguish between eudaimonic and hedonic constituents, and Saarikallio, Maksimainen and Randall [64] focus on the emotional-motivational dimensions. Although pleasure and emotion are usually distanced from one another, with pleasure being described as a sensation rather than an emotion or a mood, the two can and have been associated [23].

In addition to structural brain imaging, there has also been a long-standing interest in the role of music to trigger the release of neurochemicals [44]. Dopamine is particularly implicated in research of pleasure, and researchers have investigated the experience of ‘chills’ induced by music as one focus in the field [51]. However, it is thought that dopamine regulates motivation and goal directed behaviours, helping to anticipate future rewards rather than functioning directly as a pleasure neurochemical [67]. This finding has been consolidated and it appears that there is an interaction between the dopaminergic system and the opioid system in mediating pleasurable responses to music [35]. Beyond brain studies, self-report has also been utilised in a wide range of studies [84] including Gabrielson’s [21] renowned work on strong experiences with music. However, Juslin’s [27] contributions to the field remain the most highly cited and influential European scholarship, as are the studies of Levitin’s group in Canada [14] resulting in a strong emphasis on mechanistic understandings.

Between addressing the objectivist emphasis of music psychologists, feeling too superficial for the serious work of music therapists, and being a taken-for-granted assumption of music lovers, musical pleasure has charted an awkward path in scholarship. We agree with Ruud’s [62] contention that the intrinsic and subjective value of musical pleasure needs to be understood “broadly and contextually” and have so far used a series of three case studies (two described below, and one previously published [10] to examine the phenomenon incorporating observations, interviews, and numerical rating scales. We extend Stige’s [74] proposition that pleasure might be a sensitising notion in music therapy and focus on the affordances of musical pleasure as a mediator that might explain specifically why music therapy works. We use two interpreted case studies to examine the possibility that music therapists should focus more on evoking and strengthening a connection to musical pleasure to ensure benefits, at least in the context of anhedonic symptoms (as described below). We hope to highlight how the mediation process might work by exploring the ways that we focused on musical pleasure and how that resulted in increased pleasure generally.

Methods

Following an initial case study of one person with anhedonia [10], we were interested to see if it was possible to replicate the specific process developed with other people who tested within the clinical range for anhedonia – defined as “a diminished ability to experience pleasure from positive stimuli, or a degradation in the recollection of past pleasure” (ICD-11). Anhedonia is associated with related features of apathy, social withdrawal, and a lack of motivation [77]. According to a review of environmental contributions to anhedonia [24], one way of describing it is as a core feature of psychopathological conditions that have recent exposure to stress and trauma as central to their etiology. Clinically, it is a symptom of most mental and chronic health conditions and is associated with lower treatment adherence and diminished treatment outcomes [18].

We adopted a case study approach for this investigation because we wanted to examine a clearly defined bounded system using multiple data sources [17]. Our interest was twofold. First, we were theoretically interested in exploring the possibility that pleasure might be a fundamental mediator of impact in music therapy by examining this phenomenon with people whose subjective experience of pleasure had been lost due to anhedonia. Second, by carefully replicating the procedure from the previous published case study with two further cases, we could work towards developing a hypothesis about who this music therapy intervention would benefit by identifying overlapping characteristics between cases [76]. To this end we were interested in both quantitative data to measure change and qualitative data to explore the nuance of those characteristics.

Rather than working inductively to see whether what emerged was consistent with the first case, or deductively to test our tentative hypothesis, we chose to use an abductive logic to empirically generate reasons for the consequences we had observed – the successful treatment of the symptom in one prior case. Abductive analysis has been described as the “cultivation of anomalous and surprising empirical findings against a background of multiple existing sociological theories and through systematic methodological analysis” [76]. For example, we were genuinely surprised when our first application of this approach was successful and sustained. However, the process was not free of theory, it was targeted and based on our best understanding of the available evidence about interventions (namely Sandmann and Craske’s (2022) study, theory about music and the brain related to anhedonia, and music therapy theory about goal oriented and tailored music interventions grounded in music preferences). In addition, from the moment of conception, we had played with the possibility that pleasure may be more broadly important in music therapy than specifically in the treatment of anhedonia, since it resonated with our research and practice in the field as noted above. However, like grounded theory, we wanted to meticulously ground our construction of possibilities in data to generate explanations [15]. Hence we chose to analyse the data with an intent to theoretically explore why the music therapy intervention was successful in the previous case study, rather than focusing on whether it was successful again (deductive) or on encouraging novel findings to emerge (inductive).

In interpretive case study research, theoretical perspectives can be used as a directive tool in the selection of data and interpretations [4]. Our interest in abductive analysis led us to collect different types of data that might help understand the process and address the questions of what was helpful (which therapeutic principles remained consistently relevant, what dosage of music encounter was feasible including frequency and duration, individual participants’ perceptions), when was it helpful (were some music based activities more helpful than others, individual engagement and health), in what ways did that different between cases (all of the above)? This approach can be succinctly explained using pragmatist scientist-philosopher Charles S. Peirce’s formula [53] which heightened our focus on understanding the degree to which musical pleasure might act as a mediator of successful outcomes for people with anhedonia, and potentially, beyond.

The nature of the data analysis was therefore iterative and comprehensive, so that different possibilities could be explored. Analysis was based on plotting the daily ratings of pleasure provided by the two cases both before and after music; targeted analysis of field notes written regularly by the music therapist to identify connections between increases in pleasure experiences and changes in strategy of the therapist, dosage or life experiences; regular reflexive meetings for discussion and debate between the researchers that examined both sets of data and led to questions that would be posed in subsequent sessions for discussion between Author 1 as therapist and client; pre and post-tests of anhedonia using an accepted measure (DARS (55)—described below); and interviews by the senior researcher (Author 2) with the participants at the beginning, middle, and end of the process. We use the lens of abductive analysis based on experience in grounded theory, where ongoing and continual analysis allows the researchers to develop propositions based on the most recent data and then test whether these are sustained while other conditions are varied or reflected on. We wanted to examine the phenomenon from as many angles as possible.

After acquiring ethics approval from our local ethics committee (insert info post anonymous review) we advertised the opportunity through local social media channels that communicated to PhD researchers at our university. Graduate researchers who identified with the description “anhedonia is when your experience of pleasure is dulled or muted and it is a common symptom of various health conditions such as chronic pain, chronic fatigue, chronic illness and depression” (approved Recruitment Information) were asked to complete the Diagnostic Anhedonia Rating Scale, (DARS) [58]. The DARS is a 17-item scale that measures both anticipation and experience of pleasure and has been validated in community samples of people with Major Depressive Disorder or Bipolar Disorder, and mixed psychiatric diagnoses with reliability assessment of 0.91–0.96 for the total scale and 0.75–0.92 for the subscales. Importantly, we chose this tool because it allows participants to nominate specific pleasurable activities to be scored, rather than asking generalised questions about food, and other typically pleasurable activities.

Forty-nine people responded to the advertisement; however, we had established a strict exclusion criterion that participants could not be receiving pharmacological treatment for mental illness, which made ten of the interested participants ineligible. 27 people did not complete the brief screening survey, which is noteworthy as it is congruent with the motivational barriers associated with anhedonia. Additionally, three people did not meet the clinical cut-off for anhedonia based on our scoring of the DARS, and the remaining nine eligible respondents were provided with a Plain Language Statement and invited to enrol in the study. Three international PhD researchers consented to participate and commenced the program (from Japan, Italy, and the USA), but one person withdrew after a month of sessions, suggesting that the daily requirement was too much and that it had not been helpful to focus on music and the arts. They identified that their connection to music was energising, but in exploring the nuances of pleasure, there were other more sensory modes, such as showers, that they found more pleasurable and might focus on in future therapy.

The two remaining participants were completing PhDs in the fields of Engineering and Biomedical Science, and both had a strong existing relationship with music. One described themself as relying on music listening for coping with life adversities, enjoyed dancing, and had studied piano, but did not like singing in front of others. The other saw music as part of their identity since high school, regularly made seasonal playlists, and had recently completed a synthesizer workshop to further their skills in composition. Both described a range of hobbies including hiking in nature, had strong social networks despite having moved countries to undertake their PhD research, and were intrigued by the idea of how music therapy might address their loss of pleasure in previously pleasurable experiences.

Procedure

The music therapy program was informed by Sandman and Craske’s [66] Positive Affect Treatment model – the one intervention described in the literature as having been effective in the treatment of anhedonia. Their model comprised 3 × 5-week modules that began with pleasant event scheduling, followed by attending to the positive through cognitive exercises, and finally, cultivating the positive through loving kindness, generosity, appreciative joy, and gratitude. In the previous case informing this study [10] it had taken four months of brief, daily music activity to reach a point of data saturation at which time the participant’s anhedonic symptoms had receded and have still not returned. We replicated the broad structure of that process in the two cases presented in this study. Brief daily music activity (approximately 5-10min per day) was undertaken independently by participants, supported by the music therapist through daily text prompts and regular check-in sessions (approximately weekly) for planning and debriefing. In all cases, the nature of the daily music activity was tailored flexibly and specifically over time in response to the presentation and trajectory of each participant and their individual music interests and resources. Participants also completed a short daily pre-post activity log intended to prompt cognitive reflection of their anticipation and experience of pleasure during daily music activity.

We developed therapeutic principles to explain our approach and were inspired by Rolvsjord, Gold and Stige’s [61] protocol for music therapy in resource oriented mental health care which identifies the unique, essential, acceptable, and proscribed therapeutic principles rather than pre-determining exactly how the program should look. Results

Case 1

Serena (pseudonym) brought a strong existing relationship with music into this study. Her tastes were diverse and quite eclectic, ranging from pop to K-pop, to rock, to nostalgic Italian classic and popular songs that connected with her upbringing in Italy. In the past, Serena learnt piano and dancing, and had a history of creating playlists for “when things (we)re happening in life”. She described how historically, engagement in music had offered solace and an emotional outlet. At the time of the study, Serena described herself as listening to music “all the time”. She noted that music no longer connected her to her emotions as it previously had and also described a more general lack of connection to feelings of pleasure, which was what prompted her to enrol in the study.

Participant engagement

Despite the significant motivational challenges associated with anhedonia, Serena was highly engaged throughout the entire study. She reported 119 instances of daily music activity, missing only 19 days over the duration of the study period, mostly when on holiday. Throughout the study, Serena’s choice of musical activity centred almost exclusively around music listening, involving 1–2 songs per pleasure-focused daily listening instance. She explored different approaches to listening, including while walking, and at different times of day, and planned her intended listening around her plans for the day and what ‘vibe’ she wanted to experience. On 14 occasions, she spontaneously chose to dance or sing along with the recorded music when her mood prompted her, always in the privacy of her home.

Planning process

The approach taken to planning Serena’s daily music activity evolved over the 4-month (17 week) process and was led by Serena, with guided support from Author 1 (music therapist and research team member). Early weekly plans were highly detailed, including the song, time of day, and mode of listening, sometimes even selecting an overall ‘theme’ for the week (See Table 1 for an example music plan). After 7 weeks, Serena developed playlists and selected from them daily based on her mood. After 10 weeks, her plan was simply to listen with intention to 1–2 songs each day. Serena’s planned independent music activity was scaffolded by daily text prompts and check-in/planning meetings (every 1–2 weeks) with Author 1. According to Serena, “those daily texts were essential to keep on doing things. It’s not that I forget to do it, I just don’t have the motivation to reserve the 10 min without the prompt”. This insight from Serena highlighted the level of scaffolding needed for someone experiencing anhedonia. Impact

Prior to participation in the study, Serena reported a score of 31 on the DARS. Upon re-testing at the conclusion of the study period, she reported a score of 42. This signified a clear improvement which brought her score very close to the ‘healthy’ range for hedonic capacity on the DARS (44–68). This improvement was also evidenced in Serena’s interview data. She describes the real-world impact of the program as follows:

“ When I started using in the beginning it was a bit tough because I didn’t quite understand why it would work. I was thinking: Is this going to really work? And then slowly I just realised that there are some songs that if I listen to them it can work. Some of them connect to things – memories – they might be happy or sad, and so now I know that if I’m feeling that, I’m not feeling anything, I have some songs that make me feel something again – which is like oh, ok, now I feel a bit better.” (Serena, 4-month interview)

Case 2

Jen (pseudonym) had highly eclectic music tastes, ranging from punk, to country, to 60 s and 70 s pop and soul. She self-identified as seeking out niche music that was new and uncommon, but also enjoyed well known ‘classic’ songs that were personally nostalgic, often connected to her family and childhood experiences living in southern USA. Jen expressed having a very strong relationship with music and regularly made playlists for different time periods, seasons, and purposes (ie. exercise). She was keen to learn to play synthesizer, enjoyed diverse modes of artistic expression including drawing and puppetry, and was interested in Buddhism and meditation. Prior to nominating herself for the study, Jen had noticed that she “wasn’t getting excited by things” and was diagnosed with anhedonia due to burnout.

Participant engagement

Jen showed a strong commitment to the study, engaging regularly in daily music activity over 19 weeks for a total of 86 recorded instances. This commitment persisted even when Jen was travelling without regular mobile phone reception towards the end of the study period, at which time she switched to physical prompts and paper-based notetaking that was reported back to Author 1. This showed an impressive level of self-motivation for someone with anhedonia. Jen explored a variety of music activities, including music listening (40 times), music and drawing (18 times), dancing (14 times) and singing (14 times), and selected these activities based on her mood, the time of day, and her physical environment.

Over the course of the study, Jen’s daily choice of music activity became progressively more intentionally focused on enhancing pleasure and joy, supported by guiding conversations with Author 1. In this example from Jen’s daily reflection data, she notes: “…I was such a grump in the heat. But I knew I needed to do something and have fun, and I've had fun doing the drawing and listening… I think sometimes when I pair the two activities, I get into a flow state. But I was looking forward to getting out of my stress head state.” (Session notes)

Planning process

Initially, Jen’s music plans were detailed, including the song, time of day and activity, as see in Table 1. Around week 10, Jen’s preferred four activities (above) were well established and she felt able to choose these daily without planning, however songs were pre-selected for ease of decision-making. In week 11, Jen chose to draw on existing playlists to choose her daily songs, and at this stage planning became more focused on the intention of her engagement with music activity – ultimately to reconnect to pleasure. Jen travelled extensively in the weeks 15 to 19 of the project, and plans were adapted around this. Specifically, Jen used ‘music tags’ hidden in her car and luggage to spontaneously prompt her to do music each day. Jen found that this mimicked text prompts – which Author 1 sent at different times of day – better than a scheduled reminder that she was likely to ignore. This proved to be a fun and effective strategy when Jen was out of phone reception.

Impact

The impact of participation in the study was clearly evident, both in Jen’s pre- and post-study DARS rating and in her description of what she learnt through participation. Notably, Jen’s DARS score moved from 32 to 45; a significant shift that brought her score into the ‘healthy’ range for hedonic capacity. Just as importantly, Jen was able to describe the impact of her participation to Author 2, describing how this study changed her perception of musical pleasure and its value as an ongoing resource in her life.

“Because it’s really easy to use a thing until you get better and then not do that thing anymore. And I guess when I’ve said I want to continue (the music activity), it’s this idea that this has been helpful and useful, and I want to maintain this because it’s been useful, not just in getting past the anhedonia… but also in today’s society I don’t think I’ve ever been taught to connect with pleasure specifically, and I also think that it’s really easy to live a life that just feels really busy and stressful all the time… Even if you’re not experiencing anhedonia, I think that connecting intentionally to pleasure is a really important thing.” (Jen, 4-month interview)

Consideration of participant challenges

Despite the large time demands on participants in this study, a high level of engagement was evidenced, with neither person reporting this as challenging. This commitment was demonstrated from commencement, both in their sincere interest in participating and perhaps related to their status as PhD researchers who understand the demands of research participation from their own experience. Both participants reported experiencing periods of high pressure due to PhD demands and life changes, but this did not impact on their level of participation in the project. In alignment with the study protocol, adjustments were made as required to support the ease of participant engagement for logistical reasons such as when participants were travelling. Notably, the level of scaffolded support to participate (via texts and planning) was high at the beginning of data collection and reduced over time.

Discussion

Music therapists are frequently required to prove what is specifically useful about the musical aspects of our programs compared to other non-musical, psychological interventions. This is usually driven by an economic rationale that assumes funding resources are scarce and therefore decisions need to be made that privilege one service over another. This is a challenging proposition for many reasons, not least of which the findings from the widely acclaimed common factors research [31] which suggests that specific therapeutic techniques only account for approximately 15% of an observed outcome in psychotherapy. The remaining factors that contribute to the effectiveness of therapy are considered to comprise extra-therapeutic aspects (estimated at 40%), the therapeutic relationship (estimated at 30%), along with expectancy and placebo effects (at 15%). Our research into music therapy and anhedonia may address this so-called dodo-bird verdict, based on the Alice in Wonderland scenario where the queen declares that “everybody has won, and all must have prizes”. In this case, the suggestion is that all psychotherapies are considered approximately equal [25], and yet music therapists still need to prove themselves to be greater, or unique, in order to be funded.

If there is something specific about music therapy that makes it effective, it may be the way that music can ‘tickle the senses’ [54] since numerous brain studies have shown that music activates the limbic system [12, 65, 72] among other parts of the brain. However, music therapists do not often focus on the pleasurable aspects of musical engagement, at least in theorising about practice, as Stige [74] has highlighted, and even more rarely is musical pleasure an articulated intention or priority of music therapy. Our case studies suggest that for musical pleasure to be impactful for these participants, it not only required the therapist to overtly target pleasure and joy, but also for the participants to be engaged in this task. In our study this may be for two reasons. First, because this reactivates muted feelings of pleasure that are not elicited by being in the presence of music. Both participants described how the therapist’s encouragement to focus on the pleasurable dimensions helped them to connect more strongly to it. Secondly, this also enabled them to experience pleasure independently, daily and outside the weekly therapeutic encounter which meant it became a resource for their ongoing coping. Given the lasting effects of the program so far, this might be critical.

In carefully examining the ways people described engaging with musical pleasure, we noted the interaction of two components – the act of pleasure-focused musicking and the experience of musical pleasure. The qualities of pleasure-focused musicking seemed to include being accessible, regular, safe, and intentional. The qualities of the musical pleasure experience were described as embodied, aesthetic, playful, nostalgic, and/or emotional. These facets are like the active factors of creative arts therapy proposed by Koch [29] which include aesthetics, hedonism, nonverbal communication/metaphor, enactive transitional support, and generativity. However, we perceived another dimension that was necessary to complement these internal components, specifically, the scaffolding of a targeted therapeutic alliance that is responsive to change and growth over time. The function of the therapeutic alliance may be in compensating for barriers to engagement with musical pleasure, and to build participants’ skills and knowledge about what ‘works’ for them. The articulation of these interacting dimensions was also shaped by the authors’ prior experiences in Case 1. In recruiting PhD researchers in this study, we sought to engage them as informants of our investigation, recognising them as a cohort with a strong capacity for observation and reflection on their experiences. Past experience [39, 41, 79] has shown that early career researchers are, unsurprisingly, intelligent, articulate, curious, and frank in their contributions, which is particularly helpful during the discovery phase of research.

Beyond the rich descriptions provided by the participants, their scores on the DARS [58] demonstrate a noticeable change pre- and post-intervention using a standardised tool. Both these participants moved from being in the clinical range for anhedonia to being close to or within the normal range by the end of their four months participation in the study. The participant in our first case (Author 1) remains in remission after 6 months and we will continue to follow up with each case.

Both participants offered interesting insights into the assessment tools. One was surprised to learn that they did not get access to their pre-test answers, noting that this would be a more effective way for them to score their lived experience since it would allow them to reflect on whether they still felt that way or not. The pseudo-objectivity of the self-reporting did not align well with her engineering logic. When this was reported to the other participant, she laughed and noted that was precisely what she had done since she has a photographic memory and could recall her previous answers.

Both participants felt that the therapist’s scaffolding, guiding, support and encouragement were critical to their ability to realise benefits from musicking. Although it is tempting to assume that a mobile app might be able to substitute for the therapist for participants to be fully independent, this was not the expressed opinions of the participants. The role of the therapist incorporated psychoeducation about anhedonia that was intimately linked to their unique presentation. Both participants had access to online information prior to and during the process, but neither found that having access was sufficient to overcome the motivational hurdles to acting. The therapist therefore also used motivational coaching strategies, reminding the participants of what they had committed to using daily text messages and helping them identify what activities would be feasible in the week ahead and plan for how they would fit music into their daily schedule. In addition, the therapist’s presence was described as warm and caring and her ability to hold the stories that emerged about other struggles whilst also ensuring that the coaching and educative aspects were achieved required great skill and attention.

A final dimension of the relationship was in the mutual appreciation of music. This may be a requirement for successful music therapy treatment of anhedonia, which is the loss of previous pleasures [1], as opposed to the establishment of new sensations of pleasure in music. The one participant who dropped out of the program suggested that this had made a difference for her. She felt that she could be more motivated in the pursuit of other pleasures, whereas music did not feel as comfortable or natural to her. It is unknown whether that is the case, and it might have been other factors that were more prominent for her, but people’s relationship to music as a historical source of pleasure is a factor that requires further examination as we continue to study this phenomenon.

In addition, we suggest further investigation of objective measures to see whether they illuminate another perspective on this phenomenon. If this music therapy program is effective in addressing the transdiagnostic symptom of anhedonia it could be useful to examine how this can be evidenced. Given the subjective nature of the phenomenon, self-report seems most relevant and we have used both interviews, daily ratings and pre-post testing to gather that. However, the literature also suggests that brain activity stimulated by musical activity can be captured and therefore it may be possible to see an increase in activity in the limbic system both structurally and functionally. If so, this might satisfy those who prefer objective evidence, even when it is illuminating a subjective phenomenon.

Conclusions

These two case studies are a small sample by objective standards, making claims for generalisability or representation impractical. However, case studies have been an important strategy for developing new insights in psychotherapy and the careful examination of the unique case [81] can be productive and enlightening. As we continue to examine the ways that music therapy could be useful in treating anhedonic symptoms, we are excited to explore the boundaries of the phenomenon—what are the limits of its effectiveness with diverse individuals and at what point do they become clear. In the meantime, we celebrate how successful it has been for three people thus far, including a sentinel case study [10], and are curious about what we might discover next.
