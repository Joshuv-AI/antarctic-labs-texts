Generative diffusion meets domain adaptation: a framework for EEG cross-subject motor imagery classification
Authors: Jiacheng Zhang, Haolan Zhang, Youpeng Yang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractCross-subject motor imagery classification remains challenging due to EEG data scarcity and inter-subject variability. This study proposes a novel framework integrating generative data augmentation with domain adaptation. First, we employ a diffusion probabilistic model to generate high-fidelity synthetic EEG samples, effectively enriching the training data. Subsequently, we propose the AMSC-DANN architecture, which synergizes an Adaptive Multi-Scale Convolution (AMSC) module for extracting multi-granular features with a Domain Adversarial Neural Network (DANN). This combination enables the model to learn discriminative temporal-spectral representations while simultaneously aligning feature distributions across different subjects. Extensive experiments on BCI Competition IV datasets 2a and 2b demonstrate that our proposed framework outperforms state-of-the-art baselines, validating its effectiveness in enhancing cross-subject generalization.Keywords: Motor imagery, EEG, Diffusion, Domain adaptation

Introduction

Brain-computer Interface (BCI), as a cutting-edge human-computer interaction technology, aims to establish a direct communication path between the brain and external devices [1]. In recent years, BCI technology has demonstrated great application potential in fields such as medical rehabilitation, transportation, device control, and entertainment, attracting extensive attention from both the academic and industrial communities [2]. Motor Imagery (MI) stands as one of the most prevalent paradigms in BCI. During the imagination of body movements, neural oscillations in the sensorimotor cortex undergo event-related desynchronization (ERD) or synchronization (ERS) [3]. These variations in neural activity can be effectively captured by Electroencephalography (EEG) devices [4].

EEG-based BCI systems primarily comprise three major types of stimulation paradigms: event-related potentials [5], steady-state visual evoked potentials [6], and sensorimotor rhythms [7]. Among them, Motor Imagery EEG (MI-EEG) is recognized as a vital paradigm for motor rehabilitation, as it effectively captures the sensorimotor rhythms induced by the imagination of motor tasks. The rise of deep learning (DL) has significantly improved the performance of Motor Imagery (MI) decoding. Unlike traditional methods relying on manual feature extraction like CSP [8], DL models such as CNNs [9], EEGNet [10], can automatically learn complex representations from raw EEG data. However, their performance often deteriorates significantly in cross-subject tasks. First, there is the common problem of the scarcity of EEG training data [11]. Since collecting large-scale, high-quality EEG datasets is hindered by time constraints and subject fatigue, most research suffer from a severe scarcity of labeled samples. This data limitation causes deep learning models to overfit and fail in learning features. Second, the inherent non-stationarity of EEG signals and the individual differences among subjects also affect the accuracy of recognition. Due to diverse physiological states, unique cortical folding, and varying electrode impedances, EEG patterns often exhibit drastic distribution shifts across different subjects or even different sessions of the same subject [12]. This phenomenon, technically referred to as domain shift, inevitably leads to performance deterioration when a model trained on existing subjects is applied to an unseen subject. Consequently, relying solely on data expansion is insufficient to guarantee universality.

Related work

Convolutional neural networks in motor imagery decoding

In recent years, Deep Learning (DL) has achieved remarkable success in Motor Imagery classification, gradually superseding traditional machine learning methods based on manual feature engineering, such as Filter Bank Common Spatial Pattern (FBCSP) [13]. Convolutional Neural Networks (CNNs) [14], in particular, have become the dominant architecture due to their powerful capability in automatically extracting spatial-temporal representations from raw EEG signals. Representative models, including EEGNet [10], ShallowConvNet and DeepConvNet [14], utilize compact convolutional kernels to capture local dependencies and frequency-specific patterns. [15] introduced a dynamic graph neural network model, MCGNet+, which improves motor imagery classification accuracy by leveraging mutual information to construct evolving brain connection graphs. However, a fundamental limitation of these standard architectures lies in their reliance on fixed receptive fields (kernel sizes). EEG signals are inherently non-stationary, characterized by complex dynamics that span multiple frequency bands and varying temporal scales. Consequently, employing a single, static kernel size restricts the model’s ability to simultaneously capture broad contextual information and fine-grained local transient features.

Emerging research has introduced multi-branch convolutional frameworks to Motor Imagery classification, yielding substantial performance gains compared to traditional single-branch baselines. Wu et al. [16] proposed the Multi-Scale Filter Bank Convolutional Neural Network (MSFBCNN) employs temporal convolution layers with multiple scales for extracting diverse features, which are subsequently integrated into a spatial convolution block. Building upon established architectures, Altuwaijri et al. [17] introduced MBEEGNet and MBShallowConvNet. These models extend the standard EEGNet and ShallowConvNet frameworks by integrating a multi-branch design that operates across three distinct kernel sizes. Despite these advancements, the potential of input-dependent dynamic fusion remains largely unexplored in these fixed multi-branch structures in EEG decoding.

Data augmentation strategies for EEG analysis

Data augmentation for multivariate EEG time series is primarily implemented through signal processing techniques that manipulate features within either the time or frequency domains. In the temporal dimension, strategies prioritize geometric transformations and stochastic perturbations to diversify the sample distribution. Conventional methods, such as jittering, scaling, and time warping, modify temporal morphology by injecting Gaussian noise or stretching signal segments, while techniques like channel shuffling recombine data across distinct channels to enhance structural robustness [18]. Regarding the frequency domain, methods typically involve converting signals into time-frequency images and applying specific noise [19]. Recent advancements, such as FreqNoise and FreqMasking, target specific spectral attributes: the former injects complex noise into amplitude and phase information to mimic realistic artifacts, while the latter selectively masks discrete frequency intervals to compel the model to learn more robust representations [20].

Originating from advancements in image synthesis, generative models—specifically Generative Adversarial Networks (GANs) [21] and Diffusion Models [22] have become fundamental frameworks for autonomous distribution modeling. In the context of EEG data augmentation, GANs have seen extensive application, primarily to address class imbalance and enhance signal quality. One study employed GANs to augment a class-imbalanced sleep EEG dataset [23], other researchers utilized GANs to construct a low-sensitivity dataset from a high-sensitivity one, attempting to address the super-resolution reconstruction problem in motor imagery tasks [24]. Despite the effectiveness of GANs in EEG signal processing, recent research has revealed that samples generated by classic GAN models are prone to mode collapse. This issue compromises the similarity between the augmented and original samples [25]. A study employed a diffusion model with a Mixup strategy to generate EEG motor imagery data, by performing data augmentation in both the time and frequency domains, the research achieved favorable results on BCI datasets, offering a potential solution to the scarcity of MI-EEG samples [26]. It was verified that the diffusion model not only boosts the augmented samples’ ability to capture intrinsic features but also prevents GAN-associated mode collapse, effectively mitigating conflicts within ERD/ERS phenomena.

Transfer learning and domain adaptation in EEG decoding task

A major bottleneck in current deep learning methodologies lies in the pronounced heterogeneity of MI-EEG signals across subjects. To address this issue, several Brain-Computer Interface (BCI) studies have adopted transfer learning techniques, which utilize knowledge acquired from a source domain to assist learning in a target domain [27]. Raza et al. [28] introduced a novel framework for detecting and adapting to covariate shifts to alleviate distributional differences in feature spaces. Liu et al. [29] focused on capturing shared features across different subjects to reduce inter-subject variability. Within the broader scope of transfer learning, Domain Adaptation (DA) has emerged as a prominent paradigm specifically designed to align the statistical distributions of source and target domains. Phunruangsakao et al. [30] proposed a few-shot domain adaptation model to reduce inter-subject discrepancies and boost performance on unseen targets. In [31], a dynamic joint domain adaptation framework was put forth, which employed multiple discriminators to effectively reduce distributional shifts between domains. To address cross-subject heterogeneity, [32] proposed a Wasserstein-based adversarial domain adaptation framework that leverages attention mechanisms to extract discriminative features and explicitly minimizes the distributional discrepancy between source and target domains. Huang et al. [33] introduced a shallow Inception domain adaptation framework, which extracts informative deep features from data across multiple subjects to achieve accurate Motor Imagery (MI) recognition. Additionally, some scholars proposed a Deep Domain Adaptation Framework based on Correlation Alignment (DDAF-CORAL) [12] to address the problem of distribution divergence in cross-domain Motor Imagery classification. A study proposed using Multi-Source Self-Selected Joint Domain Adaptation (MSS-JDA) [34] to address the cross-subject problem. The multi-source domain adaptation framework entails building separate branches for each subject’s EEG data. It leverages one-to-one domain alignment to capture domain-specific representations, allowing for inference via a multi-branch architecture [35].

Data preprocessing

The original data of BCI Competition IV is stored in the Continuous Common Data Format (GDF). When directly used for the training of deep learning models, not only is the dimensionality disaster severe, but also the signal-to-noise ratio is extremely low. There are often multiple artifacts mixed in the data, such as electromyography (EOG), electromyography (EMG), and power frequency interference. Therefore, this study designed a standardized preprocessing method that can extract pure motion intention features and convert them into time-frequency graph representations suitable for processing by convolutional neural networks. First, perform basic processing on the data using Python, and then convert it into time-frequency images through short-time Fast Fourier Transform (STFT) for subsequent data augmentation.

We used the open-source neurophysiological signal analysis library MNE-Python in the Python environment to load and clean the original GDF file. The specific processing procedure is as follows:

Data loading and electrode mappingUse the mne.io.read.raw.gdf function to read the original file of the BCI Competition IV 2a/2b dataset. According to the international 10-20 system standard, the data channels were mapped to the corresponding scalp spatial positions, the useless EOG channels (the last three channels in Dataset 2a) were eliminated, and the EEG channels were retained for subsequent analysis.

To eliminate low-frequency drift and high-frequency electromyographic noise while preserving the complete moving-related frequency band, we apply a fourth-order Butterworth bandpass filter with the frequency range set from 4 to 38 Hz. This frequency band covers theta,alpha,Beta and low-frequency gamma waves, which can fully capture the time-frequency dynamic characteristics of motion intentions.

For the 4-class BCI IV 2a dataset, our method achieves an average value of 0.71. This performance significantly outperforms the baseline Conformer () and the competitive method DAFS (). According to Cohen’s interpretation guidelines, a value in the range of 0.61–0.80 represents substantial agreement. This indicates that the high accuracy of our model is not an artifact of random guessing or class bias, but stems from the model’s genuine discriminative capability across four distinct motor imagery tasks. Similarly, on the BCI IV 2b dataset (2-class), our method maintains superior consistency with a of 0.60, whereas other state-of-the-art methods fluctuate between 0.52 and 0.58. The consistent improvement in Kappa values across both datasets demonstrates that our proposed framework is more robust against inter-subject variability and provides trustworthy predictions, effectively minimizing random classification errors even in challenging EEG environments.

For Dataset 2a, the model demonstrates robust discriminative ability, with the diagonal accuracy for all four classes ranging from 0.75 to 0.82. Notably, the ‘right hand’ imagery achieves the highest recognition rate (0.82). The most prominent source of error lies in the reciprocal misclassification between contralateral movements, specifically ’left hand’ and ’right hand’ (e.g., 11% of left hand samples are misclassified as right hand). From a neurophysiological perspective, this confusion is a well-known challenge in BCI: motor imagery of either hand primarily triggers Event-Related Desynchronization (ERD) in the bilateral sensorimotor cortices (C3 and C4 channels). The spatial proximity and the occasional bilateral cortical activation during unilateral imagery make their EEG patterns highly overlapping. Furthermore, minor confusions are observed between ’feet’ and ’right hand’ (0.11), which may stem from subject-specific variations in the spatial mapping of cortical source signals to the scalp electrodes.

For Dataset 2b, the binary classification task further isolates this contralateral confusion. The matrix indicates a balanced and strong predictive capability, with the accuracies for ’left hand’ and ’right hand’ reaching 0.78 and 0.82, respectively. The remaining misclassification (around 18%-22%) directly reflects the inherent difficulty in disentangling highly symmetric brain activities. Nevertheless, these stable diagonal values across both datasets confirm that our multi-scale adaptive feature extraction strategy effectively captures the distinct spatial-temporal patterns of motor imagery, maintaining high reliability even under complex cross-subject scenarios.

While WGAN-GP improves upon DCGAN by stabilizing training, it still struggles to capture the fine-grained high-frequency details typical of EEG spectrograms. Our diffusion approach, through its iterative denoising process, effectively models these complex local textures, resulting in more realistic samples.

Traditional alignment methods like MMD and CORAL alleviate this by minimizing empirical statistical distances (e.g., mean embeddings or covariance matrices) between domains, bringing the average accuracy up to approximately 74.8% (2a) and 75.7% (2b). However, these methods rely on shallow statistical moments and often struggle to capture the complex, non-linear distribution shifts inherent in heterogeneous EEG data.

In contrast, our proposed method consistently outperforms these baselines, achieving the highest average accuracies of 78.5% (2a) and 80.1% (2b). This superior performance stems from our specific architectural design. As illustrated in our framework, we employ an adversarial adaptation strategy based on a Domain-Adversarial Neural Network (DANN). By introducing a Gradient Reversal Layer (GRL) and a Domain Discriminator, the feature extractor actively learns to deceive the discriminator, dynamically mapping both source and target data into a shared, domain-invariant latent space at the bottleneck layer. Furthermore, the integration of multi-scale convolutions ( 33,55,77) and the “Adaptive block” ensures that fine-grained, task-relevant motor imagery patterns are preserved during the adversarial alignment process.

The advantage of this structural design is particularly evident in subjects with severe distribution shifts, where conventional statistical matching often fails. For instance, on subject S01 (+12.2% on 2a; +16.5% on 2b) and S04 (+14.1% on 2b), our method yields dramatic improvements over the “No DA” baseline. By effectively filtering out subject-specific noise while preserving discriminative semantic features, our adversarial framework successfully bridges the inter-subject gap, enabling robust cross-subject decoding.

Discussion

Advantages of our proposed work

Impact of Unet-based diffusion data augmentation

The scarcity of labeled EEG data and the high inter-subject variability are the main obstacles in BCI calibration. Our results, particularly the significantly reduced standard deviation, demonstrate the effectiveness of our diffusion-based augmentation strategy. Unlike traditional geometric augmentations (cropping, noise injection) that may disrupt the temporal continuity of EEG signals, or GAN-based methods that often suffer from mode collapse, the diffusion model learns the intrinsic data manifold to generate diverse and high-fidelity samples.

By expanding the training set with these generated samples, we effectively bridge the distribution gap between the source and target domains. This “dense” feature space allows the classifier to learn smoother decision boundaries, thereby improving robustness against noise and outliers. This is evident in the substantial performance boost on difficult subjects, where the generated data supplemented the sparse features of the original low-quality signals.

Efficacy of the AMSC-DANN architecture

While data augmentation provides high-quality inputs, the specific design of our AMSC-DANN architecture ensures that the most discriminative features are extracted. A critical innovation here is the Adaptive Multi-Scale Convolution (AMSC) based on the Selective Kernel (SK) network mechanism [40]. Traditional multi-scale methods fuse features from fixed kernel sizes statically. In contrast, our AMSC module dynamically adjusts the receptive field size based on the input signal characteristics. EEG signals exhibit significant non-stationarity; for instance, the duration and frequency of motor imagery patterns (ERD/ERS) can vary dramatically across subjects and trials.

Through the attention-guided selection mechanism, the AMSC module can automatically assign higher weights to the most relevant kernel branch. This means the network adaptively decides whether to focus on short-term transient details or long-term rhythmic dependencies for each specific input sample. Combined with the domain adversarial alignment, this adaptive capability allows our model to capture robust, subject-invariant representations that are often missed by fixed-scale architectures, directly contributing to the state-of-the-art accuracy achieved in our experiments.

Limitations and future work

While our proposed method achieves competitive overall performance, the confusion matrices (Fig. 8) reveal a persistent bottleneck: the mutual misclassification between highly symmetric tasks, particularly ’left hand’ and ’right hand’. To further alleviate this confusion in future work, several advanced strategies can be explored:

Spatial attention mechanisms: Although our multi-scale convolutions capture broad spatial features, integrating a dynamic spatial attention module could help the network strictly focus on the discriminative activation differences between the C3 (left hemisphere) and C4 (right hemisphere) regions, thereby suppressing ipsilateral noise.

Supervised contrastive learning: The current cross-entropy loss focuses on absolute class probabilities. By introducing a supervised contrastive loss, the network can be explicitly forced to push the latent representations of ’left hand’ and ’right hand’ further apart in the feature space, maximizing inter-class margins for these hard-negative pairs.

Brain functional connectivity: Traditional CNNs extract localized grid-based features. Incorporating Graph Neural Networks (GNNs) to model the phase-locking value (PLV) or coherence between different brain regions could capture the asymmetrical functional connectivity routing during unilateral motor imagery, providing a critical complementary feature to distinguish contralateral movements.

Despite the performance, our framework entails higher computational costs compared to lightweight models like EEGNet, primarily due to the iterative denoising process of the diffusion model. Training time is significantly increased as the model learns to reconstruct the complex EEG manifold. Future work will focus on exploring accelerated sampling methods, such as DDIM [55] or consistency models, to further improve the training efficiency and facilitate rapid calibration for new users.

Conclusion

In this paper, we addressed the critical challenges of data scarcity and inter-subject variability in EEG-based motor imagery classification. We proposed a comprehensive framework that synergizes generative data augmentation with a domain-adversarial deep learning architecture. Specifically, we leveraged a diffusion probabilistic model to synthesize high-fidelity EEG samples, effectively expanding the training distribution and mitigating the risk of overfitting. Furthermore, the proposed AMSC-DANN architecture integrates Adaptive Multi-Scale Convolutions to capture dynamic temporal-spectral features at varying granularities, coupled with a DANN to enforce feature alignment across subjects. Extensive empirical evaluations on the BCI Competition IV datasets demonstrate that our method outperforms state-of-the-art baselines, achieving classification accuracies of 78.5% on dataset 2a and 80.1% on dataset 2b, while maintaining relatively low standard deviations. Ablation studies further verified the distinct contribution of each module: the adaptive mechanism is crucial for extracting discriminative patterns from non-stationary signals, while the adversarial strategy effectively reduces the distribution divergence between source and target domains. Future work will focus on refining the core components. We aim to enhance the diffusion model with efficient sampling strategies and advanced conditioning to further improve signal fidelity. Additionally, we plan to upgrade the domain adaptation module by incorporating class-conditional or metric-based strategies, enabling fine-grained feature alignment to minimize negative transfer and boost generalization.
