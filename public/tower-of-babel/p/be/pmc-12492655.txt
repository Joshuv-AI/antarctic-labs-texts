Soundwaves of innovation: a qualitative exploration of POCUS in Australasian ambulance and retrieval services
Authors: Jake Donovan, Kelly-Ann Bowles, Brendan Shannon, David Anderson, Samuel Burton, Benjamin Meadley
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractObjectivesPoint-of-Care Ultrasound (POCUS) represents an advancement in diagnostic ability and procedural success for paramedics in out-of-hospital settings. Despite its potential to rapidly identify life-threatening conditions and improve patient management, the adoption of POCUS faces multiple challenges, including the need for standardized training, credentialing, and robust governance. This study aims to evaluate the current use of POCUS by paramedics in Australasia, focusing on training, credentialing, governance, and auditing systems. It also explores the main barriers and facilitators that influence its effective integration and operational effectiveness in out-of-hospital environments.MethodsThis qualitative study utilized reflexive thematic analysis following Braun and Clarke’s methodology to capture deep insights into POCUS usage. Participants included medical directors and clinical leads from jurisdictional ambulance services and retrieval services across Australasia. They were selected via purposive sampling and interviewed using semi-structured techniques, which allowed for an in-depth exploration of their experiences and perceptions related to POCUS.ResultsFour major themes were identified: (1) Pillars of POCUS: highlighting the necessity of robust governance, education, and infrastructure for effective POCUS programs; (2) System Buy-In: emphasizing the need for medical and institutional support to maximize the benefits of POCUS; (3) Culture Change in Paramedicine: discussing the shifts required in paramedic practices and attitudes towards adopting new diagnostic technologies; and (4) What’s the Worth: evaluating the cost-effectiveness and value of POCUS in the face of budget constraints and its potential to improve patient outcomes.ConclusionsThe study underscores significant disparities in POCUS training and a lack of standardized governance across Australasian services, which are major impediments to its adoption. Additionally, financial considerations and the perceived value of POCUS impact its acceptance and expansion. To fully leverage the capabilities of POCUS in enhancing paramedic practice, development of comprehensive, standardized frameworks addressing these challenges is essential. Future efforts should focus on demonstrating the economic and clinical benefits of POCUS to support its widespread adoption in emergency medical services.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s13049-025-01471-7.

Introduction

Point-of-care ultrasound (POCUS) is distinct from formal diagnostic ultrasound performed by sonographers, cardiologists or radiologists and is best defined as “acquisition, interpretation and immediate clinical integration of ultrasonographic imaging performed by a treating clinician at the patient’s bedside” [1]. POCUS is emerging as a promising tool for paramedics to improve diagnostics and procedural success in out-of-hospital settings. Some evidence exists to support paramedic use of POCUS including identification of hemoperitoneum and pneumothorax [2, 3]. Recent studies have also explored the potential of lung ultrasound and ultrasound-guided intravenous access, which could further enhance the diagnostic and therapeutic capabilities of paramedics in the field [4–6]. Despite the many potential benefits, concerns have been raised about the widespread adoption of the technology in the absence of standardized training and governance [7] and questions remain regarding barriers to implementation, education, governance, and scope of use.

Several barriers hinder the implementation of POCUS by paramedics, notably cost, lack of formal governance, and limited evidence from robust studies [4, 8, 9]. Practical issues include technological failure, battery problems, and poor image quality have also been reported [4, 8–10]. Barriers specific to out-of-hospital settings remain underexplored, and addressing these is crucial for developing effective POCUS programs for paramedics.

While the use of POCUS in the out-of-hospital environment has become a key feature of critical care practice for both physicians and paramedics [11, 12], there is currently no overarching scope of practice framework for paramedics. Therefore, scope of practice in paramedicine is largely dictated by the employer in either the public or private sector. The scope of practice for POCUS users in other health professions outside of medicine varies significantly and is often self-determined [13]. Clinical governance plays a crucial role in the implementation of POCUS into paramedic practice. In an effort to provide clearer guidance, Cormack et al. (2015) proposed a “five pillars” framework for POCUS programs, one of which is governance itself [14]. Establishing a clear scope of practice and standardized governance for POCUS in paramedic practice may enable the safe and effective use of ultrasound in the field and may lead to improved patient outcomes. There is little available literature regarding the governance and audit requirements for paramedic POCUS though some general recommendations do exist for other professions [15, 16].

This lack of overarching guidance has led to a wide range of POCUS training programs with varying durations and outcomes, making it challenging to identify the ideal education and training required for paramedics [3, 13, 17, 18]. In a recent scoping review, duration of training in POCUS was found to range from two minutes to two days and a variety of teaching methods were described including a mix of online and in person teaching [17]. This inconsistency in the training and education for paramedics may result in differing levels of competency.

Understanding the current ‘state-of-play’ of POCUS utilization in Australasia is imperative to developing a framework for implementation of POCUS into paramedic practice. Therefore, the aim of this research is to examine the current use of POCUS by paramedics in Australasia, including an exploration of the training, credentialing, clinical governance, and audit systems used. A secondary aim is to identify the barriers and enablers influencing the adoption and use of POCUS by paramedics in out-of-hospital care in Australasia.

Methodology

Qualitative approach

In this study, we utilized a reflexive thematic analysis methodology, guided by the principles set out by Braun and Clarke [19], chosen for its flexibility and suitability for qualitative research. This approach is particularly appropriate for our study’s exploratory design, which seeks to uncover insights into the varied experiences and perceptions of stakeholders regarding the use of POCUS in out-of-hospital settings. We adopted a pragmatist perspective, emphasizing the practical application of ideas by assessing their truths through the success of their practical outcomes [20, 21]. Data analysis was conducted using an inductive strategy, where themes and patterns were identified from detailed descriptions provided by participants regarding their experiences with POCUS. This analytical stance was aimed at thoroughly examining the systems surrounding training, credentialing, clinical governance, and auditing processes in the use of POCUS by paramedics, thereby aligning with our research objectives.

Researcher characteristics and reflexivity

The lead researcher is a male intensive care paramedic currently working in an Australasian jurisdictional ambulance service (JAS). The researcher uses POCUS in their clinical practice on-road and has undertaken proctored scanning in an emergency department and intensive care unit. The researcher has completed formal credentialling in eFAST and ultrasound-guided vascular access and holds a microcredential in lung ultrasound through Monash University. This experience and training has informed the study design and allowed for greater insight into the challenges of using POCUS in the out-of-hospital environment. The wider research team consists of a medical specialist, academics, and paramedics all of whom have a wealth of knowledge and experience in education, research, clinical practice and governance.

Research paradigm

This stance will be informed by a systems theory lens [22, 23]. General systems theory has been of particular interest in healthcare settings to investigate and overcome ‘systemic’ issues. While system theory has been traditionally used in disciplines such as engineering, healthcare systems can also be incredibly complex, involving multitudes of different healthcare professionals and siloed areas of practice [22, 23]. In this study, the systems theory lens guided the development of the interview guide and informed the interpretation of the results. The questions in the interview guide were designed to explore participants’ views on the implications of POCUS, not only for individual patients, but also for practitioners, JAS, RS and interprofessional teams across the wider healthcare system. During analysis, while a deductive codebook approach was not undertaken, systems theory knowledge was used to assist the researchers to identify systems-based patterns of interaction and feedback loops, such as how paramedic POCUS might influence downstream emergency department processes and resource allocation. Adopting a systems theory perspective enabled the research team to conceptualize the integration of POCUS into paramedic practice, not as an isolated clinical skill, but as a change with implications at a systems level that span patient care, professional practice, organizational processes, and the broader health system.

Study participants and setting

In Australasia emergency medical services are provided by ten JAS. These services operate autonomously under the authority of a territory or state government in Australia and Te Whatu Ora - Health New Zealand in Aotearoa New Zealand. Each JAS produces its own guidelines and scope of practice for paramedics [24]. Eight adult retrieval services (RS) were identified throughout Australasia. These services attend primary out-of-hospital work as well as interhospital transfers, though the makeup of the work differs based on location and crew mix. The retrieval service crews are staffed by physicians, specialist paramedics or nurses depending on the jurisdiction. The primary crew mix in use is specialist paramedic and physician or paramedics alone in the more heavily populated areas of Australasia [25]. Conversely, the more remote areas are often staffed by physicians and dual-qualified nurse/midwives [26, 27].

Both JAS and RS operate under a medical director or medical advisory committee who are responsible for the clinical oversight and governance of the service. Medical directors were targeted for participation in this study due to their inherent integration into organizational governance structures and their intimate knowledge of the implementation of POCUS. Clinical leads and local subject matter experts (SME) were targeted due to their specialized knowledge of the subject matter. The authors aimed to capture as many of the potential participants as possible to gain a diversity of perspectives and a complete view of the use of POCUS in Australasian JAS and RS.

Recruitment process

This study recruited participants by utilizing an established mailing list of medical directors for JAS and RS in Australasia. An introduction was provided by a member of the research team. Once this was provided the research team member concluded their involvement and all further communication was with the lead researcher (JD) to avoid any perceived coercion. The inclusion and exclusion criteria can be seen in Table 1. A proforma letter was sent asking for participation in the study, or alternatively if no paramedics were using ultrasound in their service, advice of non-participation. The letter asked the medical directors to nominate either themselves or another person in their organization to be interviewed as a subject matter expert on the use of POCUS by paramedics within that organization. An explanatory statement was provided to participants with the initial contact. Participants who agreed were asked to sign a consent form if they agreed to progress to the interview stage, this was done using an e-signature service.

Dataset generation

Semi-structured interviews were conducted via recorded videoconferencing software. The interview questions were created by the research team and pilot-tested with a mock participant who would be considered an organizational SME for one of the JAS. The interviewer was located in a private meeting room at the university campus for all interviews and no other parties were present. During the interviews, participants were asked demographic questions pertaining to the service they represented. The audio-visual recording was transcribed by an external digital transcription service (Otter.ai, Mountain View, California, USA) and output was checked for accuracy and edited where required.

Data analysis

The transcribed interviews were analyzed using the six-step reflexive thematic analysis framework as described by Braun and Clarke [28, 29]. NVivo software (Version 14, Lumivero, Colorado USA) was used to record coding and theme generation. An inductive approach to coding at the sematic level and theme development was utilized. The interviews, coding and initial theme development stages of analysis were undertaken by the lead researcher. Further theme development and refinement were undertaken by multiple authors. This approach ensures trustworthiness by addressing credibility, confirmability, dependability and transferability by following the guidelines of Graneheim and Lundman [30] as shown in Table 2. This article also complies with the Standards for Reporting Qualitative Research [31].

Four themes were generated from the data: The Pillars of POCUS, System Buy In, Culture Change in Paramedicine and What’s the Worth. Included within these themes were four subthemes: Clinical Governance, Education, System Infrastructure, and In Gestation. These themes are presented in Fig. 1.

Themes and subthemes

Theme 1: Pillars of POCUS

This theme is defined as the necessary components of a framework for POCUS programs. The central organizing concept of this theme is the requirement of the ‘pillars’ in order to achieve a functional POCUS program. The subthemes of Clinical Governance, Education, and System Infrastructure; were explored in detail by participants. Each ‘pillar’ seemed to have a substantial impact on the function of a POCUS program or were identified as being a key missing feature of some programs.

Sub Theme: Clinical Governance

Clinical governance was, with a few exceptions, notably lacking in many of the participants’ systems. One insight was the dissatisfaction felt by the participants who lead, or were a part of, a system with limited to no governance of paramedic POCUS.

“So I’m extremely embarrassed to say we are not (ensuring competency)” (P04)

“What they (paramedics) are actually describing is an observation of inappropriately performed, poorly governed point-of-care ultrasound in the prehospital environment” (P04).

Conversely, there was a sense of pride in the participants who worked in systems with very strong clinical governance. Participants described varying clinical governance systems, which included medical directors, sonographers and credentialled physicians.

“Every one of our ultrasounds is quality assured in depth and so we put the paramedics through exactly the same education process that the physicians did. Because you can’t compare apples with apples unless you do that.” (P02).

Quality assurance emerged as a central component of clinical governance and participants engaged in discussion of methods to ensure high standards in this area. This included accreditation prior to using the scans to make clinical decisions, regular auditing of scans, and comparison with in-hospital imaging. Clinician feedback was another important element of quality assurance that arose as something often missing from programs that could have an impact on skill development. Quality assurance was identified by participants as a key requirement of health system integration and poor-quality assurance as a major barrier to realizing a benefit to patient outcomes.

“Our audit process includes radiology from the hospital. So the hospitals are quite good to us. They allow us to uplink the radiology. So, you’ll see a patient all the way through to, to finish. And so often we’ll compare the ultrasound to the CT or what happened with the patient.” (P02).

Subtheme: System Infrastructure

Participants reported that the system infrastructure supporting POCUS was a foundational element of high-performing systems. Infrastructure in this context is inclusive of image archiving and storage systems as well as equipment, workflows and infection control. Participants raised the provision of adequate infrastructure as a barrier to implementation or as a challenging area. Another barrier mentioned by the participants to introduction of paramedic POCUS involves the storage and archiving of images to comply with the legal and privacy requirements of patient data storage. One participant described using a work-around in place of a fit for purpose image storage and archiving. Participants described frustration at the need for a workaround and a desire to have a functional system in line with other capabilities of the service.

“They’re (the scans) all put into PowerPoints. Now we’re about to actually have a new one (audit platform) developed in house for us. We’ve got we’ve actually got people who can do it, just getting them away from all the other systems they’re going to build for us. But it’s, it’s painful at the moment. We used to have a system where we, it was quite good, but so we’d have to open a PowerPoint on each case. It is a work around.” (P02).

Conversely, some participants had access to a digital imaging and communication in medicine (DICOM) server attached to the organizations auditing platform. This was uncommon among the participants. By far the most common set-up described by the participants was either no archiving at all, or a simple shared drive on an internal server.

“Yeah, drive, just a shared drive, obviously password protected for the specific people to be able to access.” (P11).

Subtheme: Education

Reported initial and ongoing education for POCUS varied widely. Participants noted that education ranged from short informal sessions to tertiary-level training.

“A small group that have completed the 15 point at (university), postgraduate radiography paper for ultrasound that (redacted) delivers. And then we would tack on a one-day credentialing course, which would be an internal course. And then there will be a set number of scans for each indication that they’ll have to acquire in the field.” (P09).

Participants described undertaking or engaging with external education through private companies, and some services required a formal credentialling through the Australasian Society of Ultrasound in Medicine prior to paramedics undertaking independent scans.

“Our medical director has, I suppose, made sure that we’ve all done a basic introductory into ultrasound use, particularly just for eFAST and that was through a four-hour course. And that was pretty much just a verbal thing. (P06)

Some participants noted significant difficulty with ongoing education support after the initial training period. Those participants in more remote areas reported this as a significant barrier to improving their use of POCUS.

“one of the main challenges I feel is the inability to get someone who’s accredited, proficient and confident to assist with what we might think is the right decision, or a good scan or a you know, a positive image of whatever we’re looking for, to actually go out and do the skill, we can do it, but we’re sort of the blind leading the blind.” (P06).

Theme: System Buy-in

System buy-in as a theme was defined as the need for medical support and system integration to achieve the greatest benefit for paramedic POCUS programs. This theme was raised in different ways by the participants but was associated with the medical support of paramedic POCUS (or lack thereof) as well as integration or acceptance into the wider healthcare system. An idea articulated by participants was that the benefits of a well-governed paramedic POCUS program cannot be fully realized without health system or specific hospital ‘buy-in’ of the program. According to the participants, the current use of POCUS by paramedics throughout Australasia is largely restricted to eFAST scans. One participant described the time savings in a high-performing system that eFAST could provide.

“So 2 minutes there will save 45 minutes downstream. That’s what we tell people. And It did show that. we started we’ve got some work to show that it does move people through the operating room and to CT faster.” (P02).

This represents a true quantifiable benefit of paramedic POCUS, positively affecting patient outcomes. Only one of the participating organizations had reached this level of system buy-in. Participants identified requirements to achieve system buy-in including: significant stakeholder engagement, education benchmarked against other healthcare professions and a clinical governance that meets or exceeds the requirements of other healthcare professions.

“Does that mean we have to do everything? I don’t know. And we’re… but whatever we do, we, we need to do better than them (the hospital).” (P02).

Another key aspect to system buy-in was identified as the support of medical staff. While this might seem intuitive, the services with more evolved clinical governance all had a great deal of support from both medical directors and hospital staff.

“And so my main function is actually to turn that to positive. To explain what ultrasound offers, to explain how valuable it is in the pre-hospital environment, and to also allude to what is happening in other jurisdictions.” (P01).

Trust in the paramedics performing the scans was identified as an integral part of system buy-in and the medical directors seemed to play an intermediary role in bridging the gap between out-of-hospital and hospital. Conversely, if medical support was lacking, participants identified this as a major barrier to training and feedback.

“A bigger challenge is basically getting a buy-in from the hospital. There’s a lot of red tape and policies and politics that you need to get over.” (P11).

Theme: Culture Change in Paramedicine

This theme is defined as the presence of cultural precepts in paramedicine that present barriers to implementation of POCUS into paramedic practice. This theme also encompasses the challenges and re-evaluation of established practices that come with introducing new technologies. Participants expressed that the integration of POCUS into paramedicine represents a significant cultural shift within the profession. Several deeply embedded cultural precepts were highlighted by participants, such as an overemphasis on the importance of scene time and traditional views that ‘paramedics don’t diagnose’. The presence of these precepts were central to the discussion about the implementation of POCUS into paramedic practice.

One prominent cultural precept in paramedicine reported by participants is the emphasis on minimizing scene time. The introduction of POCUS challenges this precept as there is a commonly held belief it's use will add to scene time. Participants addressed this precept in various ways, some advocated that the additional scene time was not as great as imagined.

“The performing of point-of-care ultrasound shouldn’t alter scene time unless it’s clearly going to alter the definitive management or the destination of the patient.” (P04).

Conversely, others advocated for doing the scan whilst in motion to hospital whenever possible.

“Our experience in our HEMS service is that we do a lot of scanning in motion in movement … they’ll frequently scan a patient whilst the logistics of getting a stretcher and extrication device and stuff all ready, you know, and then do repeat serial scans enroute.” (P03).

Another cultural precept of paramedicine is that ‘paramedics don’t diagnose’. POCUS represents the first diagnostic imaging available to paramedics working in the out-of-hospital environment. Integrating POCUS into paramedic practice requires a shift in thinking away from treating symptoms and towards working diagnosis.

“But took a really long time before I really started to appreciate the power it gives me … because it’s to me, it’s the first diagnostic tool I have that truly visually clearly tells me what’s going on inside of the body, rather than guessing from the you know, heart rate or blood pressure.” (P11).

Subtheme: In Gestation

One participant described the state of paramedic POCUS in their organization as ‘gestational’. Participants described the use of POCUS generally as limited. However, most of the participants had plans to expand the use of POCUS in their organizations, identifying it as a key part of future paramedic practice.

“This is just something that we have to do. Otherwise we’ll be left behind. So it’s a it’s almost a non-negotiable.” (P04).

A common message portrayed by the participants was that the use of POCUS in paramedicine is inevitable, but perhaps their services weren’t ready for it.

“So we said ultrasound is something that we think has a huge future in paramedicine in (JAS). However, we are not ready just yet to, to go ahead and expand its use. We think that might be a couple of years away for us.” (P07).

Theme: What’s The Worth?

This theme was generated as a major barrier to the introduction or expansion of paramedic POCUS. Participants recognized that JAS in Australasia are all in a state of austerity in the post COVID-19 era. Recognizing that it’s not just the cost of the devices but the training of staff and maintenance of devices, as well as the purchase of the infrastructure for archiving and audit.

“You know, it’s not cheap equipment, even although it’s, you know, ultrasound whole devices become more portable robust and, you know, relatively cheaper but still, it’s a big investment for the organization, so that the whole cost benefit is still totally unknown.” (P11).

One participant’s organization trialed the rollout of POCUS among a suite of other technologies, but found that other technologies were of more benefit and so chose to discontinue the use of POCUS in that service.

“So last year we ran a year-long trial. We called it an Extended Scope pilot where ultrasound was part of a suite of new things that we were going to trial. We decided at the end of that pilot that ultrasound did add benefit, but there were other devices that would add more benefit and have more bang for the buck.” (P07).

Discussion

This study investigating the organizational use of paramedic POCUS in Australasian JAS and RS developed four themes and four subthemes. These themes collectively illustrate the experiences of participants concerning the implementation and current utilization of paramedic POCUS. The findings highlight that there is variability in clinical governance, education, and the cultural integration of POCUS within JAS. The study results show the importance of robust governance frameworks and continuous quality improvement measures to ensure the efficacy and safety of paramedic POCUS programs.

Clinical governance is a definitive requirement for any high-performing POCUS system and the leadership and oversight of these programs are key drivers of success [32]. Clinical governance in this context exists to oversee the other elements of a POCUS program and to ensure patient safety [14]. Quality assurance of POCUS is necessary for any system that prioritizes patient safety, and is a mandatory requirement for emergency medicine physicians in Australasia [33]. In the European context a recent study found that more than 25% of HEMS services did not save images or video clips for quality assurance and less than one third of services surveyed had any credentialling processes [9]. It’s important to note that many European HEMS services are staffed by physicians yet face similar issues to those identified in this study. Aziz et al. (2024) described a governance and audit system for paramedic and physician POCUS in a European HEMS service. This study found that the governance framework implemented in this system resulted in a high image quality score and high agreement with the expert reviewer. The service managed to achieve a quality assurance rate of 96.7% [34]. There are some leaders in this space who have demonstrated benefits such as reduced time to theatre and decreased iatrogenic harm [35]. Conversely, there are many services in this study that are struggling to maintain adequate quality assurance and governance. Clinical governance should be a mandatory component of any prospective paramedic POCUS system.

There is an expanding literature demonstrating system integration of out-of-hospital interventions improving patient outcomes. The foremost example of this is the implementation of emergency department bypass for patients with ST elevation myocardial infarction (STEMI). A study by Hutchison et al. (2013) demonstrated significant reduction in door-to-balloon times (100 min vs. 54 min) by implementing out-of-hospital transmission of 12-lead ECGs to the ED for early activation of cardiac catheterization lab [36]. This intervention integrated paramedic management into the healthcare system and allowed for a more seamless transition of care as well as early activation of in-hospital specialist teams, which translated to improved patient outcomes. Similarly, a study by Bodnar et al. (2013) showed a significant difference in time to in-hospital intervention between out-of-hospital positive and negative eFAST or patients where eFAST was not performed (20 min vs. 138 min) [35]. The performance of the out-of-hospital eFAST by this paramedic/physician team led to more timely operative intervention in patients with hemorrhagic shock. These two examples showcase the potential impact on patients that out-of-hospital interventions can achieve when integrated into the broader healthcare system.

Paramedicine in Australasia is a relatively new nationally regulated profession. When compared to other healthcare professions, Paramedicine has historically been perceived as a ‘blue-collar’ occupation with a focus on technical tasks [37]. Over the past decade, the professionalization of paramedics through higher education and professional registration has progressed in some jurisdictions, while others continue to lag behind [37, 38]. Despite these advancements, the field of paramedicine remains challenged by cultural issues that may impede the implementation of a POCUS program. Chief amongst these is an overreliance on scene time as a key performance indicator. Globally, ambulance services have prioritized reducing scene time, a practice heavily influenced by extensive research on trauma patient outcomes and the concept of the ‘Golden Hour.’ This emphasis on minimizing scene time has deeply influenced the professional culture of paramedicine and EMS professionals internationally [39–41]. Concerns that POCUS will extend scene times are prevalent, however these concerns are not supported by the literature. A study by Gamberini et al. (2023) found that performance of eFAST scans does not significantly affect scene times and there is some literature to suggest that performing these exams in a moving vehicle is feasible and accurate [42, 43]. Despite this, the benefits of allocating additional scene time to obtain high-quality scans, particularly when integrated systems support emergency department bypass or the activation of massive transfusion protocols reducing the time to definitive care, outweigh the risks of an inconsequential delay at scene.

When introducing new technology or skills in health care services, health economics are a vital consideration, and economic evaluations are increasingly employed to inform these decisions [44]. High-cost technologies, such as out-of-hospital extracorporeal membrane oxygenation, which only benefit a limited patient population, may be overlooked in favor of alternatives that provide benefits to a broader group. In their 2020 study, Wong et al. examined the health economics of POCUS worldwide, demonstrating notable financial benefits within Australasian healthcare settings such as hospitals, remote clinics, and general practices [45]. There are many potential benefits of POCUS use by paramedics such as improved procedural success, improved diagnostic ability and more targeted management, but there is much less research to draw from to inform an economic analysis [3]. Outcome measures such as ‘disability adjusted life years’, ‘years of life lost’ and ‘morbidity’ and ‘mortality’ can provide a more useful economic outcome measure to inform practice [46]. As POCUS continues to expand in paramedicine, measures such as ‘number needed to scan’ [47] could be utilized alongside comprehensive economic evaluations that consider entire patient journey outcomes, rather than focusing solely on the benefit experienced in RS or JAS.

Finally, in interpreting participants’ accounts, we interpreted POCUS implementation within a socio-technical system, drawing on a systems theory lens to inform the analysis [22, 23]. This lens treats outcomes as emerging from interactions between people (paramedics, medical directors, hospital teams), processes (education, credentialing, audit), and infrastructure (handheld devices, image archiving, data flows) across micro (clinician/patient), meso (service/organization), and macro (policy/funding) levels. Framed this way in light of the themes generated, Pillars of POCUS reflects interdependent couplings among clinical governance, education, and system infrastructure; System Buy-In captures inter-organizational alignment required for downstream efficiencies; Culture Change highlights recalibration of local norms (e.g. scene-time metrics) to whole-of-journey value; and What’s the Worth foregrounds the resource dependencies that ultimately determine feasibility. This systems stance implies that improvement efforts should prioritize coordinated redesign of clinical governance, education, and infrastructure with pathways for archiving, audit, and bidirectional feedback rather than falling into the trap of implementing isolated training or equipment purchases to support POCUS implementation [22, 23].

Limitations

There are some important limitations to this study. Firstly, there was a disproportionately limited response rate from RS as opposed to JAS. However, all of the JAS in Australasia were respondents, allowing a full picture of the JAS portion of the sample. Further, the reflexive thematic analysis methodology of perceptions and experiences of medical directors and clinical leads means that the results are context specific to Australasian JAS and RS, which may reduce the transferability of the results to other settings. The results of this study should be read in the context of these limitations.

Conclusion

While the adoption of POCUS for paramedics shows promise and has been implemented in certain jurisdictions across Australasia, ongoing efforts are required to standardize education, ensure robust clinical governance, and justify economic investment. Additionally, the cultural shift required to integrate POCUS into paramedic practice, especially the emphasis on minimizing scene time and the traditional view that 'paramedics don’t diagnose', present notable challenges. Addressing these barriers will require targeted education, stakeholder engagement and demonstrable benefits of POCUS integration. Future efforts should focus on developing standardized training programs, robust clinical governance frameworks, and conducting comprehensive economic evaluations to support the widespread adoption of POCUS in paramedic practice, ultimately leading to enhanced patient care and outcomes.
