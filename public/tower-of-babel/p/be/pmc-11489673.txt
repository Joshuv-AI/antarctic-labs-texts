Modal-nexus auto-encoder for multi-modality cellular data integration and imputation
Authors: Zhenchao Tang, Guanxing Chen, Shouzhi Chen, Jianhua Yao, Linlin You, Calvin Yu-Chian Chen
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
AbstractHeterogeneous feature spaces and technical noise hinder the cellular data integration and imputation. The high cost of obtaining matched data across modalities further restricts analysis. Thus, there’s a critical need for deep learning approaches to effectively integrate and impute unpaired multi-modality single-cell data, enabling deeper insights into cellular behaviors. To address these issues, we introduce the Modal-Nexus Auto-Encoder (Monae). Leveraging regulatory relationships between modalities and employing contrastive learning within modality-specific auto-encoders, Monae enhances cell representations in the unified space. The integration capability of Monae furnishes it with modality-complementary cellular representations, enabling the generation of precise intra-modal and cross-modal imputation counts for extensive and complex downstream tasks. In addition, we develop Monae-E (Monae-Extension), a variant of Monae that can converge rapidly and support biological discoveries. Evaluations on various datasets have validated Monae and Monae-E’s accuracy and robustness in multi-modality cellular data integration and imputation.Subject terms: Machine learning, Biomedical engineering, Computational models, Data integration, Computational science

Introduction

Current advancements in single-cell measurement technologies enable the acquisition of multi-modality data at cellular resolution, encompassing RNA and DNA accessibility. The rapid growth of these datasets necessitates the development of deep learning methodologies for multi-modality analysis, aimed at elucidating the life states of organisms and facilitating applications in fields like drug discovery1,2. Recent studies have identified the basic tasks in multi-modality single-cell data analysis: integration and imputation3. Figure 1 shows the definitions of multi-modality integration and imputation. Integration involves bridging the gaps between modalities and identifying biological clusters within a comprehensive multi-modality data atlas to unveil cellular identities and functions4. Imputation addresses the deduction of missing signals attributable to measurement constraints, aiming to restore the authentic state of biological systems and thereby extend the applicability of imputation counts to complex downstream analyses5,6. This paper proposes a unified framework to address the principal challenges in integration and imputation, respectively.

Illustration of the multi-modality integration and imputation.

a Integration across modalities. Modal data initially exhibit gaps that are bridged through integration to obtain the unified embeddings (separating cell types). b Intra-modal imputation. Sparse observations within a single modality are recovered to produce a complete data matrix. c Cross-modal imputation. Information from a source modality is leveraged to predict the target modality matrix for cell-unpaired scenarios. d The proposed method supports simultaneous integration and imputation on unpaired data. For integration, cross-modal cells of the same cell type are clustered together. For imputation, our method generates both intra-modal counts (fixing gray dropout observations) and cross-modal counts (filling blanks in dashed rectangles or circles) based on the integrated cell representations.

The first challenge arises from integration. Multi-modality single-cell datasets usually consist of unpaired cells with cross-modal feature mismatches. As illustrated in Fig. 1 a, we represent single-cell data with structured matrix format. Rows in the matrix are the single-cell samples (cell entities), and columns in the matrix are the characteristics (features) that describe the single-cell samples. For matrices of different modalities, their feature spaces are independent of each other, and there is no pairing relationship between samples across modalities (we don’t know whether the observations from two modalities come from the same cell). Due to the inconsistent feature spaces of the two modalities, there will be a significant gap in the embeddings of the same cell type between heterogeneous modalities. The goal of integration is to narrow the gap and group cells of the same type together (maintain biological heterogeneity)7.

Mapping multi-modality data in different feature spaces into a unified joint space is a challenge due to the lack of known cell type annotation and cell-paired information. Traditionally, multi-modality data are projected into a joint feature space, followed by adjustments to mediate modal discrepancies. However, some studies have found that projection will directly destroy the biological heterogeneity8–10. Therefore, recent methods transform the multi-modality integration task into end-to-end cell representation learning11,12. Experiments have proven that multi-view embedding learning can enhance cell representation, thereby improving multi-modality integration performance13. Moreover, techniques such as Concerto, employing contrastive learning, scBERT, utilizing the Transformer model for gene representation, and CLUE, constructing the cross-modal guidance graph, illustrate the advancements in cell representation learning (different views on cell representation), albeit primarily within RNA modality or RNA and ATAC modalities14–16. In this paper, due to the expensive computational overhead of computing all gene embeddings, we focus on cross-modal regulatory graph learning and cell contrastive learning as application trends to address the integration challenge.

The second challenge arises from imputation. Current measurement technologies commonly face the shortcomings of low capture rates. For instance, in scRNA-seq data, a significant portion of gene expression levels are recorded as zero or low counts, referred to as dropout events. Dropout events introduce high noise into the true representation of cellular data, making downstream analysis tasks difficult. Imputation serves as a corrective method to restore real data values. Figure 1b outlines the process of denoising single-cell data from a given modality to produce counts reflective of the actual modality. The intra-modal imputation ensures that zero values or low expression counts in the original data are restored to approximate true values. Moreover, as depicted in Fig. 1c, cross-modal imputation leverages data from one modality to reconstruct data in another, termed modality prediction (or modality translation). This process is particularly relevant in scenarios lacking joint measurements17.

Since RNA modality imputation is extensively utilized, most existing methods are specifically designed for this modality and fall into two categories: model-based methods18–22 and deep learning-based methods23–26. Distinguishing between ”false” (dropout) and ”true” (biological gene silencing) zero counts is a challenge for them if only RNA modality is utilized27. Therefore, a few methods have been designed for multi-modality data and implemented cross-modal imputation17,28. These approaches validate that leveraging multi-modality information aids in distinguishing dropout, yet their application remains constrained to cell-paired datasets. It is worth mentioning that multi-task methods that simultaneously achieve integration and imputation have been proposed recently11,12,29, but these methods fall far short of methods dedicated to integration when faced with scenarios where multi-modality data are completely unpaired, and the imputation performance will also be affected.

Based on these existing studies on integration and imputation tasks, we propose two hypotheses:

For integration, learning regulatory relationships between modalities can facilitate contrastive learning in the joint space and eliminate gaps between modalities. Clustering can enhance the separation between clusters, thereby preserving biological heterogeneity.

In this study, we propose Modal-Nexus Auto-Encoder (Monae) based on two hypotheses, an unsupervised learning method for simultaneous integration and imputation. Monae learns embeddings from two views of multi-modality single-cell data. Specifically, the feature connection across modalities simulates a regulatory relationship. After we construct a graph with feature connection information, Monae learns the regulatory relationship by reconstructing the graph. Moreover, contrastive learning between cells enhances the heterogeneity of each sample embedding. Monae utilizes contrastive learning combined with adaptive clustering to enhance the discriminability of cell representations. Finally, the contrastive cell embeddings serve as integration cell representations. The integration result not only eliminates the gap between modalities but also preserves biological heterogeneity. For imputation, intuitively, the missing information in the current modality can be supplemented by other modalities, and the cell representation based on modal nexus will be able to comprehensively characterize the state of each cell. Therefore, Monae utilizes integrated cell representations to generate accurate imputed counts, as depicted in Fig. 1 d. Specifically, Monae supports multiplying graph-linked embedding and contrastive cell embedding to generate imputation counts in specified modalities. In addition, we can add entities such as cross-modal cells and motifs as nodes to graph-linked embedding learning to build Monae-E (Monae-Extension). These extended entities provide more biological knowledge, can significantly accelerate Monae-E convergence, and enable us to discover cell type-specific marker genes and TF-target genes network through entity embedding. Experimental results on various datasets demonstrate that Monae and Monae-E are accurate and robust to multi-modality cellular data integration and imputation. Results

Model architecture and experiments

Monae is an unsupervised learning framework composed of multiple autoencoders, as shown in Fig. 2. Monae builds a coarse-grained graph (guidance) based on the regulatory relationships between different modalities. The nodes in the graph correspond to an independent feature in a certain modality. The graph VAE in Monae learns the representation of each node, depicted in Fig. 2a. Within Monae, distinct autoencoders represent individual modalities, and their asymmetric networks generate the positive and negative samples essential for contrastive learning (Fig. 2b). Contrastive learning within Monae is enhanced by the incorporation of adaptive clustering, augmenting the distinction among cell clusters (Fig. 2c). Monae fuses cell representation from the joint space with modal-nexus node features to compute imputation counts. Through iterative epochs, Monae acquires graph-linked and contrastive cell embeddings, with the latter serving as the integrative cell representation (Fig. 2d). Furthermore, we add multi-modality cells (and optional item: motifs entities) as nodes to the graph-linked guidance in Fig. 2a. We call this variant Monae-E (Monae-Extension). We can obtain the embeddings of all nodes from Graph VAE, including cell embeddings and feature embeddings. We use cell embeddings to replace the count's input of the asymmetric network, and the subsequent process is consistent with Monae, as shown in Fig. 2e. Details of Monae and Monae-E are given in Methods.

The overall architecture of Monae.

a Graph-linked embedding learning. The coarse graph-linked guidance is constructed from multi-modality relationships, and a graph encoder learns the feature embedding of multi-modality nodes. A graph decoder then generates the fine graph-linked guidance. b Construction of positive and negative samples. Asymmetric networks for each modality (e.g., scRNA-seq, scATAC-seq) transform cells into embeddings. Positive sample pairs are formed from teacher and student branches. c Contrastive cell embedding learning. Positive sample pairs are pulled together while negative samples are pushed apart through contrastive learning, yielding discriminative cell embeddings. d The learned contrastive cell embeddings serve as integration representations for unsupervised clustering. They are also combined with the multi-modal feature (node) embeddings to reconstruct imputation counts. e Further extending (a) to build Monae-E (Monae-Extension). Cells are added as nodes to the graph-linked guidance, and Graph VAE outputs all node embeddings. The following process is the same as (b), (c), and (d). Monae-E uses the cell embedding as the input of the asymmetric network and the feature embedding to reconstruct the input.

Batch integration in multi-modality

For a single modality in multi-modality data, multiple batches of measurements can lead to batch effects within a single modality. Monae eliminates challenges such as batch effects in the process of contrastive learning. Taking the cell embedding of the Teacher branch as the cell representation, each cell is assigned a positive sample. Positive samples come from cell embeddings of the Student branch. In the real world, although no identical cells exist, representations of cells of the same type are closer than representations of cells of different types. Therefore, Monae clusters Teacher embeddings and Student embeddings of the same cell together, excluding the representation of any other cells. Eventually, similar cells clump together because they are of the same type. Monae has batch integration as well as multi-modality integration. We evaluate the batch integration performance of Monae, Monae-E, and three representative methods on Ma-2020 and Muto-2021 (Supplementary Fig. 5). There are 4 and 5 batches, respectively, in a single modality of Ma-2020 and Muto-2021. It is known that the adversarial learning strategy in GLUE can achieve excellent batch integration. Monae and Monae-E achieve batch integration performance similar to GLUE. In addition, Muto-2021 has obvious batch effects, and the SAS (Seurat alignment score) of Monae exceeds that of GLUE. The visualization of Monae batch integration results is shown in Supplementary Fig. 6.

Interpretable integration results

Monae facilitates the integration of multiple modalities, including but not limited to three distinct modalities. In our study, we employed Monae to integrate the Mouse Cortex dataset, which encompasses three modalities: RNA, ATAC, and Methylation. Each modality is associated with different criteria for cell type annotation, specifically, the RNA modality identifies 8 cell types, the ATAC modality identifies 10 cell types, and the Methylation modality identifies 16 cell types. It is important to note that these three modalities provide different phenotypic views of the mouse cortex. Consequently, we can select a single modality classification criterion to annotate single-cell samples. In Supplementary Fig. 7a, we provide the UMAP visualizations derived from the classification criteria specific to the RNA modality. The results indicate that Monae successfully performs integration. However, as illustrated in the left section of Supplementary Fig. 7a, clusters such as the one corresponding to the cell type CGE exhibit insufficient density. We can obtain fine-grained cell subtypes based on these clusters.

Previous studies have pointed out34,35 that under the RNA modality classification criterion, MGE and CGE can be divided into two subtypes; under the ATAC modality classification criterion, L6-IT and Vip can be divided into two subtypes. In this study, we can pay additional attention to the rare cell type mDL-3. Since the Methylation modality classification criterion has fine-grained categories, we take Methylation data as the reference dataset. Let the cell embeddings of the RNA modality and ATAC modality be used as query datasets, and we use kNN to implement automatic annotation. For convenience, we merge mSst-1 and mSst-2 into mSst and merge mNdnf-1 and mNdnf-2 into mNdnf in the Methylation modality. The complete cell classification results are shown in Supplementary Fig. 7b.

The flow of categories is quantified from Supplementary Fig. 7a, b, in order to derive Supplementary Fig. 7c. Cells associated with mPv and mSst are designated with a light blue flow, while those related to mNdnf and mVip are marked with a light pink flow, and cells pertaining to mDL-3 are indicated with a light green flow. The findings presented in Supplementary Fig. 7c demonstrate that the joint embedding generated by Monae aligns with established knowledge, thereby confirming the interpretability of integration results.

Large-scale datasets contain a rich variety of cell types and, thus, greater biological heterogeneity. Therefore, large-scale data scenarios are a challenge for multi-modality integration. We integrated Human 15 organs (RNA modality: 4,062,980 cells, ATAC modality: 720,613 cells) using GLUE, CoVEL, Monae, and Monae-E. We first evaluate the efficiency of these methods on datasets of different scales, including the time spent on unsupervised training and memory usage (RES and MEM), as shown in Fig. 4a. Due to the additional biological information, Monae-E can converge significantly faster, and it takes the least training time. It also means that Monae-E needs more memory to process the additional information, but Monae-E’s memory requirements are much lower than CoVEL. Considering the efficiency of time and memory, Monae-E can be used as practical software.

Imputation of intra-modality

RNA modality imputation is often referred to as denoising, corresponding to intra-modal imputation. Our intuition for using the imputation of Monae and Monae-E comes from: for the RNA modality, the measurement techniques will lead to many zero values in the counts, and we use the cell representation information from other modalities (such as ATAC modality) to fill in the cell representation from the RNA modality. Therefore, multi-modality integration provides us with modal complementary cell representations that can be used to generate realistic counting signals.

We use MultiVI, as well as methods dedicated to intra-modal imputation: MAGIC and DCA as baselines. We evaluate baselines and our methods on Muto-2021. Following the benchmark’s standard27, the quality of the imputation results can be reflected by the clustering (PCA dimensionality reduction on raw counts or imputation counts), because there are too many zero-value signals (dropout) in the raw counts, which can reduce the biological heterogeneity of the cellular PCA embedding. As a result, the differences between clusters are not significant enough, as shown in ’Raw’ of Fig. 5a. In Fig. 5a, the clusters of cells after Monae and Monae-E imputation are more separated from each other, and thus our methods effectively fill in the dropout in the raw data. The quantitative results are shown in Fig. 5b, where the overall performance of Monae and Monae-E outperforms baselines. In addition, Supplementary Figs. 11, 12 show the RNA modality imputation results (intra-modal imputation) on 10x-Multiome, Chen-2019, and Ma-2020.

Imputation of cross-modality

Certain properties of ATAC modality data make analysis more difficult compared to RNA modality. The read counts of ATAC modality are very low, resulting in very sparse data. Since the ATAC modality is sparser than the RNA modality, the difference in cell clusters of the former is significantly lower than that of the latter. Therefore, the clustering metrics of the ATAC modality are all lower than those of the RNA modality. To improve the biological heterogeneity of ATAC modality data, one approach is to impute data within the ATAC modality, which is the same approach as using Monae or Monae-E to impute data within the RNA modality. The other is to directly generate data in the ATAC modality based on the RNA modality. For the latter, see Generation of imputation counts. The cross-modality imputation can also be called modality prediction or modality translation. In practical applications, when facing multi-modality data with unmatched cells, Monae’s and Monae-E’s cross-modality generation can complete unpaired multi-modality samples, thereby saving the high cost of joint measurements.

We use scButterfly, MultiVI, JAMIE, UnitedNet, and BABEL as baselines. We translate RNA modality data on Muto-2021 to ATAC modality. The evaluation results of cross-modal imputation are shown in Fig. 5c, and the results on 10x-Multiome, Chen-2019, and Ma-2020 are shown in Supplementary Fig. 14. Excellent imputation performance is achieved for both Monae and Monae-E. For these four datasets, the UMAP visualization of translation from RNA modality to ATAC modality is shown in Supplementary Fig. 13, and the proposed methods recover the biological heterogeneity under ATAC modality. Overall, Monae and Monae-E translated atlases proficiently characterize differences between cells and have the potential to reduce noise in the original modality and facilitate cell type identification. Similarly, we evaluate the imputation results from the ATAC modality to the RNA modality. The evaluation results of cross-modal imputation on Muto-2021 are shown in Fig. 5d. While scButterfly performs better, it is worth noting that Monae and Monae-E achieve performance close to that of scButterfly. The UMAP visualization of translation from ATAC modality to RNA modality is shown in Supplementary Fig. 15. The results on 10x-Multiome, Chen-2019, and Ma-2020 are shown in Supplementary Fig. 16. Monae achieves outstanding performance on these three datasets.

Biological discovery application of Monae-E

We validate Monae-E’s ability to discover biological insights on Muto-2021. The following facts are known: HNF4A encodes a transcription factor (TF) that drives the differentiation of proximal tubules (PT)46; the AP-2 transcription factor family includes proteins encoded by TFAP2A and TFAP2B, which respectively regulate the development of the distal convoluted tubule (DCT) and the thick ascending limb (TAL)47; PT_VCAM1 cells are a subset of proximal renal tubular epithelial cells that express VCAM133.

During graph-linked embedding learning, cells are added as nodes, allowing us to obtain joint embeddings of cells and genes, as visualized in Fig. 6a. All four marker genes appear close to the corresponding cell types. This result demonstrates that Monae-E correctly preserves cellular heterogeneity and retains relevant genes for specific cell types. In addition, we visualize the predicted gene expression patterns of these four marker genes on Monae-E’s imputation results. As shown in Fig. 6b, Monae-E’s imputation results also retain the differential expression patterns of marker genes (highlighted in specific cell clusters).

For the most similar peaks: chr1:100719411-100719996, which is a region located about 60 kb upstream of the VCAM1 gene and has been confirmed to interact with the VCAM1 promoter33.

This result once again verifies the regulatory relationship between peaks and genes (known research: RELA binding sites are located in the chr1:100719411-100719996 region. For the blue nodes (no research support found), we choose the top 5 genes. The expression patterns of these five candidate genes in the two modalities are consistent with HNF4A (Fig. 6g). The above results verify Monae-E’s ability in biological discovery.

Discussion

In this study, we propose Monae, an unsupervised learning method for the integration and imputation of multi-modality cellular data. The design of Monae follows two novel motivations, and we use experiments to validate them: (1) For integration, learning regulatory relationships between modalities can facilitate contrastive learning in the joint space and eliminate gaps between modalities. Besides, adaptive clustering of Monae can enhance the separation between clusters, thereby preserving biological heterogeneity. (2) For imputation, the missing information in the current modality can be supplemented by other modalities. Therefore, Monae utilizes integration cell representations to generate accurate imputed counts. Our extensive experiments demonstrate the robustness and effectiveness of Monae across a range of datasets and scenarios, including multi-modality integration, intra-modal and cross-modal imputation. The results obtained suggest that the Monae not only advances the current state-of-the-art but also serves as a versatile tool for the comprehensive analysis of complex multi-modality single-cell data. Through the application of Monae, we achieve more discriminative joint embedding and more accurate imputation counts. In addition, we established a variant of Monae, Monae-E (Monae-Extension), by adding entities such as cross-modal cells and motifs. Monae-E introduces additional biological knowledge, which can accelerate convergence and support biological discoveries, such as cell type-specific marker genes and TF-target genes network. Overall, Monae and Monae-E improve biological heterogeneity preservation compared to existing methods. These enhancements facilitate a deeper understanding of the cellular identity and functional states within biological systems. Moreover, the flexibility of Monae and Monae-E in dealing with various data modalities and scales positions them as scalable solutions for diverse cellular data analysis.

Related work

Integration

Methods for integrating multi-modal single-cell data can be categorized into two primary types: semi-supervised and unsupervised approaches55. Semi-supervised approaches employ paired observations from multiple modalities. MultiVI employs a series of variational auto-encoders to effectively learn from multi-modal data and facilitate the alignment of joint embeddings11. CLUE implements auxiliary tasks to facilitate the alignment of heterogeneous feature spaces16. scMoGNN employs graph neural networks (GNNs) to identify connections between different cell modalities and utilizes established single-modal embeddings to facilitate the learning of a joint embedding that encompasses multiple modalities56. However, due to the high cost of cell-paired data, unsupervised methods have become popular. Unsupervised methodologies are employed to amalgamate multi-modal measurements in the absence of paired data. Seurat8, LIGER9, and Harmony10 initially conduct feature transformation prior to addressing the discrepancies between modalities within a shared space. However, this process of feature transformation may compromise the biological heterogeneity inherent in the data. The matrix factorization approach known as iNMF41 is capable of circumventing the need for projection operations, but linear properties cannot capture complex biological interactions. For other methods, bindSC is constrained by its reliance on RNA and ATAC modalities, and it exhibits limitations in terms of scalability40. Pamona57 employs nonlinear manifold alignment to synchronize data from various modalities; however, it is unable to effectively align data that is uniformly distributed. GLUE39 uses a guidance graph to fuse multi-modality information, but its output integration results still have room for improvement. CoVEL13 fully utilizes and fuses the information in single-cell multi-modality data. Experiments have proven that multi-view embedding learning can enhance cell representation, thereby improving multi-modality integration performance. However, the architecture of CoVEL is complex and difficult to apply to a wide range of datasets.

Imputation

Since imputation is widely used in RNA modalities, most current methods are only designed for RNA modality and are divided into two categories: model-based methods and deep learning-based methods. Model-based methods restore the expression counts of target genes based on similar cell information or similar gene information. SAVER18 exploits information between genes and cells to improve the expression values of all genes. MAGIC19 applies data diffusion to share information between similar cells to account for missing values. Deep learning-based methods can capture the hidden distribution of gene expression counts and learn the parameters of the distribution model to estimate missing values. AutoImpute58 is an auto-encoder-based imputation method that learns the inherent distribution of input data and estimates missing values. DCA26 captures nonlinear gene-gene correlations by introducing a negative binomial model with zero inflation while accounting for the count distribution sparsity of the data to impute missing values. These single-modality methods lack cell state signals from other modalities. However, only a few methods are currently designed for multi-modality data. UnitedNet17 uses multi-task learning to achieve cross-modal imputation, but it can only be used on datasets with cell pairing information. scButterfly29, MultiVI11, and JAMIE12 support multi-modality integration and cross-modal imputation. Similar to UnitedNet, it is necessary to provide partial pairing signals. Monae and Monae-E are methods based on deep learning and support multi-modality integration, intra-modal imputation, and cross-modal imputation in scenarios without paired information.

Methods

In this section, we first provide a general overview of Monae (Overall of Monae). For integration, Monae learning embeddings from two views (graph embeddings and sample embeddings): the feature connection graph across modalities simulates a regulatory relationship (Graph embedding learning); contrastive learning between cells enhances the heterogeneity of each sample (Contrastive cell embedding learning). For imputation, Monae utilizes integration cell representations multiplied with graph-linked embedding to generate accurate imputation counts (Generation of imputation counts). Then, we build a variant of Monae: Monae-E (Variant of Monae: Monae-E). Finally, we show the usage of Monae and Monae-E (Usage of Monae and Monae-E).

Overall of Monae

Specifically, Monae is an unsupervised learning framework composed of multiple autoencoders, as illustrated in Fig. 2. Initially, we build a coarse-grained graph (guidance) based on the regulatory relationships between different modalities. One node in the graph corresponds to an independent feature in a certain modality. The graph VAE in Monae learns the representation of each node, and message passing will enable each node to aggregate regulatory relationships from other modalities. The graph VAE then constructs a refined graph-linked framework characterized by dense connections reflective of modal-nexus features, depicted in Fig. 2a. Within Monae, distinct autoencoders represent individual modalities, and their asymmetric networks generate the positive and negative samples essential for contrastive learning (Fig. 2b). Contrastive learning within Monae is enhanced by the incorporation of adaptive clustering, augmenting the distinction among cell clusters (Fig. 2c). Ultimately, Monae fuses cell representation from the joint space with modal-nexus node features to compute imputation counts. Through iterative epochs, Monae proficiently acquires graph-linked and contrastive cell embeddings, with the latter serving as the integrative cell representation indicative of successful multi-modality integration (Fig. 2d). Moreover, Monae is designed to facilitate the multiplication of graph-linked and contrastive cell embeddings to yield imputation counts for designated modalities.

There are ∑k=1K∣Vk∣ nodes in guidance graph, each node is a feature in modalities. E denotes the set of edges. Contrastive cell embedding learning

In this section, we exploit contrastive learning to enhance the discriminability of cell representation. Specific details include three steps: 1. construct positive and negative samples for each cell, 2. use contrastive learning to improve the discriminability of cell representation, and 3. adaptive clustering further increases cluster separability in the latent space.

Generation of positive and negative samples

Each modality corresponds to an asymmetric network. The asymmetric network corresponding to each modality is parameter-non-shared. We generate positive samples of cell representations in an asymmetric network. Figure 2 b shows an example of the RNA modality. The n − th cell in RNA modality x1(n)∈X1(n=1,…,N1) is transformed into x1(n),input∈X1input. x1(n),input is gene expression of cell n (for Monae-E, x1(n),input is the initial embedding of cell n). Further, we use the parameter-sharing Dense layer to reduce the dimensionality of the count's data to the latent space to output the cell representations of the Teacher branch and Student branch. In addition, we add a random dropout mask before the output of the Student branch for data augmentation in the latent space (simulating different sequencing depths). We set 2 Dense layers for the Teacher branch and the Student branch. The cell representations output by the Teacher branch and the Student branch both correspond to the same cell. We use a pair of vectors zteacher∈Rm and zstudent∈Rm to construct a positive sample pair. For N1 samples, we obtain two corresponding positive sample matrices Z1teacher∈RN1×m and Z1student∈RN1×m, where cells located in the same row across two matrices form a positive sample pair. Therefore, in the k-th modality, we can obtain Zkteacher∈RNk×m and Zkstudent∈RNk×m. In the context of a given cell, all other cells and their associated positive samples are considered negative samples for that specific cell.

For manual data augmentation, such as perturbing the input data, the expression of key genes or peaks may be destroyed. Recent work has shown that perturbations at the embedding level are more suitable for data augmentation63. Therefore, we use the Teacher output and Student output from the same cell to construct positive samples, and other cell embeddings in the same batch as negative samples, which has been proven to be effective in natural language research64.

Variant of Monae: Monae-E

Inspired by entity joint learning66,67, we add multi-modal cells as nodes to the graph-linked guidance in Fig. 2a. This design can promote the model to learn feature embedding and cell embedding at the same time, and we call this variant Monae-E (Monae-Extension).

Specifically, the process of extending graph-linked guidance is as follows. For RNA modality, if gene i is expressed in a given cell j, an edge is added between gene i and cell j. We normalize gene expression to [0,1] as the weight of edge wij. For ATAC (or other modality), if peak (or other feature) i is expressed in a given cell j, an edge is added, and the weight of edge wij is set to 1. In the guidance of Monae-E, we cancel the edge sign, that is, let any sij = 1 (as shown in Supplementary Fig. 8, in Monae, the edge sign is beneficial to biological heterogeneity, but canceling the edge sign does not significantly affect the overall score). Next, we initialize and update the node embedding using the same method as Graph embedding learning.

We can obtain the embeddings of all nodes from Graph VAE, including cell embeddings and feature embeddings. We use cell embeddings to replace the count's input of the asymmetric network, and the subsequent process is consistent with Contrastive cell embedding learning and Generation of imputation counts, as shown in Fig. 2e.

For Monae and Monae-E, the inputs are a guidance graph and a per-modal expression matrix. The outputs are embeddings of all cells in the joint space (Fig. 2d) and imputation counts. Generally, when we input raw multi-modality single-cell data, after Monae (or Monae-E) is unsupervisedly trained on the data, Monae (or Monae-E) will return the integration cell representations, which remove the gaps between modalities and retain biological heterogeneity. We are able to identify biological clusters from integration cell representations, thereby discovering the cellular identity and function of biological systems. In addition, Monae (or Monae-E) generates imputation counts that recover biosignals lost due to measurement techniques. Therefore, the imputation counts can be generalized to more complex downstream tasks.

Monae-E can explore biological knowledge based on feature embeddings. First, based on the flexible extended graph, we can further add motif entities as nodes to the graph-linked guidance. If there is a motif in the region where the peak is located, an edge is added between the peak and the motif. After Monae-E’s graph-linked embedding learning, we can get the feature embeddings corresponding to the gene, peak, and motif. Further, we use feature embeddings to infer the TF-target genes network. We follow the similarity-based post-processing method to infer target genes67, which follows the following assumption: the target gene embedding is close to the TF motif embedding and the TF gene embedding, indicating that the expression of the target gene is related to the expression of the TF gene and the accessibility of the TF motif.

Statistics and reproducibility

We utilized the NVIDIA GeForce RTX A6000 to train Monae and Monae-E. The Adam optimizer, set at a learning rate of 0.001, was employed for the adjustment of the model parameters68. Let (α1 = 0.3, α2 = 0.25, α3 = 0.25, α4 = 0.2). We set the batch size to 16. We randomly split 10% of the cells as a validation set for stopping training. If the validation loss does not decrease for 5 consecutive epochs, the learning rate is multiplied by 0.1.
