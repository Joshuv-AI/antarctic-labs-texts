Anomer-Selective Vorbrüggen Reaction for the Catalytic Synthesis of C2‑Deoxynucleoside Analogues
Authors: Tanner L. McMinn, Caleb T. Kozuszek, Bradley A. Owen, Nick M. Legaux, William A. Hearne, Frank A. Leibfarth, Michel R. Gagné
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
The stereoselective formation of N-glycoside linkages in 2-deoxycarbohydrates remains challenging, with relevance to antiviral and anticancer nucleoside analogues. Here, we identify imidodiphosphorimidate (IDPi) silylium Lewis acid catalysts as highly effective for the anomer-selective, high-yield coupling (up to 98%) of 2-deoxy and 2,6-dideoxy donors with TMS–N-based acceptors. The highest performing catalyst was general with respect to glycosyl donors, including 2-deoxyriboses, galactosides, and 2,6-dideoxypyranosides, and nitrogen-containing heterocycles, including carbazoles, indoles, and purines. Catalyst structure–selectivity relationships indicated the importance of matching the stereochemistry of the catalyst to the sugar as well as the electron-deficient aryl groups on the BINOL scaffold of IDPi. Mechanistic studies, including density functional theory (DFT), VT NMR spectroscopy, and HRMS suggested the viability of an IDPi–oxocarbenium covalent intermediate that is analogous to the powerful glycosyl triflate. Should it be capable of SN2-like reactivity, this structure could compete with the classic ion-paired scenario (SN1).

Introduction

Therapeutic nucleoside, nucleotide, and base analogues, collectively known as “nucleoside analogues”, are potent agents that inhibit essential cellular processes, like DNA and RNA synthesis and purinergic signaling. , These compounds play pivotal roles in cancer therapy and as antivirals, boasting numerous FDA-approved analogues and representing the largest class of small-molecule antivirals today. , The development of azidothymidine (AZT) was a groundbreaking advancement for HIV/AIDS treatment in the 1980s, and recent innovations, like molnupiravir for COVID-19 and midostaurin for leukemia, continue to broaden therapeutic options (Scheme A). − Structurally, these compounds feature a sugar or sugar-like moiety and a nitrogenous heterocycle (N-heterocycle) linked by a critical α- or, more commonly, β-N-glycosidic bond. The current “state-of-the-art” for forging this bond relies on excess Lewis acid [e.g., trimethylsilyl triflate (TMSOTf) or SnCl4] to generate electrophilic oxocarbenium fragments that react with a silylated N-heterocycle (Vorbrüggen reaction) (Scheme B, top). , When starting materials are equipped with effective stereochemical handles for substrate control (e.g., neighboring group participation, C2-OAc), high anomeric selectivities (for β) result. However, donors lacking biasing functionality at the 2 position (e.g., 2-deoxy or 2-F), tend to be poorly selective (see islatravir; Scheme B).

A conventional approach to improving 2-deoxy N-glycosylation selectivity relies on halogenated carbohydrates or other glycosyl electrophiles (such as triflates or p-toluenesulfonates), which couple nucleobases and glycoside electrophiles through stereospecific SN2-like reactions. − Notable examples include Hoffer’s glucose-derived halosugar and Robin’s industrially applicable sodium salt method. − While these strategies can be effective, they often suffer from limited substrate scope, low yields, and the formation of undesired byproducts via non-stereospecific pathways. In particularly demanding situations (e.g., when the C2 center is also beta), de novo approaches to N-glycoside synthesis using acyclic stereocontrol have been used to obtain the desired anomer. − Despite these advances, methods that offer precise stereocontrol over the direct formation of the glycosidic C–N bond from easily accessed precursors remain elusive. −

Silylium-based catalytic approaches − have long been recognized for their efficacy in glycosidic activation, including recent reports from our lab showing that chiral Cu complexes could heterolytically cleave TMS–X (X = N3 and N-heterocycle) nucleophiles to generate the reactive pair of a silylium electrophile and a Cu-bound chiral N-nucleophile. , The existing challenges motivated a search for a robust, metal-free method of generating silylium Lewis acids, which led us to investigate the List imidodiphosphorimidate (IDPi) catalysts. − These BINOL-derived acid catalysts effectively initiate silylium catalysis while also providing a significant enantio bias in the addition of nucleophiles to otherwise achiral cyclic oxocarbenium intermediates. List has more recently shown that these catalysts can effectively differentiate diastereotopic faces of 2-deoxycarbohydrates for stereoselective O-glycosylation. The compelling combination of (1) competent silylium Lewis acid activity, (2) stability to silylated nucleophiles, and (3) potential for stereochemical control by asymmetric ion-pairing catalysis prompted an assessment of their applicability in the catalysis of the Vorbrüggen N-glycosylation reaction. Herein, we report a successful implementation of this approach for the stereoselective synthesis of challenging 2-deoxy-N-glycosides (Scheme C).

In this case, standard conditions uniformly afforded the α-N-galactosides for indole- and carbazole-based nucleophiles (16–20). N-Glycoside 19B was characterized by X-ray methods, and the N-heterocycle was equatorially disposed in the solid state, as was observed with ribose-based compounds 4A and 6A. X-ray crystallography on 22C revealed that the α form of these products also adopt a pseudo-equatorial orientation of the nucleobase. NMR spectroscopy on the remaining products in Tables and was consistent with the solid-state structures of 19B and 25D (see the Supporting Information).

Several important questions related to the mechanism were briefly addressed. First, kinetic control over the C–N coupling was established by subjecting isomerically pure β-1A to 5 mol % TMSOTf at 0 °C. Over typical reaction times (22 h) that complete erosion to a 1.2:1 α/β mixture of 1A were observed (Scheme A). In a similar experiment using pure α-1A and 5 mol % TMSOTf, erosion to 1.1:1 α/β was observed. These outcomes are similar to Vorbrüggen conditions tested in Table (entry a) and likely represent near thermodynamic ratios of N-anomers. An unexpected anomer-dependent stability was noted when pure α-1A did not react with 5 mol % silylated (S,S)-IDPi-1 (Scheme B), while the anomeric purity of β-1A was completely eroded under the same conditions (Scheme C). This paradoxical behavior suggested that a reaction excluding a substrate and silyl nucleophile was not a good mimic for catalytic conditions, and these additional reactants attenuated the aggressiveness of silylium Lewis acids, which serendipitously protected the kinetic selectivities.

Next, we sought to model relevant intermediates using density functional theory (DFT) to understand any potential structural features of the catalyst and substrate that gave the high degrees of selectivity described above. Two distinct mechanisms informed our hypothesis for the relevant intermediates. In a SN1-like mechanism (Figure A), glycosyl oxocarbenium is ion-paired to the IDPi anion and IDPi directs addition of the nucleophile to one diastereoface of oxocarbenium. Alternatively, in a SN2-like mechanism (Figure B), the glycosyl substrate is covalently bound to IDPi in a glycosyl-IDPi adduct. Here, a Curtin–Hammett scenario can be envisioned, where α and β covalent adducts are in equilibrium and stereoinvertive nucleophilic attack affords the N-glycoside.

(A) SN1-type mechanism, where nucleophilic attack on a planar oxocarbenium intermediate dictates selectivity. In this case, ΔΔG ⧧ for attack at the si or re face describes product distribution. (B) Curtin–Hammett scenario where C–N bond formation occurs by a SN2-type mechanism and the IDPi anion is the leaving group. (C) Single potential energy surface connecting the catalyst ion pair and covalent (pro-α and pro-β) structures with low barriers for interconversion. DFT calculations thus indicate that fast exchange is likely.

Given the List group's extensive work in enantioselective catalysis − and a recent disclosure of O-glycosylation, we directed our interest to a putative ion pair between the chiral IDPi anion and the glycosyl oxocarbenium intermediate as the source of stereochemical control in the C–N coupling (Figure A). For this hypothesized intermediate, attack of the nucleophile on planar oxocarbenium would occur with a selectivity determined by ΔΔG ⧧ for attacking the pro-α or pro-β faces (Figure A). To identify key structural features of the putative ion pair, a DFT study was initiated to find representative IDPi ion-pair structures. Interestingly, our search of a priori reasonable ion-pair starting geometries led to several local minima best described as covalent adducts between IDPi and oxocarbenium in addition to the expected contact ion pairs.

In these covalent adducts, one of the IDPi catalyst’s sulfonyl oxygen atoms forms a bond with carbohydrate C1 with relatively short lengths (1.5–1.6 Å). One of many representative low-energy-contact ion pairs was selected as a starting point for a single reaction path calculation (Figure C), along which the covalent adducts were found to be on par or slightly more stable than the analogous ion pair structures. The covalent adduct and formal ion pair existed within ∼4 kcal/mol of one another, with interconversion barriers of 1–3.5 kcal/mol. In many cases, the barriers were within typical error for DFT-based free energy calculations. These low barriers led us to conclude that the modeled intermediates (including pro-α and pro-β forms) were distinct but were likely in equilibrium under our standard reaction conditions and, thus, subject to the Curtin–Hammett principle. Intriguingly, the covalent binding mode was similar to glycosyl triflates, which are electronically similar to IDPi and enormously important donors in glycosidic coupling reactions (Scheme ). , They are characterized by intermolecular SN2-like α/β triflate (OTf–) interconversions that operate under Curtin–Hammett control for glycosylations. The similarities between triflates and IDPi led us to hypothesize that the IDPi counterion could be operating through a similar mechanism to triflate anions where the glycosylation occurs by selectively reacting with one anomer of an equilibrating α/β mixture.

Anomerization in Glycosyl Triflate and a Hypothesis for the Same in IDPi

Within this mechanistic framework, several limiting scenarios could be envisioned. First, the covalent adducts are off-cycle, and coupling requires the generation of a more reactive oxocarbenium ion, whose coupling selectivities are controlled by the asymmetric environment created in the ion pair (SN1-like). Alternatively and in parallel to glycosyl triflates, the covalent adducts are reactive and IDPi is the leaving group (SN2-like). Provided that a pre-equilibrium anomerization was operative, IDPi could influence which diastereomer was more reactive in a SN2 process. An attractive anomerization scenario is the intramolecular invertive displacement of one sulfonyl group by C 2-related sulfonyl. While the second NSO2Ar group would obviously not be rigorously C 2-related in the adduct, the inherent C 2 symmetry of the IDPi subunit would ensure that an invertive oxocarbenium transfer could anomerize the C1 center without generating an ion pair. In this “windshield wiper” scenario, the α adduct is thus the pro β product and vice versa. Several intermediate scenarios can also be envisioned. Evidence supporting the viability of a covalent glycosyl–IDPi intermediate was obtained via low-temperature NMR spectroscopy. Combining a preformed solution of TMS–(S,S)-IDPi-1 and B revealed that, above 10 °C, catalyst heterolysis occurred and silylium rapidly exchanged among the Lewis basic OAc groups and broadened all signals (see the Supporting Information for details). Under these conditions, however, IDPi heterolysis was non-productive and reversable, as lowering the temperature from +15 to −20 °C returned both starting TMS–(S,S)-IDPi-1 and B (see the Supporting Information). A similar variable-temperature NMR spectroscopic experiment with OBn-protected B2 revealed that a productive reaction took place between −70 and −50 °C (Figure A), which partially consumed the anomer resonance of B2 at 6.3 ppm and generated a new major peak at 6.1 ppm. Likewise, adduct formation in CD2Cl2 gave rise to a new major C–H at ∼6.1 ppm (see the Supporting Information for details), which compared favorably to the chemical shifts obtained for glycosyl triflates by Crich, Gin, and Boltje (∼6.1 ppm, CD2Cl2). − Furthermore, 2-deoxyglucosyl oxocarbenium ions have been generated with non-coordinating anions in solution, and they resonate considerably more downfield (∼9 ppm). Additionally supportive of a glycosyl-IDPi adduct were 1D NOESY cross-peaks from C1–H of the putative adduct to the IDPi aromatic region (Figure C). Notably, NOE effects were not observed in the aromatic region of B2 prior to exposure to IDPi (see the Supporting Information for details). The 31P­{1H} NMR spectrum was also informative, as it indicated that the IDPi unit had its C 2 symmetry disrupted, an observation easily accommodated by a covalent adduct instead of a dynamic ion pair; a minor set of doublets was also observed, perhaps for the other anomer. Additionally, quickly injecting a sample formed at −60 °C into the mass spectrometer revealed a molecular ion for [S,S-IDPi-1·B2-OAc] (Figure E). Such a species could either be a tight ion pair or the covalent adduct, but it clearly demonstrates the feasibility of an adducted reactive intermediate.

Conclusion

In conclusion, the (S,S)-IDPi-1 catalyst effectively promotes the anomer-selective coupling of simple 2-deoxy donors and TMS-protected N-heterocycles. Both 2-deoxy and 2,6-dideoxy sugars are well-behaved along with a significant range of acceptors, including indoles, carbazoles, and purines. In addition to the classically proposed ionic reactive intermediates, mechanistic investigations suggest a role for covalent adducts whose structure resembles that of the powerful glycosyl triflates.
