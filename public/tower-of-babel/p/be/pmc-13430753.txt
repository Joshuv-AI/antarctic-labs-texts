Machine learning prediction of long-term postoperative pneumonia risk: a retrospective cohort study
Authors: Cheng-An Lin, Kuan-Lin Sung, Chun Lee, Sheng-Feng Sung
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundPostoperative pneumonia is a significant complication, highlighting a patient’s ongoing vulnerability. While traditional tools focus on short-term outcomes, the perioperative period offers a unique “stress test” window to identify high-risk patients. This study developed and validated a machine-learning-based prognostic framework to predict pneumonia risk up to one year after surgery.MethodsThis retrospective study examined 11,655 surgical encounters at a tertiary hospital. Multiple machine learning algorithms, including random forest (RF), extreme gradient boosting, support vector machine, multilayer perceptron, and penalized logistic regression, were compared using 5-fold cross-validation. Background

Postoperative pneumonia is one of the most common and clinically important complications after anesthesia and surgery [1]. It is associated with longer hospital stays, increased intensive care use, higher mortality rates, and substantial healthcare costs [1–3]. Although advances in perioperative care have reduced early postoperative pulmonary complications, pneumonia risk can persist long after hospital discharge [4, 5]. Surgical stress, general anesthesia, and perioperative immobility cause lasting impairments in respiratory mechanics, airway clearance, and immune defense [6–10], predisposing vulnerable patients to delayed infections. Identifying patients at risk for both early and late-onset pneumonia is therefore crucial to improving perioperative care, enhancing long-term outcomes, and informing postoperative monitoring strategies.

Current prediction models and scoring systems based on established risk factors have proven useful for managing early postoperative pulmonary complications; however, their predictive value is limited for late-onset pneumonia. These traditional tools were primarily designed and validated for short-term risk assessment [11–13]. While immediate postoperative pneumonia (within 30 days) is well-recognized, emerging evidence indicates that physiological disturbances caused by major surgery may increase the risk of pulmonary complications over a longer period. The pathophysiological insults from surgery and anesthesia, such as impaired respiratory mechanics [9] and sustained immune suppression [14], may not resolve after discharge, leaving patients vulnerable to late-onset pneumonia [4, 5].

Furthermore, this long-term risk likely reflects both the direct effects of surgery and anesthesia on respiratory function and, more importantly, the fact that perioperative assessment captures baseline patient vulnerability, including chronic cardiopulmonary disease, immunocompromise, frailty, and comorbidity burden, which predispose to respiratory infections over extended periods. Perioperative data may thus serve as a high-fidelity marker of chronic vulnerability relevant to long-term outcomes. Therefore, there is a clinical need for tools that can identify at-risk patients beyond the initial postoperative period.

In recent years, machine learning has emerged as a powerful tool for predicting short-term surgical outcomes using high-dimensional electronic medical record (EMR) data [15–17]. Machine learning algorithms can identify meaningful patterns in complex datasets, uncovering key predictors of postoperative risk and capturing nonlinear interactions that traditional risk scores overlook [18]. However, a significant knowledge gap remains: these advanced techniques have not been applied to predict the long-term, one-year risk of postoperative pneumonia.

Therefore, this study examines pneumonia events within 12 months after surgery, recognizing that the initial procedure acts as a physiological “stress test” that exposes latent vulnerabilities. This extended time frame is appropriate for assessing the utility of sustained interventions, such as vaccination, smoking cessation programs, pulmonary rehabilitation, dysphagia management, and chronic disease management, which can be initiated based on perioperative risk assessment but require months to fully develop their protective effects. Specifically, this study aimed to develop and validate a machine-learning-based prognostic framework to identify patients on a high-risk trajectory for pneumonia up to one year after surgery. We sought to identify the key factors associated with this long-term risk, laying the groundwork for resource-optimized, data-driven postoperative surveillance strategies.

Methods

Study setting and data sources

This retrospective study was conducted at a 1,000-bed tertiary teaching hospital. Patient-level data were collected from an electronic anesthesia record database, which included preoperative assessments and anesthesia records. Laboratory data and outcome variables were obtained from the hospital’s research database, a de-identified repository containing administrative claims, EMRs, and National Death Index vital status data [19]. The two data sources were linked using unique patient identifiers. Before model development, all variables underwent automated range-check filters to identify biologically implausible values, such as extreme physiological outliers. Continuous variables were inspected for distribution anomalies, and cross-referencing between the electronic anesthesia database and the hospital’s research database was performed using unique patient identifiers to ensure 100% matching. Audit trails were maintained for all preprocessing steps.

The study was conducted in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki. The study protocol received approval from the Institutional Review Board of Ditmanson Medical Foundation Chia-Yi Christian Hospital (approval number: IRB2024065). Due to the retrospective nature of the study, use of de-identified records, and absence of direct patient contact, the requirement for informed consent was waived. All data management adhered to institutional policies and relevant regulations on confidentiality and data protection, with access controls and audit trails in place.

Study population

We identified 11,767 patients aged 20 years or older, accounting for 13,876 surgical encounters performed under general anesthesia between January 1, 2023, and December 31, 2023. The unit of analysis was the surgical encounter (index surgery), with each encounter treated as an independent observation for model development. Repeat encounters for the same patient separated by more than 30 days were considered distinct index episodes. Repeat encounters occurring within 30 days of each other were excluded to minimize within-patient dependence (n = 834).

We applied the following prespecified exclusion criteria sequentially: (i) encounters classified as American Society of Anesthesiologists Physical Status (ASA-PS) class 6 (n = 1); (ii) specialty departments with low case counts (fewer than 10 encounters) to prevent unstable estimates—Fracture & Trauma Surgery (n = 5), Emergency Department (n = 2), Department of Pediatrics (n = 1), Pediatric Orthopedics (n = 1), Special Needs Dentistry (n = 1), and Neurology (n = 1); (iii) records with unknown European Society of Cardiology (ESC) surgical risk (n = 1,208), because ESC surgical risk was a core procedure-level predictor and imputing this ordinal variable risked introducing unverifiable assumptions and misclassification; and (iv) records with more than 10% missingness across the prespecified candidate variables were also excluded (n = 167). After applying these criteria, the final analytic cohort included 11,655 surgical encounters.

Predictor and outcome variables

The outcome of interest was pneumonia occurring within 365 days after the index surgical encounter. Pneumonia events were identified using International Classification of Diseases, Tenth Revision, Clinical Modification (ICD-10-CM) codes J12–J18 [20] recorded in inpatient, emergency department, or outpatient encounters. The 12-month observation window was chosen to capture both early postoperative pneumonia events and those occurring during the extended recovery period, when patients may remain vulnerable due to ongoing inflammation, immunosuppression, or worsening comorbidities resulting from surgical stress. We understand that pneumonia events later in this timeframe might have multiple causes; however, preoperative and surgical factors remain key determinants of this longer-term risk.

Predictor variables (Table 1) included demographics, perioperative factors (surgical specialty, anesthesia method, anesthesia duration, ASA-PS class, ASA-PS “E” designation, and ESC surgical risk), lifestyle factors (alcohol use, betel-nut chewing), comorbidities (hypertension, coronary artery disease, chronic obstructive pulmonary disease [COPD], etc.), and routine laboratory measurements. The ASA-PS “E” designation indicates emergency surgery [21]. Because ESC surgical risk is intended for non-cardiac surgery, for this study, we assigned all cardiac surgery to the high-risk category.

Including surgical specialty as a predictor variable allows the model to capture differences in pneumonia risk across surgical types that differ significantly in physiological effects, operative trauma, and baseline patient populations. Additionally, ESC surgical risk classification is procedure-specific, capturing the inherent physiological stress, blood loss, fluid shifts, and tissue trauma associated with the operation, regardless of the patient’s underlying health conditions. By incorporating both patient-level factors and surgical characteristics, the model can identify distinct risk patterns specific to different surgical contexts.

Variables with more than 50% missing data were prespecified for exclusion; however, no variable met this criterion. For variables measured multiple times, we used the value closest to the start of anesthesia. Comorbidities were identified from the one-year period before the index operation.

Model building and interpretation

We randomly split the cohort into a training set (80%) and a holdout test set (20%). Missing data were handled using multivariate imputation by chained equations (MICE). The MICE algorithm fills in missing values one variable at a time, using the remaining variables to predict them [22]. For continuous variables, the conditional model was Bayesian ridge regression with predictive mean matching, using only observed training set cases. For categorical variables, we used multinomial logistic regression with class-probability sampling. All conditional models were fit on the training set, and imputations for the holdout test set used the fitted models, with a donor pool restricted to the training data to prevent information leakage. We generated five imputations and formed a single completed dataset by averaging imputed values for continuous variables and taking the mode for categorical variables. Preprocessing was performed within the learning pipeline.

Categorical variables were one-hot encoded, with the first level dropped. Numeric variables were handled using a model-aware strategy: they were standardized for scale-sensitive learners, including least absolute shrinkage and selection operator (LASSO), ridge, and elastic-net regressions, linear-kernel support vector machine (SVM), and multilayer perceptron (MLP), but left unstandardized for tree-based models such as random forest (RF) and extreme gradient boosting (XGB). This choice reflects a well-documented property of axis-aligned trees: splits depend on the order of feature values rather than their absolute magnitudes, so strictly monotonic rescaling does not alter candidate splits or the induced tree structure.

To address class imbalance, we compared random oversampling (ROS) and random undersampling at minority-to-majority ratios of 1:1, 1:2, 1:3, 1:4, and 1:5. We trained LASSO, ridge, and elastic-net logistic regressions, RF, linear-kernel SVM (with probability estimates), MLP, and XGB. Hyperparameters were optimized with Optuna using the tree-structured Parzen estimator sampler and hyperband pruning. We selected Optuna because it (i) provides a flexible define-by-run application programming interface for mixed continuous, discrete, and categorical spaces; (ii) implements efficient, model-agnostic early stopping via hyperband to terminate unpromising trials and reallocate computational budget; and (iii) offers robust study management, including seeding, warm-start via enqueued default trials, and resumable studies.

For each algorithm-resampling-ratio configuration, we allocated up to 150 trials for hyperparameter search. This budget aligns with common practice in hyperparameter optimization benchmarks, where 100–200 configurations are routinely evaluated. Model selection was based on the area under the receiver operating characteristic curve (AUC), estimated via five-fold cross-validation repeated six times. Resampling was performed only within the training folds to prevent leakage from the validation folds. Hyperparameter spaces are summarized in Supplementary Table 1. For each model, we report the corresponding validation AUC in Supplementary Table 2. The holdout test set was kept untouched for confirmatory evaluation and not used for model selection. Shapley additive explanation (SHAP) analysis [23] was applied to the holdout test set to interpret model outputs and identify important features.

Experiments were conducted on a Windows 10 machine using Python version 3.11.5 with scikit-learn 1.5.1, XGBoost 2.1.3, Optuna 4.1.0, and SHAP 0.45.0.

Statistical analysis

Patient characteristics were summarized using counts and percentages for categorical variables and medians with interquartile ranges for continuous variables. Group differences were assessed using chi-square or Fisher’s exact tests for categorical variables and Wilcoxon rank-sum tests for continuous variables.

To evaluate the performance of the developed machine learning model, we calculated AUC, sensitivity, specificity, positive predictive value (PPV), and negative predictive value (NPV). ASA-PS, a simple categorization of a patient’s physiological status during the perioperative period, served as the baseline model for comparison. The ASA-PS threshold was ≥ 3 [24]. The AUCs between models were compared using DeLong’s method [25].

Statistical analyses were conducted using R version 4.3.2 (R Foundation for Statistical Computing, Vienna, Austria). Two-tailed p-values of 0.05 were considered statistically significant.

Results

Characteristics of the study population

The final analytic cohort comprised 11,655 surgical encounters; postoperative pneumonia within 365 days occurred in 238 encounters (2.04%). The incidence of pneumonia peaked in the second month after the index surgery (Fig. 1). Encounters with pneumonia involved older patients, lower body weight, and a higher proportion of males. Thoracic and neurosurgical departments accounted for a larger share of pneumonia cases, whereas orthopedics and urology accounted for a smaller share. Higher ASA-PS classes and higher ESC surgical risk were more frequent in the pneumonia group. Several comorbidities, including COPD, stroke, dementia, diabetes, renal dysfunction, and end-stage renal disease, and preoperative abnormalities, such as airway abnormality assessed preoperatively, were more prevalent among patients with pneumonia. Selected laboratory values, including lower hemoglobin and estimated glomerular filtration rate (eGFR), higher postprandial glucose, and higher blood urea nitrogen, also differed between groups.

Model development and internal validation

In repeated 5-fold cross-validation, mean AUCs across algorithms and resampling strategies ranged from 0.834 to 0.875. The RF with ROS at a 1:4 ratio achieved the highest cross-validated AUC (0.875), followed by XGB with ROS (1:5; 0.870). Linear-kernel SVM, MLP, and penalized logistic regression models yielded mean AUCs of approximately 0.840–0.860 (Supplementary Table 2; Fig. 2). Based on cross-validated AUCs, the RF with ROS (1:4) was selected as the final model.

Discussion

In this retrospective cohort study, we developed and validated machine learning models to predict the risk of postoperative pneumonia up to one year after surgery. The incidence of pneumonia within this 365-day window was 2%, confirming that respiratory risk extends well beyond the acute perioperative period. Among the algorithms tested, the RF model trained with ROS (1:4 ratio) performed best, achieving an AUC of 0.886 on the holdout test set. Using SHAP analysis, we identified preoperative hemoglobin, ESC surgical risk, age, ASA-PS class, and eGFR as the most significant drivers of long-term risk. These findings highlight the potential of machine learning to capture complex, long-term physiological trajectories that traditional 30-day risk scores may miss.

Long-term risk of pneumonia

Current perioperative risk stratification tools, such as the ARISCAT score, are primarily designed for inpatient or 30-day outcomes [11, 12]. However, our results indicate that pneumonia events continue to accrue throughout the first postoperative year (Fig. 1). This persistence of risk aligns with the observation that surgical stress can induce a prolonged state of physiological debt. While the framework of persistent inflammation, immunosuppression, and catabolism syndrome offers a possible biological hypothesis for delayed infections [26], our model primarily serves as a prognostic instrument to identify baseline vulnerability. Pneumonia is the most clinically significant and resource-intensive postoperative complication, with a distinct pathophysiological link to anesthesia (e.g., intubation, impaired ciliary function, and atelectasis). We acknowledge that 12-month pneumonia is multifactorial, influenced by post-discharge behaviors and chronic disease progression. However, we argue that the perioperative window provides a high-fidelity “baseline snapshot.” The index surgery serves as a definitive catalyst, converting latent frailty into an active clinical risk trajectory. This observation aligns with recent longitudinal data; for instance, a study demonstrated that in elderly cancer patients, the risk of pneumonia remains elevated well beyond the immediate postoperative period [4].

A main aspect of our study is that pneumonia events occur within 12 months after surgery. While pneumonia episodes later in this period might be influenced by other health issues or worsening chronic conditions, we believe that the index surgery serves as a key physiological stressor that could reveal vulnerabilities or lead to prolonged immunosuppression, as outlined in the persistent inflammation, immunosuppression, and catabolism syndrome framework [26]. It is also important to recognize that our model does not claim perioperative interventions have direct, long-lasting physiological effects lasting 12 months. Instead, perioperative data act as a high-fidelity marker of baseline patient vulnerability. In other words, the preoperative risk factors identified by our model, including ASA-PS classification, anemia, and renal dysfunction, represent baseline susceptibilities that are heightened by surgical stress and continue to affect infection risk over a long period.

Moreover, although post-discharge factors such as lifestyle behaviors, oral hygiene, environmental exposures, and medication adherence undoubtedly influence long-term pneumonia risk, these factors are not randomly distributed. Patients with poor preoperative health are more likely to face adverse post-discharge circumstances, causing perioperative variables to serve as proxies for unmeasured social and behavioral determinants of health. This conceptual framework aligns with prognostic models in other clinical areas. Cardiovascular risk scores predict 10-year events based on a single time-point assessment of traditional risk factors [27], as these baseline factors reflect chronic vulnerability rather than immediate causes.

Our model’s ability to achieve meaningful discrimination (AUC > 0.70) for 12-month pneumonia also provides empirical evidence that perioperative data contain prognostic signals extending beyond the immediate perioperative period. Future research using time-varying covariates and competing risk analyses could further clarify the distinct effects of preoperative factors, surgical stress, and post-discharge events on pneumonia risk across different postoperative periods.

Interpretation of key predictors

A major strength of this study is the use of SHAP analyses to provide clinical interpretability. Among these top features, preoperative hemoglobin was the leading predictor of long-term pneumonia. With a prevalence exceeding 30% in non-cardiac surgeries [28], untreated anemia mechanistically compromises systemic oxygen delivery and immune surveillance, leaving patients vulnerable to infection [29]. This risk is not limited to severe cases; even mild anemia (Hb < 13 g/dL in males; <12 g/dL in females) and the need for allogeneic blood transfusions are independently associated with increased pulmonary complications or adverse perioperative outcomes [30, 31]. While anemia is a well-established driver of immediate adverse outcomes [32], our findings indicate that the physiological debt it imposes creates a prolonged state of vulnerability that extends well beyond the acute postoperative period.

ESC surgical risk was identified as the second most important predictor of postoperative pneumonia. This metric is determined by the type of surgical procedure patients receive [33]. Since the incidence of postoperative pneumonia varies across surgical specialties [34], the association between ESC surgical risk and postoperative pneumonia is expected. Age and ASA-PS class are established predictors of postoperative pneumonia [11, 24]. Renal function, as measured by eGFR, emerged as another top-tier predictor in our model, reinforcing the crucial role of “kidney-lung crosstalk” in postoperative complications [35, 36].

The association between renal insufficiency and pulmonary complications is increasingly recognized as bidirectional and synergistic [35, 36]. Mechanistically, the retention of uremic toxins and pro-inflammatory mediators, specifically IL-6, lipocalin-2, and chemokine ligand-2, induces pulmonary endothelial injury and increases vascular permeability [37]. This disruption promotes neutrophil trapping and alveolar edema, driving tissue remodeling that compromises host defense and heightens susceptibility to infection [37]. This finding is corroborated by a recent matched cohort study, which demonstrated that preoperative renal insufficiency is a strong, independent predictor of postoperative pneumonia and septic shock [38]. Furthermore, the study highlighted that the risk of adverse outcomes escalates significantly in patients with advanced renal impairment [38].

Machine learning models

Our analysis provides a direct comparison of machine learning algorithms, including tree-based models and linear approaches, for predicting the long-term risk of postoperative pneumonia. The RF model outperformed linear models such as penalized logistic regression. Although the absolute difference in AUCs may appear modest, in the context of population health screening, this margin translates to a significant improvement in identifying at-risk individuals who would otherwise be missed. This finding contributes to the ongoing debate about model complexity in clinical prediction. A 2025 study found that, for predicting 30-day complications following proctological surgery, advanced machine learning algorithms performed as well as logistic regression, likely because of the limited number of predictors and the short-term, specific outcome [39].

However, our results suggest different conclusions regarding syndromic and long-term outcomes, such as pneumonia. Unlike immediate surgical complications, often driven by procedural factors, late-onset pneumonia results from the complex, nonlinear interaction of host physiology (e.g., the compounding effects of anemia and renal dysfunction [38]) and environmental stressors [40] over time. Tree-based algorithms are inherently better equipped to model these high-order interactions without manual specification, offering superior discriminative power for systemic complications that evolve over the long term [41].

Clinical implications

The transition from a 30-day to a one-year risk perspective requires a paradigm shift in how we allocate surveillance resources. We acknowledge that the model’s PPV (7.4%) limits its use as a standalone diagnostic tool. However, in the context of a low-prevalence outcome (2%), this represents a 3.7-fold increase in risk detection over the baseline population. The true clinical value of the model lies in its high negative predictive value (99.6%), functioning as a robust “rule-out” tool. By safely identifying low-risk cohorts, healthcare systems can concentrate resource-intensive interventions, such as telemedicine monitoring, integrated primary care handoffs, and outpatient pulmonary rehabilitation, on the specific subgroup of patients identified as high-risk. This targeted approach provides incremental utility over general frailty assessments by incorporating procedure-specific stressors (e.g., ESC surgical risk) and anesthesia-specific physiological data. The current standard of care—terminating rigorous follow-up upon resolution of acute surgical issues—leaves a vulnerable cohort unprotected during a high-risk window [42]. Our model suggests that “recovery” should be redefined as a long-term trajectory rather than a short-term event.

Clinically, these findings support multi-phase interventions throughout the perioperative period. The first phase involves prehabilitation, including identifying modifiable drivers, such as low hemoglobin levels, and promoting preoperative anemia management (e.g., iron supplementation) from a blood-conservation strategy to a primary preventive measure for long-term respiratory health. The second phase comprises perioperative interventions during the index hospitalization, such as pneumococcal and influenza vaccinations for high-risk patients before discharge [43], enhanced aspiration precautions and dysphagia screening for patients with neurological conditions or prior stroke, aggressive pulmonary hygiene protocols and early mobilization for high-risk patients, and education for patients and families on pneumonia recognition and prevention.

The third phase includes extended surveillance and post-discharge intervention programs. Patients identified as high-risk by our model, specifically those fitting the “frail-anemic-renal” phenotype, may benefit from targeted follow-up strategies, such as telemedicine monitoring or integrated primary care handoffs, to detect early signs of respiratory decline in the months after discharge. High-risk patients with respiratory impairment can participate in outpatient pulmonary rehabilitation programs [44] that focus on inspiratory muscle training, airway clearance techniques, and exercise conditioning. Those with neurological conditions or a history of stroke should undergo comprehensive swallowing evaluations with continuous speech therapy, dietary adjustments, and improved oral hygiene [45]. Ongoing support for smoking cessation is highly recommended. The perioperative period is a critical “teachable moment” for quitting smoking [46]. Better management of diabetes, heart failure, COPD, and other chronic illnesses through more intensive outpatient follow-up decreases the risk of long-term infections.

Moreover, high-risk patients can be scheduled for earlier and more frequent post-discharge follow-up with patient education on recognizing early pneumonia symptoms and establishing low thresholds for clinical evaluation. The 12-month outcome window aligns with sustained interventions initiated based on perioperative risk assessment, not brief preoperative conditioning. Our model’s value lies in identifying patients who will benefit most from resource-intensive, sustained intervention programs extending well beyond the immediate postoperative period.

An important consideration when interpreting our findings is the substantial heterogeneity in surgical procedures and patient populations within our cohort. Different surgical specialties have distinct characteristics: for example, young patients undergoing minimally invasive urological procedures have fundamentally different risk profiles compared to elderly patients undergoing major neurosurgical interventions. These differences encompass baseline patient characteristics (age, comorbidity burden, frailty), surgical factors (operative trauma, duration, physiological impact), and postoperative recovery trajectories.

This heterogeneity could introduce confounding or limit model generalizability if not addressed. Our study accounts for this through several mechanisms. First, surgical specialty/procedure type as a predictor helps the model learn distinct risk patterns across categories. Second, adjusting for patient-level characteristics (age, ASA-PS, comorbidities, labs) differentiates baseline vulnerability. Third, machine learning identifies complex interactions between surgical type and patient factors, recognizing that risk factors can vary depending on context.

Nevertheless, we acknowledge that residual confounding may remain due to unmeasured factors such as intraoperative complications, surgical complexity, surgeon experience, or institutional variations. Developing a general prediction model involves balancing broad applicability with specialty-specific nuances. Our approach emphasizes clinical utility: a model that performs well across various surgical contexts is more useful than multiple specialty-specific models with only slight improvements. Future research might investigate ensemble methods combining a general model with specialty-specific adjustments to enhance both generalizability and precision.

Strengths and limitations

The strengths of this study include a large dataset and the separation of training and testing data to prevent information leakage during modeling. Additionally, the application of SHAP analysis transforms the “black box” of machine learning into clinically transparent insights and validates the model against known physiological principles.

However, several limitations must be acknowledged. First, as a single-center study, these findings require external validation to generalize to other healthcare systems. Second, reliance on ICD-10-CM codes for the primary outcome may introduce classification bias, although this method is standard in administrative data research and the codes used have been validated [20]. Third, although we accounted for comorbidities such as COPD, granular data on smoking pack-years were unavailable, potentially underestimating the specific dose-response relationship of tobacco use.

Finally, our model provides a static baseline risk estimate based on perioperative data and does not account for changes during the 12-month follow-up period, such as medication adherence, healthcare utilization, exposure to infectious contacts, functional decline, or lifestyle modifications. Furthermore, although our data show a peak in pneumonia incidence in the second postoperative month (Fig. 1), we did not perform time-stratified analyses at specific intervals, such as 30, 90, or 120 days. While a unified 12-month window is essential for identifying the overall “vulnerable trajectory” and informing long-term care planning, we acknowledge that the biological drivers and predictive importance of specific features may shift across different postoperative phases. Future research utilizing time-varying covariates or competing risk analyses could further clarify the distinct effects of preoperative factors versus post-discharge events. Dynamic models that incorporate longitudinal follow-up data may improve the precision of risk estimates at these specific intervals.

Additionally, while we accounted for common comorbidities, granular data on factors like oral hygiene, social determinants of health, and specific smoking pack-years were unavailable, which could further refine the identification of the high-risk cohort. However, our model is for “prognostic stratification” (which involves identifying patients at higher risk who need intervention, even if some of the risk comes from unmeasured factors) rather than “causal prediction” (which requires controlling for confounders to establish causation). Thus, our model identifies baseline vulnerability, not all determinants of long-term pneumonia.

Conclusions

In conclusion, postoperative pneumonia is a significant complication that extends well beyond the acute recovery period. By leveraging machine learning, we demonstrated that this long-term risk is predictable using a snapshot of perioperative data. While driven by a constellation of physiological deficits, notably anemia, renal insufficiency, and frailty, the surgery serves as the critical window for identification. Our model provides a data-driven foundation for secondary screening, allowing clinicians to rule out low-risk patients and extend the horizon of perioperative care to improve long-term survival for those on a vulnerable trajectory.
