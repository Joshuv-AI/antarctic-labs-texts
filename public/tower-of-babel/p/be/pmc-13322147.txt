The Influence of Older Age on RBC Transfusion Decisions in ICU Patients

ABSTRACT
Objectives:RBC transfusions are common in the ICU. Recent studies suggest that a restrictive transfusion policy is noninferior or superior to a liberal policy. However, few studies focus on the influence of age in transfusion. In elderly ICU patients, reduced physiologic reserves may shift the perceived risk-benefit balance of transfusion, potentially leading to different transfusion practices. This study examines whether transfusion practices in ICU patients differ across patient age.Design:This is a substudy of the International Point Prevalence Study of ICU Transfusion Practices (InPUT), a global, multicenter, prospective observational cohort study.Setting:ICUs from 233 centers across 30 countries. Data were collected from March 2019 to October 2022 in prespecified weeks.Patients:Adult ICU patients (≥ 18 yr) admitted during predefined study weeks. Patients were categorized by age (< 65, 65–75, 75–85, and > 85 yr).Interventions:None.Measurements and Main Results:A total of 3643 patients from 233 centers across 30 countries were included. Of these, 53% were younger than 65 years, 26% were 65–75 years, 17% were 75–85 years, and 4% were older than 85 years. RBC transfusion rates ranged from 23% to 26% across all age groups (p = 0.91). Patients older than 85 years had higher stated hemoglobin thresholds (median, 10.0 g/dL) compared with younger patients (median, 8.0 g/dL; p < 0.001). “Age” and “improve general state” were more frequently cited as reasons for transfusion in patients older than 85 years. However, after adjustment, age was not associated with the probability of receiving an RBC transfusion.Conclusions:Different transfusion strategies are applied in patients older than 85 years old. These differences appear to be driven by age-related differences in physiology and diagnoses rather than motivated by older age itself.

KEY POINTS

Question: What are the differences in transfusion practices in elderly patients admitted to the ICU compared with younger ICU patients?

Findings: In this multinational observational cohort study, ICU patients older than 85 years had higher hemoglobin transfusion thresholds (median, 10.0 vs. 8.0 g/dL in younger patients). “Age” and “improve general state” were cited more frequently as transfusion reasons in the oldest group. However, after adjusting for covariables, age was not independently associated with the probability of receiving an RBC transfusion.

Meaning: Observed differences in transfusion practices are likely driven by different patient characteristics across age strata rather than age itself.

Blood transfusion is administered commonly in ICUs. A recent international prospective cohort study has shown that 25% of all intensive care patients received one or more RBC transfusions during their ICU stay (1). Although the overall epidemiology of RBC transfusion is fairly well-characterized, there is little data on international transfusion practices among elderly patients in the ICU.

In both the general population and the ICU setting, an increasing body of research has shown that a restrictive transfusion policy (often defined as a hemoglobin of 7–8 g/dL, depending on specific subpopulation) is noninferior to a liberal transfusion policy (defined as hemoglobin of 9–10 g/dL) for the ICU population and across various ICU subgroups (2–9). But the evidence specifically for elderly ICU patients is limited. This is further complicated by the varying definition of the term “elderly.” The World Health Organization defines elderly patients as those 60 years old and older (10), but many ICU studies now adopt higher definition thresholds as the proportion of old and very old individuals in the ICU has been steadily increasing (11–14). The question arises as to whether these growing number of elderly patients are treated differently regarding RBC transfusions.

Underlying comorbidities can reduce physiologic reserves and increase frailty in elderly patients, potentially necessitating a higher hemoglobin buffer. Conversely, these factors could also induce elderly patients’ susceptibility to transfusion-related side effects, such as hemolysis, alloimmunization, transfusion-related infections, transfusion-related acute lung injury, transfusion-associated circulatory overload, and possibly severe long-term risks (15–19). While plasma and platelet transfusions are assumed to carry higher risks of side effects, RBC transfusions are not without harm (18, 20, 21). In vulnerable patients, age-related physiologic changes make it vital to carefully consider these risks.

Current literature on transfusion thresholds in elderly patients in the ICU is limited and conflicting. Several large RBC transfusion trials did not find evidence of treatment effect heterogeneity across age categories. Several large RBC transfusion trials did not find evidence of treatment effect heterogeneity across age categories. In a large trial in cardiac surgery patients, a restrictive transfusion strategy in cardiac surgery was associated with a lower risk of the composite outcome (death, myocardial infarction, stroke, or new-onset renal failure requiring dialysis by day 28) in patients 75 years old or older, but not in younger patients (2–9). Because subgroup analyses are almost universally underpowered and involve multiple comparisons, the validity of both the presence and absence of apparent treatment effect modification by age is uncertain (22). A small randomized pilot among older mechanically ventilated patients, defined as 55 years or older, showed results consistent with benefit rather than harm from a liberal transfusion strategy (23). A 2017 systematic review likewise suggested that a liberal transfusion policy might yield better outcomes in non-ICU elderly patients compared with a restrictive policy (24). This contradicts the recommendations for restrictive transfusion strategies in current guidelines and the results of various subgroup analysis in large transfusion threshold trials showing consistent results of noninferiority of the restrictive threshold independent of age (25). Because the benefits of different transfusions strategies in old and frail patients in the ICU are unclear, physicians may be inclined to choose different transfusion strategies than for younger patients.

Our aim was to assess transfusion practices in elderly patients admitted to the ICU and compare these practices to those applied to younger patients. In absence of a clear definition of “elderly,” we stratified the analysis by age groups and investigated age as a continuous variable in transfusion decisions. We hypothesized that older ICU patients receive more frequent RBC transfusions and at higher hemoglobin levels compared with younger patients. The results can inform the research agenda on transfusion policies, generate guideline questions, and inform the design of future observational studies and randomized controlled trials.

MATERIALS AND METHODS

Study Design and Population

This is a sub study of the International Point Prevalence Study of ICU Transfusion Practices (InPUT). The InPUT study was an international, multicenter, prospective, observational cohort study evaluating transfusion practices in the ICU. The data used were collected during the main study and adhered to its quality standards (1). Data collection took place in two prescheduled timeframes of 8 weeks from March 2019 to October 2022. All patients 18 years old or older, admitted to the ICU during these predefined weeks were included. A total of 3643 patients from 233 centers across 30 countries were included. Elderly patients were defined as 65 years or older.

The study was approved by the institutional review board of Amsterdam University Medical Center, location AMC (approval number W18_390#18.449; approval date November 16, 2020), followed by approval from local and national ethical committees of the participating centers. The study procedures were in line with the Declaration of Helsinki. The InPUT trial registration can be found on the website of the World Health Organization, International clinical Trials Registry Platform main ID number: NL-OMON28052.

Data Collection

Data were collected at ICU admission and daily during ICU stay up to 28 days, discharge, or death. The data collected at admission included baseline demographics such as age, medical history, Acute Physiology and Chronic Health Evaluation IV score, referring specialty, and emergency admission. The daily data included laboratory values, the presence of supportive treatment (respiratory and/or kidney), and the development of complications. Transfusion was defined as the administration of one or more RBC units. For each transfusion event (i.e., administration of an RBC unit), a separate transfusion event form was filled in, giving an overview of the clinical reasons (including low hemoglobin value, patient’s age, and coronary ischemia) for transfusion. If the clinician decided to administer two RBC transfusions in a single decision, it was considered one transfusion event. However, if a second RBC unit was given after the first (e.g., due to the patient’s condition not improving), it was counted as two separate transfusion events.

Outcomes

The outcomes included the occurrence rate of RBC transfusions in elderly, the used transfusion thresholds and the number of units of RBC transfused during each transfusion episode. We described the main stated reasons for RBC transfusions, and whether elderly patients were more or less likely to be transfused compared with younger patients. Furthermore, we analyzed if age influenced the decision to transfuse or not. These outcomes were stratified by age category (< 65, 65–75, 75–85, and > 85 yr) or reported by using age as a continuous variable.

Statistical Analysis

All analyses were performed using R (Version 4.3.2; R: A Language and Environment for Statistical Computing, Boston, MA) and the R-studio interface (latest version 4.2.1 [June 23, 2022]). Descriptive statistics were reported as mean (sd) or as median (interquartile range [IQR]). Categorical variables were expressed as count (%).

Comparisons, such as transfused vs. nontransfused or elderly vs. nonelderly (stratified in age categories), were conducted using the chi-square test. When comparing a numerical variable across more than two groups, the one-way analysis of variance test (in case of parametric data) or the Kruskal-Wallis test (nonparametric data) were used.

Variables affecting the probability of receiving a blood transfusion across age strata were assessed using a mixed effects logistic regression model, assessing transfusion at any day as the dependent variable, a set of potential determinants as covariables, and patient and hospital as random effects. Based on current knowledge and expert opinion, a set of covariables potentially influencing transfusion were identified. The set of covariables included sex, medical history of acute coronary syndrome, Sequential Organ Failure Assessment (SOFA) score, acute brain injury as reason for ICU admission, nadir hemoglobin value, surgery within 24 hours of ICU admission, day of ICU stay, estimated blood loss, mechanical ventilation, renal replacement therapy, and ECMO.

In total, three different mixed effects logistic regression models were evaluated. The base model fit the probability of transfusion as a function of the set of covariables excluding age. The other models included age as continuous variable or age as a continuous variable with a spline function to assess a possible nonlinear relationship. The models were compared using the Akaike information criterion (AIC) value, with a lower value indicating a more parsimonious fit. A separate sensitivity analysis was performed, evaluating the influence of care limitations (defined as do not intubate and/or do not resuscitate) on the probability of receiving a blood transfusion.

Definitions of the used variables can be found in the Standard Operating Procedure, attached as a Supplementary File (https://links.lww.com/CCM/H948) to the main InPUT article (1).

The differences in transfusion threshold and reasons for RBC transfusion in patients 85 years old and older could be due to different transfusion decisions or a result of differences in patient characteristics between younger and older patients. To assess whether these differences are due to age-related characteristics or transfusion decisions, mixed effects logistic regression modeling was used.

In the base model excluding age as a predictor, the following variables were found to be predictors of the probability of receiving an RBC transfusion on any day: lowest daily hemoglobin, history of acute coronary syndrome, surgery within the preceding 24 hours, admission day, daily estimated blood loss, daily SOFA score, ECMO, and invasive mechanical ventilation (AIC, 5105.2; and e-Tables 3 and 4 and e-Fig. 1, https://links.lww.com/CCM/H948). Adding age as a continuous covariable did not improve the model fit (AIC, 5107.2; p = 1.0 compared with base model; and e-Table 5 and e-Fig. 2, https://links.lww.com/CCM/H948), indicating that age was not an independent predictor of the probability of receiving RBC transfusion on any day. To visualize the model, four hypothetical patients were created, each representing the mean age of one of the four age categories, which resulted in overlapping lines (Fig. 2). A model with a spline over age (knot at 85 yr) to allow for a nonlinear effect of age also did not improve the model fit (AIC, 5109.8; p = 0.4994 compared with base model; and e-Table 6 and e-Fig. 3, https://links.lww.com/CCM/H948). Suggesting that even accounting for nonlinear effect for patients 85 years old or older (where the most differences in transfusion practice were seen compared with younger patients), elderly patients did not have a different probability to be transfused compared with younger patients. A sensitivity analysis excluding patients with care limitations resulted in a reduction of the AIC value to 4941.0 (e-Table 7, https://links.lww.com/CCM/H948). However, age remained nonsignificant as a predictor for the likelihood of receiving an RBC transfusion on any given day. DISCUSSION

The purpose of this study was to compare the occurrence rate, indications, and physiologic context for RBC transfusions between elderly and younger ICU patients. We found that only patients 85 years old and older had higher transfusion thresholds, although the occurrence rate was similar across age groups. Reasons for transfusion varied by age: in the older than 85 group, age and improving general condition were common reasons, while coronary ischemia was frequent in the 65–75 group. Importantly, age itself did not influence the probability of receiving an RBC transfusion after accounting for acute physiology, organ support, and medical history, suggesting that observed differences in transfusion practices are due to other patient characteristics, not age itself.

Two apparently contradictive results require explanation. First, patients 85 years old and older who were transfused had higher (more liberal) stated transfusion thresholds compared with younger patients who were transfused but did not receive more transfusions. This may be explained by differences in underlying etiology, with more frequent admissions for heart failure and less frequent admissions within 24 hours after surgery in the oldest age category, resulting in fewer instances where a transfusion threshold is actually met. This is also supported by the higher hemoglobin levels observed before transfusion in patients 85 years old and older (among transfused patients; Table 2), despite all-time lower hemoglobin levels (among all patients, e-Table 2, https://links.lww.com/CCM/H948).

Second, in older patients, the stated reasons for transfusion more often included age itself, but after multivariable adjustment, age did not appear to be a determinant of transfusion decisions. It is likely that age was often cited as an additional reason for transfusion but was not the primary reason, as multiple reasons for RBC transfusion could be indicated for each transfusion event. Indeed, more reasons per transfusion were given in older compared with younger patients (Table 2). The actual decision to transfuse is apparently more influenced by the severity of the patient’s condition (including their hemoglobin level) and their physiologic response to anemia rather than age itself. Together, these findings suggest that clinicians consider the broader clinical context rather than age alone when evaluating transfusion needs. Additionally, the group of patients 85 years old and older is small compared with other age groups, limiting the statistical power to detect a modest age-related difference in transfusion strategies.

Previous research on transfusion practices in the elderly ICU population is limited. Many large transfusion trials comparing restrictive vs. liberal transfusion strategies in the ICU, have conducted subgroup analyses for elderly patients, yielding results consistent with the main analysis, indicating that a restrictive strategy is noninferior to a liberal strategy (2–8). Research on transfusion practices in the elderly ICU population is limited. Age-defined subgroup analyses in large transfusion trials comparing restrictive vs. liberal transfusion strategies have shown conflicting results (2–8). Trials specifically focusing on transfusion in elderly ICU patients are scarce. A 100-patient pilot randomized controlled trial did investigate RBC transfusion thresholds in ICU patients of 55 years and older and requiring more than 4 days of invasive mechanical ventilation (23). Transfusion rates were lower in the restrictive group, and 180-day mortality trended higher in the liberal group. However, the study did not describe reasons for RBC transfusion or compare results with younger ICU patients. The mean age in this study (mean, 67 yr [sd, 7 yr]) was similar to the 61 years (sd, 16 yr) reported in the InPUT study (1) and also comparable to the mean age in other landmark transfusion trials in the ICU population (2–7). Other randomized trials investigating transfusion in elderly have only been performed in noncritically ill patients (26).

Overall, previous evidence has not identified age as a significant effect modifier in transfusion risks and benefits. The present study indicate that while some practice variation exists across age groups, its magnitude is likely modest. These findings suggest that there is currently limited empirical support for consistent age-related differences in transfusion effects. While this may reduce the immediate rationale for a large randomized trial focused solely on older patients, this group remains clinically important due to higher comorbidity and risk. Future research may therefore benefit from investigating treatment effect heterogeneity along related dimensions, such as multimorbidity or frailty, rather than age alone. For clinicians, our findings indicate that current transfusion decisions in elderly ICU patients appear largely driven by physiology and clinical context rather than age itself. This suggests that an explicit age-based adaptation of transfusion thresholds is not strongly supported by the observed practice patterns. Compared with earlier large observational studies on transfusion practices in the ICU, the InPUT study found similar overall transfusion rates (1). The present analysis shows that this transfusion rate of approximately one in four patients is also stable across age categories.

Compared with current transfusion guidelines, the present findings suggest some divergence from recommendations across age groups. Notably, “age of patient” and “to improve general state” were more frequently cited as transfusion reasons in the oldest age category. Neither of these reasons is supported by guidelines, which prioritize hemoglobin or hematocrit thresholds over such alternative triggers (25). This deviation from guideline recommendations may inform implementation efforts and may warrant more explicit consideration in future guideline revisions, although a very strong focus on age seems unwarranted given the limited adjusted influence of age on transfusion decisions.

A strength of our study is its multinational and multicentric design. Although low-income countries are underrepresented, the study results are representative of actual practice in 233 centers in 30 countries on six continents. In addition, we evaluated the influence of age on transfusion strategies from two complementary perspectives: the “stated” reasons and threshold for transfusion as well as the modeled “actual” transfusion decisions.

Several limitations must be acknowledged. First, we did not assess frailty, which reflects overall vulnerability and biological reserve. Very old ICU patients are typically admitted cautiously, likely being in better physical condition, which may result in lower frailty scores compared with those 65–85 years old. Second, the definition of “elderly” remains a challenge. We stratified our analysis into age groups and assessed age as a continuous, nonlinear predictor, finding the most significant differences between younger patients and those over 85. However, the small number of patients 85+ years old limited statistical precision. Third, data on inotropic or vasoactive infusions were unavailable, which could have added granularity to the analysis of transfusion practices. Finally, not all reasons for RBC transfusion were contextualized or explicitly outlined in the study’s standard operating procedure. This lack of clear definition may lead to varying interpretations, potentially resulting in inconsistencies in how these reasons were classified. However, the use of broader definitions reflects how transfusion practices are often approached in daily clinical practice.

CONCLUSIONS

Different transfusion strategies are applied to elderly compared with younger patients. However, after adjusting for covariables, age itself did not affect the probability of receiving an RBC transfusion. This suggests that differences in practices may be driven by age-related physiologic and diagnostic differences, not age alone, although the small number of patients 85 years old or older, limits statistical precision. In the context of existing evidence, large age-specific randomized trials may be a lower priority than studies that examine treatment effect heterogeneity along dimensions such as comorbidity or frailty. Our study underscores the importance of evaluating transfusion practices through the lens of physiology, comorbidities, and individual patient needs, rather than focusing primarily on age.
