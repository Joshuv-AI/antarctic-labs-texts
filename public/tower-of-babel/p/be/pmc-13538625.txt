Characterisation of the chloroplast genome of Macrotyloma species: comparative analysis and phylogenomic insights
Authors: Ramawatar Nagar, Vandna Patial, Divya Joshiya, MS Nimmy, Manavi Raizada, Pradeep Kumar Jain
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Macrotyloma is an underutilised legume genus within the tribe Phaseoleae (Fabaceae) that includes nutritionally and agronomically important crops such as horse gram (Macrotyloma uniflorum) and Kersting’s groundnut (Macrotyloma geocarpum). Despite their importance, knowledge of the chloroplast (cp.) genome of this genus remains limited. In this study, we assembled and analysed the complete chloroplast genomes of three Macrotyloma species: M. uniflorum, M. geocarpum, and M. axillare. The chloroplast genomes were assembled into two isoforms that differ in the orientation of the small single-copy (SSC) region. Genome sizes ranged from 150,811 to 151,013 bp and exhibited the canonical quadripartite structure, comprising a pair of inverted repeats (IRa and IRb; 26,416–26,436 bp each), a large single-copy region (LSC; 80,229–80,446 bp), and a small single-copy region (SSC; 17,710–17,711 bp). Each genome encoded 110 unique genes, including 4 rRNA genes, 30 tRNA genes, and 76 protein-coding genes. All three species also possessed the ~ 50 kb inversion in the LSC region, a synapomorphy shared among a large clade within the Papilionoideae subfamily of Fabaceae. Although overall chloroplast genome structure and organisation were highly conserved among Macrotyloma species, gene-wise nucleotide diversity analysis identified seven relatively variable genes: rps18, rps15, ccsA, ndhA, ycf1, ycf4, and psaI. Phylogenomic analysis based on complete chloroplast genomes robustly resolved Macrotyloma as a monophyletic group within the Phaseolinae clade of the Papilionoideae subfamily. Within the genus, M. uniflorum and M. axillare formed a strongly supported sister pair, with M. geocarpum sister to this clade. Overall, this study provides valuable insights into chloroplast genome evolution in Macrotyloma and enhances understanding of its phylogenetic placement within Phaseoleae, offering genomic resources for future evolutionary, taxonomic, and conservation studies of this underutilised legume genus.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at https://doi.org/10.1038/s41598-026-55457-1.

Introduction

The genus Macrotyloma belongs to the legume family Fabaceae, subfamily Papilionoideae, tribe Phaseoleae1. It comprises primarily African and Asian herbaceous or twining species, several of which are valued as forage crops and as important dietary protein sources for humans, particularly in arid and semi-arid regions2. While many Macrotyloma species occur in the wild and serve as valuable reservoirs of genetic diversity, a few have been domesticated due to their ecological resilience and nutritional attributes. Among the cultivated species, Macrotyloma uniflorum (Lam.) Verdc., commonly known as horse gram, kulthi, or Madras gram, is a nutritionally rich yet underutilised pulse crop, cultivated in many parts of India as well as in parts of Southeast Asia and Africa3. The seeds of M. uniflorum are rich in protein, carbohydrates, dietary fibre, and essential minerals such as iron and calcium4. Other important species within the genus include Macrotyloma geocarpum (Harms) Maréchal & Baudet and Macrotyloma axillare (E. Mey.) Verdc. Macrotyloma geocarpum, commonly known as Kersting’s groundnut, is cultivated and consumed in parts of West Africa5. Macrotyloma axillare is a perennial species grown as a forage legume, valued for its high protein content and tolerance to heat and drought6. It is distributed across both the Indian subcontinent and Africa and closely related to M. uniflorum and is considered a likely perennial progenitor of this cultivated species7.

The chloroplast (cp.) genome is a circular, highly conserved, and predominantly maternally inherited organellar genome in most angiosperms. It plays a central role in photosynthesis as well as in essential metabolic pathways, including fatty acid and amino acid biosynthesis8. In most land plants, chloroplast genomes range from approximately 125 to 150 kilobase pairs (kb) and exhibit a conserved quadripartite structure, comprising a large single-copy (LSC) region and a small single-copy (SSC) region separated by two inverted repeats (IRs)9–14. Variation in chloroplast genome size is generally limited and is primarily attributed to the expansion and contraction of the IR regions15–17. Chloroplast DNA (cpDNA) has long been utilised in plant phylogenetics and taxonomy due to its haploid nature, uniparental inheritance, and relatively moderate evolutionary rate18–21. Individual chloroplast genes such as rbcL, matK, and trnL have been widely used in phylogenetic studies. For instance, analyses combining matK sequences with morphological and chemical characters have contributed to the classification of Fabaceae into six subfamilies22,23. However, reliance on a limited number of loci often provides insufficient resolution to resolve complex evolutionary relationships, particularly among closely related tribes and genera.

Advances in high-throughput sequencing technologies have enabled the rapid generation and public availability of complete chloroplast genome sequences. This has facilitated whole-plastome-based phylogenomics, offering substantially improved resolution across multiple taxonomic levels24–26. Comparative chloroplast genomics has further enabled detailed investigation of genome structure, rearrangements, IR boundary dynamics, mutational hotspots, and selective pressures, thereby contributing to a more comprehensive understanding of plant evolutionary history27.

In this study, we report the complete chloroplast genome sequences of three Macrotyloma species: the cultivated M. uniflorum, the underutilised M. geocarpum, and the perennial wild relative M. axillare. We characterised their genome structure, gene content, and repetitive elements, and performed comparative analyses to identify patterns of genomic divergence within the genus. Using whole chloroplast genome data, we further elucidated the phylogenetic placement and evolutionary history of Macrotyloma within the tribe Phaseoleae and across the Fabaceae family. Overall, this study enhances our understanding of chloroplast genome evolution in Macrotyloma and provides valuable genomic resources for species identification, phylogenetic inference, germplasm conservation, and the future utilisation of this underutilised legume genus.

Materials and methods

Plant samples, DNA extraction, and sequencing

Seeds of Macrotyloma uniflorum, commonly known as kulthi in India, were procured from the local market in New Delhi, India. For DNA extraction, seeds of Macrotyloma uniflorum were germinated under controlled laboratory conditions, and seedlings were grown for two weeks. Total genomic DNA was extracted from the leaf tissue of a single healthy seedling using a modified cetyltrimethylammonium bromide (CTAB) protocol28. High-quality genomic DNA was used for library preparation with the Nextera DNA Flex Library Prep Kit (Illumina, USA) following the manufacturer’s instructions. Cluster generation and paired-end sequencing were carried out on the Illumina NextSeq 2000 platform using 2 × 150 bp paired-end chemistry, according to standard protocols. The resulting paired-end reads were used for downstream genome assembly and analysis.

Chloroplast genome assembly and annotation

The quality of raw sequencing reads was assessed using FastQC v0.12.0 (https://www.bioinformatics.babraham.ac.uk/projects/fastqc/). Adapter sequences and low-quality reads were removed using Trim Galore v0.6.5, which was run with default adapter auto-detection and quality trimming (Phred score threshold 20) (https://www.bioinformatics.babraham.ac.uk/projects/trim_galore/). Chloroplast genome assemblies were generated using two independent tools, NOVOPlasty v2.7.229 and GetOrganelle v1.7.2 (option used the assembly, -R 15 -k 21,45,65,85,105 -F embplant_pt)30, to ensure assembly accuracy and consistency.

To compare the chloroplast genome of the M. uniflorum with other Macrotyloma species, chloroplast genomes of Macrotyloma axillare and Macrotyloma geocarpum were assembled from publicly available Illumina short-read data. Raw reads for M. axillare were retrieved from accession SRR19031527 (BioProject: PRJNA834023), while those for M. geocarpum were obtained from accession SRR31328150 (BioProject: PRJNA1185675). Genome annotation was performed using multiple chloroplast-specific tools, including GeSeq v2.0631, CPGAVAS2 v2.1.032 and PGA33. Annotated gene models were manually curated to confirm start codons, stop codons, and the absence of internal stop codons. Circular chloroplast genome maps were visualised using OGDRAW v1.3.134. The fully annotated chloroplast genome sequences were submitted to the NCBI GenBank database, and accession numbers were obtained.

The Codon W v1.4.4 in the Galaxy web server (https://galaxy.pasteur.fr/?form=codonw) was used to access the codon usage frequency and relative synonymous codon usage (RSCU) of protein-coding genes. SSRs were identified using the MISA Perl Script v2.1 using default criteria (https://webblast.ipk-gatersleben.de/misa/)35 with the following minimum repeat unit thresholds: mononucleotide ≥ 10 repeats, dinucleotide ≥ 6, trinucleotide ≥ 5, tetranucleotide ≥ 5, and pentanucleotide ≥ 5.

Comparative analysis of chloroplast genome structure

To investigate structural variation in the chloroplast genomes of Macrotyloma species, comparative analyses were performed with representative members of the tribe Phaseoleae, including Cajanus cajan (NC_031429.1), Phaseolus lunatus (NC_060403.1), Vigna radiata (NC_013843), Sphenostylis stenocarpa (PP920094.1), Lablab purpureus (NC_054310), and Glycine max (NC_007942.1). In addition, two members of the inverted repeat–lacking clade (IRLC), Cicer arietinum (NC_011163) and Medicago sativa (NC_042841.1), were included, with Arabidopsis thaliana (NC_000932) used as a reference. Chloroplast genome sequences were retrieved from the NCBI database. All genomes were reoriented relative to the rbcL gene using the Rotate v1.0 tool36 to ensure consistency and subsequently reannotated using GeSeq v2.0631. Whole-genome alignments were performed using the progressiveMauve algorithm implemented in Mauve to identify locally collinear blocks (LCBs)37. The boundaries of the large single-copy (LSC), small single-copy (SSC), and inverted repeat (IR) regions were visualised using CPJSdraw v1.0.038.

Nucleotide diversity analysis

To estimate gene-wise nucleotide diversity in the chloroplast genomes of Macrotyloma species, protein-coding genes, rRNA genes, and tRNA genes were extracted from annotated GenBank files using a custom Python script (https://github.com/Ram-Nagar/chloroplast-genome-analysis-scripts/tree/main). Each set of shared genes was aligned individually using MAFFT v7.52539. Nucleotide diversity (π) for each gene alignment was calculated using DnaSP v640. The π values were estimated following the method of Nei and Li41, which calculates the mean number of pairwise nucleotide differences per site among all possible sequence pairs. The nucleotide diversity values for each gene were visualised using ggplot2 v4.0.2 in the R environment.

Phylogenomics analysis

Phylogenetic relationships were inferred using chloroplast genome data from 34 species, including the three Macrotyloma species analysed in this study. Five species from the subfamily Caesalpinioideae (Neltuma juliflora, Neltuma glandulosa, Libidibia coriaria, Acacia ligulata, and Inga leiocalycina) were included as outgroups to root the tree. Only RefSeq-validated chloroplast genomes were selected to ensure annotation reliability. Protein-coding sequences (CDS) shared across all species were extracted from GenBank files using a custom Python script (https://github.com/Ram-Nagar/chloroplast-genome-analysis-scripts/tree/main). A total of 42 orthologous protein-coding genes were identified and concatenated in a consistent gene order for all taxa. The concatenated CDS alignments were generated using MAFFT v7.525 with the --auto option39. Poorly aligned and ambiguous regions were removed using trimAl v1.5.rev0 with automated trimming settings (-automated1)42. Maximum-likelihood phylogenetic analysis was conducted using RAxML v8.2.12 (raxmlHPC-PTHREADS) under the GTRGAMMA nucleotide substitution model with 1,000 bootstrap replicates (-m GTRGAMMA -N 1000 -p 12345)43. The tree was visualised and annotated in the R environment using the ggtree v4.1.1 and ggplot2 v4.0.2 packages44,45.

Results

General features of the chloroplast genomes of Macrotyloma species

The chloroplast genomes of all three Macrotyloma species were assembled into two isoforms that differ in the orientation of the small single-copy (SSC) region (Figure S1). This structural heteroplasmy is commonly reported in land plant plastomes. All three genomes exhibited a highly conserved quadripartite structure, consisting of a large single-copy (LSC) region, a small single-copy (SSC) region, and two inverted repeat (IR) regions (Fig. 1, Figure S2-3; Table 1). The overall genome size was highly conserved, ranging from 150,811 bp in M. geocarpum to 151,013 bp in M. uniflorum. The LSC regions ranged from 80,229 to 80,446 bp, while the SSC regions were nearly identical across species (17,710–17,711 bp). The IR regions were also highly similar in length (52,832–52,872 bp), indicating stable expansion–contraction dynamics among the three plastomes. Gene content was consistent across all species, with each genome encoding 110 unique genes, including 76 protein-coding genes, 4 rRNA genes, and 30 tRNA genes (Table 1). The overall GC content was similarly conserved, ranging from 35.3% to 35.4%.

Gene content and functional categories

The chloroplast genomes of Macrotyloma uniflorum and its related species encode a complete set of genes associated with photosynthesis, including ATP synthase, photosystem I and II, the cytochrome b₆/f complex, ribulose-1,5-bisphosphate carboxylase/oxygenase (rbcL), and NADH dehydrogenase (ndh) subunits (Table 2). Genes involved in self-replication comprise multiple ribosomal protein genes associated with both the small (SSU) and large (LSU) ribosomal subunits, as well as the four RNA polymerase subunits (rpoA–rpoC2). Additional essential genes, including ccsA, cemA, clpP, and matK, were also identified. The plastomes further contain conserved open reading frames (ycf1, ycf2, ycf3 and ycf4). Genes duplicated within the inverted repeat (IR) regions include ribosomal protein genes (rpl2, rpl23, rps7, rps12, and rps19), the NADH dehydrogenase subunit ndhB, several tRNA genes (trnA-UGC, trnI-CAU, trnI-GAU, trnL-CAA, trnN-GUU, trnR-ACG, and trnV-GAC), and all four rRNA genes (rrn4.5, rrn5, rrn16, and rrn23). The duplication of these genes within the IR regions contributes to increased gene dosage and enhanced transcriptional activity. A total of 15 genes containing introns were identified, including 9 protein-coding genes and 6 tRNA genes (Tables 2 and 3). Among protein-coding genes, clpP and ycf3 each contain two introns, while the remaining seven, atpF, ndhA, ndhB, petB, petD, rpl16, rpoC1, and rpl2, each contain one intron. Intron-containing tRNA genes included trnA-UGC, trnG-UCC, trnI-GAU, trnK-UUU, trnL-UAA, and trnV-UAC (Table 3). Several intron-containing genes are located within the IR regions, including rpl2, ndhB, trnA-UGC, and trnI-GAU, each present in two copies corresponding to IRa and IRb. The largest intron was observed in trnK-UUU (2,561 bp), which harbours the matK open reading frame within its sequence.

SSR contents and composition

A total of 66, 58, and 64 SSR loci were identified in the chloroplast genomes of M. axillare, M. geocarpum, and M. uniflorum, respectively (Fig. 2A and B; Table S4–S6). Across all three species, the majority of SSRs were in intergenic spacer (IGS) regions (45, 42, and 45 loci, respectively), followed by gene (coding) regions (12, 11, and 11 loci) and introns (9, 5, and 8 loci). Mononucleotide repeats were overwhelmingly dominant, comprising 60, 53, and 58 SSRs in M. axillare, M. geocarpum, and M. uniflorum, respectively, while dinucleotide SSRs were less frequent, with 6, 5, and 6 loci detected in each species. The consistent predominance of mononucleotide SSRs and their preferential distribution in IGS regions are features commonly observed in legume chloroplast genomes. The overall SSR composition and distribution were highly conserved across the three Macrotyloma species, with M. geocarpum consistently showing slightly fewer loci than the other two species, likely reflecting its marginally smaller genome size (150,811 bp versus 150,989 bp and 151,013 bp).

Codon usage analysis

The total number of codons in the chloroplast genomes of Macrotyloma species ranged from 20,587 to 20,595 (Table S1–S3). Codon usage analysis showed that codons such as TTT (Phe), ATT (Ile), TTA (Leu), GAT (Asp), GAA (Glu), and TAT (Tyr) were used more frequently, indicating a strong preference for A/T-rich codons. This pattern is consistent with the overall AT-rich nature of chloroplast genomes. In contrast, codons like ATC (Ile) and TGT (Cys) were less frequently used, suggesting selective constraints on codon usage. Overall, all genomes displayed a clear AT bias, especially at the third codon position. TTT (Phe) and ATT (Ile) were the most used codons, leucine was the most abundant amino acid, cysteine was the least abundant, and TAA was the preferred stop codon.

Comparative genome structure analysis

To examine structural differences, chloroplast genomes of Macrotyloma species were compared with closely related legume species and Arabidopsis thaliana using Mauve (Fig. 3). The cp. genomes of all three Macrotyloma species (M. axillare, M. geocarpum, M. uniflorum) are highly conserved and collinear, with no major rearrangements. This syntenic organisation is also shared with close relatives such as Sphenostylis stenocarpa and Lablab purpureus. All the legumes included, comprising Macrotyloma species and the Phaseoleae reference taxa Glycine max, Cajanus cajan, Phaseolus lunatus, Vigna radiata, Sphenostylis stenocarpa, and Lablab purpureus, carry the characteristic ~ 50 kb LSC inversion shared among a large clade within Papilionoideae. The size of the inversion varies slightly between the Macrotyloma species; M. uniflorum has a slightly larger inversion (~ 500 bp) compared to the other two species. As expected, members of the inverted repeat-lacking clade (IRLC), Cicer arietinum and Medicago sativa, have lost one inverted repeat and exhibit substantial genome rearrangements and inversion events relative to both Macrotyloma and Arabidopsis thaliana.

Inverted repeat contraction and expansion analysis

The boundaries and sizes of chloroplast genome regions were compared among Macrotyloma species and selected members of the Phaseoleae tribe (Fig. 4). The lengths of the LSC (80,229–80,446 bp), SSC (17,710–17,711 bp), and IR regions (~ 26,416–26,436 bp) were highly similar across all Macrotyloma species. The rps19 gene consistently crosses the IRb–LSC junction (JLB). The ycf1 gene spans both the IRb–SSC (JSB) and SSC–IRa (JSA) junctions, resulting in a truncated ycf1b pseudogene in the IRa region in related species. The ndhF gene is located entirely within the SSC region in all three species. These patterns are largely similar to those observed in Phaseolus vulgaris and Vigna radiata, indicating that the IR boundary structure is well conserved across the Phaseoleae tribe, with only minor differences in gene extension lengths at the junction regions.

Nucleotide diversity analysis

Although the chloroplast genomes of Macrotyloma species are highly conserved in structure and gene content, nucleotide diversity analysis revealed differences in evolutionary rates among genes (Fig. 5). Most genes showed very low nucleotide diversity, indicating strong purifying selection. However, seven genes, rps18, rps15, ccsA, ndhA, ycf1, ycf4, and psaI, exhibited higher nucleotide diversity, suggesting relaxed selection or species-specific variation. Among these, psaI, a component of Photosystem I, was the most variable. This gene is often associated with the accD-psaI region, which is known as a hotspot of variation in chloroplast genomes. The gene ycf1, one of the most variable plastid genes in angiosperms, also showed high diversity in Macrotyloma, supporting its usefulness as a molecular marker for species identification and population studies. Overall, these results show that while the chloroplast genome is largely conserved, some genes evolve more rapidly, providing useful targets for future evolutionary and functional studies.

Phylogenetic analysis

To resolve the evolutionary relationships of Macrotyloma species within the Fabaceae, a maximum-likelihood (ML) phylogeny was reconstructed using the concatenated coding sequences (CDS) of 42 shared chloroplast genes across 34 taxa. The dataset comprised the three Macrotyloma species analysed in this study, 26 additional representatives of the subfamily Papilionoideae spanning its major clades, and five Caesalpinioideae species, Neltuma juliflora, Neltuma glandulosa, Libidibia coriaria, Acacia ligulata, and Inga leiocalycina, used as outgroups to root the tree.

The resulting phylogeny robustly placed Macrotyloma within the Phaseolinae clade of Papilionoideae (Fig. 6). All three Macrotyloma species formed a strongly supported monophyletic group (bootstrap = 100%), confirming their close evolutionary relationship and the coherence of the genus. Within this clade, M. uniflorum and M. axillare were resolved as sister taxa, with M. geocarpum occupying a position sister to this pair. This topology is consistent with the hypothesis that M. axillare, a perennial wild species, is the progenitor of the domesticated annual M. uniflorum. The Macrotyloma clade was most closely allied with Sphenostylis stenocarpa (African yam bean) and Lablab purpureus, both of African origin, underscoring the biogeographic affinity of Macrotyloma with other indigenous African Phaseoleae.

Discussion

General features of the Macrotyloma species chloroplast genome

The chloroplast genome size of Macrotyloma species ranged from 150,811 to 151,013 bp, which is similar to other members of the Phaseoleae tribe, such as Vigna radiata, Phaseolus vulgaris, and Glycine max13,14,46. The small variation in genome size of Macrotyloma species primarily arises from minor differences in the length of the IR regions, a feature known to drive chloroplast size polymorphism47–50. The overall GC content (35.3–35.4%) was nearly identical among the three species and comparable to other legumes of the Phaseoleae tribe10,14. The GC content varied across regions, being higher in the IR (~ 41.5%) than in the LSC (32.7–32.8%) and SSC (28.6–28.7%) regions, mainly due to the presence of rRNA and tRNA genes in the IR51,52. Each genome encoded 76 protein-coding genes, 30 tRNA genes, and 4 rRNA genes. The number and density were similar to those of the close members, Glycine max and Phaseolus vulgaris13,46. Most protein-coding genes use the standard start codon ATG, except for ndhD and psbL, which use ACG but are post-transcriptionally converted to the canonical AUG start codon through C-to-U RNA editing53–55.

A total of 15 genes contained introns, with clpP and ycf3 having two introns each. The largest intron was found in trnK-UUU, which contains the matK gene56. The trans-splicing of rps12 reflects a conserved gene structure commonly seen in plant chloroplast genomes57. Analysis of repetitive sequences identified 58–66 chloroplast SSRs (cpSSRs) in Macrotyloma species. Chloroplast SSRs are considered valuable molecular markers for evolutionary and taxonomic studies due to their maternal inheritance and relatively low mutation rates58,59. These cpSSRs represent informative genetic markers capable of capturing fine-scale genetic variation and therefore hold considerable potential for population genetic and phylogeographic studies in this underexplored genus60.

Structural variation and IR contraction and expansion

The organisation of the chloroplast genome is largely conserved across plant species. However, inversions, rearrangements, and expansions or contractions of the inverted repeat (IR) regions have been frequently reported61. Within the Fabaceae, several lineage-defining structural variations have been documented62. For example, a large inversion of approximately 50–70 kb in the LSC region is characteristic of the Papilionoideae subfamily63. Furthermore, within this subfamily, a monophyletic group of legumes has completely lost one copy of the inverted repeat, defining the inverted repeat-lacking clade (IRLC)62. The chloroplast genomes of all three Macrotyloma species were assembled into two isoforms differing in the orientation of the SSC region. This structural heteroplasmy is caused by flip-flop recombination64. Except for this structural heteroplasmy, the plastomes of M. axillare, M. geocarpum, and M. uniflorum are highly conserved and collinear. The plastid of Macrotyloma species has retained the ~ 50 kb inversion in the LSC spanning from trnK to accD, a synapomorphy of Papilionoideae63,65.

Sequence Divergence and Hypervariable Regions

Chloroplast genomes are generally subject to strong purifying selection, which limits sequence variation across most protein-coding genes66. Nevertheless, certain chloroplast genes consistently emerge as hypervariable regions across angiosperms and legumes67. Gene-wise nucleotide diversity (π) analysis revealed moderate to elevated diversity in several coding regions, including rps18, rps15, ccsA, ndhA, ycf1, ycf4, and psaI. Among these, ycf1 is widely recognised as one of the most variable chloroplast coding genes68,69. The moderate diversity observed in ycf4, ccsA, and ndhA further supports the notion that ribosomal protein genes and photosynthesis-related genes can accumulate lineage-specific variation despite overall plastome conservation. These hypervariable regions represent promising molecular markers for resolving phylogenetic relationships within Macrotyloma and across the Leguminosae. In many previous studies, genes such as ycf1, accD, ndhF, and noncoding regions including trnL–trnF and atpB–rbcL have been used to infer evolutionary relationships, domestication histories70,71, and centres of origin in legume crops such as Phaseolus lunatus and Quercus bawanglingensis72,73.

Phylogenetic Relationships

The phylogenetic placement of Macrotyloma within the Fabaceae has previously been inferred from a limited molecular dataset74. The availability of complete chloroplast genome sequences has substantially enhanced phylogenetic resolution in plant systematics75. In this study, we used concatenated coding sequences of 42 shared chloroplast genes across 34 taxa to resolve both the intra- and intergeneric relationships of Macrotyloma. The phylogeny robustly places Macrotyloma within the Phaseolinae clade of the Papilionoideae subfamily76,77.

All three species, M. uniflorum, M. axillare, and M. geocarpum, form a well-supported monophyletic clade. Within this clade, M. uniflorum and M. axillare appear as sister taxa, consistent with hypotheses that M. axillare is a perennial progenitor of the domesticated M. uniflorum7,74. The African species M. geocarpum clusters close to this pair, forming a cohesive lineage distinct from other Phaseoleae genera. The closest relatives to the Macrotyloma subclade are Sphenostylis stenocarpa (African yam bean) and Lablab purpureus, both of African origin. This relationship underscores Macrotyloma’s biogeographic affinity with other indigenous African legumes and suggests that the genus’s cultivated members were derived from the same ancestral gene pool. The horsegram (M. uniflorum) is widely grown in India and other parts of Asia78, whereas Kersting’s groundnut (M. geocarpum) is a traditional legume of West Africa5. The observed phylogeographic separation of the Asian species (M. uniflorum) and the African species (M. geocarpum) is consistent with geographically distinct diversification events within the genus. However, rigorously distinguishing wild lineage divergence from independent human-mediated domestication events would require nuclear genomic data or population-level analyses across a broader sampling of wild and cultivated accessions74. In summary, our phylogenomic analysis affirms the monophyly and distinct evolutionary trajectory of Macrotyloma within the Phaseoleae tribe. This placement refines our understanding of its domestication history and supports targeted efforts for genetic improvement and conservation of these underutilised legumes23,77,79.

Conclusions

This study presents the first comprehensive comparative analysis of complete chloroplast genomes from multiple Macrotyloma species, providing new genomic resources for an underutilised but agriculturally important legume genus. Although the chloroplast genomes of Macrotyloma species exhibit a high degree of structural conservation in gene content and organisation, they show moderate levels of nucleotide diversity across genes. A few genes stand out as highly variable: rps18, rps15, ccsA, ndhA, ycf1, ycf4, and psaI, and can be used as barcoding and species identification markers. Chloroplast phylogenomic analyses robustly resolve Macrotyloma as a monophyletic lineage within the Phaseolinae clade of Papilionoideae and clarify its relationships with other Phaseoleae genera. The close sister relationship between M. uniflorum and M. axillare is consistent with previous hypotheses of a close evolutionary affinity. M. geocarpum is resolved as sister to the M. uniflorum–M. axillare clade, highlighting an early divergence within the genus. Together, these findings refine our understanding of Macrotyloma evolution and its position within legume phylogeny. Beyond evolutionary insights, the annotated chloroplast genomes and identified cpSSRs reported here provide valuable tools for future studies on population genetics, phylogeography, and conservation. Given the importance of Macrotyloma species for climate-resilient agriculture and food security, these chloroplast genomes offer a genomic resource to support germplasm conservation and the genetic improvement of these lesser-known legume crops.
