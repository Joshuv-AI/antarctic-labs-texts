Genetic Dissection of Rice Plant-Specific Non-ε Group 14-3-3 Proteins in Response to Phosphate Starvation
Authors: Hudong Kuang, Meng Yan, Siqi Chen, Tingyue Deng, Wang Chen, Mengyang Xie, Zhujun Wang, Yanhua Zhu, Honghui Lin, Jian Yang
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
The 14-3-3 proteins are highly conserved and widely distributed across eukaryotes. Some 14-3-3 proteins have been identified as regulators of phosphorus (Pi) deficiency tolerance in rice, but their diverse functions remain largely unexplored. In this study, we characterized the role of rice plant-specific non-ε group 14-3-3 proteins (OsGF14a-f) in response to Pi starvation by mutating these genes. We found that the expression of OsGF14a decreased in response to Pi starvation, while the expression of other non-ε group genes was induced. Subcellular localization studies transiently expressing them in tobacco leaves showed that OsGF14a was present in both the cytoplasm and nucleus, whereas the other proteins were predominantly localized in the cytoplasm. By developing single and multiple mutants, we demonstrated that OsGF14a plays a negative role in Pi homeostasis and root growth, while OsGF14b, OsGF14c and OsGF14f may act as positive regulators of Pi homeostasis and root growth in rice. However, all non-ε group 14-3-3 genes positively regulated rhizosphere acidification. Furthermore, the mutation of OsGF14a enhanced Pi accumulation and plant growth under various Pi supply conditions, likely due to the induction of OsPHR3, OsPT2 and OsPHO1;2 in the roots. Overall, this study highlights the diverse functions of plant-specific non-ε group 14-3-3 proteins in response to Pi starvation in rice and identifies the mutation of OsGF14a as a potential strategy to improve rice tolerance to Pi deficiency.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12284-025-00840-1.

Background

Phosphorus is essential for the growth and reproduction of plants, as it is a key component of various bio-macromolecules, including DNA, RNA, ATP, phospholipids, and metabolites (Yang et al. 2024). However, the availability of phosphate (Pi) in soil often falls short of what is necessary for optimal plant growth. In agriculture, farmers apply large quantities of P-fertilizers to enhance and sustain crop yields (Elser et al. 2024). Unfortunately, only 20–30% of the phosphate applied is absorbed and utilized by plants, with the majority leaching into the environment, leading to pollution (Campos-Soriano et al. 2020; Poirier et al. 2022). Additionally, Pi rock, the primary source of P-fertilizers, is a non-renewable resource (Paz-Ares et al. 2022). Therefore, improving the efficiency of Pi acquisition and utilization in crops is crucial to minimize the waste of P-fertilizers and reduce environmental pollution, thereby supporting sustainable development for both humanity and the ecosystem.

Plants have developed a variety of strategies to adapt to phosphate (Pi) deficiency. These strategies can be categorized into two main aspects: phenotypic modifications and physiological responses. In response to Pi starvation, many plants prioritize root growth, particularly by increasing the density and length of root hairs, which enhances the surface area in contact with the soil (Lynch 2011; Kochian et al. 2015). Additionally, plant roots can actively exclude organic acids and hydrogen ions (H+) to release soluble Pi from iron-phosphate (Fe-P), aluminum-phosphate (Al-P), and calcium-phosphate (Ca-P) compounds present in the soil (Plaxton and Tran 2011). Furthermore, they may engage in communication with soil microbes by supplying sugars, lipids, and metabolites, thereby increasing the concentration of available Pi (Vigneron et al. 2018; Meng et al. 2020). Pi deficiency can inhibit shoot growth and delay flowering time, which may ultimately be advantageous for accumulating sufficient Pi for reproductive success (Peng et al. 2020). At the physiological level, plant shoots can release Pi from lipid-bound and nucleic acid-bound phosphorus, facilitating Pi remobilization among various tissues (Veneklaas et al. 2012; Lopez-Arredondo et al. 2014).

The 14-3-3 proteins, also known as GRF or GFP proteins, represent a highly conserved family of acidic proteins found in eukaryotic organisms (Zhao et al. 2021). These proteins consist of a conserved central core flanked by two divergent regions at both ends. In Arabidopsis, there are 15 identified members, while rice contains 8 members (GF14a-h). Phylogenetic analysis categorizes plant 14-3-3 proteins into two groups: the ε group (GF14g-h) and the plant-specific non-ε group (GF14a-f) (Wang et al. 2023a, b). The 14-3-3 proteins act as chaperones or molecular scaffolds, engaging in physical interactions with phosphorylated targets. This interaction leads to alterations in the tts’ activity, subcellular localization, stability or the composition of protein complexes (Taoargeka et al. 2011; Takeo & Ito 2017; Campo et al. 2012). Research has shown that these proteins play a crucial role in regulating plant growth and reproduction, and responding to various biotic and abiotic stresses, including phosphate (Pi) starvation (Purwestri et al. 2009; Mayfield et al. 2012; Zhang et al. 2010). Plants uptake of Pi depends on H+/Pi symport system. Increased activity of the plasma membrane (PM) H+-ATPase has been evidenced in improvement of Pi absorb in Pi-deficient conditions (Shen et al. 2006). 14-3-3 proteins could function as natural ligands of PM H+-ATPase by directly interacting and displacing the auto-inhibitory domain of the H(+)-ATPase, then activating proton pumping (Jahn et al. 1997). Apoplastic H+ can improve root growth by loosening the cell wall. 14-3-3 proteins have been found to regulate root growth by affecting PM H+-ATPase (Wang et al. 2021). 14-3-3 proteins are also play roles in carbon allocation in Arabidopsis and tomato to modify root architecture (Xu et al., 2012a, b; Zhang et al. 2018). Although the specific functions of 14-3-3s in Pi homeostasis have yet to be validated through mutation or overexpression. Pepper Ca14-3-3 could interact with CaWRKY58 to enhance its activity, and the later could regulate Pi homeostasis (Cai et al. 2021). These findings suggest that 14-3-3 proteins may also play a role in maintaining Pi homeostasis in plants. Given that rice serves as a staple food for half of the global population, understanding the diverse functions of 14-3-3 proteins in response to Pi starvation is essential. Investigating the rice 14-3-3 protein family in this study may uncover both general and specific roles, providing potential candidates for enhancing Pi acquisition and utilization efficiency in crops.

In this study, we explored the role of plant-specific non-ε group OsGF14 in response to phosphorus (Pi) starvation, highlighting its isoform specificity and redundancy in Pi accumulation in rice. Notably, phylogenetic analysis and subcellular localization studies revealed that OsGF14a possesses distinct functions compared to other non-ε members, further reinforcing its unique contribution to Pi homeostasis. Additionally, OsGF14a was identified as a potential target to improve P acquisition efficiency and P utilization efficiency in rice.

The Evolutionary Relationships and Subcellular Localization of Plant-Specific Non-ε Group of the OsGF14 Protein Family

A phylogenetic analysis of non-ε members was conducted within several plant species, including Physcomitrella patens, Selaginella moellendorffii, Arabidopsis thaliana, Vitis vinifera, Manihot esculenta, Glycine max, Malus × domestica, Zea mays, Brachypodium distachyon, Sorghum bicolor, Setaria italica, and rice (Fig. 1). The results revealed that the non-ε members in rice could be categorized into two distinct clusters: one cluster exclusively containing OsGF14a, and the other comprising OsGF14b through OsGF14f. This finding suggests that OsGF14a has a more unique evolutionary relationship with other non-ε OsGF14 proteins.

14-3-3 proteins are widely localized in diverse subcellular compartments, implicating their multifunctional roles in cellular processes. We investigated the subcellular localization of OsGF14 proteins belonging to the plant-specific non-ε group by transiently expressing them fused with green fluorescent protein (GFP) in N. benthamiana leaves. When OsGF14a-GFP or GFP alone were expressed in tobacco leaves, fluorescent signals were observed in both the cytoplasm and nucleus, whereas the other proteins were predominantly localized in the cytoplasm (Fig. S1). Based on the phylogenetic analysis and subcellular localization results, it appears that OsGF14a may function differently from other plant-specific non-ε members, while the remaining non-ε members may operate redundantly in rice.

Expression Pattern of Plant-Specific Non-ε OsGF14 Genes in Rice

To investigate the role of OsGF14 proteins in response to Pi starvation, we analyzed the expression levels of OsGF14a to OsGF14f during the seedling stage (10 days post-germination), the tillering stage (~ 40 days), and the flowering stage using RT-qPCR (Fig. S2). At the seedling stage, OsGF14a, OsGF14c, OsGF14d, OsGF14e and OsGF14f showed higher expression levels in leaves compared to roots. Notably, the expression of OsGF14a, OsGF14c, OsGF14d, and OsGF14e in leaves gradually decreased from the growth stage to the flowering stage, while OsGF14f expression remained stable throughout the rice life cycle (Fig. S2). Additionally, OsGF14a and OsGF14b maintained relatively stable expression levels in the roots throughout the life cycle of rice. Overall, the plant-specific non-ε group proteins exhibited the highest expression levels in the spikelets, indicating their significant roles in seed development.

We established a Pi-deficient environment (Fig. S3) and investigated the response of plant-specific non-ε OsGF14 expressions under such conditions. In shoots, Pi deficiency suppressed the expression of OsGF14a while promoting the expression of OsGF14d, OsGF14e and OsGF14f (Fig. 2A-F). In roots, Pi starvation led to the increased expression of OsGF14b, OsGF14c, OsGF14e and OsGF14f (Fig. 2G-L). Additionally, we observed that other mineral element deficiencies also influenced these expressions. For instance, nitrogen deficiency induced OsGF14f but inhibited OsGF14a in shoots. Notably, all plant-specific non-ε members, except OsGF14a, were induced in roots following nitrogen deficiency. Potassium deficiency reduced the expression of OsGF14a, OsGF14b, and OsGF14c in shoots, while enhancing the expression of OsGF14b, OsGF14c, OsGF14d and OsGF14f in roots. Calcium deficiency had a minimal impact on the expression of non-ε members, only inhibiting OsGF14a in shoots. Conversely, magnesium deficiency significantly increased the expression of OsGF14d, OsGF14e and OsGF14f in shoots, as well as OsGF14c, OsGF14d and OsGF14e in roots. Iron deficiency induced the expression of OsGF14b and OsGF14e in both shoots and roots (Fig. 2). These findings suggest that OsGF14a may play a distinct role in the response to Pi starvation compared to other plant-specific non-ε members.

The Plant-Specific Non-ε OsGF14 Genes Have Distinct Functions in Maintaining Phosphate Homeostasis

To investigate the functional redundancy among plant-specific non-ε OsGF14 genes in rice, we initially generated single mutants for all plant-specific non-ε members using CRISPR Cas-9 (Fig. S4-S6). Ten-day-old seedlings of Osgf14 mutants and wild-type plants were cultivated under both Pi-sufficient (200 µM) and Pi-deficient (10 µM) conditions for four weeks. Under Pi-sufficient conditions, only the Osgf14a mutants demonstrated a significantly higher accumulation of Pi in both shoots and roots compared to wild-type plants, while the other single mutants showed no significant differences (Fig. 3A and B). Pi concentration remained comparable among all single mutants and ZH11 in both shoots and roots under Pi-deficient conditions (Fig. 3A and B).

Based on expression patterns, phylogenetic analysis, and subcellular localization of OsGF14 family members, we subsequently generated double mutants Osgf14a/Osgf14f (af), Osgf14b/Osgf14c (bc), Osgf14c/Osgf14f (cf.), and Osgf14d/Osgf14e (de), as well as triple mutants Osgf14a/Osgf14c/Osgf14f (acf) and Osgf14b/Osgf14d/Osgf14e (bde) and a quadruple mutant Osgf14b/Osgf14c/Osgf14d/Osgf14e (bcde) through hybridization to further explore the redundant functions of these members. Under Pi-sufficient conditions, the Pi concentration in the rice shoots of af and acf mutants was significantly higher compared to the wild type, while other double, triple, and quadruple mutants did not show such differences (Fig. 3C). In rice roots, all double mutants was similar to that of the wild type; however, acf, bde, and bcde mutants showed significant reductions (Fig. 3D). Under Pi-deficient conditions, the Pi concentration in the rice shoots of af and de mutants accumulated more Pi, whereas bc, cf., acf, bde, and bcde mutants accumulated less Pi compared to wild-type plants (Fig. 3C). In rice roots under Pi-deficient conditions, af mutants maintained a higher Pi concentration than wild-type plants, while bcde accumulated less Pi, and other mutants displayed no significant changes (Fig. 3D).

Mutation of Plant-Specific Non-ε OsGF14 Decreased Rhizosphere Acidification Capacity

Rhizosphere acidification is a key strategy for enhancing the release of insoluble Pi from soil minerals, thereby facilitating Pi absorption by plants. To assess the rhizosphere acidification capacity of plants, we transferred 4-day-old mutants under LP conditions (5 µM) to agar medium containing 0.02% (w/v) bromocresol purple (pH 5.6) (Fig. 4A). We utilized Image J to calculate the discoloration area in the agar medium, which enabled us to infer the H+ secretion from the roots of rice plants. Among the mutants studied, disruption of OsGF14a, OsGF14b, and OsGF14c individually resulted in a reduction of approximately 30% in rhizosphere acidification capacity compared to the wild-type (Fig. 4B). In contrast, the single mutants Osgf14d, Osgf14e, and Osgf14f, as well as the double mutants cf. and de, exhibited a rhizosphere acidification capacity comparable to that of the wild-type. Further mutations of OsGF14a in both Osgf14f and cf. led to a decrease in their rhizosphere acidification capacities. Notably, the rhizosphere acidification capacity in the bde and bcde mutants further declined to 50% of that observed in the wild-type. These findings suggest that plant-specific non-ε OsGF14 proteins play significant roles in H+ transport capacity.

The Mutations of Non-ε OsGF14 Exhibited Altered Root Growth

Pi starvation has been found to promotes rice root elongation. The seminal root lengths of Osgf14 single and multiple mutants and ZH11 plants were cultivated with (200 µM) or without (0 µM) Pi for 7 days (Fig. 5A). Indeed, the root length of ZH11 cultivated under Pi-deficient conditions was longer than that grown in Pi-sufficient conditions. Under Pi-sufficient (200 µM) conditions, there were no significant differences in root length among all single mutants compared to the wild type (Fig. 5B). Correspondingly, the root lengths of all single mutants (except for Osgf14d) were similar to that of the wild type under Pi-deficient (0 µM) conditions (Fig. 5C). Interestingly, regardless of the Pi conditions, the af, cf., bc, de double and bde triple mutants showed similar root lengths compared to the wild type, but acf triple and bcde quadruple mutant showed shorter roots (Fig. 5B, C). These results indicate that plant-specific non-ε OsGF14 redundantly modulate root growth in rice.

The Mutation of OsGF14a Enhanced Tolerance to Pi Starvation

We further investigated the phenotype and Pi concentration of Osgf14a plants under varying Pi supply conditions. Ten-day-old Osgf14a and wild-type seedlings were cultivated for an additional 4 weeks under Pi-sufficient (200 µM; HP), Pi-moderate (50 µM; MP), Pi-deficient (5 µM; LP), and extremely Pi-deficient (1 µM; EP) conditions (Fig. 6A). Under HP conditions, there were no significant differences in plant height and root length between Osgf14a and wild-type plants (Fig. 6B and C). Notably, Osgf14a mutants exhibited increases of at least 22.4% and 19.6% in plant height under MP and EP conditions, respectively (Fig. 6C). Additionally, the root length of Osgf14a plants was elongated by 15% and 19% under LP and EP conditions, respectively, compared to wild type (Fig. 6B). Pi concentration in shoot of Osgf14a mutants were higher than that in wild type under HP, MP and EP conditions (Fig. 6D). However, in roots, Osgf14a mutants exhibited significantly higher Pi accumulation compared to wild type only under HP conditions (Fig. 6E). We further compared the expression levels of some Pi-responsive genes, including OsPHO1;2, OsPHT1;2, OsPHR3, and OsIPS1, between wild type and Osgf14a lines in the roots. Under HP conditions, the expression of OsPHO1;2 in Osgf14a lines was up-regulated by ~ 1-fold, OsPHT1;2 by ~ 2.5-fold, and OsPHR3 ~ 2.2-fold, respectively (Fig. 6F). These findings suggest that OsGF14a negatively regulates both plant growth and Pi concentrations in response to Pi starvation in rice.

Discussion

The 14-3-3 proteins primarily form homo- or heterodimers, allowing for the simultaneous interaction with two different phosphor-targets. (Chevalier et al. 2009; Camoni et al. 2018). This dimeric topology positions of 14-3-3 proteins as versatile scaffolds that can coordinate signal integration across various pathways, such as abiotic stress adaptation (Oecking 2009; Camoni et al. 2018; Huang et al. 2022). Previous studies have linked specific 14-3-3 isoforms to Pi starvation responses (Xu et al. 2012a; Yuan et al. 2017a; Zhang et al. 2018). However, the contributions of 14-3-3 proteins in rice response to Pi starvation remain unclear. In this study, we found that plant-specific non-ε 14-3-3 proteins in rice confer functional similarities and specificity in function in root growth and Pi uptake, particularly highlighting the key regulatory role of OsGF14a in negatively regulating plant growth and Pi absorption in response to Pi deficiency in rice.

The high degree of sequence conservation among the 14-3-3 isoforms results in significant functional redundancy (Camoni et al. 2018), characterizing the phenotype of individual 14-3-3 mutants has historically been challenging (Roberts 2003). In this study, among all the single mutants examined, only Osgf14a demonstrated a change in Pi accumulation in both shoots and roots. (Figure 3A and B). Notably, Pi concentration of af double mutant was consistently higher than that of acf triple mutant irrespective of the Pi supply, suggesting OsGF14c serve as positive regulator of Pi homeostasis in rice (Fig. 3C and D). Moreover, Pi concentration in root of bcde under Pi-deficient conditions was lower than that in bde further support it. Under Pi-deficient conditions, the Pi concentration in shoots of bde was decreased compared with that in de, indicating the positive regulators in Pi homeostasis of OsGF14b. The cf. double mutants showed a lower Pi level in shoot under Pi-deficient conditions, while their single mutants not, reflecting the positive function of OsGF14f in Pi homeostasis (Fig. 3C). However, for OsGF14d and OsGF14e, it is challenging to draw a conclusion in regulation of Pi homeostasis. On the one hand, the de double mutants showed a higher Pi level in shoot under Pi-deficient conditions, while their single mutants not (Fig. 3A and C). On the other hand, while roots of bc and de double mutants accumulated similar level of Pi compared to the wild type under Pi-sufficient conditions, Pi concentration in root of bcde was lower than that in the wild-type (Fig. 3D). These results indicate that OsGF14a act as a negative regulator of Pi homeostasis, while OsGF14b, OsGF14c and OsGF14f may serve as positive regulators of Pi homeostasis in rice, and revealing the functional redundancy of OsGF14b to OsGF14e in Pi homeostasis. Interestingly, among them, only the expression of OsGF14a was reduced after Pi deficiency, while the expressions of others were induced or remained stable. This phenomenon was also observed in the 14-3-3 genes of tomato (Xu et al., 2012). Cao et al. (2007) revealed that the expressions of some characterized 14-3-3 genes were reduced in response to Pi starvation, while others were stable. The modification of expression along with Pi changes in mutants revealed the diversity of 14-3-3 genes in response to Pi starvation.

14-3-3 proteins can bind phosphorylation of the conserved Thr residue at the C-terminus of H+-ATPase, which enhances its proton pump activity and acidify the rhizosphere (Xu et al. 2012b; Li et al. 2022; Kabała and Janicka 2023). Our research shown that Osgf14a, Osgf14b, Osgf14c resulted in approximately a 30% reduction in rhizosphere acidification capacity compared to wild type, while Osgf14d, Osgf14e, Osgf14f, cf., and de maintained normal acidification levels. Notably, af, acf, bde, and bcde exhibited more severe acidification defects (Fig. 4A and B). Root length of bcde mutants was only reduced about 13% of wild type, while roots of bde mutants was similar with the wild type (Fig. 5). The rhizosphere acidification capacity in the bde and bcde mutants declined to 50% of that observed in the wild-type (Fig. 4), strongly validate their function in regulating rhizosphere acidification capacity. These results indicate that all rice plant-specific non-ε 14-3-3s act as positive roles to activate H+-ATPase and drive H+ efflux. Because the nutrient solution used in this study with 2-Morpholinoethanesulphonic acid (MES), the changes of Pi accumulation in these 14-3-3 mutants did not result from modification of pH surrounding roots, but changed the activity of Pi transporters in root.

Although the 14-3-3 protein sequences are highly conserved, variation does exist within the N- and C-terminal domains, which have functions in isoform specificity (Aitken 2006; Xu et al. 2012a; Huang et al. 2022). This observation aligns with reported functional heterogeneity among 14-3-3 isoforms in rice roots (Zhang et al. 2017), and differential H+-ATPase activation capacities of 14-3-3 isoforms in Arabidopsis (Pallucca et al. 2014). Importantly, the certain cell types exhibit strict functional dependence for specific 14-3-3 isoforms (Paul et al. 2012), which provides a potential mechanistic framework for the observed isoform specificity in this study. By application of R18 peptide, the inhibitor of 14-3-3 proteins, Wang et al.(2021) revealed the role of 14-3-3 proteins in regulating root growth with or without Pi supply. Our genetic results further confirmed it, as the bcde quadruple mutant indeed showed a shorter root with or without Pi supply. However, we found that mutation of OsGF14a significantly improved root growth in response to Pi starvation (Fig. 6A and B). Results indicated that functional diversity of rice plant-specific non-ε 14-3-3 proteins on root growth in response to phosphate starvation.

The 14-3-3 proteins regulate the subcellular localization of target proteins by specific binding motifs (Fan et al. 2023). While the predominant cytoplasmic localization of 14-3-3 isoforms function as cytoplasmic anchors (Purwestri et al. 2009), OsGF14a exhibits a unique nucleo-cytoplasmic dual localization distinct from other 14-3-3 isoforms in tobacco leaf epidermal cells (Fig. S1), suggesting their multilayered role in Pi homeostasis and Pi starvation response. Thus, OsGF14a might bind to the phosphorylated target proteins in the cytoplasm or/and nucleus to shuttle their localization, thereby modulating Pi starvation response. OsPHR3, a homolog of the core PSR-regulating transcription factor OsPHR2, directly binds to the promoters of PSR genes and enhancing their expression. In rice roots, the mutation of OsGF14a induced the expression of OsPHR3, OsPT2, and OsPHO1;2, but unaffected OsIPS1 expression under Pi-sufficient conditions (Fig. 6F). 14-3-3 proteins had been found to regulate carbon allocation in plants (Xu et al., 2012; Zhang et al. 2018). Our previous report and others revealed sugars could improve phosphate accumulation by induction expression of Pi transporter (Paz-Ares et al. 2022; Yan et al. 2022). Thus, mutation of OsGF14a accumulated more Pi in root might be resulted from change carbon allocation and induced expression of Pi transporters, although expression of OsGF14a was reduced in shoot but not root under Pi deficiency. OsPT2 and OsPHO1;2 plays crucial roles in root-shoot Pi translocation (Ai et al. 2009; Secco et al. 2010; Ma et al. 2021). Mutation of OsGF14a enhanced about 20% of the root-shoot Pi translocation (Fig. 6D and E), which might be resulted from induction of OsPT2 and OsPHO1;2 in roots. Because mutation of OsGF14a improved rice plant tolerance to Pi starvation, similar to the effect of overexpression of OsPHR3 (Guo et al. 2015), OsGF14a might regulate Pi starvation response largely through OsPHR3.

Previous reports have found that OsGF14a, OsGF14b, OsGF14e, and OsGF14f proteins are localized to the nucleus and cytoplasm in rice, and OsGF14c is localized to the cytoplasm (Chen et al. 2006; Dong et al. 2023; Fu et al. 2021). The differences observed when using different systems may result from the regulation of post-translational modifications of 14-3-3 isoforms in rice but not others, which in turn regulates subcellular localization. The plant 14-3-3 proteins are phosphorylated in vivo (Yang et al. 2019), which may affect their subcellular localization, function and stability. Previous reports indicate that phosphorylated 14-3-3 isoforms translocate to the nucleus to stabilize CBF proteins under cold stress, thereby enhancing cold tolerance in Arabidopsis (Liu et al. 2017). Thus, the differences observed among different species in this study indicated the significance of post-translational modifications in regulating the localization of 14-3-3 isoforms.

Conclusions

This study revealed that the functional redundancy and isoform-specific of rice non-ε 14-3-3 proteins in regulating Pi homeostasis, rhizosphere acidification capacity and root growth. While OsGF14a negative regulator of Pi homeostasis and plant growth, other non-ε OsGF14 may positively regulate Pi homeostasis and rhizosphere acidification. We also found that the mutation of OsGF14a enhanced Pi accumulation and plant growth under various Pi supply conditions, likely due to the induction of OsPHR3, OsPT2 and OsPHO1;2 in the roots, providing a potential strategy to improve rice tolerance to Pi deficiency.

Materials and Methods

Plant Materials and Growth Environments

The Osgf14a, Osgf14b, Osgf14c, Osgf14d, Osgf14e and Osgf14f mutants were generated using CRISPR-Cas9 technique with Oryza sativa cv. Zhonghua 11 as the genetic background, and the mutants were obtained from FUTURE BREEDING (Hainan) Biotechnological Co., LTD (http://www.wimibio.com/). The mutation sites of OsGF14a-f were identified by sequencing analysis (Fig. S4-S6). The primers utilized for identifying mutants and target sequences were detailed in Table S1. An Osgf14a homozygous allele was crossed with an Osgf14f homozygous allele. Double mutants af were obtained from the F1 population. The other double mutants, designated bc, cf., de, were obtained using the same method along with the triple mutant and quadruple mutants, designated acf, bde and bcde.

Filled seeds were soaked in 0.7% HNO3 for 18 h (25℃), disinfected with 4% NaClO for 5 min, and then washed with ddH2O several times. Subsequently, seeds were incubated in sterile distilled deionized water (ddH2O) at 37 °C in darkness to enhance germination. All plants were grown in a greenhouse with a 12-hour light (30 ℃)/12-hour (22 ℃) dark photoperiod, with an optical density of about 200 µM m−2 s−1 and approximately 60% relative humidity (Yang et al. 2016). The normal nutrient solution contained 0.2 mM NaH2PO4, 1.429 mM NH4NO3, 0.1915 mM K2SO4, 0.366 mM CaCl2, 0.547 mM MgSO4, 0.005 mM MnCl2, 0.03 mM H3BO3, 0.001 mM (NH4)6Mo7O24, 0.004 mM ZnSO4, 0.002 mM CuSO4, 0.1 mM Fe-EDTA and 2 mM MES and then the nutrient solution was adjusted to pH 5.5 using HCl or NaOH. Nutrient solutions were refreshed every 7 days (Yang et al. 2019).

The Nutrient Deficiency Conditions

The N, P, K, Ca, Mg, and Fe deficiency treatments refer to rice nutrient solutions lacking NH4NO3, NaH2PO4, K2SO4, CaCl2, MgSO4, and Fe-EDTA, respectively. Notably, the S element was supplemented with H2SO4 to the appropriate concentration after the K and Mg deficiency treatments. Additionally, the S deficiency solution contained 0.2 mM NaH2PO4, 1.429 mM NH4NO3, 0.383 mM KCl, 0.366 mM CaCl2, 0.547 mM MgCl2, 0.005 mM MnCl2, 0.03 mM H3BO3, 0.001 mM (NH4)6Mo7O24, 0.004 mM ZnCl2, 0.002 mM CuCl2, 0.1 mM FeCl3 and 2 mM MES and then the nutrient solution was adjusted to pH 5.5 using HOC(COOH)(CH2COOH)2·H2O. The remaining operations and growth environment measures are consistent with the normal nutrient solution cultivation method.

RNA Extraction and Quantitative Real-Time PCR (qRT-PCR) Analysis

Total RNA was extracted using an RNA extraction kit (NucleoSpin RNA Plant; Macherey-Nagel, Duren, Germany). Under different nutrient deficiency conditions, the total RNA was extracted from the leaves and roots of 17-day-old seedlings. To investigate the expression pattern of non-ε OsGF14, total RNA was extracted from the roots and leaves of plants during the seedling stage (10 days post-germination) and tillering stage (~ 40 days). During the flowering stage, total RNA was also extracted from leaves, roots, nodes, Basal stems, rachises and spikelets of plants. Reverse transcription was performed using a Hifair III 1 st Strand cDNA Synthesis SuperMix for qPCR (Yeasen, cat. no. 11141ES10). PCR was performed using Hieff qPCR SYBR Green Master Mix (No Rox) (Yeasen, cat. no. 11201ES08). The relative expression was calculated using the 2−ΔΔCt method as previously described with three replicates (Yan et al. 2024). The primers used for qRT-PCR were listed in Table S1.

Phylogenetic Analysis

Amino acid sequences of OsGF14a-f were obtained from NCBI under the following accession numbers: OSGF14a (Os08g0480800), OsGF14b (Os04g0462500), OsGF14c (Os08g0430500), OsGF14d (Os11g0546900), OsGF14e (Os02g0580300) OsGF14f (Os03g0710800). We employed the BLAST tool available on the Phytozome website (https://phytozome-next.jgi.doe.gov/blast-search) and chose the corresponding species database for comparison with target putative proteins (OsGF14a-f), then we selected the sequences in the BLAST results with an identity exceeding 80%. A multiple sequence alignment of the full-length protein sequences was conducted using Clustal W, and the results were imported into MEGA 11.0.13 to construct an unrooted phylogenetic tree using the maximum likelihood method, with the bootstrap replication number set to 1000. Finally, we utilized the iTOL tool (https://itol.embl.de/) to enhance the visualization of the phylogenetic tree (Yan et al. 2024).

Subcellular Localization Analysis

To analyze subcellular localization, the full-length coding sequences (CDSs) of OsGF14a, OsGF14b, OsGF14c, OsGF14d, OsGF14e, and OsGF14f were amplified using PCR and subsequently fused into the 35 S-1300-eGFP vector. The primers utilized for these constructs are detailed in Table S1. For subcellular localization analysis in tobacco leaf epidermal cells, Agrobacterium tumefaciens-mediated transient transformation of N. benthamiana was performed as described (Ni et al. 2016). A. tumefaciens strain GV3101(pSoup) (WEIDI, cat. no, AC1002S) with binary constructs was grown in LB medium at 28 °C until the OD600 reached 0.8-1. The cells were centrifuged of 5000 rpm and resuspended in a solution of 10 mM MgCl2, 10 mM MES (pH 5.7), and 0.2 mM acnullnulletosyringone (Yang et al.2018).The leaves of 4–5-week-old tobacco were infiltrated with the bacterial culture. Subsequently, fluorescence images were captured with a Leica TCS SP5 II confocal microscope. Excitation/emission wavelength was 488 nm/495 to 540 nm for GFP.

Detection of Rhizosphere Acidification

Four-day-old wild type and mutants were washed with ddH2O to ensure that their root systems were clean. The roots were then spread on agar plates with a pH of 5.6, which contained 0.7% agar, 0.02% bromocresol purple, 2 mM NH4Cl, and 1 mM CaSO4. These plates were incubated in darkness for 12 h to monitor rhizosphere acidification. After incubation, phenotypic images were captured, and the acidification zones around the roots were visualized and quantified using ImageJ. The ratio of the rhizosphere acidification area was calculated by comparing the acidified region of each sample to that of the wild type.

Measurement of Pi Content

The concentration of phosphorus in the shoots and roots of rice was measured as previously described (Yan et al. 2024).
