The association between foot morphological development and bone maturation in children: a cross-sectional study
Authors: Si-Yuan Xie, Xiao Li, Zi-Yu Feng, Jing-Yi She, Yin Xie, Xin-Yao Hu, Jian-You Li, Kai-Rui Zhang, Hua-Jun Wang, Qi-Wei Pan, Jun-Jie Cao, Tian-Yi Li, Hao-Yong Hong, Yi-Ling He, Chen-Zhe Gao, Fu-Hui Lin, Yi-Kai Li, Chao Chen
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractPurposeAccurate assessment of pediatric foot development is essential for guiding clinical interventions. While chronological age (CA) is commonly used, it may not account for individual variations influenced by skeletal maturity. This study hypothesizes that bone age (BA) correlates more strongly with foot morphology than CA, particularly in predicting pediatric flatfoot progression.MethodsA retrospective cross-sectional study was conducted with 141 healthy children aged 9–13 years. BA was assessed via hand-wrist X-rays (China 05 RUS-CHN method), and 3D foot parameters were measured using laser scanning. Participants were categorized into delayed, normal, and advanced maturity groups based on BA-CA differences. Correlation analyses and principal component analysis (PCA) were performed to evaluate associations between BA, CA, and foot morphology.ResultsBA exhibited stronger correlations with foot length (FL), outer ankle height (OAH), ball width (BW), and ball girth (BG) compared to CA. Stratified analyses revealed sex-specific differences, with BA-FL correlations in males (r = 0.722) surpassing females (r = 0.572). However, BA showed limited associations with arch morphology parameters (KY index [KYI] and arch height [AH]; |r| < 0.3). PCA further demonstrated that general foot size (PC1) correlated robustly with BA (r = 0.671), while arch morphology (PC2) remained independent of both BA and CA (r = -0.113).ConclusionBA is a superior predictor of foot size growth (e.g., FL, BW) compared to CA, particularly in males, but offers limited utility in assessing arch maturation. These findings suggest BA may enhance clinical foot size predictions, yet arch development likely depends on other factors beyond skeletal maturity. Integrating BA into pediatric flatfoot management requires careful consideration of its context-specific benefits and limitations.Keywords: Bone age, Foot morphology, Pediatric, Flatfoot

Introduction

Accurate and prompt assessment of abnormalities in pediatric foot development is crucial for implementing preventive measures and guiding pediatric orthopedic interventions. Previous studies have indicated that all normally developing children are born with flexible flatfoot, and the arches gradually form as they age, leading to a decrease in the incidence of flatfoot [1, 2]. As a result, many researchers now view asymptomatic flatfoot as a physiological variation rather than a pathological condition [3–6]. However, in a small subset of children, flatfoot persists and progresses into pathological flatfoot, resulting in foot pain, impaired mobility, and reduced quality of life [1]. Numerous studies have investigated how foot morphology evolves with chronological age (CA) and the normal trajectory of foot arch formation [7–9]. However, there remains no consensus on the specific age at which flatfoot is considered a physiological phenomenon rather than a pathological condition [10]. Additionally, no reliable methods currently exist to predict the risk of persistent flatfoot in children [11]. This uncertainty presents challenges for both parents and clinicians in making informed decisions. For instance, the timing of medial longitudinal arch (MLA) development in children has yet to be universally agreed upon. This ambiguity complicates the decision-making process regarding whether, and when, to intervene in cases of pediatric flatfoot. As a result, clinical interventions are often guided by the clinician’s personal experience and judgment rather than evidence-based protocols [10].

Furthermore, the maturation of foot structure is closely linked to the development of bones, muscles, and tendons, a process influenced by factors such as genetics, nutrition, hormone levels, and physical activity. Therefore, the development of foot morphology can vary significantly between individuals when chronological age is used as the sole variable [11]. Misinterpreting these individual differences could lead to unnecessary treatments or missed opportunities for early intervention [5, 12]. Thus, it is critical to explore a more accurate and personalized assessment system that accounts for these variations in foot development.

Assessing bone age (BA) through radiographic evaluation of skeletal maturity offers a potential solution to this challenge. BA is widely used to predict height, diagnose growth abnormalities (such as precocious puberty, dwarfism, etc.), and provide reference points for formulating treatment plans (such as for scoliosis) [13, 14]. Given its established role in evaluating skeletal development, we hypothesize that BA may have a stronger correlation with foot morphology development than CA. To explore the potential of BA as a predictor of foot morphology, we conducted a retrospective study analyzing the relationship between BA and three-dimensional foot morphology in children.

Methods

Study design and participants

This is a retrospective cross-sectional study analyzing data from health examinations conducted at Dongfeng East Road Primary School in Guangzhou, China, between May 2024 and June 2024. This study was approved by the Medical Ethics Committee of Nanfang Hospital of Southern Medical University (NFEC-2024-278). Clinical trial number: not applicable. Informed consent was obtained from the parents of all the children. The study was conducted in accordance with all relevant national regulations and institutional policies and the tenets of the Declaration of Helsinki. This study collected information on children’s gender, date of birth, height, weight, BA, foot three-dimensional scan measurements, ultrasonic bone density, and parental height. The inclusion criteria were as follows: (1) aged between 9 and 13 years; (2) apparently healthy. The exclusion criteria were as follows: (1) presence of foot or ankle deformities or fractures; (2) a history of foot or ankle surgery; (3) recent growth hormone treatment; (4) neurological disorders that could affect the results. A total of 141 children were ultimately included in this study.

Measurement

The children’s gender, date of birth, and parental height were reported by the children’s parents. The children’s height and weight were measured by the same physician using a scale and a stadiometer. Body mass index (BMI) is calculated by using a formula weight/height2. Foot three-dimensional measurements were obtained from both feet using a three-dimensional laser scanner (eFoot-350Pros, 3DOE Ltd., Shenzhen, China) while the participants were standing and barefoot. Each child underwent two scans, with the average of the two measurements used for analysis. A total of eight morphological parameters were selected for subsequent analysis, with their definitions provided in Fig. 1A. Flatfoot assessments were performed using the Sztriter-Godunov index (KY index, KYI), which has been proven to be strongly correlated with the Staheli index and Chippaux-Smirak index [15–18]. The KYI calculation method is illustrated in Fig. 1B. A value of ≥ 0.76 is indicative of flatfoot.

Hand and wrist X-rays were obtained using a portable bone age measurement machine (IDR-301 h, AZK BIOTECHNOLOGY Co., Ltd., Hangzhou, China). To ensure blinding in the assessment, all DICOM format files were exported from the machine and reviewed by the same experienced pediatrician, who evaluated BA using the China 05 RUS-CHN method [19], without knowledge of the participant’s CA. The consistency and reliability of the BA assessment results were assessed using the Intraclass Correlation Coefficient (ICC). Children were classified into three groups based on the difference between BA and CA (DBA−CA): advanced group (DBA−CA>1), normal group (−1 ≤ DBA−CA≤1), and delayed group (DBA−CA<−1). Calcaneal bone mineral density was measured using an ultrasonic bone densitometer (OSTEOKJ3000M+, China). A higher speed of sound (SOS) value typically indicates lower bone density.

Data analysis

Data were processed using the SPSS 25.0 software (SPSS Inc, Chicago, IL). Age categories were defined based on the participants’ birth dates (5 age groups, 9–13 years). The normality of all variables was assessed using the Kolmogorov-Smirnov test. Continuous variables that followed a normal distribution are presented as mean ± standard deviation (SD), while those that did not follow a normal distribution are expressed as median with interquartile range (IQR). Categorical variables are reported as number and percentage [N (%)]. Differences between groups were analyzed using one-way ANOVA or the Kruskal–Wallis test. Correlation analyses were conducted using Pearson’s correlation for normally distributed data and Spearman correlation for non-normally distributed data. The significance threshold was established at α = 0.05. The correlation coefficient r is interpreted as follows: |r| ≥ 0.8 signifies a strong correlation; 0.5 ≤ |r| < 0.8 indicates a moderate correlation; 0.3 ≤ |r| < 0.5 reflects a low correlation; and |r| < 0.3 suggests a weak correlation. Two-sided p-values < 0.05 were considered statistically significant.

Principal component analysis (PCA) was performed to further analyze the relationship between foot morphology and BA and CA. Bilateral foot measurements (left and right) were averaged to generate composite variables. Each variable was standardized to Z-scores to eliminate scale differences: , where µ is the mean and σ is the standard deviation of the variable. PCA was conducted on the standardized data, and principal components (PCs) with eigenvalues greater than 1 were retained, as per the Kaiser criterion. The loadings of the components, which represent the correlation between the original variables and the principal components, were analyzed. A component with absolute loadings ≥ 0.5 was considered to make a significant contribution to that principal component. Individual scores for each principal component (PC) were computed as linear combinations of standardized variables weighted by their respective loadings. The Foot Morphological Index (FMI) was derived as a weighted sum of the scores of the retained principal components, where the weights correspond to the proportion of variance explained by each component. Sample size calculation

The required sample size was calculated to detect a significant correlation between BA and foot morphological parameters in children, with an anticipated moderate correlation coefficient of r = 0.30. Using a two-tailed significance level of α = 0.05 and a desired statistical power of 80% (β = 0.20), the sample size was determined based on the formula for correlation studies using Fisher’s z-transformation. To account for potential dropouts and ensure adequate power for subgroup analyses (e.g., by sex and maturity groups), the sample size was increased by 20%, resulting in a target sample size of at least 102 participants.

Results

A total of 141 children aged 9–13 years (54 girls, 87 boys) participated in this study. Based on the difference between bone age and chronological age (DBA−CA), the children were divided into three groups: delayed maturity (Del., N = 19), advanced maturity (Adv., N = 35), and normal maturity (Nor., N = 87). As shown in Table 1, no significant differences were observed among the three groups in terms of CA and ultrasound bone density. However, significant differences were found in bone age, sex distribution, height, weight, and BMI. Consistent with expectations, the delayed maturity group had a significantly lower bone age (9.4 ± 1.2 years) compared to the other two groups, with the advanced maturity group showing the highest bone age (13.0 ± 0.9 years) and the normal maturity group falling in between (11.6 ± 1.2 years). Sex distribution differed significantly, with the advanced maturity group showing a significantly different male-to-female ratio compared to the other two groups, while no significant difference was observed between the delayed and normal maturity groups. Furthermore, the advanced maturity group had significantly greater height (154.9 ± 9.1 cm), weight (47.5 ± 10.5 kg), and BMI (18.1 ± 6.2 kg/m²) compared to the delayed and normal maturity groups.

Table 3 presents the analysis of the correlation between BA and CA with various foot morphological parameters. Overall, BA demonstrated stronger correlations with multiple foot morphological parameters compared to CA. Specifically, BA showed significant positive correlations with FL, OAH, BW, and BG (p < 0.001 for all), as well as a significant negative correlation with the KYI (left foot r = −0.216, right foot r = −0.210, p < 0.05). In contrast, while CA also exhibited positive correlations with most foot parameters, the correlation coefficients were generally lower, and in some cases, such as with the KYI, the correlations were not statistically significant. Further stratified analysis by sex revealed distinct differences in the correlation patterns between BA and foot anthropometrics. In the male group, BA was strongly correlated with FL (r = 0.680), OAH (r = 0.472), BW (r = 0.631), and BG (r = 0.698) (p < 0.001 for all), with a notable negative correlation with the KYI (left foot r = −0.239, right foot r = −0.268, p < 0.05). Compared to BA, CA in males showed similar but slightly weaker correlations with these parameters. In the female group, the correlations between BA and foot parameters, while significant, were generally weaker than in males. The correlations between the KYI and BA were not significant in females (left foot r = −0.123, p = 0.376; right foot r = −0.023, p = 0.871). Similarly, CA in females exhibited a similar pattern of correlations as BA, but with overall lower strengths. The complete data analysis results are shown in Table 4.

PCA was performed to further explore the relationship between foot morphology and BA and CA. After excluding the HVA variable due to its weak correlation with age-related variables (BA and CA), the remaining variables were subjected to PCA. Two principal components with eigenvalues > 1 were retained, collectively explaining 69.64% of the total variance (Table 5). The first component (PC1), accounting for 39.40% of the variance, exhibited strong positive loadings on FL (0.773), BW (0.748), BG (0.780), OAH (0.680), and IAH (0.574), representing a composite measure of general foot size. The second component (PC2), explaining 30.24% of the variance, was characterized by high positive loadings on AH (0.676) and negative loadings on the KYI (−0.752), reflecting arch morphology (Fig. 2).

Discussion

One of the key findings of our study is that BA is more strongly correlated with most foot morphological parameters than CA in children aged 9–13 years. Further PCA results found that while general foot size parameters (PC1) exhibit strong correlations with BA, the arch morphology component (PC2) remains largely independent of both CA and BA.

The distinction between BA and CA is particularly relevant in the context of growth-related disorders. For example, BA plays a critical role in assessing a child’s growth potential and predicting adult height. Unlike chronological age, which simply measures time, bone age reflects the biological and skeletal maturity of a child. This is crucial because children of the same chronological age can vary significantly in their growth and development. As shown in Fig. 2, children with advanced BA but normal CA exhibit foot morphology more characteristic of older children, while those with delayed BA display features typical of younger children. The underlying biological mechanisms driving these differences are rooted in skeletal development, which is influenced by genetics, hormones, and nutrition.

The issue of pediatric flatfoot remains a significant gap in the current literature [10]. While many researchers assert that asymptomatic flatfoot, often described as a “physiological deviation,” typically resolves by the age of 10, there is considerable controversy surrounding the nuances of this developmental process [4]. Some studies suggest that foot development occurs rapidly before the age of 6, with minimal changes thereafter [20, 21]. In contrast, a cross-sectional study conducted by Wang, which included 1,744 children, indicates differing patterns of foot development [12]. The age range of 7 to 9 years is particularly contentious, especially regarding the MLA. While some studies argue that the MLA remains stable during this period [20, 22, 23], others present opposing views. Onodera’s research indicates that the MLA is gradually developing during this timeframe [24]. Additionally, two cross-sectional studies conducted in Taiwan reveal that the arches of 8-year-old children are flatter than those of 7-year-olds, only to show a resurgence in arch height by age 9 [25, 26]. Xu et al. further highlight that the peak of foot development velocity in girls occurs between ages 7 and 9 [27]. The existence of these discrepancies may be attributed to several factors, including a lack of consensus on the definition of flatfoot, variations in measurement techniques, differences in ethnic groups studied, and the diverse natural and social environments in which these populations reside. Such variability complicates our understanding of when the transition from flatfoot to a normative foot structure should occur, as well as the expected degree of arch development at specific ages [10]. This uncertainty poses significant challenges for the management of pediatric flatfoot. It not only complicates clinical decision-making but also leads to anxiety among parents. In several countries, concerns about foot health are a primary reason parents seek consultations in pediatric orthopedic clinics [28].

Previous research on pediatric flatfoot has frequently neglected the influence of skeletal age in both intervention strategies and clinical practice. Our study reveals that skeletal age correlates more strongly with various parameters of foot morphology than chronological age, suggesting that skeletal age may provide a better predictive framework for foot developmental outcomes. This insight is crucial for developing more targeted clinical intervention strategies. Foot orthoses is a common treatment modality for pediatric flatfoot, yet the decision regarding whether and when to utilize this intervention primarily hinges on clinical symptoms and physical signs, with insufficient consideration of skeletal maturity [5]. Orthoses work by providing external support to guide the arch’s development toward a normative shape, improving gait, alleviating clinical symptoms, and preventing the further progression of flatfoot. For patients with younger skeletal ages, their arches are likely still within a plastic stage, making early orthotic intervention beneficial by leveraging the potential for skeletal growth. In contrast, for those whose skeletal age is approaching maturity—regardless of their chronological age—the efficacy of orthotic treatment may be limited, necessitating a shift towards surgical intervention. Arthroereisis, which involves the insertion of metallic, silicone, or biodegradable implants into the subtalar joint, serves to restrict excessive joint mobility. This stabilization can facilitate remodeling of the joint surfaces and tightening of the joint capsule and associated tendons. However, these beneficial changes can only occur in children who possess sufficient developmental potential [23]. A critical challenge arises when evaluating a patient with a lower chronological age but a skeletal age that indicates near maturity. In these instances, the effectiveness of arthroereisis may be compromised. Careful consideration must be given to the selection between arthroereisis and other surgical options, such as osteotomy or arthrodesis, to ensure optimal outcomes.

However, the univariate results of this study indicate that the difference between BA and CA is not reflected as a significant variation across all foot morphological parameters, especially in terms of KYI and AH, which show poor correlations (absolute r values < 0.3). Furthermore, the results of the principal component analysis (PCA) reveal that although (general foot size) showed robust correlations with BA (r = 0.671), whereas PC2 (arch morphology) exhibited negligible associations with both BA and CA (r < 0.2). This divergence suggests that skeletal maturation primarily governs dimensional growth, while arch development may depend on localized factors such as ligament laxity, muscle strength, or fat pad distribution, which are less influenced by skeletal maturity. For instance, the medial longitudinal arch relies on dynamic interactions between the plantar fascia and intrinsic foot muscles, factors not captured by BA assessment. Clinically, this implies that BA assessment—while informative for predicting foot size—offers limited utility for evaluating arch maturation. Given the weak correlations and the non-negligible radiation exposure associated with hand/wrist X-rays, clinicians should carefully consider whether BA assessment justifies its risks in flatfoot management, particularly when arch parameters are the primary concern.

Moreover, the increasing incidence of precocious puberty in recent years raises important questions regarding its impact on pediatric foot development. One critical area of exploration is whether early maturation leads to the earlier formation of a mature arch or, conversely, whether premature closure of the growth plates prior to proper arch development contributes to the incidence of flatfoot. Despite studies indicating that children aged 6.7 to 8.2 years with non-flatfoot conditions exhibit superior lower limb motor skills compared to their flatfoot counterparts [9], one must question whether this is inherently beneficial. As previously mentioned, a consensus exists that children’s arches gradually increase in height throughout development, reaching near-adult levels by approximately age ten. However, if a particular child demonstrates a more developed arch compared to peers, does this potentially indicate a greater BA? This question warrants further investigation, as an accelerated arch development could suggest underlying differences in skeletal maturity.

The study also revealed gender-specific differences in the correlation patterns between BA and foot morphology. In males, the correlations between BA and foot parameters were generally stronger than in females. This difference may be attributed to variations in growth patterns between boys and girls during the pubertal years. Males typically experience a later but more intense growth spurt, driven largely by testosterone, which may lead to more pronounced changes in foot morphology that are closely aligned with BA. In contrast, females tend to enter puberty earlier and have a more gradual growth trajectory, which may explain the weaker correlations between BA and foot morphology. This finding suggests that sex-specific approaches may be necessary when assessing foot development and planning interventions.

To our knowledge, this represents the first quantitative mapping of the correlation between bone age and foot development in children, providing valuable insights into how skeletal maturity influences foot morphology. This study has several limitations. First, the participants’ age range does not fully cover the critical period between 7 and 10 years, when the MLA matures, and the lack of longitudinal developmental data may result in missing key developmental changes. Second, as a retrospective study, we lacked data on clinical symptoms and motor abilities, and due to ethical concerns, we did not collect foot X-rays, which limits our ability to comprehensively assess foot health. Additionally, the sample size for females was smaller, which may reduce the reliability of sex-specific correlation analyses. Future research will address these limitations by conducting a prospective trial with a broader age range, incorporating ultrasound for skeletal age assessment [29], and examining the relationship between foot morphology, symptoms, and functionality.

Conclusion

In conclusion, this study finds that BA is more strongly correlated with most foot morphological parameters than CA in children aged 9–13 years, particularly for general foot size. However, BA shows limited correlation with arch morphology, suggesting that skeletal maturation influences foot dimensions, while arch development may depend on other factors. Gender differences in these correlations were also observed, with stronger associations in males. These findings suggest that BA could be a useful tool for predicting foot development, but its role in assessing arch maturation is limited. Further research is needed to refine management strategies for pediatric flatfoot.
