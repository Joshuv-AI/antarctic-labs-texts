RIPK1 ubiquitination regulates its kinase-independent function in development and inflammation
Authors: Ming Li, Jianling Liu, Mingyan Xing, Han Liu, Lingxia Wang, Xiaoxia Wu, Yangjing Ou, Xiaoming Zhao, YangYang Wang, YangYang Xie, Hanwen Zhang, Zhuyang Wu, Jincheng Hao, Hong Li, Yu Li, Haibing Zhang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
SignificanceReceptor-interacting protein kinase 1 (RIPK1) is a central regulator of apoptosis, necroptosis, and inflammation, with distinct functions determined by its scaffold role and kinase activity. Posttranslational modifications of RIPK1-particularly ubiquitination-play critical roles in controlling its activation. Here, we show that loss of ubiquitination at lysine 376 not only causes embryonic lethality through RIPK1 kinase-dependent cell death but also drives systemic inflammation in adult mice through a kinase-independent, scaffold-dependent mechanism. This inflammatory response is mediated by RIPK3-dependent inflammasome activation and subsequent IL-1β secretion, with additional contributions from TNFR1-induced cell death. These findings reveal previously unrecognized dual roles of RIPK1 in development and inflammatory regulation and provide a conceptual framework for understanding the pathogenesis of RIPK1-associated inflammatory diseases.

Receptor-interacting protein kinase 1 (RIPK1) is a serine/threonine kinase implicated in the regulation of cell death, inflammation, and immunity (1). In humans, biallelic loss-of-function mutations in RIPK1 result in profound immunodeficiency and early-onset inflammatory disorders such as inflammatory bowel disease and arthritis (2, 3). In mice, Ripk1 deletion causes perinatal lethality, primarily due to uncontrolled apoptosis mediated by FADD/Caspase8 (abbreviated as Casp8) and necroptosis driven by the RIPK3/MLKL axis (4–8).

While the kinase activity of RIPK1 is indispensable for both RIPK1-dependent apoptosis (RDA) (9–12) and necroptosis (1, 13), genetic inactivation via kinase-dead mutations (D138N, K45A) in mice show normal survival (14, 15). In fact, these kinase mutant mice were observed to be resistant to TNFα-induced systemic inflammatory response syndrome (16, 17), and multiple neurodegenerative diseases (18, 19), suggesting that RIPK1’s kinase activity is not only required for cell death but also contributes to pathological inflammation. Concurrently, cleavage of RIPK1 by Casp8 is a critical checkpoint for preventing its aberrant activation; failure of this cleavage leads to spontaneous inflammation and lethality, which can be partially rescued by abrogating RIPK1 kinase activity (20–26). These observations raise the possibility that activated RIPK1 may promote inflammation through mechanisms beyond its kinase function.

Genetic studies demonstrated that introduction of kinase-inactivating mutations in RIPK1 completely rescued the embryonic lethality caused by D324A mutations (Casp8 cleaved sites), but the double-mutant mice ultimately succumbed, and did not survive past weaning. This demonstrates that activated RIPK1 mediates inflammatory functions though its kinase-independent signaling pathways (22, 24). Mice that are catalytically inactive of Casp8 promote pyroptosis through its scaffold function, particularly when apoptosis and necroptosis are inhibited (23, 24, 27). However, whether the RIPK1 contributes to inflammasome activation or inflammatory reprogramming through its scaffold function remains poorly defined.

The ubiquitination status of RIPK1 has been demonstrated to be critical for multiple functional transitions. Previous studies have reported that RIPK1 undergoes ubiquitination at various sites (K376, K115, K627, etc.) (28–34), with the ubiquitination of K376 (human at K377) being particularly crucial for regulating RIPK1 activation. In vitro studies revealed that the K377R mutation inhibited TNFα-mediated NF-κB activation and promoted TNFα-induced apoptosis. Additionally, we and others have previously shown that mice expressing the RIPK1 K376R mutant experienced embryonic lethality at embryonic day 12.5 (E12.5) with excessive cell death in embryonic tissues and the yolk sac (29–31). However, employing a genetic crossing strategy, embryonic lethality in Ripk1K376R/K376R mice could be fully rescued by ablating both apoptosis and necroptosis genes (Fadd−/−Ripk3−/−, Fadd−/−Mlkl−/−, Casp8−/−Ripk3−/−), indicating that RIPK1 K376R promotes both apoptosis and necroptosis during embryonic development. Although in vitro experiments revealed that RIPK1 K376R-induced cell death relies on its kinase activity, the administration of Nec-1 only delayed lethality to E13.5 (30, 31). Therefore, whether the embryonic lethality caused by the RIPK1 K376R mutation is kinase-dependent remains unknown.

In this study, we generated a double-mutant mouse model (Ripk1K376R,D138N/K376R,D138N) by introducing a kinase-dead D138N mutation into the K376R background. Strikingly, these mice rescued the embryonic lethality seen in Ripk1K376R/K376R mice but developed spontaneous systemic inflammation postnatally. Genetic dissection revealed that this inflammation was rescued by deletion of Casp1/11-but not TRIF-implicating intrinsic inflammasome activation. Mechanistically, we found that the K376R mutation of RIPK1 drives both kinase activity-dependent cell death and RIPK1 kinase-independent inflammatory responses. Inflammatory responses independent of RIPK1 kinase activity are triggered when activated RIPK1 engages RIPK3 to reprogram arachidonic acid metabolism, leading to prostaglandin overproduction, resulting in intrinsic NLRP3 inflammasome activation and downstream IL-1β secretion. Collectively, our results reveal that the RIPK1 K376R mutant not only promotes kinase activity-dependent cell death during embryogenesis but also contributes to kinase-independent inflammation postnatally.

Results

Ripk1K376R,D138N/K376R,D138N Mice Rescue Embryonic Lethality of Ripk1K376R/K376R Mice, But Develop Systemic Inflammation.

We and others have demonstrated that embryonic lethality in Ripk1K376R/K376R mice can be fully rescued by ablating both apoptosis and necroptosis genes and that RIPK1 K376R-induced cell death relies on its kinase activity in MEFs, the administration of Nec-1 only delayed lethality from E12.5 to E13.5 (30, 31). To further dissect the role of RIPK1 kinase activity in RIPK1K376R-induced embryonic lethality, we generated knock-in mice harboring both K376R and D138N mutations (Ripk1K376R,D138N/K376R,D138N, hereafter Ripk1K,D/K,D), in which the critical kinase residue D138 is substituted with asparagine (N138) on the RIPK1K376R background (Fig. 1A). In contrast to Ripk1K376R/K376R mice, which died at embryonic day 12.5 (E12.5), Ripk1K,D/K,D mice survived into adulthood (Fig. 1 B and C), indicating that lethality is driven by RIPK1 kinase hyperactivation.

To investigate the impact of RIPK1 kinase inactivation on cell death induced by K376R mutants, we treated immortalized mouse embryonic fibroblast (MEF) cells from Ripk1+/+, Ripk1K376R/K376R, and Ripk1K,D/K,D mice with various cell death stimulus. In contrast to Ripk1K376R/K376R MEFs, which exhibited excessive apoptosis (TNFα+BV6; TB) and necroptosis (TNFα+BV6+zVAD; TBZ), Ripk1K,D/K,D MEFs were protected from both cell death models (SI Appendix, Fig. S1A). Furthermore, due to previous studies showing a significant decrease in the ubiquitination of RIPK1 and elevated levels of p-RIPK1(S166) in TNFα-stimulated Ripk1K376R/K376R MEFs, we further investigated the impact of RIPK1 D138N on this. We stimulated immortalized MEFs from Ripk1+/+, Ripk1K376R/K376R, and Ripk1K,D/K,D with flag-TNFα to immunoprecipitated TNF-RSC (TNF-R1 Signaling Complex I). We found that, compared to Ripk1K376R/K376R MEFs, the increased p-RIPK1(S166) was inhibited, but the decreased ubiquitination of RIPK1 was not reversed in Ripk1K,D/K,D immortalized MEFs upon TNFα stimulation (SI Appendix, Fig. S1B). Consequently, downstream executioner markers including cleaved Caspase-3, p-MLKL, and p-RIPK3 were all diminished in Ripk1K,D/K,D MEFs (SI Appendix, Fig. S1 C and D). Moreover, since RIPK1 regulates NF-κB signaling pathways based on its ubiquitination function rather than the kinase activity, we observed that the NF-kB activation in Ripk1K,D/K,D MEFs still exhibited defects similar to Ripk1K376R/K376R MEFs upon TNFα stimulation (SI Appendix, Fig. S1E). Therefore, these results demonstrate that kinase-inactive RIPK1 D138N inhibits RIPK1 activation and protects against cell death but does not restore the reduced RIPK1 ubiquitination in TNFα-treated Ripk1K376R/K376R cells.

Although Ripk1K,D/K,D mice were viable, they displayed progressive growth retardation and low body weight (Fig. 1C and SI Appendix, Fig. S2D). Histopathological analyses revealed spontaneous inflammation in the skin and liver but not in lung or gut tissues (Fig. 1D and SI Appendix, Fig. S2 A–C). The spleens of Ripk1K,D/K,D mice appeared normal at 2 wk but became enlarged by 8 wk of age (Fig. 1E). Immune profiling revealed reduced CD3+ T cells and increased B220+ B cells and Gr-1+CD11b+ neutrophils in the spleen (Fig. 1E and SI Appendix, Fig. S2E), indicative of systemic inflammatory response.

RNA-seq of Ripk1K,D/K,D skin tissue showed marked upregulation of genes involved in inflammation (Il1b, Il6, Cxcl2, Acod1) and cell death (Casp4, Nlrp3, Ripk3, Mlkl) (Fig. 1F) (35). Differential gene cluster analysis showed that the subcluster_1 gene set was significantly upregulated in Ripk1K,D/K,D skin tissues (SI Appendix, Fig. S3A), and functional enrichment analysis of subcluster_1 gene set revealed associations with immune response and immune cell recruitment pathways (SI Appendix, Fig. S3 B and C). RT-PCR validation confirmed increased expression of representative cytokines, while the expression of Tnfa remained unchanged. (Fig. 1G). These results suggest that the systemic inflammation in Ripk1K,D/K,D mice results from kinase-independent functions.

To test whether RIPK1 scaffold dosage influences inflammation, we examined Ripk1K,D/− mice, lacking one RIPK1 allele. These mice were phenotypically normal and lacked the inflammatory pathology observed in Ripk1K,D/K,D mice (SI Appendix, Fig. S3 D and E). Gene expression signatures of inflammation and cell death were also normalized (SI Appendix, Fig. S3F), and the subcluster_1 gene set was significantly restored (Fig. 1H). These findings indicate that two copies of the RIPK1 scaffold are required for its kinase-independent functions to drive systemic inflammation.

Caspase-1/11 Signaling Exacerbates RIPK1 Scaffold-Driven Systemic Inflammation.

Given that RIPK1 kinase-inactivating mutations suppress apoptosis and necroptosis (SI Appendix, Fig. S1 A–D) and that IL-1β expression is elevated in skin and serum of Ripk1K,D/K,D mice (Fig. 1 F and G and SI Appendix, Fig. S4A), we next investigated whether pyroptosis-related signaling pathways were activated. Western blotting revealed elevated levels of Caspase-11 (abbreviated as Casp11), cleaved GSDMD, and cleaved Caspase-1 in the skin of Ripk1K,D/K,D mice (Fig. 2A), but not in other tissues (SI Appendix, Fig. S4 B and C).

Due to inflammasome activation in the skin of Ripk1K,D/K,D mice, we crossed these mice with Trif−/− and Casp1/11−/− mice to further determine the roles of pyroptosis in promoting skin inflammation in Ripk1K,D/K,D mice. Histology revealed that Casp1/11 deletion, but not Trif deletion, markedly reduced epidermal thickening and immune cell infiltration in both skin and liver (Fig. 2 B–D). At 8 wk, Casp1/11-deficient Ripk1K,D/K,D mice also displayed reduced splenomegaly and neutrophil accumulation (Fig. 2 E–H). Given the critical role of pyroptosis in LPS-induced endotoxic shock, we next injected Ripk1K,D/K,D mice with a lethal dose of LPS (50 mg/kg) to assess the activated RIPK1 scaffolding functions in this model. Results showed that Ripk1K,D/K,D exhibited significantly higher mortality rates than WT controls in LPS-induced endotoxic shock models (Fig. 2I). Previous studies have reported that knockout of either Trif or Casp1/11 confers resistance to LPS-induced endotoxic shock model (36, 37). However, only Casp1/11 knockout, but not Trif knockout, reduces the hypersensitivity phenotype of Ripk1K,D/K,D mice in the LPS-induced endotoxic shock model (Fig. 2I). Together, these data indicate that Casp1/11-mediated inflammasome activation, but not TRIF signaling, contributes to RIPK1 scaffold-dependent inflammation in LPS-induced endotoxic shock.

RIPK1K376R Mutant Promotes Intrinsic NLRP3 Inflammasome Activation.

Based on the observation that Casp1/11 knockout significantly rescues systemic inflammation in Ripk1K,D/K,D mice, we hypothesized that RIPK1’s scaffolding function promotes Casp1/11-mediated inflammasome activation. To test this, we first examined cytokine production in LPS-stimulated Bone Marrow–Derived Macrophages (BMDMs) from Ripk1K,D/K,D and Ripk1+/+ mice. Ripk1K,D/K,D BMDMs secreted markedly higher levels of IL-1β, but not IL-6 or TNFα, compared to controls (Fig. 3A). The increased IL-1β was significantly abrogated by YVAD, a Caspase-1 inhibitor, confirming its dependence on Caspase-1 activation (Fig. 3B). Given the capacity of the NLRP3-dependent inflammasome to activate Casp1, we utilized the specific NLRP3 inhibitor MCC950 to validate that both Casp1 activation and IL-1β secretion are mediated by the NLRP3 inflammasome in LPS-stimulated Ripk1K,D/K,D BMDMs (Fig. 3 C and D). However, classical NLRP3 inflammasome activation induced by LPS plus Nigericin stimulation remained impaired in Ripk1K,D/K,D BMDMs relative to Ripk1+/+ controls (SI Appendix, Fig. S5 A and B), likely due to defective NLRP3 expression regulated by NF-κB during priming (SI Appendix, Fig. S5 C and D). This finding correlates with the inability of Trif knockout to rescue inflammation in Ripk1K,D/K,D mice (Fig. 2 B–H). Collectively, these results indicate that RIPK1K376R mutation promotes scaffolding-dependent intrinsic NLRP3 inflammasome activation and IL-1β secretion.

To explore how RIPK1 mutation drives intrinsic NLRP3 inflammasome activation, we performed enrichment analysis of the differentially expressed gene set (Subcluster_1), revealing enrichment not only in immune activation pathways but also in Reactive Oxygen Species (ROS) production pathways (Fig. 3 E and F and SI Appendix, Fig. S3 B and C). Indeed, Ripk1K,D/K,D BMDMs exhibited significantly increased basal and LPS-induced ROS production (Fig. 3G), suggesting a potential role of ROS in inflammasome activation. Previous studies have shown ROS to be an activator of intrinsic NLRP3 inflammasome (38–40). Consistently, treatment with ROS scavengers Butylated Hydroxyanisole (BHA) and N-acetylcysteine (NAC) inhibited excessive IL-1β secretion in Ripk1K,D/K,D BMDMs following LPS stimulation (Fig. 3H).

Since mitochondria are a major ROS source, we assessed mitochondrial integrity and function. Morphological analysis revealed increased mitochondrial fragmentation in Ripk1K,D/K,D BMDMs (SI Appendix, Fig. S5E). Mitochondrial membrane potential, measured by TMRE and JC-1 staining, was significantly reduced in Ripk1K,D/K,D cells, indicating its depolarization (SI Appendix, Fig. S5 F and G). Correspondingly, mitochondrial ROS levels were elevated at baseline and after LPS stimulation in Ripk1K,D/K,D BMDMs compared to controls (Fig. 3I). Scavenging mitochondrial ROS with MitoTEMPO partially attenuated IL-1β secretion (Fig. 3J), demonstrating that mitochondrial ROS partially contributes to intrinsic NLRP3 inflammasome activation downstream of RIPK1 scaffolding.

RIPK1-Driven Rewiring of Arachidonic Acid Metabolism Fuels Prostaglandins Overproduction.

Our data demonstrate that BHA robustly suppresses IL-1β secretion, whereas NAC exerts only partial inhibition in Ripk1K,D/K,D BMDMs (Fig. 3H), indicating that ROS is partially responsible for the intrinsic NLRP3 inflammasome activation. Untargeted metabolomics of Ripk1+/+ and Ripk1K,D/K,D BMDMs identified lipid metabolism as the most significantly dysregulated pathway (Fig. 4 A and B and SI Appendix, Fig. S6A), with LipidTOX Red staining confirming excessive lipid accumulation in Ripk1K,D/K,D BMDMs after LPS stimulation (SI Appendix, Fig. S6B). Pharmacological blockade of key lipid metabolic pathways markedly reduced IL-1β secretion (SI Appendix, Fig. S6C). Previous studies reported that the oxidation of fatty acids is involved in the activation of inflammasomes (41), whereas fatty acid uptake and HADHA enzyme activity remained unchanged in Ripk1K,D/K,D BMDMs (SI Appendix, Fig. S6 D–F), suggesting that alternative lipid metabolic pathways predominate in this context.

Pathway enrichment analysis highlighted arachidonic acid (AA) metabolism as significantly upregulated (Fig. 4 B–D), characterized by elevated prostaglandin production through phospholipase A2 (PLA2)-COX1/2 signaling (42). Given BHA’s known inhibition of cytosolic PLA2 (cPLA2) (43), we hypothesized that BHA suppresses IL-1β secretion by targeting cPLA2-mediated AA metabolism. We used inhibitors targeting different components of the PLA2–COX1/2 signaling pathway. Specifically, MAFP inhibits both cPLA2 and iPLA2, BEL targets sPLA2, and CAY10650 is a selective cPLA2 inhibitor. We observed that MAFP exerted a stronger inhibitory effect than CAY10650, whereas BEL showed no effect, suggesting that sPLA2 is not involved in this process (Fig. 4E). These findings indicate that cPLA2, rather than sPLA2, plays an important role in regulating RIPK1 scaffold-mediated IL-1β release. We then treated cells with selective COX inhibitors to validate downstream COX1/2 involvement. These agents potently inhibited IL-1β secretion in Ripk1K,D/K,D BMDMs (Fig. 4F).

We next explored how RIPK1K376R mutation modulates AA metabolism to regulate IL-1β secretion. LPS stimulation triggered a striking increase in AA-derived metabolites, specifically PGE2 and PGD2 in Ripk1K,D/K,D BMDMs (Fig. 4 G and H and SI Appendix, Fig. S6G). While IL-1β can amplify PGE2 production (44, 45), analysis of Ripk1+/+, Ripk1K,D/K,D, and Ripk1K,D/K,DCasp1/11−/− BMDMs revealed that Casp1/11 knockout abolished IL-1β secretion but did not reduce PGE2 levels (Fig. 4I), indicating RIPK1 potentiates AA metabolism and PGE2 production independently of IL-1β feedback. To assess whether the reported RIPK1 activation mutations universally enhance prostaglandin synthesis, we reconstituted RIPK1-knockout immortalized BMDMs with mutants (D324A, K376R, K612R) and found all mutants increased PGE2 production under LPS+Nec-1 conditions relative to wild-type RIPK1 (Fig. 4J), confirming that activating mutations of RIPK1 universally enhance scaffold-dependent PGE2 production.

Previous studies have established that PGE2 activates phospholipase C (PLC) via the EP3 receptor (46–48), leading to increased intracellular calcium (Ca2+) through IP3 receptor activation and downregulation of intracellular cGAMP. Both Ca2+ and cGAMP are essential for inflammasome activation and IL-1β release (49). Inhibiting EP3 with L798106 significantly reduced IL-1β secretion in Ripk1K,D/K,D BMDMs, highlighting EP3’s pivotal role (Fig. 4K). Blocking PLC with 2-APB (which reduces intracellular Ca2+) and elevating cGAMP with Forskolin both markedly suppressed IL-1β release (Fig. 4K). In contrast, PGD2 receptor (DP1i) inhibition had no effect (Fig. 4K). Importantly, we treated Ripk1+,+/+,+ and Ripk1K,D/K,D cells with PGE2 and found that PGE2 enhanced IL-1β secretion in a concentration-dependent manner (SI Appendix, Fig. S6H). Furthermore, increased ROS production was observed in Ripk1K,D/K,D cells following PGE2 treatment (SI Appendix, Fig. S6I). Consistently, pretreatment with the COX1/2 inhibitor indomethacin significantly rescued LPS-induced lethality in Ripk1K,D/K,D mice (Fig. 4L), confirming that AA-COX1/2 signaling is an important mediator of RIPK1-dependent inflammation.

The RIPK1/RIPK3 Signaling Axis Orchestrates COX2 Activation.

Our preceding data established that RIPK1K376R mutation promotes scaffold-dependent IL-1β secretion via the arachidonic acid (AA)–cPLA2–COX1/2–PGE2 axis. To dissect how RIPK1 scaffolding regulates this pathway, we first examined cPLA2 activation by assessing phosphorylated cPLA2 (p-cPLA2) levels. No significant differences were observed under either short- or long-term LPS stimulation between the two genotypes (SI Appendix, Fig. S7 A and B), indicating RIPK1 does not directly modulate cPLA2 activation. Similarly, COX1/2 protein expression was unchanged upon LPS stimulation (SI Appendix, Fig. S7C), yet COX enzymatic activity was markedly increased in Ripk1K,D/K,D BMDMs (Fig. 5A). Given that COX2 inhibitors strongly suppressed IL-1β and PGE2 secretion (Fig. 4 F and H), we hypothesized the direct regulation of COX2 by RIPK1.

Coimmunoprecipitation assays in 293 T cells overexpressing RIPK1 and COX2 confirmed the interaction (Fig. 5 B and C). Truncation mapping revealed that RIPK1’s kinase domain mediates this binding (Fig. 5 D and E). Importantly, the kinase-dead mutant RIPK1D138N retained COX2 binding capacity (Fig. 5F), indicating that the scaffold function, rather than kinase activity, underlies this interaction.

Considering RIPK1’s known scaffold interaction with RIPK3 and their association in Ripk1K,D/K,D skin tissue (Fig. 5G), we investigated whether RIPK3 mediates RIPK1-dependent COX2 regulation. BMDMs from Ripk1+/+, Ripk1K,D/K,D, and Ripk1K,D/K,DRipk3−/− mice revealed that RIPK3 deficiency significantly diminished PGE2 production in Ripk1K,D/K,D cells (Fig. 5H), demonstrating RIPK3’s dependence. Overexpression experiments showed RIPK3, but not its downstream effector MLKL, interacts with COX2 (Fig. 5I and SI Appendix, Fig. S7 E and F). Molecular docking and mutagenesis identified the RIP homotypic interaction motif (RHIM), rather than kinase activity, as essential for RIPK3–COX2 binding (Fig. 5J and SI Appendix, Fig. S7 G and H). Consistently, RIPK3 kinase inhibition did not suppress IL-1β secretion in Ripk1K,D/K,D BMDMs (Fig. 5K). Given that the RIPK3-RHIM domain primarily interacts with the RHIM domain of RIPK1, we hypothesize that the interaction between RIPK3 and COX2 may be mediated through RIPK1. We performed experiments using Ripk1-deficient cells to further examine the RIPK3–COX2 interaction. These analyses demonstrated that RIPK3 fails to interact with COX2 in the absence of RIPK1, indicating that RHIM domain mutations in RIPK3 disrupt the RIPK1–RIPK3 association, which in turn impairs the RIPK3–COX2 interaction (SI Appendix, Fig. S7I).

Previous studies indicated COX2 activation requires dimerization (50–53). We found that LPS stimulation promotes formation of a RIPK1–RIPK3–COX2 complex (Fig. 5L) and induces COX2 dimerization (Fig. 5M). Genetic ablation of Ripk3, but not Mlkl, significantly inhibited IL-1β secretion in Ripk1K,D/K,D BMDMs (Fig. 5N). Moreover, COX2 inhibition with Celecoxib improved survival in LPS-challenged Ripk1K,D/K,D mice (Fig. 5O). However, COX2 inhibitors exhibited a weaker efficacy in improving the survival of LPS-induced Ripk1K,D/K,D mice compared with indomethacin, a non-selective COX1/2 inhibitor. This finding can be attributed to the compensatory role of COX1 in ameliorating the functional deficits induced by COX2 inhibition, as COX1 is constitutively expressed and both genetic ablation and pharmacological inhibition of COX1 confer a protective effect against LPS-induced neurological injury (54, 55). Given the overlapping catalytic activities and structural similarity between COX1 and COX2, a potential role for COX1 in regulating this pathway cannot be excluded, and whether COX1 also participates in this pathway in a RIPK1-dependent manner remains to be determined.

Collectively, these results reveal that RIPK1 and RIPK3 cooperatively regulate COX2 activation through scaffold-dependent interactions, promoting PGE2 and IL-1β production. This mechanism depends on RIPK3’s RHIM domain and represents a kinase-independent, non-canonical function of the RIPK1–RIPK3 complex in inflammatory signaling.

RIPK3, But Not MLKL, Mediates RIPK1K376R Mutant Induced Systemic Inflammation.

Having established that RIPK1 promotes scaffold-dependent IL-1β secretion via RIPK3, we next assessed whether RIPK3 is critical for systemic inflammation in vivo. Ripk3 knockout (Ripk3−/−) in Ripk1K,D/K,D mice markedly reduced immune cell infiltration in skin and liver (Fig. 6 A and B and SI Appendix, Fig. S8 A and C), and alleviated splenomegaly and immune dysregulation (Fig. 6C and SI Appendix, Fig. S8D). In contrast, Mlkl knockout (Mlkl−/−) failed to improve these phenotypes (Fig. 6 A–C and SI Appendix, Fig. S8 A, C, and D). Combined Mlkl and Casp1/11 deletion restored immune infiltration, indicating MLKL-independent pathways contribute to inflammation (Fig. 6 A and B and SI Appendix, Fig. S8 A–C). Importantly, Ripk3−/−, but not Mlkl−/−, rescued LPS-induced premature death in Ripk1K,D/K,D mice (Fig. 6D). These results indicate that RIPK1-mediated systemic inflammation depends on RIPK3-driven inflammatory signaling rather than MLKL-dependent necroptosis.

Although Ripk3 knockout largely rescued systemic inflammation in Ripk1K,D/K,D mice, residual skin inflammation and epidermal hyperplasia persisted (Fig. 6 A and B). Western blot analysis of skin tissues revealed ongoing apoptotic signaling in Ripk1K,D/K,DRipk3−/− mice (Fig. 6E). Similarly, apoptosis was found in LPS-stimulated BMDMs from Ripk1K,D/K,DRipk3−/− mice (Fig. 6F). This suggests that besides RIPK3-mediated inflammation, apoptosis may also be involved in skin inflammation. Given that TNFR1 signaling is the major pathway for RIPK1-mediated apoptosis, we investigated its role in Ripk1K,D/K,D skin inflammation. Genetic deletion of Tnfr1 significantly reduced immune cell infiltration in the skin and liver of Ripk1K,D/K,D mice (SI Appendix, Fig. S9 A–D) and suppressed apoptotic signaling and inflammasome activation in skin tissues (Fig. 6G). Furthermore, Tnfr1 knockout partially improved survival rates in LPS-challenged KD mice (SI Appendix, Fig. S9E).

Together, these data demonstrate that RIPK1 scaffold-dependent systemic inflammation is orchestrated by RIPK3-dependent inflammatory signaling and a parallel RIPK3-independent apoptotic mechanism mediated by TNFR1.

Discussion

The serine/threonine kinase RIPK1 has long been recognized as a central mediator of inflammation and cell death, integrating signals downstream of various pattern recognition and death receptors. While its kinase-dependent roles in apoptosis and necroptosis are well established, the nonenzymatic scaffold functions of RIPK1 remain less defined. In this study, we reveal that scaffold-dependent functions of RIPK1 drive systemic inflammation via metabolic reprogramming, identifying a noncanonical RIPK1–RIPK3–inflammasome axis that bridges cell signaling and immunometabolism. Notably, the inflammatory phenotypes observed in RIPK1K376R/D138N double-mutant mice closely resemble those seen in RIPK1-deficient backgrounds (6–8), indicating that dysregulation of the RIPK1 scaffold-rather than its complete loss-can similarly trigger IL-1β–driven systemic inflammation. In particular, Tnfr1−/−Ripk1−/− mice exhibit pathological features comparable to those of RIPK1K376R/D138N mice, supporting the notion that inflammasome-mediated inflammatory responses substantially contribute to disease manifestation in both genetic contexts, a possibility that warrants further genetic validation.

Our previous work and others have shown that the K376R mutation of RIPK1, which disrupts ubiquitination at a key regulatory lysine residue, can lead to embryonic lethality via hyperactivation of RIPK1 kinase activity. However, administration of Nec-1 to pregnant mice only delayed the lethality of Ripk1K376R/K376R embryos by a few days (30, 31). It remains uncertain whether the mutation of RIPK1 on K376 regulates cell death and inflammation in vivo dependent on the kinase activity or other functions. To address this, we generated a kinase-inactivated mutant (D138N) in the background of Ripk1K376R/K376R mice. The inhibition of RIPK1 kinase activity completely rescued the embryonic lethality of Ripk1K376R/K376R mice. Considering that RIPK1 K376R mutation leads to defective activation of NF-κB signaling (28), and loss of key NF-κB components (Rela, Nemo, Ikkβ, etc.) also cause embryonic lethality similar to Ripk1K376R/K376R mice (56–61), we investigated the involvement of NF-κB signaling in the lethality. The NF-κB signaling in Ripk1K,D/K,D cells revealed inactivation, similar to Ripk1K376R/K376R cells. This suggests that the embryonic lethality caused by the K376R mutation is not mediated by NF-κB signaling defect.

Despite their postnatal viability, Ripk1K,D/K,D mice developed spontaneous, tissue-specific inflammation, particularly in the skin and liver. This inflammatory phenotype was absent in Ripk1K,D/– mice, implicating the K376R mutation in promoting inflammation through a scaffold-dependent mechanism. These findings suggest a dual-functional model wherein RIPK1 kinase activity governs developmental cell death, while its scaffold function modulates inflammatory signaling in adulthood.

Previous studies have proposed that RIPK1 activation can induce inflammatory responses by promoting necroptosis (1, 8, 17, 62–66). Distinguishing the contributions of “RIPK1’s kinase activity” versus its “scaffolding function” remains challenging due to their overlapping regulatory roles in cell death and inflammation. The K376R mutation in RIPK1 simultaneously activates both its kinase activity and scaffold-mediated functions. We therefore utilized this murine model (RIPK1K376R,D138N) to dissect the scaffold-dependent functions of RIPK1K376R mutation. We demonstrated that RIPK1 “promotes Casp1/11 signaling through its scaffolding function,” expanding its role beyond apoptosis and necroptosis. Previous studies have reported that LPS stimulation can trigger intracellular fatty acid metabolic pathways (67). On the one hand, this process enhances fatty acid uptake and de novo synthesis, resulting in intracellular fatty acid accumulation; on the other hand, a portion of these fatty acids enters the arachidonic acid signaling pathway and is subsequently converted into various prostaglandins, including PGE2 and PGD2. Our results indicate that RIPK1 can regulate LPS-induced PGE2 production by interacting with COX2. Moreover, in vivo administration of a COX2 inhibitor significantly reduced inflammation in Ripk1K,D/K,D mutant mice subjected to LPS-induced endotoxic shock, supporting the functional relevance of this mechanism. Although the interaction between RIPK1, RIPK3, and COX2 was detected in cell-based systems, we were unable to confirm this complex in tissue lysates, possibly due to the low abundance or transient nature of the complex in vivo. Therefore, the formation of the RIPK1–RIPK3–COX2 complex in vivo may be context-dependent and requires further investigation. Notably, RIPK1 regulates AA metabolic pathways via RIPK3-dependent signaling, independent of the necroptosis executor MLKL (61, 68–79).

Both Ripk3 and Casp1/11 knockout improved systemic inflammation in Ripk1K,D/K,D mice, but Ripk3 knockout modestly attenuated inflammation in skin tissues. This suggests the presence of a RIPK3-independent signaling pathway modulating the activation of the Casp1/11 signaling cascade. Although kinase-dead mutations suppress both apoptosis and necroptosis, residual apoptotic activity was detected in the skin tissues of Ripk1K,D/K,D mice. Previous studies demonstrated that apoptotic signaling can promote Casp1/11 activation (72, 74, 80–84). Therefore, we hypothesize that a “RIPK3-independent apoptotic pathway” mediates Casp1/11 activation. TNFR1 is a primary signaling axis for RIPK1-dependent apoptosis, and Tnfr1 knockout effectively suppressed apoptosis in Ripk1K,D/K,D skin tissues and rescued epidermal hyperplasia and immune cell infiltration. However, Tnfr1 knockout only partially attenuated mortality in LPS-challenged Ripk1K,D/K,D mice, whereas Ripk3 knockout exhibited stronger protective effects. These findings indicate that “RIPK1/RIPK3-mediated PGE2-IL-1β secretion” independently of TNFR1-driven apoptosis.

Understanding this bifurcation in RIPK1 function not only deepens our mechanistic insight into innate immunity but also informs the design of next-generation therapeutics aimed at selectively targeting RIPK1 scaffold activity without disrupting its essential roles in development.

Materials and Methods

Mice.

All mice were housed in a specific pathogen-free facility at Shanghai Institute of Nutrition and Health, Chinese Academy of Sciences. Ripk1K376R,D138N/+,+ mice were generated at D138(GAC) site of exon 3 specific to N138(AAC) based on Ripk1K376R/+ mutant background. Details of the mice used are provided in the SI Appendix.

Cell Culture, Western Blotting, and Immunoprecipitation.

All primary MEFs were from E13.5 or E14.5 embryos (except for Ripk1K376R/K376R MEFs from E10.5 embryos). Cell pellets were lysed in RIPA lysis buffer and the protein concentration in the collected supernatants was determined using a BCA protein assay kit (Thermo Scientific, 23225). Detailed procedures for cell culture, western blotting, and immunoprecipitation are described in the SI Appendix.

Cell Viability Assay.

Cells were seeded to 80% confluence in 96-well plates and treated with TNFα, BV6, zVAD, and in different combinations at the indicated times. Cell viability was measured using the CellTiter-Glo Luminescent Cell Viability Assay kit (Promega, G7572), according to the manufacturer’s instructions. Details of the cell viability assay are provided in the SI Appendix.

Generation of Reconstitution Lines.

For the reconstitution of RIPK1-WT, K376R, D324A, and K612R reconstituted cell lines, RIPK1-KO BMDMs were infected with retroviral particles expressing RIPK1 (WT, K376R, D324A, and K612R, with PAM mutated to avoid additional editing by Cas9) in the pMSCV retroviral background. Details of the generation of reconstitution cell lines are provided in the SI Appendix.

Untargeted Metabolomics.

Including sample preparation, LC-MS analysis, and data processing. Briefly, primary BMDMs were cultured in a 10-cm dish. The culture medium was quickly removed and the cells were washed twice with cold PBS. Dishes were placed on dry ice, and 400 μL of extraction solution was added for metabolite extraction. Detailed procedures for untargeted metabolomics are described in the SI Appendix.

Flow Analysis.

Single-cell suspensions from the spleen were stained with various fluorophore-conjugated antibody cocktails. Information on antibodies and the flow analysis procedure are described in the SI Appendix.

Protein–Protein Interaction Prediction.

Initial structures of COX2 (3HS5) and RIPK3(4M69) were downloaded from the Protein Data Bank (PDB, https://www.rcsb.org/). Details of the protein–protein interaction prediction are provided in the SI Appendix.

Reagents, Cytokine Assay.

Reagents including antibodies and inhibitors used in this study. Serum and cell supernatants were measured using ELISA kits. Additional reagents and information on the cytokines assay are provided in the SI Appendix.

COXs Activity Assay.

The cyclooxygenase (COX) activity was determined using the COX Activity Assay Kit (Cat. No. ab204699, Abcam). Details regarding the COXs activity assay are described in the SI Appendix.

Immunostaining.

Briefly, tissues were collected in ice-cold 1xPBS and then fixed in PFA (4%) at 4 °C overnight. After washing in cold PBS three times, tissues were embedded in the OCT (Sakura) and stored at −80 °C. Cryosections 10 µm in thickness were collected on positively charged slides and stored at −40 °C. Details of the immunostaining procedure are provided in the SI Appendix.

RNA-Seq and RT-PCR.

Total RNAs were extracted using Trizol from Ripk1+,+/+,+, Ripk1+,+/−, Ripk1K,D/K,D and Ripk1K,D/− mice skin tissues. RNA samples were sequenced by Majorbio Tech Solutions. Details of RNA-Seq and RT-PCR are provided in the SI Appendix.

Statistical Analysis.

Data presented in this article are representative results of at least three independent experiments. A log-rank (Mantel–Cox) test was used to compare mouse survival. The statistical significance of data was evaluated by Student’s t test, one-way-ANOVA, and two-way ANOVA. The statistical calculations were performed with GraphPad Prism software.
