Effective and potentially retrievable: will leadless pacemakers become gold standard for pause prevention pacing?
Authors: Silvia Magnani, Maria Giovanna Bucci, Hussam Alì, Riccardo Cappato
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractLeadless pacemakers (LPMs) constitute a significant advancement in the management of bradyarrhythmias by eliminating complications associated with transvenous leads and subcutaneous pockets. Accumulating evidence demonstrates substantial clinical benefits, including a marked reduction in complications, particularly infections, and fewer system revisions as compared with transvenous pacemakers (TVPMs). However, several limitations preclude their widespread adoption. Cardiac tamponade occurs in up to 2% of cases, requiring sternotomy in approximately one third. Management of devices at the elective replacement interval through intracardiac abandonment raises concerns, particularly in younger patients; experience with extraction remains limited and entails substantial risks, with severe complications occurring in up to 3% of cases. Limitations in atrioventricular synchronization persist despite technological advances, and cardiac resynchronization therapy remains unavailable. LPMs are emerging as the preferred option for selected populations at high risk of infection, whereas TVPMs maintain a predominant role owing to their versatility and capacity to deliver advanced therapies. LPMs are gaining traction in specific high-risk subgroups and may expand their indications as technology evolves; however, they are likely to coexist with TVPMs rather than supplant them entirely in the medium term.Keywords: Leadless pacemaker, Transvenous pacemaker, Bradyarrhythmia, Cardiac tamponade, Device extraction, Cardiac resynchronisation

Introduction

Since the first implantation in 1958, transvenous pacemakers have constituted the gold standard for the management of bradyarrhythmias.1 Leadless pacemakers (LPMs), which are implanted in the right ventricle, were developed to eliminate complications associated with transvenous leads and subcutaneous pocket systems. To date, more than 200 000 leadless devices have been implanted worldwide, with encouraging results.2

Three generations of LPMs are currently available. The Micra VR (Medtronic, 25.9 mm in length, 0.8 cc in volume) employs four tines for fixation and incorporates an accelerometer for rate modulation, providing VVI(R) pacing. The Micra AV incorporates mechanical sensing of atrial activity, thereby enabling VDD synchronization. It features a hybrid lithium battery with an anticipated longevity of 12 years.

The AVEIR VR (Abbott, 38 mm in length, 1.1 cc in volume) employs a dual-helix anchoring system and provides the capability for pre-anchoring mapping to identify the optimal implantation site with enhanced sensing characteristics. It incorporates a lithium carbon-monofluoride battery with an anticipated longevity of 17.6 years.

The AVEIR DR (2023) incorporates an atrial module (32 mm in length) that can be implanted independently in patients with sinus node disease without substantial atrioventricular (AV) conduction abnormalities. It communicates wirelessly with the ventricular module to provide dual-chamber DDD pacing, albeit with reduced longevity of approximately 5 years. The implementation of AAI stimulation for the atrial module and VVI stimulation for the ventricular module is designed to prolong battery life.2

Implantation is performed under local anaesthesia. The traditional access route is transfemoral: following puncture of the femoral vein, a 23–27 French introducer (depending on the system) is advanced to the right atrium. The delivery system is then positioned in the right ventricle under fluoroscopic guidance. The interventricular septum represents the preferred target site to minimize perforation risk and achieve optimal ventricular activation with a narrower QRS complex. The jugular approach, recently developed for patients with challenging femoral access, permits immediate postprocedural mobilization.2

Advantages of leadless pacemakers

Absence of leads

Benefits of the absence of transvenous leads and a subcutaneous pocket

The elimination of transvenous leads and a subcutaneous pocket constitutes the fundamental rationale for LPMs, obviating complications associated with these components. Pocket-related complications—occurring in 5–8% of transvenous pacemakers (TVPMs)—include haematomas requiring surgical evacuation, wound dehiscence necessitating revision, skin erosion with generator exposure, and localized infections that may progress to systemic involvement.3

Acute lead-related complications include pneumothorax (1–2% of implantations, requiring drainage in 50% of cases), cardiac perforation with tamponade, and lead dislodgment requiring repositioning.3 Chronic complications encompass venous occlusions (subclavian, innominate, or superior vena cava), affecting 10–15% of patients during long-term follow-up; late lead-related infections; and endocarditis, the latter associated with a mortality rate of 15–30% despite antibiotic therapy and complete system extraction.3

The absence of upper-extremity venous access requirements confers particular advantages in specific populations: patients with congenital or acquired venous occlusion (Paget–Schroetter syndrome and post-central venous catheter thrombosis); those with arteriovenous fistulas for haemodialysis requiring preservation of contralateral venous access; and patients who have undergone mastectomy with axillary clearance and lymphedaema, who face elevated risk for pocket-related complications owing to impaired lymphatic drainage.2 In these subgroups, LPMs frequently constitute the only viable percutaneous option, obviating the need for surgically implanted epicardial systems.

Reduction of infections

The principal clinical advantage of LPMs resides in the substantial reduction in device-related infections. The Micra IDE (Investigational Device Exemption) study (725 patients) reported a 99.2% implantation success rate and freedom from major complications in 96.0% of patients at 6 months.4

The Micra PAR (Post-Approval Registry) (1817 patients) confirmed a 2.7% incidence of major complications at 12 months, with an infection rate of 0.1%.5

The Micra CED (Coverage with Evidence Development) study, comparing 6219 LPMs with 10 212 TVPMs, demonstrated substantial benefits: an 80% reduction in system infections (0.5% vs. 2.4%; P < 0.001), a 43% reduction in overall complications, and a 41% reduction in revisions at three years.6

The near-elimination of infections represents a substantial clinical advancement, given that cardiac implantable electronic device infections necessitate complete system extraction, prolonged antibiotic therapy, extended hospitalization, and delayed reimplantationprocedures associated with 5–10% mortality and considerable healthcare cost. A meta-analysis confirmed a 51% reduction in complications at 1 year.7

Five-year follow-up from the Micra PAR documented a cumulative infection incidence of 0.3%, reinforcing the superiority of LPMs in this setting.8

Specifically, a study by Boveda et al. involving 9858 patients demonstrated benefits in high-risk subgroups—diabetic, oncologic, and immunocompromised patients—with a 78% reduction in infections. In addition, among patients with chronic obstructive pulmonary disease, the risk of pneumothorax was eliminated.9 These data support the selective use of LPMs in high-risk populations.

Limitations

Risk of cardiac tamponade

The most significant complication is cardiac tamponade resulting from ventricular perforation, with incidence varying among devices. For the Micra system, the incidence has progressively declined: 1.6% in the IDE study and 0.8% in the PAR registry, attributable to refined implantation techniques.2 The four-tine design distributes mechanical force over a broader surface area, thereby reducing localized myocardial trauma.

For the AVEIR device, the reported incidence is 1.9%.2 The dual-helix system, although providing secure fixation, concentrates mechanical force over a smaller area, potentially elevating perforation risk during the deployment process.

Of all tamponades, 27.3% require sternotomy and cardiothoracic surgical intervention, with an associated mortality of 1.0%.10 Risk factors include age greater than 85 years, female sex, body mass index below 20 kg/m², chronic obstructive pulmonary disease, and limited operator experience. A risk stratification score incorporating these variables enables identification of high-risk (4.2%) vs. low-risk (0.3%) patients.11

Conversely, complications associated with TVPMs (haematomas, superficial infections, and pneumothorax) are generally amenable to conservative management or minor interventions in standard hospital settings, without requiring dedicated cardiothoracic surgical resources. This more favourable risk profile supports the continued predominance of TVPMs as the standard of care for most patients and centres.

Evolution of atrioventricular synchronization

First-generation LPMs precluded implantation in 30–40% of patients with AV block and preserved sinus function, as only ventricular pacing in VVI mode was available. The Micra AV partially addressed this limitation: employing a triaxial accelerometer, it detects mechanical atrial contractions, thereby enabling VDD synchronization in 89% of patients at rest.2

However, several limitations persist: loss of synchronization during exercise owing to fusion of mechanical signals; a maximum trackable rate of 115 beats per minute, inadequate for physically active patients; inability to provide atrial pacing; and suboptimal performance in atrial fibrillation.

The AVEIR DR (2023) represents a substantial technological advancement. An atrial module (32 mm in length), implanted at the base of the right atrial appendage, communicates wirelessly with the ventricular module (38 mm in length) via 1.35 MHz impulses. The system provides AAI(R) pacing for sinus node dysfunction, DDD(R) pacing for complete AV block, and AAI + VVI—an innovative mode that delivers primary atrial pacing while permitting intrinsic conduction when present, activating ventricular pacing only upon development of conduction block, thereby preserving battery longevity and physiological conduction. AV synchronization exceeding 95% is achieved in 97% of patients.2,10

However, the dual-device system presents several challenges: the risk of atrial module dislodgment is 1.3% during the peri-procedural period and 2.7% during follow-up; the procedure necessitates two sequential implantations, thereby increasing procedural complexity; costs are 3–4 times higher than dual-chamber TVPMs; and long-term data regarding the durability of wireless communication remain limited.

Management of generator depletion and device removal: progress and challenges

The strategy recommended by manufacturers for LPMs with battery depletion is abandonment in situ with implantation of a new unit at an adjacent location. This approach is supported by anatomical studies demonstrating the capacity of the right ventricle to accommodate up to three Micra devices without substantial haemodynamic or electrical interference.12 The depleted device is electrically deactivated via programmer and remains embedded within the myocardium, whereas the new LPM is implanted at a different septal location—typically more apical or more basal relative to the prior site.

This strategy obviates the risks inherent to percutaneous extraction but raises concerns in younger patients (aged less than 50 years) with life expectancy exceeding 30 years who may require 4–5 replacements, thereby accumulating multiple intracardiac devices. The long-term sequelae of this scenario remain uncertain given the absence of data beyond 10 years.13

Percutaneous removal constitutes an alternative to abandonment, particularly in the context of infection, device malfunction, or the need to upgrade to a transvenous system. For the AVEIR device, Abbott has developed a dedicated retrieval system: an outer catheter advances along the device body to the junction with the helical anchor, whereas an inner catheter counter-rotates the helix to disengage it from the myocardium.2 This system enables controlled removal while minimizing myocardial trauma.

For the Micra device, extraction necessitates deflection of the tines using a dedicated catheter (Micra Retrieval Catheter) followed by controlled traction subsequent to eversion of the anchoring elements.14

Extraction entails substantial risks related to the degree of endothelialization and fibrosis. In devices with greater than 80% endothelialization—typically achieved within 3–6 months and progressively increasing thereafter—removal may result in complications including anchor detachment with embolization of metallic components, myocardial disruption with haemopericardium or tamponade, and formation of arteriovenous fistulae owing to injury of septal coronary branches. Published series report success rates of 90–100%, albeit with severe complications occurring in 2.7% of cases, predominantly in extensively endothelialized systems.

Critically, safety data regarding extraction after 7–9 years—corresponding to anticipated battery depletion—derive primarily from experience with the Nanostim (St. Jude Medical),15 the first commercialized LPM (2013), which was withdrawn from the market in 2016 owing to battery-related issues. Nanostim’s screw-based fixation system is analogous to that of the AVEIR device, rendering certain aspects of this experience applicable; however, structural differences (length 42 mm vs. 38 mm, larger diameter) limit direct extrapolation. For the Micra device, comprehensive published data on extraction beyond 5 years are currently lacking.

Leadless pacemakers show a 43–51% reduction in total complications compared with TVPMs (Micra 2.7–4.9% vs. TVPM 7.1% at 3 years), primarily due to the elimination of lead-related complications (fractures and dislodgements) and pocket-related complications (infections 0.1–0.2% vs. 1–2% and pneumothorax absent vs. 1–2%).

However, LPMs exhibit a higher risk of pericardial effusion (0.8–1.9% vs. <0.5%), particularly when implanted in the right ventricular apex. The system revision rate is significantly lower for LPMs (3.2% vs. 6.0% at 3 years). Mortality is similar between groups after adjustment for comorbidities.2

Among immunocompromised patients, haemodialysis patients, those with prior device infections, or those with compromised venous access, LPMs are already emerging as the preferred therapeutic option. The reduction in infections of 78% among diabetic patients and 82% among oncologic patients (0.2% vs. 3.1%) demonstrates clear superiority in these clinical contexts. For these populations—representing 20–30% of pacemaker indications—LPMs are indeed becoming established as first-line therapy.

However, substantial limitations weigh into the risk–benefit equation, including life-threatening complications such as cardiac tamponade (1.0–1.9%, varying by device type), frequently requiring cardiothoracic surgical intervention, and the elevated risks associated with extraction, with severe complications occurring in 2.7% of cases.

Conclusions

In conclusion, LPMs have supplanted TVPMs as the first-line therapeutic option in populations at elevated risk of infection, wherein the 80% reduction in infectious complications proves clinically decisive despite comparatively higher procedural risks. LPMs may further expand their clinical role with continued technological advancement.
