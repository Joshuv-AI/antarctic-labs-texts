Beyond genomes: functional signatures reveal evolutionary patterns across the tree of life
Authors: Antonio Muñoz-Mérida, Antonio Jesús Muñoz-Pajares, Klara Hlouchova
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractProtein function evolution provides a powerful lens to uncover biological complexity. Here, we introduce the concept of the pan-functionome—the full set of protein functions encoded by the proteome of individuals belonging to a taxonomic group—and explore its evolutionary implications. By analyzing over 1,000 annotated proteomes across major branches of life, we identify systematic differences in functional composition that reflect deep evolutionary patterns. The number of biological processes per protein increases nonlinearly over time, with functional diversification rather than protein expansion driving organismal complexity. Distinct taxonomic divisions invest differently in biological processes, highlighting signatures of multicellularity, metabolism, and stress response. Phylogenetic analyses suggest that the evolution of protein functions follows a non-neutral model. Furthermore, functional profiles allow robust taxonomic classification and reveal unique adaptations in individual organisms. Our findings suggest that the functionome provides a complementary perspective on evolution, with potential applications in taxonomy, evolutionary biology, and comparative genomics.Keywords: Proteomics, Functionomics, GO Slim Terms

Understanding the evolution of protein function provides critical insights into the origins and diversification of life. Modern proteins exhibit a complex distribution of biological roles, resulting from dynamic processes that yield multifunctional, modular, and adaptable molecules—departing from the early paradigm of “one gene, one protein, one function” (Jeffery 1999). Gene duplication was one of the first mechanisms proposed to enable ancestral proteins to acquire new roles, allowing one copy to retain the original function while the other accumulates mutations (Ohno 1970). Following the discovery of protein domains as functional units (Wetlaufer 1973), additional mechanisms were described, such as domain reorganization (also known as domain shuffling or accretion) (Patthy 1999), whereby proteins evolve through the addition, internal duplication, or rearrangement of domains, transforming simple ancestral proteins into multidomain architectures with increased functional versatility. Molecular co-option/recruitment (True and Carroll 2002) and molecular tinkering (Jacob 1977) further describe the reuse of pre-existing proteins for new roles through minor or substantial modifications, respectively. However, protein function evolution is not solely domain-dependent: moonlighting proteins perform multiple, often unrelated functions within a single polypeptide chain, without distinct domains (Jeffery 1999; Jeffery 2009).

The advent of gene ontology (GO) (Ashburner et al. 2000) greatly advanced the study of protein function by providing a standardized classification scheme for comparing functions across genomes. Genomic abundance of GO terms has been used to reconstruct phylogenies, providing a timeline for the emergence of ancestral functions (primarily metabolic) and derived ones (primarily regulatory and interactive) (Kim and Caetano-Anollés 2010; Koç and Caetano-Anollés 2017). GO analyses have also revealed differences in molecular function vocabularies, with archaea exhibiting the smallest, oldest, and most homogeneous repertoire, bacteria showing greater complexity, and Eukarya highlighting functional innovation (Caetano-Anollés 2021). Recent studies further demonstrate that organismal complexity correlates strongly with the diversity of protein families and domains rather than genome size alone, emphasizing functional diversification as drivers of transitions such as multicellularity (Ocaña-Pallarès et al. 2022; Alvarez-Ponce and Krishnamurthy 2025).

Although valuable, much of this knowledge relies on protein domains (eg PFAM) or GO molecular function terms derived from limited datasets (often dozens to a few hundred organisms) sourced from public repositories. These annotations are frequently heterogeneous—varying in pipelines, versions, and coverage across taxa—and subject to taxonomic bias toward model species, potentially skewing evolutionary inferences. Here, we argue that focusing on GO biological process terms may complement Molecular Function analyses by providing a higher-level view of integrated cellular processes, an approach applied less frequently in phylogenomic reconstructions. Additionally, we propose that annotating every studied proteome using a uniform strategy and including all isoforms reduces assignment biases and provides a more accurate representation. To this end, we compiled over one thousand sequenced proteomes from NCBI (labeled as “reference” and “representative”), categorized according to GenBank standards into eight taxonomic divisions: archaea (n = 142), bacteria (n = 117), fungi (n = 389), protozoa (n = 78), invertebrates (n = 86), plants (n = 67), mammals (n = 53), and other vertebrates (n = 55). In contrast to the “functionome” (the potential functional repertoire of a single genome or metagenome (Takami et al. 2012), we define the pan-functionome as the complete set of functions encoded across all individual proteomes within a taxonomic group.

This manuscript demonstrates that these considerations—uniform annotation, isoform inclusion, and a pan-functionome perspective—help to elucidate the evolutionary forces shaping protein function over time. Specifically, our approach addresses the following questions: How many biological process functions does an average protein accumulate across different lineages of the tree of life when isoforms are considered? Can individual functional profiles (versus group averages) reveal unique adaptations in specific organisms beyond general taxonomic patterns? Does the number of biological processes per protein increase linearly, exponentially, or in another pattern across the tree of life?

Functionomes across the tree of life

We successfully annotated 77% of the 12.8 million protein predictions composing the 1,020 studied proteomes. The annotation count per organism varies significantly across taxonomic divisions, ranging from an average of 2,231 proteins in archaea to over 26,000 in Mammals and Plants (Fig. 1a and Table S3.2). To characterize the set of functions codified for every studied proteome we have used the GO slim terms (also known as GO slims or GO subsets), a simplified, reduced version of the GO. This standardized database describes gene functions and gene products across three main categories: biological processes, molecular functions, and cellular components (Ashburner et al. 2000). GO Slim terms are species-neutral and specifically appropriate for comparative summaries across genomes or distant taxa (Primmer et al. 2013). For the sake of simplicity, all the results reported in this manuscript consider only the first of these three categories, which is composed of 70 terms.

Collectively, we documented more than 27 million biological process terms across the dataset. The number of functions annotated per organism (counted redundantly as the total sum of occurrence of every term across the entire proteome) also varies widely. For instance, some archaea species exhibit four times more functions than others. This intra-group variation is even more pronounced in other groups like plants, where the disparity between the maximum and minimum number of annotated biological processes can reach a factor of 38 (Table S5.3). Despite this high intra-group variation, the stamp of the evolutionary process is reflected in the mean number of annotations per taxa group, with mammals showing 70 times more biological process annotations than archaea (averages 148,095 vs 2,124, respectively; Fig. 1a and Table S5.3).

Although the number of proteins and the number of biological process functions have both increased throughout the evolutionary history of life, the rate of increase for proteins has been slower than that observed for biological process functions. The reason is that, instead of producing new proteins to perform new functions, proteins have gained new additional functions by gaining pre-existent structural domains. This trend can be discerned through various analytical approaches. For instance, the number of annotated functions shows a strong correlation with the number of annotated proteins but showing different slopes for every taxonomic group (Fig. 1a inset), suggesting that the number of functions performed per protein is relatively stable within every taxonomic group but varies across them. This pattern is further confirmed after dividing the number of biological processes identified in an organism by the total number of proteins annotated in the same organism. Thus, while archaea and bacteria show on average one function per protein, the average protein in mammals show 5.7 functions (Fig. 1b and Figure S5.4).

We have inspected the evolution of the number of functions per protein across the entire tree of life using three different approaches to account for global (average) patterns, specific patterns, and phylogenetic patterns (Supplementary material, Sections 5.2 and 5.3). First, we observed an exponential increase in the number of functions performed per protein over time when averaging across taxonomic divisions (Fig. 2a). The strength of this relationship is evident not only by the correlation value itself but also by the significant P-value despite the limited sample size (R2 = 0.87 P-value = 7 × 10−4, N = 8 taxonomic divisions). This strong temporal association suggests that changes in the number of functions performed per protein may have contributed to the evolutionary divergence and differentiation of major taxonomic divisions. Second, by tracking specific proteins across the different taxonomic lineages, we can observe the accretion of biological processes. The study of four proteins critical to life processes in all branches of the tree of life (namely, those with hemo domains, aminoacyl-tRNA synthetase, ribosomal L23 protein, and cytochrome-b5 reductase) shows that they have notably accumulated additional functions over evolutionary time (Fig. 1c). Third, we have found a significant phylogenetic signal of the number of functions performed per protein after considering the entire dataset (Fig. 2b) and within all taxonomic divisions except vertebrates (including mammals) and protozoa (Table S5.5, Figure S5.11). More interestingly, estimates obtained for the kappa and the lambda statistics (Pagel 1999) are remarkably different, suggesting that the number of functions performed per protein has evolved following a non-neutral model. To explore this hypothesis, we have fitted and compared different evolutionary models resulting in the confirmation that the Brownian model (representing neutrality) inadequately captures the evolution of the trait (Fig. 2c and see Section 5.3.2 in the Material for details). In fact, the best models correspond to those highlighting a bad performance of neutrality (lambda and kappa, Fig. 2b and Table S5.6). Even after considering a restricted set of models, the Ornstein–Uhlenbeck model (representing stabilizing selection) clearly outperformed the Brownian model except for Fungi (Table S5.8). We also found that differences observed in the trait within and across taxonomic divisions are not due to directional selection (Section 5.3.2.3 in the Material). Our interpretation of these results is that the evolution of the number of functions performed per protein has been shaped by stabilizing natural selection and trait values have changed in a non-gradual manner. This temporal pattern may resemble the episodic shifts described by the Punctuated Equilibrium model for species diversification (Eldredge and Gould 1972), where changes in the number of functions per protein could occur in bursts, interspersed with periods of evolutionary stasis. This interpretation is also consistent with the clear different patterns shown by taxonomic divisions (Fig. 1b) and may suggest that the number of functions performed per protein could be a key trait to promote deep evolutionary changes such as those required for the emergence of a new kingdom.

Investment in protein functions

The number and identity of the biological processes observed in the different organisms importantly depend on their taxonomic classification (Fig. 1d). Certain biological processes are scarcely found (or even lacking) in bacteria and archaea. These extremely low frequencies are most likely annotation artifacts, as these prokaryotic organisms are not expected to possess genes associated with such processes. However, the rare non-zero annotations could, in principle, reflect evolutionary precursors or promiscuous ancestral functions that later became specialized in eukaryotic lineages—a possibility that would merit further targeted investigation. This is the case of cytoskeleton-dependent intracellular transport, pigmentation, plasma membrane organization and cell junction organization, which are rarely present or entirely absent in these two groups (found in 0% to 8% of these organisms, see Table S5.13 and white cells in Fig. 1d). Other taxonomic divisions lacking biological processes are Protozoa (2 terms missing) and Fungi (3 terms missing, white cells in Fig. 1d and Table S5.11). Conversely, plants, invertebrates, and vertebrates (including mammals) show all the 70 GO slims terms in every studied organism (Table S5.11 and Table S5.12). Regardless of term absences, taxonomic divisions greatly differ in the abundance of biological processes. In fact, only two pairs of taxonomic divisions (namely, protozoa/fungi and archaea/bacteria) are not statistically different in terms of biological processes after Bonferroni correction and another two pairs (plants/invertebrates and plants/vertebrates) showed marginally significant tests. The remaining 24 pairwise comparisons were significantly different (Table S5.10), suggesting the existence of a clear evolutionary footprint that will be further elucidated.

From an individual perspective, we might focus on the proportion of biological processes allocated to a specific function. This value will inform about the proportion of the proteome dedicated to every function. Taken together, the three biological processes most recurrently found in archaea and bacteria represent more than 40% of all processes identified per organism. This value drops drastically in the other taxonomic divisions, ranging from 16% to 20% (Fig. 3 and Table S5.15). The observation that none of the processes represents more than 7% except for archaea and bacteria suggest the evolutionary acquisition (and usage) of new biological processes. Interestingly, the most abundant functions per organism reflects the impact of multicellularity and heterotrophism during the evolution of taxa: Biosynthetic processes and metabolic processes involving nitrogen are the most abundant processes in archaea, bacteria, plant, fungi, and protozoa, whereas anatomical structure development, cell differentiation, and signal transduction are the most abundant processes for the remaining taxonomic divisions (Fig. 3). Thus, bacteria and archaea dedicate most of their protein functions to biosynthetic and metabolic processes but pluricellular animals require a more balanced proportion of functions to properly differentiate and develop their tissues. Plants exhibit a particular pattern reflecting their relatively complex anatomical structure and their autotrophic nature.

Eight biological processes recurrently rank among the top three most represented functions per organism (Fig. 3 and Table S5.14). These functions exhibit remarkable fluctuations across taxonomic divisions, consistent with their potential importance in lineage-specific evolutionary adaptations. The variation in function investment of these eight biological processes across taxonomic divisions shows three different patterns (Fig. 3). First, processes that are more frequent in archaea and bacteria than in the remaining groups. This is the case of metabolic processes (namely biosynthetic process, cellular nitrogen compound metabolic process, and small molecule metabolic process). Second, processes that are less frequent (in many cases almost absent) in archaea and bacteria than in the remaining groups. This is the case of anatomical structure development, cell differentiation, signal transduction, and cellular protein modification process, all of them associated with multicellularity and tissue differentiation (Rim et al. 2022; Miao et al. 2024). Third, terms showing similarly high frequency in all the studied taxonomic divisions. This is the case of response to stress, suggesting that all the organisms invest a similar proportion of their protein functions (2.6% to 3.8%) to defeat stressful conditions. The taxonomic group with the highest proportion of terms dedicated to response to stress is plants (5.2%), maybe because the sessile nature of these organisms force them to defeat biotic and abiotic changes by potentiating this particular function.

Biological process and taxonomic classification

The study of the proportion of the proteome dedicated to every function provides valuable information about the origin and evolution of these functions but also about the evolution of organisms themselves. The representation of the heterogeneity in the proportion that every biological process represents for every taxonomic group confirms the existence of an evolutionary footprint (Fig. 1d, Fig. 2). This footprint may help us to (i) visualize the evolutionary process and (ii) identify the taxonomic position of an unknown organism on the basis of the functions codified by its proteome.

On the one hand, the graphical representation of the proportion of every proteome dedicated to every biological process functions can be depicted in a multivariate space using a principal components analysis. In such multivariate space, organisms are grouped by taxonomic divisions (Fig. 4). This approach offers a complementary visualization of the evolutionary history of life on Earth, addressing certain limitations of traditional phylogenetic trees—such as the strict representation of bifurcating nodes and challenges in depicting horizontal gene transfer—by capturing functional similarities directly. Archaea and bacteria are separated from the remaining groups, emphasizing the division between eukaryotic and prokaryotic organisms. Fungi, plants, and protozoa are also clearly different, whereas invertebrates and vertebrates (including mammals) have overlapping positions that nonetheless align with our understanding of their evolutionary relationship. The first principal component distinguishes prokaryotes (characterized by a high frequency of cellular nitrogen compound metabolic processes and small molecule metabolic processes) from eukaryotes (which exhibit a higher investment of cytoskeleton organization and plasma membrane organization). Within the second axis, animals (ie, invertebrates and vertebrates including mammals) are defined by locomotion and cell motility. The third axis shows the highest values for plants, showing high investment in secondary metabolic processes and cell wall organization and biogenesis. Thus, each major branch of the tree of life is represented not only by a characteristic investment in protein functions but also by the predominance of specific functions. Representing evolutionary relationships using biological processes may overcome the limitation of sequence saturation and could be particularly informative for deep branches of the tree of life. In this sense, detailed studies in bacteria and archaea might clarify which group is older based on the basis of the identity and number of functions observed per annotated protein (Koonin 2010; Forterre 2013; Alvarez-Ponce and Krishnamurthy 2025). Despite its potential usefulness for deep branches, the frequency of biological processes seems to preserve phylogenetic information at least to the phylum level (Figure S6.5).

On the other hand, the idiosyncratic frequency of biological processes can be used as an additional trait to classify life forms as it allows discriminating the taxonomic divisions with a global error rate around 5% (Section 5.7 in the Supplementary material). Archaea achieved the highest accuracy in classification (100%), whereas protozoa showed the lowest (87.7%), primarily due to misclassifications of 5% of such organisms as Fungi (Fig. 5a and Table S5.20). The most frequent misclassifications occur between bacteria and archaea (7.5%, Fig. 5a and Table S5.20). The biological processes more importantly contributing to every discriminant dimension tend to be mainly associated with particular groups such as plasma membrane organization, macromolecular complex assembly, and photosynthesis (Section 5.7.1 in the Supplementary material). Indeed, it is possible to create a dichotomous key based on the proportion of functions dedicated to 18 particular biological processes (Fig. 5b). For instance, prokaryotes are uniquely characterized by dedicating less than 0.1% of their functions to plasma membrane organization and macromolecular complex assembly, while archaea (unlike bacteria) allocate more than 0.77% to protein maturation and less than 0.45% to cell morphogenesis (Fig. 5c).

Highlighting functional peculiarities

Beyond the taxonomic and evolutionary questions described above, pan-functionomes are useful to address issues in many other contexts. For instance, the systematic analysis of genome functions allows us to put a newly annotated proteome into the context of other organisms. This contextualization is crucial not only for a deeper understanding of genomes themselves but also to highlight peculiarities in a proteome that may deserve further investigation. To illustrate this, we present two case studies (Fig. 6). Firstly, when the proteome of robusta coffee (Coffea canephora) is compared with annotated organisms within its phylum (Streptophyta), kingdom (Viridiplantae), and even superkingdom (Eukaryota), it becomes evident the high proportion of secondary metabolic processes, sulfur compound metabolic processes, and responses to stress. Indeed, the production of secondary metabolites to combat biotic (pathogenic) stress, such as caffeine, is the characteristic that has made coffee one of the most widely consumed plants worldwide.

Secondly, the comparison of the proteome of the Oomycete Phytophthora megakarya against our dataset of annotated eukaryotes allows us to identify seven biological processes that are significantly underrepresented: cell cycle, cell division, protein complex assembly, reproduction, growth, cellular protein modification process, and cellular nitrogen compound metabolic process. This underrepresentation aligns with the parasitic nature of the organism. Parasites (and more particularly those living inside their hosts) often adapt their life cycles and metabolism to efficiently utilize the resources available within the host (Auld and Tinsley 2015). Such specialization implies that many protein modifications or metabolic processes of nitrogenous compounds are less essential for these parasites compared to free-living organisms. In addition, P. megakarya showed two biological processes with significantly higher frequencies (namely homeostatic process and DNA metabolic process). Parasites may require advanced homeostasis mechanisms to survive in a hostile environment including DNA maintenance and repair, which must be performed in a more sophisticated way to avoid being detected and destroyed by the host's immune system (Mandel et al. 2024).

Limitations and robustness of the pan-functionome approach

This study offers a complementary perspective on molecular evolution by examining the frequency and distribution of protein functions (GO biological process terms) across the entire tree of life—an approach that remains underexplored due to well-known limitations of functional annotations. GO terms are unevenly distributed across taxa, largely because annotations in public repositories are inherited through automated homology-based pipelines and are heavily biased toward well-studied model organisms. As a result, species distant from model organisms tend to receive fewer or less accurate annotations.

The novelty of our work lies not only in the large taxonomic coverage (>1,000 proteomes) but also in our efforts to minimize these biases. Rather than relying on pre-existing public annotations (which vary in pipeline, version, and quality), we performed uniform functional annotation of all proteomes using the sma3s tool (Casimiro-Soriguer et al. 2017). Although sma3s is homology-based, it incorporates an additional domain-based annotation step: if a protein lacks direct orthologs but shares conserved domains with annotated proteins in the database, GO terms associated with those domains are transferred when a sufficient number of sequences in the same cluster share the same annotation. This domain-aware propagation reduces dependency on species-specific homology and ensures more consistent functional inference across divergent taxa. Consequently, nearly all proteins in our dataset receive annotations, with unannotated cases limited to proteins lacking any detectable similarity to UniProt entries—an extremely rare occurrence. Furthermore, we restricted our analyses to the GO Slim subset of biological process terms, which collapses the ontology into broader, less redundant categories. This facilitates meaningful grouping and comparison while mitigating issues related to the hierarchical structure and fine-grained redundancy of the full GO. While these strategies substantially improve annotation consistency and reduce taxonomic bias, we acknowledge that some incompleteness and annotation uncertainty remain inherent to any large-scale GO-based approach. These limitations are discussed further in the context of our results and warrant caution when interpreting rare or borderline annotations (eg low-frequency terms in prokaryotes).

Our pan-functionome dataset

We have examined the comprehensiveness of the dataset created for this research and found that robust results can be achieved across taxonomic divisions with sample sizes as small as 20 proteomes per division, with the exception of archaea and bacteria, which require a minimum of 40 to 60 organisms (Section 6.1 in the Supplementary material). Regarding species completeness, the analysis of multiple Aspergillus proteomes showed that patterns obtained using only two taxa per species are congruent with those obtained using 35 taxa (Section 6.2 in the Supplementary material). This suggests that, although the accuracy (and usefulness) of any database increases with size, our data may correctly represent general patterns of organisms pan-functionomes. Our dataset can be found in the Supplementary material and contains two files per organism: (i) a list with the annotation of every protein in the proteome and (ii) a summary statistic file. All the analyses performed to obtain any result in this article can also be found in the same Supplementary material. Understanding protein functions evolution, characterizing pan-proteomes and putting a proteome into context are just a few applications of an exhaustive annotation of proteins under a unified approach. These applications have been explored in this manuscript, but many others are yet to be unraveled.

Methods

We have used sma3s (Casimiro-Soriguer et al. 2017) to annotate the 1,020 proteomes downloaded from GenBank that passed our filtering criteria (proteomes belonging to reference or representative species and maximum two species from the same genus to avoid bias toward more studied groups). For each proteome, we recorded the number of annotated proteins and the abundance of every GO slim term belonging to biological process (GOBP). We also used NCBI Taxonomy Browser and Timetree5 (Kumar et al. 2022) to obtain the taxonomy of the studied organisms and a dated phylogeny, respectively. To quantify the number of functions performed by four proteins showing homology across all taxonomic divisions (namely, hemo domain, aminoacyl-tRNA synthetase, ribosomal L23 protein, and cytochrome-b5 reductase), we extracted all the core proteins as defined by BUSCO (Manni et al. 2021). We used the downloaded phylogeny to estimate divergence time of every organism and quantified the phylogenetic signal of the number of functions per protein as implemented in the phytools package in R (Revell 2024). We visualized the GOBP matrix as a heatmap using plot.matrix (Klinke 2022) and as a three dimensional PCA produced using pca3d (Weiner 2020). The same GOBP matrix was employed for discriminant analysis, utilizing the MASS package in R (Venables and Ripley 2002). We have tested various evolutionary models with phytools and geiger (Pennell et al. 2014), and directional selection on the number of functions per protein using caper (Orme et al. 2023). Sequence and phylogenies manipulations were conducted using ape (Paradis and Schliep 2019) and dataset rarefaction was performed using vegan (Oksanen et al. 2025). We have used the R version 4.4.1 (R Core Team 2024) to conduct all these analyses and to perform regressions and plots and also to create custom scripts for a wide range of statistical analyses. Further details on these procedures can be found in the Supplementary material.
