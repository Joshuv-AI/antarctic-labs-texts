A senescence-associated gene signature predicts radiotherapy prognosis in non-small cell lung cancer and identifies CSF1 as a target to potentiate radioimmunotherapy
Authors: Shaochuan Liu, Yuhan Zhang, Liuze Li, Huanhuan Wang, Hao Yuan, Deyi Zhang, Donghe Zhao, Yong Guan, Zhiyong Yuan
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractThe role of cellular senescence as a tumor-associated hallmark and therapeutic target in influencing radiotherapy or radioimmunotherapy outcomes remains incompletely understood. To address this, we developed a senescence-associated gene signature to predict prognosis in non‑small cell lung cancer (NSCLC) patients treated with radiotherapy. A risk model based on nine genes—including COL4A1 and CSF1—effectively stratified patients into high‑ and low‑risk groups. High‑risk patients exhibited significantly poorer overall survival and a tumor microenvironment characterized by reduced immune infiltration and an immune‑excluded phenotype. CSF1 was identified as a pivotal gene within this signature. Mechanistically, radiotherapy induces the expansion of a CSF1‑high‑expressing exhausted T‑cell population, which exhibits characteristics of both exhausted and senescent T cells and forms a positive feedback loop with M2‑like macrophages, thereby reinforcing the immunosuppressive microenvironment. Preclinical studies demonstrated that combining a CSF1‑neutralizing antibody with radiotherapy and anti‑PD‑1 therapy effectively reduced exhausted T cells and M2 macrophages, leading to a significant enhancement in therapeutic efficacy. Taken together, our work establishes a nine‑gene signature for risk stratification in NSCLC and provides proof‑of‑concept that targeting CSF1 can potentiate radioimmunotherapy, offering a novel translational strategy for improving patient survival.Graphical Abstract Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at https://doi.org/10.1186/s13062-026-00901-5.Keywords: Senescence-associated gene signature, Non-small cell lung cancer, Radiotherapy, Immunotherapy, CSF1

Introduction

Lung cancer remains the leading cause of cancer-related mortality worldwide, with non-small cell lung cancer (NSCLC) accounting for > 85% of cases [1]. While advanced NSCLC treatment has entered the immunotherapy era, radiotherapy—a cornerstone modality—exerts dual immunomodulatory effects that present both new opportunities and challenges for combination strategies [2, 3]. On one hand, radiotherapy can induce DNA damage, activate the cytosolic cGAS‑STING pathway, and drive type‑I interferon production, thereby enhancing dendritic cell maturation and antigen presentation, as well as promoting CD8⁺ T‑cell infiltration [2, 4]. On the other hand, radiotherapy may also lead to systemic lymphopenia and bone marrow suppression, while enriching immunosuppressive Tregs within the tumor microenvironment [3, 5]. Particularly, a recent study published in Nature further reported that radiotherapy can induce the release of amphiregulin, an epidermal growth factor receptor ligand, which may promote distant tumor metastasis [6]. This necessitates urgent development of novel biomarkers to predict radiotherapy prognosis and identify patients likely to benefit from radioimmunotherapy.

Cellular senescence involves progressive loss of proliferative capacity and function, typically accompanied by a senescence-associated secretory phenotype (SASP) [7]. Senescent cells can be identified through senescence-associated β-galactosidase (SA-β-Gal) staining [8]. Emerging evidence indicates that the SASP can significantly alter the tumor microenvironment by releasing various cytokines, such as IL-1α, IL-1β, IL-6, IL-8, etc., thereby affecting the initiation and progression of cancer [9–11]. In particular, senescent cell populations, such as senescent fibroblasts, can inhibit the activity of CD8+ T cells, thereby compromising the efficacy of immunotherapy [12]. Radiotherapy induces acute DNA damage that promotes senescent cell accumulation, thereby limiting therapeutic efficacy [13]. Moreover, radiotherapy can also induce senescence in normal brain cells, thereby recruiting peripheral myeloid cells to promote the formation of an tumor immunosuppressive microenvironment [14]. Although radiotherapy is known to induce cellular senescence, how this process and the genes involved affect patient responses to radiotherapy or radio-immunotherapy is not well-defined. The underlying mechanisms also remain unclear.

To investigate senescence-related genes in radiotherapy and tumor microenvironment dynamics, we developed a 9-gene prognostic signature. This model effectively predicted clinical outcomes and radiotherapy responses in NSCLC patients, establishing a robust framework for risk stratification and treatment decisions across malignancies. Within this signature, we identified a radiotherapy associated specific cell population—CSF1+ exhausted T cells and the CSF1 they release as a key mediator of macrophage—exhausted T cell crosstalk, serving as a novel therapeutic target for enhancing the efficacy of radioimmunotherapy in NSCLC.

Results

Identification of overlapping DEGs and SAGs in NSCLC

As illustrated in Fig. 1A, we outline the research framework of this study. In GSE162945 dataset, a total of 1,194 differentially expressed genes (DEGs) were identified between pre- and post-radiotherapy tumor samples from NSCLC patients (Fig. 1B). These DEGs were intersected with senescence-associated genes (SAGs), resulting in 243 overlapping genes defined as radiotherapy-associated senescence genes (RASGs) (Fig. 1C). A heatmap was generated to visualize the expression patterns of these 243 RASGs, followed by KEGG and GO enrichment analyses (Fig. 1D). The results revealed that the most significantly enriched pathway was the PI3K-AKT signaling pathway, while the top GO terms were associated with extracellular matrix (ECM)-related functions and structures (Fig. 1E and F).

Identification of post-radiotherapy senescence signatures

To construct a prognostic risk model for radiotherapy-treated lung adenocarcinoma (LUAD) patients, we first identified 113 patients who underwent radiotherapy from the TCGA-LUAD dataset, divided into training and validation cohorts at an approximate 1:1 ratio. In the training cohort, LASSO regression was applied to refine candidate RASGs, yielding 9 genes (Fig. 2A-B). First, we conducted an analysis of the prognostic significance of these genes in the TCGA-LUAD dataset. We found that patients with high expression of CSF1 had a better survival prognosis, whereas conversely, patients with high expression of NDUFS6 had a worse survival prognosis (Fig. S1A). Furthermore, univariate and multivariate analyses revealed that CSF1 and EPHX2 expression serve as independent risk factors affecting the survival prognosis of LUAD patients who received radiotherapy (Fig. 2C-D). Subsequently, we calculated the risk score, a risk score formula was derived as: Risk Score =(0.45287160 × COL4 A1 expression) + (0.26576701 × GIPC3 expression) + (-0.93994092 × EPHX2 expression) +(-0.05473687 × CCDC50 expression) + (-0.85503087 × CSF1 expression) + (0.39786397 × PLA2G7 expression) +(0.67459371 × NDUFS6 expression) + (0.03207024 × VTN expression) + (-0.29493934 × MASP2 expression). Risk scores and gene contributions are visualized in Fig. 2E. Additionally, we analyzed the correlations between EPHX2, CSF1, NDUFS6, and CCDC50 with the risk score. The results showed that the expression levels of EPHX2 and CSF1 were negatively correlated with the risk score, whereas the expression of NDUFS6 and CCDC50 exhibited a positive correlation (Fig. S2A).

Prognostic stratification and validation

Patients were divided into low-risk and high-risk subgroups based on the median risk score. Kaplan-Meier (K-M) survival analysis was performed on the training subset, validation subset, and the entire cohort, revealing significantly better prognosis in the low-risk group compared to the high-risk group (Fig. 3A-C). Survival status distribution plots (using the median risk score as the threshold) demonstrated that the majority of patients in the high-risk group experienced mortality, with consistent trends across the training set, validation set, and overall cohort, further validating the stability of the radiotherapy-associated senescence signature. Furthermore, external validation was conducted in the TCGA-lung squamous cell carcinoma (LUSC) dataset, which yielded consistent results showing that patients with high-risk scores had a poorer prognosis (Fig. S2B). A heatmap illustrated the expression patterns of 9 signature genes in high- and low-risk subgroups, showing consistent trends across all three datasets (Fig. 3C). A forest plot highlighted the hazard ratios of clinical features, with the risk score emerging as the predominant independent risk factor (p < 0.001) (Fig. 3D). ROC analysis in the TCGA-all cohort yielded AUC values of 0.767, 0.743, and 0.704 for predicting 1-year, 3-year, and 5-year survival rates, respectively (Fig. 3E). Furthermore, a nomogram was constructed based on age, clinical stage, and risk score (Fig. 3F).

WGCNA of radiotherapy-associated senescence signatures

To elucidate the associations between RASGs, risk scores, and overall survival (OS), and to identify their co-expression modules, we performed weighted gene co-expression network analysis (WGCNA) (Fig. 4A-B). We identified 8/9 key RASGs distributed across four modules: MEdarkorange (correlation with OS.time: 0.17; risk score: -0.21), MEred (correlation with OS.time: 0.006; risk score: 0.11), MEblue (correlation with OS.time: -0.045; risk score: 0.5), and MEyellow (correlation with OS.time: -0.087; risk score: -0.15) (Fig. 4C-D). KEGG pathway enrichment analysis revealed distinct functional annotations for each module: MEdarkorange: Enriched in cytokine-cytokine receptor interactions and calcium signaling pathways. MEred: Enriched in ribosome. MEblue: Enriched in cell cycle. MEyellow: Enriched in Notch signal pathway and cancer metabolism (Fig. 4E). Taken together, we observed that the MEdarkorange module contains a higher proportion of RASGs, and these genes are significantly enriched in signaling pathways such as “cytokine-cytokine receptor interactions and calcium signaling pathways”. These findings further suggest that the RASGs encompassed in this module may play a biologically more significant role in predicting the efficacy of radiotherapy combined with immunotherapy.

Immune cell infiltration and immune microenvironment characteristics

The immune cell infiltration exerts a significant influence on the survival prognosis of NSCLC patients receiving radiotherapy. We further analyzed the relationship between high/low risk scores and infiltration of 22 immune cell subtypes in NSCLC patients. Results demonstrated that compared with the high-risk group, the low-risk group exhibited higher proportions of resting CD4+ memory T cells and dendritic cells, while showing lower infiltration of plasma cells (Fig. 5A-B). The correlation heatmap among 22 immune cell subtypes revealed that CD8+ T cells, for instance, displayed positive correlations with memory CD4+ T cells, M1 macrophages, and T follicular helper cells, but negative correlations with other immune cell subsets (Fig. 5C).

Further heatmap analysis of correlations between RASGs and infiltrating cell subtypes showed: CCDC50 expression positively correlated with naive B cells but negatively with resting dendritic cells and mast cells; COL4A1 expression positively correlated with M2 macrophages but negatively with T follicular helper cells; CSF1 expression positively correlated with memory B cells, resting dendritic cells, M2 macrophages, and monocytes, while negatively correlating with naive B cells, T follicular helper cells, and plasma cells; EPHX2 expression positively correlated with naive B cells but negatively with M0 macrophages and activated memory CD4+ T cells; GIPC3 expression negatively correlated with M1 macrophages; NDUFS6 expression positively correlated with activated mast cells and T follicular helper cells, while negatively with resting CD4+ memory T cells; PLA2G7 expression positively correlated with M1/M2 macrophages, activated memory T cells, and gamma delta T cells, but negatively with activated dendritic cells and plasma cells; VTN expression positively correlated with M2 macrophages and negatively with plasma cells (Fig. 5D).

ESTIMATE analysis further indicated that the risk score positively correlated with tumor purity but inversely correlated with immune scores (r = -0.2, p = 0.036) (Fig. 5E). Additionally, TILs assessment through H&E staining based on previous literature [15] showed no significant difference in TILs infiltration percentages between the risk groups (Fig. 5F-G). Taken together, these findings establish the risk score as a potential biomarker for predicting immune responses and guiding treatment in a TIL-independent manner.

Assessment of immune microenvironment and treatment response

To further evaluate the immune escape potential between high- and low-risk groups, we calculated multiple indicators. We observed significantly elevated expression of Exclusion and MDSCs in the high-risk group compared to the low-risk group (Fig. 6A-J), suggesting stronger immune escape potential and potentially poorer immunotherapy efficacy in the high-risk group. Using the EaSIeR method to predict immunotherapy response based on gene expression profiles, we found that although the high-risk group appeared to have a higher proportion of non-responders (NR), no statistically significant difference was observed (Fig. 6K-M). Furthermore, no significant differences were detected in predictive scores (Fig. 6N-O).

We further assessed the correlation between RASGs and immune checkpoint-related indicators. PLA2G7, CSF1, GIPC3, and COL4A1 expression showed significant positive correlations with immunosuppressive checkpoints, particularly CSF1 and PLA2G7, while NDUFS6 exhibited negative correlations with multiple immune checkpoints (Fig. 7A). Differential gene GO and KEGG analyses of the high- and low-risk groups revealed genes enrichment in the cell cycle, mitotic sister chromatid segregation, lipopeptide binding, and condensed chromosome centromeric region (Fig. 7B-C). In drug sensitivity predictions, the high-risk group demonstrated greater sensitivity to ATM inhibitors, NUAK2 inhibitors, and mTOR inhibitors, whereas the low-risk group showed higher sensitivity to lapatinib and sinularin (Fig. 7D). Finally, no significant correlations were observed between clinical characteristics (age, gender, or clinical stage) and risk scores (Fig. 7E). Taken together, these findings suggest that the tumor microenvironment in the high-risk score group exhibits a more immunosuppressive phenotype. This phenotype shows limited correlation with the extent of T-cell infiltration, but instead indicates significantly elevated infiltration levels of MDSCs and macrophages.

Single-cell analysis of radiotherapy-induced CSF1⁺ exhausted T cells

In subsequent analyses, we performed single-cell RNA sequencing on mouse tumor tissues collected 72 h after hypofractionated radiotherapy (data sourced from GSE256051) (Fig. 8A–B). Dimensionality reduction and clustering of the single-cell data identified 10 distinct cell populations: pericytes, M1 macrophages, M2 macrophages, M-tumor cells (where the majority of tumor cells are in the M phase), G-tumor cells (where the majority of tumor cells are in the G phase), cancer-associated fibroblasts (CAFs), T cells, endothelial cells, neutrophils, and B cells (Fig. 8A). Key marker genes for each cluster were visualized (Fig. 8B).

Comparative analysis of cell type proportions between radiotherapy-treated and control samples revealed a significant increase in the proportions of M1 and M2 macrophages following radiotherapy (Fig. 8C–D). Given these changes, we further investigated intercellular communication by integrating three biological replicates from the radiotherapy group and comparing them to controls. This analysis indicated that after radiotherapy, several cell subsets—including pericytes, CAFs, M-tumor cells, M2 macrophages, T cells, and endothelial cells—exhibited enhanced communication activity.

As key players in anti-tumor immunity, T cells showed increased interactions with M2 macrophages in the RT group, with elevated signal number and strength both from M2 to T cells and from T cells to M2 (Fig. 8E). Sub-clustering of T cells identified 8 subsets: Eomes⁺ NK-like cells, Tpex, progenitor T cells (pTs), Tregs, Dusp4⁺ exhausted T cells (Tex), GzmD⁺ NK-like cells, Csf1+ Tex, and naïve T cells (Tn), with key markers displayed for each subset (Fig. 8F–G). Interestingly, we noted that during the detailed sub-clustering of T cells, only one Csf1-expressing exhausted cell population showed a significant increase in the RT group, whereas it was virtually absent in the NT group (Fig. 8H).

Expression analysis of 9 key genes in T cells revealed that only Ccdc50, Csf1, and Ndufs6 were detectably expressed. Among these, Csf1 and Ccdc50 were specifically enriched in Csf1+ Tex (Fig. 8I). Further comparison of these genes post-radiotherapy showed significant upregulation of Ndufs6 and Csf1 (Fig. S3A). Pseudotemporal trajectory analysis supported these findings, demonstrating that expression of Csf1 and Ndufs6 increased along the differentiation continuum (Fig. 8J).

We also evaluated senescence markers in T cells and observed that Cdkn1a expression was significantly upregulated in the RT group, predominantly in Csf1+ Tex cells (Fig. S3B-C). We therefore considered whether Csf1+ Tex cells might also exhibit senescent features. We first analyzed the correlation between senescence-related genes and Csf1 across all cell subsets and found that the association between Csf1 expression and senescence-associated genes was most pronounced in T cells (Fig. S3D). Furthermore, we performed GSEA analysis on the significantly expressed genes in the Csf1+ Tex cell population, which revealed significant enrichment in cellular senescence-related pathways (NES = 1.52 and P = 0.04) (Fig. S3E).

To explore the potential biological role of Csf1 expression in T cells, we performed a virtual knockout of Csf1 specifically in this cell population. The analysis revealed that a total of 113 genes, including the senescence-related gene Ccna2, were significantly perturbed (p < 0.05) following the virtual knockout (Fig. S3F). Functional enrichment analysis indicated significant enrichment in pathways related to cell activation, response to external stimuli, mononuclear cell chemotaxis, T-cell cytotoxicity, and others (Fig. S3G). These results suggest that CSF1 expression in T cells also significantly influences their differentiation, chemotaxis, senescence, and function. In summary, these findings demonstrate that radiotherapy induces the expansion of a CSF1⁺ Tex cell population, which exhibits characteristics of both exhausted and senescent T cells, and that CSF1 serves as one of the key regulatory factors in this process.

Additionally, analysis of the GSE162945 dataset also indicated that CSF1 expression was significantly increased following radiotherapy (Fig. S4A). Furthermore, we retrospectively analyzed a previously published single-cell dataset from our group (accession number: CRA011692) [16], which comprised CD45+ immune cells derived from an ovalbumin-expressing Lewis lung carcinoma (LLC-OVA) mouse model. The samples were divided into three groups: a control group (10 days post-tumor inoculation), an RT-04 group (14 days post-inoculation, 4 days after radiotherapy), and an RT-10 group (20 days post-inoculation, 10 days after radiotherapy). Following dimensionality reduction and clustering, 9 distinct immune cell populations were identified: NK, NKT, gdT, Mki67+Cd8+ Tex, Csf1+Cd8+ Tex, Cd8+ Tem, Isg15+Cd8+ T cells, Cd4+ Th, and Treg (Fig. S5A-B). The expression of cluster-specific marker genes is shown in Fig. S5C. Importantly, the proportion of Csf1+Cd8+ Tex was significantly increased in the RT-10 group relative to the control group (Fig. S5D). Consistently, Csf1 and Ccdc50 were found to be specifically enriched in the Csf1+Cd8+ Tex cell population, and pseudotemporal trajectory analysis further corroborated these observations (Fig. S5E-F). In addition, radiotherapy induced Csf1 expression, and multiple senescence-associated markers were elevated in Csf1+Cd8+ Tex cells (Fig. S6A-C). The correlation analyses between Csf1 and various senescence-related genes revealed consistent results (Fig. S6D). Taken together, these findings suggest that radiotherapy upregulate CSF1 expression, potentially enhancing the crosstalk between macrophages and T cells in the tumor microenvironment accelerate T cell senescence.

CSF1-mediated limitation of radioimmunotherapy efficacy

To further investigate the role of CSF1 in the combination of radiotherapy and immunotherapy, we constructed a mouse transplantation tumor model and performed the corresponding interventions and treatments as shown in Fig. 9A. The results showed that radiotherapy combined with anti-CSF1 mAbs and anti-PD-1 therapy had the best anti-tumor growth effect (Fig. 9B-C). Subsequently, we collected transplanted tumor tissues from mice, prepared single-cell suspensions, and performed flow cytometry analysis following the gating strategy outlined in Fig. S7A and B. The results showed that compared with the control group, the number of CD3⁺CD8⁺ T cells infiltrating the tumor was significantly increased in the radiotherapy group (Fig. 9D-E). Meanwhile, the proportions of CSF1+CD8⁺ T cells and senescence-associated T cells were also significantly elevated in the radiotherapy group (Fig. 9F-G). We also found that compared with CSF1⁻CD8⁺ T cells, CSF1⁺CD8⁺ T cells highly expressed the exhaustion-associated marker Tim‑3 (Fig. 9H). These experimental findings are consistent with our single‑cell sequencing analysis, further suggesting that radiotherapy can induce the emergence of an exhausted CD8⁺ T cell population with high CSF1 expression within the tumor. Furthermore, we also found that the percentage of PD-1+Tim-3+Tex-term increased after radiotherapy in animal experiments (Fig. 9I-J). Interestingly, radiotherapy combined with anti-PD-1 mAbs plus anti-CSF1 mAbs (Triple therapy group) significantly increased the proportion of tumor-infiltrating T cells, while reducing the proportion of exhausted T cells and enhancing T cell function (Fig. 9J). This suggests that anti-CSF1 mAbs application can improve radioimmunotherapy efficacy.

Since CSF1 promotes macrophage expansion and polarization, we examined macrophage infiltration in tumors. We found that radiotherapy significantly increased macrophage infiltration, whereas radiotherapy combined with anti-CSF1 mAbs and anti-PD-1 therapy reduced macrophage infiltration in the tumor tissue (Fig. 9K-L). In addition, we also found that CSF1 expression was significantly upregulated in tumor tissues after radiotherapy, a result consistent with the GSE162945 cohort (Fig. 9M). This finding suggest that radiotherapy promotes CSF1 expression in tumor cells. We also found that radiotherapy promoted macrophage M2 polarization, accelerating immunosuppressive microenvironment formation. In contrast, anti-CSF1 mAbs significantly inhibited M2 polarization (Fig. 9M-N). Regarding distant effects, the triple therapy (radiotherapy combined with anti-PD-1 mAbs plus anti-CSF1 mAbs) better inhibited distant tumor growth compared to anti-PD-1 mAbs plus radiotherapy, achieving a 40% complete response rate (Fig. 9O-P). Furthermore, we validated these findings using a TC1-OVA transplanted tumor mouse model, a model of lung cancer. Following a similar treatment regimen, we observed that the combination of radiotherapy, anti-CSF1 mAbs, and anti-PD-1 therapy significantly inhibited the progression of both primary and distant tumors (Fig. S8A-B). In survival analysis, this treatment group also achieved the greatest survival benefit (Fig. S8C). Consistent results were obtained from flow cytometry analysis. Specifically, radiotherapy markedly increased the proportions of CSF1+CD8+ T cells and senescent T cells, and among CSF1+CD8+ T cells, the expression of Tim-3 was also significantly elevated (Fig. S8D–F). Furthermore, compared with the control group, radiotherapy significantly promoted the increase in the proportion of terminally exhausted T cells within the tumor. However, compared with radiotherapy alone, the combination of radiotherapy with anti-CSF1 mAbs and anti-PD-1 therapy markedly reduced the proportion of PD-1+Tim-3+ exhausted T cells (Fig. S8G). Regarding the proportion of M1, we again observed that, compared with the control group, the combination treatment group (radiotherapy plus anti-CSF1 mAbs and anti-PD-1 therapy) significantly increased their proportion (Fig. S8G). Taken together, this confirms that anti-CSF1 therapy can enhance both local and distant anti-tumor effects in radioimmunotherapy. Additionally, at the mechanistic level, we further induced T cell exhaustion in vitro following previously established protocols [17, 18] and treated the cells with CX4945 to reverse or delay T cell exhaustion. Our results showed that following CX4945 treatment, the proportion of exhausted CD8⁺T cells was significantly reduced. Meanwhile, CSF1 expression in CD8⁺T cells and CSF1 levels in the culture supernatant were also markedly decreased (Fig. S8H-J). Admittedly, the precise molecular mechanisms underlying these effects warrant further investigation. Nevertheless, at the cellular level, our findings further reveal a correlation between exhausted T cells and CSF1.

Discussion

As one of the most prevalent cancers globally, NSCLC has garnered increasing research attention regarding the diversification of its therapeutic strategies. Radiotherapy, a cornerstone of NSCLC treatment, plays a significant role in the era of immunotherapy [19, 20]. Current literature indicates that radiotherapy induces cellular senescence, consequently impacting the tumor microenvironment [21]; however, the precise relationship between these phenomena remains incompletely understood. To better predict survival prognosis in patients receiving radiotherapy, we identified a novel signature based on senescence-associated genes, demonstrating stable predictive value for both prognosis and response to radiotherapy.

This signature comprises 9 genes: COL4A1, GIPC3, EPHX2, CCDC50, CSF1, PLA2G7, NDUFS6, VTN, and MASP2. Based on these genes, we established a risk model, which revealed that the high-risk group was associated with unfavorable survival prognosis, reduced immune infiltration, and an immune-excluded phenotype in tumor microenvironment. Notably, the risk score showed no significant correlation with clinical characteristics such as age, sex, or TNM stage. Taken together, this underscores the potential of our model as an independent prognostic factor for predicting outcomes in NSCLC patients receiving radiotherapy.

Among these genes, CSF1 (encoding macrophage colony-stimulating factor, M-CSF, a homodimeric protein) exerted a major influence within our model. M-CSF plays a crucial role in inducing the differentiation of macrophages towards both M1 and M2 phenotypes [22, 23]. M2 macrophages, an immunosuppressive phenotype, are implicated in tumor progression and resistance to immunotherapy in lung cancer and various other malignancies [24, 25].Recent preclinical studies in various cancers, including colon, prostate, and pancreatic cancer, suggest that combining radiotherapy with antagonists targeting the CSF1-CSF1R pathway can effectively inhibit tumor progression [26–28]. Interestingly, another member of the CSF family, GM-CSF, displays a distinct functional trend: in the “PRaG” regimen (radiotherapy combined with immunotherapy), the administration of GM-CSF has shown promising anti-tumor efficacy in advanced tumors. This seemingly paradoxical observation indicates that the CSF family may play a complex and pivotal dual regulatory role in radioimmunotherapy [29, 30]. Unfortunately, previous studies have largely focused on the interplay between macrophages and tumor cells, with relatively limited investigation into the deeper regulatory mechanisms involving T cells. Consequently, the precise mechanisms by which CSF1 influences the crosstalk between radiotherapy and immunotherapy, as well as its clinical significance as a prognostic biomarker in NSCLC patients receiving radiotherapy, remain to be fully elucidated.

Our study revealed that CSF1 expression acts as a protective factor; high CSF1 levels predicted improved prognosis in patients receiving radiotherapy. Furthermore, high CSF1 expression positively correlated with multiple immunosuppressive receptors. It is noteworthy that the current study revealed a seemingly paradoxical observation: CSF1 expression was consistently higher in the low-risk group, despite its well-characterized role as an immunosuppressive and pro-tumorigenic factor. Several factors may explain this paradoxical observation. Higher CSF1 expression in the low-risk group could be linked to higher radiation doses, which correlate with better survival. Alternatively, a disconnect between CSF1 mRNA and protein levels due to post-transcriptional regulation cannot be excluded. Additionally, CSF1-mediated immunosuppression may be offset by other protective immune mechanisms in the low-risk group, whereas the high-risk group may harbor other immunosuppressive factors such as COL4A1. These possibilities underscore the complexity of multi-gene signatures, and the underlying mechanisms warrant further investigation.

These observations also suggest that radiotherapy may elicit its beneficial effects by upregulating CSF1 expression, which in turn mediates immune suppression. This mechanistic insight leads us to hypothesize that CSF1 is a key player in modulating the response to combination therapy involving radiotherapy and immunotherapy. Interestingly, however, our single-cell sequencing analysis revealed that a CSF1 high expressing exhausted T-cell population was significantly increased in the radiotherapy group, whereas this population was virtually absent in the non-radiotherapy group. These cells exhibited dual characteristics of exhaustion and senescence, participated in driving macrophage polarization toward the M2 phenotype, and enhanced the crosstalk between exhausted T cells and macrophages. Moreover, following virtual knockout of CSF1, both functional and senescence-related pathways in T-cell population were markedly perturbed. This finding further underscores the pivotal role of CSF1 in radiotherapy, influencing not only intercellular communication but also intracellular effector functions and senescent states.

T cell exhaustion is a dysfunctional state characterized by progressively impaired effector functions and elevated expression of inhibitory receptors [31]. These exhausted T cells exhibit immunosuppressive activity within the tumor microenvironment and can compromise the efficacy of immunotherapy [31]. Previous clinical studies have revealed that radiotherapy in patients with esophageal squamous cell carcinoma leads to a substantial increase in tumor-infiltrating CD8⁺ T cells, accompanied by a marked expansion of the exhausted CD8⁺ T cell subset [32]. In addition, murine models also demonstrate a rapid increase in the proportion of exhausted T cells within tumors 7 days after radiotherapy. Furthermore, this population shifts toward a more terminally differentiated state, as indicated by a decline in the ratio of precursor exhausted to terminal exhausted T cells [33].

Previous literature has reported that exhausted T cells can release cytokines such as CSF1 and form a positive feedback loop with tumor-associated macrophages [34]. While our animal experiments identified a similar phenomenon, the difference we noted was that this interaction specifically occurred between CSF1⁺ Tex cells and macrophages, whereas no comparable crosstalk was observed with other exhausted cell subsets. Moreover, this distinct cell population was highly expressed exclusively under radiotherapy conditions.

Administration of a CSF1 neutralizing antibody effectively reduced the number of exhausted T cells and the proportion of macrophages, particularly M2 macrophages, post-radiotherapy, thereby disrupting this positive feedback mechanism. Unlike previous studies on radiotherapy combined with CSF1/CSF1R blockade, most prior research used CSF1R inhibitors with radiotherapy and rarely included concurrent immunotherapy. In our study, we added a CSF1 neutralizing antibody to radiotherapy and anti-PD-1 therapy. This therapeutic strategy showed superior efficacy compared with radioimmunotherapy alone. While the underlying mechanisms warrant further investigation, this research holds significant value for clinical translation. Several limitations of this study are as follows: (1) the radiotherapy dose used in this study is primarily applicable to animal models, and the correspondence between this dose and clinically practical radiotherapy doses or their biologically equivalent doses remains unclear, warranting further research to establish a reliable dose conversion strategy; (2) the biological characteristics of CSF1+ exhausted T cells, the regulatory mechanisms underlying CSF1 production, and the interaction networks between these cells and macrophages or other immune cells within the tumor microenvironment require further in-depth molecular mechanistic exploration; (3) the predictive model constructed in this study has not yet been independently validated in prospective clinical trials, and its predictive efficacy in real-world clinical populations awaits further evaluation.

In conclusion, we developed a 9-gene risk stratification tool to identify NSCLC patients with distinct survival prognosis following radiotherapy. Moreover, we identify a novel, radiotherapy-induced population of exhausted T cells that exhibit high CSF1 expression and possess dual hallmarks of senescence and exhaustion. This subset engages in crosstalk with M2, thereby contributing to the establishment of an immunosuppressive tumor microenvironment. Consequently, targeting CSF1 emerges as a promising therapeutic strategy to potentiate the efficacy of radio-immunotherapy in NSCLC and alleviate radiotherapy-triggered T cell exhaustion. These findings establish a conceptual framework for further investigation into the role of CSF1 in combined radio-immunotherapy approaches for cancer. Not only do they pinpoint CSF1 as a novel actionable target in NSCLC immunotherapy, but they also provide a rationale for developing more personalized treatment paradigms, with the ultimate goal of improving clinical outcomes and quality of life for patients.

Methods

Data sources

Clinical information and gene expression profiles of LUAD and LUSC patients were obtained from UCSC Xena. H&E-stained histological images were acquired from the TCGA database. The dataset for identifying differentially expressed genes before and after radiotherapy was downloaded from the GEO database (accession: GSE162945). Single-cell RNA sequencing data was downloaded from the GEO database (accession: GSE256051) and Genome Sequence Archive (CRA011692). Senescence-related signatures (relevance score > 5) were curated from GeneCards, and detailed information about these genes is provided in Table S1.

Drug sensitivity analysis

To evaluate the correlation between drug sensitivity and senescence-related signatures, the half-maximal inhibitory concentration (IC50) of different drugs was calculated by the “oncoPredict” R package [35]. Differences in IC50 values between the low-risk and high-risk groups were compared by the Wilcoxon’s test.

Immune cell infiltration analysis

The CIBERSORTx algorithm [36], a bioinformatics tool employing linear support vector regression, was applied to deconvolute the expression matrix of 22 immune cell types and estimate their relative abundances. Using the R packages CIBERSORT and ggplot2, the proportions of distinct immune cell subsets were quantified and visualized based on normalized gene expression data.

Construction and validation of the prognostic risk model

DEGs in tumor tissues before and after radiotherapy were analyzed by the limma R package, with screening criteria of FDR < 0.05 and |log2FC| >0.5. The overlapping genes between these DEGs and senescence-related genes were defined as radiotherapy-associated senescence genes (RASGs). Key RASGs were further screened via stepwise Cox regression analysis and LASSO regression. The discriminative ability of the risk signature was validated through ROC analysis and principal component assessment. Additionally, the risk model was independently validated using the TCGA-LUSC dataset.

Nomogram construction

A nomogram was developed based on age, clinical stage, and risk score to predict survival outcomes in radiotherapy-treated patients. The constructed calibration curves demonstrated the relationship between the actual and predicted probabilities of 1-year, 3-year, and 5-year OS for LUAD patients receiving radiotherapy. The predictive performance of the risk score for 1-year, 3-year, and 5-year OS was further evaluated by ROC analysis.

Functional enrichment analysis

Functional enrichment analysis, including KEGG and GO analyses, was performed by the ClusterProfiler R package. This analysis focused on: Differentially expressed genes screened before and after radiotherapy, DEGs identified in LUAD patients based on risk scores, and Modules encompassing key RASGs from the WCGNA analysis.

Clinical correlation analysis

Box plots of risk scores across T stage, N stage, M stage, age, and clinical stage were performed by the ggplot2 package. Additionally, Kaplan-Meier survival analysis was performed to evaluate survival differences between LUAD patients with high and low expression of key RASGs.

Weighted gene co-expression network analysis

WGCNA was employed to identify key modules associated with RASGs, investigate their correlations with phenotypes of interest, and pinpoint hub genes within the most significant modules. KEGG pathway enrichment analysis was further conducted on the critical modules harboring RASGs to elucidate their biological functions.

Single-cell sequencing analysis

The single-cell RNA sequencing data employed in this analysis were obtained from previous study [37]. Cells from the NT, RT1, RT2 and RT3 groups were subjected to quality control filtering using the following thresholds: (1) Number of expressed genes (nFeature_RNA): >250 and < 2,500; (2) Total RNA molecules (nCount_RNA): >500 UMIs (unique molecular identifiers); (3) Mitochondrial gene content: <10%. Only cells passing all three criteria were retained for clustering and downstream analysis using the Seurat package.

Virtual knockout experiment of Csf1

To explore the effects of Csf1 gene perturbation, virtual knockout (KO) experiments were conducted employing scTenifoldKnk, an R package implementing a machine-learning workflow for scRNA-seq data analysis [38]. This methodology allowed the identification of genes affected by the virtual knockout of Csf1. Significant differentially expressed genes (p < 0.05) were subsequently analyzed via the Metascape platform for functional enrichment, aiming to assess the potential alterations in biological functions resulting from the Csf1 KO in NSCLC.

Animal model establishment and flow cytometry analysis

Five- to six-week-old female C57BL/6J mice were purchased from an SPF facility and subcutaneously injected with 1 × 10⁶ LLC-OVA cells or TC1-OVA cells in 100 µL PBS to establish transplanted tumor models. When tumor volumes reached ~ 100 mm³, pharmacological interventions were administered: single-fraction radiotherapy (8 Gy), anti-PD-1 mAbs (150 µg/mouse, twice weekly), and anti-CSF1 mAbs (10 mg/kg, three times weekly). The radiotherapy dose and fractionation regimen were chosen based on our research group’s previous study [39]. Tumor volume was calculated as V = (width² × length)/0.5 according to established methodology [40]. The survival endpoint was defined as the mean volume of LLC-OVA and TC1-OVA tumors exceeding 1500 mm³, or the maximum tumor diameter exceeding 1.5 cm.

Single-cell suspensions from mouse tumor tissues were prepared using enzymatic digestion and mechanical milling, as previously described [18]. After erythrocyte lysis, cells were subjected to flow cytometry analysis. Surface and intracellular staining procedures followed the BioLegend manufacturer’s protocols, while SA-β-Gal staining was performed according to Beyotime’s instructions. Flow cytometry data acquisition and analysis were performed using FlowJo and CytExpert software.

In vitro induction of CD8+ T cell exhaustion

The spleens collected from OT-1 female mice were mechanically fragmented and filtered through a 70 μm nylon mesh to remove impurities. The cell suspension was treated with ACK lysis buffer at room temperature for 5 min to lyse erythrocytes. Subsequently, the splenocytes were washed twice with pre-chilled T lymphocyte-specific medium. CD8+ T cells were then isolated from the splenocyte suspension using magnetic bead sorting (Miltenyi Biotec). The purified CD8+ T cells were resuspended in T lymphocyte-specific medium at a density of 1.5–2 × 10⁶ cells/mL and supplemented with IL-15 (5 ng/mL) and IL-7 (5 ng/mL). In accordance with previously reported protocols [17], the OVA257−264 peptide (10 ng/mL) was added to continuously stimulate the cells for 5 days to generate exhausted T lymphocytes. The induced exhausted CD8+ T cells were divided into two groups. One group was treated with CX4945 (1 µM, which has been shown to inhibit T cell exhaustion according to our previous research [18]), and the other group received an equal volume of DMSO as a control. Both groups were incubated at 37℃ in a constant temperature incubator for two days. Subsequently, the cell culture supernatants and immune cells were collected for flow cytometry analysis and ELISA, respectively.

Statistical analysis

All analyses were performed using R version 4.4.2. Survival curves were generated using the Kaplan–Meier method, and between-group differences were evaluated with the log-rank test. For quantitative data comparisons, either the Wilcoxon tests or Student’s t-test was applied. Correlation analyses were performed using Pearson’s test or Spearman’s rank correlation. A two-sided P < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.
