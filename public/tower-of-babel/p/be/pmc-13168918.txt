Patient‐reported outcome measures of percutaneous nephrostomy vs JJ stent in obstructive urolithiasis: results of the STent Or NEphrostomy (STONE) randomised controlled trial
Authors: Hugo W Schuil, Nora Hendriks, Ruben G Duijnhoven, Bart C H Oskam, Tom J H Arends, Diederick Duijvesz, Alexander D Horsch, Bart van der Heij, Irene M Tjiam, Hans van Overhagen, Armand B G N Lamers, Otto M van Delden, Wout A Scheepens, Jamie M A Drossaerts, Frank C H d'Ancona, Steven Boering, Janneke I M van Uhm, Rutger W van der Meer, J Herman van Roijen, Martin de Kiefte, Ad JM Hendrikx, Saskia Weltings, Rob C M Pelger, Harrie P Beerlage, Barbara M A Schout, Guido M Kamphuis
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractObjectiveTo compare the effects of percutaneous nephrostomy (PCN) and JJ ureteric stent (JJ) for urinary tract decompression in patients with obstructive urolithiasis on health‐related quality of life (HRQoL), pain scores, urinary symptoms, and patient satisfaction.Patients and MethodsIn 11 hospitals, 204 patients with obstructive urolithiasis were randomised to JJ or PCN placement. Patient‐reported outcome measures (PROMs) were assessed until stone passage, surgical removal, or at 3 months.ResultsBoth PCN and JJ effectively improved HRQoL index scores (PCN: pre‐decompression 0.70, 1 day post‐decompression 0.82, P = 0.003; JJ: pre 0.59, post 0.85, P < 0.001), pain scores (PCN: pre 4.9, post 1.3, P < 0.001; JJ: pre 5.5, post 1.3, P < 0.001), and achieved patient satisfaction (score at discharge PCN: 7.9; JJ: 8.2), with no significant differences between interventions in overall HRQoL (generalised estimating equation [GEE] P = 0.7) or mean daily pain scores (GEE P = 0.4). PCN led to more self‐care difficulties (patients reporting no problems: PCN 74%, JJ 91%, P = 0.023), while JJ recipients reported more urinary symptoms and higher peak pain intensity (pain score PCN: 2.3, JJ: 3.7, between groups P = 0.018). Despite symptoms, more JJ recipients preferred the same intervention in future (60%), than PCN recipients (31%) (P < 0.001). Survey‐based data collection may introduce response bias (response rate 59%).ConclusionIn patients with obstructive urolithiasis, urinary decompression with PCN and JJ improved HRQoL and pain comparably. JJ causes more urinary symptoms and peak pain but allows better self‐care. Baseline self‐care and urinary symptoms should guide shared decision‐making. Drainage duration should be minimised.Keywords: health‐related quality of life, JJ ureteric stent, obstructive urolithiasis, percutaneous nephrostomy, PROMs, randomised controlled trial

Introduction

Incidence of urolithiasis is growing, and the condition can cause substantial morbidity and even mortality when blocking passage of urine through the urinary tract [1, 2, 3]. Obstructive urolithiasis requires urinary tract drainage in case of severe colicky pain, infection and/or kidney function deterioration, typically via a percutaneous nephrostomy (PCN) or a JJ ureteric stent (JJ) [4]. Both methods are widely used and have been proven to be effective in relieving symptoms of obstruction [5, 6]. The European Association of Urology (EAU) guideline currently does not recommend one method over the other based on a lack of high‐quality evidence proving superiority [7]. Nevertheless, each technique presents distinct advantages and disadvantages concerning definitive stone treatment, type of complications, costs, and health‐related quality of life (HRQoL) [8, 9, 10, 11, 12, 13, 14]. It is therefore important to consider and discuss these factors when counselling patients on method of decompression.

Few studies have directly compared patient‐reported outcome measures (PROMs) between PCN and JJ in obstructive urolithiasis. Previous studies assessing PROMs after PCN and JJ placement were often non‐randomised, underpowered and focused on a limited range of patient‐reported domains, limiting their ability to inform shared decision‐making. Some indicate no differences in HRQoL, while others suggest worse HRQoL and more urinary symptoms with JJ, yet poor mobility and difficulties with personal hygiene with PCN [9, 11, 13, 14]. High‐quality randomised controlled trials (RCTs) on PROMs are needed for balanced, evidence‐based patient‐specific advice and shared decision‐making.

We have performed the STent Or NEphrostomy (STONE) RCT (Clinicaltrials.gov identifier: NCT04594161) comparing PCN to JJ in obstructive urolithiasis with the primary endpoint of time to recovery, which has been reported previously [6]. The present study reports the analysis of the comprehensive comparison of PROMs, including HRQoL, pain, urinary symptoms, and patient satisfaction.

Patients and Methods

Study Design and Setting

This multicentre RCT, performed between June 2020 and December 2023, was conducted across 11 secondary and tertiary medical centres in the Netherlands. This study was performed according to the ethical standards described in the 1964 Declarations of Helsinki and its later amendments and was reviewed and approved by the Medical Ethics Review Committee (METC‐LDD decision NL70822.058.19, 20 January 2020).

Patients diagnosed with obstructive urolithiasis, aged ≥18 years, and with confirmed urinary stones by ultrasonography or CT were randomly assigned to undergo either JJ or PCN procedures after providing written informed consent. Inclusion criteria were: symptoms or laboratory findings indicative of obstructive urolithiasis causing severe pain, infection and/or deterioration of kidney function, and feasibility and safety of both drainage techniques as determined by the attending physician. Exclusion criteria were: pregnancy and inability to understand Dutch.

Randomisation was stratified by the participating centre to accommodate practice variations, including anaesthesia, sedation, placement setting, and method. Each centre followed its own standard procedures. Randomisation was conducted using random permuted blocks of sizes two and four, aiming to enrol 204 patients based on a statistical power calculation regarding the anticipated difference in recovery time, the primary endpoint. No separate power calculation was performed for PROMs.

Patients were monitored until stone removal or passage for up to 90 days. The primary outcome of this study was time to recovery, which has been published previously [6]. During this period, PROMs regarding HRQoL, pain scores, urinary symptoms, and satisfaction were recorded. PROMs were pre‐specified as secondary outcomes in the original trial protocol. Analyses were performed on an as‐treated basis, reflecting the drainage method actually received, as patient‐reported outcomes were expected to be influenced by the intervention in place rather than by initial randomisation.

Collected Data and Questionnaires

Health‐related quality of life was assessed using the Dutch version of the EuroQol five Dimensions five Levels (EQ‐5D‐5L) questionnaire at presentation and at 1, 14, and 30 days post‐decompression [15]. The questionnaire comprises five dimensions: mobility, self‐care, usual activities, pain/discomfort, and anxiety/depression, each with five response levels, ranging from ‘no problems’ to ‘extreme problems’. The EQ‐5D‐5L scores are converted into a single patient HRQoL index score using the Dutch EQ‐5D tariff, ranging from −0.446 to 1 [16]. Negative scores indicate a health state worse than death, while a score of 1 represents perfect health.

Pain was assessed at initial presentation, 1 day post‐decompression, daily during hospitalisation, and at 14 and 30 days post‐decompression. Patients used numeric rating scale (NRS) or visual analogue scale while in hospital to quantify pain levels. At 14 and 30 days post‐intervention, patients scored average and maximum daily pain intensity scores using a NRS in a digital questionnaire.

We developed a 10‐item questionnaire to evaluate urinary symptoms related to decompression, including a query on preferred drainage method for future scenarios (available as Supplementary Material). Adapted from the Ureteric Stent Symptom Questionnaire [17], it addressed both drainage types, but it has not been formally validated for PCN. The surveys were conducted digitally at 14 and 30 days post‐decompression.

Satisfaction was scored on a 1–10 NRS upon discharge from the hospital following decompression and at the 30‐day follow‐up.

Statistical Analysis and Data Storage

The HRQoL index scores showed skewness, and ceiling effects with many patients reaching the maximum score (1). Mann–Whitney U tests and Wilcoxon signed‐rank tests were used for comparisons between groups and within groups. Total change over time between groups was assessed via generalised estimating equations (GEE).

To address skewness in pain scores resulting from a considerable number of patients scoring ‘0’ during follow‐up, differences between intervention groups at each assessment timepoint were assessed using bootstrapped independent t‐tests (1000 replications). Differences between assessment timepoints were also evaluated using dependent t‐tests. Total change over time between groups was assessed via GEE.

Answers of the urinary symptoms survey were compared between intervention groups using the chi‐square test. Patient satisfaction scores were compared between intervention groups using independent t‐tests.

A generalised linear model with interaction terms was used to assess possible heterogeneity of treatment effects by age (grouped as ≤50, 51–65, and ≥66 years) and sex on changes in HRQoL and pain scores within intervention groups over time as compared to baseline scores, as these patient characteristics were considered likely to be associated with complaints from the drainage devices.

As the analyses of PROMs were intended to provide a comprehensive descriptive assessment of multiple patient‐reported domains, no formal adjustment for multiple testing was applied.

Data were collected using Castor Electronic Data Capture (CIWIT BV, Amsterdam, the Netherlands) and analysed using the IBM Statistical Package for the Social Sciences (SPSS®) for Windows, version 28 (IBM Corp., Armonk, NY, USA) and the Statistical Analysis System (SAS), version 9.4 (SAS Institute Inc., Cary, NC, USA).

The HRQoL

The EQ‐5D‐5L questionnaire response rates were 81% at presentation, 64% at 1 day post‐decompression, and 59% at 14 and 30 days. Results for index scores and dimensions per intervention group are shown in Fig. 2 and Table S2.

Upon presentation, patients in the PCN group reported a median (interquartile range [IQR]) HRQoL index score of 0.70 (0.48–0.83), compared to 0.59 (0.45–0.81) in the JJ group (between groups P = 0.5). Following decompression, these median (IQR) scores improved to 0.82 (0.70–0.87) for the PCN group (within group, P = 0.003) and 0.85 (0.70–0.89) for the JJ group (within group P < 0.001) on the first day after intervention (between groups P = 0.4). Throughout the follow‐up period, the median index scores remained relatively stable, measuring 0.82 and 0.85 for the PCN group and 0.81 and 0.80 at 14 and 30 days (between groups P = 0.6 and P = 0.16) for the JJ group. Changes in index scores over time did not differ significantly between the treatment groups (GEE: P = 0.7).

Of the EQ‐5D‐5L dimensions, daily activities and pain/discomfort were the domains in which patients reported the most problems in both groups across all timepoints. Specifically, at 14 and 30 days, 26% and 30% of PCN patients and 36% and 30% of JJ patients reported no limitations in daily activities, respectively. Similarly, only 28% and 37% of PCN patients and 20% and 17% of JJ patients reported no pain or discomfort whatsoever. On four of the five HRQoL dimensions (mobility, usual activities, pain/discomfort, and anxiety/depression) no significant differences in proportion distribution over time between intervention groups was found. However, for self‐care there was a difference favouring the JJ group at 14 days post‐intervention (patients reporting no problems: PCN 74%, JJ 91%, P = 0.023) (Fig. 2a).

Pain

Response rates for pain scores were 95% at presentation, 86% at 1 day post‐decompression, and 57% at both 14 and 30 days. Results are summarised in Fig. 3 and Table S3. The mean pain scores did not significantly differ between intervention groups at baseline (PCN: 4.9, JJ: 5.5, P = 0.20). Both groups showed decreased pain scores the day after intervention (PCN: 1.3, within group P < 0.001; JJ: 1.3, within group P < 0.001; between groups P > 0.9). Throughout follow‐up, scores of the average pain intensity during the day were similar between groups (GEE: P = 0.4). However, patients with a JJ reported a significantly higher level of pain intensity when it is at its worst during the day at 14 days (pain score PCN: 2.3, JJ: 3.7, between groups P = 0.018) and 30 days (pain score PCN: 1.5, JJ: 3.3, between groups P = 0.010) post‐intervention (GEE: P = 0.042).

Effects of Sex and Age on HRQoL and Pain

Figure 4 shows the results of the difference in HRQoL index scores and pain scores from baseline between patients receiving a PCN or JJ per subgroups of sex and age during follow up. Difference in HRQoL index score changes during follow‐up between PCN and JJ were marginally different for men and women, with men favouring PCN (difference in means −0.07 (95% CI −0.16 to 0.01)) and women favouring JJ (difference in means 0.11 (95% CI −0.01 to 0.22) (P for subgroup effect 0.01). For pain scores no statistically significant subgroup effects were found for men or for women between interventions. The HRQoL index scores and pain scores did not differ within defined age groups between interventions.

Urinary Symptoms and Patient Preference

Response rates were 76% at baseline, 60% at 14 days, and 59% at 30 days. Baseline data revealed no significant differences in urinary symptoms between intervention groups (Tables S1 and S4). Figure 5 displays findings from the urinary symptoms questionnaire completed at 14 days post‐decompression. Patients in the JJ group reported significantly higher levels of urinary frequency (P < 0.001) and nocturia (P = 0.014). Additionally, they reported significantly more symptoms of urinary urgency (P = 0.018), vesical tenesmus (P = 0.001), and strangury (P < 0.001) compared to those undergoing PCN. No significant differences were observed in UTIs, urinary incontinence, visible haematuria, or limitations in social activities.

Urinary symptoms 14 days after intervention. P values calculated using chi‐square test.

When questioned about their preference for a hypothetical future intervention, patients with a JJ were more inclined to choose the same intervention again (60%) compared to those who underwent PCN (31%) (P < 0.001). Of the patients who had received general or sedative anaesthesia before placement, 61% of JJ patients would choose the same intervention compared to 43% of PCN patients.

Satisfaction

Upon discharge, patients with a PCN and JJ reported patient satisfaction scores of 7.9 and 8.2, respectively (between groups: P = 0.3). However, by 30 days post‐intervention, these scores had declined to 6.2 for PCN (within group: P < 0.001) and 6.6 for those with a JJ (within group: P < 0.001; between groups: P = 0.4).

Discussion

This RCT demonstrates that both PCN and JJ effectively improve HRQoL, reduce pain and achieve patient satisfaction in patients with obstructive urolithiasis. No significant differences were found between intervention groups on these measures. However, PCN patients reported more challenges with self‐care during follow‐up. The JJ recipients experienced more urinary symptoms such as frequency, nocturia, urgency, vesical tenesmus, and strangury and had a higher level of pain intensity when at its worst during the day. Subgroup analysis revealed that male patients had slightly better HRQoL improvement with PCN and female patients with a JJ. Furthermore, a majority of JJ recipients (60%) expressed a preference for the same procedure in a hypothetical scenario, double the proportion of PCN patients opting for a future PCN (31%).

Our study partially aligns with findings from four smaller prospective studies examining HRQoL in patients undergoing renal decompression. These studies, mostly non‐randomised with smaller sample sizes, utilised a narrower range of PROMs. Joshi et al. [13] (2001) observed 21 JJ and 13 PCN patients, reporting no significant difference in overall HRQoL scores and higher urinary symptom occurrence in the JJ group, similar to our results. Likewise, Mokhmalji et al. [9] (2001) found no significant HRQoL differences in 40 randomised JJ or PCN patients, noting a trend favouring PCN for males and younger patients, aligning with our observations for improvement of HRQoL in males. De Sousa Morais et al. [11] (2018) reported on 18 PCN and 32 JJ patients, observing more urinary symptoms in JJ patients, consistent with our results. However, contrary to our findings, they noted a decline in HRQoL post‐intervention for JJ patients, while we observed an increase in HRQoL for both groups compared to pre‐intervention scores. An explanation for this difference might be the timing of pre‐intervention questioning, although not precisely described in de Sousa Morais et al. [11], as our measurement was during acute presentation. Shoshany et al. [14] (2019) studied 75 PCN or JJ patients for urolithiasis, finding decreased self‐care levels for PCN patients compared to JJ patients but with comparable overall HRQoL scores between groups at discharge after drainage, consistent with our study.

The present study is, to our knowledge, the largest to date reporting on HRQoL, pain, urinary symptoms and satisfaction of patients with a PCN or JJ for obstructive urolithiasis. Although the study was not specifically powered for PROMs, the sample size represents the largest randomised cohort reported to date for this comparison. Randomisation reduced a chance of any confounding resulting from the physician assigning one of the interventions. However, four patients did opt for a different intervention after randomisation. While the urinary symptoms questionnaire was adapted and not formally validated for PCN due to lack of applicability for PCN and in the absence of other validated stent‐related questionnaires, overall HRQoL and pain were assessed using validated PROM instruments. Participating centres were instructed to perform the studied interventions according to their local protocols for anaesthesia and procedural execution, enhancing generalisability but introducing heterogeneity. In particular, JJ placement was more frequently performed under general or sedative anaesthesia, which may have influenced pain, satisfaction and preference outcomes. Further weaknesses include potential response biases due to the survey‐based data collection, as any differential loss or reporting of data driven by patient (dis)satisfaction may introduce bias. However, comparison of baseline characteristics between responders and non‐responders at 14 days among patients with a drainage device still in situ did not reveal clinically meaningful differences, suggesting that differential non‐response is unlikely to have substantially influenced the results (Table S1). Response rates for HRQoL questionnaires completed in hospital ranged from 64% to 81%, while those via e‐mail during follow‐up were at 59%, equivalent to suggested acceptability response rate thresholds (60%) [18, 19] and response rates in previous e‐mail‐survey‐based PROMs studies in the Netherlands (45–62%) [20]. As this study reports secondary PROMs outcomes from a trial powered for time to recovery, the PROMs findings should be interpreted in the context of the study's pre‐defined secondary objectives.

Both interventions had similar impacts on overall HRQoL and satisfaction, but differences were noted in self‐care, maximum daily pain scores, and urinary symptoms. The extracorporeal component of PCN may contribute to more self‐care complaints due to limitations in movement and manual dexterity required for catheter management. JJ recipients reported more urinary problems and higher maximum pain scores, possibly due to the stent's effect on bladder activity and irritation. These urinary symptoms did not translate into a lower overall HRQoL and a significantly larger portion of patients opted for the same intervention in a possible later scenario of obstructive urolithiasis, possibly influenced in part by the higher use of anaesthetics during JJ placement. Satisfaction scores declined during follow‐up, possibly due to the burden of the drainage devices and the frustration of not yet having definitive stone treatment. A qualitative study with patient interviews could provide further insights in their preferences.

Clinical Implications for Shared Decision‐Making

Although overall HRQoL and average daily pain were comparable between PCN and JJ, differences in specific patient‐reported domains may support individualised shared decision‐making. PCN was associated with more self‐care difficulties, likely related to the external catheter, whereas JJ was associated with more urinary symptoms and higher peak pain intensity. Patients with limited self‐care ability may therefore benefit from a JJ, while those with pre‐existing urinary symptoms may experience fewer complaints with a PCN.

Conclusions

This large RCT demonstrates that both PCN and JJ improve overall HRQoL in patients with obstructive urolithiasis who need renal decompression, with no significant differences in average daily pain scores or satisfaction. While JJ recipients report more urinary symptoms and higher peak pain intensity, they also experience better self‐care ability and are more likely to choose the same intervention again. In contrast, PCN may be preferable for those seeking fewer urinary symptoms but requires consideration for patients with impaired self‐care capability. Although drainage duration was not directly studied the observed impairments in daily activities, discomfort and declining satisfaction scores over time, indicate an increasing burden of temporary drainage devices, supporting efforts to minimise time spent with a PCN or JJ and to proceed to definitive stone treatment where possible.
