Machine learning-based forecasting of CO2-related economic growth and agricultural land change in IORA countries
Authors: Xiaohan Xu, Guojie Liang, Roy Anthony Rogers, Chyan Zheng Siow
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Understanding the predictive associations among anthropogenic \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$CO_{2}$$\end{document} emissions, economic development, and land-use change is important for sustainability-oriented analysis in vulnerable coastal regions. This study examines these environmental-economic linkages in four member countries of the Indian Ocean Rim Association (IORA), namely Malaysia, Mauritius, Sri Lanka, and Madagascar, using historical data from 1960 to 2020. To this end, a machine learning-based forecasting framework, termed Xavier Online Sequential Extreme Learning Machine with Genetic Algorithm (XOS-ELM-GA), is proposed to predict GDP and agricultural land area using \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$CO_{2}$$\end{document} emissions as a key explanatory indicator. Given the nonlinear and dynamic nature of the statistical relationships among these variables, advanced forecasting methods are required. Preliminary statistical analyses were first conducted to examine the historical relationships among \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$CO_{2}$$\end{document} emissions, GDP, and agricultural land area in the selected countries. On this basis, the proposed XOS-ELM-GA framework was developed to perform correlation-based forecasting rather than causal inference. Methodologically, the model integrates Xavier-based weight initialization and an optimized genetic algorithm to improve forecasting accuracy while mitigating common limitations of conventional OS-ELM, including prediction uncertainty, parameter sensitivity, and computational inefficiency. Experimental results show that XOS-ELM-GA consistently outperformed ELM, OS-ELM, and OS-ELM-GA across the four IORA countries. For example, in annual GDP forecasting, it achieved average MSE/SMAPE values of 4.47E-03/10.13% for Malaysia, 1.12E-03/10.48% for Mauritius, and 2.98E-02/12.13% for Sri Lanka. In agricultural land forecasting, the model also delivered competitive performance, with average SMAPE values of 3.77% for Sri Lanka and 2.99% for Madagascar. These findings demonstrate that the proposed model provides more reliable forecasts than standard ELM-based online variants. The resulting forecasts offer useful decision support for policymakers seeking to balance economic development with environmental sustainability in IORA countries, and provide practical insights for advancing the United Nations Sustainable Development Goals, particularly SDG 8, SDG 13, and SDG 15.

Introduction

Increasing attention has been paid to how vulnerable coastal and island regions respond to the combined pressures of economic development and environmental change. Within this context, countries in the Indian Ocean Rim Association (IORA) provide an important setting for examining the evolving relationships among economic growth, carbon emissions, and land-use change under conditions of environmental vulnerability. IORA is a crucial regional organization comprising 23 member states spanning Africa, the Middle East, South Asia, and Southeast Asia. Established in 1997, IORA aims to promote economic cooperation, maritime security, and sustainable development among its diverse member states. Given the region’s significance in global trade, natural resource management, and environmental conservation, understanding the economic and environmental dynamics within IORA countries is of great relevance. The increasing exposure of many IORA nations to climate change-related risks, including rising sea levels and extreme weather events, further necessitates comprehensive research on the interplay between economic growth, land use, and CO2 emissions.

For IORA members, many of which are island or coastal states highly vulnerable to climate change, navigating the path to sustainable development is a paramount challenge. A core dilemma lies in balancing the imperative for economic growth, often driven by industrialization and resource use, with the urgent need to mitigate environmental degradation, particularly from CO2 emissions, and to manage finite natural resources like agricultural land. Understanding the future trajectory of these interacting variables–GDP, CO2, and land use–is therefore not merely a statistical exercise, but a necessary step in formulating strategies to achieve key United Nations Sustainable Development Goals, particularly SDG 8 (Decent Work and Economic Growth), SDG 13 (Climate Action), and SDG 15 (Life on Land).

While IORA includes numerous member states, this study focuses on Malaysia, Mauritius, Sri Lanka, and Madagascar as representative economies that collectively illustrate key regional dynamics. These four countries were selected due to their strategic importance within IORA, serving as illustrative cases of varying economic dependency on agriculture, industry, and services while facing significant environmental vulnerabilities.

Malaysia, Mauritius, Sri Lanka, and Madagascar encompass both island and coastal economies that are highly exposed to the effects of climate change, making them relevant cases for examining the predictive relationships among economic growth, land use, and CO2 emissions. Furthermore, these nations have actively engaged in regional cooperation initiatives within IORA, particularly in sectors related to sustainable development, environmental policies, and economic integration, making them well-suited for an analysis of economic-environmental interdependencies. Accordingly, the present study focuses on the predictive relationships among CO\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$_\text {2}$$\end{document} emissions, GDP growth, and agricultural land use in these countries, with the aim of developing a forecasting framework that can support sustainability-oriented policy discussion.

Previous studies have explored the relationship between CO\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$_\text {2}$$\end{document} emissions and economic indicators such as GDP and agricultural land area. For instance, researches have shown that higher CO\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$_\text {2}$$\end{document} emissions are often correlated with increased industrial activity and economic growth1,2. Similarly, changes in CO\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$_\text {2}$$\end{document} emissions can influence agricultural land use patterns and productivity3. However, these studies often rely on complex models with multiple variables, which can be computationally intensive and may not always provide the desired level of accuracy or robustness.

At the same time, with the advancement of Artificial Intelligence (AI) and the evolution of machine learning algorithms, scholars are increasingly employing these algorithms to forecast significant economic indicators. Sundaravalli presented a machine learning model for predicting GDP based on CO\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$_\text {2}$$\end{document} emissions per person and the percentage of service workers in the country4. Its experiments provided the evidence to show that Decision Tree Regression model achieved the best performance in prediction. Paper5 explored machine learning techniques, such as, Gradient Boosting, Random Forest, and Linear Regression, to accurately predict GDP by analyzing various socio-economic parameters from 1970 to 2018, and deployed the model into a web application for estimating and forecasting GDP based on input attributes. However, these types of machine learning algorithms still have their limitations. For example, majority of studies only focused on single target variable in the process of building model to forecast GDP. The complexity of computation is an issue in the training process when the model faces the large-scale data. The other limitation is the parameter dependency, which directly impacts the forecast performance.

Although process-based studies can provide mechanistic interpretations of human–environment interactions, data-driven approaches remain valuable when the purpose is to capture nonlinear statistical relationships and generate near-term forecasts from historical observations. In this sense, the present study does not seek to construct a mechanistic Earth-system model, but instead develops a correlation-based forecasting framework that examines whether CO\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$_\text {2}$$\end{document} emissions, together with historical target information, can provide useful predictive signals for annual GDP and agricultural land area.

The primary objective is therefore to build a forecasting model that alleviates common limitations of conventional OS-ELM, including prediction uncertainty, parameter dependency, and computational inefficiency. In addition, the study seeks to analyze the changing trends of Gross Domestic Product (GDP) and agricultural land area based on the prediction results, providing insights into the future economic and environmental trajectories of these countries. It should be emphasized that the present study does not attempt to identify causal effects among CO\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$_\text {2}$$\end{document} emissions, GDP, and agricultural land area. Instead, statistical analysis is first employed to examine the historical relationships among these variables, and the forecasting model is subsequently constructed on this basis to capture predictive associations and temporal dependencies. In this study, we focus on the distinct economic and environmental contexts of Malaysia, Mauritius, Sri Lanka, and Madagascar, which are shaped by their geographical locations, development structures, and environmental conditions. The proposed model employs the Xavier weights selection approach and optimized GA algorithm to overcome key limitations of the conventional OS-ELM framework and to provide more stable forecasting evidence for policy-oriented analysis.

Literature review

The prediction of GDP and agricultural land area is crucial for economic planning and sustainable development. With the advancement of artificial intelligence (AI), various models, including machine learning and deep learning algorithms, have been developed to enhance the accuracy and reliability of these predictions. Recent studies have further shown that data-driven methods are increasingly applied not only to macroeconomic forecasting, but also to land-use prediction, environmental change analysis, and sustainability-oriented decision support. These studies suggest that nonlinear learning models are often more effective than conventional linear approaches in capturing evolving relationships among socio-economic and environmental variables. However, the reported performance of such models remains highly dependent on data scale, variable selection, parameter setting, and model structure, which limits their robustness and general applicability.

First, machine learning algorithms have been extensively used for GDP and agricultural land area predictions. Taking Support Vector Regression (SVR) as an example, it is good to handle high-dimensional data and non-linear relationships and achieve good performances in in various prediction tasks. Emsia et al. applied optimized SVR to predict GDP growth rates and proposed model predicted test data accurately based on Turkey data6. The other conventional machine learning algorithm — BP neural networks and its variants, with their ability to learn complex mappings, have also been applied in economic forecasting7. In addition, Decision Tree Regression, Gradient Boosting, Random Forest, and Linear Regression have also been explored for GDP forecasting under different socio-economic conditions, showing that machine learning methods can provide flexible alternatives to conventional forecasting approaches4,8,9. However, BP networks can suffer from slow convergence and getting stuck in local minima, which can affect prediction accuracy10 and the kernel trick in the training process of SVR can lead to complex computation11. Moreover, many existing machine learning studies focus on a single target variable and provide limited discussion on the coupled forecasting of environmental and socio-economic variables. As a result, their applicability to dynamic and sustainability-oriented forecasting tasks remains constrained.

Second, deep learning models, particularly Long Short-Term Memory (LSTM) and Gated Recurrent Units (GRU), have shown significant promise in time series prediction. LSTM, with its memory cells and gates, can effectively capture long-term dependencies in data, making it suitable for predicting GDP and agricultural land area trends over time12,13. GRU, a variant of LSTM, simplifies the architecture by combining the forget and input gates into a single update gate, which can reduce computational complexity while maintaining performance. Besides, Transformer also played vital role in GDP prediction based on CO\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$_\text {2}$$\end{document} data, which proved that transformer model had superior forecasting ability to other machine learning algorithms, including Generalized Regression Neural Network, Extreme Learning Machine and Support Vector Regression14. However, deep learning models require large amounts of data for training and can be computationally expensive and transformer model only can achieve good performance under large training data. Although these deep learning models have demonstrated strong predictive ability, their dependence on larger datasets and higher computational cost may reduce their practicality in relatively small-sample and continuously updated forecasting scenarios such as those considered in this study.

Recently, randomization-based models have gained attention for their unique structure, which offers significant advantages, particularly in reducing computational requirements. Random Vector Functional Link (RVFL) neural network, with its random projection and single hidden layer, provided a fast and efficient way to train neural networks15. Extreme Learning Machine is explained as the baseline model, which is a variant model of RVFL. It applied randomly selected weights to build a single-layer feedforward network proposed by Huang in 2004 16. They offered the high learning efficiency and good generalization performances in the time series prediction tasks2,17,18. However, these models can be sensitive to the choice of hyperparameters and may not always capture long-term dependencies as effectively as deep learning models. The other weakness is the random nature of weight initialization in randomization-based model, which can lead to uncertainty in prediction. Besides, few studies have considered online learning for GDP and agricultural land area predictions. The aforementioned algorithms are predominantly offline learning models, which necessitate retraining with all data when new data arrives, thereby limiting training efficiency. Therefore, while randomization-based models are attractive because of their training efficiency, their instability under random initialization and limited adaptability to newly arriving data remain important issues in practical forecasting applications.

Then, online learning models have been introduced, which can update the model incrementally as new data arrives, making it suitable for real-time applications and dynamic environments. This adaptability is crucial for accurate and timely predictions of GDP and agricultural land area, especially in the face of changing economic and environmental conditions. Recently, a representative online learning model, called Online Sequential Extreme Learning Machine (OS-ELM), attracted attention from researchers. Conceptual framework within environmental earth sciences

The conceptual framework shown in Fig. 1 provides an environmental and sustainability-oriented context for examining the predictive relationships among CO\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$_{2}$$\end{document} emissions, GDP, and agricultural land area in selected IORA countries. The framework highlights the broader relationships among anthropogenic activities, socio-economic outcomes, and land-surface processes. Genetic algorithm

The parameter dependency problem appears in the conventional OS-ELM. For example, the number of hidden neurons directly influences the performance of the prediction. Therefore, we propose a new structure Genetic Algorithm (GA) to search for the most suitable parameter in the time-series model, which overcomes the parameter dependency.

The Genetic Algorithm (GA) is an artificial optimization heuristic inspired by the principles of natural selection, designed to optimize the target by the process of evolution in a computational framework. Meanwhile, other optimization algorithms, such as Quantum-behaved Particle Swarm Optimization (QPSO), Grey Wolf Optimization (GWO), and Artificial Bee Colony (ABC), are applied to optimize parameters of models2,22,23. Compared with other optimization algorithms, GA was selected for its proven effectiveness in handling non-linear optimization problems with complex parameter spaces, its ability to avoid local minima, and its robustness in finding global optima for neural network architecture optimization, as demonstrated in previous comparative studies24,25. It consistently achieves optimal performance with minimal iterations by effectively searching the most suitable parameter configurations for predictive models. Therefore, this study applies GA to search for the suitable parameter in the prediction model.

In the construction of a neural network, the prediction performance is predominantly influenced by the number of neurons in the hidden layers. To improve forecast performance and mitigate parameter dependency, this study employs GA to automatically identify the optimal number of hidden neurons within the proposed model. In our proposed GA, it mainly includes three phases to search the number of hidden neurons, including mating, crossover, and mutation. The different part with conventional GA appears in the crossover and mutation phase. In the crossover phase, it exchanges segments of two parent chromosomes and recombines them to form two offspring, a process well-suited for the manipulation of continuous numerical values. In the mutation phase, to demonstrate the mutational characteristic of offspring, we randomly select one in the offspring population to change the last number. Figure 2 shows the flow chart of GA, including selected parents, crossover, and mutation phase.Fig. 2The flow chart of GA.

The flow chart of GA.

To reduce the risk of premature convergence to local optima, the proposed GA maintains population diversity through random population initialization, parent selection based on fitness ranking, crossover between parent chromosomes, and mutation applied to offspring in each iteration. These operations enable the parameter search to explore multiple candidate hidden-neuron configurations rather than following a single deterministic search path, thereby improving the robustness of optimization. This study defines the number of iterations as thirty. The range of the initial population is set between 10 and 500. The initial population size is defined as five and the number of parents mating is set as four. First, the five initial populations are randomly selected using an integer form of the normal distribution. These random selected parameter are called ’parents’. Assume that we have four parents: 117,53, 239, 247, and 365. The fitness values, which determine the model’s error percentage, are calculated based on these parents. Next, the selection phase mainly selects the best parents in the population for next step. Then, parents based on their error values are sorted from lowest to highest. The top four parents are selected with lower error values. Subsequently, the crossover phase occurs, facilitating the exchange of numerical values between the parents’ genes. For instance, if one parent possesses the genes ’2’, ’3’, ’9’, and the other has ’3’, ’6’, ’5’, we proceed to swap ’2’ with ’3’ and ’9’ with ’5’. The offspring generation is produced, consisting of one offspring with genes ’3’, ’3’, ’5’ and another with genes ’2’, ’6’, ’9’. The subsequent phase is mutation, where one gene in an offspring is modified to generate a new variant. For instance, with an offspring having genes ’2’, ’6’, ’9’, if the middle gene undergoes a mutation, we replace gene ’6’ with gene ’5’. Consequently, the mutated offspring is produced with the new gene sequence ’2’, ’5’, ’9’. These offspring, resulting from both the crossover and mutation phases, are cycled back to the initial step and proceed through the subsequent phases repeatedly until the maximum number of iterations is achieved.

As the economy expands and the development of industries, regulatory policies, and technologies, the environment undergoes over time. The Indian Ocean Rim Association (IORA), founded in 1997, is a regional group focusing on countries around the Indian Ocean, which seeks to foster economic cooperation, sustainable development, and maritime security. With 23 member states from Africa, South Asia, Southeast Asia, and Oceania, IORA covers a range of cultures and economies, cooperating in areas like trade, maritime security, tourism, and disaster management. IORA also advocates for a green economy and sustainable development, urging members to cut carbon emissions and find a balance between environmental protection and economic growth.

Mauritius, Madagascar, Sri Lanka, and Malaysia, as representative members of IORA, reflect the diversity of the organization in terms of geographic location, economic structure, and development stages. Mauritius, a small island nation primarily relying on tourism and financial services, exemplifies a small open economy. Madagascar, with its rich natural resources and agricultural base, is a typical resource-driven economy. Sri Lanka, located at a strategic maritime hub in the Indian Ocean, is a crucial node for regional logistics and shipping, highlighting its geopolitical economic significance. While Malaysia, as a major economy in Southeast Asia, is known for its diversified industrial base and export-oriented economy. These countries are actively engaged in addressing climate change, reducing carbon emissions, and promoting sustainable development. By selecting these four countries for this study, a comprehensive reflection of the GDP growth trends and development trajectories of IORA members under different economic structures and environmental policies can be achieved, providing essential insights into economic cooperation and environmental protection in the region. Their time series data sets are collected in the Database of the World bank (https : //data.worldbank.org/country/). For Madagascar, GDP increased markedly between 1960 and 1980, remained relatively stable during 1981–2003, and then entered another period of growth. Agricultural land area fluctuated in Madagascar and Sri Lanka, showing alternating periods of expansion and contraction, whereas Mauritius and Malaysia experienced a more consistent decline over time.

The observed relationships among CO\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$_\text {2}$$\end{document} emissions, GDP, and percentage of agricultural land in Madagascar, Mauritius, Sri Lanka, and Malaysia from 1960 to 2020 reveal a general positive correlation between economic expansion and carbon emissions. These findings underscore the intricate dynamics between economic progress, environmental consequences, and land utilization in the studied countries.

Experiments

In this section, we employ transformed data to evaluate the forecast ability in annual GDP and area of agriculture land for our proposed model in the different four data sets, including Malaysia, Mauritius, Sri Lanka, and Madagascar. To prove the superior ability in Xavier weights selection method and GA, this study compares the performances among models named conventional ELM16, OS-ELM19, OS-ELM-GA, and XOS-ELM-GA.

Experimental setting and evaluation

The forecasting performance of the models is critical for accurate sustainability planning, as reliable predictions enable policymakers to design proactive interventions that balance economic growth with environmental protection. The superior performance of XOS-ELM-GA provides a robust foundation for evidence-based sustainable development strategies. Here, we introduce two sets of experiments designed to assess not only the enhancements of our proposed methods but also to demonstrate the superior forecasting capabilities of our model in predicting annual GDP and agricultural land area.

The first experiment using CO\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$_\text {2}$$\end{document} emission and historical annual GDP from four IORA countries to test the amount of annual GDP in the future. The second experiment employs CO\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$_\text {2}$$\end{document} emission and area of agriculture land from four IORA countries to test the area of agriculture land in the future. The national strategy in the short and long term is generally planned by five and ten years. Thus, this study applies ten years historical characteristics to forecast ten years future trend in amount of annual GDP and area of agriculture land for four IORA countries. Following the data transformation approach outlined in Section Methodology, we utilize a decade of CO\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$_\text {2}$$\end{document} emissions and historical annual GDP data as training features, with the subsequent decade’s annual GDP serving as target values. Similarly, we employ a decade of CO\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$_\text {2}$$\end{document} emissions and historical data as training features, with the subsequent decade’s agricultural land area designated as label data. Therefore, the size of prediction window (D) and the number of prediction steps (P) are all defined as ten. The training features and their corresponding target data for both the GDP prediction model and the agriculture land prediction model are transformed by equations (6) and (7) for the former, and equations (10) and (11) for the latter. The Least One Out (LOO) cross-validation is applied to test the models in the experiments.

Furthermore, this study evaluates the forecasting performance of all models using two metrics: Symmetric Mean Absolute Percentage Error (SMAPE) and Mean Square Error (MSE). SMAPE is a measure of prediction accuracy that quantifies the error as a percentage28, and it is defined by the following equation:22\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\begin{aligned} SMAPE = \frac{100 \%}{n}\sum _{t=1}^{T} \frac{\vert \hat{Y_{t}}-Y_{t}\vert }{\left( \vert \hat{Y_{t}} \vert +\vert Y_{t} \vert \right) /2}, \end{aligned}$$\end{document}while MSE calculates the average of the squares of the differences between the actual and predicted values29. It is a measure of the quality of a predictor or estimator. It is defined as the equation (23).23\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\begin{aligned} MSE = \frac{1}{n} \sum _{t=1}^{T}\left( (Y_{t}-\hat{Y_{t}})^{2}\right) , \end{aligned}$$\end{document}where, T is the number of predictions (\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$t = 1, 2, \ldots , T$$\end{document}). \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\hat{Y_{t}}$$\end{document} is the prediction value in t-th time, and \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$Y_{t}$$\end{document} is the t-th actual target value.

At the same time, to ensure a fair comparison in experiments, this study ensures that the parameters are searched and optimized for all models involved. The conventional ELM uses grid search to identify the optimal parameter (the number of hidden neurons), which sets the within a specified range of 5 to 200 with an interval of 5 to search the best performance by average value of SMAPE. For OS-ELM-based models, the initial training subset was fixed at 60% of the total training samples in order to balance initialization stability and sequential adaptability. A smaller initial proportion may weaken the robustness of the hidden-layer representation and output-weight estimation, whereas a larger proportion may reduce the effective role of sequential updating. Therefore, the current setting was selected as a compromise between these two considerations. We acknowledge that different values of \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\mu$$\end{document} may affect both the forecasting performance and the GA-optimized number of hidden neurons, and a dedicated sensitivity analysis on this parameter will be included in future work. The number of hidden neurons in the conventional OS-ELM models is determined by a grid search approach, scanning a range from 5 to 200 neurons with an interval of 5. This systematic search allowed us to identify the optimal number of hidden neurons that would yield the best performance for each model. In the case of OS-ELM-GA and XOS-ELM-GA, the optimization of the number of hidden neurons is handled by a Genetic Algorithm, which is known for its ability to efficiently search large and complex spaces. The average SMAPE is selected as the fitness value for the Genetic Algorithm when optimizing parameters, which effectively guides the search towards models that minimize prediction errors.

Conclusion

This study developed a novel online forecasting framework, XOS-ELM-GA, to model the predictive relationships among CO\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$_\text {2}$$\end{document} emissions, annual GDP, and agricultural land area in four representative IORA countries. It transformed the original time-series data into a trainable matrix structure, incorporated Xavier-based weight initialization to reduce instability in OS-ELM, and applied a genetic algorithm to optimize the hidden-layer parameter setting. Experimental results demonstrated that the proposed model achieved more accurate and more stable forecasting performance than ELM, OS-ELM, and OS-ELM-GA across the country-level datasets. The study further showed that the forecasted trajectories of GDP and agricultural land use provided useful evidence for interpreting sustainability trade-offs in vulnerable coastal and island economies.

The XOS-ELM-GA model proves to be a highly effective tool in time-series forecasting, integrating Xavier weight initialization and genetic algorithm optimization to overcome common challenges such as parameter dependency, model uncertainty, and the need for large-scale training data. Compared to conventional machine learning models, XOS-ELM-GA exhibits higher accuracy and greater stability in predicting future GDP and agricultural land area, making it particularly suitable for economic planning in island economies where environmental and economic variables are deeply intertwined. The model successfully forecasts the economic trends until 2030, indicating that GDP in these island countries will continue to grow but may face challenges due to environmental constraints and shifting economic policies.

Beyond the methodological improvements, the present study offers forecasting-based evidence for discussing possible sustainability pressures associated with economic growth, carbon emissions, and land-use change in IORA countries. In this sense, the findings may be relevant to the broader discussion of the United Nations Sustainable Development Goals, particularly in relation to economic development (SDG 8), climate action (SDG 13), and sustainable land management (SDG 15). For rapidly industrializing IORA countries such as Malaysia and Mauritius, the forecast trajectories suggest that continued economic expansion may remain closely associated with carbon-emission pressure. For agriculture-dependent contexts such as Sri Lanka and Madagascar, the projected land-use changes may indicate greater pressure on land systems and resource management. At the regional level, these results may provide exploratory scenario support for discussing differentiated development pathways and sustainability challenges across IORA member states.

At the same time, the policy relevance of the present study should be interpreted with appropriate caution. The proposed framework is correlation-based, uses a relatively restricted predictor set, and is evaluated on a limited number of countries. Accordingly, the results are more appropriately understood as exploratory forecasting evidence rather than direct policy prescription. Within this scope, the XOS-ELM-GA model may still serve as a useful tool for scenario-oriented analysis by translating anthropogenic driver data into conditional projections of key economic and land-use variables, thereby supporting discussion of sustainable development challenges in environmentally vulnerable regional settings.

Limitations and future works

While this study provides useful forecasting evidence, several limitations should be acknowledged. The first concerns the predictor specification of the proposed framework. Although CO\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$_{2}$$\end{document} emissions provide statistically meaningful predictive information in the selected countries, the current input design remains intentionally parsimonious and does not fully represent the broader set of factors that may influence GDP growth and, especially, agricultural land dynamics, including energy consumption, industrial structure, trade intensity, climate variables, policy intervention, commodity-price change, urban expansion, and institutional conditions. This issue is particularly relevant to the agricultural land forecasting task, because in countries where the statistical association between CO\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$_{2}$$\end{document} emissions and agricultural land is relatively weak, the interpretive and predictive value of the forecasts is inherently more limited. Under such conditions, the forecasting results should be regarded as indicative predictive evidence based on a simplified input specification, rather than as a complete structural account of land-use change. In addition, the long-horizon forecasts reported in this study are conditional on the adopted CO\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$_{2}$$\end{document} input setting used in the recursive forecasting procedure. A further limitation lies in the geographical scope of the analysis, which is currently restricted to four representative IORA countries. Future research should therefore expand both the predictor set and the country coverage, and may further consider alternative CO\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$_{2}$$\end{document} scenarios or explicit forecasting strategies for exogenous inputs, in order to improve the realism, structural completeness, and interpretability of the proposed forecasting framework.
