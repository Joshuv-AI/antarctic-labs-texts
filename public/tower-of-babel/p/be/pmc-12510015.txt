Climate-induced migration and climate immobility in Ghana: A socio-ecological mixed method study
Authors: Seth Christopher Yaw Appiah
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
Highlights•Limited global and Ghanaian-specific evidence exists on climate immobility.•A mixed-method approach was employed, hinged on a concurrent mixed-method design.•Climate change caused 21.7 % of people to migrate, while 49.9 % remained immobile despite climatic conditions exposures.•Persons 36–45 years and above 55 years showing an increased likelihood of being climate immobile.•Strengthening adaptive capacities is crucial for managing climate-induced migration and immobility.

Introduction

The term "migration" describes the movement of individuals either domestically (internal migration) or internationally (cross-border migration) (Bauer and Zimmermann, 2018). For many people, it has become a way of life for various reasons (Achieng et al., 2020), especially for economic and educational opportunities (Prosper and Khan, 2018). Global research and policy discussions are beginning to acknowledge the connection between migration and the environment (Carling and Collins, 2020; Hunter et al., 2015). Environmental pressures forces people and households to relocate in pursuit of more stable livelihoods include decreasing agricultural production, water scarcity, and extreme weather events (Banu and Fazal, 2025; Ayeb-Karlsson, 2018).In addition, migration is used as an adaptation strategy to help households diversify their sources of income, lessen their vulnerability, and become more resilient to environmental change. The factors driving migration are gradually changing worldwide as climatic elements concurrently affect food systems and livelihood activities, leading to climate-induced migration (Achieng et al., 2020; Prosper and Khan, 2018).

Climate-induced migration is referred to as the movement of people driven by sudden or progressive changes in weather or climate conditions (Prosper and Khan, 2018). Extreme weather events and increased drought frequency are climatic risks that might cause population movement and/or (in)voluntary immobility in the region (Williams et al., 2020). Displacement from floods in central Europe, Brazil, Mozambique, and Kenya has been well-documented, while massive floods affected many millions in Pakistan and China in 2010 (Hunter et al., 2015; Banu and Fazal, 2025; Ayeb-Karlsson, 2018; Osei-Amponsah et al., 2023; Williams et al., 2020). In Sub-Saharan Africa, recurrent droughts are a major driver of climate-induced migration, affecting an estimated 85 million people, equivalent to about 4 % of the region’s total population (Banu and Fazal, 2025). A complex web of factors influences migration decisions in the context of the climate disaster with social, economic, and cultural consequences (Nabong et al., 2023).

The minimalists view migration as a potential solution or risk reduction strategy, while maximalists view it as a last resort after unsuccessful adaptation to climatic hazards (Prosper and Khan, 2018). Scientists and policymakers have raised concerns about the potential involuntary displacement of populations due to climate change (Ayeb-Karlsson, 2018). Analyzing the intricate relationships between environmental stress and migration remains difficult as it exacerbates social inequalities among vulnerable groups, necessitating interdisciplinary solutions to address these disparities (Bezirtzoglou et al., 2011). Climate migrants experience lower subjective well-being, leading to poverty and suicide (Bauer and Zimmermann, 2018). Children in Small Island Developing States and Least Developed countries are more severely affected (Kimutai et al., 2024),and this vulnerability is further complicated by the growing discourse on immobility, which highlights how some populations despite facing significant risks are unable or unwilling to move in response to climate change (Barbosa and Coates, 2021; Mugabe et al., 2021).

Climate change causes voluntary or involuntary immobility, with resilience and vulnerability affecting individual choices and outcomes (Nabong et al., 2023). Studies in Peru revealed that people will be particularly at risk of becoming “trapped” in unsafe areas when more climate impacts erode the ecosystem on which they depend (Thiede, 2024; Pearson et al., 2023). The Foresight report on Migration and Global Environmental Change emphasized that while some individuals choose to stay in a certain region, others are simply unable to leave, resulting in what the study labeled "trapped populations" (Black et al., 2011). Immobility is largely associated with deep-rooted traditions and attachments to ancestral lands, limited access to relocation assistance and the fear of uncertainty that accompanies climate-induced migration (dos Santos, 2023; Upadhyay et al., 2024). Azumah and & Ahmed’s study in the five northern regions of Ghana makes effort at differentiating the specific type of climate change that could trigger specific outcomes including farmers migration (Azumah and Ahmed, 2023). Several of the previous studies focused on the effect of either one climate change stressor or used one of the stressors to represent the entirety of climate change stressors on climatic conditions (Boas et al., 2022; Cundill et al., 2021).

Ghana, like other West African countries, has seen population migrations from pre-colonial times (Zickgraf, 2021). While the northern geographical regions have been places of recurrent out-migration, the Upper East Region, which is home to the Frafra ethnic group, has been highlighted as a key source of climatic migration to southern Ghana (van Daalen et al., 2021; Bharadwaj and Huq, 2022). The northwestern area of Ghana has been defined by significant ecological change and resource decline in recent years (Boas et al., 2022; Cundill et al., 2021). Rapid environmental degradation, along with climate/rainfall unpredictability and chronic floods, has reduced agricultural production in the region (Zickgraf, 2021; Bergmann, 2020). Migration from this area is frequently a coping mechanism in which households diversify their livelihoods through seasonal or long-term mobility, primarily in reaction to environmental stressors (Yakubu, 2017).

Research on climate change, migration, and immobility necessitates an interdisciplinary approach, given the intricate interplay of legal, cultural, economic, and psychological elements. However, studies that have made efforts in understanding the multi-dimensional relationship between and among climate change triggers have focused on its impacts on public health outcomes (Azumah and Ahmed, 2023), microbiological contamination of water and single climate change events with much focus on floods. The climate change and immobility literature asserts mobility receives more attention than immobility (Nabong et al., 2023; Thornton et al., 2023), something also noted in mobility literature more broadly (Bauer and Zimmermann, 2018). The empirical data grounded in existing literature on climate change and immobility across the west African region and more particularly on Ghana is only emerging and almost silent in the case of Ghana on the relationship between climate change and immobility.

The perspectives of persons who remain immobile, whether by choice or constraint, are underexplored (Jang et al., 2021; Rigaud et al., 2021), resulting in an insufficient understanding of vulnerability and resilience in Ghana. The climate-migration literature in Ghana has focused almost exclusively on measuring the short-term effects of climatic variability on geographic mobility (dos Santos, 2023; Upadhyay et al., 2024), with little attention given to the relationship between climate migration and causes of immobility. The extant of research conducted in the sub-Saharan region and northern regions of Ghana on climate induced migration have often looked at direct relationships and also individual climate change stressors rather than the effects that the combined climate change stressors (flood, drought, extreme heat windstorm) make on both mobility and immobility (Kassim et al., 2021; Vinke et al., 2020).The question of how migration is used as an adaptation strategy as espoused elsewhere is missing in the Ghanaian literature (Cundill et al., 2021).

There is a knowledge and evidence deficit in Ghanaian literature about both voluntary and involuntary immobility in the context of environmental or climate change. The relationships between migration, displacement, and trapped populations on the one hand and the characteristics of extreme events on the other are unclear in the Ghanaian context (Osei-Amponsah et al., 2023). This calls for the adoption of a holistic non silo approach to interrogate how the different climate change stressors combine to yield both migration and immobility outcomes far beyond environmental, food security, crop yield and public health concerns that have largely shaped the existing literature. These identified gaps are necessary for the conduct of this study to inform policymaking.

A more useful theoretical framework with fit for purpose objectives is the socioecological system (SES) theory (Azumah and Ahmed, 2023). This theory is underpinned by the idea that individuals constitute living systems and that individuals must be open since open systems evolve and adapt to the environment around them without following any predetermined patterns of behavior (Cioffi-Revilla, 2016). An open system can be altered by the environment around it and can also induce change within its environment. Ghana is an important case study since recurring floods, droughts, and land degradation already have an impact on rural and urban population migration. However, policy and scholarly debates frequently presume that climate change would ultimately result in mass migration, ignoring the substantial proportion of people who remain behind whether owing to financial restraints, cultural ties, or social commitments. A better comprehension of this complex and dynamic relationship promises to inform effective policy framing and interventions designed to support the mobility and displacement need of affected individuals' and communities' mobility. In contrast to more limited frameworks like livelihoods methods that concentrate on household assets or the Push-Pull model, which highlights distinct drivers of migration, the SES framework incorporates the dynamic interaction between ecological processes and social systems. This makes it more appropriate for examining both mobility and immobility in the Ghanaian context (Kassim et al., 2021).

In applying this theory, a more holistic approach is employed such that beyond the qualitative alone or quantitative alone perspectives that are employed to interrogate climate change events and its impact on migration, a mixed method approach with an all-encompassing climate change triggers (floods, extreme heat, windstorm and drought) is interrogated to examine their impacts on not only migration but also immobility. In this way lived realities and perceptions of the community dwellers in respect of climate change stressors are qualitatively interrogated and understood with the context of quantifiable demographic parameters and socio-environmental variables. This study examined drivers of climate-induced migration and climate immobility in peri‑urban communities in Ghana from a socio-ecological perspective. The broader question that this study answers relates to the question of what drives, shapes or independently determine climate-induced migration and climate immobility among rural and peri‑urban Ghanaian residents in the northern and upper-east regions of Ghana.

Methodology

Study area

The study was carried out in two districts/municipals from two regions (Northern and Upper East) in the northern part of Ghana. The study districts are the Savelugu Municipal Assembly in the Northern Region and the Talensi District in the Upper East Region. The Savelugu Municipal with Savelugu as its capital has a total population of 122,888 as of 2021 (Vinke et al., 2020). In the Savelugu District, 13.2 % of families used surface waters, 18.6 % relied on piped water, and 39.6 % used boreholes for drinking water in 2010. (Statistical Services of Ghana, 2014a). Agriculture is the primary economic activity in the Savelugu Municipal Assembly, accounting for 74.1 % of the economically active population. The White Volta River passes through the Savelugu municipality, offering rural populations possibilities for fishing and farming which is largely subsistence farming. The roads in the municipality are gravel, earth and paved.

The majority of communities are connected by feeder roads, which may not be accessible during the rainy season. An improvement of the sanitation situation in the municipality especially proper disposal of solid and liquid waste is a priority of the Assembly, due to the sanitation issues in the municipality. The Talensi district with its capital being Tongo has a total population of 87,021 as of 2021. Majority of these communities are rural, where many of the households (64.8 %) obtained their drinking water from boreholes in 2010. 90 % of its workforce is employed primarily in arable agriculture. The lands in the municipality have been affected by soil erosion where large tracts of land have been destroyed by water erosion leading to soil depth reduction, soil fertility decline and siltation of rivers and reservoirs. Alternative sources of income include logging, food production, fuelwood extraction, tourism, livestock husbandry, and poultry farming (Ghana Statistical Services 2014b). The total number of health facilities is insufficient; nonetheless, those that exist are inadequately equipped. The service providers are from both the public and private sectors, including non-governmental organizations (NGOs) and traditional practitioners. The district has a total of 123 schools, the majority of which are in poor condition. In addition to being in poor circumstances, many of them are deprived of water and sanitary amenities, as well as substandard infrastructures and access roads. These districts were purposively selected due to their disproportionate recurrent occurrence of floods, storms, and drought leading to climate-induced spillage from Bagre Dam around August and September each year (Awumbila et al., 2019; Anabila et al., 2020).

Study approach and design

The study adopted a mixed-method approach, hinged on a concurrent mixed-method design to analyze climate-induced mobility and immobility in the Upper East and Northern Regions of Ghana. The complementarity of the mixed methods approaches used strengthens the evidence. This means that the quantitative data revealed demonstrable patterns of climate-induced mobility and immobility, whilst the qualitative data gave in-depth insights into the socio-cultural and environmental elements that underpin these trends. Qualitatively, a descriptive phenomenological design was employed. The quantitative design is grounded on an analytical cross-sectional survey of all persons 15 years and above who lived in the selected communities and met the eligibility criteria. This quantitative design was chosen because it enables for an examination of present patterns, drivers, and implications of climate-induced mobility across a set timeframe. This was necessary for establishing baseline data in under-researched areas. The study included all persons 15 years and above who had lived in the communities for a minimum of 24 months. Individuals who had witnessed at least two seasons where the Bagre dam spillage had taken place as well as documented drought exposure, defined as residence during ≥1 officially recognized drought year (district agricultural / meteorological records) and recall of a specific drought impact in parts of the study districts were eligible for enrollment.

Population and sample size estimation

The sample size was arrived at based on the following assumptions: an assumption that at least 50 % of all women, children, and men in the 2 districts may have experienced at least one form of flood, drought extreme heat, and displacement with recurrent adaptations. Sampling procedure

A multi-stage sampling technique was adopted. In both the Upper East and Northern regions, one district was purposefully chosen based on predetermined climate exposure criteria (the documented occurrence of at least one high heat event, flood, drought, or windstorm during the previous five years, as reported by the Ghana Meteorological Agency and NADMO). Six communities were selected from each district to maintain a 2:4 urban-rural ratio (district capital to rural town). The district capital (or its immediate urban enclave) was automatically included, while the remaining four communities were sampled from a list of eligible rural settlements ranked by reported climate stress exposure. A household listing was carried out in both the Savelugu and Talensi districts to ascertain the total number of households. The total number of households was 73,653 in Savelugu and 17,729 in Talensi, making a total of 91,382.

The overall sample size was 2125 households, which were distributed proportionally across the two districts depending on the number of households. The selection began by randomly selecting the third household as our starting point using a mobile random number generator. From there, the data collection team worked through the neighborhood, selecting the third, ninth, and fifteenth households, and so on until we reached our required number of sample size. If a selected household was unavailable after two revisits, the next eligible household was used as a replacement. A total of 709 households were proportionally allocated between Savelugu (571 households; 80.6 %) and Talensi (138 households; 19.4 %) based on their share of the district household frame (73,653 and 17,729 respectively). Sampling three eligible respondents per household yielded an expected total of approximately 2125 participants.

Key informant interviews

In respect of the qualitative key informant interviews, qualitative data were collected guided by data saturation. A semi-structured interview guide was developed based on existing literature on climate mobility/immobility, social vulnerability, and health. The interview guide covered three thematic domains: (1) lived experiences of climate stressors (flood, drought, heat, windstorm); (2) duration and frequency of displacement; and (3) seasonal predictability of displacement. Six people from a demographically similar but non-sample community were chosen to pilot the drafted interview guide. Based on their feedback, the guide was revised to (a) simplify technical terms, and (b) combine overlapping probes on relocation constraints. Trained multilingual researchers performed 45–70-minute interviews in English or local languages, which were audio-recorded with consent. Field notes document nonverbal cues and ecological observations. Persons interviewed to elicit their perspectives on the incidence of climate-induced immobility and climate migration in the communities were Assembly members (2 each from the two districts −4 in total), farmer group leaders (4 two each, community chiefs (4 two each, identified NGO leader (1 each), religious leaders (2- one each, educational heads (6-three each).

Data collection process

The survey was conducted in April 2024 in the two study sites. A total of seven research assistants and six enumerators provided by the National Disaster Management Organizations (NADMO) were organized into teams to carry out the data collection. All team members in the two study sites attended a workshop lasting for a day before the fieldwork, which covered the study objective, ethical issues, informed consent protocols, and the administration of the survey instrument. The nature and goal of the research were also explained to and discussed with district assembly leaders in both study locations in order to get their support and guarantee alignment with community priorities. Despite not being directly involved in the study design, the official representatives of the target groups provided their insights during initial consultations, which were then incorporated into the qualitative interviews.

Contacts were established with the representatives of the National Disaster Management Organizations (NADMO) in the study sites to discussion the concurrent climate included disaster is the district, with a presentation made to them about the purpose of the study. An interview guide was developed and used to facilitate the interview sessions. In each district, two qualitative data collectors supported the data collection bringing the qualitative data collectors to four. In addition, three residents each were interviewed at random from each of the districts through random recruitment of participants from any of the six communities within each district. The sampling of participants was identified based on their relevance to the research questions, availability, and willingness to participate, making the approach within purposive sampling technique.

The qualitative data collected had support from research assistants who were language transcribers and supported in transcribing the language into English which was transcribed immediately after the collection. The field coordinator offered continuous supervision to resolve issues and uphold quality during the survey time.

The questionnaire was designed after an extensive review of literature. Before the main data collection, the questionnaire was pre-tested with a community with similar characteristics to the study sites to ensure that the questions were clear, culturally appropriate, and relevant. Minor changes were made based on feedback, such as rewording ambiguous questions and changing response alternatives to ensure better comprehension. These improvements improved the instrument's validity and reliability.

Measurement

Dependent and independent variables

The study had two main dependent variables which included (1) climate-induced migration experience and (2) climate immobility. Climate-induced migration is measured as a categorical variable on respondents’ verdict of their movement from their original place of abode in the past six to twelve months preceding exposure to climatic situations such as floods, extreme heat, windstorms, and droughts. Climate immobility was measured at the categorical level on respondents' expressed desire to migrate but were unable to execute migration intent and desire despite exposure to climatic conditions of rainstorms, floods, heat waves, or drought. In this study, the key exposures were climate-related stressors, which have been found to influence migration and immobility decisions. Drought frequency floods, extreme heat, and windstorms were all measured at the household or community level using self-reports. The independent variables which were adopted through the extensive review of literature. This includes, gender, age, educational level, district of participants, health conditions of participants, household wealth quintile and participants experience with climate induced displacement. These variables were measured at the categorical level.

A researcher- administered questionnaire was developed after an extensive review of literature on climate variability, environmentally driven migration, and displacement impacts in low‑resource settings (Bauer and Zimmermann, 2018; Achieng et al., 2020; Prosper and Khan, 2018; Carling and Collins, 2020). The questionnaire was structured into three sections. The first section measured the sociodemographic characteristics of participants. The second section measured the prevalence of climate-induced migration among community residents in the Savelugu and Talensi districts, and the last section sought to solicit information on the experience of individuals with climate-induced displacement. A multidisciplinary expert panel in public health, sociology, climate adaptation, and local community development assessed the questionnaire to ensure it was complete, culturally relevant, and well-written. The questionnaire was pretested with a small sampled population demographically and culturally similar to the study sites to test for clarity, reliability and cultural appropriateness before the main study.

Data analysis

Real-time data was collected using the ODK data capture platform after the questionnaire was scripted and uploaded. Descriptive statistics have been performed for all variables and expressed as frequencies and percentages using tables using SPSS. Multiple regression models were employed to investigate the predictors of climate migration and climate immobility(outcomes). The independent (predictor) factors were respondents' sociodemographic including age, income, educational level, community residence, occupation, ethnicity, household wealth quintiles and their experiences with climate-induced displacement. The adjusted models were used, and statistical significance was determined at p < 0.05. In terms of the qualitative analysis, comparative thematic analysis forms the basis of the qualitative data analysis and is supported using NVIVO software. The original interviewer transcribed the recordings verbatim and, if applicable, translated them into English, after which a second team member independently examined them for semantic fidelity. The transcription quality protocol comprised double-listening verification for 20 % of transcripts, a structured error log, and 10 % back-checking by an external reviewer who was not involved in the initial transcription. An initial deductive code frame was paired with inductive, with emergent codes generated by open coding an initial subset of the transcripts.

Intercoder agreement was examined at the parent code level, and it met the significant agreement criteria. Discrepancies were addressed during adjudication meetings, and the codebook was revised (definitions, inclusion/exclusion criteria, judgement rules). The modified codebook was then applied to the entire dataset, with frequent reliability spot checks (every ten transcripts) to verify no drift occurred. Axial coding organized linked subcodes into higher-order categories, facilitating thematic synthesis. Key themes are identified and compared. Because a concurrent mixed-method design was employed, data integration happened during interpretation. Triangulation was used to evaluate findings, emphasize convergence, and identify complementary ideas by comparing patterns from quantitative results to qualitative themes.

Ethics and data protection

This study was conducted in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki. Ethical clearance for the study was sought from the ethical review board of the Kwame Nkrumah University of Science and Technology ((HuSSREC/ AP/102/VOL.3). Before data collection commenced, residents provided both verbal and written informed consent. All respondents were assured of their confidentiality and freedom to withdraw from participation without any restrictions. To ensure anonymity, the names, contact numbers, and sensitive information about respondents were not solicited. All mobile data collected was encrypted at the point of entry and transferred to a password-protected server with limited access in order to protect the data. Responses were labelled using a unique code system, and raw data files were saved to secure, encrypted cloud storage. Access to sensitive data based on roles and permissions.

Discussion

This study investigates the complicated link between climate change and human mobility in Ghana, focusing on climate-induced migration and climate immobility. Using a socio-ecological mixed-method approach. In the present study, two in ten respondents have experienced migration 462(21.7 %) due to weather, rain, floods, dam spillage, or drought in their present location. This indicates a high prevalence of climate-induced displacement among Upper East and Northern Ghana families. The proximity of the regions to the White and Black Volta rivers raises the risk of floods and dam spills, displacing communities that live near riverbanks and in low-lying areas. This reflects the SES framework's statement that migration is a result of the interplay between ecological shocks and the socioeconomic structures in which individuals and groups operate. This confirms the report of an earlier study in Nepal (Amenga-Etego, 2011) and the USA (Antabe et al., 2020), where rapid occurrences and long-term climatic changes have caused communities to migrate, with populations using migration to adapt to climatic regimes and climate disasters. Consistent with previous studies, the evidence from this study shares a similar trajectory with previous reports elsewhere in Africa where millions of people were reported to have been displaced due to climate change in 2011 (Awumbila et al., 2014) and 2020 (Bauer and Zimmermann, 2018). Although this finding is the first primary data driven study to report on the effect of climate change on migration and immobility in Ghana with estimates levels considered high where one in 5 of the affected population remains a victim, the study finding is lower compared to estimates reported in five countries with the highest figures in descending order being: Pakistan, the Philippines, China, India, and Nigeria. In all such instances, flooding was the most prevalent cause of displacement and migration with floods displacing 6 out of 10 displaced persons (Awumbila et al., 2014). In this study, four in ten of the individuals 1060 (49.9 %) had no intentions to relocate from their present location notwithstanding climate change conditions. Individual's reactions to environmental stresses are mediated by the resources, restrictions, and opportunities available at different levels, as explained the SES theory. The present results show that the adults aged 36–45 years, 46–55 years and those above 55 years were unwilling to migrate, remaining immobile compared to the more fluid younger generation except but for students who despite being young were less likely not to migrate amid climate change obviously to be able to resettle in safe environments/locations to continue their education. The reasons for the higher and nearly twofold odds of climate immobility among the adult resident may be explained from their strong connection to ancestral homes, community, and networks, making relocation undesirable (Nabong et al., 2023; Yaro, 2013). Younger persons may not have the financial means to migrate as well as the network to migrate to and stay beyond their surroundings.

This is corroborated by previous research, which found that many people opt to stay due to place connection and cultural motives (Yakubu, 2017; Dzodzomenyo et al., 2022). Similarly, evidence from rural Ethiopia (Farbotko and McMichael, 2019) and Bangladesh (Schewel, 2020) indicated migration was just a coping option available to some, their last alternative, while others stayed in situ because of conflict or poverty or because disasters reduced financial means with which to travel. Supported by a study in Senegal by while the observation has been made that older individuals had no desire to leave their village to other regions despite exposure to climate conditions, with reasons of being born and growing up in the district and had no reason to leave, the case may not always be explained by ancestral ties but by economic reasons and ability to do (Wiegel et al., 2019). Immobility is, therefore, a means for people to define a future that is historically, culturally, and spiritually valuable. Consistent with the study's findings, (Nabong et al., 2023) it was stated that immobility challenges revolve around varying degrees of susceptibility and resilience, with individuals who have established a high level of resistance being less inclined to migrate despite being exposed to climatic conditions in Asia. (Thornton et al., 2023) in the Pacific Islands argue that voluntary immobility contributes to self-determination in the face of imminent territory loss due to global warming by emphasizing the need for cultural and spiritual resilience.

Individuals with no formal education or who had attained primary level education have reduced odds of expressing the desire to migrate irrespective of climate change exposure compared to those with a JHS level of education. Many individuals with little to no schooling rely on traditional professions related to their local environment, such as farming or fishing, deterring them from moving to other locations. This is explained from the perspective that such persons might not be qualified or have the necessary abilities to find employment in other places, which would deter migration. This finding aligns with (Azumah and Ahmed, 2023), who discovered that education had a greater effect than religion or culture on people's perceptions of climate change and migration.

The study shows evidence that residents' occupations were connected to climate immobility, as those who were students were much less likely to express the intention to relocate regardless of climatic change circumstances than those who were government workers. This contradicts an earlier study, which suggested that those who are not employed in the core agricultural/aquacultural industries of rural communities are much more likely to migrate as in the case in Bangladesh (Abu et al., 2014), and with similar findings that argue that people engage in agricultural employment have more transferable assets and weaker rural ties, allowing them to move more freely (Folke et al., 2003). In our case, the government workers who have been transferred to these communities and possess more transferrable assets are highly likely to migrate due to climate change. Similar to the observation by (Thornton et al., 2023) that resources are essential to all types of climatic immobility, whether shelter or cultural and traditional preservation. Resources, however, will be difficult to come by, not least because climatic immobility competes for resources with mobility but also for larger adaptation and mitigation considerations. Our findings support a study in Cape Verde, where they developed the aspirations-ability framework, which demonstrated how poverty in a migratory culture hampered people's capacity to leave Cape Verde despite their ambitions (Cioffi-Revilla, 2016).

Persons who have experienced weather-induced displacement and had rarely sought community help had a lower likelihood of migrating than those who have not experienced weather-induced displacement and ever seek community support regularly. Those who do not seek community support may have developed coping strategies to withstand environmental challenges independently. The finding that displaced people who rarely sought community assistance were less likely to migrate is consistent with SES theory, which holds that human migration is influenced not just by environmental factors but also by the interaction of ecological stressors and social support networks. Community support serves as a socio-ecological buffer, allowing households to mobilize the resources required for migration or adaption. More so, individuals who have been exposed to climate displacement may have developed resilience to the shock of climate change. Another important observation is the realization that some people may have developed resistance and become used to the occurrence of such disasters and have become adaptive to their ramifications. Since such persons rarely seek support, those who seek often may be more likely to migrate because their social support network may become fed up with them, hence, the use of migration as an adaptive strategy. Similar trends were observed in earlier studies, which reported that immobility after exposure to weather conditions is considered an adaptive response (Blocher et al., 2021; Yaro, 2013) and an expression of resilience (Thornton et al., 2023). In terms of explaining mechanisms for the aforementioned finding, (May, 2022) argue that a lack of financial capital is a barrier to migration: movement has a (financial) cost, which many of the poorest individuals cannot afford. The findings show that inhabitants whose family income was high were less likely to relocate than those in the first quintile. Previous research has suggested that the poor are more vulnerable to environmental dangers and more prone to move (Abu et al., 2014; GSS, 2021; Abass et al., 2022). Contrary to the above findings, (Schewel, 2020) reported that although those with fewer resources may be less likely to migrate, there does seem to be some merit to the “last resort” thesis that the poor are distinctively affected in their migration decisions by environmental stressors. In the present study, however, the fourth wealth quintile is suggestive of well-to-do persons who have the economic and financial capacity to migrate, a finding consistent with the existing literature on resource utilization on migration.

Members of other ethnic groups compared to the Dagomba ethnic group, a predominant ethnic group in Northern Ghana, had higher odds of migration. The other ethnic group may not consider the study setting as their ancestral home, so they may migrate to their hometown, as compared to the Dagombas who originally had the Savelugu district as their home district. or bound to their home by obligations (Yakubu, 2017) gave three reasons for non-migration: high levels of satisfaction, resource barriers, and low mobility potential. Two of the three constructs have been confirmed in this study, except for the satisfaction level which has not been examined in our study. Residents whose dwellings were built using mud bricks were less likely to migrate than those who were built with cement. This is supported by previous research by (Faure, 2003), in Burkina Faso (Gray and Mueller, 2012), and Zambia (Adger et al., 2018), who found that residents in climate change areas prefer to build with mudbricks, which have no structural stability during flooding, as an adaptive capacity because it is less expensive to build than cement.

Notably, we found that individuals who have experienced climate-induced displacement due to weather and have rarely sought support from the community are more likely to migrate. According to (Mallick and Vogt, 2014), government and non-governmental organization (NGOs) services are extremely limited for displaced people, and the majority of them live on khash land (government-owned fallow land to which no one has property rights), where further displacement could jeopardize their livelihood. (Mallick and Vogt, 2014) highlighted that tragedy maximizes 'exclusion' for the original group while increasing 'inclusion' demands in the new community. According to an earlier study, the Ghanaian government launched a modest resettlement effort, relocating a small number of people affected by coastal erosion in Ghana's Volta region (McLeman and Brown, 2011). Instead of supporting the afflicted people after the flood, the government's efforts mostly focus on giving early warning indicators before the flood.

Individuals whose present health condition was poorer than one year ago had a greater chance of moving than those with better health status. Consistent with earlier findings most of the impacts of climate change on physical, ecological, and social systems affect human health via changes in food yields, freshwater flows and quality, stability of infectious disease patterns, air quality, social cohesion, and family income and livelihoods [(Gray and Mueller, 2012; IDMC – Global Report on Internal Displacement (GRID), 2023; Mallick and Rahman, 2020)], increase the tendency of people to migrate to different places to avoid death and hunger.

Conclusion

This paper is the largest primary data-driven in Ghana to explore by way of using primary data from a larger sample size to understand the direct effect of different climate change conditions measured from the perspective of the people themselves on their migration patterns and immobility experience. The findings revealed that 21.7 % of respondents reported experiencing migration due to weather-related events such as floods, dam spillage, or drought, while 49.9 % expressed no intention to relocate despite these environmental challenges. These patterns indicate that while some individuals view migration as an adaptive strategy, many remain immobile due to cultural attachment to land, limited resources, or perceived community resilience. The findings reveal a complex mix of resilience, and climate adaptation strategies such as migration, and environmental accommodation, which refers to the practical steps respondents take to adapt to climate stressors without moving from their communities. However, climate immobility can also be seen through sociological and cultural perspectives, such as a strong sense of belonging and what participants called "ancestral-homesickness," rather than only a resource-deficit perspective. This expresses the deep feelings and spiritual ties that a person has to their family’s country of origin and cultural heritage. According to the respondents, leaving their villages would entail losing important social links and "abandoning the graves of our forefathers." Despite the dangers posed by climate events, these cultural ties frequently played a role in the decision to remain. While the narrative has always been on flooding and the role of the Bagre dam spillage in shaping the climate change debates and displacement incidents in the northern regions of Ghana, the realities of extreme heat, windstorms, and drought intersperse potently to cause climate immobility and climate-induced migration. Prioritizing the quality-of-life programs, risk and climatic change impact minimization interventions within their natural environment hold the potential to bring about meaningful livelihood and quality-life outcomes among the most vulnerable persons affected in resource-limited environments. There is the need to Improve the Ghana Meteorological Agency's local forecasting and work with NADMO (National Disaster Management Organization) to create community-level early warning systems for dam spills and extreme weather. The ministry of health must expand the use of mobile health clinics through Ghana Health Service outreach programs, particularly in hard-to-reach communities affected by Bagre Dam flooding, to address climate-related health risks such as cholera and malaria in the Savalugu and Talensi district.

Limitation and future research

This cross-sectional study is based on self-reported migration history and intentions, which may be influenced by recall and social desirability bias. The study focuses on 12 rural and peri‑urban communities in Talensi (Upper East) and Savelugu (Northern Region), limiting its applicability to other Ghanaian ecological zones or urban centers. Furthermore, "intention to remain" may not convert into actual immobility over time, especially in areas affected by recurring risks like Bagre Dam spillages. Future research should use longitudinal or seasonal panel designs to track shifts in mobility and immobility, incorporate objective environmental exposure data (e.g., flood mapping, rainfall anomalies), and analyze intra-household dynamics, including children, older adults, and resource-constrained "trapped" populations, as part of the socio-ecological system shaping climate (im)mobility.
