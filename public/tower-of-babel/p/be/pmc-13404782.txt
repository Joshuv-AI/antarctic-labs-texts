Functional Role of Piezo1 in Regulating Whole‐Lung Vascular Reactivity
Authors: Sophia Parmisano, Jiyuan Chen, Yuqin Chen, Pritesh P Jain, Lauren Yung, Wei‐Ting Wang, Ting Wang, Xiang Li, Angela Balistrieri, Sophia A Ferrizzi, Cassia Pedron, Owen Brooks, Mona Alotaibi, Jian Wang, Joe GN Garcia, Patricia A Thistlethwaite, Ayako Makino, Jason X‐J Yuan
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTMechanosensitive cation channels expressed in pulmonary arterial smooth muscle (SMC) and endothelial (EC) cells contribute to the regulation of vasoconstriction and vasodilation of the pulmonary vasculature. Here, we report that activating Piezo1, a mechanosensitive cation channel, with Yoda1 induced a mild increase in pulmonary arterial pressure (PAP) via vasoconstriction in the intact pulmonary vascular system. Inhibition of nitric oxide (NO) synthase (NOS) with l‐NAME significantly potentiated Yoda1‐mediated pulmonary vasoconstriction. The Yoda1‐mediated vasoconstriction was inhibited by Gd3+, a non‐selective blocker of cation channels, but not affected by nifedipine, a dihydropyridine blocker of voltage‐dependent Ca2+ channels (VDCC). Alveolar hypoxia alone increased PAP via hypoxic pulmonary vasoconstriction (HPV); Yoda1‐mediated pulmonary vasoconstriction overlaps with HPV in the isolated and perfused/ventilated lung. On top of the Yoda1‐mediated increase in PAP, intrapulmonary perfusion of high K+ (25 mM) solution or phenylephrine (PE, 30 µM) induced an additional increase in PAP. These data indicate that, under normal conditions, the activation of Piezo1 (by Yoda1) caused pulmonary vasodilation due to Ca2+‐associated activation of NOS in ECs and vasoconstriction due to Ca2+ influx in SMCs. In conclusion, Piezo1 or Ca2+ influx through Piezo1 in ECs and SMCs functions differently in the regulation of pulmonary vasculature. When the endothelium‐derived relaxing factor (e.g., NO) is decreased due to EC injury and/or inflammation, Piezo1 or Ca2+ influx through Piezo1 channels is an important trigger for pulmonary vasoconstriction in the whole‐lung vascular system.Keywords: calcium ion, endothelium‐dependent vasodilation, mechanosensitive cation channel, pulmonary vasoconstriction, Yoda1

Excitation‐contraction (E‐C) coupling in pulmonary vascular smooth muscle plays an important role in regulating tonic and sustained pulmonary vasoconstriction and endothelium‐dependent pulmonary vasodilation. Classical E‐C coupling involves the membrane depolarization‐mediated smooth muscle contraction; this voltage‐dependent pulmonary vasoconstriction is mainly due to the opening of VDCC and Ca2+ influx through VDCC [1, 2, 3]. For the lung vasculature, neural electrical signals and/or neurotransmitters, humoral factors/agonists, and mechanical force (e.g., membrane stretch) are all excitatory stimuli that cause pulmonary vasoconstriction. Indeed, pulmonary arterial denervation is reported to have therapeutic potential for pulmonary arterial hypertension (PAH) [4, 5, 6, 7]. In addition, receptor‐operated opening of voltage‐independent cation channels, such as TRPC6 [8, 9, 10], and mechanical stimulation‐induced opening of mechanosensitive cation channels, such as Piezo1 [11, 12, 13], are involved in controlling pulmonary vascular function and structure via regulating Ca2+ signaling in various pulmonary vascular cells.

An increase in cytosolic Ca2+ concentration ([Ca2+]cyt) in pulmonary artery smooth muscle cells (PASMCs) triggers PASMC contraction and pulmonary vasoconstriction. A rise in [Ca2+]cyt in pulmonary artery endothelial cells (PAECs), however, activates eNOS to produce NO and subsequently induces endothelium‐dependent vasodilation. Therefore, Ca2+ signaling in PASMCs and PAECs plays an important role in regulating pulmonary vascular tone. The ultimate effect of a rise in [Ca2+]cyt due to Ca2+ influx in PASMCs on pulmonary vascular tone may be different from an increase in [Ca2+]cyt due to Ca2+ influx in PAECs. Multiple Ca2+‐permeable cation channels are expressed in PASMCs and PAECs and regulate Ca2+ signaling and homeostasis, and thus pulmonary vascular tone [3, 14]. In addition to VDCC (e.g., l‐type and T‐type), receptor‐operated Ca2+ channels (ROCC, e.g., TRP channels) and store‐operated Ca2+ channels (SOCC, e.g., STIM/Orai) in PASMCs and PAECs, the mechanosensitive cation channels (MSCC, e.g., Piezo1) and mechanosensitive signaling cascades have been demonstrated to contribute to the regulation of pulmonary vascular function and structure [15, 16, 17].

The lung vasculature is constantly subjected to mechanical stimuli, or stretch, due to respiration‐associated vascular retraction and right ventricular stroke‐mediated vessel extension. Piezo1 is a mechanosensitive cation channel expressed in systemic and pulmonary vascular SMCs and ECs [13, 18, 19]. It has been reported that Piezo1 channels are involved in stretch‐mediated pulmonary vasodilation using isolated vessel preparations [20]. Given the divergent or opposite effect of Ca2+ influx through Piezo1 channels in PASMCs (which presumably causes PASMC contraction and pulmonary vasoconstriction) and PAECs (which likely causes eNOS activation and NO production, and pulmonary vasodilation), it is important to understand the integrative role of Piezo1 channels in the regulation of pulmonary vascular function. The isolated and perfused/ventilated mouse lung is a well‐established model to study pulmonary vascular reactivity of the whole‐lung vasculature. Changes of pulmonary arterial pressure (PAP) in the isolated and perfused/ventilated lung reflect an integrative balance of vasoconstriction and vasodilation in the large, medium‐sized and small arteries as well as the peripheral arterioles. In this study, we aimed to examine the effect of Ca2+ influx through Piezo1 channels in PASMCs and PAECs on vascular reactivity in the whole‐lung vasculature and investigate whether Piezo1, or Ca2+ influx through Piezo1 channels, is involved in alveolar hypoxia‐induced pulmonary vasoconstriction using the isolated and perfused/ventilated mouse lungs. Our study is among the first to describe the functional role of Piezo1 in regulating whole‐lung vascular reactivity or vasoconstriction and vasodilation. Given the dramatic distribution differences of Piezo1 across different lung cells, it is important to understand how the whole‐lung vasculature responds to stimuli that open Piezo1, a mechanosensitive non‐selective cation channel.

Materials and Methods

Ex Vivo Isolated Perfused/Ventilated Mouse Lung Experiment

The animal experimental protocol was approved by the Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee (IACUC) of the University of California, San Diego (UCSD) and the University of Florida (UF) Scripps Institute. C57BL/6 mice (8‐10 weeks old, male, approximately 25‐gram body weight) were used in this study. First, the mice were anesthetized with pentobarbital sodium (Euthanasia solution, Virbac, TX, USA) via intraperitoneal injection. The mice were placed in an open perfusion system chamber for an isolated lung (IL‐1 Type 839, Harvard Apparatus, USA) with a heated water jacket at 37°C. Then, a tracheostomy was performed, and the lungs were immediately ventilated with normoxic gas (21% O2/5% CO2) using a mouse ventilator (Model 845, volume controlled, Harvard Apparatus, USA). The respiration rate was adjusted to 80 breaths/min with a tidal volume of 250 µl. A differential pressure transducer (Type 399/2, Hugo Sachs Elektronik, Harvard Apparatus, Germany) was connected to the tracheal catheter to measure the end inspiratory pressure, while the positive end expiratory pressure was adjusted at 2 cmH2O. After tracheal intubation, the chest was opened along the middle of the sternotomy, and adipose and thymus tissue were carefully removed. 20‐IU heparin (400‐30, SAGENT Pharmaceuticals, IL, USA) was injected into the RV to prevent blood from clotting. A catheter was inserted into the main PA via the RV. A 6‐0 black silk suture was used to ligate the catheter with the ascending PA and aorta. PAP in the isolated, perfused/ventilated lung was continuously measured by the PA catheter, which was connected to a pressure sensor (Type 379, Hugo Sachs Elektronik, Harvard Apparatus, Germany). Another catheter was inserted into the left ventricle (LV) to allow the solution to flow out of the lung. The solution flow rate was maintained at 1 ml/min using a peristaltic pump (ISM 834 C, ISOMATEC, USA). The PowerLab data acquisition system (AD Instruments, CO, USA) and LabChart software were used to record and analyze the data. The pulmonary vessels were constantly perfused with the physiological salt solution (PSS). 40 mM K+‐PSS (40 K) was perfused into the isolated lungs three times to stabilize the basal PAP and the amplitudes of the 40K‐induced increases in PAP before experimentation. To elicit alveolar hypoxia‐induced increase of PAP, hypoxic gas (1% O2 in N2) was used for ventilation via the mini ventilator. The pharmacological effects of various drugs were examined by intrapulmonary perfusion with PSS containing each drug. PSS or saline was occasionally applied to the isolated lungs to maintain lung tissue moisture.

Solutions and Chemicals

The composition of physiological salt solution (PSS) (intrapulmonary perfusate) consisted of 120 mM NaCl, 4.3 mM KCl, 1.8 mM CaCl2, 1.2 mM MgCl2, 19 mM NaHCO3, 1.1 mM KH2PO4, 10 mM glucose 10, and 20% fetal bovine serum (pH 7.4). High‐K+ (e.g., 40‐mM K+) solution was prepared by replacing NaCl with equimolar KCl (e.g., 40 mM). A Ca2+‐free (0Ca) solution was prepared by replacing CaCl2 with equimolar MgCl2; 1 mM EGTA was added to remove or chelate the residual Ca2+ in the Ca2+‐free solution.

The following drugs were used in this study: 2‐[5‐[[(2,6‐Dichlorophenyl)methyl]thio]‐1,3,4‐thiadiazol‐2‐yl]‐pyrazine or Yoda 1 (30 µM, Cat. #: SML1558, Sigma), a specific allosteric activator of the mechanosensitive Piezo1 channel [21]; L‐NG‐nitroarginine methyl ester (l‐NAME, 300 µM, Cat. #: 80210, Cayman), a non‐selective inhibitor of nitric oxide synthase (NOS) [22]; ogerin (50 µM, Cat. #: HY‐110279, MedChemExpress LLC, Monouth Junction, NJ 08852, USA), a small‐molecule allosteric activator of GPR68, Gd3+ (gadolinium, 30 µM, Cat. #: 4741, Tocris), a non‐selective blocker of stretch‐activated cation channels [23]; 9,11‐Dideoxy‐9a,11a‐methanoepoxy prostaglandin F2α or U46619 (0.1 µM, Cat. #:16450, Cayman), an analog of thromboxane A2 (TXA2) and a selective agonist of prostaglandin H2 (PGH2)/TXA2 TP receptors [24]; phenylephrine (PE, 1 µM, Cat. #: P‐6126, Sigma), an agonist of α‐adrenergic receptors; acetylcholine (ACh, 10 µM, Cat. #: A6625, Sigma), an agonist of muscarinic cholinergic receptor that releases EDRF [25]; nifedipine (Nif, 0.1 µM, Cat. #: N7634, Sigma), a dihydropyridine blocker of voltage‐dependent Ca2+ channels; and GsMTx4 (10 µM, Cat. #: HY‐P1410A, MedChemExpress), a blocker of mechanosensitive cation channels including Piezo [26] and TRPC1/6 [27] channels.

Ex Vivo Lung Angiogram

Pulmonary angiogram was utilized to show the whole‐lung vasculature [28, 29]. Heparin was first injected into the RV to prevent coagulation, and PBS was perfused through the main PA to wash out residual blood within the pulmonary vasculature. Then, the casting polymer (MV‐122, Flow‐Tech Inc., Carver, MA) was perfused into the PA using a pump (Farmingdale, New York, USA) at 0.05 ml/min for 1‐2 min until the casting polymer was infiltrated into all branches of the pulmonary vessels. The casting polymer‐filled lungs were stored at 4°C overnight and then dehydrated by immersion in ethanol to reveal the vascular structure. The dehydrated lungs were then submerged in methyl salicylate solution (Sigma, Cat. #: M6752). 12 h later, the lung was photographed with a digital camera through a microscope.

Statistical Analysis

The data are shown as means ± standard error (SE). Paired or unpaired Student's t‐tests for two‐group comparison and one‐way analyses of variance (ANOVA) with multiple comparison tests for multiple group comparison were used for statistical analysis. p‐value < 0.05 was considered statistically significant. Significant differences are indicated in the figures or figure legends as *p < 0.05, **p < 0.01, and ***p < 0.001, as well as # p < 0.05, ## p < 0.01, and ### p < 0.001.

Results

The pulmonary vasculature is characterized by numerous arterial branches and bifurcations (or junctions). Based on the 5 sets of experiments (each set includes 5‐6 mice), the estimated numbers of vascular branches and junctions in the peripheral area of the mouse lungs are 542.0 ± 52.6 mm−2 and 271.0 ± 23.6 mm−2, respectively, with a total length of branches at 23.2 ± 1.0 mm/mm2 (Figure 1A,B). Shear stress from the large number of pulmonary bifurcations and branches, circumferential stretch due to periodic right ventricular (RV) contraction (or RV stroke), and longitudinal stretch due to breathing are the constant mechanical stimulations for the pulmonary vasculature. Therefore, mechanosensitive pulmonary vasodilation is an important mechanism that helps maintain low pressure and resistance in the pulmonary circulation system under normal conditions (or in healthy subjects).

Piezo1 Is Expressed in Lung Endothelial and Smooth Muscle Cells

In this study, we used an isolated, perfused/ventilated mouse lung model to examine the functional role of Piezo1 channels in regulating pulmonary vascular tone. The preparation of isolated, perfused/ventilated lungs includes all lobes of the mouse lungs: left lung (one single lobe) and right lung consisting of the superior lobe, middle lobe, inferior lobe, and post‐caval lobe (Figure 1A,B). The change in PAP measured during the experiment reflects the balance between vasoconstriction and vasodilation across all vascular beds in the whole lungs (Figure 1A,B). Piezo1, a mechanosensitive cation channel, is involved in the regulation of vascular development, blood pressure, lung development, and lung injury [13, 30, 31]. Based on the analysis of the data from the web interfaces of LungMap CellRefs [32], a dictionary of single‐cell transcriptomic datasets from 40 normal mouse lung samples, Piezo1 is expressed in all lung cells, including endothelial cells, epithelial cells, mesenchymal cells and immune cells (Figure 1C), but is highly distributed in lung ECs, including artery EC (AEC), venous EC (VEC), and capillary EC (CAP‐EC) in comparison to lung pericytes and vascular SMCs (VSMC) (Figure 1D–F). As expected, all ECs (AEC, VEC and CAP EC) highly expressed eNOS encoded by the Nos3 gene (Figure 1D) and thromboxane receptor encoded by the Tbxa2r gene (Figure 1D). In addition, lung ECs also expressed Piezo2 and Orai1 (a store‐operated cation channel) in high levels (Figure 1D–F). In lung vascular SMC and pericytes, TRPC6 (a mechanosensitive receptor‐operated cation channel) and Tbxa2r are highly expressed in comparison to their expression level in lung ECs (Figure 1D–F). The ratios of the AEC expression level to the VSMC expression level (AEC/VSMC), calculated by the data shown in the LungMap CellRefs, are 5.09, 3.04, and 8.92 for Piezo1, Piezo2, and Orzi1, respectively. In contrast, the ratio of AEC/VSMC for TBXA2R and TRPC6 is 0.34 and 0.03 (Figure 1F). GPR68, a mechanosensitive G protein‐coupled receptor [33, 34, 35, 36], is also expressed in lung cells, but the expression level seems to be low in comparison to the mechanosensitive cation channels Piezo1/2 and store‐operated cation channel Orai1 (Figure 1D,E). The ratio of AEC/VSMC for GPR68 is, however, 2.00 (Figure 1F), indicating a higher level of distribution of GPR68 in lung ECs than in pulmonary vascular smooth muscle cells. These results indicate that Piezo1, along with eNOS (or Nos3), is highly expressed in AECs compared to VSMCs in the lungs.

Activation of Piezo1 Causes Pulmonary Vasoconstriction in the Isolated Lung

Yoda1 is an allosteric agonist of the Piezo1 channel that binds to the mechanosensory domain near the central pore and functions as a wedge to facilitate force‐induced conformational changes and channel activation [21, 37]. In the isolated and perfused/ventilated mouse lungs, intrapulmonary perfusion of Yoda1 (30 µM) rapidly and reversibly increased pulmonary arterial pressure (PAP) by causing pulmonary vasoconstriction (Figure 2A). Acute application of 300 µM l‐NAME, an inhibitor of endothelial nitric oxide (NO) synthase (eNOS), resulted in a slight increase in the baseline PAP, but significantly enhanced Yoda1‐mediated increase in PAP (Figure 2B,C). Inhibition of eNOS or reduction of endothelium‐derived NO by l‐NAME enhanced Yoda1‐mediated pulmonary vasoconstriction by almost three times (Figure 2B,C). Removal of extracellular Ca2+ almost abolished Yoda1‐mediated increases in PAP or Yoda1‐induced pulmonary vasoconstriction in the absence or presence of l‐NAME (Figure 2B). These data indicate that Piezo1 contributes to causing pulmonary vasoconstriction and vasodilation. Ca2+ influx through Piezo1 channels in PASMCs causes PASMC contraction and pulmonary vasoconstriction, whereas the opening of Piezo1 in PAECs results in pulmonary vasodilation by Ca2+ influx‐mediated activation of eNOS and increases in synthesis and release of NO. In the intact PA or the pulmonary vasculature, the endothelial Piezo1‐mediated vasodilation and smooth muscle Piezo1‐mediated vasoconstriction functionally interact with each other to regulate pulmonary vascular tone. Indeed, inhibition of eNOS with l‐NAME, a non‐selective inhibitor of eNOS, resulted in a 3.30 ± 0.06 (n = 15) times enhancement of Yoda1‐mediated increase in PAP (Figure 2C). These data also imply that endothelial dysfunction or damage would significantly enhance acute vasoconstriction induced by activation of mechanosensitive and receptor‐operated cation channels in the pulmonary vasculature.

Blockade of Piezo1 With Gd3+ Inhibits Yoda1‐mediated Pulmonary Vasoconstriction

So far, no specific blocker of Piezo1 has been identified, whereas Gd3+, ruthenium red, and GsMTx4 [26, 38, 39] have been shown to inhibit Piezo1 channels [38]. Pretreatment of the lung vasculature by intrapulmonary perfusion of Gd3+ (30 µM) significantly and reversibly inhibited Yoda1‐mediated increases in PAP (Figure 3A). In the presence of l‐NAME, pretreatment with the same dose of Gd3+ (30 µM) resulted in the same inhibitory effect on Yoda1‐mediated increases in PAP (Figure 3A, lower panels). Although the amplitude of Yoda1‐mediated increases in PAP varied, the Gd3+‐mediated inhibition of Yoda1‐mediated increases in PAP was similar in the absence and presence of l‐NAME (Figure 3B). As shown in Figure 3B, the Gd3+‐mediated percentage inhibition of Yoda1‐induced increase in PAP was 67.4 ± 10.7% in the absence of l‐NAME (Figure 3A, upper panels) and 60.8 ± 13.7% in the presence of l‐NAME (Figure 3A, lower panels). Pretreatment and treatment with nifedipine, a dihydropyridine blocker of voltage‐dependent Ca2+ channels, had negligible effect on Yoda1‐mediated increase in PAP (Figure 3C). Nevertheless, when Yoda1 was applied first to the lung vasculature via intrapulmonary perfusion, Gd3+ seemed to lose its inhibitory effect on Yoda1‐mediated increase in PAP in the absence or presence of l‐NAME (data not shown). These data indicate that the conformational change of Piezo1 by Yoda1‐mediated allosteric binding may preclude Gd3+ from blocking Ca2+ influx through the channel.

Exogenous TXA2 Analog, U46619, Enhances Yoda1‐mediated Pulmonary Vasoconstriction

Inhibition of the endothelium‐derived relaxing factor (EDRF), such as NO or the endothelium‐derived NO (EDNO), with l‐NAME significantly enhanced Yoda1‐mediated pulmonary vasoconstriction (see Figure 2). The next set of experiments was designed to examine whether exogenous U46619, an analog of TXA2 that is one of the most important endothelium‐derived constricting factors (EDCF), affects Yoda1‐mediated pulmonary vasoconstriction. Intrapulmonary perfusion of U46619 (0.1 µM) first induced a slight increase in PAP with an amplitude similar to the Yoda1‐mediated increase in PAP (Figure 4A). On top of the U46619‐mediated increase in PAP, Yoda1 resulted in a huge increase in PAP, which was much greater than that in the absence of U46619 (Figure 4A,B). The U46619‐enhanced Yoda1‐mediated increase in PAP was rapid and reversible. After 5‐10 min washout of U46619, the amplitude of Yoda1‐mediated increase in PAP returned to the level similar to that of control or Yoda1‐mediated increase in PAP before U46619 treatment (Figure 4B). These data indicate that intrapulmonary application of exogenous U46619 (an analog of the EDCF TXA2) significantly and reversibly enhances Yoda1‐mediated pulmonary vasoconstriction. The similar enhancing effects of l‐NAME (an inhibitor of EDRF) and U46619 (an analog of EDCF) on Yoda1‐mediated pulmonary vasoconstriction suggest that the activation of Piezo1 channels in PAECs plays an important role in pulmonary vasodilation or endothelium‐dependent relaxation (EDR) under normal conditions.

Yoda1‐mediated Pulmonary Vasoconstriction Is Independent of High (25 mm) K+‐Induced Pulmonary Vasoconstriction

Increasing extracellular [K+] from 4.7 mM to 25 mM shifts the equilibrium potential for K+ from approximately −82 mV to −42 mV, which results in membrane depolarization and subsequent opening of voltage‐dependent Ca2+ channels (VDCC). The Ca2+ influx through VDCC then increases [Ca2+]cyt in PASMCs and causes PASMC contraction and pulmonary vasoconstriction. In the isolated and perfused/ventilated lung, intrapulmonary perfusion of 25 mM K+ (25 K)‐containing PSS increased PAP to a level that is approximately 65% of the 30 µM‐Yoda1‐mediated increase in PAP (Figure 5A). On top of the 25K‐mediated increase in PAP, Yoda1 further increased PAP (Figure 5A). The amplitude of Yoda1‐mediated increase in PAP in the presence of 25 K was similar to the amplitude of 25K‐mediated increase in PAP, but slightly (with no statistical significance) lower than the amplitude of Yoda1‐mediated increases in PAP in the absence of 25 K (Figure 5A). These data indicate that Yoda1‐mediated increase in PAP is not affected by 25K‐mediated membrane depolarization and Ca2+ influx through VDCCs in PASMCs.

Similar to the effect of 25 mM K+, intracellular perfusion of 1 µM phenylephrine (PE), an agonist of α‐adrenergic receptor, increased PAP to a similar level of that induced by Yoda1 (Figure 5B). On top of the PE‐induced increase in PAP, Yoda1 was able to further increase PAP by the same amplitude as that induced by Yoda1 in the absence of PE (Figure 5B). These data indicate that Yoda1‐mediated increase in PAP or pulmonary vasoconstriction is not affected by PE or agonist of α‐adrenergic receptors.

Yoda1‐mediated Pulmonary Vasoconstriction Is Independent of Hypoxia‐Induced Pulmonary Vasoconstriction

Alveolar hypoxia by ventilation of 10% O2 gas mixture via a mini ventilator increased PAP because of hypoxic pulmonary vasoconstriction (HPV) (Figure 6A). Both Yoda1‐mediated increase in PAP and alveolar hypoxia‐induced increase in PAP seem to exist concurrently, although the amplitude of hypoxia‐induced increase in PAP was inhibited during the intrapulmonary perfusion of Yoda1 in comparison to the hypoxic response before Yoda1 application (Figure 6A). On top of the Yoda1‐mediated increase in PAP, however, alveolar hypoxia caused an initial decrease in PAP due to pulmonary vasodilation followed by a sustained increase in PAP due apparently to HPV (Figure 6A right panels). If we calculate the amplitude of hypoxia‐induced increase in PAP from the level of PAP during the hypoxia‐induced initial pulmonary vasodilation, the amplitude of hypoxia‐induced increase in PAP in the presence of Yoda1 is approximately 30% of the amplitude of hypoxic response before Yoda1 application and is similar to the amplitude of hypoxia‐induced increase in PAP after Yoda1 application (Figure 6A right panels). These results indicate that while Yoda1‐induced pulmonary vasoconstriction arises from Ca2+ influx through mechanosensitive cation channels such as Piezo1, alveolar hypoxia‐induced pulmonary vasoconstriction may result from an overlapping mechanism involving Ca2+ influx through multiple Ca2+‐permeable cation channels, including voltage‐gated, receptor‐operated, and mechanically activated channels.

As described earlier, l‐NAME significantly potentiated Yoda1‐mediated increases in PAP (Figure 2B); however, l‐NAME pretreatment only slightly enhanced alveolar hypoxia‐induced increases in PAP or acute hypoxic pulmonary vasoconstriction (Figure 6B). These data suggest that, although HPV is potentially caused by an intrinsic and multifactorial mechanism within the pulmonary arterial smooth muscle cells, the endothelium‐derived relaxing factors like NO play an important modulatory role in alveolar hypoxia‐induced pulmonary vasoconstriction and, in general, the regulation of pulmonary vascular reactivity.

Ogerin‐Induced Pulmonary Vasodilation Is Independent of eNOS Activity and EDRF

In addition to mechanosensitive cation channels (e.g., Piezo1) in the pulmonary vasculature, there are mechanosensitive receptors that may engage in regulating pulmonary vasodilation and vasoconstriction. GPR68, also termed as ovarian cancer G protein‐coupled receptor 1 (OGR1) [36, 39], is a G protein‐coupled receptor (GPCR) that senses both mechanical and chemical stimuli [33, 34, 35]. The next set of experiments was designed to test whether activation of the mechanosensitive GPR68 exerts a similar or differential effect on the pulmonary vasculature as activation of the mechanosensitive cation channels. We used ogerin, a small‐molecule positive allosteric modulator of GPR68, to replace Yoda1 for the ex vivo isolated, perfused/ventilated lung experiments. Intrapulmonary perfusion of ogerin (50 µM) caused a significant decrease (19.37 ± 1.21%, n = 25 mice) in PAP under baseline conditions due, obviously, to pulmonary vasodilation (Figure 7A,B) or inhibition of pulmonary vascular tone. The ogerin‐mediated pulmonary vasodilation or decrease in PAP was reversible; washout of ogerin with physiological salt solution restored the baseline PAP close to the level before perfusion of ogerin. Inhibition of eNOS by short‐term (2‐5 min) treatment with l‐NAME (300 µM) had negligible effect on ogerin‐mediated pulmonary vasodilation or decrease in PAP (Figure 7C,D). Long‐term (5‐10 min) treatment with l‐NAME significantly increased the baseline PAP but the percentage of ogerin‐mediated decrease in PAP was actually enhanced due, possibly, to the high level of PAP (Figure 7C). These results indicate that ogerin, by activating GPR68 [36], causes pulmonary vasodilation via a mechanism independent of eNOS activity or endothelium‐derived NO. Given the mechanosensitive property of GPR68, these data also suggest that activation of Piezo1 and GPR68 confers different physiological effects on the lung vasculature.

Discussion

The lungs are the only organ that receives the entire cardiac output. The right ventricular stroke in the range of 60‐70 times per minute enforces a periodic mechanical stretch of the lung vasculature. In addition, spontaneous breathing at a rate of 12‐16 times per minute is another mechanical stimulus (via tracheal radial traction) that is periodically applied to the pulmonary vasculature. Therefore, the pulmonary vasculature is constantly subjected to mechanical stimulation from the circulatory and respiratory systems. To maintain low vascular resistance and low arterial pressure in the pulmonary circulation, the pulmonary artery (PA), especially the small resistance or distal PAs, needs a regulatory mechanism that balances vasoconstriction and vasodilation (Figure 8). An increase in [Ca2+]cyt in PASMCs triggers PASMC contraction, causes pulmonary vasoconstriction, and increases pulmonary vascular resistance (PVR) and PAP. A rise in [Ca2+]cyt in PAECs, however, activates eNOS and increases NO synthesis. The resultant release of endothelial NO activates soluble guanylate cyclase (sGC) in PASMC, increases cGMP/PKG, and causes PASMC relaxation and pulmonary vasodilation [40, 41, 42, 43, 44].

Proposed mechanisms by which Piezo1‐mediated Ca2+ influx in pulmonary arterial endothelial cells (PAEC) and smooth muscle cells (PASMC) results in pulmonary vasodilation and vasoconstriction. Yoda1‐mediated opening of Piezo1 in PAEC induces Ca2+ influx and the increase in cytosolic free Ca2+ concentration ([Ca2+]cyt), which then stimulates endothelial NO synthesis (eNOS) and enhances NO production and release. The endothelium‐derived NO (EDNO) diffuses into PASMCs to stimulate soluble guanylyl cyclase (sGC), increasing cGMP production, a second messenger that causes PASMC relaxation and ultimately pulmonary vasodilation. Yoda1‐mediated opening of Piezo1 in PASMC increases [Ca2+]cyt and causes PASMC contraction by activating myosin light chain kinase (MLCK), which then causes pulmonary vasoconstriction. Inhibition of eNOS with l‐NAME or increase of thromboxane A2 (TXA2) in ECs both significantly enhance Piezo1‐mediated pulmonary vasoconstriction. AA, arachidonic acid; DAG, diacylglycerol; PGH2, prostaglandin H2; ROC, receptor‐operated cation channel; TPR, thromboxane receptor.

Using an ex vivo model of the whole‐lung vasculature, we examined how Piezo1, a mechanosensitive non‐selective cation channel, contributes to the integrative regulation of pulmonary vascular tone. The results of this study showed that activation of Piezo1 channels by Yoda1, an allosteric agonist of Piezo1 [37, 45], caused a small but statistically significant increase in PAP in isolated and perfused/ventilated lungs, resulting from pulmonary vasoconstriction. Inhibition of eNOS with l‐NAME, a non‐selective NO synthase inhibitor [45], significantly and reversibly enhanced the Yoda1‐mediated increase in PAP. Intrapulmonary perfusion of l‐NAME (300 µM for 10–15 min) resulted in more than 3 times (330.32 ± 24.90%, n = 15) of enhancement of Yoda1‐mediated increase in PAP (from 2.35 ± 0.18 mmHg without l‐NAME to 10.13 ± 0.59 mmHg with l‐NAME, p < 0.001). These results provide compelling evidence that activation of Piezo1 in the pulmonary vasculature leads to two distinct, opposing effects on pulmonary vascular tone. Ca2+ influx through Piezo1 channels in PASMCs causes PASMC contraction and pulmonary vasoconstriction (which leads to an increase in PAP), while Ca2+ influx through Piezo1 in PAECs results in NO‐mediated PASMC relaxation and pulmonary vasodilation (which leads to a decrease in PAP).

In addition to the enhancing effect of l‐NAME, intrapulmonary perfusion of the analog of TXA2, an EDCF, significantly enhanced Yoda‐1‐mediated increase in PAP in the isolated, perfused/ventilated lungs. The data indicate that when EDCF is increased, or EDRF is inhibited (e.g., by l‐NAME‐induced inhibition of eNOS), activation of Piezo1 channels tends to contribute more to vasoconstriction in the pulmonary vasculature. The balance between the Ca2+‐induced eNOS/NO pathway in EC and the Ca2+‐induced activation of MLCK in SMC coordinately determines the physiological outcome of Piezo1 activation. In other words, the protective or vasodilative effect of Piezo1 channels in EC relies on the integrity or normal function of ECs. If ECs are injured or dysfunctional, for example, the stretch‐mediated activation of Piezo1 channels in PASMCs would primarily lead to sustained pulmonary vasoconstriction. Lung endothelial injury and reduced EDRF, as shown in patients with idiopathic PAH [41, 42, 46], would thus enhance the Piezo1‐mediated pulmonary vasoconstriction; while the increased EDCF, such as TXA2 and endothelin‐1 (ET‐1) [46, 47] would synergistically enhance Piezo1‐mediated pulmonary vasoconstriction.

In this study, we also found that Yoda1‐mediated pulmonary vasoconstriction in the whole‐lung vasculature is not affected by intracellular perfusion with a high‐K+ (25 mM K+‐containing) solution or nifedipine (a dihydropyridine blocker of VDCC), or by alveolar ventilation with hypoxic gas (10% O2). Raising extracellular K+ concentration in the perfusate shifts the K+ equilibrium potential, depolarizing the membrane and activating VDCC in PASMC, thereby inducing pulmonary vasoconstriction. Removal of extracellular Ca2+ or extracellular application of nifedipine, however, abolished or significantly inhibited high K+‐induced pulmonary vasoconstriction and alveolar hypoxia‐induced pulmonary vasoconstriction [48, 49, 50]. These data imply that Ca2+ influx through Yoda1‐sensitive Piezo1 channels contributes minimally to alveolar hypoxia‐mediated pulmonary vasoconstriction and voltage‐dependent pulmonary vasoconstriction (via Ca2+ influx through VDCC).

As we previously reported, upregulation of Piezo1 is involved in PASMC contractile‐to‐proliferative phenotypic transition and PASMC proliferation [51]. The current study provides evidence that Piezo1 in contractile PASMCs also regulates pulmonary vascular tone in the whole‐lung vasculature. Pulmonary vasoconstriction due to Ca2+ influx through Piezo1 channels in PASMC is enhanced by intrapulmonary perfusion of EDCF (TXA2 or U46619). It is unclear, however, whether TXA2 (or U46619) directly reinforces a functional priming effect on Piezo1 channels, or affects the mechanical sensitivity of the channel [37, 52, 53]. The potent EDCF, such as TXA2 (or its analog, U46619), may also promote other Ca2+ influx mechanisms to enhance Piezo1‐mediated Ca2+ influx in PASMCs and pulmonary vasoconstriction [54].

Piezo1, a mechanosensitive cation channel, is involved in the regulation of vascular development, blood pressure, lung development, and lung injury [13, 30, 31]. The trimeric Piezo1 channel with three mechanosensing blade domains and a central pore is permeable to monovalent (Na+, K+ and Cs+) and divalent (Ca2+, Ba2+ and Mg2+) cations. The selectivity sequence of mammalian (or mouse) Piezo1 channels is Ca2+ > K+ > Na+ > Mg2+ [52, 53, 55]. Recently, Yuan et al. [53] found that Drosophila Piezo1 channels are highly selective and permeable to Ca2+ and Cl‐, with a P Ca/P Cs ratio of 4.3 and a P Cl/P Na ratio 2.2, which are approximately 3 and 17 times, respectively, of the ratios for mouse Piezo1 channels (P Ca/P Cs = 1.29, and P Cl/P Na = 0.13) [52, 53, 55]. Based on patch clamp experimental data, the single‐channel conductance of Piezo1 ranges from ~5 pS (Drosophila Piezo1) [53] to ~30 pS (mouse Piezo1) [52] and 35‐55 pS (human Piezo1) [56]. Ca2+ influx through Piezo1 channels in the plasma membrane thus regulates various Ca2+‐sensitive cell functions (e.g., contraction, proliferation, and migration).

Piezo1 expression and distribution of Piezo1 in the lungs are different among distinct types of cells. Based on the web interfaces of LungMap CellRefs [32], a dictionary of single‐cell transcriptomic datasets from 48 normal human lungs and 40 normal mouse lung samples, Piezo1 is expressed in all lung ECs, pericytes, and SMCs, but highly distributed in lung ECs, including artery EC (AEC), venous EC VEC) and capillary EC (CAP EC) in comparison to pericytes and lung SMCs. Piezo1 is highly expressed in human lung ECs (25%–32% of cells) compared with pericytes and SMCs; venous ECs have the highest proportion (31.3%), followed by AECs (25.54%), with an AEC/VSMC ratio of 5.82. Mice have a similar trend, with 17.5% of VEC and 7.19% of AEC expressing Piezo1, and an AEC/VSMC ratio of 5.1 (Figure 1C–F).

In summary, Piezo1 is a mechanosensitive Ca2+ channel expressed in lung vascular EC and SMC. Activity (and expression) of Piezo1, a mechanosensitive Ca2+ channel in PAECs and PASMCs, engages in the regulation of pulmonary vasoconstriction and vasodilation or pulmonary vascular tone. Ca2+ influx through Piezo1 channels in PASMCs triggers SMC contraction and induces pulmonary vasoconstriction, whereas Ca2+ influx through Piezo1 channels in PAEC contributes to inducing pulmonary vasodilation by activating endothelial eNOS and increasing the synthesis and release of endothelial NO under normal conditions (Figure 8). When the lung vascular endothelium is dysfunctional due, for example, to endothelial injury and inflammation, the Piezo1‐associated Ca2+ influx in PASMCs and thus vasoconstriction gains the advantage over the mechanical signaling‐associated endothelial NO release and vasodilation. Then, the mechanically activated Ca2+ signaling in the pulmonary vasculature tends to cause pulmonary vasoconstriction, contributing to the increases in pulmonary vascular resistance and pulmonary arterial pressure.

The protective (vasodilatory) effect of endothelium‐dependent relaxation on the lung vasculature is important for the physiological regulation of vascular functional homeostasis. While the endothelium is injured or eNOS is uncoupled, the beneficial effect of Piezo1‐mediated endothelial Ca2+ signaling (via eNOS activation) would be over‐replaced by the contractile and proliferative effects of Piezo1‐mediated smooth muscle Ca2+ signaling, resulting in sustained vasoconstriction. This study, however, does not explore the chronic effects of Ca2+ influx through Piezo1 in PASMCs and PAECs on pulmonary vascular function and structure (e.g., vascular remodeling and intimal lesions), nor their contribution to, or relationship with, pulmonary vascular disease or pulmonary hypertension. Our previous study showed that upregulation of Piezo1 was involved in PASMC contractile‐to‐proliferative phenotypic transition and PASMC proliferation [51]. In combination with the results of this study, we believe that the Piezo1 channel plays an important role in the regulation of acute pulmonary vascular reactivity and chronic pulmonary vascular remodeling.
