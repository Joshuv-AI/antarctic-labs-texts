Severe Calcific Bicuspid Aortic Valve Stenosis Presenting as Reversible Complete Heart Block
Authors: Pramod Tulsidas Gitte, Milind Kharche, Umesh Khedkar, Sumit Rawal
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundSevere calcific bicuspid aortic valve (BAV) stenosis rarely presents with complete heart block, although extensive subvalvular calcium may compress the atrioventricular conduction axis.Case SummaryA 42-year-old man presented with presyncope and dyspnea, and his electrocardiogram showed complete atrioventricular dissociation with a ventricular escape rhythm. Echocardiography and computed tomography aortography demonstrated severe calcific type 0 BAV stenosis, with dense calcium extending into the membranous septum. Surgical aortic valve replacement with a mechanical prosthesis was performed. Postoperatively, the patient showed progressive restoration of intrinsic conduction, and temporary pacing wires were removed by day 6. At 3-month follow-up, he remained asymptomatic in stable sinus rhythm with a well-functioning prosthetic valve.DiscussionThis case highlights severe calcific BAV stenosis as an uncommon but reversible cause of complete heart block.Take-Home MessageEarly recognition of this mechanism is essential to prevent unnecessary permanent pacemaker implantation and underscores the value of multimodality imaging in evaluating conduction abnormalities.

History of Presentation

A 42-year-old Asian man, nondiabetic and nonhypertensive, presented to the emergency department with presyncope for 2 days and dyspnea on exertion (NYHA functional class II) for 1 year. On arrival, his pulse rate was 42 beats/min, blood pressure 100/60 mm Hg, and oxygen saturation 98% on room air. Cardiovascular examination revealed a soft A2 and a grade IV/VI ejection systolic “to-and-fro” murmur in the right second intercostal space radiating to the carotids with a carotid shudder. Past Medical History

He had no prior cardiac illness, diabetes, hypertension, or a family history of premature cardiovascular disease or conduction abnormalities.

Investigations

Echocardiography demonstrated severe calcific bicuspid aortic valve (BAV) stenosis with a peak/mean gradient of 116/53 mm Hg (atrioventricular [AV] Vmax 5.4 m/s), mild aortic regurgitation, severe concentric left ventricular hypertrophy, and reduced left ventricular global longitudinal strain (–14%) (Figures 1C and 1D, Video 1, Video 2, Video 3). Laboratory investigations, including thyroid stimulating hormone 2.4 μIU/mL, parathyroid hormone 35.1 pg/mL, and angiotensin converting enzyme 21.6 U/L, were within normal limits. A computed tomographic aortogram revealed a type 0 BAV (no raphe and commissures at 7- and 2-o’clock) with dense calcium extending into the subvalvular region and a normal ascending aorta (Figures 2B and 2D). Management

The patient underwent surgical aortic valve replacement (SAVR) using a 23-mm TTK Chitra mechanical (TTK healthcare limited) prosthesis. Intraoperatively, a calcified type 0 BAV with heavy subvalvular calcium extension was confirmed (Figure 2C), meticulous surgical debridement was performed to facilitate prosthetic valve implantation while minimizing injury to the conduction system, and temporary epicardial dual-chamber pacing wires were placed. On postoperative day 2, an electrocardiogram revealed sinus rhythm with left bundle branch block morphology on backup pacing at 50 beats/min (Figure 2A). By postoperative day 6, the patient maintained a stable sinus rhythm, and the pacing wires were removed.

Outcome and Follow-Up

Echocardiography demonstrated a well-functioning prosthetic valve with a peak/mean gradient of 28/16 mm Hg without paravalvular leak (Figure 2E, Videos 4 and 5). He was discharged on oral anticoagulation (target international normalized ratio 2.5-3.5) with scheduled follow-up. At 3 months, the patient remained asymptomatic in sinus rhythm with a normally functioning prosthetic valve and a stable international normalized ratio of 2.8.

Discussion

BAV is the most frequent congenital cardiac anomaly, affecting 1% to 2% of the population, and is prone to accelerated calcific degeneration leading to significant stenosis at a younger age compared with tricuspid aortic valves.1,2 Progressive fibrocalcific remodeling results from chronic mechanical stress and endothelial dysfunction, predisposing to valvular rigidity and outflow obstruction.3,4

Conduction disturbances, including complete heart block (CHB), are rare in BAV and typically occur due to calcific extension into the membranous septum and the His-Purkinje system, which lie in close proximity to the aortic annulus.5 The anatomical relationship between the left ventricular outflow tract and the AV conduction axis makes it vulnerable to compression by extensive subvalvular calcium deposition. This mechanism has been confirmed in histopathologic and surgical studies demonstrating direct infiltration or compression of the conduction bundle by calcific masses.6

Although extensive calcification of the left ventricular outflow tract and aortic annulus is commonly observed in severe aortic stenosis, progression to complete AV block is uncommon and typically requires direct calcium extension into the membranous interventricular septum, which contains the AV node and proximal His bundle.7, 8, 9 The presence of a BAV further predisposes to this phenomenon, as BAVs are associated with eccentric turbulent flow, asymmetric wall shear stress, and accelerated fibrocalcific remodeling, leading to earlier and more extensive annular and subannular calcium deposition compared with tricuspid valves.1,2,10

In this patient, CHB resolved completely after SAVR, strongly supporting a mechanical compression mechanism rather than irreversible fibrosis or ischemic injury. Similar reversibility of conduction block after SAVR or transcatheter aortic valve replacement in severe calcific aortic stenosis has been reported in a minority of cases, underscoring its transient nature.7,8 Importantly, despite the absence of a raphe, type 0 BAV morphology can still be associated with severe calcific burden due to abnormal cusp orientation and persistent flow eccentricity, which may promote aggressive calcification extending toward the membranous septum.6 Early recognition of this pathophysiology is essential because premature pacemaker implantation may be avoidable once the obstructive and compressive lesion is relieved. Although permanent conduction disturbances after SAVR or transcatheter aortic valve replacement are well documented, preoperative CHB that reverses postoperatively is distinctly uncommon.9 Thus, in the present case, the combination of type 0 bicuspid valve morphology, early-onset severe calcification, and direct membranous septal involvement, rather than left ventricular outflow tract calcification alone, most plausibly explains the development of CHB.7, 8, 9

Such presentations highlight the spectrum of valve-conduction system interaction, especially in heavily calcified BAVs, where the calcium often extends eccentrically toward the membranous septum.

This case reinforces the need for integrated structural-electrophysiologic evaluation in young patients presenting with unexplained CHB and evidence of severe valvular calcification. In such cases, echocardiography and computed tomography can delineate the extent and direction of calcific spread, guiding both surgical strategy and pacing decisions. Early surgical correction not only relieves the mechanical obstruction but can also restore AV conduction, preventing long-term pacemaker dependence and preserving physiologic rhythm.

Thus, clinicians should maintain a high index of suspicion for reversible conduction block secondary to calcific valvular disease, particularly in atypical young presentations.

Conclusions

Severe calcific BAV stenosis can present with reversible CHB due to calcium extension into the conduction system.
