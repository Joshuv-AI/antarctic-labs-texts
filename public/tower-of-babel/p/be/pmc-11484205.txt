Clinical investigation into risk factors for delirium post-cardiac surgery and its implications for nursing intervention guided by behavior change theory
Authors: Youwei Zhao, Shichao Guo, Zhiyuan Wang, Yanbo Dong, Wei Wei, Zhenyu Su
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
BackgroundThis study explores the factors contributing to the occurrence of delirium following cardiac surgery and devises nursing strategies rooted in behavior change theory.MethodsA cohort of 320 cardiac surgery patients was selected, categorized into two groups: 93 cases where postoperative delirium (POD) was anticipated, and 227 cases where it was not. Preoperative, intraoperative, and postoperative factors of POD were scrutinized using single-factor analysis, while binary logistic regression analysis was employed to pinpoint risk factors.ResultsAmong the 320 patients, 93 displayed POD symptoms post-surgery, yielding an incidence of 29.06%. Preoperative univariate analysis disclosed significant differences in gender, age, smoking, hypertension, and diabetes (P < 0.05). Intraoperatively, significant differences were noted in the American Society of Anesthesiologists (ASA) anesthesia grade (II, III, and IV), surgery time, cardiopulmonary bypass duration, and aortic occlusion duration (P < 0.05). Post-surgery, significant differences were observed in the duration of Intensive Care Unit (ICU) stay, mechanical ventilation time, and visual analogue scale (VAS) scores (P < 0.05). Multivariate Logistic regression identified surgery time (OR = 2.334, P < 0.001), ICU admission duration (OR = 1.457, P < 0.001), mechanical ventilation time (OR = 1.235, P = 0.004), and VAS scores (OR = 2.986, P < 0.001) as independent risk factors for POD. ROC curve analysis indicated higher sensitivity and specificity in predicting POD with surgery time, ICU stay duration, mechanical ventilation time, and VAS scores.ConclusionIrrespective of the surgical intervention type, surgery time, ICU stay duration, mechanical ventilation time, and VAS scores are recognized as risk factors for POD in cardiac surgery patients. Hence, continuous patient monitoring and early intervention tailored to specific risk factors are essential in clinical practice to mitigate POD incidence.

Introduction

As per the fifth edition of the American Psychiatric Association (DSM-5), the quintessential symptoms of delirium encompass confusion, inattention, emotional volatility, and disruptions in the sleep–wake cycle [1]. Delving deeper, delirium manifests in three subtypes [2]: hyperactive, hypoactive, and mixed, with hyperactive individuals exhibiting heightened alertness and restlessness, while hypoactive patients typically display lethargy, reduced motor activity, and slowed cognition. Hypoactive delirium often evades detection due to its subtle presentation [3]. Postoperative delirium (POD), whether following cardiac surgery or other surgical procedures, is linked with adverse outcomes such as prolonged hospitalization, heightened mortality rates, cognitive decline, memory impairments, and the necessity for long-term care [4]. Despite advancements in surgical techniques, the incidence of post-cardiac surgery delirium remains notably high, ranging between 26 and 52% [5]. Managing delirium poses a significant challenge for ICU nurses specializing in cardiac surgery. Consistent screening, early detection, and appropriate sedation are pivotal in delirium management. Previous studies have outlined various risk factors for POD, including age, depression, and prior stroke, among others [6]. However, the reliability of univariate/multivariate analyses in identifying these factors remains uncertain.

Participants and methods

Participants

This article presented a retrospective study involving a cohort of 320 individuals who underwent heart surgery at the Cardiovascular Surgery Department of our medical facility between May 2022 and January 2024. The enrollment process was detailed in Fig. 1. Inclusion criteria encompassed individuals who underwent heart surgery and were promptly transferred to the ICU for postoperative monitoring, with an observation period of at least 24 h. Additionally, participants had to voluntarily consent to participate in the study, and both subjects and their families were required to sign informed consent forms. Exclusion criteria comprised patients with liver dysfunction, preoperative delirium, recent history of drug or alcohol abuse, signs indicative of increased intracranial pressure, cerebrovascular accidents, traumatic brain injuries, or communication impairments.Fig. 1The flow diagram showing the enrollment process

Methods

Study group

In this study, 320 cardiac surgery patients were divided into two cohorts: those experiencing POD (POD group, n = 93) and those who did not (non-POD group, n = 227). Single-factor analysis was employed to scrutinize pre-, intra-, and postoperative factors contributing to delirium. Binary logistic regression analysis was then utilized to discern risk factors, with the ultimate goal of elucidating the etiology of POD following cardiac surgery and devising nursing interventions rooted in behavior change theory.

All surgeons in this study performed CABG surgery. The minimum DO2 level supported during CPB was 98–100%, and the temperature during CPB was 25 °C. Patients were stratified into two groups based on the presence of POD: the POD group comprised 93 cases, while the non-POD group encompassed 227 cases. The criteria for identifying POD were as follows [7]: when the Richmond Agitation-Sedation Scale (RASS) reached or exceeded-3, delirium assessment was conducted using the Confusion Assessment Method (CAM-ICU), administered by ICU nurses utilizing the French version of CAM-ICU.20. Evaluation for POD occurred every 8 h, twice daily at 08:00 and 20:00. Given the fluctuating nature of POD, occurrences occurring outside of these intervals, such as in the wards or during nighttime, were documented through chart reviews. Manifestations indicative of POD included (1) sudden onset or recurrent fluctuations in consciousness, (2) inattention or impaired attention, (3) disorganized thinking, and (4) alterations in the level of consciousness. A diagnosis of POD was made when the patient exhibited symptoms meeting criteria (1) and (2) plus either (3) or (4). The data for this study were obtained from hospital and patient medical records. All information was used only with the patients’ written informed consent. The sample size of this project was calculated using the following formula: N = Z2 × {P × (1 − P)}/E2. N represents the sample size; Z is the statistic when the confidence level is 95%, z = 1.96; when the confidence level is 90%, z = 1.64; E is the error value; and P is the probability value. A minimum of 320 samples is required for this quantitative research.

Observation indicators

Preoperative factors analysis of POD in patients

Preoperative factors under scrutiny encompassed gender, age, smoking and alcohol consumption habits, history of hypertension, diabetes, atrial fibrillation, cerebrovascular diseases, and prior ICU admissions. Additionally, the New York Heart Association (NYHA) cardiac function classification (I, II, III–IV) was assessed.

Analysis of intraoperative factors of POD in patients

Factors during the surgical procedure included the ASA anesthesia classification (grades II, III, and IV), duration of the operation, cardiopulmonary bypass time, duration of aortic occlusion, intraoperative blood loss, and blood transfusion.

Analysis of postoperative factors of POD in patients

Postoperative factors analyzed comprised duration of ICU stay, duration of mechanical ventilation, Acute Physiology and Chronic Health Evaluation (APACHE) II score, and Visual Analog Scale (VAS) scores.

Statistical methods

Statistical analysis was conducted using SPSS software (version 24.0) developed by Inc. in Chicago, Illinois, USA. The data in this study was evaluated for normality and confirmed to follow a normal distribution. Continuous variables were represented as mean ± standard deviation, while categorical variables were represented as frequencies. Analysis techniques included one-way analysis of variance. Binary logistic regression analysis was used to identify risk factors. Receiver operating characteristic (ROC) curves were employed to evaluate predictive values. Difference was statistically significant if P < 0.05.

Discussion

Through our investigation, we identifies several factors that heighten the risk of POD following heart surgery, including advanced age, hypertension, diabetes, smoking history, higher ASA anesthesia classification, and prolonged stays in the ICU or on mechanical ventilation. We anticipate that these findings will enhance daily nursing care in the cardiac surgery ICU and aid in the early identification of high-risk patients prone to delirium. Out of the 320 participants in our study, 93 individuals experienced POD at varying intervals following surgery, resulting in a POD rate of 29.06%. Additionally, numerous studies have reported that POD can impact the long-term survival of patients undergoing cardiac surgery [8–10]. Wang et al. discovered that patients who did not experience POD during cardiac surgery exhibited a higher survival rate over more than a decade compared to those who did develop delirium [11].

The precise etiology of delirium following heart surgery remains uncertain. However, several hypotheses have been proposed to explain cognitive impairments, including neuroimaging-detected brain alterations, inflammation associated with sepsis, genetic predisposition, biomarkers, and neurotransmitters [12–14]. Moreover, the utilization of cardiopulmonary bypass (CPB) in cardiac procedures can induce significant endothelial cell activation, systemic inflammation, and thromboembolic events, potentially leading to brain inflammation and injury, thereby contributing to the increased incidence of delirium following heart surgery [15]. Data indicates that delirium can result in prolonged hospital stays, increased healthcare costs, diminished cognitive function, and elevated mortality rates, inflicting both immediate and long-term harm on patients [16]. Early detection of delirium is crucial for healthcare providers to promptly implement preventive and treatment strategies aimed at minimizing the adverse effects of delirium.

This study categorizes the risk factors for POD into preoperative, intraoperative, and postoperative factors, aiming to facilitate the translation of research findings into clinical nursing practice. Among preoperative factors, older patients with hypertension, diabetes, and a history of smoking exhibit a heightened susceptibility to POD (P < 0.05). Similarly, Teller et al. conducted an analysis on the risk factors for POD following coronary artery bypass surgery, uncovering a robust association between diabetes, hypertension, and an increased risk of POD [17]. Khalifa et al. identified coronary artery disease and hypertension as risk factors for POD in transcatheter aortic valve implantation, while age and diabetes showed no statistical significance [18].

In the POD group, the duration of surgery, cardiopulmonary bypass, and aortic occlusion were significantly prolonged compared to the non-POD group (P < 0.05). Previous research has indicated a correlation between extended surgical duration and an elevated likelihood of delirium in patients [19, 20]. Our data corroborates these previous findings. However, no notable distinctions were observed between the POD and non-POD groups concerning preoperative medication durations and preoperative ASA classification. To date, only a limited number of studies have explored whether patients with delirium experience a more complex and prolonged hospitalization process, or if the high incidence rate is the primary contributing factor. One study confirmed that prolonged aortic occlusion increased the incidence of POD and extended the intensive care time of patients [21].

In the postoperative period, patients in the POD group experienced significantly prolonged durations of stay in the ICU and mechanical ventilation compared to those in the non-POD group, accompanied by notably higher VAS scores in the former (P < 0.05). The VAS score is considered a risk factor for POD. According to a study by Duan et al., patients experiencing moderate to severe postoperative pain have a 5.856 times greater likelihood of developing delirium compared to those with no or mild pain [22]. Previous research has indicated that effectively managing postoperative pain can aid in the prevention and treatment of delirium upon awakening from surgery [23, 24]. However, the exact mechanism remains unclear and could be linked to the sustained elevation of cortisol due to the stress response from pain, potentially causing harm to the central nervous system. Extended use of mechanical ventilation can also directly impact delirium, serving as an independent risk factor, corroborating findings from researchers such as Choi et al. [25].

This study underscores the multifactorial nature of delirium, suggesting that nursing interventions should be grounded in behavior change theory and encompass various perioperative and multidisciplinary measures to prevent delirium. The current NICE guidelines advocate for a comprehensive approach to recognizing, preventing, and managing delirium, focusing on enhancing orientation, activity, nutrition, mood, and sleep, while also minimizing medication use, pain, and anxiety. These interventions are primarily recommended upon admission to mitigate the risk of POD and associated complications. Nursing interventions should be tailored to each patient’s individual circumstances before, during, and after surgery to address specific contributing factors. In elective surgeries, integrating preoperative optimization into perioperative care can mitigate the likelihood of delirium. Furthermore, it’s crucial to consider the severity and duration of delirium, as well as its impact on patients’ quality of life. While intervention measures may not always prevent delirium, they can alleviate its impact on patients and healthcare systems, enhancing patients’ overall quality of life.

To conclude, the duration of surgery, length of stay in the ICU, duration of mechanical ventilation, and VAS pain score are all independent contributors to the risk of POD in individuals undergoing cardiac surgery. Therefore, vigilant monitoring of patient condition and early intervention, combined with addressing specific risk factors, are vital in reducing the incidence of POD in clinical practice.
