Ten-year outcomes of short dental implants (≤ 6 mm): a systematic review and sensitivity meta-analysis
Authors: Linni Lin, Yijie Ren, Enhui Zhu, Xia Wang, Qianqian Yao
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ObjectiveThis study aimed to explore the ten-year survival rates (SR), complication rates, and marginal bone loss (MBL) of short dental implants (≤ 6 mm).Materials and methodsLiterature searches were conducted in four databases: PubMed, Web of Science, Embase, and the Cochrane Library. Randomized controlled trials (RCTs) and prospective studies reporting clinical outcomes of short dental implants with ten years of follow-up were included. Both single-group meta-analyses and paired-comparison meta-analyses were conducted utilizing the statistical software R version 4.3.1. An interpolation model was employed to handle missing data for the sensitivity meta-analysis of SR. The number of PROSPERO was CRD42024522613.ResultsA total of 8 studies (4 RCTs and 4 prospective) were included. The single-group meta-analyses encompassed 8 studies. The pooled estimate of 10-year SR was 91.2% (95% CI: 0.843–0.965) at the patient level (PL) and 93.7% (95% CI: 0.878–0.980) at the implant level (IL). Sensitivity meta-analysis estimates were 89.7% (PL) and 92.8% (IL). Subgroup analyses revealed that screw retention implants had lower SR than cemented retention. The incidence of technical complications was 23.5%, while biological complications included peri-implant mucositis (33.6%) and peri-implantitis (0.4%). The pooled MBL estimates over ten years were 0.277 mm for both the PL and IL. Paired-comparison meta-analyses of four RCTs showed that short implants had a significantly lower 10-year SR than long implants (RR, 0.920 (95% CI: 0.857–0.987) at the PL and RR, 0.943 (95% CI: 0.896–0.992) at the IL), but no significant differences were observed in technical, biological complications, or MBL.ConclusionFor patients with jawbone atrophy, short implants may represent a viable long-term treatment option, although their 10-year survival rate is slightly lower than that of standard implants. However, given the limitations of this study, the above conclusions and their clinical implications should be interpreted with caution.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12903-026-08742-4.

Introduction

Dental implant placement is the most common method for treating patients with edentulous or partially edentulous teeth, with a long-term survival rate (SR) exceeding 90% [1–3]. However, due to periodontal disease, maxillary sinus pneumatization, alveolar atrophy, previous surgeries, iatrogenic factors, tooth extraction due to dental caries or prosthetic indications, and traumatic injuries, patients’ alveolar bone height may be insufficient for implants of traditional length. More intricate operations are needed, such as guided bone regeneration and maxillary sinus floor elevation, which escalate the risk of the procedure and the cost of care [4]. In this situation, using short dental implants for restoration may be an alternative method to simplify the surgical process and reduce costs [5, 6].

The definition of short dental implants remains controversial, with a broad range of implant lengths (varying from ≤ 6 mm to ≤ 10 mm) [7]. The 2018 International Team for Implantology (ITI) Consensus Conference defined an implant ≤ 6 mm in length as a short dental implant [8]. It is noteworthy that implants of this length (≤ 6 mm) are also frequently reported in the literature under the specific designation of “extra-short implants” [2, 3]. According to the ITI report and supporting literature, the mean survival of short implants after 1–5 years of functional loading was approximately 96%, which is comparable to that reported for longer implants [2, 3, 6, 8–10]. However, another study found that short implants in function for more than three years have a higher failure rate than those less than three years [11]. Existing systematic reviews often combine data from different follow-up periods, making it difficult to distinguish clinical outcomes across different stages. Furthermore, the follow-up period in most studies did not exceed five years [12–16], and data spanning ≥ 10 years are even scarcer. Moreover, most reviews have not adequately addressed the potential impact of loss to follow-up on the assessment of long-term efficacy, which may undermine the reliability of the conclusions.

Therefore, this systematic review and meta-analysis aim to evaluate the 10-year clinical performance of short dental implants (≤ 6 mm), including survival rates, complication rates, and marginal bone resorption, to provide clinicians with an evidence-based assessment of long-term risks. Furthermore, this study will employ data interpolation methods to conduct sensitivity analyses for patients lost to follow-up, thereby reducing attrition bias and enhancing the scientific rigor of the conclusions.

Materials and methods

The systematic review and meta-analysis have been registered in PROSPERO (CRD42024522613). This review followed the Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Reviews and Meta-Analyses (PRISMA) guidelines [17, 18].

Eligibility criteria

The inclusion criteria of this systematic review were designed following the PICOS format.

(P) Population: completely or partially edentulous adults receiving dental implants. (I) Intervention: patients have received at least one short dental implant (≤ 6 mm). (C) Comparison: patients have received at least one long dental implant (≥ 10 mm) (if available). (O) Outcomes: 10-year SR of the dental implant (primary outcome), complications, and MBL (secondary outcome). (S) Study design: randomized controlled clinical trials (RCTs) or prospective cohort studies.

The exclusion criteria were as follows: (1) Retrospective study; (2) Not human research; (3) Review or meta-analysis; (4) The number of research participants is less than 10.

Literature search protocol

We searched PubMed, Web of Science, Embase, and the Cochrane Library up to 20 March 2026. The complete search strategy is attached to the Supplementary Material (see Table S1).

Data collection and extraction

All studies were imported into EndNote 20 for duplicate removal and subsequently transferred to the Rayyan web-based systematic review platform for screening. On the Rayyan platform, two independent reviewers (NL and YR) screened the titles, abstracts, and full texts of the retrieved records against the predefined eligibility criteria. Any disagreements during the screening phase were resolved through discussion with a third senior reviewer (YQ). Inter-reviewer agreement was assessed using Cohen’s kappa (κ) statistics.

Following the screening process, the same two reviewers (NL and YR) independently extracted relevant data from the included studies using a standardized data extraction form. Discrepancies in data extraction were adjudicated by consensus with the third reviewer (YQ). Extracted data included: (1) the general study characteristics (author, country, study design); (2) population characteristics (age, gender, number of patients and implants, number of LTFU); (3) implant characteristics (Implant location, implant systems, implant size, loading protocol, prosthesis connection, prosthesis design, and CIR); (4) reported outcome parameters.

Risk of bias

Two reviewers (LL and XW) used the Cochrane Risk of Bias tool (RoB 2) to assess potential bias in RCTs [23] and the Newcastle-Ottawa Scale (NOS) to assess the quality of observational studies [24]. Disagreements between reviewers were decided by a third examiner (QY).

Statistical analysis

The meta-analysis was performed using R version 4.3.1. For the single-group meta-analyses, dichotomous variables were pooled using the ‘metaprop’ package [25], with variances stabilized by Freeman-Tukey Double arcsine transformation [26]; for zero events, a continuity correction of 0.5 was added to each cell. Continuous variables were integrated using ‘metamean’ [25]. For the pairwise comparative meta-analyses, statistical analyses were performed using the ‘metabin’ and ‘metacont’ packages. Dichotomous variables were expressed as relative risks (RR) with 95% confidence intervals (CI). Continuous outcomes were represented by mean differences (MD) with 95% CI. The Q-statistic and the I2 value were used to estimate statistical heterogeneity. Heterogeneity is indicated if the Q-statistic is statistically significant (P < 0.05) or if I2 is larger than 50% [27, 28]. A random-effects model was used due to anticipated variation across studies, with the between-study variance estimated using the DerSimonian-Laird (DL) method. The publication bias of the included literature was analyzed using funnel plots and Egger tests. Sensitivity analysis was used to assess the reliability and stability of the meta-analysis results [29].

Results

Study selection (Fig. 1)

A total of 8778 publications were identified through the database search, of which 3488 were from PubMed, 3797 from Web of Science, 929 from Embase, and 564 from Cochrane Library. There were 4173 records after removing duplicates, 56 of which were assessed in full text after screening by title and abstract. Finally, 8 publications were included in the analysis [30–37] (inter-reader agreement κ = 0.97).

Study and patient characteristics

The general characteristics of the included studies are shown in Table 1. Eight included studies consisted of 4 RCTs and 4 prospective cohort studies (PCSs) with a total of 318 short dental implants in 214 patients. Of these, 4 RCTs included 161 short implants (6 mm) in 128 patients and 167 long implants (≥ 10 mm) in 131 patients [31, 32, 34, 36]. The included studies employed various implant systems, including Straumann, Astra Tech, and Bicon. Furthermore, all studies had implants with diameters greater than or equal to 4.0 mm. Concerning the implant superstructure, single crowns were chosen in all studies except for two studies [31, 37]. As for the prosthetic connection, five studies used cemented fixation [30, 31, 34, 35, 37], two studies used screw fixation [32, 33], and one study used both [36]. Five studies reported mean CIR ranging from 1.6 to 2.23 for short implants, and two of them reported mean CIR of 1.14 and 0.99 for long implants [30, 32, 33, 35, 36]. The 10-year LTFU rates ranged from 2.86% to 37.6% across studies. The main outcomes for each study are shown in Table 2.

Risk of bias

Figure 2 demonstrates the quality assessment of four PCSs. Two studies achieved a score of 6 [30, 35], while two studies only scored 5 due to their dropout rate reaching or exceeding 20% [33, 37]. A fundamental methodological limitation common to all PCSs was the absence of an independent external control group, which introduces a high risk of selection and comparability bias. Figure 3 presents the quality assessment results of four RCTs. A consistent methodological constraint across these trials was the inability to blind surgeons and participants, resulting in an elevated risk of performance bias. Overall, the included studies were judged as having moderate‑to‑high risk of bias. Consequently, the interpretation of the meta‑analysis results was performed with greater caution.

Results of the meta-analysis

Survival rates of short implants

After ten years of follow-up, a total of 255 short implants in 173 patients completed the follow-up across 8 studies. Only 21 implants in 19 patients failed, 85.7% of them failed after loading. The pooled estimate of 10-year SR for short implants was 91.2% (95% CI: 0.843–0.965, I2 = 35%) at the patient level (PL) and 93.7% (95% CI: 0.878–0.980, I2 = 57%) at the implant level (IL) (Fig. 4A and B). After processing the LTFU data using the interpolation model, a complete dataset containing 214 patients and 318 short implants was obtained. The pooled estimate for SR stands at 89.7% (95% CI: 0.805–0.965, I2 = 63%) at the PL and 92.8% (95% CI: 0.861–0.976, I2 = 71%) at the IL (Fig. 4C and D), both exhibiting moderate heterogeneity.

Subgroup analyses of implant location, implant superstructure, and prosthetic connection were conducted in two scenarios (complete case vs. imputed data) (Table S2). No statistical significance was found in the subgroup analyses at the PL. Whereas at the IL, significant differences were observed only in the prosthetic connection subgroup. The SR estimate for screw retention (83.6% and 80.7%, respectively) was significantly lower than that for cement retention (97.0% and 96.8%, respectively).

Furthermore, pairwise comparative meta-analyses showed that short implants have poorer SR than long implants (Fig. 5). In the complete case analysis, the RR for SR was 0.920 (95% CI: 0.857–0.987, I2 = 0) at the PL and 0.943 (95% CI: 0.896–0.992, I2 = 0) at the IL. In the imputed data analysis, the RR was 0.912 (95% CI: 0.854–0.974, I2 = 0) at the PL and 0.924 (95% CI: 0.877–0.973, I2 = 0) at the IL.

Complications

The estimated overall incidence of technical complications was 23.5% (95% CI: 0.047–0.494, I2 = 90%) (PL, Fig. 6A). Among biological complications, the estimated incidence of peri-implant mucositis was 33.6% (95% CI: 0.173–0.521, I2 = 76%) (IL, Fig. 6B), while the incidence of peri-implantitis was 0.4% (95% CI: 0.000–0.024, I2 = 0%) of (IL, Fig. 6C). No significant differences in these complications were observed when compared with long implants (Fig. S1).

MBL of short implants

The estimated MBL was 0.277 mm (95% CI: -0.021–0.575, I2 = 56%) at the PL and 0.277 mm (95% CI: 0.137–0.283, I2 = 80%) at the IL (Fig. 7). Both displayed no significant difference when compared with long implants (Fig. S2). After excluding high-risk studies based on sensitivity analysis results (Fig. S3), the estimated MBL dropped to 0.154 (95% CI: -0.013–0.322, I2 = 0) at the PL (Fig. S4A) and 0.185 (95% CI: -0.61–0.209, I2 = 3%) at the IL (Fig. S4B).

Discussions

The aim of this study was to evaluate the 10-year survival rate of short dental implants (≤ 6 mm). A comprehensive analysis indicates that, although the 10-year survival rate of short implants is relatively high, it remains lower than that of long dental implants (≥ 10 mm).

The single-group meta-analysis showed that, after imputing missing failures at twice the rate of completed follow-ups, the 10‑year survival rate of short implants remained > 89% but was lower than the reported 96.4% for standard‑length implants [1]. Pairwise comparisons confirmed that 6 mm short implants had a significantly lower 10‑year survival rate than ≥ 10 mm long implants. Despite this statistical difference, a survival rate of > 89% is clinically acceptable for patients with severely insufficient posterior bone height who cannot receive conventional long implants [16]. Regarding temporal trends, a previous review reported similar 1‑year survival between ≤ 6 mm short implants and > 6 mm long implants, but lower 5‑year survival for short implants [38]. Fernandes et al. (mean follow‑up 3.88 years) found a lower but non‑significant survival rate for ultrashort implants (93.12%) vs. long implants (95.98%) [39]. In contrast, our study found that short implants had significantly lower 10‑year survival than long implants, suggesting an accelerated decline over time. Moreover, 85.7% of short‑implant failures in our study occurred late, consistent with the literature showing higher failure rates with > 3 years of follow‑up than with < 3 years [11].

Subgroup analysis showed that screw‑retained short implants had a significantly lower survival rate than cement‑retained, a difference likely related to the biomechanical properties of the two retention systems. Screw‑retained crowns have a screw access hole, and the restorative material can alter the direction of occlusal forces, predisposing to non‑axial loading. In contrast, cement‑retained crowns lack such a hole; the cement layer absorbs stress and distributes occlusal loads more evenly [40, 41]. Moreover, short implants typically have a high CIR. An increased CIR is positively correlated with higher mean stress in bone tissue [42], making stress concentration more likely under non‑axial forces [42]. In short implants with inherently high CIR, even minor non‑axial forces produce larger bending moments. When the retention mechanism cannot dissipate these loads, stress concentration at the bone–implant interface rises exponentially, and sustained loading may increase failure risk [43]. It is worth noting that, compared with screw retention, cement-retained restorations carry higher biological risks; for example, residual cement can lead to peri-implantitis [44]. In summary, both retention methods have their own advantages and disadvantages, and clinical application requires careful selection.

It is also worth noting that all short implants included in this study had a diameter ≥ 4.0 mm. The wider diameter compensates for the biomechanical drawback of reduced length by increasing bone‑implant contact and improving load distribution. Finite element analysis has shown that, under vertical and oblique loading, wider‑diameter implants reduce peri‑implant von Mises stress, strain, and micromovement compared with narrower‑diameter implants [45]. Furthermore, splinting of short‑implant crowns appears beneficial. Subgroup analysis showed that the success rate of single crowns (91.7%) was numerically lower than that of splinted crowns (98.8%), although the difference was not statistically significant. This trend aligns with an 8‑year retrospective study reporting that more extensive splinting (≥ 4 implants) may better preserve marginal bone than shorter‑span splinting (≤ 3 implants) [46]. Thus, splinting may serve as a load‑sharing strategy for multiple short implants, particularly in scenarios with high biomechanical demands.

Analyses performed for secondary outcomes showed no significant differences in 10-year technical complications, biological complications, and MBL between short and long implants. The 2018 ITI Consensus Report states that the complication rate for short implants is 6.8%, without distinguishing between technical and biological complications [8]. This study found that the 10-year technical complication rate for short implants was 23.5%, and the most common of these were abutment screw loosening and crown chipping. However, this analysis was marked by extremely high heterogeneity (I² = 90%), likely due to inconsistencies across primary studies in restoration type, retention mode, and reporting standards. Regarding biological complications, the pooled incidences of mucositis (33.6%) and peri‑implantitis (0.4%) were lower than those reported in epidemiological studies, particularly for peri‑implantitis [47]. This may be related to patient selection (e.g., most included studies excluded heavy smokers) and loss of high-risk patients to follow-up, both of which could lead to underestimation of the true incidence. Furthermore, available evidence suggests that the rate of biological complications for short implants is lower than that for standard-length implants [39, 48].

Our meta-analysis showed a 10‑year MBL of 0.277 mm for short implants at both patient and implant levels, with high heterogeneity. In the patient-level analysis, the study of Storelli et al. posed a relatively high risk [31]. It should be noted that this study had a notably small sample size in the short‑implant group, with only 8 patients available at the 10‑year follow‑up. Such a limited sample increases the sensitivity of the mean MBL estimate to individual patient variations and may not adequately represent the broader clinical population. Furthermore, all restorations in this trial utilized splinted crowns, a design often selected for more demanding clinical situations, whereas the other included studies predominantly employed single crowns. After excluding this study, the MBL decreased to 0.154 mm. For the implant level, Rossi et al.‘s study posed a high risk [30], which mentioned that deeper implant placement could result in higher MBL due to biological width establishment and bone adaptation to functional load [49]. Excluding this study, MBL at the implant level decreased to 0.185 mm. In summary, the low MBL level after ten years suggests stable bone levels over time, consistent with previous studies [8, 50, 51].

Limitations

This review has several limitations at the synthesis level. The patient sample size included in the meta‑analysis was limited, and the vast majority of short implants were 6 mm in length (only one study involved 5 mm implants). While this relative concentration of specifications reduces variability, it also limits the representativeness of the findings for shorter implants (e.g., ≤ 5 mm). The imputation model assumed a failure rate twice as high in patients lost to follow‑up as in completers, but the true rate remains unknown, and alternative assumptions would yield different estimates. After imputation, heterogeneity increased markedly at both the patient level and the implant level, suggesting that the imputation model may have amplified between‑study variance. Moreover, despite using a random‑effects model, pooling heterogeneous study designs may still introduce bias.

At the individual study level, most included studies had a high risk of attrition bias, with 10‑year loss‑to‑follow‑up rates ranging from 2.86% to 37.6%. Although adjustments were made using the imputation model, high attrition rates may still bias survival estimates. Furthermore, the included studies varied considerably in experimental design, implant type and location, restoration type, and connection method, which not only increased the difficulty of direct comparison and pooled estimation but also contributed to substantial heterogeneity.

Future long-term studies on short implants should focus on the following priorities: prospective randomized controlled trials with larger sample sizes and follow-up of at least 10 years; standardized reporting of technical and biological complications; subgroup analyses by location (maxilla vs. mandible, posterior vs. aesthetic zones) and parafunctional habits (e.g., bruxism, clenching) to better define indications; and optimal restorative strategies in relation to implant length and crown-to-implant ratio (e.g., splinted vs. single crowns, screw-retained vs. cement-retained).

Conclusions

This systematic review assessed the ten-year clinical outcomes of short dental implants. The results showed that the survival rate of short dental implants was lower than that of long dental implants. However, there were no significant differences between short and long implants in terms of complications and MBL. For patients with jawbone atrophy, short implants may represent a viable long-term treatment option. However, given the limitations of this study, the above conclusions and their clinical implications should be interpreted with caution.
