Hybrid surgical management of a recurrent inguinal hernia complicated by an Amyand’s hernia and appendicitis: a case report
Authors: Awinita Barpujari, Maryam Morris, Pooja Shah, Franklin Burg, Aley E. Tohamy
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Amyand’s hernia is the presence of the appendix within an inguinal hernia sac. A surgical rarity, this accounts for approximately 1% of all inguinal hernias, mostly in children, and presents a unique set of diagnostic and management challenges. This case report details a 61-year-old male presenting with suppurative appendicitis within a recurrent right inguinal hernia, managed through a hybrid approach involving a combination of open and laparoscopic surgical techniques. The purpose of this case report is to raise awareness among surgeons regarding the management of Amyand’s hernia with suppurative appendicitis and to recognize the need for further research pertaining to the use of biosynthetic meshes as an alternative to tension-free tissue-based repairs in such cases.

INTRODUCTION

Amyand’s hernia is the presence of the appendix within an inguinal hernia sac. A surgical rarity, this accounts for approximately 1% of all inguinal hernias, mostly in children, and presents a unique set of diagnostic and management challenges [1]. Here, we report the case of a 61-year-old male who presented with abdominal pain, nausea, and vomiting and was diagnosed with an Amyand’s hernia and appendicitis in a recurrent inguinal hernia (Fig. 1).

CASE REPORT

We present the case of a 61-year-old male who presented with 5 days of abdominal pain associated with nausea, vomiting, and poor oral intake. His past medical history was noncontributory, and his past surgical history included a recurrent right inguinal hernia for which he had undergone both laparoscopic and open repairs, and an umbilical hernia mesh repair. All prior hernia repairs were performed at the same institution. This current procedure represents the third hernia repair for this patient—the initial inguinal repair was performed using tension-free mesh, and the first recurrence was also repaired with the tension-free mesh technique. The repeated recurrences may reflect multifactorial patient-related risk factors including obesity and poor wound healing/nutrition. Technical factors from prior repairs may have contributed, including inadequate mesh overlap or excessive dissection. Computed tomography (CT) imaging demonstrated a recurrent right inguinal hernia with the appendix in the hernia sac. The appendix appeared enlarged with unclear wall continuity, especially at the tip, with haziness of the surrounding adipose tissue, and a multilocular fluid collection. The decision was made to perform an elective laparoscopic appendectomy and an open hernia repair after manual reduction had been attempted.

Cefazolin (2 g) and metronidazole (500 mg) were administered preoperatively and throughout the patient’s inpatient stay. The procedure began with an open incision at the site of his previous open right inguinal hernia repair. A significant amount of scarring was noted, with incorporation of previous meshes. After opening the aponeurosis of the external oblique muscle, there was a complex cystic hernia sac in the scrotum attached to the right testicle, which was successfully reduced back into the inguinal canal. We then turned our attention to separating the hernia sac from the vas and cord structures; however, the mass was irreducible despite extensive lysis of adhesions.

At this point, the decision was made to proceed laparoscopically to identify additional contents within the hernia sac as it was completely irreducible and incarcerated. Laparoscopic lysis of adhesions was performed around the previous umbilical hernia mesh repair site. The right inguinal canal was noted to be wide open due to the hernia. Upon further exploration of the hernia sac contents, an inflamed appendix was identified adhering to the omentum inside the hernia sac. With further dissection, the purulent appendix was completely reduced into the abdomen (Fig. 2). The appendix was then transected with a stapler. After the appendectomy, the abdomen was desufflated and the inguinal hernia repair was resumed in an open fashion. The hernia sac was opened, and incarcerated omentum was found within. The hernia sac was dissected from the cord structures and omentectomy was performed. The hernia sac was then transected at its base and sutured in purse-string fashion. The hernia sac measured 15 × 15 × 3 cm. The floor of the hernia was repaired primarily in a tension-free repair without mesh, secondary to the nature of the suppurative appendicitis, using the Bassini technique, where the posterior wall of the inguinal canal was reconstructed by suturing the conjoint tendon to the inguinal ligament, without creating tension on the tissues. A relaxing incision was made in the anterior rectus sheath during the Bassini repair to reduce tension on the sutures while closing the defect. This is the surgeon’s usual practice in mesh-free inguinal hernia repairs. Limited laparoscopic wound irrigation was employed to prevent dissemination of intra-abdominal purulence. Standard open irrigation was utilized during the closure of the various layers of the open Bassini repair. The patient tolerated the procedure well and was discharged home on postoperative day one with a 6-day course of amoxicillin/clavulanic acid. The patient followed up in the outpatient clinic 2 weeks later with no evidence of hernia recurrence or infection, and once again at 8 weeks postoperation for clearance to resume lifting and other activities. There was no recurrence of the condition, nor was there any evidence of infection. During the most recent visit, the patient exhibited no symptoms, and there was no indication for a repeat CT scan; therefore, one was not planned. The patient was informed about the possibility of hernia recurrence and the potential need for further surgery should it occur. Final pathology reports confirmed acute purulent appendicitis.

DISCUSSION

Amyand’s hernia, characterized by the presence of the appendix within an inguinal hernia sac, is an uncommon clinical entity, accounting for approximately 1% of all inguinal hernias [1]. Acute appendicitis within such a hernia is even more rare, with an incidence ranging from 0.07% to 0.13% [2].

The optimal surgical strategy for Amyand’s hernia is influenced by the inflammatory nature of the appendix and the presence of contamination. In cases where the appendix appears non-inflamed, reduction of the appendix into the abdominal cavity without appendectomy, followed by mesh hernioplasty, is generally recommended [3]. However, in the presence of suppurative appendicitis, as in our patient, appendectomy is warranted, and the decision to use mesh becomes more complex due to the increased risk of infection [4]. Given the risk of contamination, a primary tissue repair without mesh was chosen to mitigate the risk of mesh infection. This approach aligns with recommendations suggesting that mesh should be avoided in contaminated fields to reduce the risk of infection [4].

The utilization of laparoscopy in this case provided both diagnostic and therapeutic benefits. Laparoscopy provides superior visualization of the abdominal cavity, allowing for a more thorough examination of the appendix and surrounding structures. This is particularly beneficial in cases where the appendix is inflamed (as in this case) or perforated, as it allows for a more precise and controlled appendectomy. Through a laparoscopic approach, we were able to identify and reduce the hernia contents, including the incarcerated appendix. This minimally invasive approach is supported by literature indicating that laparoscopy is a safe and effective modality for managing Amyand’s hernia, offering additional advantages such as reduced postoperative pain and shorter hospital stays [5]. Following a laparoscopic appendectomy, the inguinal hernia was repaired in open fashion, allowing for a tailored, mesh-free repair based on the condition of the inguinal canal and surrounding tissues of our patient, who had undergone previous open and laparoscopic inguinal hernia repairs. The use of a hybrid approach (both open and laparoscopic) in the management of recurrent inguinal hernias has been described, but not compared, in the literature [6,7].

The decision to use mesh in grossly contaminated inguinal hernia repairs remains a matter of surgical judgment, as robust evidence guiding mesh selection in such cases is lacking. While several studies have explored the outcomes of synthetic, biologic, and biosynthetic meshes in contaminated ventral hernia repairs, their findings cannot be directly extrapolated to inguinal hernias, where anatomical and physiological factors differ. In the setting of gross contamination such as perforated appendicitis or suppuration within the hernia sac, the risk of mesh infection, chronic sinus formation, and the need for mesh explantation becomes a serious concern. Given the absence of high-quality studies focusing specifically on contaminated inguinal hernia repairs, and considering the additional rarity and complexity of Amyand’s hernia, it is reasonable and prudent to avoid mesh placement in such cases. Primary tissue repair remains a safe and acceptable approach in this context. Future research is needed to better understand the long-term outcomes of mesh use in contaminated inguinal hernias, particularly in rare presentations like an Amyand’s hernia, to help guide surgical decision-making. Finally, newer biosynthetic meshes may offer a more durable, cost-effective option for tension-free mesh repairs in contaminated fields with lower infection risk, as compared to older biologic meshes. However, further study is required, particularly for inguinal hernia repair and laparoscopic inguinal hernia repair. We recognize that the 8-week follow-up period is limited, as most recurrences become clinically apparent within 2–3 years. Unfortunately, the patient was lost to follow-up after several months, precluding systematic long-term surveillance. Ideally, monitoring would include clinical examinations at 1, 6, and 12 months, then annually for at least 5 years.

Incidence of appendicitis within a recurrent inguinal hernia is extremely rare. Reports in the literature describe a standard open approach for appendectomy; however, laparoscopy or a hybrid approach is also a safe alternative. The aim of this case report is to increase awareness among physicians about the surgical management of Amyand’s hernia with suppurative appendicitis, which was managed with primary tissue repair. Newer biosynthetic meshes may offer another option of tension-free repair with lower infection risk and warrant further research.
