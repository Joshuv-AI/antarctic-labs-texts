Modulating the crystallinity of biphasic calcium phosphate composites balances surface and ionic cues to enhance osteogenesis via integrin-mediated cytoskeletal signaling
Authors: Xinzhi Liang, Shuai Yuan, Ning Zhang, Zhisheng Xiao, Dong Guo, Chengyun Zhang, Wei Lu, Guoyan Xian, Luhui Zhang, Denghui Xie
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
Biphasic calcium phosphate (BCP) composites are widely employed for bone repair, with osteogenic performance governed by both the HA:β-TCP ratio and crystallinity. While compositional tuning has been extensively investigated, the role of crystallinity as a multiscale regulator of composite properties remains insufficiently explored. Here, BCP composites with controlled crystallinity were fabricated by adjusting calcination temperature, and systematically characterized across multiple length scales. Reducing crystallinity decreased calcium ion release but enhanced surface roughness, hydrophilicity, and protein adsorption, while maintaining mechanical competence sufficient for load-bearing environments. These physicochemical changes synergistically promoted cytoskeletal extension, integrin-mediated signaling, and osteogenic gene expression in bone marrow stromal cells. Among the tested BCP composites, BCP2, with moderately low crystallinity, showed the best osteogenic outcomes both in vitro and in vivo, due to its balanced combination of favorable surface properties and moderate ion release. In contrast, BCP1, with the lowest crystallinity and superior surface properties, exhibited slightly lower osteogenesis than BCP2, likely due to insufficient calcium release. BCP3, although highly crystalline with abundant calcium release, showed the lowest osteogenic performance, possibly due to insufficient stimulation of surface characteristics during cell-material interactions in vitro and in vivo. Transcriptomic profiling further confirmed that BCP2 activated integrin-mediated cytoskeletal pathways to drive osteogenesis. Overall, this study identifies crystallinity as a key tunable design parameter that indirectly regulates osteogenesis through its coupled effects on surface and ionic cues, providing strategic insights for the rational development of BCP composites for bone regeneration.

Introduction

Clinical treatment of bone defects primarily relies on autologous bone grafts, allogeneic bone grafts, and synthetic bone substitute materials [[1], [2], [3], [4]]. While autografts are constrained by limited donor availability and the need for secondary surgery, allografts pose risks of immunogenic rejection [5,6]. To overcome these limitations, synthetic calcium phosphate (Ca-P) bioceramics have gained prominence in bone tissue engineering due to their compositional and structural similarity to the mineral phase of natural bone [[7], [8], [9], [10], [11]]. Among these, biphasic calcium phosphate (BCP), composed of hydroxyapatite (HA) and β-tricalcium phosphate (β-TCP) with adjustable ratios, offers a balance between the osteoconductive stability of HA and the resorbable bioactivity of β-TCP. By modulating the HA/β-TCP ratio, BCP composites can be optimized to enhance bone regeneration while maintaining structural integrity at the defect site [12,13]. However, beyond composition, a critical but underexplored parameter influencing the physicochemical properties and biological responses of CaP-based ceramics is crystallinity, which refers to the degree of atomic or molecular order within a crystal structure.

Natural bone apatite exhibits low crystallinity, a critical feature linked to its superior biological properties [14,15]. Numerous studies have shown that synthetic low-crystallinity HA bioceramics demonstrates enhanced bioactivity compared to its highly crystalline counterpart [[16], [17], [18]]. Besides, low-crystallinity HA exhibits higher protein-binding affinity [19]. Moreover, nanostructured HA bioceramics with the lowest crystallinity has been shown to significantly promote cell adhesion and proliferation. Specifically, reducing the crystallinity of HA bioceramics has been associated with enhanced surface roughness, protein adsorption and cell proliferation in vitro, as well as improved bone formation in rabbit femoral defects [20]. Given that reduced crystallinity in CaP-based materials like HA has been shown to affect their osteogenic properties, it raises the question of whether a similar phenomenon might also occur in.

Studies have shown that with increasing calcination temperature, the crystallinity of BCP composites generally increase [21,22]. However, the effect of altering BCP crystallinity on its osteogenic performance has not yet been evaluated. Recent studies further suggest that low-crystallinity (e.g., BCP/Cs) possess a greater capacity to induce apatite layer formation in simulated body fluid and demonstrate superior osteogenic activity compared to conventional BCP composites [23]. Although these studies did not directly compare BCPs with various crystallinities, the incorporation of chitosan as a crystallinity-modulating agent indirectly supports the osteogenic advantages of low-crystallinity BCP composites. Nevertheless, whether modulating the crystallinity of BCP composites alone can yield similar biological benefits remains an open question.

In this study, we fabricated BCP composites with distinct crystallinity by modulating the calcination temperature and evaluated their physicochemical properties and osteogenic performance both in vitro and in a rat femoral condyle defect model. Materials and methods

Materials preparation

Powder synthesis: The preparation of CDHA was prepared by chemical precipitation method. The prepared diammonium phosphate solution (0.15 mol/L) was gradually introduced to an equal volume of calcium nitrate solution (0.225 mol/L). During the reaction, ammonia water was added to maintain the pH value at about 11, to obtain CDHA precursor slurry with a Ca/P ratio of 1.55. Then the slurry was centrifuged and lyophilized, and crushed to obtain CDHA precursor powder.

Ceramic preparation: The CDHA precursor powder obtained above was pressed into ceramic disks. The specific steps are as follows: take a certain amount of powder, place it in a constant temperature and humidity box, and keep it in a condition with humidity greater than 90 % for 12 h. Then, weigh the powder at 0.3 g per disk, and use a pressing machine to press the powder. Apply 10 MPa pressure and keep the pressure for 60 s. The formed disks were then treated with cold isostatic pressing, applying 200 MPa pressure and keeping the pressure for 120 s. Finally, BCP composites with different crystallinities were obtained by calcining them at 700 °C, 800 °C, and 1100 °C for 2 h, respectively, which were named BCP1, BCP2, and BCP3. At the same time, different sizes of ceramic specimens were prepared using the same method for mechanical strength testing and animal experiments.

Characterization of BCP ceramic

Phase characterization: Phase analysis of BCP was conducted by employing an X-ray diffractometer (XRD, D-5000 Bruker AXS) with Cu Kα radiation (1.54178 Å). The angular range of 10–70° 2θ was analyzed.

Chemical structure analysis: BCP composites with different crystallinities were ground into powders and the vibrational peaks of different groups were determined using Fourier transform infrared spectroscopy (FTIR; VERTEX 70, Bruker, Germany) in the range of 400–4000 cm−1; The crystallization of the powder samples was analyzed by a micro-Raman spectrometer (Raman; LabRAM Aramis, HORIBA Jobin Yvon, France) with scanning wavelengths in the range of 100–1200 cm−1.

Microstructure: The BCP ceramic disks were polished, subjected to thermal etching at 1000 °C for 30 min, subsequently cooled, and then surface-coated with gold. The resulting morphology was examined using a high-resolution field emission scanning electron microscope (FSEM; Merlin, Carl Zeiss, Germany).

Characterization of physical and chemical performance

Surface outline and roughness

In this experiment, the surface of BCP composites with different crystallinities was scanned by a 3D optical profilometer (RTEC Up Dual-Mode, RTEC Company, USA) to obtain the 3D micro-topography of the target samples. The 3D topography was processed by Gwyddion software to obtain the surface roughness of BCP composites with different crystallinities.

Surface contact angle test

The contact angle of BCP composites with different crystallinities was tested by a surface contact angle tester (contact angle measuring system, Dataphysics, Germany). The test medium was water, and the waiting time after adding water was 3 s.

Surface zeta potential

In this experiment, the surface potential of ceramic samples was tested by using a solid surface Zeta potentiometer (Surpass, Anton Paar Company, Austria) under the condition of pH = 7.4.

Protein affinity evaluation

A solution of bovine serum albumin (BSA; Sigma Aldrich, USA) with a concentration of 500 μg/mL was utilized to evaluate the protein adsorption of the ceramics. Initially, the ceramic disks were submerged in a BSA solution with a solid-liquid ratio of 200 mg/mL at a temperature of 37 °C for a duration of 5 h. Subsequently, the protein content in the supernatant was quantified using a BCA kit (Pierce BCA Protein Assay Kit, Thermo Scientific, USA). The absorbance measurement was conducted at 562 nm utilizing a microplate reader (Varioskan Flash Multimode Reader, Thermo Scientific, USA).

Opening porosity and bulk density

The open porosity and volume density of ceramics were determined using the Archimedes drainage method. The procedure involved weighing the dry weight of ceramics (m0) using an electronic balance, followed by immersing the ceramics in a beaker filled with anhydrous ethanol under −100 kPa negative pressure for 60 min to ensure complete penetration of ethanol into the ceramic pores. Subsequently, the floating weight of ceramics in anhydrous ethanol (m1) was measured. Finally, excess liquid was removed from the ceramic scaffold immersed in anhydrous ethanol, and its wet weight (m2) was measured in air. Utilizing these data, the following formulas were employed to calculate the volume density (ρv) and open porosity (Popen) of ceramics.(1)ρV=(ρH2Om0)/(m2−m1)×100%(2)Popen=(m2‐m1)/(m2‐m0)×100%

Compressive strength test

A cylindrical ceramic specimen (Φ 8 mm × 12 mm) was prepared using a mold of specific dimensions. The universal testing machine (5567, Instron, Britain) was then employed to initiate the test at a loading rate of 1 mm/min, with the compressive strength being determined by formula (3).(3)σ=4F/(πd2)

The compressive strength of ceramic in MPa is denoted by σ, while the maximum loading force in N required for the destruction of ceramic is represented by F. Additionally, d indicates the diameter of the test specimen in mm. Each group of BCP composites consists of four samples.

Surface vickers hardness

The surface hardness of BCP composites was evaluated using a diamond-tipped Vickers hardness tester (Wilson-Wolpert Tukon 2100B, Instron, USA) under a load of 10 N for a duration of 15 s. The resulting Vickers hardness values were then converted to indentation sizes [17].

Co-culture of cells with BCP composites

Neonatal (1–3 days old, up to 2 weeks) SPF-grade SD rats were euthanized by cervical dislocation and sterilized with 75 % ethanol. Under aseptic conditions, femurs and tibias were isolated after removing surrounding tissues, then rinsed three times with PBS containing penicillin–streptomycin. Both ends of the bones were cut to expose the marrow cavity, which was flushed repeatedly with PBS until the bones appeared white. The collected bone marrow was filtered through a 100 μm cell strainer and centrifuged; the pellet was resuspended in complete medium and cultured at 37 °C with 5 % CO2. After 48 h, non-adherent cells were removed by medium change, followed by medium replacement every 2 days. When cells reached 80–90 % confluence, they were digested with 0.25 % trypsin and passaged at a 1:2–1:3 ratio. The multipotency of BMSCs was verified by tri-lineage differentiation (Supplementary Fig. 1). Cells were cultured in high-glucose Dulbecco's modified eagle's medium (H-DMEM, Gibco USA) with 10 vol% fetal bovine serum (FBS, Gibco USA). Sterile ceramic discs were placed onto culture plates, ensuring even distribution of cells. The cells were then cultivated in a temperature-controlled incubation chamber at 37 °C with a 5 % CO2 environment. To maintain optimal conditions, the culture medium was refreshed every other day.

Viability, morphology, proliferation, and attachment of BMSCs on the BCP composites

Viability: In this study, the live/dead cell fluorescence kit (Calcein AM/EthD-III, Biotium, USA) was used to stain BMSCs to analyze the activity of cells on the ceramic surface. The cells were seeded to the ceramic surface at a concentration of 2 × 104/well and cultured for 1 and 3 d. After eliminating the complete medium, 150 μL of the prepared live/dead cell fluorescence stain was added to each well under light avoidance. The culture plate was placed in the cell incubator for light avoidance for 15 min. Finally, the live/dead cells on the ceramic surface were observed and photographed by an inverted fluorescence microscope (ZEISS, Germany).

Morphology: The cells were cultured on various BCP ceramic surfaces at a density of 2 × 104 cells/well. Following 24 h of culture, the cells were washed thrice with PBS and subsequently fixed with a 4 vol% paraformaldehyde solution for 30 min. After fixation, permeabilization was achieved using 0.1 vol% Triton X-100 (Sigma Aldrich, USA) for 5 min. The nuclei and cytoskeleton of BMSCs were stained using 40,6-diamidino-2-phenylindole (DAPI, Bioway, China) and the cell navigation F-actin labeling kit (AAT Bioquest), respectively. Subsequently, images were acquired utilizing a confocal laser scanning microscope (SP5, Leica, Germany)

Proliferation: The WST-8 experiment was performed using the CCK-8 kit (Dojindo Laboratories, Japan) to evaluate the proliferation of BMSCs on different BCP ceramic surfaces. The cells were seeded on the ceramics at a density of 2 × 104 cells/well, and the complete medium was removed after 1, 3 and 5 days of culture. Then, the cells were incubated with the CCK-8 working solution at 37 °C for 1 h. Finally, the absorbance of the supernatant was measured at 450 nm wavelength using a microplate reader.

Attachment: The adhesion of BMSCs was evaluated by assaying the adhesion genes of the cells cultured on the ceramic surface. The cells were cultured in 48-well plates with a density of 2 × 105 cells per well. After 24 h of culture in the plates, RNA was extracted and analyzed from the cells.

Alkaline phosphatase (ALP) activity and osteogenic differentiation of BMSCs on the BCP composites

ALP activity: The BMSCs were seeded at a density of 2 × 104 cells/well on BCP composites in a 48-well plate and cultured in osteogenic medium containing 10 mM β-glycerophosphate (Merck Millipore, USA), 100 nM dexamethasone (Sigma Aldrich, USA), and 50 mg/L ascorbic acid (Sigma Aldrich, USA). The culture medium was refreshed every other day. After 7 and 14 days of co-culture, the cells were rinsed gently using PBS for three rounds, followed by lysis at 4 °C for 1 h in a Tris-HCl solution (pH 7.4) containing 500 μM MgCl2 and 0.1 vol% Triton X-100. The supernatant was crushed, centrifuged at 4 °C, and the total protein content was determined using the BCA protein detection kit (Thermo Scientific). ALP enzyme activity was measured using p-nitrophenylphosphatase (p-NPP, Sigma-Aldrich). Normalization of the enzyme activity to the total protein content was employed for the calculation of ALP activity.

Osteogenic differentiation: The same cell culture protocol used for measuring ALP activity was employed to detect osteogenic genes. Following 7 and 14 days of ceramic-induced culture, the cells were gently rinsed thrice with PBS and cleaved. Subsequently, the mixture underwent purification using the HiPure Total RNA Micro Kit (Magen Company, China). The resulting RNA was transcribed into cDNA utilizing the iScript cDNA Synthesis Kit (Bio-Rad, USA) for PCR analysis. The expression levels of osteogenic genes, including ALP, COL1A1, and osteocalcin (OCN), were assessed using Applied Biosystems QuantStudio 6 Flex (ThermoFisher Scientific). The relative content of target genes was calculated by formula (Δ Ct = Ct (target gene)- Ct (GAPDH), gene expression = 2-Δ Ct).

Cytological experiments on the extracts of BCP composites with different crystallinities

Preparation of extracts: The ceramic disks and the basic medium were mixed at a concentration of 200 mg/ml, followed by incubation on a shaking table at 37 °C for 24 h. Subsequently, the medium was collected and prepared into the corresponding complete medium and osteogenic induction solution for subsequent cell culture, maintaining the same proportions as in the previous cell experiment. Additionally, to determine changes in calcium and phosphorus concentrations in the extraction solution, the basic medium obtained after extraction was analyzed using an inductively coupled plasma atomic emission spectrometer (ICP-AES, Optimal 5300DV; Perkin Elmer, USA).

Cellular experiment: The process of cell culture with extracts is essentially identical to the above experiment process, except that the obtained extracts are utilized during the replacement of the cell culture medium.

In vivo evaluation of BCP composites with different crystallinities

Animal model: Adult female rats weighing 250 ± 25 g at the age of 2 months were utilized as experimental subjects. The implant sites were sterilized using a solution of 70 % ethanol and 10 % povidone-iodine (βdineTM). All animals underwent laparoscopic surgery under general anesthesia with a dosage of 0.1 ml pentobarbital sodium (Tokyo Chemical Industry) per 100 g body weight. A circular defect (Φ 3 mm × 3 mm) was created at the lateral trochlear process of the rat femur, followed by implantation of BCP ceramic samples, subcutaneous tissue and skin were sutured. To minimize the risk of infection, a penicillin antibiotic (Penicillium; Tai Yu Chemical & Pharmaceutical) was administered subcutaneously to the rats for three consecutive days after the operation at a dose of 40 mg/kg. The rat femurs with implants were harvested for further analysis at culture durations of both 4 and 8 weeks, with five rats sacrificed for each time point.

Micro-computed tomography (micro-CT) analysis: After the femur was removed from the rats, the femur was placed in 10 % formalin (BOSTER) and fixed at room temperature for 24 h. Then, the osteogenesis around the ceramic implants was evaluated by using microCT (SkyScan 1176, Bruker microCT), and the image was reconstructed by using the Feldkamp convolution back projection algorithm, and the image was segmented into binary images by using the adaptive local threshold method, and the volume ratio of new bone tissue around the ceramics (BV/TV) was calculated.

Histological analysis: At 4 and 8 weeks post-implantation, the rats were sacrificed, and the femurs with ceramic implants were harvested. The samples were decalcified with 10 % ethylenediaminetetraacetic acid (EDTA), then embedded in paraffin and sectioned with a microtome (SAT001, Auricon, Chengdu, China). The obtained sections (thickness: 15 μm) were stained with hematoxylin and eosin (H&E). The sections were photographed with a digital slide scanner (Pannoramic 250/MIDI, 3D HISTECH, Hungary).

Statistical analysis

All results were reported as mean values accompanied by the standard deviation. Multiple group comparisons were conducted using one-way ANOVA analysis followed by a post hoc examination. Statistical significance was determined at ∗P < 0.05 or ∗∗P < 0.01 or ∗∗∗P < 0.001.

Results and discussions

Evaluation of BCP ceramic crystallinity via phase composition, chemical structure, and morphological analyses

In this study, the BCP materials were not synthesized by mixing different ratios of HA and β-TCP. Instead, they were prepared by compacting a CDHA precursor followed by calcination, resulting in BCP materials with a similar HA/β-TCP ratio. Therefore, we aimed to conduct subsequent experiments under conditions that exclude the influence of compositional variation in the HA/β-TCP ratio. Compared with altering the HA/β-TCP ratio, adjusting crystallinity offers a more practical and controllable strategy to optimize surface properties, degradation rate, ion release, and mechanical strength of BCP ceramics. Crystallinity can be tuned simply through processing parameters, such as sintering temperature, without changing the chemical composition or reformulating raw powders, which is advantageous for translational manufacturing. However, as shown in this study, excessively high or low sintering temperatures lead to suboptimal crystallinity, and only maintaining a moderate level through appropriate sintering conditions achieves the best pro-osteogenic performance. To assess the crystallinity of BCP composites prepared at different calcination temperatures, their phase composition, chemical structure, and surface morphology were analyzed using XRD, FTIR, Raman spectroscopy, and SEM.

Phase composition analysis revealed crystallinity

As shown in Fig. 1A, XRD patterns confirmed the successful preparation of three groups of BCP, each exhibiting characteristic diffraction peaks of HA (JPCDS no. 09–0432) and β-TCP (JPCDS no. 09–0169). BCP composites were synthesized via thermal decomposition of poorly crystalline CDHA (1.55Ca/P), with calcination temperatures adjusted to modulate crystallinity. A clear gradient in crystallinity was observed across the three BCP composites. The BCP1 ceramic, prepared at the lowest temperature, still maintained a certain amount of poorly crystalline HA, as evidenced by broader HA diffraction peaks with a larger full width at half maximum (FWHM), indicating lower crystallinity. In contrast, BCP2 and BCP3, synthesized at higher calcination temperatures, exhibited progressively narrower HA peaks with smaller FWHM, reflecting a gradual increase in crystallinity. These results demonstrate the successful fabrication of three groups of BCP composites with distinct crystallinities.Fig. 1XRD patterns (A), FTIR spectra (B), Raman spectra (C) and SEM images (D) of the as-prepared BCP composites with different crystallinities.Fig. 1

XRD patterns (A), FTIR spectra (B), Raman spectra (C) and SEM images (D) of the as-prepared BCP composites with different crystallinities.

Chemical structure analysis reflected crystallinity

Fig. 1B illustrated the FTIR spectra of BCP composites with different crystallinities. The wavelength range of 1000–1100 cm−1 is a typical broad absorption band of calcium phosphate. The bands from 962 cm−1 and 567-607 cm−1 are contributed to the n1 and n4 symmetric P-O stretching vibrations of the PO43−, respectively. Additionally, the peaks detected between 962 cm−1 and 1100 cm−1 can be attributed to the vibrational modes (n3) associated with PO43−, which are widely recognized as the most prevalent and intense signals in phosphate spectroscopy. The bands at 3571 cm−1 and 2000 cm−1 are attributed to OH− groups, which could also prove that BCP was successfully prepared. To summarize, several characteristic peaks confirmed that BCP was prepared, such as PO43− groups and OH− groups. There were also characteristic peaks that changed significantly as the crystallinity of BCP composites increased, which further corroborated the variability of the chemical structures of BCP with different crystallinities, such as OH− groups.

Further Raman spectroscopic analysis of BCP with different crystallinities was carried out and the results are shown in Fig. 1C. When BCP crystals are analyzed by Raman scattering, the main signal comes from PO43− unit. The Raman displacement corresponding to the vibration mode of PO43− can be summarized as follows: ν3 = 1050-1100 cm−1, indicating the asymmetric elongation of the P-O bond; ν1 = 950-970 cm−1, indicating the symmetric elongation of the P-O bond; ν4 = 600 cm−1, indicating the asymmetric bending of the O-P-O bond; and ν2 = 440 cm−1, indicating the symmetrical bending of the O-P-O bond. Of these, the ν1 mode is theoretically more Raman-active. According to the established reports, the FWHM of the ν1 signal provides valuable insights into the extent of crystal disorder, and can be used as an index of crystallinity. In this study, increasing crystallinity resulted in narrower ν1 peaks with higher intensity, indicating enhanced lattice order. These findings are consistent with the XRD and FTIR results, collectively confirming the successful modulation of BCP crystallinity by adjusting the calcination temperature.

Grain morphology analysis indicated crystallinity

SEM images (Fig. 1D) revealed distinct morphological changes in grain structure corresponding to different crystallinity levels. All BCP composites exhibited closely packed grains, but grain size increased markedly with higher crystallinity. It is worth noting that the grain sizes of the BCP1 and BCP2 samples remain below 100 nm, classifying them as nano-ceramics. Additionally, the grain shapes transition from angular to rounded as crystallinity increases. In particular, the BCP3 ceramic exhibited a significant increase in grain size, which could potentially impact its physicochemical as well as biological properties.

Physicochemical properties of BCP ceramic with different crystallinities

Since the BCP composites were compacted into blocks before calcination, they exhibited a solid internal structure. To clarify how crystallinity affects the surface characteristics and mechanical performance of BCP composites with different crystallinities, we systematically evaluated their surface properties, including morphology, wettability, zeta potential, and protein adsorption, as well as their structural and mechanical properties. These physicochemical properties directly influence the cell–material interactions and play a critical role in bone regeneration.

Surface roughness increased with decreasing crystallinity

Previous research has demonstrated that increased surface roughness can enhance cell adhesion and subsequently promote downstream cellular responses on the material surface [24,25]. Therefore, in this experiment, different regions of the BCP ceramic sample surface were scanned using a 3D optical profiler to quantitatively analyze the roughness and examine variations in roughness among BCP ceramic surfaces with different crystallinities. Fig. 2A illustrates the 3D microscopic topography of the BCP ceramic surface with varying crystallinities. As illustrated in the figure, the ceramic surface displays noticeable variations in color intensity and surface irregularities. With increasing crystallinity, surface features became progressively smoother and more uniform, indicating a reduction in roughness. Quantitative analysis of ceramics' surface roughness also confirmed these findings, revealing respective values for average roughness (Ra) of 581 nm, 548 nm, and 440.5 nm for three groups of BCP composites with increasing crystallinity. Surface wettability increased with decreasing crystallinity

The wettability of the material surface plays an important role in the adhesion and spreading of the cells. Increased wettability contributes to cell adhesion [26,27] and enhances the interface between the material and bone tissue [28]. Since contact angle is a widely accepted indicator of surface wettability, it was measured to evaluate how crystallinity influences the hydrophilicity of BCP composites (Fig. 2B). Based on the results, it is evident that the contact angle of BCP composites is greatly influenced by its crystallinity. An increase in crystallinity leads to an increase in contact angle, indicating a decrease in wettability of the ceramics. Specifically, as the crystallinity gradually increases, the contact angles of BCP composites were observed to be 12.4°, 18.2° and 73.7°. Some studies have indicated that materials with smaller grain sizes exhibit improved interfacial wettability, which is primarily attributed to their higher surface free energy — a key driving force for surface wetting [28,29]. Revealed by the SEM images of BCP composites, it is evident that an increase in crystallinity leads to a concurrent enlargement of grain size while inducing a gradual decline in surface wettability.

Surface electropositivity and BSA adsorption increased with decreasing crystallinity

The surface potential of the material also significantly affects cell adhesion. The surface zeta potential of BCP composites with different crystallinities is illustrated in Fig. 2C. The surface electronegativity of ceramics demonstrates a positive correlation with their crystallinity, as evidenced by the observed increasing trend. Compared to BCP1 (−5.31 ± 0.21 mV), the groups with higher crystallinity (BCP2 and BCP3) exhibited a higher level of negatively charged surface (−6.58 ± 0.43 mV, −10.27 ± 0.31 mV). At the same time, protein affinity assessment was conducted on BCP composites with different crystallinities (Fig. 2D). The protein adsorption characteristics of BCP composites are also influenced by the crystallinity. Among the different groups, the BCP1 group demonstrates the highest affinity for proteins, while the capacity for protein adsorption gradually decreases with an increase in crystallinity. Protein adsorption is typically influenced by several factors, including grain size or specific surface area, surface charge, and surface energy. Given the negatively charged nature of BSA, its adsorption capacity generally decreases with increasing surface electronegativity.

Notably, BCP with low crystallinity exhibits smaller grain sizes, a larger specific surface area, lower electronegativity, and higher surface free energy, all of which contribute to enhanced protein adsorption.

Structural and mechanical properties influenced by crystallinity

Given the requirements for materials to support bone defect repair, it is essential evaluate not only surface characteristics but also the internal porosity, hardness and compressive strength. The mechanical correlation performance of BCP composites with different crystallinities was investigated (Fig. 2E and F). Firstly, the open porosity and relative volume density of HA ceramics with different crystallinities are measured by the Archimedean drainage method, and the results are shown in Fig. 2E. The results revealed a clear trend: with increasing crystallinity, the porosity decreased while the volume density increased. During the ceramic sintering process, elevated temperatures promote grain growth through fusion and the exclusion of pores, resulting in reduced porosity and increased material densification. This can be confirmed from the SEM image of the ceramic cross-section in Fig. 1D.

The compressive strength of BCP composites with different crystallinities was further measured in Fig. 3F. The results clearly showed that the crystallinity significantly improved the mechanical strength of BCP composites. Specifically, with the gradual increase of crystallinity, the strengths of the three groups of BCP composites were 43.36 ± 8.67 MPa, 70.67 ± 12.05 MPa and 218.23 ± 49.87 MPa, respectively. Notably, these values fall within the range of natural cancellous bone (2–220 MPa), indicating that even low-crystallinity BCP can provide sufficient mechanical support for load-bearing applications [30]. The porosity measurements also indicated that the highly crystalline BCP composites exhibit enhanced densification (Fig. 3E), thereby unequivocally contributing to an augmentation in the ceramic strength. Normal human cancellous bone has an interconnected porous structure with a porosity of 30 %–90 %. It is well known that the scaffold porous design can increase the similarity with the bone structure, which is favorable for the growth of new tissues [31]. Biocompatibility and osteogenic differentiation performance of BCP composites with different crystallinities

BMSC viability and proliferation

The viability of BMSCs co-cultured with BCP composites of different crystallinities for 3 and 7 days was assessed by live/dead staining. The fluorescence staining images of BMSCs co-cultured with BCP composites of varying crystallinities for 3 and 7 days are presented in Fig. 3A. As depicted in the figure, the cells exhibited healthy morphology and strong viability on day 3 of co-culture with BCP composites, with no evident cell death. By day 7, a marked increase in cell density was observed, indicating continued proliferation under favorable conditions. The proliferation of BMSCs on BCP composites with varying crystallinities was evaluated using the CCK-8 assay after 1, 3 and 5 days of co-culture (Fig. 3B). No significant differences in the OD values were observed among all samples on day 1. Subsequently, all groups exhibited a continuous increase in OD values over time. By day 3, both BCP2 and BCP3 showed significantly higher OD values than BCP1. Notably, BCP2 exhibited the highest OD value among all groups by day 5. These results indicate that BCP2 consistently supported superior cell proliferation throughout the culture period, suggesting that its moderately low crystallinity may provide a more favorable microenvironment for cell growth.

ALP activity and osteogenic gene expression

To evaluate the pro-osteogenic capacity of BCP composites with different crystallinities, we examined alkaline phosphatase (ALP) activity and the expression of osteogenesis-related genes in BMSCs. Fig. 3C and D displays the ALP activity of BMSCs co-cultured with BCP1, BCP2 and BCP3 ceramics at 7 and 14 days. At 7 days of culture, lower crystallinity ceramics (BCP1, BCP2) showed higher ALP activity than higher crystallinity ceramics (BCP3). When the culture time was extended to 14 days, compared with the other three groups, the BCP2 group showed more significant ALP activity. The osteogenic-related gene expression of BMSCs co-cultured with different BCP ceramic groups at 7 and 14 days was presented (Fig. 3E–G). The results showed that at 7 days of culture, compared with BCP3 groups, ALP, Col-Ι and OCN were significantly upregulated in BCP1 and BCP2 groups, and the most significant upregulation was observed in the BCP2 group. However, OCN expression was lower at day 14, possibly because its transcription peaks early and then declines as mineralization progresses. Collectively, these findings indicate that BCP composites with lower crystallinity, particularly BCP2, more effectively promote osteogenic differentiation of BMSCs in vitro.

Effect of BCP-derived ionic release on BMSC proliferation and osteogenic differentiation

Interestingly, although both BCP1 and BCP2 were low-crystallinity materials, BCP2 consistently exhibited superior pro-osteogenic performance. This discrepancy prompts further consideration of additional factors that may influence the observed outcomes. Biodegradable ceramics can indirectly influence cell behavior by altering the ionic concentration in the culture environment. Based on this, extracts from BCP composites with different crystallinities were prepared for subsequent cellular experiments. The results are presented in Supplementary Table 2. Firstly, the concentrations of calcium (Ca2+) and phosphate (PO43−) ions were measured in the extracts. Notably, with decreasing BCP ceramic crystallinity, the Ca2+ concentration in the extracts decreased progressively, which were 85.67 ± 0.6, 15.43 ± 0.4, 6.61 ± 0.17 ppm, respectively, while the PO43− concentration did not change much, as shown in Table S2. This counterintuitive result, lower ion concentrations despite lower crystallinity, may be attributed to the strong calcium ion uptake of poorly crystalline apatite during its spontaneous transformation into more stable crystals. This process will continuously absorb the surrounding calcium ions to improve the lattice, resulting in a significant decrease in the concentration of calcium ions in the surrounding liquid phase [32,33].

To verify the potential influence of ion release on the osteogenic differentiation of BMSCs, we performed experiments using extracts derived from BCP composites with varying crystallinities. Live/dead cell staining and CCK-8 assays (Supplementary Fig. 2A–B) revealed that the extract from the higher-crystallinity BCP2 group exhibited stronger cell proliferation compared to BCP1, supporting the importance of ionic stimulation. Osteogenic differentiation was further evaluated by measuring ALP activity and the expression levels of osteogenesis-related genes (Supplementary Fig. 2C–G). The results showed that the BCP2 extract promoted higher ALP activity and increased expression of ALP, OCN, and COL1A1 compared to BCP1. These results indicate that excessively low crystallinity (BCP1) may reduce extracellular calcium via ion uptake, thereby limiting its capacity to promote osteogenic differentiation.

Taken together, BCP2 achieved optimal osteogenic performance both in direct-contact and extract-based models through a balanced combination of favorable surface properties and moderate Ca2+ release (15.4 ppm). Specifically, BCP2 exhibited moderate surface roughness, wettability, zeta potential, protein adsorption, and ion release due to its moderately low crystallinity (Table S4). The combination of these surface characteristics enhanced cell adhesion, protein binding, and ion-mediated signaling, thereby creating a favorable microenvironment for osteogenic differentiation. In contrast, BCP1, despite its favorable surface features, may impair cell function due to excessive calcium ion uptake and insufficient Ca2+ stimulation (∼6.6 ppm). BCP3, despite releasing abundant Ca2+ (∼85.7 ppm) and showing strong effects in extract-based assays, failed to promote osteogenesis in direct-contact conditions, likely due to poor surface activation. The discrepancy between extract-based and direct-contact outcomes in BCP3 indicates that surface properties are more critical than ionic release in mediating material–cell interactions and bone formation.

In vivo osteogenic ability of BCP composites with different crystallinities

To evaluate the in vivo bone regeneration potential of BCP composites with varying crystallinities, femoral condyle defect models were established in rats(Fig. 4A). Micro-CT analysis and histological staining were performed at 4 and 8 weeks post-implantation. As shown in the 3D reconstructed micro-CT images (Fig. 4B), new bone (green) was observed surrounding the cylindrical ceramic implants in all groups. At 4 weeks, both BCP1 and BCP2 groups exhibited pronounced peri-implant bone formation, with BCP2 showing a more continuous and denser distribution. In contrast, the BCP3 group displayed limited and fragmented bone formation. By 8 weeks, bone regeneration was further enhanced in all groups. Notably, BCP2 presented the most extensive and uniform new bone coverage, followed by BCP1, whereas BCP3 still exhibited sparse bone formation and considerable unhealed defects. Quantitative analysis of micro-CT images (Table S3) further supported the imaging observations. At 4 weeks post-implantation, the bone volume fraction (BV/TV, %) was 21.3 ± 0.36 for BCP1, 24.4 ± 0.16 for BCP2, and 8.5 ± 0.12 for BCP3. With prolonged implantation, all groups showed increased bone formation, reaching 26.7 ± 0.22 (BCP1), 31.3 ± 0.33 (BCP2), and 14.8 ± 0.17 (BCP3) at 8 weeks. BCP1 and BCP2 ceramics, both exhibiting lower crystallinity, induced significantly greater new bone formation than the highly crystalline BCP3 ceramic at both time points. To validate the formation and spatial distribution of new bone, histomorphometric analysis of the ceramic–bone interface in bone defects was performed at both time points using hematoxylin and eosin (H&E)-stained sections (Fig. 4C). At 4 weeks post-implantation, new bone formation was observed between the ceramic and bone interfaces in all groups. The osteogenic ability of different groups of ceramics was evaluated by the new bone area, and it was found that BCP1 and BCP2 induced more extensive bone formation than BCP3. The local enlarged images showed that with the increase crystallinity of BCP ceramic, the area of new bone was decreased, and some fibrous tissue appeared at the edge of BCP3 group, indicating poor biocompatibility. At 8 weeks post-implantation, new bone formation at the ceramic–bone interface increased significantly. However, the BCP1 and BCP2 groups with lower crystallinity produced more new bone, whereas the high-crystallinity BCP3 group exhibited the least new bone formation. The local enlarged images also clearly showed the osteogenic ability of low crystallinity BCP.

Taken together, these findings demonstrate that reducing BCP crystallinity (BCP1 and BCP2) enhances in vivo bone formation, consistent with the in vitro results. Among these ceramics, BCP2, with moderately low crystallinity, showed the best performance, likely due to its balanced surface properties and moderate ion release, which together create a favorable environment for cell and tissue growth. Despite its favorable surface properties, BCP1 showed slightly lower osteogenesis than BCP2 both in vivo and in vitro, likely due to insufficient calcium ion release. Although BCP3 ionic extract enhanced osteogenic gene expression, its bulk material induced the lowest bone formation in both direct-contact and in vivo models, suggesting that ion release alone is insufficient and that surface properties are more critical when cells directly interact with the material. Notably, the in vivo osteogenic potential of all BCP composites closely paralleled the outcomes observed under direct cell–material interaction (Table S4), but not those from ionic extracts, further confirming that surface-mediated cues play a dominant role in regulating osteogenesis. Overall, the osteogenic performance of BCP composites depends on the combined effects of surface properties and ionic release, both modulated by crystallinity, with surface properties outweighing ionic release under direct cell–material interaction.

Low-crystallinity BCP2 promoted BMSC osteogenic differentiation through enhanced cell adhesion and cytoskeletal signaling pathways

Differentially expressed genes were identified using DESeq2 following bioinformatics analysis, enabling the construction of corresponding gene expression profiles. Comparative analysis of overall gene expression between the BCP2 and BCP3 groups identified 954 DEGs, including 540 upregulated and 414 downregulated genes (Fig. 5A). Gene Ontology (GO) enrichment analysis further indicated that upregulated pathways in the BCP2 group were associated with osteogenesis, and cell adhesion, all of which contribute to BMSC-mediated bone formation (Fig. 5C). Moreover, Gene Set Enrichment Analysis (GSEA) further demonstrated significant activation of cytoskeletal regulation in the BCP2 group (Fig. 5D). Here we identified upregulated expression of integrins, a principal family of cell adhesion molecules. Upon binding to extracellular matrix (ECM) components such as fibronectin or vitronectin at the plasma membrane, integrin-ligand interactions establish the fundamental mechanotransduction pathway and initiate downstream signals that promote cytoskeletal responses [[34], [35], [36]]. The cytoplasmic domain of the β subunit plays a critical role in integrin-mediated signaling, functioning through its interactions with various actin-binding proteins [37,38]. We propose that BCP2, characterized by lower crystallinity and a rougher matrix morphology, influences the biological effects of BCP by promoting BMSC adhesion and cytoskeletal responses. In fact, previous studies have demonstrated that changes in surface topography can regulate cell adhesion and cytoskeletal organization, thereby influencing the osteogenic differentiation of BMSCs [39]. The natural extracellular matrix (ECM) possesses abundant micro- and nanoscale topographical features, and increasing evidence suggests that these micro/nanostructures play a crucial role in regulating cell adhesion and mechanotransduction [39,40]. This is consistent with our findings.

To validate this hypothesis, we evaluated the mRNA expression levels of integrin proteins related to cell adhesion in BMSCs cultured on BCP2 and BCP3 using qPCR. Compared to the BCP3, BMSCs cultured on BCP2 exhibited significantly higher integrin mRNA expression levels (Fig. 5E). Furthermore, FDA/PI staining and phalloidin/DAPI staining revealed that BMSCs spread over a larger area with increased nuclear size on BCP2 (Fig. 5F). Notably, BCP2, with its rougher matrix morphology, promoted greater cell spreading and actin cytoskeleton assembly.

Overall, BCP with low crystallinity more effectively promoted the expression of osteoblast adhesion genes and cytoskeleton-related genes (Fig. 5G). This effect may be related to the physical morphology associated with low crystallinity. Osteointegration is dependent on the osteogenic differentiation of stem cells on the implant surface, and the rough surface of BCP composites with low crystallinity provides a more favorable microenvironment for cell adhesion and cytoskeletal organization.

Conclusions

This study demonstrated that a moderate reduction in BCP crystallinity enhanced bone regeneration indirectly through the combined effect of favorable surface properties and adequate calcium ion release. Among the tested groups, BCP2, with moderately low crystallinity, showed the best osteogenic performance both in vitro and in vivo. This was attributed to its optimal surface roughness, better hydrophilicity, increased protein adsorption, and moderate calcium ion release. In contrast, BCP1 (lowest crystallinity) and BCP3 (highest crystallinity) exhibited lower osteogenesis than BCP2, likely due to insufficient calcium release or inadequate surface cue activation, respectively. Notably, the in vivo osteogenic potential of BCP composites was consistent with results from cell–material contact, but not with those from ionic extracts, indicating that surface cues outweigh ionic effects alone. Transcriptomic analysis further revealed that BCP2 enhanced osteogenesis via activation of integrin-mediated cytoskeletal signaling. Together, these findings highlight that modulating crystallinity indirectly regulates osteogenesis by tuning the interplay between surface and ionic cues, offering a promising strategy for optimizing the bone regenerative capacity of BCP-based materials.
