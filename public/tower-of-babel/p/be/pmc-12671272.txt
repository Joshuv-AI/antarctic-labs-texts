Moisture‐Enabled Electricity Generation from a Single‐Layer Composite CNT‐Polymer Membrane
Authors: Ioanna Tzoumani, Konstantinos C. Andrikopoulos, Amaia Soto Beobide, Joannis K. Kallitsis
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractClean and renewable power sources are crucial toward the development of sustainable energy technologies, to address the global energy demand. Moisture‐enabled electricity generators (MEGs) have emerged as promising candidates to harness ambient low‐grade energy stored in the humidity of the atmosphere. In this study, single‐layer composite membranes were developed, consisting of a hydrophilic cross‐linked polymeric matrix of sodium poly(4‐styrene sulfonate‐co‐glycidyl methacrylate) (P(SSNa‐co‐GMA)) and polyacrylic acid (PAA), and incorporating various types of carbon nanotubes (single‐, double‐, and multi‐walled carbon nanotubes). These composite membranes can spontaneously absorb humidity and display mixed conductivity in a range of relative humidity (RH) environments. The combination of high swelling capacity, hydrophilic surface, and interconnected nanoscale pathways facilitates the formation of a strong ion concentration gradient under humid conditions, leading to higher and more stable voltage output. For example, MEGs based on MWCNTs reaches ∼0.65 V. These findings indicate the importance of fine‐tuned morphology and nanotube dispersion in designing efficient MEG devices, as low‐cost and self‐sustaining energy systems.

Introduction

The increasing global demand for energy, stemming from both rapid population growth and economic advancements, has led to an ongoing search for sustainable and clean energy.[ 1 ] The abundant low‐grade energy stored in the atmosphere is considered an attractive solution to veer away from resource‐intensive energy‐production technologies. Exploring low carbon footprint solutions gave rise to different energy‐generating technologies, such as photovoltaic, piezoelectric, triboelectric, and thermoelectric principles, which seamlessly integrate into self‐powered, battery‐free electronic systems, thus mitigating the environmental impact associated with conventional batteries.[ 2 ] Among emerging energy‐harvesting technologies, wet gases‐such as water vapor or atmospheric humidity‐can serve as a substantial resource for new power generation devices. In this framework, Moisture Electricity Generators (MEGs) represent a highly innovative technology. MEGs provide a novel way to generate electricity, tapping into the vast reservoir of energy stored in atmospheric humidity, which originates from the interactions between water molecules and hydrophilic functional groups.[ 3 ] Such materials have potential applications in self‐powered electronic systems, healthcare, information storage and portable electronics.[ 4 , 5 ]

To take advantage of the plentiful atmospheric moisture, the crucial component in MEGs is the hygroscopic film, which is tailored to both facilitate moisture absorption and subsequently generate current.[ 6 ] The working mechanism of MEGs mainly involves two phenomena, ion dissociation and the establishment of an ion concentration gradient in the hygroscopic film, that will facilitate current generation through the directional migration of mobile ions.[ 7 ]

Conventional approaches often rely on the fabrication of a heterogeneous or asymmetric chemical structure, to establish an ion concentration gradient. However, such systems involve complicated manufacturing processes, leading to significant challenges for practical applications. For example, gradient materials usually employ moisture‐electric annealing,[ 3 , 8 ] laser treatment[ 4 , 9 , 10 ] or sequential casting[ 11 , 12 , 13 ] to construct a chemical gradient and subsequent ion concentration gradient.Similarly bilayer systems, although effective, are prone to delamination and mechanical degradation under prolonged exposure to moisture, compromising long‐term stability and performance. These methods not only require precise control but also suffer from scalability problems and poor interface quality, potentially limiting the efficacy of charge migration. Thus, utilizing a homogeneous single‐layer membrane offers a reasonable alternative bearing structural simplicity and eliminating the need for complex fabrication protocols. In this case, ion concentration gradient can be achieved by providing moisture in a directional manner or using electrodes with different adsorption capabilities.[ 14 , 15 ]

The hydrophilic groups (─OH, ─COOH, ─NH2, SO3H etc.) of the active material, upon moisture absorption, can dissociate to generate mobile ions (more commonly H+). In such systems, the generated ions can diffuse through the material, driven by the establishment of an ion concentration gradient, creating an ionic current which leads to the generation of an output voltage. Fundamentally, MEGs spontaneously convert the latent heat (i.e., the chemical potential of water) during the phase transition from gas to liquid water into electricity.[ 16 ] This occurs by generating a gradient of water concentration within the active material.[ 17 , 18 , 19 , 20 ]

In recent studies, a range of materials have been brought to the foreground, exhibiting the ability of electrical output. These include carbon‐based materials (graphene, graphene oxide, carbon nanotubes, carbon black etc.), inorganic solid oxides (TiO2, Al2O3 etc.), organic polymers, natural biomaterials and composite systems that have been integrated in MEG‐type devices, to improve their output performance.[ 2 , 21 , 22 , 23 ] Specifically, carbon nanotubes (CNTs), owing to their exceptional intrinsic characteristics, such as physical, chemical and electronic properties, have attracted significant attention and are being explored as extremely promising materials in the field of MEGs.[ 24 , 25 ]

In our previous study,[ 26 ] single‐layer membranes composed of partially‐carboxylated multiwalled carbon nanotubes (MWCNTs‐COOH) that were embedded within the functional polymeric matrix, resulted in operationally stable MEGs. Building upon this knowledge, the present work explores the incorporation of unmodified single‐walled (SWCNTs) and double‐walled carbon nanotubes (DWCNTs), aiming to achieve uniform dispersion and thereby maximize the performance of the resulting composite systems. Furthermore, composite membranes based on MWCNTs‐COOH were reevaluated under the same conditions and compared directly with the newly developed SWCNT and DWCNT‐based composites. Herein, we present moisture‐enabled electricity generation based on single‐layer composite membranes, where the numerous hydrophilic groups of the polymeric matrix impart high moisture uptake and subsequent ion dissociation, while the carbon nanostructures (using unmodified SWCNTs and DWCNTs as dispersions) allow for effective ion transport. In case of SWCNTs‐based composites, the MEG is able to spontaneously produce continuous electrical output, and yields an output voltage of ∼0.53 V at room temperature with a relative humidity (RH) of 95%. For a membrane incorporating MWCNTs‐COOH with enlarged dimensions, the output voltage reached up to 0.65 V, while using an asymmetrical electrode configuration, a voltage of approximately 0.60 V was generated. The underlying mechanism is revealed as the spontaneous absorption of water molecules from the atmosphere, and the directed migration of abundant dissociated ions within the composites mainly contributes to electricity generation. The present research provides new insights into moist‐enabled electricity generation as well as paving the way for the development of a facile, scalable, and cost‐effective electricity generator operating in a wide range of environments for practical applications.

Results and Discussion

A single‐layer composite membrane was fabricated by combining a polymeric matrix (that is, a blend of sodium poly(4‐styrene sulfonate‐co‐glycidyl methacrylate40) (P(SSNa‐co‐GMA40)) and polyacrylic acid (PAA)) with CNTs, in which the incorporation of CNTs endows the composite membrane with nanochannels. The hydrophilic groups within the polymeric matrix (─COOH, ─SO3H, ─OH) enhance the hydrophilicity of the composites, enabling them to spontaneously adsorb water. The water uptake capability of the composite membranes was evaluated by measuring their swelling ratio, after immersion in water for 24 hours. As shown in Table 1, all composite membranes showed the ability to absorb water, attributed to the abundance of hydrophilic functional groups within the polymeric matrix. Especially, SWCNTs‐containing composites demonstrate significantly higher swelling ratios than their DWCNTs counterparts, reaching 775% for 10% SWCNTs content. An increase in SWCNTs content resulted in a decrease in swelling ratio, to 435% and 260% for 20% and 30% SWCNTs respectively. In contrast, DWCNTs‐containing composites reached lower swelling ratios of 110% and 75% for 20% and 30% respectively. This variation highlights the differences in the morphology and surface characteristics of the composites that is brought about by exchanging the carbon nanofillers. Additionally, as evidenced by the low soluble fraction values, the crosslinking reaction between the epoxy groups of the GMA units, derived from P(SSNa‐co‐GMA), and the ─COOH groups of PAA, prominently enhances their stability in the aqueous environment.[ 26 , 27 ] The ability of these membranes to absorb substantial amounts of water is crucial for promoting ion dissociation and mobility.

To evaluate the response of the composite membranes to moisture, the bending angle of narrow strip samples was measured after exposure to a prewetted filter paper (Figure S1). The rectangular strips demonstrated rapid bending deformation upon exposure to a high moisture environment and the curvature characteristics of the composite membranes were analyzed over time. The calculated curvature values K (cm− 1), are shown as graphs, illustrating their variation over time for the different CNT kinds in the membranes. The moisture‐responsive behavior could be attributed to asymmetric exposure in a high moisture environment, resulting in membrane expansion and contraction. Asymmetric swelling capability is a prerequisite for membranes to achieve bending motions. Due to the membranes’ single‐layer structure, differentiation in response to moisture and consequently, mechanical movement, takes place when the membranes are exposed to a moisture gradient environment (i.e., moisture gradient on both sides of the membrane). In a gradient moisture environment, the upper and lower surfaces of the actuator absorb different amounts of moisture, causing it to swell asymmetrically and eventually bend. This process is enhanced by the strong hydrogen bonding interactions between water molecules and hydrophilic functional groups (─COOH, ─OH, SO3H). At the same time, the presence of CNTs, with their hollow nanochannels and intrinsic hydrophobic domains, may contribute to the transport and rapid desorption of water molecules. The observed trend in curvature is primarily governed by the interplay between mechanical flexibility and interactions with moisture. Highly flexible materials like SWCNTs enable significant bending, while the intrinsic rigidity of MWCNTs results in a more limited curvature despite their water affinity.

Water contact angles on surfaces of composite membranes with different contents of SWCNTs or DWCNTs are shown in Figure 1a. Incorporation of SWCNTs in the polymeric matrix led to an increase in contact angle, and an even greater increase in contact angle was obtained for composite membranes with DWCNTs.[ 26 ] For SWCNTs, nanocomposite membranes showed a more hydrophobic nature in comparison to the control membrane, although no profound alterations were observed by tuning their ratio in the composite films. Only a marginal decrease from 72 to 69° was observed. Presumably, the smaller diameter and higher surface‐to‐volume ratio allow a more effective interaction with the copolymer and by increasing the percentage of SWCNTs, the surface roughness rises due to the formation of aggregates (for percentages above 20%), leading to a slight decrease in the contact angle.[ 28 ] Regarding DWCNTs, their larger diameter and stronger van der Waals interactions favor aggregation within the polymeric matrix leading to the formation of a denser and less porous membrane morphology (Figure 1e). Consequently, this aggregation tendency gives rise to agglomerated domains that expose more hydrophobic surfaces, thereby increasing the overall hydrophobic character of the nanocomposites as the DWCNT content rises and in turn results in higher contact angles.

Scanning electron microscopy (SEM) images of the composite membranes provide critical insights into their morphology and its influence on their properties. SEM analysis revealed good dispersion of CNTs throughout all composite membranes, highlighting the successful integration within the polymer matrix as shown in Figure S2. Another important observation is the morphological changes of the membranes after exposure to water (Figure 1b–e). To examine this, samples with 30 wt% SWCNTs or DWCNTs were immersed in water to induce swelling, then freeze‐dried to preserve their expanded structure. Among them, membranes containing SWCNTs exhibit a more porous microstructure compared to those with DWCNTs. These morphological differences are consistent with the swelling behavior, where SWCNT‐based composites demonstrate significantly higher swelling ratios, reflecting their enhanced capacity to absorb and retain water. In contrast, membranes containing DWCNTs, although finely dispersed, presented a denser and less porous morphology which could be attributed to the inherently more hydrophobic nature of DWCNTs. The relationship between swelling behavior, surface wettability, and porosity suggest that the SWCNT‐based membranes provide a more effective medium for water uptake and ion transport. These findings are expected to enhance MEG performance by promoting the formation of more pronounced ion concentration gradients and facilitating charge movement under humid conditions.

Furthermore, X‐ray diffraction (XRD) analysis was conducted to evaluate the efficiency of incorporating CNTs into the polymer matrix. Figure 1f shows the XRD patterns of composite membranes containing SWCNTs at concentrations ranging from 10 to 30 wt%. The diffraction peaks corresponding to the SWCNTs appear at 2θ angles of 26° and 43°, labeled as d(002) and (101), respectively, which are characteristic of graphite reflections. These findings are consistent with those reported in other studies.[ 29 ] The broad peak at 16° corresponds to the polymer phase, and its intensity decreases as the SWCNT concentration increases, due to the higher proportion of SWCNTs in the composite relative to the polymer. Likewise, in Figure 1g the results for the composite membranes containing DWCNTs are shown.

Electrochemical Characterization

The composite membranes were examined in terms of their electrical characteristics, both at ambient conditions and in a controlled humidity atmosphere. To elucidate the effect of the nature of CNTs in the composite membranes, SWCNTs and DWCNTs were used. While individual metallic SWCNTs have a higher intrinsic conductivity, only about one third are metallic, which limits the effective conductivity of bulk SWCNT networks. In the case of DWCNTs, both the inner and outer walls can be metallic, which leads to a higher probability of metallic conduction pathways, explaining the higher DWCNTs‐based composites’ conductivity. Therefore, it is expected that DWCNTs should display better performance at comparable loadings and morphology, which is indeed reflected in the results. Comparing the I‐V spectra for SWCNTs and DWCNTs (Figure 2), with 30% content, the composites containing DWCNTs exhibit higher current flow, at 9.5 × 10−5 A (at 1 V bias voltage), while their SWCNTs counterparts reach a maximum of 2.3 × 10−5 A at 1 V bias voltage. By lowering the CNTs content, current flow decreases in all cases. For SWCNTs with 20% content, current reaches 10−5 A, while for DWCNTs it reaches 1.22 × 10−5 A. Moreover, it is worth noting that partially carboxylated MWCNTs utilized in a previous study[ 26 ] showed higher current flow for their respective CNTs content, which could be attributed to their affinity to the hydrophilic polymers that constitute the membranes’ matrix, thus achieving better dispersion in the polymer matrix. In general, composites with DWCNTs exhibit higher current flow compared to those with SWCNTs, the denser network that DWCNTs can create, which creates more percolation pathways than SWCNTs. Lastly, the ohmic character of all composites with 20% and 30% CNTs content at ambient conditions is observed, from the linear relationship between the applied potential and the current flow. For 10% SWCNTs, a hysteresis loop is observed in the I‐V graph of the composite membranes (Figure 2a , inset), which points to a higher percentage necessary to reach the percolation threshold for SWCNTs. As such, in this case, the current flow has a more pronounced ionic nature.

I‐V spectra for the different percentages of SWCNTs a) and DWCNTs b) at ambient conditions.

The effect of humidity on the electrical characteristics of the composites was also investigated. For SWCNTs, a hysteresis loop is observed for 10% SWCNTs content which points to a capacitive behavior (Figure 3a), while for 20% and 30% SWCNTs, a mainly resistive behavior is evidenced from the linear relationship of the potential with the current flow (Figure 3b, c). Moreover, while for 10% SWCNTs current flow increases with rising humidity levels, the phenomenon is reversed for higher CNTs content (20% and 30% SWCNTs). This points to ionic conduction for composites with lower CNTs content, and a more pronounced electronic conduction for higher SWCNTs content.

I‐V graphs of the composite membranes with 10% SWCNTs a), 20% SWCNTs b), 30% SWCNTs c), 20% DWCNTs d), and 30% DWCNTs e) at different RH percentages.

The same trend is observed for the membranes with DWCNTs, showing a resistive behavior for both 20% and 30% DWCNTs content, while also displaying a drop in current flow as the RH increases. It is interesting to note that especially for 30% DWCNTs (Figure 3, e), current is an order of magnitude higher than the membranes with 30% SWCNTs (Figure 3, c). This stability of the current flow to humidity changes that DWCNTs display compared to SWCNTs, is a result of the higher density of the DWCNTs network, as observed from the SEM analysis for the swelled composite membranes (Figure 1c).

To gain a deeper understanding of the effect of the morphology of the composites and the RH level on their conductivity, we also created resistance versus RH graphs (Figure 4a, b). We can see a general trend of a lower resistance with rising CNTs loadings, both for SWCNTs and DWCNTs, as expected. This is likely caused by the more robust CNTs network that is created from the higher loadings of CNTs. By examining composites with SWCNTs closely, we can see a clear trend of decreasing resistance as the RH level of the composite increases. The composites with 10% SWCNTs show a high resistance at low RH levels (RH ≤ 29%). A remarkable drop is then exhibited for RH > 29, as the membrane swells and creates ion conducting pathways (Figure 4a , red line). The fact that the morphology of the membrane, and more precisely its swelling capacity, aids in the higher current flow is further reinforced by the exceptionally high swelling ratio it exhibited (775%, Table 1). From this behavior, it is assumed that the main mechanism for conduction is ionic conduction, further backed by the negative steady‐state currents observed through the RH range (Figure 4d). The same trend is also exhibited for 20% SWCNTs, where the drop in resistance happens immediately from 0% to 11% RH and continues up to 57% RH. However, beyond 57%, the membrane swells and disrupts the SWCNTs network, increasing the resistance of the composite, since the main component of the conduction mechanism is electronic (Figure 4a , blue line). This is also evident from the small drop of the steady‐state current (Figure 4c , blue line) and is likely caused by the disruption of the conducting SWCNTs network due to high swelling of the polymeric matrix. The composite with 30% SWCNTs has a more robust SWCNTs network that leads to a stronger electronic component, as resistances stay largely in the same order of magnitude across the RH range (Figure 4a , pink line). The situation is more straightforward for both 20% and 30% DWCNTs (Figure 4b), as in this case the DWCNTs network is denser (Figures 1e, and S2f, g), and the aggregation tendency of DWCNTs leads to a more mechanically robust composite, that does not swell upon hydration. As a result, the conducting network remains largely the same. In this case, again, the main mechanism is likely electronic conduction, with a small ionic component.

Further regarding the steady‐state current for the membranes was investigated at a steady applied voltage of 1 V. For lower SWCNTs content, negative current flows through the membranes (Figure 4d), which points to positive charge carriers as the main contributors to current flow. However, once the percolation threshold is reached, positive current flows through the membranes, both for SWCNTs and DWCNTs (Figure 4c,e), pointing to electronic conduction. Again, it is observed that DWCNTs display higher currents compared to SWCNTs, further supporting the idea that the more mechanically robust morphology of the composite and the higher aggregation tendency of DWCNTs have a strong impact on their electrical performance. Lastly, the in‐plane conductivity of the composites was measured and correlated with the CNTs content at ambient conditions. As expected for each type of CNTs, conductivity rapidly increases with higher content, as the high‐electric conductivity of the CNTs dominate the composites. In terms of the type of CNTs in the composites, a trend is observed of higher conductivity for CNTs with double or multiple walls (σMWCNTs >σDWCNTs > σSWCNTs). The addition of the CNTs to the polymer matrix provides the membrane with a conducting network, which increases the in‐plane conductivity,[ 30 ] while the presence of the ─COOH groups in the MWCNTs further increases the interaction between them and the polymer matrix, thus creating a uniform network, with fewer clustered regions. An additional increase in conductivity is brought about by their increased surface area and multiple layers. Thus, membranes with MWCNTs exhibit higher conductivity, especially for loadings of 30%.[ 26 ]

At ambient conditions, the open circuit voltage was measured to be in the millivolt range, however, a remarkable increase in output voltage was exhibited at dynamic humid airflow, demonstrating the importance of humidity gradients in the performance of our composites. In our previous studies, the electricity generation ability of composite membranes with the same polymeric matrix, but with 30% partially carboxylated MWCNTs dispersed in the matrix was exhibited, using the vapors of boiling water as humidity source.[ 26 ] In that setup, a maximum output voltage of 296 mV was demonstrated after 7.5 minutes of humidification. Due to the uneven hydration by the hot vapors as a function of time, as well as the nonuniform water droplets that condense on the surface of the membranes, which directly decrease the active surface area that can adsorb more water and can lead to localized and uneven electricity production, alternative hydration schemes should be used. Use of a humidifier to drive electricity generation will ameliorate these issues and yield better results, due to lower temperature of the vapors and more uniform humidity feed. As shown in Figure 6a, using a humidifier yields the same results as previously achieved for partially carboxylated MWCNTs, however, it is key that the output voltage is reached faster (at about 1 minute after humification starts), and more importantly, the output voltage is stable for over 12 minutes of continuous humidification. The surface area of the MEGs fabricated from the composite membranes was also investigated, to gauge the effect of dimensional scaling on performance. When a larger surface area is used, a longer initial time is needed for voltage generation (about 10 minutes), but the maximum output potential increased from ∼0.3 V to ∼0.6 V (Figure 6a). One possible reason for this observation may be the greater distance that the charges must diffuse through, in order to establish the concentration gradient, which is the key factor in generating an electric current. The longer time that is needed to generate voltage could also stem from the longer time needed for the hydration of a larger polymer network. Lastly, increasing the surface area of the membrane increases the time required for the membrane to reach the saturation point in terms of moisture absorption, delaying the build‐up of charges and the gradient of their concentration. Nevertheless, the enlarged surface area as expected provided a higher output potential, providing encouraging results for further study of these systems in the field of atmospheric moisture power generation.

To highlight the role of the CNTs network, we first investigated the performance of neat membranes without CNTs. As shown in Figure 6b (black line), these membranes produced negligible current and voltage compared to CNT‐based composites, due to the absence of nanochannels. The almost zero output of the neat membranes clearly demonstrates that the conductive CNT network is indispensable for charge transport and electricity generation. This observation underlines that even though the porous structure of the neat polymeric membrane can assist in ion transport,[ 31 , 32 ] it cannot sustain percolation pathways, confirming the necessity of CNTs for efficient MEG performance. We also correlated the different kinds of CNTs used in this study to the ability of the membranes to generate voltage, in order to highlight the influence of the fillers on electricity generation efficiency, under humid conditions. Upon loading the membrane with nanotubes, the nanochannels, along with the porous structure of the membrane, assist in the ion transport and diffusion through the material, making voltage generation possible. As a result, composites with MWCNTs and SWCNTs can generate appreciable output voltage (Figure 6b , green and blue lines). In detail, SWCNTs‐based composites can reach a high output voltage of 0.53 V, for the time measured, while MWCNTs generate about 0.3 V. DWCNTs, on the other hand, show only a marginal voltage compared to MWCNTs and SWCNTs. The tendency of DWCNTs toward agglomerates might hinder extensive percolating networks, which does not occur to the same extent in the case of SWCNTs or MWCNTs that can better disperse in the polymeric matrix. The ancillary role of the CNTs percolating network on current flow is also evidenced by the absence of appreciable output voltage of the neat membrane, without carbon‐based fillers. Lastly, the low performance of the DWCNTs‐based composites can be further explained by considering that they mainly operate through electronic conduction, with moisture playing a minor role in improving their electrical characteristics. The dense network with many aggregates brings about better electron mobility, while the small swelling capacity of the DWCNTs‐based composites leads to their inability to successfully transport ions in the polymeric matrix.

The two predominant mechanisms that have been proposed for the generation of electricity in MEGs are the ion diffusion model and the streaming current model. They are distinct mechanisms; however, it is generally thought that the two have a synergistic relationship on the performance of MEGs. The high hydrophilicity of the polymeric matrix (found from the swelling measurements) drives water accumulation on the surface of the membranes. Then, water molecules are adsorbed in the membrane and can aid the dissociation of the carboxylic acid groups present in the matrix, forming H+ mobile ions. These cations, as well as mobile Na+ cations from the PSS comonomers can diffuse in the membrane, driving the buildup of an electric potential along the membrane (Figure 6c). The diffusion of the cations is aided by the establishment of a gradient in their concentration, as they are initially formed at the interface of the membrane with the water vapors, as well as the sites on the membrane closer to the vapors’ source. This directional gradient and subsequent diffusion are what drive current flow. In this model, CNTs will play a major role in further aiding the directional movement of the ions through the percolating networks that they create. In that respect, the superior performance of SWCNTs‐based composites in MEG applications can be explained by the more uniform, continuous network they create, evidenced from the SEM analysis of the composites, which as stated enhances the effective surface area for water interaction and facilitates ion transport. On the other hand, composites with DWCNTs, while generally exhibited better electrical characteristics than composites with SWCNTs, due to their more hydrophobic nature, further supported from water contact angle measurements (Figure 1a), perform more poorly in MEG devices. This intriguing result could be explained by the higher affinity of SWCNTs toward water molecules, demonstrated from their higher swelling in water percentages (Table 1), their lower contact angles (Figure 2a), and their higher porosity (Figure 2b, d). This water affinity indicates a surface more favorable to moisture interaction. It likely establishes robust ion diffusion pathways in the SWCNTs‐based composites, which could directly translate to improved MEG performance.

To further explore device optimization, some preliminary experiments were conducted, changing both the architecture of the MEG device based on these composite membranes, and the hydration scheme. We tested an asymmetric electrode configuration, where aluminum and nickel electrodes were chosen because of their different electrochemical potentials, forming a galvanic‐like pair that facilitates more efficient charge extraction compared to identical electrodes, leading to a higher output voltage (Figure 6c). This approach was motivated by previous literature, where asymmetric electrode configurations have been reported to facilitate charge separation and to enhance MEG performance.[ 2 , 22 ] In this arrangement, the composite membrane based on 30% MWCNTs is sandwiched between an Al electrode (protected from moisture by a glass substrate) and a perforated Ni electrode that is used both for charge collection and to hydrate the membrane. On all sides, the device is hermetically sealed to ensure hydration from one direction. As shown in Figure S3, the optimized device shows a high output voltage of ∼0.6 V, highlighting the potential of these composite membranes for electricity generation, through careful fine‐tuning of the device architecture. The advantage of this asymmetric configuration further lies in the stability of the output voltage, as the device gave a stable voltage output of about 0.4–0.5 V for 1 hour.

Conclusion

In conclusion, composite single‐layer membranes comprising a hydrophilic polymeric matrix reinforced with CNTs, were fabricated through an environmentally benign process. Comparative analysis revealed that composites containing SWCNTs exhibited improved voltage output performance reaching 0.53 V, compared to their DWCNTs counterparts, which displayed limited performance. This could be attributed to the favorable morphology of SWCNTs‐based composites, high water uptake, and hydrophilic surfaces, as well as interconnected nanoscale pathways that promote efficient ion transport and the establishment of ion concentration gradients under humid conditions. Remarkably, while DWCNTs‐based composites showed higher electrical conductivity under ambient conditions, this did not translate directly to higher MEG output, again reflecting that optimal performance is not solely dependent on electrical conductivity but also on the material's morphology, swelling capability, and ion transport capability. In addition, this study showed that both device architecture and hydration method significantly influence output voltage. By using larger membranes, the output voltage was increased from 0.3 V (1 × 0.5 cm MWCNTs‐based MEG) to 0.65 V (1 × 1,5 cm MWCNTs‐based MEG). Moreover, an asymmetric electrode configuration, combined with controlled humidification, resulted in a peak output voltage of ∼0.6 V in the case of MWCNTs‐based MEGs, but more importantly displayed a stability of the output voltage for up to 1 hour of humidification. These findings emphasize the importance of both material selection and structural design in optimizing MEG performance. Our study provides valuable insights into the design principles of moisture electricity generation and paves the way for scalable, environmentally friendly solutions for low‐grade energy harvesting from atmospheric humidity. These materials are proposed for future studies to assess the challenges and perspectives of MEGs.
