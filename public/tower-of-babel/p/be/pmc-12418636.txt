Mycorrhizal inoculation mitigates drought stress in borage (Borago officinalis L.): Evidence from biochemical, physiological, and growth responses
Authors: Zahra Bakrani, Parviz Ehsanzadeh
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBorage (Borago officinalis L.) is an annual medicinal plant awaiting scientific examinations of its growth, biochemical, and physiological responses to drought upon mycorrhizae inoculation. Hence, two pot experiments were conducted to shed light on the ameliorative effects of mycorrhizae ((Funneliformis mosseae) (T.H. Nicolson & Gerd.) C. Walker & A. Schuessler)) on the drought-induced alterations in different plant attributes, ranging from physiological to biochemical attributes and from root characteristics to shoot growth, and dry mass of two borage genotypes. Drought resulted in suppression of root mycorrhizal colonization, leaf chlorophyll concentration, maximum quantum efficiency of photosystem II, relative water content, P concentration, membrane stability index, plant above-ground dry mass, root dry mass and volume, and plant’s total (shoot + root) dry mass. Though, it led to increases in the leaf carotenoids/chlorophyll ratio, carotenoids, proline, soluble sugars, total phenolic content, and total flavonoids content. Mycorrhizae inoculation lessened drought penalties of leaf chlorophyll concentration, maximum quantum efficiency of photosystem II, relative water content, membrane stability index, P concentration, root volume and dry mass, plant above-ground dry mass, and plant’s total dry mass. The inoculation increased total phenolic and flavonoids contents, and carotenoids concentrations, particularly under drought stress conditions. These results underline for the first time the importance of the plant’s mycorrhizae inoculation in mitigating adverse effects of drought and enhancing the antioxidative capacities and physiological functions of this less-known medicinal plant species.Keywords: Symbiosis, Medicinal plant, Fv/Fm, Funneliformis mosseae, Boraginaceae

Introduction

Borage (Borago officinalis L.) is an annual herbaceous multipurpose (i.e. vegetable, ornamental, and medicinal) plant species native to the Mediterranean regions. Being traditionally used as a medicinal crop [1], it has been recently proposed as a valuable source for production of ready-to-eat fresh cut leaves (microgreens) rich in phenolic compounds [2]. Its highly-pigmented blue flowers contain antocyanins and flavonoids [3] and seeds oil contains nearly 20% gamma linolenic acid (GLA) [4] and, hence, capable of being consumed as a nutraceutical and functional food source. Its products are consumed in some African countries in the form of snack foods [5]. Furthermore, its flower extract fatty acid contains 18% saturated fatty acids, 27% monounsaturated fatty acids, and 55% polyunsaturated fatty acids with nearly 54% linoleic acid (omega-6) [6]. Moreover, its flowers are used as culinary ingredient, as they contain a notable quantity of polyunsaturated fatty acids and exhibit antioxidative, neuroprotective, and anti-aging properties [6]. Plants native to the Mediterranean and arid-semiarid climates, such as borage, may produce higher concentrations of phytochemicals without compromising normal plant development, i.e. increasing and/or maintaining the quality without compromising a great amount of plant dry mass upon exposure to some environmental stresses [7].

The present global population of more than 7.5 billion is predicted to rise to nearly 10 billion by 2050, half of which (i.e. 5 billion people) is expected to be living in areas that are already faced with water scarcity [8]. Drought strikes all organisms and is often the main driving force for evolution of drought-adapted life history strategies in plant species [9]. Drought is a common constraint and hence major determinant of distribution of plant species in terrestrial ecosystems [9]. Nutrient deficiencies, e.g. nitrogen and phosphorus (P), are frequently observed in the Mediterranean climates, characterized by long dry and/or warm seasons; yet an inevitable aggravation of aridity under the current climate change may exert further negative effects on availability of nutrients, worldwide [10]. Annual losses imposed by drought to crop yield world-wide are estimated to be more than losses caused by all pathogens combined [8]. The current trend of climate change is going to bring about further, though complex, alterations in surface hydroclimatic conditions, leading to the worsening of droughts in many regions around the world [9]. In fact, projections suggest that the ongoing decreasing regional precipitation, more intense and frequent dry spells, and global warming driven increase in evaporative demand will fuel the evolution of water scarcity and lengthening of droughts, particularly across mid-latitude and subtropical arid-semiarid regions [11]. As it has been emphasized by Gupta, 2020 [8], agriculture is a thirsty enterprise. Finding avenues to tackle this thirst may lessen competition for freshwater resources in the growing global population. Plant measures against stresses vary from modifications in their metabolism to physiological and morphological attributes [12] including deep roots, annual growth, and small waxy leaves, trichomes, leaf toughness, toxic compounds such as cyanides and alkaloids, phenological and regrowth characteristics, and indirect defenses such as volatiles and branching architecture. Drought may also affect plant life indirectly through regulating soil communities and their function [9].

The nutrient-rich soil area influenced by plant roots, i.e. rhizosphere, hosts an array of soil-born and root-associated microbiome and organisms that are often beneficial to plant nutrition, health, and quality [13]. These organisms are attracted to the root secretions and rhizodeposits; variation in the secretions and deposits gives shape to certain degrees of species-specificity in the type and diversity of the association that is harbored by the plant species. Interactions promoting the growth of the host plant stem from different mechanisms, ranging from improved nutrient acquisition to hormonal stimulation. Arbuscular Mycorrhizal (AM) fungi as root obligate biotroph symbionts are estimated to exchange mutual benefits with nearly 80% of plant species [14]. They may provide the host plant with water, nutrients, and pathogen protection, in exchange for plant photoassimilates; they are, hence, considered natural biofertilizers. The AM fungi may solubilize phosphate from either organic or inorganic bound phosphate, making P more readily available to the host plant [13].

The focus of the researches on the plant-based nutraceuticals, functional foods, and pharmaceuticals has primarily been on plant’s own ingredients. But, production of bioactive phytochemicals has been attributed recently, at least in part, to associated microbes or through interaction with their host [13]. Though, no sufficient data is available on the significance of the AM in the reliance on the production of osmoticums and phytochemicals by the water-deprived host plants. There is still little knowledge concerning the contribution of root-associated AM to borage’s adaptation to drought. The aim of this study was, therefore, to shed light on the influence of AM colonization on drought response of borage, with particular interest in its water relations, osmotic, phenolic and flavonoids contents, photosynthetic and growth attributes.

Materials and methods

Two pot experiments were conducted under natural open-air conditions, where borage plants were exposed to prolonged drought conditions in the presence of AM inoculation.

Experiment 1

This experiment was carried out as a 3-replicates randomized complete block design pot experiment, where two borage genotypes (namely ‘Pakan’ and ‘Dashtyar’) were exposed to three levels of irrigation water (namely non-drought control, moderate drought, and severe drought) and two levels of AM ((Funneliformis mosseae) (T.H. Nicolson & Gerd.) C. Walker & A. Schuessler)) inoculation (namely non-AM and AM-inoculated) in 2021. Pakan and Dashtyar seeds were obtained from the Isfahan PakanBazr Seed Company and Isfahan Dashtyar Seed Company, respectively, Isfahan, central Iran. The healthy and uniform seeds of two B. officinalis genotypes, were surface-sterilized with a 5% sodium hypochlorite (NaOCl) solution for 5 min, and then rinsed thoroughly with distilled water. The seeds were germinated and planted in seedling trays containing autoclaved coco peat in a controlled environment chamber (23 ºC day/16 ºC night, 16 h light/8 h dark, and 50 ± 5% relative humidity) for six weeks, when they were approximately in their 4–5 leaf stage. Four seedlings were transferred to a loam-textured experimental field soil (pH = 7.8, electrical conductivity (EC) = 4.1 dS/m, organic matters = 1.1%, P = 13.5 mg/kg, N = 0.44%, K = 175 mg/kg, Fe = 3.7 mg/kg, Zn = 1.5 mg/kg), in pots containing 7 kg of the soil. Soil inoculation to the AM was done by mixing a 20 g inoculant at the 5 cm soil depth of each pot prior to planting. The F. mosseae fungal species was obtained from TURAN Biotechnology Company, Semnan, Iran. This material contained hyphae, soil, and approximately 100 fungal spores/g.

Plants of all pots (i.e. experimental units) were irrigated similarly with sufficient amount of water prior to the 6-leaf stage. For determining the volume of the irrigation water and irrigation timing in the control (non-drought) pots, a tansiometer was installed into the soil of these pots. The control plants were irrigated at 50% depletion of maximum allowable depletion (MAD). The moderate and severe droughts were implemented by irrigating at 60% and 80% MAD, respectively. Since irrigation interval for all of the irrigation levels was the same (i.e. fixed irrigation interval-variable irrigation depth), the moderate and severe drought stress pots received a fraction of the volume of irrigation water of the control pots. In the “fixed irrigation interval-variable irrigation depth” scenario, all pots are irrigated at the same time with different water depths applied to the different irrigation levels. Hence, initiation of an irrigation event was based on soil water depletion in the crop root zone with MAD equal to 50%.

Chlorophyll (Chl) and carotenoids (Car) content were determined in fresh leaf segments of 200 mg, being extracted in 10 mL of 80% (v/v) acetone and centrifuging (5810R, Eppendorf Refrigerated Centrifuge, Germany) and reading absorbencies (U-1800 UV/VIS Spectrophotometer, Hitachi, Japan) of the acetone extracts at 663, 647, and 470 nm for Chl a, Chl b, and Car, respectively. Finally the Chl a + b concentration was calculated according to Lu et al., 2017 [16] and Car/Chla + b was determined.

Chlorophyll fluorescence was assessed by a fluorometer (Opti-Sciences OS30p Inc., Chlorophyll fluorometer, Hudson, NH, USA) using leaves that had been dark-adapted for 20 min. Maximum (Fm) and minimum (F0) fluorescence’s and Fv/Fm were determined following exposure to saturating light at an intensity of 3000 µmol (photon)/m2s for 2 s, where Fv = Fm – F0.

Relative water content (RWC) was measured according to Smart and Bingham, 1974 [17], using the formula: RWC = (FW–DW)/(TW–DW) × 100. Fresh weight (FW) of leaf discs were determined, their turgid weight (TW) was measured by maintaining them in distilled water for 4 h at room temperature, and then the dry weight (DW) was obtained by drying at 70 °C in an oven for 48 h.

Proline content was measured using the method of Bates et al., 1973 [18] on 500 mg of fresh leaf samples that had been grinded in a mortar with 10 mL of 120 mM sulfosalicylic acid, and homogenized. Finally, the supernatant was added to 2 mL of glacial acetic acid + 2 mL of acid ninhydrin and boiled at 100 °C for 1 h. The extraction was performed in 4 mL of toluene, absorbency was measured at 520 nm, the proline concentration was determined using a standard curve prepared from an L-proline standard, and expressed as µmole/g leaf FM.

Soluble sugars content was measured by homogenization of 300 mg of the fresh samples in 95% ethanol according to the procedure explained by Irigoyen et al., 1992 [19] and the details given in Shafeiee and Ehsanzadeh, 2019 [20] based on reading the absorption of the samples at 625 nm. A glucose standard curve was used for estimating TSC of the samples.

For measurement of total phenolic content (TPC), 500 mg samples were extracted in 95% Ethanol. Then, 200 µL of the solution was added to 1 mL of a 10% Folin-Ciocalteu reagent, and 800 µL of a 7.5% sodium carbonate was added to it and acted according to Singh et al., 2002 [21]. The solution was kept at room temperature for at least 30 min and until it turned bluish, which is an indicator of the TPC. Then, absorbencies at 765 nm were measured by the spectrophotometer, compared with the standard concentrations and expressed as mg g−1 leaf FM. Total flavonoids content (TFC) was assayed using the aluminium chloride reagent according to Zhang et al., 2015 [22] with some modifications. In the first step, 100 µL methanolic extract was mixed with 900 µL distilled water and 60 µL sodium nitrite solution (5%). Five min later, 120 µL aluminum chloride solution (10%) was added to the mixture and left at room temperature for 5 min and then, 400 µL sodium hydroxide solution (4%) was added to this solution. In the final step, 400 µL distilled water was also added to the mixture and kept at room temperature for 15 min. Then, the absorbance of each sample was recorded by the spectrophotometer at 510 nm based on a standard curve of quercetin.

For measuring membrane stability index (MSI), leaf discs of approximately 150 mg were obtained and placed in 10 mL of double-distilled water in two series of tubes. One series of the tubes were put in a hot water bath at 40 °C for 30 min and the second series were transferred to 100 °C for 10 min. Then, the electrical conductivity (EC) of samples was measured with EC meter (Model Cyberscan, Singapore). The MSI was calculated as described by Bajji et al., 2002 [23].

For measuring P, leaf samples were washed with distilled water to wipe out the dust and debris, dried at 72 °C for 48 h, 200 mg of the dried sample powdered in a pistil, and ashed at 550 °C for 6 h, digested in a 2 N hydrochloric acid at 100 °C, filtered, the filtered extract of 100 ml was obtained by the double-distilled water. For determining P concentration, the absorbency of the above-mentioned extract was measured at 870 nm by the spectrophotometer and expressed as mg/g leaf DM.

Plant above-ground dry mass (SDM) was determined by drying the harvested plants at 72 °C for 72 h. Root dry mass (Root DM) was determined by careful separation of the roots from the soil, washing, blot-drying by paper towel, weighing, and drying at 72 °C for 72 h. Plant’s total (root + shoot) dry mass (TDM) was calculated from the SDM and Root DM. RootV was assessed by adding the roots to a known volume of tap water in a measuring cylinder.

For assessing the AM colonization, plant root system was carefully separated from the soil by immersing them in a bucket of water and gently agitating the root to remove the soil. Clean fragmented roots were obtained and caught by carefully washing the roots with water from a hose over a 2 mm screen. The roots were preserved in Formalin-Acetic acid-Alcohol (FAA) plant fixative solution in tightly sealed plastic vials until further processing and measurements. For discoloring according to procedures detailed by Brundrett et al., 1996 [24] the roots were washed with water to rinse the alcohol and a KOH 10% (w/v) solution was used to clear the pigmented root sections of 1–2 cm in length. Root clearing was accomplished using glass containers in room temperature for more than 72 h. Cleared roots were caught on a fine sieve and rinsed with tap water and prepared for transferring into the staining solution. The cleared roots were stained with Trypan Blue with a concentration of 0.05% (w/v) for 72 h. For increasing the staining quality, the stained roots were subjected to destaining in 50% glycerol for 2–3 days to allow excess stain to leach from the roots. For estimating the proportion of roots that contain AM structures (arbuscules, vesicles, and AM hyphae) a gridline intersection procedure [25] with a dissecting light microscope (Nikon Eclipss E 600) was used.

Experiment 2

This experiment was conducted as a randomized complete block design pot experiment in 3-replicates, where two borage genotypes (namely ‘Pakan’ and ‘Dashtyar’) were exposed to two levels of irrigation water (namely non-drought control and moderate drought) and two levels of AM (F. mosseae) inoculation (namely non-AM and AM-inoculated) in 2021. The six-week old seedlings of the two borage genotypes were transferred to pots containing 8 kg of a sandy-loam soil. The experiment set-up, soil, pots, AM inoculation, and implementation of irrigation treatment were the same as the Experiment 1. Albeit, irrigation treatment consisted of only two levels, i.e. non-drought control (irrigation at 50% MAD) and moderate drought stress (irrigation at 60 MAD). Assessing the AM colonization, Chl and Car concentrations, Fv/Fm, P and proline concentrations, RWC, TPC and TFC, MSI, root volume (RootV), Root DM, and SDM were assessed according the procedures explained for Experiment 1.

Statistical analyses

Data gathered from the two pot experiments were subjected separately to analyses of variances through General Linear Model of SAS (SAS Institute Inc., Version 9.4, Cary, North Carolina, USA). Mean comparisons of each of the experiments were conducted using the Least Significant Difference at 0.05 level of statistical significance (LSD0.05). Pair-wise correlation coefficients of a number of examined traits in Experiment 1 were calculated for non-drought vs. severe drought and non-AM vs. AM inoculation conditions using PROC CORR of SAS. Principal component analysis (PCA) was conducted to identify important criteria with greater contribution to principal component in Experiment 1.

AM × irrigation interaction

Leaf Chl a (Table 3), Chl b (Fig. 2a), and Fv/Fm (Table 3) of the drought-stressed plants were declined both in the presence and absence of AM inoculation. Though, while decreases of Chl a and Chl b concentrations of the non-AM plants were greater, that of Fv/Fm of the AM-inoculated plants were greater averaged over the two genotypes. In contrary to the above photosynthetic attributes, leaf Car concentration (Table 3 Fig. 2b) and Car/Chla + b (Table 3, Fig. 2c) tended to enhance upon exposure to drought; the extent of enhancement was greater in the non-AM plants, compared to the AM-inoculated ones. Root DM of the drought-stressed plants was suppressed but the degree of the suppression for the non-AM plants was smaller than the AM-inoculated ones (Table 3).

Genotype × AM interaction

AM colonization of the inoculated plants was enhanced but the degree of the enhancement for genotype ‘Pakan’ (increase from nearly 11% to 30%) was greater than ‘Dashtyar’ (increase from nearly 9% to 27%) (Table 3). Leaf Chl b (Table 3) and Fv/Fm (Table 3) of both genotypes were enhanced upon AM inoculation, the extent of enhancements was greater in the genotype ‘Pakan’, than ‘Dashtyar’. Leaf TSC concentration of both genotypes decreased in response to AM inoculation, the extent of decrease was greater in genotype ‘Pakan’, than ‘Dashtyar’ (Table 3). The TPC of genotype ‘Dashtyar’ tended to increase but that of the ‘Pakan’ remained unchanged in response to AM (Table 3). The AM-induced increase of TPC was observed rather in the drought-stressed plants, than the non-stressed ones. Leaf P concentration of both genotypes increased when grown in the presence of AM, the extent of increase being greater for the genotype ‘Dashtyar’, compared to ‘Pakan’ (Table 3).

Correlation and principle component analyses for non-drought vs. drought and non-AM vs. AM inoculation conditions

Correlation analyses were suggestive of positive associations between Chl a and RWC, on one hand, and between Fv/Fm and MSI of the non-stressed plants on the other (Table 4). Moreover, while Fv/Fm of the non-stressed plants was negatively correlated with TSC concentration, RWC was positively correlated with P concentration.

Discussions

Drought suppresses phosphorus, photosynthetic, physiological, and shoot and root growth attributes of borage

The observed depression in the borage’s P concentration under drought conditions (Tables 3 and 6) stemmed from the alterations in the nutrients dynamics of the soil solution. With depletion of soil moisture, not only soil nutrient supply by mineralization but nutrient diffusion and mass flow in the soil are also hindered, resulting often in a lowered roots nutrient uptake under drought conditions [10]. Drought may suppress the P mineralization and hence decrease the release of inorganic phosphorus from the P bound to organic matter of the soil [26]. Soil phosphatase activity is not independent from its moisture content, as alkaline and particularly acid phosphatases activities are suppressed under drought conditions [27]. Drought-associated decreases in uptake of minerals such as N, P, K, Fe, Zn and hence plant minerals content of bread wheat, chickpea, and lentil have been reported [28]. Based on the above-mentioned literature and our data indicating decreases in P concentration in the examined borage genotypes, it is reasonable to conclude that a reduced activity of the soil phosphatases activities has led to a decrease in the P availability and hence its concentration in the stressed plants.

An aggravated Chl fluorescence (mirroring a non-photochemical quenching) results in a decreased Fv/Fm in the stressed plants. Hence, suppression of the Fv/Fm in the stressed borage plants of the present study (Table 3) might have been an outcome of the presumed oxidative damages to the photosynthetic functions. Electron transport machinery functioning in the mitochondria and chloroplasts lead to the generation of reactive oxygen species (ROS); albeit, a maintained ROS production under normal circumstances plays role rather in signaling than in oxidative damage [29]. Nonetheless, an aggravated production of ROS under stressed conditions leads generally to the induction of oxidative damages. These damages are initially imposed to the electron transport of the photosynthesis, where they impede the function of the photosystems in the photosynthetic apparatus. The decreased concentrations of Chl a (Tables 3 and 6) and Chl b (Tables 3 and 6, Fig. 2a) are further indications of the photosynthetic penalties of drought in the examined borage genotypes. Even though stress-induced decrease in Chl concentration may be a result of either Chl degradation or a lowered Chl synthesis, it often stems from the lowering of Chl synthesis [30]. We did not measure the ROS status of the borage plants herein, but the decreased MSI of the drought-stricken plants (Fig. 1d) is suggestive of membrane damages due perhaps to an imbalance in the ROS production under drought conditions. Increase in the H2O2 concentration of the salt-stressed borage plants was associated to increases in the MDA concentration and the loss of cell membrane integrity [29]. Increased MDA concentration is often associated with lipid peroxidation and, hence, membrane damage [31].

The foregone drought-induced depressions in Chl concentration, Fv/Fm, and MSI were accompanied to decreases in RWC (Fig. 1a) and, in the same time, increases in the proline (Fig. 1c, Table 6) and TSC (Fig. 1b) concentrations. This suggests that the adverse alterations in Root DM (Tables 2, 3, and 6), SDM (Fig. 3c, Table 6), and TDM (Fig. 3d, Table 6) were related to the altered water status, whose inevitable consequent Chl damages and photosynthetic lowering are documented in different plant species [32]. Positive correlative associations between RWC and plant growth attributes such as SDM, TDM, and Root DM (Table 4) are also suggestive of importance of water status sustainment in lessening the adverse effects of drought stress. Our data indicates that drought-induced suppression of SDM was more notable than the suppression of the Root DM and RootV (Fig. 3b) in the examined borage genotypes. Drought reduces the shoot growth more seriously than the root, leading to decrease in the shoot:root ratio of the drought-stricken plants across different plant species [33]. As has been found with plant species from 15 different genera (34), adaptive regulation of the shoot:root growth ratio is a developmental mechanism evolutionarily conserved across terrestrial plant species, meaning that under conditions where water and mineral nutrients are not limiting, shoot growth is favored. Consequently, under limited water and nutrient conditions, plants are predicted to have a low shoot:root ratio as a strategy towards securing resource acquisition. A decreased specific root length of drought-stressed cauliflower was interpreted as an outcome of higher dry matter deposition in the fine root fraction, leading to a lowered shoot:root ratio, compared to the non-stressed plants [34]. Observation of lack of difference in allocating photoassimilates into roots, despite a decrease in root elongation led to the conclusion that drought-stressed plants avoid longitudinal rhizosphere extension, compared to the non-stressed maize plants [35]. Based on the above-mentioned literature, we may conclude that the present drought-stressed borage plants (particularly those of the genotype ‘Pakan’) have invested in the soil exploitation capabilities, i.e. through production of fine roots, at the cost of soil exploration capacity. Different water conservation tools including leaf rolling and abscission, stomatal closure, and increased root mass particularly at deeper layers maintain water status in plants, and are necessary for obtaining higher crop yield and biomass [36]. Thus, it may be surmised that genotype ‘Pakan’ has benefited from its greater RootV and Root DM to uptake water and P, maintain the RWC, and consequently indicate greater SDM and TDM compared to genotype ‘Dashtyar’.

Mycorrhizal inoculation enhances phosphorus acquirement, photosynthetic, physiological, and growth attributes of borage

Drought causes a strong decrease in total root exudations and biotic flora in dry soil, as it reduces the root growth and the zone where exudates are released [35]. Thus, the decrease in the AM colonization of the drought-stressed borage plants (Table 3) was not far from our expectation. Nonetheless, response of the examined borage genotypes, particularly genotype ‘Pakan’, to the AM inoculation was in the range reported for some other plant species [32]. Soil exploration and exploitation capabilities of the root system matter to the root uptake of water and nutrients [37] and help the plants to avoid drought stress. Soil exploration is defined as the size of the soil domain explored by the roots. Root traits modulating its architecture (i.e. axial root growth angle, growth rate, number of axial roots, lateral root branching) as well as degree of AM colonization alter soil exploration capacity [37]. Mycorrhizae formation may compensate for the lacuna of root hairs. Exploration is expected to be more determining either if soil profile is poor in terms of resources or when resources are only available at greater depths. Soil exploitation indicates how extensively resources are obtained within a given soil domain. Root hairs, exudates released by the root, status of nutrient transporters, intensity of AM colonization, and extent of root-soil contact contribute to soil exploitation [37]. As anthropogenically degraded agricultural soils are often characterized by a lowered diversity and presence of AM, re-establishing the natural level of AM richness can ameliorate the graveness of degradation. One of the viable strategies that can be adopted to achieve the goal of sustainable agriculture is the direct re-introduction of AM propagules (inoculum) into a target soil, particularly because it represents a valid alternative to conventional fertilization practices [14]. AM fungi are capable of enhancing plant mineral nutrient acquisition, particularly in nutrient-poor soils [14]. Both of the borage genotypes showed a notable degree of plasticity in root traits and AM colonization in response to both water availability and AM inoculation, with those of genotype ‘Pakan’ tending to be more pronounced. Roots of mycorrhizal trifoliate orange (Poncirus trifoliata L.) seedlings indicated an enhanced expression of plasma membrane’s H+-ATPase, whose acidification function through H+ efflux into cell wall space is necessary for optimal primary root growth and root hair development [38]. Thus, the AM-induced improvements in RootV (Fig. 3b) and Root DM (Tables 3 and 6) of the non-stressed and drought-stressed borage plants of the present study may have stemmed at least partially from an enhanced expression of the plasma membrane’s H+-ATPase genes and, thereby, root development.

In addition to the above-mentioned alterations in the root morphology and architecture, AM-associated downregulations in the plant cell aquaporins genes and, consequently, prevention of water loss and sustainment of RWC have been documented [39]. Therefore, the greater AM colonization intensity of genotype ‘Pakan’ may have had implications in downregulation of aquaporins and the greater RWC, at least under drought conditions (Fig. 1a). Despite the fact that an increased Pi availability does not necessarily lead to a growth improvement, AM symbiosis clearly induces the expression of plant Pi transporters and the fungus is capable of transferring the Pi to the host plant [14]. The AM-induced increase in the P concentration of the borage plants examined herein (Tables 3 and 6) confirms the involvement of the fungus in the mineral nutrition of this medicinal plant species. As it has been documented for crop species such as Medicago truncatula Gaertn. [40], drought accentuated the involvement of AM in P uptake by the borage plants of the present study. It may be deduced that AM colonization of the borage plants of the present study contributed to soil exploration and exploitation, resulting in an enhanced uptake of the somewhat immobile P from the soil and its increased concentration in the plants particularly under stress conditions. We did not attempt measuring the K content of borage plants of the present study; though, not only evidence for up-regulation of a plant K transporter has been reported in mycorrhizal plant roots, the AM-induced increase in K has been also found to be positively correlated to plant tolerance to stresses such as salinity and drought [14]. Mycorrhizal stimulation of photosynthetic attributes, photosynthetic rate and efficiency was found to be associated with stimulation of shoot dry weight and percent of root colonization [41]. Furthermore, mycorrhizal-induced alleviation of plant dry mass penalties of drought stressed maize (Zea mays L.) was brought about in part by enhancement of Fv/Fm [39]. Our data agree with the mentioned literature, as AM inoculation of borage plants led to enhancement of leaf Chl (Fig. 2a, Tables 3 and 6) and Fv/Fm (Table 3) and these increases were concomitant to increased leaf P concentration (Tables 3 and 6), SDM (Fig. 3c), and TDM (Fig. 3d). According to some recent studies [32], AM colonization is rather beneficial to the photosynthetic functions of stressed C3 plants than C4 ones. Our results are in line to the above findings, as they reveal for the first time that photosynthetic attributes such as Chl (Fig. 2a, Tables 3 and 6) and Fv/Fm (Table 3) of the C3 borage respond positively to AM colonization. Positive correlative associations between leaf P concentration and plant growth attributes such as RootV and Root DM (Table 5) are also suggestive of importance of AM in improving rooting attributes and thereby plant access to mineral nutrients, including soil P.

Mycorrhizal inoculation and drought improve carotenoids, phenolics, flavonoids, and osmoregulative measures of borage

Plants have various defense mechanisms for coping with different stress conditions, including accumulating a wide array of secondary metabolites as well as photoprotective substances. These metabolites are often involved in protective functions of the stressed plants through antioxidative activities. Among such metabolites are TPC (Table 3) and TFC (Fig. 3a), whose concentrations were notably increased in the present borage genotypes, upon exposure to drought. Phenolic compounds are found in all tissues of higher plants and known to be the largest group of bioactive secondary metabolites [42]. They play an important role in plant adaptations to environmental stresses and eliciting defense mechanisms. Phenolic compounds are products of the phenylpropanoid pathway, where the activity of phenylalanine ammonia-lyase (PAL) is key to increased production of these compounds [43]. Among several compounds produced by the mentioned pathway, phenolic acids and flavonoids indicate antioxidant activity and hence render the plants suitable as sources of nutraceuticals, pharmaceuticals, and functional foods. Drought-induced increase in the TPC in the examined borage genotypes (Table 3) indicates that this multipurpose species is a good source of production of bioactive compounds, particularly when grown in the presence of drought. The above finding is in line to the report of Bulgari et al., 2017 [42], indicating borage is a rich source of bioactive compounds with potential applicability as biostimulants for enhancing different physiological, biochemical, growth, and agronomic attributes of different plant species. The ROS scavenging capacity of the examined borage genotypes, mirrored by the notable increase in TPC, particularly in the stressed plants, is in line to the findings about the neuroprotective capabilities of borage flower extract in relation to some model organisms [6]. Previous studies also demonstrated that secondary products such as TPC and TFC were remarkably enhanced in the stressed safflower (Carthamus tinctorius L.) [44], thyme (Thymus daenensis Celak.) [45], artichoke (Cynara cardunculus L. subsp. scolymus) and cardoon (Cynara cardunculus L. var. altilis) plants [43]. Another indication of a boosted defensive capability subsequent to drought exposure of borage was reflected in the increased Car concentration (Fig. 2b, Tables 2 and 6) and Car/Chla + b (Fig. 2c, Tables 2 and 6) of the stressed plants of both examined genotypes. Car is involved either in the photosynthesis (i.e. as an accessory component for light absorption by the photosystems) or in the photoprotection of chloroplast membranes and photosystems therein upon exposure to excessive light [46, 47]. Thus, the increased Car concentration of the stressed borage plants of the present study can be thought of as a preventive measure against the presumed damage of excessive light energy. Changes in pigment ratios, i.e. Car/Chla + b, bring about acclimation and adaptation to different environmental conditions [47]. Although a decrease in Car/Chla + b is suggestive of adaptation towards increased light capture, an increase in the Car/Chla + b may enable the stressed plants to dissipate excess energy harvested by the light-harvesting complexes. The increased Car/Chla + b of the drought-stricken borage plants herein is, presumably, an evidence for adaptation and/or acclimation in favor of dissipation of excess energy absorbed by the photosynthetic pigments of the stressed plants. Stress-induced suppression of leaf RWC is an indication of an altered water status and, hence, a maintained leaf RWC implies plant’s tolerance to drought. At the cellular level, water loss marks the first event of drought stress. Upon an occurrence of drought, roots sense water insufficiency from soil and trigger a systemic communication system [8]. Water inadequacy, in turn, triggers drought signals which promote the production of stress-protectant metabolites such as proline (Fig. 1c) and soluble carbohydrates (Fig. 1b), enhance the antioxidant system to maintain redox homeostasis, and prevent acute damage to membranes and cytosolic functions by upregulation of antioxidative enzymes activities [8]. Our data are suggestive of occurrence and activation of all of the above-mentioned events in the borage, upon its exposure to the prolonged drought. Osmolytes accumulation in stressed plants is often taken as evidence for the contribution of these osmolytes in osmoregulation and ROS scavenging. The extent of this osmoregulation response of the stressed plants varies not only among species, but also may depend on genotype [30]. Osmoregulation is mainly manifested by increased cellular concentrations of compatible solutes, such as soluble sugars and free amino acids. Sufficient evidence in support of undertaking osmoregulation by drought-stressed borage plants of the present study was gathered, where substantial and modest increases in the concentration of soluble sugars and proline, respectively, was observed in both of the examined genotypes (Fig. 1b, Fig. 1c). The leaves of the stressed plants undertake abscisic acid biosynthesis and, in turn, respond by stomatal closure and maintained RWC and water balance. It seems that osmoregulative measures, depicted in TSC increase and RWC sustainment, were taken more effectively in the drought-stricken plants of genotype ‘Pakan’ than the ‘Dashtyar’.

Apart from drought-induced enhancement in the antioxidative and osmoregulation functions, symbiotic associations may also promote flavonoid production in the plants [13]. Mycorrhizal plants of lettuce indicated improved mineral and antioxidative compounds [48]. Moreover, elevated levels of PAL in plant roots colonized by AM [49] and differential flavonoids accumulation upon differences in plant growth organ and AM species participating in colonization [50] is documented in some plant species. From the increased TPC (Table 3) and TFC (Fig. 3a) in AM-inoculated borage plants under drought conditions, it appears that the proposed enhancement of PAL is accountable for the boosted antioxidative capabilities of this plant species. One of the beneficial effects of plant-AM symbiosis is the stimulation of accumulation of solutes and hence sustainment of an optimal water balance in the stressed plants. A meta-analysis on the extent of AM involvement in the osmoregulation response of salt-stressed plants indicated that despite the lack of prloine accumulation, soluble carbohydrates with 19–28% increases were a major player in the osmoregulation of the mycorrhizal plants under stress conditions [51]. The borage’s yield penalty in the present study seemed to be dependent on the degree of osmoregulative and antioxidative measures in response to water insufficiency and in relation to degree of mycorrhizal associations. Evidence in support of the above inference was several-fold. The evidence included superiority of the AM-inoculated plants of genotype ‘Pakan’ in growth attributes such as RootV (Fig. 3b), SDM (Fig. 3c), and TDM (Fig. 3d), greater RWC (Fig. 1a), TSC (Fig. 1b), and to some extent proline (Table 6) concentration, and more notable TPC (Table 3), TFC (Fig. 3a), and Car concentrations and Car/Chla + b (Table 6), compared to genotype ‘Dashtyar’. Moreover, the greater intensity of AM colonization in genotype ‘Pakan’ (Table 3) may have contributed to its greater TPC and TFC and hence antioxidative capability and stress tolerance. The greater capacity for accumulation of the latter antioxidative compounds of ‘Pakan’ may have, further, been concomitant to increase in the activity of the antioxidative enzymes, as mycorrhizal-associated increases in the activity of catalase and superoxide dismutase in the drought-stricken plants of a C3 grass (Leymus chinensis Trin.) has been documented [32]. From the PCA analyses results, we may infer that inoculation to AM in both examined genotypes enhances the drought tolerance, mainly by improvement in TSC, proline, and Car/Chla + b (Fig. 4a). Albeit, from the PCA analyses results in relation to genotype ‘Pakan’, it seems that the above-mentioned AM-induced enhancement in the TSC and Car/Chla + b is achieved at the cost of plant dry mass production capacity. The latter conclusion was drawn from the positive association of SDM and TDM in the non-AM plants of this genotype, particularly under drought condition (Fig. 4b).

The presented data, taken together, depict mycorrhizal inoculation as an agronomic strategy promising for increasing the quantity and quality of borage products applicable in microgreens, nutraceuticals, and pharmaceuticals production.

Conclusions

We examined the suitability of mycorrhizal inoculation to mitigate the adverse effects of drought stress in borage and present it as a strategy of choice in the face of the inevitable water insecurity in the arid-semiarid conditions. Even though drought-imposed adverse modifications in photosynthetic components, water status, and shoot and root growth attributes were notable, examined AM species increased the potential fitness of borage plants under drought stress by lessening the drought penalties of water status, photosynthetic, and root and shoot growth attributes. Our data indicates that a targeted mycorrhizal treatment could benefit the photosynthetic, biochemical, and growth attributes and hence F. mosseae AM species represents a tool to alleviate the effects of drought in borage. Adoption of alternative medicinal species such as borage in combination to encouragement of mycorrhizal symbiosis was proven herein as a viable agronomic opportunity that fully meets our today’s endeavor to improve the quality of human health and nutrition. The presented findings will have implications in organic production of nutraceuticals, pharmaceuticals, and microgreens from borage with the aim of supplying natural medicinal products and healthier foods.
