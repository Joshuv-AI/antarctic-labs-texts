Evaluation of the Coronal Plane Alignment of the Knee (CPAK) classification in arthritic knees following robotic total knee arthroplasty
Authors: Jai Thilak, Mohammad Salil, Gayathri Jyothish, Vivek Joy
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundThe Coronal Plane Alignment of the Knee (CPAK) classification describes knee morphology using arithmetic hip-knee-ankle angle (aHKA) and joint line obliquity (JLO). Currently there is uncertainty about the relevance and restoration of coronal alignment following total knee arthroplasty (TKA), particularly among Asian populations. This study examined changes in coronal alignment following robotic TKA using functional alignment, along with concordance between CPAK JLO and knee joint line obliquity (KJLO) – this would help develop strategies to optimize outcomes of TKA among Asian patients.MethodsA retrospective analysis was undertaken for 333 knees that received cruciate-retaining robotic TKA during 2019–2023. Pre- and 6-week postoperative radiographs were used to assess aHKA, JLO, CPAK phenotypes, and KJLO. Maintenance of constitutional CPAK alignment and crossover in aHKA and JLO categories were estimated. The concordance between CPAK JLO and KJLO at pre- and post-operative levels were also estimated.ResultsPreoperatively, 72% showed varus aHKA, 82% apex distal JLO, and CPAK type I was predominant (63%). Postoperatively, varus alignment persisted in 50% and CPAK type IV in 32%. Constitutional aHKA and JLO was maintained in 46% and 42% knees, respectively. Less than 7% patients crossed over to a category beyond neutral considering aHKA or JLO. The most frequent phenotypic change was from CPAK type I to IV. Concordance between CPAK JLO and KJLO increased from 14% to 41% postoperatively, with highest agreement for apex proximal knees and lowest for apex distal knees.ConclusionsWhile constitutional CPAK was maintained in less than 50% patients, overcorrection in CPAK was minimal. Therefore, functional alignment with robotic assistance facilitates optimization by limiting extreme adjustments to constitutional CPAK following TKA.

Background

The coronal plane alignment of the knee (CPAK) classification system developed by Macdessi and colleagues was introduced to facilitate personalized approaches in total knee arthroplasty (TKA) by understanding the constitutional knee alignment [1]. It comprises a 3 × 3 matrix of knee phenotypes based on joint line obliquity (JLO) and the arithmetic hip-knee-ankle angle (aHKA). Since its advent, several studies have indicated considerable overlap in the distribution of CPAK phenotypes between healthy and arthritic individuals across ethnicities [2]. Recent reports also evaluated the restoration of constitutional CPAK alignment along with achieving a well-balanced knee, however, there’s conflicting evidence regarding its impact on patient outcomes and survivorship [3]. Interestingly Keltz et al. showed that only the patients that crossover postoperatively beyond the neutral category experience poorer functional outcomes [4]. While CPAK provides a framework for constitutional alignment, its relationship with Knee Joint Line Obliquity (KJLO) remains poorly understood, particularly in arthritic Asian populations. Current evidence regarding the concordance between these two measures is limited to non-arthritic cohorts. Understanding this relationship is essential for optimizing robotic-assisted functional alignment strategies, which aim to preserve the soft-tissue envelope while managing joint line orientation [5, 6].

Another consideration for TKA in ensuring proper alignment and function is the knee joint line obliquity (KJLO). It refers to the angle of the knee joint line relative to the horizontal plane. It is considered crucial in understanding knee biomechanics - excessive obliquity can lead to uneven load distribution, cartilage wear, and osteoarthritis progression [7]. The KJLO provides both a numerical estimate and the orientation of the obliquity (e.g., apex proximal, neutral, or distal). As KJLO is unaffected by constitutional varus deformities, it is considered one of the most reliable methods for evaluating knee joint inclination [8, 9]. We wondered whether KJLO and CPAK JLO are closely associated in determining the same apex position. To the best of our knowledge, the only other study on this topic found a weak relationship between KJLO and CPAK JLO in healthy or minimally arthritic patients [10]. Interestingly, the concordant (with same apex position rendered via CPAK JLO and KJLO) and discordant (with different apex positions rendered via CPAK JLO and KJLO) groups did not differ in CPAK JLO but were significantly differed with respect to some of the other measures including KJLO and aHKA, indicating inherent differences between them. Given that the study did not include severely arthritic patients and lacked postoperative measurements, we wanted to further investigate the relationship between the two measures.

The purpose of this retrospective study was to evaluate postoperative changes in aHKA, JLO, and CPAK phenotype in patients who underwent TKA using robotic assistance and functional alignment. We hypothesized that this approach would minimize changes to the constitutional knee alignment. Additionally, we sought to evaluate the mutual concordance between CPAK JLO and KJLO in determining the apex position.

Methods

This retrospective study involved patient who underwent TKA from 2019 to 2023, following approval from institutional ethics committee. Patients above 50 years, with severe knee osteoarthritis with radiographic data were included. Patients with both varus and valgus deformities were included. The Functional Alignment (FA) principles remained consistent across all deformity types: the surgical goal was to restore the patient’s constitutional joint line orientation while maintaining component positioning within the established ‘safe zones’. For valgus knees, the lateral compartment was balanced primarily through anatomical bone resections rather than extensive soft tissue releases, matching the implant position to the patient’s native anatomy. the patient selection (Table 1).

Surgical procedure

All patients underwent primary cruciate retaining cemented TKA (Triathlon, Stryker) with robotic assistance (Mako Robotic Arm, Stryker). All surgeries were performed by a single senior orthopaedic surgeon using a medial parapatellar approach and a functional alignment technique. Initial implant positioning was planned based on preoperative CT scans to achieve the following “safe zone” boundaries: 0°–5° varus for the tibia and 3° valgus to 3° varus for the femur. Femoral flexion of 0°–5°, Tibial slope of 3°–5° and femoral rotation of 0°–3°. Intraoperatively, the functional alignment workflow was executed as follows: Gap Assessment: Using the robotic software, medial and lateral gaps were assessed in full extension and at 90° of flexion. Balancing Goals: Our primary goal was to achieve a stable medial joint line while respecting the natural lateral laxity. Medial gaps in flexion and extension were targeted at 0–2 mm. For the lateral compartment, we aimed for a more compliant gap of 3–6 mm in flexion. Refinement: If gaps were imbalanced, implant adjustments to the resection planes (within the defined safe zones) were performed to achieve balance without the need for extensive soft-tissue releases.

Radiological assessment

Pre-operative and 6-week post-operative long-leg standing X-ray radiographs were obtained using a digital radiography system. Patients were positioned barefoot with full weight bearing and in extension of knees. The medial proximal tibial angle (MPTA), lateral distal femur angle (LDFA) and KJLO were measured by two independent observers to calculate inter-observer reliability of these manually calculated measurements.

The MPTA was calculated as the medial angle between the proximal tibial joint line tangent and the mechanical axis of the tibia. The LDFA was calculated as the lateral angle between the distal femoral joint line tangent and the mechanical axis of the femur. Constitutional limb alignment (aHKA) was defined as aHKA = MPTA – LDFA. A negative aHKA indicated varus alignment, and a positive aHKA indicated valgus alignment. The coronal boundaries for neutral aHKA was 0°±2°, varus aHKA, <-2°, and valgus aHKA, >+2°. The CPAK JLO was calculated as MPTA + LDFA. The coronal boundaries for neutral JLO was 180°±3°, apex distal JLO, < 177°, and apex proximal JLO, > 183°. Based on these definitions, the knees were classified into nine distinct phenotypes [1].

The KJLO demonstrated the position of the proximal tibial plateau relative to the ground and was assessed using the joint line orientation angle by tibial plateau (JLOAT) measurement [11]. The medial side was considered the apex of the joint. A neutral KJLO boundary was defined as 0°±2° [10]. An apex position deviation of 2° or more was classified as either apex proximal or apex distal, while values within this range were considered apex neutral [10].

Outcomes and data analysis

The key outcomes were the proportion of knees in which constitutional coronal alignment, mainly, aHKA and JLO, were maintained postoperatively. Considering the method recently used by van de Graaf and colleagues [6], the target restoration of aHKA was defined as a difference in pre-operative and post-operative values of ≤ 2°. Similarly, the target restoration of JLO was defined as a difference in pre-operative and post-operative values of ≤ 3°. Crossover to a category beyond neutral for aHKA and JLO was also evaluated as described by Keltz et al. [4]. Both the measures of maintenance and crossover (beyond neutral category) of the constitutional aHKA and JLO were expressed as proportion of knees. The pre-operative and post-operative aHKA, JLO, and CPAK phenotypes were also compared to evaluate the changes. Another key outcome was the estimate of mutual concordance between CPAK JLO and KJLO i.e., instances when apex position was same using both CPAK JLO and KJLO methods. The mutual concordance was expressed as proportion of knees.

The distribution of continuous variables was assessed for normality using the Shapiro-Wilk test. As the data were found to be non-normally distributed, categorical variables were expressed as frequencies and percentages, while continuous variables were presented as median and interquartile range (IQR; Q1–Q3). The Wilcoxon Signed-rank test was employed to evaluate the statistical significance of changes between pre-operative and post-operative continuous radiological measurements. For categorical changes in CPAK phenotypes and apex positions, McNemar’s Chi-square test and Pearson’s Chi-square test were utilized. The level of statistical significance was considered as P < 0.05. All statistical analyses were performed using IBM SPSS software version 21.0.

Results

Demographics, baseline characteristics, and the distribution of radiological parameters are provided in (Table 2). Inter-observer reliability analysis revealed varying levels of agreement across the different radiographic parameters (Table 3). Notably, post-operative LDFA and pre-operative KJLO showed excellent agreement (ICC > 0.90), while MPTA measurements reflected poor to moderate reliability, consistent with the challenges of landmark identification in arthritic populations. The low reliability for pre-operative LDFA may be attributed to the presence of significant distal femoral bone loss in the severe valgus/varus cases in our cohort. All CPAK phenotypes (I through IX) were represented in the study population, confirming the versatility of the functional alignment approach across different constitutional alignment patterns. The most frequent pre-operative CPAK phenotype was types I (62.8%) and the post-operative was type IV (31.5%)(Fig. 1). The predominant JLO was apex distal, in 81.1%, and varus in 69.1%. The median (Q1-Q3) absolute HKA at baseline was 11.75° (7°−15.5°), which decreased to 2.75° (1.5°−4.3°) post-operatively (P < 0.001). The median (Q1-Q3) absolute JLO at baseline was 173.5° (171.1∘−176.4∘), which increased to 180.1° (177.6°−181.6°) post-operatively (P < 0.001).

Change in coronal alignment parameters and phenotypes

The distribution of preoperative coronal alignment based on aHKA and JLO changed significantly following TKA (P < 0.001). Despite the changes, varus alignment was dominant postoperatively (47.7%) similar to the pre-operative distribution. Postoperatively, neutral JLO was the dominant type (67.3%). The overall constitutional aHKA was restored postoperatively in 46.4% knees and only 6.3% patients the crossed over to a category beyond neutral (i.e., change from varus to valgus and vice versa). Similarly, the constitutional JLO was maintained postoperatively in 42.4% knees and only 6.9% patients crossed over beyond the neutral category. Given the predominance among Indian population, phenotype type I was evaluated closely. In type I, aHKA was restored in 22.5% and JLO was restored in 12.9% (Fig. 2). However crossover in aHKA (to type III, VI, IX) and in JLO (to type VII, VIII, IX) was observed in only 6.2% knees for both.

The CPAK phenotype remained unchanged post-operatively for 17.7% type I, 11.8% type II, 46.7% type IV, 43.5% type V, and 33.3% type VI, and 100% type VIII, but none for type III knees. Most of the type III knees realigned to either type V (22.2%) or VI (44.4%). Among all, the most frequent phenotypic change was from type I to type IV in 37.3% knees. Due to the uneven number of CPAK classes i.e., 8 classes preoperatively and 9 postoperatively - statistical testing was done after excluding the less frequent classes.

Concordance between CPAK JLO and KJLO

The pre-operative distribution according to KJLO was 106 knees as apex neutral, 32 knees as apex distal, and 195 knees as apex proximal. On the other hand, according to CPAK, the JLO distribution was 62 knees as neutral, 270 knees as apex distal and 1 knee as apex proximal. The overall pre-operative concordance between KJLO and CPAK JLO was 13.6%, with statistically significant difference in the distributions (P < 0.001). The Fig. 3a shows the pre-operative concordance was highest for neutral (30.6%) and lowest for apex proximal (0).

The post-operative distribution according to KJLO was 144 knees as apex neutral, 20 knees as apex distal, and 169 knees as apex proximal. Discussion

Following robotic-assisted TKA using functional alignment, prominent changes were seen in the CPAK phenotype, aHKA, and JLO in this study. The constitutional aHKA and JLO were restored in 46% and 42% knees, respectively, and varus alignment was dominant both pre- and post-operatively. Further analysis revealed that overcorrection in coronal alignment was minimal - less than 7% knees crossed over to a category beyond neutral, considering aHKA or JLO. The tendency to crossover was observed in cases with varus or valgus close to the neutral category. These findings were similar in the predominant CPAK type I with varus alignment and apex distal JLO, which comprised majority (209 out of 333 knees) of the study population - only 6.2% with native type I knees crossed over postoperatively. In a previous report on robotic-assisted TKA [12], restoration of native CPAK was more frequent among patients with functional alignment (53.9%) than those with mechanical alignment (25.9%; p < 0.05). Yet another study reported the rate of restoration of the CPAK class after robotic-assisted functionally aligned TKA to be approximately 50% [13] - this is similar to the restoration rate found in our study with Asian patients.

It appears that functional alignment and robotic assistance are more consistently associated with improvements in clinical outcomes; however, preservation of native coronal alignment may not always translate into such a benefit [12]. Robotic-assisted TKA has demonstrated greater accuracy in implant positioning and reduced soft tissue trauma [14]. Functional alignment focuses on positioning components to minimally disrupt the knee’s soft-tissue envelope, thereby restoring the joint’s natural plane and obliquity [15]. It helps achieve balanced extension and flexion gaps and optimal soft tissue tension by precisely adjusting bone resections and component placement, and reducing the need for extensive soft tissue releases, often with robotic assistance [16]. Our surgical “safe zones” were set as 5° varus to 0° valgus for tibial coronal alignment and 3° valgus to 3° varus for femoral coronal alignment, which was close to the functional safe zones approved by the US FDA and fits with the ranges for most native knees [17]. By allowing this 5° safe zone, we aimed to reconstruct the joint line in a more anatomical position, thereby reducing the reliance on extensive ligamentous releases which can lead to post-operative instability or stiffness. Functional alignment (FA) philosophy is gaining increasing acceptance as a means to achieve a more ‘natural’ feel and improved patient satisfaction by respecting individual constitutional anatomy. Unlike mechanical alignment, FA prioritizes joint line orientation and soft-tissue laxity over rigid axial correction. This meta-analysis by Giovanoulis et al. has shown that FA, with robotic assistance, achieved safe radiographic targets and component positions without signs of instability or anterior compartment overstuffing. This was also accompanied by favourable clinical outcomes, and low overall complication rate [30, 31].

However, it must be noted that while early results are promising, the body of evidence supporting FA is currently characterized by limited follow-up durations. Most studies, including our own, focus on the immediate postoperative radiographic restoration and short-term clinical stability. While the minimal early revision rates reported in the recent meta-analysis are encouraging, long-term data (beyond 5–10 years) are still required to determine if these anatomical corrections lead to premature polyethylene wear or aseptic loosening, particularly when the tibial component is placed in more significant varus. Our study contributes to this growing field by validating the radiographic reproducibility of these targets within a 0°–5° ‘safe zone,’ though we acknowledge that our 6-week results represent only the initial phase of this reconstructive strategy.

Studies that evaluated CPAK changes and clinical outcomes at 1 year and 5 years following TKA with functional alignment showed that the preservation of native CPAK did not improve Forgotten Joint Score and Knee Society Score [12, 13]. Considering this reference [4], it may be deemed that the CPAK changes in this study may not have an impact on long-term patient outcomes for a vast majority of the patients. While the CPAK classification is widely used among knee surgeons for assessing coronal alignment, it’s unclear how it can be integrated with personalized, dynamic alignment strategies tailored to individual patients to enhance outcomes. The CPAK system does not account for sagittal and rotational variations of the lower limb [17], and therefore may not fully represent the complexities of TKA phenotypes [18].The bone alignment according to CPAK alone cannot predict the balancing pathways and functional soft tissue needs. In fact, bony alignment can considerably impact laxities and necessitate precise intraoperative adjustments to achieve stability and functional gains. Hence the CPAK phenotypes should be evaluated in the context of laxities, surgeon’s preference on coronal boundaries, and strategies to maintain knee balance.

The distribution of CPAK phenotype among osteoarthritic population in Asia is mainly concentrated in type I (41.9%) and type II (24.9%) [2]– this is similar to our study, except that the contribution of type I was higher (62.8%)than the overall Asian estimate, followed by type 2 (10.2%) with a lower occurrence in our study. Similar distribution patterns have been reported in Japan, China, and Korea [19–21]. This contrasts with the osteoarthritis patients in the West with phenotype II as most prevalent, highlighting potential ethnic or geographical differences in knee morphology [1]. Bellman et al.[22] introduced the concept of constitutional varus, describing a natural varus alignment present at skeletal maturity, with an estimated prevalence of 32% in males and 17% in females. Recently, it was shown that Asians with constitutional varus alignment, specifically CPAK type I, are more susceptible to worsening of osteoarthritis [23]. Interestingly, while the constitutional CPAK distribution in our study was skewed towards varus alignment, the postoperative was more balanced across various types, mainly I, IV, and V, i.e., varus/neutral alignment with apex distal/neutral JLO. This was expected as functional alignment with intraoperative adjustments facilitated by the robotic technology, typically aims to restore the knee to a neutral or slightly varus alignment according to the natural biomechanics of most patients. Moreover, the post-operative apex proximal JLO, a predictor for poor clinical outcomes of TKA [3], was also less frequent (~ 10%) in this study.

Our study also assessed the concordance between CPAK JLO and KJLO. Interestingly, we found that the concordance rate increased from nearly 14% pre-operatively to 41% post-operatively. Additionally, the concordance was consistently high for neutral knees and low for apex distal knees. The post-operative concordance rate found in this study was similar to that found in Şahbat et al. [10] among healthy adults and minimally arthritic patients. Many factors could be responsible for the change in concordance rate from pre- to post-operative states in this study. Firstly, it may be attributable to the precision in restoring coronal alignment and reducing outliers in joint line orientation with robotic assistance. The correction of the pre-operative osteoarthritis, deformities, and soft tissue imbalances with robotic assistance could have reduced post-operative discrepancies, thereby increasing concordance. Secondly, it has been shown previously that concordance between these parameters is dependent on many variables, such as aHKA. The increased concordance observed postoperatively could be due to a considerable reduction in aHKA in this study. Nevertheless, discordance remained above 50% post-operatively, indicating a significant risk of misleading apex position predictions, which could negatively impact joint line assessment and potentially patient outcomes. Therefore, it is important to note other differences between the measures.

The high discrepancy between CPAK JLO and KJLO measurements can be attributed to several factors. These include the patient-specific factors, measurement differences, lack of consideration for soft tissue, and compensatory mechanisms from other joints like the spine, hip, and ankle [24, 25]. The CPAK classification does not explicitly account for the position of the ankle, which can influence overall lower limb alignment and potentially lead to incorrect predictions of the apex position [24]. Certain patients may get impacted more than the others. For example, patients with severe pre-operative varus or valgus deformities may have residual malalignment even after correction, affecting JLO [6]. The CPAK JLO may also be more susceptible to component alignment (femoral and tibial), use of mismatched or incorrectly sized implants, over- or under-release of ligaments during TKA. Furthermore, post-operative changes in ligament tension, capsular stiffness, or muscle balance can affect JLO over time. Post-operative radiographs may not always accurately capture true JLO due to patient positioning (e.g., rotation, flexion, or weight-bearing status). The KJLO, on the other hand, considers the relative position of the proximal tibial articular surface with respect to the ground. However, it can be more challenging to measure precisely. In reports by Kim et al. [23] and Grant et al. [26] factors such as gender, race, and the severity of osteoarthritis were recognized to influence variations in knee alignment measurements. As osteoarthritis advances, cartilage degeneration and subchondral bone remodelling could alter tibiofemoral alignment, thereby affecting JLO. However, MacDessi et al. [1] observed that the distribution of CPAK phenotypes remained consistent before and after osteoarthritis development, suggesting overall CPAK classifications are stable despite disease progression. Phruetthiphat et al. [27] explored gender-specific differences in knee alignment and found that CPAK and KJLO may yield different results due to variations in joint line orientation and anatomical differences between genders. Our analysis of inter-observer reliability reflects the inherent challenges of the CPAK classification system. While certain parameters showed excellent reliability, the overall moderate to poor agreement for measurements like the MPTA underscores the findings of Bouché et al. [29], who reported that CPAK classification reliability is often limited (Kappa 0.39–0.48). This discrepancy is likely due to the sensitivity of the classification to minor angular variations, where a difference of only 1–2 degrees in MPTA or LDFA can reclassify a patient into a different phenotype. Consequently, while the CPAK matrix provides a valuable framework for understanding knee alignment, clinicians must be cognizant of these reliability thresholds when using it for precise surgical planning or outcomes analysis. These complex factors underscore the need for precise pre-operative planning, meticulous surgical execution, and thorough post-operative assessment to minimize discordance and improve TKA outcomes. Future research should prioritize the standardization of measurement techniques by identifying factors contributing to variability and developing more consistent, reproducible methods for assessing JLO and KJLO. Moreover, advanced imaging modalities, such as 3D modelling, should be explored to improve the accuracy of joint line evaluation.

Given that the CPAK system characterizes knee morphology in two-dimension only, it may have limited applicability particularly for Asian patients with severe arthritis in whom, the bony landmarks are altered. Hence it is worthwhile to look into alternate approaches for this framework. Recently Woo et al. [18] proposed a three-dimensional framework of 4 distinct anatomic types using coronal, axial, and sagittal planes, accounting for arthritic progression, outliers, and deformity. Hsu et al. [28] proposed a modification of the CPAK classification to address its irregular distribution in Asian populations, characterized by greater varus alignment and a wider range of lower limb alignment. Their modifications included redefining aHKA and JLO boundaries and introducing actual JLO (aJLO = 90° – (LDFA + MPTA)/2) to more accurately represent joint line obliquity parallel to the ground. The neutral aHKA range was also expanded to 0° ± 3° to better reflect the wider distribution in Asian populations.

While this study offers valuable insights, it is important to acknowledge certain limitations. First, biases associated with a retrospective cohort study are applicable. Although our study included an adequate number of cases, the single-center design and patient population with advanced deformities drawn from a specific region of our country limits generalizability of our findings to a broader population. Our study population had significant arthritic bone loss and extra-articular deformities, which may not be common across other regions. Third, the absence of healthy individuals for comparison was not possible due to the nature of this study. Fourth, potential radiological measurement errors due to rotational malposition and fixed flexion contractures could not be entirely ruled out. Advanced imaging techniques could provide greater accuracy in future assessments. Fifth, while we analysed the concordance between CPAK JLO and KJLO, it is important to note that soft tissue conditions around the knee may change over time, potentially altering joint line obliquity at different time points, which was not evaluated. Sixth, this study has a short-term follow-up period of 6 weeks. While this timeframe is sufficient for the primary objective of assessing radiographic component alignment and the restoration of joint line orientation (KJLO) via the CPAK matrix, it is a significant limitation regarding clinical outcomes. Long-term follow-up (typically > 1 year) would be required to evaluate the impact of these alignment strategies on patient-reported outcome measures (PROMs), functional scores, and long-term implant survivorship. Consequently, our findings should be interpreted as a technical assessment of radiographic restoration rather than a measure of long-term clinical success. Finally, the impact on clinical outcomes was not assessed and could have provided additional insights. Despite these limitations, our findings contribute to understanding of knee phenotypes among Indian patients along with measures for assessment of JLO.

Conclusions

Robotic-assisted functional alignment TKA restored constitutional aHKA and JLO in fewer than 50% of knees and overcorrection beyond neutral category occurred in < 7% cases. A persistent varus dominance and a shift to more balanced postoperative distribution of CPAK phenotypes was observed. The CPAK classification appears to be valuable for preoperative phenotypic characterization—especially type I predominance in Indian patients—but intraoperative robotic adjustments prioritizing balance leads to frequent alteration in native alignment, limiting its postoperative predictive utility.

Despite frequent CPAK shifts, low overcorrection rates suggest functional alignment with robotic assistance minimizes extreme adjustments to constitutional CPAK. The preservation of outcomes without strict constitutional restoration has been supported by recent Asian TKA studies showing lack of strong correlation between phenotype changes and satisfaction. Therefore, for Asian cohorts with severe varus arthritis, integrating CPAK with soft-tissue laxity assessment and 3D planning enhances personalization. Prospective multicenter trials should correlate CPAK/KJLO discordance with patient-reported outcomes (e.g., Knee Injury and Osteoarthritis Outcome Score - Joint Replacement or KOOS-JR) and explore modified CPAK boundaries or 3D frameworks for Asian populations.
