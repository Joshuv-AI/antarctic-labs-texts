The E3 ubiquitin ligase MARCH9 alleviates pyroptosis by regulating NLPR3 ubiquitination following myocardial ischemia reperfusion
Authors: Hongquan Lu, Gang Ma, Cong Ding, Ping Wang, Qiuyue Ruan, Yun Zeng, Lingkun Ma, Min Lu, Yilin Li, Wenjing Sun
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
NLRP3, a member of the NOD-like receptor family, mediates pyroptosis via inflammasome activation, contributing significantly to myocardial ischemia-reperfusion (MI/R) injury. However, the molecular mechanisms remain unclear. This study aimed to elucidate the mechanisms underlying NLRP3 inflammasome activation and its role in pyroptotic myocyte death following MI/R injury, with a particular focus on the effects of MARCH9, an E3 ubiquitin ligase that encodes a RING finger domain. In our in vivo experiments, we established an MI/R injury model by ligating the anterior descending branch of the coronary artery in mice. Cardiac tissues were analyzed using various methods, including proteomic analysis, TTC and Evan’s blue dual-dye staining, immunohistochemical staining (F4/80, CD11b and Ly6G), and immunofluorescent staining (GSDMD-N). Western blotting was also performed to assess the expression of key pyroptosis-related proteins, such as MARCH9, NLRP3, caspase-1, GSDMD-N, and AIM2. In vitro studies in H9C2 and HEK293 cell lines involved western blotting, co-immunoprecipitation, and immunofluorescent staining to examine protein interactions and functional domains. Our findings reveal that MARCH9 plays a key regulatory role in NLRP3 inflammasome activation and pyroptosis during MI/R injury. MARCH9 was identified as an E3 ubiquitin ligase that interacts with NLRP3, promoting its K48-linked polyubiquitination and subsequent proteasomal degradation. This process inhibits NLRP3 activation, thereby mitigating pyroptosis and reducing myocardial injury. In conclusion, MARCH9 suppresses pyroptosis by modulating NLRP3 ubiquitination in response to MI/R injury, highlighting its potential as a novel cardioprotective target for therapeutic intervention.Graphical abstract Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1007/s00018-025-05861-z.

Introduction

Acute myocardial infarction is a leading global cause of mortality [1, 2]. There is a global consensus that prompt and successful reperfusion of ischemic myocardial tissue is vital for preserving viable myocardium; [3, 4] however, during the myocardial ischemia/reperfusion (MI/R) process, concomitant injuries, such as cardiomyocyte death, can lead to heart failure [4]. Therefore, understanding the complex mechanisms underlying cardiomyocyte death is essential for mitigating myocardial injury following MI/R, thereby improving the management of patients who have suffered MI/R injury [3, 4].

Various forms of cardiomyocyte death can occur during MI/R, including necrosis, apoptosis, necroptosis, and pyroptosis [4]et al. [5, 6] Recently, it has been established that myocyte pyroptosis represents a novel form of cellular necrosis within the context of MI/R injury [7]. Unlike traditional necrosis, pyroptosis can lead to the disintegration of the plasma membrane [6]. Furthermore, pyroptotic bodies and cellular membrane blebbing are not observed in necrosis but are characteristic of pyroptosis, making the process potentially controllable [8]. Consequently, efforts to revert pyroptosis are worthy of consideration.

Two classic pattern recognition receptors (PRRs), including toll-like receptors (TRLs) and NOD-like receptors (NLRs) [9], are capable of initiating the assembly of inflammasomes; subsequent activation of the caspase cascade leads to pyroptosis [10]. Members of the NLR family share similar structural domains, and NLRP3, in particular, has been implicated in the process of inflammasome-mediated pyroptosis that has the potential to modulate infarct size and cardiac dysfunction following MI/R injury [2, 11]. Nonetheless, the mechanisms by which NLRP3 and inflammasome-mediated pyroptosis contribute to MI/R injury remain incompletely understood.

Ubiquitination is a reversible post-translational modification that precisely regulates NLRP3 activity [12]. Among these processes, E3 ubiquitin ligases serve as key catalytic factors, mediating substrate-specific recognition and determining the type of ubiquitination [13, 14]. In resting cells, E3 ligases typically attach K48-linked ubiquitin chains to NLRP3, targeting it for proteasomal degradation and thereby preventing unintended inflammasome activation [13]. Upon receiving a priming signal, deubiquitinating enzymes remove these inhibitory chains, a step that ‘licenses’ NLRP3 for subsequent activation [15]. This allows NLRP3 to respond to a second signal, such as potassium efflux, mitochondrial dysfunction, or lysosomal rupture, by assembling into an active inflammasome complex [16]. Additionally, K63-linked ubiquitination can regulate NLRP3 by modulating its protein–protein interactions without directing it for degradation [13, 17, 18]. Collectively, this dynamic ubiquitination and deubiquitination events ensure that NLRP3 remains tightly suppressed under basal conditions yet is poised for rapid activation in response to genuine inflammatory cues.

MARCH9, a membrane-associated RING finger protein and member of the MARCH family of E3 ligases, has recently emerged as a significant regulator of immune responses [19, 20]. The MARCH family plays pivotal roles in immunoregulation by mediating ubiquitination and stabilizing membrane proteins [21]. Notably, MARCH9 has been implicated in inflammatory processes and immunoregulation [22]. However, its specific function as an E3 ubiquitin ligase in modulating NLRP3 stability during I/R injury remains unclear and warrants further investigation.

This study delineates the mechanism by which MARCH9 inhibits NLRP3 inflammasome activation-induced pyroptosis. MARCH9 interacts with NLRP3, leading to K48-linked polyubiquitination and subsequent proteasomal degradation of NLRP3 in H9c2 cells. These findings provide a novel insight into the role of MARCH9 in modulating NLRP3 inflammasome activation-induced pyroptosis, underscoring its potential as a therapeutic target for the management of MI/R injury.

Materials and methods

Mice

MARCH9 transgenic C57BL/6J mice (MARCH9-Tg) were obtained from GemPharmatech Co., Ltd. (Nanjing, China) through the CRISPR/Cas9 gene-editing technique. Briefly, Cas9 mRNA and single-guide RNA (sgRNA) were employed to target the Rosa26 locus. The donor template included the Rosa26 promoter, MARCH9 coding sequence, and a 3′ polyA sequence. The sgRNA sequence used was 5′-CAGGAAAGAGGAGGAGGAG-3′. The targeted Rosa26 locus was injected into mouse zygotes to generate the transgenic line.

Both MARCH9-Tg mice and their wild-type (WT) littermate controls were housed in a specific pathogen-free facility to ensure a controlled and contaminant-free environment. Genotyping was performed using the following primers: Forward: 5′-AGGACCCTGCTCCTCTCAGTATTTCT-3′; Reverse: 5′-AGTATATAGATCTGGTGCCAGCTGGG-3′.

All animal experiments were conducted in compliance with the Guideline for the Ethical Review of Laboratory Animals and were approved by the Ethics Committee of the institution. The sample collection protocol was also authorized by the Ethics Committee of GemPharmatech Co., Ltd. (Approval No. BST-MICE-PZ-20230426-01). These measures ensured that all research involving animals adhered to ethical guidelines and received proper institutional oversight.

MI/R model

Male WT mice and MARCH9-Tg mice (6 ~ 8 weeks old) were subjected to MI/R as outlined in prior study [23, 24]. Mice were narcotized using 1% pentobarbital before the procedure was carried out. The hearts were carefully exposed by locating them between the fourth and fifth ribs, following which the left anterior descending coronary artery was tied off using a 6 − 0 silk suture. Depending on the experimental conditions, the slipknot was either loosened after 1–6 h following a 45-minute period of occlusion. In the Sham-operated animals, same suture was passed the same artery without being tied off.

Evaluation of the infarct infracted myocardial tissues

Six hours after reperfusion, the size of the infarcted region was evaluated using a double-staining method with TTC and Evan’s blue, as described in previous studies [23, 24]. Heart slices were photographed, and the images were analyzed using ImageJ software to quantify the infarcted myocardial tissue.

Proteomic analysis

Proteomic analysis was conducted in collaboration with PTM Biolab (Hangzhou, China). For this, peptides were extracted from enzymatically digested cardiac tissue and subjected to proteomic technology for further investigation. Semi-quantitative proteomics was employed, with protein relative abundance determined based on the measurement of peak areas. Detailed methods could be found in the study of Kim et al. [25].

Immunohistochemistry

In short, cardiac tissue that had been fixed in paraformaldehyde and embedded in paraffin was sliced into 4 μm-thick sections. The slides went through dehydration using a series of graded ethanol. Next, the sections were treated with 0.3% hydrogen peroxide in PBS to eliminate endogenous peroxidase activity. Following that, the sections were blocked with 5% goat serum for 2 h and then exposed to the primary antibodies overnight at 4 °C: rabbit anti-F4/80 (Proteintech, Wuhan, China; 1:100, #29414-1-AP), rabbit anti-CD11b (ABclonal, Boston, MA, USA; 1:100, #A24120). The subsequent day, after three rounds of washing, the sections were treated with anti-rabbit IgG horseradish peroxide (beyotime, Shanghai, China; 1:50, #A0216) and subjected to the diaminobenzidine tetrahydrochloride reaction, followed by counterstaining with Mayer’s hematoxylin. Image J Software was utilized for the analysis of captured images.

Cell culture

Primary fetal mouse cardiomyocytes were isolated from mice as previously described [26]. Hearts were carefully excised from fetal mice, and the atria and right ventricle tissues were removed. The remaining heart tissue was finely minced. For the isolation of primary fetal mouse cardiomyocytes, the minced heart tissue was incubated with 0.2% collagenase II (Sigma-Aldrich, #C2-BIOC) at 37 °C for 20 min. This step was followed by an incubation with 0.25% trypsin (Sigma-Aldrich, #T6689) for 8 to 10 min. After these enzymatic digestions, the primary fetal mouse cardiomyocytes were collected by centrifugation at 1000 g for 5 min. The collected cells were then resuspended in DMEM-F12 medium (Hyclone, Logan, UT, USA; # SH30023.02) that was supplemented with 10% fetal bovine serum (FBS, BI, Israel; #04-001-1ACS), along with 100 IU/ml penicillin and 100 mg/ml streptomycin (Hyclone, Logan, UT, USA; #SV30010). These cardiomyocytes were cultured at 37 °C in an atmosphere containing 5% CO2 in the same DMEM-F12 medium.

H9c2 (#BNCC337726) and HEK293T (#BNCC340228) cell lines were procured from Bei Na Chuang-lian Biotechnology (Wuhan, China). These cell Lines were maintained at 37 °C under a 5% CO2 atmosphere. They were cultured either in DMEM-F12 (Hyclone, Logan, UT, USA; #SH30023.02) or DMEM medium (Hyclone, Logan, UT, USA; # SH30243.02), both of which were supplemented with 10% FBS, and 100 U/ml penicillin and 100 mg/ml streptomycin (Hyclone, Logan, UT, USA; # SV30010).

In summary, to induce oxygen-glucose deprivation injury in H9c2 cells, the cells were incubated for 2 h in a hypoxic chamber set at 1% O2, 5% CO2, and 94% N2 without serum medium. Following this, the medium was replaced with a complete DEME medium containing serum, and the cells were reoxygenated at 37 °C in an incubator infused with CO2 for another 2 h.

qPCR analysis

Total RNA extraction was performed using TRIzol reagent following manufacturer’s protocol (TaKaRa, Beijing, China; #9108). A Light Cycler and a SYBR RT-PCR kit (Invitrogen, CA, USA; #04707516001) were utilized for real-time Quantitative RT-PCR analysis. Sequences of gene-Specific primer pairs used for RT-PCR are 5’-TTGAAGAGTGTG-ATCTGCGGAA-3’ (F) and 5’-GAAAGTCATGTGGCTGAAGCTG-3’ (R) for mouse NLRP3; 5’-AGAAGGTCCAGATTGCTGCC-3’ (F) and 5’-GATGCGGTAGACAGA-GGAGC-3’ (R) for mouse MARCH9; 5’-CACAGAAGGAGTGGCTAAGGACC-3’ (F) and 5’-ACGCAGTAGGTTTGCCGAGTAGA-3’ (R) for mouse GAPDH. The data presented reflect the relative abundance of the specified mRNA, which has been normalized to the level of the mouse GAPDH.

Western blot analysis and immunoprecipitation

The techniques involved in western blot analysis were detailed in a previously published research article [27]. To extract proteins from either cells or cardiac tissues for subsequent examination, the initial step was performed. The determination of protein concentration was achieved by utilizing the BSA protein kit (beyotime, Shanghai, China; #P0006). After undergoing separation through SDS-PAGE (beyotime, Shanghai, China; #P0012AC), the proteins were transferred onto PVDF membranes. Next, the membranes underwent blocking with non-fat milk before being subjected to an overnight incubation with primary antibodies directed towards specific proteins, including MARCH9 (1:1000, Thermo Fisher, USA; #BS-9343R), NLRP3 (1:1000, Adipogen, USA; #AG-20B-0014-C100), Caspase-1 (1:1000, Adipogen, USA; #AG-20B-0042-C100), ASC (1:1000, Proteintech, Chicago, IL, USA; #83858-3-RR), GSDMD (1:1000, Abcam, Cambridge, UK; #ab209845), c-GSDMD (1:1000, Abcam, Cambridge, UK; #ab255603), and GAPDH (1:5000, Proteintech, Chicago, IL, USA; #60004-1-Ig). Upon completion of the incubation, a secondary HRP-conjugated IgG antibody (1:1000, ABclonal, Boston, MA, USA; #AS080) was employed for detection, and protein visualization was accomplished using the ECL kit (beyotime, Shanghai, China; #P0018S). To standardize the expression levels of the target proteins, either β-actin or GAPDH was utilized as controls.

To perform immunoprecipitation (IP), cells were lysed using Cell Lysis Buffer (Thermo Fisher, USA; FNN0011) containing a protease inhibitor while maintained on ice. Subsequently, the resultant supernatant was subjected to an overnight incubation with IP antibodies of Flag (1:200, beyotime, Shanghai, China; #AF519), Myc antibody (1:200, beyotime, Shanghai, China; #AM933) and protein A/G-magnetic beads. After the immunoprecipitants were washed, they were boiled and analyzed through SDS-PAGE with specific antibodies for further exploration.

In vitro ubiquitination assays

In the in vitro ubiquitination assay, Flag-MARCH9 and HA-Ub, along with specific variants HA-K48 (K48) and HA-K63 (K63), were co-transfected with either WT or the Q257H mutant of Flag-MARCH9 into HEK293T cells for a duration of 24 h. Following this transfection period, the cells were exposed to the proteasome inhibitor MG132 (40 µM, Selleck, Shanghai, China; #S2619) for an additional 6 h to inhibit protein degradation. Subsequently, the cells were lysed using 150 µl of cell lysis buffer (Thermo Fisher, USA; #FNN0011) containing 5 mM N-ethylmaleimide (NEM, MCE, Shanghai, China; #HY-D0843) and a complete protease inhibitor mixture (40 µM, Selleck, Shanghai, China; #S2619). The lysis process included centrifugation at 12,000 g at a temperature of 4 °C for 15 min to separate cellular components. For the analysis, 20% of the lysed cells were processed in loading buffer (beyotime, Shanghai, China; #P0015) to serve as the input, while the remaining supernatants were incubated overnight at 4 °C with either anti-Flag magnetic beads (MCE, Shanghai, China; #HY-K0207) or anti-Myc magnetic beads (MCE, Shanghai, China; #HY-K0206) to facilitate the immunoprecipitation of the tagged proteins. After the overnight incubation, the beads were washed three times with 500 µl of IP buffer to remove any nonspecific binding proteins. Immunoprecipitants were then harvested by centrifuging at 12,000 g at 4 °C for 1 min. Following the centrifugation, a 40 µl aliquot of 2×Laemmli sample buffer was added to the collected beads for subsequent sample preparation. The prepared samples were ultimately resolved by SDS-PAGE to analyze the proteins involved in the ubiquitination process.

Immunofluorescence staining and confocal analysis

H9c2 cells were initially cultured on a confocal dish and treated with 4% paraformaldehyde solution for 15 min, followed by permeabilization using 0.1% Triton X-100 (beyotime, Shanghai, China; #P0096) for an additional 15 min. The cells were then subjected to blocking using normal goat serum for 1 h. After being washed three times, the cells were incubated overnight at 4 °C with primary antibodies: rabbit anti-MARCH9 (1:200, Thermo Fisher, USA; #BS-9343R), rabbit anti-NLRP3 (1:200, Proteintech, Wuhan, China; #30109-1-AP), and mouse anti-ASC (Santa Cruz, USA; #sc-271054). Following three washes with PBS, the cells were exposed to secondary antibodies (Alexa Fluor 488 goat anti-mouse IgG, Cy3 goat anti-rabbit IgG or Alexa Fluor 647 goat anti-rabbit IgG) for 1 h at room temperature. Nuclei were counterstained with DAPI (Thermo Fisher, USA; #H3569). Confocal microscopy (Olympus, Tokyo, Japan) was used to capture images for colocalization analysis.

Plasmids transfection

Dr. Wei Zhao from Shandong University, Shandong, China, generously provided the HA-Ub WT, K48 mutant, and K63 mutant plasmids. The Myc-NLRP3 and Flag-MARCH9 plasmids, along with their corresponding mutants, were obtained from Stratagene (Shanghai, China). Amplified target genes and their mutants were generated using restriction endonuclease digestion. Two Flag-tagged vectors (pCMV-N-Myc and pCMV-N-Flag, Beyotime, Shanghai, China; #D2756 and #D2722) were used following the manufacturer’s instructions. HEK293T cells were transiently transfected using Lipofectamine 3000.

Lentivirus transfection

H9c2 cells were cultured in a six-well plate and the next day, the medium was replaced with new medium containing lentivirus carrying the MARCH9 gene (Len-MARCH9) and lentiviral vector carrying green fluorescence (Len-GFP). These vectors were stably transfected into H9c2 cells for 8 h, followed by the addition of puromycin for further selection. Two days after transfection, an ischemia/reperfusion (I/R) model of H9c2 cells was established as previously described [24].

Si-RNA transfection

H9c2 were cultured in six-well plate, On the following day, the medium was exchanged, and cells were transfected with a Lip 2000 (Invitrogen, Thermo Fisher, USA; #11668030) and MARCH9 siRNA #4 (GenePharma, Shanghai, China), according to manufacturer’s instruction. 48 h after transfection, cells were subjected H/R as described above.

Statistical analysis

All numerical results were expressed as means ± SEMs. The statistical assessments were conducted using GraphPad Prism 9. To evaluate the distribution of variables, the Kolmogorov-Smirnov test was applied. For statistical comparisons, a two-unpaired student’s t-test was utilized for single measured variables, while one-way or two-way ANOVA was employed for multiple comparisons. A P-value less than 0.05 was considered to indicate statistical significance. The analysis of animal survival data was performed using the log-rank test.

Results

MARCH9 expression is markedly downregulated following myocardial I/R

Firstly, we conducted proteomics on wild-type mice that underwent either a sham procedure or ischemia/reperfusion surgery to identify potential candidate molecules related to ubiquitination that may play a role in the pathogenesis of myocardial I/R injury. It was found that the key candidates included genes MARCH9, RNF170, HECTD3, NEDD4l and TRIM25, among which MARCH9 exhibited the most pronounced change (Fig. 1A, B). Then, to investigate the role of MARCH9 in the heart following myocardial I/R, we first detected the expression of MARCH9 in the heart by RT-qPCR. We found that MARCH9 mRNA levels were substantially decreased in the mice heart subjected to I/R, while the mRNA levels of NLRP3 was significantly increased in a time-dependent manner (Fig. 1C-D, P < 0.05). We further evaluated expression of MARCH9 in the heart at different reperfusion time points by immunoblotting, our results showed that the MARCH9 protein levels were markedly reduced in the mice heart subjected to 45 min ischemia and 1–6 h reperfusion (Fig. 1E-G, P < 0.05). It is noteworthy that there was a negative correlation between cardiac MARCH9 expression and NLRP3.

MARCH9 ameliorates myocardial I/R injury in mice

To further determine the effect of MARCH9 on myocardial I/R injury, transgenic mice, MARCH9-Tg, were generated. Firstly, the expression of MARCH9 was determined by Western blot in the MARCH9-Tg mice. The result reveled increased MARCH9 levels in MARCH9-Tg mice in comparison with sham controls (Fig. 2A, P < 0.05). And then, survival analysis was analyzed following MI/R in mice. Our results showed that wild-type mice were more susceptible to death than MARCH9-Tg mice following myocardial I/R (Fig. 2B, P < 0.05). Subsequently, the role of MARCH9-Tg on the myocardial infarct size at 6 h after reperfusion was estimated (Fig. 2C). Compared to the WT-I/R group, the ratio of infarct size to area at risk (AAR) was found to be smaller in the MARCH9-Tg I/R group (Fig. 2C, P < 0.05). However, the AAR as the percentage of the LV (AAR/LV) was significantly decreased in the MARCH9-Tg I/R group compared to that of WT-I/R group (Fig. 2C, P < 0.05). We next observed the inflammatory infiltration by immunohistochemistry and discovered that MARCH9-Tg alleviated F4/80+ macrophages, CD11b+ and Ly6G neutrophils infiltration in the heart compared to that of the WT group following myocardial I/R (Fig. 2E-G, I-K, P < 0.05). We additionally evaluated MARCH9-Tg on myocardial tissue pyroptosis by immunofluorescence and indicated that MARCH9-Tg could decreased cardiomyocyte pyroptosis after myocardial I/R (Fig. 2D, H, P < 0.05). Together, these data suggested that MARCH9 overexpression could decrease myocardial infarct size, inflammatory infiltration and cardiomyocyte pyroptosis.

MARCH9 inhibits NLRP3 inflammasome activation and pyroptosis

To explore the mechanism of MARCH9 during NLRP3 inflammasome activation, the expression of MARCH9 and NLRP3 inflammasome associated protein was first observed in H9c2 subjected to 2 h hypoxia and 2 h reoxygenation. Our results indicated that the expression of MARCH9 was decreased, whereas the expression of NLRP3, caspase-1 and GSDMD-N was higher in hypoxia reoxygenation group compared with the control group in H9c2 (Supplementary Fig. 1A-B, P < 0.05). To further determine the effect of MARCH9 on NLRP3 inflammasome activation, small interfering RNA (siRNA) targeting MARCH9 was used to suppress the expression of endogenous MARCH9 in H9c2 (Supplementary Fig. 1A-B, P < 0.05). Reduction in MARCH9 resulted in an increased presence of NLRP3, caspase-1 (p20) and GSDMD-N in H9c2 subjected to 2 h hypoxia and 2 h reoxygenation (Fig. 3A-E, P < 0.05). However, knocking down MARCH9 does not regulate mRNA levels during NLRP3 activation induced by H/R (Fig. 3J, P < 0.05). Accordingly, upregulation of MARCH9 levels resulted in a decreased presence of NLRP3, caspase-1 (p20) and GSDMD-N in primary neonatal mouse cardiomyocytes subjected to 2 h hypoxia and 2 h reoxygenation (Fig. 3F-I, P < 0.05).

To further investigate the mechanism of MARCH9 regulating NLRP3 inflammasome activation and pyroptosis, we next designed MARCH9 lentiviral overexpression vector and transfected it into H9c2. Similarly, MARCH9 overexpression significantly inhibited the expression of NLRP3, caspase-1 and GSDMD-N in H9c2 subjected to 2 h hypoxia and 2 h reoxygenation (Supplementary Fig. 1C-D, P < 0.05). Additionally, we found that knocking down MARCH9 resulted in an increased the capase-1 activity in H9c2 (Fig. 3K, P < 0.05). However, MARCH9 overexpression can reduce the capase-1 activity (Fig. 3L, P < 0.05). These results suggest that MARCH9 is negative regulatory NLRP3 activation medicated pyroptosis following H/R in H9c2.

MARCH9 interacts with NLRP3

To delve deeper into the molecular mechanism by which MARCH9 protects against MI/R injury, our initial examination focused on determining if MARCH9 negatively regulates NLRP3 inflammasome activation through a direct interaction with NLRP3. A co-immunoprecipitation (Co-IP) assay was performed for this purpose. HEK293T were transfected to express Myc-tagged NLRP3 or Flag-tagged MARCH9. As shown by the Co-IP assays, there was a direct interaction between MARCH9 and NLRP3 (Fig. 4A-B). This finding was corroborated by the detection of an interaction between endogenous MARCH9 and NLRP3 in H9c2 cells (Fig. 4C). Furthermore, we found that this interaction is enhanced following 2 h of hypoxia and subsequent 2 h of reoxygenation. Confocal microscopy further confirmed the colocalization of Flag-MARCH9 and Myc-NLRP3 in H9c2 cells (Fig. 4D).

MARCH9, a protein belonging to the MARCH E3 ubiquitin ligase family, is characterized by an N-terminal RING-CH finger along with two or more transmembrane (TM) domains [28]. To determine which domain(s) of MARCH9 facilitate the interaction with NLRP3, four truncated mutants were generated (see Fig. 4E). Results from the Co-IP assay indicated that when amino acids 163–320 or 321–346 were deleted, MARCH9 lost its ability to bind to NLRP3 (see Fig. 4F). These results suggest that both the TM and PDZ domains of MARCH9 are essential for its interaction with NLRP3.

NLRP3 has a structural composition comprising of three primary regions: a leucine-rich repeat (LRR) at the C-terminus, a NACHT oligomerization domain at the center, and a pyrin domain (PYD) at the N-terminus [11, 29]. In Fig. 4G, three truncated mutants of NLRP3 were generated. Results from Co-IP analysis indicated that while the NLRP3 PYD domain exhibited interaction with MARCH9, the LRR and NACHT domains did not (Fig. 4H). As a result, the NLRP3-PYD domain plays a crucial role in mediating the interaction between NLRP3 and MARCH9.

MARCH9 facilitates proteasomal degradation of NLRP3

To investigate how MARCH9 reduces NLRP3 levels, we first confirmed that MARCH9 knockdown markedly increased NLRP3 inflammasome activation and pyroptosis in H9c2 (Fig. 5A, P < 0.05). Consistently, MARCH9 knockdown also greatly increased the expression of NLRP3 in H9c2 subjected to 2 h hypoxia and 2 h reoxygenation (Fig. 5B, P < 0.05). But, MARCH9 knockdown has no influence on the NLRP3 mRNA levels (Fig. 5A-B). Similarity, overexpression of MARCH9 significantly decreased the expression of NLRP3, while not influence on the levels of AIM2 and NLRC4 in NMCMs (Fig. 5C, P < 0.05). According to the data, indicating MARCH9 governs the expression of NLRP3 at the protein level, rather than at the transcription level.

Considering the functions of MARCH9 in facilitating the degradation of specific proteins, it is likely that MARCH9 plays a crucial role in suppressing the expression of NLRP3 by enhancing its degradation. To investigate the specific pathway through which MARCH9 facilitates NLRP3 degradation, a variety of inhibitors were used. Our data indicated that the capacity of MARCH9 to decrease NLRP3 levels was reversed following treatment of cells with the proteasome inhibitor MG132, rather than the lysosome inhibitor chloroquine or autophagy inhibitor 3-MA, indicating that the MARCH9-driven NLRP3 degradation needs the activity of the proteasome (Fig. 5E-F). Then the dynamics of MARCH9 degradation were investigated after treatment with cycloheximide for different periods. Silencing MARCH9 markedly inhibited the degradation process when compared to the control (Fig. 5D, P < 0.05).

MARCH9 facilitates K48-linked ubiquitination of NLRP3

Next, we utilized in-cell assays focusing on ubiquitination to explore whether MARCH9 enhanced the ubiquitination of NLRP3 leading to its accelerated degradation.

In HEK293T cells, HA-ubiquitin and WT MARCH9 were co-transfected with NLRP3. The ubiquitination of NLRP3 was significantly enhanced when the MARCH9 expression plasmid was present (Fig. 6A). Interestingly, the MARCH9 point mutation (Q257H), where the glutamine residue at positions 257 was replaced by histidine, resulted in the loss of its ability to facilitate the polyubiquitination of NLRP3 (Fig. 6A). This suggests that MARCH9 can enhance the ubiquitination of NLRP3.

To examine the forms of MARCH9-mediated NLRP3 polyubiquitination, vectors containing mutants of ubiquitin at positions K48 and K63 were used in transfection experiments. These mutants had arginine substitutions at all lysine residues except for the ones at positions 48 and 63, respectively. The results showed that MARCH9 facilitated the polyubiquitination of NLRP3 in the presence of the K48 plasmid, rather than with the K63 plasmid (Fig. 6B). This indicates that MARCH9 mediates the K48-linked polyubiquitination of NLRP3.

In physiological settings, the endogenous NLRP3 exhibited forceful ubiquitination with both K48 and K63 Linkages following 2 h hypoxia and 2 h reoxygenation (Fig. 6C). Nevertheless, the K48-linked polyubiquitination of NLRP3 was mostly eliminated in MARCH9 deficient H9c2 (Fig. 6C). These data suggest that MARCH9 facilitates K48-linked ubiquitination and proteasomal degradation of NLRP3 by exerting its E3 ubiquitin ligase function.

As a ubiquitinating enzyme, MARCH9 was further investigated to determine if the degradation of NLRP3 is reliant on its enzymatic function. Subsequently, we transfected HEK293T cells with plasmids for WT MARCH9, MARCH9 with a mutant TM domain, or the MARCH9Q257H mutant, along with MYC-tagged NLRP3, and then assessed the expression of the NLRP3 protein. We found both the MARCH9 mutant TM domain and the mutant MARCH9Q257H led to lose the ability to promote degradation of NLRP3 (Fig. 6D). Similar results were obtained in H9c2 (Fig. 6E). These data suggest that MARCH9 facilitates K48-linked ubiquitination and proteasomal degradation of NLRP3 by exerting its E3 ubiquitin ligase function.

Discussion

Cardiomyocyte death is central to the pathogenesis of myocardial infarction and MI/R injury, driven by mechanisms involving the innate immune system. Macrophages play a key role in amplifying the inflammatory response by interacting with immune sensors such as NLRs [30], TLRs, and PRRs, which detect damage-associated molecular patterns from dying cardiomyocytes [9]. Programmed cell death pathways, including apoptosis, autophagy, and pyroptosis, contribute to this process [2, 31]. Pyroptosis is distinct from apoptosis and necrosis, as it is both a form of cell death and a potent driver of inflammation. It is primarily mediated by the activation of the NLRP3 inflammasome [15], which triggers caspase-1 activation and the subsequent maturation and release of interleukin-1β and interleukin-18 [32, 33].

Recent studies have increasingly highlighted pyroptosis [11, 34]. Pyroptosis is a form of inflammatory programmed necrotic cell death characterized by rapid cell membrane rupture mediated by gasdermin D, which is activated downstream of caspase-1 [35]. This process heavily depends on the activation of the inflammasome, a multiprotein complex comprising NLRP3, apoptosis-associated speck-like protein containing a CARD (ASC), and caspase-1 [36]. These components synergistically trigger the inflammatory response, culminating in pyroptotic cell death [36, 37]. Previous studies have implicated NLRP3 inflammasome-mediated pyroptosis as a pivotal mechanism driving myocardial I/R injury via various pathways [27, 30]. However, the precise mechanisms underlying NLRP3 inflammasome-mediated pyroptosis in myocardial I/R injury remain incompletely understood.

Emerging evidence suggests that posttranslational modifications play a critical role in fine-tuning innate immune responses by regulating NLRP3 inflammasome activity. Such modifications include phosphorylation and ubiquitination [12]. Wang et al. reported that the E3 ligase β-TrCP1 enhances NLRP3 proteasomal degradation through K27-linked ubiquitination at lysine 380, a process that is disrupted by the interaction between NLRP3 and the transcription co-activator YAP [38]. Similarly, Cai et al. demonstrated that USP5 recruits the E3 ligase MARCHF7 to facilitate K48-linked ubiquitination of NLRP3, leading to its proteasomal degradation [39]. Additionally, ARIH2, another E3 ligase, interacts with NLRP3 to promote its ubiquitination [40]. The absence of ARIH2 suppresses NLRP3 ubiquitination, resulting in heightened NLRP3 activation [41].

Despite these advancements, the identification of novel E3 ubiquitin ligases regulating NLRP3 activity remains an important area of investigation. Our study introduces MARCH9 as a natural regulator of NLRP3 expression, shedding new light on the intricate mechanisms governing I/R-induced myocardial injury.

MARCH9 is an important protein that plays a critical role in regulating the innate immune system by inhibiting the activation of proinflammatory responses [42]. Previous studies have shown that MARCH9 regulates cellular immunity by reducing the expression of key immune-related molecules, including MHC I, CD4, and ICAM-1 [20]. However, its role in I/R-induced myocardial injury remains largely unexplored.

Our findings demonstrate that MARCH9 exerts a protective effect by attenuating the inflammatory response. In vivo, mice overexpressing MARCH9 showed significantly reduced myocardial damage, smaller infarct sizes, lower inflammatory cell infiltration, and decreased pyroptosis rates compared to controls. Notably, while MARCH9 expression was downregulated following I/R, NLRP3 inflammasome activity was concurrently upregulated, highlighting a clear inverse relationship between these two proteins.

In our in vitro experiments, silencing MARCH9 in cardiomyocytes led to enhanced caspase-1 activation, whereas MARCH9 overexpression produced the opposite effect, confirming its inhibitory influence on NLRP3 inflammasome activation. Mechanistically, our data indicate that MARCH9 directly interacts with NLRP3 via its transmembrane and PDZ domains. Under H/R conditions, MARCH9 facilitates the K48-linked polyubiquitination of NLRP3, tagging it for proteasomal degradation. Furthermore, lysine 48 residues mutation of NLRP3 significantly abrogated this degradation process, underscoring the specificity of this regulatory mechanism.

Conclusions

MARCH9 inhibits NLRP3 inflammasome activation and myocardial pyroptosis by mediating K48-linked polyubiquitination and proteasomal degradation of NLRP3. This regulatory mechanism underscores MARCH9 in preserving cardiac function during I/R injury, positioning it as a promising therapeutic target for mitigating myocardial damage. Further investigation into the molecular interactions of MARCH9 could advance treatments for cardiac inflammatory diseases.
