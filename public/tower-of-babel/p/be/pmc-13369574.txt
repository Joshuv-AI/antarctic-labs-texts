Knowledge and Awareness of HPV, the HPV Vaccine, and Cervical Cancer Among Women From a Brazilian Community: A Community‐Based Cross‐Sectional Study
Authors: Alessandra Monteiro Ramos, Cláudia Vicari Bolignani, Leila Bernarda Donato Göttems, Ana Maria Costa, Heleno Rodrigues Corrêa Filho, Wania Maria do Espírito Santo Carvalho, Fábio Ferreira Amorim
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTBackground and AimsCervical cancer (CC) remains a major public health problem despite effective screening and vaccination strategies. This study aimed to assess women's knowledge and awareness of human papillomavirus (HPV), HPV vaccination, and CC in a Brazilian community‐based Family Health Strategy/Primary Health Care (FHS/PHC) setting, and to evaluate factors associated with knowledge of HPV transmission, awareness of the HPV‐CC association, and certainty about vaccinating children against HPV.MethodsA community‐based cross‐sectional survey was conducted from August 2023 to February 2024 among women aged ≥ 18 years registered at a Brazilian FHS/PHC unit, using a structured closed‐ended questionnaire that included a health services evaluation component based on the Brazilian version of the Primary Care Assessment Tool.ResultsThree hundred and two women participated, most with low educational attainment and low household income. Overall, 56.6% were aware of HPV, whereas only 18.5% were aware of the HPV‐CC association. Although 96.4% had heard of the Pap smear test, only 19.9% reported screening according to the recommended protocol. Among women aged ≤ 22 years, 87.5% reported HPV vaccination. Women aged ≤ 22 years had lower awareness across several preventive domains and of the HPV‐CC association than women aged ≥ 23 years (p = 0.047). Increasing age was independently associated with lower knowledge of the HPV transmission through unprotected sexual intercourse (OR: 0.962; 95%CI: 0.904–0.984; p < 0.001), whereas higher income was associated with greater knowledge (OR: 2.404; 95%CI: 1.556–3.715; p < 0.001). Greater certainty about vaccinating children was independently associated with television as an information source (OR: 2.295; 95%CI: 1.335–3.950; p = 0.003) and awareness of the HPV‐CC association (OR: 2.376; 95%CI: 1.086–5.200; p = 0.030).ConclusionThese findings suggest that substantial knowledge and awareness gaps persist despite favorable vaccination attitudes, reinforcing the need to strengthen health education, screening adherence, and communication strategies within FHS/PHC services.

Introduction

Human papillomavirus (HPV) represents a major global public health challenge because of its strong association with cervical cancer and other HPV‐related diseases. Cervical cancer is the fourth most common cancer among women worldwide, and remains an important cause of cancer‐related mortality, particularly in low‐ and lower‐middle‐income countries, with approximately 660,000 new cases and 350,000 deaths estimated globally in 2022 [1, 2, 3, 4, 5, 6]. HPV is a small DNA virus that infects basal cells of the skin and mucous membranes, potentially leading to various manifestations such as anogenital warts and cervical cancer. Additionally, HPV infection is associated with cancers of the oropharynx, anus, penis, vulva, and vagina [7, 8, 9].

HPV is primarily transmitted through sexual contact, and the risk of infection is influenced by factors such as the number of sexual partners, age at first sexual activity, and specific sexual behaviors. HPV infection is highly prevalent worldwide, and it is estimated that most sexually active men and women will acquire HPV at some point in their lives [1, 9, 10]. Although sexual transmission is the main route of HPV acquisition, other routes, including vertical transmission during childbirth, self‐inoculation, and fomite‐related transmission after prolonged exposure to contaminated objects, have also been described [4, 7, 8]. In the context of health education, assessing women's understanding of both sexual and non‐sexual transmission routes is relevant because misconceptions about HPV transmission may affect perceptions of risk, prevention, vaccination, and screening [1, 4, 7, 8, 9, 10].

HPV vaccination and cervical cancer screening are the main strategies for reducing HPV‐related disease burden [4, 7, 11, 12]. Vaccination is particularly effective when administered before exposure to HPV and has contributed to reducing HPV infections and HPV‐related precancerous lesions [4, 7, 13]. The World Health Organization (WHO) recommends HPV vaccination for females between the ages of 9 and 14, as most of them have not yet begun sexual activity [1]. Additionally, in the United States, the Advisory Committee on Immunization Practices (ACIP) recommends routine HPV vaccination for males aged 11 and 12, starting as early as nine. For those who were not adequately vaccinated at a younger age, older males up to age 21 and females up to age 26 are also recommended to receive the HPV vaccine [12, 14, 15]. In this regard, various countries have implemented HPV vaccination programs to address HPV‐related diseases, with different age groups and catch‐up strategies according to national policies [4, 7, 12, 16].

In Brazil, these global prevention strategies are implemented mainly through the public health system, including the National Immunization Program and primary healthcare services. HPV vaccination was introduced into the Brazilian National Immunization Program in 2014 for girls aged 11–14 years. The program was subsequently expanded to include boys and to lower the minimum vaccination age to 9 years [16, 17]. Cervical cancer screening is also available through the public health system, and Brazilian guidelines recommend Pap smear screening for women aged 25–64 years who have initiated sexual activity [18, 19]. Despite these public health strategies, challenges remain regarding vaccine uptake, screening adherence, health literacy, and awareness of the HPV‐cervical cancer association [4, 7, 20]. In this context, cervical cancer remains a relevant public health problem in Brazil, with 17,010 new cases estimated per year for the 2023–2025 period [21].

Insufficient information, misconceptions about vaccine safety, concerns about the potential influence of vaccination on sexual behavior, and limited awareness of HPV‐related cancer prevention may contribute to vaccine hesitancy and suboptimal prevention practices [7, 22, 23]. These barriers highlight the importance of educational strategies, healthcare provider engagement, and community‐based initiatives, particularly within primary healthcare settings. However, data remain limited on women's knowledge, awareness, and perceptions regarding HPV, HPV vaccination, cervical cancer prevention, and healthcare services in Brazilian Family Health Strategy/Primary Health Care (FHS/PHC) settings, despite the central role of primary health care in vaccination guidance, health education, cervical cancer screening, and follow‐up of women in the public health system [4, 23, 24, 25].

This study aimed to assess women's knowledge and awareness of HPV, HPV vaccination, and cervical cancer in a Brazilian community‐based FHS/PHC setting. As secondary objectives, the study evaluated factors associated with women's knowledge of HPV transmission, awareness of the HPV–cervical cancer association, and certainty about vaccinating their children against HPV.

Methods

Study Design

This community‐based cross‐sectional study was conducted from August 2023 to February 2024 by administering a survey among women aged ≥ 18 years who were registered and receiving care at a unit of the FHS/PHC in Santo Antônio do Descoberto, Goiás, Brazil.

The Institutional Review Board of the Education and Research Foundation of Health Sciences, Brasília, Federal District, Brazil, approved the study. The study adhered to the principles outlined in the Declaration of Helsinki. All participants provided informed consent before completing the survey.

Settings and Participants

Santo Antônio do Descoberto is a municipality in the eastern part of the Brazilian state of Goiás, located 35 km from the Brazilian capital, Brasília. According to the latest Brazilian census, conducted in 2022, the population was recorded at 72,127 inhabitants, with 36,494 females (50.6%) and a Human Development Index (HDI) of 0.665. Primary health care in the municipality comprises 18 Family Health Strategy teams, covering 86% of the population.

The study population consisted of adult women registered and followed at an FHS/PHC unit in Santo Antônio do Descoberto. The inclusion criteria were being registered and receiving care at the participating FHS/PHC unit, and being aged 18 years or older. Exclusion criteria included any condition that could impair reliable questionnaire completion, such as physical, cognitive, hearing, or visual limitations. These criteria were assessed at the time of participant approach.

After applying the inclusion criteria, each eligible woman was assigned an identification number. A simple random sampling procedure was then used to select identification numbers from this list until the required sample size was reached. Selected women were then contacted and invited to participate in the study. All women approached were able to understand and complete the questionnaire. Therefore, no women were excluded based on the predefined criteria, and none refused to participate.

Given a finite population of 1398 adult women registered and receiving care at the participating FHS/PHC unit, the sample size was calculated for prevalence estimation using an expected prevalence of 50%, a 95% confidence level (95%CI), and a 5% margin of error. The expected prevalence of 50% was adopted because no robust prior estimate was available for the main outcomes in the target population and because this assumption yields the most conservative sample size estimate. Based on these parameters, the required sample size was 302 participants.

Data Collection

Data were collected using a structured questionnaire with closed‐ended questions covering sociodemographic characteristics, knowledge, awareness, and practices regarding cervical cancer, HPV infection prevention, Pap smear screening, HPV vaccination, sources of health information, and evaluation of FHS/PHC services.

The health services evaluation component was based on the Brazilian version of the Primary Care Assessment Tool (PCATool), which has been culturally adapted and validated for use in Brazilian Portuguese [26, 27]. The items assessing sources of health information were based on a questionnaire previously used in a study conducted by our research group [28]. The remaining items were developed by the researchers specifically for this study, based on the study objectives and relevant Brazilian recommendations on cervical cancer screening and HPV vaccination. These researcher‐developed items were not pilot‐tested or psychometrically validated, which should be considered when interpreting the findings.

In Brazil, women aged 25–64 years who have initiated sexual activity are advised to undergo Pap smear screening. After two consecutive negative results, the screening interval should be extended to every 3 years rather than annually. These guidelines, endorsed by the Brazilian Department of Health in collaboration with the Brazilian National Cancer Institute, are aligned with the WHO recommendations [18, 19].

Knowledge and awareness variables were derived from closed‐ended questionnaire items and analyzed as categorical variables. In this study, “awareness” referred to whether participants had heard of, recognized, or reported being aware of a given topic, such as HPV, Pap smear screening, cervical cancer prevention examination, HPV vaccine availability, HPV vaccine target group, cervical cancer causes, or the HPV–cervical cancer association. “Knowledge” was used to describe correct recognition of specific information or associations assessed in the questionnaire, such as the association between HPV infection and unprotected sexual intercourse.

Statistical Analysis

Quantitative data are presented as mean ± standard deviation (SD) or median and interquartile range 25%–75% (IQ25‐75%), according to data distribution, which was assessed using the Kolmogorov–Smirnov test. Categorical variables are expressed as absolute numbers and percentages (%). Unless otherwise stated, percentages were calculated using the total number of respondents, either the total sample or the subgroup‐specific denominator. For variables allowing multiple responses, such as information sources, percentages were calculated using the total number of respondents in the corresponding column and may not sum to 100%.

The dataset was checked for missing responses before analysis. No missing data were identified for the variables included in the analyses. Therefore, all analyses were performed using the full sample of 302 women, and no imputation was applied.

Statistical comparisons of quantitative variables were performed using the Student's t‐test or the Mann–Whitney U test, depending on the data distribution. Categorical variables were analyzed using contingency tables. Pearson's χ 2 test was used when assumptions were met, Fisher's exact test was used for 2 × 2 tables when appropriate, and the Fisher–Freeman–Halton exact test was used for contingency tables larger than 2 × 2 when exact testing was required.

To evaluate independent factors associated with women's knowledge of the association between HPV infection and unprotected sexual intercourse and women's intention to vaccinate their children against HPV, defined as the response “Certainly yes”, binary logistic regression analysis was performed using the enter method. The models included non‐collinear variables associated with the outcome in the univariate analysis at p < 0.05 and potential confounding factors selected based on prior knowledge and presenting p < 0.20 in the univariate analysis. Non‐collinearity was accepted when the tolerance was greater than 0.10, and the variance inflation factor (VIF) was less than 10.0. Odds ratios (ORs) were reported with their respective 95%CIs.

Statistical analyses were performed using IBM Statistical Package for Social Sciences (SPSS) version 23.0 for Mac (IBM Corp.) and Jamovi version 2.3.24 (https://www.jamovi.org). The threshold for statistical significance was set at a two‐tailed p value < 0.05.

Results

The study included 302 women whose sociodemographic characteristics are presented in Supporting Information S1: Table S1. The mean age was 37.1 ± 14.3 years, and 56 (18.5%) self‐identified as White. Six women (2.0%) reported college or university level education, and 125 (41.4%) were married. Most participants reported sex with a regular sexual partner only (n = 200, 66.2%). The median household income was 1.4 Brazilian monthly minimum wages (IQR: 0.9–2.0), corresponding to approximately $361.70 during the study period. Among the participants, 48 women (15.9%) were aged ≤ 22 years, constituting the group that should have received HPV vaccination since its introduction into the Brazilian National Immunization Program. Of these, 42 (87.5%) reported receiving the HPV vaccination.

As shown in Supporting Information S1: Figure S1, the most cited sources of health information were television (n = 175, 57.9%), healthcare professionals (n = 144, 47.7%), and Instagram (n = 124, 41.1%).

Table 3 presents knowledge, awareness, and perceptions regarding HPV, cervical cancer, and HPV vaccination among all women, as well as among women ≤ 22 years and ≥ 23 years. Overall, 96.4% (n = 291) reported awareness of cervical cancer prevention examination, whereas 20.5% (n = 62) had participated in cervical cancer‐related educational activities. Regarding cervical cancer awareness, 43% (n = 130) reported awareness of its causes. Most women had heard of the Pap smear test (n = 226, 74.8%), and 69.9% (n = 211) had previously undergone the test, although only 19.9% (n = 60) reported having the Pap smear test according to the recommended screening protocol, defined as screening among women aged 25–64 years who had initiated sexual activity, with a 3‐year interval after two consecutive negative results. Over half of the women, 56.6% (n = 171), were aware of HPV, and 18.5% (n = 56) were aware of the association between HPV and cervical cancer. Regarding HPV transmission routes, 57.0% (n = 172) reported unprotected sexual intercourse as a risk factor, 16.2% (n = 49) reported kissing, 15.6% (n = 47) reported contaminated personal items, 13.6% (n = 41) reported childbirth, and 1.3% (n = 4) reported handshakes. Women aged ≤ 22 years showed lower awareness of cervical cancer prevention examination (p < 0.001), lower participation in cervical cancer educational activities (p = 0.008), lower awareness of cervical cancer causes (p = 0.002), lower awareness of the Pap smear test (p < 0.001), lower previous Pap smear testing (p < 0.001), lower frequency of Pap smear testing according to the screening protocol (p = 0.029), and lower awareness of the HPV and cervical cancer association (p = 0.047) compared to women ≥ 23 years. Most women knew the HPV vaccine was free of charge (n = 218, 72.2%), and 42.4% (n = 128) were aware of the HPV vaccine target group. Regarding intention to vaccinate children against HPV, 72.7% (n = 218) answered “Certainly yes,” 8.7% (n = 26) answered “Yes, but only if they wish to,” 15.7% (n = 47) answered “I do not know yet,” and 3.0% (n = 9) answered “No, in any case”. Women ≤ 22 years were more likely to be aware that the HPV vaccine is free of charge (p = 0.026), but less likely to report a definite intention to vaccinate children against HPV than women aged ≥ 23 years (p = 0.027).

Discussion

Cervical cancer remains a major global public health issue, particularly in low‐ and middle‐income countries, where incidence and mortality rates remain disproportionately high despite the availability of effective preventive strategies. Although largely preventable, cervical cancer continues to rank among the most common cancers affecting women worldwide [1, 2, 3, 29]. The World Health Organization has proposed a global elimination strategy centered on HPV vaccination, organized screening programs, and timely treatment of precursor lesions and invasive disease. Within this framework, understanding women's knowledge, awareness, and preventive practices is essential to guide targeted interventions [30].

Regarding cervical cancer screening, despite the availability of Pap smear screening through the Brazilian Unified Health System, adherence to screening recommendations remained suboptimal in the present study. Although most participants had heard of the Pap smear test, only 19.9% reported screening according to the recommended protocol by the Brazilian Department of Health. Similar discrepancies between awareness of the Pap smear test and adequate understanding of its preventive purpose have been reported in previous Brazilian studies. For example, studies conducted in Fortaleza and Recife found that most women were familiar with the Pap smear test. However, only a smaller proportion understood its role in cervical cancer prevention [18, 31]. These findings indicate that making the test available and ensuring that women have heard of it may not be sufficient to achieve adequate screening adherence. Additional educational and organizational strategies are needed, as knowing the test exists does not necessarily translate into understanding its purpose, recommended periodicity, or its importance for early detection [18, 24, 30, 31].

Regarding HPV vaccination among women aged ≤ 22 years, its coverage reached 87.5%, exceeding the 80% target established by the Brazilian Department of Health at the launch of the national immunization campaign [16, 17, 18]. However, high reported vaccination coverage coexisted with significant gaps in HPV and cervical cancer awareness. Only slightly more than half of participants were aware of HPV and recognized unprotected sexual intercourse as a transmission route, and fewer than 20% were aware of the association between HPV infection and cervical cancer. This finding suggests that vaccination uptake alone may not ensure adequate understanding of HPV‐related disease prevention, highlighting the need to integrate vaccination strategies with sustained health education on HPV transmission, cervical cancer causation, screening, and long‐term prevention [18, 25, 30].

Recent mathematical modeling studies further support the importance of HPV vaccination for reducing HPV transmission and cervical cancer burden. For example, MCMC‐based modeling has suggested that increasing HPV vaccine uptake may substantially reduce HPV transmission and cervical cancer progression [32]. Other modeling approaches have similarly reinforced the population‐level impact of HPV vaccination on cervical cancer reduction [33]. However, the population‐level impact suggested by these models depends on implementation conditions and vaccine coverage [32, 33]. This evidence should be interpreted within the broader WHO cervical cancer elimination framework, which requires integration of HPV vaccination, screening, and timely treatment [30]. In this context, the present findings reinforce that vaccination programs should not be viewed as isolated interventions. Instead, they should be integrated with sustained health education, screening adherence, and community engagement, particularly among younger and socioeconomically vulnerable women.

Similar knowledge deficits have been documented in Brazilian and international studies, particularly among socially vulnerable populations. Limited understanding of HPV pathogenesis and its oncogenic potential may compromise sustained adherence to preventive behaviors, including participation in screening programs, adoption of safe sexual practices, and confidence in vaccination [7, 18, 25]. International evidence also confirms that limited screening knowledge is not exclusive to Brazil. Studies conducted especially among migrant, refugee, and low‐income populations worldwide reveal similar barriers, reinforcing that informational inequities represent a global challenge in cervical cancer control [7, 34, 35]. In the present study, socioeconomic vulnerability emerged as an important contextual factor, with approximately three‐quarters of participants reporting household incomes of up to two Brazilian monthly minimum wages. Lower income levels have been consistently associated with reduced access to health information, lower educational attainment, and diminished awareness of sexually transmitted infections. These structural determinants may contribute to persistent inequities in cancer prevention and reinforce the need for socially tailored educational strategies within primary healthcare settings [18, 25, 31, 36].

Persistent gaps in women's knowledge regarding cervical cancer prevention highlight the need to strengthen educational and outreach practices promoted by healthcare professionals, especially for socially vulnerable populations [7, 25, 36]. In this context, FHS/PHC services may play a key role in promoting equity in cervical cancer prevention, given their proximity to communities and their capacity to provide continuous follow‐up, vaccination guidance, health education, and screening recommendations [37, 38]. Educational initiatives led by primary healthcare teams, particularly nurses, have demonstrated effectiveness in increasing Pap smear uptake and improving preventive behaviors [7, 18, 25]. Informed women may also act as community multipliers by disseminating knowledge and encouraging screening adherence among peers, especially among those who do not undergo screening regularly or fail to follow recommended intervals [18, 31, 34, 36].

Age‐stratified analyses revealed important generational differences in perceptions of FHS/PHC services and in knowledge of cervical cancer prevention. Women aged ≥ 23 years reported more positive perceptions of FHS/PHC services and greater trust in healthcare professionals. Conversely, women aged ≤ 22 years showed lower awareness across several domains, including cervical cancer causes, Pap smear testing, recommended screening practices, and the HPV‐cervical cancer association. They were also less likely to have undergone Pap smear testing according to national screening recommendations and reported lower participation in cervical cancer‐related educational activities. Similar findings have been reported in other Brazilian studies, including a study conducted in the state of São Paulo that identified low awareness among adolescents regarding the oncogenic role of HPV [26]. These results are concerning because younger women represent the cohort most directly exposed to HPV vaccination campaigns. The findings suggest missed opportunities for health education during routine consultations, vaccination encounters, and school‐ or community‐based interventions. Strengthening youth‐focused communication strategies within FHS/PHC services may help bridge the gap between vaccine uptake and understanding of HPV‐related cancer prevention [18, 26].

Although most women in the present study reported willingness to vaccinate their children against HPV, these positive attitudes should be interpreted with caution, as recent years have been marked by declining vaccination coverage in Brazil [17, 39, 40]. This contrast between stated intention and real‐world uptake suggests that structural, informational, and behavioral barriers may continue to limit program effectiveness. In addition, the expansion of anti‐vaccination movements, increased circulation of misinformation, and declining trust in scientific and health institutions may influence parental decision‐making and contribute to vaccine hesitancy, even in settings with historically robust immunization programs [41, 42, 43]. In this context, communication strategies should be accompanied by actions that facilitate access, reinforce trust in vaccination, address misinformation, and adapt public health messages to contemporary digital environments [41, 42, 43].

Within this framework, and considering the increasing availability of digital technologies, health communication should extend beyond face‐to‐face educational practices and incorporate broader dissemination channels that reach diverse population groups [44]. Media platforms, including television and the Internet, may serve as complementary tools for implementing educational initiatives and amplifying awareness campaigns. In particular, audiovisual communication can enhance the educational process by engaging both auditory and visual senses, thereby facilitating improved comprehension, retention, and recall of health information [45]. Consistent with this integrated communication perspective, the present study revealed a marked generational shift in access to health information, driven by the widespread expansion of internet connectivity. Younger women aged ≤ 22 years had universal internet access and reported greater use of digital media, particularly social networks and messaging applications, with Instagram, Facebook, and WhatsApp surpassing television as information sources within this group. These findings align with growing evidence highlighting the increasing role of digital media in health communication and education, particularly among younger populations [44, 45, 46, 47, 48]. At the same time, television as an information source, together with awareness of the HPV‐cervical cancer association, was independently associated with greater certainty about vaccinating children against HPV. This finding reinforces the strategic role of communication campaigns in sustaining vaccine acceptance. It suggests that traditional mass media remain relevant, particularly in Brazil, where television has historically achieved broad population reach and has played a central role in national immunization campaigns [40, 49]. Given its association with greater certainty about vaccinating children in the present study, television should continue to be valued as a public health communication channel, including as part of strategies to counterbalance misinformation from less reliable sources. At the same time, these efforts should be integrated with digital strategies tailored to younger audiences, given the generational differences in information access observed in the present study.

This study has limitations that should be acknowledged. First, its cross‐sectional design precludes causal inferences among knowledge, awareness, attitudes, and preventive behaviors, limiting the ability to determine temporal or directional relationships among the variables. Therefore, it is not possible to establish whether knowledge deficits preceded or resulted from patterns of health service utilization or preventive practices. Second, data on prior Pap smear testing, HPV vaccination, participation in educational activities, and intention to vaccinate children against HPV were self‐reported and may therefore be subject to recall and reporting biases. Besides, responses may also have been influenced by social desirability bias, especially because interviews were conducted in health service environments, where participants may have felt inclined to provide answers perceived as socially or medically appropriate. Another limitation is the lack of objective verification of vaccination status or screening records. The lack of linkage with immunization registries or clinical databases prevented confirmation of reported behaviors and may have led to misclassification. Furthermore, unmeasured sociocultural variables, such as religious beliefs, gender norms, partner influence, exposure to misinformation, and previous negative experiences with health services, may have shaped perceptions and responses but were not fully captured by the study instrument. Additionally, the study was conducted in a socially vulnerable population receiving primary healthcare services in a specific territorial context. Although this focus may strengthen the relevance of the findings for similar settings, it may limit external generalizability to populations with different socioeconomic profiles, healthcare access patterns, or educational backgrounds. Finally, instrument‐related limitations should also be considered. Although structured questionnaires facilitate comparability, they may not fully capture the complexity of knowledge, beliefs, and decision‐making processes related to HPV vaccination and cervical cancer prevention.

Despite these limitations, our findings may contribute to public health implications. They support the view that HPV vaccination is essential but not sufficient as a standalone strategy for cervical cancer control. Comprehensive prevention should integrate immunization with sustained health education, screening adherence to screening recommendations, and active community engagement. In FHS/PHC settings, healthcare professionals may play an important role in reinforcing the HPV‐cervical cancer link, clarifying the purpose and periodicity of Pap smear screening, addressing vaccine misinformation, and tailoring communication strategies to younger and socioeconomically disadvantaged women. These actions are aligned with the global call to eliminate cervical cancer and may contribute to more equitable access to vaccination, screening, and timely preventive care. Cervical cancer is a preventable disease, and reducing its burden requires coordinated efforts that combine health system strengthening, culturally tailored education, and sustained engagement with vulnerable populations [25].

In conclusion, this study highlights persistent gaps in knowledge regarding HPV infection, cervical cancer, and preventive practices among women assisted by primary health care services, despite favorable vaccination attitudes and high reported willingness to vaccinate children. The coexistence of substantial HPV vaccine acceptance with limited understanding of disease between HPV infection and cervical cancer and of cervical cancer screening underscores a disconnect between preventive behaviors and health literacy. Our findings reinforce the central role of primary health care, particularly the FHS/PHC settings, in delivering context‐sensitive health education, strengthening risk communication, and engaging socially vulnerable populations. The observed generational shift in information access emphasizes the need to integrate traditional communication channels, such as television, with digital and social media platforms to optimize the reach and effectiveness of cervical cancer prevention strategies. The persistence of suboptimal screening adherence, combined with declining vaccination coverage nationwide, underscores the importance of reinforcing both primary and secondary prevention efforts. Strengthening organized screening programs, improving public understanding of the preventive purpose of Pap smears, and sustaining confidence in HPV vaccination remain essential components of comprehensive cervical cancer control.
