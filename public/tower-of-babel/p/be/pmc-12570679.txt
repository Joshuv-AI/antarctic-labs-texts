Inappropriate GFR equations misrepresent CKD epidemiology and risk interpretation in scientific reports
Authors: Sina Bazmi, Nasrollah Ghahramani, Mojtaba Farjam
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
BackgroundThe accuracy of chronic kidney disease (CKD) prevalence and its associations with metabolic risk factors depends heavily on the formula used to estimate glomerular filtration rate (eGFR). In Iran, many epidemiologic studies still rely on outdated equations, which may distort epidemiologic findings and misinform public health estimates.MethodsWe analyzed 9,911 adults from the Fasa Adult Cohort Study (FACS) at baseline and 2,977 at 5-year follow-up. eGFR was calculated using Cockcroft-Gault, MDRD, and CKD-EPI 2021 equations. CKD was defined as eGFR < 60 mL/min/1.73 m². Associations of metabolic dysfunction-associated steatotic liver disease (MASLD) and the fatty liver index (FLI) with CKD prevalence and incidence were examined using logistic regression, with polynomial regression applied for continuous eGFR outcomes.ResultsAt baseline, mean eGFR varied substantially by equation (Cockcroft-Gault: 80.7 ± 20.6; MDRD: 71.0 ± 11.1; CKD-EPI: 82.7 ± 13.3). CKD prevalence was estimated at 15.1% with Cockcroft-Gault, 14.5% with MDRD, but only 3.5% with CKD-EPI (χ² p < 0.001). Similar discrepancies were observed for 5-year incidence (23.0%, 29.5%, and 9.9%, respectively; χ² p < 0.001). In longitudinal analyses, MASLD was associated with higher CKD risk when using CKD-EPI (OR 1.35, 95%CI 1.01–1.79) and MDRD (OR 1.32, 95%CI 1.08–1.62), but lower CKD risk with Cockcroft-Gault (OR:0.10,95%CI:0.08–0.13,p < 0.001). Continuous analyses showed the same directionally inconsistent associations between FLI and eGFR depending on the equation used.ConclusionsThe choice of GFR estimating equation substantially alters CKD prevalence estimates and the observed relationships with MASLD. CKD-EPI 2021 produced results consistent with biological plausibility and guideline recommendations. For accurate epidemiology, public health planning, and international comparability, researchers in Iran and globally should adopt the most updated eGFR equations.Clinical trial numberNot applicable.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12882-025-04537-x.

Introduction

The CKD-EPI 2021 formula is a comprehensive, race-free equation last modified recently in 2021 [1]. It has since been adopted as the preferred method for estimating GFR and diagnosing CKD [2]. However, many studies conducted and published in Iran between 2022 and 2025 continue to use the older MDRD and Cockcroft-Gault formulas. This persists despite current clinical practice guidelines, including KDIGO 2024, recommending CKD-EPI over MDRD as the preferred method for estimating GFR and diagnosing CKD [2–4]. The Cockcroft-Gault formula is considered outdated for GFR estimation and CKD diagnosis, with its primary remaining application being drug dosing [5].

In a review of Iranian studies published since 2022 that assessed kidney function or defined CKD using GFR, and where the full texts were available and the formula used was explicitly stated, the majority relied on the Cockcroft-Gault formula [6–12], followed by a substantial number using MDRD [13–20], particularly in studies where CKD was a key variable. Only a minority employed the currently recommended CKD-EPI formula [21–27]. Additionally, while conducting a study on the relationship between hepatic steatosis —assessed using the Fatty Liver Index (FLI), a validated composite score based on BMI, waist circumference, triglycerides, and γ-glutamyl transferase [28]—and kidney function in our cohort of general population, we observed that the choice of GFR formula substantially influenced the apparent associations. This observation underscored the methodological issue of relying on older formulas. Importantly, prior studies have shown that hepatic steatosis and metabolic dysfunction are linked with higher CKD risk through mechanisms such as insulin resistance, systemic inflammation, and altered renal hemodynamics [29, 30]. Thus, variation in findings across equations may reflect methodological artifacts. As a result, we decided to report these observations in a rapid communication, with the hope of encouraging a shift toward more accurate and up-to-date practices.

Methods

This study used data from the FACS cohort (n = 10,138), a rural subset of the PERSIAN cohort on non-communicable diseases. Adults aged 35–70 from Sheshdeh and 24 nearby villages in Fasa, Iran, were enrolled between 2015 and 2016 through structured questionnaires, exams, and lab tests [31]. Data were stored securely per FACS protocol. This study used data from the FACS cohort (n = 10,138 at baseline). Of these, 3,034 participants were randomly selected and re-examined after 5 years [32]. CKD prevalence was estimated using the full baseline cohort, while CKD incidence was assessed in the 5-year follow-up cohort. For analyses involving MASLD or FLI, additional exclusions were applied: participants with missing baseline data on liver indices, GFR, or major chronic diseases, as well as those with cancer, pregnancy, or hepatitis B/C, were excluded. In longitudinal liver–kidney analyses, individuals with baseline CKD or missing follow-up GFR were also excluded (see Figure S1). Thus, the analytic sample size differs across analyses depending on availability of both exposure and outcome data.

This cohort was selected because prior studies using it reported unexpectedly high CKD prevalence estimates, raising concerns about potential bias from older GFR estimation formulas.

Steatotic liver disease (SLD) was identified using the Fatty Liver Index (FLI), a validated non-invasive marker based on BMI, triglycerides, waist circumference, and GGT [28]. Metabolic dysfunction-associated steatotic liver disease (MASLD) was defined as the presence of SLD, determined by an FLI above geographically based cutoffs for Iran (men: 46.9; women: 53.8) [33], in combination with at least one metabolic risk factor [34].

Chronic kidney disease (CKD) assessment

CKD was defined as an estimated glomerular filtration rate (eGFR) < 60 mL/min/1.73 m² [35]. Albuminuria (urine albumin-to-creatinine ratio, ACR) was not available in our dataset; therefore, CKD was defined solely on the basis of reduced eGFR. While the KDIGO 2024 guidelines recommend defining CKD by both eGFR and ACR, our findings should be interpreted with this limitation in mind. We compared CKD prevalence and 5-year incidence estimates across formulas.

eGFR was calculated using three commonly applied formulas: Cockcroft-Gault [36], Modification of Diet in Renal Disease (MDRD) [37], and CKD-EPI 2021 [1]:

Statistical analyses

Associations between MASLD and CKD were assessed using multivariable logistic regression in both cross-sectional and longitudinal analyses. We also explored the continuous relationship between FLI and eGFR using linear and polynomial regression models. These exploratory analyses were performed to illustrate how different GFR equations may alter the apparent dose–response pattern. Beta coefficients are expressed in units of change in eGFR (mL/min/1.73 m²) per 1-unit increase in FLI. All models were adjusted for potential confounders (Table 1), with p < 0.2 in the univariate analyses.

Statistical analyses were performed using SPSS version 23, with a p-value < 0.05 considered statistically significant. A separate, full-length study is underway to examine the associations in detail using the updated equation. That paper will include full methodology, population characteristics, and statistical details. Due to space limits, these elements were omitted here. The purpose of this brief communication is solely to highlight the methodological bias introduced by outdated GFR equations.

Results

Analyzed population characteristics

Of the 9,911 participants enrolled at baseline (mean age: 48.64 ± 9.55 years; 54.4% female), mean GFR values were 82.73 ± 13.27 (CKD-EPI 2021), 71.02 ± 11.10 (MDRD), and 80.71 ± 20.57 (Cockcroft-Gault). At the 5-year follow-up (n = 2,977; mean age: 50.18 ± 8.47; 54% female), corresponding GFR values were 76.82 ± 14.29, 67.10 ± 12.56, and 74.70 ± 20.20, respectively. The prevalence of diabetes (12.3%), hypertension (20.0%), and ischemic heart disease (10.8%) reflected the expected burden of cardiometabolic disease in this rural Iranian population. MASLD was also common at baseline (34.3%) (Table S1). Mean eGFR values differed substantially across the three equations, both at baseline and follow-up (Table S2), underscoring the methodological impact of formula choice. We compared baseline CKD prevalence between the full cohort (n = 10,138) and the analytic subset used for MASLD-CKD analysis (n = 2,906). CKD prevalence estimates were nearly identical across the two groups, indicating that exclusions did not materially alter prevalence patterns.

At follow-up, participants showed an increase in mean body weight from 67.0 ± 13.2 kg at baseline to 68.6 ± 13.5 kg. A decline in kidney function was observed across all eGFR estimation formulas. The mean eGFR decreased from 80.7 ± 20.6 to 74.7 ± 20.2 mL/min/1.73 m² by Cockcroft-Gault, from 71.0 ± 11.1 to 67.1 ± 12.6 mL/min/1.73 m² by MDRD, and from 82.7 ± 13.3 to 76.8 ± 14.3 mL/min/1.73 m² by CKD-EPI 2021 (Table S2). Concurrently, hepatic parameters worsened, with the mean FLI increasing from 39.5 ± 27.7 at baseline to 45.4 ± 27.8 at follow-up, indicating progression of liver steatosis.

1. The Association Between MASLD and CKD Depends Critically on the GFR Estimation Formula.

In cross-sectional analysis, the estimated association between MASLD and CKD differed substantially depending on the GFR estimation method (Table 1). When using the Cockcroft-Gault formula, MASLD showed a strong inverse association with CKD (OR:0.10,95%CI:0.08–0.13,p < 0.001), suggesting a protective effect, which is not consistent with current biological understanding. In contrast, both MDRD and CKD-EPI 2021 formulas demonstrated significant positive associations (MDRD: OR = 1.42,95%CI:1.25–1.61; CKD-EPI: OR = 1.55,95%CI:1.23–1.95; both p < 0.001), indicating an increased odds of CKD in individuals with MASLD.

These trends persisted in longitudinal analyses over a 5-year follow-up. Again, Cockcroft-Gault showed an inverse association (OR:0.33,95%CI:0.25–0.44, p < 0.001), while MDRD (OR:1.32,95%CI:1.08–1.62, p = 0.006) and CKD-EPI (OR:1.35,95%CI:1.01–1.79,p = 0.040) supported a positive longitudinal association between MASLD and CKD incidence.

These contrasting results demonstrate that use of the Cockcroft-Gault formula may severely distort the true direction of association and introduce substantial bias.

2. Higher FLI Is Associated with Lower GFR Only When Using Updated GFR Estimation Methods.

The relationship between FLI and GFR also varied according to the GFR formula used (Table 1). In cross-sectional analysis, Cockcroft-Gault yielded a positive linear association (β = 0.31 mL/min/1.73 m² per unit increase in FLI, 95%CI:0.29–0.33, p < 0.001), implying that higher FLI corresponds to higher GFR. However, MDRD and CKD-EPI showed negative associations (MDRD: β=-0.03, CKD-EPI:β=-0.04; both p < 0.001), indicating that higher FLI was associated with lower eGFR, aligning with the expected detrimental relationship between metabolic dysfunction and renal function. Nonlinear models (quadratic and cubic) consistently confirmed the significance of these patterns. While the absolute changes in eGFR per FLI unit are small and of limited direct clinical significance, the directionality of the associations illustrates how outdated equations can produce biologically implausible relationships.

In longitudinal analysis over 5 years, the same pattern persisted. Cockcroft-Gault again showed a positive linear association (β = 0.24,95%CI:0.20–0.28, p < 0.001), whereas MDRD and CKD-EPI both showed negative associations (β=-0.06;both p < 0.001), again reflecting a more physiologically plausible link between fatty liver and GFR decline.

3. CKD Prevalence and Incidence Are Substantially Overestimated by Cockcroft-Gault and MDRD.

The choice of GFR formula significantly influenced the estimated prevalence and incidence of CKD (Table 2; Fig. 1). At baseline, CKD prevalence was estimated at 15.1% using Cockcroft-Gault and 14.5% using MDRD, but only 3.5% with CKD-EPI (χ² test for difference, p < 0.001). Similarly, the 5-year CKD incidence was 23.0% with Cockcroft-Gault and 29.5% with MDRD, compared to 9.9% with CKD-EPI (χ² test for difference, p < 0.001). These statistical tests confirm that the observed discrepancies across formulas are highly significant.

These findings clearly indicate that Cockcroft-Gault and MDRD formulas yield substantially higher CKD prevalence and incidence estimates compared with CKD-EPI, and their use may result in spurious or misleading associations in epidemiological studies.

Discussion

Our findings demonstrate the critical impact of GFR estimation methods on associations involving CKD. The Cockcroft-Gault formula consistently produced paradoxical results, such as a protective association between MASLD and CKD, which contradict biological understanding [29]. In contrast, MDRD and CKD-EPI 2021 revealed positive associations more consistent with known pathophysiology [30]. Similarly, higher FLI was linked to lower GFR only when modern formulas were used, while Cockcroft-Gault suggested misleading inverse relationships. CKD prevalence and incidence were reported at much higher levels by both Cockcroft-Gault and MDRD compared with CKD-EPI, as stated in prior population-based research [38]. While we cannot establish which equation reflects the true prevalence without a measured GFR gold standard, the discrepancies underscore how reliance on older formulas can distort epidemiologic inference, particularly in settings like Iran, where their use remains widespread. Our review of recent Iranian studies shows continued reliance on Cockcroft-Gault, despite its obsolescence for GFR estimation and CKD diagnosis.

We selected MASLD and FLI as the focal exposures for two main reasons. First, MASLD is a highly prevalent, metabolically driven condition with known bidirectional links to kidney dysfunction, making it an ideal test case for evaluating CKD-related associations. Second, in our initial analyses, we observed that the direction and magnitude of association between MASLD/FLI and CKD varied dramatically depending on the GFR formula used. Cockcroft-Gault suggested an inverse association, whereas MDRD and CKD-EPI indicated positive associations consistent with the known metabolic and inflammatory pathways linking MASLD to CKD [29, 30]. Previous meta-analyses have shown that MASLD is associated with increased risk of CKD [29], supporting the plausibility of the CKD-EPI findings. Thus, our results illustrate how outdated equations like Cockcroft-Gault may produce biologically implausible associations that diverge from established evidence. This made MASLD an especially illustrative and biologically relevant variable to highlight how outdated GFR equations can mislead epidemiologic inference.

The Cockcroft-Gault formula has been used for decades in both clinical practice and research as a simple bedside method to approximate kidney function. Developed from a small cohort of 249 hospitalized patients in 1976 [36], it is an older formula whose main advantage was its simplicity, allowing for rapid bedside calculations. However, with mobile calculators now readily available, this ease of use has become irrelevant. Moreover, the formula has several limitations. It estimates creatinine clearance rather than true GFR, and was developed in a small, homogeneous patient sample. The original cohort included only Caucasian individuals, with just 4% being women. Additionally, serum creatinine was not measured using a standardized method. The inclusion of weight as a covariate introduces significant inaccuracies and biases, particularly in individuals with abnormal BMI, such as those who are underweight or obese [39]. Today, the Cockcroft-Gault formula is primarily used for drug dosing recommendations.

The MDRD formula, introduced in 1999 to estimate actual GFR, was derived from a U.S. cohort of 1,628 patients with CKD and a mean GFR of 40. This cohort included 40% women and 12% African Americans [40]. To better account for endogenous creatinine production, the formula incorporated age, sex, and ethnicity and was indexed to body surface area (BSA), eliminating the need to include weight. Multiple studies have confirmed its superiority over the Cockcroft-Gault formula. Nonetheless, MDRD has important limitations. It was developed exclusively in a CKD population, whereas the relationship between serum creatinine and GFR differs in healthy individuals. As a result, MDRD tends to underestimate GFR values above 60 [41] and overestimate the prevalence of stage 3 CKD (GFR < 60) in the general population. Moreover, the creatinine measurements were not obtained using precise standardized methods [41], limiting the formula’s accuracy.

The CKD-EPI formula was developed to overcome the limitations of MDRD. It was created using data from 26 different cohorts, including MDRD, comprising a total of 5,504 individuals and validated internally in 2,750 subjects [37]. External validation was performed in 3,896 individuals from a separate dataset in which GFR was measured using a different methodology. Unlike the MDRD cohort, many individuals in this population had GFR values above 60 (mean GFR: 68), and many were potential kidney donors. CKD-EPI significantly reduced estimation bias, and its superiority over MDRD has been demonstrated in various epidemiological studies [3, 4]. However, some studies suggest that CKD-EPI may overestimate GFR values below 90 [42]. The first version of CKD-EPI was developed in 2009 [37], validated in 2012 [43], and most recently revised in 2021 [1]. The CKD-EPI 2021 formula, which no longer includes race as a variable, is the latest and most accurate GFR estimation tool. It has been endorsed as the standard method by the National Kidney Foundation and the American Society of Nephrology [2], and is now widely used by nephrologists.

Our goal in reporting the results is to demonstrate that in studies where GFR is used, especially for assessing the primary relationships, employing the Cockcroft-Gault formula can distort associations, to the extent of producing results that are completely contrary to biological evidence. Additionally, using the MDRD formula can substantially inflate CKD prevalence or incidence estimates, which in turn may potentially yield incorrect associations between CKD and other variables. However, CKD-EPI produced results more consistent with established biology and international guidelines. This is particularly concerning given that many studies conducted in Iran still rely on these older formulas.

In Iran, several factors may further accentuate discrepancies across equations. The population has a high prevalence of obesity [44], metabolic syndrome [45], and fatty liver disease [46], all of which alter body weight and composition. Since Cockcroft-Gault directly incorporates weight, shifts in weight distribution with disease progression could distort results. Moreover, rural Iranian populations may differ from Western validation cohorts in dietary patterns, muscle mass, and creatinine generation [47, 48]. These differences may partly explain why reliance on Cockcroft-Gault and MDRD led to particularly inflated CKD prevalence estimates in our dataset.

Several limitations should be acknowledged. First, CKD was defined using eGFR alone, without urine albumin-to-creatinine ratio (ACR), which may underestimate early CKD. According to KDIGO 2024, both eGFR and albuminuria are required to fully characterize CKD. Therefore, our prevalence and incidence estimates may underestimate the true CKD burden, especially for early disease stages characterized by preserved eGFR but elevated ACR. Future studies should incorporate both parameters to provide a more comprehensive assessment. Second, while our cohort is large and population-based, the findings may not generalize to all Iranian subpopulations or to other countries with different creatinine generation or body composition. Third, although we compared three widely used GFR equations, no measured GFR was available as a gold standard, so we cannot definitively determine which estimates are closest to true kidney function. Finally, because this was a brief communication, we focused on illustrating methodological discrepancies rather than providing a comprehensive etiologic analysis. Taken together, these limitations indicate that our results should be interpreted with caution. Nonetheless, they highlight the importance of formula selection when defining CKD in epidemiologic research and public health surveillance.

In conclusion, our findings underscore that the choice of GFR estimating equation substantially influences CKD prevalence estimates and the observed associations with outcomes such as MASLD. Using CKD-EPI 2021 yields results that are more consistent with current biological understanding and international guideline recommendations. While our study cannot establish a gold standard, it highlights the importance of carefully selecting the most updated and validated equations when defining CKD in epidemiologic research and public health surveillance. Doing so will improve comparability across studies and enhance the accuracy of population estimates. Public health estimates, health policy planning, and meta-analyses may be undermined if this methodological issue is not addressed, especially in Iran.
