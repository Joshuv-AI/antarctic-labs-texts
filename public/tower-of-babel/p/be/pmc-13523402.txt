Shortness of Breath Due to a Large Spontaneous Left Atrial Intramural Hematoma
Authors: Anton AW Mulder, Dominique Vergeer, Jules R Olsthoorn, J Joost Kardux
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractCase summaryA 58-year-old man who had recently run a marathon and a 10-mile run was admitted with acute severe shortness of breath. Echocardiography showed a large left atrial mass. Cardiac magnetic resonance imaging showed intermediate signal intensity on T1-weighted images and slightly elevated signal on T2-weighted images. There was no enhancement of the mass on either first-pass or late-gadolinium enhancement images. The mass was considered to be very likely a spontaneous left atrial intramural hematoma (LAIH). The patient underwent surgery, where the diagnosis was confirmed.Take-Home MessagesLAIHs are mainly iatrogenic, caused by interventions such as mitral valve surgery, percutaneous coronary interventions, and radiofrequency catheter ablations. Spontaneous LAIH can be due to an underlying pathology, such as mitral annular calcification, mitral valve endocarditis, myocardial infarction, cardiac amyloidosis, or without an underlying pathology, such as in our case.Key words: cardiac magnetic resonance, computed tomography, imaging

Clinical Vignette

A 58-year-old man was sent to the first cardiac care unit with acute severe shortness of breath, with a saturation of 70%. The patient had run a marathon 3.5 weeks before and also completed a 10-mile run 3 days before. He had no medical history and did not use any medication.

Electrocardiography demonstrated a sinus tachycardia. A chest x-ray showed mainly perihilar interstitial edema with diffuse spots of consolidations, consistent with either pneumonia or acute congestion (Figure 1). Laboratory tests showed an elevated C-reactive protein of 239 mg/L, N-terminal pro–B-type natriuretic peptide of 6,093 ng/L, and troponin of 883 nL/L. Creatine kinase was normal. An echocardiography showed a mildly hypertrophic left ventricle with normal systolic function and a large mass in the left atrium of 65 × 51 mm with a subvalvular mitral valve stenosis due to the mass (Figure 1, Video 1). What stood out were the partitions in the mass.

Chest computed tomography showed a large lenticular mass (Figure 1), which did not enhance in the arterial or the portal venous phase. The lungs showed interstitial edema and patchy consolidations, most likely due to pulmonary congestion. Possibly superimposed infectious infiltrate. The mass measured 50 Hounsfield units, which is consistent with blood or acute hemorrhage.

Cardiac magnetic resonance imaging (CMR) showed a large, smoothly lenticular 70 × 67 × 54 mm left atrial mass originating from the posterior wall of the left atrium (Figure 1, Video 2). On T1-weighted and T2-weighted imaging, the mass was inhomogeneous and had an intermediate signal intensity on T1-weighted and slightly elevated on T2-weighted images. On STIR (short-tau inversion recovery) imaging, the mass had a high signal intensity, which is consistent with high fluid content. First-pass perfusion imaging with gadolinium showed no enhancement. Early-gadolinium enhancement and late-gadolinium enhancement (LGE) PSIR (phase-sensitive inversion recovery) imaging showed no enhancement (Figure 1). Also, LGE imaging with a prolonged inversion time (600 ms) showed no enhancement—a finding consistent with thrombus or clot.

The multiloculated mass at echocardiography,1 the large smoothly lenticular mass on computed tomography measuring 50 Hounsfield units, in combination with the CMR findings using T1-weighted, T2-weighted, first-pass enhancement, and LGE imaging made the diagnosis of a spontaneous left atrial intramural hematoma (LAIH) very likely.

The patient underwent urgent surgery. During the operation the mass was inadvertently opened, releasing blood and thereby confirming the diagnosis.

After surgery the patient had a speedy recovery. An echocardiogram and CMR performed 1 and 5 months after surgery showed only a minimal remnant (Supplemental Material).

Discussion

Intramural hematoma is a very rare cause for a mass in the left atrium.2 In the atrial wall a small false, blood-filled cavity arises, which can grow out into a large atrial mass.2 In the literature there are only a small number of cases in which LAIH is described, with various causes.2 The causes could be categorized in surgical or nonsurgical.2 Nonsurgical LAIH is further categorized as iatrogenic or spontaneous.2 Spontaneous LAIH can be due to an underlying pathology or without underlying pathology.

In our case, the patient had no past medical history, was healthy, very athletic, and had a completely normal heart, with exception of the spontaneous LAIH.
