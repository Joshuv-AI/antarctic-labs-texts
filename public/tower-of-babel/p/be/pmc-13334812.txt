DNA barcoding to delineate species boundaries and assess genetic divergence in halophytes of the United Arab Emirates
Authors: Rahul Jamdade, Mariam Alsallani, Khawla Al Shaer, Sanjay Gairola, Tamer Mahmoud, Hatem A Shabana
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractHalophytes represent a unique group of salt-tolerant plants that are critical to the ecology of arid and semi-arid environments, yet taxonomic resolution of some taxa remains challenging due to morphological plasticity and limited molecular data. This study employed a multilocus DNA barcoding approach using two chloroplast markers, rbcL and matK, and the nuclear ribosomal marker ITS2 to assess species-level resolution among halophytic plants from the United Arab Emirates (UAE). A total of 137 field-collected samples representing 38 species were processed, generating 373 high-quality barcode sequences across the three loci. Because PCR amplification and sequencing success varied among markers, not all samples yielded usable sequences for every locus. Consequently, the final marker-specific datasets comprised 132 sequences for rbcL, 125 for matK, and 116 for ITS2. To improve representation of species sampled by one or two individuals, 36 additional sequences were retrieved from GenBank, resulting in 409 sequences analyzed in total. The rbcL showed the highest amplification and sequencing success but the lowest species-level discrimination. In contrast, matK and ITS2 exhibited higher sequence variability and interspecific divergence, with ITS2 providing the strongest resolution for closely related taxa. Barcode gap and OTU-based ASAP analyses revealed limitations in resolving species within taxonomically complex genera, particularly Cyperus, Zygophyllum, and Tamarix. Supervised machine learning classifiers, especially Support Vector Machines, improved taxonomic assignment compared with traditional distance-based methods and achieved up to 97.7% classification accuracy. However, unresolved cases remained in closely related genera, reflecting biological constraints such as low interspecific divergence, recent divergence, hybridization, polyploidy, and limited barcode gaps. The present study demonstrates that multilocus barcoding combined with machine learning can improve species identification of UAE halophytes, while also highlighting the limitations of standard barcode loci in complex plant groups. The generated reference dataset contributes to the regional DNA barcode library and provides a molecular framework for biodiversity monitoring, conservation, and the sustainable utilization of salt-tolerant plant diversity in arid ecosystems.Keywords: Halophytes, Multilocus DNA barcoding, Genetic diversity, Machine learning, United Arab Emirates

Introduction

Halophytes are salt-tolerant plants found in both coastal and inland saline habitats, and their ability to thrive in harsh conditions, particularly high salinity, is attributed to the development of specialized morphological and physiological traits.1, 2 Currently, about 2600 halophyte species are reported in scientific literature.3 According to Ghazanfar et al.4 about 120 halophytic taxa are found in the Arabian Peninsula, making up about 4% of the region's total flora. In the United Arab Emirates (UAE), approximately 74 species are considered halophytes, which are distributed across 19 families, primarily found along the coast, and also inland in saline areas.5 The halophytic plants of the Arabian Peninsula hold significant ecological and socioeconomic value. Literature suggests that many halophytes have the potential to be used for medicinal, nutritional, and phytoremediation purposes, as well as for the reclamation of heavy metal-contaminated soils and the management of saline soils.6, 7, 8, 9 Halophytes also play a crucial role in protecting coastal habitats by serving as sand dune stabilizers to prevent erosion and acting as a barrier against seawater intrusion into freshwater ecosystems.10, 11 Recently, a review by Mann et al.12 highlighted the potential of halophytic genes in enhancing salt tolerance in crops and emphasized that the genetic diversity of salt-tolerant species could uncover key regulatory mechanisms for improving resilience. As a result, there is growing interest in these species, highlighting their importance from both a conservation and sustainable utilization perspective.

In recent years, DNA barcoding has become a highly efficient and commonly employed technique for the rapid and precise identification of plant species. This advancement has greatly enhanced the accuracy of plant species identification, their conservation, utilization, and related research. Several studies have assessed the effectiveness of plant DNA barcodes in taxonomic and floristic contexts.13, 14, 15, 16, 17, 18 Studies have also shown that DNA barcoding in plants is challenging, as accurate species identification often requires more than one barcode region. The rbcL, matK, trnHpsbA, ITS2, and ETS markers have been employed to DNA barcode species in the UAE flora.19, 20, 21, 22, 23, 24 In a study by Jamdade et al.,22 multilocus markers including chloroplast DNA (rbcL, matK, and trnHpsbA) and nuclear ribosomal DNA (ITS2 and ETS) were evaluated for their effectiveness in delimiting the halophyte Salicornia persica Akhani and discriminating its populations in the UAE. Similarly, the capability of the nuclear ITS2 barcode, and the plastid DNA barcodes rbcL and matK to identify 20 medicinally important plant species of the UAE belonging to the family Caryophyllaceae was evaluated by Jamdade et al.,23 and it was found that the discrimination effectiveness of these barcode loci varied among plant species within the same family.

More recently, Jamdade et al. (2025, under review) used the combination of rbcL, matK, and ITS2 regions to barcode 58 medicinal plants of the UAE, and evaluated their ability to accurately identify species. However, the large number of the UAE's flowering plant species still needs to be barcoded, and our efforts to continue this work are ongoing. The focus of this study was to DNA barcode selected halophytes of the UAE using the rbcL, matK, and ITS2 regions. The findings will contribute to the DNA sequence database for halophytic plants and highlight our continued efforts in DNA barcoding the country's flora.

Materials and methods

Sample collection and specimen documentation

Several field surveys were conducted between 2018 and 2023 to collect voucher specimens of target halophytic plant species from different locations across the United Arab Emirates (Fig. 1). Fresh plant material was subsampled in silica gel during fieldwork, and the samples were subsequently stored at room temperature (20 ± 2 °C) until DNA extraction.

Specimen Collection Sites of Halophytes in the United Arab Emirates.

Species identification was initially carried out in the field and later confirmed using diagnostic morphological characters, standard regional floras, and relevant taxonomic literature.25, 26, 27 For each specimen, herbarium vouchers were prepared and deposited in the Sharjah Seed Bank and Herbarium (SSBH) to ensure traceability and future reference. All voucher records were incorporated into a local sample database and also submitted to the Barcode of Life Data Systems (BOLD v5; dx. doi.org/10.5883/DS-HALOPHYT).

A total of 137 samples belonging to 38 species representing 30 genera and 15 families were collected (Table S1), with most species represented by 3–5 individuals. Species were selected based on their ecological relevance as halophytes, distribution across sampling locations, and representation of major taxonomic groups in the UAE flora. In cases where species were represented by one or two individuals due to limited distribution, low abundance, or restricted accessibility, additional sequences were retrieved from GenBank to supplement the dataset. Detailed sampling and accession information are provided in Tables S1 & S2.

DNA extraction

Silica-dried plant tissues were finely ground using a Bead Blaster 24 microcentrifuge homogenizer, and genomic DNA was subsequently extracted (Table S1). The DNeasy Plant Mini Kit (Qiagen, Hilden, Germany) was used following the manufacturer's guidelines, with minor modifications to optimize efficiency. The modified protocol included the initial addition of proteinase K, followed by AP1 buffer and RNase A, with an extended incubation period of three hours. DNA was eluted in nuclease-free water; its quality was assessed using 1% agarose gel electrophoresis (BioRad, Hercules, CA, USA). DNA concentration was quantified by spectrophotometry (Denovix, Wilmington, DE, USA).

A total of 137 samples were processed for DNA extraction and amplification. However, due to variation in PCR amplification and sequencing success among markers, not all samples yielded high-quality sequences for each locus. Consequently, marker-specific datasets consisted of 132 sequences for rbcL, 125 for matK, and 116 for ITS2, which were retained for downstream analyses.

PCR amplification, purification, and sequencing

Polymerase Chain Reaction (PCR) was used to amplify two plastid barcode markers, rbcL and matK, and the nuclear ribosomal marker ITS2 using region-specific forward and reverse primers (Table S3). PCR conditions were adapted from published protocols,13, 28, 29, 30 with necessary modifications. Reactions were performed in a 25 μL volume using a 5× FIREPol master mix (BioRad, Singapore) on an Applied Biosystems Veriti Thermal Cycler (Life Technologies Holdings Pvt. Ltd., Singapore). For samples that failed amplification under standard conditions, the KAPA 3G Plant PCR kit was used as an alternative. PCR products were validated on a 2% agarose gel, purified using the MEGAquick-spinTM Plus Total Fragment DNA Purification Kit (Intron Biotechnology), and submitted for commercial sequencing.

DNA sequence alignment

Sequences were aligned using Geneious Prime (version 2023.2.1), selected for its advanced alignment algorithms and intuitive interface, ensuring high-accuracy multiple sequence alignments. Default settings were used, including standard gap opening and extension penalties, to maintain sequence integrity.31 The sequences generated in this study were submitted to NCBI GenBank, and their accession numbers are listed in Table S2.

DNA sequence analysis

Sequence characteristics for each marker were calculated from aligned FASTA files using R (v4.3.1) with the ape, pegas, and spider packages.32, 33, 34 GC content was calculated using GC.content().32 Variable sites were identified as alignment positions with >1 nucleotide state. Parsimony-informative (PI) sites were defined as positions with ≥2 nucleotide states each occurring in ≥2 sequences.34 Conserved sites were derived by subtracting the alignment length from variable sites. Nucleotide diversity (π) was computed using nuc.div().33 Amplification and sequencing success were calculated from experimental records as described in 2.3, 2.4.

Genetic divergence and barcode gap analysis

Genetic divergence at different taxonomic levels was estimated using the Kimura 2-parameter (K2P) model within the distance summary tool on the BOLD Systems V5 web server.35 The K2P model was chosen for its efficiency36 in assessing evolutionary distance.37 Barcode gap analysis was conducted by comparing intra-specific and nearest-neighboring distances for the rbcL, matK, and ITS2 datasets to delineate species boundaries.38 This method was instrumental in identifying overlaps between intra- and interspecific variations, improving taxonomic resolution.

Genetic distance-based taxonomic analysis

Operational Taxonomic Unit (OTU) assignment was performed using the Assemble Species by Automatic Partitioning (ASAP) tool through Spart Explorer (spartexplorer.mnhn.fr/delimitation),39 employing the JC69 genetic distance model. ASAP determines species boundaries by optimizing sequence similarity partitions. To analyze these partitions, a custom Python script was developed to extract and categorize species and sample IDs. Classification categories included:

1.True: Subsets containing only one species, with no overlap in other Subsets.

True: Subsets containing only one species, with no overlap in other Subsets.

2.Ambiguous: Subsets with species names appearing across multiple partitions.

Ambiguous: Subsets with species names appearing across multiple partitions.

3.Incorrect: Subsets containing multiple, distinct species.

Incorrect: Subsets containing multiple, distinct species.

The results were compiled into an Excel summary table for visualization and further analysis.

Character-based taxonomic analysis using machine learning

Sequence data previously aligned in Geneious Prime were converted to ARFF format using the DNA Barcode FASTA2WEKA Converter. This tool transforms each nucleotide position into a categorical attribute (A, T, G, C, or gap) while preserving positional information across the aligned sequence region. Species identities were designated as the class attribute for supervised learning.

All machine learning analyses were performed using 10-fold cross-validation in the WEKA platform (version 3.8.6)40 to ensure robust and unbiased performance estimates.

2.8.1.Classifier Selection via WEKA Experimenter

Classifier Selection via WEKA Experimenter

To identify the most effective machine learning algorithm for halophyte species identification, we conducted an initial comparative evaluation using the WEKA Experimenter module.40 Seven supervised learning algorithms representing diverse methodological approaches were assessed: Naive Bayes (probabilistic classifier), Support Vector Machine (SMO implementation with polynomial kernel), k-Nearest Neighbors (IBk, k = 1), Multi-Class Classifier (with logistic regression as base classifier), Decision Table, Random Forest (100 trees), and J48 Decision Tree (C4.5 implementation). Full parameter configurations for each classifier are provided in Table S4 (Section A).

All classifiers were evaluated using 10-fold cross-validation on each DNA barcode marker dataset (rbcL, matK, ITS2), with the dataset randomly partitioned into 10 Subsets. The model was trained on 9 Subsets and tested on the remaining 1 subset, with this process repeated 10 times using different fold assignments. This ensures that every specimen contributes to both training and testing, providing unbiased performance estimates. Performance was assessed based on overall classification accuracy, with the highest- performing algorithm selected as the base classifier for subsequent class-imbalance analyses. This systematic comparison ensured that the chosen classifier was optimally suited for the specific characteristics of halophyte DNA barcode data.

2.8.2.Class Imbalance Handling with Selected Classifier

Class Imbalance Handling with Selected Classifier

Based on the Experimenter results, the Support Vector Machine implemented using the Sequential Minimal Optimization algorithm (SMO) with a polynomial kernel was selected as the base classifier for all subsequent analyses. To address class imbalance arising from variable sample sizes across species, four strategies were compared using 10-fold cross-validation (Table S4, Section B): (1) No Filter baseline – SMO applied directly to original sequence data without modification; (2) CostSensitiveClassifier – meta-learner applying uniform misclassification costs via a 37 × 37 cost matrix with random seed S = 1; (3) SpreadSubsample – undersampling within FilteredClassifier to achieve uniform class distribution (−M 1.0, −X 0.0, —S 1), applied only to training folds to prevent data leakage; and (4) ClassBalancer – instance weighting within FilteredClassifier to equalize class contributions (−num-intervals 10) while preserving all specimens, with SMO natively supporting weighted instances via the WeightedInstancesHandler interface. Performance metrics from 10-fold cross-validation included overall accuracy, per-species accuracy, precision, recall, F1-score, kappa statistic, and area under the ROC curve (AUC). Confusion matrices were generated to visualize misclassification patterns, with results reported at both the species level (taxonomic units) and the specimen level (individual samples). Following established criteria for DNA barcoding studies with imbalanced data,41, 42 species were categorized as Correct (all specimens correctly assigned), Ambiguous (specimens distributed across multiple clusters), Incorrect (merged with other species), or Singleton (single specimen representation). Based on a comparative evaluation, ClassBalancer was selected as the primary method because it preserved all specimens, maintained the near-optimal accuracy of matK (97.7%) and ITS2 (97.0%), and improved rbcL performance from 86.4% to 90.5% at the specimen level. rbcL was the only marker to benefit from imbalance correction.

Visualization and statistical analysis

Figures representing genetic divergence, ASAP partitions, WEKA Experimenter results, and SVM classification performance were generated using a Python script executed in Google Colab. Data visualization relied on Matplotlib and Seaborn, which are widely used for scientific graphing.43, 44

Phylogenetic tree construction and annotation

Phylogenetic trees were constructed using MEGA 12 following a model test to determine the most appropriate evolutionary model for each dataset: T92 + G + I for rbcL, GTR + G + I for matK, and K2 + G + I for ITS2.45 Bootstrap support was evaluated using MEGA's adaptive bootstrap method to ensure statistical robustness. The resulting trees were annotated using the iTOL web server, integrating results from both genetic distance-based and machine learning-based classifications for comprehensive visualization.46

Results

A total of 137 field-collected samples representing 38 halophytic species were processed in this study. However, because sequence recovery varied among barcode loci, the final marker-specific datasets differed in the number of species and sequences retained for analysis. The rbcL and matK datasets each included 37 species, whereas the ITS2 dataset included 36 species. Among the sampled taxa, five species are of conservation significance: Limonium carnosum (Boiss.) Kuntze is categorized as endangered (EN), Avicennia marina (Forssk.) Vierh. as near threatened (NT), and Halopyrum mucronatum (L.) Stapf, Limonium axillare (Forssk.) Kuntze, and Mesembryanthemum nodiflorum L. as Vulnerable (VU) according to the recent IUCN Red List assessment of UAE flora.47

DNA sequence analysis

The study included a total of 137 samples belonging to 38 species. However, amplification success varied across markers despite multiple attempts. Cistanche tubulosa (Schenk) Wight ex Hook.f. consistently failed to amplify for rbcL, resulting in a final dataset of 37 species for this marker. Similarly, Juncus rigidus Desf. failed in matK despite repeated attempts, also yielding 37 species, while Cyperus conglomeratus Rottb. and L. carnosum failed for ITS2, reducing the dataset to 36 species for this marker. Following multiple amplification and sequencing attempts, high-quality sequences were successfully obtained for the majority of samples, resulting in final datasets of 132 (rbcL), 125 (matK), and 116 (ITS2) sequences used for downstream analyses.

The rbcL marker showed the highest laboratory performance during the initial attempt with 76.5% amplification success (101 of 132 samples successfully amplified) and 98.1% sequencing success (99 of 101 amplified samples yielded high-quality sequences). It combined moderate variability (37.1% variable sites; π = 0.08; GC = 42.0%) with the most reliable data recovery. The matK marker offered greater discriminatory power with 68.0% variable sites and π = 0.21, but demonstrated the lowest laboratory performance with only 61.2% amplification success (77 of 125 samples amplified) and 90.2% sequencing success (69 of 77 amplified samples sequenced successfully). Its lower GC content (32.0%) likely contributed to the reduced amplification efficiency. The ITS2 marker exhibited the highest variability, with 79.4% variable characters and π = 0.24, along with high GC content (62.0%) and parsimony-informative sites (71.9%). It showed intermediate laboratory performance with 70.5% amplification success (82 of 116 samples amplified) and 88.8% sequencing success (73 of 82 amplified samples sequenced successfully).

Collectively, these results underscore that rbcL ensures broad data recovery, matK captures extensive genetic variation despite lower amplification efficiency, and ITS2 provides fine-scale resolution of closely related taxa with balanced laboratory performance.

Genetic divergence (K2P) analysis

The genetic divergence analysis using the K2P model revealed marker-specific patterns across taxonomic levels. The rbcL exhibited low divergence within species (mean 0.07%) and genera (mean 0.64%) and moderate divergence at the family level (mean 3.06%) (Table 2), making it more suited for higher taxonomic comparisons. matK showed slightly higher divergence within species (mean 0.15%) and genera (mean 1.17%) than rbcL, with substantial divergence at the family level (mean 7.95%) (Table 2), indicating its better resolution for genus- and family-level differentiation. ITS2, the most variable marker, exhibited notable divergence within species (mean 0.14%) and genera (mean 10.37%), with exceptionally high divergence at the family level (mean 20.25%) (Table 2), highlighting its utility for resolving closely related taxa and distinguishing distant relationships.

Barcode gap analysis

The barcode gap analysis of halophytes from the UAE using rbcL, matK, and ITS2 markers revealed distinct patterns in intra- and inter-specific genetic divergence (Fig. 2). rbcL exhibited low intra-specific divergence (0–0.79%) and narrow nearest-neighbor distances (0.18–6.1%), indicating limited resolution for closely related taxa (Fig. 2(a)). Significant overlaps in genetic distances were observed in about 22 species across 15 genera, where intra-specific and inter-specific divergence converged, limiting its utility for species-level differentiation. matK, with higher intra-specific divergence (up to 1.17%) and larger nearest-neighbor distances (1.49–6.1%), provided improved genus-level and species-level resolution (Fig. 2(b)). However, narrow barcode gaps leading to overlaps among 16 species belonging to 10 genera were observed, further highlighting its moderate resolving power. ITS2 displayed the highest intra-specific variability (up to 2.52%) and the largest nearest-neighbor distances (0–43.47%) (Fig. 2(c)), consistently producing clear barcode gaps for most species, leaving only four unresolved species (Zygophyllum qatarense Hadidi, Zygophyllum mandavillei Hadidi, Tamarix nilotica (Ehrenb.) Bunge, and Tamarix aphylla (L.) H.Karst.) belonging to two genera (Zygophyllum and Tamarix).

Genetic distance-based OTU assignment

The ASAP (Assemble Species by Automatic Partitioning) method was used to evaluate the species-level resolution of the three DNA barcode markers rbcL, matK, and ITS2 based on genetic distance-based OTU partitioning (Fig. 3). Prior to running ASAP, all species represented by fewer than two individuals were validated using NCBI BLAST to identify conspecific sequences showing >98% similarity. Only species with confirmed matches in GenBank were retained in the final dataset (Tables S1 & S2). This verification step ensured that each taxon included in the analysis was represented by reliable sequence data and minimized erroneous OTU formation. It also helped mitigate biases commonly associated with insufficient sampling, incomplete taxon representation, and misassigned sequences, which are known to affect species delimitation and barcode performance41, 42.

ASAP Analysis: Recognizing the Most Correct Species from the Identified Subsets for (a) rbcL, (b) matK, and (c) ITS2 datasets. Partition numbers are shown in the callout boxes.

For each marker, the partition with the highest number of correctly classified species was selected for further analysis. In the rbcL dataset, Partition 2 (ASAP score = 5.5) correctly identified 24 species (64.86%) out of 37 analyzed species (Fig. 3 (a)), while 8 species (21.62%) were misclassified, and 5 species (13.51%) remained ambiguous (Table 3). At the specimen level, 96 of 147 specimens (65.31%) were correctly assigned, with a misclassification rate of 21.09%, and 13.61% of cases were ambiguous (Table 3). The matK dataset exhibited improved classification accuracy, with Partition 1 (ASAP score = 6.0) correctly identifying 27 species (72.97%). Among the analyzed specimens, 98 of 130 (75.38%) were correctly assigned (Fig. 3 (b)), while 22.31% were misclassified and 2.31% remained ambiguous (Table 3). The ITS2 dataset demonstrated the highest classification efficiency, with Partition 3 (ASAP score = 8.0) correctly identifying 30 (83.33%) of 36 species, misclassifying only 2 (5.56%), and categorizing 2 (5.56%) as ambiguous (Fig. 3(c)). At the specimen level, ITS2 correctly assigned 100 out of 113 specimens (88.5%), with only 4.42% misclassified and 11.11% ambiguous cases (Table 3).

Analysis of misclassified taxa (merged OTUs) revealed that Zygophyllum (Z. qatarense and Z. mandavillei) and Cyperus (C. rotundus L., C. conglomeratus, and C. arenarius Hance ex C.B.Clarke) consistently formed merged OTUs, resulting in incorrect classification from the rbcL to the matK datasets and further failing to distinguish between them (Fig. S1 & S2). Moreover, in addition to the consistent incorrect classification (merged OTUs) observed in the Zygophyllum genus, the Tamarix (T. nilotica and T. aphylla) genus also exhibited incorrect or ambiguous classification due to merged OTUs in rbcL and split OTUs (for 1–2 individuals) in the matK and ITS2 datasets. These two species remained unresolved across all three markers, likely due to their high genetic similarity (Fig. S1 & S2). Notably, ITS2 exhibited the lowest misclassification rate, with only six species being misclassified (2 incorrectly and 4 ambiguously), suggesting that ITS2 provides better species resolution for closely related taxa (Fig. S3).

Along with the Tamarix genus, ambiguous classifications (split OTUs) were observed for Sesuvium and Suaeda in rbcL, indicating potential intraspecific variation (Fig. S1). Arthrocnemum macrostachyum (Moric.) K.Koch also exhibited ambiguous classification in matK, being split into multiple OTUs, highlighting potential limitations in marker resolution (Fig. S2).

While rbcL and matK provided reasonable species identification accuracy, ITS2 demonstrated superior classification efficiency, with the highest percentage of correctly assigned species and the lowest misclassification rate. However, the presence of merged and split OTUs across all markers highlights the limitations of single-locus barcoding in resolving closely related species. The misclassification of Zygophyllum and Tamarix species underscores the need for a multi-locus barcoding approach combined with a robust classification method, such as machine learning-based OTU delineation, to enhance taxonomic resolution.

The Support Vector Machine (SMO algorithm) showed the highest classification accuracy across all three datasets (Fig. 4) and was selected as the base classifier for subsequent analyses.

Baseline classification performance

Baseline classification using SMO without any imbalance correction yielded marker-specific performance patterns. For rbcL, overall specimen-level accuracy was 86.4% (127/147 specimens correctly classified) with a kappa statistic of 0.860 (Table S5), indicating substantial agreement between predicted and observed species identities (Fig. S1; Table 3 and Table S5). At the species level, 78.4% (29/37) of taxa were correctly classified, while 18.9% (7/37) showed misclassification (Table S5). Seven species exhibited reduced true positive rates (<1) and precision scores, suggesting genetic overlap with closely related taxa. Notably, C. conglomeratus showed ambiguous classification, with specimens distributed across multiple classes.

The matK marker demonstrated higher discriminative capacity, achieving 97.7% specimen-level accuracy (127/130 specimens) and a kappa statistic of 0.976 (Table S5). Species-level classification reached 94.6% (35/37), with only two species misclassified (5.4%). However, C. conglomeratus and T. nilotica showed lower true positive rates of 0.667 and 0.333, respectively, indicating persistent resolution challenges in these genera.

The ITS2 marker achieved 97.0% specimen-level accuracy (128/132 specimens) with a kappa statistic of 0.968 (Fig. S3; Table S5). Species-level classification was 91.7% (33/36), with two species misclassified (5.6%). The genus Tamarix (T. aphylla and T. nilotica) showed complete misclassification (true positive rates <1), consistent with patterns observed in distance-based analyses. Z. mandavillei, represented by a single specimen with <98% similarity to GenBank references, remained unclassified.

Comparative evaluation of class imbalance handling methods

Given the variable number of specimens per species (ranging from 1 to 5 individuals; Table S1), four strategies were compared to assess whether class imbalance correction could improve classification performance: (1) baseline SMO without filtering, (2) CostSensitiveClassifier with uniform cost matrix, (3) SpreadSubsample undersampling, and (4) ClassBalancer instance weighting (Table S4). All methods employed identical SMO parameters and 10-fold cross-validation (Table S4).

The CostSensitiveClassifier with a uniform cost matrix produced classification results identical to those of the baseline SMO across all markers, indicating that uniform misclassification costs do not affect SVM optimization when class imbalance is moderate. The SpreadSubsample filter, which randomly undersamples majority classes to achieve uniform distribution, showed variable effects across markers. For rbcL, specimen-level accuracy improved marginally from 86.4% to 87.8% (129/147), while species-level accuracy remained unchanged at 78.4% (29/37) (Table S5). However, for matK, SpreadSubsample reduced both species-level accuracy (from 94.6% to 89.2%) and specimen-level accuracy (from 97.7% to 95.4%), with the number of misclassified species increasing from two to four (Table S5). ITS2 accuracy remained similar with or without undersampling (97.0% vs. 96.2% at the specimen level) (Table S5).

The ClassBalancer filter, which assigns instance weights inversely proportional to class frequencies without discarding specimens, demonstrated the most consistent performance. For rbcL, ClassBalancer improved specimen-level accuracy from 86.4% to 90.5% (133/147) and species-level accuracy from 78.4% to 81.1% (30/37) (Table 3 & Table S5). The kappa statistic increased from 0.860 to 0.902, and the precision from 0.855 to 0.900 (Fig. 5a). For matK and ITS2, ClassBalancer maintained the classification accuracy achieved by baseline SMO (97.7% and 97.0%, respectively), with identical species-level correct classifications (35/37 for matK, 33/36 for ITS2) (Table 3 & Table S5, Fig. 5b & c). Notably, ClassBalancer achieved this without removing any specimens, thereby preserving all taxa, including those with limited sample representation.

Species-level classification patterns with ClassBalancer

Confusion matrices generated for each marker using the ClassBalancer and SMO approach revealed that misclassifications were predominantly confined to congeneric species pairs, particularly within Tamarix, Cyperus, and Suaeda (Fig. 5d-f). For rbcL, correct classifications were observed along the diagonal, with misclassifications concentrated in taxonomically challenging groups (Fig. 5d). C. conglomeratus showed confusion with C. arenarius (three specimens), while Haloxylon salicornicum (Moq.) Bunge ex Boiss. had two specimens misclassified as Cornulaca monacantha Delile. Bidirectional misclassification occurred between T. aphylla and T. nilotica (two specimens each), and Suaeda aegyptiaca (Hasselq.) Zohary showed one misclassification as Suaeda vermiculata Forssk. ex J.F.Gmel (Fig. S1).

The matK confusion matrix showed minimal off-diagonal values, confirming its high resolution (Fig. 5e). Only three misclassifications were observed: C. conglomeratus (one specimen as C. arenarius), T. nilotica (two specimens as T. aphylla), and T. aphylla (two specimens correctly classified) (Fig. S2). All other species achieved perfect diagonal classification (Fig. 5e).

The ITS2 confusion matrix confirmed 128 correct classifications, with the Tamarix genus remaining the primary source of error both T. aphylla and T. nilotica showed bidirectional misclassification (two specimens each). Z. mandavillei (single specimen) remained unclassified (Fig. S3). All remaining 33 species showed correct diagonal classification (Fig. 5f).

Discussion

The present study, along with our previous studies,19, 20, 21, 22, 23, 24 highlights the progress in DNA barcoding, database contributions, and the taxonomic coverage of the UAE's flora. In the present study on DNA barcoding of 37 halophytes, five species are of conservation significance, as they are listed under various threat categories. These include L. carnosum (Endangered, EN), H. mucronatum, L. axillare, and M. nodiflorum (Vulnerable, VU) and A. marina (Near threatened, NT), according to the recent IUCN Red List assessment of UAE flora.47 The Red List assessment of the UAE's native flora indicates that of the total 598 species considered as native, 8 species are classified as Critically Endangered (CR), 18 species as EN, 12 species as VU, and 20 species as NT.47 DNA barcoding could aid in accurate identification and offer valuable insights for the future conservation and management of species within these threat categories, as well as for native plants in general.

Marker efficacy and limitations

In the present study, the DNA barcoding of 37 UAE halophytes using rbcL, matK, and ITS2 revealed marker-specific strengths and weaknesses. The chloroplast rbcL gene amplified and sequenced most reliably, consistent with previous UAE studies,19, 20 but yielded low sequence variability.23 Indeed, rbcL showed very narrow K2P distance ranges, as reported for other dryland plants.48 Consequently, rbcL often failed to distinguish closely related taxa. By contrast, matK was more variable but more problematic: PCR amplification success was lower (∼60%), and multiple primer sets were often required. These findings mirror other barcoding efforts in arid-region floras, where matK tends to outperform rbcL in resolution but suffers from amplification gaps and alignment ambiguities.23, 24

The nuclear ribosomal ITS2 region exhibited the highest interspecific divergence and clearest barcode gaps among the halophytes in our dataset. This aligns with previous findings that the ITS2 region provides distinct separation between intraspecific and interspecific distances in arid plant taxa.36, 48 ITS2 is thus the most discriminating single-locus marker for species-level identification; however, it is not without limitations. These include occasional sequencing difficulties and the need for careful alignment due to its structural complexity in the nuclear genome.49

No single marker achieved complete taxonomic resolution across all halophyte species. Consistent with recommendations by the Consortium for the Barcode of Life (CBOL) and other researchers, our findings support the use of a multilocus approach. Specifically, Saddhe et al.18 emphasized that the core plastid barcode combination (rbcL + matK) “does not provide suitable coverage” and must be supplemented with a hypervariable nuclear marker such as ITS2 to achieve reliable identification and resolution.

Genetic divergence and barcode gaps

Consistent with general barcoding theory, average K2P divergences were highest for ITS2, intermediate for matK, and lowest for rbcL (Table 2). Mean interspecific distances under ITS2 often exceeded those of the plastid loci by an order of magnitude, producing clearer separation of species-level clusters. This matches observations in other dryland floras, where “the genetic variability of the ITS2 locus was much higher than both matK and rbcL loci.”.48, 51, 52 In our dataset, most species exhibited an inter−/intraspecific gap in ITS2 (Fig. 2(c)), whereas rbcL showed extensive overlap (Fig. 2(a)).

Despite these trends, classic barcode gaps (non-overlapping intra- vs interspecific distances) were not ubiquitous. In many cases, the maximum intraspecific distance approached or exceeded the minimum interspecific distance, especially for rbcL. A similar lack of a clear gap has been reported in other plants15, 51 and our data suggest it as well: closely related species often lacked a distinct gap. For example, in all three of our datasets, the Zygophyllum and Tamarix complexes pairwise distances overlapped, preventing a simple threshold-based split. We therefore complemented distance analyses (e.g., K2P pairwise matrices) with operational taxonomic unit (OTU) clustering (ASAP) and machine learning to improve species delimitation.

Comparison with other barcoding studies

Our findings broadly align with barcoding studies of regional and global flora. In our previous study on Arabian vascular plants, the use of the Finland Barcode reference set yielded only ∼70% species resolution with the combined rbcL + matK markers,24 and our current observations echo these results. Although matK generally outperforms rbcL, it suffers from missing data issues–precisely as we observed in several halophyte genera. Jamdade et al.24 reported species resolution ranging from only 58–100% for rbcL and 35–69% for matK across Arabian vascular plants, illustrating the variability in marker utility. More pertinently, Song et al. (2023) developed a barcode library for 890 species of arid flora in NW China and found single-locus success rates of ∼80% (rbcL, matK) and ∼ 85% (ITS or ITS2), whereas the three-locus (rbcL + matK + ITS) combination yielded ∼75% species resolution. They ultimately recommended sequencing entire plastomes for recalcitrant groups such as Zygophyllum, which they included. Our results are comparable: ITS2 as a single locus gave the highest resolution, and adding plastid markers improved identification but did not reach 100%. Notably, Zygophyllum, in their study, needed plastome data to resolve species, a pattern that likely extends to our Zygophyllum samples.17 Comparisons with halophyte-specific studies are limited, but the general trend holds that multi-locus barcodes are often required. For instance, a DNA-barcoding survey of Tamarix (Mediterranean species) found that even two-locus combinations (matK + ITS, ITS + ycf1) only correctly identified ∼70% of specimens, with many closely related species indistinguishable by standard barcodes.52 Similarly, our Tamarix species showed very low sequence divergence across all three markers, echoing the need for supplementary data (microsatellites or whole plastomes) reported in the literature.

In summary, the marker performance and resolution rates in the present study largely agree with previous findings from Arabian and global plant barcoding studies: rbcL provides baseline identifications, whereas matK offers improved discriminatory power when successfully amplified and sequenced,19, 20, 24 and ITS2 yields the highest species-level resolution.21, 23, 48 Yet, as other studies have concluded, these markers alone cannot fully resolve complex genera.22, 52

Machine learning improves species delimitation

Supervised machine learning provided an alternative framework for species identification that does not rely on fixed genetic distance thresholds. Among the seven classifiers evaluated, the SVM algorithm showed the highest classification accuracy across all barcode datasets. Baseline SMO classification achieved specimen-level accuracies of 90.5%, 97.7%, and 97.0% for rbcL, matK, and ITS2, respectively (Table 3). Similar improvements in species identification accuracy using machine learning classifiers have also been reported in other DNA barcoding studies when compared with conventional distance-based approaches, especially in cases of low interspecific genetic divergence.23, 53, 54

Because the dataset contained unequal numbers of specimens per species, several imbalance-handling strategies were evaluated. Uniform cost-sensitive learning produced results identical to the baseline classifier, whereas undersampling using SpreadSubsample reduced classification accuracy for the matK dataset, likely due to the removal of informative instances. In contrast, instance weighting using the ClassBalancer filter produced consistent results and improved classification accuracy for the rbcL dataset, increasing specimen-level accuracy from 86.4% to 90.5% and species-level accuracy from 78.4% to 81.1%, while maintaining the performance observed for matK and ITS2. Instance weighting approaches are commonly recommended for moderately imbalanced biological datasets because they adjust class influence without discarding observations.55

Confusion matrices showed that most misclassifications occurred between closely related congeneric taxa, particularly within Tamarix, Cyperus, and Suaeda. These patterns reflect reduced discriminatory power of barcode loci in taxa with low interspecific sequence divergence.16, 50

Taxonomic challenges in Zygophyllum, Tamarix and Cyperus

In the present study, classification inconsistencies were primarily observed in Zygophyllum, Tamarix, and Cyperus, where taxa such as Z. mandavillei and Z. qatarense, T. aphylla and T. nilotica, and C. conglomeratus and C. arenarius showed misclassification, merged clustering, or failure of assignment across barcode loci and machine learning analyses. These patterns indicate that, despite high overall classification accuracy, certain taxa remain unresolved due to biological and evolutionary constraints.

In Zygophyllum, both species were poorly resolved by rbcL, matK, and ITS2, reflecting minimal barcode gaps and low interspecific divergence. Previous studies have similarly reported limited discriminatory power of standard barcode markers in this genus, with whole plastome data sometimes required for reliable species delimitation.17 Our results are consistent with this pattern, as both OTU-based and machine learning approaches occasionally merged distinct taxa or split single species.

Tamarix exhibited comparable limitations. Species such as T. aphylla and T. nilotica showed low interspecific divergence, with multiple samples sharing identical or nearly identical sequences. This pattern is consistent with earlier findings from the region and reflects limited cpDNA variation in Mediterranean–Arabian Tamarix.52 The reduced resolution is likely associated with recent radiation and potential hybridization, which obscure species boundaries and reduce the effectiveness of standard barcode loci.

In Cyperus, the absence of fixed sequence differences among species such as C. conglomeratus and C. arenarius further limited species discrimination across all markers. This is consistent with the known evolutionary complexity of the genus, including frequent hybridization, polyploidy, and rapid speciation, which constrain barcode divergence.56

Collectively, these findings highlight an inherent limitation of DNA barcoding: standard loci such as rbcL, matK, and ITS2 may lack sufficient resolution in closely related or recently diverged taxa. While overall classification accuracy remains high, unresolved cases are biologically meaningful and reflect underlying evolutionary processes rather than methodological limitations. In such systems, improved resolution may require additional loci, plastome-scale data, or nuclear markers.

Conservation and applied implications

Building a DNA barcode library for UAE halophytes has immediate ecological and conservation benefits. Halophytic plants stabilize coastal soils, support unique wildlife, and are an underutilized resource for saline agriculture. Five of our sampled species are officially listed as threatened in the UAE National Red List (e.g., Including species associated with salt flat and mangrove habitats). For these taxa, molecular identification is especially valuable. Barcodes enable accurate recognition even from fragmentary or juvenile material, avoiding misidentification of rare plants.14 This supports enforcement against illegal collection and informs restoration: for example, when replanting a threatened halophyte, barcodes can verify that nursery stock is truly the native species. More broadly, DNA barcoding can flag cryptic diversity or undescribed taxa among halophytes, informing conservation priorities. As Safhi et al.56 note in the context of Saudi Arabian flora, accurate species delimitation is ‘the first crucial step in conserving and managing threatened species.’ By contributing to the UAE reference library, our data enable rapid species verification from environmental samples (e.g., seed mixtures or the gut contents of herbivores) and complement traditional morphological surveys. In practice, barcodes have been used to track illegal trade of endangered plants, monitor biodiversity of protected areas, and tag germplasm in seed banks.57 For example, if a medicinal or forage halophyte is collected from the wild, barcoding can confirm its identity and detect adulteration with common look-alikes.

In our multilocus barcoding study of Salicornia persica populations in the UAE, we showed that combining supervised machine-learning classifiers with multiple barcode loci reliably discriminates cryptic morphotypes at both species and population levels.22 Applied to agro-ecology, halophytes such as Salicornia and Arthrocaulon (syn. Arthrocnemum) are being promoted as “climate-resilient crops” under national saline-irrigation initiatives. DNA barcoding, and especially metabarcoding of seed mixtures, ensures that only the intended, salt-tolerant taxa are used in field trials, preventing the accidental introduction of invasive or less resilient species. In sum, a well-developed DNA barcode framework can support both conservation of wild halophyte diversity and responsible development of halophyte agriculture in the region. As one review emphasized, DNA barcoding can support the identification of illegally traded endangered plants and assist “conservation planning” by clarifying taxonomy 64. Our study contributes to these goals by expanding the genetic toolkit for UAE halophytes.

Multilocus barcoding strategies and future directions

The accumulated evidence from our study and previous research shows that single-locus barcoding is often insufficient for complete resolution at the species level. Multi-marker approaches, possibly combined with machine learning, are the way forward. In practice, we recommend using at least three barcode loci. For instance, the combination rbcL + matK + ITS2 (RMI) would integrate the universality of rbcL, the variability of matK, and the high resolution of ITS2. Song et al. (2023)17 showed that such a 3-locus set achieved much higher genus- and species-level resolution (over 90% at genus level) than any single marker, even though some species still overlapped. Critically, they found that adding whole chloroplast genomes (“super-barcodes”) dramatically improved outcomes for problem genera.17 In the context of the Arabian Peninsula, Jamdade et al.23, 24 similarly argue that “the combination of more than one method could assist in the resolution of morphologically similar or closely related taxa”, and that rbcL + matK alone “do not grant a suitable coverage of plant species”. Our results support this finding by demonstrating that integrating multiple barcode loci and analytical methods (distance, OTU, and ML) provides much higher overall resolution than any single approach.

Practically, we suggest the following approach for halophyte barcoding: (1) Use multi-locus barcodes. Combine plastid and nuclear markers to offset each other's weaknesses (e.g., ITS2 plus one or two plastid genes). (2) Implement supervised classifiers. As demonstrated in this study and in other Arabian studies,23, 24 SVM/RF on multi-locus data can substantially raise identification accuracy. (3) Build reference libraries. Deposit high- quality barcode sequences for all known UAE halophytes into public databases (GenBank and BOLDSYSTEMS). While 82% of our sequences (112/137) are from UAE field collections, we acknowledge that the inclusion of 18% GenBank-derived sequences and limited sampling represent limitations. Even our data indicate that sparse sampling (singletons) can skew delimitations,24 so future work should include multiple individuals per species. (4) Explore genomics for difficult clades. For genera like Zygophyllum or Tamarix, targeted approaches such as chloroplast genome sequencing or SNP-based genotyping may be necessary.

In conclusion, this study demonstrates that a multilocus DNA barcoding approach combined with supervised machine learning shows promise for delineating halophyte species in the UAE flora. The combination of rbcL, matK, and ITS2 markers, together with instance weighting using (ClassBalancer) and SVM classification, improved species identification compared with distance-based methods alone. However, the reported high classification accuracy should not be interpreted as complete resolution across all taxa. Persistent misclassification in taxonomically complex genera such as Tamarix, Cyperus, and Zygophyllum reflects biological constraints, including low interspecific divergence, recent divergence, hybridization, incomplete lineage sorting, and polyploidy. These cases highlight the inherent limitations of standard barcode loci and indicate that additional markers or genomic resources may be required for complete resolution of difficult clades. Despite these constraints, the reference dataset generated here contributes to the regional DNA barcode library and provides a foundation for future biodiversity monitoring, conservation, and sustainable utilization of the UAE's salt-adapted flora. This work represents a step toward integrating machine learning with DNA barcoding for plant identification in arid ecosystems, although broader application will benefit from expanded sampling and independent validation across the Arabian Peninsula.
