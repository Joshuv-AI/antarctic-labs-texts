Comparison of closed and open kinetic chain exercises following total knee arthroplasty in elderly women: a randomized controlled trial
Authors: Jae Ang Sim, Kyeong-Sik Kong, Moon Hee Kim, Kyung Jin Kim, Byung Hoon Lee
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractThis study investigated the comparative effects of a 12-week closed kinetic chain (CKC) versus open kinetic chain (OKC) exercise program on muscle strength, balance, range of motion (ROM), gait, knee function, and pain in elderly women after total knee arthroplasty (TKA). Thirty-six elderly women post-TKA were randomized into three groups: CKC exercise (n = 12), OKC exercise (n = 12), and control (n = 12). The intervention groups followed a structured 12-week exercise regimen. Outcome measures included knee flexion and extension strength, single-leg standing time, Timed Up and Go (TUG) test, six-minute walk test (6MWT), ROM, Western Ontario and McMaster Universities Osteoarthritis Index (WOMAC), and Visual Analog Scale (VAS) scores for pain. All groups exhibited significant improvements in knee extension strength over time. The CKC group showed a 122% increase (mean difference = 10.8 Nm; 95% CI, 7.8 to 13.8; p < 0.001), outperforming the OKC (102%) and control groups (97%). ROM and static and dynamic balance improved similarly across groups without significant differences. Gait function, measured by the 6MWT, increased significantly in all groups, with the CKC group demonstrating the greatest gains. No significant differences were observed among groups for pain (VAS) and function (WOMAC) scores. Both CKC and OKC exercise programs effectively enhance lower limb strength, ROM, and balance following TKA in elderly women. However, CKC exercises confer superior benefits in knee extension strength and gait performance, suggesting their preferential use in post-TKA rehabilitation protocols.Keywords: Total knee arthroplasty, Closed kinetic chain exercise, Osteoarthritis, Open kinetic chain exercise, Rehabilitation, Randomized controlled trialSubject terms: Diseases, Health care, Medical research, Rheumatology

Introduction

Total knee arthroplasty (TKA) has been proven to be highly effective in providing pain relief and improving physical function, thereby enhancing the quality of life of patients 1,2. This surgical procedure can effectively alleviate pain, bone deformity, and joint contracture due to severe arthritis. Despite these benefits, patients frequently experience postoperative muscle atrophy and neuromuscular deficits, which can negatively impact the ability to perform functional movements, such as climbing stairs and walking 3. Bade et al. reported that older adults who underwent TKA experienced an 18% decrease in walking speed, a 51% decrease in stair climbing speed, and a 40% reduction in quadriceps femoris strength within a year after surgery compared to older adults who did not undergo the procedure 4. These findings highlight the importance of targeted postoperative rehabilitation to restore functional capacity. Therefore, it is recommended that patients begin exercising as early as possible after TKA to achieve optimal surgical outcomes, including pain relief and improved lower limb function 5.

The post-surgical rehabilitation program for TKA can vary based on the environment, but it typically focuses on pain and edema management, restoring the range of motion (ROM) of the knee joint, strengthening the lower limbs, achieving a normal walking pattern, and training for functional activities6,7.

Rehabilitation exercises following joint replacement surgery are currently primarily based on open kinetic chain exercises 4. However, these exercises are not considered function-oriented due to their limited similarity to everyday movements, and they may increase shear forces acting on the artificial joint 8,9. Closed kinetic chain (CKC) exercises, by contrast, involve weight-bearing movements that more closely resemble daily activities and may enhance balance, proprioceptive input, and joint stability. These exercises can increase joint compression, effectively stimulate proprioceptive systems 7, and improve muscle strength, power, stability, and balance, thereby supporting functional recovery. Despite these theoretical advantages, evidence supporting early CKC-based rehabilitation following TKA remains limited. In particular, there is a lack of comparative data directly evaluating the effects of CKC versus OKC exercise programs in elderly women, a population with a high prevalence of TKA and distinct functional and rehabilitation needs. Moreover, few studies have compared these structured exercise programs with real-world usual care during the early postoperative period. Consequently, changes in rehabilitation strategies have not yet been supported by sufficient empirical evidence 8. This study aimed to examine the effects of 12-week closed kinetic chain exercise programs and open kinetic chain exercise programs on muscle strength, balance, ROM, and functional gait ability in patients who underwent TKA. We hypothesized that an early CKC exercise program would lead to greater improvements in muscle strength, balance, and gait performance compared with OKC exercise or standard postoperative care in elderly women following TKA.

Results

For outcomes showing baseline heterogeneity, ANCOVA adjusting for baseline values was applied, whereas repeated-measures ANOVA was used when baseline homogeneity was satisfied. Statistical significance and descriptive (clinical) changes are reported separately to avoid interpretive ambiguity. Comparison of measurement items among groups

Knee extension strength

Rehabilitation exercise following TKA resulted in statistically significant changes in knee extension strength over time (p < 0.001) and between groups (p = 0.013). Post-hoc analyses demonstrated significant within-group improvements from baseline to 3 months in all groups (all p < 0.001). Specifically, CKCG increased from 8.80 ± 3.94 Nm to 19.60 ± 4.50 Nm, OKCG from 7.87 ± 3.62 Nm to 15.90 ± 3.10 Nm, and CG from 6.75 ± 2.30 Nm to 13.13 ± 3.55 Nm (Fig. 1A).

At 3 months, the mean change from baseline was + 10.8 Nm in CKCG (95% CI: 7.8–13.8; Cohen’s d = 2.48), + 8.03 Nm in OKCG (95% CI: 5.2–10.7; d = 2.16), and + 6.38 Nm in CG (95% CI: 3.6–9.1; d = 1.92). Between-group comparisons revealed significantly greater gains in CKCG compared with OKCG at 2 months (17.1 ± 4.4 vs. 14.3 ± 3.4 Nm, p = 0.029) and 3 months (19.5 ± 4.5 vs. 16.5 ± 3.8 Nm, p = 0.032). Both exercise groups showed significantly greater improvements than CG at 3 months (CKCG vs. CG, p = 0.018; OKCG vs. CG, p = 0.021).

Knee flexion strength

Baseline knee flexion strength was included as a covariate and significantly influenced outcomes (F = 25.196, p < 0.001). However, no statistically significant group-by-time interaction was observed (p = 0.463), nor were there significant main effects of group (p = 0.213) or time (p = 0.096).

Although mean values increased descriptively at 3 months—by 36% in CKCG (11.6 ± 3.9 to 15.8 ± 3.8 Nm), 46% in OKCG (7.4 ± 2.1 to 10.9 ± 1.5 Nm), and 57% in CG (6.5 ± 2.3 to 10.2 ± 3.7 Nm)—these changes did not reach statistical significance and should therefore be interpreted as descriptive trends rather than confirmed intervention effects (Fig. 1B).

Balance performance

No statistically significant group-by-time interaction was detected for static or dynamic balance outcomes (p = 0.348). Static balance (single-leg stance time) increased descriptively across all groups, from 17.67 ± 17.07 to 53.45 ± 44.33 s in CKCG, 8.44 ± 7.66 to 26.61 ± 28.59 s in OKCG, and 4.51 ± 4.33 to 25.21 ± 35.97 s in CG.

Similarly, TUG performance showed no significant main effects of group (p = 0.427) or time (p = 0.185). Nevertheless, mean TUG times decreased across all groups over the intervention period (CKCG: − 35%; OKCG: − 52%; CG: − 40%). Given the absence of statistical significance, these findings are presented as descriptive improvements and do not support definitive between-group conclusions.

Knee flexion range of motion (ROM)

Flexion ROM significantly increased over time in all groups (p < 0.001), with no significant group-by-time interaction (p = 0.299). At 3 months, ROM improved by 31% in CKCG (79.1 ± 12.3 to 103.7 ± 17.7°), 56% in OKCG (58.1 ± 16.6 to 90.7 ± 14.2°), and 45% in CG (65.3 ± 30.7 to 95.2 ± 20.5°). Because between-group differences were not statistically significant, these improvements are interpreted as general postoperative recovery rather than differential intervention effects.

Six-minute walk test (6MWT)

Walking capacity increased significantly over time in all groups (all p < 0.001). At 3 months, CKCG increased from 265.1 ± 121.3 m to 508.0 ± 67.6 m (91%), OKCG from 213.1 ± 119.2 m to 425.3 ± 91.7 m (99%), and CG from 242.2 ± 127.6 m to 391.1 ± 107.0 m (61%) (Table 1).

Discussion

The primary objective of this study was to investigate the effects of a 12-week closed kinetic chain exercise program compared with an open chain program on muscle strength, balance, range of motion, gait, activities of daily living, and pain in older patients after knee arthroplasty. In the following discussion, these findings are interpreted in the context of their clinical significance and implications.

Comparison to previous literature and novelty

While the benefits of rehabilitation following total knee arthroplasty (TKA) are well established, the comparative value of closed kinetic chain (CKC) and open kinetic chain (OKC) exercise programs relative to standard outpatient care remains insufficiently characterized. Most prior investigations have assessed combined chain protocols or guideline-based rehabilitation, rather than isolating CKC and OKC as distinct interventions. In contrast, the present trial implemented a strict separation of CKC and OKC programs, initiated uniformly at two weeks post-TKA, and compared them directly with an unstructured standard-care group. Furthermore, the program design—including session frequency, progression criteria based on ratings of perceived exertion, and individualized adaptations—was tailored to elderly East Asian women, a population underrepresented in predominantly Western studies. By providing head-to-head data on two fundamental rehabilitation strategies and situating these against real-world practice, our findings address a critical evidence gap, particularly relevant to health systems where protocolized supervised rehabilitation is not consistently available.

Muscle strength

After knee arthroplasty, patients with functional limitations in daily life typically exhibit knee extension strength levels that are 30% lower than those of the healthy side, while patients without functional limitations showed an average of 12% lower strength levels 18. The more severe the decrease in lower limb muscle strength after TKA, the higher the average pain level 19. To address these issues, the goal of the treatment program for patients who undergo TKA is to restore and improve lower limb strength, and functional exercises are important 20,21. Early initiation of strength training after TKA has been shown to reduce knee pain, restore ROM, restore lower limb strength, and prevent knee stiffness 22. Wei-Hsiu et al. 23 reported that a group that received progressive resistance exercises for 12 weeks displayed significant improvements in knee extensor and flexor strength compared to the control group among patients undergoing knee arthroplasty.

This study identified statistically significant differences in knee extension strength both between groups and over time. Post-hoc analyses revealed significant differences between the CKCG and CG at all post-intervention time points (1, 2, and 3 months), whereas the OKCG and CG showed a significant difference only at 3 months post-intervention. In contrast, no statistically significant differences were observed between the CKCG and OKCG at any time point. Consistent with the findings of Wei-Hsiu et al.23, progressive strength training resulted in significant improvements in knee extension strength, with the CKCG demonstrating the greatest gains. These results suggest that closed kinetic chain exercises may play a key role in enhancing knee extension strength following total knee arthroplasty. Importantly, the robustness of these findings was supported by large effect sizes. The partial eta squared values for the main effects of time and group indicated that a substantial proportion of the variance in knee extension strength was attributable to the intervention. Furthermore, the large Cohen’s d values observed in the CKCG indicate not only statistical significance but also clinically meaningful improvements. Collectively, these findings reinforce the relevance of closed kinetic chain exercises as a functionally effective rehabilitation strategy following total knee arthroplasty.

In contrast to knee extension strength, no significant differences were found in knee flexion strength. However, at both baseline and after three months, all three groups showed improvements in strength, with CKCG increasing by 36%, OKCG by 46%, and CG by 57%. Although CKCG showed the lowest improvement, its records at three months far surpassed those of the other groups. The lack of significant difference may be attributed to insufficient exercises targeting knee flexion strength in the CKCG exercise program. Therefore, additional exercises to improve knee flexion strength should be considered in the future.

Balance

Patients who underwent TKA scored lower on tests of balance and mobility than those who did not undergo surgery 2,24. The ability of the knee joint to provide balance is diminished after TKA, thereby increasing the risk of falls 12. Postoperative pain and functional movement impairments, such as balance deficits, can lead to a loss of proprioceptive ability 25. Therefore, exercises for postoperative functional recovery are necessary. Balance is generally divided into static and dynamic balance. Static balance refers to maintaining balance while staying still, whereas dynamic balance involves maintaining stability while the body is in motion. Patients who undergone TKA may experience long-term functional impairment, chronic pain, and instability, leading to a progressive deficiency in proprioception. Rätsepsoo et al. 26 demonstrated that strengthening the extensor muscles of the knee joint increases stability and reduces the fear of falling.

This study evaluated the ability to maintain static balance by performing the single-leg stance test with eyes open. However, statistically significant differences were not observed. However, an analysis of the change in the mean over time revealed that all three groups showed a high level of improvement. Among them, CKCG recorded the highest mean value at three months. This is speculated to be due to the effectiveness of closed kinetic chain exercises on both concentric and eccentric contractions and the significant role of deceleration and control in body movement. In this study, the Timed Up and Go test was performed to evaluate the dynamic balance, but there was no statistically significant difference. However, all three groups demonstrated improvement in the mean values over time. CKCG showed a decrease of 35% from baseline at 3 months, OKCG decreased by 52%, and CG decreased by 40%. CKCG showed the lowest improvement, but the measured results at three months showed an average of less than 6 s. Achieving 6 s or less in the TUG measurement is considered a high level of performance. Therefore, although significant results were not observed in CKCG, it is inferred that there was improvement to a level of normal function. From a clinical perspective, achieving functional thresholds such as a Timed Up and Go performance of 6 s or less may represent a meaningful recovery milestone in elderly TKA patients, even in the absence of statistically significant between-group differences. In future research, if homogeneity is ensured in pre-tests and the effectiveness is verified through comparisons between pre-intervention and post-intervention, it is expected that favorable results will be obtained.

Range of motion

The limitation of knee joint flexion is a major factor contributing to mobility impairment in patients with degenerative knee osteoarthritis 27. After TKA, severe pain and discomfort make voluntary exercise participation challenging, leading to gradual muscle weakness in the legs and stiffness in the joints. Within one month after surgery, quadriceps femoris strength can decrease by 60%, functional performance by 88%, and 52% of patients report issues with functional activity limitation 5. Therefore, it is recommended to initiate exercise as early as possible after TKA to achieve optimal surgical outcomes, alleviate pain, increase joint mobility, and enhance the functional performance of the knee joint 5. It has been reported that restoring joint mobility beyond a certain level after surgery is also an important determinant of surgical outcomes 28.

In this study, there was no statistically significant interaction effect of groups and periods on the ROM of knee flexion, but there was a significant difference over time. All three groups showed improvement over time, but CKC exhibited the lowest improvement. These results suggest that the intervention program applied in this study focused on improving function rather than improving ROM. In the CKCG intervention program, there were no movements where the knee flexion angle exceeded 90º, and it is speculated that higher results were not observed due to the tension of the thigh muscles caused by continuous strength exercises. Furthermore, the ROM measurement in the prone position conducted in this study requires relaxation of the quadriceps femoris and contraction of the hamstrings. Therefore, in future studies, passive and active stretching interventions and changes in measurement methods should be considered to improve the ROM of knee flexion.

Gait

Patients with knee arthritis have abnormal walking patterns due to pain, joint instability, and muscle weakness 29,30. Fantozzi et al. 31 reported a decrease in knee flexion angle and step length during walking after knee arthroplasty, along with simultaneous contraction of the rectus femoris muscle, hamstring, gastrocnemius, and tibialis anterior muscles. The issues with walking after surgery can be expected to improve with the recovery of lower limb strength in the early stages following surgery 3.

In this study, the 6MWT showed a significant difference over time, and when comparing the baseline and three months post-intervention for all three groups, CKCG showed a 91% increase, OKCG showed a 99% increase, and CG showed a 61% increase. These results are consistent with the report of Kwon et al. 7, indicating that the intervention program not only led to increased simple muscle strength but also improved joint stability and balance through movements such as single-leg stance and stair climbing, resulting in enhanced proprioception. It should be noted that although the OKCG primarily emphasized open kinetic chain exercises, limited functional tasks such as step-up and single-leg stance were intentionally incorporated to ensure safety, functional continuity, and real-world applicability during later rehabilitation stages. While this pragmatic design may have partially attenuated the contrast between CKC and OKC interventions, it reflects typical clinical rehabilitation practice rather than strictly isolated biomechanical conditions. It is suggested that further research should be conducted to identify exercise movements that can increase walking ability, leading to improvements in walking.

Knee function evaluation

In this study, the analysis of WOMAC scores revealed no statistically significant differences. While numerical reductions were observed in each group, the interpretation here emphasizes that CKCG showed relatively greater improvement in patient-reported function. The reason CKCG showed the greatest improvement is attributed to exercises based on daily activities such as sitting and standing up from a chair, climbing stairs, and walking sideways, which enhance joint proprioception in the knee, hip, and ankle joints 32,33 and led to improvements in daily life activities. The lack of significant difference in the WOMAC scores in the study may be attributed to subjective factors involved in completing the questionnaire, assistance from caregivers in completing the survey, and daily variations in condition. Despite clinically meaningful decreases in WOMAC and VAS scores across all groups, the absence of statistically significant group or time effects may reflect limited sample size and associated risk of Type II error rather than a true lack of intervention efficacy for these secondary measures. In the context of elderly patients following total knee arthroplasty, even modest improvements in patient-reported outcomes may translate into meaningful gains in independence, confidence during ambulation, and performance of activities of daily living, suggesting potential clinical relevance beyond conventional thresholds of statistical significance. Therefore, considering the results of this and previous studies, it is expected that additional research will be required in the future.

Limitations of this study include the gender-specific nature of the sample (all elderly women of East Asian origin), which may limit the generalizability of the findings to broader TKA populations; a modest sample size that, while adequately powered for the primary outcome, may have been insufficient for detecting differences in secondary outcomes (potential Type II error); In addition, two participants withdrew after randomization, and because post-baseline outcome data were unavailable for these individuals, a per-protocol analysis was conducted rather than an intention-to-treat approach. Given the small number of dropouts and their balanced distribution across groups, the potential impact on the overall findings is considered limited; however, some degree of attrition-related bias cannot be entirely excluded. The short follow-up period (3 months), which does not capture long-term sustainability of functional gains; and the fact that although outcome assessors were blinded, complete elimination of bias is not possible. In addition, the fixed nature of the exercise protocol meant that adjustments for individual pain levels or functional status were limited. the fixed nature of the exercise protocol meant that adjustments for individual pain levels or functional status were limited. Additionally, some overlap in functional task components between the CKC and OKC programs may have reduced the magnitude of between-group differences, although this overlap was deliberately included to maintain clinical feasibility and patient safety. An additional limitation relates to the control group, whose physical activity levels were not objectively monitored using wearable devices or structured activity logs. Instead, activity levels were assessed through self-report during follow-up visits. While this approach may introduce recall bias or variability in activity reporting, it was intentionally selected to reflect real-world post-discharge conditions, where structured monitoring is rarely implemented. Importantly, participants in the control group reported no engagement in organized physical therapy or protocolized exercise programs beyond standard care, supporting the validity of the control condition as representative of usual post-TKA practice.

Pain

No significant reduction in VAS score was observed in this study. However, when comparing the pre and post-intervention of the three groups, CKCG showed a decrease of 64%, OKCG decreased by 67%, and CG decreased by 42%. Compared to CKCG and OKCG, CG exhibited a lower reduction in pain intensity. This suggests that increased ROM, muscle strength, and functional movement in the exercise groups may have accelerated the reduction in pain intensity. The lack of significant difference may be because the exercise program could not be tailored to the pain level of each individual, and the muscle soreness experienced during exercise may have influenced the results. This pattern, in conjunction with wide confidence intervals, raises the possibility of inadequate sample power rather than an absence of real intervention effects for pain and function. Future studies with larger cohorts or stratification for baseline pain and function are warranted to confirm these observations.

Sample size considerations

The sample size of this trial, while relatively modest, is consistent with early-phase explanatory studies in surgical rehabilitation, and the study was appropriately powered for the primary outcome (knee extension strength improvement). The effect sizes observed in the primary endpoint suggest that even moderate sample sizes allow detection of clinically relevant improvements, justifying the feasibility and informative value of the current investigation.

Generalizability and clinical Utility

The pragmatic inclusion of a non-exercise-controlled group model real clinical pathways and enables estimation of the magnitude of effect attributable to structured kinetic exercise versus routine care. This real-world approach directly informs both patients and providers deliberating exercise prescriptions post-TKA, especially where rehabilitation resources may be variable or limited.

Importantly, the absence of statistically significant between-group differences for some secondary outcomes does not preclude their potential clinical relevance. In postoperative TKA populations, even modest improvements in pain, balance, and patient-reported function may translate into meaningful gains in mobility, confidence, and independence, particularly in older adults. From a rehabilitation perspective, changes that fall below conventional thresholds for statistical significance may still exceed minimal clinically important differences (MCIDs) or contribute cumulatively to functional recovery in real-world settings. Therefore, the observed descriptive improvements across exercise groups should be interpreted as complementary to, rather than contradictory of, the statistically confirmed effects on primary outcomes.

Conclusions

Closed and open kinetic chain exercises for 12 weeks are effective in improving lower limb strength, ROM, and static and dynamic balance abilities in older women who underwent TKA. Notably, closed kinetic chain exercises produced superior improvements in knee extension strength and gait performance compared with open kinetic chain exercise and standard post-discharge care.

This randomized controlled trial demonstrates that rigorously applied closed and open kinetic chain exercise protocols confer significant improvements in lower limb strength, range of motion, and gait ability in older women following TKA, compared to standard post-discharge care. These findings support the preferential inclusion of closed kinetic chain–based rehabilitation protocols in post-TKA programs for elderly women, particularly in settings where functional, task-oriented, and resource-efficient interventions are required. This evidence fills a critical gap in understanding the incremental benefit of distinct kinetic chain interventions, providing actionable data for tailoring post-TKA rehabilitation strategies.

Methods

This study was conducted as a post-market clinical investigation of a medical device approved by the Korean Ministry of Food and Drug Safety (MFDS). The study protocol received approval from the hospital’s Institutional Review Board (IRB), and therefore, no separate registration was made on external clinical trial registries. This approach aligns with regulatory practices for post-market studies conducted under IRB oversight without external trial registration. The first related date of this post-market study is 20/07/2023. Ethical oversight and study transparency were ensured through prior IRB approval and documented informed consent procedures, in accordance with regulatory requirements for post-market clinical investigations.

Study design and rationale

This study was a prospective, single-blind, randomized controlled trial (with blinded outcome assessors) designed to specifically compare the efficacy of distinct closed kinetic chain (CKCG) and open kinetic chain (OKCG) exercise programs against a standard-care (unregulated physical activity) group following total knee arthroplasty (TKA) in older women.

The inclusion of a control group receiving standard post-discharge care (no protocolized exercise intervention) reflects real-world clinical practices in certain settings, particularly in resource-limited or outpatient community contexts where not all patients consistently access structured rehabilitation. This approach enables assessment of the incremental gains achievable with well-defined kinetic chain interventions, which remains a clinically relevant and under-investigated question.

Unlike previous studies, which often compare types of exercises but do not benchmark them to non-protocolized usual care, this trial was specifically powered and designed to quantify real-world gains for each exercise modality relative to standard post-discharge experience.

This study was conducted from [07/2023] to [06/2024] at G Hospital, Incheon, Republic of Korea. The study enrolled female patients aged 65 to 80 years who underwent TKA at G Hospital in Incheon Metropolitan City. Patients with cognitive impairments bilateral TKA during the same admission, significant cardiopulmonary comorbidities, those unable to engage in continuous exercise for three months, or with a history of lower limb muscle weakness or pathology related to central or peripheral neurological disorders were excluded.

All outcome measurements were performed by trained physical therapists who were blinded to group allocation to minimize assessment bias. Due to the nature of the exercise interventions, blinding of participants and treating therapists was not feasible. To reduce potential performance and expectation bias, outcome assessors were strictly blinded, standardized verbal instructions were used during assessments, and no feedback regarding performance or group superiority was provided to participants during intervention sessions.

The primary outcome was knee extension strength. Secondary outcomes included knee flexion strength, static and dynamic balance, knee flexion ROM, gait performance (6MWT), WOMAC score, and VAS pain score.

Sample size determination

Sample size was estimated using the G*Power program (version 3.1), employing an effect size of 0.30, significance level (α) of 0.05, and a power of 0.80, appropriate for a three-armed ANOVA design. The selected effect size reflects a small-to-moderate magnitude and was chosen based on previously published randomized controlled trials evaluating postoperative rehabilitation interventions after total knee arthroplasty, which commonly report effect sizes in this range for muscle strength, functional performance, and gait-related outcomes.

Given the heterogeneity of recovery trajectories in older TKA populations and the absence of robust pilot data for the specific kinetic-chain interventions examined, this effect size was intentionally selected as a conservative assumption to avoid overestimation of treatment effects. This yielded a minimum required sample of 30 subjects, with 36 enrolled to allow for attrition. Although the sample size per group is modest, it matches the power requirements for detecting moderate clinical effects in early phase or pilot rehabilitation trials and is consistent with published precedents in geriatric orthopedic research. Randomization and allocation concealment procedures are as previously described.

The 36 study subjects were randomly assigned to three groups: the experimental group (CKCG, n = 12); the comparison group (OKCG, n = 12); and the control group (CG, n = 12). During the initial interviews, the randomization was performed by an independent administrator who did not participate in the study, using sealed envelopes. The random allocation sequence was generated in advance using a computer-based random number generator with a 1:1:1 allocation ratio. The allocation sequence was prepared by an independent administrator who was not involved in participant recruitment, intervention delivery, or outcome assessment. Group assignments were concealed using sequentially numbered, sealed opaque envelopes, which were opened only after completion of baseline assessments. During the study, one participant from the experimental group and one from the control group dropped out, resulting in the exclusion of two subjects. Therefore, the data of 34 participants were analyzed (Fig. 3). The general physical characteristics of the participants are presented in Table 2. Detailed explanations of the study purpose, methods, and procedures were provided to the participants. Written, informed consent was obtained from all participants. The study procedures were approved by the Institutional Review Board (IRB) of G University Hospital (IRB No: GDIRB2022-095). All methods were carried out in accordance with relevant guidelines and regulations, including the ethical standards of the institutional and/or national research committee and the 1964 Helsinki Declaration and its later amendments or comparable ethical standards. All surgeries were performed by the senior authors using a medial parapatellar approach and a posterior-stabilized, high-flexion total knee prosthesis (LOSPA Knee System, Corentec Co., Ltd., Seoul, South Korea; Anthem, Smith & Nephew Inc., Memphis, TN, USA; Vanguard Complete Knee System, Zimmer Biomet, Warsaw, IN, USA).

Interventions

Importantly, while elements of the closed kinetic chain (CKC) and open kinetic chain (OKC) exercise protocols were based on established rehabilitative approaches, the sequencing, timing (initiation at 2 weeks postoperatively), exercise progression patterns, and specific adaptations to the local patient population constitute novel aspects not directly replicated in previous literature. All exercise interventions were initiated two weeks after surgery and were conducted for 12 weeks.

Closed kinetic chain exercise group (CKCG)

Participants assigned to the CKCG performed a structured closed kinetic chain exercise program designed to promote functional lower-limb strength, joint stability, and neuromuscular control. Exercises emphasized weight-bearing activities involving simultaneous motion of the hip, knee, and ankle joints, such as sit-to-stand movements, mini-squats, step-ups, and controlled single-leg stance tasks.

Exercise intensity and progression were individualized based on the participants’ ratings of perceived exertion (RPE). RPE was assessed at the end of each exercise set using the Borg 6–20 scale, following standardized verbal instructions provided by the supervising physical therapists. Progression criteria were predefined to target a moderate-to-somewhat-hard intensity range (RPE 12–15). When participants consistently reported RPE values below the target range, exercise difficulty was increased by modifying task complexity, resistance, or duration, whereas progression was deferred if RPE exceeded the target range or if pain or instability was reported. This standardized approach was applied uniformly across participants and therapists to ensure intervention fidelity and safety.

Open kinetic chain exercise group (OKCG)

Participants in the OKCG completed a structured open kinetic chain exercise program primarily targeting isolated knee joint movements and muscle strengthening. Exercises focused on non–weight-bearing knee extension and flexion activities performed in seated or supine positions.

As with the CKCG, exercise intensity and progression were adjusted according to perceived exertion and participant tolerance, following predefined progression criteria. RPE was assessed using the same Borg 6–20 scale and target range (12–15), and progression decisions were guided by standardized instructions to ensure consistency between therapists and across sessions.

Control group (standard post-discharge care)

The control group was designed to reflect real-world post–total knee arthroplasty (TKA) care following hospital discharge. Participants assigned to the control group were instructed to follow standard post-discharge advice provided by the hospital, which included general guidance on gradual return to daily activities and self-directed mobility as tolerated. They were specifically instructed not to participate in any structured or supervised rehabilitation or protocolized exercise program during the study period.

Physical activity levels in the control group were not formally monitored using wearable devices or supervised exercise logs. Instead, participants were asked during follow-up visits to self-report any additional exercise or rehabilitation activities performed outside of routine daily activities. No participant in the control group reported engagement in organized physical therapy or structured exercise programs beyond standard post-discharge care.

Intervention adherence and compliance

Attendance at each supervised exercise session in the CKCG and OKCG was recorded throughout the intervention period. Adherence was defined as completion of at least 80% of the prescribed exercise sessions. Reasons for participant withdrawal were documented and included personal reasons and loss to follow-up. Only participants who completed post-intervention outcome assessments were included in the final analysis.

Exercise intervention programs

The exercise intervention programs were conducted for one hour twice per week for 12 weeks. The experimental group (CKCG) received a program based on closed kinetic chain exercises, and the comparison group (OKCG) received a program based on open kinetic chain exercises. The programs for both groups consisted of 10–15 min of warm-up, 35 min of main exercises, and 10–15 min of cool-down exercises during the 1-h sessions. The control group was provided with the institution’s standard post-discharge instructions, including advice for unsupervised home walking and progressive return to activities, but did not receive a structured, supervised exercise program. The warm-up exercises included stretching exercises targeting the hip, knee, and ankle joints. The main exercises were conducted according to each exercise program, gradually increasing the load based on the Rating of Perceived Exertion (RPE). The cool-down exercises included walking exercises and adjusting the walking speed based on the RPE. The exercise intervention programs are described in Table 3.

Measurement items and methods

Evaluation of muscle strength

A portable muscle tester (MicroFET 2, Hoggan Health Industries, UT) was used to measure the patient’s knee flexion and extension strength. Knee flexion was measured by placing the device on the Achilles tendon, while knee extension was assessed at the closest point to the fibula head. The measurements were repeated three times, and the highest value was used in the analysis. The reliability of this test is ICC = 0.86–0.94 11,12.

Measurement of static balance

To measure single-leg balance ability, participants were instructed to stand on one leg with their eyes open, and maintain the stance with both hands on their hips. The measurement ended when the foot touched the floor, with a maximum duration of 120 s. The measurements were repeated three times, and the maximum value was used. The reliability of the single-leg standing test is ICC = 0.75–0.97 13.

Measurement of dynamic balance

The Timed Up and Go (TUG) test was conducted to measure dynamic balance ability. The participant sat on a chair 50 cm high, and upon hearing the starting signal, stood up, walked to a point 3 m ahead, turned around, and returned to their seat. The time taken for this task was recorded. The subjects performed this test without assistance from others, at a brisk walking pace, but not running. The test was repeated twice, and the mean value was calculated. The TUG test is a reliable test for quantifying functional improvement after TKA 14,15, with a reliability level of ICC = 0.91–0.92 16.

Measurement of range of motion

To measure the ROM of the joints, a digital goniometer (HALO) was utilized as the measuring tool. The subjects took a prone position with the goniometer attached to the ankle. After aligning the zero point, the knee joint was flexed as much as possible, and the angle was measured. The mean value was obtained by repeating the measurement three times to minimize the error between measurements. The maximum flexion angle was set at 135°, and a higher number indicates greater flexibility.

Gait test

The 6-Minute Walk Test (6MWT) was conducted to evaluate gait ability. The 6MWT is a reliable test for evaluating overall mobility after TKA 17. In the 6MWT, the individual is asked to walk as far as possible for 6 min on a flat, solid surface, and the total distance covered during this time is recorded. The fastest walking pace without running achieved for 6 min is also measured.

Knee function assessment

The Western Ontario and McMaster Universities Osteoarthritis Index (WOMAC) was used to evaluate physical function. WOMAC is highly reliable and is widely applied in patients who have undergone total arthroplasty. The evaluation involves five items for pain, two for stiffness, and 17 for physical function. The maximum score is 96 points, with higher scores indicating better physical function, whereas lower scores indicate poorer physical function and greater disability.

Lower limb pain evaluation

The Visual Analog Scale (VAS) was used to measure pain intensity. Participants were requested to subjectively rate their level of pain using a pain scale ranging from 0 (no pain) to 10 (extreme pain that is difficult to tolerate).

Data processing

The data collected in this study were analyzed using SPSS version 23.0 (IBM Corp., Somers, NY, USA). Descriptive statistics were expressed as mean ± standard deviation. Normality of continuous variables was assessed using the Shapiro–Wilk test, and homogeneity between groups was examined using independent samples t-tests. Variables that did not strictly satisfy normality assumptions were further evaluated through visual inspection of distribution patterns and skewness. Given the balanced group sizes and the robustness of parametric repeated-measures analyses to moderate deviations from normality, parametric statistical methods were retained for primary and secondary outcomes. Results for variables exhibiting non-normal distributions were interpreted with caution, with greater emphasis placed on consistency of observed trends, effect sizes, and confidence intervals rather than sole reliance on p-values.

A two-way repeated-measures analysis of variance (ANOVA) was used to assess changes over time and between groups according to the intervention methods. For outcome variables demonstrating significant baseline heterogeneity between groups, analysis of covariance (ANCOVA) was performed, with baseline values included as covariates to adjust for pre-intervention differences. When significant main or interaction effects were identified, post-hoc comparisons were conducted using the Bonferroni correction. Between-group differences at specific time points were assessed using independent samples t-tests. Effect sizes for repeated-measures ANOVA and ANCOVA were reported as partial eta squared (partial η2), and Cohen’s d was additionally calculated for primary outcome comparisons. The level of statistical significance was set at α = 0.05.
