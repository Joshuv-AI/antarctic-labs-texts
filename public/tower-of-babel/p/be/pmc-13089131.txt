Outcomes Following Retrograde Intramedullary Threaded Nail Fixation of Distal Ulnar Neck Fractures
Authors: Alexander D. Jeffs, Stephen M. Himmelberg, Nicholas C. Bank, Sarah C. Conlon, Gregory M. Knoll, J. Megan M. Patterson, Reid W. Draeger
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
PurposeUnstable fractures of the distal ulna present a clinical challenge. The purpose of this study was to describe the outcomes following fixation of unstable distal ulnar neck fractures via a retrograde intramedullary threaded nail (IMTN).MethodsWe retrospectively identified patients at a single level 1 trauma center, between 2022 and 2025, who underwent surgical fixation of an unstable distal ulnar neck fracture with a retrograde IMTN. We included patients at least 1 year postinjury. Demographics, fracture characteristics, functional outcomes, radiographic outcomes, and complications were collected. Patients were grouped into 1-, 2-, and 3-year follow-up groups to determine rates of hardware removal. Current visual analog scale pain scores and patient-rated wrist evaluation scores were collected over the phone.ResultsSeventeen patients were identified. The mean age was 65 years. Distal ulnar neck fracture morphologies included nine neck fractures with styloid fractures and eight extra-articular neck fractures. All were associated with an ipsilateral operative distal radius fracture. Average time to radiographic union was 8.5 ± 2.9 weeks and average ulnar variance was −0.55 mm ± 1.34 mm. One patient developed a nonunion secondary to IMTN back out. There were no occurrences of postoperative superficial-deep infection, dorsal sensory branch of the ulnar nerve injuries, or persistent-recurrent distal radioulnar joint instability. The average visual analog scale pain score was 0.29 ± 0.8, and the average patient-rated wrist evaluation score was 11.8 ± 7.9. Ulnar-sided wrist pain, localized to the distal radioulnar joint, was reported by two patients. Hardware removal rates were 5.9%, 0.0%, and 0.0% at 1 year, 2 years, and 3 years, respectively.ConclusionsA rare complication of retrograde IMTN fixation of a distal ulnar neck fracture is nonunion with subsequent implant back-out. The present cohort demonstrated a low removal of hardware rate and an absence of insertion-site pain, suggesting a potential advantage of intramedullary implant tolerability for the treatment of distal ulnar neck fractures.Type of study/level of evidenceTherapeutic IV.

Fractures of the distal ulna, particularly unstable ulnar neck fractures, present a unique challenge because of their proximity to the distal radioulnar joint (DRUJ) and the triangular fibrocartilage complex (TFCC).1, 2, 3 The distal ulna is a key component of forearm rotation, load transfer, and wrist stability; malunion or nonunion may lead to chronic pain, weakness, and or DRUJ instability.1,3, 4, 5

Treatment options for these injuries range from nonsurgical immobilization to various types of internal fixation, or in select chronic cases, distal ulna resection.1, 2, 3,6,7 Prior work has suggested that in low-demand or elderly patients, nonsurgical management may yield outcomes comparable to surgical treatment with plate and screw fixation.1,2,6,8 However, these results may not be transferable to methods of fixation that minimize soft-tissue disruption and irritation, such as intramedullary fixation.7,9, 10, 11

Plate and screw fixation is the gold standard method of fixation for unstable distal ulnar neck fractures, offering rigid stability with rotational control for early range of motion (ROM).12 Yet, the limited soft-tissue envelope at the distal ulna complicates this technique, given the proximity of the dorsal sensory branch of the ulnar nerve (DSBUN), the extensor carpi ulnaris subsheath, and the DRUJ capsular-ligamentous components. Accordingly, hardware prominence, irritation, and reoperation for removal of hardware are established complications in up to one-third of cases.1,3,13

Intramedullary techniques eliminate hardware prominence, and early clinical outcomes for various intramedullary techniques have demonstrated reliable union, restoration of forearm motion, and reduced hardware irritation.7,9, 10, 11 Given the potential equivalence of nonsurgical and plate-based management in select patients, and the growing interest in intramedullary fixation, further evaluation of this technique is warranted. We have previously published a technique for retrograde intramedullary threaded nail fixation (IMTN).9 However, sample size, duration of follow-up, and patient-reported outcomes were limited.

The purpose of this study was to describe the outcomes at up to 3 years following fixation of unstable distal ulnar neck fractures via a retrograde IMTN. We hypothesized that this method provides fixation with high union rates and low complication rates while minimizing soft-tissue morbidity.

Methods

Institutional review board approval was obtained. We retrospectively identified patients at a single level 1 trauma center from 2022 to 2025 who had a distal ulnar neck fracture and underwent surgical fixation with an IMTN by a fellowship-trained hand surgeon. Two fellowship-trained hand surgeons with level 5 expertise in the technique were included, and all patients had their distal ulna fracture treated with a 4.5-mm ExsoMed INnate Nail 75 mm in length (Acumed).14

All distal ulnar neck fractures were assessed for instability with the ballottement test. If an ipsilateral distal radius fracture was present, this was fixed first and then the stability of the distal ulna was assessed. If unstable, the wrist was radially deviated and the ulnar styloid was palpated. After marking the ulnar styloid, the entry site at the soft spot, distal, and volar to the extensor carpi ulnaris tendon was identified. A 1-cm longitudinal incision was made at the entry site for a guidewire. The forearm was kept in neutral rotation to avoid the DSBUN. After mobilizing the underlying subcutaneous tissue, a 9-in. by 0.045-in. diameter guidewire was inserted retrograde under fluoroscopic guidance into the concave portion of the ulna head. The start point was the denervated central disc of the TFCC, and the foveal insertion was avoided. The start point was confirmed on orthogonal fluoroscopic views, and the fracture was closed reduced with finger manipulation. The guidewire was then passed proximal to the fracture site, and soft-tissue protectors were placed to avoid soft-tissue damage when overdrilling. For the 4.5-mm IMTN, the canal was overdrilled with a 3.4-mm drill bit the entire length of the intended implant proximal and distal to the fracture site. The IMTN was then inserted over the guidewire and advanced until the head of the implant was buried beneath the articular surface. DRUJ stability and unobstructed ROM were confirmed following fixation.

After surgery, all patients were made nonweightbearing on the operative extremity and were immobilized for 10–14 days with a plaster above-elbow sugar tong splint. After their first follow-up, all patients were referred to a hand therapist per our standard clinical practice, given a thermoplastic volar below-elbow splint, and were instructed to advance their active unassisted wrist (flexion-extension) and forearm (pronation-supination) ROM out of the splint, under the supervision of the hand therapist, as tolerated. When not with the hand therapist, patients were instructed to stay in the below-elbow splint for 4 weeks. Patients were kept nonweightbearing until 6 weeks after surgery. After weightbearing was advanced, the below-elbow splint was discontinued, and patients were instructed to advance their active wrist and forearm ROM as tolerated with no restrictions. We included all patients with fractures who were at least 1 year postinjury.

Through manual chart review, patient demographics, mechanism of injury, and fracture characteristics were collected. Fracture characteristics included date of injury, fracture location, fracture morphology, closed versus open injury, and the presence or absence of an ipsilateral hand or upper-extremity injury. Distal ulnar neck fractures were defined based on the classification system by Biyani et al.15 Time to union was calculated, in weeks, as the elapsed time from the date of injury to the date of radiographic union. Ulnar variance at final follow-up was calculated from a neutral wrist posterioranterior (PA) radiograph as the distance between the distal cortical surface of the distal radius at the sigmoid notch and the distal ulnar head.

Surgical complications including superficial infection, deep infection, and DSBUN injury were collected. Postoperative complications including nonunion, hardware failure, hardware removal, DRUJ instability, and ulnar-sided wrist pain were collected. Wrist flexion-extension and forearm pronation-supination at 6 and 12 weeks were collected. Patients were grouped into 1-, 2-, and 3-year follow-up groups based on the elapsed time from their date of injury, and hardware removal rates were determined. Patients were contacted over the phone to identify patients who potentially underwent hardware removal at another institution. At the time of contact, the current visual analog scale (VAS) pain score and patient-rated wrist evaluation (PRWE) score were collected. Outcomes were summarized with descriptive statistics and were reported as means with standard deviation for continuous data and as percentages for categorical data.

Results

Seventeen patients were identified who met the inclusion criteria (Table). Mean age was 65 years (range 51–82 years) with 2 males and 15 females. Most patients denied current or prior nicotine use (11/17). The mechanism of injury was a low-energy ground-level fall in the majority of patients (13/17). All but four distal ulna fractures were closed injuries (76.5%). All fractures were associated with operative intra-articular distal radius fractures. In the majority of cases, the distal radius fracture was treated with volar locking plate and screw fixation (15/17). The volar locking plates used were Acumed Acu-Lok plates (12/15) or Zimmer Biomet distal volar radius plates (3/15). There was one distal radius fracture treated with percutaneous Kirschner wire fixation, and one treated with fragment-specific radial styloid plate and screw fixation (Acumed). For the distal ulnar neck fractures, all were indicated for surgery based on intraoperative confirmation of persistent fracture site instability, based on the ballottement test, after fixation of the ipsilateral distal radius fracture. The distal ulnar neck fracture morphologies included 9 neck fractures with styloid fractures (Biyani type 3, Fig. 1) and 8 extra-articular neck fractures (Biyani type 1, Fig. 2). After surgery, there were no reported superficial infections, deep infections, or DSBUN injuries. There was no documented persistent or recurrent DRUJ instability based on ballottement testing at the last follow-up. Average wrist flexion was 33.2° ± 12.9 at 6 weeks and 52.3° ± 18.2 at 12 weeks. Average wrist extension was 44.1° ± 16.1 at 6 weeks and 64.5° ± 11.3 at 12 weeks. Average forearm pronation was 74.3° ± 27.0 at 6 weeks and 86.9° ± 7.5 at 12 weeks. Average forearm supination was 64.3° ± 22.4 at 6 weeks and 72.9° ± 23.9 at 12 weeks. Current ulnar-sided wrist pain was reported by 2 patients (11.8%). Pain was localized to the DRUJ joint in both patients; both patients denied pain at the IMTN insertion site. The average VAS pain score was 0.29 ± 0.8, and the average PRWE score was 11.8 ± 7.9. Hardware removal rates were 5.9%, 0.0%, and 0.0% at 1 year, 2 years, and 3 years, respectively.

Discussion

In this retrospective series of 17 patients with unstable distal ulnar neck fractures treated with retrograde IMTN, we observed reliable outcomes. Complications were uncommon: there were no superficial or deep infections and no documented DSBUN injuries. One patient (5.9%) developed a nonunion associated with hardware failure from implant back-out, requiring revision fixation (Figs. 4 and 5). Patient-reported outcomes showed high return to function, based on PRWE score, and low residual wrist pain, based on VAS pain score, with only 2 patients reporting current ulnar-sided wrist pain, which was localized to the DRUJ rather than the retrograde IMTN insertion site. ROM improved over time, and there was a near-complete return to normal pronation-supination with more limited wrist flexion-extension. DRUJ instability, based on postoperative ballottement testing, was neither persistent nor recurrent in any patient at final follow-up. Pertinently, hardware removal was rare (5.9% at 1 year and 0% at 2 and 3 years), supporting the premise that an intramedullary construct may mitigate the soft-tissue irritation that can accompany traditional plate and screw distal ulna fixation.

When contextualized within the existing literature, our findings differ from the established concerns regarding plate and screw constructs at the distal ulna.1,3,13 Reported reoperation rates, specifically for removal of hardware, after plate and screw fixation have been up to one-third in some series.1,3,13 The present IMTN cohort demonstrated low removal of hardware rates at all studied time points and an absence of insertion-site pain complaints, suggesting a potential advantage of intramedullary implant tolerability.7,10,11 Plate and screw fixation can achieve high union rates, but the distal ulna’s limited soft-tissue envelope makes plate fixation particularly prone to symptomatic implant prominence.7 Similarly, although nonsurgical management may be reasonable for select distal ulna fractures with adequate alignment and stability, it might not be generalizable to the unstable patterns included in our cohort, which have reported higher rates of nonunion.3,6

Prior studies of intramedullary fixation for distal ulna fractures have reported high union rates and favorable functional outcomes, and our findings align with this trend.7,10,11 Niemann et al7 treated nine unstable distal ulna fractures with a retrograde, noncompressive threaded pin fixation and found a high union rate and good functional recovery based on Disabilities of the Arm, Shoulder, and Hand score and VAS pain score. However, their minimum follow-up was only 6 months, which limits the interpretation of longer-term outcomes. Oh et al11 similarly reported favorable outcomes in 11 patients treated with intramedullary headless compression screws, but follow-up was once again limited to 6 months. In contrast, the present study provides extended follow-up, up to 3 years, with longer-term patient-reported outcomes and rates of hardware removal to better characterize the survivorship of intramedullary fixation in the distal ulna.

The single case of nonunion in this cohort, with subsequent hardware removal, merits focused discussion. The mode of failure observed in Figure 4 is unique to an intramedullary-threaded device. The implant backed out from the distal segment. Potential contributors include (1) a short distal segment with insufficient distal purchase; (2) fracture morphology, specifically fracture site comminution, that was less forgiving to an unlocked intramedullary implant; (3) technical factors such as canal fill that may have influenced endosteal fit and fatigue resistance to toggle; (4) immediate postoperative ROM that could have promoted micromotion before biologic stability was achieved; and (5) poor bone quality secondary to potential osteoporosis and current smoking status. Importantly, the successful revision with volar distal ulna plate and screw fixation suggests that when IMTN fixation fails, conversion to a more rigid plate and screw construct is a rare but effective salvage pathway.

Aside from postoperative complications, important aspects of the technique must be highlighted to avoid surgical complications. As a minimally invasive technique, an understanding of the relevant anatomy and fluoroscopic interpretation is vital to success. Care must be taken to avoid branches of the DSBUN. If a volar entry site is used, this can be performed by maintaining neutral forearm rotation and avoiding pronation.16 Consideration of a more dorsal entry site, in line with the 6R arthroscopy portal, can also be used to potentially increase distance from the DSBUN. Additionally, utilization of a long 9-in. guidewire is key. There is the potential for the guidewire to advance too far proximally in the ulna during overdrilling and IMTN insertion. A long guidewire can help combat this. Additionally, care must be taken to avoid the foveal insertion of the TFCC. Damage to the foveal insertion could lead to unintended DRUJ instability. There is also the potential for overadvancement of the IMTN if excessive force is used during insertion or if the metadiaphyseal bone quality is poor.

This study should be interpreted with several limitations. The single-center retrospective design, small sample size, and lack of a comparison group limit generalizability and restrict the ability to identify predictors of time to union, ulnar-sided wrist pain, or implant failure. The distal ulna fractures operated on in this study were all indicated, based on instability, following fixation of an ipsilateral distal radius fracture. Therefore, the decision to go to the operating room and the subsequent outcomes were confounded by the ipsilateral distal radius fracture. The lack of a plate and screw or nonsurgical comparison group is a limitation that a future study must address to contextualize the findings of this series. As a retrospective review, we were limited to available data and unfortunately were unable to report ipsilateral grip strength and contralateral measures of motion and function. Additionally, the patient-reported outcomes were obtained at a single follow-up point via virtual patient contact, introducing potential response bias, specifically a courtesy bias. Outcomes up to 3 years may not fully capture longer-term outcomes of TFCC damage or degenerative changes, particularly in the setting of concomitant operative distal radius fractures that could confound pain and functional scores. Next steps should include larger or multicenter comparative studies with standardized radiographic criteria, serial patient-reported outcome measure collection, and focused DRUJ/TFCC clinical assessments. Additionally, future biomechanical work focused on distal fixation strength and resistance to fatigue would aid in the optimization of technique, implant selection, and postoperative protocols to minimize the risk of hardware failure or nonunion while preserving the soft-tissue advantages of an intramedullary approach.
