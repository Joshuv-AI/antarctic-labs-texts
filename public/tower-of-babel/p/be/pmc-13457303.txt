Pain Alleviation by Electronic Acupuncture Pen versus Ethyl Chloride Spray during Local Anesthetic Injection Procedures: A Randomized Split-mouth Clinical Trial
Authors: Meghna Bhargava, Pallavi Vashisth, Sathyajith Naik, Shivangi Sharma, Resham Payak, Shilpa Kumari
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundThe most tiresome aspect for pediatric patients is the fear and pain caused by injections, called “belonephobia.” There are numerous methods that can help in reducing the pain during LA administration. Injection-related discomfort has been reduced by topical anesthetics, acupuncture, distraction tactics, and the use of heat or cold.AimThis study aimed to evaluate the effectiveness of the electronic acupuncture pen and ethyl chloride spray in alleviating pain during local anesthetic (LA) injection procedures.Materials and methodsTwenty-four children aged 12–16 years were selected in the study to receive LA in a bilateral split-mouth technique. Each participant was divided into two groups. In group A, an acupuncture pen was used on the first visit on one side, and cryoanesthesia through the ethyl chloride spray was administered on the next visit, on the opposite side. In group B, cryoanesthesia through the ethyl chloride spray was applied on one side on the first visit, and on the next appointment, an acupuncture pen on the other side was administered. Numerical rating scale and Wong–Baker Facial Pain Scale (WBFPS) were used as tools of evaluation after local anesthesia was administered.ResultThe results indicated significant changes in pulse rate over time within each treatment group, but showed a greater increase in the acupuncture pen group. On the numerical rating scale (NRS), the mean score for the transcutaneous electrical nerve stimulation (TENS) group was 4.550 ± 1.468, while the ethyl chloride group had a lower mean score of 2.851 ± 1.424 (p = 0.001). On the WBFPS, the TENS group had a mean score of 3.400 ± 1.465, whereas the ethyl chloride group recorded a significantly lower mean score of 1.200 ± 1.005 (p = 0.001). These results indicate that the ethyl chloride group experienced significantly lower pain levels compared to the TENS group.ConclusionTENS through the electronic acupuncture pen and ethyl chloride has proved its efficacy as a topical pain reduction measure during dental treatment, and also overcomes the shortcomings of the anesthetic gels, abolishing the chances of hypersensitivity and disagreeable taste.

Introduction

Pain is frequently referred to as a “double-edged sword” in dentistry, meaning that while pain is the primary reason a patient visits the dentist, fear of pain also deters people from visiting the dental office.1 Dental pain is an unpleasant sensation that is frequently brought on by strong or harmful stimuli.2 Pain has been an unwavering tormentor and fear since a long time.3 If not properly addressed, such fears can cause averting behavior in patients, ultimately resulting in the postponement or complete avoidance of dental treatment.4

Analgesia is a critical element of pediatric dental care, particularly during invasive procedures involving local anesthetic (LA) administration, to ensure patient comfort.5 A tiny amount of a pharmacological agent that alters brain structures and stops the transmission of painful stimuli is injected to produce analgesia.2

A variety of approaches, including pharmacological and nonpharmacological, are employed to alleviate discomfort linked with LA injections and reduce their discomfort.5 The techniques involve the use of topical anesthetics, distraction techniques, and use of heat and cold to reduce injection related pain.5 Topical anesthetics are used in various forms such as spray, gel, ointment, etc.1

Acupuncture has been practiced for over a thousand years, with its origins rooted in ancient medicine. It is the third most common technique for treating pain. Electroacupuncture involves electric stimulus to a point with needles on the body and connecting it to an electromachine to stimulate the effect.3 Thus, transcutaneous electrical nerve stimulation (TENS) was introduced and is built on the concept that vibration and pressure are generated, which temporarily blocks the transmission of nociceptive messages.1 The pain reduction through the use of vibration is centered on the theory stating that pain is reduced by stimulation of nerve fibers through vibration. The emergence of this has devised a new technique, i.e., electronic acupuncture pen, which is a noninvasive method for bringing out analgesia.1

Cold therapy, also known as cryotherapy, is thought to have been practiced since Hippocratic times and involves applying cold to the affected area for therapeutic effects. For numbing pain and lowering inflammation, topical cooling is also a reliable first aid treatment.6 Cooling the site causes vasoconstriction, reduces tissue metabolism, and inhibits the sense of pain by activating pain pathways.7

Ethyl chloride is usually used by dentists. It is also used in medicine as a pre-injection anesthetic for medical procedures.4 Ethyl chloride was used to treat myofascial pain, mild sports injuries, and minor surgical operations.8 There are a few dental studies that report the use of cold or cryotherapy in this field. Harbert performed the first study in 1989 and discovered that cooling the palate before LA reduced pain perception.8

Due to the sparsity of literature on use of acupuncture pen and ethyl chloride in reducing pain while injecting local anesthesia, this study was intended. Thus, the study was carried out to assess the effectiveness of TENS application through an electronic acupuncture pen and ethyl chloride (cryotherapy) as an intraoral anesthetic agent.

Materials and Methods

The study was conducted in the Department of Pediatric and Preventive Dentistry. The study commenced after obtaining clearance from the Institutional Ethics Committee (IEC/320/2024/09) from December 2024 to February 2025.

The sample size (24) was calculated using G*power 3.1 software (Franz Faul Universität, Kiel, Germany), which consisted of two groups, 12 samples per group. A sample size of 24 patients is deemed to be appropriate to conduct the study at a 0.05 level of significance.

The participants included: (1) were aged 12–16 years; (2) required bilateral mandibular or maxillary LA block or local infiltration; (3) graded 3 or 4 in Frankl's behavior scale.9

The patients: (1) presented with any infections or systemic diseases; (2) were especially abled; (3) allergic to local anesthesia/electrode/tape gel; (4) had any skin condition or open wounds; and (5) grade 1 or 2 in Frankl's behavior scale10 were excluded.

Informed Consent

Before the study started, informed consent was obtained from each participant and their legal guardian. An information and consent form provided a clear explanation of the study's nature and goal.

Only those who agreed to participate voluntarily were enrolled in the study. Confidentiality was strictly maintained; participants' identities were anonymized, and their results were reported without any personally identifiable information.

Randomization and Blinding

Using the lottery approach, participants were randomly assigned to two groups (group A and group B), each consisting of 12 individuals, resulting in a total of 24 sites bilaterally.

Simple random sampling was done using a lottery system, in which 24 sealed opaque envelopes (12 for each group) were sealed and kept in a box, and each participant was asked to pick up one envelope, after which that envelope was discarded.

A second-year resident, supervised by a senior faculty member, performed the treatment for both the groups as a single operator. All of the children's Wong–Baker Facial Pain Scale (WBFPS) scores and spO2 measurements were taken by a single dentist who was blind to the participants' group assignment.

Application of Acupuncture Pen

Application of Acupuncture Pen

A cotton roll was used to dry the buccal mucosa for 30 seconds and isolation was maintained. The pen (Rechargeable Meridian Energy massage pen) (Fig. 1) was adjusted as mentioned by the manufacturer at a setting of '3' and placed where local anesthesia had to be administered (Fig. 2). The same region was gently massaged using small circular motions for 1 minute, with the instrument remaining in contact with the buccal mucosa throughout. After that, a short 27-gauge Safe-Plus needle was used to slowly and carefully inject a LA solution containing 2% lidocaine and 1:80,000 adrenaline into the designated location over the course of 1 minute.

Ethyl chloride spray (Passive Ethyl Chloride IP spray) (Fig. 3) was sprayed on a small-sized cotton ball, which was soaked with the spray and positioned on the area for 30 seconds (Fig. 4). A digital thermometer was used to measure the temperature of ethyl chloride, which ranged from −2°C to 0°C (Fig. 5). A small 27-gauge needle (Safe-plus needles) was then used to inject the LA solution (2% lidocaine with 1:80,000 adrenaline) at a slow pace for 1 minute into the desired location.

Results

The research comprised 24 patients who required LA administration (48 sites). The children's average age was 13.5 years. No adverse events were observed during the study while using acupuncture pen or ethyl chloride spray.

The mean score of SpO2 at various time intervals for the TENS group was 90.500 ± 10.394, 91.300 ± 10.280, and 91.950 ± 10.585, while the ethyl group had an average score of 91.000 ± 8.220, 89.400 ± 0.524, and 92.500 ± 6.134 with a p-value of >0.05 (Table 1).

The ethyl group scored 1.200 ± 1.005 on the WBFPS, a far lower mean score than the TENS group, which scored 3.400 ± 1.465 (Table 4). These findings show that on both pain evaluation scales, the ethyl group's pain levels were noticeably lower than those of the TENS group, as shown in Table 4.

Statistical Analysis

The data was entered in the Microsoft Excel 2007 and analyzed using the SPSS statistical software 23.0 Version. The level of the significance was fixed at 5%, i.e., p < 0.05.

The Shapiro–Wilk test was used to examine the data distribution, Levene's test was used to examine the homogeneity of the variables, and the Mann–Whitney U test was used for the intergroup comparison, and the Wilcoxon signed rank test for the intragroup comparison between the various time periods.

Discussion

Pain is a distressing sensory and emotional feeling that arises from actual or possible tissue injury, or is expressed as if such damage has occurred. A child, when exposed to the prick pain caused by a needle, leads to an increase in the child's fear, leading to denial of treatment. Approximately 30% of patients reported moderate to severe pain during the administration of LA, according to Nusstein and Beck's study.4 Thus application of topical anesthesia becomes an indispensable requisite before any pediatric procedures, as its use before anesthesia reduces puncturing pain due to the insertion of the needle.3 Fear associated with needle procedures may lead to heightened avoidance behavior and efforts to minimize any potential needle exposure.12

According to Szmuk et al., 10% of the general population is affected by needle phobia, the etiology of which is complex and multifactorial.13 The application of topical anesthesia becomes mandatory. The major shortcoming of commercially accessible topical anesthetic gels is the lack of bioadhesive properties for the oral mucosa, which can prevent the targeted site from receiving the necessary amount of the active agent. Additionally, their unpleasant taste may cause patient discomfort. Other adverse responses such as overdose, hypersensitivity, and anaphylaxis may be anticipated.12

A few ideas suggest that anxiety and stress-related physiological reactions, such as heart rate, are related. It is possible that a vicious loop exists in which anxiety levels rise in response to perceived increases in heart rate, which in turn causes heightened awareness of heartbeats, which exacerbates the anxiety.14

Stress can set off a series of physiological reactions that might cause anxiety and alter heart rate and blood pressure.6 Heart rate variability, i.e., pulse rate, was elevated during LA administration in a few studies conducted by Manepalli S et al.15 Thus, during LA administration, we considered measuring pulse rate using a pulse oximeter.

In their study, Taddio et al. found that 51% of children aged 13–17 years, 68% of children aged 6–8 years, and 65% of children aged 9–12 years self-reported a high fear of needles.16 Thus, we selected the age group aged 12–16 years to eliminate bias, as the lower age group tend to experience more anxiety. Also, it would be difficult to make out whether the rating on the scale is due to pain or anxiety.

In our study, we have used a TENS pen and ethyl chloride spray as two interventions.

Voll created the technique known as “electroacupuncture” by creating an electrical stimulation (ES) tool to administer to “acupoints.” ES can be divided into five categories: electroacupuncture (EA), transcutaneous electrical acupoint stimulation (TEAS), auricular electroacupuncture (AEA), TENS, and electrical heat acupuncture (EHA). Developed using the AL-TENS principle, the pen provides a noninvasive method of transmitting electrical impulses to alleviate pain.17

The anesthetic effect produced by the pen is explained primarily by two mechanisms: the gate control theory of pain and the endogenous opioid theory. The former, introduced by Melzack and Wall, the substantia gelatinosa functions as a modulatory center that regulates sensory input from peripheral nerve fibers, thereby influencing the opening and closing of the “pain gate” in the spinal cord.3,18 In a study by Deulkar et al., children aged 6–12 years received local anesthesia using a bilateral split-mouth design—topical anesthetic gel on one side and a TENS pen on the other during separate visits. The results indicated that TENS applied through the pen was effective in minimizing pain during dental treatment.3 Similar results were obtained in our study, where the electronic acupuncture pen proved to alleviate pain during LA administration. There was another observation made during the study, i.e., the onset of the effect of LA was delayed in acupuncture; this could be ascribed to the closing of large fibers.

Cryoanesthesiology is an adjunctive pain management technique that works by inhibiting local neural transmission through cooling, which is achieved by applying refrigerant sprays or rubbing ice to the targeted area.5 Ethyl chloride is commonly used as a standard agent to assess baseline pulp sensibility. It is widely employed in dentistry as a pre-injection anesthetic to reduce pain and discomfort.6

When compared to topical spray, Chilakamuri et al. found that using a precooling agent prior to LA significantly reduced their pain levels.6 Aminabadi et al. found that using ice for 2 minutes prior to an infra-alveolar nerve block injection effectively reduced pain perception, which is in accordance with our study, where ethyl chloride spray, when used at −2°C to 0°C, alleviated pain during LA administration.19 Hindocha et al. found that the analgesic effects of application of ice were comparable to those of lidocaine gel when used as a topical anesthetic.20 Abbasi et al. concluded in their study that ethyl chloride significantly alleviated pain perceived while injecting local anesthesia.4 This is in accordance with our study, which also reported the same results. Cryoanesthesia is believed to reduce the ability of pain fibers to transmit pain signals. This method works by blocking local neural transmission through cooling the affected area.5

But when both methods were compared, ethyl chloride was found to be a better alternative in reducing pain while injecting local anesthesia.

There is currently no research comparing the effectiveness of an acupuncture pen with ethyl chloride spray, and very limited studies have been directed on the usage of an acupuncture pen. Hence, this study is the first of its kind and is unique in its approach.

Limitations of the Study

A limited number of participants may reduce the statistical power and generalizability of the results to the wider population.

Variability in how the acupuncture pen is used (pressure, duration, location) could affect results and make reproducibility difficult.

Conclusion

Managing pain during LA injections remains a significant challenge in pediatric dentistry, particularly among children with needle phobia. The fear of needles not only affects a child's cooperation during treatment but may also shape negative attitudes toward dental care.

The TENS-based acupuncture device temporarily disrupts pain signal transmission. This method is appreciated for being drug-free and free from side effects such as unpleasant taste, hypersensitivity reactions, or the risk of overdosage that may occur with anesthetic gels.

On the other hand, ethyl chloride spray acts rapidly by inducing surface anesthesia. It is simple to apply, fast-acting, and well-accepted by pediatric patients. Its immediate anesthetic effect, combined with ease of use, makes it an efficient tool for routine dental procedures.

Comparative clinical experiences suggest that ethyl chloride spray offers a more effective and convenient means of reducing discomfort during LA injections in children, especially when time and patient cooperation are critical.

Clinical Significance

This study is clinically significant as it evaluates two noninvasive methods for reducing pain during LA injections in dental procedures. By comparing their effectiveness, it offers alternative, drug-free options for pain management, especially valuable for patients with needle anxiety, allergies, or contraindications to topical anesthetics. The findings support a more patient-centered, cost-effective approach to improving comfort during dental care and may guide clinicians in selecting appropriate techniques based on individual needs.
