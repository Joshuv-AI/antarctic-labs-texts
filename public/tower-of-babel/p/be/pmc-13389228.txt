Relationship Between ST‐Segment Resolution and Clinical Outcomes After Primary Percutaneous Coronary Intervention of Bifurcation Lesion in Patients With ST Elevation Myocardial Infarction: A Cohort Study
Authors: Sara Mirzamohamadi, Haleh Ashraf, Hamidreza Hekmat, Shahrokh Karbalai Saleh, Naser Saadat, Abbas Soleimani
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTBackground and AimsST resolution (STR) is associated with improved cardiac outcomes after primary percutaneous coronary intervention (PPCI) and is a marker of success. In this study, we aimed to determine the relationship between the STR degree and outcomes after coronary bifurcation PPCI.MethodsThis cohort study was conducted at Sina Hospital, Tehran, Iran, from January 11, 2017, to March 20, 2022. Introduction

The treatment choice in ST‐elevation myocardial infarction (STEMI) is primary percutaneous coronary intervention (PPCI). After reperfusion, we need a tool to assess recanalization and myocardial function. Electrocardiography (ECG) is a non‐invasive, simple, and rapid tool for this goal. ST‐segment changes reflected myocardial flow rather than epicardial [1]. Therefore, measuring ST resolution (STR) could demonstrate to changes in myocardial flow after reperfusion therapy and reflect the success of PPCI. Many studies mentioned the STR degree associated with cardiac mortality and major adverse cardiovascular events (MACE) [1, 2, 3], and no STR has meant microvascular obstruction than MACE and higher mortality rates in short‐term and long‐term follow‐up [4]. Also, STR predicts infarct size and microvascular flow [5]. Restoration of myocardial perfusion is the physiological basis of STR. After PCI, microvascular spasm and embolism could cause persistent coronary microvascular dysfunction, myocardial, and endocardial ischemia. Ischemia leads to a decreased in extracellular potassium ions, prolonged repolarization, and delayed STR. Therefore, low STR reflects coronary microvascular and left ventricular dysfunction [6]. Studying about other predictive tools including SYNTAX score II (SSII), showed that SSII was an independent prognostic factor for in‐hospital death in patients with cardiogenic shock after PPCI due to STEMI [7].

Bifurcations in the coronary artery are susceptible to atherosclerosis due to their lower intimal stress for tear [8]. Approximately 15% of percutaneous coronary interventions (PCIs) involve bifurcation lesions in the United States [9]. Treating bifurcation lesions is challenging because of worse outcomes and a higher rate of stent thrombosis, revascularization, and periprocedural myocardial infarction (MI) [10].

Many studies have investigated STR in STEMI patients' outcomes, but no studies have studied STR in PPCI of bifurcation lesions. For the importance of the coronary bifurcation, we determined the relationship between the STR degree and outcomes after PPCI for coronary bifurcation.

Methods

Study Design

This cohort study was conducted at Sina Hospital, Tehran, Iran, from January 11, 2017, to March 20, 2022. This study was performed based on our previous study in bifurcation lesion PPCI (unpublished data). Methodological details of the Primary Angioplasty Registry of Sina (PARS) registry have been published previously [11]. Briefly, our PARS registry, based at Sina Hospital, is ongoing, prospective, hospital‐based registry of STEMI patients undergoing PPCI STEMI in at a high‐volume referral hospital with a catheterization lab in southern Tehran, Iran. This registry began enrolling patients in November 2016, following the implementation of Project 247 by the Iranian Ministry of Health and Medical Education.

Additionally, patients with STEMI who were referred from the emergency department (ED) and other hospitals in the southern zone of southern Tehran have been added to this registry. The Project‐247, derived from the 24‐h day and 7 days a week availability, was designed to facilitate faster out‐of‐hospital diagnosis of STEMI in patients presenting with MI and its principal manifestations. The Pars registry is intended to align with the detailed principles of the STROBE guidelines [11].

Study Population

Patients with acute STEMI were admitted to the ED or transferred through a 24/7 service to a catheterization lab that underwent PPCI, with the bifurcation lesion diagnosis included in this study. Patients with cardiac arrest and death in the catheterization lab during PPCI, patients not treated with PPCI and referred to coronary artery bypass graft, side branch (SB) diameter < 2 mm, and patients with incomplete follow‐up or missed data were excluded from this study. Of the 1853 patients with STEMI who underwent PPCI during this period, 332 (17.91%) patients had lesions at the coronary bifurcation. Finally, after exclusion, 219 patients were included in the analysis.

Data Collection

Studied variables included age, sex, diabetes mellitus (DM), hypertension (HTN), smoking, dyslipidemia (DLP), bifurcation type, bifurcation classification, PPCI strategy, type of MI, culprit vessel, door to device time (D2dt), contrast volume, post PPCI thrombolysis in myocardial infarction (TIMI), cardiac death, MACE, type of stent, ejection fraction (EF), and STR.

Demographic and past medical history were collected from the patient's documents. Bifurcation and PPCI classification were obtained from a review of angioplasty records following PPCI. Follow‐up was performed by telephone or office visit. Follow‐up was performed for every patient from the PPCI date to November 2022, with a 34 ± 25 (median ± IQR) months. STR calculated maximum ST elevation (Max STE) from a 90 min ECG after PPCI [12]. Max STE was measured maximum ST‐segment deviation in the single lead. Max STE has three groups: low‐risk, medium‐risk, and high‐risk (Table 1).

SB diameter ≥ 2 mm had been considered a bifurcation lesion. Bifurcation PPCI is performed based on the KISSS principle (keep it swift, simple, and safe). Our patients were divided into the main vessel (MV) intervention group and the MV/SB intervention group. Lesions with good SB flow underwent MV stenting. However, if the SB was occluded or would probably be occluded after MV stenting, SB wiring was performed. Lesions without good SB flow are candidates for MV stenting plus SB intervention. Plain balloon angioplasty (POBA) was the first choice intervention in SB; however, if POBA balloon angioplasty in SB often led to dissection or did not provide sufficient flow, SB stenting was required.

Definitions

MACE is defined as any cardiac death, cerebrovascular accident (CVA), MI, and revascularization in patients after PPCI until the end of the follow‐up peroid. Cardiac death included defined as death after MI and any death without a clear non‐cardiac cause. MI type is divided into anterior, inferior, and posterior MI. Bifurcation lesions are classified according to the Medina classification. True bifurcation lesions included 1.1.1, 1.0.1, and 0.1.1 types of Medina classification are defined as true bifurcation lesions [13]. The culprit vessels included the left anterior descending artery (LAD), left circumflex artery (LCX), right coronary artery (RCA), and others (diagonal, PDA, PLB, OM, ramus).

Statistical Analysis

The values are presented as counts and percentages or mean ± standard deviation (SD). The chi‐square test was used to compare nominal or categorical variables. Analysis of variance (ANOVA) was used to compare the means of quantitative variables with normal distribution between the different STR groups. A non‐parametric Kruskal–Wallis one‐way ANOVA test is used to compare continuous variables with non‐normal distribution. Bonferroni's method also determined which group differences were statistically significant. Univariate and multiple logistic regression were used to identify predictors of cardiac death and EF. Statistical significance is accepted at p‐values < 0.2 in univariate logistic regression and p < 0.05 in other tests. Data analysis was performed with SPSS 26.

Ethics

This study was performed in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki. The Research Ethics Committee of Tehran University of Medical Sciences approved it (Approval ID: IR.TUMS.SINAHOSPITAL.REC.1399.057). During the data collection, informed consent was obtained from the patients.

Result

Among 219 patients who underwent PPCI for bifurcation lesions, 105 (48.16%) were low‐risk STR, 75 (34.4%) were medium‐risk, and 39 (17.43%) were high‐risk. Men showed less STR (high‐risk group) than women, but the difference was not statistically significant. The mean age (SD) was higher in the high‐risk group; however, the difference was not statistically significant. Forty‐seven (47.5%) of patients with low‐risk STR had a significantly normal EF (≥ 50%) compared to nine people (12.3%) in the medium‐risk group and six (17.1%) in the high‐risk group (p < 0.001). Patients with true bifurcation lesions had less STR than those with non‐true bifurcation lesions, although the difference was not‐significant. People with intervention in MV/SB showed a greater number of high‐risk STRs relative to the intervention in MV non‐significantly. Patients with inferior MI had significantly were low‐risk STR (44 (46.3%)) than medium‐risk STR (7 (10.1%)) and high‐risk STR (8 (24.2%)) (p < 0.001). When LAD was the culprit vessel, 51 (52.6%) patients were low‐risk, 61 (87.1%) were medium‐risk, and 26 (74.3%) were high‐risk (p < 0.001). Cardiac death in the high‐risk group (6 (17.1%)) was significantly higher than in the other two groups (4 (4.3%) in low‐risk and 3 (4.3%) in medium‐risk) (p = 0.04). Patients with high‐risk STR (16 (45.7%)) had non‐significantly more positive MACE during follow‐up (Table 2).

Discussion

This study yielded several key findings, including that approximately half of the patients (48.16%) had a low‐risk STR. EF in the low‐risk group was higher than in the medium and high‐risk groups. Also, anterior MI and occlusion in the LAD had higher rates of medium and high‐risk STR. Risk factors, including DM, HTN, and smoking, did not affect STR. We considered bifurcation PPCI; the PPCI strategy in MV or MV/SB was associated with cardiac death in addition to the STR. Also, STR was a predictor of EF after PPCI.

In this study, we observed that patients with anterior MI had less STR than those with inferior MI. In this regard, other studies have shown that the low‐risk group included inferior MI more than twice as often as anterior MI. This observation was consistent across all STR evaluation methods [1, 14]. Also, a higher rate of complete STR was observed in anterior MI in patients who underwent PPCI after remote ischemic conditioning underwent PPCI [15]. In the study of the CAMI (China Acute Myocardial Infarction) registry, patients with STR < 50% had more anterior infarction than those in to the complete STR and STR ≥ 50% groups [16]. In addition, in the TOTAL trial, the incomplete or no STR (STR < 70%) groups had more anterior MI, and the complete STR (STR ≥ 70%) group had more inferior MI [4].

Other studies considered that the high‐risk group was older and had risk factors including DM, heart failure, and HTN [1, 17]. Furthermore, our study's high‐risk group was older and had higher rate of risk factors, including HTN, DM, and DLP, but this difference was not‐significant. In the study by Unikas et al., conditions such as DM, HTN, and smoking do not differ between complete and incomplete STR groups [14]. We observed that high‐risk patients, in contrast to other risk factors, had a lower rate of smoking than other groups, which was not statistically significant. Some studies have suggested the “smoker's paradox” as a reason for this observation, that smokers had better STR [18].

In our study, STR was a predictor of cardiac death, along with addition to the PPCI strategy. In this context, previous studies conducted in STEMI patients have shown that STR is an independent predictor of cardiac death [1, 2]. HORIZONS‐AMI ECG sub‐study reported that the degree of STR was an independent predictor of MACE, for mortality, after adjustment in multivariate analysis whereas it was not a predictor of mortality after adjustment in multivariate analysis [19]. Another study mentioned that ST depression resolution was an independent predictor of MACE, and patients with incomplete ST depression resolution had larger infarct sizes [20]. Wu et al. observed a significantly higher rate of MACE and cerebrovascular events and 2‐year all‐cause mortality in patients with STR < 50% than in those with STR to the ≥ 50% and complete STR groups [16]. APEX AMI (Assessment of Pexelizumab in Acute Myocardial Infarction) study reported that patients with STR < 50% had an increased risk of cardiac death and transfusion requirement [21]. In the study by Soner et al., incomplete STR was associated with in‐stent restenosis, in‐hospital, 1‐year, and 5‐year all‐cause mortality [22]. In the TOTAL trial, incomplete and no STR (STR < 70%) had an association with a higher increased rate of the composite outcome of cardiovascular death, recurrent MI, cardiogenic shock, or new or worsening NYHA class IV heart failure during 1‐year follow‐up [4].

In our study, the D2dt did not differ significantly among low‐, medium‐, and high‐risk groups. The ATLANTIC trial study noted that the D2dt was similar and brief in both complete and in‐complete STR groups; in contrast, symptom‐to‐treatment time differed between STR groups and was identified as one of the independent predictors [1, 14, 17, 18]. In the TOTAL trial, patients with incomplete or no STR have had longer times from symptom onset to certain end‐points [4]. Also, Dong et al. [6] observed that the STR < 40.15% group had significantly prolonged pain ‐to ‐balloon time.

We demonstrated that EF was higher in the low‐risk group than in the medium and high‐risk groups. Additionally, medium‐risk STR was a predicted of lower EF. STR reflected myocardial reperfusion rather than epicardial flow; therefore, STR was reduced with myocardial flow improvement after PPCI. On the other hand, EF is affected by myocardium function and blood supply. Then, we could use STR to predict EF after PPCI and estimate the probability of heart failure. In this line, another study found that LVEF was significantly reduced in patients with STR < 40.15% compared with those with STR > 40.15% [6]. Additionally, Bendary et al. demonstrated that complete STR was associated with improved EF and a reduced risk of heart failure [23]. Another study reported that STEMI patients with LAD as the culprit vessel had different EFs in complete versus in‐complete STR groups in STEMI patients with LAD as the culprit vessel. At the same time, in non‐LAD infarcts, there were no differences in FE between complete versus in‐complete STR groups [3, 24].

In our study, patients with LAD STEMI had less STR than the other vessels. Most people have a left‐dominant coronary blood supply, and the LAD supplies more areas of the myocardium than other coronary vessels. According to previous studies, STR reflects infarct size; therefore, we expected LAD STEMI to show less STR. Further studies have confirmed this observation [18], and Dizon et al. [24] noted that there may be a better collateral flow associated with this difference between LAD and non‐LAD infarcts. Another study showed that LAD was a predictor for STR < 50% [25]. Kim et al. demonstrated that patients with incomplete STR had mostly LAD infarcts [3]. Also, we observed that LAD STEMI was a predictor for lower EF.

In our study, the low‐risk STR group had a higher rate of TIMI 3 than other groups non‐significantly. In this line, in the TOTAL trial, incomplete or no STR groups had lower pre‐PCI and post‐PCI TIMI flow grades [4]. Also, Wu et al. showed that two‐thirds of patients with TIMI flow 0 to 2 had STR ≥ 50% and complete STR, and their 2‐year mortality rate was lower than that of the STR < 50% group. 8.9% versus 29.4% in the STR < 50% group. They observed that in TIMI 3, that STR ≥ 50% and complete STR were associated with lower 2‐year mortality compared to the STR < 50% group [16].

Our study had some limitations. To compare these methods, we could not calculate other STR methods, including sum STR and single lead STR, in addition to the max STE. We do not have the correct time of symptoms‐onset times for all patients to calculate symptoms ‐to ‐devise time accurately. Because some patients were older and did not answer correctly, some had DM, and their symptoms did not start with pain and the usual symptoms. Also, the PPCI strategy was left to the operator and was influenced by coronary complexity and operator experience.

Conclusion

This study is the first investigation of STR in bifurcation lesions undergoing PPCI. Previous studies reported STR in acute MI without a separate location of coronary artery occlusion. We observed that the PPCI strategy was as critical as STR for predicting cardiac death in bifurcation lesions. Additionally, STR was a predictor of lower EF and heart failure.
