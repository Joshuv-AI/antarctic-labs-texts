Meal Patterns, Food Consumption, and Weight Changes in Children Prone to Overeating
Authors: Catharina Sarkkola, Reetta Lehto, Sohvi Lommi, Satu Männistö, Heli Viljakainen
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractIntroductionOvereating – eating beyond nutritional needs or consuming large amounts quickly – is linked to weight gain. We studied whether self-reported (SR) or parent-reported (PR) overeating in children is cross-sectionally associated with differences in meal patterns and food consumption and longitudinally predicts changes in body mass index z score (BMIz) during adolescence.MethodsThis study included 5,827 children (mean age 11.2 years) from the Finnish Health in Teens cohort. At baseline, parents reported their child’s tendency to overeat, while children self-reported overeating, specific dietary factors, and physical activity. BMIz were calculated at baseline and after 2.7 years. Longitudinal associations were analysed using general linear models with backward stepwise model selection to identify the minimal adequate model.ResultsThe prevalence of SR overeating was 4.2%, PR overeating 9.3%, and their combination 0.8%. Irregular meal patterns and frequent unstructured snacking were more common among children with SR or both SR and PR overeating, and they consumed sugary products and fast food more frequently than children with only PR overeating or no overeating. Compared with children without overeating, the BMIz increase was 0.28 (95% CI 0.10–0.46) units higher for both SR and PR overeating, 0.15 (95% CI 0.07–0.23) units higher for SR overeating, and 0.08 (95% CI 0.03–0.14) units higher for PR overeating. The model was adjusted for sex, age, physical activity, and BMIz at baseline. Since meal patterns and food consumption were excluded during the modelling, they did not predict BMIz change.ConclusionSR overeating was a stronger predictor of BMIz increase than PR overeating, while having both was the most predictive. Interventions should focus on addressing overeating to aid in weight management during adolescence.

Introduction

Overeating is a behavioural risk factor for unhealthy weight gain in children [1]. It typically refers to the consumption of an unusually large amount of food in a short period of time [2]. Overeating can also be defined as an energy intake that exceeds a person’s energy expenditure but is not limited to a single eating episode [3]. In research, overeating in children is often self-reported by questionnaires. However, studies based on parental reports can offer a broader perspective [4–6]. There is a gap in knowledge regarding the extent to which these types of overeating reflect the same behaviour.

As reviewed recently, eating behaviours play an important role in shaping dietary intake in children [7]. Children with food-approach behaviours, such as high food responsiveness – defined as sensitivity to food cues and potentially contributing to overeating – tended to consume more fruits and berries, vegetables, snacks, meat, and white bread compared to those with low food responsiveness. In contrast, the results for food-avoidant behaviours were more inconsistent. For example, high satiety responsiveness – that is, sensitivity to fullness cues that helps prevent overeating – was associated with low overall food consumption but high daily snacking. Nonetheless, there remains a lack of studies specifically examining food consumption in children who experience overeating episodes as most research primarily focuses on more severe binge eating or loss of control eating [8, 9].

These macro-level eating behaviours contribute to excess energy intake and unhealthy weight development [10]. Several cross-sectional studies from different continents have shown that characteristics of overeating are linked to measures of adiposity, such as body mass index z score (BMIz) or BMI percentile, in children [11]. However, findings on changes in BMIz are more inconsistent. We recently demonstrated a higher and relatively stable BMIz trajectory between ages 8 and 16 years in those with parent-reported tendency towards overeating (PR overeating) compared with no PR overeating [12]. Likewise, a meta-analysis indicated a strong co-occurrence between binge eating and obesity: one in four children living with overweight or obesity reported binge eating [13]. Furthermore, high consumption of sugar-sweetened beverages and fast food, as well as irregular meal patterns, have been identified as risk factors for overweight and obesity in children [14–16].

Despite previous findings, there is a lack of studies on how self-reported (SR) or PR overeating in children relates to meal patterns, food consumption, and short-term weight changes. Therefore, we aimed to investigate, for the first time, whether the same children exhibit both SR and PR overeating and whether their meal patterns and food consumption differ from those without SR and/or PR overeating. We also examined how SR and PR overeating predict changes in BMIz between the average ages of 11 and 14 years and whether meal patterns or food consumption can predict BMIz changes in the entire sample and in children with SR or PR overeating.

Methods

Study Design and Participants

This study involved participants from the prospective Finnish Health in Teens (Fin-HIT) study, which encompasses Finland’s largest cities and their surroundings [17]. The cohort was recruited between 2011 and 2014, primarily through schools, and initially included 11,407 children aged 9–12 years, along with 6,046 parents, of whom 87% were mothers. In this study, we comprised a subsample of 5,827 children for whom data were available on SR and PR overeating. For the longitudinal analyses, we included 4,585 children with available BMIz data at two time points. We confirmed data on children’s age and sex through linkage to the National Population Information System at the Population Register Centre.

The study protocol was reviewed and approved by the Coordinating Ethics Committee of the Hospital District of Helsinki and Uusimaa (169/13/03/00/10). The children and one parent for each child provided their written informed consent.

Measurements

SR and PR Overeating

At baseline, the children answered a question on overeating: During the past year, how often have you eaten so much food in a short period of time that you would be embarrassed if others saw you? The response options were 1, never (n = 4,064; 69.7%); 2, a couple of times (n = 1,222; 21.0%); 3, less than once a month (n = 246; 4.2%); 4, 1–3 times a month (n = 166; 2.8%); 5, once a week (n = 66; 1.1%); and 6, more than once a week (n = 63; 1.1%). The children were categorized with overeating if they reported overeating episodes at least once a month (response options 4–6). This resulted in a dichotomous variable for SR overeating (overeating or no overeating). This question, along with the cut-off of once a month, was adapted from the Growing Up Today Study (GUTS, USA) and has been validated in the context of binge eating among 9–14-year-olds [18].

In addition, the parents answered a question on their child’s tendency towards overeating: Do you agree or disagree with this statement: “If I did not guide or regulate my child’s eating, s/he would eat too much.” Responses were scored on a five-point Likert scale: 1, agree (n = 153; 2.6%); 2, slightly agree (n = 436; 7.5%); 3, neither agree nor disagree (n = 234; 4.0%); 4, slightly disagree (n = 659; 11.3%); and 5, disagree (n = 4,345; 74.6%). We created a dichotomous variable for PR overeating by combining the first two response options (overeating) and the last three response options (no overeating). The question was adapted from a questionnaire in the Avon Longitudinal Study of Parents and Children (ALSPAC, UK) [19], a parent-administered questionnaire at 10.5 years. Based on the variables for SR and PR overeating, we constructed a combined variable with four groups: both SR and PR overeating, SR overeating only, PR overeating only, and no overeating.

Body Mass Index Z Score

We utilized anthropometric measurements from two time points in the Fin-HIT study: baseline and first follow-up. At baseline, trained fieldworkers measured the height and weight of children at school [17]. Twenty percent of the children participated from home and self-reported their measurements with adult assistance. These participants received detailed written instructions, a measuring tape, and were instructed to weigh themselves using a personal scale. At follow-up, all participants were asked to self-report their measurements in a similar manner at home. These self-reported measurements were proven valid for epidemiological studies [20]. Thirty-six percent (n = 2,096 out of 5,827) of the enrolled children did not participate in the follow-up. For 42% (n = 889 out of 2,096) of these children, we obtained measured height and weight data from the national Register of Primary Health Care visits (Avohilmo), maintained by the Finnish Institute for Health and Welfare [21]. This register includes growth measurements from health examinations conducted by school nurses. We used data collected during 2015–2016, corresponding to the time when the follow-up was conducted. Based on height and weight, we calculated BMIz according to the age- and sex-specific reference values by the International Obesity Task Force (IOTF) [22]. The change in BMIz from baseline to follow-up was determined by subtracting the baseline BMIz from the follow-up BMIz. Our previous study indicates that BMIz values from Fin-HIT and register sources within 6 months are consistent [12].

Dietary Factors

In the baseline questionnaire, children reported their typical meal patterns during school days, including breakfast, dinner, and unstructured snacking, characterized as grazing throughout the day. Responses were given on a six-option scale ranging from “rarely or never” to “5 days a week.” A regular breakfast or dinner pattern was defined as having the meal 5 days a week, while fewer days indicated irregular patterns. Frequent unstructured snacking was defined as occurring 5 days a week and infrequent snacking as less than 5 days.

The children also completed a food propensity questionnaire, in which they assessed their consumption frequency of 16 food items over the previous month on a seven-point scale, with ratings ranging from “not at all” to “several times a day.” This questionnaire was adapted from the World Health Organization’s Health Behaviour in School-Aged Children Study and was originally validated in Belgium and Italy [23, 24]. We computed the total frequency of weekly sugary product consumption, referred to as the sweet treat index, based on chocolate and sweets, biscuits and cookies, sweet pastries, ice cream, sugary juice drinks, and sugary soft drinks [25]. The index ranged from 0 to 84, with higher scores reflecting a higher consumption frequency. Similarly, a fast food index was calculated based on the items pizza, hamburger or hot dog, and salty snacks [12], while the plant consumption index was determined by fresh vegetables, cooked vegetables, and fruits or berries [26]. These two indexes ranged from 0 to 42.

Other Lifestyle Factors

The children reported additional lifestyle factors, including typical leisure-time physical activity (measured in hours per week) and screen time (which encompasses watching TV or videos, using a computer, playing TV games, etc.; recorded in hours per day during weekdays and weekend days) [27]. The weighted average of daily screen time was calculated by considering five weekdays and two weekend days. Lastly, the children reported their typical bedtimes and wake-up times for both weekdays and weekend days, which were used to calculate the weighted average of daily sleep hours. These variables were used as confounding factors.

Statistical Analyses

We compared the background and behavioural factors across the four overeating groups (both SR and PR overeating, SR overeating only, PR overeating only, no overeating) using the χ2 test for categorical variables. For normally distributed continuous variables, we reported means and standard deviations (SD) and employed analysis of variance. For non-normally distributed continuous variables, we presented medians and interquartile ranges and utilized the Kruskal-Wallis test. To examine the association between overeating and change in BMIz from baseline to follow-up, we applied a general linear model adjusted for sex and baseline age. In addition, we tested several other variables: follow-up time, baseline BMIz, breakfast pattern, dinner pattern, snacking frequency, sweet treat index, fast food index, plant consumption index, physical activity, screen time, and sleep duration. We used backward stepwise model selection until we arrived at the minimal adequate model, which included only the statistically significant variables. Thus, these remaining variables were considered predictive of change in BMIz. Similarly, we conducted subgroup analyses to evaluate the association between dietary factors and change in BMIz among children with SR overeating and those with PR overeating. Our approach was exploratory, which guided our variable selection and statistical modelling to identify associations. We set the level of statistical significance to p < 0.05. All statistical analyses were performed using the software package IBM SPSS Statistics version 29.0.

Results

At baseline, the mean age of the participants was 11.2 (SD 0.8) years, and the mean follow-up time was 2.7 (SD 0.9) years (Table 1). The proportion of children with SR overeating was 4.2%, PR overeating 9.3%, and both SR and PR overeating 0.8%. SR overeating was nearly equally prevalent in both sexes, while PR overeating was more common in boys. BMIz at baseline was higher in children with PR overeating and those with both SR and PR overeating compared with the other groups. The largest absolute change in BMIz from baseline to follow-up was observed in children with SR overeating.

Of all children, 83% and 85% ate breakfast and dinner, respectively, every school day (Table 1). The proportion was highest among children without overeating (83% and 86%), followed by children with PR overeating, SR overeating, and lowest among children exhibiting both eating behaviours (71% and 75%). The proportion of children who engaged in unstructured snacking every school day was highest among those with SR overeating and both SR and PR overeating. These children also consumed sugary products and fast food more frequently than children with PR overeating only, or no overeating. In contrast, the consumption frequency of vegetables, fruits, and berries did not differ across the four groups.

SR overeating was a stronger predictor of change in BMIz over the 2.7-year follow-up than PR overeating; however, having both was the strongest predictor (shown in Fig. 1). Compared with children without overeating, the increase in BMIz was 0.28 (95% confidence interval [CI] 0.10–0.46) units larger in children who exhibited both SR and PR overeating, 0.15 (95% CI 0.07–0.23) units larger in children with SR overeating, and 0.08 (95% CI 0.03–0.14) units larger in children with PR overeating. This model was adjusted for sex, age, physical activity, and BMIz at baseline. None of the dietary factors remained in the final model when examining the association between overeating and change in BMIz in the entire sample (Table 2). This was due to their lack of statistical significance, indicating that these factors did not predict a change in BMIz. Similarly, in children with either SR overeating or PR overeating, dietary factors showed no association with a change in BMIz.

Discussion

This is the first study to simultaneously examine both SR and PR overeating, along with their associations with meal patterns, food consumption, and changes in BMIz during the transition from childhood to adolescence. In this cohort study of approximately 5,000 Finnish children, both SR and PR overeating predicted an increase in BMIz between the average ages of 11 and 14 years. However, there were differences among the types of overeating: SR overeating was a stronger predictor than PR overeating, while the presence of both types together served as the strongest predictor of BMIz increase.

In our study, the proportion of children exhibiting both SR and PR overeating was only 0.8%, suggesting that these are distinct behaviours with limited overlap. We propose that PR overeating reflects parents’ general perceptions of their child’s tendency to overeat, while SR overeating refers to specific episodes of overeating that can occur in secret, often without parents’ awareness and is likely to contribute to more pathological health consequences than PR overeating. SR overeating may occur in the absence of hunger and extend beyond satiety. Thus, these represent two different types of overeating. The overall prevalence of SR overeating was 5.1%, with only minor differences between the sexes. Previous US studies have reported varying prevalence of SR overeating (with or without loss of control) across sexes: 0.9% of girls and 0.5% of boys aged 9–12 years [28]; 15.9% of girls and 9.9% of boys aged 12 years [2] and 15.6% of girls and 10.1% of boys aged 11–12 years [29]. However, these studies defined overeating as having occurred “ever” during the past year, whereas our study required at least one episode per month, which may complicate comparisons between studies. Our stricter definition captures more frequent overeating and excludes those with only occasional episodes. In our study, the overall prevalence of PR overeating was 10.1%, with 8.7% among girls and 11.6% among boys. In a Portuguese study involving 2,647 6–18-year-olds, the corresponding percentages for PR overeating were 22.4% among girls and 25.7% among boys [5]. Parents used a 3-point scale to report whether their child overeats, defining “sometimes” and “often” as indicators of overeating. Again, our study employed a stricter definition of overeating compared to the Portuguese study, but we observed similar sex differences, with overeating being more prevalent in boys than in girls.

Overeating is influenced by several factors, including emotional eating, which involves consuming food in response to negative emotions; external eating, which refers to eating due to external cues such as the sight or smell of food and social settings; and high food responsiveness, which encompasses eating in response to both hunger cues and external food cues [30–32]. Conversely, satiety responsiveness refers to sensitivity to fullness cues and helps prevent overeating. In our study, we interpret that SR overeating may be due to emotional eating or external eating, while PR overeating may arise from high food responsiveness, low satiety responsiveness, or a combination of both, without ruling out other factors.

Children with SR overeating, and those with both SR and PR overeating, were the most likely to skip regular breakfast and dinner, opting instead to snack frequently – likely compensating for missed main meals with energy-dense foods. In turn, these two groups reported the highest consumption frequency of sugary products and fast food. In contrast, children with PR overeating reported the lowest consumption frequency of sugary products. This may reflect parental or self-imposed restrictions on energy-dense foods, particularly for children living with overweight or obesity. A similar pattern was observed in our previous study, where a high consumption frequency of sugary products was associated with a lower risk of overweight [25].

Among 7-year-old Portuguese children, a higher number of daily snacks was associated with higher satiety responsiveness [33]. However, in that study, snacking was defined as all eating occasions outside the main meals of breakfast, lunch, and dinner, while we measured unstructured snacking beyond regular afternoon and evening snacks. Two Swedish cross-sectional studies on 10–13-year-olds reported a weak association between the consumption of sugar-sweetened soft drinks and sweets and emotional and external eating [34, 35]. In a Finnish study involving 6–8-year-olds, higher food responsiveness was associated with a higher consumption of fruits and berries, as measured by a 4-day food record [36]. This contradicts our findings as we observed no difference in the consumption frequency of fruits, berries, and vegetables between the overeating and no overeating groups. However, the dietary assessment methods used in the studies, the measures of overeating, and the age groups differed.

In our study, children with both SR and PR overeating had the highest mean BMIz at baseline, and children with PR overeating had the second highest. Their mean BMIz did not increase during the follow-up period as it did in children with SR overeating or no overeating. One might assume that those who easily gain weight in early childhood would continue to experience weight gain. However, we have previously demonstrated a high stable BMIz trajectory from ages 8–16 years among children with PR overeating [12], indicating that PR overeating stimulates weight gain in early childhood but BMIz stabilizes before adolescence. In the present study, we introduced a new aspect of SR overeating and aimed to demonstrate the independent association of both types of overeating with short-term changes in BMIz during adolescence, adjusting for baseline BMIz.

Previous studies on the characteristics of overeating and changes in BMIz show varying results. A Norwegian study found that high food responsiveness at age 6 was associated with an increase in BMIz during a 2-year follow-up [37], while a Chinese study indicated that low satiety responsiveness among 11–17-year-olds was linked to an increase in BMIz 1.5 years later but only in girls [38]. In contrast, other studies on food and satiety responsiveness conducted in Norway [39] and Portugal [40] found no such associations.

We were unable to identify any specific meal patterns or food consumption that would predict changes in BMIz, either in the entire sample or in the two subgroups of children with SR or PR overeating. This was the case despite differences in habitual meal patterns and food consumption between the groups. Our findings suggest that the tendency towards overeating itself – rather than the number of meals or type of food consumed – is the primary driver of weight gain. Previous research on meal patterns, food consumption, and overweight has focused on children in general, highlighting a gap in studies that specifically consider children with a tendency towards overeating. Several mainly cross-sectional studies from Europe and the USA have found that skipping meals is associated with an increased risk of overweight in children, although the definition of regular meal patterns varies across studies [16, 41]. Recent meta-analyses indicated that a higher intake of sugar-sweetened beverages and fast food was associated with higher BMI or higher odds of overweight, while the consumption of sweet pastries was linked to lower odds [14, 15]. However, the use of different methodologies complicates the comparison of these results with our findings. For instance, we used the sweet treat index, which measures consumption frequency – not total consumption – of both sugar-sweetened beverages and sweet pastries. Finally, given the exploratory nature of this study, it would be beneficial to confirm our findings in future hypothesis-driven studies.

Strengths and Limitations

The main strength of the Fin-HIT study lies in its large sample size, with school-aged children from urban and semi-urban areas of Finland. Additionally, we utilized longitudinal data collected at two time points. Most height and weight measurements were conducted by trained fieldworkers, and we enriched our dataset with objective register data from school healthcare checkups. The BMIz values derived from these different sources have been proven comparable [12, 20].

There are some limitations related to the questionnaire data. SR and PR overeating were measured using single items, not validated questionnaires. The question on SR overeating has been validated in the context of binge eating in the USA in a corresponding age group; however, we used only the first question, excluding the follow-up question regarding feelings of loss of control over eating (binge eating). Individuals with obesity may experience shame or receive negative comments about their eating, which could influence how they respond to this question. While the question regarding PR overeating is not validated, it can still serve as a simple and useful tool for monitoring eating behaviours. The question aims to capture the parent’s overall perception of the child’s overeating. However, it also includes a parental regulatory aspect, and “too much” food is not specifically defined in terms of amount, consumption frequency, or other aspects. Due to the differing angles of SR and PR overeating, they cannot be used to assess child-parent reporting concordance. Moreover, the food propensity questionnaire allowed us to assess only consumption frequencies, not the overall consumption amount of specific food items.

Self-report tools present certain challenges, but they are widely used and the most feasible in large-scale studies. Typically, children older than 8 years are able to reliably report their food intake [42]. For the 9–12-year-olds in our study, assessing the regularity of meals may be easier than accurately estimating hours spent on screens. Nevertheless, some random errors in self-reported meal patterns or screen time are possible and should be considered when interpreting the results. Screen time questions similar to those used in our study have demonstrated acceptable reliability among 11–12-year-olds in previous research [43–45]. Finally, all lifestyle factors were reported at baseline, so we cannot exclude the possibility that these behaviours may have changed during the follow-up period.

Conclusions

In this study, we demonstrated longitudinally that both SR and PR overeating predicted an increase in BMIz during adolescence. However, habitual meal patterns and food consumption varied depending on the type of overeating. It is important to assess overeating from both children’s and parents’ perspectives and to recognize the distinct behaviours, including the general tendency towards overeating and specific episodes of overeating. SR overeating may reflect broader behavioural or psychological patterns, such as eating-related shame. Understanding these differences in overeating behaviours and identifying individuals exhibiting them can help in designing targeted interventions that promote healthy dietary patterns and healthy weight gain during adolescence.
