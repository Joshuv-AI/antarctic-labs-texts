First Objective Evidence Characterizing Differences in Cervical and Thoracic Spinal Cord Neurophysiology Using ECAP-Controlled Closed-Loop Technology
Authors: Johnathan H. Goree, Harold Nijhuis, Gregory L. Smith, Erika A. Petersen, Jason E. Pope, Ajay B. Antony, Chau M. Vu, Dawood Sayed, Christopher M. Lam, Usman Latif, Shrif J. Costandi, Paul Verrills, Lalit Venkatesan, Weirong Ge, Ian Gould, Jan Willem Kallewaard
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
IntroductionSpinal cord stimulation (SCS) is a widely accepted therapy in patients with chronic intractable neuropathic pain in the trunk and limbs. However, open-loop (OL) SCS systems, which rely on fixed stimulation parameters and subjective feedback, face limitations in delivering consistent neural activation and durable pain relief. Anatomical and physiological characteristics of the cervical spinal cord, such as decreased cerebrospinal fluid thickness and increased mobility, exacerbate these challenges. Limited evidence exists on differences in cervical and thoracic neurophysiology, and the corresponding impact on neural activation in SCS. This post hoc analysis characterizes neurophysiological differences between the cervical and thoracic regions using evoked compound action potential (ECAP)-controlled closed-loop (CL) technology to assess implications for SCS dosing and therapy optimization.MethodsGlobal study and real-world chronic pain patients implanted with ECAP-controlled CL-SCS systems were included. To identify differences between cervical (n = 187) and thoracic (n = 1899) neurophysiology, the relationship between stimulation current and neural activation was analyzed. Additionally, neural activation stability was evaluated in both in-clinic and out-of-clinic settings.ResultsThe cervical spinal cord demonstrated significantly lower ECAP thresholds (p < 0.001) and > 100% higher spinal cord sensitivity compared to the thoracic region (p < 0.001). Cervical therapeutic dosing range was ≥ 48% narrower (p < 0.001), increasing the risk of overstimulation with OL-SCS. CL-SCS significantly improved dose accuracy in both regions (p < 0.001) during postural changes simulating activities of daily living. These findings highlight the superior precision and consistency in neural dosing with ECAP-controlled CL systems.ConclusionsThis is the first study to objectively characterize differences in cervical and thoracic spinal neurophysiology using SCS. ECAP-controlled CL-SCS maintains consistent neural activation in both cervical and thoracic regions. Given the heightened sensitivity and narrow dosing range in the cervical region, ECAP-controlled CL-SCS may enhance therapeutic outcomes through more precise and consistent neural dosing compared to OL systems.

Introduction

Spinal cord stimulation (SCS) is a well-established therapy for managing chronic intractable neuropathic pain of the trunk and limbs [1]. SCS efficacy largely depends on its ability to provide consistent and precise neural activation of the dorsal column [2, 3]. Traditional open-loop (OL) SCS systems, which constitute the majority of devices used in clinical practice to date, operate using fixed stimulation parameters [4–7] and rely heavily on subjective patient feedback [8]. OL-SCS includes both low-frequency (40–100 Hz) [4, 5] and high-frequency (1–10 kHz) [7, 9] approaches. OL-SCS devices require manual adjustments by the patient or clinician, often based on patient-reported paresthesia thresholds [8] or predetermined amplitudes [10]. As a result, adjustments depend on subjective patient input rather than objective, physiological feedback, which collectively limits their ability to provide precise and adaptive neural dosing.

Application of OL-SCS in the cervical spine presents unique challenges that could impact its therapeutic outcomes [11]. The cervical spine differs from the thoracic region in terms of anatomy [12], cerebrospinal fluid (CSF) dynamics [13–16], and spinal cord neurophysiology [16–18]. The cervical spinal cord has a higher density of white matter tracts and larger cross-sectional dimensions than the thoracic region [12, 19], making it more sensitive to stimulation parameter changes. Furthermore, the cervical CSF thickness is smaller and more variable as the neck region is more mobile [15–17, 20]. These factors can alter the electrical field generated by SCS leads due to postural changes, resulting in inconsistent neural activation [21–23]. In OL-SCS systems, these variations are not accounted for, and may lead to over- or under-stimulation, which may reduce therapeutic efficacy and patient satisfaction [21].

Furthermore, static paresthesia threshold guidance for programming and therapy optimization [8, 16] is inherently imprecise, as it depends on patient perception, which varies significantly due to individual differences in posture [24], neurophysiology [25], medication use [26, 27], and cognitive factors [28, 29]. Additionally, paresthesia-based programming does not provide real-time feedback on whether the stimulation is effectively activating the targeted neural pathways. In subthreshold stimulation approaches, where no paresthesia is perceived, the absence of sensory feedback makes it even more challenging to verify adequate neural activation and optimize therapy settings.

Recent longitudinal studies have underscored these limitations, revealing that patients treated with OL-SCS often experience diminishing pain relief over time and are at risk of device explantation due to loss of efficacy [30–32]. These findings suggest that inconsistent neural activation and lack of objective guidance for therapy adjustments may result in suboptimal long-term outcomes observed with OL-SCS.

Evoked compound action potential (ECAP)-controlled CL-SCS provides real-time, objective measurements of neural activation and automatically adjusts stimulation to maintain a consistent, prescribed neural dose [3, 33]. By eliminating the need for subjective input and accommodating the unique neuroanatomical and physiological characteristics of each patient, ECAP-controlled CL-SCS offers a personalized and adaptive approach to therapy. This technology has the potential to overcome the shortcomings of OL-SCS, particularly in the sensitive and dynamic environment of the cervical spinal cord.

This study aims to objectively characterize the differences in neurophysiology between the cervical and thoracic spinal cord using ECAP-controlled closed-loop SCS and to explore its implications for clinical practice.

Methods

The patient cohort used in this post hoc analysis comprises chronic pain patients receiving CL therapy using the Evoke® System, for whom SCS device data were available (n = 2086; January 2017 to June 2025; Saluda Medical, Minnetonka, MN, USA) across multiple ethics-approved clinical studies and real-world settings.

Data for this analysis were obtained from the following clinical studies: EVOKE leads were implanted in the cervical area in 187 patients (real-world: 159; ECAP: 17; BRIGHTON: 9; DURABILITY: 2), and 1899 patients (real-world: 1361; ECAP: 214; Neural Panel: 85; EVOKE: 59; DURABILITY: 21) had leads in the thoracic region.

The relationships between stimulation current and neural activation levels (activation plots [21, 34] and ECAP thresholds) were analyzed to evaluate differences in neurophysiological response between cervical and thoracic spinal regions. Ethical Approval

The study was designed, implemented and reported in accordance with the International Council for Harmonisation (ICH) Guidelines for Good Clinical Practice, with applicable local regulations, and with the ethical principles laid down in the Declaration of Helsinki. Data for this analysis were obtained from the following clinical studies that were granted ethical approval by the Ethics Committee and/or Institutional Review Board (IRB): EVOKE (NCT02924129), ECAP (NCT04319887), BRIGHTON (ACTRN12618001808235), DR (DR-UK: ISRCTN27710516; DR-NL: NL7889; DR-DE: NCT05272137), DURABILITY (NCT04627974), and Neural Panel (NCT06229470) studies. All patients provided written (signed) informed consent prior to participation in the study.

Statistical Analysis

Descriptive statistics, including mean, median, standard error of the mean (SEM), and interquartile range (IQR), were calculated for continuous variables. Non-parametric statistical tests, such as the Kruskal–Wallis test, were applied for non-normally distributed data. Statistical significance was assessed at the 5% level. Median differences and corresponding 95% confidence intervals were estimated using the bootstrap resampling method to account for non-normal data distributions. Device data processing and statistical analyses were performed using Python version 3.10.7 and SAS statistical software version 9.4 (SAS Institute).

These findings indicate that cervical SCS has a much smaller therapeutic dosing range, making it more prone to over- or under-stimulation when using OL-SCS, which lacks neural dosing feedback.

Dose Ratio

Dose ratio at perception threshold (determined by the current (mA) at perception threshold divided by the current (mA) at the ECAP threshold) was significantly lower for the cervical region [1.02 (IQR 0.86–1.17)] than for the thoracic area [1.08 (IQR 0.95–1.20)], with a median difference of 0.05 (95% CI 0.10–0.02, p < 0.001). Dose ratio was similar at maximum threshold [cervical 1.61 (IQR 1.46–1.85) vs. thoracic 1.63 (IQR 1.45–1.88)] with a median difference of 0.02 (95% CI 0.07–0.02, p = 0.64). Median dose ratio used for SCS therapy was significantly lower for the cervical region [1.20 (IQR 1.06–1.38)] than for the thoracic region [1.32 (IQR 1.15–1.47)], with a median difference of 0.11 (95% CI 0.15–0.07, p < 0.001).

Dose Accuracy

Out-of-Clinic Data

Out-of-clinic median dose accuracy (the deviation of the observed ECAP response from the target ECAP response) was more accurate for thoracic SCS [5.5 µV (IQR 2.8–8.4)] than for cervical SCS [7.1 µV (IQR 3.9–10.9)], with a median difference of 1.5 µV (95% CI 0.6–2.6 µV, p < 0.001).

In-Clinic Postural Evaluations

To further investigate the impact of OL versus CL therapy modes on dose accuracy, in-clinic data collected in the ECAP study were analyzed for cervical (n = 16) and thoracic (n = 202) patients for whom postural changes mimicking activities of daily living were collected with both OL-SCS and CL-SCS. Matched OL and CL postural evaluations were not conducted in the other studies and thus not reported. During cervical SCS, neural activation variability was significantly reduced (p < 0.001) with CL-SCS systems compared to OL-SCS. Neural activation deviation in the cervical region was < 4 µV for CL-SCS systems but exceeded 30 µV for OL-SCS systems (Fig. 4A). Thoracic dosing accuracy also demonstrated significant differences (p < 0.001), with CL-SCS systems achieving deviations of < 3 µV compared to > 21 µV for OL-SCS systems (Fig. 4B). Discussion

This study provides the first large-scale, objective characterization of neurophysiological differences between the cervical and thoracic spinal cord using evoked compound action potential (ECAP)-controlled closed-loop (CL) spinal cord stimulation (SCS). The findings reveal significant differences in neural activation thresholds, spinal cord sensitivity, and therapeutic dosing ranges, emphasizing the need for anatomically tailored SCS strategies.

Cervical stimulation required significantly lower current and charge to elicit ECAP responses compared to thoracic stimulation. This is consistent with known anatomical differences, including reduced cerebrospinal fluid (CSF) thickness [16], which likely enhances responsiveness because the electrodes are positioned closer to neural targets. The markedly higher sensitivity in the cervical region (114% for current and 173% for charge) further underscores the need for precise dosing, as small changes in stimulation can produce substantial shifts in neural activation.

Our analysis demonstrates that the cervical spinal cord has a significantly narrower therapeutic dosing range than the thoracic region, with reductions of 48% and 58% in current- and charge-based dosing, respectively. This narrow range increases the risk of over- or under-stimulation, particularly in open-loop (OL) systems that lack real-time feedback to cope with the changing electrode–cord distance during postural changes and other physiological effects such as breathing and heartbeat. These findings highlight the importance of CL-SCS for maintaining therapeutic efficacy [2, 3, 36] and minimizing adverse effects [2, 3, 36].

The difference between ECAP and perception thresholds was characterized using the dose ratio metric [3, 33–35], and it underscores the limitations of perception threshold-based, subjective SCS programming [8]. Perception thresholds are influenced by individual variability in neurophysiology [25], medication use [26, 27], and cognitive factors [28, 29], and tend to overestimate SCS dosing requirements, as evidenced by the higher dose ratios in both the cervical and thoracic region. ECAP thresholds provide an objective, quantifiable, and reproducible measure of dorsal column fiber activation, enabling precise estimation of neural dosing. The lower dose ratio utilized for SCS therapy in the cervical region (1.20 vs. 1.32 for thoracic region) suggests a reduced margin between threshold and therapeutic stimulation levels, reinforcing the need for precise and personalized dosing.

In-clinic postural evaluations simulating daily activities showed that CL-SCS systems significantly outperformed OL-SCS in both regions by maintaining consistent neural activation during positional changes. This dosing stability minimizes the risk of overstimulation or underdosing, enhancing both efficacy and patient comfort [2, 3]. Dosing precision resulting in consistent neural activation achieved with ECAP-controlled CL-SCS has been identified as a key factor in the superior and sustained improvements in pain relief, physical function, sleep quality, and mood reported over 36 months in the EVOKE randomized clinical trial [3].

Implications for Real-World Clinical Practice

The ability to prescribe the neural dose based on individual spinal cord sensitivity positions ECAP-controlled CL-SCS as a personalized approach to neuromodulation. By accounting for patient-specific neurophysiology, these systems offer a more reliable and reproducible treatment experience, ensuring optimal therapeutic benefit.

Incorporating objective neurophysiological data into SCS programming has transformative implications for clinical practice. First, it enables evidence-based decision-making [33, 34], moving beyond the subjective to an objective biomarker of neural activation. This study demonstrates target-level differences in neural activation, spinal cord sensitivity, and dosing ranges, marking a shift from empirical to precision-guided therapy.

Second, the availability of objective neural metrics (i.e. ECAP threshold, spinal cord sensitivity, dose ratio, dose accuracy) creates new opportunities for therapy optimization and long-term care. The established dose–response relationship in SCS [34] underscores the importance of precise dosing for durable pain relief. The neurophysiological metrics and parameters described in this article also have broad-reaching practical implications, as this knowledge can help guide initial cervical stimulation programing parameters knowing that differences exist in this anatomic region.

By tracking ECAP responses over time, clinicians can monitor changes in spinal cord sensitivity, assess therapy adherence and its durability, and make proactive adjustments to maintain efficacy. This level of precision and adaptability has the potential to improve patient outcomes, reduce costs associated with reprogramming or device explantation, and set a new standard for patient-centered care.

Limitations

While this study provides valuable insights into the differences in cervical and thoracic neurophysiology, the limitations should be acknowledged. First, the post hoc nature of the analysis introduces potential biases related to consistency and variability in clinical practice across sites. Although the dataset is large and multicenter, differences in patient selection and lead placement techniques may influence outcomes. Second, comparing cervical and thoracic cohorts in the closed-loop SCS group without randomization may yield outcome differences confounded by factors like demographics, medication use, or pain conditions. Due to the real-world nature of the majority of the dataset, these covariates were not consistently available for all patients, and statistical adjustment (e.g., matching or weighting) was not feasible. As a result, the observed differences should be interpreted with caution, and future studies with more comprehensive data collection are warranted to better control for potential confounding. Third, data were pooled from multiple primary studies, and we did not adjust for “study” as a source of variation in the analysis. Although neurophysiological data were collected using standardized device-based methods, unmeasured differences between studies could contribute to outcome variability. Future analyses should consider including “study” as a factor to account for potential clustering and heterogeneity. Fourth, matched postural evaluations comparing OL and CL therapy were only available from a subset of patients (ECAP study), potentially limiting the applicability of posture-related dose accuracy findings to broader patient populations.

Conclusions and Future Directions

This study highlights the unique neurophysiological challenges associated with cervical SCS and the limitations of OL systems in managing the region’s heightened sensitivity and narrow therapeutic dosing range. ECAP-controlled CL-SCS offers a robust solution by delivering consistent, personalized neural activation through real-time feedback. This approach enhances dosing accuracy and therapy stability, addressing the anatomical and physiological complexities inherent to the spine.

By integrating objective neurophysiological data into therapy delivery, ECAP-controlled systems represent a paradigm shift in SCS by transforming treatment from subjective and reactive to data-driven and proactive.
