Parental perspectives and willingness towards digital paediatric research: a cross-sectional survey in two Chinese cities
Authors: Nairong Guo, Heping Fang, Xueling Li, Ruolin Xian, Yan Zhao, Yan Hu, Ying-Cun Li
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractObjectiveThis study aims to explore disparities in eHealth literacy and perspectives on digital health among parents of preschool children from different cities and examine how these characteristics influence their willingness to participate in digital paediatric clinical research.DesignCross-sectional online survey.SettingConducted between November and December 2023 among parents of preschool children in Yongchuan District (Chongqing) and Shunyi District (Beijing), China.ParticipantsParents of preschool children (aged 3–6 years). Among 1340 unique survey visits, 1274 started the questionnaire (response rate: 95.1%). Ultimately, 621 (321 from Yongchuan and 300 from Shunyi) were included in the final analysis.Primary and secondary outcome measuresThe primary outcome was parents’ willingness to participate in paediatric clinical research. Secondary measures included eHealth literacy (assessed by the Chinese version of the eHealth Literacy Scale), conceptual understanding of digital health, behavioural inclinations toward digital tool use and concerns regarding digital research.ResultsOf the respondents, 36.9% expressed willingness to participate in paediatric clinical research, with a significantly higher proportion in Yongchuan compared with Shunyi (45.5% vs 27.7%, p<0.001). Yongchuan parents reported higher adequate eHealth literacy (59.8% vs 44.7%, p<0.001), better digital health understanding (25.9% vs 18.7%) and higher willingness to use digital tools for daily data collection (65.1% vs 54.3%) (both p=0.02). Regarding smartphone-related concerns, parents in Yongchuan were more likely to express concerns about ‘Health impacts of the tools (such as radiation)’ (45.8% vs 38.0%, p=0.049) and ‘Possible expenses’ (38.0% vs 29.7%, p=0.03). Logistic regression analysis, after adjusting for confounding factors, identified ‘Support digital tools in paediatric research’ as a significant predictor of higher parental willingness (OR=2.21, 95% CI 1.48 to 3.26, p<0.001). Additionally, the willingness of parents from Yongchuan was more influenced by their digital device usage behaviours compared with those in Shunyi.ConclusionParental eHealth literacy and digital health perspectives significantly influence willingness to participate in paediatric research.

Introduction

Recruitment difficulties and high attrition rates are pervasive in paediatric clinical research.13 Parental perspectives, modulated by factors such as age, education and income,46 largely determine clinical research participation and protocol adherence, often overriding children’s preferences.7 This challenge intensifies with the advent of the Holistic Health Era, which expands research focus to include children in suboptimal health or healthy cohorts.8 However, most healthy children and their parents are unfamiliar with clinical research and have no inherent motivation to participate in clinical trials. This unfamiliarity with clinical research among these populations complicates recruitment, particularly as digital tools9 (eg, smartphones, wearables) emerge as dual-edged solutions: while enabling innovative recruitment and remote monitoring,10 11 their adoption may exacerbate health disparities due to unequal access to digital infrastructure in resource-limited settings.1215

Despite broad recognition of digital technologies’ role in health research advancement,16 17 parental perspectives on digital health-related paediatric studies remain underexplored. Our previous work in Chongqing revealed higher parental acceptance in suburban vs urban districts,18 yet this finding, when considered alongside unassessed variables such as eHealth literacy, underscores the need to compare digital participation attitudes between distinct socioeconomic contexts. This knowledge gap limits understanding of how localised socioeconomic and technological factors associate with recruitment disparities.

Accordingly, this cross-sectional study evaluated eHealth literacy, conceptual understanding of digital health, and tool-use inclinations among parents of preschool children in Chongqing and Beijing via an online questionnaire, two regions selected as contrasting socioeconomic prototypes. Through this comparison, we aim to identify associations between regional socioeconomic disparities, parental factors, and participation attitudes, thereby generating preliminary evidence to inform regionally tailored strategies for equitable recruitment in paediatric studies.

Methods

Study design

This cross-sectional study was conducted between November and December 2023 to investigate parental perspectives on digital health in two distinct urban Chinese contexts. The sites were selected to reflect broader regional disparities: Beijing, the national capital, represents a highly developed northern metropolis with advanced healthcare digitalisation, while Chongqing exemplifies the rapid technological transition characteristic of inland China.19 Within these two municipalities, Shunyi District (Beijing) and Yongchuan District (Chongqing) were chosen as the specific study settings. Both districts function as non-core secondary economic hubs with populations exceeding one million, offering a comparable urban baseline. Despite these similarities, they exhibit marked socioeconomic gradients that mirror the broader developmental gap between their respective regions. For example, Shunyi reports a higher per capita income (¥51 237 vs ¥43 276) and greater physician density (3.5 vs 2.73 per 1000 residents) than Yongchuan.20 21 We used the Strengthening the Reporting of Observational Studies in Epidemiology (STROBE) reporting checklist when editing, included in online supplemental materials.22

Recruitment and participants

Participants were recruited through a school-based approach. Survey links were distributed to local kindergartens and then forwarded to caregivers by class teachers; participation in sharing the links was entirely voluntary. After data collection was completed, the research team downloaded the dataset from the cloud server for secure local storage and subsequently deleted it from the cloud to protect participant privacy.

The eligibility of participants was defined as follows. Inclusion criteria were: (1) residence in Yongchuan or Shunyi; (2) having a child aged 3–6 years; (3) kindergarten attendance; (4) questionnaire submission within the designated timeframe (Shunyi: 25 November–30 November 2023; Yongchuan: 20 December–25 December 2023); (5) voluntary completion by a parent. Exclusion criteria were: (1) failure to pass verification questions; (2) self-report of inattentive responding and (3) completion of the questionnaire by a relative other than the parent.

Participants’ eHealth literacy levels were assessed using the validated Chinese version of the eHealth Literacy Scale (SC-eHEALS), as reported by Xu et al.25 26 This 8-item instrument employs a 5-point Likert scale (1=‘strongly disagree’ to 5=‘strongly agree’) to measure self-perceived ability to locate, evaluate and apply health information via digital technologies. In this study, the scale demonstrated excellent internal consistency (Cronbach’s α=0.921). Exploratory factor analysis confirmed a unidimensional structure (Kaiser–Meyer-Olkin measure=0.915, χ²=3379.14, p<0.001). The SC-eHEALS score was calculated as the sum of all eight items, with higher scores indicating greater literacy. Parents with a total score>29 (range 8–40) were classified as ‘Possessing adequate eHealth literacy’.27

Sample size determination

Sample size estimation followed methods used in previous quality-of-life research,18 which recommended 5–10 responses per core item (25 items total). Accounting for potential selection bias and logistical considerations, a minimum sample size of 500 participants was required. Given historically low response rates (approximately 50%) in public online surveys,28 the final recruitment target was set at approximately 1000 households to ensure adequate statistical power for analysis. All collected questionnaires were screened according to the inclusion and exclusion criteria. 1340 online unique visits were documented during the survey. Of these, 1274 started the questionnaire (response rate of 95.1%), and 1119 completed it (completion rate of 83.5%). Following the exclusion of unreliable or logically inconsistent responses, 878 questionnaires were considered valid (valid rate: 878/1119 = 78.5%). This step reflects the proportion of completed questionnaires that were answered seriously and consistently, serving as a quality filter rather than a CHERRIES-defined response metric. (A detailed Checklist for Reporting Results of Internet E-Surveys (CHERRIES) is provided in the online supplemental materials.) Among the 878 valid questionnaires, 621 (70.7%) met all eligibility criteria and were included in the final analysis. This corresponds to an overall inclusion rate of 46.3% (621/1340) of unique survey visits. A flow chart of the participant selection process is presented in figure 1.

Data processing

Statistical analyses were performed using R software V.4.5.1. Categorical variables are presented as frequencies and percentages, and continuous variables as medians with IQRs following normality testing. Group comparisons were performed using χ² tests or Fisher’s exact tests for categorical variables and Mann-Whitney U tests for continuous variables. These comparisons examined differences between the two geographical regions, as well as between parents who expressed willingness to participate and those who expressed hesitation. To examine regional disparities in eHealth literacy and perspectives on digital health, variables with p<0.05 in univariate analyses were entered into binary logistic regression models, adjusting for significant demographic confounders. A binary logistic regression model was used to identify factors associated with the primary outcome—parents’ willingness to participate in paediatric clinical research. Adjustments were made for demographic characteristics including region, child’s age and sex, parental age and education, and primary caregiver. Furthermore, subgroup analyses stratified by region were conducted to explore distinct regional patterns in the factors associated with participation willingness. All statistical tests were two-sided, and p<0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Patient and public involvement

Patients or the public were not involved in the design, or conduct, or reporting, or dissemination plans of our research.

General characteristics

Most respondents were mothers (90.3%). The median age of the primary caregivers was 33 years (IQR 30–36), and 61.5% held a bachelor’s degree or higher. Of the children, 50.2% were male, with a median age of 4.7 years (IQR 4.0–5.6). Only 8.2% of children had previously participated in medical research, and 7.1% had participated in research recruited online.

Demographic differences were observed between the two districts. Parents from Shunyi were older (median age 34 vs 32 years, p<0.001) and more highly educated (bachelor’s degree or higher: 75.7% vs 48.3%, p<0.001). In contrast, they were less likely to report initial participation willingness when invited to a medical study for their children (27.7% vs 45.5%, p<0.001). Further supporting this pattern, children in Shunyi also participated less frequently in online-recruited medical research than those in Yongchuan (4.3% vs 9.7%, p=0.01).

Factors associated with willingness to participate

Univariate analysis identified region and parental education level as the primary demographic factors associated parents’ willingness to participate in paediatric research, with region showing the strongest association. Yongchuan parents had significantly higher willingness to participate (OR=1.88, 95% CI 1.30 to 2.74, p=0.006). After adjusting for demographic confounders, the perspective most strongly associated with parental willingness was ‘Support digital tools in paediatric research’ (adjusted OR=2.21, 95% CI 1.48 to 3.26, p<0.001). Additionally, previous participation in online-recruited paediatric research also showed a non-significant positive association with willingness (OR=2.09, 95% CI 0.97 to 4.48, p=0.059) (table 7).

Discussion

This study investigated the disparities in parental eHealth literacy and perspectives on digital health between Shunyi District (Beijing, northern core-developed region) and Yongchuan (Chongqing western rapidly advancing region), China. We further analysed how these factors associate with parental willingness in digital-based paediatric research. The results demonstrated significant regional differences in participation willingness, associated with variations in eHealth literacy and digital health perspectives among parents of preschool children.

The distinction between functional proficiency and conceptual understanding emerged as a key finding: 52.5% of parents possessed adequate eHealth literacy (mean SC-eHEALS score was 28.83, comparable to those reported in previous studies,29 suggesting a moderate overall level. However, only 22.4% reported a clear understanding of digital health. This highlights an important conceptual difference: eHealth literacy is a functional, skill-based construct centred on the ability to access, evaluate and apply online health information, whereas digital health understanding reflects a conceptual and cognitive awareness of the ‘digital health’ paradigm itself.30 31 Because our data were collected through an online survey, the sample likely represents parents with higher eHealth literacy and greater participation willingness than the general population. The actual levels in the broader population are therefore likely lower than reported, suggesting an even wider gap between current capabilities and the conceptual understanding needed for meaningful engagement with digital health.

Our findings suggest that while parents may develop technical competence through daily device use,32 they often lack a comprehensive conceptual knowledge. This necessitates targeted public education transcending skill acquisition to foster systemic awareness and effective health improvement.33 For instance, researchers could leverage established trust networks,34 such as kindergartens and community health centres, to distribute educational brochures or integrate informational sessions into routine health check-ups. Furthermore, streamlined digital content via social media may better align with the information-seeking habits of modern parents,30 35 thereby enhancing research engagement.

Consistent with this limited conceptual grasp, further analysis revealed relatively limited parental support for applying digital tools to paediatric clinical research or daily health management (eg, only 36.4% actively used smart devices for health management). Several factors may explain this. First, the low baseline medical demand among families with generally healthy children reduced perceived relevance of digital tools. Second, digital health behaviours in the surveyed population remained at an early adoption stage compared with international reports.12 Third, scarce exposure to existing digital paediatric studies hindered familiarity. Notably, parents more willing to participate demonstrated stronger conceptual understanding of digital health and superior functional eHealth literacy, suggesting that improving public awareness could enhance research engagement among general paediatric populations.

Despite having lower education levels and belonging to a region with a relatively lower economic gradient, Yongchuan parents demonstrated significantly better performance in functional eHealth literacy and digital health behaviours. This contrasts with previous studies reporting lower eHealth literacy among older or lower-income populations.34 36 This reversal manifested in two key dimensions: First, Yongchuan parents achieved significantly higher eHEALS scores than Shunyi parents (30.13 vs 27.43, p<0.001), with the highest scores for actively using online information (Q4, Q5), suggesting stronger operational skills. In contrast, Shunyi parents scored lowest on distinguishing information quality (Q7) and making health decisions based on it (Q8), reflecting greater scepticism about online information credibility. Second, while Yongchuan parents reported better self-reported understanding of digital health (25.9% vs 18.7%, p=0.02), adjusted multivariate analysis revealed their sole significant behavioural difference was willingness to use digital tools for daily data collection. Importantly, these Yongchuan parents also showed higher willingness to participate in research if they reported ‘Using smartphones or other devices for health management’ and ‘Willing to use digital tools for daily data collection’ (ORs 1.91 and 1.84, respectively). In Yongchuan, limited offline resources might necessitate reliance on digital tools for child health management, and frequent practical use may improve skills while fostering research acceptance; conversely, Shunyi parents’ better offline care access might reduce digital dependency while increasing scepticism, leading to lower participation.

When considering concerns about using smartphones for digital paediatric research, among all respondents, concerns about ‘Personal privacy issues’ (77.5%) and children’s ‘Possible addiction to the tools’ (57.3%) emerged as key barriers. Parents from both regions primarily worried about ‘Personal privacy issues’. In addition, Yongchuan parents expressed significantly greater concern about ‘Possible expenses’ and ‘Health impacts of the tools (such as radiation)’. Crucially, parental willingness was independently associated with support for digital tools specifically in the context of paediatric research, rather than broader technological acceptance. This indicates that parental hesitation stems from prudent considerations regarding children’s well-being and safety rather than a resistance to the technology itself. These findings imply that addressing financial burdens and safety perceptions is vital for less developed regions. Future research should employ qualitative methods to further dissect these nuanced concerns, such as focus groups or in-depth interviews. Beyond addressing specific concerns, our findings highlight the necessity of establishing robust data governance frameworks and ethical design principles in paediatric digital research.33 Building parental trust requires more than technical security; it demands transparent consent mechanisms and clear communication about risk mitigation. Such frameworks are essential for ensuring that digital health research aligns with international ethical standards and gains broader social acceptance.

Several methodological limitations should be noted. First, the cross-sectional design and absence of children’s clinical health data preclude causal inference and analysis of disease-specific motivations. Second, parental eHealth literacy and perspectives were assessed using self-reported measures, which lack objective validation from performance-based assessments. Third, the online survey format and exclusion criteria likely introduced selection bias, resulting in a sample with higher eHealth literacy and greater participation willingness than the general population. Finally, although this study provides a foundational comparison between two secondary economic centres, the findings should be generalised cautiously. Future multi-regional studies are needed to validate and extend.

Conclusions

Parental eHealth literacy and perspectives on digital health influence their willingness to participate in paediatric research. Diverging from previous technical-oriented studies, this research emphasises parental psychological and behavioural viewpoints. The findings reveal substantial disparities across diverse geographical and resource contexts, highlighting the necessity of tailoring study protocols to regional characteristics. To enhance public engagement in digital paediatric clinical research, it is essential to strengthen parental perspectives, ensure robust information security, and implement region-specific strategies.
