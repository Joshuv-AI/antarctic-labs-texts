Vincristine in FSGS: Rediscovering an Old Therapeutic for FSGS
Authors: Ashwani Kumar, Pallavi Prasad, Madhav C Menon
Published: 2025

See Translational Research on Page 3998

Focal segmental glomerulosclerosis (FSGS) is one of the most common causes of nephrotic syndrome in adults and children.1 Its incidence is increasing from approximately 3.2 per 100,000 person-years in 2004 to 2013, to an almost 40% increase from the previous decades.2 Risk of end-stage kidney disease with FSGS is higher than that in other primary glomerular diseases (membranous nephropathy or IgA nephropathy) with nearly 50% of patients with FSGS progressing to end-stage kidney disease in 5 to 10 years.3 FSGS is a histopathological pattern of injury encompassing diseases of different etiology—primary or “idiopathic” FSGS, genetic FSGS, secondary FSGS, or FSGS of undetermined cause.1 The critical pathogenetic components of FSGS are podocyte cytoskeletal injury, podocyte loss, glomerular destabilization, and sclerosis. Treatment modalities in FSGS vary depending on the clinical presentation and underlying cause, although the main goal is to stabilize or rescue podocyte injury and mitigate proteinuria. Broadly, immunosuppression is the first-line modality in patients presenting with primary nonfamilial FSGS, whereas treatment of underlying etiology, and glomerulo-protective therapy to reduce hyperfiltration stress (e.g., use of renin-angiotensin system blockers) is the mainstay in secondary FSGS. Several monogenic FSGS syndromes identified point to the critical role of podocyte cytoskeletal proteins in etiopathogenesis of FSGS.4 Indeed, in some of these familial cases, immunosuppression has shown some value.5 Recent discovery of antinephrin antibodies correlating with primary6 and posttransplant recurrence of FSGS7 has stressed the role of immunological events in the pathogenetic chain of FSGS.

In this issue of KI Reports, Mason et al.8 conducted a classical bedside-to-bench work, longitudinally studying a patient with steroid-resistant nephrotic syndrome from childhood into adulthood (which in itself is illustrative of the progressive nature of FSGS). Using this index case, the researchers investigated the specific therapeutic role of vincristine (VC) in the management of this index case and more generally in FSGS. To explore the underlying mechanisms, they used patient sera during periods of relapse as well as VC treatment and remission, and simultaneously evaluated model systems including immortalized human podocytes to study cytoskeleton, and a glomerulus-on-a-chip (GOAC) platform; that is, a microfluidic system that recapitulates key features of the glomerular filtration barrier, to study functional consequences of observed cytoskeletal changes.

To first detect podocyte transcriptional changes, they performed RNA sequencing on immortalized human podocytes exposed to patient sera collected before and after VC treatment, as well as during remission and relapse phases. This approach was designed to evaluate favorable VC-associated transcriptional changes in podocytes, which the authors hypothesized would overlap with those induced by sera during remission. These analyses suggested significant alterations in genes related to cytoskeletal structure and dynamics following exposure to VC, and similar to remission sera. These transcriptomic findings were then corroborated at the protein level where immunofluorescence assays demonstrated that cortical F-actin network was preserved with VC and dispersed by affected sera. To test the functional relevance of these cytoskeletal changes, they assessed the effects of VC by supplementing patient sera (from 4 individuals with FSGS) with low and high concentrations of VC and measuring albumin permeability in the GOAC, an ex vivo system incorporating podocytes and endothelial cells on a collagenous basement membrane to model the glomerular filtration barrier. These experiments demonstrated that VC could improve altered glomerular permeability to albumin in a dose-dependent manner in this model. Together, this series of experiments suggested that VC may exert direct protective structural effects on the podocyte cytoskeleton, counteracting changes induced by FSGS sera, which could be relevant to its observed therapeutic role in the index patient, as well as in a literature review of VC use in FSGS that the authors included. To assess if potentially pathogenic IgG were the source of the cytoskeletal disruption induced by the index patient’s sera, they performed IgG depletion studies, which concluded that sera without IgG induced pathogenic cytoskeletal changes. These experimental findings and the summarized long-term trajectory of responses of the index patient show evidence of therapeutic benefit for VC and provide a putative a mechanism for this observation with some generalizability to other patients with FSGS (summarized in Figure 1).

A summary of the challenges and opportunities in FSGS therapeutics. Vincristine, a microtubule polymerization inhibitor, has had reported anecdotal therapeutic roles in FSGS. The current manuscript by Mason et al.,8 suggests that a direct and functionally favorable perturbation of podocyte cytoskeleton may underlie the observed benefit of vincristine in FSGS. This interesting study has many implications. First, VC is a vinca alkaloid in clinical use for several decades which inhibits mitosis by disrupting microtubule polymerization,9,S1 and it is not entirely surprising that an effect on podocyte actin cytoskeleton was observed because microtubule proteins are known to interact with the actin cytoskeleton in nonmuscle cells.S2,S3

In addition, it is consistent of the previous observation that VC had a direct protective role in adriamycin-induced nephropathy, mediated through modulation of α3β1 integrin and focal adhesion kinase overexpression,S4 pathways which were however not tested in the current work. It is important to note that this study involved human sera, which likely contains additional circulating factors that could cause broader perturbations of podocyte injury pathways than those reported in adriamycin injury. Next, although the authors did not find any pathogenic genetic abnormalities in podocyte cytoskeletal genes in the index patient treated with VC, sera from a patient with a homozygous nephrin (NPHS1) mutation was tested. Adding VC decreased the FITC-albumin leakage into the glomerular space in the GOAC in this patient. Additional data from VC use in genetic FSGS would be particularly interesting, especially with NPHS1 mutations, because current guidelines do not recommend immunosuppression in genetic FSGS. In addition, as exemplified by one of the cases in the series, VC induced benefit in GOAC experiments in spite of use of other immunosuppressants, suggesting a potential additive role for VC even in combination with other agents. From a pathogenetic perspective, given the recent role of anti-NPHS1 IgG antibodies in primary FSGS6 and the demonstration of plasmapheresis-related depletion of such antibodies inducing remission in posttransplantation cases,S5 it is intriguing that the authors did not observe any changes in GOAC permeability before or after IgG depletion. This raises a potential and distinct role for non-IgG sources of podocyte injury in primary FSGS, in addition to the role of antinephrin. Finally, it must be noted that VC has well-known neurologic and myelotoxic side effects, which ultimately will need to be considered before expanding its use for FSGS.

In summary, despite being a study on a single patient, this work is commendable for its prolonged follow-up, careful bedside-to-bench experiments which combined functional studies on the GOAC, together supporting an adjuvant or primary role for VC in FSGS therapy, including in particular genetic FSGS cases. Nevertheless, further clinical and experimental studies are needed to identify the optimal scenarios for VC use in FSGS to maximize benefit and minimize potential side effects.
