Proteomic Profile After Intervention With Eplerenone Among Persons With HIV
Authors: Anirudh Murthy, Márton Kolossváry, Allie R Walpert, Eva Sanchez, Shady Abohashem, Ahmed Tawakol, Gail K Adler, Steven K Grinspoon, Suman Srinivasa
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundPersons with human immunodeficiency virus (HIV) well-treated on antiretrovirals have increased arterial inflammation, which can lead to development of atherosclerotic disease. We have previously shown that treatment with eplerenone can significantly reduce arterial inflammation, as measured by target-to-background ratio (TBR) on cardiac 18F-fluorodeoxyglucose positron emission tomography/computed tomography, among people with HIV. The mechanisms by which eplerenone may have treatment effects remain unclear.MethodsWe performed a targeted discovery-based approach to identify a proteomics signature associated with eplerenone treatment that may provide a plausible mechanism for the reduction in arterial inflammation. In an exploratory study, we leveraged analyzable samples from participants who completed the 12-month, placebo-controlled, randomized controlled trial MIRABELLA HIV to evaluate 276 proteins (Olink Target 96 Cardiometabolic, Cardiovascular II, Cardiovascular III), with relevance to cardiovascular and cardiometabolic disease.ResultsWe identified 11 proteins that differed in expression between treatment groups. Eight proteins (CDH1, CES1, ADM, IL-4RA, FGF-21, FS, FABP2, Gal-4) decreased and 2 proteins increased (CSTB, MPO) in expression with eplerenone compared to placebo. An increase in expression of IL-27 was prevented among eplerenone-treated versus placebo-treated groups. Changes in TBR of the most diseased segment of the index vessel correlated with changes in 3 of these proteins: CDH1 (ρ = 0.53, P = .02), FGF-21 (ρ = 0.64, P = .003), and Gal-4 (ρ = 0.58, P = .009).ConclusionsThrough this proteomics approach, we discovered that 3 key proteins, CDH1, FGF-21, and Gal-4, are decreased in parallel with reductions in arterial inflammation after treatment with eplerenone. Eplerenone-induced reductions in these proteins, known to be related to inflammation, epithelial barrier disruption, vascular dysfunction, and metabolic dysregulation, provide mechanistic insight into pathways by which eplerenone may improve cardiovascular disease in HIV.Clinical Trials RegistrationNCT02740179.Keywords: arterial inflammation, CDH1, eplerenone, HIV, proteomics

Despite the advent and widespread administration of antiretroviral therapy (ART), people with human immunodeficiency virus (HIV-1) remain at elevated risk for cardiovascular disease (CVD). Epidemiologic studies indicate that people with HIV (PWH) experience approximately 1.5–2.0 times greater rates of myocardial infarction compared to persons without HIV (PWoH), even after controlling for traditional risk factors [1, 2]. Early and continuous ART partly mitigates this risk, but a residual burden of CVD persists, and optimal strategies to predict and prevent HIV-associated CVD continue to be investigated [3]. As life expectancy has increased for PWH, CVD has emerged as a major cause of morbidity in this population, underscoring the need to understand unique drivers and effective interventions [4].

The mechanisms underlying heightened CVD risk in PWH are multifactorial. Traditional CVD risk factors, such as tobacco use, dyslipidemia, and hypertension, occur at high rates among PWH, but CVD risk remains high even after controlling for these variables [1]. Chronic immune activation persists in PWH despite virologic suppression in the setting of microbial translocation due to altered gut permeability or viral coinfection, leading to elevated levels of inflammatory mediators that promote atherosclerosis [5]. In addition, HIV is associated with metabolic disturbances, such as dyslipidemia, insulin resistance, and altered body composition (including excess adiposity and decreased lean muscle mass), some of which may be further exacerbated by certain ART regimens [6]. Notably, increases in visceral adipose tissue (VAT) have been observed among well-treated PWH and can be linked to greater atherosclerotic plaque burden [7, 8]. This combination of persistent inflammation and excess visceral fat may synergistically contribute to CVD development in PWH beyond traditional risk factors [9].

One pathway of interest in HIV-associated CVD is the renin–angiotensin–aldosterone system (RAAS). RAAS overactivation is known to promote hypertension, vascular injury, and cardiac remodeling [10–12]. We have previously demonstrated that there is excess RAAS activation among PWH when compared to PWoH. In particular, PWH with increased visceral adiposity have demonstrated higher aldosterone levels than PWoH [13]. Additionally, aldosterone levels were found to be an independent predictor of insulin resistance in PWH, supporting a link between excess RAAS activity and cardiometabolic dysfunction in this population [13]. Furthermore, RAAS activation in PWH has been associated with heightened inflammation and metabolic abnormalities [13–15]. These observations may implicate RAAS signaling in the pathogenesis of HIV-related CVD and suggest that targeting this pathway could be beneficial.

Aldosterone, acting via the mineralocorticoid receptor (MR), can drive proinflammatory and profibrotic processes that contribute to cardiovascular damage [16]. Given our RAAS findings, there is a strong rationale for therapeutic blockade of the MR in PWH at risk for CVD. Prior work by our group in PWH has shown that a randomization to eplerenone for 12 months can significantly improve arterial inflammation [17]. However, the mechanisms underlying these benefits are not fully elucidated in HIV or the general population, and it is unknown how MR blockade might alter the chronic inflammatory milieu or cardiovascular tissue remodeling in this population. In this context, the present study explored serum proteins that may potentiate the effects of MR antagonism with eplerenone among well-treated PWH, addressing a critical gap in knowledge about RAAS-targeted interventions in the HIV population.

METHODS

Participants

Participants included in this proteomics analysis were enrolled and randomized as part of the Mineralocorticoid Receptor Antagonism for Cardiovascular Health in HIV (MIRACLE HIV) parent study [18], a 12-month double-blinded, randomized controlled trial that investigated eplerenone versus placebo for cardiovascular disease. The trial was conducted at the Mass General Brigham between February 2017 and March 2022. Only those participants enrolled in the MIRACLE HIV study with no current statin use were eligible to participate in the Mineralocorticoid Receptor Antagonism By Eplerenone to Lower Arterial Inflammation in HIV (MIRABELLA HIV) substudy [17]. Participants in MIRABELLA HIV substudy underwent 18F-fluorodeoxyglucose positron emission tomography (18F-FDG-PET) to assess the impact of eplerenone versus placebo on arterial inflammation over 12 months.

Participants were required to be 40–65 years of age and treated with a continuous ART regimen for >12 months with a HIV viral load <100 copies/mL (level of detection ≥20 copies/mL or log 1.28 copies/mL). Furthermore, participants were included for increased visceral adiposity as measured on computed tomography (CT) of the abdomen using a cutoff of VAT ≥110 cm2, given that this threshold was associated with increased RAAS activation in our prior study [13].

Exclusion criteria included known history of CVD (coronary artery disease and heart failure), cerebrovascular disease, uncontrolled diabetes (hemoglobin A1c ≥7.5% and/or insulin use), liver disease (alanine aminotransferase >3 times the upper limit of normal), and kidney disease (creatinine >1.5 mg/dL or estimated glomerular filtration rate <60 mL/minute/1.73 m2) [18].

Participants provided informed consent to participate. This study was approved by the Mass General Brigham Human Research Committee.

Randomization and Masking

The randomization was leveraged from the parent study [18]. Study participants and investigators remained blinded to the randomization. Randomization occurred by stratification of sex, presence of plaque (yes or no), and statin use (yes or no). A permuted block algorithm with a random block size of either 2 or 4 was utilized, and participants were allocated 1:1 to eplerenone or matching placebo. Participants were invited to enroll in the MIRABELLA HIV substudy after randomization in the parent study. After completion of baseline study procedures, eplerenone dosing was initiated at 50 mg daily for 1 week, then titrated to 50 mg twice daily for the duration of the 12-month trial. Adherence to study medication was assessed by manual pill count of the returned supply.

Study Outcomes

In the current study, we assessed the proteomic profile at baseline and 12 months after treatment of eplerenone versus placebo from fasting plasma samples stored at −80°C. Participants in the MIRABELLA HIV study additionally underwent cardiac 18F-FDG-PET/CT at baseline and 12 months under standardized sodium conditions [17].

Proteomics Panel

Two hundred seventy-six proteins were evaluated using from 3 commercially available multiplex immunoassay panels from Olink (Target 96 Cardiometabolic, Cardiovascular II, Cardiovascular III), selected based on their relevance to CVD. A list of proteins and their associated genes can be found in Supplementary Table 1. Further information regarding reproducibility and validation are provided by the manufacturer at: https://www.olink.com. The panels were performed in a blinded fashion to treatment allocation. The quality of both assay performance and of individual samples were monitored using 4 internal controls that were added to each sample. The quality control (QC) was performed in 2 steps: (1) Each sample plate was assessed based on the standard deviation (SD) of the internal controls, expected to be <0.2 normalized protein expression (NPX) and (2) the quality of each sample was evaluated using the deviation from the median value of the controls for each individual sample. Samples that deviated <0.3 NPX from the median passed the QC. Only data that passed QC were reported (n = 28 participants). Intra-assay coefficients of variability were as follows: Target 96 Cardiometabolic 4%, Target 96 Cardiovascular II 3%, Target 96 Cardiovascular III 3%. In general, 98%–100% of proteins were detected on the 3 immunoassay panels. Data are presented as NPX values, which is an arbitrary unit on log2 scale utilized by Olink Proteomics.

Cardiac 18F-FDG-PET

The index vessel FDG uptake, or target-to-background ratio (TBR), is taken as the average of the maximum TBR activity for all axial segments that compose the index vessel—defined as the vessel (aorta, left carotid, and right carotid) with highest mean of maximum TBR values at baseline [17, 19]. The average of the maximum TBR activity within the most diseased segment of the index vessel was also assessed [17, 19]. The most diseased segment is determined to be the 1.5-cm arterial segment centered on the slice of artery demonstrating the highest FDG uptake at baseline and calculated as a mean of maximum TBR values derived from 3 contiguous axial segments [17, 19]. Cardiac magnetic resonance imaging was used to assess stress myocardial blood flow as part of the MIRACLE HIV study [18].

Statistical Analysis

Variables are expressed as mean (SD) for normally distributed variables, as median (Q1, Q3: 25th percentile, 75th percentile) for nonnormally distributed variables, and as percentages for categorical variables. Change between baseline and 12-month values were compared between randomization groups using the Mann–Whitney U test. A nominal P value <.05 was considered significant in assessing changes with respect to treatment due to the small sample size. Given the exploratory nature of the current investigation, corrections for multiple comparisons were not performed. Correlations between change in protein expression and change in arterial inflammation were determined using Spearman correlation coefficient. Statistical significance was determined by a 2-tailed P < .05 for the correlation analyses. Analyses were performed using SAS JMP (version 17) and R (version 4.0.2).

RESULTS

Participant Flow

Forty participants were included, and of these, 20 participants each were randomized to receive eplerenone or placebo. A total of 33 participants completed the study, and samples from 28 participants (eplerenone, n = 13; placebo, n = 15) were available for proteomics analysis. Only those participants who were not current statin users underwent cardiac 18F-FDG-PET and had data available for TBR.

Demographics and Clinical Characteristics

Randomized groups (eplerenone vs placebo) were generally similar by age (mean [SD], 53 [8] vs 56 [6] years) and proportions of male sex (69% vs 73%) and White race (46% vs 60%). Ten-year atherosclerotic cardiovascular disease (ASCVD) risk scores were in the low to intermediate range in the eplerenone versus placebo arms (median [Q1, Q3], 4.2% [3.3%, 7.9%] vs 8.3% [3.4%, 15.2%], respectively). Both treatment arms had good immunological control based on CD4+ count (mean [SD], 744 [265] vs 731 [314] cells/μL) and reported a long duration of HIV (mean [SD], 19 [8] vs 21 [7] years) and ART use (mean [SD], 16 [6] vs 17 [7] years) (eplerenone vs placebo, respectively). The majority of participants had undetectable viral loads in both treatment groups. Lipid, glycemic, liver, and renal parameters were generally normal among randomized groups (Table 1).

Changes in Proteins Among Randomized Groups

After 12 months, changes in 11 proteins were significantly different between groups. Treatment with eplerenone reduced expression of 8 proteins (pro-adrenomedullin [ADM], Cadherin-1 [CDH1], liver carboxylesterase 1 [CES1], fatty acid–binding protein, intestinal [FABP2], fibroblast growth factor 21 [FGF-21], follistatin [FS], Galectin-4 [Gal-4], interleukin 4 receptor subunit alpha [IL-4RA]) and increased expression of 2 proteins (Cystatin B [CSTB], myeloperoxidase [MPO]) when compared to treatment with placebo. In addition, an increase in expression of interleukin 27 (IL-27) was prevented among the eplerenone-treated group compared with the placebo-treated group (Table 2, Figure 1). Baseline and 12-month protein values for these 11 proteins are shown in Supplementary Table 2.

Correlations Between Proteins Among All Participants

Changes in CDH1 correlated with changes in FABP2 (ρ = 0.41, P = .03) and Gal-4 (ρ = 0.53, P = .003). Changes in CES1 related to changes in FS (ρ = 0.40, P = .03), FABP2 (ρ = 0.41, P = .03), CSTB (ρ = −0.40, P = .04), and MPO (ρ = −0.40, P = .03). Changes in ADM were further correlated with changes in IL-4RA (ρ = 0.40, P = .03), IL-27 (ρ = 0.72, P < .0001), FGF-21 (ρ = 0.46, P = .01), and FS (ρ = 0.57, P = .002). In addition, changes in IL-27 correlated with changes in FS (ρ = 0.67, P < .0001), changes in FABP2 with changes in Gal-4 (ρ = 0.59, P = .0009), and changes in CSTB with changes in MPO (ρ = 0.74, P < .0001) (Supplementary Figure 1).

Correlations Between Proteins and Arterial Inflammation Among All Participants

Changes in TBR of the index vessel were correlated with changes in FGF-21 (ρ = 0.48, P = .04) and Gal-4 (ρ = 0.59, P = .008). Changes in TBR of the most diseased segment of the index vessel were correlated with changes in CDH1 (ρ = 0.53, P = .02), FGF-21 (ρ = 0.64, P = .003), and Gal-4 (ρ = 0.58, P = .009) (Table 3). Correlations are shown stratified by treatment group in Supplementary Table 3A and 3B. Only changes in CDH1 were further correlated with changes in stress myocardial blood flow (ρ = −0.48, P = .04). The clinical relevance of these 3 proteins is shown in Table 4.

DISCUSSION

The MIRABELLA HIV study was a small, randomized, placebo-controlled trial evaluating whether blocking RAAS with eplerenone could improve arterial inflammation among PWH on stable ART without clinically evident CVD. In the trial, we demonstrated that eplerenone significantly improved arterial inflammation, suggesting potential CVD benefits in this population [17]. In the current exploratory analysis, aimed at discerning a potential mechanism, we discovered a unique proteomic signature of 11 proteins whose expression differed with eplerenone versus placebo treatment over 12 months. Among these proteins, we further identified 3 proteins of interest whose changes correlated with changes in arterial inflammation: CDH1, FGF-21, and Gal-4. The literature suggests these circulating proteins are implicated in inflammation, metabolic regulation, and gut barrier integrity, which have an important role in the pathogenesis of CVD.

CDH1 (E-cadherin) is a calcium-dependent adhesion molecule that plays an essential role in epithelial barrier integrity and regulating immune interactions [31]. As the core component of adherens junctions, E-cadherin secures epithelial cohesion and limits microbial translocation across mucosal surfaces, which is especially relevant in HIV infection, where gut barrier damage is thought to drive chronic inflammation [32, 56]. Beyond structural functions, E-cadherin also has systemic effects. Upon tissue injury or inflammation, membrane-bound E-cadherin is proteolytically cleaved to generate soluble E-cadherin (sE-cadherin), which circulates systemically [56]. sE-cadherin can act on CD8+ T cells, suppressing IFN-γ production and impairing cytotoxic responses against infected CD4+ T cells, thereby fostering viral persistence [33]. In PWH, sE-cadherin levels are elevated and correlate strongly with viral load, underscoring its role as a marker of epithelial damage and immune activation [33]. This dynamic may be influenced by the RAAS, which may be linked to increased circulating E-cadherin levels. Angiotensin II promotes loss of epithelial CDH1 via epithelial to mesenchymal transition, potentially driving epithelial CDH1 cleavage to soluble CDH1 [36]. Clinically, circulating sE-cadherin is proposed as a biomarker of barrier injury and inflammation [56]. Interestingly, we saw that reductions in CDH1 correlated with beneficial changes in both arterial inflammation and myocardial perfusion. The reduction in circulating CDH1 levels with eplerenone may reflect improved epithelial and vascular barrier integrity, potentially limiting microbial translocation, immune dysregulation, and vascular inflammation in PWH.

FGF-21 is an endocrine hormone produced primarily by the liver, adipose tissue, and skeletal muscle in response to metabolic stressors such as fasting, obesity, and inflammation [37, 38]. FGF-21 promotes glucose uptake, improves insulin sensitivity, enhances fatty acid oxidation, and stimulates thermogenesis [37, 38]. In healthy models, these effects protect against diet-induced obesity, diabetes, and cardiovascular injury. However, paradoxically, chronic metabolic diseases including obesity, heart failure, and HIV-associated lipodystrophy, are characterized by persistently elevated FGF-21 levels, suggesting a state of FGF-21 resistance. In HIV infection, multiple studies have shown that FGF-21 levels are higher in PWH compared to controls, particularly in those with central adiposity and chronic inflammation [42, 43]. Elevated FGF-21 correlates strongly with proinflammatory cytokines such as IL-6, IL-8, and TNF-α, highlighting its dual role as both an anti-inflammatory mediator and a biomarker of immune activation [43]. Thus, in states of chronic metabolic dysfunction, a therapeutic strategy may be aimed at reducing FGF-21 resistance, in which there may be a compensatory decrease in FGF-21. In this regard, we have similarly shown that lifestyle intervention can reduce FGF-21 levels among PWH and is related to improved energy homeostasis [42]. FGF-21 is also known to interact with the RAAS. In preclinical models, FGF-21 treatment reduces angiotensin II–induced vascular injury and cardiac hypertrophy [49, 50]. Clinically, high FGF-21 levels predict worse outcomes in heart failure [39], likely reflecting a compensatory response to persistent cardiometabolic stress. Therefore, FGF-21 may provide a critical link between metabolism, inflammation, RAAS modulation, and cardiovascular health in HIV. Eplerenone-associated reductions in FGF-21 may signify a reversal of chronic metabolic stress and inflammation, suggesting improved cardiometabolic homeostasis and reduced RAAS-driven vascular injury in PWH.

Galectin-4 (Gal-4) is a tandem-repeat lectin predominantly expressed in the intestinal epithelium, where it performs specialized functions in mucosal immunity and epithelial stabilization [26]. Functionally, it plays a central role in innate defense against gut pathogens. Intracellular Gal-4 binds to bacterial lipopolysaccharide, tagging bacteria within the intestinal lumen and triggering caspase-1 activation and IL-18 release, which promotes inflammasome-mediated clearance [57]. This mechanism helps prevent systemic bacterial dissemination, as demonstrated in Gal-4 transgenic mice resistant to enteric infection [57]. While its direct role in HIV pathogenesis remains understudied, Gal-4's functions are likely relevant, since chronic HIV infection disrupts epithelial barriers [57]. Similar to FGF-21, there may be a compensatory rise in Gal-4 in HIV, highlighting the severity of microbial translocation which may be a nidus for systemic inflammatory disease. Gal-4 binds to CD14 on monocytes, triggering their differentiation into macrophages and contributing to chronic low-grade inflammation and microvascular atherosclerosis in ischemic disease [26, 53, 54]. Although direct evidence for Gal-4 in fibrosis is limited, galectins broadly are thought to promote fibrosis by stimulating myofibroblast extracellular matrix production and may contribute to heart failure development [51]. The observed decrease in Gal-4 following eplerenone use may indicate enhanced gut mucosal defense and reduced monocyte-driven inflammation, supporting its role as a potential biomarker of improved barrier function and decreased systemic immune activation.

Data on observed proteomic changes with eplerenone treatment are limited. Further, few studies have reported on proteomic changes with spironolactone treatment, another medication in the same class. In the Heart Omics in Aging (HOMAGE) study, spironolactone treatment was associated with changes in 4 proteins in a group of individuals at risk for heart failure. Of note, one of the proteins that decreased with spironolactone was E-selectin, which has similar functions to E-cadherin as a cell adhesion molecule, but in contrast is found on endothelial versus epithelial cells [58]. The TOPCAT study compared spironolactone versus placebo in a population with heart failure with preserved ejection fraction and demonstrated changes in 7 proteins generally related to caspase inhibition and collagen-related pathways [59]. While none of these protein changes overlapped with our study, the changes could have varied based on the unique patient populations studied.

In addition, we observed a relative increase in MPO among participants randomized to eplerenone, which may seem discordant with MPO's actions as a mediator of inflammation. This discordance may be seen as circulating MPO protein levels do not necessarily reflect the functional enzymatic activity of MPO [60], and some studies suggest circulating MPO is relatively inactive compared to localized MPO found within inflamed tissue [61]. It could also be that eplerenone's actions are posttranslational, such that a decrease in enzymatic activity could occur even in the presence of increased MPO protein. Eplerenone has reported to decrease MPO activity in mice [62].

Our study has several limitations. We had a small sample size based on enrollment into the clinical trial, limiting our power to use highly conservative treatment comparisons in adjusting for multiple comparisons. Additionally, some baseline clinical characteristics, including ethnicity, ASCVD status, statin use, and integrase inhibitor use, were imbalanced between treatment groups, and these variables should be controlled for in a larger study. Nonetheless, we were able to highlight eplerenone effects on select proteins with highly relevant applications to CVD, which need to be further investigated in a larger study. Our work provides further insight into the pathways through which HIV-mediated CVD may occur and highlights targets for future intervention. Moreover, to our knowledge, this is the first investigation to profile proteomics changes after intervention with eplerenone in HIV, and only few other studies are available related to this class of medication in other populations. As we assessed circulating protein levels, we are unable to draw conclusions about eplerenone's effects at the tissue level, such as the arterial wall, which was beyond the scope of the study.

Following completion of the MIRABELLA HIV study, clinical guidelines were updated to recommend statin therapy in HIV for ASCVD risk reduction [63]. By trial design, eplerenone's effects on arterial inflammation were evaluated among non–statin users in the MIRABELLA HIV study to avoid the potential confounding effects of statin therapy on arterial inflammation. It would be important to understand whether eplerenone would provide additional clinical and proteomic benefit in combination with statins.

In conclusion, this exploratory analysis suggests that MR blockade with eplerenone could be associated with molecular pathways involving inflammation, vascular dysfunction, epithelial barrier disruption, and metabolic dysregulation among PWH. While we specifically observed that CDH1, FGF-21, and Gal-4 were reduced with eplerenone, we cannot discern whether they were directly affected by RAAS blockade or whether they are compensatory changes indicating general improvements in epithelial barrier disruption, metabolic dysregulation, and immune activation. Additional mechanistic studies are needed to understand these relationships further to clarify causal relationships. Integrating these mechanistic insights with clinical outcomes will be essential to develop targeted biomarker strategies and therapies aimed at mitigating CVD in this high-risk population.
