Exploring the alteration of gut microbiota in the rats with steroid-induce osteonecrosis of the femoral head
Authors: Chengxiong Huang, Yiming Zhao, Liming Qing, Juyu Tang, Chuwei Min, Panfeng Wu
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
BackgroundOsteonecrosis of the femoral head usually occurred after glucocorticoid (GC) therapy. The gut microbiota was considered an indispensable part of maintaining human health, and the concept of the gut microbiota -bone axis was also proposed. In our study, we aimed to elucidate the link between steroid-induce osteonecrosis of the femoral head (SIONFH) and the gut microbiota; Methods: The methylprednisolone (MPS) was used to establish the rat model with SIONFH. During rat modeling, some plasma indexes and the gut microbiota were detected by ELISA and 16 S rRNA gene sequencing. In addition, we deeply explore the relationship between these plasma indexes and gut microbiota by using various bioinformatic methods; Results: Our data revealed that there were specific microbes colonized in the gut of rat with SIONFH. Compared to the control group, Prevotellaceae and Oscillospiraceae were replaced by Lactobacillaceae and Bacteroidaceae families in the SIONFH group. Equally, we found that the decreased beneficial gut microbiota maybe a mediation effect between the SIONFH and the increased IgM level; Conclusions: Oscillospiraceae and Prevotellaceae had bone-protective effects and balance the osteoblast-osteoclast balance. Our study showed that the loss of Oscillospiraceae and Prevotellaceae was associated with SIONFH, an effect mediated by elevated plasma IgM.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12866-025-03897-6.Keywords: Glucocorticoids, Osteonecrosis of the femoral head, gut microbiota, Immune, IgM

Introduction

Osteonecrosis of the femoral head (ONFH) is a disease which brings deliberating pain and limited motion, making patients who suffer such disease away from normal lives [1]. Glucocorticoids (GC) is primary risk factor for non-traumatic ONFH, and the prevalence of which can comes to 40% [2]. It is very clear that the incidence of steroid-induced osteonecrosis of femoral head (SIONFH) increased with dose and duration and is even worse with long-acting drugs. There are many hypotheses regarding the pathological mechanism of SIONFH, such as lipid metabolism disorder, endothelial damage and vascular coagulation, apoptosis and autophagy, oxidative stress, genetics and epigenetics [1, 3–5]. However, the final common pathway of these mechanisms above is the impairment of blood supply of the peripheral and interior region of the femoral head, impeding the transportation of nutrient to the watershed areas of the femoral head, leading to bone death. Nevertheless, GC not only put its influence on femoral head, but also altering other process, which may affect the development of ONFH.

The gut microbiota has been considered to be of great importance regulating health via an ancient evolutionary symbiotic relationship [6]. The diversity of microbiota can be influenced by many intrinsic and extrinsic factors which may disturb physiological homeostasis of the host [7]. Many studies demonstrated that the gut microbial dysbiosis was linked with diabetes, Alzheimer disease, inflammatory bowel disease and cancers [8–11]. Recent studies suggested that gut microbiota played a critical role in bone remodeling, growth and development as well as necrosis [12, 13]. Chen et al. [7] found that extracellular vesicles from Lactobacillus animalis (L.animalis-EVs) contained abundant functional proteins and could enter the femoral head to exert proangiogenic, pro-osteogenic, and antiapoptotic effects, oral supplementation of L.animalis mitigated steroid-induce ONFH and reduced cell apoptosis. Nevertheless, there is, as far as we know, no specific relationship between gut dysbiosis and SIONFH has been investigated. Inflammation and immune response have been verified that associated and regulated by gut microbiota [14]. Meanwhile, inflammation has been considered to play an indispensable role in the development of SIONFH [15–17]. Li et al. [18] discovered eight promising serum levels of the candidate markers which can be applied as a multi-biomarker-based prediction model. However, the specific relationship between gut microbiota and SIONFH has not been investigated.

In this study, we simulated the development of SIONFH by using the methylprednisolone (MPS) to establish the rat model, and collected the blood and feces samples in regular time intervals during the model-establishing period. Some plasma indexes were tested to estimate host basic metabolism, inflammatory reaction and immune state. In addition, the 16 S rRNA gene sequencing was used to screen the microbiota that might be associated with SIONFH. The correlations between these plasma indicators and gut microbiota were explored to, which influence the characteristic of ONFH. This may provide an idea for elucidating the function of the microbiota-bone axis in the development of SIONFH and exploring new prognostic and promising markers and intervention strategies.

Materials and methods

Animals and treatment

In this study, a randomized controlled experimental design was adopted, and the sample size calculation was based on the primary outcome (bone volume/tissue volume) which can reflect successful SIONFH animal model building using a two-tailed t-test. Assuming a large effect size (Cohen’s d = 3) derived from pilot data, a minimum of 3 rats per group was required (α = 0.05, power = 80%). Four rats per group was determined based on prior rodent microbiome studies [19, 20].

Therefore, animals were randomly divided into two groups: control group (N = 4) and SIONFH group (N = 4). Male Sprague Dawley (SD) rats (weight, 300–350 g) at 8-weeks-of-age were provided by the Department of Laboratory Animals, Central South University. Surgical procedures and other animal-related operations were implemented by strictly following the approval of the Laboratory Animal Committee of Central South University and the guidelines of the China Council of Animal Care. All the rats were fed in standard conditions (humidity 50–60%, temperature 23–25 °C, and 12-hour light/dark cycle) by providing free access to food and water. All rats were maintained under specific pathogen–free conditions. To induce GC-induced ONFH, 8-week-old rats were intramuscularly injected with methylprednisolone (MPS, 20 mg/kg per day; Pfizer, New York, USA) into the left thigh on the first three days of a week for three weeks. Six weeks later, all animals were euthanized by CO2 after reaching the end of observation. During these 6 weeks, we collected feces and blood samples from rats at a fixed time before the start of MPS injection every week, starting from week 0, for a total of 7 times. Rat feces were collected by abdominal stimulation method, and 3–4 pellets were collected each time using a sterile EP tube. Blood samples were collected from the suborbital vein. The Feces were performed 16 S rRNA gene sequencing, and blood samples were used to detect plasma indexes.

µCT analysis

The femoral head sample was fixed with 4% paraformaldehyde for 2 days, followed by µCT scanning (voltage, 70 kV; current, 400 µA; x-ray tube potential, 55 kVp; integration time, 400 ms; and voxel size, 11.4 μm) by vivaCT 80 (SCANCO Medical AG, Bruttisellen, Switzerland). The entire volume of the trabecular bone of the femoral head was selected for analysis, excluding the cortical shell region of the femoral head. Images of the femoral heads were reconstructed by NRecon, and the trabecular bones in the femoral head were analyzed by CTAn v1.11 to determine bone marrow density (BMD), bone volume/tissue volume (BV/TV), trabecular thickness (Tb. Th), trabecular number (Tb. N), and trabecular separation (Tb. Sp).

Serum cytokine and immunoglobulin measurements

At the beginning of each week, we used the suborbital vein blood collection method to collect 0.5 ml of peripheral venous blood from rats which was drawn and interleukin 6 (IL-6) and tumor necrosis factor a (TNF-a) were measured by enzyme-linked immunosorbent assay (ELISA) (Beckman, CA, USA). Immunoglobulin A (IgA), IgG, and IgM were quantified using an IMMAGE-800 automatic immunochemistry system (Beckman, CA, USA) and accompanying test kits.

DNA extraction and 16 S rRNA gene sequencing

The fecal samples were accessed 16 S rRNA gene sequencing. In detail, DNA Extraction Total genomic DNA samples were extracted using the OMEGA Soil DNA Kit (M5635-01) (Omega Bio-Tek, Norcross, GA, USA), following the manufacturer’s instructions. PCR amplification of the bacterial 16 S rRNA genes V3–V4 region was performed using the forward primer 338 F (5’-ACTCCTACGGGAGGCAGCA-3’) and the reverse primer 806R (5’-GGACTACHVGGGTWTCTAAT-3’). Sample-specific 7 bp barcodes were incorporated into the primers for multiplex sequencing. The PCR components contained 5 µl of buffer (5×), 0.25 µl of Fast pfu DNA Polymerase (5 U/µl), 2 µl (2.5 mM) of dNTPs, 1 µl (10 µM) of each Forward and Reverse primer, 1 µl of DNA Template, and 14.75 µl of ddH2O. Thermal cycling consisted of initial denaturation at 98 °C for 5 min, followed by 25 cycles consisting of denaturation at 98 °C for 30 s, annealing at 53 °C for 30 s, and extension at 72 °C for 45 s, with a final extension of 5 min at 72 °C. PCR amplicons were purified with Vazyme VAHTSTM DNA Clean Beads (Vazyme, Nanjing, China) and quantified using the Quant-iT PicoGreen dsDNA Assay Kit (Invitrogen, Carlsbad, CA, USA). After the individual quantification step, amplicons were pooled in equal amounts, and pair-end 2 × 250 bp sequencing was performed using the Illlumina NovaSeq platform with NovaSeq 6000 SP Reagent Kit (500 cycles).

16 S rRNA gene sequencing data processing and analysis

For 16 S rRNA gene sequencing data, a series of processing on the raw sequence data were performed. Firstly, the primers of sequence data were trimmed with Cutadapt [21] (version 3.4). And the sequences were merged and truncated to 400 bp using Vsearch [22] (version 2.7.0). We performed quality control in Qiime2 [23] version 2021.4.0) with ‘quality-filter’ function by default parameters. Next, the clean reads were filtered chimera, denoised, clustered to Operational Taxonomic Unit (OTU) and profiled with ‘deblur’ function in Qiime2. To reduce false positives of obtaining data, only the OTUs mapped reads more than 10 were retained and rarefied to the minimum read counts and perform rarefaction for alpha-diversity analysis using R package ‘picante’ [24]. Meanwhile, the representative sequence was selected from each OTU to perform taxonomic classification by feature-classifier function in Qiime2 against the Silva (Version 132) database [25].

Microbial diversity and ROC analysis

To investigate the variation of microbial communities across samples, the ACE and Pielou evenness indexes were calculated. At the same time, the Bray-Curtis distance metrics were calculated based relative abundance of OTUs and visualized via nonmetric multidimensional scaling (NMDS) by R package ‘vegan’ [24]. To assess the predictive potential of microbial data for classification prediction, the receiver operating characteristic curve (ROC) analysis was performed on the two dimensions from NMDS analysis using the R package pROC [26]. The area under the curve (AUC) was used to measure the model’s performance.

Co-occurrence networks

To explore the co-occurring microbial community between two groups, the SparCC [27] co-occurrence networks were constructed using FastSpar [28] (Version 1.0) with OTUs presented in greater than or equal to 30% of the samples in each group. To reduce false positives in detecting microbial co-occurrence relationships, only statistically significant (FDR < 0.05 and |R-value| > 0.6) correlations were incorporated into network analysis, Network visualization, modularity and parameters analysis were performed with the igraph (version 0.9.10) package in python.

Mediation linkage inference

For phenotypic and plasma indexes associations to the same microbial feature, we only checked whether the plasma indexes significantly enriched in SIONFH were associated with the significantly differential microbes between two groups. Next, mediation analysis was carried out using the mediate function from the R package mediation (version 4.5.0) to infer the causal role of the microbiome in contributing to the different phenotypes through plasma indexes.

Other statistical analysis and visualization

LDA Effect Size (LEfSe) analysis [28] was performed to detect biomarkers in different groups. In this study, only the taxa with LDA scores > 4 and FDR < 0.05 were considered as biomarkers. The distance-based redundancy analysis (db-RDA) can help to associate plasma indexes with different microbial features. Db-RDA was performed using R package ‘vegan’, and tested using ‘Anova’ method to carry out F statistic F test with P-value. All other statistical tests were using ‘scipy’ or ‘statsmodels’ package in python. Most data visualization was applied using ‘ggplot2’ in R or ‘matplotlib’ in python.

Results

Study design

In this study, we aimed to investigate the relationship between steroid-induced osteonecrosis of femoral head (SIONFH) and gut microbiota (Fig. 1A). Two parallel groups of mice (Control and SIONFH) were raised under the same conditions except for the injection of different substances in the first three weeks. The rats in SIONFH were modeled by methylprednisolone [7]. To monitor the gut microbes in two groups, we collected fecal samples every week, and performed 16 S rRNA gene sequencing of them (N = 56). Some plasma indexes (IL-6, TNF-α, IgA, IgG, IgM, triglyceride and glucose) were also checked during all 6 experimental weeks to estimate host basic metabolism, the inflammatory reaction and immune state (Fig. 1A). In addition, the computed tomography (CT) was carried out and measured on the rats at the week 6 of this experiment to estimate the severity of the SIONFH (Fig. 1B). The results showed that the bone volume/tissue volume (BV/TV), trabecular thickness (Tb.Th), trabecular number (Tb.N), and bone marrow density (BMD) were all significantly decreased in the SIONFH than control group (P < 0.05). And the degree of trabecular separation (Tb.sp) was also significantly increased in the SIONFH group (Fig. 1C).

Gut microbiota as a predictor of SIONFH

The Abundance-based Coverage Estimator (ACE) and Pielou evenness were two indexes to estimate the microbial community richness and evenness, respectively. The significant higher ACE and lower Pielou evenness indexes suggested alteration of microbial alpha-diversity in the SIONFH group (Fig. 2A, Figure S1). In addition, the results of NMDS showed the gut microbial composition between two groups were significantly different (Fig. 2B, PERMANOVA, P < 0.0001). Notably, we found the distance between two groups seemed to increasing with SIONFH progress (Fig. 2B, Figure S2), which emphasized the relationship between gut microbes and SIONFH.

Two-sided Mann-Whitney-Wilcoxon test, ** represents p < 0.01, and **** represents p < 0.0001). (B) Microbial composition in two groups (control and SIONFH) with NMDS analysis. The top of the figure shows the P-value of microbial composition under different variable using PERMANOVA test. (D) The correlation between two dimensions (MDS1 and MDS2) from NMDS analysis and different indexes which to estimate the severity of SIONFH

To further explore the predictive potential of the gut microbiota for SIONFH, we constructed prediction models using two dimensions from NMDS (MDS1 and MDS2) which can represent microbial composition. Model performance was assessed using the area under the curve (AUC) of the receiver operating characteristic (ROC). In our modelling results, microbial taxa showed a high performance (AUCMDS1 = 0.97, AUCMDS2 = 0.59, Fig. 2C). Besides, we additionally performed the regression analysis microbial data between gut microbial data and SIONFH, and a correlation between dimensions (MDS1 and MDS2) and quantitative indexes of SIONFH was found (Fig. 2D). These results demonstrated that the altered composition of gut microbiota could be used as a credible predictor for the model of classifying the SIONFH.

The alteration of gut microbiota under SIONFH

Afterwards, we explored the dynamic taxa alteration between two groups. At the phylum level, control and SIONFH were dominated by Firmicutes, however, Bacteroidetes began to increase after injection of MPS (Figure S3A). At the family and genus level, the microbial composition was obviously consistent at the initial week 0. Compared to control group, the relative abundance of Prevotellaceae and Oscillospiraceae were decreased, however, the relative abundance of Lactobacillaceae and Bacteroidaceae families were increased in the SIONFH group (Fig. 3A). At genus level, the relative abundance of UCG-005 genus was decreased, however, the relative abundance of Bacteroides and Ligilactobacillus was increased in the SIONFH group (Figure S3B). To elucidate the statistic differences in microbial composition between two groups, the LEfSe analysis was performed and only the significantly different (LDA > 4 and FDR < 0.05) taxa were remained as the biomarkers between two groups (Fig. 3B). The results were highly consistent with the difference observed before. To further illustrate the interaction and relationship of microbes in terms of changes in relative abundance among groups, we constructed two co-occurrence networks using Sparcc method [27]. The degree distributions of two co-occurrence networks conformed to the power-law distribution, indicating that the gut microbial community was constructed in a non-random way (Figure S4). Both co-occurrence networks were dominated by positive relationships, however, the SIONFH network contained more negative relationships. Compared to the control network, microbial communities in the SIONFH group had a more complex network with higher edges (n = 144). Interestingly, the node belonged to Prevotellaceae in the core position in the control network, while it changed to be Bacteroidaceae in the SIONFH network. Equally, the leading families with the most degree proportion were altered from Prevotellaceae and Oscillospiraceae to Lactobacillaceae and Bacteroidaceae, which were consistent to the alteration of biomarkers between two groups (Fig. 3C). These results indicated that the change of partial microbial abundance in the gut from SIONFH leaded to the alteration of the co-occurrence microbial network and the imbalance of the dysbacteriosis.

The microbiome contributes to SIONFH through IgM

SIONFH was considered to be related to the immune and inflammatory response of the body [29]. In addition, some metabolic indicators were also observed to be different in SIONFH [30]. Therefore, we checked a series of plasma indexes, and the IL-6 and IgM was found to be significantly increased in SIONFH (Fig. 4A). To further demonstrate the relationship between these plasma indexes and gut microbiota, we performed the RDA based on Bray-Curtis distance and analysis of variance (ANOVA). Similarly, the RDA results suggested that the IL-6 (FDR < 0.05) and IgM (FDR < 0.05) were significantly correlated with the gut microbiota between two groups (Fig. 4B), which corroborated the role of gut microbiota in the SIONFH.

Previous study has reported that IgM was associated the osteonecrosis of femoral head [31], and gut microbiome was also considered as an important factor to influence immune status [32]. Our mediation analysis suggested that various microbial composition may contribute to these effects. For instance, two kinds of gut microbes were found to obtain significant mediating effect (Proportion of mediated effect > 45%, Pmediation < 0.05, Fig. 4C, Table S1), however, it was not observed mediating effect after swapping the dependent and independent variables, which indicated that the decreased abundance of UCG-005 from Oscillospiraceae family and Prevotellaceae contribute to SIONFH by increasing plasma IgM levels.

Discussion

ONFH is a progressive disease that often leads to collapse of the femoral head and loss of hip joint function [33]. Studies have shown that non-traumatic ONFH is mostly related to the use of GC [34]. However, the cause of ONFH caused by GC (SIONFH) is not yet known. The current study shows that the pathogenic mechanism of SIONFH may be related to induced endothelial cell dysfunction, apoptosis, and regulatory imbalance between osteogenesis and osteoclasts [35–37]. Although there were some evidences that gut microbiota might influence bone health through the gut-bone axis [38], the gut microbiota related to the occurrence of femoral head necrosis has little known.

Zheng et al. [39]found that ONFH patients can cause changes in intestinal flora, leading to changes in fecal metabolites. In addition, some studies have found that Lachnospiraceae may induce the occurrence of ONFH by mediating a decrease in butyric acid levels [40]. In our study, we found that the diversity of the gut microbiota was significantly increased in the SIONFH group, which suggested that there was gut microbial disorder in SIONFH group. In addition, SIONFH and the control group showed two different trends, which indicated that the use of GC changed the composition of gut microbiota. The ROC analysis showed that our verification was reliable. Moreover, we quantitatively analyzed SIONFH through µCT analysis, and the related BV/TV, Tb.Th, Tb.N, BMD, and Tb.sp all showed strong correlation with gut microbiota. Therefore, we speculated that changes in gut microbiota may affect the progression of SIONFH.

Subsequently, we tried to explore what gut microbiota affects the occurrence of SIONFH. The results showed that the Lactobacillaceae and Bacteroidaceae in the SIONFH group replaced the Prevotellaceae and Oscillospiraceae that were usually dominant in normal health conditions. Studies have shown that Prevotellaceae can promote bone formation to prevent osteoporosis and inhibit abnormal lipid metabolism [41]. In addition, Han et al. [42] also found that there was a positive relationship between the increase of Prevotellaceae and the inhibition of bone density decline and the protection of bone microstructure. It is worth noting that Oscillospiraceae has also been found to potentially regulate the balance of osteoblasts and osteoclasts, thereby affecting bone health[ [43]. The above studies revealed that the decrease in the abundance of Prevotellaceae and Oscillospiraceae may be related to the occurrence of SIONFH, which is consistent with our results. However, the effects of gut microbiota are not solely the result of a certain type of microbiota. Gut microbiota usually interacts with each other to produce a series of complex and intricate effects. Therefore, we constructed a co-occurrence network to explore the interactions and relationships of gut microbiota. Interestingly, the core node in the control co-occurrence network belonged to Prevotellaceae and Oscillospiraceae, while the core nodes of SIONFH were Bacteroidaceae and Lactobacilliaceae. The above results indicated that gut microbiota may play a key role in affecting the process of SIONFH.

According to our above results, the altered gut microbiota was related to the progression of SIONFH. However, how gut microbiota exerted their effects was unknown yet. Inflammation was considered to be one of the main causes of femoral head necrosis [44, 45]. Wang et al. [45] found that in the SIONFH model, IL-6, TNF-α and SIONFH showed a positive correlation. Interestingly, recent studies have published that immune regulation may also be closely related to the occurrence of ONFH [46]. Jin et al. [47] found that inhibiting the polarization of M1 macrophages can prevent the progression of ONFH. In addition, imbalance of T cell subsets is also considered to be involved in the development of ONFH [48]. Studies [49] have found that inhibiting lipid metabolism leads to fat accumulation in the femoral head and induces ONFH. In addition, population-based studies have shown that higher TG and lower HDL-C are also inextricably linked to ONFH [50]. Therefore, we speculated whether gut microbiota would participate in the occurrence of SIONFH through immune status, inflammatory response and lipid metabolism. In addition, since basal metabolic indicators may also be a key factor affecting the function of gut microbiota [51]. Our results showed that IL-6 and IgM were significantly elevated in SIONFH. We speculated whether IL-6 and IgM were intermediate mediators between gut microbiota and SIONFH. We performed RDA and the results showed that IL-6, IgM were main factors related with gut microbes. In addition, we performed mediation analysis on the two groups of related gut microbes. Interestingly, we found that reduced UCG-005 abundance of the Oscillospiraceae family and Prevotellaceae contributes to SIONFH by increasing plasma IgM levels. which forms a closed loop. Therefore, these results indicated that GC may cause SIONFH by decreasing the abundance of Oscillospiraceae and Prevotellaceae and increasing plasma IgM.

Our study indicates that gut microbiota may influence the progression of SIONFH by promoting inflammation. Although this study has the limitation of relatively small sample size, it provides an idea that the development of SIONFH may be delayed by oral administration of beneficial intestinal microbiota. And the future large cohorts with multi-omics approaches can help to focus on the inflammatory changes driven by gut microbiota. More strategies could be developed to regulate gut microbiota in order to mitigate the inflammatory response, thereby slowing the progression of SIONFH and preventing bone loss.

Collectively, this study highlighted the relationship between gut microbiota and SIONFH, and lays the foundation for a better understanding of gut microbiota roles in SIONFH, providing insight into the manipulation of intestinal microbiota to improve host bone health. We thus expect these results to represent the next step in understanding and managing individual bone health through interplay with the gut microbiota.
