Bone marrow endosteum houses Hedgehog-susceptible Dlx5-expressing osteoblast precursor cells
Authors: Keita Kondo, Yuki Matsushita, Shion Orikasa, Yuta Nakai, Aiko Kuroda, Shinsuke Ohba, Wanida Ono, Noriaki Ono
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
The bone marrow endosteum provides a robust source of bone-making osteoblasts by housing various types of osteoblast precursor cells, including endosteal stem cells expressing fibroblast growth factor receptor 3 (Fgfr3) and their downstream progenitor cell populations. In fetal bone development, Dlx5 marks early perichondrial cells that populate the diaphyseal bone and marrow stroma in the postnatal stages. However, the location of Dlx5-expressing cells in the postnatal bone marrow and how they contribute to osteogenesis remain undefined. Here, we show that Dlx5+ cells are preferentially localized to the endosteal space in the postnatal bone marrow and provide a robust yet transient source of trabecular bone-making osteoblasts. Cell-lineage analyses by Dlx5-creER reveal that these Dlx5+ endosteal precursor cells robustly contribute to osteoblasts and reticular stromal cells, whereas cell type-specific Hedgehog activation by Ptch1-deletion prematurely shifted their cell fates toward marrow adipocytes, leading to cortical and trabecular bone thinning with spontaneous fractures. Therefore, our findings identify postnatal Dlx5+ cells as Hedgehog-susceptible osteoblast precursors, unraveling a new mechanism governing osteogenic fates of bone marrow endosteal precursor cells.

Introduction

Bones continue to grow robustly through early postnatal to juvenile stages. Remarkably, the length of the long bones increases by ~3 to 4 times during these stages in mice1. At the same time, bone continues to accrue its mass, with its peak bone mass achieved in the late twenties in humans2. Stem and progenitor cells of the skeletal cell lineage, termed skeletal stem cells (SSCs), play an essential role in supporting continuous bone growth and accrual by sustaining a robust output to terminally differentiated, bone-making osteoblasts3. SSCs, previously described under the blanket definition as mesenchymal stem cells (MSCs), were initially isolated from the bone marrow as colony-forming fibroblasts with osteogenic capacity in the 1960s4. Since their discovery, SSCs have been extensively studied for their potential roles in bone physiology and regenerative medicine5,6.

The current concept holds that SSCs represent diverse cell populations that reside in various compartments of bones rather than a single monolithic group of stem cells7. Technical and conceptual advances in single-cell transcriptomics and mouse lineage-tracing studies have facilitated extensive investigation over the past decade8. As a result, spatiotemporally specific SSC populations have been identified, including those in the metaphyseal and central bone marrow9–12, periosteum13,14, and growth plate15–17. Recently, we identified a novel group of SSCs in the endosteal space of the bone marrow, highlighting the bone marrow endosteum as an essential SSC niche. Endosteal stem cells—SSCs in the bone marrow endosteum—express fibroblast growth factor receptor 3 (Fgfr3) and possess a unique characteristic of osteoblast-chondrocyte transitional (OCT) identities; these cells are abundant in the young bone marrow and robustly contribute to active osteogenesis under homeostatic and regenerative conditions18,19. Within the endosteal space, these stem cells coexist with their downstream progenitor and precursor cells, which have limited potential for self-renewal and differentiation, including osteoblast precursor cells expressing Gas118. These heterogeneous groups of skeletal stem, progenitor, and precursor cell populations with various osteogenic and adipogenic potential maintain their hierarchy within a highly dynamic microenvironment of the bone marrow endosteum while closely interacting with vasculature and peripheral nerve endings.

Critical transcription factors tightly regulate the hierarchy of the osteoblast lineage. The most well-described are the pair of master osteogenic transcription factors, namely Runx2 and Osx (encoded by Sp7). Runx2 is expressed at an early stage of osteoblast differentiation, which is shortly followed by the onset of the expression of Osx. The concerted action of these transcription factors is required to progress osteoblast differentiation20,21. The transcription factor Dlx5 is also essential in supporting Runx2 and Osx functions. Dlx5 promotes Runx2 expression to initiate osteogenic differentiation of the developing mesenchyme of cranial sutures22. Dlx5 drives osteoblast-specific gene expression with Mef2 by promoting Runx2 enhancer activities23. Furthermore, Dlx5 directly binds with Osx in osteoblasts to form a transcriptional complex and promotes gene expression by targeting AT-rich motifs24.

In early endochondral bone development, Osx-positive cells are localized to the inner layer of the perichondrium adjacent to the cartilage template25. In contrast, Dlx5-positive cells are localized to the outer layer of the perichondrium without overlapping with Osx+ cells26. These two types of perichondrial cells concomitantly migrate into the cartilage template, generating the nascent bone marrow with invading vasculature during primary ossification. Interestingly, Osx+ inner perichondrial cells are short-lived and mostly disappear from the marrow space in postnatal stages7, whereas Dlx5+ outer perichondrial cells contribute permanently to diaphyseal osteoblasts and marrow stromal cells26,27. Therefore, Dlx5+ outer cells are earlier than Osx+ inner cells in the perichondrial cell lineage, suggesting that a lineage hierarchy is inscribed within the perichondrium.

Whether the same lineage hierarchy between Dlx5+ and Osx+ cells is maintained in the postnatal bone marrow remains undefined. In postnatal stages, Osx+ cells are almost exclusively localized near the bone surface or within bones. Unlike in the fetal stage, Osx+ cells in the early postnatal stage are long-lived and give rise to osteoblasts and reticular stromal cells, while those in the later postnatal stage contribute almost exclusively to osteoblasts27,28. In addition, Osx+ cells participate in cortical bone regeneration after a localized drill-hole injury12, indicating that Osx+ cells in the endosteal space possess at least some properties of osteoblast precursor cells under regenerative conditions. However, the location of Dlx5-expressing cells in the postnatal bone marrow and how they contribute to osteogenesis remain undefined.

In this study, we aim to identify the fate of Dlx5-expressing cells in the postnatal bone marrow and how these cells are regulated by Hedgehog signaling, a major pro-osteogenic signaling pathway. Our in vivo lineage-tracing findings reveal that Dlx5+ cells in the bone marrow endosteum function as osteoblasts precursor cells, contributing to trabecular bone-making osteoblasts and reticular stromal cells in postnatal stages. However, unexpectedly, we found that Hedgehog activation in Dlx5+ cells results in a stark bone thinning with spontaneous fractures, unraveling cell type-specific functions of Hedgehog signaling in osteogenesis.

In postnatal stages, Osx is expressed by osteoblasts and their immediate precursor cells, whereas Fgfr3 is expressed by bone marrow endosteal stromal cells, including endosteal stem cells (Fig. 1A, right)18,27. To define the fate of Dlx5+ cells in the postnatal stage, we performed in vivo lineage-tracing analyzes using a Dlx5-creER knock-in allele29, which activates a Rosa26-tdTomato reporter in a tamoxifen-dependent manner, in addition, a Col1a1(2.3 kb)-GFP reporter was added to the bigenic system to provide a readout for osteoblasts (Fig. 1B). Of note, as the Dlx5-creER allele is a loss-of-function allele and, therefore, Dlx5creER/+ mice represent a haploinsufficient model. First, we injected tamoxifen into Col1a1-GFP; Dlx5-creER; R26RtdTomato mice at postnatal day (P) 6 to permanently mark Dlx5+ cells at the early postnatal stage. After 48 h of the chase at P8, Dlx5-creER-marked tdTomato-expressing cells (Dlx5CE-P6 cells) were localized in proximity to the bone surface in the metaphysis and diaphyseal bone marrow, in the perichondrium (the groove of Ranvier) and periosteum, as well as in the growth plate among proliferating and hypertrophic chondrocytes (Fig. 1C). In higher magnification, GFP-negative fibroblast-like Dlx5CE-P6 cells were found in the bone marrow endosteum, while some Dlx5CE-P6 cells were also positive for Col1a1-GFP+ (Fig. 1D). Many Dlx5CE-P6 cells co-expressed ALPL, with fewer cells co-expressing ACAN or LepR (Supplementary Fig. 1A). We postulate that these Dlx5+Col1a1-GFPneg cells in the bone marrow endosteum represent osteoblast precursor cells that possess molecular characteristics of Osteo-CAR (CXCL12-abundant reticular) cells; these cells represent an intermediate cell population between osteoblasts and marrow stromal cells, as delineated in the latter part of the study. Quantitatively, 7~10% of Col1a1-GFP+ osteoblasts/osteocytes were marked by tdTomato at P8 (Fig. 1E). Notably, almost no Cxcl12-GFP+ reticular stromal cells were marked by tdTomato at this stage (Fig. 1F), confirming their identities as osteoblast precursor cells located in proximity to the bone surface.

After the chase, Dlx5CE-P6 cells contributed to trabecular and cortical osteoblasts/osteocytes and metaphyseal marrow stromal cells as the postnatal bone formation progressed to P13 and P21 (Fig. 1C–F). Notably, Dlx5CE-P6 cells contributed to a significant fraction of osteoblasts/osteocytes both in the metaphysis and diaphysis by P21; however, their contribution to metaphyseal osteoblasts/cytes substantially declined at 8 weeks (W) (Fig. 1C–E), indicating the transient nature of Dlx5CE-P6 cells in the high turnover trabecular bone compartment. Dlx5CE-P6 cells progressively contributed to bone marrow stromal cells, initially contributing to those in the metaphyseal bone marrow at P13 (Fig. 1C), then gradually expanding their domain to diaphyseal and endosteal regions at P21 (Fig. 1C, F), and eventually, contributing throughout the entire marrow space at 8W (Fig. 1C). In addition, after the extended chase of 6 months and 1 year, Dlx5CE-P6 cells persisted in the cortical bone but were markedly decreased in the metaphyseal bone marrow (Fig. 1C, D). Quantitative analysis revealed that the proportion of tdTomato+ osteoblasts/cytes among total Col1a1-GFP+ osteoblasts/cytes significantly increased at P13 and P21, then decreased remarkably in the metaphyseal region, but was maintained in the cortical region, by 1 year (Fig. 1E).

These findings demonstrate that Dlx5-expressing endosteal stromal cells provide a persistent source of cortical bone osteoblasts and marrow stromal cells at least for 1 year, while providing a transient source of trabecular bone osteoblasts.

Contribution of Dlx5+ endosteal precursor cells at juvenile and adult stages

The above-described lineage-tracing results highlight the contrast between early postnatal Dlx5+ bone marrow endosteal cells and fetal Dlx5+ outer perichondrial cells regarding their contribution to osteoblasts and bone marrow stromal cells. This observation prompted us to ask whether Dlx5+ cells show similar cell fates in later postnatal stages. To this end, we pulsed Col1a1(2.3 kb)-GFP; Dlx5-creER; R26RtdTomato mice at the juvenile (P21), young adult (8W), and adult (5 months, 5M) stages.

Short-chase analyzes at P23 revealed that Dlx5CE-P21 cells were localized near the bone surface in a manner similar to Dlx5CE-P6 cells, distributed across the bone marrow in the metaphyseal and endosteal regions as well as the periosteum and the proliferating and hypertrophic layers of the growth plate cells (Fig. 2A, left panels). In the adult stages (8W and 5M), Dlx5-creER similarly marked endosteal cells adjacent to osteoblasts on the bone surface after the short chase (Fig. 2B, C, left panels, D, Supplementary Fig. 1C, D). After the chase, these Dlx5CE-8W and Dlx5CE-5M cells similarly contributed to osteoblasts and marrow stromal cells (Fig. 2B, C, center, and right panels, D). Notably, compared with the earlier stages, tdTomato+ osteoblasts and stromal cells continued to reside within the metaphysis for a long time (Fig. 2B, C, right panels), likely reflecting the fact that little longitudinal bone growth from the growth plate occurred in the adult stage.

Taken together, Dlx5+ endosteal precursor cells in adult stages contribute to osteoblasts and marrow stromal cells, with some variations in the degree of their respective lineage contribution, contributing to more than 40% of trabecular and cortical bone osteoblasts, regardless of the bone growth.

Single-cell RNA-seq characterization of Dlx5+ endosteal lineage contribution

We further aimed to characterize the lineage contribution of postnatal Dlx5+ endosteal precursors to osteoblasts and bone marrow stromal cells using single-cell RNA-seq analyzes. RNAscope analyzes revealed that Dlx5+ and Fgfr3+ endosteal stromal cells18 were mutually exclusive, as Fgfr3 mRNA was not detected in Dlx5CE-tdT+ cells in Dlx5-creER; R26RtdTomato mice pulsed at P6 or P21 and analyzed at P8 or P23, respectively (Supplementary Fig. 2A). Conversely, Dlx5 mRNA was not detected in Fgfr3CE-tdT+ cells in Fgfr3-creER; R26RtdTomato mice pulsed and analyzed under the same conditions (Supplementary Fig. 2B). To highlight the differential contribution that Dlx5+ endosteal precursor cells make compared to that of Fgfr3+ endosteal stem cells, we harvested cells from Col1a1-GFP; Dlx5-creER; R26RtdTomato and Cxcl12-GFP; Fgfr3-creER; R26RtdTomato mice (both pulsed at P21) at P49, when these cells make a robust lineage contribution (Fig. 3A). Cells were enzymatically isolated from the bone marrow of the femur after the periosteum and growth plate were mechanically removed. To capture the osteoblast-stromal cell lineage, we isolated both GFP+ (Col1a1-GFP+ osteoblasts or Cxcl12-GFP+ CAR cells) and lineage-marked tdTomato+ cells by fluorescence-activated cell sorting (FACS) (Fig. 3A). The integrated scRNA-seq dataset included a total of 7267 cells (Dlx5CE-P21: 2920 cells, Fgfr3CE-P21: 4,347 cells) distributed across 13 clusters, which included Osteoblast (Cl.1: Col1a1+ and Bglap+), Osteo-CAR cell (Cl.3: Tnc+ and Spp1+), Adipo-CAR cell (Cl.0: Cxcl12+ and Apoe+), Chondrocyte (Cl.5: Col2a1+ and Col11a1+), Hypertrophic Chondrocytes (Cl.9: Ibsp+ and Col10a1+), Osteoblast-Chondrocyte Transitional-identity cells (OCT) (Cl.2: Col1a1+ and Col2a1+)18, and other populations (Fig. 3B). The presence of “chondrocytes” in bone marrow stromal cell preparation in our dataset is consistent with previous studies18,32,33, indicating that the epiphyseal growth plate could not be completely removed by the procedure. Importantly, this integrated scRNA-seq dataset represented the entirety of the osteoblast-stromal cell lineage in the bone marrow, encompassing Osteoblasts (Cl.1), Osteo-CAR (Cl.3), and Adipo-CAR (Cl.0) cells with contiguous but discrete cell identities. The dataset also included several non-stromal hematopoietic cell populations with low tdTomato-WPRE mRNA expression, including lymphoid (Cl.4, Cd24a+), erythroid (Cl.6: Gypa+), and myeloid (Cl.7: Lyz2+, Cl.8: S100a8+) cells, which were considered as contaminating populations through the FACS procedure34.

Subsequently, to investigate the contribution of the Dlx5+ endosteal precursor and Fgfr3+ endosteal stem cell derivatives to the osteoblast-stromal cell lineage, we first computationally extracted tdTomato+ cells (n = 3132 cells) from the original integrated scRNA-seq dataset (“D+F tdT”, Fig. 3C, left). As expected, the “D+F tdT” dataset encompassed fewer contaminated cells than the parental “D+F” dataset, presenting the opportunity to interrogate the lineage contribution in a more purified cellular composition. We further subset the data into the Dlx5CE (1445 cells) or Fgfr3CE (1687 cells) to examine their respective lineage contribution. Notably, both Dlx5CE-P21 and Fgfr3CE-P21 cells contributed to Osteoblasts (Cl.1), Osteo-CAR cells (Cl.3), and Adipo-CAR cells (Cl.0) at approximately equivalent proportions (Dlx5CE: Osteoblasts, 30.7%; Osteo-CAR, 19.0%; Adipo-CAR, 27.1%; Fgfr3CE: Osteoblasts, 7.4%; Osteo-CAR, 11.1%; Adipo-CAR, 9.1%) (Fig. 3C, upper right), indicating that Dlx5+ endosteal precursor cells can contribute to the entire spectrum of the osteoblast-stromal cell lineage as Fgfr3+ endosteal stem cells do. However, a notable difference was observed in their contribution to Chondrocytes (Cl.5) and OCT (Cl.2) clusters. First, Dlx5CE did not contribute to Chondrocytes (Cl.5), as expected from our lineage-tracing findings. Second, Dlx5CE contributed to a significantly lower fraction of OCT (Cl.2) (Dlx5CE: 11.6%, Fgfr3CE: 39.7%) (Fig. 3C, upper right), indicating that the OCT identity is an essential feature of Fgfr3+ cell-derivatives. These data also support that Dlx5+ cells represent a precursor cell population but maintain their diverse lineage potential to osteoblasts, Osteo-CAR, and Adipo-CAR cells. We postulate that Dlx5+ precursor cells, which provide a transient source of trabecular bone-making osteoblasts and a persistent source of marrow stromal cells, primarily coincide with Osteo-CAR cells, which exhibit an intermediate transcriptional profile of osteoblasts and marrow stromal cells expressing Tnc and Spp1, as shown in Fig. 3B.

We further asked if there is any difference in the gene expression profiles between Dlx5CE-P21 and Fgfr3CE-P21 cells among Osteoblast (Cl.1), Osteo-CAR (Cl.3), and Adipo-CAR (Cl.0) cell clusters. Interestingly, chondrocyte marker genes, such as Matn3, Acan, and Col9a2, were specifically expressed in Fgfr3CE-P21 cells among these three clusters; in contrast, osteoblast markers such as Dmp1 were highly expressed in Dlx5CE-P21 cells particularly among Osteo-CAR and Adipo-CAR cells (Fig. 3C, right).

Collectively, these results lead us to speculate that Dlx5+ endosteal precursor cells contribute to osteoblasts and two subsets of reticular stromal cells, namely Osteo-CAR and Adipo-CAR cells; however, unlike those derived from Fgfr3+ endosteal stem cells, these cells might no longer maintain a chondrocyte-like signature or contribute to OCT cells, indicating their cells-of-origins might dictate a defined lineage hierarchy.

Hedgehog activation in Dlx5+ osteoblast precursor cells leads to bone fragility

Next, we aimed to define the functional significance of Dlx5+ osteoblast precursor cells in postnatal osteogenesis. To this end, we investigated how manipulating signaling pathways in this cell population affects bone phenotypes. We focused on Hedgehog signaling, a major osteogenic signaling pathway in the context of bone development and heterotopic ossification35–37. Discussion

In this study, we identified a population of Dlx5-expressing osteoblast precursor cells in the endosteal space of the postnatal bone marrow, representing one of the stem/progenitor/precursor populations residing in the highly dynamic endosteal niche. These Dlx5+ endosteal precursor cells provide a robust source of bone-marking osteoblasts and hematopoiesis-supporting marrow stromal cells through postnatal stages; interestingly, Dlx5+ cells provide a transient source of trabecular bone-making osteoblasts in the metaphysis of young growing mice, where the growth plate dictates the longitudinal bone growth. We postulate that Dlx5+ endosteal precursor cells represent earlier cells than Osx+ precursor cells in the osteoblast lineage in the metaphysis and diaphysis, although our approach cannot formally exclude the contribution of cells from the growth plate. Dlx5 and Osx form a transcriptional complex in osteoblasts, which binds to the AT-rich motif and activates osteogenesis24. Osx+ cells behave as osteoblast precursor cells in the context of bone development as described by previous studies18,25–27,41. Notably, Dlx5+ cells exhibit a more prolonged capacity to provide osteoblasts and reticular stromal cells than Osx+ cells do; Osx+ cells possess these capabilities only in early postnatal stages27. Although highly speculative, our findings suggest a lineage hierarchy that may exist within the pool of osteoblast precursor cells of the bone marrow endosteum.

Multiple populations of SSCs are involved in postnatal bone formation, including those identified among growth plate resting zone chondrocytes15,17, metaphyseal marrow stromal cells9, and endosteal stromal cells18, to name a few. The emerging concept is that the SSC properties are developmentally prescribed within the cells-of-origins of the fetal bone anlage26. Our scRNA-seq analyzes revealed that the gene expression profile differs depending on the cells-of-origins, even among cells within the same unbiasedly determined cell cluster. Our findings demonstrate that Dlx5+ endosteal precursor cells and Fgfr3+ endosteal stromal cells can similarly contribute to osteoblasts, Osteo-CAR cells, and Adipo-CAR cells; however, those derived from Fgfr3+ cells universally expressed genes indicative of a chondrogenic identity, whereas those derived from Dlx5+ cells expressed signatures of mature osteoblast markers. It is intriguing to speculate that the skeleton is composed of cells that retain the memory of their original cell identities.

Our investigation reveals that Hedgehog signaling plays a role as a cell fate regulator of Dlx5+ osteoblast precursor cells. We unexpectedly found that Hedgehog activation drives adipogenic fates of Dlx5+ cells at the expense of osteogenic fates in postnatal stages, inducing substantial bone fragility, indicating that Dlx5+ cells are Hedgehog-susceptible osteoblast precursor cells. The Hedgehog signaling pathway plays a critical role in bone development and homeostasis. Hedgehog receptor PTCH1 suppresses SMO activity in the absence of Hedgehog ligands. This inhibition is relieved upon ligand binding to PTCH1, allowing SMO to activate downstream signaling in the Hedgehog pathway. In the absence of PTCH1, SMO becomes constitutively active, resulting in the persistent activation of the Hedgehog signaling cascade, independent of the presence of Hedgehog ligands. Although multiple studies have demonstrated the pro-osteogenic role of Hedgehog signaling in bone development using various mouse genetic models, how Hedgehog signaling regulates osteogenesis remains controversial. Ptch1-deficient (Ptch1+/-) mice and patients with nevoid basal cell carcinoma syndrome showed a high bone mass with high bone turnover in adults42. In contrast, conditional deletion of Ptch1 using constitutively active Osteocalcin-cre showed significant bone thinning in P52 mice, and Smo deletion in Osteocalcin-cre lineage cells increased bone mass in 1-year-old mice43. While these phenomena seem contradictory, high bone turnover phenotypes are shared between the heterozygous Ptch1 deletion and the Osteocalcin-cre-driven conditional one, which is believed to target the mature osteoblast lineage. Distinct aspects of Hedgehog signaling in bone metabolism may be highlighted during the embryonic and postnatal stages; Hedgehog signaling activation may lead to increased bone mass in the embryonic stage but decreased bone mass during the postnatal stage. Mak et al. proposed that Ptch1 deletion in mature osteoblasts causes high bone turnover because of the upregulation of both osteoblastic and osteoclastic genes, leading to an imbalance where osteoclastogenesis is activated more than osteoblastogenesis38. Interestingly, however, it is reported that Smo deletion in Osx-cre caused significant bone thinning and promoted adipogenesis44. Notably, osteoclast activity remains unchanged in these mice. Chan et al. discussed that this could be attributed to the stage-specific and cell context-dependent roles of Hedgehog signaling in regulating osteoclastogenesis44. They also proposed that Hedgehog signaling is inactive in mature osteoblasts, and Osteocalcin-cre marks a majority of osteoblasts and CAR cells45 like Osx-cre in the adult stage. In addition, Rodda et al. demonstrated that deletion of Smo in Osx-positive cells did not affect osteoblast differentiation at embryonic stages46. We recently reported that Hedgehog activation through Ptch1-deficiency enhances clonal competency of PTHrP+ cells within the growth plate, which results in efficient chondrocyte-to-osteoblast transformation and increased trabecular bone formation, emphasizing cell type-specific outcomes of Hedgehog activation. Therefore, the action of Hedgehog signaling is highly context-dependent, and it can be pro-osteogenic or anti-osteogenic depending on cell types and the timing of activation.

Ours is one of the first studies to show a stage-specific effect of Hedgehog activation in specific osteoblast precursor cell types. Notably, postnatal Hedgehog activation in Dlx5+ osteoblast precursor cells induces more severe cortical bone thinning than in Osx+ pre-osteoblasts and osteoblasts/osteocytes. The severe cortical bone thinning induced by Hedgehog activation in Dlx5+ osteoblast precursor cells results in an unusual spontaneous fracture that consistently occurs in the tibial midshaft as soon as the mice initiate ambulation at P21. This phenotype likely reflects a near-complete absence of cortical bone apposition in the mutant mice ever since Hedgehog signaling was activated in Dlx5+ cells at P6, indicating that the suppression of Hedgehog signaling is essential for Dlx5+ cells to facilitate cortical bone formation. Hedgehog-activated Dlx5+ precursor cells underwent an abnormal cell fate shift toward marrow adipocytes in the juvenile stage. Mechanisms underlying this Hedgehog-induced abnormal cell fate shift remain undefined, requiring further investigations. Overall, Dlx5+ osteoblast precursor cells represent a uniquely Hedgehog-susceptible cell population whose proper osteogenic fates require the turning-off of Hedgehog signaling (Fig. 6I).

It should be noted that the Dlx5-creER knock-in allele results in Dlx5 haploinsufficiency. A Dlx5 heterozygous loss-of-function model has been reported to show only modest skeletal changes compared with wild-type, although this model is not identical to our Dlx5-creER allele47.

In conclusion, our findings identify postnatal Dlx5+ cells as Hedgehog-susceptible osteoblast precursors, unraveling a new mechanism governing osteogenic fates of bone marrow endosteal precursor cells.

Methods

Mouse strains

Cxcl12GFP/+48 and Osx-creER25 mice have been described previously. Fgfr3-creER (JAX025809), Dlx5-creER (JAX010705), Rosa26-CAG-loxP-stop-loxP-tdTomato (Ai14: R26R-tdTomato, JAX007914), Col1a1(2.3 kb)-GFP (JAX013134), Ptch1-floxed (JAX030494), Rosa26-loxP-stop-loxP- iSmoM2 (R26R- iSmoM2, JAX005130), Rosa26-SA-loxP-GFP-stop-loxP-DTA (JAX006331), FVB/NJ (JAX001800), and C57BL/6J (JAX000664) mice were acquired from the Jackson laboratory. All procedures were conducted in compliance with the Guidelines for the Care and Use of Laboratory Animals approved by the University of Texas Health Science Center at Houston’s Animal Welfare Committee (AWC), protocol AWC-21-0070 and AWC-24-0075, and the Animal Care and Use Committee of Nagasaki University Graduate School of Biomedical Sciences, protocol 2204261788. All mice were housed in a specific pathogen-free condition and analyzed in a mixed or the C57BL/6J background. Mice were housed in static microisolator cages (Allentown Caging, Allentown, NJ). Access to water and food (irradiated LabDiet 5008, Richmond, IN) was ad libitum. Animal rooms were climate-controlled to provide temperatures of 22–23 °C and 40–65% humidity on a 12 h light/dark cycle (lights on at 0600). For all breeding experiments, creER transgenes were maintained in male breeders to avoid spontaneous germline recombination. Mice were identified by micro-tattooing or ear tags. Tail biopsies of mice were lysed by a HotShot protocol (incubating the tail sample at 95 °C for 30 min in an alkaline lysis reagent, followed by neutralization) and used for PCR-based genotyping (GoTaq Green Master Mix, Promega, and Nexus X2, Eppendorf). Perinatal mice were also genotyped fluorescently (BLS miner’s lamp) whenever possible. Mice with the desired genotype were used, regardless of sex. Mice were euthanized by over-dosage of carbon dioxide or decapitation under inhalation anesthesia in a drop jar (Fluriso, Isoflurane USP, VetOne).

Tamoxifen and induction of cre-loxP recombination

Tamoxifen (Sigma T5648) was mixed with 100% ethanol until completely dissolved. Subsequently, a proper volume of sunflower seed oil (Sigma S5007) was added to the tamoxifen-ethanol mixture and rigorously mixed. The tamoxifen-ethanol-oil mixture was incubated at 60 °C in a chemical hood until the ethanol evaporated completely. The tamoxifen-oil mixture was stored at room temperature until use. Tamoxifen was injected at a dose of 0.25 mg to P6 mice, 1 mg to P21 mice, and 2 mg to P8W and P5M mice intraperitoneally using a 26-1/2-gauge needle (BD309597).

Histology and immunohistochemistry

Samples were dissected under a stereomicroscope (Nikon SMZ-800) and fixed in 4% paraformaldehyde for a proper period, typically ranging from 3 h to overnight at 4 °C, then decalcified in 15% EDTA for a proper period, typically ranging from 3 h to 14 days. Subsequently, samples were cryoprotected in 30% sucrose/PBS solutions and then in 30% sucrose/PBS:OCT (1:1) solutions, each at least overnight at 4 °C. Samples were embedded in an OCT compound (Tissue-Tek, Sakura) under a stereomicroscope and transferred on a sheet of dry ice to solidify the compound. Embedded samples were cryosectioned at 14 µm using a cryostat (Leica CM1850) and adhered to positively charged glass slides (Fisherbrand ColorFrost Plus). Sections were postfixed in 4% paraformaldehyde for 15 min at room temperature. For immunostaining, sections were permeabilized with 0.25% TritonX/TBS for 30 min, blocked with 3% BSA/TBST for 30 min, and incubated with goat anti-osteopontin polyclonal antibody (1:500, R&D, AF808) or rabbit anti-perilipin A/B polyclonal antibody (1:500, Sigma, P1873), rabbit anti-SP7 monoclonal antibody (1:200, Abcam, ab209484), rabbit anti-Aggrecan polyclonal antibody (1:200, Sigma, AB1031), goat anti-ALPL polyclonal antibody (1:200, R&D systems, AF2910), goat anti-LepR polyclonal antibody (1:200, R&D systems, AF497), and rabbit anti-Cathepsin K monoclonal antibody (1:200, Abcam, ab300569) overnight at 4 °C, and subsequently with Alexa Fluor 647-conjugated donkey anti-rabbit IgG (A31573) or Alexa Fluor 633-conjugated goat anti-rat IgG (A21049) (1:400, Invitrogen) for 3 h at room temperature. Sections were further incubated with DAPI (4’,6-diamidino-2-phenylindole, 5 µg/ml, Invitrogen D1306) to stain nuclei prior to imaging.

RNAscope in situ hybridization

RNAscope in situ hybridization was performed with RNAscope Multiplex Fluorescent Detection Kit v2 (Advanced Cell Diagnostics 323100) using Dlx5 (478151), Fgfr3 (440771), and Ptch1 (476231) probes according to the manufacturer’s protocol.

Imaging and cell quantification

Images were captured by an automated inverted fluorescence microscope with a structured illumination system (Zeiss Axio Observer Z1 with ApoTome.3 system) and Zen 3 software. The filter settings used were: FL Filter Set 34 (Ex. 390/22, Em. 460/50 nm), Set 38 HE (Ex. 470/40, Em. 525/50 nm), Set 43 HE (Ex. 550/25, Em. 605/70 nm), Set 50 (Ex. 640/30, Em. 690/50 nm) and Set 63 HE (Ex. 572/25, Em. 629/62 nm). The objectives used were: Fluar 2.5x/0.12, EC Plan-Neofluar 5x/0.16, Plan-Apochromat 10x/0.45, EC Plan-Neofluar 20x/0.50, EC Plan-Neofluar 40x/0.75, Plan-Apochromat 63x/1.40. Images were typically tile-scanned with a motorized stage, Z-stacked, and reconstructed by a maximum intensity projection function. Differential interference contrast was used for objectives higher than 10x. Representative images of at least three independent biological samples are shown in the figures. Quantification of cells on sections was performed using NIH Image J software.

Cell preparation

Soft tissues were carefully removed from the dissected femurs. The periosteum was carefully removed by a raspatorium (Fine Scientific Tools 10091-12). After carefully removing distal epiphyseal growth plates with a dull scissors and cutting off proximal ends, femurs were cut roughly and incubated with 2 Wunsch units of Liberase TM (Sigma/Roche 5401127001) in 2 ml Ca2+, Mg2+-free HBSS at 37 °C for 60 min on a shaking incubator. After cell dissociation, cells were mechanically triturated using an 18-gauge needle with a 1 ml Luer-Lok syringe (BD) and a pestle with a mortar (Coors Tek), and subsequently filtered through a 70 µm cell strainer (BD) into a 50 ml tube on ice to prepare a single cell suspension. These steps were repeated five times, and dissociated cells were collected in the same tube. Cells were pelleted and resuspended in an appropriate medium for subsequent purposes.

Flow cytometry

Dissociated cells were stained by standard protocols with the following antibodies (1:500, eBioscience). Allophycocyanin (APC)-conjugated CD31 (390, endothelial/platelet), CD45 (30F-11, hematopoietic), and Ter119 (TER-119, erythrocytes). Flow cytometry analysis was performed using a four-laser BD LSR Fortessa (Ex. 405/488/561/640 nm) and FACSDiva software. Acquired raw data were further analyzed using FlowJo software (TreeStar). Representative plots of at least four independent biological samples are shown in the figures.

Single-cell RNA-seq analysis of fluorescence-activated cell sorting (FACS)-isolated cells

Cell sorting was performed using a four-laser BD FACS Aria III (Ex.407/488/561/640 nm) high-speed cell sorter with a 100 µm nozzle. EGFP+ and tdTomato+ cells were directly sorted into ice-cold DPBS/1% BSA, pelleted by centrifugation, and resuspended in the appropriate amount of DPBS/1% BSA (1000 cells/μl). Cell numbers were quantified by Countless II automated Cell Counter (ThermoFisher) before loading onto the Chromium Single Cell 3′ v2 microfluidics chip (10x Genomics Inc., Pleasanton, CA). cDNA libraries were sequenced by Illumina NovaSeq 6000 using two lanes and 50-cycle paired-end reads, generating a total of ~770 million reads. The sequencing data were first pre-processed using the 10X Genomics software Cell Ranger. For alignment purposes, we generated and used a custom genome fasta and index file by including the sequences of tdTomato-WPRE in the mouse genome (GRCm39). Further downstream analysis steps were performed using the LIGER30,31 R package. We filtered out cells with less than 500 genes per cell and more than 20% mitochondrial read content. The downstream analysis steps include normalization, identification of highly variable genes across the single cells, scaling based on the number of UMI, dimensionality reduction (principal component analysis [PCA], canonical correlation analysis [CCA], and uniform manifold approximation and projection [UMAP]), unsupervised clustering, and the discovery of differentially expressed cell-type specific markers. Differential gene expression was performed to identify cell-type specific genes using the nonparametric Wilcoxon rank sum test.

Three-dimensional micro-computed tomography analysis

Specimens were placed in a 19 mm diameter specimen holder and scanned using a microCT system (µCT100 Scanco Medical, Bassersdorf, Switzerland). Scan settings were: voxel size 12 µm, 70 kVp, 114 µA, 0.5 mm AL filter, and integration time 500 ms. Analysis was performed using the manufacturer’s software (Scanco µCT Evaluation Program). Trabecular analysis was performed in a region beginning 120 µm below the growth plate and ending after 360 µm (30 slices); a fixed global threshold of 18% (180 on a grayscale of 0–1000) was used to segment mineralized tissue from mineralized tissue. Cortical analysis was performed in the region beginning 600 µm below the growth plate and ending after 360 µm (30 slices) using a fixed global threshold of 23% (230 on a grayscale of 0–1000); a secondary ROI was used to exclude any trabecular bone located in the region of cortical analysis.

Statistical analysis

Results are presented as mean values ± S.D. Statistical evaluation was conducted by the two-tailed Mann–Whitney’s U-test, two-tailed Wilcoxon matched-pairs signed rank test, two-tailed One-way analysis of variance (ANOVA) followed by Dunnett’s multiple comparison test or Log-rank (Mantel–Cox) test using GraphPad Prism software. A P value of <0.05 was considered significant. No statistical method was used to predetermine the sample size. The sample size was determined on the basis of previous literature and our previous experience to give sufficient standard deviations of the mean so as not to miss a biologically important difference between groups. The sample number includes both male and female mice, and data from both sexes were aggregated for lineage-tracing analyzes in Figs. 1 and 2. Male mice were used for scRNA-seq analysis in Fig. 3. Male Dlx5-Ptch1 cKO mice (Figs. 4 and 5) and female Dlx5-Rosa26Smo mice (Fig. 5) were used for statistical analysis. The experiments were not randomized. All of the available mice with the desired genotypes were used for experiments. The investigators were not blinded during experiments and outcome assessments. One femur from each mouse was arbitrarily chosen for histological analysis. Genotypes were not particularly highlighted during quantification.
