Computed tomography poorly detects gastroesophageal varices compared with endoscopy: a real-world single-center retrospective review
Authors: Anvit Reddy, Mohamed Elmasry, Carlos Palacio, Tommy Nguyen, Amy Crisp, Maged Ghali
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundScreening esophagogastroduodenoscopy (EGD) is recommended for all cirrhotic patients at the time of diagnosis, however, non-invasive screening practices for varices have been emerging. CT scans have demonstrated high sensitivity in the detection of esophageal varices. The purpose of this study was to evaluate the sensitivity of CT scans in detecting esophageal and gastric varices when compared to the gold standard of EGD.MethodsAfter institutional board review was obtained, patients from any University of Florida- Jacksonville location with esophageal varices, gastric varices, or both seen on CT abdomen and pelvis, CT chest, CT chest and abdomen pelvis, or EGD were included (n = 470). Exclusion criteria comprised cases where medical record numbers were invalid (n = 239), if esophageal or gastric varices were not reported (n = 25), varices other than esophageal or gastric were present (n = 5) and if CT scans were performed after an EGD. 201 total patients were included in the analysis.ResultsCompared to EGD-verified varices, CT scans demonstrated a sensitivity of 38.3% (95% CI [0.312–0.457]), with scans performed within 7 days of each other improving sensitivity to 39.51% (95% CI [0.294–0.501]), and 40.1% (95% CI [0.327–0.478]), in contrasted studies. CT scans correctly detected varices in 45.90% of thrombocytopenic patients (95% CI [36.85%- 55.16%]).ConclusionAlthough this study’s sensitivity differs markedly from that reported in prior literature, the findings can be understood in the context of methodological limitations. More than the absolute percentages, the observed trends highlight which patient groups may derive the greatest benefit from CT scan surveillance of varices. Overall, routine CT scans may not be surrogates for dedicated radiographic variceal screening. By presenting real-world data on variceal detection rates with CT, this study underscores the potential role of routine imaging not only in risk stratification but also in guiding resource allocation, such as identifying patients who may safely defer endoscopic evaluation versus those who warrant earlier interventions.Trial registrationRetrospectively registered.Keywords: Esophagogastroduodenoscopy (EGD), Computed tomography (CT), Varices, Screening, Non-invasive

Introduction

Varices are enlarged submucosal veins that develop due to portal hypertension and are closely associated with cirrhosis [1]. Complications of varices include variceal hemorrhage, which occurs at a yearly rate of 5–15% and has an associated mortality of at least 20% within six weeks [1]. Consequently, screening esophagogastroduodenoscopy (EGD) is recommended for all cirrhotic patients at the time of diagnosis based on the American Society for Gastrointestinal Endoscopy guidelines [2].

Screening EGD is both diagnostic and therapeutic, serving as secondary prophylaxis in the management of GEV [3]. However, non-invasive screening practices for varices have emerged as they improve patient satisfaction, are cost-effective and are timesaving [2, 4, 5]. One such non-invasive method is utilizing computed tomography (CT) scans. CT scans have been found to be 89–91% sensitive in identifying large esophageal varices as determined by EGD and 87% sensitive for gastric varices [4–6]. Another study found that CT scans had a 97.5% sensitivity in detecting esophageal varices when compared to EGD [7]. The purpose of this study was to evaluate the sensitivity of CT scans in detecting esophageal and gastric varices when compared to the gold standard of EGD.

Methods.

Institutional review board approval was obtained. Valid medical record numbers were evaluated retrospectively. Patients aged 18–70 at the time of their encounter, seen at any University of Florida Health Jacksonville (UFJ) location between January 1, 2017, and January 31, 2024, who were diagnosed with either bleeding or nonbleeding esophageal and/or gastric varices were included. Patients who underwent either a CT abdomen and pelvis, CT chest, or CT chest and abdomen pelvis with or without contrast within the proposed timeframe were also included. Exclusion criteria comprised cases where EGD and CT scans were not performed at a UFJ location, if esophageal or gastric varices were not reported, if varices were diagnosed outside the proposed timeframe and if CT scans were performed after an EGD.

The CT scans at UFJ locations utilized GE Brightspeed 16-slice, GE Revolution 64-slice, and Siemens Dual Source-Somatom Definition Flash 128-slice systems. Scans were interpreted by radiology residents and board-certified radiology physicians. The contrast used was either intravenous iodinated or oral contrast. Endoscopic procedures at UFJ utilized Olympus endoscopes. EGDs performed at UFJ were conducted by gastroenterology fellows and board-certified gastroenterology physicians or solely by board-certified gastroenterology physicians.

Initially, 231 patients met the inclusion criteria (Fig. 1). Of these, 30 were excluded due to the absence of esophageal or gastric varices (25 patients without varices recorded on EGD or CT scan, and 5 patients with other varices noted on CT scan). Patient demographics and characteristics were manually extracted, coded, and compiled (Table 1). Variceal grade and/or size were determined by UFJ Gastroenterologists using the Modified Paquet classification and were extracted from their procedural reports [8]. After calculating true positives, population characteristics were compared. Sensitivity and specificity of CT scans for varices relative to EGD were analyzed along with subgroup analysis (Tables 2, 3, 4, 5 and 6). Subgroup analysis was performed by the Center for Data Solutions at the University of Florida College of Medicine – Jacksonville. Categorical variables were compared using either chi-squared testing or Fisher’s exact test, with a p-value < 0.05 indicating statistical significance. 95% confidence intervals were calculated using Clopper-Pearson Exact Method.

Results

There was no significant difference in patient characteristics regarding sex, race, BMI, age at the time the CT scan was completed, CT scan type, contrast use, indication for CT, age at the time EGD was completed, indication for EGD, grade of varices, size of varices, presence of cirrhosis, etiology of cirrhosis, beta blocker use, and time between EGD and CT scan. A significant difference was noted in platelet levels, with more true positives found in the lower platelet groups and more false negatives in the higher platelet groups.

Compared to known gastroesophageal varices diagnosed via EGD, the overall sensitivity of CT scans was 38.3%. The sensitivity of CT scans performed within one year of the EGD was 38.9%, significantly differing from CT scans performed more than one year after EGD, which had a sensitivity of 33.3%. When stratifying the 178 patients who had their EGD and CT scans within one year of each other into stricter groups, there was a significantly different change in CT scan sensitivity for variceal detection, improving to 39.51% in patients who had the imaging done < 7 days apart from each other (90 patients). Additionally, there was a significant difference in CT scan detection of varices in contrasted studies (40.1%) versus non-contrasted studies (23.8%). When stratifying patients based on platelet levels, there was a significant difference in CT sensitivity for variceal detection, improving to 45.90% in patients with platelet levels < 150. The false positive rate for CT scan detection of varices was significant: 22.2% for esophageal varices, 66.7% for gastric varices, and 11.1% for both esophageal and gastric varices. There was no significant difference in the probability of detecting varices based on their size.

Discussion

In our study, the overall sensitivity of CT scans in detecting esophageal, gastric, or both types of varices was 38.3%, with a specificity of 0%. In one study, CT scans exhibited a sensitivity of 58–89% in detecting varices, with sensitivity increasing to 65–100% when specifically evaluating large varices (>4 mm) [9]. Their study involved two radiologists evaluating CT images obtained within 90 days of EGD for the presence of varices [9]. Our study differed as it was retrospective, so radiologists were not specifically prompted to assess for varices. This likely explains the drastically different sensitivity, as varices were likely detected and underreported rather than undetectable by radiologists. Our subgroup analysis showed that sensitivity increased to 39.51% for scans performed within 7 days of each other compared to 33.3% for those performed >1 year apart, indicating that a shorter interval between EGD and CT scans enhances sensitivity.

In a similar study by Manchec et al., esophageal varices were identified in 94.8% of EGDs and 68% of CT scans, with high-risk patients (defined as grade III or large varices) having esophageal varices detected in 86.4% of CT scans compared to 39.5% in low-risk patients [10]. Our study did not find a significant difference in CT scan sensitivity when performing subgroup analysis between large and small varices, suggesting that varix size is not a reliable characteristic for screening with CT scans. Our study contradicting these findings could be explained by reporting differences at our institution. Some EGD reports included grade or size only, while other reports included both. This could potentially misclassify high and low risk varices.

CT scans may demonstrate utility in detecting gastric varices. Fourteen CT scans identified gastric varices not seen on EGD, with a false positive rate of 66.7% for gastric varix detection and 11.1% for both esophageal and gastric varices. It is possible that EGD failed to detect these varices, while CT scans successfully identified them. In one study, CT scans identified 66.2% of gastric varices, whereas EGD identified only 20% [11].

CT scans may demonstrate utility in detecting GEV in thrombocytopenic patients. In our study, there was a 45.9% sensitivity for CT scan detection in patients with platelets < 150. This finding has been explained in the literature, where CT scans were able to detect GEV with a sensitivity of 100% when patients had platelet levels less than 119,000 [12]. The sensitivity in their study when compared to ours could be from a different patient cohort (median age 14.8 and patients who underwent Fontan surgery).

Overall, our study provides a real-world analysis of CT scan reporting for varices. Although there is extensive literature demonstrating validity for CT scan detection of varices, these scans were done with the intention of variceal detection. Our study showcased that routine CT scans are not surrogates for dedicated CT scans when it comes to variceal surveillance.

This study has limitations. The generalizability of this study is limited due to the methods in which the study was conducted. There likely is an underreporting bias. In patients receiving EGDs, variceal detection is critical, particularly for variceal screening (108 patients) and gastrointestinal bleeding (62 patients), compared to patients receiving CT scans for other reasons, where varices may not be as significant to the overall clinical picture and thus may be underreported. Additionally, with CT scans performed for nonspecific causes and not for the sole purpose of variceal screening, radiologists could have experienced reporting fatigue when creating their reports. Given the study’s purely retrospective nature, the lower sensitivity for variceal detection in this review lacks validity. There is inherent subjectivity among endoscopists in determining the size and location of varices [13]. Patients were not excluded if endoscopic procedures, such as banding, were performed, however this was controlled by including patients who had CT scans before EGDs were performed. The protocol for performing CT scans should also be considered. Dedicated imaging for variceal detection, such as CT esophagography with air insufflation or triple-phase CT scans, may improve sensitivity [10]. The CT scan type could also have affected results. 64-slice CT scanners were found to be superior to 16-slice CT scanners for variceal detection [5]. This study did not perform a subgroup analysis of the type of CT scanner used. Additionally, the time between CT scans and EGD completion could influence results, with shorter intervals possibly increasing CT scan sensitivity, however our interval of < 7 days apart from EGD and CT scan did not drastically affect CT scan sensitivity [10]. Future studies could compare EGDs and CT scans in patients at risk for varices versus those not at risk, evaluating their sensitivity and specificity further. Additionally, a prospective study where CT scans that detected varices were correlated with EGDs afterwards would provide information on whether EGDs potentially under-diagnosed varices, specifically gastric varices.

Conclusion

In our study, when compared to EGD-verified varices, CT scans demonstrate a sensitivity of approximately 38.3%, with contrasted studies showing sensitivity at 40.1%. CT scans detected varices in 45.90% of thrombocytopenic patients, indicating that CT scans sensitivity improve in the setting of worsening liver function. Although this study’s sensitivity differs markedly from that reported in prior literature, the findings can be understood in the context of methodological limitations. More than the absolute percentages, the observed trends highlight which patient groups may derive the greatest benefit from CT scan surveillance of varices. By presenting real-world data on variceal detection rates with CT, this study underscores the potential role of routine imaging not only in risk stratification but also in guiding resource allocation, such as identifying patients who may safely defer endoscopic evaluation versus those who warrant earlier intervention. Establishing standardized CT review patterns could enhance diagnostic consistency across institutions, reduce variability in practice, and ultimately integrate imaging more effectively into longitudinal care pathways for patients at risk of variceal bleeding. More real-world studies are needed to assess both the sensitivity and specificity of EGDs and CT scans in detecting gastroesophageal varices, particularly in the context of gastric varices.
