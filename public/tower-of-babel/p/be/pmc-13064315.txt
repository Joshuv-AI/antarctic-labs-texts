Metabolic effects following initiation of single-inhaler triple therapy in COPD: a prospective observational study
Authors: Noriane A Sievi, Felix Schmidt, Diego M Baur, Kai Fricke, Jonas Herth, Dora Serényi, Maurice Roeder, Nicole Mosca, Silvan Vesenbeckh, Silvia Ulrich, Christian F Clarenbach, Malcolm Kohler

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundChronic obstructive pulmonary disease (COPD) is characterized by airflow limitation, airway inflammation, and oxidative stress. Triple inhaled therapy improves lung function and reduces acute exacerbations, yet its underlying systemic effects remain poorly understood. Exhaled breath analysis offers a non-invasive approach to assessing treatment-related metabolic changes.The objective of this study was to characterize the metabolic changes associated with the initiation of single-inhaler triple therapy in COPD patients through real-time mass spectrometry of exhaled breath.MethodsIn this prospective observational study, patients with COPD were assessed before initiation and after at least 8 weeks of single-inhaler triple therapy. Clinical data, spirometry, symptom scores, and inflammatory markers were collected. Exhaled breath was collected and analyzed by secondary electrospray ionization high-resolution mass spectrometry, and changes in metabolic profiles were evaluated using paired statistics, dimensionality reduction, and pathway enrichment analysis.ResultsTwenty-four patients with COPD (mean (standard deviation) age 65 (7) years, 42% female, median (quartiles) forced expiratory volume in the first second (FEV1) 41 (30, 57) % predicted) completed both visits. Background

Chronic obstructive pulmonary disease (COPD) is a leading cause of morbidity and mortality worldwide, characterized by persistent airflow limitation, chronic airway inflammation, and increased oxidative stress. Despite advances in pharmacological and non-pharmacological treatments, COPD remains a progressive disease marked by acute exacerbations (AECOPD), continuing symptoms such as dyspnea and cough, and gradual functional decline(Viegi et al. 2007; Berry and Wise 2010).

Inhaled therapies are the cornerstone of COPD management. Long-acting bronchodilators form the basis of most regimens, and triple inhaled therapy, i.e. combining an inhaled corticosteroid (ICS), a long-acting beta-agonist (LABA), and a long-acting muscarinic antagonist (LAMA), is recommended by the Global Initiative for Chronic Obstructive Lung Disease (GOLD) guidelines for patients with severe disease, persistent symptoms and/or high risk of further AECOPD despite established inhaled therapy (GOLD 2024).

Randomized controlled trials have demonstrated that triple inhaled therapy reduces exacerbation rates and mortality, and improves lung function, suggesting the effect results from a reduction of airway inflammation, bronchodilation, and blockage of muscarinic receptors to reduce bronchoconstriction (Lipson et al. 2018; Singh et al. 2025). Although these clinical benefits are well documented, the underlying biological mechanisms remain incompletely understood. This gap limits our ability to identify patients most likely to benefit, detect residual disease activity despite treatment, and rationally develop adjunctive or personalized therapeutic strategies. Previous studies have mainly focused on isolated inflammatory or metabolic markers, particularly in relation to ICS (Jen et al. 2012; Lea et al. 2023; Overveld et al. 2008). However, COPD airway inflammation is driven by a complex network of interrelated metabolic and immune pathways, rather than single mediators (Wechsler and Wells 2024). Thus, a narrow focus on individual biomarkers is unlikely to capture the broader biological consequences of inhaled therapy or its early metabolic effects.

Exhaled breath analysis provides a promising non-invasive, real-time window into metabolic and biochemical processes of the body (Wallace and Pleil 2018). Recent studies have shown that exhaled breath analysis can detect disease-related metabolic signatures in COPD that seem to be associated with airway inflammation and oxidative stress-related pathways (Maniscalco et al. 2024; Tirelli et al. 2024; Basler et al. 2024; Gaugg et al. 2019). Notably, real-time breath analysis has recently been used to predict bronchodilator responsiveness to a short-acting bronchodilator in patients with COPD, demonstrating that rapid changes in exhaled metabolites reflect airway responsiveness and bronchoconstriction (Mustafina et al. 2025). Moreover, inhaled drugs themselves induce measurable metabolic responses in the lung that are detectable in exhaled breath. Studies using mass spectrometry–based breath analysis have shown that bronchodilator administration leads to rapid, reproducible changes in endogenous breath metabolites in patients with asthma and COPD (Brinkman et al. 2020; Gaugg et al. 2017).

These findings support the concept that inhaled drugs exert measurable metabolic effects that are detectable directly in exhaled breath. While acute bronchodilator-induced changes in breath compositions have been described, far less is known about how long-term disease-modifying therapies, such as ICS-containing triple inhaled therapy, reshape airway and systemic metabolic pathways in COPD. Understanding these mechanisms is clinically relevant as persistent metabolic dysregulations may contribute to ongoing symptoms, exacerbation risk, and incomplete treatment response despite guideline-based therapy. The extent to which triple inhaled therapy influences metabolic pathways related to airway inflammation and oxidative stress, and the implications of these changes for treatment response, remains uncertain.

We aimed to characterize early metabolic changes associated with initiation of single-inhaler triple therapy in patients with COPD using real-time breath analysis. By mapping treatment-related alterations across multiple metabolic pathways, we sought to generate insights into the interplay between pharmacological therapy, systemic metabolism, and disease modification.

Study design and methods

Study design and subjects

This study is a prospective sub-analysis of the ongoing study Analysis of exhaled breath from patients with lung-/airway diseases (BigExBrESs), conducted at the University Hospital Zurich. The sub-study was conducted between September 2022 and September 2024 and included patients with clinically stable, long-standing COPD, diagnosed according to standard spirometry criteria (post-bronchodilator FEV₁/FVC < 0.7), for whom escalation to single-inhaler triple therapy was recommended by their treating pulmonologist in accordance with Global Initiative for Chronic Obstructive Lung Disease (GOLD) guidelines (GOLD 2024). Eligible participants were aged 18 years or older, had a pre-existing clinical diagnosis of COPD, and were initiating budesonide/glycopyrronium/formoterol fumerate dihydrate (single-inhaler triple therapy, TRIXEO®). Patients unable to provide informed consent or adhere to study procedures due to physical or cognitive limitations were excluded. Clinical stability was defined as the absence of a COPD exacerbation requiring systemic corticosteroids and/or antibiotics within 4 weeks, and no hospitalization for COPD within 8 weeks prior to study inclusion. Patients not meeting these criteria were excluded. Two study visits were scheduled: the first (V1) before single-inhaler triple therapy initiation and the second (V2) at least 8 weeks after initiation. At both visits, exhaled breath analysis was performed and clinical data were collected including exacerbation history, smoking status, medical history, adherence to prior inhaled therapy, spirometry (PFTstik, Geratherm Respiratory GmbH, Hartmannsdorf, Germany) according to ATS/ERS guidelines (Miller et al. 2005) (FEV1% predicted and FVC % predicted, referenced to Global Lung Function Initiative (GLI) reference values of 2022), and symptom scores (COPD Assessment Test (CAT) and modified Medical Research Council (mMRC) dyspnea scale). High-sensitivity C-reactive protein (hs-CRP), blood eosinophil counts, and fractional exhaled nitric oxide (FeNO) were assessed when available as part of routine clinical care on the same day as breath sampling. FeNO was measured using the Vivatmo system (Bosch Healthcare Solutions GmbH, Waiblingen, Germany). The study was approved by the cantonal ethics committee of Zurich (BASEC-Nr. 2021–00539) and adhered to the principles of the Declaration of Helsinki and Good Clinical Practice. The study was registered at ClinicalTrials.gov (NCT05458934) and all participants provided written informed consent.

Breath analysis

Online breath analysis was performed using a Secondary Electrospray Ionization Source (Fossil Ion Technology, Spain) coupled with a Q Exactive Plus high-resolution mass spectrometry (Thermo Fisher Scientific, Germany) (SESI-HRMS). All measurements were performed in the same clinical room under standardized conditions, with ambient temperature maintained at a stable level. Participants were instructed to refrain from eating, drinking (except water), smoking, chewing gum, or brushing their teeth for at least one hour prior to breath sampling. The methodology for breath analysis has been previously described in detail (Gisler et al. 2022; Singh et al. 2019). In short, participants exhaled through a sterile, single-use, commercially available spirometry filter (MicroGard II, Vyaire Medical, USA) into a 50-cm stainless steel tube (4-mm internal diameter, Silconert-coated) connected to the curtain AUX gas port of an Orbitrap spectrometer. To prevent water condensation and minimize compound loss on the tube walls, the tube was heated to 130 °C and insulated with heating tape. A digital capnograph continuously monitored the exhaled breath volume and CO2 concentration to determine alveolar breath (end-tidal). For each participant, approximately 9 to 12 L of exhaled air were collected across six reproducible exhalations under both positive and negative ionization modes, with data acquired in real time.

Spectra analysis

The final dataset included 48 mass spectrometer and capnograph files (24 from V1 and 24 from V2). Preprocessing was performed using a proprietary pipeline developed by Deep Breath Intelligence AG (DBI AG, Switzerland). Mass spectral data were extracted directly from RAW files using proprietary software based on Thermo Fisher Scientific's RawFileReader.

Statistics

To evaluate within-subject changes in breath feature intensities between V1 and V2, fold-change values were calculated for each feature as log2-transformed ratio of signal intensities (log2FC). For each breath feature, one-sample t-test was performed to assess whether log2FC values differed from zero across participants. Resulting p-values were adjusted for multiple testing by computing their corresponding q-values using the Storey-Tibshirani false discovery rate method (Storey 2002).

For exploratory visualization of treatment-associated exhaled breath patterns, breath features were filtered based on both statistical evidence and effect size. Features with |log2FC| ≥ 1 and q < 0.22 were retained, resulting in a subset of 40 breath features. This selection was data-driven and not pre-specified. These features were then used for principal component analysis (PCA) to visualize whether they formed a coherent multivariate pre-post treatment pattern, rather than to provide independent discrimination between visits. PCA loadings were inspected to identify the breath features contributing most strongly to PC1 and PC2, and paired subject trajectories were examined to assess the direction and magnitude of within-subject changes between V1 and V2. To explore biological associations with inflammatory activity, pairwise Pearson correlations were computed between each of the 4,809 breath features and the clinical markers FeNO and hs-CRP, respectively. For each marker, breath features showing relevant correlations (r > 0.5) and associated statistical evidence (q-value < 0.22) were selected and subjected to PCA. The first principal component derived from these correlated feature sets was then correlated with the corresponding clinical marker to assess whether coordinated exhaled breath patterns tracked airway or systemic inflammatory signals.

Statistical analysis was performed with R (version 4.4.1, R Core Team, Vienna, Austria).

Compound assignment

To interpret breath features in a biological context, pathway enrichment analysis was performed using MetaboAnalyst v6.0 with the mummichog algorithm (Pang et al. 2021). This approach enabled hypothesis generation by mapping ions to potential metabolic pathways. All breath features were ordered by decreasing statistical significance based on one-sample t-test p-values, and the top 10 % were selected as a feature-ranking threshold for exploratory pathway enrichment analysis rather than as a criterion for statistical significance. Ionization adducts considered in the analysis are detailed in the supplementary material (Table E1); mass tolerance was 2 ppm. Putative molecular annotations were assigned by matching exact masses to the Human Metabolome Database (HMDB) and cross-referencing volatile organic compounds previously reported in human breath (Gisler et al. 2022; Drabińska et al. 2021; Moura et al. 2023). Compound annotations are reported as Schymanski level 4 (Schymanski et al. 2014).

Results

At University Hospital Zurich, 29 patients with long-standing COPD initiating triple inhaled therapy with TRIXEO® were enrolled. Five patients were lost to follow-up, leaving 24 for analysis (Fig. 1). Mean (SD) age was 65 (7) years, and 42 % were female patients. At V1, 54 % were current smokers and 42 % former smokers, with a smoking history of median (quartiles) of 44 (30, 50) pack-years. Median (quartiles) FEV1 % pred. and FVC % pred. was 41 (30, 57) % and 66 (59, 87) %, respectively. 5 of these 24 patients had a co-existing asthma diagnosis. 17 of 24 patients had at least two moderate or one severe AECOPD in the previous year. Patients were symptomatic with a mean (SD) CAT of 19 (7) points. Median (quartiles) FeNO was 35 (20, 69) ppm, hs-CRP 4.8 (1.2, 54.0) mg/L, and eosinophil count of 0.11 (0.06, 0.30) G/l. None of the participants were receiving systemic corticosteroids or antibiotics at the time of breath sampling. The follow-up visit (V2) occurred a median (quartiles) 75 (56, 94) days after initiation of single-inhaler triple therapy. During the study period, no severe adverse events including pneumonia or exacerbations of COPD occurred. Four patients reported mild anticholinergic side effects (dry mouth and/or nasal dryness), while no changes in concomitant medications were recorded between visits. Between V1 and V2, patients showed improvements in symptom scores (CAT and mMRC), whereas lung function did not improve in response to initiation of single-inhaler triple therapy. Changes in breath features between visit 1 and 2

The processed data matrix contained 4,809 breath features, consisting of 3,004 mass-to-charge ratios (m/z) acquired in positive ion mode and 1,805 in negative ion mode. Of the 4,809 breath features, 172 were statistically significantly upregulated and 63 downregulated at V2. After multiple-testing correction, 40 breath features with log2FC ≥ 1 or ≤ −1, and q-value < 0.22 were retained for exploratory analysis. These features were then used for PCA. The first principal component (PC1) accounted for 65.1 % of the variance, while the second principal component (PC2) explained 8.2 % of the variance. PC1, which was driven by the overall variability in breath metabolites, primarily captured inter-individual differences and did not clearly distinguished visits. In contrast, PC2 showed a visual distinction between V1 and V2. (Supplementary Figure E1) The loadings of PC1 and PC2, which help explain the contributions of specific breath features to each component, are presented in Supplementary Figure E2.

Metabolic pathway alterations after initiation of single-inhaler therapy

Pathway enrichment analysis revealed predominant changes in metabolic pathways associated with beta-oxidation, linoleate metabolism, and glycosphingolipid metabolism. At V2, breath features putatively associated with beta-oxidation (hydroxy-tetradeca-dienoate, hydroxy-tetradec-enoate, and hydroxy-tetradeca-trienoate) were increased. Within the linoleate pathway, metabolites tentatively annotated as lipid peroxidation products, including trans-4,5-epoxy-decenal, 3,4-epoxynonanal, azelaic acid, 4-hydroxynonenal, and 4-oxo-2-nonenal, increased, whereas others such as gamma-linoleate, 9,10-epoxyoctadecenoic acid (EpOME), 12,13-EpOME, and 13S-hydroxyoctadecadienoic acid decreased. Changes in pathways associated with glycosphingolipid metabolism included higher levels of breath features putatively assigned to phosphorylcholine, hexadecanal, and galactose/glucose, alongside lower levels of malonic acid, dehydrosphinganine, and phytosphingosine, indicating alterations in membrane lipid and sphingolipid homeostasis. Discussion

Triple inhaled therapy is widely used in the management of COPD, yet its systemic biological effects remain incompletely understood. Our findings suggest that initiation of single-inhaler triple therapy is associated with early changes in exhaled breath profiles consistent with modulation of airway inflammation and oxidative pathways. Rather than demonstrating direct pathway inhibition, the observed patterns are compatible with reduced inflammatory activity and altered oxidative stress-related metabolism. These results point to a potential mechanistic trade-off between inflammation control and oxidative burden, emphasizing the importance of clarifying the long-term metabolic consequences of triple inhaled therapy in clinical practice.

Because COPD is a progressive disease in which symptom burden and exacerbation risk typically increase over time, pharmacological treatment follows a stepwise escalation strategy. Triple inhaled therapy, combining a LAMA, a LABA, and an ICS, represents the most advanced inhaled regimen and is recommended for patients with moderate-to-severe COPD who remain symptomatic and/or at elevated risk of exacerbations despite dual therapy (GOLD 2024). The rationale lies in their complementary mechanisms: LAMA reduces acetylcholine-mediated bronchoconstriction, LABA enhances bronchodilation via β₂-receptor activation, and ICS suppresses inflammation by regulating glucocorticoid-responsive genes and inhibiting pro-inflammatory cytokines (Tashkin and Fabbri 2010). When combined, these agents exert superior, synergistic effects compared with LAMA/LABA or LABA/ICS combinations (Lipson et al. 2018). In our cohort, treatment escalation involved initiation or substantial increase of ICS exposure, with all but one patient experiencing an increase in ICS-equivalent dose upon transition to single-inhaler triple therapy.

While most clinical trials of triple inhaled therapy focused on AECOPD, lung function, and symptoms(Lipson et al. 2018; Singh et al. 2025), less attention has been given to the biological pathways underlying these benefits, particularly ICS-mediated protection against AECOPD. Although ICS are known to reduce airway inflammation, their precise molecular targets and the subgroups of patients most likely to benefit remain incompletely defined. Moreover, corticosteroids do not target all the inflammatory pathways in COPD pathogenesis(Jen et al. 2012; Lea et al. 2023).

Our data indicate that ICS within triple inhaled therapy is associated with suppression of linoleate-derived pathways. Specifically, ICS were associated with a reduction in breath features assigned to cytokine-driven inflammatory pathways, potentially mediated through inhibition of the cyclooxygenase pathway (COX-2). COX-2, which is upregulated in response to pro-inflammatory stimuli, metabolizes arachidonic acid into prostaglandins (Mitchell et al. 1994). Consistent with this mechanism, we observed a marked reduction in breath features putatively assigned to gamma-linoleate, a precursor in the arachidonic acid pathway, following initiation of single-inhaler triple therapy.

In addition, linoleate can be metabolized by cytochrome P450 (CYP450) epoxygenases into the linoleic epoxides 9,10-EpOME and 12,13-EpOME, also known as leukotoxin and isoleukotoxin, respectively, which are produced by inflammatory leukocytes (Spector and Kim 2015). These metabolites have been reported to be elevated in lung lavage samples from patients with ARDS (Moura et al. 2023; Ishizaki et al. 1999), while experimental studies demonstrated that high doses of leukotoxin can induce pulmonary cell damage (Hildreth et al. 2020). Consistent with this, we observed decreased levels of breath features putatively assigned to 9,10-EpOME and 12,13-EpOME following initiation of single-inhaler triple therapy, supporting ICS-mediated suppression of leukocyte-driven inflammatory pathways.

In addition to the conversion of linoleate into arachidonic acid or EpOMEs, it can also undergo lipid peroxidation reactions, generating reactive aldehydes. We observed increases in metabolites putatively annotated as products of lipid peroxidation pathways, such as 3,4-Epoxynonanal and 4-Hydroxynonenal (4-HNE), both consistent with 15-LOX-mediated linoleate metabolism, after initiation of single-inhaler triple therapy. Elevated aldehyde levels are widely recognized markers of oxidative stress and have been reported in stable COPD(Breitzig et al. 2016; Spickett 2013; Rahman and Adcock 2006; Boots et al. 2003). Importantly, oxidative stress is a central driver of COPD pathogenesis, contributing to persistent inflammation, disease progression, and comorbidities (MacNee n.d.); Kirkham and Barnes 2013; Lee and Thomas 2009; Bellanti et al. 2025).

However, the impact of ICS on oxidative stress remains insufficiently explored. Notably, emerging evidence from small studies suggests that corticosteroids may paradoxically exacerbate oxidative stress in patients with COPD, thereby further reducing corticosteroid sensitivity through mechanisms involving phosphorylation of the glucocorticoid receptor and inactivation of histone deacetylase-2 (HDAC-2) (Ito et al. 2005; Lewis et al. 2021; Adcock and Barnes 2008).

These observations give hypothesis for a mechanistic model in which ICS may suppress COX-2 and CYP450 epoxygenase pathways, thereby increasing substrate availability for LOX-driven peroxidation pathways. This shift could favor the generation of cytotoxic aldehydes such as 4-HNE and other LOX-derived metabolites, some of which have been linked to steroid-resistant inflammation (Panda et al. 2017).

In line with this, we identified increases of additional breath features after initiation of single-inhaler triple therapy, putatively annotated as markers of lipid peroxidation and membrane damage pathways, including hexadecanal and phosphorylcholine, which could compromise mitochondrial integrity (Frostegard 2022; Philippova 2014). Decreased levels of metabolites putatively assigned to hydrosphinganine and malonic acid further suggest disturbances in sphingolipid biosynthesis and fatty acid catabolism, processes essential for membrane stability and efficient β-oxidation (Pralhada Rao et al. 2013; Bartlett and Eaton 2004).

We also detected elevated breath features putatively annotated to intermediates of incomplete fatty acid oxidation after initiation of single-inhaler therapy, such as hydroxy-tetradeca derivatives, consistent with impaired mitochondrial β-oxidation and accumulation of reactive oxygen species, thereby exacerbating oxidative stress (Aqeel et al. 2025; Bialas et al. 2016).

Taken together, our findings support the hypothesis that triple inhaled therapy may reduce cytokine-driven inflammatory pathways while simultaneously favoring lipid peroxidation pathways and mitochondrial dysfunction in patients with COPD. As all metabolites were annotated at Schymanski level 4, these assignments are putative and based on accurate-mass matching. Therefore, pathway interpretations should be considered hypothesis-generating rather than definitive biochemical identification.

Current GOLD recommendations for the use of triple inhaled therapy are grounded in evidence from large randomized controlled trials, which primarily demonstrated significant reductions in exacerbation risk (Lipson et al. 2018; Singh et al. 2025). While these trials firmly established efficacy on clinical endpoints, the underlying biological mechanisms driving these benefits remain largely unknown. Our findings extend this evidence by generating the hypothesis that, in addition to reducing cytokine-driven inflammatory pathways, triple inhaled therapy could also modulate oxidative and metabolic pathways. Clinically, the hypothesis that triple inhaled therapy may suppress inflammation while unmasking oxidative stress highlights the need to further investigate both the strengths and residual vulnerabilities of current COPD management. Future studies with longer follow-up should determine whether these early biological and metabolic changes are maintained, evolve, or normalize over time, and whether they ultimately translate into improvements in clinical outcomes such as symptoms, exercise capacity, and lung function. Moreover, further studies are required to validate these exploratory findings and to establish whether breath-based signatures can be used to guide or support the development of adjunctive therapeutic strategies, such as antioxidants or mitochondrial modulators (Rahman 2006; Fairley et al. 2023). Incorporating breath-based biomarkers into clinical trials may enable broader detection of treatment responses, more accurate risk stratification, and progress toward precision medicine in COPD care.

Although no established biomarkers exist for airway inflammation and oxidative stress in COPD, we explored correlations between breath metabolites and conventional inflammatory markers (Munakata 2012; Larsson 2008). In exploratory analyses, inverse correlations with FeNO suggest that changes in breath signature may be sensitive to therapy-associated modulation of airway inflammatory activity, while associations with hs-CRP may reflect subtle variations in systemic inflammatory status. These results highlight the potential of composite breath signatures to capture nuanced metabolic shifts beyond dominant inflammatory pathways, though they require validation in larger cohorts.

This study has limitations. First, baseline therapy was heterogeneous, with some patients already receiving ICS-containing regimens. However, ICS-equivalent dose calculations demonstrated that all but one patient experienced a relevant increase in ICS exposure (ranging from 1.07- to > 14-fold) upon initiation of single-inhaler triple therapy. Although this heterogeneity introduces variability, the vast majority of patients underwent either a substantial increase in ICS dose and/or a change in formulation, indicating that the transition to single-inhaler triple therapy was biologically meaningful and likely contributed to the observed changes in exhaled breath profiles. To evaluate whether baseline ICS exposure biased the results, we performed an exploratory PCA of log2FC in all breath features stratified by baseline ICS use, which showed no clustering according to prior ICS exposure (Supplementary Figure E5). Nevertheless, patients already receiving triple therapy may have exhibited attenuated or qualitatively different responses, and the small size of this subgroup precluded formal stratified analyses. Accordingly, baseline treatment heterogeneity may limit generalizability of these findings. In addition, only one single-inhaler formulation (budesonide/glycopyrronium/formoterol) was studied, and other triple combinations may yield different metabolic effects, although the active components are likely the main drivers. Second, the lack of a contemporaneous control group limits causal inference. Although breath measurements were performed under highly standardized conditions (same room, stable temperature, and pre-measurement restrictions on eating, drinking except water, smoking, chewing gum, and tooth brushing) and visits were distributed over a two-year period with a median interval of 75 days, residual confounding by environmental, seasonal, behavioral, or physiological factors cannot be fully excluded. Longitudinal SESI-HRMS data demonstrated that intra-individual variability in breath metabolites typically ranges from 6–7 %, while inter-individual differences can exceed 40–50 % (Dev Singh et al. 2019). This variability may contribute to both intra-individual fluctuations (within-subject variability) and inter-individual differences (between-subject variability), but the consistent observed changes between V1 and V2 indicate metabolic changes beyond what would be expected from normal intra-individual variability. Despite clinical stability at inclusion, defined by the absence of exacerbations requiring systemic corticosteroids or antibiotics within the preceding 4 weeks and no hospitalization within 8 weeks, baseline systemic inflammatory activity varied substantially, as reflected by elevated hs-CRP values in a subset of patients. This variability likely reflects the well-recognized inflammatory heterogeneity of stable COPD rather than unrecognized acute infection; however, underlying inflammatory processes that may have contributed to interindividual variability in treatment responses cannot be excluded. To assess this potential confounding, we performed an exploratory PCA of within-subject changes stratified by baseline hs-CRP status, which showed no clustering or separation of patients with elevated versus normal hs-CRP levels (Supplementary Figure E6). These findings suggest that elevated baseline hs-CRP did not drive the observed shift in exhaled breath pattern.

Changes in smoking behavior occurred in a minority of patients (n = 3) and may have contributed to reduced inflammatory signals. However, exploratory PCA of within-subject changes stratified by smoking cessation status showed no clustering of these individuals, suggesting that smoking cessation did not drive the overall shift in exhaled breath pattern (Supplementary Figure E7). Short-term influences, such as timing of medication intake, diet, or subclinical recovery, may also have affected individual metabolites and cannot be fully excluded.

Third, the small sample size limits statistical power, but the study design provided valuable within-subject insights. Finally, definitive molecular identification in breath analysis remains challenging, as exhaled metabolites are present at extremely low concentrations and many chemically distinct compounds share the same elemental composition, and therefore the same accurate mass, leading to overlapping signals and ambiguous annotations. As a consequence, most detected features can only be assigned Schymanski level 4 (putative) annotations or remain unannotated. Confirmation of molecular identity would require chromatographic separation by ultra-performance liquid chromatography coupled to tandem mass spectrometry (UPLC–MS/MS) and comparison with authentic reference standards. However, this is technically demanding for breath samples due to their ultra-trace concentrations and the volatility and instability of many breath metabolites, which often preclude efficient trapping, separation, and fragmentation. Although confirmation with UPLC-MS/MS and authentic standards was beyond the scope of this work, the high mass accuracy achieved provides a robust foundation for future targeted validation studies.

In this study, metabolite identification and pathway analysis were based on database matching of accurate mass measurements, with a mean mass difference of approximately 0.38 ppm. Pathway enrichment analysis was performed using the mummichog framework on a ranked subset of features (top 10 %), which may include weak or unstable signals and depends on assumed adduct species. Although the adducts considered in our study have been described previously in SESI-HRMS, the enrichment results should be interpreted as hypothesis-generating rather than confirmatory.

Despite these limitations, our results highlight the potential of breath metabolomics to capture early therapy-associated metabolic signatures related to inflammatory and oxidative stress pathways in COPD. In the future, these breath-based signatures could potentially contribute to the development of non-invasive approaches for treatment monitoring, patient stratification, and early detection of metabolic responses. Larger longitudinal validation studies incorporating predictive modeling will be required before breath analysis can be considered for clinical application.
