A Bailout Multiwire Technique for Retrieval of a Deployed Coronary Stent Entrapping a Guidewire
Authors: Zhenzhen Ding, Shufu Chang, Mengzhang Wu, Qing Qin, Rende Xu, Jianying Ma, Junbo Ge
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundGuidewire entrapment during percutaneous coronary intervention (PCI) is a rare but potentially life-threatening complication. Retrieval becomes particularly challenging when the guidewire is trapped beneath a deployed stent.Case SummaryA 72-year-old man with severe calcified left main artery disease underwent PCI. During stent deployment from the left main to the left anterior descending artery, a guidewire in the left circumflex artery became entrapped beneath the stent. After conventional retrieval techniques failed, a bailout strategy using multiple guidewires to entangle and extract the deployed stent was successfully performed.DiscussionIn selected high-risk scenarios, percutaneous retrieval of a deployed stent using a multiwire entanglement technique may be considered when conventional methods fail.Take-Home MessagesMaintaining a high index of recognition for guidewire entrapment is essential throughout the procedure of PCI. Intentional coronary stent retrieval via a multiwire entanglement technique may serve as a feasible bailout strategy for entrapped guidewires.Key words: complication, percutaneous coronary intervention, stents

Illustration Summarizing the Management of the Patient

In a case of percutaneous coronary intervention for severe left main calcification, the left circumflex artery guidewire was entrapped under a deployed left main-to-left anterior descending artery stent. When conventional retrieval failed, a multiwire technique resulted in the successful retrieval of the deployed stent and the entrapped guidewire.

History of Presentation

A 72-year-old man presented with recurrent exertional chest tightness for 2 months, which had worsened over the preceding week. Coronary computed tomography angiography performed at an outside hospital revealed calcified plaques in the left main (LM) artery, with a maximum stenosis of 85%. Additional findings included 60% stenosis in the proximal left anterior descending artery (LAD), a myocardial bridge in the mid-LAD, and 30% stenosis in the mid–left circumflex artery (LCX). No significant stenosis was noted in the right coronary artery. The patient had been treated with statins before admission.

Medical History

The patient had no history of hypertension, diabetes mellitus, or smoking.

Differential Diagnosis

The differential diagnosis included gastroesophageal reflux disease, peptic ulcer disease, and thoracic radiculopathy.

Investigations

Electrocardiography and transthoracic echocardiography showed no significant abnormalities. Laboratory tests before the procedure revealed a cardiac troponin T level of 0.01 ng/mL, a B-type natriuretic peptide level of 77.4 pg/mL, and a low-density lipoprotein cholesterol level of 1.63 mmol/L.

Management

Percutaneous coronary intervention (PCI) was performed via right radial access using a 6-F BL 3.0 (Terumo) guiding catheter. VersaTurn (Abbott) and Sion (Asahi Intecc Medical) guidewires were advanced into the distal LAD and LCX, respectively.

After predilation with a 2.5 × 15-mm noncompliant balloon, intravascular ultrasound (IVUS; Boston Scientific) demonstrated diffuse fibrocalcific plaques extending from the LAD to the LM (Figures 1A to 1C). A 3.0 × 18-mm drug-eluting stent was deployed from the LM ostium to the proximal LAD (Figure 1D). Postdeployment angiography revealed that the LCX guidewire had formed a loop beneath the stent and could not be retrieved (Figure 1E). Attempts using small balloons and a microcatheter failed to cross the distal stent edge. During these attempts, the microcatheter tip fractured and remained within the stent (Figure 1E).

Diagnostic Coronary Angiography and Entrapment of Coronary Guidewire

(A) Cranial and (B) right anterior oblique caudal views of the left coronary artery. (C) Intravascular ultrasound showing diffuse fibrocalcific plaques extending from the LAD to the LM. (D) A 3.0 × 18-mm drug-eluting stent inserted to the ostial LM and proximal LAD. LAD = left anterior descending artery; LM = left main artery.

A femoral approach was then established using a 7-F EBU 3.5 guiding catheter (Medtronic). Three guidewires (1 Sion and 2 Sion Blue; Asahi Intecc Medical) were advanced through the stent struts into the distal vessels (Figures 2A and 2B). These wires were rotated simultaneously to achieve tight entanglement with the stent (Figure 2A). Gradual traction enabled successful mobilization and retrieval of the stent into the ascending aorta. After pulling the stent back into the EBU3.5 guiding catheter, the original Sion and VersaTurn guidewires were withdrawn, during which the entrapped guidewire fractured due to the tight entanglement (Figure 2C, Video 1). Then the stent accompanied with the fractured microcatheter tip were successfully retrieved via the femoral artery (Figure 2D).

Management of Guidewire Entrapment With Retrieval of the Stent

(A, B) "Multiwire" technique to entangle the deployed stent through Ping-Pong guide catheters. Guidewires (blue) were intentionally crossed through the stent struts, with one advanced into the LCX and the other 2 into first diagonal artery (D1). All guidewires were simultaneously locked and concurrently rotated with torque. (C) The original wires were extracted from entangled wires in the ascending aorta. (D) Successful retrieval of the stent (asterisk). (E) Satisfactory final angiographic result. LAD = left anterior descending artery; LCX = left circumflex artery.

Subsequent angiography and IVUS confirmed no dissection or hematoma (Video 2). A new stent (3.5 × 20 mm) was implanted from the LM to the proximal LAD, followed by postdilation with noncompliant balloons. Final imaging demonstrated optimal stent expansion without residual stenosis (Figure 2E).

Follow-Up

The patient remained clinically stable, with no adverse cardiovascular events during 3 months of follow-up.

Discussion

Guidewire entrapment is an uncommon complication of PCI, with an incidence of 0.08% to 0.2%,1,2 but it carries significant risks including thrombosis, vessel perforation, and myocardial infarction.3 High-risk lesion characteristics include bifurcations, tortuous anatomy, heavy calcification, chronic total occlusions, and in-stent restenosis.4

In this case, heavy calcification and procedural complexity contributed to guidewire entrapment beneath a deployed stent. Notably, excessive focus on precise stent positioning at the LM ostium may have contributed to overlooking early warning signs. When the guidewire remains intact, standard bailout strategies include advancing a microcatheter or small balloon to increase surrounding space and facilitate extraction.3 If unsuccessful, surgical intervention is often required.3 In our patient, the guidewire was folded and compressed beneath the LAD stent, making conventional techniques ineffective.

Percutaneous retrieval of a fully deployed stent is generally avoided due to the high risk of vascular injury.5 However, in this scenario, leaving the entrapped guidewire in situ posed a greater clinical risk. We therefore adopted a multiwire entanglement technique using a larger guiding catheter via femoral access to enhance support and maneuverability. The use of 3 guidewires significantly increased entanglement strength, enabling successful retrieval of the stent. Importantly, IVUS confirmed the absence of vascular complications after the procedure.

This case highlights that, in carefully selected situations, percutaneous stent retrieval may serve as a feasible bailout strategy.

Conclusions

Prevention remains paramount in avoiding PCI complications. In calcified and complex lesions, careful monitoring of side branch guidewires is essential. When guidewire entrapment occurs and conventional techniques fail, percutaneous retrieval of a deployed stent using a multiwire technique may be considered in experienced centers.
