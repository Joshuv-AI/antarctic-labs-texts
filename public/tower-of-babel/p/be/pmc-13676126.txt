Heterogeneity in proteomic risk factors for coronary heart disease by sex and menopause status
Authors: Janina M Herold, Aditya Surapaneni, Josef Coresh, Lucas Mavromatis, Kelly V Ruggles, Eric Boerwinkle, Harmony Reynolds, Christie M Ballantyne, Bing Yu, Carina Flaherty, Morgan E Grams
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Highlights•Circulating protein profiles differ by sex and menopausal stage.•Proteomic changes appear during the early menopausal transition.•Proteomic associations with CHD risk differ by sex and menopausal status.•Sex-and menopause-informed proteomic markers may improve CHD risk prediction.•Findings support further studies of underlying biological mechanisms.Keywords: Coronary heart disease, Proteomics, Sex differences, Menopause, Risk prediction

Introduction

Coronary heart disease (CHD) is the leading cause of morbidity and mortality worldwide [1]. Sex differences in the risk for CHD are well described, with women more likely to present with atypical signs and symptoms and less likely to receive interventions [2,3]. Sex differences in risk also vary by age, with men more susceptible to heart disease in mid-life and women increasingly susceptible in late life [4]. Some of the differences in the risk of CHD between men and women can be explained by risk factors like hypertension, dyslipidemia, diabetes, smoking, or obesity [5]. However, standard risk tools like SCORE2 and the pooled cohort equation, which incorporate most of these risk factors, often fail to identify many high-risk women [6]. Hormonal changes around menopause, including a decline in estrogen, progesterone, and testosterone levels, may lead to adverse lipid profiles and detrimental vascular effects. Indeed, many sex differences in risk factor prevalence and severity attenuate after age 55–60 years [[7], [8], [9], [10]].

Unravelling the molecular basis of CHD separately in women and men might improve the understanding of sex differences, help to optimize risk prediction models, and identify potential sex-specific therapeutic targets. Proteomic profiles reflect genetic background as well as environmental influences and provide a promising approach to understanding the molecular mechanisms driving sex differences in CHD. Prior studies have identified proteomic associations with incident and progressive CHD [11,12], but they have not consistently conducted sex-stratified analyses, assessed menopausal status, utilized long-term follow-up, and each had fewer proteins measured [[13], [14], [15]].

To address these gaps, our study leveraged data from the Atherosclerosis Risk in Communities (ARIC) study, comprising 4,205 middle-aged men and 5,829 women with proteomic profiling of 4,877 proteins and an extended observational period for CHD spanning up to 30 years. Our study aimed to investigate sex-specific and menopausal differences in circulating protein levels and their associations with coronary heart disease (CHD), elucidate underlying biological pathways contributing to disease heterogeneity, and evaluate the potential added value of integrating protein data into established risk equations.Methods

Study design and participants

The ARIC study is an ongoing community-based cohort study of 15,792 participants from four communities in the United States (Washington County, MD; Forsyth County, NC; suburbs of Minneapolis, MN; Jackson, MS). This study was covered under the NYU Langone Health Institutional Review Board site approval for ARIC (i23–01,344). Informed consent was obtained from all participants in the ARIC study. We performed cross-sectional analyses including participants from visit 2 (1990–1992) who had no previous CHD event whose proteomic profiling passed quality control. The small number of participants who were neither White nor Black race at any center as well as the non-White participants in Minnesota and Maryland were excluded per standard ARIC analytical protocol. We excluded individuals with missing values in any covariables resulting in an analyzed sample of 10,034 individuals (Supplementary Figure 1).

Protein measurements and proteomic data processing

Plasma concentrations of 5,284 aptamers were measured using the SomaScan version 4 assay provided by SomaLogic, Inc. (Boulder, CO). As described previously [16], the measurement is based on short, modified DNA aptamers (SOMAmers™) that bind specifically to target proteins. After binding, the amount of each protein is inferred by quantifying the DNA sequence of its corresponding SOMAmer using fluorescence-based detection [16,17]. Measurement reliability was assessed via the Bland-Altman (CVBA) method using blind duplicates from 514 participants at visit 2, yielding a median CVBA of 6.3% and an intraclass correlation coefficient of 0.93. Of the 5,284 SOMAmers assessed in this study, 447 were excluded due to poor reproducibility (Bland-Altman coefficient of variation >50% or low variance), non-specific binding, or targeting non-proteins, yielding 4,877 proteins assayed at one of three dilution factors [16).

Outcome definition

Incident CHD was defined as the first occurrence of either a hospitalization due to a definite or probable myocardial infarction (MI) or fatal CHD between visit 2 and Dec 31st, 2022. Case ascertainment was conducted via telephone follow-up (annually before 2012, and semiannually thereafter) with participants or next of kin, active surveillance of hospital health and discharge records, and linkage to the National Death Index or local death registries, and all cases were adjudicated by an expert panel [18].

Covariable ascertainment

Covariable definitions followed established ARIC cohort methodology [19,20]. Age was derived from birth date. Information on sex, race, medical history, medication use, and smoking status (current/former/never), was obtained using standardized self‐report questionnaires. Three seated blood pressure measurements were obtained after a 5-minute rest using a random-zero mercury sphygmomanometer, and blood pressure was recorded as the mean of the last two readings. Hypertension was defined as elevated blood pressure (>140/90 mmHg) or taking antihypertensive medications. Hormone-replacement therapy (HRT) was defined as the intake of medroxyprogesterone acetate, conjugated estrogens, estradiol, estrogen (unspecified), progesterone, or norethindrone. Height and weight were measured by study technicians to calculate Body Mass Index (BMI) as weight in kilograms divided by the square of height in meters. HbA1c was measured in whole blood using HPLC methods aligned to the Diabetes Control and Complications Trial [21]. High‐density lipoprotein (HDL) cholesterol and total cholesterol concentrations were measured by using automated enzymatic assays. eGFR was calculated from serum creatinine and cystatin C (eGFRcr-cys) using the Chronic Kidney Disease Epidemiology Collaboration equation [22].

Menopause status (pre-/peri‑/postmenopausal) was assessed via self-reported menopause or menstrual periods during the past 2 years. Menopausal status was assessed at the time of protein measurement: Women who reported no menstrual periods within the two years prior to visiting the study center were classified as postmenopausal. Perimenopausal women were classified based on reported changes in cycle regularity or menopausal symptoms but reporting menstrual bleeding within the past two years. Premenopausal women reported having regular menstrual periods.

Statistical analyses

We summarized descriptive statistics as count with percentages for categorical variables and mean ± standard deviation for continuous variables.

Protein concentrations were log2-transformed. This allows interpretation of differences on a relative scale, where a one-unit difference corresponds to a two-fold change in protein quantification which estimates relative protein concentration.

Sex and menopausal differences in protein concentrations

First, we investigated which proteins showed differences in their plasma concentrations between men and women. We tested each of the 4,877 proteins separately via linear regression with sex and adjusting for known CHD risk factors (i.e., age, race-center, smoking, hypertension medication, SBP, HbA1c, BMI, and eGFRcr-cys). To evaluate differences by menopausal status, we performed separate linear regression analyses among women, again adjusting for known CHD risk factors listed above. The Bonferroni-corrected two-sided P-value used to determine statistical significance was <1.03 × 10−5 (<0.05/4,877). To assess whether cross-sectional findings were consistent with those observed as women transitioned to menopause, we examined within-person distributions of protein levels between visit 2 and visit 3 (approximately 3 years apart) by changing menopausal status among women.

To identify biological pathways and functional categories associated with sex or menopause-related differences in protein levels, we performed over-representation analyses using the clusterProfiler R package [23]. Proteins were included if they met a significance threshold of P < 0.05 and a fold-change threshold of 1.5. Predefined biological pathways Gene Ontology (GO; Biological Process, version 2025 [24], and Reactome (version 2025 [25]) were used as the reference gene sets. The background set for enrichment testing comprised all proteins included in the analysis to ensure that enrichment was assessed relative to the full set of tested proteins. Normalized enrichment scores (NES) and false discovery rate (FDR)–adjusted p-values were reported, with FDR < 0.05 considered significant.

To support our cross-sectional analysis and to assess within-person changes in protein levels across the menopause transition, we analyzed women with proteomic measurements available at both visit 2 and visit 3 (1993–1995). We investigated the change in protein levels between visits among women who transitioned from pre- to post-menopausal stage between visit 2 and visit 3 and analogously for women who transitioned from peri‑ to postmenopausal stage between visit 2 and visit 3. We compared changes in women with menopausal transition women who were already post-menopausal at both visits.

Differences in the association of proteins with CHD by sex and menopausal stage

Next, proteins were tested for their association with incident CHD from visit 2 via Cox regression models fitted separately for each protein and adjusting covariables. To assess sex and menopausal differences in the association of the proteins with incident CHD from visit 2, we fitted two different Cox regression models for each protein on incident CHD including an interaction between protein and (i) sex in the full population or (ii) menopause status among women adjusting for the above mentioned covariables. In sensitivity analyses, we adjusted also for total cholesterol and HDL cholesterol given the known relationship between lipids and CHD as well as the influence of menopause on lipid concentrations. We also tested whether HRT was associated with CHD in women.

Assessment of proteins as predictors of Atherosclerotic Cardiovascular Disease (ASCVD) risk

To see if circulating protein adds information to standard risk prediction, we applied least absolute shrinkage and selection operator (LASSO) regression to identify proteins that independently improved risk prediction beyond the 30-year ASCVD PREVENT equation [26]. We conducted the analyses separately in men and in pre-, peri‑, and post-menopausal women. The linear predictor of the PREVENT score was forced into all models, and candidate proteins were selected using a LASSO-penalized Cox model with 10-fold cross-validation. PREVENT model performance with and without added proteins was evaluated using Harrell’s C-statistics to assess improvement in discrimination compared to the base model without proteins. Confidence intervals for the difference in C-statistics were obtained via 500 bootstrap repetitions. Next, we compared observed probability of CHD between the baseline PREVENT equation and the PREVENT with added proteins among the quintiles of predicted CHD risk in each subgroup. Results

Participant characteristics

The analyzed sample consisted of 4,205 men and 5,829 women with a mean age of 57 years (Table 1). On average, women were less likely to be current or former smokers. Other risk factors were slightly higher in women compared to men, including BMI, total cholesterol, and the prevalence of hypertension (all p < 0.001). Of the 5,829 women, 597 were in the premenopausal, 509 were in the perimenopausal and 4,139 were in the postmenopausal phase. On average, post-menopausal women had higher systolic blood pressure, higher HbA1c, higher total cholesterol, and higher prevalence of diabetes than premenopausal women (all p < 0.001).

Sex and menopausal differences in the incidence of CHD

In women, there were 843 CHD events over the entire follow-up period (mean follow-up, 22.8 years), whereas in men there were 1102 (Table 1). Women had markedly lower incidence rates compared to men (Table 2, p < 0.001). Postmenopausal women showed a higher incidence of CHD compared to pre- and perimenopausal women (p = 0.02 and p = 0.32, respectively). However, when adjusting for age, the difference in incidence rates between menopausal stages was not significant (Table 2).

Sex and menopausal differences in protein concentrations

There were 1,878 proteins that differed by sex at baseline after full adjustment (p-value <1.03 × 10–5). Compared to men, 768 proteins showed higher levels in women and 1110 showed lower levels (Fig. 2a, Supplementary Table 1). The most pronounced differences in protein concentrations were observed for pregnancy-zone protein which was 9.3-fold higher in women compared to men (effect size 9.30 95% CI: 8.86 - 9.75), follicle stimulating hormone (5.48, CI: 5.35 - 5.62), human chorionic gonadotropin (4.55, CI: 4.41 - 4.70), and luteinizing hormone (3.61, CI: 3.53 - 3.69).

Pathway enrichment analysis of proteins with different plasma levels between women and men revealed overrepresentation of lipid metabolism–related pathways, including adipogenesis and inflammatory signaling. Among the most enriched pathways, triglyceride metabolism, ghrelin production and secretion, differentiation of brown and white adipocytes were upregulated in women (Supplementary Table 2).

Among women, 270 proteins differed significantly between pre- and postmenopausal women (p-value <1.03 × 10–5) with 162 proteins showing lower plasma concentrations in premenopausal women (Fig. 2b, Supplementary Table 1). The most pronounced differences were observed for follicle stimulating hormone which was 62% lower in premenopausal women (effect size 0.38, CI: 0.36 - 0.40) and proteins belonging to the iron metabolism including hepcidin (0.35, CI: 0.33 - 0.38) and ferritin (0.39, CI: 0.36 - 0.42) with significantly lower levels in premenopausal women compared to postmenopausal. Odorant-binding protein 2b (1.42, CI: 1.35 - 1.49) and chordin-like protein (1.66, CI: 1.61 - 1.71) showed higher levels in premenopausal compared to postmenopausal women. Comparing post- and perimenopausal women, 38 proteins were observed with significant differences in plasma concentrations (p-value <1.03 × 10–5, Fig. 2c). Consistent with findings in premenopausal women, levels of proteins related to iron metabolism including hepcidin, ferritin heavy and light chain proteins were lower in perimenopausal than in postmenopausal women. Functional enrichment analysis showed that contrasting proteins differing between pre- and postmenopausal women or peri‑ and postmenopausal women were overrepresented in pathways reflecting iron and transition metal handling, including iron transport and homeostasis, along with scavenger receptor activity, cytokine and metal ion binding, and Golgi-related vesicle biogenesis and trafficking (Supplementary Table 2).

In sensitivity analyses, models adjusted additionally for total- and HDL-cholesterol resulted in 1714 proteins with significant differences in plasma levels by sex, 248 proteins with significant difference between pre- and postmenopausal women, and 36 proteins that differed significantly between peri‑ and postmenopausal women (Supplementary Table 3). Effect sizes were comparable between the fully adjusted model and the model without total cholesterol adjustment (Supplementary Figure 2).

Within-Person changes in protein levels across the menopause transition

When comparing women who transitioned from pre- to post-menopause from visit 2 to visit 3 (N = 92) to women who were post-menopausal at visit 2 and visit 3, the proteins with the largest changes between visits included chordin-like protein 2, follicle-stimulating hormone, hepcidin, as well as proteins belonging to iron metabolism, with all significant findings overlapping the cross-sectional findings (Fig. 3, Supplementary Table 4&5). Similarly, when comparing women who transitioned from peri‑ to post-menopause from visit 2 to visit 3 (N = 282) to women who were post-menopausal at visit 2 and visit 3, there were seven proteins with significant differences in their levels, also including also chordin-like protein 2 and iron-related proteins.

Differences in the association of proteins with CHD by sex

To explore potential sex differences in protein–CHD associations, HRs were compared between women and men for proteins showing significant associations in either sex (p-value <1.03 × 10–5). In women, 54 proteins showed a significant association with CHD (Fig. 4a, Supplementary Table 6). In men, eight proteins were significantly associated with CHD (Fig. 4b, Supplementary Table 6). Comparing HRs between men and women, most proteins showed the same direction in both sexes (Fig. 4a & b). Two proteins overlapped between the two sexes (MMP-12, CRYZL1, Fig. 4c). Only two proteins demonstrated a statistically significant sex difference in their association with CHD risk (SIGLEC7, interaction p-value=1.52 × 10–6 and APLP1, interaction p-value=4.31 × 10–6). Among the 60 proteins that showed significant CHD associations in at least one sex, 32 proteins were among those which showed also significant difference in plasma concentrations between men and women (Supplementary Table 1&6).

Differences in the association of proteins with CHD by menopausal status

In total, 17 proteins were significantly associated with CHD in postmenopausal women (Fig. 5a), six proteins were significantly associated with CHD in perimenopausal women (Fig. 5b), and no proteins were associated with risk of CHD in premenopausal women. Among the 60 proteins showing significant associations with CHD in either men or women, 13 also were associated with risk of CHD in postmenopausal women (Supplementary Table 6) The strongest association was observed for MMP-12 (HR: 1.83, 95% CI:1.55–2.15). In contrast, the risk associated with MMP-12 was not significant in pre- or perimenopausal women (premenopausal women HR: 1.19, 95% CI: 0.70–2.03; perimenopausal women, HR: 1.08, 95% CI: 0.61–1.90), although the interaction was not statistically significant. The proteins associated with CHD in perimenopausal women were distinct from those associated with CHD in postmenopausal women (Fig. 5c).

Improvement of ASCVD risk prediction integrating proteins

Adding proteins to the PREVENT 30-year ASCVD equation, which includes established risk factors [26], improved discrimination by 0.03 (95% CI: 0.03–0.04) in women and 0.05 (95% CI: 0.04 to 0.05) in men (Fig. 6a & b), Supplementary Table 7). In men, the extended model including proteins showed improved risk stratification compared to the established model, as the difference between the lowest (Q1) and highest quintile (Q5) became more pronounced (Fig. 6c, Supplementary Table 8). Between men and women, five proteins were overlapping: MMP-12, GDF-15, NAGK, NT-pro-BNP, and Troponin T (Fig. 6, Supplementary Table 7). Similar to men, protein inclusion improved risk stratification in women, resulting in a clearer separation between Q5 and Q1. (Fig. 6d).

Discussion

This study of 4,877 proteins measured in 10,034 individuals with up to 30 years of follow-up identified differential proteomic profiles as well as differences in proteomic associations with CHD risk by sex and menopausal status. Incorporation of these proteins in existing risk scores may improve risk stratification, particularly among women, and additionally may inform future investigation of pathways that differ across sex and hormonal-status subgroups.

Our findings were consistent with literature suggesting that men have a higher risk of CHD than women, but that this difference narrows with increasing age [27]. Additionally, we found that risk among women increased progressively from the pre- to peri‑ and postmenopausal stages, even among women of the same age. This suggests that earlier onset of menopause is a risk factor for CHD, consistent with observed menopause-related changes in CHD risk factors, including alterations in lipid metabolism, redistribution of body fat [28], and the loss of estrogen’s vascular and metabolic protective effects [29]. These results support the idea that reproductive aging can affect cardiovascular risk profiles. This is consistent with previous prospective studies that have linked age at menopause to the development of cardiovascular conditions, such as heart failure. This further highlights the importance of menopausal timing in cardiovascular health [30].

At the proteomic level, we confirmed hallmark changes associated with sex, including proteins involved in reproductive hormone signaling, such as pregnancy-zone protein, follicle-stimulating hormone, luteinizing hormone, and human chorionic gonadotropin. Differences in these same proteins were also reported across the menopausal transition, although these findings were based on a small sample size (N = 92). Animal models support the role of sex hormones in CVD risk: ovariectomy in rats has detrimental effects on vascular function and inflammatory markers but reverses with estradiol administration; similar studies in mice suggest heightened susceptibility to angiotensin II-induced vascular damage [31] with estrogen depletion. We also found that levels of iron metabolism–related proteins increased during the menopause transition, with increasing concentrations of hepcidin and ferritin. Both proteins are key regulators of systemic iron homeostasis, and these increases may partly reflect rising iron stores after the cessation of menstrual blood loss [32]. However, they are unlikely to be explained solely by cessation of menses, as experimental evidence suggests that estrogen can directly regulate hepatic hepcidin expression, indicating an additional contribution of hormonal changes across menopause to altered iron homeostasis [33]. The dysregulation of hepcidin and ferritin has been linked to oxidative stress, atherosclerosis, and heart failure [34]. In knockout mouse models of Hamp (hepcidin), there was decreased proinflammatory macrophage activation and protection from atherosclerosis, which may suggest that the increase observed in menopausal women contributes to the uptick in CVD risk [35].

In the analysis of proteomic signatures of CHD, 55% of the identified proteins differed between men and women. MMP-12 emerged in both sexes as a strong CHD risk–increasing protein, whereas GDF-15 was associated with risk of CHD in women alone, findings that align with previous studies [15]. GDF-15 has been reported as a prognostic biomarker in acute heart failure and all-cause mortality, supporting its role as a marker of adverse cardiovascular outcomes [36,37]. MMP-12, a matrix metalloproteinase involved in extracellular matrix remodeling, is elevated in atherosclerosis [38]. By degrading extracellular matrix components, MMP-12 contributes to the thinning and destabilization of the fibrous cap, thereby increasing the risk of plaque rupture and vascular events [39]. Other proteins associated with CHD risk in women—most prominently tetranectin and cathepsin S, both involved in extracellular matrix remodeling and vascular inflammation—have also been previously identified as potential cardiovascular biomarkers [40,41]. However, formal evidence for sex-specific heterogeneity was limited, given that only two proteins demonstrated statistically significant interaction by sex.

Integrating protein biomarkers into established cardiovascular risk equations has gained relevance in precision prevention [15,42]. Interestingly, fewer proteins were associated with CHD in men than in women, and distinct sets of protein were identified for each sex. Our findings support the concept of sex-specific risk prediction algorithms rather than applying uniform models to both sexes. The present study was intended as proof of principle and not to develop a new risk equation. The development of novel prediction tools requires rigorous external validation, calibration, and assessment of clinical utility in independent populations. Risk stratification tools can be used in patient counseling, entry to clinical trials, or to direct treatment approaches, with different implications for people estimated to be at high risk compared with those estimated to be at low risk. Future work should also explore the integration of sex-specific clinical risk factors, particularly in women, e.g. oral contraceptive use, to further improve risk stratification. However, the potential to refine risk prediction across menopausal stages remains largely unexplored.

Our study has several notable strengths. The comprehensive proteomic profiling, encompassing many measured proteins, provides a valuable complement to existing large-scale studies investigating sex-specific proteomic profiles in the context of cardiovascular diseases [15]. Furthermore, the large and well-characterized study population, including a clearly defined classification of menopausal stages and the inclusion of perimenopausal women, allowed detection of longitudinal changes in menopausal transition and added valuable insights from an often-underrepresented subgroup in studies of menopausal stages. Despite its strengths, this study has several limitations that should be acknowledged. The relatively low number of CHD events among pre- and perimenopausal women reduced the statistical power to detect protein associations with CHD risk. Another limitation is the definition of perimenopausal women, which may have introduced some misclassification. This stage is inherently difficult to capture in cohort settings because it is characterized by dynamic and sometimes subtle changes in cycle regularity and symptoms, while also representing a relatively small subgroup. Furthermore, the absence of an independent external validation cohort is a major limitation as it prevents robust evaluation of the generalizability of the prediction models; therefore, the observed improvements should be considered preliminary and proof of principle only. The generalizability of our findings is also limited by the inclusion of only self-identified White and Black participants, and the results may therefore not fully extend to populations with greater racial and ethnic diversity. This large-scale proteomic study revealed protein signatures associated with CHD that differ by sex and menopausal status. These subgroup-specific patterns are consistent with possible heterogeneity in CHD-related biology, although the present study does not provide robust evidence for distinct mechanisms. Variation across menopausal stages may reflect differences in hormonal, metabolic, or vascular processes, although this remains to be determined. These findings support consideration of sex and reproductive stage in cardiovascular risk assessment and encourage further study of sex- and menopause-specific biomarkers for improved early detection and refine prediction accuracy to guide the prevention of CHD.
