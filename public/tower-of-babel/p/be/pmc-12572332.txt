Uniaxial creep characteristics and constitutive model of arkose sandstone with different moisture contents under repeated freeze thaw cycles
Authors: Tian Chen, Yanlong Chen, Peng Wu, Xinfeng Cao, Lingyao Kong, Zhiguo Chang
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
To investigate the creep characteristics of water-containing slope rocks in high-cold regions, this study conducted indoor physical and mechanical tests on arkose sandstone specimens, with moisture content and freeze-thaw cycle number as test variables. The test results indicate that under the same stress level, increasing moisture content and freeze-thaw cycle number lead to three key trends: (1) increased instantaneous creep strain of arkose sandstone specimens; (2) decreased long-term creep strength; and (3) intensified material damage. Based on the test data, this study developed and verified a modified Nishihara creep model that incorporates the coupled damage effect of moisture content and freeze-thaw cycle number. This study provides a theoretical basis for predicting the long-term creep behavior of arkose sandstone in water-containing slopes of high-cold regions and preventing slope disasters in open-pit mines.

Introduction

With the continuous growth of the global population and the steady rise in Gross Domestic Product (GDP), the consumption of mineral resources has increased steadily. As an important method for mine development in China, open-pit mining holds significant status and practical importance. A large number of open-pit mines in China are distributed in cold regions north of 38°N latitude. These regions typically experience high rainfall and large diurnal temperature variations, which significantly expose open-pit mine slopes to the effects of water intrusion and freeze-thaw cycles1. In recent years, as open-pit mining depths continues to increase, slope angles have increased accordingly, making slope stability a core concern in open-pit mining operations2. Meanwhile, under the combined action of water intrusion and freeze-thaw cycles, the risks of geological hazards (e.g., slope cracking and instability) are increasing, which seriously threatens the safe operation of open-pit mines3–6. As a common slope rock type, arkose sandstone—composed primarily of arkose minerals—is prone to hydrolysis when in contact with water. Under freeze-thaw cycles, the mechanical properties of arkose sandstone continue to deteriorate. Under the long-term load of the overlying rock mass, arkose sandstone slopes are highly susceptible to local deformation, which can further lead to overall slope instability. Therefore, in-depth research on the creep mechanical properties and deterioration mechanism of arkose sandstone under the deterioration effect of water induced freeze-thaw cycles is of crucial importance for preventing slope hazards in open-pit mines.

In the field of rock creep characteristic research, many scholars have conducted in-depth investigations into the effects of a single factor, either water erosion or freeze-thaw cycles. Regarding the role of water, researchers have systematically investigated the creep behavior of various rocks under different moisture conditions using triaxial creep test systems, disturbed creep test benches, and other experimental methods7–11. These studies consistently show that increased moisture content significantly weakens rock strength and accelerates rock creep deformation. Regarding the role of freeze-thaw cycles, scholars have revealed the creep deterioration characteristics, failure modes, and damage mechanisms of red sandstone12–14and marble15 under different freeze-thaw cycles and temperature ranges using methods such as triaxial creep tests, microscopic detection, and triaxial multi-stage loading-unloading creep tests. These studies confirm that freeze-thaw cycles induce the accumulation of internal damage, which leads to a significant reduction in the long-term strength and deformation resistance of rocks.

To apply the results of laboratory rock creep tests to practical engineering and provide a theoretical basis for explaining and predicting rock creep behavior, it is crucial to establish a reasonable rock creep model16–19. Many scholars have proposed various models, such as the empirical creep model for rock structural planes20, the creep model incorporating damage variables that account for the combined effects of freeze-thaw and loading21, the fractional-order rheological model considering moisture state and freeze-thaw cycles22, and the creep damage constitutive model accounting for the spatiotemporal evolution characteristics of deformation modulus23.

However, most existing studies have focused on single environmental factors24, with insufficient attention to the synergistic coupling effect of moisture and freeze-thaw cycles under the actual working conditions of open-pit mine slopes in cold regions—especially a lack of systematic research regarding the creep mechanism of vulnerable rock masses (e.g., arkose sandstone) under these two coupled factors. The complexity of this coupling lies in the positive feedback deterioration mechanism formed by their mutual promotion: moisture exacerbates frost heave damage during freeze-thaw cycles, while freeze-thaw induced microcrack networks serve as channels for moisture migration. Current research still has limitations in elucidating this coupling mechanism and fails to determine which factor—moisture content or freeze-thaw cycles—exerts a more significant influence on the creep mechanical properties of rocks.

Therefore, this study systematically investigates the effects of moisture content and freeze-thaw cycle number on the creep mechanical properties of arkose sandstone. Through moisture content tests, freeze-thaw cycle tests, uniaxial creep tests, and additional experiments methods, this study reveals the coupling deterioration laws of arkose sandstone, quantifies the nonlinear attenuation of creep mechanical properties, and clarifies the dynamic evolution of creep failure. Based on the theory of frost heave force and the inhibition effect of phase transition in small pores, this study innovatively proposes the microscopic mechanism termed “moisture gradient regulating frost heave damage domain”.

The research results address the theoretical problem of long-term creep prediction for arkose sandstone in open-pit mine slopes in cold regions, and offer a key scientific basis for the dynamic stability evaluation of open-pit mine slopes.

Specimen Preparation

The arkose sandstone investigated in this study was sourced from an open - pit mine in Xinjiang, as depicted in Fig. 1. XRD (X - ray diffraction) analyses and thin - section identifications were performed on the selected arkose sandstone specimens to determine their main phase composition. As indicated by the thin - section identification results, the arkose sandstone exhibits a fine - grained sandy texture and is primarily composed of clasts (quartz 48%, arkose 30%, rock fragments 3%, mica and others. 1%) and interstitial materials (calcareous 13%, ferruginous-argillaceous 5%). As indicated by the XRD analysis results, the main mineral phases of the arkose sandstone were identified as quartz (48.2%), plagioclase (24%), calcite (13.2%), potassium arkose (8.3%), chlorite (3%) iron mineral (2.3%) and mica (1%). Based on a comprehensive assessment, the specimens were classified as fine - grained calcareous arkose sandstone.

In accordance with the requirements specified in the Specification (DZ/T − 0276), the rock specimens were fabricated into standard specimens with a diameter of Φ = 50 mm and a height of H = 100 mm. Ultrasonic wave velocity measurements were performed on the prepared rock specimens, and those with significant disparities in ultrasonic wave characteristics were excluded.

Experimental scheme

Moisture content tests, F-T cycle tests, uniaxial compression tests and uniaxial creep tests were conducted in this study. The moisture content test was conducted using a DHG9076 electrothermal constant temperature drying oven. The F-T cycle tests were executed with an XY-QDR-50 fully automatic low-temperature F-T testing machine. The uniaxial compression tests and uniaxial creep tests were conducted using an MTS816 electro-hydraulic servo rock testing system. The testing procedures are illustrated in Fig. 2.

Fig. 2Test procedure.

Test procedure.

Moisture content test

The prepared specimens were placed into the DHG9076 electrothermal constant - temperature drying oven, and the temperature was set to 105 °C. After 24 h of continuous drying, the mass of each specimen was measured every hour. The process was terminated once the mass discrepancy between two consecutive measurements was under 0.02 g. At this point, the specimen was considered to be in a dry state. Each specimen was allowed to cool naturally to room temperature inside the drying oven, after which its dry mass was recorded. The dry specimens were completely immersed in distilled water using a vacuum negative - pressure air - extraction device. The negative pressure for air extraction was set at 100 kPa to generate the water - content curve of the arkose sandstone specimens. Based on the results of the water content curve, three water contents were selected—natural state (1.5%), inflection point (2.3%), and saturated state (2.87%)—for the preparation of the arkose sandstone samples.

F-T cycle test

The arkose sandstone specimens with three different ω gradients were completely sealed in plastic wrap to prevent moisture loss and then placed into the XY-QDR-50 fully automatic low-temperature F-T testing machine. The melting temperature T was set at 20 °C, with a holding duration of 4 h. The freezing temperature T was set at − 20 °C, with a holding duration of 4 h25–27. The number of F-T cycles (N) were set at 0, 5, 10, 15, and 20 cycles, respectively. Thus, arkose sandstone with varying ω and different N were obtained.

Uniaxial compression test

The arkose sandstone specimens-with distinct moisture contents (ω = 1.5%, 2.3%, 2.87% for three groups) and different N (N = 0, 5, 10, 15, 20 for five groups), were placed into the MTS816 electro - hydraulic servo rock testing system for uniaxial compression tests. Throughout the uniaxial compression test, the loading rate was maintained at 0.2 mm/min. The stress and strain data were measured and recorded.

Uniaxial creep test

The uniaxial compressive strength (UCS) of the specimens was used as the basis for incremental loading in the uniaxial creep tests. The loading rate of uniaxial creep was set to 0.05 kN/s, and the creep holding duration for each increment was controlled to 20 h. To prevent the specimens from undergoing brittle failure under high stress, the stress gradient in the later stage of the loading process was reduced by half. Incremental loading was conducted incrementally in this manner until specimen failure. The stress and strain data were measured and recorded.

Uniaxial creep strain

The creep tests employed a multi - stage loading protocol, where stress levels were determined based on the previously obtained UCS values. Specifically, for arkose sandstone samples with distinct moisture contents, the creep stress level was based on their corresponding 1/7 UCS. The specimens exhibited stage - dependent stress increments of 3.57 MPa (ω = 1.5%), 2.55 MPa (ω = 2.3%), and 1.53 MPa (ω = 2.87%) between successive loading phases. The loading rate of uniaxial creep was set to 0.05 kN/s, and the creep holding duration for each increment was controlled to 20 h. To prevent the specimens from undergoing brittle failure under high stress, the stress gradient in the later stage of the loading process was reduced by half. It is evident from Figs. 5 and 6 that the overall characteristics of the full - process creep curves of arkose sandstone under varying ω and different N are similar. As the stress - loading stage progresses, axial elastic strain is generated in the specimens. At low stress magnitudes, the arkose sandstone has entered the steady - creep stage, and the strain keeps approximately constant. As the stress increases, the gradient of the creep curve becomes steeper. The arkose sandstone then enters an unstable creep stage, exhibiting decelerated creep and constant - rate creep phases. Once the stress exceeds the critical magnitude, the arkose sandstone exhibits accelerated creep characterized by exponential expansion. Over time, the creep rate increases gradually and the specimens will fail within a short period.

Moreover, when ω is held constant, both the creep - failure time and the total creep strain of the arkose sandstone decrease as N increases. When N is fixed, the creep - failure stress of the arkose sandstone decreases as ω increases.

ω, along with N, plays a major role in deteriorating mechanical properties of arkose sandstone specimens28. This is because the frost - heaving force29,30 generated by the water within the rock pores during the freezing process causes the pores to expand continuously. When the rock undergo thawing, the water stored in its pores migrates into newly - formed cracks. Subsequently, the water in these cracks undergoes freezing again. Through this cyclic process, the internal damage of the rock accumulates31,32, leading to a degradation of the its mechanical properties. Furthermore, due to water’s relatively high specific heat capacity—which imparts a degree of thermal insulation—as ω increases, the temperature difference between the rock’s surface and interior intensifies, inducing tensile stress on the specimen’s surface27. This tensile stress causes particles on the rock’s surface to loosen, thereby reducing the rock’s strength and deformation resistance.

Moreover, in small pores, the interaction between the pore walls and water molecules is relatively strong, which substantially lowers the freezing point of water and elevates its thawing point. In large pores, the restraining effect of the pore walls on water molecules is relatively weak; thus, the freezing and melting behavior of water more closely resembles that of free water. Hence, based on the theory of frost heave force and the inhibition effect of small pore phase transition, the paper innovatively proposes the microscopic mechanism of “moisture gradient regulating frost heave damage domain”. At the low moisture content gradient, during freeze-thaw cycles, only the water in large pores freezes and expands, and the frost heave force acts on the walls of large pores. However, due to the sufficient space in large pores, the frost heave force is released, only causing slight expansion of large pores. The damage domain is limited to the vicinity of large pores and does not extend to small pores. At the medium moisture content gradient, during freeze-thaw cycles, the frost heave damage in large pores accumulates continuously. Meanwhile, a small amount of water in small pores begins to freeze, and the pore wall pressure generated by the frost heave force starts to induce the initiation of microcracks in small pores. The damage domain expands from large pores to the edges of small pores, but the damage between small pores is not yet connected. At the high moisture content gradient, during freeze-thaw cycles, the frost heave damage in large pores has stabilized, while a large amount of water in small pores freezes, directly causing the rupture of small pores. Additionally, the microcracks in small pores interconnect and connect with the damage domain of large pores, forming a penetrating damage network of “large pores - small pores - microcracks”, and the damage domain fully covers the interior of the rock. This mechanism reveals the essence of moisture content being the key controlling factor for the freeze-thaw deterioration of arkose sandstone: the moisture gradient directly determines the action location and intensity of frost heave damage.

For instance, when N is maintained at 20, \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$${\sigma _s}$$\end{document} corresponding to ω = 1.5%, 2.3%, and 2.87% are 16.19 MPa, 12.85 MPa, and 7.86 MPa, respectively. The maximum rate of decrease is 51.45%.

When considering Fig. 13(a) and Fig. 13(b) together, the extent of deterioration of \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$${\sigma _s}$$\end{document} of the arkose sandstone specimen due to ω is more pronounced than that due to N. This is because a higher ω not only implies a greater frost - heaving force29,30, generated by the freezing and expansion of water within the rock, but also renders the arkose in the arkose sandstone more susceptible to hydrolysis. This, in turn, results in larger internal pores, increased damage within the rock, and diminished mechanical properties. Moreover, under low - moisture - content conditions, the water - ice phase transition predominantly occurs in large pores, and the water in small pores is less responsive to the F-T cycle. In contrast, under high - moisture - content conditions, the water - ice phase transition in large pores tends to reach saturation, and a substantial amount of water freezes and infiltrates into small pores, exerting pressure on the pore walls. This results in an increase in rock damage and an exacerbation of the detrimental impact on the mechanical characteristics.

Hence, ω is a crucial factor in the F-T cycle effect, and it directly governs the sensitivity of the arkose sandstone specimen to F-T damage.

Failure morphology of the specimen

Schematic illustrations of the uniaxial creep failure modes of arkose sandstone under varying N and different ω are presented in Fig. 14.

Fig. 14Schematic illustrations of the uniaxial creep failure patterns of arkose sandstone under varying ω and different N.

Schematic illustrations of the uniaxial creep failure patterns of arkose sandstone under varying ω and different N.

It is evident from Fig. 14(a) that when ω is maintained at 2.87%, as N increases, the main creep failure cracks of the arkose sandstone evolve from a single crack to multiple cracks. Specifically, when N = 5, an inclined throughgoing primary crack emerges on the surface of the arkose sandstone. When N = 10 and N = 15, an “N”-shaped failure, which is formed by the connection of three main cracks, emerges on the surface of the arkose sandstone. When N = 20, the number of main cracks of the arkose sandstone further grows, and the failure mode gradually transitions from shear failure to tensile - shear failure mode9, and the degree of fragmentation increases significantly. The underlying mechanism is that the F-T cycling effect causes the internal pores of the specimen to expand, impairs the inter-particle biting force within the specimen, reduces the specimen’s deformation resistance, and increases the fracture area.

It is evident from Fig. 14(b) that when N is fixed at 0, as ω increases, an “N”-shaped failure, which is formed by the connection of three main cracks, emerges on the surface of the arkose sandstone, and the creep failure of the arkose sandstone exhibits a trend of the gradual development of secondary cracks. Specifically, when ω = 1.5%, only a distinct main crack of the “N”-shaped failure can be observed on the surface of the arkose sandstone. When ω = 2.3%, secondary cracks propagate between the main cracks. When ω = 2.87%, the secondary cracks between the main cracks further extend, and there is a tendency for the secondary cracks outside the main cracks to evolve into main cracks.

This is because the arkose component the arkose sandstone will undergo hydrolysis in the presence of water, thereby reducing the rock’s strength. Secondly, water acts as a lubricant between pores and particles, and a water film forms on the surface of the arkose component, leading to a reduction in friction and a decrease in the rock’s deformation resistance. Moreover, during the F-T cycle, the tensile stress induced by the temperature difference between the interior and the surface of the rock causes the particles on the rock surface to loosen3, and the rock’s deformation resistance decreases.

Based on the strain equivalence hypothesis, Lemaitre first proposed defining the damage variable using the material’s undamaged elastic modulus and damaged elastic modulus. The arkose sandstone in a dry state without undergoing F-T cycles is defined as being in an undamaged state. When ω and N vary, UCS of the arkose sandstone decreases correspondingly. Fitted creep curve of arkose sandstone.

As clearly observed in Fig. 17, creep curve fitting for arkose sandstone specimens reveals that the established creep constitutive model (incorporating water-containing F-T damage) can effectively capture the creep evolution laws of arkose sandstone with stress level and time. Moreover, the fitted curves exhibit a high degree of consistency with the experimental data, with a coefficient of determination above 0.94. This model effectively captures the influences of ω and N on the creep behavior of arkose sandstone.

To highlight the advantages of the modified Nishihara creep model, this study used the Burgers model to fit the same experimental data, with the fitting results presented in Fig. 18.

Fig. 18Fitted creep curve of Burgers model.

Fitted creep curve of Burgers model.

By synthesizing the results from Figs. 17 and 18, it is evident that while the Burgers model can effectively capture the instantaneous deformation, decelerated creep, and constant-rate creep behaviors of the arkose sandstone specimens, it cannot accurately characterize the specimens’ accelerated creep behavior. In contrast, the modified Nishihara creep model proposed in this study can precisely fit the accelerated creep behavior of the specimens. Therefore, in characterizing the creep mechanical behavior of water-containing F-T damaged arkose sandstone, the modified Nishihara creep model proposed in this study exhibits distinct advantages over conventional creep models.

Conclusion

In this study, arkose sandstone specimens were used as the research object, with ω and N as the experimental variables. Three sets of experiments were conducted on the specimens: moisture content measurement, uniaxial compression tests, and uniaxial creep tests. Additionally, a creep constitutive model incorporating water-containing F-T damage was established. The following conclusions are drawn based on the analysis and discussion of the experimental results and model performance:

(1) ω of the arkose sandstone specimen increases rapidly at the initial stage of the water absorption process. Subsequently, the rate of moisture absorption gradually decreases until the moisture content reaches a stable state. UCS of arkose sandstone specimens decreases with ω and N, showing a significant deterioration in mechanical properties.

(2) At a constant ω, as N increases, the creep failure time of the arkose sandstone decreases, and \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$${\varepsilon _e}$$\end{document} of the arkose sandstone at the same stress level increases significantly. At a constant N, as the moisture content increases, the creep failure stress of the arkose sandstone decreases, and \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$${\varepsilon _e}$$\end{document} of the arkose sandstone at the same stress level also increases substantially.

(3) When ω remains constant, \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$${\sigma _s}$$\end{document} of the arkose sandstone specimen progressively decreases as N increases. When N stays constant, \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$${\sigma _s}$$\end{document} of the arkose sandstone specimen gradually decreases as ω increases. The extent of deterioration in \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$${\sigma _s}$$\end{document} of the arkose sandstone specimen induced by ω is more significant than that induced by N.

(4) When ω is maintained at 2.87%, as N increases, the primary creep failure cracks in the arkose sandstone transition from a single crack to multiple cracks. The failure mode of the specimens gradually transitions from pure shear failure to a tensile-shear composite failure mode, and the degree of fragmentation increases substantially. When N is kept at 0, as ω increases, the creep failure of the arkose sandstone is characterized by for the gradual development of secondary cracks.

(5) A creep constitutive model incorporating the influences of ω and N was developed, and the corresponding creep constitutive equation was derived. Using the experimental data, parameter identification and validation were performed for the creep constitutive model (incorporating water-containing F-T damage). The fitted curve exhibits a high degree of consistency with the experimental data, indicating that this model can accurately characterize the decelerated creep, constant-rate creep, and accelerated creep behaviors of the arkose sandstone specimens, and effectively captures the effects of ω and N on the creep process.
