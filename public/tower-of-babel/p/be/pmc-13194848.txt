Steering the microbial ecosystem: how ethanol boosts coalbed methane output
Authors: Yingying Li, Yu Meng, Huairen Zhang, Mingqiang Qiao, Xiuqing Yang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractMicrobial-enhanced coalbed methane (MECBM) technology is considered a key approach to increasing CBM yields. However, the biogenic conversion of coal to methane is limited by coal’s recalcitrant macromolecular structure, which constrains production. Therefore, enhancing microbial degradation efficiency is critical for improving CBM yields. To elucidate the mechanism through which ethanol enhances coal biodegradation and methanogenesis, this study employed an integrated approach combining 13C-stable isotope tracing with multi-omics analyses. This strategy enabled investigation of the bioconversion dynamics related to methane, volatile fatty acids (VFAs), and the ethanol-responsive microbial community. The addition of ethanol (2 mg/mL) markedly enhanced methane and hydrogen production by 10.9-fold and 3.1-fold, respectively. It also significantly elevated the soluble chemical oxygen demand (SCOD) level. Ethanol selectively stimulated the proliferation of key hydrogen-producing acetogens, including Clostridium, Syntrophomonas, and Petrimonas, while concurrently enhancing the cooperative interactions between these bacterial populations and methanogenic archaea. Isotope tracking confirmed that the direct incorporation of ethanol-derived carbon into methane, carbon dioxide, and VFAs. Furthermore, the abundance of key functional genes involved in ethanol oxidation, acetic acid metabolism, and central carbon pathways was enriched. This genetic response correlated with increased organic acids production and enhanced mobilization of soluble compounds derived from coal. Collectively, these findings offer novel insights into the stimulatory effects of ethanol on coal bioconversion and highlight a promising strategy to improve coalbed methane (CBM) recovery and utilization.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s13568-026-02047-z.Keywords: Coalbed methane (CBM), Ethanol, Anaerobic, Methanogenic archaea

Introduction

As a clean and efficient energy resource, coalbed methane (CBM) has gained increasing attention owing to its potential to optimize energy structures, reduce carbon emissions, and alleviate air pollution (Agyarko and Mansoori 2013; Hu et al. 2021; Zhang et al. 2018). Compared to direct coal combustion, which releases substantial pollutants, CBM combustion generates fewer greenhouse gases and significantly reduces the emissions of criteria air pollutants such as sulfur dioxide, nitrogen oxides, and particulate matter (Burnham et al. 2012; Hamawand et al. 2013). Microbial bioconversion of coal represents one of the most promising pathways for CBM production due to its environmental compatibility, sustainable enhancement of methane recovery, and successful transition from laboratory to pilot-scale applications (Hamawand et al. 2013; Hu et al. 2021). In particular, microbial-enhanced coalbed methane (MECBM) technologies has demonstrated to be a practical and environmentally compatible strategy for converting organic carbon into methane (Guo et al. 2019, 2021b; Scott 1999; Wang et al. 2021; Zhang et al. 2023a). From initial laboratory-scale explorations to pilot field applications, MECBM has evolved into an ecologically sustainable method for enhancing CBM recovery (Park and Liang 2016).

MECBM fundamentally relies on four key strategies to enhance methane production: biostimulation, enhancement of functional microbial populations, expansion of the coal-microbe interface, and activation of the organic matrix within coal to improve conversion efficiency (Scott 1999). Among these, substrates addition has been widely recognized as an effective means of enhancing methane production. For instance, the supplementation of complex organic phosphates and nitrogen compounds has been demonstrated to significantly promote methane production (Barker and Dallegge 2006). Similarly, co-fermentation of corn stover with bituminous coal substantially enhanced the yields of biogenic gas (Guo et al. 2021a). In other studies, phosphorus source supplementation was shown to enhance microbial activity and CBM production efficiency (Bates et al. 2011; Jarrell and Kalmokoff 2011; Lu et al. 1999). Furthermore, optimizing trace element availability or incorporating algal amendments have been found to stimulate microbial metabolism (Barnhart et al. 2017; Davis et al. 2019; Ünal et al. 2012). Subsequently, to support this, Smith et al. observed that algal biomass addition markedly increased the CBM production (Smith et al. 2021). Collectively, these investigations demonstrate that external additives can stimulate microbial metabolism, and thereby enhance CBM production.

Notably, compared to conventional additives, ethanol has emerged as a particularly promising additive due to its distinct advantages, such as high aqueous solubility, for direct coal seams injection, dual functionality as both a carbon source and a metabolic stimulant; and low toxicity and cost. Its efficacy is evidenced by studies demonstrating enhanced methane production across coal types of varying ranks. For instance, ethanol was shown to effectively promote methane release from Illinois and San Juan bituminous coals of comparable rank (Wang et al. 2019). Correspondingly, it also significantly enhanced methane production from lignite by stimulating the metabolic activity of methanogenic archaea (Yang et al. 2019). From a mechanistic perspective, ethanol is hypothesized to promote coal solubilization, thereby enhancing substrate bioavailability and facilitating microbial biomethane generation (Liu et al. 2013). Although prior studies have confirmed the stimulatory effect of ethanol on biogenic methane production, most have focused on quantifying methane production, profiling microbial community, or inferring solvent-related mechanisms. Consequently, systematic investigations into the comprehensive mechanism by which ethanol enhances CBM production, especially regarding its integrated impact throughout the entire bioconversion process, remain limited.

This study aims to deliver a comprehensive mechanistic understanding of the role of ethanol in coal bioconversion. The microbial composition and functional gene were analyzed by 16 S rRNA gene sequencing and metagenomic to elucidate how ethanol selective stimulates key methanogenic archaea (e.g., Methanobacterium) and synergistic bacteria (e.g., Pseudomonas). Furthermore, the 13C-labeled ethanol was employed to trace carbon flux pathways. Finally, by integrating multi-omics techniques, we systematically reveal the synergistic effects of ethanol across four interconnected dimensions: microbial community structure, metabolic function, carbon flux, and physicochemical dynamics.

Materials and methods

Coal samples and microbial strains

Long-flame coal obtained from the Baode Mining Area (Northeastern ordos basin) in Shanxi Province, China, was used as the experimental substrate. The results of the industrial analysis are presented in Table 1. The coal was crushed, sieved, and the fraction passing through a 140-mesh screen (particle size < 0.109 mm) was collected. The resulting fine powder was oven-dried at 80 °C for 6 h and stored for subsequent experiments.

The microbial inoculum was enriched from coalbed production water from the Baode Mining Area (Hedong coalfield). The coalbed production water was collected from the producing CBM well. Samples were collected in sterile bottles which were immediately sealed, stored on ice, and transported to the laboratory for processing. Enrichment involved two sequential 3-month anaerobic culture stages: first with acetate (1.8 g/L) as the sole carbon source, then with sterile coal (10 g/L) replacing acetate. Transfers (10%, v/v) were performed monthly at 35 °C, following Yang et al. (Yang et al. 2018). The final consortium was used as the inoculum.

The resulting cultures were employed as inoculum for all experimental treatments. Prior to be used, all media were purged with high-purity nitrogen gas (≥ 99.999%) for at least 20 min to remove dissolved oxygen. Under sterile anaerobic conditions, reducing agents (0.5 g/L L-cysteine hydrochloride and 0.5 g/L sodium sulfide) were added to each liter of medium. All procedures were conducted in an anaerobic workstation. The enrichment protocol follows the method described by Yang et al. (Yang et al. 2018).

Experimental set-up

Experiments were conducted in 500 mL serum bottles containing 300 mL of reaction mixture, which consisted of 30 mL of microbial inoculum and 10 g of long-flame coal. Ethanol was added at final concentrations of 0, 2, 5, 10, and 20 mg/mL. Although the 5 mg/mL group exhibited the highest specific ethanol conversion efficiency, the 2 mg/mL concentration was selected for subsequent mechanistic studies, including microbial community and metabolic pathway analyses, as it sufficiently activated methanogenesis (10-fold increase) while ensuring cost-effectiveness for the requisite 13C-labeled ethanol tracer experiments. Hereafter, the group supplemented with 2 mg/mL ethanol (containing coal and enriched microbial inoculum) was referred to as Group E, and the group without ethanol supplementation (containing coal and enriched microbial inoculum) was referred to as Group C. Ethanol was introduced immediately after inoculation.

To confirm the biological origin of methane production and to trace carbon flow, heat-killed controls were prepared by autoclaving the inoculum at 121 °C for 20 min. Two control groups were established: one with ethanol addition (Ehk) and one without (Chk), while maintaining identical coal content and medium composition. All treatments were performed in biological triplicates. Cultures were incubated at 35 °C under anaerobic conditions. Medium preparation and general cultivation procedure followed the protocol described by Yang et al. (Yang et al. 2018). The methanogenic medium contained the following (in g/L): yeast extract, 2; KH2PO4, 1.5; K2HPO4, 2.9; MgCl2, 0.4; NH4Cl, 1.8; NaCl, 0.8; KCl, 0.1; MgSO4·7H2O, 0.2; and CaCl2·2H2O, 0.04.

Isotope tracing with ¹³C‑ethanol

For isotope tracing experiments, 100 mL serum bottles were used, each containing 60 mL of reaction mixture comprising 6 mL of enriched microbial inoculum (derived from production water), 2 g of long-flame coal, and ethanol at 2 mg/mL. The medium composition and cultivation conditions were identical to those described above. Ethanol was added immediately after inoculation. Two isotopically labeled ethanol variants, 13CH313CH2OH(1,2-13C2,99%) (< 6%H2O) and unlabeled 12CH312CH2OH, were prepared and incubated, along with a control containing only coal and medium without ethanol. Headspace gas samples were collected every 7 days to measure the production of 13CH4/12CH4, 13CO2/12CO2, and volatile fatty acids (VFAs).

To quantify the contribution of ethanol-derived carbon to methane, a binary isotope-mixing model was constructed based on 13C-labeled ethanol. The detailed methodology is provided in the Supplementary Materials.

Genomic DNA extraction and microbial community analysis

Samples for genomic DNA extraction were collected every seven days. Genomic DNA extraction was performed according to a previously described method (details in Supplementary Materials) (Zoetendal et al. 2006). The purity and concentration of the extracted DNA were assessed using a microspectrophotometer. After quality evaluation, samples were stored at − 20 °C temporarily, and then preserved at − 80 °C for long-term storage. Sequencing and metagenomic analyses were conducted by Beijing Biomarker Biotechnology using the Illumina NovaSeq platform.

Metagenome assembly was performed using MEGAHIT (Li et al. 2015), with contigs shorter than 300 bp discarded. Assembly statistics are provided in Table S8. Quality-filtered reads were aligned to a non-redundant gene catalog using Bowtie2 (v2.1.0) (Langmead and Salzberg 2012), and SAMtools (v1.5.0) (Qin et al. 2010) was used to determine read alignment lengths. Gene abundance was calculated based on read count and gene length. Differential abundance analysis of functional genes between sample groups was performed using DESeq2 (Barra et al. 2017), using threshold of |log2 fold change (FC)| > 1 and false discovery rate (FDR) < 0.05. Kyoto Encyclopedia of Genes and Genomes (KEGG) pathway enrichment analysis was performed on the set of significantly differential abundant genes using a hypergeometric test with FDR correction (Benjamini-Hochberg). KEGG pathways with adjusted p < 0.05 were considered significantly enriched. All analyses were performed on the BMKCloud platform (http://www.biocloud.net/), with key parameters set as default unless otherwise specified.

To construct microbial symbiotic networks, low-abundance genera were first filtered by retaining only those present in at least two samples and exhibiting a total relative abundance above 0.1%. Genera falling below this threshold were excluded from subsequent analysis. A co-occurrence relationship between taxa was considered robust if the Spearman’s correlation coefficient (ρ) exceeded 0.6 and the associated P-value was less than 0.05 (Barberán et al. 2012). The identification of keystone taxa was based on a multi-indicator integrative evaluation strategy. Firstly, the betweenness centrality of all nodes in the Spearman correlation network was calculated, and the top 10 taxa were designated as topological hubs. Secondly, linear discriminant analysis (LDA) effect size (LEfSe) was applied to detect taxa significantly enriched in specific treatment groups, using a threshold of LDA score > 4.0 and p < 0.05 for bacteria, and LDA score > 3.0 and p < 0.05 for archaea. Finally, taxa that were simultaneously identified as topological hubs and significantly enriched biomarkers were defined as keystone species within the microbial network of this study.

Quantitative PCR for assessing the anaerobic bacteria and methanogenic archaeal abundance

The abundance of bacterial and archaeal communities was assessed by quantitative polymerase chain reaction (qPCR) targeting on the 16 S rRNA gene. The bacterial primers 338 F (5′-ACTCCTACGGGAGGCAGCAG-3′) and 518R (5′-ATTACCGCGGCTGCTGG-3′), as well as the archaeal primers 357 F (5′-CCCTACGGGGCGCAGCAG-3′) and 691R (5′-GGATTACARGATTTCAC-3′) were used in this study. Detailed protocols and reaction conditions are provided in the Supplementary Materials.

Chemical analysis

Gas Analysis: Headspace gas (0.3 mL) was periodically sampled using a gas-tight syringe. Concentrations of hydrogen, methane, and carbon dioxide were measured using a GC-6890 A gas chromatograph (GC) equipped with a GDX-502 column (4 m × 3 mm; Hui Fen, Beijing, China). Cumulative gas volume released is measured with an airtight syringe statistical. According to the method described by Yansim and Buyong (Yasim and Buyong 2023), cumulative yield was calculated after correction to standard conditions (STP), taking into account headspace volume, ambient temperature, and pressure. All gas production values represent the mean of three biological replicates. High-purity nitrogen (99.999%) was used as the carrier gas. The column temperature was maintained at 60 °C, with the injection port and thermal conductivity detector (TCD) set at 150 °C and 100 °C, respectively.

The analysis of 13CH4 and 13CO2 was conducted using an online gas chromatograph-isotope ratio mass spectrometry (GC-IRMS) system (Thermo-Finnigan, USA). Specific procedures for isotope measurements are also detailed in the Supplementary Materials.

VFAs Analysis: VFAs were analyzed using a GC-6890 A GC fitted with a SH-PolarD Cap column (30 m × 0.32 mm × 0.25 μm; Hui Fen, Beijing, China). Nitrogen (99.999% purity) was used as the carrier gas. The initial column temperature was set at 120 °C and held for 1 min, then increased to 145 °C at a rate of 5 °C/min, followed by a ramp to 240 °C at 20 °C/min and held for 8 min. For isotope-labeled VFAs, 13C quantification was performed using a GC-IRMS system (Thermo Fisher Scientific, USA). Specific analytical procedures are detailed in the Supplementary Materials.

Soluble chemical oxygen demand (SCOD) was determined according to a previously established standard method (Liu et al. 2015).

Results

Enhancement of methane production via ethanol stimulation

Following three sequential enrichment cycles using coalbed production water, a methanogenic microbial consortium was successfully obtained with coal as the primary carbon source. Biogenic gas production dynamics are shown in Fig. 1A and B. Methane production in the group supplemented with 2 mg/mL ethanol was 10.9 times higher than that in the control. Notably, the combination of ethanol and coal increased methane production by more than 500 fold relative to ethanol alone (Fig. S1). During the 42-day incubation, no methane was detected in either the Ehk or the Chk. Carbon dioxide concentrations in both groups exhibited only minor non-biological background fluctuations (Table S1). Compared to the inoculated control without ethanol, methane production increased by a factor of 87 when the ethanol concentration was raised to 20 mg/mL. Furthermore, methane production persisted throughout the entire incubation period in the 2 mg/mL ethanol-supplemented group, and hydrogen accumulation exceeded the control level by 2.5 times (Fig. 1C). From day 10 to day 42, hydrogen and carbon dioxide were continuously generated and subsequently consumed as substrates for methanogenesis in the 2 mg/mL ethanol-supplemented cultures.

Enhancement of bioavailable organic substrates

As shown in Fig. 1E and F, ethanol addition significantly enhanced the release of organic compounds from coal, and the extent of leaching showed a positive correlation with ethanol concentration. Relative to the control, the ethanol-treated group displayed a substantially larger increase in SCOD by day 5. A rapid decline in SCOD was observed in all groups between days 5 and 21. Thereafter, SCOD stabilized in the control, while it continued to decrease in the ethanol-treated group, indicating persistent microbial consumption of dissolved organic substrates.

Ethanol-driven restructuring of microbial community architecture

The substantial increase in gas production and associated geochemical shifts prompted a detailed investigation into the underlying microbial mechanisms. Analysis of structural transitions within the coal seam microbiota elucidated that ethanol supplementation facilitated the formation of denser and functionally more efficient microbial co-occurrence networks (Fig. 2A and B), thereby strengthening microbial connectivity and cooperative metabolic interactions, which in turn enhanced CBM production. Ethanol selectively enriched several key microbial taxa, including Anaerostignum, Desulfovibrio, Synergistaceae, Clostridium, Petrimonas, Soehngenia, Methanobacterium, and Methanoculleus (Fig. 2C and F, S5, and S6), all of which may play central roles in syntrophic metabolism and methanogenesis.

To further evaluate the impact of ethanol on microbial interactions between bacteria and archaea, microbial interaction networks (MINE) were constructed based on bacterial taxa with relative abundances ≥ 1% and dominant archaeal genera (Fig. 2G and H). Compared with the control, the ethanol-treated group exhibited a higher number of network edges, suggesting greater topological complexity and stronger microbial connectivity. In particular, ethanol enhanced cooperative interactions between hydrogen-producing acetogens and methanogenic archaea, indicating strengthened syntrophy that likely contributed to the observed methane yield improvement.

Ethanol-induced shifts in microbial abundance revealed by qPCR

To quantify the impact of ethanol on microbial community dynamics, we measured the absolute copy numbers of 16 S rRNA genes for total bacteria and methanogenic archaea throughout the fermentation process (Fig. S2). On day 7, bacterial abundance in the ethanol-treated group (Group E) peaked at (3.14 ± 0.38) × 1013 copies/mL (mean ± 95% confidence interval (CI)), significantly higher than that in the control group (Group C), which reached (1.06 ± 0.09) × 1013 copies/mL (Student’s t-test, p < 0.001). While bacterial abundance in both groups declined thereafter, Group E maintained significantly higher levels on days 14 and 28 (p < 0.001). In contrast, the archaeal response was delayed but more pronounced. During the initial 21 days, there was no significant difference in archaeal abundance between the two groups, and in some cases, Group E exhibited slightly lower levels. However, from day 28 onward, a rapid increase in archaeal abundance was observed in the ethanol-treated group, coinciding with the onset of accelerated methane production (Fig. 1). By the end of the fermentation period (day 35), archaeal abundance in Group E remained significantly higher than in Group C.

The qPCR results suggest that ethanol initially stimulated rapid proliferation of the bacterial community (day 7), followed by a delayed but sharp increase in methanogenic archaeal populations beginning on day 28.

Ethanol-induced accumulation of VFAs reveals shift in metabolic flux

VFAs, key intermediate metabolites in anaerobic fermentation were monitored as indicators of metabolic status and precursors to final gas products. As shown in Fig. 3A and B, ethanol supplementation altered both the composition and concentration of VFAs, particularly elevating butyric acid and hexanoic acid levels. The ethanol-treated group exhibited greater VFAs diversity and higher total VFAs concentrations than the control, despite identical fermentation durations. Hexanoic acid was uniquely detected in the ethanol-supplemented group, suggesting activation of the reverse β-oxidation (RBO) pathway, a known response to ethanol addition (Kenealy et al. 1995). Correlation analysis further revealed that strong positive correlations (ρ > 0.80) between most ethanol-elevated VFAs and methane production, underscoring a coordinated metabolic network (Fig. S3).

Tracing fate of ethanol-derived carbon via 13C-labeling

Temporal dynamics of 13CH4/12CH4 and 13CO2/12CO2 ratios during fermentation are shown in Fig. 3C and D. Both isotopic signals increased simultaneously during early stages of biogenic gas production (day 7–14). At the methane production peak (day 21), 13CH4 constituted 40.2% of total methane, while 13CO2 accounted for 31.7% of total carbon dioxide. From day 28 to day 35, the 13CH4 ratio gradually decreased to approximately 17–18%, whereas the 13CO2 ratio continued to increase, reaching 44–47%. The sharp synchronous rise in both 13CH4 and 13CO2 on day 14 indicates active anaerobic microbial metabolism of the 13C-labeled ethanol.

A mass balance model based on 13C-labeled ethanol tracing revealed dynamic shifts in carbon sourcing for methane (Fig. 3E and Table S4). The direct contribution of ethanol-drived carbon to methane (fethanol) increased steadily from 2.46% on day 7 to 16.72% on day 28. However, absolute source decomposition analysis demonstrated that ethanol primarily acted as a biostimulant rather than a direct substrate (Fig. 3F). By day 28, ethanol-induced stimulation generated an additional 1.00 ± 0.19 mmol/g of coal-derived methane, accounting for 66.02% ± 12.78% of total methane yield. In contrast, methane formed directly from ethanol contributed only 16.72% ± 2.47%. These results suggest that approximately 75–84% of the total methane enhancement originated from stimulated coal biodegradation, rather than direct ethanol conversion.

In addition, isotopic analysis of VFAs revealed incorporation of ethanol derived carbon into both even- and odd-chain acids (Table S2). Acetate, butyrate, and hexanoate exhibited the highest 13C enrichment, identifying them as primary metabolites of ethanol assimilation. Propanate, isobutyrate, and isovalerate showed lower enrichment, suggesting limited ethanol-derived carbon incorporation. These findings provide direct evidence for partitioning of ethanol derived carbon into distinct VFAs pools alongside methanogenesis.

Metagenomic insights into metabolic potential and key gene enrichment

Stable isotope tracing clearly demonstrated carbon flux from ethanol through the microbial metabolic network. To complement these findings with genetic-level insights, metagenomic analysis was conducted to examine the abundance of key functional genes within the microbial community. As shown in Fig. 4, ethanol supplementation enriched genes involved in chain extension (CE) reactions, particularly in taxa, such as Desulfovibrio, Methanobacterium, and Syntrophomonas. Genes associated with acetate conversion to acetyl-CoA were also significantly enriched. Notably, abundance of core enzymes responsible for ethanol oxidation, including alcohol dehydrogenase (ADH), acetaldehyde dehydrogenase (ALDH), phosphate acetyltransferase (PTA), and acetate kinase (ACKA), were markedly elevated in the ethanol-treated group. These enzymes catalyze the stepwise transformation of ethanol into acetate and its subsequent assimilation into central carbon metabolism. Metagenomic analysis of methanogenesis pathways further identified key methanogenic genes, including cdhC (encoding acetyl-CoA decarbonylase), mcrG (encoding methyl-coenzyme M reductase subunit beta), and mtrA (encoding tetrahydromethanopterin S-methyltransferase subunit), in both control and ethanol-treated samples (Fig. 5). However, the abundance of acetate-converting genes (acs, ackA, pta) was significantly higher in the ethanol-supplemented group. These results suggest that ethanol not only serve as a direct carbon source for methanogenesis but also enhances precursor availability for chain-elongation processes, such as hexanoic acid production. Importantly, ethanol did not suppress methanogenic pathways; instead, it promoted concurrent acidogenic and methanogenic activity by augmenting substrate supply.

KEGG pathway enrichment analysis further revealed that genes associated with the methane metabolism pathway, including fdh, fwd, fmd, and mcrA, were significantly upregulated in the ethanol-treated group (log2FC > 1, padj < 0.05) (Fig. 5). These results provide quantitative transcriptional evidence that ethanol addition actively enhanced the functional expression of the methanogenesis pathway, rather than merely inducing shifts in microbial community composition.

Discussion

Ethanol as a dual-phase stimulant: beyond a substrate

The maximum methane content achieved in this study was 79.00%, representing a 14.94% increase compared with the highest reported value (67.62%) for coal‑corn‑straw co‑fermentation systems (Liang et al. 2022; Zhang et al. 2022). Moreover, the methane yield obtained with ethanol addition (2.65 mmol/g) exceeded the performance of previously reported CBM enhancement strategies, including nutrient amendments, algal co-substrates, and trace element stimulation, which typically achieved yields ranging from 0.04 to 1.71 mmol/g (Barnhart et al. 2017; Davis et al. 2019; Guo et al. 2020, 2021a; Piao et al. 2018; Zhang et al. 2023b; Zhu et al. 2024).

SCOD, a widely recognized indicator of substrate solubilization and microbial bioavailability (Du et al. 2024), was used to assess organic matter conversion efficiency. Ethanol addition accelerated the release of coal-derived organic compounds by enhancing solubilization and promoting the conversion of solid-phase material into soluble intermediates. Fourier transform infrared spectroscopy (FTIR) analysis of solid coal structures indicated that ethanol enhances the leaching of organic matter, particularly aliphatic and oxygen-containing constituents (Fig. S7). In addition, Zhang et al. found through GC-MS analysis of the leachate in the ethanol-added system that long-chain fatty acids, such as hexadecanoic acid and octadecanoic acid, as well as aromatic acids, such as phenylacetic acid, were the main soluble degradation products (Wang et al. 2019). These compounds are typical small-molecule products resulting from the cleavage of fatty side chains and aromatic structures in coal macromolecules (Yang et al. 2019). Thus, ethanol plays a dual role: it serves not only as a direct substrate but also an effective solubilizing agent, acting both as an initial stimulant and a sustained nutrient source. This dual functionality likely underlies the phased pattern of gas production observed during fermentation.

Microbial community restructuring driven by ethanol and metabolic intermediates

Ethanol supplementation reshaped the microbial community into a denser, more cohesive network (Fig. 2A and B), consistent with reports that nutrient enrichment enhances inter-microbial interactions (Banerjee et al. 2019; de Graaff et al. 2019). The elevated network connectivity suggests the formation of tightly coordinated microbial consortia driven by metabolite exchange. Ethanol systematically improved gas production efficiency by strengthening metabolic linkages and promoting syntrophic cooperation. In particular, it stimulated the enrichment of representative genera, including Anaerostignum, Desulfovibrio, Clostridium, and Methanobacterium, which are key players in organic matter degradation, acidogenesis, and electron transfer (Conrado et al. 2024; Meng et al. 2022; Nazina et al. 2020; Qian et al. 2021; Zhao and Zhang 2019; Zheng et al. 2021).

The establishment of microbial communities, along with the concurrent increase in total microbial abundance (Wu et al. 2015), biogas production and VFAs levels (Figs. 1 and 2) suggests that ethanol primarily stimulates metabolic activity. Additionally, this study confirmed that ethanol addition led to a marked VFAs accumulation, consistent with earlier observations (Holtzapple et al. 2015). Correlation analysis revealed a strong positive association between ethanol-induced VFAs accumulation and the relative abundance of acetoclastic methanogens, such as Methanosaeta, a relationship weak or absent in the control group (Fig. S4). These results indicate that ethanol functions not only as a carbon substrate but also as a modulator of metabolic flux, redirecting carbon flow within the anaerobic system.

Furthermore, accumulated VFAs may disrupt the coal matrix, releasing additional organic matter (Xu et al. 2021). Therefore, given its ability to stimulate VFAs synthesis, ethanol can be regarded as a central regulator of anaerobic system functionality, enhancing microbial syntrophy, optimizing community interactions, and improving overall metabolic efficiency.

Redirection of carbon flow: an integrated view from metabolites and isotopes

Isotopic tracing of 13CH4 and 13CO2 confirmed that ethanol-derived carbon enters both methane and carbon dioxide pools. The observed ratios of 13CH4/12CH4 and 13CO2/12CO2 suggest a major contribution from acetic acid fermentation pathway, consistent with the higher enrichment of 13CO2 (Rodrigues Oliveira et al. 2024).

In summary, ethanol enhances CBM production not through a single metabolic pathway, but via a synergistically integrated network of microbial processes. As a primary substrate, ethanol is rapidly converted into VFAs intermediates, particularly acetic acid, through the RBO pathway (Kenealy et al. 1995). Subsequent metabolism of these intermediates drives the carbon dioxide-reduction-to-methane pathway. This ethanol-driven transformation establishes an energetically activated micro-ecosystem that significantly enhances the co-fermentation of coal-derived organic matter. The redirection of carbon flux and shifts in energy metabolism, centered on acetic acid as a key metabolic node, represent principal mechanisms underlying the observed increase in methanogenic efficiency.

Metagenomic evidence for dominance of fermentative pathways

In the RBO cycle, acetyl-CoA acts as the key carbon donor for each chain-elongation step (Lv et al. 2024). The cycle is initiated by acetyl-CoA C-acetyltransferase, encoded by the genes atoB and ACAT, which catalyze the condensation of two acetyl-CoA molecules to form acetoacetyl-CoA, representing the first committed step in carbon-chain elongation (Lv et al. 2024). In this study, ethanol supplementation significantly enriched for the abundance of atoB and ACAT, indicating that the microbial community facilitated the elongation of short-chain fatty acids (e.g., acetic acid) into medium-chain fatty acids (e.g., hexanoic acid) via the RBO pathway (Fig. 6). These findings are consistent with previous reports that ethanol enhances hexanoate biosynthesis by upregulating the expression of carbon chain elongation-related genes (e.g., atoB, ACAT) and fatty acid synthesis-associated genes (e.g., CBR4/gpr, FAS) (Wang et al. 2024).

Proposed mechanism by which ethanol enhances CBM production

Metagenomic analysis revealed that ethanol supplementation significantly enriched for the abundance of functional genes involved in ethanol oxidation, acetate biosynthesis and utilization, and central carbon metabolism (Fig. 5). These results elucidate the underlying metabolic activation mechanism: upon ethanol addition, microbial communities initiate a coordinated cascade in which ethanol serves as both a carbon source and an electron donor, driving the sequential processes of ethanol oxidation, acetogenesis, and ultimately methanogenesis. The carbon backbone of ethanol is primarily channeled through the acetic acid pathway, which simultaneously redirects central carbon metabolic flux and markedly increases intracellular acetyl-CoA levels. This metabolic reprogramming supplies both acetoclastic and hydrogenotrophic methanogenic archaea with elevated levels of key substrates, such as acetate and H2/CO2, thereby synergistically enhancing overall methane production in the anaerobic system (Fig. 6).

Mechanisms of ethanol in enhancing CBM production

As a polar solvent, ethanol disrupts non-covalent interactions, particularly hydrogen bonds, within the coal matrix (Li et al. 2024). This promotes the dissolution of macromolecular organic compounds, such as long-chain alkanes and aromatic structures. The resulting physicochemical effect significantly increases the SCOD, thereby expanding the pool of bioavailable substrates for microbial metabolism. Simultaneously, VFAs produced from ethanol degradation, including acetate and butyrate, further penetrate and destabilize the coal matrix, promoting the release of additional soluble organics (Fig. 6). Moreover, ethanol selectively enriches key functional bacteria, including Clostridium and Soehngenia, which are proficient in degrading complex organic matter into fermentable intermediates.

At the molecular level, ethanol supplementation enriches core functional genes involved in ethanol oxidation, acetate metabolism, and central carbon pathways, thereby reinforcing the metabolic flow toward methane synthesis (Fig. 6). Furthermore, ethanol enhances syntrophic interactions between hydrogen-producing acetogens and methanogenic archaea, strengthening microbial network connectivity and metabolic cooperation. These synergistic effects position ethanol not only as a readily carbon source but also as a multifunctional modulator of microbial community structure and metabolic function, ultimately driving a substantial increase in CBM yield.

Conclusion

This study systematically elucidates the mechanism by which ethanol stimulates CBM production. The addition of 2 mg/mL ethanol significantly enhanced gas output, increasing cumulative methane and hydrogen yields by 10.9-fold and 3.1-fold, respectively, relative to the control. Tracing with 13C-labeled ethanol demonstrated that approximately 75–84% of the total methane increase resulted from stimulated microbial degradation of coal rather than direct ethanol conversion. Ethanol also promoted the enrichment of key hydrogen‑producing acetogens, such as Clostridium, Soehngenia, and Anaerostignum. Furthermore, the abundance of genes involved in ethanol oxidation, acetate metabolism, and central carbon pathways was significantly enriched. Together, these findings offer mechanistic insights and a theoretical foundation for optimizing microbia enhanced CBM production.
