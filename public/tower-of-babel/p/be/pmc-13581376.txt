Staged EUS-guided gastroenterostomy for malignant gastric outlet obstruction
Authors: Xiangyang Wang, Hui Chen, Peng Liu, Ya Peng
Published: 2026

Dear Editor,

In malignant gastric outlet obstruction (MGOO), complete obstruction often leads to severe malnutrition and electrolyte imbalance. The direct implementation of EUS-guided gastroenterostomy (EUS-GE) is technically challenging and associated with high risks of complications. This article reports a staged endoscopic intervention strategy for seven patients with high-risk malnutrition. In the first stage, a nasojejunal nutrition tube was inserted into the distal jejunum of the obstruction under endoscopic guidance to establish an enteral nutrition pathway to correct hypoproteinemia and electrolyte imbalance. In the second stage, EUS-GE was performed after the improvement of nutritional status. This strategy optimizes the physiological state of patients through nutritional support, creates anatomical and physiological conditions for subsequent EUS-GE, and is applicable to MGOO patients with cachexia.

We present seven cases of staged EUS-GE for MGOO. These patients had extremely poor nutritional status and electrolyte imbalance, making primary EUS-guided gastrojejunostomy (EUS-GE) highly difficult and risky. After multidisciplinary team discussion, a staged EUS-GE was planned:

STAGE 1: NASOJEJUNAL FEEDING TUBE PLACEMENT

After aspirating gastric retention fluid under gastroscopy, a guidewire successfully passed the stricture under X-ray guidance, and a nasobiliary tube was placed as a substitute feeding tube. Contrast medium was injected through the nasobiliary tube, and good opacification of the distal jejunum was observed (Figure 1C). The feeding tube was adjusted and fixed. Postoperatively, enteral nutrition support (Peptisorb 500 mL/d gradually increased to 1000 mL/d), red blood cell transfusion, albumin supplementation, and correction of electrolyte imbalance were administered, and the patient’s nutritional status was improved with no abdominal distension or signs of intestinal obstruction.

A, Computed tomography (CT) shows a tumor in the head of the pancreas invading the duodenum. B, Gastroscopy reveals invasion at the junction of the duodenal bulb and descending part by a tumor. C, A nasobiliary catheter was placed to replace the feeding tube and advanced to the jejunum; contrast radiography shows visualization of the distal small intestine. D–E, A double-flowered mushroom-shaped covered metal stent (HOT-AXIOS) was implanted. F, Gastroscopy shows methylene blue refluxing into the gastric lumen with the stent in good position.

STAGE 2: EUS-GE

Dilute contrast medium, methylene blue solution, and normal saline were injected into the intestinal lumen through the enteral feeding tube.Under EUS (Olympus ME-2) and X-ray guidance, an appropriate proximal jejunum was identified. Anisodamine was injected to inhibit intestinal peristalsis, and a dual-mushroom covered metal stent (HOT-AXIOS) was deployed (Figures 1D–F). Postoperative gastroscopy revealed methylene blue reflux into the gastric cavity, with the stent in a satisfactory position, confirming the successful completion of gastrojejunostomy. The operation lasted approximately 15 to 20 minutes (Figure 1E). The patient started a liquid diet on postoperative day 2 and gradually transitioned to a semi-liquid diet. No perioperative complications occurred, and the patient was discharged in good condition on postoperative day 2 to 4.

Therapeutic approaches for MGOO include surgery, endoscopic duodenal stent placement, and EUS-GE. Compared with surgical gastrojejunostomy, EUS-GE has comparable technical and clinical success rates, and shorter time to oral intake, shorter hospital stay, and lower adverse event rates. Compared with endoscopic duodenal stenting, EUS-GE can achieve higher clinical success, faster resumption of oral feeding, and lower recurrence of gastric outlet obstruction. Based on these advantages, the European Society of Gastrointestinal Endoscopy recommends EUS-GE as an alternative to endoscopic stenting or surgical gastrojejunostomy for MGOO in centers with appropriate expertise.[1,2]

Factors associated with EUS-GE technical success include: (1) Operator experience and skill: The procedure is technically demanding and requires extensive EUS interventional experience.[3,4] (2) Technique selection: use of stable techniques such as catheter assistance may improve success rates.[3] (3) Patient anatomy: excessive distance between the stomach and small bowel or interposition of critical vascular structures may prevent safe puncture, representing a major cause of technical failure.[5]

To date, no literature has investigated the impact of complete obstruction on EUS-GE success and complications. We hypothesize that in cases of complete obstruction, guidewires, and catheters may fail to enter the jejunum, making catheter- or balloon-assisted EUS-GE unfeasible, thus reducing technical success and increasing complication risk. Although no studies have analyzed the relationship between malnutrition severity and EUS-GE adverse events, extensive evidence confirms that malnutrition systematically increases perioperative complications, mortality, and healthcare utilization across various surgeries. Poor nutritional status significantly elevates postoperative complication risk in a dose-dependent manner. For patients with complete obstruction and severe malnutrition, EUS-GE is technically challenging, with inevitably prolonged procedure time and increased complication risk, especially for less experienced operators.

A staged endoscopic intervention strategy using preoperative jejunal tube enteral nutrition for patients with complete MGOO and severe malnutrition offers multiple advantages: (1) Staging strategy reduces single-procedure duration and intraoperative complications: In complete obstruction, guidewire passage may be time-consuming. One-stage EUS-GE may increase stress, bleeding, or perforation risk. Separating the procedure into feeding tube placement followed by EUS-GE shortens single endoscopic time and lowers intraoperative risk. (2) Enteral nutrition improves nutritional status and reduces postoperative complications: Jejunal feeding rapidly corrects hypoalbuminemia and electrolyte disturbances, improves physiological condition, enhances tissue healing and immunity, and reduces postoperative infection, anastomotic leakage, and other complications. (3) Preoperative observation excludes distal small bowel obstruction and ensures feasibility of EUS-GE: monitoring abdominal distension and bowel movements during nutritional support allows indirect assessment of distal small bowel patency. This step is critical because unrecognized multiple small bowel strictures may lead to EUS-GE failure.

In conclusion, for patients with MGOO and malnutrition, staged EUS-GE can shorten single-procedure time, improve nutritional status, reduce postoperative risk, and exclude distal small bowel obstruction. This staging strategy might provide a safe and feasible endoscopic treatment for patients with MGOO and malnutrition.
