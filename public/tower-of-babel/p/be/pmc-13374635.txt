Challenging Patterns of Fine‐Scale Genetic Structure in Galicia and East‐to‐West Genetic Divergence Across the Iberian Peninsula—When Technical Batches Rewrite History
Authors: Jacobo Pardo‐Seco, Alberto Gómez‐Carballa, Conrado Martínez‐Cadenas, Federico Martinón‐Torres, Antonio Salas
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTThe influential 2019 study by Bycroft et al. reported remarkably pronounced ultrafine‐scale genetic structure within a small region of Galicia (northwestern Iberia). Using fineSTRUCTURE clustering of ChromoPainter coancestry profiles, the authors identified multiple internally coherent clusters characterized by high levels of within‐group haplotype sharing and reduced sharing with neighboring populations. On a broader national scale, they also reported a marked East–West genetic differentiation and North–South homogeneity across the Iberian Peninsula, patterns which they largely attributed to historical population movements associated with the gradual territorial expansion of Christian kingdoms between the 8th and 15th centuries following the initial Islamic conquest. Several subsequent studies have adopted similar interpretations, but all relied on the same genetic resource, the EPICOLON Phase I cohort. Following a detailed reexamination of Bycroft et al., we identify multiple methodological issues, most notably signals consistent with unaccounted batch effects in EPICOLON, some of which were recognized by the authors in earlier studies. In contrast, we highlight recent analyses based on independent genomic datasets that consistently report high levels of genetic homogeneity within Galicia and across central and southern Iberia, with no evidence supporting a pronounced East–West genetic divide across Iberia. In addition, recent studies have documented North African demographic influences in Iberia, particularly in Galicia, predating the period of Islamic rule and therefore challenging the historical interpretation proposed by Bycroft et al. We also show that while fineSTRUCTURE is a powerful tool for detecting subtle population stratification, it may be highly sensitive to technical confounders, which can magnify or distort inferred clustering patterns. This study demonstrates that technical artifacts can strongly affect fine‐scale population structure inferred by fineSTRUCTURE, emphasizing the need for rigorous quality control and cautious interpretation of highly resolved genetic clusters.Keywords: batch effects, fineSTRUCTURE, GALOMICS, medieval Spain, whole‐genome sequencing

Background

Bycroft et al. [1] conducted an in‐depth genetic analysis of the Iberian Peninsula, revealing significant fine‐scale population structure and deep genetic stratification. Utilizing the fineSTRUCTURE method and the GWAS Spanish dataset known as EPICOLON Phase I (see details in Supporting Information), they identified 145 distinct clusters, with a particular emphasis on what they considered highly reliable cluster assignments, especially in geographically localized regions such as Galicia (located in the northwestern corner of the Peninsula) and the Basque Country (in the North). The study also highlighted a major axis of genetic differentiation running East to West across the peninsula and minor genetic differences in the North–South direction across Iberia, patterns that the authors attributed to population movements during the Reconquista, arguing that migrations from northern Christian kingdoms into newly conquered territories generated genetic boundaries that closely reflect historical regional divisions. The study has received significant academic attention and widespread discussion on social media platforms.

Following these findings, both Pimenta et al. [4] and Ferreiro et al. [5] reported Galicia as a genetically distinctive region within the Iberian Peninsula, attributing this pattern to a combination of historical, cultural, linguistic, and demographic factors. Pimenta et al. [4] emphasized the close genetic relationship between Galicia and Portugal and proposed that genetic structure across Iberia reflects historical political and cultural boundaries. Ferreiro et al. [5] similarly identified Galicia as genetically differentiated and argued that historical isolation, language persistence, and demographic processes contributed to the observed patterns. However, both studies relied on the same EPICOLON dataset. Consequently, their findings should not be considered independent confirmations of the reported patterns, but rather as interpretations derived from essentially the same underlying genetic resource. An additional indication of the influence of findings by Bycroft et al. [1], likely magnified by its publication in a high‐impact journal, comes from Garcia‐Calleja et al. [6], who used an independent dataset but supported the same hypothesis without presenting new evidence (Supporting Information).

We have thoroughly examined the study of Bycroft et al. [1] and identified numerous significant anomalies, most likely arising from technical artifacts and other methodological issues, that may account for most of the patterns reported and their conclusions. Given that the same dataset was used in other studies addressing similar historical questions [4, 5], there is a clear risk that all of them may also be affected by the same technical artifacts, potentially reinforcing historical interpretations derived from the defective dataset.

While our main focus is a close inspection of the EPICOLON dataset and the pioneering study by Bycroft et al. [1], we also highlight that recent analyses of the Galician population, using independent genomic datasets [2], reveal high genetic homogeneity within Galicia, contrasting with the pronounced stratification reported by [1]. Additionally, analyses by Pardo‐Seco et al. [2] have recently reveal genetic continuity along an East–West axis across the southern and central Iberian Peninsula, and a lack of homogeneity along the North–South axis, again differing from the patterns reported in [1]. Finally, we provide a proof‐of‐concept showing that, while fineSTRUCTURE is a powerful tool for detecting fine‐scale population stratification based on haplotype‐sharing patterns, its results may be sensitive to technical artifacts capable of altering coancestry profiles and generating spurious clusters.

Results

The EPICOLON Phase I Dataset and Technical Issues

Bycroft et al. [1] used genomic data from Fernández‐Rozadilla et al. [7], which were part of the EPICOLON Phase I colorectal cancer genome‐wide association study (GWAS). The EPICOLON cohort consisted of 1548 donors, including 881 individuals with colorectal adenocarcinoma and 667 controls, with ~68% recruited from five hospitals in Catalonia (northeast of Spain) and Hospital do Meixoeiro in Vigo, Galicia (northwest of Spain). However, information about the recruiting centers remains ambiguous. This issue is particularly sensitive, given that the main argumentation in the present report concerns potential batch effects (Supporting Information).

As explained in their manuscript, 1413 samples were retained after quality control and used to generate the main fineSTRUCTURE dendrogram. Of these, 726 individuals whose four grandparents were born within 80 km of their birthplace were retained for the final geographic analysis. These individuals were assigned to clusters inferred from the full set of 1413 samples. Consequently, the interpretation of the results relies on the assumption that the inclusion of the remaining samples, for which equivalent geographic constraints were not applied, did not materially influence the clustering structure inferred from the full dataset. One of the most notable findings was the unexpectedly pronounced ultra‐fine‐scale genetic structure reported within Galicia, suggesting its genetic uniqueness relative to other regions of Spain.

However, Bycroft et al. [1] did not address key technical issues, particularly batch effects related to the hospital of origin; concerns that had already been raised in connection with the EPICOLON dataset (Supporting Information). Thus, Fernández‐Rozadilla's PhD dissertation [8], one of the coauthors in [1], along with other previous publications using the EPICOLON dataset [7, 9], highlighted the risk of population stratification arising from batch effects stemming from variations in sample collection and DNA extraction procedures. As mentioned in Fernández‐Rozadilla et al. [7], the EPICOLON dataset was processed in 23 separate batches, each containing between 16 and 99 samples according to hospital of origin, introducing the potential for batch‐related distortions (Supporting Information).

As we argue below (and in Supporting Information), batch effects in EPICOLON may offer a more parsimonious explanation for their extraordinary findings. This omission is particularly significant given the high sensitivity of fineSTRUCTURE for detecting subtle genetic stratification. As we demonstrate below, this tool can be highly sensitive to both genuine and artifactual patterns in genetic data. Moreover, the peer review process failed to identify these technical limitations (reports are publicly available on the journal's website).

A comprehensive description of the anomalies discussed in this section is provided in the Supporting Information. Figures S1–S11 in this document are intended to support the interpretation of the methodological and technical issues discussed in sections 2.2, 2.3 and 2.4. Several of these figures contain edited versions of the maps published by Bycroft et al. [1] intended to facilitate interpretation and highlight key features.

Artificial Ultra‐Fine‐Scale Stratification in Pontevedra, Galicia

Bycroft et al. [1] reported extensive fine‐scale genetic structure within Spain, with an unexpectedly high level in the province of Pontevedra (in the south‐western corner of Galicia), where they identified an extremely dense concentration of genetic clusters, particularly around the port city of Vigo and its surrounding southern and eastern areas. The authors stated that fineSTRUCTURE inferred 145 distinct clusters in the entire Spanish (EPICOLON) dataset, noting that “by far the strongest sub‐structure is seen within a single province in Galicia, Pontevedra, which contains almost half of the inferred clusters in all of Spain”. To put this in context, the area referenced by the authors is a small region within Pontevedra, corresponding roughly to a compact, rectangular area approximately 20 km North–South, and 60 km East–West. The authors attributed this fine‐scale genetic differentiation to geographic features such as hills and river valleys, despite the absence of relevant barriers in the region, and argued that genetic structure could be detected at scales as small as 10 km.

The clustering process employed by the authors is problematic, as it was based on the full cohort despite the absence of geographic origin data for approximately half of the samples (726 out of 1,413). Consequently, geographic labels were assigned to clusters inferred from haplotype‐sharing similarity, even though sample provenance metadata were incomplete (Supporting Information). The subsequent analyses rely heavily on this initial clustering step and therefore inherit these limitations.

Upon closer examination, the results appear even more extraordinary than those described by the authors. A meticulous inspection of their complete fineSTRUCTURE Iberian tree (Figure 1A) reveals that more than 72% of the inferred clusters and internal splits (> 105 of 145) are concentrated solely in Pontevedra, far exceeding the “about half” reported by the authors. This pattern indicates an exceptionally high degree of haplotype‐sharing differentiation within this very small area. In contrast, most other regions of continental Spain are represented by only one to three branches; for example, the entire Basque Country has just seven. Most strikingly, a count of the individuals shown in the authors' maps (their figure 1B and the zoomed‐in genetic map in their figure 3A) suggests that there are more inferred clusters than sampled individuals fulfilling the criterion of having four grandparents born within the same Galician region (Supporting Information). This discrepancy calls for closer scrutiny of the robustness of the EPICOLON dataset used in their genetic clustering analyses and raises significant concerns about potential technical and sampling‐related biases in the interpretation and representation of their findings.

The high degree of localized genetic differentiation observed in Pontevedra, particularly at resolutions under 10 km, may be influenced by technical issues in the EPICOLON dataset. Previous reports of the authors have noted chemical and procedural problems in sample processing that could have artificially induced fine‐scale stratification [7]. A critical issue is the potential sampling bias, as all Galician samples were collected more than a decade before the publication of the study from a single institution, Hospital do Meixoeiro (Vigo city), originally for biomedical research purposes. This hospital is in a highly urbanized area with well‐established transportation links. This characteristic of the region contradicts the authors' assertion of extreme genetic isolation, as such urban and economic connectivity should theoretically promote genetic exchange rather than isolation. The interconnected nature of areas like Vigo (a historically significant port city located in the core of this highly stratified region), Tui, A Guarda, Ponteareas, and Tomiño, with populations exceeding 10,000–20,000, further undermines the claim of extreme genetic isolation of this key area. Moreover, the authors' assertion that Galicia's genetic structure is primarily shaped by rural living and linguistic uniqueness is difficult to reconcile with observations from other linguistically distinct regions of Spain, such as Catalonia and the Basque Country (speaking a pre‐Indo‐European language), which do not exhibit comparable genetic fragmentation. In addition, the widespread use of a common language throughout Galicia (Galician) would not be expected to promote genetic differentiation within the region. Additionally, the region's long history as an important urban and religious center, including cities like Tui, also located at the epicenter of the area identified as highly genetically stratified, challenges the portrayal of Galicia as a genetically isolated area. Tui, for example, was one of the most important Christian centers during the early medieval territorial expansion of Christian kingdoms, making the claim of genetic isolation at that time even more dubious (Supporting Information).

Previous research by Fernández‐Rozadilla et al. [7, 8, 9] documented technical biases, particularly related to sample processing, which, in our view, provide a more parsimonious explanation for these unusual patterns than do genuine historical or geographical factors. Accordingly, the extraordinary findings raise concerns about methodological flaws, suggesting that the observed genetic patterns may reflect technical artifacts rather than genuine historical or geographical factors. In addition, the marked contrast between the highly structured results from Bycroft et al. [1] and the very limited genetic differentiation observed in the high‐resolution Whole Genome Sequencing (WGS) data generated within the GALOMICS (GAL) project [2], further supports this hypothesis. This discrepancy warrants careful consideration (see below and Supporting Information).

Sample Quality Issues and National‐Level Genetic Patterns

Bycroft et al. [1] identified national‐scale genetic patterns that may also stem from technical artifacts rather than genuine population structure. For instance, they described Galicia as exhibiting the earliest genetic split from the rest of Spain. This result appears to contradict earlier studies suggesting that the region was a genetic cul‐de‐sac shaped by post‐glacial migrations rather than continuous habitation [10] (see a more detail argumentation in Supporting Information). While the Cantabrian Mountain range, which runs from East to West parallel to the northern coast of the Iberian Peninsula, has historically acted as a barrier to gene flow toward the southern part of the peninsula, the authors observed greater genetic continuity across it than within the relatively small Pontevedra region, where no significant geographical barriers are present. This inconsistency raises concerns about the validity of their inferred North–South genetic homogeneity and East–West genetic stratification across the Iberian Peninsula.

Two major national‐scale patterns in the Spanish maps provided by Bycroft et al. [1] are particularly problematic (Supporting Information). The considerations discussed below should be viewed as interpretative and exploratory, as their validity can only be formally assessed through examination of the original data and metadata. First, their “Central” cluster (yellow band in e.g., Figures S4–S6) may closely align with a batch of samples derived from their National DNA bank (NDNAB) dataset, all of which could have been processed in the same laboratory. The uniform appearance of this cluster, extending from North to South and broadly distributed across the Iberian Peninsula, suggests that sample processing, rather than true genetic similarity, may have contributed to its distinct grouping. Second, their “West” cluster (red band in e.g., Figures S4–S6) exhibits greater haplotype‐sharing similarity with samples from Catalonia and the Balearic Islands (distantly located in the easternmost part of the Iberian Peninsula and the Mediterranean Sea, respectively) than with geographically adjacent regions, such as Cantabria, Navarra, and the Basque Country (see their fineSTRUCTURE tree of figure 1A). This unexpected pattern raises the possibility of technical bias rather than reflecting genuine historical genetic connections. For instance, if many of these samples assigned to the “West” cluster were processed at the same facility, such as the Hospital de Donostia (Basque Country), shared technical factors could have introduced systematic biases that contributed to the anomalous grouping. Notably, some members embedded within the ‘red band’ cluster belong to typical Basque clusters (their figure 1B; Supporting Information). Collectively, these issues cast doubt on Bycroft et al.'s [1] claim of an East–West genetic divide in Iberia.

Close inspection of the study by Bycroft et al. [1] revealed further notable inconsistencies that are not a matter of interpretation but are directly observable from their published descriptions and figures. The authors included 107 Portuguese individuals for whom no biogeographic information was available (as stated by the authors). These samples were displayed as being geographically randomly distributed across mainland Portugal, although the authors did not elaborate on the rationale underlying this decision. These samples received the same color in their maps as the ‘Main’ Galician cluster. This arbitrary decision artificially suggests a smooth, longitudinal, homogeneous genetic pattern between Northern Galicia and Southern Portugal, thereby making their purported East‐to‐West pattern across the entire Peninsula appear much more convincing. Also problematic, this view completely obscures the very distinctive genetic features they describe for the Pontevedra area, which, according to the authors, contains almost half of the inferred clusters in all of Spain. Furthermore, although they identified 145 genetic clusters in Spain, their analyses assigned only a single cluster to all of Portugal.

Finally, the inclusion of Portuguese samples caused Galicia to be split into two clear coancestry‐based non‐sister branches within the fineSTRUCTURE dendrogram. This pattern is difficult to reconcile with an explanation based on recent isolation alone. Rather than reflecting recent demographic separation, the results imply a strong and persistent differentiation in haplotype‐sharing profiles within Galicia. This would require the long‐term historical coexistence of two markedly dissimilar and minimally admixed coancestry profiles within a geographically very small and historically well‐connected region within Galicia and with Portugal (Supporting Information).

Sampling Bias and Its Impact on Results

Although not explicitly stated, the maps in Bycroft et al. [1] exhibit a notable urban sampling bias, with most individuals seemingly recruited from major provincial capitals or large hospitals in cities such as Barcelona, Lleida, Tarragona, Zaragoza, and Oviedo (Supporting Information). The spatial distribution of their samples suggests a particularly high concentration of urban participants in regions like Catalonia, especially from cities like Barcelona, Lleida, and Tarragona, whereas regions such as the Basque Country and Galicia appear to have more rural sampling patterns (Supporting Information). This sampling bias is important, as individuals from urban areas are generally more likely to exhibit diverse ancestral origins compared to those from rural settings. However, the study does not address this issue, which may lead to misinterpretation of the observed genetic patterns. If sampling strategies varied geographically, favoring urban centers in some regions and rural areas in others, this could artificially inflate the apparent genetic structure in certain areas while obscuring it in others. For example, while we suspect that the extreme genetic fragmentation observed in Galicia is most likely due to technical issues, it could also be plausible that an overrepresentation of individuals from specific rural locations, rather than true population structure, may have contributed to the observed patterns. Since Bycroft et al. [1] did not provide detailed information on the geographic origins of their samples, there is ambiguity regarding whether donors were from capital cities, smaller towns, or rural areas. Moreover, in Spain, province names often coincide with the names of capital cities, meaning a sample labeled “Pontevedra” could refer either to the city or the entire province (Supporting Information). It is therefore conceivable that the geographic resolution for most of the biogeographically identified donors was limited to the provincial level, forcing the authors to assign locations based on the capital of the corresponding provinces.

Peer reviewers raised concerns regarding these biases, questioning whether observed differences in population structure reflect true genetic variation or sampling‐related biases (Supporting Information). Despite acknowledging this possibility, Bycroft et al. [1] did not provide sufficient transparency about their sampling methods, limiting the reliability of their conclusions (Supporting Information).

Issues With Basque (And Other) Genetic Assignments

Bycroft et al.'s [1] dataset includes individuals assigned to Basque genetic clusters despite residing outside the Basque Country, such as in Extremadura in the South (Supporting Information). For this classification to be accurate, these individuals would need to have multiple generations of Basque ancestry without admixture with locals, despite living far outside the Basque region, as the authors state that all four grandparents of each donor were born within 80 km of their average birthplace. A more plausible explanation, however, is that these individuals were processed in the same hospital as other Basque samples, leading to systematic technical biases. To clarify this issue, it would be essential to determine whether these Basque‐assigned individuals (and all donors in EPICOLON) were sampled and processed in the same batch as other Basque donors (e.g., Hospital de Donostia). If this is the case, it would suggest that their fineSTRUCTURE genetic clustering is an artifact of data processing rather than a reflection of genuine ancestry.

Similar examples can be observed in other parts of the map and with other regions: for instance, one individual seemingly belonging to a very ‘genuine’ Galician clade (highlighted in their zoomed‐in maps of figure 3A) appears in the South of the Basque Country (Supporting Information). In this case, we hypothesize that the sample, originally from the Basque Country, may have artificially acquired a Galician cluster designation simply due to being processed in the Galician batch of Hospital do Meixoeiro. The Supporting Information provides additional examples of these counterintuitive, biogeographically ‘misallocated’ genomes.

Most Recent Analyses Contradict Bycroft et al. (2019) Findings

The recent analysis carried out by Pardo‐Seco et al. [2], using the highest level of genetic resolution possible of the GAL WGS dataset (including town‐level geographic resolution), yielded results that differ substantially from those reported by [1], while taking into account micro‐regional differences in the sampling areas. Pardo‐Seco et al. [2] identified 10 terminal branch clusters in Galicia (compared to Bycroft et al.'s > 105 out of 145 in Iberia), with only five having at least four individuals; Figure 1A,B and Figure S8. One of these clusters occupied almost the entire Galicia, while the others had a more localized distribution (figure 3 in Pardo‐Seco et al. [2]).

From a national perspective, Pardo‐Seco et al. [2] analyzed an independent GWAS database (referred to as NDNAB‐PS in [2]) that provides broad representation of Iberian provinces and includes an amount of SNPs comparable to those used by Bycroft et al. [1]; albeit with differences in geographic sampling coverage between the two studies. Rather than detecting the pronounced East–West genetic patterns reported by Bycroft et al. [1] (Figure 2A), Pardo‐Seco et al. [2] identified a main cluster covering the whole South‐Central Iberian Peninsula, spanning East to West across Andalusia, along with well‐localized clusters throughout most of the Spanish territory (Figure 2B; Figure S9). These findings suggest a markedly different pattern of population structure from that reported by Bycroft et al. [1], despite the use of datasets with broadly comparable genomic resolution.

Moreover, consistent with earlier mitochondrial DNA (mtDNA) and the Y‐chromosome studies [10, 11, 12, 13, 14], Pardo‐Seco et al. [2] found that the first hierarchical split separates the Basque Country from the neighboring Cantabria region, while Galicia appears in the final division of the tree. This pattern is in line with prior evidence suggesting that Galicia was likely one of the last regions of continental Europe to be populated in ancient times.

Bycroft et al.'s [1] attribution of the observed East–West divergence and North–South genetic continuity in the Iberian Peninsula to the historical process of the Reconquista [sic] remains highly speculative: “Our observation that genetic differences are small in the north‐south direction within Spain, and evidence of gene flow preferentially in this direction, are most straightforward to interpret in the light of historical information regarding the Reconquista, during with Christian‐controlled territory in the north moved gradually southwards from the mid‐8th Century, following the Muslim conquest of Iberia…” [1]. The hypothesis that the gradual territorial expansion of Christian kingdoms progressed from North to South may help explain a pattern of genetic homogeneity along this axis (provided that “North” is understood as referring to regions South of the big Cantabrian Mountains, rather than the Cantabrian coast itself). However, this is difficult to reconcile with the pronounced East–West genetic differentiation described by the authors and also the fact that most significant geographic barriers (rivers and mountain ranges) in Iberia run along an East–West axis, which would facilitate movements in this direction while slowing down movements from North to South (Supporting Information). Linking these genetic patterns directly to the territorial expansion of Christian kingdoms would require assuming large‐scale population movements from North to South, while simultaneously maintaining marked East–West genetic differentiation at a much finer geographic scale; this assumption is geographically lacks historical support. For instance, one of the largest resettlement initiatives of the Christian territorial expansion, the ninth‐century Repoblación (“repopulation”), involved the settlement of a vast territory between the River Duero and the southern slopes of the Cantabrian Mountains from East to West. Given its scale, this process likely increased regional gene flow, making it difficult to reconcile with the claimed East–West genetic pattern. Furthermore, Bycroft et al. [1] observed that “the broad‐scale genetic structure of the region is strikingly similar to the linguistic frontiers29 present in the Iberian Peninsula around 1300 CE” (see their Figure 1C, based on maps by Baldinger [3]; reproduced here as Figure 2C). Interestingly, this historical linguistic division appears to more closely resemble the genetic pattern described by Parso‐Seco et al. [2], in which a major lineage is almost uniformly distributed across much of the central and southern Iberian Peninsula, than to the pattern reported by Bycroft et al. [1], further complicating any straightforward interpretation of the genetic landscape in terms of this period of Christian expansion alone.

Finally, Pardo‐Seco et al. [2], along with recent ancient DNA studies [15, 16], and well‐established historical and archeological evidence (see [2] for an in‐depth discussion), bring together multiple lines of evidence that challenge the traditional hypothesis attributing North African and Middle Eastern ancestry in the northern Iberian Peninsula primarily or solely to this period of Christian expansion. Instead, these findings suggest the need to reconsider the role of earlier, pre‐Islamic contacts, dating back at least to Roman times, as significant contributors to the demographic landscape that shaped the genetic structure of northern Iberian populations, particularly in Galicia. This perspective represents a paradigm shift in understanding the historical processes underlying present‐day genomic patterns in the region.

FineSTRUCTURE Might Be Highly Sensitive to Genetic Stratification, Whether Real or Artificial

Bycroft et al. [1] used fineSTRUCTURE software to detect fine‐scale genetic patterns, a method that is more sensitive than traditional approaches, such as principal component analysis (PCA). While this sensitivity enables the identification of subtle demographic processes, it may also increase susceptibility to technical artifacts introduced during laboratory processing, even when such artifacts are not apparent using standard methods. To illustrate this, we conducted a few pragmatic in silico experiments (see Supporting Information for further details).

Before addressing this issue, we first test the sensitivity of fineSTRUCTURE to sample size. In their analysis of the entire GAL cohort, Pardo‐Seco et al. [2] identified five clusters containing at least four individuals: ‘Main’, ‘Porto do Son’, ‘Cuntis/A Estrada’, ‘A Mariña/Foz’, and ‘Central/West’. To assess the effect of sample size, we ran fineSTRUCTURE on the GAL3 dataset, which consisted of a random selection of only three individuals from each of these five clusters. Despite the substantial reduction in sample size, fineSTRUCTURE largely reproduced the topology of the original clustering tree, suffering only one minimal modification. Specifically, the analysis retained three of the main clusters and merged the ‘Main’ and ‘A Estrada/Cuntis’ clusters into a single group (Figure 3A). These results suggest that reducing sample size does not necessarily hinder cluster detection or substantially alter tree topology in our case, as long as a small but representative sample from each cluster is included.

To assess the sensitivity of fineSTRUCTURE to batch effects, we analyzed a merged dataset combining samples from the GAL (WGS) and NDNAB‐PS (genotyping array) cohorts, which were generated using different technological platforms and processed in independent laboratories. In the absence of significant batch effects, GAL samples cluster with Galician individuals from the NDNAB‐PS dataset, reflecting shared geographic origin. In contrast, fineSTRUCTURE produced a markedly different and more complex structure. Before merging, Galician individuals in NDNAB‐PS formed a distinct peripheral cluster in the fineSTRUCTURE dendrogram (Figure 3B). In this tree, Andalusia appears as the most closely related cluster, an unexpected result given the geographic proximity of Asturias and its likely more similar demographic history.

After integrating the NDNAB‐PS and GAL datasets, all Galician samples from both cohorts shifted to the deepest internal node in the dendrogram (Figure 4A), becoming separated from the remaining Iberian samples. This shift suggests an artificial signal of genetic differentiation that is difficult to reconcile with current demographic, historical, and population‐genetic knowledge of the region. Consequently, it is more plausibly explained by technical artifacts arising from the integration of datasets generated using different technologies and analytical pipelines. This mirrors the pattern reported by Bycroft et al. [1], where unexpected deep splits are likely reflecting technical artifacts rather than true population structure.

Most importantly, the merged Galician cluster itself warrants closer scrutiny. Specifically, it is divided into two major subclusters and one minor subcluster. One major subcluster and the minor one were composed almost entirely of GAL profiles (~92% and ~100%, respectively), while the other major subcluster consisted predominantly of NDNAB‐PS samples (~90%) (Figure 4A). Notably, each subcluster included individuals from multiple Galician provinces, indicating a lack of geographical coherence. This pattern strongly suggests that the observed structure is driven by technical batch effects rather than true fine‐scale geographic differentiation.

In addition, the merged dataset (based on a common set of > 599K SNPs) yielded significantly more clusters among Galicians (n = 17 clusters; Figure S10) than those identified in the individual fineSTRUCTURE analyses of the GAL dataset using 17.2 million variants from WGS (n = 10 clusters, 5 of which included at least 4 individuals; Figure 1B) and the NDNAB‐PS dataset using > 599K SNPs (n = 3 clusters). This suggests that merging datasets processed through different pipelines may lead to an artificial inflation of inferred population structure.

Finally, when we combined GAL (representing exclusively Galician samples) with only the Galicians in NDNAB‐PS, again, fineSTRUCTURE increasingly differentiated the samples based on batch rather than genetic characteristics, further emphasizing the software's sensitivity to batch effects (Figure 4B, Figure S11).

For the different datasets and combinations, we additionally explored the differences between conventional PCA based on the variance‐standarized relationship matrix and a PCA derived from the ChromoPainter haplotype‐sharing matrix (Figure 3A,B and Figure 4A,B). In each case, the PCA analyses were performed using the same genetic and sampling data used to construct the corresponding dendrograms. As expected, conventional PCA showed reduced sensitivity for resolving fine‐scale population structure, resulting in greater overlap among samples in Euclidean space. Nevertheless, it was still able to detect evidence of batch effects between the GAL and NDNAB‐PS datasets (e.g., Figure 4B). By comparison, PCA based on the haplotype‐sharing matrix provided a clearer separation of genetic clusters and more closely recapitulated the patterns observed in the corresponding fineSTRUCTURE dendrograms, reflecting the greater power of haplotype‐based approaches to resolve subtle population structure.

Discussion

A meticulous investigation of the findings reported by Bycroft et al. [1] revealed multiple lines of evidence suggesting that technical and methodological issues may have contributed to the unexpectedly large number of genetic clusters identified in Galicia. While the authors had previously acknowledged batch effects in earlier publications, this issue was not explicitly addressed in their 2019 study. We also identified anomalous patterns of genetic divergence at the national level that may reflect the same technical influences.

To further investigate this hypothesis, we conducted additional analyses using independent datasets. These analyses show that, although fineSTRUCTURE is a powerful method for detecting fine‐scale population structure, it may be highly sensitive to technical variation that may remain undetected by standard quality control approaches such as PCA. As a haplotype‐based approach, fineSTRUCTURE relies on coancestry matrices that can be vulnerable to systematic biases introduced during variant calling, batch‐specific processes (e.g., DNA extraction, library preparation, sequencing runs), and imputation errors, all of which can lead to artificial clustering.

Further research is needed to understand how this sensitivity to batch effects may influence not only population structure inference but also downstream applications such as admixture dating, as performed by tools like GLOBETROTTER. For example, while Bycroft et al. [1] attribute the main North African genetic contribution in Iberia to the Reconquista [sic], recent evidence from ancient DNA presented by Oteo‐García et al. [15] points to a different scenario. Their analysis of medieval samples from Valencia suggests that the North African component was already established before the Christian reconquest, indicating genetic continuity rather than a post‐Reconquista [sic] influx. Recently, Roca‐Rada et al. [17] demonstrated “that the village of Idanha‐a‐Velha (Portugal) emerges in the Roman period as a site of significant migration and interaction, presenting a notably diverse genetic profile that includes North African and Eastern Mediterranean ancestries”. Several Roman‐period individuals from this village show genetic shifts toward Africa in PCA and f‐statistics; their results suggest that North African ancestry was present in this village during the Roman period, long before Islamic rule. Similar conclusions were reached by Pérez‐Ramallo et al. [16] on the analysis of the bone remains of the Galician bishop Teodomiro. Additionally, findings by Pardo‐Seco et al. [2] suggest that the main episode of North African and Middle Eastern genetic influence in Galicia and the broader northern Iberian Peninsula may have occurred well before the Islamic‐ruled territories and Christian expansion, supporting a more complex and regionally sophisticated demographic history. There are other recent pieces of evidence in ancient DNA studies pointing to North African ancestry in Iberia before the Islamic rule [18, 19].

Apart from the sensitivity of fineSTRUCTURE to technical artifacts, we have also identified additional issues in the study by [1], including potential sampling bias within the EPICOLON cohort. Specifically, some regions appear to be disproportionately represented by individuals from rural areas (e.g., Galicia, Basque Country), whereas others are predominantly represented by urban populations (e.g., Catalonia, Andalusia). Such heterogeneity in sampling strategies may introduce interpretative biases and influence the clustering patterns observed across Spanish regions, potentially complicating the distinction between genuine population structure and differences arising from sample composition.

Little can be added beyond what has already been extensively discussed in the literature regarding the identification and mitigation of batch effects. Standard exploratory approaches for batch‐effect detection, such as PCA, are often sufficient to reveal systematic technical structure and to motivate appropriate corrective actions, particularly when batch effects are relatively strong. A major concern in [1] is that these standard quality‐control analyses were either not reported, discussed, or adequately incorporated into the interpretation of the findings. Importantly, evidence of batch effects was already documented by the authors in earlier publications, where conventional exploratory analyses revealed technical stratification (Supporting Information). Thus, the central issue in [1] is not whether batch effects are present in these datasets, but rather whether their potential impact was considered during data processing and interpretation of the observed population structure.

The broader challenge for the field is to develop and apply methodologies capable of identifying more subtle forms of technical variation that may not be readily apparent in standard exploratory analyses, yet can become problematic when using highly sensitive clustering approaches designed to detect very fine‐scale population structure, such as fineSTRUCTURE. Methods of this type are specifically intended to identify subtle genetic subdivisions and, consequently, may also be particularly sensitive to low‐level technical artifacts. For this reason, careful assessment of residual batch effects becomes increasingly important as the resolution and sensitivity of population‐genetic analyses continue to increase.

In summary, Bycroft et al. [1] claimed to identify ultra‐fine‐scale population structure in Galicia and across Spain, but their investigations suffer from unaccounted technical biases (explained in greater detail in the Supporting Information). The extreme genetic stratification reported in Pontevedra contradicts historical, geographical, and demographic evidence and appears highly counterintuitive, especially considering the historical and present‐day cosmopolitan nature of the region, as well as the absence of geographical barriers (contra [1]). At the national level, unexpected patterns of clustering and genetic differentiation across Iberia, together with the failure of independent high‐resolution genomic datasets to reproduce these findings, suggest that hospital‐specific batch effects may have distorted their findings. Inconsistencies in sampling methods further raise concerns about the reliability of their conclusions. Moreover, because several subsequent studies relied on the same EPICOLON dataset, potential technical artifacts may have propagated into later interpretations of Iberian population structure. Importantly, the purpose of the present work is not to categorically dismiss the possibility that some degree of fine‐scale population structure exists in Galicia or elsewhere in Iberia. Rather, we argue that such claims require robust empirical support and independent validation. At present, the available evidence from independent datasets appears inconsistent with the magnitude of structure reported by [1] and, more generally, contrasts with the prevailing conclusions of previous population‐genetic studies conducted in the region.

Future genetic studies should prioritize methodological transparency, control for potential technical artifacts, and implement strategies to detect and mitigate batch effects when using highly sensitive methods like fineSTRUCTURE to ensure more accurate representations of human population structure.

Materials and Methods

Genome Datasets

We used the genomic data as in Pardo‐Seco et al. [2]. The Whole‐Genome Sequencing (WGS) dataset GALOMICS (GAL; n = 91) represents a primarily rural cohort, where all participants have deep local ancestry. The average distance between each donor's birthplace and that of their four grandparents is 6.6 km (SD: 15.8), with a median of 0 km (IQR: 0–5.5 km). Similarly, the average distance among the birthplaces of the four grandparents is 5.4 km (SD: 10.7), with a median of 0 km (IQR: 0–5.2 km). We also used Iberian samples from the National DNA Data Bank (n = 470 donors), as done by Bycroft et al. [1]. These samples are hereafter referred to as NDNAB‐PS (to distinguish them from the NDNAB dataset in that study), and are characterized by geographic resolution at the provincial level (a ‘province’ in Spain is roughly equivalent to a ‘county’ in both area and population). Although not explicitly stated by the authors, this same level of resolution is suspected for most samples in Bycroft et al. [1]; Supporting Information. Also, according to the metadata provided by the Spanish National DNA Bank, it is not possible to determine whether the location refers to the name of the province or to its capital city; the impact of this feature most likely extends to the EPICOLON dataset (Supporting Information).

Methodological details not described here, including the generation and processing of the genomic datasets, quality‐control (QC) procedures, filtering criteria, variant calling, imputation, and sample‐selection procedures for both the GAL and NDNAB‐PS datasets, are provided in detail in Pardo‐Seco et al. [2]. The present study uses the same processed datasets and follows the methodological framework described therein unless otherwise stated.

fineSTRUCTURE Analyses

Clusters were inferred using fineSTRUCTURE following recommendations provided by the authors [20]. The number of clusters is estimated based on the underlying probability model. To estimate the coancestry matrix derived from the clusters and construct a hierarchical clustering dendrogram of the clusters based on haplotype‐sharing similarity, we used the CHROMOPAINTER package [20]. FineSTRUCTURE was run on both the GAL and NDNAB‐PS datasets to investigate population clusters within Galicia and at a national scale.

The results were compared with those of Bycroft et al. [1]; these analyses aim to highlight the severe contrast between the low‐level population structure observed using the GAL and NDNAB‐PS datasets and the extraordinarily fine‐scale population structure reported by [1], providing further insight into these discrepancies.

The Total Variation Distance (TVD) matrix was computed as half of the sum of absolute differences between the coancestry probability distributions for each pair of individuals, using coancestry chunk‐length files generated with ChromoPainter. These values were visualized as heatmaps (Figure S8, Figure S9), considering the clustering structure inferred with fineSTRUCTURE for both GAL and NDNAB‐PS.

We carried out two experiments using fineSTRUCTURE to test its sensitivity to specific characteristics of the data:

Sensitivity to sample size: We ran fineSTRUCTURE on a subset of the GAL dataset, randomly selecting only three samples from each of the five clusters identified in the full GAL dataset (referred to in the text as GAL3) as reported by Pardo‐Seco et al. [2]. The goal was to determine whether sample size affects the number of clusters detected, assuming the clusters exist. While the GAL sample size is smaller than that of EPICOLON, we hypothesize it is still sufficiently large to detect most, if not all, of the clusters present in the population, particularly given that we are using WGS data, as opposed to the lower‐resolution panel of ~693 K SNPs used by Bycroft et al. [1].

Visualization of Genetic Data in Maps

Individual frequencies for the main genetic clusters identified in the NDNAB‐PS dataset were calculated across the various sampling points. These frequencies were then spatially represented on a geographic map using SAGA GIS v. 9.6.1 (http://www.saga‐gis.org/) [21] and interpolated using the ordinary Kriging method. Frequencies for each genetic cluster were interpolated separately and subsequently combined into a single composite layer to provide a clearer and more integrated overview of cluster distribution. Each data point on our map (Figure 2B) represents multiple individual samples (sharing the same coordinates). Given that our analysis is conducted at the provincial level, points are centered on provincial capitals. This contrasts with the approach taken by Bycroft et al. [1], who applied a random spatial shift of 24 km to samples sharing the same coordinates to facilitate visualization. This method introduces an artificial sense of spatial sampling and geographic dispersion, potentially obscuring the underlying biogeographic patterns present in the EPICOLON dataset (Supporting Information).
