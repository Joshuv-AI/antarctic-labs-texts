Efficacy and safety of vancomycin versus 13 alternatives in MRSA-confirmed skin and soft tissue infections: a meta-analysis of 39 randomized controlled trials
Authors: Sujata Purja, Yomna Elghanam, Eunyoung Kim
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractPurposeVancomycin is the standard therapy for suspected or confirmed Methicillin-resistant Staphylococcus aureus (MRSA) skin and soft tissue infection (SSTI). Although newer anti-MRSA agents claim improved efficacy and safety, evidence in microbiologically confirmed MRSA-SSTI remains limited and heterogenous. Thus, this study aimed to systematically compare the efficacy and safety of alternative agents versus vancomycin in adults with confirmed MRSA-SSTI.MethodsThree databases were searched until August 05, 2025, to retrieve randomized controlled trials (RCTs) from published meta-analyses evaluating vancomycin versus alternatives involving patients with MRSA-confirmed SSTI. Pooled risk ratios (RR) and 95% confidence intervals (CI) were calculated using random-effects model. The study is registered in PROSPERO (CRD420251119756).ResultsFrom 28 meta-analyses, 39 RCTs (n = 20,285; 2,662 patients with MRSA-confirmed SSTI receiving alternative agents and 2,322 receiving vancomycin) were included. No significant difference in clinical success was observed between groups (RR: 1.012, 95% CI: 0.992–1.032; prediction interval: 0.994–1.030). Although alternatives showed modestly higher microbiological success (RR: 1.058, 95% CI 1.001–1.119; prediction interval: 0.895–1.250), the effect lost significance after publication bias adjustment. Alternative agents had significantly lower dermatologic but higher gastrointestinal and hematological risk. Tigecycline had higher adverse events (RR: 1.090, 95% CI: 1.019–1.166), and telavancin had higher drug discontinuation (RR: 1.405, 95% CI: 1.105–1.786).ConclusionDespite the growth of newer anti-MRSA agents, vancomycin remains clinically comparable for MRSA-confirmed SSTI, with apparent microbiological advantages of alternatives tempered by potential publication bias. Varying safety and pharmacological profiles of these agents emphasize the importance of individualized treatment selection.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1007/s15010-026-02745-7.Keywords: MRSA, SSTI, Vancomycin, Meta-analysis, Adverse event

Introduction

Methicillin-resistant Staphylococcus aureus (MRSA) remains a predominant cause of skin and soft tissue infections (SSTIs) worldwide, contributing substantially to hospitalizations, prolonged therapy, and healthcare costs [1]. These infections range from localized abscesses and cellulitis to severe invasive manifestations such as deep tissue infections and necrotizing fasciitis. The pathogen’s capacity for resistance, biofilm formation, and toxin production complicates treatment, particularly in severe or deep-seated infections [2].

Vancomycin has been the standard therapy for MRSA infections for decades due to its reliable bactericidal activity and clinical experience [3, 4]. However, its limitations, including slow bacterial killing and pharmacokinetic variability, reduced tissue penetration into abscesses and soft tissue compartments, nephrotoxicity, and emerging strains with elevated vancomycin minimum inhibitory concentrations (MIC “creep”), have raised concerns regarding its continued adequacy as a first-line therapy [4, 5]. Over the decades, several anti-MRSA agents, including linezolid, daptomycin, ceftaroline, lipoglycopeptides (telavancin, dalbavancin, oritavancin), and tigecycline—have been approved as potential alternatives to vancomycin. These newer agents demonstrate improved pharmacokinetics, enhanced tissue distribution, biofilm activity, more favorable safety profiles, and more convenient dosing regimens, potentially offering clinical advantages in SSTI management [3, 4]. Nonetheless, despite vancomycin’s narrow therapeutic index and the need for therapeutic drug monitoring, the adoption of AUC-guided dosing has improved exposure optimization and reduced nephrotoxicity while maintaining efficacy [6].

In this evolving therapeutic landscape, the current position of vancomycin against newer anti-MRSA agents in the management of MRSA-confirmed SSTIs remains uncertain. To establish an evidence-based hierarchy of efficacy and safety, comparative data should be derived from high-quality randomized controlled trials (RCTs) involving microbiologically confirmed MRSA cases, standardized clinical outcomes, and appropriate use of vancomycin as the definitive comparator. Moreover, existing evidence remains fragmented and inconsistent, as most systematic reviews and meta-analyses (SR-MA) have pooled heterogenous study designs, included mixed-pathogen or empirically treated SSTIs, focused on limited interventions, and rarely assessed the impact of risk of bias or publication bias on pooled estimates, thereby limiting the reliability of their conclusions [7–9]. Without addressing these limitations, it remains unclear whether alternative agents truly provide a therapeutic advantage over vancomycin in confirmed MRSA infections.

Given these uncertainties, we conducted a comprehensive meta-analysis of RCTs identified from previously published SR-MAs to compare the efficacy and safety of vancomycin with alternative anti-MRSA agents in adult patients with microbiologically confirmed MRSA-SSTI, including assessing the influence of risk of bias and publication bias.

Materials and methods

Study design

We performed a systematic review and meta-analysis of individual RCTs involving confirmed MRSA cases in adult patients. Eligible RCTs were identified through previously published SR-MA. This re-analysis was performed at the individual trial level using pre-defined inclusion criteria and outcome definitions. We reported it according to the Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Reviews and Meta-analyses guidelines (Supplementary Table S1). The protocol is registered in PROSPERO (CRD420251119756).

Search strategy and study selection

PubMed, Embase, and Web of Science were searched until August 05, 2025, to identify eligible SR-MA evaluating vancomycin compared with other interventions for confirmed or suspected MRSA infections. The search strategy is detailed in Supplementary Table S2. Individual RCTs were subsequently identified by screening the trials included within the eligible SR-MA, and only those meeting the predefined eligibility criteria were included in the present meta-analysis. To enhance completeness, we manually checked the reference lists of these eligible RCTs and performed hand searching to identify additional relevant articles that may not have been captured within the included SR-MAs. Following consensus, two authors independently screened and selected the pooled studies, resolving any disagreements through discussion.

Eligibility criteria

Eligibility was assessed at two levels: (1) the SR-MA level for study identification and (2) the individual RCT level for quantitative synthesis. This approach was chosen to ensure comprehensive identification of relevant RCTs while minimizing duplication and inconsistency across multiple overlapping primary literature searches.

For the SR-MA, we included studies that compared vancomycin with other interventions for treating MRSA-confirmed or suspected infections. Studies with mixed comparators were excluded; however, adjunctive therapies targeting gram-negative or anaerobic bacteria (e.g., aztreonam) alongside vancomycin were permitted.

For the RCTs, we included those that met the following criteria:

Population: Adult patients with MRSA-confirmed SSTIs (complicated SSTI, complicated skin and skin structure infections, or acute bacterial skin and skin structure infections including cellulitis/erysipelas, wound infections, and major cutaneous abscesses)

Population: Adult patients with MRSA-confirmed SSTIs (complicated SSTI, complicated skin and skin structure infections, or acute bacterial skin and skin structure infections including cellulitis/erysipelas, wound infections, and major cutaneous abscesses)

Intervention: The treatment group received at least one antibiotic.

Comparator: Vancomycin (adjunctive therapies targeting gram-negative or anaerobic bacteria were permitted), if vancomycin was used in more than 75% of MRSA confirmed patients as a standard therapy, then the study was included in the analysis

Outcome: The primary outcomes were clinical success (resolution or improvement in clinical signs and symptoms without the need for further antibacterial therapy) and microbiological success (elimination or presumed eradication of MRSA isolates) at the test-of-cure visit, typically 7–14 days after end of therapy in microbiologically evaluable population with MRSA infections. Secondary outcomes included any adverse events, drug-related adverse events, serious adverse events, drug-related serious events, drug discontinuation due to adverse events, drug discontinuation due to drug-related adverse events, all-cause mortality, and organ-specific adverse events.

Data collection and extraction

After reaching a consensus on the extraction items, two authors independently extracted the data. Discrepancies were addressed through discussion with a third reviewer if necessary. Data extracted from SR-MA included the first author’s name, publication year, publication type, total number of studies, funding, infection type, intervention, and comparator. Data extracted from eligible RCTs pooled from the eligible SR-MA included the first author, publication year, location, infection type, intervention and comparator, trial number, funding, age, sample size, MIC90 values, efficacy outcomes (clinical and/or microbiological success), and adverse events.

If a test-of-cure visit was not specified, we extracted data in the following order: follow-up visit, end-of-study, or end-of-treatment evaluation conducted 5–30 days after the end of therapy. Additionally, if data for the microbiologically evaluable population were not available, data from clinically evaluable populations were sought, including per-protocol, modified, or microbial intention-to-treat populations having MRSA confirmed data. These exceptions were made due to variations in the outcome measures among individual studies per anti-MRSA interventions.

Risk of bias assessment

Two reviewers independently evaluated the methodological quality of SR-MA using the 'A MeaSurement Tool to Assess systematic Reviews' (AMSTAR2) tool. Similarly, RCTs were appraised using the revised Cochrane risk of bias tool for randomized trials (RoB 2).

Statistical analysis

Meta-analyses were conducted using the random-effects model in R Statistical Software (v4.5.1). For dichotomous efficacy and safety outcomes, the pooled results were expressed as risk ratios (RR) with 95% confidence interval (CI). A fixed-effect model was applied when the included studies evaluated the same intervention using comparable study designs. Statistical significance was defined as P < 0.05. Between-study heterogeneity was assessed using τ2 and I2 and interpreted using 95% prediction intervals from random-effects models to estimate the expected range of treatment effect across future comparable studies. Subgroup analyses were performed for all outcomes based on the intervention type, with additional exploratory subgroup analyses conducted according to SSTI severity. For meta-analysis including more than ten studies, potential publication bias was evaluated using funnel plot asymmetry and Egger’s regression test. When asymmetry was suspected or detected, the Duval and Tweedie trim-and-fill method (inverse-variance model) was applied to estimate the adjusted pooled effect after imputing potentially missing studies. Sensitivity analyses were conducted using the leave-one-out method and by stratifying studies at different levels of risk of bias to evaluate the robustness of the pooled results. Exploratory benefit–risk quadrant analyses were conducted by plotting pooled RRs for efficacy outcomes against RRs for selected safety outcomes that demonstrated statistically significant differences, to visually illustrate efficacy–safety trade-offs relative to vancomycin.

Certainty of the evidence assessment

The quality of evidence for each meta-analysis was assessed using the Grading of Recommendations, Assessment, Development, and Evaluation (GRADE) framework, considering risk of bias, consistency, directness, precision, and publication bias.

Results

Study identification and selection

This study involved 28 SR-MA with a total of 157 unique studies targeting MRSA suspected or confirmed infections. After applying eligibility criteria, we screened 50 unique publications, of which 34 that had efficacy data on MRSA confirmed SSTI patient were included. References screening of the 50 publications added two unique publications, thus, in total 39 RCTs from 36 publications were included in the meta-analysis. Characteristics of included studies

Of the 28 included SR-MAs published between 2009 and 2025, only one conducted a GRADE assessment and six had pre-registered protocols (Supplementary Table S4). The RCTs, published between 1996 and 2021, compared vancomycin with BC-3781 (Lefamulin, n = 1) [10], cefilavancin (TD-1792, n = 1) [11], ceftaroline (n = 4) [12–15], ceftobiprole (n = 3) [16–18], dalbavancin (n = 2) [19], daptomycin (n = 4) [20–23], delafloxacin (n = 3) [24–26], Iclaprim (n = 3) [27–29], linezolid (n = 7) [24, 30–35], oritavancin (n = 2) [36, 37], quinupristin/dalfopristin (n = 2) [38], telavancin (n = 4) [39–41], and tigecycline (n = 4) [42–45] (Table 1). One RCTs compared vancomycin with both linezolid and delafloxacin [24]. The total sample size was 10,647 in the intervention groups and 9,638 in the vancomycin groups, with 2,662 and 2,322 microbiologically confirmed MRSA cases, respectively. MIC90 values for MRSA were reported in only 55% of the studies for both treatment groups. Several alternative agents exhibited MIC90 values ranging from 0.06 to 0.5 mg/L, lower than vancomycin, which was almost always 1 mg/L. Notably, weight-based vancomycin dosing was commonly utilized in recently published RCTs. Among the 39 included RCTs, 28 enrolled patients with complicated SSTI/SSSI, one enrolled uncomplicated SSSI, and 10 did not classified infections as complicated or uncomplicated.

Risk of bias in included studies

Supplementary Table S5 presents AMSTAR 2 ratings for the included SR-MA. The quality of the SR-MA was suboptimal, with 23 of the 28 rated as critically low and five as low. The risk of bias assessment for the published RCTs is shown in Supplementary Figure S1. Seventeen of 36 publications were rated as of high methodological quality. Twenty-five studies adopted a double-blind design, while two used a single-blind design. Funding information was reported for 35 publications, all sponsored by the pharmaceutical industry.

Clinical success in MRSA-induced SSTI

For clinical cure, data were available for 12 different interventions compared with vancomycin across 32 RCTs involving patients with MRSA-induced SSTI [10–22, 24–33, 35–37, 39–41, 44]. In total, 88.93% (n = 1,728/1,943) of patients infected with MRSA-SSTI treated with alternative agents achieved clinical success, compared with 86.02% (n = 1,421/1,652) in the vancomycin group. The pooled analysis showed no significant difference in clinical success between interventions and vancomycin (RR: 1.012, 95% CI: 0.992–1.032), with the prediction interval (95% CI: 0.994–1.030) and subgroup analyses also indicating no significant differences across intervention type (Fig. 2a).

Leave-one-out sensitivity analysis demonstrated that removal of individual studies did not alter the overall effect estimate, indicating good stability of the findings (Supplementary Figure S2). When stratified by study quality, meta-analyses restricted to trials with low or high risk of bias showed no significant difference between groups, whereas inclusion of studies rated as having some concerns yielded a higher clinical success rate with linezolid compared with vancomycin (Supplementary Figure S3). Assessment of publication bias showed no evidence of funnel plot asymmetry, and Egger’s regression test was non-significant (p = 0.390) (Supplementary Figure S4). After adjustment using the Duval and Tweedie trim-and-fill method, four studies were imputed, yet the pooled estimate remained non-significant (Supplementary Figure S5). Exploratory subgroup analyses by SSTI severity showed no significant differences between interventions and vancomycin (Supplementary Figure S6).

Microbiological success in MRSA-induced SSTI

Data on microbiological success were available for 10 interventions compared with vancomycin across 23 RCTs involving patients with MRSA-induced SSTI [11, 13, 18, 20, 21, 23–25, 27, 30–32, 34, 35, 38–43, 45]. Overall, 84.38% (n = 994/1,178) of patients infected with MRSA-SSTI treated with alternative agents achieved microbiological success, compared with 78.56% (n = 854/1,087) treated with vancomycin. Pooled analysis demonstrated higher microbiological success with alternative agents (RR: 1.058, 95% CI: 1.001–1.119), with prediction interval (95% CI: 0.895–1.250), while subgroup analyses indicated no significant differences among the interventions type (Fig. 2b).

Leave-one-out sensitivity analysis indicated that the overall effect lost statistical significance upon exclusion of certain studies. (Supplementary Figure S7). When stratified by risk of bias, no significant differences were observed among studies with low or high risk, while inclusion of studies with “some concerns” introduced a significant effect (Supplementary Figure S8). Examination of funnel plot asymmetry and Egger’s regression test (p = 0.046) indicated potential small-study effects (Supplementary Figure S4). After adjustment using the Duval and Tweedie trim-and-fill method, five studies were imputed, and the adjusted pooled estimate became non-significant (Supplementary Figure S9). In exploratory subgroup analyses by SSTI severity, alternative agents demonstrated a modestly higher rate of microbiological eradication than vancomycin among patients with complicated SSTIs (RR: 1.081, 95% CI: 1.012–1.156; Supplementary Figure S10).

Adverse events

Among the interventions, the risk for any adverse events was higher for tigecycline (RR: 1.090, 95% CI: 1.019–1.166), while telavancin had higher risk for drug discontinuation due to adverse events (RR: 1.405, 95% CI:1.105–1.786) (Fig. 3). There was no significant difference between intervention and vancomycin for the drug-related adverse event, drug discontinuation due to drug-related adverse event, serious adverse event, drug-related serious adverse event, and all-cause mortality (Supplementary Figures S11-S15). Supplementary Figure S16 illustrates the publication bias of safety outcomes. A summary of the meta-analysis results for all organ-specific adverse events across the different treatment regimens is presented in Fig. 4.

Skin and subcutaneous tissue disorders

Daptomycin was associated with a lower risk of erythema than vancomycin (RR: 0.109, 95% CI: 0.014–0.824) (Supplementary Figure S17). The overall pooled effect favored intervention, showing a lower risk than vancomycin for pruritus (RR: 0.448, 95% CI: 0.376–0.533; prediction interval 0.370–0.543), with no significant difference among intervention subgroups (Supplementary Figure S18). Similarly, alternative agents had lower risk for rash (RR: 0.597, 95% CI: 0.415–0.857; prediction interval 0.267–1.331), with tigecycline (RR: 0.334, 95% CI: 0.283–0.394) showing significantly lower risk than vancomycin among interventions (Supplementary Figure S19). No significant difference was observed for generalized pruritus and infusion/injection site extravasation (Supplementary Figures S20 and S21).

Gastrointestinal disorders

Overall, intervention had a higher risk for diarrhea (RR: 1.435, 95% CI: 1.161–1.774; prediction interval 0.718–2.870), with ceftobiprole (RR: 1.309, 95% CI: 1.137–1.506), delafloxacin (RR: 2.643, 95% CI: 1.632–4.280), linezolid (RR: 2.323, 95% CI: 1.446–3.732), and tigecycline (RR: 1.647, 95% CI: 1.029–2.637) demonstrating significantly higher risk than vancomycin across interventions (Supplementary Figure S22). Interestingly, tigecycline has higher risk than vancomycin for anorexia (RR: 7.499, 95% CI: 2.660–1.140) and dyspepsia (RR: 3.579, 95% CI: 1.793–7.146) (Supplementary Figures S23 and S24). Alternative agents also had higher risk for dysgeusia (RR: 6.041, 95% CI: 1.792–20.363), with ceftobiprole showing significantly higher risk than vancomycin among interventions (RR: 14.853, 95% CI: 12.016–18.360) (Supplementary Figure S25). Furthermore, interventions were associated with higher risk for nausea (RR: 1.625, 95% CI: 1.336–1.978; prediction interval 0.636–4.153), with ceftobiprole (RR: 1.731, 95% CI: 1.269–2.360), linezolid (RR: 2.001, 95% CI: 1.536–2.606), telavancin (RR: 1.754, 95% CI: 1.214–2.533), and tigecycline (RR: 4.020, 95% CI: 2.977–5.430) showing a significantly higher risk than vancomycin among alternatives (Supplementary Figure S26). Additionally, alternative interventions showed significantly higher risk for vomiting (RR: 1.634, 95% CI: 1.274–2.096; prediction interval 0.576–4.637), and among them, telavancin (RR: 1.838, 95% CI: 1.271–2.659) and tigecycline (RR: 5.383, 95% CI: 5.334–5.432) had significantly higher risk than vancomycin (Supplementary Figure S27). No significant difference in abdominal pain and constipation was found between vancomycin and alternatives (Supplementary Figures S28 and S29).

Nervous system and psychiatric disorders

Tigecycline had a significantly higher risk for headache (RR: 1.285, 95% CI: 1.126–1.468) compared to that of vancomycin (Supplementary Figure S30). There were no significant differences in experiencing dizziness or insomnia between vancomycin and alternatives (Supplementary Figures S31 and S32).

Laboratory abnormalities

Overall, interventions had higher risk for thrombocytopenia than vancomycin (RR: 3.523, 95% CI: 1.150–10.794; prediction interval 0.372–33.339) (Supplementary Figure S33). Tigecycline showed a significantly lower risk for anemia (95% CI: 0.486, 95% CI: 0.400–0.591) and elevating both alanine transaminase (RR: 0.221, 95% CI: 0.149–0.329) and aspartate transaminase (RR: 0.330, 95% CI: 0.198–0.550) enzymes than vancomycin (Supplementary Figures S34-S36). No significant differences were observed in the incidence of hypokalemia, elevation of creatine phosphokinase level, leucopenia, and renal dysfunction between vancomycin and alternatives (Supplementary Figures S37-S40).

Cardiovascular and general systemic disorders

Tigecycline had lower risk than vancomycin for hypertension (RR: 0.545, 95% CI: 0.430–0.691) and pyrexia (RR: 0.484, 95% CI: 0.302–0.776) (Supplementary Figures S41 and S42). No significant differences between vancomycin and alternatives were observed for the incidence of abscess limb and chills (Supplementary Figures S43 and S44).

Benefit-risk quadrant analyses

Efficacy–safety trade-offs across alternative anti-MRSA agents relative to vancomycin were illustrated using exploratory benefit–risk quadrant analyses, complementing the meta-analysis findings. Cefilavancin appeared in the quadrant representing higher efficacy and lower risk of any adverse events and adverse events leading to drug discontinuation based on pooled estimates compared with vancomycin (Fig. 5); however, this finding was derived from a single RCT (Fig. 2 and 3). Discussion

This comprehensive meta-analysis, integrating data from 39 RCTs across 36 publications and 28 previously published SR-MA, represents one of the largest comparative syntheses assessing 13 anti-MRSA therapies versus vancomycin in microbiologically confirmed MRSA SSTI.

Vancomycin has long been the standard treatment for both suspected or confirmed MRSA-related SSTI, alongside teicoplanin, with linezolid, daptomycin, and telavancin strongly recommended as alternatives [3, 4]. The lack of clinical superiority for any intervention in our analysis, despite several alternative agents showing lower MIC90 values relative to vancomycin, including subgroup and sensitivity analysis, reinforces the continued relevance of current guideline recommendations. Although recent evidence have suggested possible advantages of linezolid or daptomycin in MRSA-related infections [7–9, 46], our findings, limited to microbiologically confirmed MRSA SSTI, suggest that earlier conclusions may have been confounded by study design heterogeneity, inclusion of mixed-pathogen infections, or empirically treated populations.

Interestingly, in risk-of-bias-stratified analyses, higher clinical success with linezolid was observed in studies rated with “some concerns” of bias, which were mostly open-label and industry-funded. This underscores how methodological quality and funding sources can distort comparative efficacy. Industry-funded open label trials have been shown to report more favorable efficacy outcomes, potentially due to selective outcome reporting, comparator dosing strategies, or trial design optimization [47, 48]. Moreover, in some included RCTs, the MIC90 values for linezolid were higher than those of vancomycin [24, 35]. Importantly, differences in clinical success between vancomycin and alternative agents remained non-significant even after adjustment for publication bias, and the prediction intervals were narrow and centered around null value, strengthening confidence in vancomycin’s therapeutic equivalence. While newer agents showed slightly higher pooled microbiological eradication rates, particularly in industry funded trials rated with “some concerns”, this effect was inconsistent in sensitivity analysis and lost significance after publication bias correction. Furthermore, the wide prediction interval crossing the null value suggests that the apparent microbiological advantage is not consistently reproducible and likely reflects small-study effects or selective reporting in smaller, industry-funded trials rather than true pharmacodynamic superiority in MRSA-SSTIs.

Safety profiles varied substantially across interventions, making tolerability a crucial factor in clinical decision-making. Consistent with prior findings [46], our analysis demonstrated that alternative agents were associated with a lower risk of dermatologic reactions, but a higher incidence of gastrointestinal adverse events and thrombocytopenia, compared with vancomycin. The lower incidence of pruritus and rash among alternative agents may reflect reduced histamine release and cytokine-mediated hypersensitivity reactions compared with vancomycin, which is known to trigger “red man syndrome” via non–IgE-mediated histamine release, particularly during rapid infusion [49].

Conversely, the higher rates of gastrointestinal disturbances with newer agents are mechanistically consistent with their pharmacologic classes. The increased risk of dysgeusia with ceftobiprole may be linked to the release of diacetyl, a flavoring agent, during conversion of its prodrug to active compound [50]. Tigecycline, a glycylcycline derivative of minocycline, is well recognized for its high incidence of gastrointestinal intolerance due to activation of gut motilin receptors and local mucosal irritation [51]; consistent with our findings of increased diarrhea, nausea, vomiting, anorexia, and dyspepsia. Despite its broad-spectrum activity and excellent tissue penetration, tigecycline exhibited a consistently higher overall adverse event burden, limiting its routine clinical use. However, its lower risk of rash, anemia, hypertension, and hepatic enzyme elevation indicates potential suitability for patients with glycopeptide hypersensitivity, cardiovascular instability, anemia, or hepatic impairment.

Although pooled analysis indicated a higher overall risk of thrombocytopenia with alternative agents, subgroup analysis did not show significant differences among intervention. Linezolid, an oxazolidinone, is known to induce thrombocytopenia during prolonged use [8]. Non-significant signal likely reflects the short treatment durations of linezolid in the included trials. Nonetheless, clinicians should remain alert for hematologic toxicity during oxazolidinone therapy. Telavancin, while offering once-daily dosing convenience, showed higher discontinuation and renal event rates, restricting its use in patients with renal impairment. Given its nephrotoxic potential [41], baseline renal assessment and regular monitoring of renal function during therapy are essential. Furthermore, the lack of a significant difference in renal events between alternative agents and vancomycin may be explained by the relatively short treatment duration in SSTIs compared with pneumonia or bloodstream infections, where prolonged or high-exposure therapy increases renal risk [52]. These findings suggest that, in short-course SSTI treatment, vancomycin does not confer a higher risk of nephrotoxicity than alternative agents when contemporary dosing and monitoring practices are applied. The increasing adoption of AUC-guided vancomycin dosing likely contributed to mitigating nephrotoxicity by optimizing drug exposure while minimizing renal toxicity [6]. Understanding these risk–benefit trade-offs is crucial for optimizing therapy, particularly in elderly or comorbid patients where adverse event profiles may influence adherence, healthcare costs, and overall quality of life.

This study has some limitations. First, approximately 32% of RCTs (n = 16/50) existing in the literature did not stratify efficacy outcomes (either clinical or microbiological success) by microbially confirmed MRSA-SSTI, resulting in their exclusion, particularly important anti-MRSA agent like teicoplanin from our analysis. Second, MIC90 data were inconsistently reported for MRSA-SSTI and not available at the individual patient level, preventing analysis of the relationship between antimicrobial susceptibility and clinical or microbiological outcomes. Furthermore, nearly all trials were industry funded. Although trial-level risk-of-bias assessments and publication-bias adjustments did not suggest material inflation of treatment effects, the potential influence of sponsorship-related design and reporting biases cannot be fully excluded, emphasizing the need for independent, high-quality head-to-head trials using standardized MRSA definitions, uniform outcome measures, and pharmacokinetic-guided dosing. Additionally, although we provided scatter plots distributing the efficacy and safety value with CI for each intervention, for the individualization of treatment selection, ranking of safety events using network meta-analysis could address this limitation. Finally, our approach for identifying primary studies relied on previously published SR-MA instead of performing an extensive review of the primary literature. Although every effort was made to ensure completeness, including reference screening of all RCTs and manual searches for recent publications, some studies may still have been missed. Such omissions could influence pooled estimates if missing studies differed systematically by drug class or study size. Nonetheless, sensitivity analyses, prediction intervals, and publication-bias adjustments suggest that any such impact is unlikely to materially alter the overall conclusion.

Conclusions

In this meta-analysis of RCTs identified from existing SR-MAs, vancomycin remains comparable in clinical efficacy to newer anti-MRSA agents for SSTI, with some alternatives showing marginal microbiological advantages that diminish after bias correction. Safety profiles vary significantly across drug classes, emphasizing the need for individualized therapy guided by patient characteristics, infection severity, and risk factors. Importantly, no significant difference in nephrotoxicity risk was observed between vancomycin and alternative agents among SSTI patients. In the era of AUC-guided vancomycin dosing and antimicrobial stewardship initiatives, optimizing rather than replacing vancomycin, a widely accessible and cost-effective therapy, represents the most rational strategy for improving outcomes while minimizing toxicity and resistance.
