Life-threatening intraparenchymal hematoma due to immune thrombocytopenic purpura: illustrative case
Authors: Rha Ismail, Wael Alqassem, Farhan Alanazi, Wafa Hazem, Ossama Alamri, Maamoun Alsermani, Samer Almudaibigh, Anas Sermani, Mohamed M Aly
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBACKGROUNDLife-threatening intraparenchymal hematoma (IPH) is an uncommon yet challenging complication of immune thrombocytopenia (ITP) in adults.OBSERVATIONSA 26-year-old man with a history of medically refractory chronic ITP experienced abrupt neurological deterioration due to a large left frontal IPH. His platelet count was 8 × 103/μL. Following the administration of high-dose dexamethasone, intravenous immunoglobulin (IVIG), and platelet transfusions, an emergency craniotomy and hematoma evacuation were performed. After surgery, the patient regained consciousness, and his platelet levels initially improved. The patient was advised to undergo an elective splenectomy within a week as a definitive measure for ITP; however, his platelet count dropped to 9 × 103/μL by day 6 postsurgery. An urgent splenectomy was performed, resulting in an immediate as well as long-term improvement in platelet count.LESSONSThis case highlights critical lessons in the management of IPH secondary to ITP. In the event of life-threatening IPH, emergency craniotomy and hematoma evacuation are necessary. Platelet support is achieved through transfusion, IVIG, and steroids. Splenectomy should be performed as soon as feasible following the evacuation of a hematoma to prevent the recurrence of thrombocytopenia, which may result in rebleeding. Immune thrombocytopenia (ITP) is immune-mediated platelet destruction resulting in thrombocytopenia and the potential for severe hemorrhage.1,2 Intraparenchymal hematoma (IPH) is an uncommon complication of ITP, affecting 0.5%–1% of pediatric and 1%–2% of adult cases.3 Although rare, ITP-related IPH carries a high mortality rate (30%–50%) and substantial morbidity among survivors.4 Thrombocytopenia can delay neurosurgical intervention or cause recurrent bleeding.5 Thrombocytopenia due to ITP is particularly difficult to manage, as patients are less responsive to platelet transfusion because of ongoing destruction.1,4,6 Literature is scarce regarding the management of life-threatening IPH caused by ITP, particularly in adults. A recent article published in 2023 reported 5 cases.5 We present the case of a 26-year-old male with chronic ITP who developed a life-threatening left frontal IPH, successfully managed with hematoma evacuation followed by splenectomy. The case highlights acute neurosurgical and hematological management challenges and discusses relevant literature.

Illustrative Case

History

A 26-year-old man, who was previously diagnosed with ITP, suddenly developed right-sided weakness, dizziness, vomiting, and disturbed consciousness. There was no prior history of trauma or anticoagulant use. On examination, he was lethargic, with a Glasgow Coma Scale score of 13 and a right-sided hemiparesis grade of 3/5, and his pupils were bilaterally reactive to light. On presentation, the patient’s hemoglobin was 9.5 g/dL, and the platelet count was 8 × 103/μL. Otherwise, the coagulation profile was within the normal range. Emergency noncontrasted CT revealed a large (5 × 5 cm) left frontal IPH with substantial perifocal edema and mass effect (Fig. 1A–C).

D–F: CT scans obtained on postoperative day 1 after craniotomy and evacuation of the hematoma, showing reduced size of the hematoma and resolution of the midline shift (arrow).

Past Medical History

The patient has a long history of chronic ITP, with baseline platelet counts approximately 10–20 × 109/μL, without any significant bleeding episodes. He has shown resistance to multiple therapeutic modalities, including corticosteroids, intravenous immunoglobulin (IVIG), rituximab, eltrombopag, and romiplostim at a dosage of 250 μg.

Acute Phase Management

The patient received intravenous mannitol (1 g/kg) and a loading dose of antiepileptic levetiracetam (1500 mg intravenously). The hematology and neurosurgery teams were promptly consulted. Given the hematoma’s large size and mass effect, the patient was scheduled for an urgent craniotomy and evacuation once platelet correction measures were initiated per hematology recommendations. To boost platelet counts, the hematology team recommended starting with high-dose dexamethasone (40 mg daily) and IVIG (1 gm/kg), as well as at least two single-donor platelet units prior to and during the craniotomy. Within 2 hours of presentation, a left frontal craniotomy and open hematoma evacuation were executed, with hemostasis effectively achieved by electrocoagulation and hemostatic agents. Partial hematoma evacuation was undertaken until the brain became completely lax and nicely pulsatile at the end. The bone flap was kept back, and the wound was closed in layers.

Postoperative Course and Definitive Treatment

Postoperatively, the patient was admitted to the intensive care unit (ICU) and remained intubated. He received continuous platelet transfusions along with romiplostim and underwent a gradual tapering of steroids due to transient hematological response and concerns of side effects. A postoperative CT study revealed a significant reduction in hematoma size and mass effect (Fig. 1D–F). The patient was extubated on the 3rd postoperative day and was alert and showed improvement in right-sided hemiparesis. The platelet count increased to a peak of 350 × 10 μL on the 3rd day following surgery. A multidisciplinary team meeting was convened, consisting of an ICU physician, a hematologist, a neurosurgeon, and a general surgeon. The decision was made that the patient could benefit from an elective splenectomy after 6 days. A recurrence of thrombocytopenia was anticipated within a few days due to the weaning of the short-lived effect of steroids and IVIG. On examination of medical records, it was evident that the patient had not responded to any previous treatments for ITP.

Regrettably, patient platelets began to decline on the 6th day postoperatively, despite a platelet transfusion (Fig. 2). On the 10th day of hospitalization following platelet correction and scheduling logistics, a splenectomy was performed urgently and without complications. The patient’s platelet count increased to > 150 × 109/μL within 24 hours following the splenectomy. The patient’s platelet count increased beyond the target of 30 × 10/μL. The patient’s condition continued to improve, and they were able to ambulate by the 14th postoperative day. Following the splenectomy, the patient was scheduled for early vaccination against encapsulated organisms to reduce the risk of severe infection, along with prophylactic antibiotics.7 Daratumumab was included in the chronic therapy plan in case of relapse.

Discussion

Observations

This case emphasizes the challenges associated with managing a life-threatening IPH in a patient with severe ITP during both the acute and subacute phases.

Acute Phase Management

In the event of life-threatening IPH, an emergency hematoma evacuation is essential to relieve mass effect and elevated intracranial pressure.5 Nonetheless, the biggest challenge is the swift correction of platelet count to mitigate the risk of intraoperative hemorrhage.5 Platelet transfusion is less effective in raising platelet count in ITP patients, as autoantibodies can rapidly destroy transfused platelets.1 Although definitive guidelines for the acute correction of platelet count in ITP are lacking, the most used strategy is a mix of platelet transfusion, IVIG, and corticosteroids.1 Generally speaking, a platelet count of at least 50–100 × 103/μL is the minimum required for neurosurgical procedures to minimize the risk of intraoperative bleeding.1 However, in our case, we proceeded with craniotomy after initiating platelet corrective measures, given the rapid neurological decline. Intraoperative hemostasis was straightforward; possibly, ITP patients typically exhibit a lower risk of bleeding disproportionate to their absolute platelet count due to the selective elimination of older, less functional platelets.

Notably, thrombopoietin receptor agonists and rituximab are not recommended to correct platelet count in emergency bleeding, as they require more than 1 week to take effect.8 Alternatively, emergency splenectomy has been reported to improve the platelet count from 3 × 109/μL to 85 × 109/μL within 6 hours of the splenectomy, allowing for a safer delayed craniotomy.9,10 Nonetheless, an interdisciplinary collaboration among neurosurgeons, hematologists, and transfusion specialists is crucial for proper decision-making.

Subacute Phase Management

Once the initial hemorrhage is treated, another challenge arises: how to enhance the platelet count in the postoperative period to prevent repeated bleeding. A recurrence of thrombocytopenia is anticipated within a few days due to the weaning of the short-lived effect of steroids and IVIG.1,4 Multiple medication options exist, including thrombopoietin receptor agonists (e.g., romiplostim).6,9 Alternatively, splenectomy remains a highly effective second-line treatment for chronic ITP, by removing the primary site of platelet destruction, with a 60%–70% chance of durable remission.9,10 Given the patient’s history of unsuccessful response to all medical treatment options, a splenectomy was decided on the next available elective surgery slot after 6 days.9,11

However, platelet counts started to drop progressively from the 6th day postoperatively, reaching 9 × 103/μL by the 9th day, despite all medical measures. An emergency splenectomy was performed on the 10th day postcraniotomy and was successful, leading to a rising platelet count. One of the biggest lessons is that splenectomy, if indicated, should be done on an emergency basis, as rebound thrombocytopenia can emerge unpredictably and increase the risk of intraoperative bleeding during splenectomy.

Lessons

This case demonstrates a few crucial insights regarding the difficult management of ITP-related IPH. First, in an acute life-threatening situation, we determined that an emergency craniotomy is necessary. Platelet count must be corrected with a mix of platelet transfusion, IVIG, and steroid therapy. Second, long-term care of thrombocytopenia is critical to reducing the risk of rebleeding or new bleeding and should begin immediately after surgery. Multiple drugs are available, as well as splenectomy, which is a successful last resort for medically refractory chronic ITP cases. Overall, a multidisciplinary collaboration between neurosurgeons, intensivists, hematologists, and transfusion medicine specialists is critical for attaining a successful outcome.
