Earlier medically ready for discharge date entry associated with lower unnecessary bed days
Authors: Keely Dwyer-Matzky, Dessislava Pachamanova, Vera Tilson
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundDischarge planning plays a critical role in managing patient hospital length of stay. We report on the implementation of a program in a large hospital system where health care providers were requested to estimate and record the Medically-Ready-for-Discharge Date (MRDD) in the electronic health record (EHR) for hospitalized patients to facilitate efficient hospital stays. A new metric for discharge delays, unnecessary bed days (UBD), was tracked as the difference between actual discharge date and MRDD. The objective was to evaluate the association between MRDD timing and UBD. A secondary aim was to evaluate other factors associated with UBD.MethodsDischarges for 112,930 patient visits from 4 hospitals were considered within the period 01-January-2021/31-July-2022. Detailed subgroup analysis was conducted for 33,771 Medicine and Pediatric patients. Descriptive statistics were used to report MRDD utilization and other patient population characteristics. Multivariable regression models and tree-based machine learning methods were used to control for medical, demographic and operational characteristics to estimate factors associated with UBD.ResultsThe longer the delay in entering the MRDD forecast after admission, the greater the UBD. The longer the time between the first MRDD entry time stamp and the last MRDD value, the lower the UBD. Disposition, level of care, insurance, and day of week were significant for predicting UBD.ConclusionsA statistically significant decrease in UBD was associated with earlier entry of the MRDD forecast. Decreasing UBD can allow for improved patient throughput in capacity-constrained hospitals. Our findings of the observed consequences of implemented policies and of quantifying the impact on UBD can help other hospitals assess the appropriate scope for implementing similar initiatives.

Introduction

Managing discharges effectively can have a significant impact on patient welfare and hospital resources [1–4]. In an ever-changing health care landscape, hospitals are even more capacity-constrained with significant fiscal implications. Yet, despite attempts to predict discharge times accurately [3, 5, 6] and initiatives to improve discharge management [7–13], there continues to exist a substantial variability in realized times to discharge and scant understanding of the factors that determine efficient discharge practices [14, 15].

Discharge planning is to start on admission for the most efficient, coordinated care [16]. It is linked to accurate prediction of patient length of stay (LOS) using data available at patient arrival, which is difficult [5, 17]. Higher prediction accuracy can be obtained by taking a granular view of LOS by splitting it into operationally distinct components [18, 19]. This article explicitly focuses on provider forecasts of Medically-Ready-for-Discharge-Date (MRDD), targeting the component associated with discharge planning.

As part of an enterprise-wide patient flow quality improvement program launched at the University of Rochester (UR) hospital system in 2021, healthcare providers were requested to estimate and record MRDD and potential discharge delay categories in the electronic health record (EHR) for hospitalized patients. Medically unnecessary bed days (UBDs) were estimated as the difference between actual discharge date and MRDD. The goal of this initiative was to reduce UBDs, a measurement of discharge delays, thereby allowing the system to care for more patients [20].

In the years since the launch of the MRDD initiative at UR Hospitals, other studies have analyzed barriers to discharge for medically ready for discharge (MRD) patients. A multi-site survey of 16 hospitals [15] utilizing “point-in-time” data provided by hospitalist teams on MRD patients found that major barriers to discharge were often due to support needs that may be outside clinician control, such as need for skilled therapy or daily living support services. Similar findings were reported also in a study of patients who had been declared MRD by a multidisciplinary team at an urban 238-bed academic tertiary medical center [12].

The aim of our study is to evaluate the association of MRDD timing with UBD and contribute new insights into effective discharge management practices. Our study differs from the existing literature in significant ways. First, it uses provider estimates of MRDD in forecasting the earliest potential MRDD. Second, our analysis is based on a large dataset collected over two years across four hospitals through the implementation of an EHR tool. Third, our study focuses on the application of MRDD to track metrics of interest, such as UBD. Fourth, we report observations from provider utilization of the system, with important lessons for other hospitals that may consider implementing a MRDD forecast tool.

Methods

Setting and participants

The IRB-approved study was conducted across four hospitals including an urban community hospital (Hosp_A), a community hospital (Hosp_B), and a large quaternary academic center (Hosp_D) with a medium-sized children’s hospital evaluated as a separate hospital (Hosp_C). Obstetrics, Behavioral Health,1 and Newborn service line patients were excluded from the initiative and analysis.

Implementation and training

An MRDD-entry tool was created in the EHR in November 2020, and an organized enterprise-wide initiative with a multi-modal educational campaign started in 2021. Subject matter experts were engaged to integrate the tool into adaptive operational workflows. Medical providers, including physicians and advanced practice providers, were requested to estimate and record the MRDD in the EHR for hospitalized patients as soon as possible after admission and update the MRDD if the date changed with changes in clinical condition. Entering the MRDD would reduce the need for asynchronous communication methods, such as paging and EHR instant messaging regarding medical readiness. The goal was to make the MRDD and discharge delay information visible to the entire care team to facilitate coordination of resources to complete discharge prerequisites in tandem with the medical treatment of hospitalized patients, enabling immediate discharge when patients were MRD. MRDD was defined as the date when acute hospital-level needs had resolved, and the patient was considered clinically stable to move to a lower level of care. Data on utilization was provided to all participating hospitals from the beginning of the initiative.

Data collection and acquisition

Data on patient visits were extracted from the EHR and de-identified in accordance with HIPAA privacy rules. The dataset comprised 112,930 patient discharges from 4 hospitals within the period 01-January-2021/31-July-2022.

Data preprocessing and exclusion criteria

Patient visits with operationally meaningful MRDD entries were considered. Meaningful MRDD entries were defined as entries where the first MRDD was entered prior to the discharge order. The Medicine2 and the Pediatric service lines were analyzed in subgroup analysis: the former existed across three hospitals (A, B, D), and the latter had similar discharge processes. We excluded patients who left without being seen, left against medical advice, or expired (0.80% of all patients with meaningful MRDD). We also excluded patients whose UBD were in the top 1% or bottom 1% of all UBD values to minimize the effect of data entry errors. Adult Medicine patients placed in Hosp_C (29 discharges, 0% of the dataset) and Pediatric patients placed in Hosp_D (25 discharges, 0% of the dataset) were also removed. Analysis

For increased granularity in the actual statistical analysis, we considered ubh, the number of unnecessary bed hours between the last MRDD value and discharge, as our dependent variable. We considered two independent variables associated with MRDD entry, time between hospital admission and MRDD entry (hrs.to.first.MRDD), a proxy for the delay in providing an estimate of MRDD, and time between first MRDD stamp and last MRDD value (days.first.to.last.MRDD), a proxy for the “advance notice” given to the team regarding when a patient might be ready for discharge. Our models included the following control variables: Charlson Comorbidity Index Score [21–23] to account for case severity; patient class (inpatient, observation, other); patient discharge disposition (i.e., destination after discharge: assisted living, home or self care, home health organized care, hospice, skilled nursing facility, other facility); required level of care (acute, custodial, skilled); patient age, patient race (American Indian or Alaskan Native, Asian, Black or African American, Native Hawaiian or Other Pacific Islander, White or Caucasian, Other, Patient Refused, and Unknown), patient gender (female, male, other), patient insurance type (Private, Medicaid State, Medicaid Other,3 Medicare, Medicare Advantage, Public, Veterans Affairs (VA)), and day of the week.

Two different methodological approaches were applied to explore the association between the number of unnecessary bed hours and the independent variables: (1) a multivariable linear regression model, which assumes a linear association, and (2) a tree-based machine learning model (XGBoost) which does not assume linearity; instead it combines weak models (trees) to create a strong ensemble to capture complex interactions in the data [24, 25]. Both models were built separately for the Medicine and the Pediatric dataset.

The importance of the independent variables for explaining the dependent variable in the multivariable regression model and the XGBoost model was evaluated through p-values and Shapley additive explanation (SHAP) values, respectively. SHAP values, widely used for evaluating machine learning models, are based on game theory concepts, and measure the individual contribution of each independent variable (feature) to the final prediction of a model [26]. The model is trained on every possible subset of the features, and the overall importance of each feature is measured based on its average marginal contribution across all possible subset features. Positive (negative) SHAP values indicate features that positively (negatively) contribute to a prediction.

In addition to confirming the validity of our findings through two different methodological approaches, we performed several robustness checks. We computed the Generalized Variance Inflation Factor (GVIFs) for each input variable. GVIFs are used to assess the degree of multicollinearity and hence the reliability of regression coefficient magnitude and significance estimates in linear regression models. We also used LASSO regression, a regularization method that adds a penalty to the regression model based on the absolute values of the coefficients, and helps eliminate independent variables that are not as important for prediction of the dependent variable [27]. Additional model robustness was ensured by controlling the penalty term through a parameter that was optimized through cross-validation to find the value that minimizes prediction error.

Early MRDD entry and sufficient advance notice for discharge

Table 2 contains the regression output for Medicine (Hosp_A, Hosp_B, and Hosp_D) and Pediatric (Hosp_C) patients. This subgroup analysis showed that the longer the delay in entering the first MRDD estimate by the provider (i.e., the higher the value of hrs.to.first.MRDD), the higher the number of unnecessary bed hours (ubh). Each additional hour after hospital admission it took the provider to enter the MRDD contributed 0.04 unnecessary bed hours for Medicine patients, i.e., an additional day of delay increased UBD by one hour. The effect was not as strong for pediatric patients (each additional hour delay after hospital admission to enter the MRDD increases unnecessary bed hours by 0.02); however, the independent variable was statistically significant in both cases. Delaying MRDD entry by one day was associated with a high cost for the hospital – 1351 UBD, or 3 years of unnecessary bed days (= 0.04*33,771 Medicine discharges; see Fig. 1) for Medicine alone. We note that this association was significant after controlling for case severity, patient demographics (age, race, gender), variables associated with social determinants of health (insurance type), disposition, level of care, patient class, day of the week, and hospital-specific factors.

Additional factors associated with discharge delays

Discharge disposition to home (HomeSelfCare, reference category for disposition) had the lowest value for unnecessary bed hours of all dispositions (Table 2). The gaps between the average unnecessary bed hours for HomeSelfCare for Medicine patients were particularly large for patients going to Assisted Living, Skilled Nursing Facilities, Hospice, and Other Facilities (84.83, 41.39, 39.59, and 36.21 additional hours, respectively), whereas for pediatric patients the average gap was the largest for Other Facilities and Skilled Nursing Facilities (18.15 and 6.12 h on average, respectively).

Medicine patients with Custodial or Skilled level of care on discharge required on average 104.94 and 118.18 unnecessary bed hours more than those with Acute level of care, and those estimates were statistically significant.

The patient’s insurance also had a significant association with unnecessary bed hours. Medicine patients on VA, Medicare Advantage, Medicare, and Medicaid State insurance required 8.66, 6.74, 6.17 and 5.45 more unnecessary bed hours on average than Medicine patients on private insurance. The discrepancies were not as large for Pediatric patients: those with Medicaid State and Medicaid Other insurance required 3.43 and 1.60 unnecessary bed hours more on average than those on private insurance.

Patient age and race were both significant for explaining unnecessary bed hours of Medicine patients, with older patients requiring more unnecessary bed hours than younger patients, and Black or African American patients requiring 8.42 unnecessary bed hours more on average than White or Caucasian patients. Age had a statistically significant association with unnecessary bed hours for Pediatric patients, but the effect of race was not significant.

Finally, the day of the week was strongly associated with unnecessary bed hours for Medicine patients, although not for Pediatric patients. The largest number of discharges and the lowest number of unnecessary bed hours occured on Friday. Relative to Friday discharges, Monday discharges realized 9.83 more unnecessary bed hours on average, with Tuesday and Wednesday discharges realizing about half of that number.

Discussion

Our analysis revealed that (1) the longer the provider delays in entering the MRDD forecast after admission, the greater the UBD, and (2) the longer the time between the first MRDD entry time stamp and MRDD value, the lower the UBD. This finding supports the underlying hypothesis of the patient flow initiative, which is that the longer the lead time for coordination of discharge planning, the shorter the unnecessary time spent in the hospital.

Improving internal MRDD utilization

Despite the apparent benefits of the MRDD forecast, compliance was slow at first. Utilization of the tool varied across hospitals, with highest utilization at Hosp_D (65%) and lowest at Hosp_C (46%); with 57% overall (Table 1). Approximately 4% of patient visits had MRDD forecasts that were entered but not meaningful.

To overcome the initial challenges with MRDD utilization, UR Hospitals took a two-pronged approach. On the one hand, steps were taken to increase provider compliance with MRDD forecast entry. On the other hand, multiple services within the hospital system developed new processes to take advantage of the information in the MRDD forecast.

As the initiative continued to grow, multi-modal educational sessions were conducted at all hospitals, including broad communication campaigns in conjunction with targeted service line and resident education. The education emphasized the need for early MRDD identification and regular updates to support a shared understanding and visibility for coordinated care. Multiple new adaptive workflows were introduced, including electronic smartphrases integrated into provider daily progress notes. Campaign messaging emphasized decreasing need for asynchronous communication via paging and EHR instant messaging, targeting provider “alert fatigue,” which is a known problem. [28–29] Some initiatives were direct: for example, at Hosp_D, a forced function MRDD was eventually integrated into a general admission order set and led to a sustained increase in MRDD entry compliance within 24 h.

Social work, care coordinators, and ancillary services including physical therapy, pharmacy and occupational therapy, now rely on MRDD. The MRDD is used as a planning date for hospital discharge and discussed at interdisciplinary team rounds daily. Several process improvement projects have stemmed from the initial enterprise-wide initiative. At Hosp_A, the MRDD is used as an anchor integrated into a chronic obstructive pulmonary disease nebulizer to inhaler transition pathway, and physical therapy teams utilize the MRDD in an adaptive workflow prioritizing patient evaluation based on the MRDD. MRDD could be integrated into physician-to-physician or nurse-to-nurse handoffs. MRDD can also be used in evaluating hospital resource utilization such as prioritization of labs and diagnostic imaging tests for MRD patients.

Broader ecosystem impact

One interesting observation from our analysis of UBD is that UBD is not necessarily determined only by internal processes. Social determinants of health such as patient insurance, level of care, and race are strongly associated with UBD but may be beyond the providers’ control. For example, the increased UBD on Mondays reflects the effect of decreased weekend discharges, which has been recognized in other studies [30]. It is possibly related to level of care (Fig. 3d) and the inability to discharge SNF placement patients on the weekend. This highlights the significant strain that the critical shortage of staffed nursing home beds places on acute care hospitals. [31–32] The association of increased UBD in patients requiring skilled level of care with Medicare or VA insurance also underscores the plight of the US aging population with primary government insurance.

The association of early MRDD entry with saving thousands of UBD during the time period studied for Medicine alone translates into millions of dollars per year for the hospital when considering the ability to place new patients in beds freed (backfill). In addition, UR hospitals have utilized the UBD data to secure an 11 million-dollar grant to support the placement of complex care hospital patients awaiting SNF beds and additional SNF nurse workforce training, allowing for increased capacity in UR hospitals. MRDD tool integration therefore had both an internal institutional effect and a larger community impact.

Limitations

Although we made every effort to include all relevant control variables in consultation with medical providers, any statistical model is inherently subject to the possibility of unobserved confounding. In our setting, provider characteristics, e.g., individual provider preferences, experience and behavior, could be such confounders. We did not have access to provider characteristics data, but future studies should examine their influence on this study’s findings. Additionally, although we are not aware of technical barriers for providers to enter their MRDD forecasts in the system, it is possible that unanticipated barriers exist; future qualitative studies may explore potential physical and psychological barriers to MRDD forecast entry.

Our dataset was large and comprised patient discharge data from 4 hospitals; however, it is possible that findings may vary depending on the individual characteristics of other hospital systems. It is also unclear whether interpretations should be different in the setting of the immediate aftermath of the COVID pandemic and its effect on the health care infrastructure, staffing and landscape, despite controlling for many variables. Finally, the shortage of staffed nursing home beds in this region of the US may be extreme compared to other parts of the country, which significantly affects UBD. Nevertheless, the initiative and the valuable experience it generated carry important insights for the implementation of similar programs at other institutions.

Research and practical implications

Given the complexity of patient care and the prevalence of provider “alert fatigue,” this study suggests that there is meaningful potential in developing tools that provide relevant information to stakeholders to facilitate transparency and advanced coordinated care in the current capacity constrained hospital setting. Such tools could improve patient flow and the hospital financial viability and expedite the best care while easing the burden of provider and interdisciplinary team.

There are multiple areas of future research in relation to MRDD and potential applications. Future research can delve into understanding the exact mechanisms by which MRDD information dissemination can be most helpful to different stakeholders, improving trust and utilization of the tools, and most effective process improvement initiatives based on the tool. In addition, future studies could address how application of Artificial Intelligence (AI)-generated forecasts of MRDD can be combined with provider estimates, and the mechanisms by which the engagement of providers in helping guide better learning of MRDD through AI can alleviate providers’ own burden when it comes to MRDD entry compliance [33–36]. Finally, research focusing on best practices for coordination between acute care hospitals and structures that take over the care of vulnerable patients has the potential to substantially improve health system outcomes overall.

Conclusions

Earlier recording of MRDD in the hospital stay is associated with a decrease in unnecessary bed days in the Medicine and Pediatric service lines. “Advance notice” for when the patient will be medically ready allows for more coordinated care amongst the interdisciplinary team. The impact of saved unnecessary bed days in a health care system equates to improved patient throughput in capacity-constrained systems and equates to millions of dollars saved. In most hospitals, the change management effort involved in motivating providers to enter the MRDD into the medical record can be substantial, but there is evidence that the outcomes in relation to lower unnecessary bed days, improved coordination of the interdisciplinary team and financial benefit to the entire hospital support the implementation.
