An In-vitro Assessment of Potassium Iodide and Glutathione on Color Change Caused by Silver Diamine Fluoride Treatment
Authors: Chavva Lakshmi Charan Reddy, Sumit Nagaraj Ede, Priyanka Padmasai Bandhakavi, Shumayla Khan, K Vishnu Prasad, Gururaj Gunjalli, Hasina Khan
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTAim:To assess and contrast the impact of glutathione (GSH) and potassium iodide (KI) on tooth discoloration brought on by silver diamine fluoride (SDF) treatment on primary incisors.Materials and Methods:Standardized 2 mm × 2 mm (depth × width) of cavities were made on labial surface of each specimen, and artificial caries was created on 40 extracted primary incisors and divided into four groups based on the solution used as follows: group 1 – SDF, group 2 – SDF + saturated KI, group 3 – SDF + GSH, and group 4 – distilled water. The color assessment was recorded on days 1, 7, and 14 by using a spectrophotometer.Results:SDF caused maximum staining. Staining in groups 2 and 3 was significantly less (P < 0.05) compared to SDF, while group 3 showed the least staining at days 1 and 7, but the staining increased gradually and had no statistical difference at 14 days when compared to group 2.Conclusion:The use of GSH along with SDF helps in reducing the discoloration.

INTRODUCTION

Dental caries is a complex oral illness brought by an imbalance between tooth’s demineralization and remineralization. It is often treated by excavating the demineralized dentinal structure and then restoring it with a biocompatible substance.[1] Deep dentinal caries can be difficult to manage at times, as it requires expensive equipment, skilled dentists, and patient compliance. Due to insufficient coping skills, treating young children with dental caries presents a dilemma for the clinician. It is essential to identify several methods for altering biofilm in a positive way to improve oral health. Dental caries can be prevented or reduced by promoting the remineralization of tooth structure. As a result, dental caries management and prevention undergo a paradigm shift. More accessible, affordable, safe, and effective dental procedures that are easy to conduct in a variety of clinical settings on high-risk patients are needed.[12]

Reichi Yamaga and associates created silver diamine fluoride (SDF) in Japan in the late 1960s, a colorless, alkaline solution (pH 8–9); it combines with ammonia to produce byproducts, releases 2–3 times more fluoride than other topical fluorides and has antibacterial qualities of silver ions. It offers several benefits such as desensitizing sensitive teeth, preventing dental cavities, arresting carious lesions, and use as a root canal irrigant.[3] SDF discolors dentin and carious enamel black due to the formation of silver phosphate, which is thought to be yellow at first and then turns dark, which is a major drawback that concerns children and parents about appearance. Thus, SDF must be modified to the point where its staining is either non-existent or very little.[34] Thus, the present in-vitro study was conducted to assess and compare the staining tendency of SDF, SDF with KI, and SDF with GSH on demineralized dentine.

MATERIALS AND METHODS

Forty extracted primary incisors for orthodontic purposes or retained teeth with intact crowns, free of cavities and fractures, were included in the current in-vitro investigation. Teeth having internal resorption, developmental abnormalities, fractures, caries, restorations, and hypoplastic lesions were excluded. Written informed consent and ethical clearance were acquired. Teeth were disinfected in 0.5% sodium hypochlorite for 1 week and kept in distilled water. Using an airotor with water cooling with 835-010-FG diamond bur, standardized cavities measuring 2 mm × 2 mm in width and depth were created on each specimen’s labial surface and were set up in resin blocks. Except cavity, teeth were coated with two coats of nail polish.

The demineralizing solution was newly made of 1 L by mixing 2.2 mM calcium chloride, 2.2 mM potassium dihydrogen orthophosphate, 0.05 mM acetic acid, and distilled water with a pH of 4.5 by using 50% sodium hydroxide. Next, 10 mL of this solution was used for immersion of each sample for 4 days to simulate artificial caries. Following sample preparation, spectrophotometers were used to assess each sample’s baseline color. Additionally, the samples were split at random into 10 in each according to the substance used, such as:

Group 1-SDF: Specimens were rinsed and dried, followed by application of 38% SDF (Riva star, SDI, Bayswater, Australia) utilizing an applicator tip that was dampened over the demineralized cavity for 1 minute and rinsed.Group 2-SDF + Saturated KI: After application of SDF same as group 1, a 10% KI solution (Riva star, SDI, Bayswater, Australia) was immediately applied on the demineralized cavity by the applicator tip till creamy white precipitates turned clear and rinsed.Group 3-SDF + GSH: In a lab, 38% SDF and 20% GSH (Sigma-Aldrich, St. Louis, MO, USA) were combined in a beaker with a stirrer until the mixture was clear and free of precipitates. Using an applicator tip, the mixture was applied to the demineralized cavity for approximately 1 minute and then rinsed.Group 4-Distilled Water: One drop of distilled water was applied topically to the demineralized dentin surfaces after the samples had air dried.

After application of the solution, each specimen was rinsed with distilled water and submerged in artificial saliva for 24 hours at 37°C. Using a spectrophotometer (UV NIR-3600 Shimadzu, Japan) with a 400–700 nm range, assessment of color was recorded at three time points on days 1, 7, and 14. The color difference (∆E) for each sample between the baseline and each time interval point was determined using the following mathematical formula:[5]

∆E = [(∆L)2 + (∆a)2 + (∆b)2] 1/2

Where ∆E is the difference in color; L is the color value (lightness); a and b denote chromacity; and ΔL, Δa, and Δb are the changes in L, a, and b, respectively, between the initial time point and the next point being compared. Recorded data were analyzed using SPSS software (Version 23). One-way ANOVA analysis and Tukey post-hoc test were utilized. Statistical significance was fixed at P ≤ 0.05.

DISCUSSION

The present study aimed to evaluate the effectiveness of KI and GSH in reducing tooth discoloration caused by SDF treatment on primary incisors. The findings indicate a noteworthy distinction in staining outcomes among the different treatment groups. The maximum staining was observed in group 1. In contrast to SDF alone, the combination of SDF + saturated KI demonstrated a significant reduction in staining (P < 0.05) at multiple time points. The proposed mechanism for this protective effect could be attributed to the antioxidant properties of KI, which may neutralize the oxidative agents responsible for discoloration. Additionally, KI may form complexes with silver ions present in SDF, potentially altering their reactivity and thereby mitigating staining.[56]

The homeostatic feature of GSH, which controls the rate of silver ion release, could be the reason. GSH’s slower release of silver ions compared to KI results in a long-lasting antibacterial response, thus increasing SDF’s efficacy in preventing secondary caries.[7] Further research is necessary to refine the understanding of how GSH interacts with SDF and to explore potential modifications to enhance its protective effects. The use of distilled water as a control group also reinforces the study’s findings by establishing a baseline for tooth discoloration.

This study helps dental practitioners treating young patients, and the concern regarding its aesthetic outcomes must be addressed. Future studies should focus on further exploring the mechanisms underlying the interactions among SDF, KI, and GSH, as well as the potential long-term effects of combinations and overall health of the tooth structure. Understanding these dynamics will be essential for optimizing treatment protocols and improving patient satisfaction in pediatric dental care.

CONCLUSION

Using GSH together with SDF can help reduce discoloration effectively. Thus, GSH can be a good alternative to KI when used along with SDF in pediatric dentistry.
