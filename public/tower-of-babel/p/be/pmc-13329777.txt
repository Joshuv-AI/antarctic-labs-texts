Development of a green design reverse phase liquid chromatography method for determining the ionization profile of parabens
Authors: İlkay Konçe, Ebru Çubuk Demiralay
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
The most popular method for determining chromatographic separation selectivity is an optimization method based on the use of different solvents. This article presents a sustainable chromatographic framework using reverse phase liquid chromatography (RPLC) to determine the ionization behavior of methylparaben, ethylparaben, propylparaben, and butylparaben, which are widely used in hydroorganic mixtures. In this study, retention-mobile phase pH data in methanol–water (40–55% v/v) and ethanol–water (30–45% v/v) mixtures were analyzed using a nonlinear regression (NLREG) approach to calculate the dissociation constant values of the compounds. Simultaneously, solvent strength selectivity was determined by varying the percentage of organic solvent in the mobile phase. Experimental studies were performed at 37 °C using a Gemini NX column (250 × 4.60 mm I.D., 3.5 µm). The solvatochromic solvent polarity parameter (ENT) was used to characterize the effect of ethanol and methanol concentrations in the mobile phase on the chromatographic behavior of the studied parabens. Determining the pKa values in the correct aqueous medium is necessary to predict the ionization-dependent solubility, chromatographic behavior, and biopharmaceutical performance of ionizable compounds. The values of parabens with intermediate solubility in aqueous medium were calculated using the mole fraction and dielectric constant values of the organic solvents used. The suitability of the developed methods to the twelve principles of green analytical chemistry (GAC) and the white analytical chemistry (WAC) approach was evaluated using established tools. This study is the first to report the qualitative determination of parabens within an environmentally friendly analytical framework, using potentially non-toxic solvents and energy, and reducing waste generation.

Introduction

Today, various preservatives are used, especially in pharmaceutical, cosmetic, and food products, to extend shelf life, prevent microbial contamination, and maintain product quality. Parabens, chemical compounds derived from p-hydroxybenzoic acid esters, are among the most widely used preservatives. Examples include methylparaben, ethylparaben, propylparaben, and butylparaben. These alkyl esters are commonly used as antimicrobial preservatives in various industries, especially in personal care products and cosmetics. These substances can be used alone or in combination. As the molecular chain lengthens, the levels of lipophilicity and antimicrobial activity increase. These compounds possess high chemical stability, are sensorially neutral (imparting no taste or odour), and demonstrate broad-spectrum antimicrobial efficacy. Manufacturers often prefer parabens because they are low-cost, stable, and effective.1,2

While traditionally regarded as having low toxicity, parabens are now recognized for their ability to mimic estrogen in the body, a property that is enhanced by larger alkyl chains and the presence of branching and aromatic rings in their structure. Growing evidence raises concerns about the potential for adverse health effects related to paraben exposure.3,4 The determination of these widely used preservatives by analytical methods is quite important. In recent years, various analytical methods have been employed to determine the amounts of parabens in cosmetic and pharmaceutical products.3–7 Among these methods, high-performance liquid chromatography (HPLC) stands out due to its high sensitivity, precision, and selectivity.8–10

Reverse-phase liquid chromatography (RPLC), a widely used HPLC mode, is applied in many fields. Particularly in the last decade, more environmentally friendly analyses have been achieved in RPLC determinations. The Green Chemistry approach, which involves replacing toxic solvents used in RPLC with green solvents, improves ecological impact by reducing waste products. There are specific requirements for studies conducted in a completely aqueous mobile phase. While it is known that the free silanols on the surface of silica particles affect retention when using a highly polar solvent such as pure water, the distortion of the peak shapes of the analytes negatively impacts separation. Therefore, in analyses performed in a completely aqueous environment, better peak shapes are obtained when the surface of the stationary phase is modified with shorter hydrophobic alkyl chains not exceeding eight carbon atoms, instead of classical columns such as C18.

Studies exist where the mobile phase in RPLC is entirely water. However, when complete solubility of compounds in water cannot be achieved, mobile phases containing hydroorganic mixtures are used. This study presents a novel method to reduce the environmental and ecological damage caused by the toxic solvents acetonitrile (ACN) and methanol (MeOH), commonly used in RPLC. For this purpose, liquid chromatographic analysis of four selected parabens was performed using ethanol (EtOH) solvent, which is considered the most suitable alternative for green chemistry analyses. EtOH is less toxic than MeOH and ACN due to its lower vapor pressure and shares the same selectivity group as MeOH in solvent classifications. The main disadvantage of EtOH is its higher viscosity, which causes slightly higher pressure in the system compared to other organic solvents.11

The ionization constant (pKa) is a crucial parameter for predicting the physicochemical and biological properties of analytes. This value is a fundamental metric for evaluating a compound's ADMET (absorption, distribution, metabolism, excretion, and toxicity) characteristics. Furthermore, the pKa value is used to determine a compound's ionization behavior, as well as its lipophilicity, solubility, and membrane permeability. Various analytical methods have been described in the literature for determining pKa values in binary mixtures.11–14 Among these methods, analysis performed using potentiometric, spectrophotometric, and capillary electrophoresis methods can be problematic due to difficulties in reproducibility and temperature control if the compound is not pure, does not contain a distinct chromophore, or is present in small amounts. In contrast to these disadvantages, RPLC with UV-visible detection is generally preferred. The RPLC method is preferred for its high reproducibility, accuracy, and sensitivity. This versatile technique is based on interactions between the stationary and mobile phases.15 Although the HPLC column is a fundamental component, a comprehensive understanding of the retention properties of a compound often requires careful optimization of the mobile phase.16 The rigorous control of experimental parameters, mobile phase composition, column selection, and temperature is essential. However, for ionizable analytes, the composition and pH of the mobile phase are the most critical variables and must be precisely managed.17 For analysis using the RPLC method, the retention times (tR) of compounds are determined at a specific temperature by utilizing the difference in values depending on the organic solvent and pH change in the mobile phase.18,19

RPLC analysis performed under isocratic conditions provides a robust method for characterizing the values of many compounds. In the case of weak monoprotic acids, there are two distinct types. The ionic form (A−) exhibits weaker retention than the neutral form (HA).15–17 The observed tR is an average of the retention times of species (tRHA and tRA−). It is known that these factors are very useful for explaining the differences in the affinities of the ionised and neutral forms for the stationary phase. The equation (eqn (1)) relating the retention of a weak acid compound in an LC column to the pH of the mobile phase was proposed by Horvath et al.181

The ENTvalue can be related to properties of the ionized analyte, such as the pKa value and the tR value. This study presents comprehensive data integrating the twelve principles of green analytical chemistry (GAC) and the white analytical chemistry (WAC) approach for the determination of studied parabens. The objectives of this study are: (i) to establish an RPLC method for the qualitative analysis methylparaben, ethylparaben, propylparaben, and butylparaben in EtOH–water, and MeOH–water binary mixtures; (ii) to determine values in hydroorganic mixtures using nonlinear regression (NLREG) software; (iii) calculation of the hydrophobicity descriptor (φ0), which provides information about the hydrophobicity of the compounds, using the capacity factor (k) values of the parabens at the determined optimum mobile phase pH value;24 (iv) to determine pKa values in aqueous medium using macroscopic parameters of; (v) estimation of values at 25 °C without any experiment using Abraham solute parameters;25 (vi) assessment of compliance with the GAC and WAC principles using some commonly used tools;26–36 (vii) to determine the optimum chromatographic analysis conditions by identifying a method that meets the GAC and WAC criteria.

Materials and methods

Chemical substances

The compounds chosen for pKa determination, namely methylparaben, ethylparaben, propylparaben, and butylparaben, were high-purity reagents from Sigma-Aldrich (St. Louis, MO, USA). The reference marker compound uracil, used for dead-time determination, was also purchased from Sigma-Aldrich. The mobile phase solvents, EtOH and MeOH, were HPLC-grade and supplied by JT Baker (Phillipsburg, NJ, USA). Merck (Darmstadt, Germany) provided the following chemicals used for pH adjustment and electron calibration: uracil, o-phosphoric acid (o-H3PO4), sodium hydroxide (NaOH), ammonium chloride (NH4Cl), ammonia (NH3), and potassium hydrogen phthalate (KHP).

Apparatus and instrumentation

Chromatographic analyses were carried out using a Shimadzu LC-20A HPLC system (Kyoto, Japan) equipped with an isocratic pump (LC-20AD), UV detector (SPD-20A), column oven (CTO-20A), and a degasser unit (DGU-20A3). Data acquisition and processing were performed using LC Solution software. A Mettler Toledo (Zurich, Switzerland) pH meter and a combined Ag/AgCl glass electrode (Mettler Toledo Inlab 413) were used for pH measurements of the prepared mobile phases. All aqueous solutions were prepared using ultrapure water produced by a Millipore Direct-Q3 UV purification system (Bedford, MA, USA).

RPLC conditions

In this study, the values of methylparaben, ethylparaben, propylparaben, and butylparaben were determined in EtOH–water and MeOH–water binary mixtures. The study was conducted in EtOH–water mixtures containing 30%, 35%, 40%, and 45% (v/v) EtOH, and in water–MeOH hydroorganic mixtures containing 40%, 45%, 50%, and 55% (v/v) MeOH, within a pH range of 6.0–11.5. The pH values of mobile phases between 6.0 and 8.0 were buffered with 40 mM o-H3PO4, and pH adjustments were made with 1 M NaOH as previously established by our group.26 For mobile phases with pH values between 8.5 and 11.5, 40 mM NH4Cl was used, and the desired pH was achieved by adding concentrated NH3 to the mobile phase.

The pH value of the hydroorganic mixtures prepared for this study was measured according to International Union of Pure and Applied Chemistry (IUPAC) guidelines37,38 considering the reference pH value of National Institute of Standards and Technology (NIST) buffer solutions.39 KHP (0.05 mol kg−1) was chosen as the reference standard for electrode calibration in the studied binary mixtures.26–29 The pH value (pHx) of the hydroorganic mixture can be determined with eqn (5).5pHs and Es are the pH value and electromotive force (emf) of standard buffer solutions, respectively. pHx and Ex are expressed as the pH value and emf of the carrier electrolyte. pH measurement standardization of potentiometric systems according to IUPAC rules is carried out using a primary standard reference.37–39

Analyses with an injection volume of 20 µL were performed using a Gemini NX C18 column (250 × 4.60 mm, 3.5 µm). All analyses were carried out at a constant temperature of 37 °C and a flow rate of 1 mL min−1. The UV detector wavelength was set to 254 nm, corresponding to the absorption maximum determined from preliminary UV-Vis spectra recorded between 190–400 nm. Between analyses, the column was equilibrated for at least 40 minutes with the corresponding mobile phase until the baseline was stabilized. To ensure reproducibility, all experiments were conducted in triplicate, and the mean tR was used for further calculations. The average tR was calculated from these replicates, showing a precision with a relative standard deviation (RSD%) of approximately 0.01.

Capacity factor determination

The k value is calculated using tR, the analyte retention time, and t0, the column dead time. t0 was determined experimentally using uracil (0.01%, w/v). To minimize random errors, the average tRs of both analytes and uracil were obtained from three replicate injections.

Determination of data

Chromatographic retention data were analyzed using the NLREG software package to perform nonlinear least-squares regression based on a Henderson–Hasselbalch model.40 This approach correlates the pH-dependent retention behavior of analytes with their degree of ionization and enables estimation of values. The accuracy of the model was assessed by comparing the theoretical and experimental retention times using the residual sum of squares (RSS).

Extrapolation of aqueous pKa values

The values of methylparaben, ethylparaben, propylparaben, and butylparaben were extrapolated from their chromatographically determined pKa values obtained in hydroorganic mixtures using two complementary extrapolation models:

(1) Mole fraction model – based on the linear relationship between solvent mole fraction (X) and 41,42

(2) Dielectric constant (ε) model – based on the linear correlation between and the inverse dielectric constant (1/ε) of the mixture.43,44

Greenness and whiteness evaluation of the optimum analysis method

The environmental criteria of the developed methods, their compliance with the twelve principles of green analytical chemistry (GAC), the Analytical Greenness Measurement (AGREE), the Complex Green Analytical Procedure Index (GAPI), and the Analytical Green Star Area (AGSA), were evaluated;31–33 their compliance with the white analytical chemistry (WAC) approach was assessed using the Blue Applicability Grade Index (BAGI) tool.35 A new tool integrating WAC and GAC principles, the Environmental, Performance and Practicability Index (EPPI), has also been applied.36 Furthermore, the Green Solvent Selection Tool (GSST) was used to determine the suitability of the solvents used in the developed method for green chemistry analysis.30

Safety and waste disposal

All waste solvents were collected separately according to their composition (MeOH and EtOH mixtures) and disposed of in accordance with institutional and ISO 14001 environmental safety protocols. The substitution of EtOH as a primary organic modifier significantly reduced the volume of hazardous waste generated. EtOH-based mobile phases were neutralized and disposed of via aqueous treatment streams, consistent with ISO 14001 guidelines.45,46

Results and discussion

pH-dependent chromatographic behavior

Parabens are chemical preservatives derived from p-hydroxybenzoic acid esters. The parabens selected for this study are widely used as antimicrobial preservatives in various industrial sectors, particularly in cosmetics and personal care products. Their levels of lipophilicity and antimicrobial efficacy increase as the molecular chain lengthens. Methylparaben and ethylparaben have short-chain alkyl groups. Their hydrophilic properties are strong, while their lipophilic and estrogenic effects are weak. Propylparaben has a medium-chain alkyl group, and its lipophilic and estrogenic effects are moderate. Butylparaben, on the other hand, has a longer alkyl group in its structure compared to the others. Its lipophilic and estrogenic effects are very high (Fig. 1).

Chemical structures of parabens. (A) Methylparaben, (B) ethylparaben, (C) propylparaben and (D) butylparaben.

The water solubility (S) of studied analytes decreases with the increasing length of their alkyl groups, while their lipophilicity (log P) increases. The respective S values have been calculated as 0.781, 0.135, 0.037, and 0.011 mg mL−1, and the log P values are 1.46, 1.83, 2.19, and 2.57.47 For the analysis of parabens with varying solubilities and log P values, this study utilized a modified C18 column to overcome the limited pH operating range of conventional silica-based columns. Specifically, a Gemini NX C18 (250 × 4.6 mm, 3 µm) column was employed for the liquid chromatographic determination of the pKa values. This column features a patented technology where a polymeric silica layer is grafted onto a pure silica core. The structure, in which ethane ligands are embedded in the bridging polymeric silica, ensures very high pH stability and mechanical durability.

In this study, EtOH and MeOH were chosen as organic solvents in the mobile phase for the analysis of analytes with different polarities. Since EtOH has a higher elution power (ε0 = 3.6) than MeOH (ε0 = 3.0), it is not possible to obtain the same elution power in RPLC.28,29 Therefore, it was not possible to use the same volume percentages for both solvents in determining the tR values. In a hydroorganic mixture containing 40% (v/v) MeOH at pH 6, the tR and k values of methyl-, ethyl-, propyl-, and butyl paraben were determined as 6.752 (k: 1.920); 11.920 (k: 4.756); 21.130 (k: 8.139) and 44.939 (k: 18.437) minutes, respectively.

Propylparaben and butylparaben, in particular, showed significant interaction with the column in this medium, resulting in long retention times for their determination. If the study had been conducted under the condition containing 35% (v/v) MeOH, higher tR and k values would have been obtained, especially for these two compounds. This would lead to unnecessary solvent consumption, time, and cost, which is contrary to the principle of green chemistry. Consequently, due to EtOH's stronger elution power compared to MeOH, analyses were performed using water–MeOH mixtures (40%, 45%, 50%, and 55% v/v MeOH) and water–EtOH mixtures (30%, 35%, 40%, and 45% v/v EtOH), while keeping other chromatographic conditions constant.

In RPLC studies, the chromatographic value of parabens can be determined from the sigmoidal midpoint, which is the relationship between the average tR value and the varying mobile phase pH values. In this study, the pH of the mobile phase was chosen within the range of ± 2.0 of the estimated pKa values of parabens. For each compound, the average tR values were determined from triplicate analyses at a column temperature of 37 °C with mobile phase pH values ranging from 6.0 to 11.5. Sigmoidal curves obtained in EtOH–water and MeOH–water binary mixtures using these data are presented in Fig. 2. It was observed that as the mobile phase pH increased, the ionizable (A−) form of the compounds was retained less in the HPLC column compared to the molecular (HA) form. As the amount of EtOH and MeOH in hydroorganic mixtures increased, the elution power of the mobile phase increased (its polarity decreased), which caused the compounds to elute faster from the HPLC column. This behavior demonstrates the ionization behavior of the acidic functional group in the chemical structure of parabens.

This linear relationship is given in Fig. S1 and S2 for parabens determined in both solvent environments. The validity of the model was confirmed by calculating the theoretical retention time values from the experimental data (eqn (4)). These results show that the experimental tR values determined in both solvent environments are in agreement with the calculated tR values.

In isocratic RPLC, working in a 100% water environment is often challenging, and analysis often yields great tR values. These tR values can be estimated without any experimental work using linear solvent strength theory. It assumes that the logarithm of the retention factor (log k) is linearly related to the amount of organic solvent (φ) in the binary mixture:6log k = log kw − Sφ

In this equation, kw represents the k value of the analyte in the mobile phase containing 100% water. The solvent strength parameter, S, is a constant whose slope reflects the characteristic linear correlation and the specific interaction of the organic modifier with the stationary phase. φ value of the organic solvent ranges from 0 to 1.49,50

The k values of the parabens were calculated at pH 6.0, where they retained the most on the RPLC column at the studied organic solvent concentrations. A linear relationship between the calculated log k values of the compounds versus tφ value for mobile phases containing 30–45% (v/v) EtOH and 40–55% (v/v) MeOH was determined (Fig. 4).

The values of compounds in the water environment determined by different approaches

Column temperature is a crucial factor affecting retention and acid–base equilibria in RPLC.17 In the literature, experimentally determined pKa data for parabens at 37 °C are only available in the study by Angelov et al., apart from our study.51 pKa data at biologically relevant temperatures (37 °C) are important for studies of cellular transfer of ionizable compounds, their biological mechanisms, and often their degradation mechanisms at 37 °C. In this study, values were calculated using experimentally obtained data at 37 °C. Instead of experimentally determining these values at 25 °C, they were estimated using Abraham solute parameters.25 This calculation used the experimentally determined values at 37 °C and Abraham solute parameters for the acidic forms of the compounds (see Table S3 in the SI). The procedure consisted of two steps: First, the ΔpKa value was calculated by substituting the and Abraham solute parameters determined at 37 °C into eqn (7). Experimental studies focusing on determining the pKa values of selected parabens using any analytical method are quite limited. Furthermore, no RPLC studies are reporting accurate pH measurements in EtOH–water or MeOH–water mobile phases based on a suitable pH standardization procedure. The pKa values of methylparaben, ethylparaben, propylparaben, and butylparaben were obtained by HPLC by Angelov et al. Chromatographic analysis was performed on a C18 column at 37 °C. An isocratic mobile phase consisting of 0.02 M potassium phosphate buffer and ACN (65 : 35, v/v) was added at a constant flow rate of 1.5 mL min−1. The pH of the mobile phase was adjusted between 2.0 and 9.45 using phosphoric acid. The lipophilicity constant (log P) of the compounds was obtained by spectrophotometric study in an octanol–water binary mixture. According to the results obtained, the log P values of methylparaben, ethylparaben, propylparaben, and butylparaben were determined as 1.910, 2.343, 2.940, and 3.500, respectively.51 The log P value of the compounds at different mobile phase pH values yielded the pKa value in relation to lipophilicity (Table 4). This study, which aimed to determine the pKa value, is not suitable for the green chemistry approach due to the use of toxic ACN. In this study, the pKa values of the selected parabens were theoretically calculated using a semi-empirical quantum chemical (QC) method by Xu et al. They also determined the pKa values of methyl-, propyl-, and butylparaben using UV-Vis spectrophotometry (Table 4). In the experimental study, parabens were dissolved in a 5 mM phosphate buffer solution with pH values ranging from 5 to 12. Absorbance values of the compounds were measured at 254 nm at each pH value. The pKa values of the compounds were determined by evaluating their pH-absorbance data. In addition, the % ionization values of the compounds were calculated from the Henderson–Hasselbalch equation.52 This study is generally consistent with the green chemistry approach.

A study to determine the retention behavior of parabens by HPLC method was conducted by Maher et al. In this study, they optimized the chromatographic separation of methylparaben, ethylparaben, propylparaben, butylparaben and isobutylparaben on a C18 column (4.6 × 75 mm, 3.5 µm) by changing the mobile phase composition and pH. Using MeOH–phosphate buffer (pH 5.0) mixtures as the mobile phase, they investigated the retention behavior at MeOH concentrations ranging from 50–80% (v/v). Consistent with RPLC principles, the retention of the compounds decreased with increasing MeOH content. Since pH changes (3–7) had a minimal effect on retention, a MeOH/water mixture with a ratio of 57 : 43 (v/v) was chosen as the optimum mobile phase.53

The ionization state of a compound is a fundamental parameter that directly affects its pharmacokinetic and pharmacodynamic properties. Under physiological pH conditions, the degree of ionization determines the bioavailability of the compound and its efficiency in reaching target tissues. Using the calculated values in this study, the ionization percentages of methylparaben, ethylparaben, propylparaben, and butylparaben were calculated in the pH range of 1 to 12 using the Henderson–Hasselbalch equation.54 The ionization behavior of the analytes studied is shown in Fig. S3 (see in SI).

Ecological impact assessment

The selection of environmentally friendly solvents is crucial for the analytical methods being developed. One tool that provides a quantitative assessment for this purpose is the GSST. A green score (G) can be calculated for solvents. In this study, the G values for water, EtOH, and MeOH were calculated as 7.3, 6.6, and 5.8, respectively. G ≥ 7 indicates a sustainable solvent, G = 5–6 indicates a solvent with limited sustainability concerns, and G ≤ 4 indicates a solvent to be avoided. Accordingly, water and EtOH with higher G values are more environmentally friendly than MeOH.30

The accuracy and environmental impact of the methods were analyzed using various complementary metrics. Compliance with 12 core principles was measured using AGREE, GAPI, and AGSA tools for greenness and sustainability.31–33 The EPPI index, which combines GAC, WAC, and Green Sample Preparation (GSP) principles in a single structure, and the BAGI tool for WAC compliance were used to evaluate the methods.34–36

AGREE is a numerical and color-coded evaluation tool that scores each of the 12 principles of green analytical chemistry on a scale of 0 to 1; higher scores represent better sustainability. The AGREE tool is shown as a pictogram consisting of red, yellow, and green colors arranged clockwise. The closer the resulting score is to 1, the more environmentally friendly the method. This scale is correlated with a color scale ranging from dark green to dark red. The middle number in the appropriate color on the AGREE pictogram represents the final average numerical value obtained from the 12 data points. The range of the color map used in the graph and the corresponding values are given in Fig. S4. The performance of the procedure in each evaluation criterion is reflected by the color in the segment where the number corresponding to each criterion is located.31 The RPLC method developed with EtOH received a score of 0.80, while the MeOH-based method received a score of 0.67 (Fig. 6). ComplexGAPI, as a version of GAPI, has been improved by adding hexagons for readability, simplicity, and ease of use. Green represents an environmentally friendly step, yellow represents a moderate environmental impact, and red represents a hazardous environmental impact of that step. The working conditions cover (I) efficiency and selectivity; (II) temperature and time; (III) environmental issues, chemistry, and economics. (IVa, IVb) health risks, reagents, and solvents; (Va, Vb, Vc) instrumental analyses; and (VIa and VIb) purification and waste assessment. The E factor for waste, including everything that can be considered waste, is calculated with ComplexGAPI. An E factor of 1 indicates lower waste generation, a more sustainable, and environmentally friendly method (Fig. S4).

Evaluation of methods with green tools (A) EtOH–water (B) MeOH–water binary mixtures.

AGSA is a tool specifically designed to evaluate analytical methods according to the 12 principles of GAC, developed by Mansour et al.33 The AGSA pictogram encompasses the 12 principles, each evaluated through specific questions with three-level response options, allowing for a hierarchical differentiation among methods (Fig. S4). Higher scores indicate greater sustainability and support strategies involving minimal sample processing, low energy consumption, non-hazardous reagents, and waste minimization. These scores are cumulative, and a total of 36 points (12 principles × 3 points) represents 100%.33 Ecological impact is given as an evaluation score in the pictogram, from highest (dark green) to lowest (dark red). The AGSA metric, which provides quantitative and visual information, offers a holistic assessment. The method developed with EtOH, like other tools, has shown a high degree of environmental friendliness with an AGSA result of 80.30% (Fig. 6).

In recent years, the WAC approach has also been used as an alternative to evaluating the greenness of methods.34 WAC evaluates the quality, environmental impact, and economic feasibility of the method using red and blue colors in addition to green. Unlike traditional green criteria that focus on environmental impact, BAGI evaluates various factors important for the applicability of the analytical method.35 The BAGI tool provides two types of results: a score and an asteroid pictogram. The pictogram is divided into ten sections representing performance criteria (Fig. S4). Fig. 6 shows the BAGI pictograms for both methods. The color gradient of the pictogram indicates how well the method meets the defined criteria: dark blue indicates high compliance, blue indicates medium compliance, light blue indicates low compliance, and white indicates non-compliance. In the middle area, a total score ranging from 25 to 100 is shown; 25 indicates poor performance, and 100 indicates excellent performance. Analytical methods scoring above 60 on the BAGI pictogram are considered practical. Accordingly, the methods developed in this study are practical in terms of applicability.

A comprehensive evaluation of both methods was also carried out with the newly developed EPPI (Environmental, Performance and Practicality Index) index, which integrates the principles of GAC, Green Sample Preparation (GSP) and WAC.36 EPPI is evaluated with two sub-indices. The Environmental Impact Index (EI) indicates the greenness of the method, while the Performance and Practicality Index (PPI) show performance based on time, cost, accuracy, validation, and resource efficiency. The total EPPI score is calculated by combining the EI and PPI, each contributing 50% to the overall score (Fig. 7). Accordingly, the EPPI index scores for methods developed with EtOH and MeOH solvents were calculated as 80.1 and 66.0, respectively. The EPPI index shown in Fig. 7 is a pie chart where each method is represented by two adjacent halves. The value in the middle of the graph gives the total score. Accordingly, the method developed with EtOH has a score between 75–100, indicating that it is an environmentally friendly, practical, and efficient method.

Conclusion

This study presents a comprehensive and environmentally sustainable chromatographic strategy for the determination of ionization constants for parabens using both conventional and green RPLC methods. The consistency of the relationship between theoretical and experimental data was determined by retention modeling. In this method, the NLREG program and hydroorganic extrapolation were successfully integrated to accurately predict pKa values in EtOH–water and MeOH–water mixtures. The results showed that all four analytes exhibited acidic ionization behavior with pKa values (≈7.42) corresponding to the phenolic hydroxyl group. Quantitative results obtained from AGREE (0.80 points), AGSA (80.30 points), and BAGI (70.0 points) instruments clearly demonstrate that the method developed using EtOH–water binary mixture offers a significantly more environmentally friendly alternative compared to the MeOH–water method. The EPPI index, applied for the first time in the literature and in this study, shows the excellent applicability of the EtOH–water method. These findings confirm that EtOH can successfully replace traditional organic modifiers in RPLC pKa determination and provide a convenient and environmentally friendly analytical strategy for routine applications. In conclusion, a cost-effective, environmentally friendly, and robust analytical roadmap is presented for the routine analysis of selected parabens in widely used studies.
