Association of HLA‐B*53 With DRESS Syndrome in Patients Treated With Raltegravir: Two Case Reports and a Literature Review
Authors: Cristina Ramos‐Del Moral, Pablo Rodríguez Cortés, Marcos Navares‐Gómez, Diana Campodónico, Susana Almenara de Riquer, Ana Barrios Blandino, Gina Mejía‐Abril, Antía Gómez‐Fernández, Olga Romero Sastre, Patricia Carles García, Francisco Abad‐Santos
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTIntroductionRaltegravir, an HIV integrase inhibitor, although generally well tolerated, may cause severe reactions such as drug reaction with eosinophilia and systemic symptoms (DRESS). This syndrome has been associated with impaired drug metabolism, viral reactivation and genetic factors including HLA‐B*53:01. We describe two cases of DRESS in a raltegravir‐treated cohort and review the relevant literature.MethodologyMedical records from Hospital Universitario de La Princesa (2008–2020) were retrospectively reviewed to identify patients genotyped for HLA‐B*53 who received raltegravir. Suspected DRESS cases were assessed using the RegiSCAR scoring system and Naranjo algorithm. A structured literature search was conducted to identify additional published cases.ResultsAmong 109 patients treated with raltegravir, three (2.8%) were HLA‐B*53 carriers. Two developed DRESS, while no events occurred in non‐carriers. One presented with fever, rash, lymphadenopathy, hepatitis and HHV‐6 reactivation (RegiSCAR 6). The second developed eosinophilia, rash and pulmonary symptoms (RegiSCAR 4). The third carrier remained asymptomatic. A continuity correction yielded a RR of 133.9. Nine additional cases were identified in the literature and summarized according to clinical features and HLA‐B*53 status.ConclusionFindings support a potential genetic predisposition involving HLA‐B*53:01 in raltegravir‐induced DRESS. Screening in high‐prevalence populations may help prevent severe reactions. Further research should refine diagnostic and management strategies.Keywords: adverse drug reaction, DRESS syndrome, HLA‐B*53, pharmacogenetics, raltegravir

Background

Raltegravir was the first integrase inhibitor approved for the treatment of HIV. It is used both in the antiretroviral therapy and in post‐exposure prophylaxis, owing to its favourable tolerability profile and low potential for drug–drug interactions [1]. The drug is primarily metabolized by the enzyme UGT1A1 through glucuronidation [2].

Common adverse reactions outlined in the SmPC include decreased appetite, dizziness, headache and psychiatric disturbances such as parasomnia, depression, abnormal behaviour and psychomotor agitation [3]. Discontinuation due to adverse reactions in clinical trials occurred at rates of 5% or lower.

Although generally well tolerated, raltegravir has been associated with serious adverse drug reactions, including DRESS syndrome. This is a severe drug‐induced adverse reaction characterized by the presence of rash, eosinophilia, adenopathies and multiple organ involvement, most frequently affecting the liver [4]. Early recognition is essential; mortality may reach up to 10%, mainly due to hepatic failure.

In addition to hepatic injury, which remains the most common and potentially severe organ manifestation, DRESS can involve other internal organs with significant clinical consequences. Reported pulmonary complications include interstitial infiltrates, acute respiratory distress syndrome, pleural effusions, interstitial pneumonitis and lymphocytic interstitial pneumonia. Beyond individual reports, a systematic review of pulmonary DRESS (definite RegiSCAR cases) found 50% interstitial infiltrates and 31% ARDS; 72% presented with dyspnea/cough and 95% had at least one additional visceral organ involved [5]. Renal involvement may range from mild creatinine elevation to acute kidney injury requiring renal‐replacement therapy, although recovery of function is usually observed. Consistently, a PRISMA‐based systematic review of 71 renal DRESS cases reported 96% acute kidney injury, 30% requiring temporary renal replacement therapy, near‐universal renal recovery and an overall mortality of 13% within that enriched cohort [6]. Myocarditis, present in up to 21% of cases, may manifest as eosinophilic or necrotizing forms and can be fatal. Taken together, organ‐specific syntheses and the frequency of myocarditis reinforce the need for early recognition and prompt culprit‐drug withdrawal, as pulmonary involvement often signals severity while renal disease frequently entails resource‐intensive support (e.g., temporary renal replacement therapy) [5, 6, 7].

A high level of clinical suspicion is required for a diagnosis to be made. To facilitate it, the RegiSCAR scale was developed [8]. This scoring system classifies cases of DRESS syndrome as ‘no’, ‘possible’, ‘probable’ or ‘definite’ based on criteria such as fever, lymphadenopathy, eosinophilia, involvement of internal organs, reactivation of herpes family viruses and widespread skin rash. Detailed criteria for the RegiSCAR scale are provided in Table S1 [9].

Regarding the underlying mechanisms, DRESS syndrome has been linked to various factors, including drug metabolism defects (impaired detoxification of active metabolites and slow acetylation) and the reactivation of certain viruses, primarily HHV‐6, HHV‐7 and EBV [10]. Tissue damage has been demonstrated to be caused by the activation of cytotoxic T lymphocytes directed against various viral antigens whose replication is induced by drugs [11]. Similarly, in recent years, it has been associated with different HLA alleles, most notably in the field of HIV, such as HLA‐B*57:01 with abacavir‐mediated hypersensitivity reaction. Considering this, guidelines have been issued indicating the need for its genotyping of this HLA before abacavir prescription [12, 13].

In relation to raltegravir, the HLA‐B*53:01 allele is believed to be related to the development of DRESS [14]. One hypothesis is that the relationship would be similar to that between HLA‐B*57:01 and abacavir hypersensitivity. This would entail the non‐covalent binding of the HLA‐B*53:01 molecule specifically to raltegravir (or one of its metabolites) [15]. However, the immunological mechanism remains unknown.

In our centre, HLA‐B testing is routinely performed in HIV‐infected patients before starting ART to determine HLA‐B*57:01 carrier status (associated with abacavir hypersensitivity). The analytical technique used by the Clinical Pharmacology Department at our centre used to be INNO‐LiPA HLA‐B Update Plus (Fujirebio Europe N.V., Belgium), a reverse hybridisation technique based on oligonucleotides fixed to membrane strips that bind complementarily to DNA strands. However, since July 2019, we have been utilizing OpenArray technology, an innovative genotyping tool that updates the technique used to determine the carrier status of the HLA‐B*57:01. This technique is based on the identification of the HCP5 gene, which is in linkage disequilibrium with HLA‐B*57:01 [15]. Therefore, HLA‐B*53 is no longer included in the determinations carried out since then, raising concerns about the lack of genetic safety information before prescribing raltegravir. This study aims to explore the clinical implications of that gap.

Therefore, the objective of this study is twofold: firstly, to describe the prevalence of the HLA‐B*53 allele in our cohort of patients and secondly, to evaluate whether there are differences in the occurrence of DRESS spectrum adverse events (i.e., those included in the RegiSCAR classification) in patients who carry this allele and those who do not.

Methods

Study Design and Participants

We conducted a retrospective cohort study using medical records from patients with available genetic data who had been treated with raltegravir at Hospital Universitario de La Princesa between March 2008 and May 2020 (Study Code: INF‐RAL‐2020‐01). Patients were included if they had available HLA‐B genotyping data and documented raltegravir prescription records. Patients without complete genetic or treatment records were excluded.

We analysed adverse reactions, focusing on eosinophilia, skin lesions and the development of drug reaction with eosinophilia and systemic symptoms (DRESS). The RegiSCAR scoring system was used to classify suspected DRESS cases as possible, probable or definite [16]. Additionally, the Naranjo adverse drug reaction probability scale [17] was employed to ascertain the likelihood of raltegravir being the causative agent, compared with the backbone antiretrovirals.

Demographic and clinical variables—including country of origin, laboratory abnormalities and skin findings—were collected. Descriptive and comparative statistics were used to explore potential reactions between HLA‐B*53 and DRESS‐like events.

HLA‐B Genotyping

HLA‐B typing was conducted using INNO‐LiPA HLA‐B Update Plus technology (Fujirebio Europe N.V., Belgium), a reverse hybridization method involving oligonucleotide probes that bind complementarily to DNA strands.

Ethical Considerations

The study was conducted in accordance with the Basic & Clinical Pharmacology & Toxicology policy for experimental and clinical studies [18] and was approved by the Research Ethics Committee of Hospital Universitario de La Princesa (study registration number: 4167, meeting 13/20), in accordance with national and institutional regulations. All patient data were pseudonymised prior to analysis, and identifiers were kept separately in accordance with data protection regulations.

Literature Search

To contextualize the findings from our cohort, we conducted a structured scoping review of published reports of raltegravir‐associated DRESS, with a focus on clinical characteristics and HLA genotypes. We searched MEDLINE (via PubMed) and Embase (via Embase.com) from inception through 31 December 2020, using combinations of controlled vocabulary and free‐text terms.

In Embase, the strategy was: (‘raltegravir’/exp. OR raltegravir) AND (‘DRESS syndrome’/exp. OR ‘drug reaction with eosinophilia and systemic symptoms’ OR DRESS).

In PubMed, it was: (raltegravir) AND (‘drug reaction with eosinophilia and systemic symptoms’ OR DRESS syndrome).

Filters were applied in both databases to retrieve case reports/series and clinical studies describing adverse drug reactions. Eligibility criteria were predefined: human case reports/series of DRESS meeting RegiSCAR probable/definite, with raltegravir exposure, sufficient clinical detail and English/Spanish language. Titles and abstracts were screened by a single reviewer (CRDM), with full‐text verification by a second author; reference lists of included reports were screened to identify additional cases. Results were synthesized narratively.

We did not register a protocol or apply PRISMA, as the aim was to map a very small, case‐based literature to contextualize a genotyped raltegravir cohort, rather than to perform risk‐of‐bias assessment or meta‐analysis; this scope‐related limitation is acknowledged in the Discussion.

Results

Study Population and Genotyping

A total of 123 patients were genotyped, of whom 30 (24.4%) were female and 93 (75.6%) were male. The most prevalent allele was HLA‐B*35 (13%, 32/246 alleles), followed by HLA‐B*07 (8.53%, 21/246 alleles). Three individuals carried the HLA‐B*53 allele, representing a prevalence of 2.44% for HLA‐B*53 carriers and an allele prevalence of 1.22%. No homozygous cases for HLA‐B*53 were detected. Fourteen patients (11.38%) had not received raltegravir and were excluded, leaving a final cohort of 109 patients for analysis.

No cases of DRESS syndrome were observed in non‐carriers. Among the three HLA‐B*53 carriers, one remained asymptomatic, while two developed clinical features compatible with DRESS.

Case Descriptions

Carrier 1 is a 43‐year‐old Caucasian male with no past medical history relevant to the case [19]. He received HIV post‐exposure prophylaxis with raltegravir and tenofovir disoproxil/emtricitabine. Two weeks later, he developed fever (38.5°C), a maculopapular rash and lymphadenopathy. Bloodwork showed leukocytosis (17 500/mm3), lymphocytic predominance and eosinophilia peaking at 2000/mm3. Hepatitis was evident, with elevated bilirubin (3.19 mg/dL), AST (187 U/L), ALT (327 U/L), GGT (447 U/L) and ALP (409 U/L). Coagulation abnormalities included an increased INR (1.70) and reduced prothrombin activity (49%). Elevated CRP (8.70 mg/dL) and procalcitonin (0.68 ng/mL) suggested systemic inflammation.

Serologies, blood cultures, Mycoplasma and Chlamydia PCR and ANA tests were negative while HHV‐6 PCR was positive (113 158 IU/mL). Skin biopsy revealed spongiosis and eosinophilic infiltration, consistent with a drug‐induced cutaneous reaction. The patient was treated with prednisone 80 mg/day, which was tapered over 6 months, leading to complete resolution. Figure S1 illustrates eosinophil count dynamics.

The RegiSCAR score was 6, classifying the case as ‘definite’ DRESS. Raltegravir scored 6 (probable), whereas tenofovir disoproxil fumarate and emtricitabine each scored 3 (possible), reflecting less specific chronology and limited drug‐specific dechallenge for the backbone agents. HLA genotyping confirmed heterozygous HLA‐B*53 carrier status.

Carrier 2 is a 59‐year‐old HIV‐positive male from Guinea, diagnosed in 2002, with a history of chronic HBV infection and cerebral toxoplasmosis. In 2016, his regimen was switched to raltegravir, tenofovir/emtricitabine and zidovudine. One month later, he reported neurological symptoms, prompting a CT scan that suggested a possible infectious or ischaemic lesion.

Upon admission (May 12, 2016), he had hemiparesis, hemihypoaesthesia, widespread skin lesions and lymphadenopathy. Blood tests revealed eosinophilia (760 cells/mm3), anaemia (Haemoglobin dropped from 11 to 8.8 g/dL) and cholestatic hepatitis (AST 19 U/L, ALT 13 U/L, GGT 425 U/L, bilirubin 0.22 mg/dL, ALP 95 U/L). Gastrointestinal biopsies showed lymphocytic infiltrates and secondary sclerosing cholangitis.

Empirical treatment for toxoplasmosis (pyrimethamine, sulfadiazine, folinic acid) improved neurological symptoms. CMV PCR was positive, suggesting viral reactivation. Eosinophilia (> 20%) was initially attributed to parasitosis, but stool studies and Schistosoma serology (IgG positive) were inconclusive. Neutropenia and thrombocytopenia developed under ganciclovir, necessitating a switch to foscarnet.

At follow‐up (July 18, 2016), neutropenia and eosinophilia persisted (> 50%) but gradually resolved alongside clinical improvement. By the three‐month follow‐up, blood counts and liver function had normalized. Raltegravir, as well as tenofovir disoproxil/emtricitabine and zidovudine were discontinued, after which hematologic recovery was documented. RegiSCAR score was 4 (‘probable’ DRESS), and Naranjo score was 4 suggesting raltegravir as a possible cause. Tenofovir and emtricitabine both scored 1 (‘possible’) in the Naranjo algorithm, while zidovudine scored 3 (‘probable’). No agent reached the probable range, and drug‐specific dechallenge for the backbone agents was limited; therefore, attribution remains cautious. Figure S2 illustrates eosinophilia trends over time.

Carrier number 3, a 54‐year‐old HIV‐positive male, initiated raltegravir in 2011 and has remained on it long‐term. In 2017, he developed herpes zoster, which resolved spontaneously. In 2023, he sought emergency care for exanthematous abdominal lesions which, despite being atypical, were diagnosed as secondary syphilis, highlighting the broad dermatological variability of this stage of the disease [20].

His highest eosinophil count to date is 590 cells/mm3 (2019), but he has always remained asymptomatic and has never been diagnosed with DRESS syndrome.

Risk Estimation

Among the 109 raltegravir‐exposed patients, none of the 106 non‐carriers developed DRESS, while two of three HLA‐B*53 did, based on a RegiSCAR scores of ≥ 4. The relative risk (RR) was therefore calculated with a result of 133.9 (95% CI: 7.6–2348.7), highlighting a strong association between HLA‐B*53 and DRESS. As there were no cases of DRESS in the non‐carrier group, a correction of 0.5 was applied to the cells in the contingency table to avoid division by zero [21, 22].

These findings support a strong association between HLA‐B*53 and DRESS syndrome, reinforcing the potential role of genetic screening prior to raltegravir use in high‐risk populations.

Literature Review

The literature search yielded 12 records (9 from Embase, 3 from PubMed). After removing 2 duplicates, 10 articles were screened. Of these, 7 were excluded after reviewing titles and abstracts for not addressing the possible linkage between DRESS syndrome and HLA‐B*53 carriage in raltegravir‐treated patients. The reports were assessed in full; two met inclusion criteria, and one was excluded because the patient had not received raltegravir. One additional case was identified through citation tracking. In total, 9 published cases were included in our review, comprising 3 newly identified in the current update (2 database + 1 citation) and 6 previously known eligible reports. These 9 published cases, together with our 2 cohort cases (total n = 11), are summarized in Table S2, offering a comparative review.

Of note, five cases involved adult women of African ancestry [23, 24, 25, 27, 28], three involved men [14, 27, 31], (one of Hispanic) descent and one was paediatric [29]. All 11 patients had exposure to > 1 suspected drug, and eosinophilia was present in all but one case [28]. Time to onset ranged from 2 to 8 weeks, although it was not reported in three of the cases [26, 27, 31]. The most frequent systemic manifestation was hepatic, present in all 11 of the cases, followed by renal [14, 23, 24], respiratory [2, 28, 29] and even gastrointestinal [2].

Discussion

In our cohort of 109 patients who received raltegravir, two cases of DRESS syndrome occurred exclusively among the three HLA‐B*53 carriers, yielding a 66.7% incidence in this subgroup and no cases in non‐carriers.

Both our cases as well as those identified in the literature developed some form of rash and systemic involvement, most frequently hepatic, followed by renal.

In addition to the nine previously documented cases in the literature [14, 23, 24, 25, 26, 27, 28, 29, 30], our cohort included two additional cases of DRESS associated with raltegravir use. Notably, one of our cases is, to our knowledge, the first described in a non‐HIV Caucasian patient receiving raltegravir as post‐exposure prophylaxis, thereby expanding the ethnic and clinical profile of reported cases (Table S2).

As detailed in Results, in Case 1, raltegravir was probable (Naranjo 6) while the backbone agents were possible (3 each). In Case 2, all candidate antiretrovirals were possible (raltegravir and zidovudine 3; tenofovir and emtricitabine 1). This pattern illustrates the uncertainty inherent in multi‐drug regimens; nonetheless, reporting per‐drug categories increases transparency and, when interpreted alongside latency and clinical course after withdrawal, helps prioritize the most plausible culprit while acknowledging alternative attributions.

Our findings align with those of Lefebvre et al. (2020), who reported seven cases of raltegravir‐associated DRESS in a multicentre French cohort of 19 238 treated patients (estimated incidence: 3.6 per 10 000). Among five genotyped patients, three (60%) were HLA‐B*53:01 carriers [31]. Like our series, most cases occurred in individuals of African ancestry. However, unlike our study, Lefebvre et al. did not estimate relative risk or explore cohort‐wide HLA frequencies. Our RR of 133.9 (95% CI: 7.6–2348.7) suggests a much stronger association, albeit in a smaller cohort, underscoring the need for targeted screening in high‐prevalence groups. Several limitations should be acknowledged. First, the literature component was a structured narrative review, not a protocol‐registered PRISMA systematic review, reflecting the very small, case‐based evidence base; accordingly, we did not undertake formal risk‐of‐bias assessment or meta‐analysis. Second, within the cohort, one case was retrospectively identified through record review and was not initially recognized as DRESS. Moreover, electronic record transitions limited the completeness of older clinical data. In that case, raltegravir was discontinued due to lack of improvement, and no specific treatment was administered; however, eosinophil counts declined only after discontinuation, supporting a causal relationship. Additionally, subtyping of HLA‐B*53 was not performed, as this is not routinely available in our centre. Nonetheless, since B*53:01 accounts for > 95% of HLA‐B*53 alleles, it is likely that our patients carried this specific variant. Furthermore, changes in genotyping practice since 2019, shifting from full HLA‐B panels to targeted HCP5 screening for B*57:01, limited new inclusions. However, population data support our assumptions: a prevalence of 2% for the HLA‐B*53:01 allele was identified in a cohort of 4335 NGS‐sequenced patients in Spain, with all cases carrying the B53 group [32] and up to 10% estimated among West Africans [33], five times higher than in the Spanish cohort. This reinforces the genetic‐ethnic component of susceptibility (see Table S2). Given the reported 6%–7% mortality of DRESS syndrome [34, 35], HLA‐B*53:01 routine screening in selected populations, similar to HLA‐B*57:01 testing prior to abacavir, warrants consideration.

The pathogenesis of DRESS involves a complex interplay of genetic, immunologic and pharmacologic factors. In raltegravir‐associated DRESS, non‐covalent interaction between the drug (or metabolite) and HLA‐B*53:01 may trigger T‐cell activation, following the pharmacological interaction (p‐i) model [38]. This mechanism parallels other HLA‐associated hypersensitivity reactions, such as HLA‐B*57:01 with abacavir [38], HLA‐B*15:02 with carbamazepine and HLA‐B*58:01 with allopurinol.

However, not all carriers develop DRESS, suggesting that cofactors [39] such as viral reactivation [40] (e.g., CMV, HHV‐6) and immune status contribute. The overlap with immune reconstitution inflammatory syndrome (IRIS) [36], especially in HIV patients initiating ART–raltegravir is often introduced in antiretroviral‐naïve patients [41] or as salvage therapy in resistance cases [42], diagnostic complexity increases. However, both IRIS and VRESS (viral reactivation with eosinophilia and systemic symptoms) [38] share clinical and immunologic features with DRESS [37], including T‐cell activation and tissue injury.

A key challenge is distinguishing DRESS from immune reconstitution inflammatory syndrome (IRIS) [36], also referred to as Viral Reaction with Eosinophilia and Systemic Symptoms (VRESS), which involves viral reactivation with eosinophilia during immune recovery, also seen in transplant recipients [38]. Both syndromes involve inflammatory mechanisms causing tissue damage and their different manifestations [37]. DRESS is triggered by a drug (not present in VRESS) binding to a specific HLA, as theorized by the p‐i concept [38], inducing a T‐dependent response. This explains the link between HLA‐B*57:01 and abacavir hypersensitivity [38]. Since this association is not absolute, other factors [39], such as CMV reactivation [40], likely contribute.

Considering raltegravir is often introduced in antiretroviral‐naïve patients [41] or as salvage therapy for resistance [42], overlap between DRESS and IRIS is possible. This highlights the need for improved diagnostic criteria that differentiate eosinophilia causes and overlapping immune syndromes. The absence of travel history, environmental data or helminth screening further complicates diagnostic precision.

In our cohort, Case 1 occurred in an immunocompetent host, ruling out IRIS. In contrast, Case 2 likely represents a DRESS–IRIS overlap, where immune recovery, CMV reactivation and raltegravir exposure may have synergized to trigger eosinophilic inflammation. We suspect raltegravir haptenized with the C‐terminal alpha‐helix unique to HLA‐B*53:01 [12], stimulating CD8 + responses, potentially amplified by immune restoration.

Diagnostic tools such as RegiSCAR have limitations in this context. They do not differentiate eosinophilia origins (e.g., parasitic, viral, IRIS), nor do they incorporate genetic data. Other criteria, like Bocquet or SCAR‐J, may provide complementary diagnostic value [43]. In our review, some cases with confirmed HLA‐B*53:01 homozygosity scored low on RegiSCAR [26] and one case of definite diagnosis in which the presence of HLA‐B*53:01 is discarded [30], while others with compatible clinical profiles lacked genotyping, limiting interpretability [23, 25].

Finally, the optimal management of DRESS remains debated. Corticosteroids are widely used, yet some cases, including one of ours, resolved without them. The absence of standardized treatment protocols raises questions about the benefit–risk balance of corticoid usage, their efficacy and their impact on prognosis in these cases [44]. Further studies are needed to assess their role in raltegravir‐induced DRESS and to validate the utility of HLA‐B*53 screening as a preventive strategy.

Conclusion

In our genotyped, raltegravir‐exposed cohort we observed a denominator‐based signal concentrating DRESS among HLA‐B*53 carriers. Per‐drug Naranjo supported raltegravir over backbone agents in Case 1 (probable vs. possible) and showed residual uncertainty in Case 2 (all possible). Together with our structured narrative synthesis, these findings reinforce early recognition and prompt culprit‐drug withdrawal, especially when pulmonary or renal involvement is present.

Diagnostic uncertainty is compounded by the overlap between DRESS and IRIS after ART changes; frameworks integrating chronology vs. ART, virological/immunological trajectories, genetics (e.g., HLA‐B*53) and transparent per‐drug attribution are needed. These data are hypothesis‐generating and warrant replication with HLA‐B*53:01 subtyping and prospective IRIS adjudication; pre‐treatment HLA‐B*53 screening may be considered in high‐prevalence settings pending validation.
