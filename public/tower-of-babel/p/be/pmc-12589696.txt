Molecular imaging in the diagnostic process of neuroendocrine tumors: a systematic review on unknown primary origin and suspected NETs
Authors: Laura Alonzo, Roberto Cannella, Riccardo Laudicella, Viviana Benfante, Pierpaolo Purpura, Giuseppe Micci, Massimo Galia, Giuseppe Brancatelli, Massimo Midiri, Pierpaolo Alongi
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundNeuroendocrine tumors (NETs) are a heterogeneous group of neoplasms often arising in the gastrointestinal tract, pancreas, and lungs. Diagnosing NETs can be challenging due to their small size, slow growth, and low metabolic activity, especially in cases of unknown primary origin (CUP-NETs). Timely identification of the primary lesion is critical for tailored treatment planning, including peptide receptor radionuclide therapy (PRRT), surgery, or somatostatin analogue administration.ObjectiveThis systematic review evaluates the diagnostic performance and clinical impact of molecular imaging—especially PET/CT with radiolabeled somatostatin analogs—in patients with suspected NETs or CUP-NETs.MethodsFollowing PRISMA guidelines, a comprehensive literature search was conducted in PubMed and Scopus. Data on study design, tracers used, detection rates, and impact on patient management were extracted. Quality assessment was performed using CASP tools.ResultsPET/CT using [68 Ga]Ga-DOTA-peptides (DOTATATE, DOTATOC, DOTANOC) consistently outperformed conventional SPECT imaging with [111In]Pentetreotide (Octreoscan), achieving primary tumor detection rates of 38%–83% versus < 10% with SPECT. In suspected NETs, PET/CT demonstrated high sensitivity (up to 95%) and specificity ( > 85%), with changes in clinical management reported in up to 33% of cases. Dual-tracer imaging ([68 Ga]Ga-DOTATATE and [18F]FDG) and newer tracers such as [18F]DOPA and [18F]AlF-NOTA-octreotide ([18F]-OC) provided added diagnostic value, particularly in aggressive or rare subtypes (e.g. paragangliomas, insulinomas). Detection of unknown primaries commonly led to curative surgical options or more targeted therapies.ConclusionsMolecular imaging—particularly [68Ga]Ga-DOTA-peptide PET/CT—represents an essential component in the diagnostic evaluation of undefined/suspected NETs. It not only improves diagnostic accuracy but also has a profound impact on clinical decision-making. Its integration into diagnostic algorithms should be strongly considered for both known and suspected cases of neuroendocrine neoplasia.Supplementary informationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s41824-025-00274-4.Keywords: CUP, UPO, Neuroendocrine tumor, Positron-emission-tomography, Single photon emission tomography, Dota-peptides, Somatostatine receptor

Introduction

Neuroendocrine tumors (NETs) constitute a heterogeneous group of neoplasms that arise from neuroendocrine cells throughout the body, particularly in the gastrointestinal tract, pancreas, and bronchopulmonary system (Yao et al. 2008, Rindi et al. 2018). Although well-differentiated NETs may follow a relatively indolent course, their biological behavior varies greatly depending on location, grade, and proliferative activity (Lloyd et al. 2017, Klöppel et al. 2004). Timely and accurate diagnosis of NETs remains critical, yet is often hindered by their subtle symptomatology, small tumor size, and low metabolic activity (Öberg, 2012, Sundin et al. 2017).

Among NET presentations, a particularly challenging scenario is represented by metastatic NETs with an unknown primary tumor, classified as cancers of unknown primary origin (CUP-NETs). This condition is not uncommon, occurring in up to 15–20% of metastatic NETs at initial diagnosis (Schreiter et al. 2014, Prasad et al. 2010). Identifying the primary tumor is crucial because it may dictate curative surgical options, allow peptide receptor radionuclide therapy (PRRT), support the administration of somatostatin analogs (Sadowski et al. 2016, Alonso et al. 2014), and guide a precise follow-up. However, conventional imaging such as computed tomography (CT) or magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) may fail to detect the potentially small or well-differentiated primaries frequently seen in these tumors: indeed, the diagnostic challenge is typically represented by patients with clinical symptoms or biochemical markers suggesting neuroendocrine origin but lacking histological confirmation or definitive imaging evidence.

Molecular imaging offers enhanced diagnostic sensitivity and specificity in such cases, often surpassing conventional cross-sectional imaging.

Somatostatin Receptor Imaging (SRI) using Single-Photon-Emission-Tomography (SPECT) and Positron emission tomography (PET)/CT, has improved the diagnostic algorithm of NET for correct therapy management including peptides-receptor-radioligand-therapy (PRRT) (Hope et al. 2023). [68Ga]Ga-labeled DOTA peptides (DOTATATE, DOTATOC, DOTANOC) bind to somatostatin receptors (mainly subtype 2, SSTR2), which are overexpressed in most well-differentiated NETs (Prasad et al. 2010, Sadowski et al. 2016). These tracers have demonstrated a high detection rate (and lower radiation exposure), with PET/CT identifying the primary tumor in up to 83% of patients with CUP-NETs, in contrast to the < 10% yield of conventional SRI SPECT with [111In]Pentetreotide (Octreoscan) (Schreiter et al. 2014, Alonso et al. 2014, Tan et al. 2014, Menda et al. 2017, Imperiale et al. 2014, Chen et al. 2018).

Additionally, tracers like [18F]DOPA have been particularly effective in detecting well-differentiated insulinoma, medullary thyroid carcinoma, pheochromocytomas and paragangliomas and may provide high lesion-to-background contrast in serotonin-producing tumors (Sung et al. 2024). Newer fluorinated somatostatin analogs such as [18F]AlF-NOTA-octreotide ([18F]OC) and approaches using dual tracers (e.g., [68Ga]Ga-DOTATATE and [18F]FDG) further enhance lesion characterization, staging, differentiation grading and treatment selection (Chen et al. 2023, Sampathirao and Basu, 2017).

This systematic review aims to assess the diagnostic performance, tumor detection rate, and clinical impact of molecular imaging methods—including SPECT (Octreoscan,123/131I-MIBG), PET/CT with [68Ga]Ga -DOTA-peptides, [18F]DOPA, [18F]FDG and novel or dual-tracer techniques—in patients with CUP-NETs and suspected NETs. Outcomes of interest include imaging sensitivity/specificity, ability to localize the primary tumor, and influence on clinical management.

Materials and methods

This systematic review was carried out following the guidelines of the Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Reviews and Meta-Analyses (PRISMA) (Page et al. 2021).

Literature search

A comprehensive online literature search was performed using PubMed and Scopus as search databases. To identify relevant published articles, the following search terms were used: “PET and/or SPECT and unknown primary”, “PET and/or SPECT and suspected or undefined” in combination with “neuroendocrine tumor”. No time limits were selected.

Eligibility criteria

We included only clinical studies in the English language. After removing duplicate articles, the remaining articles were screened and excluded based on the following criteria: (1) articles that did not assess suspected neuroendocrine tumors (S-NET) or neuroendocrine carcinoma of unknown primary origin (CUP-NET); (2) case reports; (3) review or systematic reviews; (4) lack of adequate reference standard for the diagnosis.

The manuscript screening and selection, as well as the data extraction and quality assessment, were performed by two authors (L.A., a Radiologist with 6 years of experience, and P.A., a Nuclear Medicine Physician with 10 years of experience). Any discrepancy was resolved with a consensus discussion.

Data extraction

The following data were extracted during the full-text manuscript assessment: (1) name of the first Author; (2) year of publication; (3) Country of origin; (4) study design, scored as prospective or retrospective; (5) number of included S-NETs or UPO-NETs in the study cohort; (6) radiopharmaceuticals applied for PET/CT and SPECT imaging exams; (7) reference standard for the diagnosis (i.e. histology and follow-up, conventional imaging, clinical assessment); (8) detection rate of PET/CT and/or SPECT; (9) primary tumor sites analyzed through PET/CT and/or SPECT imaging.

Quality assessment

The methodological quality of the studies was critically assessed using the Critical Appraisal Study Programme (CASP) dedicated to diagnostic studies (Singh, 2013).

Statistical analysis

Continuous variables were summarized as medians and interquartile ranges after assessing normality using the Shapiro–Wilk test, and compared using the Mann–Whitney U test. Categorical variables were reported as counts and percentages, and compared using Pearson’s chi-squared test or Fisher’s exact test, as appropriate. During the full-text manuscript review, the detection rate was extracted by assessing the reported true positive rate (i.e., sensitivity), calculated as the number of correctly identified primary tumors divided by the total number of CUP-NETs or suspected NETs confirmed by the reference standard, depending on the primary aim of each study.

The level of statistical significance was set at p < 0.05. Statistical analyses were conducted using IBM SPSS Statistics, version 26.0 (IBM Corp., Armonk, NY, USA).

Results

Study selection

The flow diagram of study selection is presented in Fig. 1. A total of 1,527 articles were identified in the initial search: 562 originated from PubMed and 965 from Scopus databases. After removing duplicated articles (n = 867), 660 records were screened. 629/660 studies were excluded because not directly related to the aim of the current systematic review (not assessing S-NET nor CUP-NET). Furthermore, 2 case reports and 5 reviews and meta-analyses were excluded. One article was excluded because it was not possible to retrieve the full-text manuscript.

Flow chart of the study selection process

Of the 23 selected articles, two were excluded because they did not evaluate suspected S-NET or CUP-NET neuroendocrine tumors. Therefore, 21 studies were finally included in this systematic review (Schreiter et al. 2014, Prasad et al. 2010, Sadowski et al. 2016, Alonso et al. 2014, Hope et al. 2023, Tan et al. 2014, Menda et al. 2017, Imperiale et al. 2014, Chen et al. 2018, Sung et al. 2024, Chen et al. 2023, Sampathirao and Basu, 2017, Krausz et al. 2011, Pruthi et al. 2016, Sharma et al. 2014, Haidar et al. 2017, Kazmierczak et al. 2017, Naswa et al. 2011, Haug et al. 2012, Ambrosini et al. 2012, Li et al. 2020, de C et al. 2014). Twenty-one clinical studies (2010–2024) were included, assessing a total of 1,857 patients (420 UPO-NETs and 452 suspected NETs).

Study characteristics

A total of 21 studies assessing 1,857 patients were included in this systematic review. The main characteristics of the included studies are provided in Table 1. Overall, those studies included 420 CUP-NETs and 452 S-NETs. Only two studies included both CUP-NETs and S-NETs (Schreiter et al. 2014, Chen et al. 2018). NETs of unknown primary origin (CUP-NETs) and suspected NETs

Somatostatin receptor imaging

SPECT imaging, once the mainstay in neuroendocrine tumor diagnostics, has demonstrated poor sensitivity and suboptimal lesion localization in patients with metastatic NETs of unknown primary origin. In one of the few direct comparisons, Krausz et al. found that Octreoscan had significantly inferior lesion detection rate compared to [68Ga]Ga-DOTANOC PET/CT, identifying only a fraction of the true lesions; furthermore, differently than Octreoscan SPECT, [68Ga]Ga-DOTANOC PET/CT altered therapy management in 3 out of 19 cases (Krausz et al. 2011). Prasad et al. localized the CUP-NETs in 35/59 patients (59%) using [68Ga]Ga-DOTANOC PET/CT (versus 12/59 for CT, 20%), frequently identifying pancreatic and small bowel lesions, changing also the management in 10% of the cases (Prasad et al. 2010). Pruthi et al. assessed 68 metastatic CUP-NETs on conventional imaging, who underwent [68Ga]Ga-DOTANOC PET/CT that identified primary sites in 40/68 patients (59%, mainly intestine), impacting their management (Pruthi et al. 2016). In addition to other studies, the authors evaluated semiquantitative PET information, reporting a mean SUVmax of the detected primary sites of 25.1 ± 18.0 (median: 16.25; range, 2.1–150) and a significant positive correlation between SUVmax of detected primary site and SUVmax of the histopathologically proven sites of metastasis (r = 0.662; p < 0.0001) (Pruthi et al. 2016). Sharma et al. retrospectively analyzed 164 patients who underwent [68Ga]Ga-DOTANOC PET/CT for suspected NET based on clinical features (n = 94), and/or increased biochemical markers (n = 83), and/or imaging findings (n = 93). Namely, [68Ga]Ga-DOTANOC PET/CT was true positive in 92 patients, true negative in 58, false positive in 9, and false negative in 5, reaching overall sensitivity of 94.8%, specificity of 86.5%, positive predictive value of 91%, negative predictive value of 92%, and accuracy of 91.4%. The accuracy of [68Ga]Ga-DOTANOC PET-CT in patients with clinical features of NET was 90.4%, with raised biochemical markers was 86.7%, and with imaging findings suggestive of NET was 93.5% (Sharma et al. 2014). Kazmierczak et al. assessed 38 consecutive patients with histologically proven CUP-NETs who underwent a contrast-enhanced (ce) [68Ga]Ga-DOTATATE PET/CT for primary tumor detection and staging. ceCT and [68Ga]Ga-DOTATATE PET were analyzed singularly, observing a significantly higher sensitivity (94% vs. 63%, p = 0.005) and accuracy (87% vs. 68%, p = 0.003) for the latter. Indeed, [68Ga]Ga-DOTATATE PET/CT compared with provides an improvement in sensitivity of 50% and in accuracy of 30% than ceCT in primary tumor detection in CUP-NETs. PET clarified ambiguous diagnoses in over 80%, aiding follow-up (Kazmierczak et al. 2017). Haug et al. assessed [68 Ga]Ga-DOTATATE PET/CT data from 104 consecutive patients with clinical suspicion of NET (n = 70), elevated serum NET markers (n = 49), and image-based suspicion of NET without definitive selection of primary(n = 53). Among thirty-six histologically verified patients, [68 Ga]Ga-DOTATATE PET/CT identified NET in 29/36 cases and excluded the presence of a NET in 61 of the 68 non-NET patients, indicating a sensitivity of 81% and specificity of 90%. The PET/CT gave a false-positive result in 7 patients and a false-negative in another 7 patients, indicating positive and negative predictive values of 81 and 90%, respectively, and an accuracy of 87% (Haug et al. 2012).

Finally, in a similar setting, Ambrosini et al. evaluated the potential usefulness of [68Ga]Ga -DOTA-NOC PET/CT in one-hundred-thirty-one patients with suspected NET. Overall, the incidence of NET in the studied population was 14.5% (19/131) with sensitivity 89.5% and specificity 100% (Ambrosini et al. 2012).

The results of these retrospective and prospective studies show a detection rate of CUP-NETs and S-NETs equal to or greater than 58% by [68 Ga]Ga-DOTANOC, [18F]OC, and [68 Ga]Ga-DOTATATE-PET/CT.

Similarly, Schreiter et al. retrospectively reviewed 83 patients with CUP-NETs. Octreoscan localized the primary tumor in only 1.2% of cases, whereas [68Ga]Ga-DOTATOC PET/CT reached a detection rate of 46%, changing the treatment plans in over one-third of patients (Schreiter et al. 2014). Alonso et al. (Alonso et al. 2014) also demonstrated a clinical management impact in a retrospective study conducted in 29 patients with pathologically proven NET metastases, with CUP on conventional imaging. [68Ga]Ga-DOTATATE PET/CT identified a previously misdiagnosed primary NET in 17/29 (59%) patients, mainly in the pancreas (n = 7) and ileum (n = 7), further observing additional sites of unsuspected metastases (mainly in lymph nodes and mesentery), changing treatment in 24%, including shifts to curative surgery (Sundin et al. 2017). Similarly, Tan et al. (Tan et al. 2014) showed that [68Ga]Ga-DOTATATE PET/CT identified the primary CUP-NETs in 5/6 (83%) patients with immunohistochemically proven neuroendocrine liver metastases but inconclusive initial CT findings, changing the management in half of the cases. Menda et al. assessed 40 patients with metastatic UPO showing a 38% localization rate with [68Ga]Ga-DOTATOC PET/CT, mainly in the ileum and pancreas. Despite 50% false negatives, the results influenced surgery or biopsy (Menda et al. 2017).

These retrospective and prospective studies indicate that a detection rate of mainly CUP-NETs ranges from 32.7% to 83.3%, with uptake of [68 Ga]Ga-DOTATATE and [68 Ga]Ga-DOTATOC particularly concentrated in the gastrointestinal tract and pancreas.

Chen et al. investigated the diagnostic accuracy of 68 Ga-DOTATOC and 18F-FDG PET/CT to identify the primary foci in patients with clinically suspected NET and CUP-NETs and based on the results of pathology and findings on clinical follow-up. The overall sensitivity values of 68 Ga-DOTATOC, 18F-FDG, and conventional workup were 88, 41, and 53%, respectively, whereas the specificities were 100%, 100%, 68%, respectively. 68 Ga-DOTATOC PET/CT changed treatment in 33% of patients (Chen et al. 2018). Naswa et al. found PET outperformed CT and EUS in early pancreatic NETs and of 109 patients who underwent [68Ga]Ga -DOTA-NOC PET/CT, change in the management strategy was obtained for 21 patients (19%). On the basis of the results, six patients (5.5%) underwent surgery for primary lesions that were not detected on other imaging modalities. In another eight patients (6.4%), [68Ga]Ga-DOTA-NOC PET/CT helped in surgical planning by depicting additional surgically resectable sites. Four patients (3.6%) were spared unnecessary surgery because evidence of the advanced stage of disease was seen (Naswa et al. 2011). In these two prospective studies, medical doctors detected the majority of S-NETs. These two works also show how the use of both radiotracers [68 Ga]Ga-DOTATOC and [18F]FDG allowed discrimination between CUP- and S-NETs in the 2018 study by Chen SH et al. (Chen et al. 2018), unlike the other study in which the detected uptake was discriminatory only in terms of detecting absorption but not in CUP diagnosis.

In a heterogeneous retrospective cohort of patients underwent [68Ga]Ga-DOTANOC PET-CT for the primary diagnosis of NET, of whom 66 (52.7%) patients presented with a clinical suspicion of NET, 10 (8.3%) patients presented with a biochemical suspicion of NET, and 48 (38.8%) patients presented with a suspicious NET lesion discovered on another imaging modality. Among them, Haidar et al. showed 87.1% sensitivity and 97.7% specificity in a cohort of 124 patients (Haidar et al. 2017). In this setting the larger cohort in a prospective study was evaluated by Sadowski et al. in 131 patients underwent [68Ga]Ga-DOTATATE PET/CT detecting 95.1% of lesions vs 45.3% for anatomic imaging, and 30.9% for Octreoscan SPECT/CT (p < 0.001). Furthermore, in 4/14 patients (28.6%), [68Ga]Ga-DOTATATE PET/CT found a previously CUP, and detected primary GEP-NET, lymph node, and distant metastases in 72/113 lesions (63.7%) when compared with histopathology vs 22.1% and 38.9% by Octreoscan SPECT/CT and anatomic imaging, respectively. Based on [68Ga]Ga-DOTATATE PET/CT, 43/131 patients (32.8%) had a change in management recommendation. In carcinoid syndromic patients with negative biochemical testing, [68Ga]Ga-DOTATATE PET/CT detected lesions in 65.2% of the cases, vs 25.2% for anatomic imaging and Octreoscan SPECT/CT (Sadowski et al. 2016). In these two retrospective and prospective studies using [68 Ga]Ga-DOTANOC and [68 Ga]Ga-DOTATATE uptake, 31 and 14 CUP-NETs were detected, respectively, on 445 and 131 patients undergoing PET/CT imaging.

Other radiopharmaceuticals

Recent radiopharmaceuticals advancements regard the possibility to label somatostatin analogs with 18F as radionuclide widely available. Thus, Chen et al. retrospectively reviewed the data of patients who had undergone [18F]AlF-NOTA-octreotide ([18F]-OC) PET/CT and CT or MR. Of these 93 patients, there were 45 with suspected NET, and 48 with pathologically confirmed NET for detecting metastasis or recurrence. [18F]-OC PET/CT showed superior performance with 96.3% of sensitivity, 77.8% of specificity, and 88.9% of accuracy in diagnosing NENs compared to CT/MRI. For a total of 276 suspected NET lesions, the sensitivity, specificity, and accuracy of [18F]-OC PET/CT for diagnosis of NENs were 90.5%, 82.1%, and 88.8%, respectively, and were higher than those of CT/MRI (Chen et al. 2023).

In the study of Li et al. a total of 124 patients with hepatic metastatic carcinoma of unknown primary underwent whole body 18F-FDG PET/CT imaging. Among true positive findings in 6 patients was found a primary tumors CUP-NETs. The lesions in the perspective of a gastroscope only showed a local rough slight uplift and it was difficult to distinguish the gastric mucosal tissue lesion with naked eyes. The final pathologic results showed the presence of a neuroendocrine tumor, G3 (Li et al. 2020).

In view of accurate performances of SRI in well differentiated NET, in particular using PET/CT, the research activity on other radiopharmaceuticals compound mostly regarded a comparison with other tracers, the study of tumor heterogeneity and/or the assessment of G2–G3 NET. Chen et al. assessed 36 patients with clinically suspected NET and CUP-NETs who underwent conventional imaging (including CT, MRI, endoscopic ultrasound), [68Ga]Ga-DOTATOC, and [18F]FDG PET/CT. [68Ga]Ga-DOTATOC found primaries in 47.2% of CUP-NETs, resulting more sensitive than [18F]FDG (88% vs 41%) and more specific than conventional imaging (100% vs 68%), changing the treatment in 12/36 (33.3%) (Chen et al. 2018).

Sampathirao et al. combined [68Ga]Ga-DOTATATE and [18F]FDG in 28 UPO-NET cases, localizing primaries in 60.7% of cases and guiding PRRT by assessing heterogeneity tumor and differentiation grading. Unknown primary was detected on [68 Ga]Ga-DOTATATE in 31/51 patients, resulting in sensitivity of 60.8% whereas overall lesion detection sensitivity was 96.8%. There was a corresponding decrease in the [68 Ga]Ga-DOTATATE-to[18F]FDG uptake ratio with increasing MIB-1/Ki-67 index (Sampathirao and Basu, 2017). Etchebehere et al. compared lesion detectability in 19 patients with NET of different origin (including CUP-NETs) among [99mTc]hydrazinonicotinamide (HYNIC)-octreotide SPECT/CT, [68Ga]Ga-DOTATATE PET/CT, and whole-body diffusion-weighted MRI (WB DWI). Two unknown primary lesions were identified solely by [68Ga]Ga-DOTATATE PET/CT. [68Ga]Ga -DOTATATE PET/CT, SSRS SPECT/CT, and WB DWI demonstrated sensitivities of 0.96, 0.60, and 0.72; negative predictive values of 0.98, 0.83, and 0.88; accuracies of 0.97, 0.86, and 0.91 (with similiar specificity and positive predictive values) (de C et al. 2014). Functional imaging, using specific radiopharmaceuticals able to be absorbed and accumulated by chromaffin cells, has been included in the evaluation of some NET. Now the case [123I]MIBG SPECT/CT and[18F]DOPA PET/CT, offer high specificity for neuroendocrine paraganglioma and pheochromocytoma. In this field, in a prospective study of 32 patients with suspected paraganglioma, Sung et al. compared [123I]MIBG SPECT/CT with [18F]FDOPA PET/CT with same specificity, but superior interobserver agreement and higher sensitivity (86.2% vs 65.5%, p = 0.031) for metastatic patients for [18F]FDOPA PET/CT, with also superior interobserver agreement (κ = 0.94 vs 0.85) (Sung et al. 2024). Similarly, Imperiale et al. assessed 27 UPO-NET patients with [18F]FDOPA PET/CT (23 staging, 4 during follow-up) after negative conventional imaging and Octreoscan, showing a 65% detection rate and management changes in almost half the patients, with more metastatic regions than Octreoscan in 17 patients. Namely, the CUP-NETs was localized by [18F]FDOPA PET/CT in 12/27 (overall sensitivity, 44%; 52% in patients evaluated at initial diagnosis), leading to tumor resection in all cases; patients with positive [18F]FDOPA PET/CT had higher serum chromogranin A, serotonin, or urinary 5-hydroxyindolacetic acid. Two false-negative results were related to poorly differentiated duodenal and prostatic NETs (G3) (Imperiale et al. 2014). Based on these four studies, it was found that [68 Ga]Ga-DOTATATE or [18F]FDOPA PET/CT can detect primary NET at a rate equal to or higher than 44.4%.

Discussion - summary of results

The findings of this systematic review consolidate the evidence that molecular imaging, and in particular PET/CT with [68 Ga]Ga-DOTA-peptides, plays a pivotal role in the diagnostic work-up of undefined or suspected neuroendocrine tumors (NETs). Compared with traditional nuclear medicine modalities, PET/CT consistently demonstrated superior sensitivity and specificity, with a marked clinical impact in terms of diagnostic clarification, patient stratification, and management decisions. Importantly, this review also emphasizes the role of alternative and complementary tracers such as [18F]DOPA, as well as the emerging potential of dual-tracer strategies and novel radiopharmaceuticals.

Nevertheless, interpretation of the available evidence requires careful consideration of methodological limitations. Many studies were retrospective, single-center, or based on small cohorts, and reference standards were often incomplete. In addition, tracer availability and cost-related issues remain significant barriers to widespread adoption, and the integration of imaging results with pathological or biochemical markers is still limited.

To enhance clarity and facilitate interpretation of these findings, we provide below a structured, point-by-point synthesis of the main results of this review. This summary outlines the diagnostic performance of established tracers, compares them with alternative approaches, highlights clinical implications, discusses current limitations, and indicates future research directions.

Conclusion

Molecular imaging, particularly [68 Ga]Ga-DOTA-peptide PET/CT, is a pivotal tool in the diagnostic work-up of CUP-NETs and suspected NETs, offering superior accuracy and clinical utility compared to conventional imaging. Its adoption can significantly impact therapeutic strategies, from surgical planning to PRRT eligibility. However, variations in tracer availability and limited prospective data highlight the need for multicentre studies, standardized protocols, and cost-effectiveness analyses. Integrating advanced PET imaging into routine diagnostic pathways is crucial for improving patient outcomes. Cost-benefit considerations should also guide clinical implementation to ensure equitable access and efficient resource utilization across healthcare systems.
