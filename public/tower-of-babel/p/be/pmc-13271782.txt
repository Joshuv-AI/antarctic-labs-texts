Determinants of hyperinsulinism severity in children with Beckwith-Wiedemann syndrome
Authors: Andrew M George, Aravind Viswanathan, Jonathan H Sussman, Evan R Hathaway, Abigail Casalnova, Salima Nesbitt, Kara E Boodhansingh, Nicole M Stewart, Heather J McKnight, Katherine Lord, Diva D De León, Charles A Stanley, N Scott Adzick, Arupa Ganguly, Jennifer M Kalish
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractContextCongenital hyperinsulinism (HI) is a serious clinical feature of Beckwith-Wiedemann syndrome (BWS) causing severe hypoglycemia. The relationship between BWS genotypes and HI severity is not well understood.ObjectiveInvestigate the relationship between molecular determinants of patients with BWS and HI with measures of HI severity.MethodsThis retrospective cohort study included 85 children from 2009-2024, all patients evaluated at single, tertiary care center. BWS genotype frequency included 41 children with pUPD11, 24 with IC2 LOM, 8 with 11p15 chromosomal anomalies, 6 with GWpUPD, 4 with IC1 GOM, and 2 with CDKN1C. Retrospectively reviewed interventions included maximum glucose infusion rate (max GIR), diazoxide responsiveness, and surgery. Primary outcome was association between BWS genotypes and measures of HI severity. Secondary outcomes included the relationship between pUPD11 length and presence of KATP variants with diazoxide responsiveness and surgical need.ResultsSignificant differences presented among genotypes in max GIR (P = .004), enteral dextrose requirements (P = .029), and pancreatectomy (P = .012). Most patients with IC2 LOM, IC1 GOM or CDKN1C were diazoxide responsive and did not require surgery. Patients with pUPD11 were more likely to be diazoxide unresponsive and to require surgery, especially if pUPD11 length extended into the KATP gene region and if a pathogenic variant in the ABCC8 or KCNJ11 was present.ConclusionPatients with pUPD11 experience more severe HI, while patients with IC2 LOM, IC1 GOM, and CDKN1C exhibit milder disease. Based on our findings, we designed a genetic testing algorithm to guide clinical management.Keywords: Beckwith-Wiedemann syndrome, hyperinsulinism, hypoglycemia, insulin, diazoxide, pancreatectomy

Beckwith-Wiedemann syndrome (BWS) is an overgrowth disorder caused by imprinting defects on chromosome 11p15. The most common imprinting defects in the BWS critical region include loss of methylation at imprinting cluster 2 (IC2 LOM), paternal uniparental isodisomy of chromosome 11p15 (pUPD11), and gain of methylation at imprinting cluster 1 (IC1 GOM) (1, 2). Additional, less common causes include loss-of-function variants in cyclin dependent kinase inhibitor 1C (CDKN1C), genome-wide paternal uniparental isodisomy (GWpUPD), and chromosomal aberrations affecting the 11p15 region such as duplications, deletions, and translocations. BWS typically presents with distinct clinical features such as macroglossia, lateralized overgrowth, hypoglycemia, and an increased risk of embryonal tumors such as Wilms tumor and hepatoblastoma (1). International consensus guidelines published in 2018 reclassified BWS as a clinical spectrum due to the variability in the number and severity of features present among patients (3). These guidelines also introduced a standardized scoring system for BWS that assigns 2 points for cardinal features such as macroglossia, lateralized overgrowth, and omphalocele and 1 point for suggestive features such as ear pits/creases, organomegaly, and facial nevus simplex. Notably, the guidelines differentiated between 2 forms of hypoglycemia: hyperinsulinism, a cardinal feature in which hypoglycemia lasts beyond 1 week after birth and/or requires escalated treatment, and transient neonatal hypoglycemia, a suggestive feature that resolves within 1 week.

Hyperinsulinism (HI) is characterized by dysregulated insulin secretion from the pancreas and is the most common cause of persistent hypoglycemia in infants and children (4-6) Without prompt and effective management, the resulting hypoketotic hypoglycemia may lead to brain damage, seizures, and impaired neurodevelopment (7). Evidence for the diagnosis of HI in the presence of hypoglycemia includes increased glucose requirement, inappropriately low plasma concentrations of beta-hydroxybutyrate and free fatty acids, and a glycemic response to glucagon (8-10)

The immediate goal of HI treatment is restoring and maintaining euglycemia, which typically requires intravenous dextrose administration initially, followed by medical therapeutics, and when that fails, surgical intervention for the most severe cases (11). Diazoxide is considered the first-line therapeutic agent that acts to open the pancreatic beta-cell ATP-sensitive potassium (KATP) channel, thereby reducing insulin secretion. When patients do not respond to diazoxide, this can indicate a more severe form of HI and second-line medical therapies are considered, such as the somatostatin analogue octreotide. In cases where medical therapy proves insufficient to control hypoglycemia, surgical intervention through pancreatectomy may become necessary (12, 13).

HI can be caused by pathogenic variants in a number of genes, including 2 located on chromosome 11p in proximity to the BWS critical region. These 2 genes, ATP-binding cassette subfamily C member 8 (ABCC8) and potassium inwardly rectifying channel subfamily J member 11 (KCNJ11), encode for SUR1 and Kir6.2 subunits respectively of the KATP channel on the pancreatic beta cell and are biallelically expressed (14). However, recessive variants in the paternal copy of either of these genes, coupled with a paternal uniparental disomy event, has been reported in focal HI and BWS-related HI by our group and others, complicating the clinical picture and requiring specialized management (15-18) A large genotype-phenotype correlational study by our center in 2012 found that 97% (145/149) of focal cases of diazoxide-unresponsive HI possessed monoallelic recessive KATP variants on the paternal allele (19).

In 2016, our team reported on the clinical course of 28 patients with BWS and HI, of whom 26 had pUPD11 and 2 had IC2 LOM (16). While HI has been reported in other BWS molecular subtypes (20, 21), severe HI has predominantly been associated with the pUPD11 subtype. Four patients in the cohort with pUPD11 also presented with paternally inherited pathogenic variants in ABCC8 or KCNJ11. Despite the small sample size, we observed that patients with pUPD11 and pathogenic KATP variants experienced more severe HI compared to those without KATP variants. However, there were several severe cases that required pancreatectomy among patients without a KATP variant. Interestingly, the size of the region of loss of heterozygosity (LOH) which correlates to the extent of pUPD11 across chromosome 11, showed no significant association with HI severity. While these findings provided valuable insights into hyperinsulinism severity in BWS, they also highlighted the need for a more comprehensive investigation of specific severity measures in a larger cohort. In the present study, we examine a large cohort of patients with molecularly confirmed BWS and HI. The aim of this study was to investigate the relationship between molecular determinants, such as BWS genotype and presence of HI-related variants, and clinical measures of HI severity such as glucose requirements, diazoxide responsiveness, and the need for surgery. Based on these findings, we aimed to develop evidence-based genetic testing recommendations to guide clinical decision-making.

Methods

Patient cohort

All patients included in this study received care at the Children's Hospital of Philadelphia Congenital Hyperinsulinism Center. A retrospective chart review was conducted on medical records of patients with molecularly confirmed BWS and a diagnosis of hyperinsulinism between 2009 and 2024. Among all patients with BWS cared for at the Children's Hospital of Philadelphia between 2009 to 2024, only those with a molecularly confirmed diagnosis were eligible for inclusion and patients with only a clinical diagnosis of BWS were excluded. From this molecularly confirmed BWS cohort, patients were included if they also had a diagnosis of HI. The diagnosis of HI was established when hypoglycemia persisted beyond the first week of life and was supported by additional criteria such as increased glucose requirement, inappropriately low plasma concentrations of beta-hydroxybutyrate and free fatty acids, and a glycemic response to glucagon (8-10) Molecular testing via single nucleotide polymorphism (SNP) array and chromosome 11p15 methylation analysis was performed on various tissue types including blood, skin, pancreas, and/or liver (22). Each patient's molecular subtype was classified as IC2 LOM, pUPD11, IC1 GOM, CDKN1C, GWpUPD, or an 11p15 chromosomal anomaly (Table 1). Patients with 11p15 chromosomal anomalies had either a deletion, duplication, partial trisomy, or translocation affecting the 11p15 region. Methylation levels at IC1 and IC2 were assessed using allele-specific methylated multiplex quantitative real-time PCR, with the proportion of cells with exhibiting altered methylation calculated as previously described (22, 23). Methylation testing results on blood were reviewed, and the percentage of mosaicism was calculated using established methodology (22) (Table S1 (24)). Molecular testing for hyperinsulinism focused on Sanger sequencing of ABCC8, KCNJ11, and GCK which accounted for all pathogenic variants identified in this cohort, despite expansion of additional genes to hyperinsulinism panels as the study period progressed. BWS clinical scores were calculated for each patient according to the BWS international consensus guidelines (3). BWS cardinal and suggestive features are also listed in Table S2 (24). Continuous enteral dextrose support via gastrostomy tube to maintain euglycemia was administered alone or in combination with other therapies to allow for at-home management in patients who could not be weaned from glucose support in the hospital, and the total number of patients was captured for analysis (Table 2). Diazoxide responsiveness was defined as the ability to maintain plasma glucose concentrations >70 mg/dL (3.9 mmol/L), after an adequate dose of diazoxide (ranging from 5-15 mg/kg/day depending on the patient's age and divided into 2 doses), for at least 12 hours of fasting and/or generate appropriate hyperketonemia (plasma beta-hydroxybutyrate >1.8 mmol/L) before development of plasma glucose <50-60 mg/dL (2.8-3.3 mmol/L) (11). Chlorothiazide, a diuretic, was commonly used concurrently with diazoxide to mitigate side effects such as fluid retention (25, 26). All clinical data were abstracted from medical charts in adherence with institutional review board (IRB) 19-016549, IRB 13-010658, IRB 08-006216, and IRB 07-005772.

Statistical analysis

Statistical analyses were performed using R v4.4.2. Associations between the BWS molecular subtype and continuous variables including maximum glucose infusion rate (GIR), percentage requiring enteral dextrose, frequency of pancreatectomy, BWS clinical score, and blood mosaicism percentage were examined using Kruskal-Wallis or chi-square tests. Potential outliers were assessed using Grubbs’ test and the interquartile range (Tukey) method. All extreme values were verified against source records.

Logistic regression analysis was conducted to examine factors influencing diazoxide responsiveness and the need for pancreatectomy in this patient population. Firth's logistic regression, which applies penalized maximum likelihood estimation to mitigate bias in rare disease data and unbalanced sample distributions, was used to model likelihood of diazoxide responsiveness and the need for pancreatectomy. The stratification of pUPD11 was made to compare the effects of each molecular determinant on specific outcomes of hyperinsulinism severity. Patients in the IC2 LOM group were chosen as the reference group, due to the sizeable cohort and their predominantly diazoxide-responsive nature, with some variation. SNP arrays, performed at our institution, were analyzed to confirm the length of heterozygosity loss in each patient with pUPD11. Prespecified exclusion criteria comprised the exclusion of patients with a diagnosis of 11p15 chromosomal anomaly and GWpUPD from this regression analysis due to the increased complexity of these subtypes, which made meaningful comparisons challenging. For the diazoxide responsiveness analysis, patients who had not undergone a diazoxide trial or had their treatment with diazoxide discontinued due to side effects, were excluded.

Results

A total of 85 children with molecularly confirmed BWS and a diagnosis of hyperinsulinism were reviewed in the study. The cohort comprised 40 male children (47.1%) and 45 female children (52.9%). The most common molecular subtype was pUPD11, identified in 41 patients (48.2%), followed by IC2 LOM in 24 patients (28.2%). The cohort also included 8 patients (9.4%) with 11p15 chromosomal anomalies, 6 (7.0%) with GWpUPD, 4 (4.7%) with IC1 GOM, and 2 (2.4%) with loss-of-function variants in CDKN1C (Table 1). Clinical characteristics of the cohort by molecular subtype are summarized in Table S3 (24).

Statistical analysis revealed significant differences between molecular subtypes in key measures of hyperinsulinism severity, including maximum GIR (P = .004), percentage of patients requiring continuous enteral dextrose support alone or in combination with other therapies (P = .029), and frequency of pancreatectomy (P = .012) (Table 2). Additionally, the percentage of mosaicism in blood varied significantly across molecular subtypes (P < .001). These data suggest that the clinical course and management needs of patients with BWS varies depending on their molecular subtype.

A total of 12 patients in the cohort had HI-related pathogenic variants (Table S4 (24)). Pathogenic variants in ABCC8 or KCNJ11 were identified in 10 patients with pUPD11. One patient with IC2 LOM had a mosaic pathogenic variant in glucokinase (GCK), which was likely the primary contributory mechanism of the patient's HI (27). SNP array analysis of 39 patients with pUPD11 revealed that 30 patients had LOH extending into the ABCC8/KCNJ11 region, while in 9 patients, the LOH did not encompass this region. SNP array results were unavailable for 2 patients with pUPD11, so they were excluded from the logistic regression analysis. All 10 ABCC8 or KCNJ11 variants were found within the group of 30 patients with pUPD11 whose LOH encompassed the ABCC8/KCNJ11 region.

None of the patients with pUPD11-LOH/KATP were responsive to diazoxide treatment (Table 3). Similarly, patients with pUPD11-LOH had reduced odds of response (odds ratio [OR] = 0.087, P = .002), compared to patients with IC2 LOM, with only 4 of 16 patients showing response to treatment. In the pUPD11 group, response rates were slightly higher, with 2 of 7 patients responding to diazoxide, though the odds remained significantly lower than those observed in IC2 LOM (OR = 0.127, P = .025). These findings suggest a consistent pattern of a lack of responsiveness to diazoxide therapy across the pUPD11 spectrum. In contrast, patients with IC2 LOM were mostly responsive to diazoxide with 12 out of 15 patients responding positively. The IC1 GOM and CDKN1C subtypes all showed responsiveness (100%) to diazoxide treatment although sample sizes of these subtypes were underpowered at 4 and 2 patients, respectively (IC1 GOM: OR = 2.52, P = .53; CDKN1C: OR = 0.84, P = .92). A visual representation of the HI therapeutic implications of BWS-related genetic testing is included in Fig. S1 (24).The extent of pancreatic tissue resected for each patient and their postoperative treatments are reported in Table S5 (24). A total of 9 patients underwent near-total (≥95%) pancreatomy. All patients in the pUPD11-LOH/KATP group underwent pancreatectomy (OR = 189, P < .001). Among patients with pUPD11-LOH, 7 out of 20 required pancreatectomy with significantly higher odds compared to IC2 LOM (OR = 5.00, P = .033). Only 2 of 9 patients in the pUPD11 group underwent pancreatectomy, and this association was not statistically significant (OR = 3.00, P = .27). Only 2 patients with IC2 LOM required pancreatectomy and there were no patients in the IC1 GOM and CDKN1C group who underwent pancreatectomy (IC1 GOM: OR = 1, P = 1; CDKN1C: OR = 1.80, P = .74). While sample size may limit generalizability, these results provide insight into potential genotype-specific responsiveness that could guide future therapeutic management. Sensitivity and specificity data of both regression analyses are reported in Fig. S2 (24) and Table S6 (24).

Discussion

This expanded cohort of patients with BWS and HI reveals a broader spectrum of severity of HI across the BWS molecular subtypes than previously recognized. Our study finds that patients with BWS due to IC2 LOM, IC1 GOM, and CDKN1C in addition to pUPD11 can experience HI that may require interventions. However, these patients generally have a milder phenotype than those with pUPD11 or GWpUPD, showing more responsiveness to diazoxide treatment and lower likelihood to require surgery. The presence of pathogenic HI-related variants was found to likely exacerbate the clinical course, leading to more severe HI. The HI-related phenotype was assessed through differences found between molecular subgroups regarding specific measures of HI severity including maximum GIR, diazoxide responsiveness, and rate of pancreatectomy. Significant differences in percentage of mosaicism across molecular subtypes also suggest that mosaicism may contribute to phenotypic variability, which has been more broadly reported in patients with IC2 LOM (28). Overall, our findings offer important insights into medication responsiveness and disease severity while highlighting how comprehensive genetic testing can inform prognostic and treatment approaches.

In contrast to our previous report in 2016 (16), we identified cases of severe HI in molecular subtypes beyond pUPD11. Notably, 3 patients with IC2 LOM showed diazoxide unresponsiveness, 2 of whom ultimately required pancreatectomy. The presence of patients with IC1 GOM and CDKN1C variants with HI in this cohort also supports the idea that HI can present in all subtypes of BWS, albeit with a lower frequency compared to pUPD11 cases, and manifest with varying degrees of severity. Patients with GWpUPD, which is characterized by the combined presence of a normal cell line and additional cell line with 2 paternal copies of all chromosomes in mosaic percentages (29), demonstrated a severe HI phenotype with the highest group GIR requirements, and 5 out of 6 patients were unresponsive to diazoxide treatment. One notable case of GWpUPD previously described by our group in 2013 was found to be diazoxide unresponsive requiring a max GIR of 30 mg/kg/min and underwent a near-total (95%) pancreatectomy at 2 months of age (30). Despite removal of nearly her entire pancreas, she continued to require continuous dextrose management via gastrostomy tube and was later treated with the short acting somatostatin analogue octreotide. Even 13 years post-surgery, she continues to require medical therapy via lanreotide, a long-acting somatostatin analogue, to maintain glycemic control, thus highlighting the profound dysregulated insulin production characteristic of this molecular subtype. Given the near-total pancreatectomy, she also requires pancreatic enzyme replacement therapy for exocrine pancreatic insufficiency.

The results of regression analysis suggest that there are hierarchical relationships between genetic factors and treatment outcomes in patients with pUPD11. Patients carrying both the extended pUPD-LOH and KATP variants had the most severe clinical course, as they exhibited the lowest odds of diazoxide response and highest likelihood of requiring pancreatectomy. Patients with pUPD11-LOH also showed significantly reduced rates of diazoxide response and increased need for surgical intervention, but their HI was less severe than those in the pUPD11-LOH/KATP group. Patients with pUPD11 alone, not including the KATP region, showed the mildest presentation among these 3 subgroups, but a few patients in this group still exhibited diazoxide unresponsiveness and required surgery. These results suggest the baseline importance of pUPD11 limited to the BWS critical region of 11p15.5 in driving HI but with additive effect when the pUPD11-LOH extends through the KATP gene region on 11p15.1, potentially due to additional regulatory elements in this region. We and others have previously demonstrated the broader effect of deletions in the BWS region due to chromatin looping and regulatory regions around the BWS critical region (31, 32). These findings offer important insights for the clinical management of patients with pUPD11 and highlight the need for accurate molecular characterization.

Among the patients with IC2 LOM, who typically exhibited a mild course of HI showing good response to diazoxide, we identified a notable case that challenged the expected pattern. This patient was previously reported as part of a case series published by our group and was unresponsive to diazoxide and required pancreatectomy (27). Next-generation sequencing performed on the resected pancreas specimen showed a mosaic likely pathogenic variant in GCK likely explaining the increased severity of their HI (27). This particular case along with our broader findings highlight the need for more comprehensive genetic testing particularly in patients with BWS, even in molecular subtypes that are typically associated with milder disease.

We recommend that patients with a diagnosis of HI who exhibit at least one additional clinical feature of BWS undergo both a multigene hyperinsulinism panel and BWS methylation testing designed to account for tissue mosaicism (Fig. 2). Since patients with BWS may test negative for methylation abnormalities in blood (2, 3, 28), additional tissue testing must be considered and may include skin biopsies, tongue tissue from tongue reduction surgery, and/or normal adjacent pancreatic tissue from pancreatectomy. Additionally, for patients testing positive for pUPD11 in blood or other tissue, we recommend SNP array analysis to determine the extent of pUPD11. This genetic testing algorithm serves to improve diagnostic resolution and acts as a critical tool in tailoring patient-specific treatment strategies.

Beckwith-Wiedemann syndrome-HI genetic testing algorithm. This algorithm was developed based on the BWS-HI cohort to facilitate testing for HI genes, BWS methylation testing, and SNP array testing as indicated.

In our previous report, we hypothesized that the mechanism of BWS-related pancreatic overgrowth in HI may involve upregulation of insulin-like growth factor 2 (IGF2) at IC1 and loss of CDKN1C expression at IC2 (16). This speculation was in line with the fact that the majority of the 2016 cohort were patients with the pUPD11 subtype, thus affecting both IC1 and IC2 regions. On a cellular level, this may be due to increased mass and/or increased proliferation of BWS-affected pancreatic beta cells which in turn likely contributes to dysregulated insulin secretion. In the current report, we observed a sizeable number of patients with IC2 LOM, IC1 GOM, and CDKN1C variants with HI which suggests that isolated involvement of one instead of both BWS imprinting regions may suffice to contribute to HI development, though perhaps with a milder phenotype than seen in pUPD11.

Additionally, there is emerging discussion regarding the role of potassium voltage-gated channel subfamily Q member 1 (KCNQ1) in pancreatic beta-cell electrophysiology and its contribution to insulin secretion. Loss-of-function variants in KCNQ1, which encodes for Kv7.1, a voltage-gated potassium channel, have been associated with long QT syndrome (LQTS) (33). A review by Lubberding et al discusses the potential relationship between KCNQ1 and hyperinsulinemia in addition to LQTS, suggesting a shared pathophysiological mechanism involving impaired repolarization of the pancreatic beta-cell membrane (34). KCNQ1 is expressed on both cardiomyocytes and pancreatic beta cells, and deficient Kv7.1 activity may impair membrane repolarization leading to excessive calcium influx and inappropriate insulin release. In a 2014 study, Torekov et al reported on the occurrence of postprandial hypoglycemia in patients with LQTS and KCNQ1-inactivating mutations following oral glucose load (35). KCNQ1 is also an imprinted gene that is maternally expressed in utero and biallelically expressed postnatally (36, 37).

It is also important to note the possibility that for some patients with BWS and HI, a pathogenic HI-related variant, including mutations of intronic regions of KATP subunits, may be present in a mosaic form and remain undetected by available testing. This was nearly the case with the patient with IC2 LOM and mosaic pathogenic GCK variant (Table S4 (24)) in which case the GCK variant was likely the primary contributor of the patient's HI. The underlying mechanisms by which BWS-related alterations and deficient expression of KCNQ1 may contribute to HI pathophysiology warrant deeper exploration.

One limitation of this study includes its retrospective nature, in that genetic testing has evolved over the 15-year study period (38). However, although genetic testing platforms have expanded the number of genes suggestive of causing hyperinsulinism over the duration of the study period, diagnostic evaluation for hyperinsulinism consistently focused on ABCC8, KCNJ11, and GCK which accounted for all pathogenic variants identified in this cohort. We also lack long-term follow-up data on some of these patients who were lost to follow-up once medically stable to return home. Long-term outcomes regarding total length of treatment and neurodevelopmental findings, however, are important areas for future investigation. Future research directions may also include conducting prospective studies and multicenter cohort studies to improve generalizability, capturing a broader spectrum of clinical practice patterns Additionally, further studies should assess long-term outcomes in patients with BWS who have undergone pancreatectomy and have developed exocrine pancreatic insufficiency, which has been more broadly reported in the past (39).

Conclusion

The BWS cardinal feature of HI can present in all molecular subtypes of BWS with varying degrees of severity and can involve more than one biochemical mechanism with the common endpoint of dysfunctional pancreatic KATP channels. Patients with pUPD11 tend to experience the most severe HI, and those with heterozygosity loss extending into the ABCC8/KCNJ11 gene region and/or coexisting pathogenic KATP variants are more likely to be unresponsive to diazoxide treatment. However, patients with IC2 LOM, IC1 GOM, and CDKN1C exhibit milder disease and a more positive response to diazoxide therapy. Comprehensive genetic testing for BWS-related alterations and single-gene HI variants is therefore important for guiding clinical management and understanding the underlying mechanism of HI.
