Effects of surface treatment methods on the color stability, whiteness, and surface roughness of bleach-shade composite resins
Authors: İlayda Kutlu, Muhammet Fi̇dan
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundDespite the increasing use of bleach-shade composite resins in recent years, studies examining the optical properties of these materials remain limited. Furthermore, there are insufficient data on the effectiveness of surface treatments for removing stains from these materials. This study evaluated the effects of different surface treatment protocols on the color stability, whiteness index, and surface roughness of bleach-shade composite resins after coffee immersion.MethodsA total of 300 specimens were prepared from four bleach-shade composite resins (Estelite Asteria BL, Brilliant EverGlow BL Translucent, G-aenial A’chord BW, and Opallis E-Bleach L) and one multi-shade (Filtek Z250 A2) composite resin (n = 60 per material). The specimens were randomly allocated to six groups (n = 10): Group 1 (one-step polishing, OptraGloss), Group 2 (two-step polishing, Nova Twist), Group 3 (bleaching, Whiteness HP), Group 4 (bleaching + one-step polishing), Group 5 (bleaching + two-step polishing), and Group 6 (control). All specimens were immersed in a coffee solution for 12 days. Measurements (color and roughness [Ra]) were performed at baseline (t0), after staining (t1), and after the surface treatments (t2). Color differences were calculated using the CIEDE2000 formula (∆E00), and whiteness index (WID) values were determined using the CIELAB-based WID formula. Statistical analyses were performed using the Kruskal–Wallis, Dunn, and robust ANOVA tests (p < 0.05).ResultsAt the t1 time point, all specimens exhibited clinically unacceptable ΔE00 values and decreased WID values. The Asteria group exhibited the highest color stability at t1, while the Brilliant and A’chord groups exhibited the highest WID values (p < 0.05). At the t2 time point, while Ra increased in the bleaching groups, the lowest Ra values were found in the one-step polishing group (p < 0.05).ConclusionsCoffee exposure adversely affected the optical and surface properties of bleach-shade composite resins. Bleaching alone showed limited effectiveness, whereas polishing improved the esthetic outcomes and maintained clinically acceptable surface characteristics. Material-dependent differences were observed among the tested composites.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at https://doi.org/10.1186/s12903-026-09186-6.

Background

Increased esthetic expectations have driven interest in tooth bleaching and anterior restorative applications [1]. Bleach-shade composite resins (CRs) have been developed to match the lighter appearance of bleached teeth [2]. The clinical success of these restorations depends largely on their ability to maintain color and surface quality over time [3–5]. Nevertheless, discoloration remains a concern because both material-related factors and exposure to staining substances can affect their appearance [6, 7]. Dietary habits are among the most important external factors influencing the optical and physical properties of CRs [8]. Coffee is among the beverages most frequently associated with staining because its chromogenic compounds can penetrate the resin matrix and produce visible color changes [9–11].

Surface roughness (Ra) is an important determinant of the esthetic, biological, and functional performance of CR restorations [12, 13]. Rough surfaces promote plaque accumulation, staining, and marginal deterioration, which may compromise restoration longevity [14–16]. Consequently, achieving an adequately polished surface is essential for long-term clinical success [17, 18]. Material characteristics and polishing procedures both influence surface quality [19]. Nanohybrid CRs are often preferred because their smaller filler particles contribute to smoother surfaces and improved gloss retention [20, 21].

Because replacement of discolored restorations increases treatment costs and may require additional removal of tooth structure, conservative approaches, such as polishing and bleaching, are often preferred [1]. Their effectiveness depends on the type of stain, the material characteristics, and the polishing system used [22, 23]. However, information regarding the response of bleach-shade CRs to these procedures remains limited. Simplified one-step and two-step polishing systems have become popular because they reduce clinical time while providing satisfactory surface quality [24–26]. Among these systems, diamond-impregnated polishers have been found to be associated with lower surface roughness, potentially influencing the optical appearance of restorations [25, 27]. Therefore, diamond-impregnated spiral polishing disks were selected in the present study, although their performance on bleach-shade CRs has not been adequately investigated [28].

Peroxide-based bleaching agents can alter the polymer network of CR materials through oxidative reactions [11, 29]. Although effective in removing extrinsic stains, they may also increase surface roughness and affect optical properties [22, 30]. Such effects may be more evident in previously stained CRs because preexisting structural changes can enhance material degradation [29].

Whiteness is an important optical parameter in esthetic dentistry that is commonly used to assess the outcomes of bleaching and stain removal procedures [31, 32]. In clinical research, whiteness is frequently quantified using the Whiteness Index for Dentistry (WID), a Commission Internationale de l’Éclairage Lab (CIELAB)-based metric that correlates well with visual perception [11, 31, 33]. Previous studies have shown that bleaching agents can influence the whiteness of restorative materials and consequently affect their esthetic integration [11, 34, 35]. However, the effect of post-bleaching surface treatments on the whiteness of bleach-shade CRs remains unclear.

Although bleach-shade CRs are increasingly being used in esthetic dentistry, evidence regarding their optical performance remains limited. In particular, data on their color stability after exposure to common staining agents, changes in whiteness-related parameters, and the ability of bleaching and polishing procedures to restore their original appearance are scarce. Furthermore, comparative data evaluating the effectiveness of different polishing systems in conjunction with bleaching procedures on the optical restoration of these materials are insufficient. Therefore, this study aimed to evaluate the effects of different surface treatment protocols on the color stability, whiteness index, and surface roughness of bleach-shade CRs after coffee staining. The null hypothesis was that the applied surface treatment protocols would not significantly influence the color stability, whiteness index, or surface roughness of bleach-shade CRs after coffee immersion.

Methods

This study received ethical approval from the Usak University Non-Interventional Clinical Research Ethics Committee (Decision No. 465-465-12).

Sample size

Sample size estimation was performed using G*Power software (version 3.1.9.4; Kiel, Germany). Assuming an α level of 0.05, a power of 90%, and an effect size of 0.4 for two-way ANOVA, the minimum sample requirement was 162 specimens [36]. To preserve balanced group sizes and compensate for potential losses, the final study design included 300 specimens, corresponding to 10 specimens per group.

Specimen preparation

Five CRs were evaluated: four bleach-shade materials [Estelite Asteria–Bleach Light (Tokuyama, Japan), Brilliant EverGlow–Bleach Translucent(Coltene/Whaledent, Switzerland), G-aenial A’chord–Bleach White (GC, Japan) and Opallis–Enamel Bleach Light (FGM, Brazil)] and one conventional multi-shade material [Filtek Z250–A2 (3 M, USA)]. Filtek Z250 was included as a reference material representing a widely used conventional CR to allow comparison between bleach-shade and conventional-shade CRs. It has been widely used in previous studies to evaluate the color stability and surface properties of CRs [14, 37–39]. The A2 shade was selected because it is one of the most commonly used conventional enamel shades in clinical practice [40], allowing a clinically relevant comparison with bleach-shade materials. A total of 300 disk-shaped specimens were prepared, with 60 specimens per material group. The specimens were fabricated using Teflon molds (8 mm diameter × 2 mm thickness), in accordance with previous studies [41, 42]. A Mylar strip and glass slide were used to obtain flat specimen surfaces before light curing for 20 s using a light-emitting diode (LED) curing unit (Elipar S10; 3 M ESPE, St. Paul, USA; 1200 mW/cm²). As the curing tip’s diameter (10 mm) exceeded the specimen diameter (8 mm), the entire specimen surface was covered during curing; therefore, no overlapping irradiation was required. The output intensity of the LED curing unit was monitored using a radiometer (3 M ESPE, St. Paul, USA) after polymerization of each group. The device was recharged whenever a reduction in light intensity was detected to maintain standardized curing conditions [43]. A single operator performed all specimen preparation and polishing procedures to ensure standardization and minimize operator-related variability [41].

Finishing and polishing were carried out using the Zenit Flex Pop-On disk system (President Dental, Germany; Table 2) in four sequential steps from coarse to fine grit. Each disk was applied for 20 s at 10,000 rpm under dry conditions [13]. Between steps, the specimens were rinsed with water for 10 s and air-dried for 5 s [9]. Specimen thickness was verified using a digital caliper (Insize Co., China) and standardized at 2.0 ± 0.1 mm [19]. This thickness was selected because the optical properties of CRs are influenced by specimen thickness due to differences in light transmission and scattering. Furthermore, 2.0 mm is widely used in spectrophotometric studies and is considered clinically relevant for evaluating the color stability and translucency of resin-based CRs [41, 44]. After polymerization, the specimens were stored in distilled water at 37 °C for 24 h before coffee immersion [18, 23]. Figure 1 illustrates the study workflow.

Staining protocol

To prepare the staining solution, 3.6 g of coffee was dissolved in 300 mL of boiling distilled water and stirred for 10 min [42]. A previous study reported that 24 h of immersion corresponds to approximately one month of coffee consumption [45]. Therefore, the specimens were immersed in the coffee solution at 37 °C for 12 d to simulate approximately one year of coffee exposure [46]. The solution was renewed daily to prevent contamination [42]. After staining, the CR specimens were gently brushed with a soft toothbrush under running water for 30 s to remove residual coffee deposits and then air-dried [7].

Color measurements and color differences

Color measurements were performed at three time points: before the coffee staining (t0), after the coffee staining (t1), and after the surface treatments (t2), using a spectrophotometer (VITA Easyshade V; VITA Zahnfabrik, Germany) [47]. The color coordinates were recorded against a standardized white background (L* = 96, a* = 0, b* = +1; GC3, 023118, DANES-PICTA, Czech Republic) [48]. The measurements obtained on the white background were used to calculate the color differences (ΔE00). The use of a standardized background minimizes the influence of background reflectance on color measurements and the optical assessment of CRs under different clinical conditions [40]. During the measurements, the spectrophotometer tip was positioned perpendicular to the specimen surface. The device was calibrated according to the manufacturer’s instructions before each measurement session. Three readings were obtained for each specimen, and the mean value was used for the analysis. Color changes were evaluated for the t0–t1 and t1–t2 intervals, and color differences were calculated using the CIEDE2000 formula [35]:\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\begin{aligned}\varDelta{E}_{00}\left(1:1:1\right)=&\left[\left(\frac{\varDelta{L}'}{K_{L}S_{L}}\right)^2+\left(\frac{\varDelta{C}'}{K_{C}S_{C}}\right)^2+\left(\frac{\varDelta{H}'}{K_{H}S_{H}}\right)^2\right. \\& \left.+R_{T}\left(\frac{\varDelta{C}'}{K_{C}S_{C}}\right)\left(\frac{\varDelta{H}'}{K_{H}S_{H}}\right)\right]^\frac{1}{2}\end{aligned}$$\end{document}

In the CIEDE2000 equation, ΔL′, ΔC′, and ΔH′ denote differences in lightness, chroma, and hue, respectively. SL, SC, and SH represent the weighting functions, whereas KL, KC, and KH correspond to the parametric factors, all of which were set to 1. The rotation term (RT) was included to account for interactions in the blue region of the color space. Color differences were interpreted using the 50% perceptibility (PT = 0.8) and acceptability (AT = 1.8) thresholds [49].

Whiteness index and whiteness differences

The WID values were determined at t0, t1, and t2 using a spectrophotometer (VITA Easyshade V; VITA Zahnfabrik, Germany) on a standardized black background (L* = 8, a* = +1, b* = −1; GC3, 023118, DANES-PICTA, Prague, Czech Republic) [48]. The WID values were calculated from the measurements obtained on the black background. The use of a standardized black background minimizes the influence of background reflectance on whiteness measurements and the optical assessment of CRs under different clinical conditions [40]. Three measurements were obtained for each specimen, and the mean L*, a*, and b* values were used for the WID calculations. The whiteness difference (ΔWID) was defined as the change in WID between two time points. Changes observed during the t1–t2 interval were evaluated because this interval reflects the effect of surface treatments on previously stained materials. The ΔWID values were interpreted using the 50% whiteness perceptibility threshold (WPT = 0.72) and the 50% whiteness acceptability threshold (WAT = 2.60) [31].

Surface roughness and surface roughness change

Surface roughness was measured using a profilometer (DragonDR230, Microtest, Turkey). The device was calibrated according to the manufacturer’s instructions before each measurement. The specimens were positioned with their surfaces perpendicular to the profilometer tip and analyzed using a cut-off length of 0.25 mm and a scanning length of 4.0 mm. Five measurements were obtained in different directions for each specimen, and the mean Ra value (µm) was calculated. The Ra values were recorded at t0, t1, and t2, and the surface roughness changes (ΔRa) were calculated for the t0–t1 and t1–t2 intervals. According to previous studies, Ra values above 0.2 μm may promote plaque accumulation [50], whereas values exceeding 0.3 μm can be perceived by patients through tactile sensation [51].

Scanning electron microscope analysis

The surface morphology was evaluated using scanning electron microscopy (SEM) with a Phenom ProX microscope (Phenom, Netherlands). One randomly selected specimen from each subgroup was examined at t0 and t2. Prior to imaging, the specimens were sputter-coated with gold. Micrographs were obtained at ×1000 and ×3000 magnifications using an accelerating voltage of 4.8–20.5 kV [9].

Statistical analysis

The statistical analyses were performed using R software (version 4.4.1) and Jamovi (version 2.3.28; Jamovi Project, USA). The ΔE00, ΔRa, and ΔWID values for the t0–t1 interval were analyzed using R. Data normality was assessed with the Kolmogorov–Smirnov test. As the data were not normally distributed, intergroup comparisons were performed using the Kruskal–Wallis H test, followed by Dunn’s post hoc test.

The Ra and WID values at t0, t1, and t2, as well as ΔE00, ΔRa, and ΔWID values for the t1–t2 interval, were analyzed using Jamovi. The statistical model included material type, surface treatment, and their interaction. Because normality assumptions were not met, a robust ANOVA was applied. Quantitative data are presented as median (minimum–maximum) values. Statistical significance was set at p < 0.05.

Results

Color change

A statistically significant difference in median ∆E00 values was observed among the materials during the t0–t1 interval (p < 0.001). Asteria showed the lowest median ∆E00 value, whereas Brilliant showed the highest. All materials exceeded both the PT and AT values (Supplemental Table 1).

During the t1–t2 interval, material type, surface treatment, and their interaction significantly affected ∆E00 values (p < 0.001; Table 3). Asteria group exhibited the lowest median ∆E00 values, whereas A’chord group exhibited the highest (Table 4). Regarding surface treatment, the control group showed the lowest median ∆E00 values, while the bleaching + two-step polishing group showed the highest. For the material × surface treatment interaction, the highest median ∆E00 value was observed in the Brilliant × bleaching + two-step polishing group, whereas the lowest was recorded in the Filtek Z250 × control group (Table 4). At t0, differences in filler particle size were reflected in the surface topography, while overall surface integrity was maintained in all materials (Fig. 6). At t2, surface morphology varied according to the treatment applied. More pronounced polishing scratches were generally observed in the two-step polishing groups than in the one-step polishing groups. Despite these differences, the resin matrix–filler interface appeared intact in all materials. Microporous formations around filler particles were observed after both polishing procedures, particularly in the Asteria and Brilliant groups (Fig. 7). After bleaching, surface irregularities, resin matrix loss, and topographical alterations were observed in all groups, with more pronounced changes in the Brilliant group (Fig. 7). Limited microporosity was observed after bleaching, whereas cracks and crater-like defects were not detected in the Opallis (Fig. 8) and Filtek Z250 (Fig. 9) groups, which showed better preservation of surface integrity.

Polishing after bleaching reduced surface microporosity and microstructural alterations, although polishing scratches became more evident, particularly in the Asteria group (Fig. 7).

In the control groups, coffee immersion was associated with small pore formations, deeper polishing scratches, filler particle loss, and irregular dark surface areas. These changes were particularly evident in the Asteria control group (Fig. 7), where surface homogeneity decreased and the matrix–filler boundaries became more distinct.

Discussion

The applied surface treatment protocols significantly influenced the color stability, whiteness index, and surface roughness of the bleach-shade CRs after coffee immersion. Therefore, the null hypothesis was rejected.

The color changes observed in the bleach-shade CRs were consistent with previous reports of significant discoloration after prolonged coffee exposure [1, 2, 46]. Similar findings were observed for Filtek Z250, whose ΔE00 values exceeded clinically acceptable limits after coffee immersion [19, 27]. These color changes exceeded clinically acceptable limits, indicating that coffee-induced discoloration may adversely affect the long-term esthetic performance of restorations.

Color stability is largely influenced by resin matrix composition and filler characteristics [9, 16]. Hydrophilic monomers, such as Bis-GMA and TEGDMA, increase water sorption and staining susceptibility, whereas hydrophobic monomers, including UDMA, Bis-EMA, and Bis-MPEPP, tend to improve color stability [8, 52]. In this study, Estelite Asteria (BL) exhibited the lowest median ΔE00 value (5.52), which may be attributed to its UDMA-based matrix and homogeneous spherical filler structure, which limits pigment retention [8, 21, 27]. In addition, its low-viscosity Bis-MPEPP monomer reduces the need for TEGDMA, thereby decreasing water sorption [52]. The enhanced color stability of Asteria may also be related to its rapid amplified photopolymerization system, which uses a reduced camphorquinone content and promotes a more stable polymer network [8]. Although Bis-GMA-free CRs are generally reported to exhibit improved color stability [47], G-aenial A’chord (BW) showed greater discoloration despite lacking Bis-GMA. This finding suggests that color stability depends on the combined effects of matrix composition, filler characteristics, and polymer network structure, rather than on a single monomer. Brilliant EverGlow (BL Trans) exhibited the highest median ΔE00 value (10.45), possibly due to its heterogeneous barium glass filler structure, absence of UDMA [19], and the microporous nature of barium glass fillers, which may increase water uptake and facilitate pigment diffusion [4]. Filtek Z250 (A2) showed greater color stability than G-aenial A’chord (BW) and Opallis (E-Bleach L), which may be related to its higher filler loading and lower resin matrix content [6]. Overall, filler characteristics, filler loading, and polymerization efficiency appear to play important roles in stain resistance [4, 9].

In this study, bleaching followed by polishing resulted in greater stain removal than bleaching alone. This finding is consistent with previous reports showing that mechanical polishing enhances color recovery, particularly in microhybrid CRs [2]. The two-step spiral disk protocol demonstrated superior stain removal in both microhybrid and nanohybrid CRs [24, 28]. Although the combined treatments generally provided better color recovery than the single procedures [3, 14, 23]; two-step polishing alone produced comparable results in some groups, suggesting that progressive surface smoothing may be sufficient to remove superficial stains [15, 24].

Because color difference formulas may not fully reflect perceived whiteness, WID was also evaluated [33]. The WID interpretation was based on the WPT and WAT thresholds [31]. Excessive positive or negative ΔWID values may compromise esthetic harmony with adjacent teeth, particularly in bleach-shade materials where incomplete whiteness recovery can affect esthetic integration [11]. In-office bleaching of unstained CRs generally results in limited increases in WID [33, 35]. In this study, the WID values decreased after coffee immersion and increased significantly following the surface treatments. Similar reductions after coffee staining have been reported previously [32], whereas increases after bleaching of stained CRs have been mainly associated with increased L* and decreased b* values [34]. A previous study also reported ΔWID values exceeding the WAT after bleaching coffee-stained CRs, indicating a clinically perceptible improvement in whiteness [53]. At baseline (t0) and after the surface treatments (t2), Brilliant EverGlow (BL Trans) and G-aenial A’chord (BW) showed the highest median WID values, whereas Filtek Z250 (A2) showed the lowest values. During the t1–t2 interval, G-aenial A’chord (BW) exhibited the greatest median ΔWID and ΔE00 values, whereas Estelite Asteria (BL) showed the smallest changes in both parameters. The parallel behavior of ΔWID and ΔE00 suggests that improvements in color were accompanied by increased whiteness perception, highlighting the importance of evaluating both parameters when assessing esthetic outcomes.

For all stain removal procedures, the ΔE00 and ΔWID values exceeded the corresponding clinical thresholds, indicating visually perceptible changes. However, the t0–t2 color differences remained above clinically acceptable levels, suggesting that complete recovery of the original appearance was not achieved. Furthermore, the persistence of these changes suggests that some coffee-induced discoloration may not be completely reversible [30, 53]. Therefore, long-term consumption of staining beverages may compromise the esthetic longevity of bleach-shade CRs.

Surface smoothness achieved through polishing reduces microbial adhesion on CR materials [36, 51]. After standardization with the Zenit Flex disk system, the median Ra values remained below the 0.2 μm threshold in all groups. No significant differences were observed among the materials, indicating that the aluminum oxide–impregnated disks produced comparable surface quality regardless of CR composition [24, 50]. Similar results have been reported for the Sof-Lex polishing system [15, 36]. Coffee, with an acidic pH of approximately 5.0 and containing organic acids, may increase surface roughness by promoting degradation of the resin matrix [5, 18]. Although previous studies have reported significant increases in Ra after prolonged coffee exposure [7], a significant increase was observed only in Estelite Asteria in this study. Although CRs with higher filler loading are generally expected to exhibit lower surface roughness [26], Estelite Asteria showed the highest median Ra value after coffee immersion, despite its high filler content (82%). This finding suggests that factors other than filler loading contribute to surface behavior. Spherical silica/zirconia fillers may be more resistant to wear than the surrounding resin matrix, resulting in filler protrusion and increased surface roughness as the matrix degrades [7, 13].

Ceramic-based polishing systems are effective in smoothing CR surfaces [20, 54]. In the present study, OptraGloss, a diamond-impregnated one-step system designed for ceramics and CRs, produced median Ra values below 0.2 μm for all materials and achieved surface smoothness comparable to multi-step polishing systems [12]. OptraGloss also yielded lower Ra values than Nova Twist, supporting previous reports that ceramic polishing systems may outperform CR-specific polishers [20]. The high hardness and abrasive capacity of diamond particles likely contribute to these favorable outcomes [20, 36]. Nova Twist has been reported to perform similarly to the two-step Clearfil Twist Dia system [25]. The median Ra values of 0.15 μm for OptraGloss and 0.20 μm for Nova Twist confirmed that both diamond-impregnated systems can produce clinically acceptable surfaces [36]. Spiral disks may contribute to this effect by distributing pressure more evenly across the surface, thereby reducing surface damage while facilitating stain removal [25]. Despite its greater abrasive potential, attributed to its high diamond content, Nova Twist may produce microscopic scratches through interactions between diamond particles and the silicone matrix [51]. Additional polishing steps improved color and whiteness recovery but were associated with slightly higher roughness values. Nevertheless, all median Ra values remained below the tactile detection threshold of 0.3 μm and at or below the commonly accepted clinical threshold of 0.2 μm.

The effect of polishing after bleaching on surface smoothness remains controversial. While one study reported that multi-step polishing failed to reverse bleaching-induced roughness increases in coffee-stained CRs [3], another study found that polishing significantly reduced roughness in unstained CRs [14]. In this study, bleaching alone produced a median Ra value of 0.22 μm, whereas subsequent one-step and two-step polishing reduced this value to 0.18 μm and 0.20 μm, respectively. These findings support previous reports that bleaching agents can adversely affect resin matrices [3, 11] and suggest that polishing after bleaching may help preserve surface integrity. The G-aenial A’chord × one-step polishing and Brilliant EverGlow × one-step polishing combinations produced the lowest median Ra values, highlighting the importance of compatibility between CR materials and polishing systems for achieving optimal surface quality [26, 36]. The surface roughness values observed after stain removal procedures were generally below or close to the clinically relevant threshold of 0.2 μm, with the lowest values observed after polishing. This finding suggests that polishing may substantially mitigate the surface deterioration associated with coffee immersion [18]. Consequently, some statistically significant differences in Ra may have limited clinical relevance because most values remained within thresholds associated with plaque retention and tactile perception.

Although Ra is widely used to evaluate surface roughness, it does not fully represent three-dimensional surface topography. Therefore, profilometric measurements should be complemented by SEM analysis [13, 53]. SEM enables detailed assessment of filler morphology, resin matrix characteristics, and polishing effects on CR surfaces [24]. In the present study, all materials exhibited Ra values below 0.2 μm after initial polishing; however, SEM revealed material-dependent surface features. At t0, Estelite Asteria and Filtek Z250 showed polishing scratches and localized microporosities [24], whereas Brilliant EverGlow exhibited fewer surface scratches [50]. Exposure to acidic media may promote hydrolytic degradation of the resin matrix, resulting in surface deterioration, crack formation, and filler–matrix debonding [9, 55]. Consistent with these findings, in this study, coffee immersion produced duller surfaces, deeper scratches, and microstructural defects. After bleaching, Brilliant EverGlow exhibited the most pronounced cracking and pore formation, whereas Filtek Z250 better preserved surface integrity, showing no cracks or crater-like defects. Its high filler loading may have contributed to greater resistance against chemical degradation [13, 17].

Previous studies have reported minimal surface alterations in unstained CRs after in-office bleaching, with subsequent polishing contributing to biofilm reduction [17]. In contrast, bleaching produced noticeable surface deterioration in the stained CRs evaluated in this study, emphasizing the importance of post-bleaching polishing. An earlier report on Brilliant EverGlow described agglomerated filler detachment and pore formation of approximately 5–10 μm after polishing [51]. In the present study, pores of similar size were observed mainly after bleaching, whereas no agglomerate detachment was detected in the polished groups. Brilliant EverGlow exhibited the most pronounced cracking and pore formation after bleaching, although subsequent polishing reduced these defects and improved surface smoothness. Polishing effectiveness depends on both the polishing system and CR microstructure [24]. Although Nova Twist has been reported to produce minimal scratches and homogeneous surfaces [25], noticeable scratches were observed in some bleach-shade materials, suggesting material-dependent variability. G-aenial A’chord showed the most favorable surface morphology after polishing, with minimal scratching and a homogeneous appearance. Its UDMA-based matrix may have contributed to the lower surface roughness observed after polishing [26]. Overall, the SEM findings indicate that polishing performance is influenced by both the polishing system and the intrinsic properties of the CRs.

This study has several limitations. The in vitro design did not reproduce factors such as salivary activity, pH fluctuations, thermal changes, and mechanical wear that may influence the long-term behavior of CRs. In addition, the use of flat specimens does not fully replicate the geometry of clinical restorations and may affect light reflection, stain accumulation, and polishing outcomes. Therefore, the findings should be interpreted within the limitations of a controlled laboratory setting. Another limitation is that only a limited number of bleach-shade CRs were evaluated. Further studies including a broader range of materials and more clinically relevant aging conditions are needed to confirm the present findings.

Conclusions

Within the limitations of this in vitro study, coffee exposure adversely affected the color stability, whiteness, and surface properties of bleach-shade CRs. Bleaching alone provided limited recovery of color and whiteness and increased surface roughness, whereas polishing procedures improved the esthetic outcomes and maintained clinically acceptable surface characteristics. Overall, the response to staining and surface treatments was material dependent, emphasizing the importance of selecting appropriate CR materials and polishing protocols in clinical practice.
