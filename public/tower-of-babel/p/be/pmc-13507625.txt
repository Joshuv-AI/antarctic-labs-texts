A Multifaceted Interplay Among Hemophagocytosis, Interleukin‐18, and Type I Interferon Distinguishes Still Disease From Other Autoinflammatory Diseases
Authors: Yvonne M. Mueller, Charlotte Girard, Harmen J. G. van de Werken, Stefan J. Erkeland, Dwin G. B. Grashof, Laurie Vaillant, Renske J. H. den Dekker, Iris van Zuijen, Sharon Veenbergen, Fanny Coffin, Eric Bindels, Yvan Jamilloux, Katerina Laskari, Vassili Soumelis, Bruno Fautrel, Cem Gabay, Peter D. Katsikis
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ObjectiveThe unknown pathophysiology and the lack of specific features for systemic juvenile idiopathic arthritis and adult‐onset Still disease (collectively known as Still disease; SD) delay diagnosis and appropriate treatment. The goal of this study was to identify features and mechanisms that distinguish SD from other systemic autoinflammatory diseases (SAID).MethodsUsing the SomaScan assay and RNA sequencing (RNA‐Seq), we determined the plasma proteomes and immune cell microRNA (miRNA) and RNA transcriptomes of 372 patients with SAID, respectively. Proteomic findings were validated by enzyme‐linked immunosorbent assays. SD (n = 72) and non‐SD SAIDs (n = 300) were compared to identify distinguishing features of SD. We performed integrated and unbiased analyses of all data sets using weighted gene correlation network analysis to identify feature modules that characterize SD and stratify patients.ResultsElevated plasma heme oxygenase 1 (HO‐1) and interleukin‐18 (IL‐18) strongly correlate and characterize SD but do not associate with general inflammation. SD was characterized by ferroptosis in plasma, type I interferon (IFN) signaling in monocyte transcriptomes, and elevated natural killer cell miRNA‐146a‐5p, which is an IL‐18 induced miRNA. Finally, we identified feature modules that distinguish SD from other SAIDs and stratified patients with SD into two distinct subgroups not attributable to disease activity or inflammation but hemophagocytosis.ConclusionThis unprecedented large omics data set of SAIDs revealed that complex interactions among hemophagocytosis, IL‐18, and type I IFN signaling characterize SD. Furthermore, two distinct subgroups in patients with SD were distinguished by the degree of hemophagocytic activity. Finally, the large proteomics and RNA‐Seq data sets generated in this study can serve as an invaluable resource for the further investigation of SD and other SAIDs.

INTRODUCTION

Still disease (SD) is a systemic autoinflammatory disease (SAID), a heterogeneous group of rare and debilitating diseases in which patients suffer from recurrent episodes of inflammation usually without evidence of autoimmunity. 1 SD affects children (systemic juvenile idiopathic arthritis [sJIA]) and adults (adult‐onset Still disease [AOSD]) 1 with a prevalence that ranges from 1 patient per 10,000 children for sJIA to 0.15 to 1.5 patients per 1,000,000 adults for AOSD. 2 , 3 , 4 Although the genetic cause of some SAIDs has been identified, 5 60% to 70% of patients with SAIDs lack a genetic cause. 5 Because of SD rarity and the nonspecific nature of its symptoms, patients with AOSD go undiagnosed on average for more than seven years 5 and often undergo rounds of ineffective treatments. A serious complication of SD is macrophage activation syndrome (MAS)/hemophagocytic lymphohistiocytosis (HLH), a life‐threatening syndrome characterized by hemophagocytosis and cytokine storm. 1

The overlapping markers of inflammation and symptoms between SD and other SAIDs has hampered efforts to discover SD‐specific diagnostic biomarkers and uncover its poorly understood pathophysiologic mechanisms. Despite progress in identifying biomarkers for SD, 6 for example the use of hyperferritinemia and acute phase proteins such as C‐reactive protein (CRP) as biomarkers of SD and very high ferritin as a predictor of MAS development, 6 , 7 these are not sufficient to discriminate SD from other SAIDs, and more specific biomarkers are needed. Interleukin 18 (IL‐18) has been associated with SD8 and recently recommended as a SD biomarker, although IL‐18 testing is not always available. 9 SD has been difficult to distinguish on the basis of inflammatory cytokines, even from unrelated non‐autoinflammatory conditions such as sepsis, which explains why CRP is not a specific marker for SD. 10 Other biomarkers may be more subgroup‐specific, such as myeloid‐related protein 8/14 (MRP8/14) in sJIA 11 and S100 calcium‐binding protein A12 (S100A12) and S100A8/A9 in JIA. 12 IL‐18, however, is a cytokine that does seem to provide some discrimination between SD and sepsis. 13 , 14 In SD, IL‐18 and ferritin are elevated, 15 , 16 and IL‐18 levels associates with MAS 17 , 18 and remission. 19 This has led to the suggestion that IL‐18 may be a good biomarker for SD, especially for those patients at risk to develop MAS. 20 Overall, the most common biomarkers recommended for AOSD and sJIA are ferritin, S100 proteins, and IL‐18. 9 In addition to ferritin, heme oxygenase‐1 (HO‐1) has also been associated with SD. Plasma HO‐1 was suggested to be increased in SD by earlier studies, but the low numbers of patients with SD and the lack of comparison with other SAIDs made it unclear how specific HO‐1 is for SD. 21 , 22 , 23 , 24 Currently, however, it remains unclear how IL‐18, HO‐1, and ferritin associate in SD and if these can serve to differentiate SD from other SAIDs.

In this report, we examine 372 patients with SAIDs assembled by the European Union–funded Immunome project consortium for AutoInflammatory Disorders (ImmunAID). We directly compared the plasma proteome, plasma cytokines, and blood monocyte transcriptome of SD with other SAIDs, described potential biomarkers for SD, and uncovered active molecular pathways that could inform better treatment for these patients.

MATERIALS AND METHODS

A detailed description of all materials and methods is provided in the Supplementary Materials and Methods. The following SAIDs were included: AOSD, sJIA, Behçet disease, Cryopyrin‐associated periodic syndrome, chronic nonbacterial osteomyelitis, Familial Mediterranean fever, hyperimmunoglobulin D syndrome, inflammation of unknown origin (IUO), Kawasaki disease, pediatric inflammatory multisystem syndrome, Pyoderma gangrenosum, recurrent pericarditis, Schnitzler syndrome, Sweet syndrome, Takayasu arteritis, and tumor necrosis factor (TNF) receptor associated periodic syndrome (Supplementary Tables 1–3). Healthy controls (HC) were also included. The ImmunAID study was approved by the ethical committees of the included clinical centers and by the Geneva ethical committee. ImmunAID consortium members are listed in Appendix A. All participants signed an informed consent form.

RESULTS

Proteomics discovery analysis

To identify differentially abundant plasma proteins, we performed a proteomics discovery analysis using the SomaScan 7K assay that queries 6,596 unique plasma protein targets. We analyzed 372 patients with SAIDs with active disease collected by the ImmunAID consortium (Supplementary Table 1). Comparing SD (n = 72; n = 19 sJIA, n = 53 AOSD) with all other SAIDs (non‐SD SAIDs: 14 diseases, n = 300) revealed that the top differentially increased proteins were heme oxygenase 1 (HO‐1) (increased 3.8‐fold in SD), ferritin heavy chain (increased 3.2‐fold in SD), and ferritin light chain (increased 3.2‐fold in SD) (Figure 1A and Supplementary Table 4A). Other proteins increased in SD plasma included apolipoprotein L3 (2.2‐fold), phospholipase A2 (NPS‐PLA2) (2.1‐fold), and serum amyloid A‐1 protein (SAA) (1.9‐fold). The most reduced plasma proteins in SD were chondroadherin (0.63‐fold), collagen alpha‐2(XI) chain (0.67‐fold), leucine‐rich repeat‐containing protein 15 (0.67‐fold), and carbonic anhydrase 6 (0.67‐fold).

When comparing SD with HC (n = 65) the most increased plasma proteins were associated with inflammation and included SAA (7.0‐fold), NPS‐PLA2 (4.4‐fold), SAA2 (4.3‐fold), and CRP (3.1‐fold) (Figure 1B, Supplementary Table 4B). The proteins that differed the most between SD and non‐SD SAIDs were also increased in SD compared with HC (HO‐1: 3.7‐fold; ferritin heavy chain: 4.1‐fold; ferritin light chain: 4.2‐fold) (Figure 1B, Supplementary Table 4B). Overall, 66 and 194 proteins were differentially abundant (adjusted P value [Padj] <0.05 and absolute log2 fold change ≥0.5) in SD compared with non‐SD SAIDs and HC, respectively. From these proteomic data it is apparent that as a result of general inflammation, acute phase proteins are up‐regulated in SD, but heme signaling and metabolism most strongly distinguishes SD from non‐SD SAIDs.

HO‐1 and IL‐18 are highly increased in SD but do not correlate with general inflammation

We confirmed increased levels of plasma HO‐1 and ferritin in SD using enzyme‐linked immunosorbent assay (ELISA) for HO‐1 and electrochemiluminescence immunoassay for ferritin; both strongly correlated with SomaScan measurements in SD (r = 0.87 and r = 0.88, respectively) (Supplementary Figure 1A and B). HO‐1 was increased in both AOSD and sJIA (Figure 1C and D; Supplementary Table 5), with 66% of AOSD and 53% of sJIA being above cutoff. Kawasaki disease was 50% above cutoff. Only 7.2% of patients with IUO were above HO‐1 cutoff despite high CRP (Figure 1D, Supplementary Table 6). The increased HO‐1 levels in SD were further confirmed in an independent second cohort of patients comparing active SD (Geneva cohort) (Figure 1E).

Serum hyperferritinemia, although not specific, has aided AOSD diagnosis. 25 Ferritin was elevated in patients with AOSD and sJIA (Figure 1F and 1G, Supplementary Table 5), and 74% of patients with AOSD and 53% of those with sJIA were above cutoff, respectively (Figure 1G, Supplementary Table 6). Ferritin correlated with HO‐1 in SD but not in non‐SD SAIDs (r = 0.73 and r = 0.15, respectively) (Figure 1H and I). Because CRP is often used in the diagnosis of SD, we investigated its relationship to HO‐1. CRP was frequently elevated in all patient groups with SAIDs (Figure 1J). CRP did not correlate with HO‐1 in SD (r = 0.16) (Figure 1K) and non‐SD SAIDs (r = 0.14) (Figure 1L). In contrast, ferritin correlated with CRP in both SD and non‐SD SAIDs (r = 0.42 and r = 0.56, respectively) (Figure 1M and N). These results suggest that plasma ferritin in SD and non‐SD SAIDs is in part due to inflammatory cytokines or reactive oxygen species and not only the result of hemophagocytosis. 26

To further explore the relationship between inflammation and HO‐1 in SD, we measured plasma inflammatory cytokines. IL‐6 was not higher in AOSD and sJIA compared with most other non‐SD SAIDs (Figure 2A, Supplementary Table 5), whereas TNF, IL‐10, and IFNγ differed the most (Supplementary Figure 1C, Supplementary Table 5). IL‐12 was not detectable (data not shown). In contrast, IL‐18 was markedly elevated in patients with AOSD and sJIA (Figure 2B, Supplementary Table 5). IL‐18 strongly correlated with HO‐1 in patients with SD (r = 0.91) but weakly in non‐SD SAIDs (r = 0.21) (Figure 2C), whereas IL‐6 did not correlate with HO‐1 in either patient group (Figure 2D). IL‐18 in patients with SD did not correlate with CRP (r = 0.07) but correlated moderately in non‐SD SAIDs (r = 0.33; Figure 2E). As expected, IL‐6 correlated strongly with CRP in non‐SD SAIDs (r = 0.59) and less so in SD (r = 0.34) (Figure 2F). In agreement with these results, the correlation between IL‐18 and HO‐1 was confirmed in another cohort of SD where total and free IL‐18 also strongly correlated with HO‐1, further reinforcing the functional association between bioactive IL‐18 and plasma HO‐1 (Figure 2G). HO‐1 and total IL‐18 did not correlate in non‐SD rheumatic diseases (Figure 2G). The strong correlation between IL‐18 and HO‐1, and their dissociation from CRP or IL‐6 levels, indicate that HO‐1 and IL‐18 increases in SD are not a measure of general inflammation but reflect yet‐to‐be‐defined SD‐specific inflammatory mechanisms.

Blood monocytes are not the source of HO‐1 and IL‐18 in SD

We next sorted peripheral blood monocytes from SD and non‐SD SAIDs from the same patients and time points as mentioned previously and performed RNA‐Seq to investigate HO‐1 production and activated signaling pathways (for RNA‐Seq sample numbers, see Supplementary Table 1). Differential gene expression between SD and non‐SD SAIDs revealed that 112 genes were up‐regulated and 71 genes were down‐regulated in SD. Unsupervised clustering of gene expression in SD and non‐SD SAIDs revealed distinct gene modules differentially expressed in SD when compared with non‐SD SAIDs (Figure 3A and B). We found that monocyte HO‐1, ferritin light chain, and ferritin heavy chain and IL‐18 messenger RNA (mRNA) did not differ between SD, non‐SD SAID, and HC (Supplementary Table 7A–C) indicating that blood monocytes are not the source of these in plasma. SD monocytes had both haptoglobin and CD163 up‐regulated compared with non‐SD SAIDs (up‐regulated 1.99‐fold and 1.53‐fold, respectively), and this may suggest a propensity to uptake hemoglobin in SD (Supplementary Table 7A).

Pro‐inflammatory cytokine IL‐6 mRNA in SD was increased by 1.4‐fold compared with HC but did not differ from non‐SD SAIDs (Supplementary Table 7A–C). TNF and IFNγ did not differ between SD and non‐SD SAIDs nor with HC, whereas IL‐10 was increased in SD compared with non‐SD SAIDs and with HC (1.4‐fold and 1.8‐fold, respectively). The most up‐regulated gene in SD was the IL‐1 decoy receptor IL‐1R2, 27 , 28 which was increased in SD by 1.8‐fold compared with non‐SD SAIDs and 15.2‐fold compared with HC (Supplementary Table 7A and B). In contrast, IL‐1R1 and IL‐1α did not differ in SD, whereas IL‐1β was only increased by 1.2‐fold. As previously reported, Placenta Associated 8 29 was increased in SD compared with non‐SD SAIDs and HC by 1.75‐ and 2.46‐fold, respectively. These RNA data suggest that blood monocytes may not be the source of plasma HO‐1 and IL‐18 in patients with SD.

MicroRNA transcriptome in SD

Because microRNAs (miRNAs) regulate gene expression but also respond to inflammatory signals, we analyzed the miRNA transcriptome of fluorescence‐activated cell sorted peripheral blood monocytes, natural killer (NK) cells, B cells, and dendritic cells (DC). In keeping with the known distinct cell‐type specific expression patterns of miRNAs, 30 we find that clustering in t‐distributed stochastic neighbor embedding plots was dominated by the cell type (Figure 3D) but not disease type (Figure 3E and data not shown). Differential expression of miRNA between SD and non‐SD SAIDs revealed specific miRNA differences (Figure 3F and Supplementary Tables 8–11). NK cells had the most changes in the miRNAome compared with monocytes, B cells, and DC. The most increased miRNAs in SD compared with non‐SD SAIDs was miR‐146a‐5p, an IL‐18 responsive miRNA, 31 in NK cells (increased 2.9‐fold), followed by miR‐151a‐5p in monocytes (increased 1.2‐fold), miR‐92b‐3p in B cells (increased 1.9‐fold), and miR‐1268b in DC (increased 2.6‐fold) (Figure 3F and Supplementary Tables 8A–C). Most decreased miRNA were miR181a‐2‐3p in NK cells (decreased 1.4‐fold), miR‐365b‐5p in monocytes (decreased 1.2‐fold), and miR‐223‐3p in B cells (decreased 1.3‐fold) (Figure 3F and Supplementary Tables 8–11). No miRNA was significantly decreased in DC. These findings demonstrate that SD exhibits distinct miRNA changes in specific immune populations.

Ferroptosis and Type I IFN signaling in SD

To identify discreet pathophysiologic mechanisms in SD, we performed pathway analysis on our proteomics (Figure 3G) and monocyte RNA‐Seq data (Figure 3H, Supplementary Figure 2A and B, Supplementary Table 7A). Pathway analysis of differentially abundant plasma proteins between SD and non‐SD SAIDs identified ferroptosis as the pathway with the highest normalized enrichment (NES) score (Figure 3G). In SD, 43 plasma proteins were increased whereas only one was less abundant compared with non‐SD SAIDs (Figure 3G). Interferon αβ (IFNαβ) signaling was the top up‐regulated pathway (as determined by NES) in differentially expressed genes (DEGs) in SD monocytes compared with non‐SD SAIDs (Figure 3H). Other top pathways in SD were antigen‐processing and cross presentation as well as SARS‐CoV‐2 signaling (Figure 3H), both of which are responsive to type I IFN. Several IFN inducible genes, including IFI27 (5.7‐fold) and SIGLEC‐1 (1.73‐fold) were up‐regulated in SD compared with non‐SD SAIDs (Supplementary Table 7A). IFNαβ signaling was the fourth most up‐regulated pathway in SD when compared with HC, with inflammatory cytokine pathways also up‐regulated, as expected (Supplementary Figure 2A). IFNαβ signaling was not up‐regulated in non‐SD SAIDs compared with HC (Supplementary Figure 2B), suggesting that type I IFN signaling is specific for SD and not a general feature of SAIDs.

The pathway nuclear events associated with nuclear factor erythroid‐derived 2‐like 2 Nrf2 (NFE2L2), a pathway that regulates HO‐1 production, 32 was up‐regulated in SD (Figure 3H). Despite the pathway being activated, NFE2L2 and HMOX1 were not up‐regulated (Supplementary Table 7A).

Hemophagocytosis induces HO‐1 in macrophages and synergizes with type I IFN to induce inflammation

The remarkable association between HO‐1 and IL‐18 in SD led us to investigate whether IL‐18 induces HO‐1. Red blood cells (RBC) and heme are known inducers of HO‐1 in macrophages, 33 and we find that RBC potently induced HO‐1 protein and mRNA in monocyte‐derived macrophages (Figure 4A). HO‐1 protein was detectable in cell lysates but not supernatants. Despite IL‐18's strong correlation with HO‐1, IL‐18 alone or in combination with IL‐12 or IFNγ failed to augment HO‐1 production by RBC in macrophages (data not shown).

Because the type I IFN pathway was enriched in SD, we examined whether IFNβ affected HO‐1 or IL‐18 production. IFNβ in vitro inhibited HO‐1 production when added to RBC treated macrophages (Figure 4B) but did not induce IL‐18 (data not shown). IFNβ greatly augmented both protein and mRNA IL‐6 production in RBC exposed macrophages (Figure 4C). IFNβ does not induce HO‐1 in mice, but HO‐1 is required for toll‐like receptor (TLR) 3 and 4 induced IFNβ production. 34 , 35 Therefore, we examined RBC treatment alone or in combination with IL‐12 or IL‐18 but found no induction of IFNβ in macrophages (data not shown).

We tested whether hemophagocytosis can induce IL‐18 as it did for HO‐1 because this could explain their strong correlation in plasma. However, IL‐18 protein and mRNA were not induced by RBC in monocytes or macrophages (data not shown). We next examined patients with SD (n = 3) treated with Tadekinig alfa (r‐hIL‐18BP, an IL‐18 inhibitor) and despite the rapid decline of plasma free IL‐18, HO‐1 decreased with delayed kinetics (Figure 4D) but only in patients responding to Tadekinig alfa. A patient with no clinical response showed no effect of Tadekinig alfa on HO‐1.

These results indicate that IL‐18 and HO‐1 do not directly regulate each other, but the delayed kinetics of HO‐1 decrease after r‐hIL‐18BP treatment suggests IL‐18 indirectly affects plasma HO‐1 levels. Type I IFN, on the other hand, synergizes with hemophagocytosis to potently induce IL‐6 and contribute to inflammation in SD.

Biomarkers for SD diagnosis

To identify biomarkers that distinguish SD from non‐SD SAIDs, we generated receiver operating characteristic (ROC) curves for SD elevated markers. IL‐18, HO‐1, and ferritin ROC curves (area under the curve [AUC]: 0.881, 0.851, and 0.841, respectively) outperformed IL‐6 (AUC: 0.745) and CRP (AUC: 0.674) in distinguishing SD from non‐SD SAIDs (Figure 5A). Combinations of these analytes did not improve further the ROC curves (data not shown). In a separate cohort (Geneva cohort), HO‐1 distinguished SD from other rheumatic diseases (AUC: 0.826) (Figure 5B). Plasma HO‐1 and IL‐18 were significantly elevated in SD compared with acute lymphoblastic leukemia (ALL) and acute COVID‐19 infection, which are both conditions that may require differential diagnosis from SD (Figure 5C). HO‐1 and IL‐18 levels decreased after treatment of patients with SAID (Supplementary Figure 2C).

Given the type I IFN pathway enrichment in SD (Figure 3H), we investigated whether IFN signatures in blood monocyte RNA could serve as a biomarker. We tested two IFN signatures, a 6‐gene score (ISG15, RSAD2, IFIT1, IFI27, IFI44L, and SIGLEC1, called IFN6) associated with type I interferon‐mediated autoinflammation, 36 , 37 and a 3‐gene IFN score (IFN3) that we devised based on genes SIGLEC1, CD163, and IL1R2 that are induced by type I IFN but inhibited by IFNγ. 38 , 39 , 40 , 41 , 42 IFN6 and IFN3 scores were up‐regulated in SD compared with non‐SD SAIDs (Figure 5D), but only the IFN6 and not the IFN3 score correlated with plasma IFNγ (r = 0.71 and r = 0.19, respectively) (Figure 5E), suggesting IFN3 score is not dependent on IFNγ and its inducer IL‐18. The IFN3 score ROC curves outperformed the IFN6 score (AUC: 0.740 and 0.545, respectively) (Figure 5F) in predicting SD. Overall, our analysis suggests that HO‐1, IL‐18, and ferritin are better biomarkers of SD compared with IL‐6 and CRP, whereas a 3‐gene type I IFN‐specific score outperformed a routinely used 6‐gene IFN score.

Patient stratification by data integration

Finally, we took an unbiased approach to discover clusters of features that associate with SD. For this we integrated by weighted gene co‐expression network analysis (WGCNA) our large data sets to identify modules that distinguish SD from non‐SD SAIDs but to also stratify patients with SD into distinct groups (Figure 6A). The feature module that was most associated with SD was Yellowgreen (R = 0.59, P = 2e−43), while also having a clear negative association with non‐SD SAIDs (R = −0.29, P = 5e−10) (Figure 6A). This association was more prominent in patients with AOSD than with sJIA (Figure 6A). These modules comprised the following features: IL‐18; HO‐1; ferritin; IL‐10 from ELISA; CYP27A1 and RTN1 from monocyte RNA‐Seq; miR‐146a‐5p, let‐7i‐5p, miR‐21‐5p, miR‐1843, miR‐7‐5p and miR‐95‐3p in NK cells; miR1271‐5p, miR‐200b‐3p and miR‐128‐3p in B cells; miR‐3650 in DC miRNA; and 80 additional plasma proteins from proteomics (Figure 6B). This module within SD revealed two distinct signatures indicating two SD subgroups (Figure 6C). These signatures did not correlate with disease activity, as measured either by the Auto‐Inflammatory Diseases Activity Index (AIDAI)—a general, non‐SD‐specific activity score—or by plasma CRP levels, an indicator of systemic inflammation (Figure 6D). These signatures also associated with hemophagocytic syndrome (HLH or MAS) as indicated by the HScore 43 (Figure 6D). These two signatures that have arisen from a wealth of data may indicate SD disease subtypes with different pathophysiologies and treatment responses.

DISCUSSION

The ImmunAID consortium has amassed an unprecedented set of data for SAIDs, and in this report we queried for SD‐specific biomarkers but also possible pathophysiologic pathways that distinguish SD from other SAIDs that often share overlapping clinical and laboratory features. We find that HO‐1, IL‐18, and ferritin outperformed IL‐6 and CRP in distinguishing SD and confirmed this in a second cohort. Currently, CRP and ferritin, although not specific for SD, are commonly used for diagnosing sJIA and AOSD 15 and are predictive of MAS development. 7 , 24 Recently, recommendations for SD diagnosis included, in addition to ferritin, S100 proteins and IL‐18. 9 Previously, plasma HO‐1 was suggested to be increased in SD, but low patient numbers and the lack of other SAIDs made it unclear how specific HO‐1 is for SD. 22 , 23 We show that HO‐1 is clearly higher in patients with SD compared with other SAIDs and does not correlate with general inflammation as indicated by CRP levels. In contrast, ferritin correlated in SD and non‐SD SAIDs with CRP, suggesting that inflammatory cytokines 26 contribute to ferritin's elevation in SAIDs. Elevated HO‐1 in SD may indicate a continuum from mild SD disease to MAS where hyperferritinemia and hemophagocytosis are very high. In our cohort, some patients with SD had very high levels of HO‐1, and it would be important to investigate if this associates with MAS development.

Although proinflammatory cytokines and CRP did not distinguish SD from other SAIDs, IL‐18 was highly increased and predicted SD. SD is associated with extremely elevated levels of IL‐18 and hyperferritinemia, 15 , 16 high IL‐18 levels associate with MAS, 17 , 18 and IL‐18 serves as an indicator for disease activity in sJIA 44 and to diagnose remission. 19 IL‐18 has therefore been proposed as a good biomarker for SD, especially for those patients at risk to develop MAS. 20 The challenge, however, remains in distinguishing SD from other autoinflammatory diseases, viral infection, or hematologic malignancies that need to be excluded during differential diagnosis. We report here that IL‐18 together with HO‐1 are both elevated in SD compared with leukemia (ALL) and an acute viral infection (COVID‐19 infection). Larger cohorts, however, are needed to confirm the utility of IL‐18 and HO‐1 in the differential diagnosis of SD from leukemias and acute viral infections.

Our findings from RNA‐Seq of blood monocytes in SD are comparable to previous studies. The top pathways identified by Huang et al 45 when comparing MAS with HC or MAS with SD were IFNγ and type I IFN. Although we did not include patients with MAS in our cohort, both of these pathways were the top pathways in our patients with SD. Schulert et al 46 compared monocytes of high and low ferritin patients with sJIA. Their top 20 DEGs were also DEGs in our data when we compared SD with controls. Our analysis suggests potential pathophysiologic pathways active in SD that differ from most other patients with SAIDs. Based on our RNA data, a pattern emerges of type I IFN signaling but also of heme inducing Nrf2 signaling 32 and hemoglobin capture and signaling pathways that discriminate between SD and non‐SD SAIDs. Previous work comparing patients with sJIA with systemic lupus erythematosus and HC found IL‐1 but not type I IFN signatures in peripheral blood mononuclear cell, 47 whereas a small study reported IFN type I and II signatures in blood monocytes only in patients with SD with MAS. 45 Our data, comparing 72 patients with SD with 300 non‐SD SAIDs and 65 HC, find a clear type I IFN signature in SD blood monocytes, and this is further confirmed by the IFN3 score, a type I IFN‐specific score we propose that comprises genes SIGLEC1, CD163, and IL1R2, which are all induced by IFNα/β but negatively impacted by IFNγ. 38 , 39 , 40 , 42 The high levels of IL‐18, a potent inducer of IFNγ, in patients with SD complicates the interpretation of IFN signatures, but the role and origin of type I IFN signaling and the possible distinct role from IFNγ in SD merits further investigation. In mice, CpG‐induced MAS is completely TLR9 dependent, 48 but IFNγ does not mediate hemophagocytosis and hyperferritinemia, 48 , 49 and in our data, IFNγ does not affect in vitro HO‐1 production. In addition, inflammatory hemophagocytes are induced by TLR7 and TLR9 signaling but not IFNγ. 50 In contrast, in the mouse MAS model, disruption of type I IFN signaling abrogates anemia and hemophagocytosis, 49 indicating that type I and II IFN contribute distinctly to pathophysiology. Because HO‐1 is not secreted, elevated HO‐1 in plasma in SD could indicate ferroptosis, also suggested by our proteomics pathway analysis. Therefore, it is possible that the IFN I and II may contribute distinctly to splenic macrophage death, something mouse models could address.

The importance of IL‐18 in patients with SD is highlighted by their successful treatment with r‐hIL‐18BP. 51 The question arises of what is the link among IL‐18, HO‐1, and type I IFN? The remarkable correlation, we find, between plasma IL‐18 and HO‐1, and the observation that r‐hIL‐18BP treatment associates, albeit with delayed kinetics, with a reduction of plasma HO‐1 levels in SD, suggest that IL‐18 acts indirectly to increase hemophagocytosis, HO‐1 protein induction, or ferroptosis and HO‐1 release into the blood. This mediator could be IFNγ, however, because in vitro IL‐18 and IFNγ did not affect RBC‐induced HO‐1 by macrophages, and IFNγ does not promote hemophagocytosis in the CpG MAS model, 48 , 49 this favors the hypothesis that IL‐18 and downstream IFNγ promote the release of HO‐1 possibly via macrophage death. Plasma proteomics suggested ferroptosis as the most active pathway in SD, showed that HO‐1 itself accelerates ferroptosis, 52 and demonstrated that the ferroptosis‐associated zrt‐/irt‐like protein 8 (encoded by SLC39A8) 53 was increased in SD (1.92‐ and 2.78‐fold increases compared with non‐SD SAIDs and HC, respectively). Such ferroptosis could further contribute to the inflammation of SD by releasing DAMPs and alarmins. 54

The origin of high IL‐18 in SD is unknown, but type I IFN may contribute. Although not effective on its own, type I IFN can prime monocytes to produce IL‐18 via TLR activation, and in CpG‐induced MAS in mice, a JAK1/2 inhibitor reduces IL‐18 production. 55 , 56 In bacterial and viral infections of mice, type I IFN is required for absent in melanoma 2 inflammasome complex activation, which induces, in an IRF3‐dependent manner, IL‐18. 57 , 58 HO‐1, on the other hand, is produced by monocytes in response to various stresses and cytokines 59 and inhibits pro‐inflammatory responses, in part, by inducing IL‐10. 60 HO‐1, however, can also regulate IFNβ production and ameliorates autoimmune animal models. 34 HO‐1‐deficient macrophages exhibit reduced IFNβ production, which is due to HO‐1 being required for IRF3 activation after TLR3/TLR4 stimulation or viral infection. 34 Thus, HO‐1 overproduction in SD could also contribute to excessive type I IFN signaling, which in turn drives IL‐18 production. As mentioned, in the CpG‐induced MAS mouse model, type I IFN signaling, but not IFNγ, contributes to hemophagocytosis, anemia, and hyperferritinemia. 48 , 49 This would suggest that in SD, type I IFNs may contribute to HO‐1 production via hemophagocytosis and IL‐18 production. Conversely, HO‐1 can facilitate type I IFN production and thus a fast forward loop is set up that amplifies type I IFN and IL‐18 production via hemophagocytosis by red‐pulp macrophages in the spleen. We also found, unexpectedly, that type I IFN synergizes with hemophagocytosis to potently induce IL‐6 and inflammation. Thus, type I IFN in SD could be a common driver of both general inflammation, IL‐18, and hemophagocytosis, and could therefore be a potential therapeutic upstream target. It should be stressed, however, that despite the increases of IL‐18 and HO‐1 in plasma, the tissue of origin for these in SD remains to be established. Although mRNA for IL‐18 was not increased in the blood monocytes of SD, these cells could still be the source of IL‐18 because its production is primarily regulated at the protein level and cleavage by caspase‐1, −4 or −5. 61 IL‐18 is known to be produced by Kupffer cells and liver‐resident macrophages but also barrier epithelia cells. 8 HO‐1 mRNA was not increased in SD blood monocytes, making them an unlikely source. High levels of HO‐1 can be produced by erythrophagocytic macrophages, such as splenic red‐pulp macrophages and liver Kupffer cells and monocyte‐derived macrophages. 60 During inflammation, HO‐1 can be also produced by intestinal epithelial cells and endothelial cells. 60 Identifying the source of IL‐18 and HO‐1 in SD therefore may provide insight into the pathophysiology of the disease.

Finally, the stratification of patients with SD may lead to better tailored therapies 62 because therapies blocking IL‐1, IL‐6, or IL‐18 may be affected by patient heterogeneity. 63 IL‐18:IL‐6 ratios have been used to classify patients with SD as MAS or arthritis dominated. 64 However, in our study, IL‐18:IL‐6 ratios did not identify separate patient subgroups, and we believe this was because we did not include patients with MAS/HLH in our cohort (data not shown). Using WGCNA analysis of our extensive data sets, we could identify two subtypes of SD that did not associate with disease activity or general inflammation, as indicated by disease activity score and CRP levels, but associated instead with hemophagocytosis. These signatures may predict progression to MAS or response to therapy.

In conclusion, the ImmunAID consortium has amassed an unprecedented data set from one of the largest cohorts of patients with SAIDs ever assembled, and we report here distinct features that distinguish SD from other patients with SAIDs. We further report that integrated and unbiased analysis of these data sets can help stratify patients with SD into two distinct groups that do not associate with disease activity or general inflammation but with the levels of hemophagocytic activity. Our findings on SD advance our understanding of this complex autoinflammatory disease and indicate that IL‐18, hemophagocytosis, ferroptosis, and type I IFN signaling are involved in SD disease. Finally, with this report, we provide an invaluable and deep omic resource for the study of SD and other SAIDs that are included in the ImmunAID cohort.
