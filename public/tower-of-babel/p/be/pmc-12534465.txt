KLF2 may suppress chicken adipogenesis by enhancing the GATA2 transcription
Authors: Tingting Zhang, Qin Gao, Jiazhen Tian, Zhaoxiong Qi, Jing Ning, Yuechan Chen, Zhiwei Zhang
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
Kruppel-like factor 2 (KLF2) acts as an essential modulator of adipogenesis; nevertheless, the exact molecular pathways governing its role remain uncharacterized. This investigation characterized the expression profile of KLF2 in human adipose tissue and elucidated the mechanisms by which KLF2 modulates adipogenesis in avian models. The KLF2 mRNA expression in human adipose tissues was substantial and exhibited significant heterogeneity influenced by gender and anatomical location, suggesting that KLF2 regulated adipose tissue functionality. In chicken preadipocytes ICP1, the KLF2 protein increased at the first 24 h of oleate-induced adipogenic differentiation and subsequently decreased over time. KLF2 facilitated the cellular proliferation of ICP1 cells (P < 0.05). Moreover, KLF2 overexpression inhibited differentiation of chicken preadipocytes ICP1 (P < 0.05). KLF2 also increased the density of mitochondria and mitochondrial cristae in ICP1 cells. RNA seq and luciferase reporter assays demonstrated that KLF2 suppressed the expression of genes in PPAR signaling pathway in ICP1 cells. Additionally, KLF2 interacted with the promoter region of chicken GATA2 and upregulated GATA2 transcription in ICP1 cells. In summary, KLF2 potentially influences the PPAR signaling cascade, mitochondrial bioenergetics, and GATA2 gene expression to enhance the proliferation of avian preadipocytes while inhibiting their differentiation.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1038/s41598-025-20099-2.

Introduction

Adipose tissue is a connective tissue composed mainly of adipocytes and plays an important role in regulating energy balance in humans and animals1. Abnormality in the development or function of adipose tissue increases the risk of cardiovascular disease, diabetes, cancers and other chronic diseases2. Adipogenesis is the process of the formation of mature adipocytes from precursor cells, had been suggested as a valuable target for the treatment of metabolic related diseases3.

Numerous insights into adipogenesis had been gleaned from rodent models in vitro, highlighting the pivotal role of transcriptional regulation in governing adipogenesis4. Peroxisome proliferator-activated receptor gamma (PPARγ), CCAAT/enhancer-binding proteins (C/EBPs), Kruppel-like factors (KLFs), and GATA binding factors 2 and 3 (GATA2 and GATA3) had been identified as key regulators of adipogenesis5–7. Nonetheless, research on adipogenesis in non-rodent species remains scarce.

Chickens possess exclusively white adipose tissue8. Furthermore, their adipocytes demonstrated a restricted capacity for de novo lipogenesis, with the bulk of intracellular lipids originating from hepatic synthesis of fatty acids9. Additionally, broilers presented a marked tendency towards visceral fat accumulation under normative feeding protocols, posing a substantial challenge within the poultry industry. These traits are comparable to those observed in adult human adipose tissue. Additionally, genomic sequence alignment reveals a Fundamental homology between chicken and human genomes, with approximately 60% of chicken genes showing high similarity to human genes10. Therefore, chickens have been chosen as a model organism for studying human adipogenesis. In addition, oleate effectively enhanced the differentiation of avian preadipocytes in vitro11 along with the establishment of immortalized chicken preadipocytes12 offering a valuable model for studying adipogenesis in chickens.

Our previous study showed that KLF2 was greatly expressed in chicken abdominal adipose tissue, and differentially expressed in abdominal adipose tissue between fat and lean broilers during development13. In addition, KLF2 overexpression inhibited the differentiation of primary chicken preadipocytes in vitro13. In this study, the expression pattern of KLF2 in human adipose tissues and the underlying mechanism by which KLF2 influences the chicken adipogenesis were investigated, and the findings might shed a light on the molecular mechanisms underlying the adipose tissue development.

Methods

Cell cultures

The immortalized chicken preadipocyte line ICP112 was purchased from China Center for Type Culture Collection (CCTCC, Wuhan, China), and cultured in DMEM/F12 (Gibco, Grand Island, NY) supplemented with 10% FBS (#C04001, Viva Cell Biosciences, Shanghai, China) at 37 °C and 5% CO2. Prior to conducting functional assays, the cells were subjected to mycoplasma decontamination using the TransSafe™ Mycoplasma Elimination Reagent (TransMyco Plus) Kit (#FM421, TransGen, Beijing, China), and only those in the logarithmic growth phase were utilized for experiments. Experimental groups were randomized before transfection and were blinded to the experimental conditions.

Plasmids

Literatures detailed descriptions on pCMV-myc-KLF2, along with the promoter-reporter plasmids of chicken PPARγ, C/EBPα, FASN, FABP4, and LPL13,14. The promoter-reporter plasmid of PLIN1 was pGL3-basic (Promega, Madison, WI) containing the chicken PLIN1 promoter (nucleotides − 2203 to −230, relative to the start site of NM_001127439.1) and constructed with reference to the literature15. The promoter-reporter plasmids for GATA2, Acetyl-CoA Carboxylase Alpha (ACACA) and stearoyl-CoA desaturase (SCD) were pGL4.10 (Promega) containing the respective promoters of chicken GATA2 (nucleotides − 2001 to −1, −1290 to −1, −864 to −1, −580 to −1, −296 to −1, −157 to −1; relative to the start site of NM_001003797.1), ACACA (nucleotides − 800 to 130, relative to the start site of NM_205505.2) and SCD (nucleotides − 876 to 125, relative to the start site of NM_204890.2), respectively.

With reference to the siRNA sequences in literature sources16 the RNA interference plasmid of pGensil-1-KLF2 was constructed. Briefly, the sequences enumerated in Table 1 were cloned into the pGensil-1 plasmid through the BamHI and HindIII restriction sites to fabricate these plasmids by General Biol (Chuzhou, China).

RNA isolation, reverse transcription and qRT-PCR

The ICP1 cells were inoculated into 6-well plates, and when the cells reached 60%−80% confluence, the given plasmids were transfected into the cells using ExFect® Transfection Reagent (#T101-02, Vazyme, Nanjing, China). After 48 h of transfection, total RNA was extracted with E.Z.N.A.® Total RNA Kit I (#R6834-01; OMEGA, Norcross, GA). The RNA quality was assessed using denatured formaldehyde agarose gel electrophoresis, and only the RNA with a 28 S to 18 S ratio between 1.8 and 2.1 was used for subsequent reverse transcription. Using RevertAid First Strand cDNA Synthesis Kit (#K1622, Thermo scientific, Waltham, MA), 0.5 µg of total RNA was converted into cDNA in a 20 µL reaction system. The ChamQ Blue Universal SYBR qPCR Master Mix (#Q312-02, Vazyme) was utilized in real-time PCR. The reaction system was 20 µL, incorporating 1 µL of cDNA as the template. The primers used were shown in Supplementary Table 1. The thermal cycling conditions were set as follows: an initial denaturation at 95 ℃ for 5 min, followed by 40 cycles of denaturation at 95 ℃ for 15 s, and annealing/extension at 60 ℃ for 60 s. The procedure concluded with a melting curve analysis to confirm the specificity of the amplification. The cellular constituents within an individual well were considered a biological replicate. Each experimental outcome comprises three biological replicates.

Western blot

When the ICP1 cells cultured in 6-well plates underwent transfection for 48 h, they were lysed by the addition of 0.2 mL RIPA buffer (with PMSF at a 100:1 ratio; Beyotime, Beijing, China) per well. The lysates were mixed with 5×Loading buffer and heated at 100 ℃ for 10 min to denature proteins. The lysates were separated on 5–12% SDS-polyacrylamide gels and transferred onto polyvinylidene difluoride (PVDF) membranes. Following the blocking step, the membranes were subjected to an overnight incubation at 4 ℃ with primary antibodies. including ACTB (mouse mAB #TA-09, ZSGB-BIO, Beijing, China; 1:1000), PPARγ (rabbit mAB #2443S, CST, Danvers, MA; 1:1000), c-Myc-tag (mouse mAB #631206, Takara, Tokyo, Japan; 1:1000), GATA2 (rabbit mAB #abs105159, Absin, Shanghai, China; 1:1000), and KLF2 (rabbit mAB #abs124444, Absin, 1:1000). Subsequently, membranes were incubated for 1.5 h at room temperature with HRP-conjugated goat anti-mouse IgG (#ZB-2305, ZSGB-BIO; 1:10000) or goat anti-rabbit IgG (#ZB-2301, ZSGB-BIO; 1:10000). Detection was carried out using the BeyoECL Plus kit (#P0018S, Beyotime). The original western blot images referenced in this study are provided in Supplementary Fig. 1.

Preadipocyte differentiation

Upon the ICP1 cells reaching approximately 60% to 80% confluence, the cells were transfected with either pCMV-myc-KLF2 or pCMV-myc (Takara), and cultured until they reached 100% confluence prior to oleate induction. When instigating differentiation, the cultivation medium was replaced by an induction medium, incorporating a final oleate concentration of 160 µM within the growth medium, to trigger adipogenic differentiation of ICP1 cells. The cellular constituents within a single well were regarded as a biological replicate. Each experiment was conducted with three biological replicates.

Oil red O staining

Following PBS rinsing, cells were fixed with 4% paraformaldehyde for 30 min. Post washing with distilled water, the cells were stained with a 0.5% Oil Red O solution for 1 h. Then 1 mL of 60% isopropanol was added to each well for 10–20 s, followed by three distilled water washes. Once air-dried, lipid droplets were observed using an inverted microscope. For quantification of lipid accumulation, the dye was extracted with 1 mL of isopropanol and measured spectrophotometrically at a wavelength of 500 nm.

Cell proliferation assay

Initially, ICP1 cells were seeded into a six-well plate. Upon reaching 60–80% confluence, a total of 2.5 µg of pCMV-myc-KLF2, pCMV-myc, pGensil-1-NC and pGensil-1-KLF2 were delivered into the cells in each well, respectively. After transfection for 24 h, the cells were transferred to a 96-well plate at a density of 5 000 cells per well. Cellular proliferation was quantified using the CellTiter-Glo® Luminescent Cell Viability Assay (#G7570, Promega) or Cell Counting Kit-8 (CCK8) reagent (#BS350B, Biosharp, Anhui, China) at 24 h, 48 h, 72 h, 96 h, and 120 h post-inoculation in 96-well plate. The cellular constituents within an individual well were considered a biological replicate. Each experimental outcome comprises five biological replicates.

Flow cytometric analysis

When the ICP1 cells reach 60–80% confluence, the plasmids of pCMV-myc and pCMV-myc-KLF2 were transfected into the cells, respectively. Forty-eight hours after transfection, the cells were fixed overnight with 75% ethanol, then the flow cytometry was detected using the Cell Cycle Staining Kit (#CCS012, Multi Sciences, Hangzhou, China) following a previously described protocol17. The cellular constituents within an individual well were considered a biological replicate. Each experimental outcome comprises three biological replicates.

RNA seq

After the transfection for 48 h, the ICP1 cells were harvested using TRIzol™ Reagent (#15596026, Invitrogen, Carlsbad, CA) for subsequent RNA sequencing. The RNA-seq library preparation, sequencing and data analysis were conducted by Novogene Corporation (Beijing, China). The cellular constituents within an individual well of the cell culture plate were considered a biological replicate. Each experimental outcome comprises three biological replicates. The raw data files of RNA seq generated and analyzed during the current study are available in the Gene Expression Omnibus (GEO) repository [GEO accession: GSE302747].

When reach 60–80% confluence, a fixed amount of total DNA (0.55 µg) was transfected for the cells in each well using ExFect® Transfection Reagent (#T101-01, Vazyme). The transfection system was shown in Table 2. At 48 h after transfection, the luciferase reporter activity was conducted using the Dual-Luciferase Reporter Assay Kit (#DL101-01, Vazyme). Promoter activity was expressed as the ratio of Firefly/Renilla luciferase activity. The cellular constituents within an individual well were considered a biological replicate. Each assay was performed in four biological replicates.

Chromatin immunoprecipitation (ChIP)-qPCR

Forty-eight hours after transfection, the cells were subjected to cross-linking with 1% formaldehyde. ChIP was performed using the SimpleChIP® Plus Enzymatic Chromatin IP Kit (Magnetic Beads, #9005S, CST) and the c-Myc Monoclonal Antibody (#631206, Takara) or IgG (mouse IgG #abs20038, Absin; rabbit IgG #2729P, CST) following the provided instructions. The examination of KLF2 binding in the chicken GATA2 promoter was conducted via PCR utilizing the purified DNA product and the specific primers listed in Table 3. To ensure experimental specificity, cells transfected with pCMV-myc served as the control baseline of cells transfected with pCMV-myc-KLF2 to negate the confounding effects of non-specific myc antibody enrichment on the experimental outcomes. The quantification of myc-tagged protein enrichment on the target band in each cell line was achieved by normalizing the band intensity from myc antibody immunoprecipitation to that from IgG immunoprecipitation. Subsequently, the enrichment level of KLF2 on the target position was determined by normalizing the myc-tagged protein enrichment in pCMV-myc-KLF2 transfected cells against the myc-tagged protein enrichment in cells transfected with pCMV-myc.

Transmission electron microscopy

After transfection for 48 h, the ICP1 cells were pre-fixed overnight at 4 ℃ in 2.5% glutaraldehyde. Subsequently, the cells were fixed in a 1% osmium tetroxide solution for 1–2 h. The samples were then dehydrated through a series of ethanol concentrations, followed by two treatments with 100% ethanol, and finally dehydrated twice with propanol. The specimens were then infiltrated with a series of embedding resin and acetone mixtures in varying ratios (V/V = 1/3 for 1 h, V/V = 1/1 for 1 h, and V/V = 3/1 for 3 h, in sequence), followed by an overnight incubation in pure embedding resin at ambient temperature. Ultra-thin sections were then prepared using an ultramicrotome (LEICA, Wetzlar, Germany). These sections were subsequently stained with lead citrate and uranyl acetate solutions for 15 min each and finally analyzed with a HT7700 transmission electron microscope (HITACHI, Tokyo, Japan).

Quantification of intracellular ATP levels

The intracellular ATP concentration was measured utilizing the ATP Chemiluminescence Assay Kit (#E-BC-F002, Elabscience, Wuhan, China). Firstly, through the quantitative assessment of luciferase activities across a range of concentration standards, a calibration curve for standard concentrations was derived, represented by the linear equation y = ax + b. Secondly, 48 h post-transfection, the cells were harvested and resuspended at a density of 2 × 106 cells per 0.3 mL of Reagent I, and subjected to a 10-min incubation in a boiling water bath after mixing, followed by rapid cooling under running water. Subsequently, the cells were centrifuged at 10,000 ×g for 10 min at 4 ℃, and the supernatants were collected for luciferase activity assay. The ATP content per cell was calculated using the equation: ATP content (µmol/1 × 109) = (ΔF - b) ÷ a × V/N × f, where ΔF denotes the adjusted luciferase activity (sample luciferase activity minus blank luciferase activity), a is the slope of the calibration curve, b is the intercept of the calibration curve, V is the volume (mL) of the homogenization medium, f is the dilution factor, and N represents the cell count per 1 × 106 cells.

Bioinformatics analysis

KLF2 binding sites were forecasted utilizing the KLF2 binding site matrix (https://jaspar.genereg.net/matrix/MA1515.2/) through a Hidden Markov Model (HMM) with the following formula:

Here, S denotes the prediction score derived from the HMM, p(i) represents the probability of occurrence for each base according to the scoring matrix, and maxS is the score for the optimal sequence where all bases achieve the highest possible probability. To assess the robustness of these predictions, bootstrap resampling was employed. Specifically, the sequence under analysis was permuted 1000 times, and prediction scores were computed for each permutation. If the scores for N permutations exceeded the original score, the P value was determined as N/1000.

Statistical analysis

Data analysis was completed using R software and referenced to the literature19. The expression data for human KLF2 in tissues were derived from the human protein atlas (HPA) database (https://www.proteinatlas.org/). The Shapiro-Wilk test was employed to assess the normality of the distribution. For datasets adhering to a normal distribution, the impact of factors was evaluated using Analysis of Variance (ANOVA). When the data did not conform to a normal distribution, the Kruskal-Wallis test was utilized to examine the influence of fixed factors. Differences between two groups were assessed using the Student’s t-test or the Mann-Whitney U test. For comparisons among more than two groups, Duncan’s multiple range tests were applied. If the data followed a normal distribution, Duncan’s multiple range test was used directly. In cases where the data did not follow a normal distribution, the data were ranked initially, and then Duncan’s multiple range tests were performed on the ranks to ascertain intergroup differences.

Expression patterns ofKLF2in human adipose tissue.

An analysis was conducted on the expression patterns of KLF2 in human adipose tissue based on publicly available HPA data (Supplementary data 1). The results showed that the KLF2 transcripts of adipose tissue were greater than those of other tissues (Fig. 1A). In addition, the KLF2 transcripts of adipose tissues were significantly associated with sex, age, and the location (P < 0.05, Fig. 1B).

Hierarchical research revealed that in 9 out of the 12 layers studied, the KLF2 transcripts of adipose tissue were greater in males compared to females (Fig. 1C). Especially, at the ages of 60–69 years, the KLF2 transcripts in subcutaneous adipose tissue of males were significantly greater than those of females (P < 0.05, Fig. 1C).

In addition, significant differences in KLF2 transcripts between subcutaneous and abdominal adipose tissues were detected across 4 of the 12 layers examined (P < 0.05, Fig. 1D). For women aged 20–29 and 30–39 years, KLF2 transcripts were found to be greater in abdominal adipose tissue than in subcutaneous tissue (P < 0.05, Fig. 1D). In contrast, for men aged 60–69 and 70–79 years, KLF2 transcripts were lower in abdominal adipose tissue than subcutaneous tissue (P < 0.05, Fig. 1D).

Additionally, varying age-related changes in KLF2 expression were detected across the four examined layers. In the male subcutaneous adipose tissue, the KLF2 transcripts showed a significant age-related increase aged from 20 to 29 to 70–79 years (P < 0.05, Fig. 1E), with no similar model observed in the other layers (Fig. 1E). Moreover, a Spearman’s rank test showed a significant positive correlation between KLF2 transcripts levels in subcutaneous adipose tissue and those in visceral adipose tissue (r = 0.1629, P = 0.0010, N = 407).

KLF2 modulated the proliferation and differentiation of chicken preadipocytes

Western blot analysis revealed the presence of three distinct PPARγ isoforms ranging from 40 to 70 kDa in chicken preadipocytes (Fig. 2A), likely attributable to post-translational modifications. Following the induction of differentiation in ICP1 cells by oleate, a marked upregulation in the overall expression of PPARγ protein was observed (Fig. 2A). Quantitative assessment of the PPARγ bands at the predicted molecular weights (54 kDa) revealed that, with the exception of the 120 h time point, PPARγ expression levels were elevated at all oleate-treatment intervals compared to the untreated controls (Fig. 2A). At 120 h post-oleate induction, the expression level of the 54 kDa PPARγ isoform did not exhibit a significant increase, potentially due to post-translational modifications of PPARγ that might alter its molecular mass and electrophoretic mobility. In addition, the KLF2 expression increased within the first 24 h of oleate-induced adipogenic differentiation and subsequently decreased over time (Fig. 2A). Additionally, in ICP1 cells transfected with pCMV-myc-KLF2, a myc-tagged protein corresponding to the molecular weight of KLF2 was identified, whereas this protein was undetectable in cells transfected with the empty vector (EV, Fig. 2B). In addition, relative to the EV group, PPARγ expression was downregulated in ICP1 cells transfected with pCMV-myc-KLF2. Conversely, transfection of ICP1 cells with pGensil-1-KLF2 resulted in a marked reduction in KLF2 expression and a significant elevation in PPARγ expression compared to the negative control group (NC, Fig. 2B).

Cell proliferation assays demonstrated that in ICP1 cells KLF2 overexpression significantly enhanced cellular proliferation at 48 h, 96 h, and 120 h in comparison to the EV group, and knockdown of KLF2 expression suppressed the cellular proliferation at 24 h, 48 h, 72 h, 96 h, and 120 h comparison to the NC group (P < 0.05, Fig. 2C). The flow cytometry results revealed that compared to the EV group, cells with KLF2 overexpression showed a lower percentage in the G0/G1 phase and a greater percentage in both S and G2/M phases (P < 0.05, Fig. 2D).

Oil red O staining showed that the lipid storage was significantly lower in KLF2-overexpressed cells than EV group (Fig. 2E). KLF2 increases mitochondrial density and cristae density in chicken preadipocytes

Fluorescence light microscopy analysis revealed that compared to their control groups, KLF2 overexpression elevated the mitochondrial intensity, conversely, KLF2 knockdown resulted in a decreased mitochondrial density within ICP1 cells (Fig. 3A). In addition, in comparison to their control groups, KLF2 overexpression markedly elevated the ratio of mitochondrial DNA to nuclear DNA, while KLF2 knockdown notably reduced the ratio of mitochondrial DNA to nuclear DNA in cells (P < 0.05, Fig. 3B). Additionally, transmission electron microscopy revealed that compared to EV group, an altered mitochondrial morphology was found in ICP1 cells overexpressing KLF2, characterized by a reduction in size, improved structural integrity, and increased cristae density (Fig. 3C). The ATP quantification assay demonstrated a statistically significant elevation of ATP levels in cells with KLF2 overexpression compared to the EV control group (P < 0.05, Fig. 3D).

Effect of KLF2 overexpression on the expression patterns of chicken preadipocytes

RNA seq was utilized to examine the impact of KLF2 overexpression on gene transcription profiles in ICP1 cells. In total, valid expression data for 18,495 genes were obtained (Supplementary data 2). There were 173 differential expressed genes (DEGs) between KLF2-overexpressing cells and the EV group (P < 0.05, Fig. 4A,B). Within this subset, 73 genes were upregulated, whereas 100 genes were downregulated in the KLF2-overexpressing cells relative to the EV group (Fig. 4B). In alignment with these results (Fig. 4C), the real-time PCR analysis showed that KLF2 overexpression significantly increased the expression of interleukin 17 A (IL17A) and p53 apoptosis effector related to PMP22 beta (PERPB), while it significantly decreased the expression of cardiotrophin-like cytokine factor 1 (CLCF1) and Sp1 transcription factor (SP1), relative to the EV group (P < 0.05, Fig. 4D).

Gene Ontology (GO) enrichment analysis indicated that the upregulated DEGs in KLF2-overexpressing cells were significantly enriched in eight terms (adjusted P < 0.05), all of which were associated with the inner mitochondrial membrane protein complex and the mitochondrial respiratory chain (Fig. 4E). The Kyoto Encyclopedia of Genes and Genomes (KEGG)20,21 enrichment analysis revealed that the upregulated DEGs in KLF2-overexpressing cells were significantly enriched in the p53 signaling pathway, oxidative phosphorylation, cardiac muscle contraction, pyrimidine metabolism, and glutathione metabolism (P < 0.05, Fig. 4F). Gene Set Enrichment Analysis (GSEA) demonstrated that in KLF2-overexpressing cells, gene sets related to kinetochore (GO:0000776), mitotic nuclear division (GO:0140014), DNA replication (GGA03030), and cell cycle (GGA04110) were upregulated (Fig. 4G).

GO enrichment analysis indicated that the downregulated DEGs in KLF2 overexpressing cells did not enriched in any terms at the adjusted P < 0.05 level. Their top 10 significantly enriched GO terms included the stress-activated MAPK cascade and the regulation of brown adipocyte differentiation (P < 0.05, Fig. 4E). KEGG enrichment analysis revealed that DEGs downregulated in KLF2 overexpressing cells were significantly enriched in the MAPK signaling pathway, cytokine-cytokine receptor interactions, and arginine and proline metabolism (P < 0.05, Fig. 4F). GSEA analysis showed that the gene sets of mesodermal cell differentiation (GO:0048333), neural tube closure (GO:0001843), PPAR signaling pathway (GGA03320) and fatty acid degradation (GGA00071) were downregulated in KLF2-overexpression cells (Fig. 4G).

Effect of KLF2 on the promoter activities of function genes in PPAR signaling pathway

To elucidate the influence of KLF2 on the expression of genes in PPAR signaling cascade, its impacts on the promoter activities of PPARγ, C/EBPα, FASN, LPL, FABP4, PLIN1, ACACA and SCD were studied in ICP1 cells. The data indicated that KLF2 overexpression suppressed the promoter activities of PPARγ, C/EBPα, ACACA, and FABP4, whereas it enhanced the promoter activities of chicken FASN, LPL, and PLIN1 (P < 0.05, Fig. 5A,B). Correspondingly, silencing KLF2 expression boosted the promoter activities of PPARγ, C/EBPα, ACACA, and FABP4, while diminishing the promoter activities of FASN, LPL, and PLIN1 (P < 0.05, Fig. 5A,C). In addition, neither KLF2 overexpression nor KLF2 knockdown had a significant effect on the promoter activity of SCD (P > 0.05, Fig. 5A–C).

Discussion

KLF2 has a significant regulatory role in adipogenesis. Mouse studies demonstrated that the mRNA of KLF2 is great expressed in both white and brown adipose tissues22. Myeloid-specific KLF2 knockout mice exhibited hyperphagia and enhanced adiposity23 underscoring the pivotal role of KLF2 in adipogenesis and obesity pathogenesis.

Despite the previous report indicating that KLF2 is highly expressed in human preadipocytes but significantly reduces in mature adipocytes22 there had been no further investigation into the expression profile of KLF2 within human adipose tissue until now. This investigation demonstrated that the KLF2 transcripts were markedly elevated in adipose tissue compared to other tissue types. This finding is consistent with the expression pattern observed in its chicken ortholog revealed both in the previous report13 and chicken GTEx database. The previous report showed that KLF2 does not influence the lineage commitment of multipotent stem cells towards the preadipocyte state but rather sustains the preadipocyte phenotype, thus inhibiting their subsequent differentiation into mature adipocytes24. Thus, the elevated KLF2 expression levels in adipose tissue might be attributable to its predominant expression in preadipocytes rather than in differentiated adipocytes.

Ectopic fat hypothesis posited that adipose tissue varies in metabolic disease risk based on gender and anatomical location25. It was observed that the KLF2 transcripts of human adipose tissues were greater in the males compared to female counterparts, and the KLF2 transcripts showed an age-related increase only in the male subcutaneous adipose tissue, but not in the other human adipose tissues. The varying patterns of KLF2 transcripts in human adipose tissues based on sex and anatomical location suggested a connection between KLF2 expression and the unique biological roles of adipose tissue in various regions. However, subsequent examination revealed a statistically significant positive correlation between KLF2 transcription levels in subcutaneous and visceral adipose tissue depots, despite regional specificity. This inter-depot expression concordance indicates that KLF2 might play a role in the regulation of shared functional modules across distinct adipose structures. It is crucial to note that the absence of phenotypic data, such as body mass index (BMI), left the relationship between KLF2 expression in human adipose tissue and metabolic disease markers unresolved in this study.

Considering the comparatively elevated expression levels of KLF2 in adipose tissue relative to other tissues in both humans and chickens, the immortalized preadipocytes ICP1, derived from chicken abdominal fat tissue and possessed cellular properties akin to primary chicken preadipocytes12,26, were employed to investigate the regulatory mechanisms of KLF2 in the process of adipogenesis. Western blot analysis revealed that the KLF2 expression in ICP1 cells increased at the first 24 h of oleate-induced adipogenic differentiation and subsequently decreased over time. The sustained decrease in KLF2 expression starting from 24 h was aligned with the earlier finding13. In addition, KLF2 overexpression markedly suppressed oleate-induced adipogenic differentiation in ICP1 cells, accompanied by a downregulation in the expression of adipogenic marker genes PPARγ and C/EBPα. This was consistent with previous reports on multiple species13,22, provided another evidence that KLF2 inhibited preadipocyte differentiation in vitro. However, it should be pointed out that the KLF2 expression level in chicken preadipocytes exposed to oleate for 24 h was greater than in untreated cells, indicated that KLF2 might have additional roles beyond inhibiting preadipocyte differentiation.

The impact of KLF2 on the proliferation of preadipocytes remained uncertain. In this study, both overexpression and knockdown studies showed that KLF2 promoted cellular proliferation and the expression of classic proliferation markers PCNA and MKI67 in ICP1 cells, substantiating the role of KLF2 in promoting preadipocyte proliferation. The accurate transition of the cell cycle was crucial for the control of eukaryotic cell proliferation27. Cell cycle analysis using flow cytometry demonstrated that compared to the EV group, cells with KLF2 overexpression showed a lower percentage in the G0/G1 phase and a greater percentage in both the S and G2/M phases, it was possible that KLF2 promoted preadipocyte proliferation by facilitating the transition from G0/G1 phase to S and G2/M phase in cell cycle. In addition, RNA seq analysis showed that the KLF2 overexpression in ICP1 cells boosted the transcription of gene sets related to the kinetochore (GO:0000776), mitotic division (GO:0140014), DNA replication (GGA03030), and cell cycle (GGA04110), further indicated that KLF2 might expedite cell cycle and encourage preadipocyte proliferation by upregulating the expression of genes involved in DNA replication and cell division.

Mitochondrial functionality is a critical determinant of cellular proliferation and adipogenesis28. In addition, KLF2 had been reported as a regulator for mitochondrial biosynthesis29. In this study, the results showed that compared to the EV control group, KLF2 overexpression resulted in a diminution in mitochondrial volume, an increased mitochondrial density and the density of mitochondrial cristae, and elevated ATP levels in ICP1 cells, indicating that mitochondria might be a target for KLF2’s regulation of preadipocyte proliferation and differentiation. The oxidative phosphorylation system within the mitochondria plays a crucial role in cellular metabolism30. The significant enrichment of upregulated DEGs in KLF2-overexpressing cells within GO terms related to mitochondrial membrane components and the respiratory chain, alongside the KEGG pathway for oxidative phosphorylation, suggested that the observed increase in mitochondrial number may be attributed to KLF2’s enhancement of gene expression associated with mitochondrial architecture. Consequently, the observed reduction in mitochondrial volume and increased cristae density in mitochondria of KLF2-overexpressing cells could result from the elevated mitochondrial number, which redistributes the oxidative phosphorylation load. Thus, KLF2 might augment mitochondrial biogenesis and preserve mitochondrial structural integrity and cristae density, at least in part, by modulating the expression of genes related to the mitochondrial inner membrane structure.

Previous research demonstrated that KLF2 can directly influence the transcription of PPARγ to regulate adipogenesis13,22,31. Nonetheless, it remains ambiguous whether KLF2 impacts the expression of downstream functional genes regulated by PPARγ. RNA seq results demonstrated that overexpression of KLF2 led to the downregulation of the expression of gene set of PPAR signaling pathway. In addition, KLF2 regulated the promoter activities of several downstream genes within the PPAR signaling cascade, including PPARγ, C/EBPα, ACACA, FASN, LPL, FABP4 and PLIN1. This indicated that KLF2 might concurrently regulate the expression of multiple downstream functional genes within the PPAR signaling pathway. Additionally, the differential impact of KLF2 on the promoter activities of these genes suggested that KLF2 might modulate adipogenesis not merely by regulating PPARγ expression, but also by affecting the expression of several functional genes within the PPAR signaling pathway.

A positive correlation between KLF2 and GATA2 expression was identified in chicken adipose tissues32. To determine if KLF2 exerts its effects on preadipocytes by modulating GATA2 expression, the transcriptional regulation of GATA2 by KLF2 was investigated in this study. The findings revealed that overexpressing KLF2 markedly enhanced GATA2 promoter activity, whereas knocking down KLF2 expression significantly suppressed it. Additionally, both overexpression and knockout of KLF2 led to corresponding increases and decreases in the mRNA and protein levels of GATA2 in ICP1 cells. Furthermore, ChIP-PCR demonstrated that KLF2 could bind to the promoter region of chicken GATA2. The findings indicated that GATA2 could be a target gene for KLF2 in chicken preadipocytes.

Despite the identification of GATA2 as an upstream negative regulatory factor of PPARγ in vitro33–35 and its influence on the structural integrity of mitochondria in HIB-1B cells35, the gene expression correlation analysis in human adipose tissue conducted in this study revealed a positive association between KLF2 and GATA2 expression and a negative association between KLF2 and PPARγ expression. However, it failed to demonstrate a negative correlation between GATA2 and PPARγ expression. Consequently, the findings of this study were insufficient to substantiate GATA2 as an intermediary linking KLF2 functionality with the PPAR signaling pathway and mitochondrial function (Fig. 7).
