Artificial intelligence strategies based on random forests for detecting ischemia–reperfusion injury changes in kidney tissue during intravital imaging
Authors: Igor Pantic, Jovana Paunovic Pantic, Svetlana Valjarevic, Jelena Cumic, Peiwu Qin, Peter R Corridon
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractThis study presents a supervised machine learning approach using a Random Forest classifier to detect ischemia-reperfusion injury (IRI) in kidney tissue based on intravital two-photon microscopy data. A rodent model of unilateral renal IRI was used, with 30 min of pedicle occlusion followed by 15 min of reperfusion. Continuous imaging captured nuclear (Hoechst 33342), vascular (FITC-dextran), and mitochondrial (TMRM) changes in real time. From extracted video frames, 2000 manually segmented regions of interest (ROIs), 1000 control and 1000 injured segments, were analyzed using texture analysis. Five textural features were used as input: angular second moment (ASM) and inverse difference moment (IDM) from gray-level co-occurrence matrix (GLCM); short run emphasis (SRE) and long run emphasis (LRE) from run length matrix (RLM); and HH wavelet coefficient energy (EnHH) from discrete wavelet transform (DWT). All showed significant differences (p < 0.001) between injured and control tissue. The Random Forest model achieved 79.8% accuracy, a macro F1-score of 0.79, a Matthews Correlation Coefficient of 0.5959, and an ROC AUC of 0.83. These findings highlight the potential of AI-based texture analysis to detect early nuclear and vascular alterations during IRI. Future work should expand datasets, include 3D analyses, and incorporate multimodal imaging for greater generalizability.Keywords: Artificial intelligence, Random forest, Ischemia-reperfusion injury, Kidney, Intravital imagingSubject terms: Nephrons, Biological fluorescence, Machine learning, Fluorescence imaging, Time-lapse imaging

Introduction

Intravital microscopy (IVM) is a modern and powerful imaging method for the detection of physiological and pathological processes in live animals such as mice, rats, or zebrafish. It enables dynamic studies on membrane permeability, intercellular communication, cell migration, blood flow, and immune responses. Today, numerous real-time (intravital) imaging techniques exist, and many of them have been successfully applied in various fields of Biology and Medicine1,2. These include, among others, high-resolution IVM confocal for shallow tissue imaging, multiphoton IVM for deeper tissue penetration, as well as spinning disk IVM, which is particularly useful for the observation of rapid cell movement. Labeling nuclei in live cells and tissues during real-time imaging is a challenging task due to procedural complexity, optical limitations, and imaging platform requirements. Photobleaching, phototoxicity, probe specificity, clearance kinetics, as well as motion and other artifacts, may all limit the effectiveness of IVM, especially in parenchymatous organs such as the kidneys. However, advances in nuclear probes, such as Hoechst 33,342, have helped overcome some of these challenges. Hoechst 33,342 is membrane-permeable, diffuses readily into live cells, and binds specifically to DNA, making it particularly useful for intravital imaging with minimal toxicity and strong nuclear contrast, even in complex and dense renal tissues.

Intravital imaging using Hoechst 33,342, along with other probes like 150 kDa TRITC (Tetramethyl rhodamine Isothiocyanate) - dextran and tetramethyl rhodamine methyl ester (TMRM), has been shown to provide quality visualizations of cell nuclei and adjacent vascular components in both renal cortex and medulla3–5. Hoechst 33,342 (Blue) is a cell-permeable nuclear label that binds to A-T-rich regions of nuclear DNA and may help identify structural changes associated with cell division, DNA damage, and cytotoxicity. 150 kDa TRITC-dextran is a relatively common vascular marker, stains the structures in red color, and has a high molecular weight. Since it stays in the vascular compartment, it can serve as an indicator of certain aspects of blood vessel integrity and permeability6–8. Additionally, TMRM is also a cell-permeant probe that is widely used to assess the relative degree of membrane potential in live cells and tissues. TMRM is a cationic, lipophilic dye that selectively accumulates in active mitochondria due to their negative membrane potential. Its fluorescence intensity is proportional to ΔΨm, making it a sensitive indicator of mitochondrial health, function, and dynamics in real time9. It may be an indicator of barrier breakdown and other changes in vascular permeability and interstitial diffusion. Combination of these 3 dyes may provide a valuable insight into molecular and cellular mechanisms occurring in various animal experimental models, including the ones representing acute kidney injury (AKI).

Introduction of artificial intelligence and machine learning to IVM methods that use the abovementioned markers offers some powerful advantages, which may involve more automated cell detection and tracking, better identification of changes in vascular integrity, as well as more efficient mapping of tissue permeability and diffusion. Machine learning models may be trained to detect discrete alterations in cytoarchitecture, vascular architecture, or even small changes in chromatin organization and distribution10,11. Textural analysis methods, such as the ones based on gray-level co-occurrence matrix (GLCM) and discrete wavelet transform (DWT) algorithms, can provide an abundance of data related to second-order statistical and spatial features of tissue segments12. This especially applies to heterogeneous tissue structures such as the kidney, where even minor ischemic injury can lead to significant changes in the values of GLCM quantifiers such as angular second moment or inverse difference moment13.

Among the supervised machine learning models, random forests are particularly suitable for the purposes of classifying cell structure based on textural features11,14. These ensemble decision-tree-based models can handle various high-dimensional data (including GLCM and DWT) in two-dimensional signals obtained through intravital imaging, and may be adept at capturing non-linear spatial relationships that are often present in these experimental settings. Random forests also function well with smaller datasets, which may be an advantage when compared to more complex artificial neural networks15–17. Also, previous research indicated that random forests may be useful for cell classification in the conditions of programmed cell death18. In this work, a concept of a random forest model that is trained to identify regions of interest (ROIs) of rat kidney tissue after induction of ischemia-reperfusion injury (IRI) is described. The model simulates a moderate, acute renal injury and relies on input data derived from textural indicators of regions of interest (ROIs) obtained via intravital imaging. These ROIs are visualized using nuclear (Hoechst 33342), vascular (TRITC-dextran), and mitochondrial (TMRM) fluorescent probes. On a limited ROI sample, the model shows acceptable values of classification accuracy, area under the receiver operating characteristic curve, and other performance quantifiers.

Materials and methods

Unilateral IRI model

All experimental protocols were approved by the Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee (IACUC) at the School of Medicine, Indiana University, and the Animal Research Oversight Committee (AROC) at Khalifa University of Science and Technology. The study was carried out in compliance with the ARRIVE guidelines. All methods were performed in accordance with these guidelines and regulations. Male Sprague-Dawley rats (200–400 g; Harlan Laboratories, Indianapolis, IN, USA) were anesthetized with 5% isoflurane in oxygen and then administered an intraperitoneal injection of pentobarbital (50 mg/kg; Hospira, Lake Forest, IL, USA) for surgical anesthesia. To induce unilateral renal ischemia, the left kidney was exposed through a flank incision. A ligature was placed around the renal pedicle to occlude arterial and venous blood flow for approximately 30 min, modelling ischemic conditions known to trigger cell death pathways of interest.

Intravital two-photon microscopy

Intravital imaging was performed using two-photon microscopy to assess dynamic changes in nuclear structure and mitochondrial function. Prior to imaging, the following fluorescent probes were administered via intravenous bolus through a jugular vein catheter: 150 kDa fluorescein isothiocyanate (FITC)–dextran (TdB Labs, Uppsala, Sweden): 50 µL of a 20 mg/mL stock diluted in 0.5–1 mL of saline; Hoechst 33,342 (Invitrogen, Carlsbad, CA): 30–50 µL diluted in 0.5 mL saline; and TMRM (Invitrogen Molecular Probes, Eugene, OR): 2.5 µg diluted in 1 mL saline.

Following dye infusion, the left kidney was externalized and immobilized for imaging. Fluorescence data were acquired using an Olympus FV1000-MPE microscope (Center Valley, PA) equipped with a 60× water-immersion objective. Excitation was provided by a Spectra-Physics MaiTai DeepSee Ti: Sapphire laser tuned to 770–860 nm to allow simultaneous detection of blue (Hoechst), green (FITC-dextran), and red (TMRM) emissions. All dyes were administered approximately 5–10 min before the onset of ischemia. Continuous imaging was performed through the 30 min of ischemia and 15 min post-ischemia to monitor nuclear dynamics and mitochondrial membrane potential in real time.

Gray-level co-occurrence matrix analysis

From the intravital microscopy video obtained in a single animal experiment, separate frames of the renal cortex before and after ischemia-reperfusion damage have been created. In this study, ‘control’ ROIs were defined as regions extracted from the same cortical segment of the kidney prior to ischemia–reperfusion injury, serving as baseline references. The frames were converted to BMP format and had dimensions of 512 × 512 pixels, bit depth of 24 (Fig. 1). Each ROI was defined as a 400 × 400 pixel square randomly placed within 512 × 512 pixel frames. From the frames, a total of 2000 manually delineated ROIs (1000 ROIs of the damaged tissue and 1000 ROIs of the controls) were analyzed using GLCM, RLM, and DWT methods. ROIs were extracted from sequential frames recorded in the same cortical region during the ischemia–reperfusion process, thereby capturing tissue alterations as they evolved over time. The relatively large size of the ROIs, their overlap, and their distribution across frames contributed to the broad coverage of the imaged field and minimized the risk of sampling bias. Since the ROIs were randomly selected rather than chosen based on visible pathology, both subtle and overt structural changes were represented. On the other hand, the time-dependent nature of the injury within the same cortical area further reduced potential inter- and intra-rater variability. All 3 analyses were done in MaZda software (Institute of Electronics, Technical University of Lodz, Poland), application implemented in C++ and Delphi©, previously developed within the framework of the European COST B11 initiative “Quantitative Analysis of Magnetic Resonance Image Texture” (1998–2002) and further extended under the COST B21 project “Physiological Modelling of MR Image Formation”19–22.

As MaZda requires grayscale input for GLCM, RLM, and wavelet-based calculations, the software automatically performed RGB-to-grayscale conversion prior to feature extraction. By default, MaZda applies the standard luminance-based transformation (0.299R + 0.587G + 0.114B), which preserves intensity information from all three color channels. While this conversion may reduce certain chromatic information, it retains the spatial intensity distribution necessary for textural quantification.

Run length matrix analysis

The Run Length Matrix (RLM), or more precisely the Gray-Level Run Length Matrix (GLRLM) is yet another powerful texture and image analysis method that quantifies the length and intensity of consecutive runs of resolution units30,31. It differs from GLCM because it does not focus on unit pairs, but rather on runs which are essentially sequences of identical gray values of resolution units. Here, the matrix of elements R(g, r) is also created based on run lengths, r and gray levels, g. Each element is the frequency on occurrence of lengths, r with the intensity, g. Prior to statistical testing, normality of feature distributions was assessed using the Shapiro–Wilk test, which indicated significant deviations from normality for all five selected features. Therefore, comparisons between damaged and control ROIs were performed using the non-parametric Mann–Whitney U test.

Rationale for feature selection

As mentioned before, a total of five texture features were selected a priori for analysis: Angular Second Moment, Inverse Difference Moment, Short Run Emphasis, Long Run Emphasis, and wavelet HH subband energy. These descriptors were chosen based on their complementary roles in quantifying texture homogeneity (inverse difference moment), uniformity (angular second moment), fine versus coarse structural patterns (RLM features), and high-frequency subcellular detail (wavelet energy, Table 1). Since the dataset was limited in size, no automated attribute selection or filtering was applied, since the addition of large numbers of features could introduce redundancy and increase the risk of overfitting without improving classification performance.

Random forest machine learning

Random forest (RF) machine learning model was proposed to include five abovementioned quantifiers as inputs. The model was designed as a ROI classifier for outputs equaling 1 (damaged tissue) and 0 (controls). The ensemble method is based on a multitude of decision trees and uses bootstrapping in order for each tree to receive a random subset of the sample for training36–38. The code was developed in Python programming language and its Scikit-Learn library39 in Google Collaboratory environment using Google central processing unit (CPU). The code first uploads and reads an Excel file with the dataset after which the stratified sampling is done so that both classes are proportionally represented in the subset. The dataset was divided into training and testing subsets using an 80:20 stratified split to preserve class balance. The training set was used for hyperparameter optimization of the Random Forest classifier via a randomized search with 5-fold stratified cross-validation. The final optimized model was then evaluated on the independent test set. Five folds were chosen as a standard compromise between bias and variance, so that each fold retained the class distribution while at the same time providing sufficiently large test sets to obtain stable performance estimates. Model performance was assessed using multiple metrics, including F1-score, Matthews Correlation Coefficient (MCC), ROC AUC, overall accuracy, and confusion matrices.

For this proof-of-concept study Random Forest was selected as the classifier due to its potential robustness in small-sample settings, its ability to capture non-linear feature interactions, and its relative interpretability compared to alternative models. These traits would make RD particularly suitable for microscopy image analysis where data volume is limited and biological interpretability is important. Comparative analyses with additional classifiers will be performed in future research once larger and more diverse datasets become available.

Results

Texture analysis quantifiers

The Angular Second Moment showed a marked difference between the two groups. In damaged tissue ROIs, the ASM had a mean value of 0.0526 ± 0.0192, whereas in the control group it was significantly lower, averaging 0.0253 ± 0.0197 (Fig. 2). The difference between these groups was highly statistically significant (p < 0.001). This result implies that damaged tissues exhibit greater local textural uniformity. Similarly, the Inverse Difference Moment was also elevated in the damaged tissue. The mean IDM in the damaged group was 0.7853 ± 0.0686, compared to 0.6780 ± 0.0779 in the control group. This difference showed strong statistical significance (p < 0.001), reinforcing the interpretation that ischemia-reperfusion injury may lead to the reduction of fine-grained local variability and instead increase more uniform spatial intensity distributions.

Mean values ± standard deviations (SD) of five texture descriptors derived from gray-level co-occurrence matrix (Angular second moment, Inverse difference moment), run length matrix (Short run emphasis, Long run emphasis), and Discrete wavelet transform (HH subband energy) analyses. Data are shown separately for control ROIs and damaged tissue ROIs. Statistical comparisons between groups yielded p < 0.001 for all features.

ROIs from damaged tissue demonstrated were markedly higher LRE values (27.10 ± 11.09) compared to controls (11.22 ± 11.59), with a highly significant p-value (p < 0.001). In contrast, SRE was significantly lower in the damaged group, where its average value was 0.6194 ± 0.0462 compared to 0.6876 ± 0.0550 in controls (p < 0.001). These changes may be related to early signs of tissue degradation and attenuation of fine structural elements often observed in IRI. The changes in these quantifiers were probably the consequence of a loss of microtextural detail in damaged cortical regions.

The Wavelet Energy of the HH Subband, derived from Discrete Wavelet Transform analysis, in damaged ROIs was 0.1520 ± 0.1983. In comparison, control tissues exhibited significantly higher values (0.4228 ± 0.3067, p < 0.001). This energy is indicative of high-frequency diagonal details in the two-dimensional signal, so we may speculate that IRI led to the reduction of fine diagonal features related to complex microstructural arrangements in healthy tissue. Collapse of collagen frameworks or partial change in directional tissue organization may have been some of the factor contributing to this change. In summary, all five extracted texture features—across GLCM, RLM, and DWT domains sowed robust and statistically significant separation between the two classes of ROIs, making them potentially good candidates for the development of a machine learning classifier.

Random forest machine learning model

The Random Forest classification model was successfully developed and capable at least partially to separate damaged from control tissue ROIs. After optimization of hyperparameters, using a Randomized Search Cross-Validation approach with a 5-fold stratified configuration, the following values of hyperparameters were shown to achieve the best performance: 200 estimators, logarithmic feature selection (max_features=’log2’), maximum tree depth of 5, minimum samples per split set to 2, minimum samples per leaf set to 4, and bootstrapping enabled. This configuration enabled a certain balance between model complexity and generalizability.

A total of 2,000 ROIs (1,000 control and 1,000 injured) were used for model development. Of these, 1,600 ROIs (80%) formed the training set for hyperparameter optimization, while 400 ROIs (200 from each category; 20%) were reserved as an independent test set. All performance metrics reported below were calculated on this independent test set unless otherwise specified. The performance evaluation of the RF model using this configuration of hyperparameters and 5-fold cross-validation showed a classification accuracy of 79.8%. The model obtained a precision of 0.78 and recall of 0.82 for control tissue (class 0), and precision of 0.81 with recall of 0.77 for damaged tissue (class 1). The values of F1 scores for the control and damaged groups equaled 0.80 and 0.79, respectively, with the macro-averaged F1 score of 0.7918. This relatively high value of the macro-averaged F1 score suggested that there was a balanced performance across both classes. The Matthews Correlation Coefficient (MCC) of the Random Forest model equaled 0.5959, which suggested the ability to identify meaningful structural distinctions beyond random chance with modest residual misclassification.

The confusion matrix showed that the random forest gave correct predictions for 165 control ROIs and 154 damaged ROIs, while misclassifying 35 control and 46 damaged ROIs. The misclassification is somewhat expected given the context of histopathological variability of the kidney tissue during the onset of IRI. The model’s Receiver Operating Characteristic Area Under the Curve (ROC AUC) was 0.83, which highlighted the relatively strong discriminatory power of the random forest model (Fig. 3). This also suggested a relatively good capability of the model to distinguish between the two ROI groups over varying classification thresholds. In other words, in scenarios where probability-based decision-making is needed, the model has good chances of performing well.

Discussion

This study proposes and develops a supervised machine learning model based on random forest algorithm for the identification of damaged kidney tissue after induction of IRI. The model performed relatively well in terms of its discriminatory ability to separate damaged from intact tissue ROIs and showed acceptable classification accuracy, value of Matthews correlation coefficient and F1-score. To the best of our knowledge, this is the first supervised machine learning model for IRI identification that uses gray level co-occurrence matrix and other textural features as inputs based on intravital data that captured live injury-based events. Also, this is the first model to be trained on imaging data resulting from intravital imaging with Hoechst 33,342, TRITC - dextran, and TMRM fluorescent dyes.

Renal IRI occurs when the blood supply is first restricted and then suddenly restored. Initial cessation of blood flow (ischemia) leads to oxygen deprivation, ATP depletion, loss of ion gradients, programmed cell death and necrosis8,40–42. Proximal tubule cells and cells located in outer medulla are most commonly affected. The damage caused by initial ischemia paradoxically increases with the return of blood flow. This reperfusion phase of IRI is associated with increased generation of reactive oxygen species and inflammatory responses. Microvascular injury, derived from thrombosis with severe endothelial barrier disruption may also be present. In clinical settings, IRI is often observed in shock, major surgery and any other conditions that may cause AKI. In fundamental medical sciences, IRI is usually simulated in rodents and is widely accepted experimental model in animals for studying AKI, capturing key human AKI features such as ROS generation, inflammation and cell death.

Both GLCM and DWT are powerful texture-analysis methods and in certain conditions can recognize structural damage in tissues and cells. Quantifiers of textural uniformity such as angular second moment and local homogeneity such as inverse difference moment may be sensitive to changes in chromatin structure and distribution which are often present in early stages of apoptosis and necrosis12,43. Similarly, discrete wavelet coefficient energies of nuclear structures may be significantly altered when cell is exposed to toxic chemical environment44. When GLCM and DWT analyses are applied together, they may provide complementary information on spatial texture as well as edge and frequency patterns in biological structures. In previous studies, both techniques were used to feed a variety of supervised machine learning models in order to classify or grade pathological structures not only in histology and microscopy but also in computerized tomography and magnetic resonance imaging.

Increased values of Angular Second Moment (ASM) and Inverse Difference Moment (IDM) in damaged tissue may indicate greater local homogeneity of pixel intensities. Biologically, this may correspond to chromatin condensation during early apoptosis, cytoplasmic swelling, or loss of fine cytoskeletal detail, all of which lead to more uniform fluorescence distributions within the affected cells. Run-length–based features may offer additional insights into tissue reorganization. Short Run Emphasis (SRE) might indicate the predominance of fine textures, which may correspond to mitochondrial fragmentation or granular accumulation of fluorescent probes in injured tissue. In contrast, Long Run Emphasis (LRE) highlights the emergence of larger homogeneous regions, which can arise from cytoskeletal collapse, edema, or vascular barrier disruption leading to patchy perfusion and uniform labeling.

Discrete wavelet transform coefficient energies have been previously suggested as suitable candidates for input data for the training of random forest model in chromatin distribution analysis45. In a recently published work, a hypothetical model, very similar to the one used in our present study was proposed. The code was created in Python Scikit-learn library and apart from DWT parameters, included fractal dimension and lacunarity as attribute data. Hyperparameters that could be tuned included the number of features to consider when searching for the best split, the minimum number of samples present at a leaf node and the maximum depth of the tree, among others. It was suggested that, if adequately trained and tested the classifier could identify cells undergoing the early stages of programmed cell death.

In our previous work on Ischemia-reperfusion injury, GLCM was used in combination with fractal analysis to identify structural changes in kidney medulla46. The work was done in Wistar albino mice and digital micrographs were obtained from Periodic acid-Schiff (PAS) - stained kidney sections. Indicators such as fractal dimension, lacunarity, angular second moment, entropy, inverse difference moment, GLCM contrast, and GLCM correlation showed relatively good potential when considering the individual areas below receiver operating characteristic curves. Even without integration of machine learning GLCM was shown to be a powerful method for differentiation between normal and damaged tissue. In the future it would be worthwhile to consider applying fractal analysis as a supplement to GLCM, DWT, and RLM methods for generating input parameters for a random forest model capable of identifying IRI-related damage.

In another work on ischemia–reperfusion injury, we combined gray level co-occurrence matrix and wavelet analyses to identify subtle alterations in proximal tubule cell nuclei12. In an animal model of mild AKI (male Sprague Dawley rats), GLCM and DWT analyses were done on ROIs of the renal cortex tissue stained using the conventional hematoxylin and eosin (H&E) method. Hypothetical random forest, support vector machine (SVM), and binomial logistic regression and machine learning models were proposed, and the highest classification accuracy (79%) was calculated for the RF algorithm. The model also had excellent discriminatory power with the area under the receiver operating characteristics curve of 0.86. Similarly to our study, the GLCM and DWT analyses were performed in MaZda software of the Technical University of Lodz, Institute of Electronics, Lodz, Poland.

Our work did not use deep learning–based classifiers such as convolutional neural networks (CNNs), although this approach is often popular in experimental settings where image analysis is performed. The main reason for opting for a decision tree method rather than deep learning is the relatively small size of the available dataset, which would substantially increase the risk of severe overfitting when training neural network architectures. Random forest models are more suitable for this type of sample, particularly when trained on textural inputs that quantify biologically meaningful structural alterations. Random forests also provide some degree of interpretability of the input features, which is often difficult to achieve with “black-box” deep learning models. Nevertheless, this does not exclude the possibility of future applications of deep learning or its combination with decision tree–based models to create hybrid pipelines. A combination of traditional texture descriptors with CNN-derived features could also further enhance the classification accuracy of the machine learning model.Our study had several limitations that could impact generalizability, performance, or interpretation. First, the model was trained on a relatively small dataset of ROI which may reduce statistical power of the results. Although random forests perform on small samples much better compared to the alternatives such as artificial neural networks, there is still some risk of overfitting. Future studies, apart from increasing the dataset would also need to include rigorous independent external validation in order to develop a robust model applicable in everyday research practice.

The lack of external validation and the reliance on a single dataset derived from intravital microscopy may indeed limit the broader impact of the present results. Our primary objective, however, was to establish a proof-of-concept by demonstrating that textural features can serve as meaningful input data for the development of supervised machine learning models capable of identifying discrete changes associated with the early stages of kidney injury. To the best of our knowledge, this represents the first attempt to apply such an approach, and currently no independent dataset exists that could be used for external validation of the model. Future studies will build upon this foundation by incorporating data from additional experimental conditions and, ideally, from independent laboratories and research teams.

The next limitation refers to the use of two-dimensional projections which are not able to completely capture complex spatial relationships present in a three-dimensional biological structure such as the tissue. This is a common problem in both conventional and fluorescence microscopy, and although there are ways to create a 3D model of a tissue, subtle or volumetric changes may still evade detection. Also, the potential methodological problem that future studies will need to address is the experimental variability in regard to imaging conditions and biological processes present during the experiment. Dye concentration, photobleaching and microscope settings can all substantially affect the values of GLCM, RLM and DWT features which in turn reflects on the decreased reproducibility of the model. Also, as a potential issue, one should mention the potential data loss during the conversion of the intravital microscopy images from RGB to grayscale prior to texture analysis. Color differences can sometimes reflect biologically relevant features (e.g., distinct fluorescent probes or varying signal intensities). However, in this case, we may assume that the luminance-based conversion implemented in MaZda software did preserve at least the majority of the original intensity distribution.

Random forest algorithm itself has certain limitations that may hinder its applicability in research practice, and they are mainly related to its limited interpretability, sensitivity to noisy features, computational inefficiency and overfitting with insufficient regularization. Random forests may be considered as “black box” models because feature interactions in a mechanistic way can never be fully understood47–49. This is mainly because of the complexity and ensemble nature of the model which combines multiple decision trees to make a prediction. It is true that random forests can handle multidimensional data relatively well, but irrelevant or noisy variables may still degrade its performance and generalizability. Also, although on small dataset such as ours, the model is relatively easy to develop, it may still require significant processing power and other computational resources when applied to a database that would have tens of thousands or hundreds of thousands of statistical units of measurement. Finally, datasets with high feature-to-sample ratios may cause the random forest to overfit unless sufficient Regularization is done during the training process.

Conclusions

This work focuses on the feasibility of using a random forest model to classify regions of interest of rat kidney tissue following ischemia-reperfusion injury when the model is trained on textural input data. Intravital imaging with Hoechst, TRITC-dextran, and TMRM can provide an abundance of two-dimensional data that can be analyzed using gray-level co-occurrence matrix, run-length matrix and discrete wavelet transform methods. The resulting model might achieve acceptable classification performance, even on a limited sample size. The results highlight the potential of supervised machine learning approaches based on ensemble decision tree algorithms for detection of discrete alterations associated with cellular and vascular damage in kidneys. Future work should aim to overcome the limitations of the model by expanding the dataset, and the addition of other imaging modalities or modeling techniques.
