Toward energy-efficient sour gas treatment: exergy-based modeling and response surface methodology optimization of a diethanolamine absorption process
Authors: Hossein Yarmohammadi, Aysan Ghahremani, Hassan Pahlavanzadeh
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractGas sweetening is the removal of acid gases, such as CO2 and H2S, from natural gas using amine solvents. This process is widely used in the petroleum industry but is often energy-intensive. Thus, it is important to evaluate and improve process efficiency. In the present study, an exergy analysis is conducted on a diethanolamine-based gas sweetening unit. The goal is to identify inefficiencies and guide optimization. The process is modeled in Aspen HYSYS and validated against design data. The analysis shows that the absorber column causes the most exergy destruction, about 77.4 MW. To improve performance, key operating variables, such as solvent circulation rate, diethanolamine concentration, and solvent inlet temperature to the absorber, are first evaluated individually by sensitivity analysis. These variables are then optimized together using Response Surface Methodology. Under optimized conditions, total exergy destruction drops to about 73.8 MW. This is about a 5% improvement in exergy efficiency while meeting all process requirements. These results illustrate that multivariable optimization can improve the thermodynamic performance of the gas sweetening process.Keywords: Gas sweetening, Diethanolamine, Exergy, Energy, Response surface methodologySubject terms: Energy science and technology, Engineering, Mathematics and computing

Introduction

Associated gas is a form of natural gas that is found either dissolved in the oil or as a free gas above the oil in the reservoir. This type of gas was released as a waste product from the petroleum extraction industry and mostly burned off in the gas flares. Associated gas flaring has presented a challenge in the oil industry due to the waste of a valuable fuel source and the emission of pollutants into the atmosphere, despite global warming restrictions1,2. Natural gas, as an essential energy source, must be purified of contaminants that affect fuel quality and energy efficiency. H2S and CO2 are important impurities that cause problems such as corrosion, health hazards, and environmental hazards3,4. Gas sweetening processes are required to comply with environmental regulations and overcome chemical challenges such as corrosion, erosion, and plugging5,6.

Amine absorption (alkanolamine scrubbing) remains the industry standard for removing H2S and CO2 from sour natural gas: the sour feed is contacted with an aqueous amine solvent in an absorber where acid gases chemically react or are physically absorbed, producing a rich amine that is subsequently regenerated by steam-heated desorption to release the acid gas and recycle the lean solvent. Choice of amine (MEA, DEA, MDEA, blended systems such as MDEA + PZ) is driven by required selectivity, loading and regeneration energy tertiary amines like MDEA are widely used when high H2S selectivity and lower reboiler heat duty are desired, while activators (e.g., piperazine) or blends increase CO2 capacity and/or overall absorption rates7–10.

Operational performance and energy consumption are strongly influenced by solvent formulation and process configuration. Among industrially available alkanolamines, diethanolamine (DEA) is selected as the amine agent in this study. DEA is a widely used alkanolamine in industrial gas sweetening processes, since it has an acceptable balance between absorption capacity, reaction kinetics, and regeneration energy requirement. Compared with primary amines such as monoethanolamine (MEA), DEA has lower reaction heat with acid gases, which results in reduced stripping energy. On the other hand, tertiary amines such as MDEA generally work with lower regeneration energy; they typically require blends or activators to achieve sufficient reaction rates for CO2 removal purposes11.

DEA amine systems represent an intermediate and practical unit that provides a real trade-off assessment between regeneration energy consumption and absorption performance. Additionally, its widespread industrial use and thermodynamic behavior make it a suitable benchmark for exergy performance evaluation and optimization studies in amine gas sweetening processes.

Operational performance and energy consumption are strongly influenced by solvent formulation and process configuration: studies based on real-plant data show that optimized MDEA/PZ blends can enhance acid-gas loading markedly and reduce steam, cooling and pumping utilities compared with single amines, while process tuning (circulation rate, lean solvent temperature, absorber design) is critical to meet tight H2S specifications without excessive energy penalty. The literature also stresses that membrane or hybrid membrane–amine arrangements can be attractive alternatives or intensification options in specific cases but that conventional amine absorption still offers the most mature technical/economic balance for many gas-treating applications7,8,10.

As it mentioned, the amine gas sweetening process is mostly used for acid gas removal, this process is energy-intensive, particularly for amine regeneration section12. The process modification is required to reduce energy consumption and, consequently, operating costs13. However, the amount of H2S and CO2 in treated gas becomes under the allowable discharge concentration in the atmosphere14,15.

Exergy analysis is a useful technique for evaluating the thermodynamic performance of a process, identifying ways to operate the system more efficiently, and discussing the characteristics that affect its efficiency16–19. Some studies on exergy analysis for gas sweetening and CO2 capture have been reviewed, and their achievements are presented below.

Feyzi et al. performed an exergy analysis of the CO2 removal process from syngas using methyldiethanolamine (MDEA) activated with piperazine. Their results showed that the lowest exergy loss returns to the pump, followed by the heat recovery section, the stripper column, the absorber column, and the flasher. To reduce exergy loss, they increased the piperazine concentration, thereby reducing the solvent flow rate20.

Zarkesh et al. have used a new method in the gas sweetening process to optimize energy consumption and reduce exergy loss. According to the results, the proposed method reduced the energy consumption of the basic process by 67%. Also, in comparison with the basic process, the exergy loss of the novel process decreased by 66.5%21.

The simulation and optimization of an existing natural gas refinery plant using a lean vapor-compression method have been presented by Sun et al. The simulation results indicate that process requires 2.73 GJ/ton acid gas for solvent regeneration, with exergy destruction of 14,120.59 kW in the solvent regeneration section. The proposed method demonstrates significant improvements in the aforementioned parameters. Specifically, energy for solvent regeneration and exergy destruction in the solvent regeneration section are reduced by 16.12% and 25.04%, respectively22.

Nejat et al. compared exergy analyses for a gas treatment plant using the MDEA solvent and another physical-chemical solvent, namely sulfinol-M (a mixture of MDEA and sulfolane with water). Their results showed that acid gases absorption by sulfinol-M requires less energy and cost23.

Sernapour et al. present a new exergy analysis method to study the energy and exergy behavior in natural gas sweetening. They examine a mixture of Mono Ethanol Amine (MEA), Di Ethanol Amine (DEA), and Piperazine (PZ) to better understand process exergy. The study identifies the optimal conditions for efficiently capturing CO2 while using less energy for regeneration. The exergy analysis shows that the regenerator unit has the highest exergy destruction, followed by the heat exchanger and the cooler. These results can help improve natural gas processing, lower environmental impact, support sustainable energy, and promote more responsible fossil fuel use24.

Eyvazi et al. used vapor recompression (VRC) and bottom flashing (BF) to improve heat integration in their research. They compared these methods to traditional approaches by simulating four different procedures and measuring energy use, exergy efficiency, exergy loss, and CO2 emissions. The results show that BF heat integration is better than conventional methods because it greatly lowers energy use, exergy loss, and CO2 emissions, and improves exergy efficiency, mainly by reducing the need for hot and cold utilities in the recovery section25.

Nouri et al. created a system with low CO2 emissions by combining biomass gasification, MEA-based CO2 capture, and methanol synthesis for waste-to-energy and power-to-fuel applications. The system generates 75 MW of power, 0.545 kg/s of methanol, and 40 MW of heat. After optimization, its energy and exergy efficiencies are 53.7% and 31.91%, respectively. By applying a multi-objective Genetic Algorithm, the life cycle cost drops from $435.3 million to $399.6 million. The study reports a CO2 capture rate of 59.24%, and notes that hydrogen production remains the main challenge in methanol synthesis26.

Dwiyantoro et al. compared two carbon capture methods: Supersonic Separator and Chemical Absorption using Methyldiethanolamine (MDEA). They used Aspen HYSYS to simulate natural gas processing plants. In fields with high CO2 content, the Supersonic Separator achieved a maximum exergy efficiency of 74.01% since it does not need solvent regeneration. Chemical Absorption removed more CO2 from methane-rich streams, especially at high pressure (51.34 bar) and flow rate (50 MMSCFD)27.

Kazmi et al. explored using 1-butyl-4-methylimidazolium acetate with amine solvents like methyl diethanolamine (MDEA), 2-amino-2-methyl-1-propanol (AMP), and diisopropylamine (DIPA) to produce high-purity biomethane (≥ 99 wt%) and improve efficiency. By combining biogas upgrading with combined heat and power (CHP) technology, the system met energy needs and generated sustainable electricity. The best results were obtained with 1-butyl-4-methylimidazolium acetate and 2-amino-2-methyl-1-propanol. While this system used less energy, it still had issues, such as ionic liquid loss. Exergy analysis showed it reduced energy waste by lowering exergy destruction, making the process more efficient28.

Recent examinations have increasingly integrated advanced optimization methods with thermodynamic-based and exergy analyses to enhance the performance of intricate energy systems. Hybrid predictive techniques, including Response Surface Methodology (RSM), Artificial Neural Networks (ANN), and Genetic Algorithms (GA), are effective in identifying optimal operating conditions and minimizing energy consumption and costs. For instance, a novel biogas-fueled cogeneration system integrating the Kalina cycle, Organic Rankine Cycle (ORC), and vapor compression refrigeration cycle was recently optimized using RSM and artificial neural networks (ANN) coupled with a genetic algorithm based on energy efficiency (EE) and total cost of the plant (TCOP) as a two-objective optimization approach. The study reported high forecast precision with errors less than 2%, while exergy destruction analysis identified the combustion chamber and heat recovery unit as the primary sources of irreversibility29.

Multi-objective optimization methods have also been used for fuel-cell-based thermal cogeneration systems to simultaneously address thermodynamic, economic, and environmental objectives. One recent study introduced a CH4-fueled SOFC hybrid power and cooling system that uses an Organic Flash Cycle and an ejector refrigeration unit to efficiently recover waste heat. In this study, ANN models trained on RSM-generated data were combined with Genetic Algorithms to improve thermal efficiency, lower the system’s levelized cost, and cut carbon dioxide emissions. The results showed significant gains in both thermal and exergy efficiencies, along with improved economic and environmental performance. Exergy analysis also showed that the SOFC and the thermoelectric generator were the main sources of irreversibility in the system30.

Advanced optimization strategies have also been applied to hybrid solar-geothermal renewable energy systems involving multiple interacting operational variables. In Rostamnejad et al.‘s study, RSM, ANN, and GA techniques were simultaneously employed to predict and optimize system performance, including thermal efficiency, released carbon dioxide rate, and levelized cost of the plant using seven independent decision variables. Moreover, the use of actual solar radiation data (ParsaAbad-e-Moghan case study) provided valuable insights into the model’s viability and the potential to reduce environmental impact31.

A recent study introduced a backfill body cascaded latent heat storage system to boost exergy efficiency and thermal performance in mine geothermal phase change heat storage. The researchers developed a thermofluid-phase change-coupled model and exhibited how inter-stage temperature gradients affect the system’s heat storage and release. They used RSM and the Non-dominated Sorting Genetic Algorithm II (NSGA-II) for multi-objective optimization, accounting for thermal efficiency, heat release exergy efficiency, entransy dissipation, inter-stage temperature gradient, PCM backfill ratio, and working fluid inlet velocity. The optimized model, which couples energy, exergy, and entransy, offers a method and insights for optimizing geothermal energy storage systems in mines32.

Assareh et al. proposed an integrated solar-driven tri-generation system (heating, cooling, electricity) for passenger trains combining photovoltaic-thermal (PVT) panels, an organic Rankine cycle, an absorption chiller, and a water-heating unit. The system performance was evaluated using an exergoeconomic framework coupled with a two-stage optimization approach integrating RSM and Artificial Neural Networks (ANN). Exergy analysis identified the PVT panels, absorption chiller, and evaporator as the main sources of exergy destruction, while the optimization process improved both exergy efficiency and system cost rate. The findings contribute to the development of low-carbon rail transport solutions33.

An other study introduced a methanol-based tri-generation system that combines a high-temperature proton exchange membrane fuel cell (HT-PEMFC) with a high-temperature electrochemical hydrogen pump (HT-EHP) to produce electricity, hydrogen, and heat simultaneously. The researchers used a RSM surrogate model along with the Non-dominated Sorting Genetic Algorithm II (NSGA-II) to optimize the system for net power output, hydrogen production, and cost using Pareto solutions. They also carried out a thorough 4E analysis, covering energy, exergy, economic, and environmental aspects. The optimized system illustrated significant improvements in exergy efficiency, hydrogen purity, and economic performance while maintaining low environmental emissions34.

Eventually, a robust study by Ata investigated a gas turbine cycle-based multi-energy system capable of simultaneously producing power, heating, cooling, freshwater, hydrogen, and ammonia. The system was evaluated using integrated energy, exergy, economic, environmental, and exergo-environmental analyses. Regression models were created for five different responses depending on the decision parameters. With these response values, bi-objective, tri-objective, and multi-objective optimization studies, including all responses, were developed by RSM. Based on all response values, the system’s 4E performance was enhanced by approximately 10%35.

The review of the studies reveals that different methods have been used to identify improved values of effective parameters and their interactions to enhance exergy efficiency. One significant challenge in this field seems to be the lack of information on using RSM as an optimization method. RSM is beneficial for optimizing multiple parameters simultaneously and capturing their interactions, while general optimization methods often neglect. Since general optimization methods typically optimize a single parameter and ignore parameter interactions, they do not perform well36.

Using the design of experiments, in addition to saving time and cost, significantly reduces the number of tests37. Therefore, a combination of statistical and mathematical techniques, namely RSM, is used to model and analyze problems involving multiple variable38,39. Among available experimental design methods, Central Composite Design (CCD) is used to build second- and third-order response surface models to estimate the parameters using the least-squares method40–42.

Compared with AI-based optimization methods that require substantial computing power, RSM offers a practical, easy-to-understand way to understand how different operating variables interact. It also helps reduce both computational demands and experimental work. By combining exergy analysis with RSM, it becomes easier to identify inefficient processes and develop strategies that use less energy in industrial gas sweetening units. Many studies have examined overall system performance, but have not examined how operating factors interact to influence exergy destruction. Applying RSM in industrial gas sweetening units is a practical and efficient approach to determining optimal operating conditions and improving energy efficiency.

In summary, this study presents an exergy analysis of a gas sweetening unit to measure exergy destruction in each process component and find the main performance bottlenecks. The research examined how three main operating parameters, the circulating solvent flow rate, DEA concentration, and the inlet solvent temperature to the absorber, affect the system. RSM was then applied to identify the optimal operating conditions for minimizing exergy destruction, and a statistical model was developed to predict the system’s behavior with respect to exergy destruction.

Process description

The objective of the gas sweetening units is to remove CO2 and H2S compounds from a sour gas stream. The amine process is widely recognized as one of the most effective and commonly used methods in the gas industry. It employs alkanolamine solutions as chemical solvents to remove acid gases from natural gas streams. Alkanolamines have a strong affinity for acid gases, and several types are used, including monoethanolamine (MEA) and diethanolamine (DEA). The process that is studied in this paper is based on the DEA solution.

Following the selection of DEA as the solvent, the feed gas enters the unit at 23.5 °C, 42.31 bara, and a flow rate of 3973 kmol/h. The treated (sweet) gas is subsequently sent to the dehydration unit for further processing. Exergy analysis

While the first law of thermodynamics enables the quantitative assessment of energy within a process, it does not account for differences in energy quality. The second law of thermodynamics defines the direction of heat and work transfer, but likewise does not quantify the quality of energy. Exergy analysis, which combines the principles of the first and second laws under specified environmental conditions, provides a comprehensive means to evaluate the quality and usability of energy. Therefore, exergy analysis is an essential tool for optimizing chemical process performance46.

Exergy represents the maximum useful work that can be obtained from a system at a given state and environmental condition46. Exergy analysis is a valuable tool for assessing the overall thermodynamic efficiency of systems47 and evaluating the work potential and, therefore, the quality of energy20,48. To enhance system efficiency, exergy analysis can be applied to identify and minimize exergy losses in each unit of a plant, thereby improving the energy’s ability to perform useful work49–51. In steady-flow systems, exergy can be divided into two main components: thermomechanical and chemical. Thermomechanical exergy is further classified into physical, kinetic, and potential components. However, kinetic and potential exergy variations are generally negligible in flow exergy calculations52. Physical exergy refers to the maximum useful work that can be obtained when a substance is brought from its current state to environmental conditions through a reversible process46,53. Looking at how exergy destruction is distributed among the main process components helps us understand where thermodynamic losses and irreversibilities occur in the gas sweetening unit. Table 7 shows that the absorber column has the highest exergy destruction rate, about 68,544 kW, which is almost 88.6% of the total process exergy destruction. This matches the results reported by Suleiman et al.64 and Prentza et al.65. The regenerator, air cooler, feed/effluent heat exchanger, and pump each contribute much less to the total irreversibility.

Most exergy loss in the absorber column comes from pressure and temperature differences along the column, resistance to mass transfer, irreversible chemical reactions, fluid mixing, and entropy created by non-equilibrium transport between gas and liquid. During absorption, hydrogen sulfide (H2S) and carbon dioxide (CO2) move from the gas phase into the DEA solution through both mass and heat transfer, along with chemical reactions. These steps occur under finite concentrations and temperature gradients, which naturally increase entropy and reduce the system’s ability to do useful work. Also, the significant compositional difference between the lean amine and the incoming sour gas increases mixing losses in the absorber.

Another important factor contributing to the high exergy destruction in the absorber column is the strong dependence of acid gas solubility on solvent composition and temperature. Deviations from thermodynamic equilibrium during the absorption process, along with hydraulic performance and pressure losses across trays or packing, lead to greater irreversibilities within the column. Therefore, the absorber is the main bottleneck of the process and requires the greatest attention for improving energy efficiency and optimizing the process.

The regenerator column has exergy destruction of 4,438 kW, which mainly originates from heat transfer across the reboiler to vaporize the bottom stream, irreversible stripping of acid gases from the rich amine solution, and vapor-liquid separation. Although regeneration is an energy-intensive process, the lower exergy destruction compared to the absorber makes the point that the thermal energy supplied to the regenerator column is utilized more systematically under controlled phase-equilibrium conditions. Nevertheless, entropy generation associated with solvent regeneration and steam heating remains a significant source of thermodynamic inefficiency.

In the air cooler, the fan provides the energy for forced convection, which cools the incoming stream. Exergy destruction is about 2,838 kW, especially due to irreversible heat rejection to the environment. Since the cooling process releases heat to the surroundings without recovering useful work, a significant exergy loss happens even though the energy transfer rates are only moderate. Similarly, the feed/effluent heat exchanger incurs an exergy destruction of 897 kW, mainly due to the temperature difference between the hot lean amine and rich amine streams during heat recovery.

The pump exhibits the lowest exergy destruction rate, approximately 150 kW, indicating that mechanical energy losses associated with pressurization are comparatively insignificant relative to thermal and chemical irreversibilities within the process. This performance is expected because pumping involves less entropy generation than reactive absorption and thermal regeneration processes.

Control valves incur irreversible exergy losses due to throttling, an isenthalpic process that involves a pressure drop without useful work.

Exergy destruction in the phase separators is minimal because the pressure drop across them is small, about 0.5 bar.

Accordingly, the total exergy destruction of 77,366 kW demonstrates that the major thermodynamic inefficiencies of the DEA gas sweetening unit are mainly associated with reactive absorption and thermal separation phenomena rather than mechanical processes. The exergy distribution highlights the importance of improving absorber column operating conditions, solvent properties, and heat integration in order to reduce entropy generation and enhance overall process efficiency.

The exergy efficiencies of the major process components provide important insights into the effectiveness of energy utilization and the thermodynamic performance of the DEA gas sweetening process. Among the evaluated components, the absorber column exhibits the highest exergy efficiency, approximately 96%, indicating that the absorption process effectively utilizes the available thermodynamic potential for acid gas removal. This high efficiency expresses that most of the input exergy is successfully transferred into useful separation work rather than being dissipated through irreversibilities. Although the absorber column was identified as the major contributor to total exergy destruction due to its large processing capacity and extensive chemical and mass transfer interactions, its high exergy efficiency demonstrates that the unit still performs thermodynamically close to its intended function under the studied operating conditions. This contrast shows the difference between absolute exergy destruction and relative exergy efficiency. Because the absorber has the largest material and chemical exergy flows in the process, even a highly efficient operation can lead to considerable total exergy destruction.

The pump has a high exergy efficiency of 80%. This is typical for liquid pressurization. Such processes create little entropy and have small thermodynamic losses. Most of the mechanical energy is converted into pressure energy in the solvent stream. Losses from friction and hydraulics are low, especially compared to thermal separation processes.

The feed/effluent heat exchanger has a moderate-to-high exergy efficiency of 69%. This shows it recovers heat well between the hot lean amine and cold rich amine streams. By integrating heat, the process needs less external heating from the reboiler in the regenerator. It also uses energy more efficiently. The efficiency is not perfect because of unavoidable heat transfer and pressure losses in the exchanger. Still, the result means the exchanger works well thermodynamically. There is room for improvement with better thermal matching or heat transfer design.

The regenerator column exhibits a moderate exergy efficiency of 51.8%. This highlights the inherently energy-intensive nature of solvent regeneration in amine-based gas sweetening systems. Large quantities of thermal energy are required to break chemical bonds between the absorbed acid gases and the DEA solution. Although much of the supplied heat goes directly to acid gas stripping, considerable irreversibility occurs. This is due to heat transfer under finite temperature gradients, phase changes, and entropy generation in thermal separation. As a result, nearly half of the supplied exergy is lost through irreversible mechanisms in the regeneration section.

The air cooler shows the lowest exergy efficiency, approximately 28.9%. This is primarily because cooling operations inherently reject thermal energy to the environment without useful work recovery. In this unit, thermal exergy in the process stream is dissipated to the ambient air. This results in substantial entropy generation and exergy loss. Unlike heat recovery exchangers, the rejected heat in the air cooler is not reutilized elsewhere in the process. Therefore, low exergy efficiencies are thermodynamically expected for such equipment. Nevertheless, this result shows the potential benefit of advanced heat integration or waste heat recovery strategies to improve overall process energy efficiency.

In summary, the exergy efficiency distribution shows that thermal energy management and heat rejection, rather than mechanical operations, are the primary causes of the process’s thermodynamic inefficiencies. In order to improve the overall thermodynamic performance of the gas sweetening process, future process improvements should concentrate on improved heat integration, reduction of temperature gradients, optimization of solvent regeneration conditions, and recovery of low-grade waste heat, as indicated by the comparatively low efficiencies of the regenerator and air cooler.

Effect of operational variable changes on exergy destruction

The results of the exergy analysis presented in the previous section identified the contribution of each piece of equipment to the total exergy destruction. The objective of this study is to minimize exergy destruction by optimizing key operating parameters. In this section, the effects of varying the circulating solvent flow rate, DEA concentration, and inlet solvent temperature to the absorber on the exergy destruction of each unit are examined.

Circulating solvent flow rate

In the first case, the solvent concentration and temperature were kept constant at their operating values of 24.9 wt% and 55 °C, respectively. The circulating solvent flow rate was then reduced from the operating value of 20,123 kmol/h to 19,116 kmol/h (− 5%) and 18,110 kmol/h (− 10%). The resulting exergy destruction for each piece of equipment was calculated, and the results are shown in Figs. 2 and 3.

Effect of circulating solvent flow rate on equipment exergy destruction (Part #2).

Reducing the circulating solvent flow rate increases exergy destruction in both the regenerator and the absorber. The impact is greater in the regenerator. Lower solvent flow means less absorption of CO2 and H2S. This leads to a decrease in their concentration in the rich amine. The regenerator overhead flow stays constant. The condenser duty goes up to keep the operation going. This extra duty results in higher exergy destruction.

Reducing the circulating solvent flow rate decreases exergy destruction in the heat exchanger, pump, and air cooler. This is because less heat is transferred in the exchanger. The pump works with a lower flow rate and requires less shaft work. The air cooler needs less fan power and less heat transfer. All these changes help minimize exergy losses.

DEA concentration

In the second case, the flow rate and temperature were maintained at the plant’s operating conditions of 20,123 kmol/h and 55 °C, respectively. The DEA concentration was increased from the operating value of 24.9 wt% to 27.5 wt% and 29.0 wt%, and the exergy destruction for each piece of equipment was calculated. The results are shown in Figs. 4 and 5.

Effect of DEA concentration on equipment exergy destruction (Part #1).

Effect of DEA concentration on equipment exergy destruction (Part #2).

Increasing the DEA concentration reduces exergy destruction for both the regenerator and the absorber. Higher DEA levels help absorb more CO2 and H2S. This produces a richer amine stream with more of these compounds. As a result, the boiling point of the stream rises, which increases the reboiler duty. The regeneration column overhead rate stays the same. Because there is less water vapor, the condenser duty decreases. Also, better CO2 and H2S absorption means higher chemical exergy recovery.

However, a higher DEA concentration increases exergy destruction in the pump and air cooler. The stream’s higher specific heat increases the air cooler’s heat duty. Its higher viscosity needs more shaft work from the pump.

Inlet solvent temperature to the absorber

In the third case, the DEA concentration and solvent flow rate were maintained at their operating values of 24.9 wt% and 20,123 kmol/h, respectively. The inlet solvent temperature to the absorber was reduced from the operating temperature of 55 °C to 50 °C and 45 °C, and the exergy destruction for each piece of equipment was calculated. The results are presented in Figs. 6 and 7.

Effect of inlet solvent temperature to the absorber on equipment exergy destruction (Part#1).

Effect of inlet solvent temperature to the absorber on equipment exergy destruction (Part#2).

Lowering the inlet solvent temperature to the absorber reduces exergy destruction in the air cooler and the absorber. A lower temperature means the temperature gradient inside the absorber is smaller, so less physical exergy is destroyed. More CO2 and H2S are absorbed, which helps recover chemical exergy. As the inlet solvent temperature drops, the rich amine temperature decreases. This results in a larger temperature drop in the preheating exchanger’s hot stream. As a result, the air cooler’s heat duty decreases. On the other hand, a lower rich amine temperature also increases the heat exchanger temperature gradient, leading to greater heat exchange.

Building on this analysis, the study continues by examining the simultaneous variation of all three operational variables. The goal is not only to maintain the plant’s production within the required specifications but also to minimize overall exergy destruction.

Feyzi et al.20 reported that solvent circulation rate influences exergy destruction in amine-based gas sweetening systems, primarily through its impact on acid gas absorption capacity and regeneration energy demand. However, the results of the present study indicate that the circulating solvent flow rate is not statistically significant within the investigated operating domain.

This discrepancy can be explained by the multivariable nature of the present optimization framework. In contrast to single-factor sensitivity analyses reported in previous studies, the current work simultaneously varies DEA concentration, lean amine temperature, and solvent circulation rate while maintaining strict process constraints for treated gas quality. Under such coupled conditions, the influence of solvent circulation rate becomes less dominant because its thermodynamic effect is strongly interdependent with solvent composition and temperature.

From a process perspective, increasing solvent circulation rate generally enhances mass transfer driving force and reduces outlet acid gas concentration; however, this benefit is accompanied by increased pumping power and higher thermal duty in the regenerator. In the present study, these opposing effects are partially offset by simultaneous adjustments in DEA concentration and lean amine temperature, which directly govern absorption equilibrium and regeneration energy requirements. Consequently, the overall exergy destruction becomes more sensitive to chemical potential (DEA concentration) and thermal driving force (lean amine temperature) than to hydraulic circulation rate within the studied range.

Furthermore, under constrained operation near specification limits, the absorber tends to operate in a regime where mass transfer limitations are not primarily controlled by solvent circulation but by equilibrium and temperature-dependent solubility effects. Therefore, the circulating solvent flow rate exhibits a secondary influence compared to DEA concentration and lean solvent temperature, leading to its statistical insignificance in the reduced cubic RSM model.

Economic & environmental considerations

Although the primary focus of the present study is thermodynamic optimization based on exergy analysis, the obtained results also possess important economic and environmental implications for industrial gas sweetening operations. In amine-based gas treatment units, utility consumption associated with solvent circulation, cooling, and especially solvent regeneration accounts for a major portion of both operating costs and indirect environmental impacts. Therefore, reductions in exergy destruction can simultaneously lower energy consumption, reduce operating expenditures, and decrease greenhouse gas emissions.

The optimization results demonstrated that appropriate adjustments to operating variables, including DEA concentration and lean amine temperature, reduced the total exergy destruction of the process by approximately 5%. From an industrial perspective, even moderate thermodynamic improvements can lead to substantial long-term benefits because gas sweetening units typically operate continuously at large processing capacities. Improved thermodynamic performance in the absorber–regenerator system can decrease reboiler heat duty, cooling demand, and auxiliary utility consumption, thereby reducing fuel usage, operating costs, and associated CO2 emissions related to utility generation.

An important advantage of the proposed optimization strategy is that the obtained improvement was achieved through operational modifications rather than structural process changes. No additional major equipment, process intensification technology, or significant capital investment is required for implementation of the optimized operating conditions. Consequently, the proposed approach can be economically attractive for existing industrial facilities because operational parameter adjustment can generally be implemented with minimal modification cost and limited operational disruption.

Reducing exergy destruction in the regenerator column is particularly significant because the regeneration duty is a major contributor to steam consumption and operating costs in DEA gas sweetening units. Reduction of regeneration energy leads to lower fuel consumption in steam generation units while simultaneously decreasing indirect greenhouse gas emissions. Similarly, improvements in heat recovery and optimization of lean amine operating temperature can reduce cooling-water and air-cooling requirements, leading to economic and environmental benefits.

The relatively low exergy efficiency of the air cooler indicates that a significant portion of the thermal energy is rejected to the environment without being recovered. This result shows the potential applicability of enhanced heat integration or waste heat recovery strategies to improve both process economics and environmental sustainability. Recovery and reuse of low-grade thermal energy could reduce utility demand, improve overall plant energy efficiency, and reduce the gas sweetening unit’s environmental footprint.

Overall, the results show that exergy-based optimization can make meaningful improvements in both economic and environmental performance in industrial DEA gas sweetening processes while maintaining simplicity and practical applicability.

Conclusion

In this study, an exergy analysis of a gas sweetening unit employing diethanolamine (DEA) as the solvent was conducted, and the influence of key design parameters was systematically evaluated. The simulation results were validated against the original design data, with a maximum deviation of 5%, observed for the mole fraction of H2S in the regeneration column overhead stream. The close agreement between the simulated and design values confirms the reliability of the developed model, which was subsequently utilized for detailed exergy analysis.

The primary objective of the investigated gas sweetening unit is the removal of CO2 and H2S from a sour gas stream using DEA solvent, followed by the transfer of the sweetened gas to the dehydration unit. The results of the exergy analysis indicate that the absorber contributes the most to total exergy destruction, amounting to 77,366 kW. This high exergy destruction is primarily attributed to the pressure and temperature gradients along the column and the chemical reactions between the acid gases and DEA, during which compounds with higher chemical exergy are converted into compounds with lower chemical exergy, releasing significant amounts of heat.

To enhance the exergy efficiency of the system, the effects of individual operational parameters on exergy destruction were investigated. The significance of these parameters and their mutual interactions was subsequently analyzed using the RSM, leading to the development of a predictive statistical model for exergy destruction.

According to the optimization results, adjusting the operational variables—specifically the circulating solvent flow rate, DEA concentration, and inlet solvent temperature to the absorber—to 19,317.03 kmol/h, 32.97 wt%, and 47.03 °C, respectively, not only ensures compliance with process specifications but also reduces the total exergy destruction to 73,806 kW with 95% confidence. This corresponds to an approximate 5% improvement in exergy performance for the studied process.

One of the main limitations of the present study is that the exergy analysis and optimization were performed based on a steady-state simulation model. Although the simulation results showed good agreement with the available design data, transient operating conditions and dynamic process behavior were not considered. In industrial gas sweetening units, fluctuations in feed composition, flow rate, and operating conditions may affect the exergy performance of the system. Therefore, incorporating dynamic simulation and transient analysis could provide a more comprehensive understanding of the process behavior under real operating conditions.

Another limitation of this work is that the optimization was conducted by considering only three operational parameters, namely circulating solvent flow rate, DEA concentration, and inlet solvent temperature to the absorber. Other influential factors, such as absorber and regenerator pressure, tray efficiency, solvent degradation, and heat integration effects, were not included in the optimization framework. In addition, economic and environmental aspects were not evaluated simultaneously with exergy performance (exergoeconomic and exergoenvironmental analyses). Future studies may focus on multi-objective optimization approaches that integrate thermodynamic, economic, and environmental criteria to achieve a more practical and industrially applicable optimization strategy.

Furthermore, the developed statistical model was derived from simulation data generated under the operating constraints of the studied plant. Consequently, the applicability of the proposed model to other gas sweetening units with different feed compositions, solvents, or process configurations may be limited. Future research may extend the methodology to other amine systems and validate the proposed optimization approach using industrial operating data or pilot-scale experiments.
