Implementing the WHO/ICRC Basic Emergency Care course training for health workers in conflict-affected regions in Somalia
Authors: Barkhad Mohamed Ismail, Mohamed Mukhtar Ali, Meaghan M. Sydlowski, Mohamed Hussein, Nichole Michaeli
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
IntroductionEmergency care is critical in regions affected by humanitarian crises. Somalia’s prolonged conflict has strained the health system, leaving health workers under-prepared and limiting access to training. The Basic Emergency Care (BEC) course was developed to address gaps in emergency care by training healthcare providers to stabilize critically ill and injured patients. This study evaluates the implementation of BEC in Somalia, focusing on strategies to adapt this course to a conflict-affected setting.MethodsTwo consecutive 5-day BEC courses and a 1-day Training-of-Trainers course were conducted in Mogadishu, Somalia (October 2023). Participants were doctors and nurses providing first-contact emergency care at health facilities in Mogadishu, Jowhar, Baidoa, and Gaalkayo, selected by the Somalia Federal Ministry of Health (FMOH) in collaboration with International Medical Corps (IMC). Knowledge was assessed with multiple choice pre- and post-course exams, and confidence was assessed with pre- and post-course Likert-scale surveys. Data were analysed using paired t-test and McNemar’s test. Qualitative feedback was analyzed thematically.ResultsA total of 55 participants enrolled in BEC, 54 completed the post-test and were included in paired pre/post knowledge analyses. Post-course examination scores (90.6 %) showed a significant improvement (p < 0.001) compared to pre-course examination scores (53.6 %). Self-reported confidence in providing emergency care increased across multiple domains (p < 0.05). Participants expressed strong interest in conflict-related injury modules and recommended extending the duration of the course.ConclusionThe BEC course increased emergency care knowledge and confidence among Somali health workers. Centralized training, peer-teaching, and the integration of conflict-related modules can address issues of feasibility, sustainability, and relevance in a conflict setting. Further studies are needed to evaluate the conflict-related injury modules, assess the clinical impact of BEC training in this setting, and determine the effectiveness of sustainability strategies.

Introduction

Trauma and medical emergencies represent a significant cause of morbidity and mortality worldwide [1]. Patients with medical emergencies and traumatic injuries require systematic evaluation, rapid diagnosis, and critical interventions by trained health workers to prevent disability and death [2,3]. However, there is a shortage of trained emergency care providers around the world, particularly in low-and-middle income countries (LMICs) [4]. In Somalia and other regions affected by violence and humanitarian crises, effective emergency care is crucial to mitigate further health deterioration [5].

Over the past three decades, Somalia has endured a severe humanitarian crisis, with protracted conflict and displacement due to civil wars, impacting health outcomes and straining the healthcare system. The country faces high maternal and infant mortality rates and conflict-related wasting and stunting in children [[6], [7], [8]]. Life expectancy is only 58.8 years [9], reflecting the weakened health system, shortages of health workers and resources, and limited access to care. Somali’s health system has four levels: primary health units; health centers that deliver primary care services; referral health centers and district hospitals that offer emergency, obstetrics, surgical inpatient care; and regional hospitals that provide specialized 24-hour referral and inpatient services [10].

A recent analysis of emergency health education in Somalia highlighted the critical gap in emergency care training for health workers [11]. In response to these findings, International Medical Corps (IMC), an international non-governmental organization (NGO) with a long-standing presence in Somalia, and the Somalia Ministry of Health (MOH) reviewed existing training programs and engaged in an ongoing collaborative planning process to identify evidence-based interventions to strengthen emergency care capacity. Based on this collaborative process, the MOH and IMC prioritized the implementation of the WHO/ICRC Basic Emergency Care (BEC) course as an initiative to strengthen the capacity of frontline health workers.

The BEC course was developed by the World Health Organization (WHO) in collaboration with the International Committee of the Red Cross (ICRC) and the International Federation for Emergency Medicine (IFEM) to train health workers to manage acute illness and injury in resource-limited settings [12]. According to WHO emergency care toolkit, the BEC course follows a cascading training model. To become a BEC facilitator, candidates must complete a certified BEC provider course for 4 or 5 days and then attend a training of trainers (ToT) for 1 or 2 days conducted by a master trainer. After finishing the ToT, they are designated as provisional facilitators. Then, they must teach a full BEC course under the supervision of a master trainer to become a registered facilitator. After the registered facilitators teach at least two independent courses, they qualify to become master trainers [13,14]. In 2023, The World Health Assembly 76.2 Resolution on Integrated Emergency, Critical, and Operative Care urged member states to implement BEC training for frontline healthcare workers as a means to address deficiencies in emergency care in resource-limited settings [15]. The BEC course has been shown to effectively enhance emergency care knowledge, skill retention, and confidence for various levels of health workers across diverse settings [[16], [17], [18], [19], [20]].

While multiple studies have reinforced the BEC course as a tool to strengthen the capacity of health workers, only one study has specifically evaluated the BEC course for health workers in humanitarian crises and conflict settings [21]. This study showed the effective implementation of the BEC course for first responders in Ukraine during the Russian-Ukrainian conflict, which has damaged infrastructure, reduced healthcare resources, and resulted in the significant loss of medical professionals [21]. Somalia faces similar challenges to emergency care delivery due to the ongoing armed conflict.

This study assesses the effect of the BEC course on emergency care knowledge and self-reported confidence among doctors and nurses in Somalia, and describes key implementation strategies used to deliver the training in a conflict-affected setting.

Methods

Study design

This was a pre- and post-intervention study evaluating changes in participants’ emergency care knowledge and self-reported confidence following two 5-day BEC courses and a 1-day Training of Trainers (TOT) course conducted in Mogadishu in October 2023, using standardized examinations, surveys, and post-course feedback forms.

Participants and trainers

The study population comprised frontline doctors and nurses working in emergency and acute care roles at primary health centres, referral health centres and regional hospitals in Mogadish, Jowhar, Baidoa, and Gaalkayo. Participants were selected using purposive sampling by the Somalia MOH in collaboration with IMC to reflect geographic representation, facility level [10] and clinicians likely to provide first-contact emergency care.

The first 5-day BEC course was taught by five certified BEC instructors including, one US-based attending emergency medicine physician and four physicians from Somalia/Somaliland with experience in emergency care. The TOT course was taught by two BEC Master Trainers. The second 5-day BEC course was taught by the newly-trained BEC provisional facilitators (one nurse and five physicians) from the TOT course alongside four IMC-contracted BEC consultant instructors from the first 5-day BEC course.

Training format

The BEC courses covered the ABCDE approach to trauma, difficulty in breathing, shock, and altered mental status, as well as the newly released conflict-related injury modules on penetrating injury, blast injury, and burn injury. The courses utilized lectures, small group discussions and practical interactive skill stations to introduce basic emergency knowledge. A one-day Training of Trainers (TOT) course was held after the first BEC course to train new BEC instructors.

Implementation strategies

To adapt the BEC course for delivery in a conflict-affected setting, a series of implementation strategies were employed. Due to heightened security concerns in the regions where several participants’ hospitals were located, all participants were relocated to a secure location in Mogadishu for the course. This centralized approach allowed us to conduct the training in a more secure environment while ensuring equitable access for participants from multiple facilities. The security situation in Mogadishu was closely monitored and adjustments in the course schedule were made to minimize risks to participants and trainers. The course was conducted primarily by Somali trainers to ensure contextual relevance and cultural alignment. Although the BEC written and lecture materials were in English, the course was delivered with real-time translation to Somali to enhance participant comprehension.

Lastly, a peer-teaching model was integrated into the second week of training to strengthen local instructor capacity. IFEM guidelines stipulate that BEC provisional facilitators must teach a supervised BEC course under the direction of a BEC Master Trainer to become registered facilitators [22]. However, our implementation model incorporated increased peer feedback with 4 Master and Registered trainers supporting 6 new provisional trainers. The provisional trainers from the TOT course practiced teaching lectures, small groups, and skills stations under the close supervision of experienced BEC Master and Registered trainers and received structured, real-time feedback to refine their teaching techniques. While this model is not required under IFEM or AFEM guidelines, it was supported by AFEM as a strategy to enhance the teaching skills of the new trainers and increase the number of registered trainers in Somalia. .This strategy aimed to enhance course fidelity, empower new instructors, and support the scalability of future BEC training. This approach was employed in an effort to promote sustainability by reducing reliance on external trainers, building local instructional capacity, and enabling continuity of training in hard-to-reach areas.

Data collection tools and analysis

BEC and TOT participants’ knowledge was assessed with the standardized BEC multiple choice pre- and post-course examinations. Participants’ confidence in managing patients with severe illness or injury was assessed with the standardized BEC pre- and post-course surveys using a 4-point Likert scale. Qualitative feedback was collected from the BEC participants using the standardized BEC Feedback Form. These assessment tools are part of the BEC course package and were provided by AFEM [23]. Participants were awarded a completion certificate if they scored at least 75 % on the post-course examination, successfully demonstrated all BEC skills, and met the attendance requirement.

Scores from pre- and post-course examinations were stratified by profession and analyzed using the paired t-test. Results from participants who did not complete both the pre and post-test were excluded from the final analysis. Results of the TOT pre- and post-course examinations were compared using the paired t-test.

The percentage of participants reporting these targeted responses pre- and post-training was calculated and compared using McNemar’s test. Participants who omitted items on the pre- or post-course survey were excluded from the paired pre-/post-analysis for those specific items, while their completed responses were included in the analysis. Quantitative data were analyzed using STATA/SE 18.0 (StataCorp LLC, College Station, USA). Qualitative data of free-text responses from the BEC feedback form were analyzed using reflexive thematic analysis as described by Braun and Clarke [24]. Responses were read repeatedly for familiarization, coded inductively to identify meaningful units, grouped into candidate themes and iteratively reviewed and refined for coherence and distinctiveness. Themes were defined at the semantic level based on explicit content of responses and summarized using representative quotes (Table 4). Initial coding and theme development were conducted by one author and reviewed with a second author to enhance credibility of the final themes.

Ethical approval

This study was reviewed by the Mass General Brigham Institutional Review Board and Research and Ethical Committee at the Somali Ministry of Health (Ref/MOHHS/DGO/0871/Nov2023). It was determined that IRB approval was not required, since it does not meet the criteria for human subject research as defined by Health and Human Services regulations set forth in 45 CFR 46.

The participants worked in various healthcare settings. The majority (36 participants) were employed at regional hospitals. Additionally, six participants worked at primary health centers, and five participants were based at referral health centers (Table 1). Most participants worked in emergency-related roles (e.g., Emergency Supervisor, Anesthesia, High Dependency Unit, Surgeon, Nurse). Discussion

Somalia’s prolonged conflict has significantly impacted the health system’s capacity to deliver critically needed emergency care. The BEC course represents an intervention to build frontline capacity by improving health workers’ confidence, knowledge, and skills in emergency care. To our knowledge, this study is the first to evaluate the BEC course in Somalia, a setting characterized by conflict, resource constraints, and health system fragility. The results demonstrated an increase in emergency care knowledge and significant improvements in participants’ self-reported confidence. Greater than 70 % of participants met the target score on questions addressing comfort handling any patient requiring emergency care, understanding of the ABCDE’s of basic emergency care, management of the injured adult, and skills to manage patients with shock, difficulty in breathing, and bleeding problems. These results align with studies from Zambia, Sierra Leone, Malawi, Kenya, which showed enhanced knowledge and confidence among health workers [[16], [17], [18], [19], [20]]. However, our study extends this evidence base by confirming that such gains are also achievable in active conflict settings, where there are increased emergency care needs and more constrained resources.

Notably, pre-course examination scores included very low minimum values (as low as 8–12 %). This may reflect limited prior exposure to emergency training, language barriers when written materials are in English, or variability in baseline emergency care experience across facility types. Importantly, the large absolute increase in post-course scores suggests that BEC can effectively improve core emergency care knowledge even when baseline knowledge is extremely limited.

The findings from Kivlehan, et al provide an important comparison for our study, as it is the only other BEC study published in a conflict setting. Similar to our approach in Somalia, the BEC implementation in Ukraine relied on real-time translation to overcome language barriers [21]. While this approach was utilized in both studies, future courses should consider increasing reliance on local instructors and translating BEC materials into participants’ preferred languages. Kivlehan, et al reported attendance and completion challenges during BEC implementation in Ukraine. In our setting, course retention was high, but not complete: 55 participants enrolled, 54 completed post-course assessments and 50 met all certification requirements. This difference may be explained by the provision of per diem to our participants and the absence of conflicting professional duties, as many of the Somali participants relocated from their workplaces for the course. .

Our study also adds to the implementation strategies employed in Ukraine by focusing on local instructors and incorporating a training-of-trainer course and peer-teaching model, which increased the local instructor pool and reduced reliance on external faculty. Despite the differences, both studies demonstrate the feasibility of delivering BEC in fragile, humanitarian contexts. These studies highlight the adaptability of BEC across diverse emergency care contexts, ranging from a middle-income country with established first responder systems to a low-income country with limited health infrastructure.

In Somalia, we employed several additional strategies to successfully deliver the training. By relocating participants from their facilities across Somalia to a centralized training location in Mogadishu, we minimized security risks while ensuring equitable access to training for health workers. While this approach was necessary due to the current conflict, additional funding was required for safety and security measures beyond the standard BEC course costs, which include training materials, logistical expenses, including security, and facilitator compensation. The availability of funding may impact the ability to scale up future trainings.

We attempted to address future scalability and sustainability concerns by implementing a structured peer-teaching model. This model paired provisional facilitators with experienced BEC facilitators who provided real-time feedback and mentorship. This approach aimed to rapidly strengthen the teaching capacity of new facilitators and maintain course fidelity. This model was implemented to reduce reliance on external trainers and allow future trainings to be conducted locally in hard-to-reach, conflict-affected areas. Consistent with this goal, multiple participants recommended periodic refresher trainings and extending the course duration, highlighting the importance of training a cadre of local facilitators who can conduct future courses.

Given the prevalence of war-related injuries, we also incorporated the newly developed, optional BEC war trauma modules, including penetrating, blast, and burn injuries, into the training. Participants frequently requested additional and expanded training on conflict and war-related trauma, including penetrating, blast and burn injuries, noting that the majority of emergency presentations in their facilities were related to armed conflict. . We did not formally assess participants’ knowledge or confidence in the conflict-related injury modules, which limits conclusions regarding effectiveness. Future studies could explore the relevance and effectiveness of these modules in areas experiencing active conflict.

While this study demonstrated the success of the BEC course for frontline health workers in conflict-affected regions in Somalia, we would like to acknowledge some limitations. One such limitation is the short follow-up, which only assessed immediate post-training knowledge and confidence without evaluating long-term retention. The reliance on self-reported confidence as a study tool could also introduce bias, since participants might over or understate their confidence. Additionally, this study did not directly compare our implementation strategies with alternative approaches; therefore, we cannot attribute participants’ success solely to the strategies employed.

Conclusions

This study is the first to evaluate the BEC course in Somalia, a country affected by active conflict. Our findings demonstrate statistically significant improvements in frontline health workers’ emergency care knowledge and confidence. Our implementation strategies, including relocation of participants to a centralized training site for safety, incorporation of conflict-related injury modules, and adoption of a structured peer-teaching model, offer suggestions for adapting BEC delivery to this unique setting. However, further studies are needed to evaluate the effectiveness of the conflict-related injury modules, assess the long-term impact of BEC training on clinical practice and patient outcomes, and explore innovative strategies to ensure sustainable access to training in conflict-affected areas.
