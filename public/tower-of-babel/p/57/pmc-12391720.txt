Bronchiolar Adenoma/Ciliated Muconodular Papillary Tumour With a 17‐Year Follow‐Up: A Case Report
Authors: Yuri Hayashi, Eiji Ando, Yasuhiro Matsuo, Toshio Kasugai, Takuya Mikamo, Taro Takeda, Hideo Saka
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTBronchiolar Adenoma/Ciliated muconodular papillary tumour (BA/CMPT) is a rare pulmonary tumour that arises in the peripheral lung and exhibits both benign and malignant features. The classification and biological behaviour of BA/CMPT remain uncertain. We present a rare case of CMPT with a 17‐year follow‐up period.Keywords: bronchiolar adenoma, case report, ciliated muconodular papillary tumour, lung tumour, pulmonary neoplasm

Introduction

Bronchiolar Adenoma/Ciliated muconodular papillary tumour (BA/CMPT) is a rare pulmonary tumour first described by Ishikawa in 2002 [1]. It is characterised by ciliated columnar cells, goblet cells, and basal cells forming a papillary structure. The tumour exhibits indolent behaviour, but its classification as benign or malignant remains controversial. We report a case of BA/CMPT with a 17‐year follow‐up, emphasising its slow progression and histopathological features.

Case Report

A 46‐year‐old Japanese woman presented for a preoperative evaluation for cholelithiasis. A nodule was found in the right lung during pre‐operative examinations, leading to a first visit to the Department of Respiratory Medicine. She had no respiratory symptoms. Her past medical history included tuberculosis in adolescence, treated with multidrug therapy, and bronchial asthma. She had a smoking history of 20 cigarettes per day from age 18 to 34.

On physical examination, her vital signs were normal: temperature 37.1°C, blood pressure 127/89 mmHg, heart rate 84 bpm, and SpO2 98% on room air. No abnormal lung or heart sounds were detected.

Chest CT revealed a 24‐mm well‐circumscribed solid nodule in the right middle lobe (Figure 1a). A retrospective review of a 2007 chest CT showed a 13‐mm nodule in the same location, suggesting slow growth over 17 years (Figure 1b). PET/CT showed mild FDG uptake (SUVmax 3.33).

Chest CT images. (a) A 13‐mm nodule in the right middle lobe (2007, Kilovoltage peak; 120 kV, milliampere; 200 mA, Rotation Time; 1.0 s, Slice Thickness; 10 mm slice). (b) A 24‐mm solid nodule in the same location, showing slow progression over 17 years (2024, Kilovoltage peak; 120 kV, milliampere; Auto mA, Rotation Time; 0.5 s, Slice Thickness; 5 mm slice).

Transbronchial biopsy from the right B5b revealed squamous metaplasia with papillary hyperplasia, but no definitive malignancy.

A diagnostic and therapeutic right middle lobe wedge resection was performed. The resected tumour was well‐demarcated with mucin production. Histopathological examination confirmed BA/CMPT, characterised by ciliated columnar cells, goblet cells, and basal cells arranged in a papillary pattern (Figure 2a). Immunohistochemical staining showed p40 positivity in basal cells (Figure 2b), weak TTF‐1 positivity, and low Ki‐67 proliferation index. The postoperative course was uneventful, and the patient remains under follow‐up with no recurrence in the Department of Thoracic Surgery.

(b) H&E × 200; The ciliated, mucous, and basal cells at higher magnification. (c) H&E × 200; The histopathological image clearly shows ciliated cells. (d) P40 × 100; A p40‐positive basal cell layer was identified. (e) H&E × 100; The same HE‐stained images as the immunohistochemical‐stained images. (f) Macroscopic image; A greyish‐white, mucinous mass formation was identified. The size of the tissue was about 80 mm in width and 35 mm in height. Grossly, a greyish‐white mass formation with mucus was observed.

Discussion

BA/CMPT is a rare lung tumour with a predominantly benign clinical course. The 2021 WHO classification included BA/CMPT as a subtype of bronchiolar adenoma [2, 3]. BA/CMPT commonly presents as a peripheral pulmonary nodule, often discovered incidentally. Reports suggest a slight male predominance, with typical onset in individuals aged60 to 800 years.

Imaging findings vary, with some cases showing slow growth, as observed in our case.

The tumour doubling time in this case was 2340 days, with an average annual tumour growth rate of 0.65 mm/year. In previously reported cases, the average tumour growth rate was 0.49 mm/year for the maximum diameter [4]. The tumour growth rate in this case was almost the same as that in past cases, suggesting that the BA/CMPT in this case is also benign.

While tree‐in‐bud appearance is characteristic of tuberculosis, this case did not show such CT findings, which further supported BA/CMPT.

Histologically, BA/CMPT comprises ciliated columnar cells, goblet cells, and basal cells forming a papillary structure with mucin production. Immunohistochemical analysis helps differentiate BA/CMPT from adenocarcinoma. The presence of a basal cell layer with p40 positivity supports a diagnosis of BA/CMPT rather than malignancy.

BA/CMPT's natural history remains uncertain, but our case demonstrates its slow‐growing nature over 17 years. Previous reports of long‐term follow‐up cases (12 and 11 years [5, 6]) have similarly shown indolent behaviour. Given the potential for misdiagnosis as adenocarcinoma, complete resection is recommended for definitive diagnosis and treatment.
