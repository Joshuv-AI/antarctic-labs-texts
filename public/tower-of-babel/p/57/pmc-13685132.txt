Background

Diabetes mellitus is a major global health concern, affecting over 500 million adults worldwide [1]. Early diagnosis of both diabetes and prediabetes is essential to prevent long-term complications such as cardiovascular disease, nephropathy, retinopathy, and neuropathy [2]. The oral glucose tolerance test (OGTT) remains the reference standard for diagnosis according to World Health Organization (WHO) criteria [3]. However, the OGTT is time-consuming, requires fasting, and is often impractical for routine clinical use [4].

Consequently, glycated hemoglobin (HbA1c) has been widely adopted as an alternative diagnostic biomarker because it reliably reflects average glycemic exposure over the preceding 2 to 3 months [5, 6]. Despite its convenience, HbA1c has significant limitations. Its accuracy is heavily compromised by conditions that alter erythrocyte lifespan or hemoglobin structure. These conditions include hemoglobinopathies, iron deficiency anemia, chronic kidney disease, liver disease, and pregnancy [7, 8]. In populations with a high prevalence of such conditions, such as South Asian populations, HbA1c-based diagnosis may lead to severe clinical misclassification.

Alternative glycation biomarkers, specifically glycated albumin (GA) and fructosamine (FA), have been proposed to bridge this diagnostic gap [9, 10]. GA represents the proportion of glycated albumin relative to total serum albumin, while FA reflects total glycated serum proteins. Both biomarkers reflect glycemic exposure over a shorter duration of approximately 2 to 3 weeks and are entirely independent of red blood cell turnover. These characteristics make them highly relevant for clinical situations where HbA1c is unreliable or where short-term glycemic changes need to be assessed.

Although GA and FA overcome many of the limitations associated with HbA1c, they are not without drawbacks. Both biomarkers may be influenced by alterations in albumin concentration and protein metabolism, including liver disease, nephrotic syndrome, thyroid disorders, protein-losing conditions, and severe systemic inflammation. Furthermore, differences in assay methodology and the absence of universally accepted diagnostic cutoffs contribute to variability in reported diagnostic performance across studies [11, 12].

Recent meta-analyses have reported moderate to good diagnostic accuracy of glycated albumin for diabetes detection, while also highlighting substantial heterogeneity between studies. Variability in assay methodology, study populations, geographical regions, and diagnostic cutoff values has contributed to differences in reported performance estimates. Furthermore, evidence regarding fructosamine remains comparatively limited and less comprehensively synthesized. These findings support the need for an updated systematic review and meta-analysis evaluating both biomarkers using contemporary diagnostic test accuracy methods [13, 14].

While several studies have evaluated the diagnostic accuracy of GA and FA against the OGTT [15, 16], reported sensitivities, specificities, and optimal diagnostic cut-offs vary considerably. This variation is likely due to differences in assay methodologies, study populations, and clinical settings. Currently, the evidence remains fragmented, lacking a comprehensive synthesis using appropriate hierarchical meta-analytic approaches.

Aim

This systematic review and meta-analysis aims to rigorously evaluate the diagnostic accuracy of GA and FA for detecting diabetes and prediabetes in adults, using OGTT as the reference standard.

Methods

This systematic review is registered on the International Prospective Register of Systematic Reviews (PROSPERO) (ID: CRD420251236959) [17], and this protocol is reported in accordance with PRISMA-P (Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Reviews and Meta-Analyses Protocols) guidance (Supplementary material 1). The final review will be published in line with PRISMA (Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Reviews and Meta-Analyses) reporting guidelines [18].

Information sources

A comprehensive literature search will be conducted across MEDLINE (via PubMed), Scopus, and Web of Science from database inception to December 2025 (Supplementary material 2). Searches will be limited to English-language publications. Additionally, reference lists of included studies and relevant review articles will be manually screened to identify missed eligible studies.

Study selection process

Retrieved records will be imported into Covidence, where duplicates will be removed. Titles and abstracts will be screened independently by two reviewers (LP, CR) against the eligibility criteria. Full texts of potentially eligible studies will then be independently assessed by two reviewers, with disagreements resolved through discussion or by consulting a third reviewer (DK, BD, SP, or AJ). Reasons for full-text exclusion will be recorded, and the selection process will be documented via a PRISMA flow diagram.

Data extraction

Data will be extracted by one reviewer (LP) and independently checked by a second (CR) using a standardized, piloted Excel form. A third reviewer (KF) will verify the extracted data for accuracy and completeness. We will extract study characteristics (author, year, country, design, sample size), population demographics (age, sex, clinical setting, comorbidities, ethnicity), index test details (assay method, cut-offs), and details of the reference standard, including the OGTT protocol and diagnostic thresholds used where reported. No assumptions will be made beyond reported data.

We will also extract information on factors that may influence GA or FA interpretation, including serum albumin concentration, liver disease, chronic kidney disease, nephrotic syndrome, protein-losing conditions, thyroid disease, pregnancy, inflammatory conditions, and other disorders associated with altered protein turnover, where reported.

Diagnostic outcomes

The primary outcomes will be the diagnostic accuracy of GA and FA for detecting diabetes and prediabetes. We will extract standard diagnostic parameters, primarily sensitivity, specificity, and the AUC. Where necessary, reported percentages will be converted into absolute values to construct 2 × 2 contingency tables (true positives, false positives, false negatives, and true negatives). Secondary outcomes will explore variations in diagnostic performance based on population characteristics (e.g., anemia, hemoglobinopathies, chronic kidney disease, pregnancy), geographical regions, and assay methodologies.

Quality assessment and risk of bias

The methodological quality of included studies will be evaluated using the QUADAS-2 tool. Two independent reviewers will assess risk of bias and applicability concerns across four domains: patient selection, index test, reference standard, and flow and timing. Each domain will be classified as low, high, or unclear risk, with disagreements resolved through discussion or a third reviewer.

Data synthesis and statistical analysis

Meta-analysis will be performed where two or more studies report comparable data. Diagnostic accuracy data will be synthesized using hierarchical models. Pooled estimates of sensitivity and specificity will be calculated using a bivariate random-effects model to account for the correlation between these measures. Overall test performance will be summarized using hierarchical summary receiver operating characteristic (HSROC) curves. Separate analyses will be performed for GA and FA.

Between-study heterogeneity will be assessed through visual inspection of forest plots, HSROC curves, and model variance estimates. Potential threshold effects arising from differences in diagnostic cutoff values will be explored by examining the relationship between sensitivity and specificity across studies and through visual inspection of HSROC plots. Where sufficient data are available, subgroup analyses based on commonly reported diagnostic cutoff ranges will be performed. Where sufficient data are available, subgroup or sensitivity analyses will also be undertaken according to clinical conditions that may affect albumin metabolism or protein turnover.

Data will be grouped and analyzed according to biomarker type, clinical condition, population characteristics, and assay methodology. Where sufficient data are available, subgroup and sensitivity analyses (excluding studies at high risk of bias) will be conducted. The possibility of small-study effects will also be explored graphically if sufficient studies are pooled.

Discussion

This systematic review and meta-analysis will comprehensively evaluate the diagnostic accuracy of GA and FA for detecting diabetes and prediabetes in adults. Given the known limitations of HbA1c in conditions affecting erythrocyte lifespan, identifying reliable alternative biomarkers for diverse clinical settings is increasingly critical. Because GA and FA are independent of red blood cell turnover and reflect short-term glycemic changes, they are particularly relevant for populations with high prevalences of hemoglobinopathies, anemia, and chronic kidney disease.

Currently, the clinical utility of these biomarkers is complicated by variable assay methodologies, unstandardized diagnostic thresholds, and heterogeneous study populations. In addition, GA and FA concentrations may be influenced by conditions affecting albumin metabolism and protein turnover, including liver disease, nephrotic syndrome, thyroid disorders, pregnancy, and inflammatory states. These factors may contribute to variability in diagnostic performance and should be considered when interpreting study findings. By applying robust hierarchical meta-analytic methods and exploring sources of heterogeneity, this review aims to clarify the overall diagnostic performance of GA and FA and identify factors contributing to variability across studies.

If GA and FA demonstrate adequate accuracy, they could serve as vital adjuncts or alternatives to HbA1c, particularly in resource-limited settings or specialized patient populations. Furthermore, this review may highlight the urgent need for assay standardization and the establishment of population-specific cutoff values. While potential limitations include study heterogeneity and English-language publication bias, the rigorous methodology, including QUADAS-2 assessment and hierarchical modeling, will ensure the reliability of our findings. Ultimately, this review will inform future research, clinical guidelines, and diagnostic strategies in modern diabetes care.
