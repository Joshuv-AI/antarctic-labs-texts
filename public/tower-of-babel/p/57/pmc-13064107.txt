Alterations of static and dynamic functional connectivity of the nucleus accumbens subregions may be associated with chronic process of migraine: a resting-state fMRI study
Authors: Geyu Liu, Cunxin Lin, Dong Qiu, Xiaoshuang Li, Zhi Guo, Yuanxiang Li, Yanliang Mei, Mantian Zhang, Jianing Wang, Xin Liu, Zhaoli Ge, Zhonghua Xiong, Binbin Sui, Yonggang Wang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundThe nucleus accumbens (NAc) plays an important role in the chronic pain process and consists of core and shell subregions with distinct connectivity patterns. However, their roles in migraine progression remain unclear. This study explored static and dynamic functional connectivity (FC) patterns of NAc subregions across healthy controls (HCs), patients with episodic migraine (EM) and patients with chronic migraine (CM).Methods97 participants were enrolled: 70 migraine patients (24 EM, 46 CM) and 27 HCs. All underwent functional magnetic resonance imaging on a GE 3.0T system. Demographic and clinical characteristics were collected. The NAc core and shell were defined as ROIs, and static and dynamic seed-based FC analyses were performed across groups. Partial correlations between FC values and clinical variables were conducted. Significant static FC and dynamic FC were selected through multivariable logistic regression. Receiver operating characteristic curves were used to assess their diagnostic performance for distinguishing CM patients.ResultsCompared with HCs, EM showed increased sFC between the right NAc core and right supplementary motor area (SMA), whereas CM exhibited decreased sFC, particularly between the left NAc shell and the left fusiform gyrus and between the right NAc core and the left cerebellum crus II. Relative to EM, CM showed reduced sFC mainly between the left NAc shell and the bilateral precentral gyrus and right superior frontal gyrus (SFG), and between the right NAc core and right SMA, right inferior frontal gyrus, and left inferior temporal gyrus (ITG). The dFC analysis revealed decreased connectivity between the left NAc shell and left precentral gyrus and right SFG, and between the right NAc core and left SMA. Reduced sFC between the right NAc core and left ITG and dFC between the left NAc shell and right SFG effectively distinguished CM from EM. The combined FC calculated from sFC and dFC further improved discrimination (AUC = 0.919).ConclusionsNAc subregions in CM showed reduced connectivity with multiple brain regions, mainly in sensorimotor, executive control, and default mode networks. Significant sFC and dFC of the NAc subregions may serve as potential imaging biomarkers for CM.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s10194-026-02351-w.Keywords: Chronic migraine, Nucleus accumbens subregions, Resting-state functional connectivity, Dynamic functional connectivity

Introduction

Migraine, a neurological disorder with high global prevalence and substantial disability [1], is characterized by recurrent moderate to severe headaches lasting 4–72 h, often accompanied by nausea, vomiting, photophobia, and phonophobia [2]. It can be classified as EM and CM based on whether the monthly headache days (MHDs) exceed 15 days per month for over 3 months. Previous data has shown that approximately 2.5% patients with EM would progress to CM each year [3]. Because of longer disease duration and more severe pain intensity, patients with CM are at increased risk of headache-related disability, psychiatric comorbidities, and substantial financial and occupational burdens [4].

The pathophysiological mechanisms underlying CM remain incompletely understood, but accumulating evidence suggests that the limbic system plays a prominent role in the chronification process. Within this typical circuit, dopaminergic neurons in the ventral tegmental area (VTA) project to brain regions involved in pain perception, affective-motivation, and decision-making, including the prefrontal cortex (PFC), amygdala, and NAc [5]. The NAc, considered the central region of the limbic system, is mainly involved in the process of motivation, reward processing, and aversive avoidance. Anatomically, the NAc can be divided into two parts: the core and shell regions, which have distinct functions and projections. The NAc core, which resembles other components of the striatum, is activated at the onset of pain or in the anticipation of pain relief. The NAc shell, a transition structure between striatum and amygdala, projects diffusely to ventral pallidum, lateral hypothalamus, and amygdala and plays an important role in nociceptive hypersensitivity and negative emotions [6]. A diffusion tensor imaging (DTI) study also demonstrated that the NAc core was preferentially connected to the basal ganglia, prefrontal cortex, and thalamus, while the NAc shell was closely connected to the amygdala [7]. In addition to differences in structural connectivity, the core and shell regions exhibit different patterns of connectivity with resting-state brain networks [8]. A previous study has shown that the altered connectivity between NAc and prefrontal cortex is involved in the aetiology of chronic low back pain (CLBP), and crucial to the transition process from acute to chronic pain [9]. These findings suggest that the NAc is involved in pain chronicity, but its role in the mechanisms of CM remains ambiguous. Therefore, this study selected the two subregions of the NAc as interest regions (ROIs) to explore their potential role in migraine chronification.

Studies explicitly targeting the NAc, particularly its subregions, in migraine remain limited. Among the existing studies, recent research employing quantitative susceptibility mapping (QSM) reported that increased regional iron content in the NAc was associated with disease progression [10]. A longitudinal study demonstrated that after sphenopalatine ganglion blocks, the volume of left NAc in CM patients with MOH had been significantly increased, indicating relevant alterations of this region appear to be reversible [11]. These studies highlighted the critical involvement of the NAc in the progression of migraine, but investigations explicitly targeting its shell and core are still scarce.

Moreover, previous resting-state functional magnetic resonance imaging (fMRI) studies have primarily relied on the assumption that brain signals are relatively static over time, which may obscure dynamic fluctuations in brain connectivity. To capture this temporal variability, sliding-window analysis has emerged as a useful approach for identifying dFC features, which may provide more reliable and informative biomarkers for diagnosis and prediction [12–14].

Studies on the potential role of NAc subregions in CM pathogenesis and their FC patterns remain limited, and few have analyzed their dFC features during migraine progression. Therefore, this study aimed to advance the understanding of migraine chronification by combining sFC and dFC analyses of the NAc core and shell.

Methods

From October 2020 to March 2023, patients who were diagnosed with migraine at the Headache Center, Neurology Department of the Beijing Tiantan Hospital, Capital Medical University, according to the International Classification of Headache Disorders, 3rd edition (ICHD-3) criteria, were recruited. The inclusion criteria for patients with EM and CM partly overlapped. The general inclusion criteria for both EM and CM were as follows: (1) age: 14–65 years; (2) migraine onset < 50 years; (3) a history of migraine for more than 1 year; (4) MRI scanning during the interictal period; (5) no acute medication within the preceding 48 h; (6) no migraine prophylactic medication within 3 months before scanning; (7) volunteered to participate in the study. Additional inclusion criteria for patients with EM: confirmed diagnosis of episodic migraine without aura. Additional inclusion criteria for patients with CM: confirmed diagnosis of CM (with or without medication overuse headache [MOH]), and all patients experienced migraine without aura. For HCs, the inclusion criteria were: (1) age: 14–65 years; (2) volunteered to participate in the study.

Participants (including patients with migraine and HCs) were excluded if they had: (1) other types of headache or pain conditions in regions outside the head; (2) MRI scans of poor quality, such as excessive head motion ( displacement > 1.5 mm in any direction or a head rotation angle > 1.5°) or missing raw data; (3) contraindications for MRI scanning; (4) psychiatric disorders requiring treatment; (5) severe systemic diseases; (6) severe cognitive decline; (7) pregnancy or lactation.

This study, a sub-study of the China HeadAche DIsorders RegiStry Study, was approved by the Ethics Committee of Beijing Tiantan Hospital, Capital Medical University (KY2022-044), which was registered at https://www.clinicaltrials.gov (unique identifier: NCT05334927). All participants involved volunteered to sign an informed consent form.

Clinical characteristics

General clinical data, including age, sex and body mass index (BMI, kg/m2), were collected from all participants. Additionally, headache characteristics and neuropsychological scales were assessed in migraine patients. Headache features, including pain intensity (Visual Analogue Scale [VAS]), headache frequency and migraine history, were recorded in detail. Migraine-related disability and the impact of headache were assessed by Migraine Disability Assessment Scale [15] (MIDAS) and Headache Impact Test-6 [16] (HIT-6), respectively. Sleep disturbances and the severity of depression and anxiety were quantified using the Pittsburgh Sleep Quality Index10 (PSQI), the Patient Health Questionnaire-9 [17] (PHQ-9) and the Generalized Anxiety Disorder-7 [18] (GAD-7), respectively.

Imaging acquisition

The MRI data of all participants were acquired at a 3.0 Tesla GE Signa Premier scanner (GE Healthcare) equipped with a 48-channel head coil at the National Neurological Centre in Beijing Tiantan Hospital. Participants were instructed to keep their eyes closed and remain head and neck still during the scan. Foam padding and earplugs were also used to minimize head motion and reduce noise interference. Imaging parameters of the high-resolution 3D T1-weighted structural image using MP-RAGE sequence were as follows: resolution = 1 × 1 × 1 mm3, preparation time = 880 ms, acquisition time = 4 min, recovery time = 400 ms, field of view = 250 × 250 mm2, acceleration factor = 2, flip angle = 8°, slice number = 192. BOLD functional images were acquired using an echo planar imaging (EPI) sequence with the following settings: voxel dimensions = 2.4 × 2.4 × 2.4 mm³, Echo Time (TE) = 39 ms, Repetition Time (TR) = 100 ms, scanning time=330s, field of view = 208 × 208 mm2, acceleration factor = 6, flip angle = 64°, slice number = 65, acquisition matrix = 86.

Pre-processing of fMRI data

The fMRI data were preprocessed using the Data Processing and Analysis for Brain Imaging (DPABI) software (DPABI_V8.2_240510, http://rfmri.org/dpabi) in MATLAB 2024a (version 2024a, Mathworks, Natick, MA, USA) [19]. The detailed processing steps were as follows: (1) Format conversion: the DICOM format images acquired from the MRI machine were converted into NIFIT format using MRIcroGL, a tool for voxel-based neuroimaging visualization (https://www.nitrc.org/projects/mricrogl) [20]. (2) Remove time points: to attain a steady state of longitudinal magnetization, the initial 10 volumes were excluded, leaving 320 volumes. (3) Slice timing. (4) Realign. (5) Reorient: the origin is typically positioned at the anterior commissure. (6) Co-registration: align the functional images with the high-resolution 3D T1-weighted structural images. (7) Spatial normalization: spatial normalization was applied using the Montreal Neurological Institute (MNI) echo-planar imaging template, and the data were resampled to an isotropic voxel size of 3 × 3 × 3 mm³. (8) Smooth: data smoothing using a Gaussian kernel with a 4 mm full width at half maximum (FWHM). (9) Detrend and filter: the linear trends were removed, and band-pass filtering (0.01–0.08 Hz) was used to minimize low-frequency drift and high-frequency physiological noise. The MRI data with a displacement of > 1.5 mm in any direction or a head rotation angle > 1.5°will be excluded in the process.

Definition of regions of interest (ROI)

The brain atlas, developed by Ye Tian et al., provides a comprehensive description of subcortical structures and has been widely applied in fMRI studies [21]. Given that different subregions of the NAc exhibit distinct functional roles, our study selected four NAc subregions from the atlas as ROIs, including the bilateral NAc core and NAc shell. Static and dynamic seed-based functional connectivity analysis

The sFC analysis was as follows: the mean time series of the four selected seed regions were extracted and Pearson correlation analysis was calculated with each voxel of the whole brain to generate the sFC matrix. Each correlation coefficient (r) was then transformed into a normally distributed z-score using Fisher’s r-to-z transformation.

The dFC analysis was conducted using the Temporal Dynamic Analysis toolkit of DPABI software. Since there was no consensus on the optimal choice of window size and step, the sliding-window width was typically set between 30 and 100 seconds [22–25], and the step size was commonly set at 2 s in previous studies [26, 27]. Therefore, based on the TR of the MRI scanner and parameters established in previous studies, the length of the sliding window was set at 50 TRs (50s), and the window step was set at 2 TRs (2s) in this study to balance the reliability of brain activity and temporal changes. 136 sliding windows were acquired during the scanning process. By calculating the correlation between the mean time series of each NAc subregion and all other voxels within each window, 136 distinct correlation matrices were obtained. The standard deviation across these 136 correlation matrices was then calculated to generate a time-varying dFC map. Similarly, Fisher’s r-to-z transformation was performed to enhance the normality of the correlation distribution.

In our analysis, Voxel-wise analysis of covariance (ANCOVA) was performed to compare FC differences within each NAc subregion among the three groups, with age and sex as covariates. Significant clusters were defined as regions with a voxel-level threshold of p < 0.005 and a cluster-level threshold of p < 0.05, after Gaussian Random Field (GRF) theory correction. To control for a potential confounder when interpreting FC differences associated with chronification, MOH status was additionally included as a covariate when comparing EM and CM groups.

Statistical analysis

The Shapiro-Wilk test was conducted to assess the normality of continuous variables. Continuous variables were presented as mean (standard deviation) or median (interquartile range), depending on their distribution. For data that exhibited a normal distribution, independent-samples t-tests or one-way analysis of variance (ANOVA) were used. Comparisons of non-normally distributed data were performed using the Mann-Whitney U test (comparisons between two groups) or the Kruskal-Wallis H Test (comparisons among three groups). Categorical variables were analyzed using the chi-square test. Correlations between clinical features and FC were calculated using partial Pearson or Spearman correlation analysis with age and sex as covariates, for which multiple comparisons were corrected using FDR correction (108 correlation tests). Logistic regression models were used to select significant sFC and dFC and calculate their combined diagnostic indicator. To evaluate the diagnostic performance of sFC and dFC in distinguishing EM and CM, receiver operating characteristic (ROC) curves were constructed, and the area under the curve (AUC) > 0.7 was defined as good diagnostic performance. In addition, effect sizes were reported to quantify the magnitude of group differences. Cohen’s f²was used for ANOVA, and Cohen’s d was applied for pairwise comparisons. According to Cohen’s interpretation, small/ medium/ large effects at d ≈ 0.2/ 0.5/ 0.8 and at f²≈0.02/ 0.15/ 0.35 [28].

Most data were analyzed using Statistical Product and Service Solutions (SPSS) software (version 23.0; IBM Corp, Armonk, NY, USA). A two-tailed P value < 0.05 was considered statistically significant. The partial correlation and ROC analysis were analyzed using RStudio software (R Core Team (2025). _R: A Language and Environment for Statistical Computing_. R Foundation for Statistical Computing, Vienna, Austria).

Results

Demographics and clinical characteristics

The flowchart of the participants’ inclusion process is presented in Fig. 2. A total of 97 participants were finally enrolled, including 27 healthy controls, 24 episodic migraine patients, and 46 chronic migraine patients. No significant differences were observed among the three groups in sex, age, and BMI. Compared to the EM group, it is worth noting that patients with CM had higher headache frequency (p < 0.001). Although there was no significant difference between EM and CM in terms of migraine history and pain intensity, individuals in the CM group exhibited relatively greater severity on both measures. In addition, the MIDAS (p < 0.001), PHQ-9 (p = 0.003), and GAD-7 (p = 0.015) scores in CM patients were markedly elevated relative to the EM group. Within the CM group, more than 50% of patients had comorbid MOH (Table 1).

The sFC analysis of the NAc shell and core between the HC, EM, and CM groups

With the left NAc shell selected as the seed region for sFC analysis, significant differences were identified among the HC, EM, and CM groups in the following clusters: bilateral precentral gyrus, left opercular part of the inferior frontal gyrus, left middle temporal gyrus, left fusiform, left hippocampus, right superior frontal gyrus, left superior temporal gyrus, and right SMA. Pairwise comparisons with Bonferroni correction were then performed. Compared with HCs, patients in the CM group exhibited reduced FC between the seed region and the left fusiform gyrus (Fig. 3B-I; Table 2). In addition, CM patients had decreased FC across multiple brain regions compared with the EM group, mainly including the bilateral precentral gyrus, right superior frontal gyrus, bilateral opercular part of the inferior frontal gyrus, left hippocampus, bilateral putamen, and right middle temporal gyrus (Fig. 3B-II, Table 2). There were no significant differences observed between HCs and the EM group. Using the right NAc core as the seed region, significant differences among the three groups were observed in the left precentral gyrus, right superior frontal gyrus, right orbital part of the inferior frontal gyrus, left superior temporal pole, and left inferior temporal gyrus. Post hoc pairwise comparisons indicated that compared with HCs, EM patients exhibited increased FC in the left superior parietal gyrus and right SMA (Fig. 4B-II, Table 2), and CM patients exhibited reduced FC in the left cerebella crus II (Fig. 4B-I; Table 2). In addition, distinct patterns of FC were observed between the EM and CM groups, including the right SMA, right superior frontal gyrus, right orbital part of inferior frontal gyrus, left superior temporal pole, and left inferior temporal gyrus (Fig. 4B-III, Table 2). The above findings were robust after including MOH status as a covariate, with group comparisons showing similar cluster locations (Table S1).

The dFC analysis of the NAc shell and core between the HC, EM, and CM groups

With the left NAc shell as the seed region, significant group differences were observed among the HC, EM, and CM groups in its dFC with the left precentral gyrus. Post analysis revealed that compared with the EM group, CM patients exhibited reduced dFC between the seed region and the following regions: the left precentral gyrus and right superior frontal gyrus (Fig. 3B-III, Table 3). No significant differences were found between the HC and EM groups or between the HC and CM groups. With the right NAc core as the seed region, significant clusters among the three groups after GRF corrections were mainly located in the left SMA. Further pairwise comparisons revealed that CM patients exhibited decreased dFC with the left SMA and the right orbital part of the inferior frontal gyrus compared to EM patients (Fig. 4B-IV, Table 3). There were no significant differences found between the HC and EM groups or between the HC and CM groups. These results remained substantially consistent after additionally adjusting for MOH status (Table S2).

Correlation analysis

This study focused on the correlation of sFC and dFC with clinical characteristics in patients with CM, with age and sex included as covariates. These results are summarized in Table S3. Specifically, the sFC between the left NAc shell and the left opercular part of the inferior frontal gyrus was negatively correlated with the MIDAS score (r = -0.309, p = 0.041). The dFC between the left NAc shell and the left precentral gyrus was positively correlated with VAS score (r = 0.344, p = 0.022), and the dFC between the left NAc shell and the right superior frontal gyrus also showed a positive correlation with the PSQI score (r = 0.307, p = 0.048). Additionally, both the sFC and dFC between the right NAc core and the right orbital part of the inferior frontal gyrus showed a positive correlation with the VAS score (r = 0.349, p = 0.022; r = 0.409, p = 0.006). However, these findings did not survive False Discovery Rate (FDR) correction.

Receiver operating characteristic curve analysis

To evaluate the diagnostic accuracy of sFC and dFC in distinguishing between migraine subtypes, ROC curves were constructed to discriminate CM from EM patients (Fig. 5). To identify the optimal diagnostic features, a multivariable logistic regression analysis was used to select statistically significant sFC and dFC features. The results were as follows: the sFC between the right NAc core and left inferior temporal gyrus (B = -3.314, p = 0.031) and the dFC between the left NAc shell and right superior frontal gyrus (B = -1.781, p = 0.033) (Table S4). These significant FCs were then entered into a logistic regression model to generate a combined diagnostic indicator. ROC curve analysis demonstrated that the combined model, calculated from sFC and dFC, outperformed single indicators (AUC = 0.919, 95%CI = 0.856, 0.983], p < 0.001) (Table S5).

Discussion

Our study aimed to explore whether the changes in the FC patterns of NAc shell and core were associated with migraine progression. Integrating the findings from both sFC and dFC analyses, significantly different brain regions between CM and EM groups were located in the sensorimotor network (SMN), the executive control network (ECN), and the default mode network (DMN), with FC exhibiting an increasingly decreased pattern. Additionally, the ROC curves indicated combined FC calculated from sFC and dFC could effectively discriminate between EM and CM patients.

Differential FC patterns of the NAc shell and core between healthy controls and migraine patients

At the group level, significant differences in FC patterns were observed in both EM and CM patients compared with HCs, with more pronounced changes in the CM group. CM patients showed decreased FC compared with HCs, especially between the left NAc shell and the left fusiform gyrus and between the right NAc core and the left cerebellar crus II. The fusiform gyrus is involved in both visual perception and emotional processing. A previous fMRI study reported that migraine patients with comorbid depression exhibit functional abnormalities in this region — specifically, reduced amplitude of low-frequency fluctuations (ALFF) in the right fusiform gyrus [29]. Given that CM is easily complicated by psychological and emotional problems, the reduced FC observed in our analysis may reflect emotion-related alterations associated with disease progression.

Moreover, the cerebellum, once thought to be involved in motor control and coordination, plays a prominent role in the process of pain regulation. A prior study showed that the severity of cerebellar damage correlates with the frequency and duration of migraine attacks [30]. Another MRS study also suggested that reduced GABA levels in the dentate nucleus (DN) of the cerebellum may impair its inhibitory role in pain modulation [31]. The decreased connectivity between the seed region and the left cerebellar crus II in our study may indicate that alterations in cerebellar function potentially participate in the chronic process of migraine.

Distinct FC patterns of the NAc shell and core between EM and CM

Our findings suggest that compared with EM groups, CM patients have reduced connectivity between the NAc shell and core and brain regions mainly involved in the SMN, ECN, and DMN [32].

Abnormal FC associated with SMN

In this study, we found that the reduced FC between the seed regions and the M1 or SMA may be specific alterations associated with disease progression. The primary motor cortex (M1) is considered a key component of SMN. In animal models, the M1, particularly layer 5 and 6, suppresses sensory hypersensitivity and negative emotional valence associated with pain. Specifically, the pathway from M1 layer 6 through mediodorsal thalamus to the NAc was proven to modulate the aversive emotional components of pain [33]. When M1 is dysfunctional, the descending inhibitory system may be impaired, leading to sensory hypersensitivity and pain chronification [34]. In neuroimaging and neuromodulation research, M1 has been established as a therapeutic target for patients with CM. Evidence from a previous study showed that CM patients exhibited longer cortical silent period duration compared with EM patients [35], suggesting that cortical hypo-excitability may contribute to chronification. The therapeutic relevance of M1 is further supported by a randomized sham-controlled trial demonstrating that long-term transcranial direct current stimulation (tDCS) targeting the left motor cortex significantly reduced monthly migraine attacks [36]. SMA has also been used as a stimulation target in several studies on chronic pain treatment, with well-established analgesic efficacy [37–39].

Collectively, it can be concluded that the motor cortex, including M1 and SMA, is not only involved in the regulation of voluntary movement but also plays a crucial role in pain modulation. The reduced connectivity observed in our study aligns with this framework, suggesting that motor cortex activity may be associated with alterations in the NAc-M1/SMA pathway in patients with CM [40, 41].

Abnormal FC associated with ECN

Compared with the EM group, CM patients exhibited decreased FC between the seed regions and the lateral prefrontal cortex. The dorsolateral prefrontal cortex (DLPFC), including the superior and middle frontal gyri, is a core node of the ECN. As a key component of the corticostriatal circuit, the DLPFC-NAc pathway is involved in the modulation of sensory perception and affective experience associated with chronic pain [42]. Alterations in this pathway may contribute to pain chronification through two interrelated mechanisms. First, the DLPFC plays a critical role in the descending inhibitory pathway. In a long-term chronic pain state, reduced DLPFC function may be associated with less effective engagement of this inhibitory pathway, potentially contributing to pain persistence and nociceptive hypersensitivity. Second, the DLPFC is involved in processing pain-related emotions. Alterations in its function may be associated with catastrophizing thinking, leading to excessive attentional bias toward pain and perpetuating a vicious cycle [43]. These functional changes were supported by structural evidence showing reduced gray matter volume (GMV) in the DLPFC of CM patients [44, 45].

Interestingly, the NAc-DLPFC connectivity appears to exhibit distinct patterns across migraine subtypes. A study combining fMRI and PET demonstrated that the EM group exhibited increased FC between the left NAc and DLPFC compared with HCs [46]. Our study also observed a similar trend in EM patients in comparison with HCs, although these differences did not survive GRF correction in our analysis. Moreover, Baliki et al. reported that the FC between the bilateral NAc and PFC was increased during the initial transition from acute to chronic pain [9], suggesting that enhanced corticostriatal connectivity may represent an early adaptive response.

However, this adaptive mechanism may change as the disease progresses. Another study observed hyper-connectivity between NAc subdivisions and DLPFC in patients with longstanding CLBP compared with HCs [8], yet it did not directly compare patients with subacute and chronic pain, leaving the dynamic trajectory of this pathway during chronification unclear.

Therefore, we propose that the enhanced DLPFC-NAc FC observed in the EM group may represent a compensatory mechanism, which can not only engage the descending inhibitory pathway but also help alleviate negative emotions and support reward processing associated with pain relief. However, the reduced connectivity in CM patients may indicate a loss of this adaptive regulation as the disease progresses, potentially contributing to less effective pain modulation and persistent nociceptive perception [42]. Notably, the DLPFC has also been identified as a key target for neuromodulation. In the non-pharmacological management of CM patients, the DLPFC stimulation has shown promising results in reducing monthly headache days and alleviating headache intensity [47, 48].

The inferior frontal gyrus (IFG), as part of the ventrolateral prefrontal cortex (VLPFC), is also an important node of the ECN. This brain region is anatomically positioned to integrate motivational information of both positive and negative valence. A recent study demonstrated that neurons in the VLPFC exhibit bivalent coding properties, responding to opportunities to gain information about both rewards and punishments [49]. The orbital part of the IFG, through its connections with the NAc, is also a component of the corticostriatal circuit, supporting reward processing and decision-making. Structural evidence showed that the GMV of the IFG in patients with chronic pain was significantly reduced [50, 51]. In line with previous findings, our study identified that CM patients exhibited decreased FC between the right NAc core and the right orbital part of the IFG. This alteration may reflect less efficient processing and integration of negative information with relief-related positive signals. Such a pattern could lead to blunted reward anticipation and reduced motivation to seek relief, ultimately contributing to maladaptive cognitive-affective states that perpetuate chronification.

The reduced FC pattern of the lateral prefrontal cortex might be a reflection of the maladaptive changes in response to the long-term pain state. The interpretation of the findings in our analysis remains speculative. Compared with previous studies, our findings suggest that different FC patterns are possibly involved in distinct stages of chronic migraine pathophysiology.

Abnormal FC associated with DMN

While the SMN and ECN emerged as the primary networks affected in the CM group, the regression model further showed that the sFC between the right NAc core and the left ITG was significantly associated with CM and exhibited good discriminative performance in the ROC analysis. The ITG, a key node of the DMN, is not only involved in visual information processing as part of the ventral visual pathway, but also plays an important role in the development and maintenance of pain [52, 53]. Previous studies have found abnormal structure and function in the ITG among patients with migraine. Obermann et al. [54] reported reduced GMV of ITG in patients with vestibular migraine, and another study illustrated that this structural alteration may be associated with abnormalities in multi-sensory integration, such as amplifying vision, hearing, or olfactory stimuli, which often lead to common accompanying symptoms of migraine (e.g., photophobia, phonophobia) [55]. In addition, the abnormal sensory network (auditory network, AN and visual network, VN) was detected in CM, with altered intra-network FC [56]. Therefore, the reduced sFC between the NAc core and ITG might contribute to sensory integration abnormalities, resulting in pain hypersensitivity, which could promote pain chronification through multiple mechanisms, including neuronal plasticity, neuroinflammation, and reward system alterations [57].

After including MOH status as a covariate in the group comparison between EM and CM, the results showed that the significant inter-group brain regions were largely consistent with the primary analysis findings, with no additional clusters reported. Consistent with previous neuroimaging studies [31, 58], these findings suggest that the observed FC patterns are intrinsic characteristics of CM.

Exploratory correlation analysis between significant FC and clinical symptoms in CM

To explore whether alterations of FC associated with migraine chronification carry clinical relevance, we conducted an exploratory correlation analysis in the CM group. Although no significant correlation between fMRI results and any other clinical characteristics after FDR correction were survived, some trends were still worth noting. The FC between the left opercular part of the IFG and the left NAc shell is negatively correlated with the MIDAS score. The MIDAS score reflects the impact of migraine severity on life, work, and social interaction [59]. This negative correlation may indicate the functional alterations in the opercular part of the IFG. A previous study also reported that the nodal local efficiency of the left opercular part of the IFG was negatively correlated with the headache attack frequency [60]. Therefore, the reduced FC patterns may affect the IFG, thereby influencing pain-related cognitive regulation and contributing to migraine chronification, ultimately leading to migraine-related disability and reduced quality of life. The findings should be interpreted with caution. Future longitudinal studies with larger samples are needed to validate these observations.

Complementary insights from sFC and dFC findings

In this study, sFC and dFC were both applied to explore distinct FC patterns in patients with CM. The sFC analysis reflects the average connectivity between seed regions and the whole brain voxels over the entire scanning period, representing a relatively stable and trait-like state. While the dFC analysis calculates the connectivity of the overlapping time periods in the whole time series, representing the immediate fluctuating state of the brain [61].

The results of sFC and dFC analyses reported in our study revealed convergent patterns of reduced connectivity between the NAc and key nodes of the SMN and ECN—including the precentral gyrus, SMA, and superior and inferior frontal gyri [62, 63]. This consistent reduction across both static and dynamic analyses suggests altered information integration between these networks as a stable characteristic of chronic migraine. Additionally, the concurrent reduction across temporal scales within these networks may reflect an altered capacity for flexible network remodeling, potentially influencing the brain’s ability to efficiently transition between different states in response to ongoing pain. Such rigidity in network dynamics could contribute to the sensitization and maintenance of pain, as the brain becomes “locked” into a maladaptive connectivity pattern, thereby perpetuating the chronic pain state.

Notably, while the spatial patterns were similar, the number of brain areas and cluster size of significant regions were more constrained in the dFC analysis compared to sFC. This discrepancy likely reflects the inherent differences in the aspects of connectivity captured by each metric. Specifically, dFC analysis is designed to characterize transient fluctuations in brain activity over time. The smaller clusters and more limited brain areas observed may indicate that connectivity alterations in CM, although consistently detectable at the static level, exhibit reduced temporal variability, possibly indicating a narrowing of the brain’s dynamic range [64]. This finding was consistent with the notion of a less flexible brain state in migraine chronification.

Given that sFC analysis and dFC analysis capture brain activities from complementary insights, ROC analysis was performed to evaluate the diagnostic performance of selected FC features using logistic regression. The results showed that both metrics demonstrated good discriminative ability, and the combined FC feature outperformed either metric alone in diagnostic efficacy. Together, these findings suggest that sFC and dFC could provide non-redundant information and highlight that taking time variability into account may offer valuable insights into the pathophysiological mechanisms underlying migraine chronification.

Limitations

It must be acknowledged that this study has several limitations. Firstly, the data of included patients are from single-center and the generalizability and reproducibility of our findings remain to be validated. In addition, this was a cross-sectional study. The generalization of results and the interpretations of causal relationships still require large-sample, multicenter, and long-term follow-up. Additionally, the fMRI technique was non-invasive, and it has relatively poor sensitivity to signal changes in deep brain nuclei, which only represent indirect measurements of brain activity. Finally, there was no significant correlation between inter-group different FC and clinical features observed after FDR correction, and the ROC analysis was performed on the discovery dataset without cross-validation, resampling, or external cohort validation; therefore, these results should be interpreted with caution. Large sample sizes are still required to verify the reproducibility and validity of these findings in future studies.

Conclusion

In summary, migraine chronification is potentially associated with reduced FC between the NAc (shell and core) and multiple brain regions, primarily nodes of the SMN, ECN, and DMN, including the precentral gyrus, SMA, and lateral prefrontal cortex (Fig. 6). Altered interactions between the descending inhibitory pathway and reward system may serve as a potential underlying mechanism for chronification. Furthermore, the ROC curve constructed by combining static and dynamic analysis demonstrates higher accuracy in identifying patients with CM. Together, these findings extend previous investigations and provide potential targets for neurointervention.
