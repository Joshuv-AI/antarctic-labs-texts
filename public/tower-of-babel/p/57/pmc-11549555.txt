Pandemic storytelling and student engagement: how students imagined pandemics before COVID-19 pandemic
Authors: Moamen M Elmassry
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
AbstractStudent engagement is one of the critical issues in science classes. This commentary explores the value of storytelling in microbiology education and student engagement. It is a result of an undergraduate exercise, where students were asked to draft short stories on pandemics before the COVID-19 pandemic (i.e. conducted in 2016–2019). The analysis of student writings (total of 244 short stories) aimed to understand the students’ perception of pandemics, the diseases they chose to write about, and their level of knowledge of pandemics. Interestingly, 56.6% of students chose viruses to be the cause of their pandemics, 35.7% chose bacteria, 4.5% chose parasites, and a single student chose a fungus. Respiratory mode of transmission was the top pick in the stories (30%), followed by fecal-oral route, sexually transmitted, and skin-to-skin contact. Therefore, their choice of respiratory diseases as the focus of their short story may suggest their limited understanding of pandemics beyond such diseases. The varying levels of detail in the stories suggested that storytelling could be insightful in identifying knowledge gaps and engaging students. Two exciting questions remain: (1) For the students who wrote those short stories before the COVID-19 pandemic, how did they feel when it hit? (2) If the students are asked again to write a pandemic short story, what would it look like nowadays? A deeper understanding of epidemiology and the social impact of pandemics is crucial for developing effective pandemic preparedness and response plans, and undergraduate courses can play a vital role in this endeavor. Overall, this commentary highlights the value of storytelling in engaging students to assess their knowledge of specific subject matters.Keywords: storytelling, writing, epidemiology, viruses

Exercise motivation

Science classes are challenging for undergraduate students (Tracy et al. 2022); and even more challenging if the students lack the knowledge and/or experience with such classes, which often leads to student disengagement. This makes students’ learning experience even more challenging. Therefore, creative ways to engage students are much-needed (Fredricks et al. 2004). To ease students experiences, several approaches can be leveraged. These approaches can be based on their innate interests or strengths. Although creative writing can be difficult to master, it can be entertaining in science classes because of our inherent interest in storytelling (Kirkland 1997, Osbourn 2006). Without significant training or experience, students can write a short story about anything if they are interested in the subject, invested in the story, or hopefully both. While the written pieces may not pass/satisfy an English creative writing class, they can be valuable in a science class. That is because writing is one of the ultimate techniques to demonstrate metacognition—knowledge about own thinking (Cer 2019). Previous works indeed support the idea that storytelling can be useful in promoting student engagement and knowledge integration (Dolberry 2010, Moitra 2014, Kiselyov and Schunn 2022, Marintcheva 2022). Yet, this has not been demonstrated in a microbiology class in the context of epidemiology and pandemics. Therefore, eight years ago I embarked on a journey with my students to write short stories about pandemics before we had the chance to live one, i.e. COVID-19. Herein, I report a summary of the type of stories I received from my students.

Exercise description

The exercise started with telling the students its goal, i.e. handwrite a short story (1–2 pages) about an epidemic that would spread throughout the university campus, then worldwide becoming a pandemic. The story needed to have five integral pieces, a contagious disease, a clear mode of transmission, main characters (at least three), an index case, and a resolution. The students were free to choose any contagious disease (Fig. 1A). I hoped from that exercise that students would start thinking about modes of transmission and would be able to demonstrate their understanding of disease transmission. The students were also told that they needed to identify the index case (i.e. patient zero) to think about how the epidemic started and by whom. Finally, the students had the freedom to finish the story with a happy or sad ending, saving everyone or letting every character die. The students were free to discuss their disease of choice and its mode of transmission, if necessary, either with me or within their group (3–4 students). The students were given 15 minutes to draft their stories, then they had the chance to share them with their groups, and finally with the entire class (∼24 students).

Exercise outcome

Two hundred forty-four short stories were written by students between Spring 2016 and Spring 2019 (Fig. 1A). Overall, most of the students were successful in achieving the goals of the exercise by drafting a short story, selecting a disease/pathogen, and describing its mode of transmission. Occasionally, I received questions on the mode of transmission. While 15 minutes were allocated to the exercise, many students were so engaged that they wanted to take more time to perfect their stories. Interestingly, 56.6% of students chose viruses to be the cause of their pandemics (top three: flu, cold, then AIDS), 35.7% chose selected bacteria, 4.5% chose parasites, and a single student picked a fungus (Fig. 1B–C). Only 3% (7 students) wrote a story without identifying a clear disease responsible for the outbreak. It is unclear if students’ choices reflect their understanding of certain diseases more than others or their fear of such diseases. 30% of the students selected diseases with a respiratory mode of transmission, followed by fecal-oral route, sexually transmitted, and skin-to-skin contact diseases (Fig. 1D). Therefore, their choice of respiratory diseases as the focus of their short story may suggest a limited understanding of pandemics beyond such diseases.

The varying levels of detail in the stories suggested that storytelling can be an effective way to help students engage with the subject matter and develop a deeper understanding of complex scientific concepts such as epidemiology, which was the case study here. While some students provided a comprehensive account of the disease and its impact on society, others focused solely on the individual experiences of the protagonist. The stories written by students were reflective of their understanding of pandemics, with some stories displaying a higher level of knowledge than others. Indeed, it was shocking how little we knew that we would face an actual pandemic later in 2019 that resembled my students’ stories. I wonder how effective that writing process was for my students to deepen their understanding of epidemiology. I hope that it gave them some comfort to reflect on that experience and maybe circumvent the hardship we all had faced and still facing. In conclusion, storytelling can be an effective way to help students engage with the subject matter and identify potential gaps in their knowledge. Furthermore, the analysis of the short stories provided insight into the perception of pandemics among undergraduate students before the COVID-19 pandemic.
