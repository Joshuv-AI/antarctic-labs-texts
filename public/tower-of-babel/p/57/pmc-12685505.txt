Willingness to participate in a randomized trial of surgical vs. nonoperative care among patients with meniscal tear and knee pain after a course physical therapy
Authors: Katharine B Fox, Kyna Long, Jon Dhani, Faith Selzer, Andrea L Carland, Paul M Oh, Nomi S Weiss-Laxer, Jeffrey N Katz
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractObjectiveRandomized controlled trials (RCTs) frequently fail to meet sample size requirements within their proposed timeline. Prospective preference assessments (PPAs) estimate the number of eligible individuals likely to enroll. The COSMIC Trial (Conservative care or Surgery for Meniscal tear after IneffeCtive physical therapy), currently in planning stages, will compare arthroscopic partial meniscectomy (APM) to non-operative treatment among those with meniscal tear who experience knee pain following a course of physical therapy (PT). We conducted a PPA to estimate willingness to enroll in the hypothetical trial and identify factors associated with willingness.MethodWe invited eligible patients 45–85 years old with meniscal tear who were prescribed PT to participate in the PPA. Participants completed up to two questionnaires: one prior to a course of PT (Baseline questionnaire), and another following 10 weeks of PT (End of PT questionnaire). Both questionnaires assessed willingness to enroll in the COSMIC trial and knee treatment preferences.ResultsIn total, 94 participants completed both questionnaires, and 50 completed the End of PT questionnaire only. For those who completed both questionnaires, willingness to enroll in the hypothetical COSMIC RCT fell from 62 ​% at Baseline to 46 ​% in the End of PT questionnaires. Respondents’ strongest preference was to avoid APM (47 ​%). Preference for APM at baseline was associated with willingness to enroll.ConclusionsOur findings suggest that about half of eligible patients would be willing to participate in the COSMIC RCT and that, except for APM, participants’ preference for treatments was not strongly related to willingness to participate.Keywords: Randomized controlled trial, Meniscal tear, Physical therapy, Arthroscopic partial meniscectomy, Knee

Introduction

Randomized controlled trials are rigorous clinical research methods that can help to establish causal relationships between an intervention and an outcome [1,2]. Investigators conducting Randomized controlled trials often have difficulty enrolling sufficient participants to meet sample size requirements over the projected time of recruitment [3] and often resort to extending the recruitment period. During the trial planning phase, it would be useful for investigators to estimate the proportion of eligible individuals who are likely to enroll to inform the number of eligible individuals who should be invited to participate.

One approach to this challenge is to conduct a prospective preference assessment (PPA) [3] in the planning phases of an randomized control trial (RCT). A PPA assesses whether eligible patients would participate in the RCT if it were currently available. Operationally, in a PPA, the research team identifies a sample of individuals who meet entry criteria for the hypothetical trial and ask them whether they would agree to participate if the trial were offered today [3].

Our research team conducted a PPA to estimate the proportion of eligible participants likely to enroll in the COSMIC (Conservative care or Surgery for Meniscal tear after IneffeCtive physical therapy) Trial, which is in its planning phase. We intend for the COSMIC Trial to enroll participants ≥45 years old with meniscal tear and knee pain who remain symptomatic after a course of physical therapy (PT). These participants would be randomized to one of two arms: (1) an enhanced, non-operative treatment group that would receive a combination of personalized PT, intra-articular injections, oral pain medications, and an education/coaching support program; or (2) surgical treatment with arthroscopic partial meniscectomy (APM) followed by standard treatment of care. In the present study, we sought to (1) estimate willingness to participate among a group of eligible patients, (2) examine these individuals’ preferences for the various treatments offered in the hypothetical trial, and (3) assess whether treatment preference is an important determinant of willingness to enroll.

Method

COSMIC trial design

Once launched, the COSMIC Trial would recruit individuals with a degenerative meniscal tear confirmed by MRI and persistent knee pain following the completion of a standard 10-week course of PT. These participants would be randomized in a 1:1 fashion to either undergo APM or to complete an enhanced non-operative treatment regimen including a tailored physical therapy program, oral medications, intra-articular steroid injection, and an education/coaching support program.

Sample

We recruited PPA study participants from ambulatory orthopaedic practices at academic medical centers in Boston, MA, and Buffalo, NY. Inclusion criteria included age 45–85, suspected or confirmed symptomatic meniscal tear, and English-speaking. Exclusion criteria included radiographic evidence of bone-on-bone (Kellgren-Lawrence (KL) Grade 4) tibiofemoral osteoarthritis, inflammatory arthritis affecting the index knee, prior total knee replacement or APM on the index knee, other surgery on the index knee in the last six months, and dementia documented in the medical record. We also excluded participants claiming workers’ compensation, those taking strong opioids or prednisone, and those whose clinicians did not recommend PT.

Over a period of 13 months, research assistants (RAs) reviewed weekly clinic schedules of twenty-four orthopaedic providers to identify potentially eligible patients. Prescreen-eligible patients’ medical records were reviewed prior to and after their visit with a provider to further confirm their eligibility. RAs sent recruitment letters to patients who met the inclusion criteria and whose providers attested that their knee pain was probably or definitely due to meniscal tear. RAs then called these patients to invite them to participate in the PPA. The institutional review boards of both sites approved the study protocol.

Data collection procedures and sources

Eligible and interested participants could be initially identified at two different time-points: during an initial or a follow-up visit with a provider for a suspected meniscal tear. Participants who had yet to begin a scheduled course of PT for their knee pain or who had recently started their treatment (within 1–4 weeks of their first treatment visit) completed both the Baseline and End of Physical Therapy questionnaires (sent 10 weeks after the Baseline questionnaire). Among participants who completed baseline questionnaires, some either opted out or were ineligible (e.g did not complete a course of PT or had surgery on index knee) to receive End of PT questionnaires. Participants who had already completed 9 or more weeks of PT completed the End of Physical Therapy (End of PT) questionnaire.

Both questionnaires assessed knee pain with the Pain Subscale of the Knee Injury and Osteoarthritis Outcome Score (KOOS) [4]. The questionnaires provided a brief description of the interventions and assessed willingness to participate in the hypothetical trial:

“We are planning a randomized trial to compare two treatments for people whose symptoms have not resolved after an initial course of PT for meniscal tear. We'll explain more about the trial: In a randomized trial, you would be assigned to one of the two treatments listed below by chance, essentially by a coin toss: (1) Arthroscopic partial meniscectomy (surgery for meniscal tear); (2) Enhanced nonoperative treatment including a combination of personalized physical therapy, injections into the knee joint, education and coaching support, and medication for relieving knee pain (such as anti-inflammatories). If your initial course of PT does not improve your knee pain, and a trial were offered today, would you agree to participate in a randomized control trial (RCT)?”

Participants could select with one of five response categories that followed: “Yes, definitely,” “Yes, probably,” “Undecided,” “No, probably not,” and “No, definitely not.”

In order to understand participants’ treatment preferences, the Baseline questionnaire asked respondents to rank the following treatments using a 5-point Likert scale, from “Strong preference for receiving” to “Strong preference for avoiding”: APM, personalized PT, injections into the knee joint, education/coaching support, and medication for relieving knee pain (e.g. anti-inflammatories).

In the End of PT questionnaire, participants rated their satisfaction with the results of their PT program from 0 to 3, with 0 being “Very Dissatisfied” and 3 being “Very Satisfied,” in the following areas: Relieving knee pain, improving straightening and bending the knee, and improving ability to do housework. End of PT questionnaire respondents reporting a KOOS score less than 80 (scale 0–100 with 0 being the worst) were identified as continuing to experience mild to moderate knee pain that did not substantially improve following a course of PT. Only these identified respondents with KOOS scores <80 were asked the willingness to participate and treatment preference questions from the Baseline questionnaire: “Would you agree to participate in a RCT that compares arthroscopic partial meniscectomy (surgery for meniscal tear) versus an enhanced nonoperative multimodal treatment program for patients with meniscal tear and osteoarthritis?” with the same five response categories as described previously.

Statistical analysis

We used descriptive statistics to describe the cohort, their willingness to participate in the hypothetical RCT, and their knee treatment preferences. For willingness, we grouped respondents as being either Willing or Unwilling. We defined Willing as including both “Yes, definitely” and “Yes, probably,” and Unwilling as including “Undecided,” “No, probably not,” and “No, definitely not.”

We used contingency tables and chi-squared tests for trends (Cochran-Mantel-Haenszel) to assess the associations between treatment preference and willingness to enroll, using a nominal p-value of 0.05 to reflect statistical significance. Missing data points were uncommon and subjects with missing data were removed from the corresponding analysis.

Results

Cohort assembly

During study enrollment (September 2023–September 2024), we prescreened 5390 patients across both sites who appeared on the clinic schedules of orthopaedic providers (Fig. 1). Of those patients, 1977 (37 ​%) were found to be ineligible during prescreening, with the most common reasons including symptoms not due to meniscal tear and PT not recommended. Of the 3413 pre-screen eligible patients, 544 (16 ​%) were found to be potentially eligible after screening the clinic note (Fig. 1). We reached 369 (68 ​%) of these potentially eligible participants by phone and 211 (57 ​%) expressed interest in participating in our PPA.

Participant characteristics

The participants who completed one or both questionnaires were predominantly white (91 ​%) and non-Hispanic (97 ​%). The average age was 62.0 years (SD ​= ​8.9) with a range from 45 to 85 years. The cohort was mostly female (62 ​%) (Table 1). We did not observe differences in demographic characteristics among respondents who completed the Baseline questionnaire only, those who completed the End of PT only, and those who completed both questionnaires.

Before PT, most respondents (85 ​%) reported KOOS pain in the 40–79 range, where 0 is defined as the worst pain and 100 as no pain. Only 6 ​% reported a KOOS score <40, and 9 ​% had a KOOS score ≥80. KOOS pain scores from the End of PT questionnaire, on average, improved from Baseline to End of PT, with 35 ​% falling into the ≥80 category (Table 1).

Willingness to participate in the RCT

Overall, participants were generally willing to enroll in the COSMIC trial if the trial had been available. Prior to a course of PT (on the Baseline questionnaire), 62 ​% responded that they were “definitely” or “probably” willing to participate in the planned trial. Following a course of PT (on the End of PT questionnaire), this proportion dropped to 46 ​%. Additionally, those who were probably or definitely not willing to participate increased from 5 ​% in the Baseline questionnaire to 27 ​% in the End of PT questionnaire (Table 1).

Among 53 participants who completed both the Baseline and End of PT questionnaires and had <80 ​at End of PT, 14 (26 ​%) maintained identical levels of willingness between the two timepoints, 8 (15 ​%) had greater willingness following their course of PT, and 31 respondents (59 ​%) reported lower levels of willingness from pre-to post-PT timepoints (Table 2).

COSMIC trial treatment preferences

On both questionnaires, over half of the respondents indicated a strong preference for receiving personalized PT (62 ​% and 50 ​% on Baseline and End of PT questionnaires, respectively), with a similarly high proportion indicating a strong preference for receiving education/coaching support (50 ​% and 45 ​%). Small proportions of participants reported a slight or strong preference for avoiding additional PT; 2 ​% among those who completed the Baseline questionnaire and 4 ​% among those who completed the End of PT questionnaire. Similarly, 4 ​% of participants who completed the Baseline questionnaire and 10 ​% who completed the End of PT questionnaire indicated a preference to avoid the education/coaching support program.

In contrast, the preference to avoid APM was substantial and increased following their PT program. Those who had a strong preference for avoiding APM increased from 29 ​% to 35 ​% from Baseline to the End of PT questionnaire. About a quarter of participants wished to avoid intra-articular injections, and about a third reported a desire to avoid medications at both timepoints (Table 3).

Relationship between treatment preferences and willingness to participate

Willingness to enroll in the COSMIC RCT was associated with a treatment preference to avoid APM. We did not observe a similar association between treatment preference and willingness to enroll with other treatments. On the Baseline questionnaire, 49 ​% of participants with a strong preference to avoid APM and 58 ​% of those with a slight preference to avoid APM were willing to participate in the COSMIC trial. In contrast, 67 ​% of those with a slight preference for receiving APM were willing, and 82 ​% of those who had a strong preference to receive APM were willing (p ​= ​0.02; Table 4, Fig. 2). This trend was apparent in the End of PT questionnaire as well but was not statistically significant (p ​= ​0.21; Table 4).

Discussion

We surveyed individuals seeking clinical care for knee pain consistent with a suspected meniscal tear who were eligible for our hypothetical trial. We elicited willingness to participate in the hypothetical trial and treatment preferences (for APM, PT, education/coaching, injections, and oral medications) through questionnaires. We found that respondents were generally willing to participate in the COSMIC RCT (“Yes, definitely” or “Yes, probably”) before undergoing a first course of PT. Importantly nearly 50 ​% of those still experiencing pain after an initial course of PT were interested in participating. Our study also found that preference for our proposed treatments, other than APM, did not appear to play a role with willingness. Respondents’ preference to avoid or receive any of the treatments did not impact their willingness to participate, save avoidance of APM.

Our questionnaires assessed preference for receiving five separate treatments (available in the hypothetical RCT) alongside willingness to participate. Nearly all participants expressed a preference to receive PT and education/coaching support, which, in comparison to surgery, are less invasive and lower-risk treatment options. The proportion of participants who had a slight or strong preference to avoid APM (Baseline: 47 ​%, End of PT: 54 ​%), injections (Baseline: 24 ​%, End of PT: 24 ​%), and oral medications (Baseline: 32 ​%, End of PT: 30 ​%) were relatively consistent across the Baseline and End of PT questionnaires. We also assessed the potential impact of treatment preference on willingness to participate, finding that, of the five treatments, only APM preference was associated with willingness. Those who preferred to avoid APM were less likely to be willing to enroll at baseline (e.g. among those with strong preference to avoid APM, 49 ​% were willing, as compared with 82 ​% willing among those with strong preference to receive APM). This relationship was less pronounced at the end of PT (38 ​% vs. 57 ​% respectively among those with strong preference to avoid and to receive APM). Preferences for other treatments were not associated with willingness to enroll.

Even though all the participants queried about preference for APM on the End of PT questionnaire had persistent knee pain following a course of PT (KOOS <80), aversion to surgery remained high (54 ​% indicating a preference for avoiding APM). Of these subjects with a preference for avoiding APM, 21 of 50 (42 ​%) nonetheless expressed willingness to enroll in the trial even though half would be randomized to APM. This finding is paradoxical: qualitative studies could help understand why so many subjects who reported an aversion to APM said they would enroll in the hypothetical study.

Our estimates of willingness to participate in the COSMIC RCT are in range with previous PPA studies comparing orthopaedic operative to non-operative treatments, which have shown 46–90 ​% of respondents would be willing to participate in an RCT [2,5,6]. Furthermore, the trials themselves randomized 26–63 ​% of eligible subjects [[7], [8], [9]]. A prominent Swedish trial of ACL reconstruction vs. nonoperative care for ACL tear randomized 63 ​% of eligible subjects [10], underscoring an observation that enrollment rates of eligible subjects differ from country to country [11].

We note several caveats and potential limitations. We dichotomized “willingness to participate’ because our sample was too small to analyze ‘definitely’ and ‘probably’ willing separately. While 46 ​% of participants stated they ‘probably’ or ‘definitely’ would be willing to participate, 16 ​% stated they would ‘definitely’ be willing to participate. We cannot say for sure whether the number that would actually participate is closer to 16 ​% or to 46 ​%. We also note that our statements about the associations between treatment preference and willingness should be interpreted cautiously because the sample size is limited, willingness is dichotomized, and other unmeasured variables such as motivation may confound these associations.

Prospective preference assessments are hypothetical in nature and are therefore limited in their capacity to predict whether people would truly participate, even if they indicated they would on our questionnaires. We acknowledge that these self-reported findings may be explained in part by social desirability bias, in which respondents express greater willingness to participate in order to please the research team [12], which could potentially result in an overestimation of willingness to participate. This potential bias was likely mitigated in part because respondents were aware their individual responses were anonymous. A range of other factors could potentially have influenced self-reported willingness including prior experiences with randomized trials, physical therapy and surgery. Finally, the cohort is predominantly non-Hispanic white females, potentially limiting the generalizability of our findings to more diverse patient populations.

In conclusion, about half of the fully eligible participants reported they would probably or definitely participate in the RCT, with the proportion willing to participate decreasing by 16 ​%) after completing a standard course of PT. These data will allow us to estimate the number of people to screen for eligibility in order to meet sample size projections. When assessed prior to a course of PT, preference for treatments associated with willingness to enroll was modality-specific, meaning this trend was only observed for APM and not for other proposed treatments. Among those with residual knee pain following a PT course, we did not observe significant associations between preferences for any treatment and willingness to participate. Further research is warranted to understand the factors beyond treatment preferences that drive willingness to participate in trials. In particular, qualitative studies may permit a deeper understanding of patient decision-making processes and why they might be unwilling to enroll in trials.
