Post‐Oviposition Embryonic Staging Scheme for the Delicate Skink, Lampropholis delicata
Authors: Jinglin Wen, Oliver William Griffith
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTSkinks are an important model in evolutionary research because they have evolved a suite of features of interest in vertebrate biology, from limb loss to viviparity. Furthermore, they have been a model for understanding ecophysiology and behavior, particularly the impact of climate change on ectothermic animals. Despite studies on the impact of developmental processes on skink biology, researchers have relied on embryonic staging schemes from species that diverged from skinks over 170 million years ago. We describe Lampropholis delicata as a model and establish the first embryonic development staging scheme for a skink. We detail the morphology of post‐oviposition embryos at different stages and compare them with other reptile lineages. Embryos across 14 stages are described based on the development of limbs, eyes, brain, branchial arches, frontonasal prominence, otic vesicle, scales, and pigmentation. We distinguish early stages via the shape of the otic vesicle, and find the pineal organ is present earlier than in Zootoca and Anolis embryos. This skink embryonic staging scheme serves as a valuable tool for ongoing research into skink biology and can be used as a benchmark for genetic, evolutionary, and physiological research.

Introduction

Embryonic staging schemes are fundamental for researchers to understand the nature and evolution of developmental processes, as well as look at the impacts of environment on early life (Hall 2012; Smith‐Paredes et al. 2021). These embryo staging schemes serve as morphological references used in evolutionary comparisons, as reference points for the impact of gene editing, and molecular physiology (Cordero and Werneburg 2022; Ollonen et al. 2018; Rasys et al. 2019). Due to the differences in embryonic development across species and the diversity of animal models used in research, researchers typically rely on embryonic staging schemes from closely related lineages (Andrews et al. 2013; Grosso et al. 2020; Pezaro et al. 2013; Schlindwein, Yaryhin, et al. 2022; Schlindwein and Werneburg 2022). Despite being a popular model in evolutionary and ecophysiological studies, there is no embryonic staging scheme for any scincid lizard (family: Scincidae). To date, most researchers involving skink embryos have relied on embryonic staging schemes for Zootoca vivipara or Anolis carolinensis with both lineages being ~174 million years removed from skinks (Kumar et al. 2017).

Lampropholis delicata (De Vis 1888), delicate skink or dark‐flecked sun skink (for taxonomy see: Chapple et al. 2011), has emerged as a model species used in thermal biology, reproductive evolution, and behavioral ecology (Chapple et al. 2011; Clerke and Alford 1993; Kar et al. 2022; Michelangeli et al. 2016). It is a model species because of its ease of care and wide distribution across the south and east coast of Australia (from Brisbane to Adelaide plus Tasmania) (Burgin 1993). Furthermore, it has many ancestral features of skinks, including being oviparous, having a complete set of limbs and digits, and genetic XY sex determination (Bull and Whitaker 1975; Donnellan 1991; Joss and Minard 1985). Previous studies have focused on its distribution, behavior, and molecular aspects (Wen et al. 2022). Understanding the embryonic development of L. delicata is important to further develop the species as a model in reptile evolution and physiology.

Here we provide the first post‐oviposition embryonic staging scheme for a skink, L. delicata. Further, we compare our embryonic staging scheme to those developed for other reptile lineages, noting unique features to this skink lineage.

Materials and Methods

We studied the developmental stages of L. delicata and focused on the embryonic development in laid eggs. This species is widespread in the urban areas of Sydney. We collected gravid female L. delicata from urban parks across Sydney in mid‐October and allowed them to lay eggs in captivity. All work was approved by the Macquarie University Animal Ethics Committee (ARA 2022/007).

Lizards were examined at least twice daily for oviposition. Eggs were collected and incubated in 15 mL plastic containers with vermiculite, which was mixed with an equal weight of water to maintain humidity. We collected 61 eggs from 19 clutches and incubated them at 26°C. This is a standard incubation temperature for this species based on average nest temperatures in Sydney (Qualls and Shine 2000). For the purposes of this study, the period from egg laying to 24 h was considered Day 0, from 24 to 48 h was considered Day 1, and so on. Whole eggs were fixed in 10% neutral buffered formalin (24 h, room temperature), then partially dehydrated (70% ethanol, 3×1 h, room temperature, and stored (70% ethanol, −30°C). We fixed and dissected embryos from Days 0 to 32 and took photographs using an OLYMPUS SZ61 stereo microscope and an industrial digital camera (14MP, ½.3” colour USB2.0 APTINA CMOS SENSOR).

Results

We describe 14 developmental stages in the laid eggs of L. delicata (Figures 2, 3, 4). The incubation period is 35 days at 26°C. We describe the morphology of embryos based on features of the limbs, eyes, brain, branchial arches, frontonasal prominence, otic vesicle, scales, and pigmentation (Figure 1). There is no limbless stage in post‐oviposition embryos.

Lampropholis delicata embryos with key structures labeled. (a) Stage 31. (b) Stage 35.

Through comparison with the Dufaure and Hubert staging scheme, the first post‐oviposition stage of L. delicata showed similar features to stage 31 of Z. vivipara (Figure 2). Therefore, we began our post‐oviposition staging of L. delicata from stage 31. By not naming the first day of oviposition as Day 1, this will allow future studies to describe the pre‐oviposition development of the species and have a consistent and continuous numbering system from fertilization to hatching. Development continues to progress with fully developed young described as stage 44 (Figures 2, 3, 4).

Stage 31

Somites

More than 30.

Limbs

Forelimb‐bud and hindlimb‐bud present, both are paddle‐shaped, hindlimb is bigger than forelimb.

Branchial Arches

The maxilla extends toward the anterior part of the lens; the mandible is not visible. The second and third branchial arches are partially fused, and the fourth and fifth branchial arches are visible but diminutive.

Eye

Pigment of retina is visible, pigment is visible in the iris, lens is visible, eyelid is not visible.

Brain

The presence of the mesencephalon is prominent, the length of the telencephalon and diencephalon are almost equal, telencephalon height is slightly larger than diencephalon height, the funnel‐shaped rhombencephalon can be observed from top view (Figure 1 and Supporting Information S1: Figure 1).

Otic Vesicle

Otic vesicle is visible, appears granular, and contains white matrix (Supporting Information S1: Figure 2). Two otic vesicles sit laterally and are separated by an intervening mesenchymal domain (Supporting Information S1: Figure 3).

Frontonasal Prominence

Extends toward the anterior margin of the eye.

Tail Bud

Tail curls multiple times.

Stage 32

Limbs

Limbs are paddle‐shaped and wider than the previous stage. The area of interdigital webbing is slightly decreased due to the interdigital apoptosis, and the segmentation of digits is vaguely visible.

Eye

Pigment of retina is darker than previous stage, especially the iris and the edge of the eye. Eyelids are present on the upper portion of the eye and cover 50% of the upper portion of the eye.

Brain

Telencephalon and mesencephalon are enlarged.

Mandible extends toward the anterior margin of the eye.

Limbs

Limbs' length is longer than in the previous stage. Further decrease of interdigital webbing, the shape of claws first appeared.

Otic Vesicle

The otic vesicles migrate medially so that they now sit side by side and are next to each other (Supporting Information S1: Figure 3c).

Discussion

We present a developmental staging scheme for the delicate skink (L. delicata, Family: Scincidae). The observed developmental characters support 14 developmental stages from the time of oviposition to hatching (Figures 2, 3, 4). In addition to the morphological characters described, we observed that the otic vesicle offers an important insight into the developmental timing, immediately post egg deposition (Figure 2 and Supporting Information S1: Figure 2). Given that these structures are prominent and visible externally, future work should identify if these are a useful tool for comparative studies on fine scale differences in embryonic development. Particularly, our observations may be useful in distinguishing subtle changes in the timing of egg deposition, which may be seen during the evolution of viviparity in other skinks (Laird et al. 2019; Murphy et al. 2006; Ortiz et al. 2019). Further, we measured the fore‐ and hind‐limb lengths through each day of development (Figure 5 and Supporting Information S1: Figure 4), this provides a benchmark for limb growth and development to compare with limb‐reduced taxa. These comparative studies could allow researchers to identify the developmental checkpoints that have been altered to facilitate limb reduction (Smith‐Paredes et al. 2021).

We compared the timing of developmental processes in the delicate skink, with the most widely used squamate staging schemes Z. vivipara and A. carolinensis (Supporting Information S1: Table 1). Through this comparison, we identified several heterochronies between the three lineages, further justifying the need for lineage‐specific staging schemes, and when species‐specific staging schemes are not available, clear articulation of the features used to assign developmental progression. In the post‐oviposition embryos of L. delicata, the first stage of embryo shows similar features as stage 31 of Z. vivipara and stage 6 of Anolis. Compared with the Anolis and Z. vivipara, we found that the delicate skink requires more stages to adequately describe the development of the otic vesicle, lower jaw, and brain (Dufaure and Hubert 1961; Sanger et al. 2008). At post‐oviposition, the otic vesicle of Anolis completes white matrix filling at Day 0 to Day 3, which occurs earlier than in L. delicata (Day 5). For brain development, the pineal organ appears at an earlier stage in L. delicata (stage 38, Day 8) than in Anolis (stage 12, Day 12 to Day 14) and Z. vivipara (stage 35). In Anolis and Z. vivipara, the pineal organ or pineal eye appears once digit individualization is completed. In Z. vivipara, the paddle‐shaped limb appears at stage 31, while the lower jaw starts to develop at stage 33. However, in L. delicata, the lower jaw becomes visible just after the first paddle‐shaped limb stage. In contrast with Anolis and some other squamates (Iguana iguana, Physignathus lesueurii, and Varanus rosenbergii), L. delicata hatch without yolk internalization (Pezaro et al. 2013; Troyer 1987). These differences make the L. delicata developmental staging scheme more accurate for stage identification in skink evolutionary studies.

While we present a post‐oviposition embryo developmental staging, key developmental processes occur prior to oviposition. Despite being oviparous, much of the development including gastrulation and early limb bud formation occurs in utero. These early developmental processes are also important if researchers want to understand the impact of environmental factors at early developmental timepoints, or study the constraints on evolution imposed by early developmental processes (Charney et al. 2014; Smith‐Paredes et al. 2021). Future research should aim to develop a pre‐egg deposition embryo staging scheme for this species, to allow more research into the impacts on and evolution of early life in skinks.

Temperature plays an important role in the timing of development in ectothermic reptiles. In this project, we incubate eggs at 26°C, which represents the average temperature of L. delicata nests in nature. However, in L. delicata, eggs can survive at a wide range of incubation temperatures, albeit with effects on incubation length and size of offspring at hatching (Chapple et al. 2014; Kar et al. 2022). Often, these impacts can follow individuals throughout their lives (Crino et al. 2024). Our data will allow future studies to identify comparable developmental stages between embryos impacted by different environmental conditions, such as temperature, expanding the capacity for research into the interaction between genes, the environment, and development.

Conclusion

We provide the first post‐oviposition embryonic staging scheme for L. delicata, and also compare it with other reptile lineages. We also measured the limb length of the embryo for each stage. This resource supports L. delicata as a model for work to understand reproduction, morphology, genetics, and evolution in skinks more generally.
