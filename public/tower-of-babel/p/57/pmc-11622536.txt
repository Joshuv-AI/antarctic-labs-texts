Sucrose induces flowering by degradation of the floral repressor Ghd7 via K48‐linked polyubiquitination in rice
Authors: Lae‐Hyeon Cho, Jinmi Yoon, Gibeom Baek, Win Tun, Hyeok Chan Kwon, Dae‐Woo Lee, Seok‐Hyun Choi, Yang‐Seok Lee, Jong‐Seong Jeon, Gynheung An
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTSucrose functions as a signaling molecule in several metabolic pathways as well as in various developmental processes. However, the molecular mechanisms by which sucrose regulates these processes remain largely unknown. In the present study, we demonstrate that sucrose promotes flowering by mediating the stability of a regulatory protein that represses flowering in rice. Exogenous application of sucrose promoted flowering by inducing florigen gene expression. Reduction of sucrose levels in the phloem through genetic modifications, such as the overexpression of the vacuolar invertase OsVIN2 or the mutation of OsSUT2, a sucrose transporter, delayed flowering. Analysis of relative transcript levels of floral regulatory genes showed that sucrose activated Ehd1 upstream of the florigen, with no significant effect on the expression of other upstream genes. Examination of protein stability after sucrose treatment of major floral repressors revealed that the Ghd7 protein was specifically degraded. The Ghd7 protein interacted with the E3 ligase IPA INTERACTING PROTEIN1 (IPI1), and sucrose‐induced K48‐linked polyubiquitination of Ghd7 via IPI1, leading to protein degradation. Mutants defective in IPI1 delayed flowering, confirming its role in modulating proteins involved in flowering. We conclude that sucrose acts as a signaling molecule to induce flowering by promoting Ghd7 degradation via IPI1.Keywords: flowering time, Ghd7, IPI1, K48‐linked polyubiquitination, rice, sucrose

INTRODUCTION

Plants have a special feature that distinguishes them from yeast and mammals: they have the ability to use the sun's energy to convert carbon dioxide (CO2) into carbohydrates through photosynthesis. These photosynthetically produced carbohydrates, of which sucrose is a prominent example, are produced and temporarily stored in compartments such as vacuoles. Later, during the night, these stored carbohydrates are used by the plant in various source organs to support its growth and development. It is also transported by phloem from the source to sink organs such as flowers, seeds, and roots, where it supports their growth and development and is stored as a reserve (Yoon et al., 2021). Elevated CO2 levels reduce transpiration and increase photosynthesis rates, resulting in higher concentrations of sugars and carbohydrates in the leaves of many plant species (Curtis and Wang, 1998; Teng et al., 2006).

Various experiments have suggested that sucrose acts as a signaling molecule that regulates a wide range of plant metabolisms and growth (Eveland and Jackson, 2012; Sakr et al., 2018; Wingler, 2018; Yoon et al., 2021). Sucrose controls carbohydrate metabolism and partitioning. Fructan synthesis is induced by exogenous sucrose in detached grass leaves (Nagaraj et al., 2001). Although glucose also induces the synthesis, its effect is much weaker compared to sucrose, suggesting that sucrose likely constitutes a signal for the induction (Nagaraj et al., 2001; Martinez‐Noël et al., 2001). Sucrose also appears to be a signaling molecule that controls starch biosynthesis. Genes encoding the large subunits of adenosine diphosphate‐glucose pyrophosphorylase (AGPase) in sweet potatoes (Ipomoea batatas Lam.) and Arabidopsis (Arabidopsis thaliana) are specifically induced by sucrose (Nagata et al., 2012). Other starch metabolism genes, granule‐bound starch synthase I (GBSSI) and β‐amylase, are also induced by sucrose, but not by glucose and fructose (Nakamura et al., 1991; Wang et al., 2001). The Arabidopsis uridine=5=diphosphate‐glucose pyrophosphorylase (UGPase)‐encoding gene Ugp is upregulated by sucrose (Ciereszko et al., 2001). Sucrose has also increased UGPase activity and protein contents. This effect was apparently specific to sucrose and independent of hexokinase (HXK). Sucrose symporter genes and their protein activities in sugar beet leaves are specifically induced by sucrose (Chiou and Bush, 1998).

Exogenous sucrose induces anthocyanin accumulation by increasing the transcript levels of anthocyanin biosynthesis genes in Arabidopsis seedlings (Solfanelli et al., 2006). Other sugars, such as mannose, glucose, and fructose, have not shown such activity, indicating that the induction is sucrose‐specific. The expression levels of anthocyanin biosynthesis genes are also increased in the phosphoglucomutase mutant, which accumulates high levels of sucrose; this suggests that sucrose is a signaling molecule for anthocyanin biosynthesis (Solfanelli et al., 2006).

In dark‐adapted plants, nitrate reductase gene expression is stimulated by sucrose (Cheng et al., 1992; Morcuende et al., 1998; Kaiser and Huber, 2001). Sucrose increases both gene expression and enzymatic activity (Morcuende et al., 1998; Kaiser and Huber, 2001). The effect on nitrogen metabolism is sucrose‐specific (Morcuende et al., 1998).

Sucrose appears to act as a signaling molecule in a wide range of plant growths (Horacio and Martinez‐Noël, 2013). Sucrose promotes plant growth through promoting cell division in apical meristems (Van't Hof, 1966; Menges et al., 2006; Francis and Halford, 2006). In Arabidopsis, sucrose enhances cyclin D gene expression and promotes ribosome synthesis (Riou‐Khamlichi et al., 2000; Kojima et al., 2007). Cotyledon width is inhibited by sucrose, which is mediated by the homeodomain leucine zipper transcription factor ATHB13 in Arabidopsis (Hanson et al., 2001). Although glucose and fructose also affect growth, these effects are much weaker than those of sucrose, suggesting that sucrose is an immediate signaling molecule for cotyledon growth. Neither downregulation nor overexpression of AtHXK1 affected sucrose‐dependent developmental changes in 35S::ATHB13 cotyledons, suggesting that HXKs may play a limited role in sucrose signaling. Growth of stolons in grass species is stimulated by sucrose, but not by glucose and fructose (Willemoës et al., 1988). Sucrose induces storage organ development in several species. For example, sucrose promotes potato tuberization by reducing the levels of gibberellins, which are potent inhibitors of tuber formation (Lawrence and Barker, 1963).

Sucrose is an essential component for flower induction in most plant species. Flowering is induced in the long‐day (LD) plant Brassica campestris when sucrose is added to plants grown in vitro (Friend et al., 1984). Floral induction was observed regardless of day length. Sucrose levels increase dramatically and transiently in the exudates of Sinapis alba when flowering is induced by a single LD treatment (Bernier et al., 1993; Lejeune et al., 1993). Similarly, soluble sugar export from the leaves of Arabidopsis is rapidly and transiently increased by one LD treatment that induces flowering (Corbesier et al., 1998). The sucrose level in the phloem sap of the short‐day (SD) plant Xanthium strumarium is also increased in the plants using a single SD treatment (Houssa et al., 1991). These observations indicate that an increase in the leaf phloem loading of sucrose is associated with floral induction.

The application of sucrose induces flowering in several plant species. In B. campestris, adding 40–80 mmol/L sucrose to the growth medium promoted flowering under SD conditions (Friend et al., 1984). Addition of 1% sucrose to the medium rescued the late‐flowering mutants such as fha, ld, fve, co, and gi in Arabidopsis (Ohto et al., 2001). Spraying 50 mmol/L sucrose onto the leaves of chrysanthemum plants promoted flowering by increasing florigen transcription (Sun et al., 2017).

Genetic studies have supported the role of sucrose in flowering. Antisense suppression of AGPase in potato plants increases soluble sugar levels and promotes early flowering (Müller‐Röber et al., 1992). Leaf‐specific overexpression of sucrose‐phosphate synthase in tomato plants increases the partitioning of carbon into sucrose and reduces the time to blooming (Micallef et al., 1995). In Arabidopsis, late‐flowering phenotypes caused by defective mutations in CONSTANS (CO), GIGANTEA (GI), FCA, FPA, and FVE can be rescued through a sucrose addition (Araki and Komeda, 1993; Roldán et al., 1999). Mutants defective in an INDETERMINATE DOMAIN (IDD) transcription factor AtIDD8 have exhibited delayed flowering (Seo et al., 2011). This delay was caused by reduced expressions of SUCROSE SYNTHASE 1 (SUS1) and SUS4, which limit sucrose cleavage and sugar transport and metabolism in the mutant. Although there is ample evidence that sucrose functions as a signaling molecule in plants, the molecular mechanism by which sucrose controls gene expression is not thoroughly understood.

Flowering is induced by the production of the florigen molecule in phloem companion cells, which moves through the phloem to the shoot apical meristem (SAM). Rice has two major florigen proteins, Heading date 3a (Hd3a) and RICE FLOWERING LOCUS T 1 (RFT1), which are homologous to FT in Arabidopsis. Under non‐permissive conditions, flowering is inhibited by several repressors. These include the major repressor Grain number, plant height, and heading date 7 (Ghd7), which delays flowering by repressing the expression of Early heading date 1 (Ehd1) (Xue et al., 2008). Functional Ghd7 alleles are found in low latitude varieties, whereas non‐functional alleles are found in high latitude varieties (Xue et al., 2008). Ghd7 is regulated in a variety of post‐transcriptional ways. For example, phosphorylation by Heading date 16 (Hd16) enhances its activity (Hori et al., 2013), and protein stability is regulated by phytochrome and OsGI in the photoperiodic control of flowering in rice (Zheng et al., 2019).

The flowering time is determined by various internal genetic factors and multiple external signals (Hori et al., 2016; Cho et al., 2017). In this study, we report that sucrose induces flowering through degrading the Ghd7 protein in rice. We provided evidence that sucrose mediates Ghd7 degradation by the E3‐ligase IPA INTERACTING PROTEIN1 (IPI1).

RESULTS

Sucrose promotes flowering in rice

To investigate whether sucrose application promotes flowering in rice, plants grown under LD conditions were transferred to Yoshida solution at 46 d after germination (DAG). At 50 DAG, the plants were subjected to SD conditions and divided into three groups. Sucrose was added to the first group to create a final concentration of 25 mmol/L. The second group was treated with 25 mmol/L mannitol as a control for osmotic effects. The third group served as a baseline control with no additional substances added. Five days after the treatment, the plants were transferred to soil and grown under SD conditions until flowering (Figure 1A). The control plants in the third group as well as the second group that were transferred to the mannitol‐containing medium, as a control for osmotic effects, flowered at 82 DAG, 32 d after the treatment. However, the sucrose‐treated plants in the first group flowered at approximately 78 DAG, 4 d earlier than the control and mannitol‐treated plants (Figure 1B). Next, we conducted replicate experiments with varying treatment timings, concentrations, and durations to further investigate the effect of exogenous sucrose treatment on flowering in more detail. The results showed that even a 2‐ or 4‐d treatment with 12.5 mmol/L sucrose accelerated flowering by 3 to 4 d, but this effect was specific to the floral transition stage (Figure S1).

To evaluate whether the induced flowering resulted from an increased expression of florigen genes, hydroponically grown rice plants under LD were treated with 25 mmol/L sucrose at 50 DAG, and leaves were harvested 5 d after treatment (Figure 1C–E). An analysis of the expression levels of Hd3a and RFT1 showed that the florigen genes were significantly upregulated through the sucrose application (Figure 1C, D). By contrast, plants treated with 25 mmol/L glucose, 10 mmol/L trehalose, and 2 µmol/L trehalose‐6‐phosphate (T6P) as well as mock treated plants did not show induced expressions of the florigen genes. These findings suggested that sucrose specifically induced florigen expression. Sucrose also specifically induced the expression of Ehd1, which functions immediately upstream of florigen genes (Figure 1E). To confirm this finding, transgenic plants expressing the β‐glucuronidase (GUS) reporter as controlled by the Ehd1 promoter were treated with 100 mmol/L sucrose. Analysis of transgenic plants showed that sucrose strongly induced GUS expression in vascular tissues where Ehd1 was preferentially expressed (Figure 1F). This experiment indicated that the sucrose present in the phloem specifically induced florigen expression to promote flowering.

We measured the endogenous sugar concentration in leaves to determine whether the levels changed during the floral transition period. Plants were grown under SD conditions, and florigen genes were expressed 30 DAG (Cho et al., 2022). The concentrations of glucose and fructose remained low during the vegetative phase, but increased rapidly after 30 DAG (Figure 1G, H). Similarly, the sucrose concentration simultaneously increased (Figure 1I). This observation showed that an increase in sugar concentration coincided with the floral transition time.

Overexpression of Vacuolar Invertase 2 reduces sucrose concentration and delays flowering

To obtain further evidence that sucrose induces flowering, we studied transgenic rice plants carrying the Vacuolar Invertase 2 (OsVIN2) coding region under control of the maize (Zea mays) Rubisco small subunit (ZmRbcS) promoter, which is mesophyll cell‐specific and light‐inducible (Figure 2A, B) (Matsuoka et al., 1994). OsVIN2 encodes a vacuolar protein that cleaves sucrose into glucose and fructose (Figure 2A) (Lee et al., 2019). We selected four independently transformed plants that expressed the introduced OsVIN2 at high levels (Figure 2C). The leaf extracts of the plants showed increased invertase activity compared with that of the wild type (WT) (Figure 2D).

Measurements of sucrose levels in the leaves of the transgenic plants showed that sucrose levels were maintained at low levels during the daytime, whereas the levels in WT leaves were high (Figure 2E). However, the sucrose level was high at the beginning of the dark period and then declined in both OsVIN2 transgenic and WT plants. The daytime‐specific reduction in sucrose levels in the transgenic plants was caused by the ZmRbcS promoter, which is preferentially expressed under light. In contrast, the concentrations of glucose and fructose were much higher in the transgenic leaves than in the WT control owing to increased invertase activity (Figure 2F, G). As a result, the starch level significantly increased in the transgenic plants (Figure 2H). The flowering time of the transgenic plants was delayed compared with that of the WT by approximately 10.6 d under SD and 10.4 d under LD conditions (Figure 2I–K). The delayed flowering observed in OsVIN2 transgenic lines corresponded to a reduction in the expression of the florigen genes Hd3a and RFT1. In addition, the expression of Ehd1, the upstream promoter of these florigen genes, was also reduced (Figure S2). This observation indicated that the delayed flowering phenotype resulted from a reduced amount of sucrose during the daytime. As florigen genes and several regulatory genes that influence florigen expression are expressed during the daytime, sucrose may directly influence flowering time gene(s) or their products.

Mutations in Sucrose Transporter 2 delay flowering

To obtain further genetic evidence that sucrose functions as a positive regulator of flowering time, we examined a mutant defective in rice Sucrose Transporter 2 (OsSUT2) gene, which encodes a sucrose transporter that is located in the tonoplast membrane of leaf mesophyll cells and transfers sucrose from the vacuole to the cytosol (Figure 2A) (Eom et al., 2011). We previously reported that the mutant plants accumulated additional sucrose in the leaves, and the sucrose export ability from the leaves to sink tissues significantly decreased, suggesting that sucrose levels in the phloem are reduced in the mutants (Eom et al., 2011). Analysis of flowering time under SD conditions showed that the ossut2 mutant plants flowered approximately 12 d later than the WT controls (Figure 3A). Similarly, under LD conditions, the mutant plants flowered 11 d later than the WT plants (Figure 3B, C).

Expression analysis of the florigen genes showed that the transcript levels of Hd3a and RFT1 were significantly lower in the ossut2 mutant than in the WT (Figure 3E, F). Similarly, Ehd1 transcript levels were much lower in the mutants (Figure 3G). However, the expression of the upstream CO‐like genes Hd1, OsCOL4, and Ghd7 did not change in the mutant (Figure 3H–J). Similarly, the miR172‐regulated genes SNB and IDS1, which inhibit florigen expression, were unaffected in the mutant (Figure 3K, L). The expression of OsID1, a strong positive regulator of florigen genes, remained unaltered (Figure 3M). In addition, the ossut2 mutation did not influence the expression of OsGI and OsPhyB, which function upstream of CO‐like genes (Figure 3N, O).

In Arabidopsis, miR156 controls the flowering time by regulating sugar signaling. Sucrose reduces the abundance of endogenous pri‐miR156A and pri‐miR156C to increase their targets, that is, the SQUAMOSA PROMOTER BINDING PROTEIN‐LIKE (SBP/SPL) transcription factors (Wu et al., 2009; Yang et al., 2013). Analysis of rice miR156 and its downstream genes, OsSPL3, OsSPL12, OsSPL13, and OsSPL14 revealed that they were not affected by the mutation (Figure S3). These observations suggest that sucrose functions upstream of Ehd1, but that the tested regulator genes upstream of Ehd1 are not targets of sucrose signaling.

Sucrose promotes the degradation of Ghd7 protein

Because the expression levels of the regulatory genes that function upstream of Ehd1 were not affected in ossut2 mutants, we postulated that sucrose might function post‐transcriptionally as a floral regulator. To test whether sucrose influenced protein stability, we fused the coding regions of Hd1, Ghd7, SNB, and OsIDS1 to the hemagglutinin (HA) or Myc tag, and the fusion molecules were controlled by the maize ubiquitin 1 (ZmUbi1) promoter. These constructs were then transformed into protoplasts prepared from rice root‐derived callus suspension (Oc) cells to express the regulatory proteins. After a 12 h incubation to express the introduced genes, cycloheximide was added to stop further protein synthesis. Half of the protoplasts were incubated in 100 mmol/L sucrose solution for 4 h and the others were incubated in 100 mmol/L mannitol solution. Protein levels of the introduced genes were detected using anti‐HA or anti‐Myc antibodies. The experiment revealed that Ghd7 protein levels were much lower in protoplasts incubated in sucrose solution than in those incubated in mannitol solution (Figure S4A). In contrast, the SNB, OsIDS1, Hd1, and OsCOL4 protein levels were the same in both solutions (Figure S4B–E). These results suggested that sucrose‐dependent degradation was Ghd7‐specific. Sucrose sensitivity of the protein gradually decreased at lower concentrations and completely diminished at 1 mmol/L (Figure 4A).

To examine whether Ghd7 was specifically degraded by sucrose, we performed transient expression analysis in the presence of various soluble sugars. When the protoplasts were incubated in a 100 mmol/L glucose or fructose solution, the Ghd7 amount remained the same as that in the mannitol control. This assay suggests that the Ghd7 protein is degraded specifically when sucrose is present. Addition of the proteasome inhibitor MG132 to the sucrose solution blocked degradation, suggesting that the Ghd7 protein is degraded via the ubiquitination pathway in the presence of sucrose (Figure 4B). We studied whether Ghd7 stability was affected by okadaic acid, a serine threonine phosphatase inhibitor, and staurosporine, a broad‐specific kinase inhibitor (Figure 4C). The addition of these inhibitors did not block sucrose‐mediated Ghd7 degradation, thus suggesting that protein phosphorylation did not influence sucrose‐mediated Ghd7 degradation.

To investigate Ghd7 protein stability in planta, we generated transgenic rice plants expressing HA‐tagged Ghd7 controlled by the ZmUbi1 promoter. As observed in the transient expression assay, Ghd7‐HA was degraded by sucrose but not by glucose (Figure 4D). In addition, protein degradation by sucrose was inhibited by MG132 in the transgenic plants (Figure 4E). Furthermore, we used an anti‐Ghd7 antibody to examine Ghd7 protein levels at various developmental stages in WT plants, sampling every 2 weeks. As a negative control, we generated Ghd7 null transgenic plants using the clustered regularly interspaced palindromic repeats (CRISPR)/CRISPR‐associated protein 9 (Cas9) method (Figure S5). As expected, Ghd7 protein levels were high during the early vegetative stage but decreased dramatically starting at the floral transition stage. The protein was barely detectable after the transition when sucrose content is high in the leaves (Figure 4F).

The C‐terminal region of Ghd7 containing lysine residue of the 231st amino acid is sensitive to sucrose

Ghd7 is a transcription factor containing the CONSTANS, CO‐like, and TOC1 (CCT) domain at the C‐terminal region (Figure 5A). There were two potential protein ubiquitination sites, that is, lysine residues at the 165th and 231st codons (Figure 5A). To investigate the lysine that was associated with Ghd7 degradation, we designed truncated forms of Ghd71–230 lacking the second ubiquitination site, and Ghd71–163 lacking both sites (Figure 5A). These molecules were expressed in protoplasts with or without sucrose. Western blotting experiments revealed that neither molecule was degraded by sucrose (Figure 5B), suggesting that the N‐terminal region containing the first ubiquitination site was not required for sucrose‐dependent degradation. We designed two additional molecules, Ghd7114–257 and Ghd7163–257, that lacked the N‐terminal end of the Ghd7 protein but retained both lysine residues (Figure 5A). As expected, sucrose degraded these molecules (Figure 5C). Another N‐terminal deletion mutant, Ghd7181–257, lacking the first ubiquitination site, was degraded by sucrose (Figure 5A, C). To investigate whether the second ubiquitination site, the 231st lysine residue, plays a role in sucrose‐dependent protein degradation, we substituted this residue with arginine and subjected the protein to sucrose treatment. As expected, the Ghd7K231R variant did not undergo degradation in response to sucrose (Figure 5D).

These results indicate that the C‐terminal region carrying the second ubiquitination site, the 231st lysine residue within the CCT domain, is responsible for sucrose‐dependent degradation of Ghd7.

E3 ligase IPI1 binds to the C‐terminal region of Ghd7

To identify the protein that destabilizes Ghd7 proteins, we performed yeast two‐hybrid (Y2H) screening using a rice complementary DNA (cDNA) library. As bait, we used the C‐terminal molecule Ghd7181–257 which is sensitive to sucrose but has no transcriptional activity. Among the interacting partners, the E3 ligase IPI1 was most frequently found (Table S1). It has been reported that the protein modulates IDEAL PLANT ARCHITECTURE1 (IPA1) levels via E3 ligase activity (Wang et al., 2017a). A one‐to‐one binding assay between Ghd7181–257 and IPI1 confirmed they were bound to each other (Figure 6A). To confirm this interaction, we performed a bimolecular fluorescence complementation assay. Ghd7 fused to the N‐terminus of yellow fluorescent protein (YFP) (Ghd7‐nYFP) and IPI1 linked to the C‐terminus of YFP (IPI1‐cYFP) were co‐transferred to protoplasts, and their interactions were observed under a fluorescence microscope. The assay showed that the yellow fluorescence signal was mainly detected in the nucleus, indicating that Ghd7 interacted with IPI1 and that the protein complex was located in the nucleus (Figure 6B). Co‐immunoprecipitation (Co‐IP) assays with Ghd7 tagged with HA (Ghd7‐HA) and IPI1 tagged with Myc (IPI1‐Myc) further confirmed they were bound to each other (Figure 6C). However, the substitution mutant protein Ghd7K231R did not interact with IPI1 (Figure 6D). These results suggest that the 231st lysine residue of Ghd7 is crucial for its ubiquitination, and substitution at this site prevents both protein degradation and interaction with IPI1. Next, to identify the IPI1 region that binds Ghd7, we divided IPI1 into two fragments. The first fragment IPI11–96 that contained the N‐terminal and RING regions did not interact with Ghd7. However, the second fragment IPI197–455 contained a C‐terminal region that was bound to Ghd7 (Figure 6E, F).

Sucrose‐dependent ubiquitination by IPI1 modulates Ghd7 protein stability

It has been reported that IPI1 promotes the degradation of IPA1 by adding K48‐linked polyubiquitin chains in panicles, whereas the E3 ligase stabilizes IPA1 by adding K63‐linked polyubiquitin chains in the shoot apex (Wang et al., 2017a). To study whether IPI1 also modulates the stability of Ghd7, both proteins were co‐expressed in Oc cell protoplasts and incubated in either mannitol or sucrose solutions. In the mannitol solution, the Ghd7 protein level increased proportionally with the IPI1 concentration, suggesting that IPI1 stabilized Ghd7 (Figure 7A). However, in the sucrose solution, the Ghd7 protein was degraded regardless of the IPI1 concentration (Figure 7A). To study how IPI1 stabilized Ghd7 in mannitol solution, we investigated the polyubiquitination patterns of Ghd7 using polyubiquitination antibodies against the K48 or K63 ubiquitin chains. The experiment showed that Ghd7 did not interact with the K48 ubiquitin chain antibodies, indicating that the protein was not modified by K48 ubiquitination regardless of IPI1 (Figure 7B). However, Ghd7 reacted with the K63 ubiquitin chain antibodies, suggesting that the protein was modified by K63 ubiquitination (Figure 7B). In sucrose solution, Ghd7 was modified by K48‐linked polyubiquitination, and the amount of modified protein increased with IPI1 (Figure 7B). In contrast, Ghd7 was not modified by the K‐63 ubiquitin chain (Figure 7B). These results suggest that Ghd7 is stable in mannitol solution owing to K63 ubiquitination, but becomes unstable in sucrose solution via K48 ubiquitination. The experiment also indicated that the Ghd7 polyubiquitination enzyme was present in the protoplasts where the assay was conducted, and that IPI1 enhanced the ubiquitination reaction.

Investigation of flowering time in ipi1 null mutants

As IPI1 modulates Ghd7 stability, which is a major repressor of flowering, we investigated whether IPI1 controls flowering time. To obtain genetic data, we generated ipi1 null mutants using CRISPR/Cas9. Among several transgenic rice plants, we selected two independent homozygous lines, that is, ipi cr ‐1 carrying a 1‐bp deletion and ipi cr ‐2 carrying a 10‐bp deletion, which caused early termination of protein translation (Figure 8A). Both mutants grew normally, but the plant height was slightly reduced (Figure 8B). In addition, they flowered approximately 12 d later than the WT under LD conditions (Figure 8B, C). This suggests that IPI1 functions as a positive regulator of flowering time by destabilizing the negative regulator Ghd7 under LD conditions. On the other hand, the mutant plants flowered 2–3 d earlier than WT under SD conditions (Figure 8D). To analyze the genetic relationship between Ghd7 and IPI1, we examined the transcription levels of the genes in ipi1 or ghd7 mutants. Our results showed that mutations in either gene did not significantly affect the transcription levels of the other gene (Figure S6).

To explore the role of IPI1 in modulating Ghd7 protein stability during floral transition in response to varying sucrose concentrations, we conducted experiments using WT plants grown under LD conditions, which were subsequently transferred to Yoshida solution at 35, 49, 63, and 77 DAG (Figure S1). Concurrently, ipi1 null mutants grown under LD conditions were treated with sucrose at 91 and 98 DAG at the respective floral transition stages. The application of sucrose significantly induced early flowering, notably at the 77 DAG floral transition stage in WT plants. In contrast, ipi1 null mutants did not exhibit any flowering differences upon sucrose application during the floral transition (Figure 8E, F). These findings suggest that IPI1 plays a crucial role in promoting flowering by modulating Ghd7 protein stability in response to sucrose during the floral transition stages.

IPI1‐dependent modulation of Ghd7 protein stability

To investigate the regulation of Ghd7 protein stability by IPI1 in response to sucrose treatment, we isolated mesophyll protoplasts from both WT and ipi1 null mutant backgrounds. The protoplasts expressing Ghd7‐Myc were subsequently treated with 150 mmol/L mannitol or sucrose for 3 h. Our results revealed that Ghd7 protein levels decreased significantly upon sucrose application in WT protoplasts, whereas no notable changes were observed in the ipi1 mutants (Figure 9A). These findings indicate that the destabilization of Ghd7 protein in response to sucrose application is mediated by the E3 ligase IPI1.

To maintain substrate protein stability, IPI1 mediates polyubiquitination through K63 or K48 linkages. K63‐linked polyubiquitination typically leads to stable protein accumulation, whereas K48‐linked polyubiquitination targets proteins for degradation (Wang et al., 2017a). We demonstrated that IPI1 also regulates Ghd7 protein stability in response to sucrose treatment. To verify the impact of IPI1 on Ghd7 polyubiquitination in the presence and absence of sucrose, we examined the patterns of K63‐ and K48‐linked polyubiquitination following sucrose treatment in WT and ipi1 null mutants. Although protein expression is generally less stable in mesophyll protoplasts compared to Oc cell protoplasts, we observed increase in K48‐linked ubiquitination upon sucrose treatment in WT plants, but no such increase in ipi1 null mutants treated with sucrose. Additionally, K63‐linked polyubiquitination was most abundantly detected in WT samples treated with mannitol (Figure 9B). These results suggest that IPI1 mediates sucrose‐dependent K48‐linked polyubiquitination, leading to the degradation of Ghd7 proteins.

DISCUSSION

Although sucrose controls various phenomena in plants, the molecular mechanisms by which sucrose functions as a signaling molecule have not been thoroughly investigated. Some sucrose transporters, such as AtSUC3 in Arabidopsis and LeSUT2 located in the plasma membrane of tomato, have been proposed as sucrose sensors because they are structurally similar to the yeast glucose sensors SNF3 and RGT2 (Barker et al., 2000). However, direct evidence supporting this hypothesis is limited. The vacuolar sucrose transporter SUT4 interacts with cytochrome b5 to sense sucrose, or acts as a downstream component of sucrose signaling to inhibit seed germination in Arabidopsis (Li et al., 2012).

In the present study, we report that flowering is promoted through adding sucrose to rice plants. Our observations are consistent with previous reports that the exogenous application of sucrose induces flowering in B. campestris (Friend et al., 1984), S. alba (Deltour, 1967), and chrysanthemum (Sun et al., 2017). In addition, our findings indicate that the timing of sucrose application is the critical factor in promoting flowering in rice, rather than the concentration or duration of the treatment. While earlier flowering was observed with both 12.5 mmol/L and 25 mmol/L sucrose, the key determinant of this effect was the developmental stage at which sucrose was applied, specifically during the floral transition stage (Figure S1). This suggests that the floral transition stage is highly sensitive to sucrose, likely due to the combined effects of IPI1 and Ghd7 levels, as well as sucrose concentrations in the phloem, which may all interact in a developmentally regulated manner. Next, we genetically confirmed the role of sucrose in controlling flowering time. The first evidence was obtained from the analysis of transgenic plants overexpressing OsVIN2. Because the vacuolar OsVIN2 protein metabolizes sucrose to glucose and fructose, sucrose levels in the mesophyll cells were reduced during the daytime when the gene was expressed by the ZmRbcS promoter. Transgenic plants flowered late, indicating that flowering was delayed when endogenous sucrose levels in the leaves were reduced. Second, genetic evidence was obtained through studying OsSUT2, which encodes a sucrose transporter located in the tonoplast membrane of leaf mesophyll cells. Mutation of this gene reduces sucrose transport to the cytosol and leads to sucrose accumulation in the vacuoles of the leaf mesophyll cells, which causes delayed flowering owing to the suppression of the florigen gene expression.

Sucrose promoted flowering time by mediating the degradation of the flowering repressor Ghd7. Other sugars such as glucose, fructose, and mannitol did not mimic the effect of sucrose, indicating that the response was sucrose‐specific. Okadaic acid and staurosporine did not affect Ghd7 degradation, indicating that protein phosphorylation/dephosphorylation did not influence sucrose‐mediated Ghd7 degradation. This contrasts with the expression of several sucrose‐inducible genes. For example, the expression of the GBSSI gene in sweet potato leaves is stimulated by sucrose, and protein phosphorylation and dephosphorylation influence this induction (Wang et al., 2001). Sucrose also induces sporamin and β‐amylase genes in sweet potatoes. The sucrose induction requires Ca2+ signaling and is suppressed by inhibitors of Ser/Thr protein kinases (Ohto and Nakamura, 1995) and protein phosphatases (Takeda et al., 1994). However, gene regulation by the inhibitors also occurred with glucose and fructose, indicating that the effects were not specific to sucrose. Fructan synthesis is strongly and rapidly induced by sucrose in detached wheat leaves (Martinez‐Noël et al., 2001), and calcium, Ca2+ dependent protein kinases (CDPKs), and protein phosphatase 2 A (PP2A) influence sucrose signaling.

The Ghd7 degradation was inhibited by the protease inhibitor MG132. Therefore, we searched for an E3‐ligase that interacted with Ghd7. We observed that IPI1 bound to the C‐terminus region of Ghd7. IPI1 is a RING‐finger E3 ligase that binds to IPA1, which is the SQUAMOSA PROMOTER BINDING PROTINE‐LIKE 14 (SPL14) and regulates plant architecture (Wang et al., 2017a). IPI1 mediates tissue‐specific IPA1 stability in different polyubiquitin chains. In the panicles, IPI1 adds K48‐linked polyubiquitin chains to IPA1, thereby degrading the target protein. On the other hand, the E3‐ligase adds K63‐linked polyubiquitin chains to the target protein in the shoot apex, resulting in the stabilization of IPA1 (Wang et al., 2017a). We observed that Ghd7 protein levels increased in the presence of IPI1, suggesting that IPI1 stabilized Ghd7 via K48‐linked polyubiquitination. Analysis with polyubiquitination antibodies revealed that the K48‐linked polyubiquitin chain was added to the Ghd7 protein in the mannitol solution, whereas the K63‐linked polyubiquitin chain was added to the protein in the sucrose solution. Because K63‐linked polyubiquitination mediated the degradation of the target protein, Ghd7 was unstable in the presence of sucrose.

Our results show that high sucrose levels affect the IPI1 protein, but not as strongly as they affect the Ghd7 protein. This difference suggests that while IPI1 is influenced by sucrose, it is not clear if it undergoes sucrose‐mediated degradation. The complexity of these interactions requires more study to understand the mechanisms involved. We propose that the role of IPI1 in Ghd7 polyubiquitination might be indirectly controlled by other proteins or factors.

Recent studies have shown that E3 ubiquitin ligases IPI1 and AvrPiz‐t‐interaction protein 6 (APIP6) work together to regulate the degradation of early flowering3 (ELF3) paralogous proteins, thereby enhancing blast disease resistance by targeting OsELF3‐2. Additionally, IPI1 independently manages both disease resistance and flowering by targeting OsELF3‐1 (Yi et al., 2024; Xu et al., 2024). This supports the hypothesis that IPI1 not only plays a vital role in disease resistance and flowering by targeting different OsELF3 proteins but also regulates flowering by modulating Ghd7 protein stability in response to sucrose. However, the regulation of flowering time by IPI1 appears to vary depending on experimental conditions or cultivation environments. These findings highlight the extensive role of IPI1 in rice and suggest new opportunities for crop improvement by manipulating ubiquitination pathways to enhance both disease resistance and regulation of flowering.

Our results suggest that sucrose acts as a signaling molecule to mediate differential polyubiquitination and degradation of Ghd7, depending on the sucrose concentration in the cells (Figure 10). We observed that the sucrose concentration in the leaves increased during the floral transition. Because we measured the total amount of sucrose in the leaves, this increase could be higher in phloem cells expressing Ghd7. During the vegetative growth phase, the sucrose level in the phloem is low, stabilizing Ghd7 by K63‐linked polyubiquitination. At the floral transition time, an increased amount of sucrose in the cells promoted K48‐linked polyubiquitination of Ghd7, which caused protein degradation that stimulated florigen production.

Proposed model for regulating Grain number, plant height, and heading date 7 (Ghd7) protein stability by IPA INTERACTING PROTEIN1 (IPI1) under low or high sucrose concentrations

During the vegetative growth phase, the low concentration of sucrose in the leaves leads to the stabilization of Ghd7, a strong repressor of flowering in rice, through K63‐linked polyubiquitination by IPI1. However, when the floral transition occurs, the sucrose levels in the cells increase, leading to the K48‐linked polyubiquitination of Ghd7. This causes the protein to undergo degradation, which stimulates production of florigen.

However, it is unclear how sucrose mediates differential polyubiquitination by IPI1. EIN3 degradation by the proteasome depends on the phosphorylation status of the protein (Yoo et al., 2008). However, we observed that protein phosphorylation did not influence sucrose‐mediated Ghd7 degradation. The new plant type (NPT) architecture is governed by the function of IPA1 and controlled by OsOTUB1 (Wang et al., 2017b). OsOTUB1 leads to the deubiquitination of the K63‐linked Ub chain, which in turn promotes K48‐linked Ub‐dependent proteasomal degradation of IPA1. The physical interactions between Ghd7 and the OsOTUB1‐like deubiquitinase may therefore be induced by sucrose. Alternatively, sucrose can bind to IPI1 and alter its protein properties.

MATERIALS AND METHODS

Plant materials and growth conditions

The ossut2 mutant and OsVIN2 overexpression lines were generated using O. sativa subsp. japonica var. Dongjin. Transgenic plants overexpressing Ghd7‐HA were generated from O. sativa subsp. japonica var. Nipponbare using the ZmUbi1 promoter. IPI1 or Ghd7 knockout mutants were generated using CRISPR/Cas9 in Nipponbare. Transgenic plants were genotyped by sequencing the polymerase chain reaction (PCR) products of the target region using genomic DNA extracted from leaf blades. Seeds were germinated on 1/2 Murashige and Skoog medium, or in soil as previously reported (Cho et al., 2016). The pEhd1::GUS transgenic plants were generated as previously reported (Cho et al., 2022). Plants were grown either in an artificially controlled environment room under SD conditions (12 h light at 28°C/12 h dark at 22°C, approximately 60% humidity) and LD conditions (14 h light at 28°C/10 h dark at 22°C, approximately 60% humidity), or in an experimental paddy field in Kyung Hee University, Republic of Korea (Cho et al., 2022). For the sugar treatment, WT plants grown under LD conditions at 50 DAG were transferred to hydroponic culture solutions containing 100 mmol/L mannitol or sucrose under SD conditions.

RNA isolation and quantitative real‐time PCR analyses

Total RNA was extracted from the leaf blades using RNAiso Plus (Takara, Shiga, Japan), as previously reported (Yoon et al., 2020). The first cDNA was synthesized using approximately 2 µg of total RNA with Moloney murine leukemia virus reverse transcriptase (Promega, Madison, WI, USA; http://www.promega.com), RNasin® ribonuclease inhibitor (Promega), 10 ng of the oligo (dT)18 primer, and 2.5 mmol/L deoxyribonucleotide triphosphates. The synthesized cDNAs were then used as templates for quantitative real‐time reverse transcription ‐ PCR with SYBR Green II Prime Q‐Master mix (GeNetBio, Daejeon, Republic of Korea) and a Rotor‐Gene 6000 instrument system (Corbett Research, Sydney, Australia). Rice Ubi was used as an internal control, and the 2−∆∆Ct method was applied to calculate relative expression levels. All experiments were conducted at least thrice, and for each experiment, three or more independent samples were used at each point. All primers used to quantify gene expression for real‐time PCR are listed in Table S2.

Vector construction and rice transformation

For transient expression assays, the full‐length or truncated form of the cDNA of Ghd7 was amplified without a stop codon by PCR using the primer sets listed in Table S2. The PCR products were cloned into the Myc‐tag vector pGA3697 or HA‐tag vector pGA3698, carrying the coding region of 6x Myc or 3x HA, respectively (Kim et al., 2009). To generate null mutants using the CRISPR/Cas9 method, we screened potential target sequences using the CRISPRdirect web tool (http://crispr.dbcls.jp; Naito et al., 2015) to obtain an effective protospacer adjacent to the motif and avoid off‐target sites. The two independent target sequences were selected and cloned into the CRISPR/Cas9 binary vector pRGEB32 each to express the guide RNA. To generate overexpression lines, full‐length cDNA of OsVIN2, Ghd7 and IPI1 were amplified by PCR using the primer sets listed in Table S2. The PCR products were cloned into the binary vectors, a modified pC1300intC, which contained the ZmRbcS promoter and nopaline synthase (nos) terminator, or pGA3428 or pGA3438, which contained the ZmUbi1 promoter and nos terminator (Matsuoka et al., 1994; Kim et al., 2009). The constructs were transformed into Agrobacterium tumefaciens LBA4404 (An et al., 1989) and transgenic rice plants were generated through stable transformation of the transfer DNA via Agrobacterium‐mediated co‐cultivation, as previously described (Lee et al., 1999).

Measurement of soluble sugar and starch contents

Soluble sugar concentrations were determined as previously described (Lee et al., 2019). Briefly, approximately 0.1 g of the whole leaves without leaf sheath from WT plants grown under SD conditions were collected at Zeitgeber time (ZT) 0 h. Glucose, fructose, sucrose, and starch were measured in soluble and insoluble fractions using enzymatic methods after ethanol‐water extraction. The measured contents were normalized to their fresh weight.

Measurement of vacuolar invertase activity

Vacuolar invertase activities were determined from 20 mg leaf extracts following a previously reported method (Lee et al., 2019). Glucose produced was measured as previously described enzymatic methods.

Prediction of putative ubiquitination sites in Ghd7 protein

Putative ubiquitination sites were predicted by using the UbPred (http://www.ubpred.org/) program (Radivojac et al., 2010).

Protoplast transformation and protein expression assay

Protoplasts were isolated from Oc cells as previously described (Cho et al., 2022). Briefly, the Oc cells were incubated in an enzyme solution (1.5% cellulose RS, 1% macerozyme, 0.4 mol/L mannitol, 0.1% CaCl2, and 0.1% 2‐[N‐morpholino]ethanesulfonic acid (MES), pH 5.7) for 5 h at 28°C in the dark. After incubation, an equal volume of the KMC solution (117 mmol/L KCl, 82 mmol/L MgCl2, and 85 mmol/L CaCl2) was added to the enzyme solution. Then, the protoplasts were harvested and re‐suspended in the electroporation solution (0.4 mol/L mannitol, 70 mmol/L KCl, 5 mmol/L MgCl2, and 0.1% MES, pH 5.7) to achieve a density of 2–3 × 106 cells/mL, as quantified with a hemocytometer. Isolated protoplasts were electroporated with 20 µg of plasmid DNA using a Gene Pulser Xcell (Bio‐Rad, Hercules, CA, USA; www.bio-rad.com) at 300 V and 450 mF. Following electroporation, each preparation was thoroughly mixed to ensure uniformity. The sample was divided into several aliquots and incubated in a solution (0.4 mol/L mannitol, 20 mmol/L KCl, 0.1% MES, pH 5.7) overnight at 25°C in the dark to allow expression of the introduced gene.

After incubation for 12 h, sucrose was added at the appropriate concentration and 5 µg/mL cycloheximide (CHX) was added to stop protein synthesis. Expressed proteins were detected using monoclonal horseradish peroxidase‐conjugated anti‐Myc (#2040; Cell Signaling Technology, Beverly, MA, USA) or anti‐HA (#2999; Cell Signaling Technology). β‐tubulin was employed as an internal control to verify that equal amounts of cells were used in each treatment. To detect levels of Ghd7 protein in WT plants, an anti‐Ghd7 antibody (A20327; Abclonal Technology, Boston, MA, USA) was employed.

Co‐immunoprecipitation assays

To construct Myc or HA fusion proteins, amplified cDNAs without a stop codon were inserted into pGA3817 carrying a 6X Myc tag or into pGA3818 carrying a 3X HA tag. Co‐IP assays were performed according to previously reported methods (Cho et al., 2016; Yoon et al., 2017). These fusion molecules were co‐expressed in rice Oc cell protoplasts, then the proteins were extracted using an IP buffer (75 mmol/L NaCl, 50 mmol/L Tris‐HCl (pH 7.5), 5 mmol/L ethylenediaminetetraacetic acid (EDTA), 1% Triton X‐100, 1 mmol/L dithiothreitol, 1 mmol/L phenylmethanesulfonylfluoride, 2 mmol/L NaF, 20 µmol/L MG132, and an appropriate amount of protease inhibitor cocktail (Roche, Mannheim, Germany; http://www.roche.com)). Expressed proteins were immunoprecipitated using an anti‐HA mouse monoclonal antibody (12CA5; Roche,) or anti‐Myc mouse monoclonal antibody (#MA1‐21316; Invitrogen, USA) conjugated with A and G agarose beads (Millipore, Billerica, MA, USA; http://www.emdmillipore.com). The eluted proteins were resolved using 10% or 12% sodium dodecyl sulfate – polyacrylamide gel electrophoresis (SDS‐PAGE) and transferred to a polyvinylidene difluoride membrane (IPVH00010; Millipore, USA). Horseradish peroxidase (HRP)‐conjugated anti‐Myc monoclonal antibody (#2040; Cell Signaling Technology) or anti‐HA monoclonal antibody (#2999; Cell Signaling Technology) were used to detect proteins.

Detection of Ghd7 ubiquitination

In vivo ubiquitination of Ghd7 was detected as previously reported with some modifications (Wang et al., 2017a). Isolated Oc cell protoplasts were transfected with a construct carrying Ghd7‐HA controlled by the ZmUbi1 promoter. After incubation for 12 h, Ghd7‐HA proteins were isolated from the protoplasts and incubated in IP buffer (75 mmol/L NaCl, 50 mmol/L Tris‐HCl (pH 7.5), 5 mmol/L EDTA, 1% Triton X‐100, 1 mmol/L dithiothreitol, 1 mmol/L phenylmethanesulfonylfluoride, 2 mmol/L NaF, 20 µmol/L MG132, 10 µmol/L iodoacetamide (IAA) and an appropriate amount of protease inhibitor cocktail (Roche)). The extracted Ghd7 proteins were then incubated with an anti‐HA mouse monoclonal antibody (12CA5; Roche,) conjugated with A and G agarose beads (Millipore). After 2 h of reaction, the samples were washed, eluted with 2× SDS buffer, and boiled at 100°C for 3 min. To detect proteins, anti‐ubiquitin (AB1690; Sigma‐Aldrich), the anti‐K48 ubiquitin chain, or anti‐K63 ubiquitin chain polyclonal antibodies (D7A11 and D9D5; Cell Signaling) were used as the first antibody, and the rabbit immunoglobulin G‐HRP antibody was used as the second antibody.

Bimolecular fluorescence complementation assays

Full‐length cDNAs of Ghd7 and IPI1 were inserted into pGA3815 or pGA3816 vectors containing the ZmUbi1 promoter, nos terminator, and either the fragmented C‐terminus of Venus (aa 155–238) or the N‐terminus of Venus (aa 1–172), respectively. The plasmids were transformed into protoplasts isolated from Oc cells. The transfected cells were incubated overnight at 28°C in the dark. Fluorescence was observed using a filter‐equipped microscope (BX61; Olympus) under a bright field or YFP channel, as previously described (Cho et al., 2016).

Yeast two‐hybrid screening

The Ghd7181–257 fragments were inserted into the pGBKT7 vector for the GAL4 DNA‐binding domain as bait. The Ghd7181–257‐pGBKT7 plasmid was transformed into the yeast strain AH109, and no transcriptional activity was observed. Bait was used for Y2H screening using a rice vegetative leaf cDNA library (Panbionet Corp., Pohang, Republic of Korea; www.panbionet.com). The potential interacting partner, E3 ligase IPI1, was confirmed using a one‐to‐one Y2H assay with Ghd7181–257 baits in a synthetic defined medium lacking Leu, Trp, and His with 5 mM 3‐amino‐1,2,4‐triazole (3‐AT).

Statistical analyses

The differences among the test groups were evaluated using one‐way analysis of variance (ANOVA) Turkey Honestly Significant Difference multiple comparison test (“TurkeyHDS” function; both functions from the “Multicomp” package) in the R program (Cohen and Cohen, 2008; R Core Team, 2017).
