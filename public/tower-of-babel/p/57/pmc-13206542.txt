Synthesis of 1‐Alkynylboronates, 1,1‐Diborylalkenes, and 1,1,1‐Triborylalkanes: Challenges and Opportunities
Authors: Son Hoai Doan, Vu Nguyen Tran, Thanh Vinh Nguyen
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTIn the field of organoboron chemistry, multi(boronate) esters have emerged as crucial intermediates, with various applications in synthetic chemistry, material technology, and life sciences. This mini‐review discusses recent advancement in the synthesis of these versatile compounds, particularly alkynylboronates, 1,1‐diborylalkenes, and 1,1,1‐triborylalkanes, all of which are derived from easily accessible alkenes and alkynes. The article provides an in‐depth analysis of these methodologies, delves into their mechanistic underpinnings, and emphasises their synthetic utility. Future research avenues and unresolved challenges in organoboron chemistry are also identified in this report, aiming to inspire further development of synthetic methods used in this area.

Introduction

Organoboronates are highly valuable synthetic precursors with a wide array of applications ranging from material to life sciences due to their versatile reactivity, reasonable stability, ease of handling, and broad functional group tolerance [1]. The versatility of organoboronates has been proven as they can be used in numerous C─C and C─heteroatom bond‐forming reactions, providing direct access to a variety of organic molecules [2, 3]. The reactivities of multi(boronate) esters are strongly dependent on the positional relationship of adjacent boron moieties [1], therefore can be tuned with structural modification, making them attractive reagents in organic chemistry. A typical example is gem‐bis(boronate) alkanes, which can be activated via two complementary strategies. When an alkoxide or hydroxide base is used to deprotonate the boron‐bearing carbon center, the stabilizing effect of the boron moiety on the neighboring carbanion can facilitate base‐induced deboration, generating a highly reactive α‐boryl carbanion [4, 5, 6]. On the other hand, the use of a bulkier (hence nonnucleophilic) and stronger base, such as lithium 2,2,6,6‐tetramethylpiperidine, only leads to deprotonation to produce gem‐bis(boryl) carbanion without deboration process [7, 8]. These two types of carbanions have different downstream synthetic applications [4, 5, 6, 7, 8]. Another example is gem‐diborylalkenes, in which two geminal boron moieties exhibit distinctive reactivities due to the difference in their steric environments [9, 10, 11, 12]. In Pd‐catalyzed cross‐coupling reactions, the site/regio‐ and stereoselective transformation of these boryl groups often leads to stereocontrolled synthesis of multisubstituted olefins, which are prevalent motifs in bioactive compounds and π‐conjugated materials [12, 13, 14].

Despite their important synthetic utility, the lack of efficient and atom‐economical preparation is still a major obstacle in the application of multi(boronate) esters in organic chemistry [1]. Traditional approaches which use organic halide precursors suffer from harsh reaction conditions (high temperature, employment of large quantity of toxic reagents, or strong bases) and significant waste production. These methods also require extra preparatory steps for the synthesis of reactants [13, 15, 16]. Considering these limitations, there is a significant demand for the development of new straightforward and streamlined strategies to access multi(boronate) esters and to unlock their full synthetic potential. The abundant availability of alkenes and alkynes, derived from either renewable feedstocks or petroleum industry, makes them highly attractive precursors for such strategies [17, 18]. Thus, efficient and selective multiboration of alkenes and alkynes becomes convenient and atom‐economical approaches to synthesize valuable organoboronates. This mini‐review discusses notable advancement in the synthesis of alkynylboronates, 1,1‐diborylalkenes, 1,1,1‐tris(boronate) esters from alkynes and alkenes (Figure 1), with the focus on mechanistic insights, reaction scope, and limitations.

Synthesis of Alkynylboronic Esters (alkynylboronates)

Alkynylboronates have attracted much attention as useful building blocks in organic synthesis [19]. The coexistence of the alkynyl and boron functional groups in these compounds offers exceptional and distinctive reactivities in organic transformations that are not typically observed in either typical alkynes or other organoboron compounds [19]. Alkynylboronates serve as convenient coupling partners in the alkynyl form for Suzuki‐type coupling reactions [20, 21]. The utilization of the boryl‐substituted C≡C functionality is also attractive in other organic transformations, such as cyclotrimerization [22], [3+2] cycloaddition [23], cyclopentenone synthesis [24], hydrozirconation [25], enyne metathesis [26], and other reactions [27, 28, 29, 30], all of which aim to generate organic structures containing C─B bonds that would be challenging or impossible to introduce through late‐stage C─H or C─X borylation. Despite being of great synthetic importance and having a diverse array of applications, the production of alkynylboronates is currently hindered by the lack of efficient and atom‐economical synthetic procedures.

As an example, in 1988, Brown and his coworkers achieved a significant milestone in the synthesis of alkynylboronates B, which was prepared by deprotonation of corresponding alkynes A with n BuLi, followed by a reaction with a boronic ester and carefully controlled protonation with an anhydrous Brønsted acid (Scheme 1). This protocol allows high‐yielding synthesis of alkynylboronates B featuring various substituents in the acetylenic moiety [31]. In spite of its reliability, this three‐step approach is inefficient in terms of atom economy due to large quantity of waste and the need of organolithium precursors. In addition, due to the use of stoichiometric amounts of organometallic reagents, many functional groups are not tolerated, thus limiting the reaction scope. Consequently, there is a demand for the development of straightforward and practical strategies that can address the limitations mentioned above to unlock the full synthetic potential of alkynylboronates.

A conventional three‐step synthesis of alkynylboronates [31].

The synthesis of alkynylboronates was significantly improved when Ozerov and coworkers discovered the dehydrogenative borylation of terminal alkynes catalyzed by iridium complexes featuring a new silyl−amido−quinoline tridentate SiNN pincer ligand 3 (Scheme 2) [32]. This dehydrogenative C─H borylation, which allows direct preparation of alkynylboronates from readily available terminal alkynes, suggests an interesting and atom‐economical pathway to unlock the complete synthetic potential of alkynylboronates. This method tolerates various functional groups, including aryl, alkyl, and silyl‐substituted terminal alkynes. The proposed mechanism reveals that the catalytic cycle starts with the formation of diboryl complex 4, which reacts with the terminal alkyne to form intermediate 5 and pinacolborane (HBpin). Intermediate 5 then undergoes C─B reductive elimination to produce the desired product C and borylhydride complex 6, which finally reacts with HBpin to release H2 and regenerate complex 4. It should be noted that the catalyst exhibits high activity toward alkynes, but not toward alkenes and arenes, which could be attributed to the significantly higher acidity of alkynyl C─H bonds and the tendency of terminal alkynes to act as compact ligands that coordinate more easily than alkenes and arenes.

The catalytic dehydrogenative borylation of terminal alkynes using 3 [32].

In 2017, a highly selective copper‐catalyzed dehydrogenative borylation of terminal alkynes with HBpin was developed by Bertrand and coworkers, in which the cyclic (alkyl)(amino)carbenes (CAACs) or N‐heterocyclic carbenes (NHCs) plays a critical role in enabling this C(sp)─B coupling (Scheme 3) [33]. Various functional groups, such as methoxy, nitrile, halogens, trimethylsilyl, and methyl ester, are well tolerated in this approach, which can generate alkynylboronates with high yields. Additionally, this method is also applicable to unactivated alkyl alkynes [33]. The procedure starts with the reaction between the terminal alkyne and LCuOTf [L = cyclic (alkyl)(amino)carbene] 7 to form catalytically active σ,π‐bis(copper) acetylide 8 and ammonium triflate, which is followed by σ‐bond metathesis with HBpin via 9 to produce the desired product C and copper hydride 10. Finally, 10 reacts with triethylammonium triflate to regenerate LCuOTf 7 and triethylamine with the elimination of H2. This catalytic system enables the selective dehydrogenative borylation of terminal alkynes, producing desired alkynylboronates without generating unwanted alkenyl boronic esters. This high selectivity might be attributed to the protection of the triple bond in dinuclear complex 8, which can hinder the addition of a B─H bond across the triple bond [33].

A (CAAC)CuOTf‐catalyzed dehydrogenative borylation of terminal alkynes [33].

In 2018, the Darcel group developed a catalytic system based on Fe(OTf)2 and DABCO to promote the dehydrogenative borylation of terminal alkyne A (Scheme 4) [34]. This method is suitable for both aromatic and aliphatic terminal alkynes to give the desired alkynylboronates C with high efficiency. Nonetheless, its application is limited by harsh reaction conditions, such as prolonged reaction time and high temperature. Furthermore, competing formation of alkenyl boronic esters results in low selectivity, which may complicate the purification process [34].

A Fe(OTf)2‐catalyzed dehydrogenative borylation of terminal alkynes [34].

In 2019, Ingleson and coworkers demonstrated the effectiveness of a low‐coordinate NHC−zinc hydride complex (NHC = N‐heterocyclic carbene) as a catalyst for the dehydrogenative borylation of terminal alkynes (Scheme 5) [35]. This strategy significantly expands the reaction scopes, as it is applicable to aryl, alkyl, and thienyl‐substituted terminal alkynes. Furthermore, it can include conjugated 1,3‐enynes, which exhibit high chemoselectivity without boration at the C═C double bond [35]. Mechanistic studies suggest a key Zn─C/H─B metathesis pathway, in which transmetalation occurs between (7‐Dipp)ZnPh(NTf2) 11 and HBPin to give a low‐coordinate [(7‐Dipp)ZnH]+ species 12. This species then reacts with the terminal alkyne to release H2 and form zinc acetylide 13, which finally undergoes Zn─C/H─B metathesis to produce the expected product C and regenerate [(7‐Dipp)ZnH]+ species 12. The bulky N‐heterocyclic carbenes (NHCs) play a critical role in catalysis as they stabilize the NHC─Zn species and enable the formation of low‐coordinate (NHC)Zn─H cations, which are electrophilic yet Brønsted basic [35].

Low‐coordinate NHC−zinc hydride complex catalyzes the dehydrogenative borylation of terminal alkynes [35].

Another zinc‐catalyzed synthesis of alkynylboronates was developed by the Ma group in 2021, in which 1 mol% of commercially available ZnBr2 14 is used to catalyze the dehydrogenative borylation of terminal alkynes with HBpin under solvent‐free conditions at room temperature (Scheme 6) [36]. This method is applicable to aryl, alkyl, and heteroaryl alkynes. The catalytic cycle is initiated by the reaction of ZnBr2 with HBpin to give active zinc hydride species HZnBr 16, which then reacts with alkyne A to generate zinc alkynyl intermediate 23 and release hydrogen gas. Finally, 23 reacts with in situ generated BrBpin 19, resulting in the formation of the desired product C and the regeneration of ZnBr2 14 [36]. Interestingly, DFT calculations reveal that the transition state energy of hydroboration intermediate zinc alkenyl complex 18 is lower than that of dehydroboration intermediate zinc alkynyl complex 23, suggesting the addition path holds thermodynamic advantages. Nevertheless, the reaction is entropy‐driven, and the generation of hydrogen gas throughout the process imparts dynamic advantages to the substitution path, favorably shifting the equilibrium toward the formation of C [36].

A ZnBr2‐catalyzed dehydrogenative borylation of terminal alkynes [36].

Also in 2021, the Thomas group reported a new procedure for the dehydrogenative borylation of terminal alkynes with aluminium hydride catalyst, in which the reactivity at the aluminum hydride center is controlled by a tethered intramolecular amine ligand, which prevents hydroalumination and instead promotes selective reactions at the alkyne C─H σ‐bond (Scheme 7) [37]. With the basic amine group at the ortho position, C─H alumination of A with the catalyst is thermodynamically favored over hydroalumination [37]. This protocol enables the synthesis of alkynylboronates with high yields, broad reactant scope, excellent chemoselectivity and good functional group compatibility. A wide range of alkyl and aryl alkynes can undergo this transformation, producing corresponding alkynylboronates [37]. The key step was found to be the σ‐bond metathesis between HBpin and alkynyl aluminium intermediates. The catalytic cycle starts with the activation of dimethylalane 25 as a precatalyst to generate dimeric catalyst 26, which then deprotonates the alkyne to form zwitterionic acetylide 27. Subsequent elimination of H2 takes place to give intermediate 28, followed by σ‐bond metathesis to produce the desired alkynylboronate C and regenerate bridged aluminium dihydride 26.

A dehydrogenative borylation of terminal alkynes using aluminium hydride catalyst [37].

In 2022, Dobrovetsky and coworkers reported the synthesis of an air‐stable dicationic Zn2+ complex as an effective catalyst for the preparation of alkynylboronates from terminal alkynes (Scheme 8) [38]. This process only requires mild conditions and it tolerates many functional groups, including alkyl and aryl terminal alkynes. The precatalyst 30 activates the H−Bpin bond in a frustrated Lewis pairs (FLPs)‐type manner between the Lewis acidic Zn‐center and the Lewis basic pyridine arm to generate the actual catalyst, 31, which then reacts with the terminal alkyne to release H2 and give zinc acetylide species, 32. The borylation of the zinc acetylide moiety subsequently occurs with the Bpin group attached to the N‐center in 32, creating complex 33. Finally, the replacement of the alkynylboronate in 33 with another HBpin molecule results in the formation of the desired product C and the regeneration of 31 (Scheme 8). Remarkably, this system can selectively catalyze either the hydroboration or the C─H dehydrogenative borylation of terminal alkynes, depending on the catalyst loading and reaction temperature [38].

A dehydrogenative borylation of terminal alkynes using Zn2+‐based catalyst [38].

Despite being the most straightforward, atom‐economical approaches to generate alkynylboronates, only a few protocols for the dehydrogenative C─H borylation of terminal alkynes have been studied in the last 30 years. After the pioneering work by Ozerov and his coworkers [32], there has been some advancement in the synthesis of alkynylboronates as discussed earlier in this section, but many challenges still remain. The use of metal‐based catalysts in all previously described strategies is undesirable due to the sensitivity to air and moisture, the requirement for special handling procedures, challenging purification process, and the environmental hazards from metals. Furthermore, these approaches require multiple steps to prepare the catalysts and the ligands [32, 33, 35, 37, 38], thus suffering from poor atom economy due to the significant amount of waste generated. Another major issue is the low selectivities in some of these methods due to the formation of alkenyl boronic esters through the competing hydroboration of the triple bond [19, 32, 33, 34, 35, 36, 37, 38]. These limitations emphasize the need for straightforward and metal‐free strategies that can minimize preparatory steps for the synthesis of the catalysts and the ligands, and avoid unwanted hydroboration reactions.

Recently, in an effort to address existing limitations, our group developed the first metal‐free dehydrogenative borylation of terminal alkynes using the commercially available P1− t Bu superbase 34 as an effective organocatalyst (Scheme 9) [39, 40]. Our catalytic system enables the synthesis of alkynylboronates from a range of alkynes featuring substituents of different electronic and steric properties, providing moderate to excellent yields and high chemoselectivity. Aryl alkynes containing electron‐withdrawing groups on the aromatic rings were found to result in higher product formation than those with electron‐donating groups and alkyl alkynes, which could be attributed to the difference in the acidity of alkynyl C─H bonds of these substrates [39]. The results from variable time normalization analysis experiments show that the reaction is of the first order in both A and HBpin as well as P1− t Bu catalyst, demonstrating the involvement of A, HBpin and P1− t Bu catalyst in the deprotonation of the alkyne C─H, which is the rate‐limiting step. This reaction involves a concerted process of deprotonation and nucleophilic addition through 35, in which the P1− t Bu superbase deprotonates A, followed by the nucleophilic addition to HBpin, resulting in the formation of contact ion pair 36. This process proceeds concertedly instead of stepwise, as a result of the weak acidity of A. The production of the corresponding alkynylboronate C from ion pair 36 occurs via 37, which involves the heterolytic recombination of Hδ+ and Hδ− containing species. This process regenerates the P1− t Bu superbase catalyst 34 and releases hydrogen gas to complete the catalytic cycle (Scheme 9) [40]. Noticeably, DFT calculations provide further support for two important observations from mechanistic studies: the involvement of alkyne, HBpin, and P1− t Bu catalyst; and the occurrence of asynchronous deprotonation in the rate‐determining step. High regio‐ and stereoselectivity are achieved due to the duality of P1− t Bu catalyst: it has the Brønsted basicity to break the acidic C(sp)−H bond and the σ‐Lewis basicity to activate the boron reagent [40]. Moreover, 1,1‐diboration and 1,1,1‐triboration products can also be achieved selectively by varying the amount of HBpin and the catalyst, as well as reaction time [39]. The synthesis of multi(boronate) esters using P1− t Bu catalyst will be discussed later in this review.

A P1− t Bu‐catalyzed dehydrogenative borylation of terminal alkynes [40]

Synthesis of 1,1‐diborylalkenes

1,1‐diborylalkenes are versatile building blocks and valuable intermediates in organic synthesis because they contain two geminal boronate moieties, which can enable multiple C─C and C─heteroatom bond‐forming reactions [12, 41]. Due to the difference in their steric environments, the geminal boronate moieties in 1,1‐diborylalkenes can be transformed in a selective and stepwise manner, leading to stereoselective synthesis of multisubstituted alkenes, a significant challenge for other synthetic approaches. As a result, they have been playing increasingly important roles in the construction of π‐conjugated molecules and biologically active compounds [9, 10, 11, 12]. For example, 1,1‐diborylalkenes were demonstrated to be essential synthetic intermediates in the synthesis of the anticancer agent tamoxifen [12] and tetraarylethenes with aggregation‐induced emission (AIE) properties [42]. Despite their synthetic value and potential for diverse applications, there are not many reliable synthetic procedures for the synthesis of 1,1‐diborylalkenes.

Traditional approaches which require stoichiometric amounts of lithium reagents have many drawbacks. In 1975, Matteson and his coworkers synthesized 1,1‐diborylalkenes by the condensation between triborylmethide anion and aldehyde/ketone. The addition of the triborylmethide anion to the carbon of the carbonyl group generates cyclic borate anion 39, which then undergoes elimination to produce the desired 1,1‐diborylalkene E (Scheme 10a) [43]. Twenty five years later, the synthesis of 1,1‐diborylalkenes via geminal difunctionalization of alkenylidene‐type carbenoids was reported by the Hiyama group [15, 44]. In this approach, 1‐halo‐1‐lithioalkene 40, an alkylidene‐type carbenoid formed from the reaction of a lithium reagent ( n BuLi or LiTMP) and 1,1‐dihaloalkene or 1‐haloalkene F, reacts with bis(pinacolato)diboron (B2pin2) to generate intermediate borate complex 41. Subsequently, stereospecific 1,2‐migration takes place to produce 1,1‐diborylalkene E with the inversion of configuration (Scheme 10b) [15, 44]. These methods, however, suffer from poor functional group tolerance and low atom economy due to harsh reaction conditions, the requirement for additional preparatory steps, and stoichiometric amounts of pyrophoric lithium reagents, which restrict their applicability in organic synthesis.

Traditional methods for the synthesis of 1,1‐diborylalkenes [15, 43, 44].

In 2011, Iwasawa and coworkers developed an efficient method to synthesize 1,1‐ and 1,2‐diborylalkenes using a palladium complex with a PSiP‐pincer type ligand, which catalyzes the double dehydrogenative borylation of alkenes with B2pin2 (Scheme 11) [45]. The regioselectivity of the reaction is determined by the nature of the substituents on the alkenes. Specifically, terminal alkenes with bulky, electronically activated substituents undergo 1,1‐diborylation to produce 1,1‐diborylalkenes, while nonactivated alkenes follow 1,2‐diborylation to generate 1,2‐diborylalkenes [45]. Terminal alkenes containing various functional groups, including silyl ether, imido, chloro, and acid anhydride groups, are tolerated and can give corresponding products with good to excellent yields [45]. The catalytic cycle starts with the generation of monohydridopalladium complex 43 via transmetalation of 42 with AlEt3 followed by β‐hydride elimination. Complex 43 then reacts with B2pin2 to give HBpin and monoborylpalladium complex 44, which subsequently undergo alkene insertion and β‐hydride elimination to produce borylated products and regenerate palladium hydride 43. However, this method only allows the synthesis of 1,1‐diborylalkenes from terminal alkenes bearing electronically activating groups such as aryl and alkenyl substituents [45].

A PSiP‐Pd‐catalyzed double dehydrogenative borylation of terminal alkenes with B2pin2 [45].

In 2017, the Chirik group presented a seminar contribution on the effective synthesis of 1,1‐diborylalkenes via 1,1‐diboration of terminal alkynes with B2pin2 catalyzed by the cyclohexyl substituted pyridine(diimine) cobalt methyl complex 47 (Scheme 12) [46]. This method can tolerate a wide range of functional groups, including tert‐butyldimethylsilyl ether, acetal, ester, phthalimide, nitrile, and secondary amide, increasing its applicability for various synthetic routes [46]. Interestingly, terminal alkynes experience chemoselective 1,1‐diboration of the alkyne functionality, while the C═C double bond remains intact without any isomerization [46]. The catalytic cycle is initiated by the generation of cobalt acetylide 48 via the reaction of cobalt complex 47 and the terminal alkyne. Compound 48 then undergo diboron oxidative addition and subsequent reductive elimination to give the alkynylboronate complex 50. Vinyl cobalt intermediate 51 is formed via regioselective syn‐hydrocobaltation and then reacts with another alkyne molecule to produce the desired 1,1‐diborylalkene J and regenerate cobalt acetylide 48. While the method is highly efficient for aliphatic alkynes, the reaction yields are lower for aryl‐ and heteroaryl acetylenes due to the competitive homodimerization of the alkynes to the corresponding enynes.

A cobalt‐catalyzed 1,1‐diboration of terminal alkynes with B2pin2 and BpinBdan [46].

Also in 2017, Huang and coworkers developed a catalytic system using a bipyridyl‐phosphine (PNN)−Co complex, along with NaBEt3H and CsF, to promote the selective dehydrogenative mono‐ or diborylation of terminal aryl alkenes (Scheme 13) [47]. This method enables the selective synthesis of 1,1‐diborylalkenes and trans‐monoborylalkenes from terminal aryl alkenes containing both electron‐donating and electron‐withdrawing groups with high yields and good functional group tolerance [47]. Co(I) hydride species 53, which is formed from the reaction of the precatalyst 52 with NaBEt3H, reacts with B2pin2 (first transmetalation) to generate Co(I) boryl complex 54. Then, the 2,1‐insertion of the terminal vinylarene into the Co─B bond in 54 occurs, producing a β‐boryl‐substituted Co(I) species 55, which then undergoes β‐hydride elimination, resulting in the formation of monoborylalkene L and Co(I) hydride species 53. Following the second transmetalation of 53 with B2pin2, the monoborylalkene L goes through the second dehydrogenative borylation to give 1,1‐diborylalkene and Co(I) hydride species 53. Complex 54 is regenerated by the third transmetalation of 53 and B2pin2. The selectivity of this method depends on the loading of CsF [47]. With 0.4 eq. of this additive, the reaction favors the formation of L. However, when the loading of CsF is increased, M becomes the major product. Unfortunately, the scope of this method is limited to the selective synthesis of 1,1‐diborylalkenes from terminal aryl alkenes, whereas the double dehydrogenative borylation of terminal alkyl alkenes leads to the formation of cis‐1,2‐diborylalkenes.

A cobalt‐catalyzed dehydrogenative mono‐ or diborylation of terminal aryl alkenes with B2pin2 [47]

In 2019, a highly selective method for the synthesis of trans‐monoborylalkenes and diborylalkenes via single and double dehydrogenative borylations of terminal alkenes was introduced by Yamaguchi and coworkers. Their work focuses on the heterogeneous catalysis of dehydrogenative borylation using copper hydroxide species on metal oxide supports (Cu(OH)x/support), which has demonstrated its effectiveness on a variety of terminal aryl alkenes possessing both electron‐donating and electron‐withdrawing groups (Scheme 14) [48]. Interestingly, the catalytic process is heterogeneous, and the catalysts can be reused multiple times. The reduction of the precatalyst 57 with B2pin2 results in the formation of Cu(I)−Bpin species 58, which undergoes alkene insertion and β‐hydride elimination, leading to the production of trans‐monoborylstyrene O and Cu(I)─H species 60. The use of a ketone here as a HBpin acceptor is necessary to regenerate 58 from 60. Trans‐monoborylstyrene O then goes through the second dehydrogenative borylation to give 1,1‐diborylstyrene P. The reaction only proceeds when Cu(OH)2 is dispersed on the metal oxide support [48]. The preference of this reaction for 1,1‐diborylation products increases in the presence of Al2O3 as the support and 2‐adamantanone as the HBpin acceptor [48]. However, this method is only applicable to terminal aryl alkenes, as only monoborylated products were obtained with terminal alkyl alkenes as starting materials [48].

A copper‐catalyzed selective single or double dehydrogenative borylations of terminal aryl alkenes with B2pin2 [33].

Also in 2019, another effective strategy for the synthesis of 1,1‐diborylalkenes was developed by the Ingleson group, in which a low‐coordinate NHC‐zinc hydride complex is used to facilitate the sequential dehydrogenative borylation and the hydroboration of terminal alkynes with HBpin [35]. This approach is applicable to a wide range of terminal aryl alkynes containing both electron‐donating and electron‐withdrawing groups, producing corresponding 1,1‐diborylalkenes with modest to high yields [35]. This strategy involves two consecutive transformations, including a dehydrogenative borylation followed by a hydroboration (Scheme 15) [35]. After the formation of zinc acetylide 13, Zn─C/H─B metathesis occurs to form alkynylboronate C, which goes through hydrozincation with [(7‐Dipp)ZnH]+ species 12 and the second Zn─C/H─B metathesis with HBpin to produce the desired 1,1‐diborylalkene Q and regenerate [(7‐Dipp)ZnH]+ species 12. However, this approach is also restricted to terminal aryl alkynes [35].

Low‐coordinate NHC−zinc hydride complex catalyzes sequential dehydrogenative borylation and hydroboration of terminal alkynes with HBpin [35].

Also in 2019, the Hosoya group reported the synthesis of (2,2‐diborylvinyl)arenes via desulfanylative gem‐diborylation of 2‐arylvinyl sulfides using a catalytic system consisting of [Rh(OH)(cod)]2, P( n Bu)3, and K2CO3 (Scheme 16) [49]. This method can tolerate a wide range of functional groups, including halogens, tosyloxy, methyl sulfide, hydroxy, amide, and ester groups [49]. Mechanistic studies suggest a stepwise process involving the borylative cleavage of the C─S bond of 2‐arylvinyl sulfide 66 followed by dehydrogenative borylation of trans‐monoborylalkene 67, thus results in the production of 1,1‐diborylalkene product 68. As suggested by the authors, in situ generated HBpin during the reaction may boost the rate of this transformation [49]. Unfortunately, 2‐alkylvinyl sulfides cannot participate in this catalytic system. Despite its simplicity, the 2‐arylvinyl sulfides used in this approach are not readily available and, therefore preparatory work is required.

A rhodium‐catalyzed desulfanylative gem‐diborylation of 2‐arylvinyl sulfides with B2pin2 [34].

Recently, Zhang and coworkers proposed a method for the Zn(OTf)2‐catalysed 1,1‐diboration of terminal alkyne A with HBpin to synthesize 1,1‐diborylalkenes Q (Scheme 17). This system is applicable to various aryl acetylenes substituted with electron‐withdrawing or electron‐donating groups, as well as substituents at the ortho, meta, and para positions [50]. While this approach is straightforward, its use is limited due to prolonged reaction time and high reaction temperature. In spite of its effectiveness for terminal aryl alkynes, the production of 1,1‐diborylalkenes from terminal alkyl alkynes was not investigated in this study (Scheme 17) [50].

A Zn(OTf)2‐catalyzed 1,1‐diboration of terminal alkynes with HBpin [50].

In the past decade, Sawamura and Ohmiya, as well as the Marder group, have accomplished the direct synthesis of 1,1‐diborylalkenes from activated alkynes such as propiolates, propiolamides, and 2‐ethynylazoles, using B2pin2 or BpinBdan as the boration reagents, and t BuOLi or t BuONa as the catalysts (Scheme 18) [51, 52]. This method is applicable to a broad range of alkynoates with various functional groups, producing corresponding 1,1‐diborylalkenes with moderate to high yields and excellent selectivity [51, 52]. Similar to the transition metal‐catalyzed synthesis of 1,1‐diborylalkenes from terminal alkynes, acetylide 69 serves as the key intermediate. It is formed by the deprotonation of the terminal alkyne with Brønsted bases such as LiO t Bu/NaO t Bu. In the reaction of 69 with diboron reagent 70, the carbanion selectively attacks the Bpin moiety, which has higher Lewis acidity than the Bdan moiety due to more positive NBO charge, to give alkynyl borate intermediate 71. 1,2‐migration of the terminal boryl group in 71 to the sp‐hybridized carbon atom of the alkyne moiety generates allenoate intermediate 72, which undergoes proton transfer with t BuOH to produce the 1,1‐diborylalkene S and regenerate LiO t Bu/NaO t Bu. Due to steric effect, Z‐diborylalkenes are favored in this transformation. Moreover, the products can be easily converted to 1,1‐diborylalkanes in the presence of a proton source such as methanol [51, 52].

Brønsted base ‐catalyzed 1,1‐diboration of electron‐deficient alkynes [51, 52].

Despite being a huge breakthrough in the field of organoboron chemistry, the strategy mentioned above is restricted to activated alkynes with electron‐withdrawing substituents and only works with diboron reagents such as with B2pin2 or BpinBdan [51, 52]. Similarly, the practicality of transition metal‐catalyzed synthesis of 1,1‐diborylalkenes is limited due to the sensitive nature of the catalysts, additional preparation of the catalysts and ligands, and the environmental hazards associated with transition metals. These limitations have prompted the development of more sustainable and practical metal‐free approaches. Recently, our group reported an effective metal‐free strategy for the synthesis of 1,1‐diborylalkenes via the 1,1‐diboration of terminal alkynes with HBpin, catalyzed by the superbasic P1− t Bu (Scheme 19) [39, 40]. Our catalytic system exhibits high functional group tolerance and can be used for a variety of alkynes to form corresponding 1,1‐diborylalkenes with moderate to excellent yields. The presence of electron‐withdrawing groups on the aromatic rings of the reactant results in higher product formation. This is due to the higher acidity of the alkynyl C─H bonds in aromatic alkynes with electron‐withdrawing groups compared to those with electron‐donating groups [39, 40]. After the formation of alkynylboronate C as described in Scheme 9, the process is continued with the hydride transfer from HBpin to the C2 atom of C via 76, resulting in intermediate 77, which includes a P1 δ+−N( t Bu)−Bpin cation and a vinylideneborate species. The P1− t Bu catalyst facilitates the transfer of hydride by functioning as a Lewis base, which stabilizes the partially positive charge on the boron atom of the Bpin group. Following the formation of ion pair 77, a boryl transfer can occur via transition state 78, leading to the production of 1,1‐diborylalkene Q.

A P1− t Bu‐catalyzed sequential dehydrogenative borylation and hydroboration of terminal alkynes with HBpin [39, 40].

In this work, we also demonstrated the effectiveness of the superbasic P1− t Bu for the synthesis of 1,1‐diboryl acrylate products via the 1,1‐diboration of alkynoates with diboron reagents B2pin2 and Bpin−Bdan (Scheme 20) [40]. The reaction only requires mild conditions and can tolerate a wide range of propiolate esters and propiolamides with different electronic and steric properties, giving corresponding 1,1‐diboryl acrylates with high yields [40]. The precise control of the number of Bpin/Bdan groups incorporated into the bis(propiolate) substrates can be achieved by varying the stoichiometric ratios of B2pin2/Bpin−Bdan [40]. In the proposed mechanism, the reaction is initiated by the deprotonation of R with P1− t Bu 34 via 79, resulting in the formation of the contact ion pair species 80 comprising the acetylide anion and the protonated phosphazene. The nucleophilic addition of the newly generated acetylide anion to 70 happens through 81. Subsequently, a concerted 1,2‐migration of the boryl moiety and proton transfer from the protonated phosphazene to the methoxycarbonyl group of the substrate occur via 83, resulting in the formation of the allenol species 84 and the regeneration of P1− t Bu 34. Finally, the allenol intermediate 84 undergoes tautomerization via 85 to produce the desired product S (Scheme 20). All mixed diboryl acrylate products are selectively formed in a stereoselective manner with the Bdan group adopting a cis‐relationship (Z‐isomer) to the newly formed olefinic C─H when Bpin−Bdan is used. This stereoselectivity can be attributed to intermolecular steric repulsion between the Bpin and Bdan groups in intermediate 85 [40].

A P1− t Bu ‐catalyzed 1,1‐diboration of alkynoates with diboron reagents [40].

Synthesis of 1,1,1‐tris(boronate) Esters

Similar to the organoborons described above, 1,1,1‐tris(boronate) esters, with three boronate moieties on a single C atom, can also participate in the formation of C─C and C─heteroatom bonds. The α‐boronate moieties in these compounds play a crucial role in stabilizing the adjacent carbanion center after deprotonation, which is responsible for their versatility in various bond forming events [43, 53, 54, 55, 56, 57, 58]. However, direct synthesis of 1,1,1‐tris(boronates) has been much less developed, mainly because of significant steric hindrance and high regioselectivity requirement [1]. There are only a few straightforward procedures to effectively synthesize 1,1,1‐tris(boronates), therefore, their applications in organic synthesis have yet to be fully investigated.

Around fifty years ago, Matteson and coworkers reported a synthesis of triborylmethane 88 by the reaction of trichloride 86 with ClB(OR)2 87 in the presence of lithium metal at low temperature (Scheme 21). Unfortunately, the practicality of this method is limited due to several reasons, including low yield of the desired product, harsh reaction conditions, and the use of stoichiometric amounts of lithium metal. Lithium metal is extremely sensitive to air and moisture, highly reactive toward various functional groups, and it requires careful handling due to associated hazards [43, 58].

Matteson's synthesis of triborylmethane [43, 58].

In 2002, Marder and coworkers achieved the synthesis of 1,1,1‐tris(boronates) 91 via Rh‐catalyzed dehydrogenative borylation‐hydroboration of (E)‐styrylboronates 90 with bis(catecholato)diboron (B2cat2) (Scheme 22). This method can also give 1,1,1‐tris(boronates) 91, 1,1,2‐tris(boronates) 92, 1,1‐diborylalkenes 95, 1,1‐diborylalkanes 94, and 1,2‐diborylalkanes 93, complicating the purification process. This selectivity issue is due to competing β‐hydride elimination and reductive elimination processes, which occurred after the alkene insertion into an M−B(OR)2 bond [59]. Also, (E)‐styrylboronates 90 are not readily available and thus require additional preparatory steps [59].

Rh‐catalyzed dehydrogenative borylation−hydroboration of (E)‐styrylboronates [59].

In 2015, Huang and coworkers achieved a significant breakthrough in the synthesis of 1,1,1‐tris(boronates) with a catalytic system based on ( t BuPNN)CoCl2 and NaBEt3H to catalyze tandem double dehydrogenative borylations−hydroboration of vinylarenes and B2pin2 (Scheme 23) [60]. This strategy allows direct preparation of 1,1,1‐tris(boronates) from readily available vinylarenes containing both electron‐donating and electron‐withdrawing groups with excellent selectivity and high yields [60]. A broad range of functional groups, including ether, tertiary amine, thioether, and amide, are readily tolerated in this method, thus increasing its synthetic value [60]. Mechanistic studies suggest that the catalytic cycle starts with the reaction of the precatalyst 96 with NaBEt3H to give Co(I) hydride species 97, which then goes through transmetalation with B2pin2 to generate Co(I) boryl complex 98 and HBpin. β‐boryl‐substituted Co(I) species 99 is generated via 2,1‐insertion of styrene into the Co─B bond in 98 and subsequently undergoes β‐hydride elimination, leading to the formation of monoborylalkene L and 97. Following the reaction of Co(I) hydride species 97 with B2pin2 to give 98 and HBpin, intermediate L follows a subsequent dehydrogenative borylation step, resulting in the generation of 1,1‐diborylalkene M. Upon the insertion of M into the Co─B bond in 98, β,β,β‐triboryl‐substituted cobalt‐complex 101 is formed, which finally reacts with in situ generated HBpin through oxidative addition of H─B bond and reductive elimination of C─H bond to produce the 1,1,1‐tris(boronate) product T and regenerate 98. The selectivity of this strategy for multi(boronate) products is due to the use of B2pin2 instead of HBpin as a boron source, promoting double dehydrogenative borylations to give diboryl intermediate M [60]. Stereoselectivity is achieved with bulky t Bu groups of the catalyst promoting 1,1‐diboration and 1,1,1‐triboration [60]. The solvent also plays an important role in this reaction, as the yield of T increases when pentane is used [60].

A ( t BuPNN)CoCl2‐catalyzed synthesis of 1,1,1‐tris(boronates) from terminal alkenes [60].

1,1,1‐tris(boronates) can also be obtained using a two‐step one‐pot approach, which involves a critical 1,1‐diboration for the production of 1,1‐diborylalkene, a key intermediate to 1,1,1‐tris(boronate). In this strategy, two catalytic systems with different boron sources can be used [1]. In their work, which has been discussed in Section 3, Chirik and coworkers successfully synthesized 1,1,1‐tris(boronates) using a two‐step one‐pot strategy, with cyclohexylsubstituted pyridine(diimine) cobalt methyl complex 47 as the catalyst for the 1,1‐diboration of terminal alkynes and B2pin2, which is followed by the addition of terpyridine cobalt complex 102 to facilitate the further hydroboration of in situ generated 1,1‐diborylalkenes and HBpin. This work enables the synthesis of 1,1,1‐tris(boronates) featuring a range of structurally diverse alkyl groups attached to the triborylated carbon with high yields and good selectivity (Scheme 24a) [46]. This strategy was also used by the Ingleson and coworkers, who employed NHC─Zn─H 12 (NHC = N‐heterocyclic carbene) as the active catalyst for the synthesis of 1,1‐diborylalkenes from terminal alkynes and HBpin in the first step and then added BH3•THF to promote the hydroboration of in situ generated 1,1‐diborylalkene in the second step (Scheme 24b) [61].

Two‐step one‐pot strategies for the synthesis of 1,1,1‐tris(boronates) from terminal alkynes [46, 61].

Twenty years after their first report in 2002, Marder and coworkers presented a straightforward, atom‐economical synthesis of 1,1,1‐tris(boronates), in which a catalytic system based on Cu(OAc)2/KF/P n Bu3 is utilized to facilitate sequential dehydrogenative borylation and double hydroboration of terminal alkynes (Scheme 25) [62]. This process can occur under mild conditions and possesses good tolerance toward terminal aryl alkynes containing both electron‐donating and electron‐withdrawing groups. This method is also applicable to readily available unactivated alkyl alkynes, expanding the synthetic potential of 1,1,1‐tris(boronates) [62]. In the proposed mechanism, the reduction of Cu(OAc)2 in the presence of phosphine leads to the formation of [L n CuOAc] 104, which then reacts with HBpin and KF to generate copper hydride intermediate [L n CuH] 105 and F−Bpin. The formation of a strong F−Bpin bond may be the driving force for the formation of copper hydride intermediate [L n CuH] 105, which reacts with terminal alkyne A to give copper acetylide intermediate 106 and release H2. Through the σ‐bond metathesis of the highly polarized copper‐carbon bond with HBpin, alkynyl boronic ester intermediate C and [L n CuH] 105 are obtained. Syn‐addition of [L n CuH] 105 to alkynyl boronic ester C affords alkenyl copper species 108, which then undergoes σ‐bond metathesis with HBpin to provide 1,1‐diborylalkene Q. Finally, Q goes through Cu‐catalyzed hydroboration to produce 1,1,1‐tris(boronate) T and regenerate [L n CuH] 105. The utility of 1,1,1‐tris(boronates) as synthetic intermediates for the synthesis of carbocyclic organoboronates and α‐vinylboronates was also demonstrated in this work [62].

A Cu(OAc)2‐catalyzed synthesis of 1,1,1‐tris(boronates) from terminal alkynes [62].

In 2023, our group developed the first metal‐free approach to produce 1,1,1‐tris(boronates) via 1,1,1‐triboration of terminal alkynes with HBpin using the superbasic P1− t Bu as an effective organocatalyst (Scheme 26) [39]. Our protocol enables the synthesis of 1,1,1‐tris(boronates) with high yields, good functional group tolerance, and excellent chemoselectivity [39]. Haloaryl‐substituted alkynes, which are typically sensitive to low‐valent transition metals, are well tolerated under the applied conditions, selectively yielding corresponding 1,1,1‐tris(boronates). Our catalytic system also works effectively with polyaromatic and heteroaromatic alkynes. Noticeably, in the 1,1,1‐triboration reaction of methyl 4‐ethynylbenzoate, the ester functionality can remain intact even when excess amount of HBpin is used at elevated temperature for a long time [39]. The 1,1,1‐triboration of terminal alkynes here involes sequential dehydrogenative borylation and double hydroboration. The sequence of dehydrogenative borylation and the first hydroboration of terminal alkynes to generate 1,1‐diborylalkene Q are described in Scheme 19. Similar to the first hydroboration, the second hydroboration proceeds through a hydride transfer with 110, leading to ion pair 111. This is followed by a boryl transfer via 112, resulting in the generation of the desired product 1,1,1‐triborylalkane T (Scheme 26). The CH─π interaction, as observed in the first hydroboration, is the key factor in determining the regioselectivity of the second hydroboration reaction [39].

A P1− t Bu‐catalyzed 1,1,1‐triboration of terminal alkynes with HBpin [39].

Selective multiborylation remains an attractive research topic in organic chemistry. In 2025, Zhao, Ma, and Dong reported a chemodivergent and selective approach to synthesise various multiborylated compounds from commercially available chemicals [63]. Even though transition metal catalyst is needed, this method possesses a broad substrate scope and high functional group tolerance. The reaction features silyl enol ethers (SEEs) as starting materials, B2pin2 as the boron source, cobalt(II) acetylacetonate (Co(acac)2) as the catalyst, and terpyridine as an additive (Scheme 27a). In the presence of Co(acac)2 and terpyridine, SEEs undergo regioselective polyborylation to form multiborylated compounds via C─O bond functionalization while avoiding unwanted deoxyborylation and dehydroborylation due to the steric hindrance of bulky silyl groups. A wide range of functional groups with different steric and electronic properties are well tolerated in this reaction, including alkyl, phenyl, OMe, OBn, OTBS, OBz, F, TMS, Bpin, and acetal. With β‐diaryl SEEs as starting materials, 1,1‐diborylalkenes can be achieved in decent yield (37%–60%). Mechanistic studies suggest that this diborylation process involves dehydroborylation, deoxyborylation, and hydroboration. Interestingly, β‐disubstituted SEEs with cycloalkyl substituents form 1,1,1‐triborylalkanes (with 47–75% yields) instead of 1,1‐diborylalkenes. This difference may be attributed to the higher steric hindrance of two aryl groups in β‐diaryl SEEs, which prevents further hydroboration to give triborylated product. This strategy also features α‐substituted SEEs, giving corresponding 1,1,1‐triborylalkanes in moderate to excellent yields (56%–88%) (Scheme 27b). The triborylation process is suspected to proceed through tandem double dehydroborylation/deoxyborylation/hydroboration, as suggested by mechanistic studies. Despite the use of transition metal, this work still marks an important milestone in the development of multiborylation synthesis [63]. It is the first report on the synthesis of β‐disubstituted 1,1,1‐triborylalkanes in good yields, and also a chemodivergent, convenient, and selective method to access a variety of multiborylated compounds.

Synthesis of 1,1‐diborylalkenes from β‐diaryl SEEs and 1,1,1‐triborylalkanes from α‐ and β‐substituted SEEs [63].

Conclusion and Outlook

The synthetic value of alkynylboronates, 1,1‐diborylalkenes and 1,1,1‐tris(boronates) continues to drive efforts toward their efficient preparation. In recent decades, many effective routes using both main‐group and transition‐metal catalysts have been developed. While these metal‐based methods are powerful, they often suffer from air‐/moisture‐sensitivity, special handling, laborious catalyst/ligand preparation, and environmental concerns. Current metal‐free systems alleviate some of these issues but still show narrow substrate scopes, as a certain catalytic system is only good for a specific range of substrates. On another hand, these three families of organoboronates are mechanistically connected: alkynylboronates serve as intermediates en route to 1,1‐diborylalkenes, which in turn are precursors to 1,1,1‐tris(boronates). This hierarchy suggests that universal catalysts are conceivable: systems designed to promote, in one platform and with switchable control, selective mono‐, di‐, and triboration.

Such catalysts should (i) broaden alkyne scope to include unactivated (especially aliphatic) substrates and aryl alkynes with electron‐donating groups; (ii) engage a wider set of boron sources to access mixed multi(boronate) esters and diversified boryl groups; and (iii) maintain high chemoselectivity for dehydrogenative C(sp)─B formation by outcompeting or suppressing alkyne hydroboration. The latter may be achieved by favoring rapid, efficient C(sp)─H cleavage relative to B─H addition, or by transiently coordinating/protecting the C≡C bond to hinder hydroboration pathways. We believe that such system has been partially realized with our phosphazene‐based catalytic boration of alkynes, but there is still so much to explore in this chemical space [39, 40].

As a remaining major challenge in this field, access to organoboronates bearing diverse boryl groups is synthetically important. External nucleophiles can trigger boryl elimination during downstream functionalisation; stability and reactivity therefore depend strongly on the boryl identity (e.g., BMIDA often proves optimal owing to intramolecular coordination at boron). Mixed multi(boronate) esters offer complementary reactivity between boryl groups, enabling chemoselective, stepwise and stereocontrolled elaborations. Accordingly, practical catalytic routes that deliver specified boryl patterns would open otherwise inaccessible transformations, particularly with typically unstable organoboronates.

Another important remaining challenge is that 1,1,1‐tris(boronates) remain underexplored in both synthesis and application, compared with chemistry of alkynylboronates and 1,1‐diborylalkenes. We anticipate that their reactivity as radical manifolds (e.g., 1,1‐diboryl radicals derived from 1,1,1‐tris(boronates)) will further expand access to small building blocks, natural products, and bioactive targets. Advancements in this particular direction will also enrich the potential applications of other multi(boronate) esters and mixed multi(boronate) esters.

Finally, emerging technologies such as photoredox catalysis, electrochemistry, and continuous flow chemistry should be leveraged both to construct these organoboron motifs and to exploit their unique reactivity under mild, sustainable conditions. We advocate prioritising metal‐free platforms that (a) deliver mixed multi(boronate) esters with programmable boryl identities and (b) generalise across mono‐/di‐/tri‐ and even higher degree of multiboration. Progress along these lines is well positioned to deliver substantive advances in organoboron chemistry and its applications.
