Metabolomic profiling of goji fermentation: enhancing functional metabolites with high-glucose-adaptive LAB
Authors: Cong-Rong Shi, Xiao-Juan Zhang, Han Xie, Wei Zhang, Rui-Xin Tang, Zhen-Ming Lu, Yan Geng, Ran Lu, Fang-Zhou Wang, Li-Juan Chai, Hui-Ling Zhang, Hong-Yu Xu, Jin-Song Shi, Zheng-Hong Xu
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Hyper-osmotic stress in high-sugar fruits inhibits the fermentation performance of lactic acid bacteria (LAB). Besides, there is a prevailing trend of low-sugar diets for health concern. Therefore, it is essential to develop LAB strains with high-sugar tolerance and efficient sugar metabolism. In this study, three high-sugar-adaptive strains (2–12 mutations) were obtained through random mutagenesis and screening at 150 g/L glucose. When applied to high-sugar goji juice fermentation, they reduced glucose from 92.26 to 28.85 g/L, reached 2.56*109 CFU/mL, and shifted the sensory profile from sweet and herbal to fruity and pleasantly sour. Metabolomics revealed enrichment of bioactive metabolites such as indole-3-lactate (+22.32%), linoleate (+16.37%) and spermine (+64.50%) in strain LP-4-3*. Mechanistically, high-glucose-adaptive LAB accumulated more maltose, galactitol, and glycosylated phenolic compounds to alleviate osmotic pressure induced by high glucose. This study highlights the potential of high-glucose-adaptive LAB in developing low-sugar and health-promoting fermented fruit juices.

Introduction

Goji (Lycium barbarum) berries are renowned for their high nutritional and medicinal value, offering benefits such as protecting eye health, reducing blood lipid, and exerting an antioxidation effect1. Except dietary fibers, vitamins, and minerals, they are rich in a range of health-promoting ingredients, including polyphenols (2.6–4 mg/g), carotenoids (1.2–4 mg/g), polysaccharides (16–48 mg/g), spermine, and unsaturated fatty acids2. Due to the health benefits of goji berries and the challenges in the storage and transportation of fresh berries, goji berry processing products have gained popularity. Among these products, functional fermented beverages produced via lactic acid bacteria (LAB) fermentation have been particularly favored in the market for their added probiotic benefits as well as modified organoleptic characteristics3. Beyond lactate, LAB are known to produce various health-promoting metabolites, including γ-aminobutyric acid (γ-GABA), bioactive peptides, short-chain fatty acids, exopolysaccharides, and vitamins4. Goji juice fermented by LAB, including Lacticaseibacillus paracasei, Lactiplantibacillus plantarum, Lacticaseibacillus rhamnosus, Lactobacillus reuteri, and Streptococcus thermophilus have been reported5–7. The result showed that LAB improved the sensory quality of the juice by increasing acetic acid, heptanoic acid, ethyl phenylacetate, and linalool, produced health-promoting compounds such as indole-3-lactate, and enhanced the protective effect against liver injury5–7.

In addition to the aforementioned benefits, reducing the sugar content in fruit materials is another significant advantage of LAB fermentation, although this aspect has been rarely discussed. Goji berries are naturally high in sugars, primarily glucose, fructose, and sucrose, with contents ranging from 10.31 to 77.59 g/100 g8. Freshly squeezed goji juice typically contains 130–170 g/L sugar, and even after fermentation by LAB, the residual sugar content often exceeds 100 g/L5. The high residual sugar content in goji berries and goji processed food has raised health concerns among consumers, as it may contribute to cardiovascular disease, excessive weight gain, and diabetes9. In line with the World Health Organization’s recommendations for sugar control10, it is essential to develop LAB with high-sugar tolerance and efficient sugar metabolism to address this issue.

Except from reducing residual sugar content, further enhancing macronutrient bioconversion and increasing the bioactive function of fermented goji juice is important. LAB has the ability to transform glucose into functional organic acids and amino acids, liberate phenolics and carotenoids of fruit matrix into free forms, and convert them into bioavailable derivatives with higher antioxidant and anti-inflammatory activity11. It is hypothesized that high-glucose-adaptive LAB with enhanced sugar metabolism could accelerate these metabolic shifts and enrich the nutritional profile of fermented goji juice. However, it remains unclear whether these high-glucose-adaptive LAB can effectively promote these metabolic transformations and significantly enhance the bioactive function of fermented goji juice during an actual fermentation context.

In this study, we address the issue of high residual sugar content in goji juice fermentation by employing mutagenesis and high glucose screening strategies to develop three high-glucose-adaptive strains, including one Lactiplantibacillus pentosus and two Lactiplantibacillus plantarum strains. These strains enhanced sugar metabolism, thereby reducing residual sugar levels in fermented goji juice, improving sensory characteristics, and increasing viable cell counts. From a metabolomics perspective, we utilized high-performance liquid chromatography (HPLC) for targeted detection of organic acids, amino acids, and indole-3-lactate, and combined ultrahigh-performance liquid chromatography-mass spectrometry (UPLC-MS/MS) for non-targeted metabolomics analysis. This study demonstrated that these evolved strains markedly strengthened sugar utilization while simultaneously reprogrammed metabolic pathways to enrich bioactive metabolites, such as indole-3-lactate and functional fatty acids. By elucidating two adaptive strategies (compatible solutes accumulation and distinct phenolic glycosylation), this work provides a mechanistic foundation and a practical blueprint for the low-sugar and high-function fermentation of sugar-rich fruit matrices. Furthermore, these strains offer a robust and food-grade chassis for the industrial production of value-added nutraceuticals.

Results

Evolution of LAB for adaptability to high-glucose stress

Glucose is commonly abundant in fruits and is preferentially utilized as a carbon source. Goji berries contain a high content of glucose, ranging from 19.98 to 30.19 g/100 g12. LAB with higher adaptability to high-glucose stress (high-sugar tolerance and efficient sugar metabolism) is beneficial for enhanced fruit matrices fermentation. Considering the glucose tolerance of LAB, strains LPE-2, LP-D, and LP-4-3 were screened out from the LAB strains stored in the laboratory (Supplementary Fig. 1). These strains were then subjected to atmospheric and room temperature plasma (ARTP) mutagenesis to further increase their adaptability to high glucose. Mutants which exhibit higher optical density at 600 nm (OD600; an indicator of biomass accumulation) or glucose consumption were selected and re-exposed to ARTP (Fig. 1a). After two, four, and two rounds of evolution based on strains LPE-2, LP-D, and LP-4-3, respectively, totally nineteen high-glucose-adaptive strains were obtained. Most high-glucose-adaptive strains exhibited higher glucose consumption capabilities compared with their corresponding parental strain in MRS medium containing 150 g/L glucose (Fig. 1b). Glucose consumption in high-glucose-adaptive strains LPE-2-2C, LP-D-8A, and LP-4-3-11F was enhanced to 47.58 g/L, 53.21 g/L, and 38.88 g/L, respectively, marking increases of 65.50, 78.73, and 69.87% over that of their parental strains, respectively (Fig. 1b). OD600 of high-glucose-adaptive strains LPE-2-1G, LP-D-9E, and LP-4-3-10D was enhanced to 4.60, 4.82, and 4.15, respectively, representing increases of 27.34, 18.74, and 9.33% compared with that of their corresponding parental strains LPE-2, LP-D, and LP-4-3, respectively (Supplementary Fig. 2). Accumulation of lactate inhibited cell growth and further led to inhibited glucose consumption. With the addition of calcium carbonate to neutralize the accumulated organic acids13, glucose consumptions of all strains were further enhanced to varying degrees (with increases of 8.85–45.72 g/L) (Fig. 1b). Glucose consumption of high-glucose-adaptive strains LPE-2-1F, LP-D-4C, and LP-4-3-11F was the highest (reaching 81.31 g/L, 77.40 g/L, and 83.23 g/L, respectively), which increased by 27.46, 69.58, and 52.35% in comparison with that of their corresponding parental strains LPE-2, LP-D, and LP-4-3, respectively (Fig. 1b). These results confirmed that high-glucose-adaptive LAB possessed better glucose consumption capability than corresponding parental LAB.

Improved fermentation of goji juice by high-glucose-adaptive LAB

It was considered that the screened high-glucose-adaptive LAB might be suitable for the fermentation of goji juice. Whether strengthened glucose consumption ability could improve the effect of fermentation? Ten well-performed high-glucose-adaptive strains in MRS medium were inoculated into goji juice separately, including three, four, and three strains evolved from parental strains LPE-2, LP-D, and LP-4-3, respectively.

After 38 h of fermentation, the sensory evaluation of fermented goji juice over aroma and taste was analyzed (Fig. 2). The sensory attributes of fermented goji juice were significantly altered. Sweet and raw material aroma decreased, while acidic and fermented aroma increased (Fig. 2a). Sweetness reduced, while sourness and astringency increased (Fig. 2b). The change was similar to the conclusions reached in a previous report regarding the fermentation of goji juice by several LAB5. While the main organoleptic profile of fermented goji juice was similar among strains, the goji juice fermented by high-glucose-adaptive LAB represented decreased sweet aroma and sweetness, strengthened fruity and acidic aroma, as well as enhanced sourness (Fig. 2). After fermentation, viable cell counts of high-glucose-adaptive strains LPE-2-1F, LP-D-4G, and LP-4-3-10D were enhanced to 1.32*109 CFU/mL, 1.28*109 CFU/mL, and 2.56*109 CFU/mL, respectively, increased by 66.81, 81.60, and 321.43% in comparison with that of their corresponding parental strains (Fig. 3b). This result indicated that high-glucose adaptivity helped to improve survival rates of probiotic LAB in fermented goji juice, increasing quality of fermented goji juice.

Metabolic profiling of fermented goji juice by high-glucose-adaptive LAB

The superior glucose consumption and biomass accumulation of the high-glucose-adaptive strains LPE-2-1F, LP-D-4G, and LP-4-3-10D indicated an enhanced metabolic activity that might underlie the increased synthesis of functional metabolites. To test this hypothesis, a comprehensive metabolic analysis was conducted to compare parental and high-glucose-adaptive LAB, focusing on bioactive compounds enrichment and metabolic pathway shift. For qualitative and quantitative analysis of supernatants of goji juice fermented by high-glucose-adaptive LAB (marked as LPE-2*, LP-D*, and LP-4-3*) and that fermented by the corresponding parental LAB (marked as LPE-2, LP-D, and LP-4-3), HPLC-based targeted analysis on glucose, fructose, organic acids, amino acids, and indole-3-lactate (Tables 1–3), combined with UPLC-MS/MS-based non-targeted metabolomics were conducted, using non-fermented goji juice (CK) as reference. Base peak chromatograms showed clear separation of samples under both positive and negative ion modes (Supplementary Fig. 3). 8310 and 4473 metabolites were tentatively identified by positive and negative ion modes, respectively (Supplementary Data 1). Principal component analysis (PCA) showed that the total metabolic profiles of LAB-fermented goji juice were distinct from that of non-fermented goji juice (Supplementary Fig. 4a, b). The differences between goji juice fermented by high-glucose-adaptive LAB and that fermented by their corresponding parental LAB were clearly distinguished based on the dataset from negative ion mode (Supplementary Fig. 4b). Sugar, such as glucose and fructose, could be catabolized by LAB into organic acids, amino acids, and other carbon skeletons of important compounds. These processes mainly occurred through central metabolic modules, including glycolysis (EMP), the tricarboxylic acid cycle (TCA), the phosphoketolase pathway, and the pentose phosphate pathway (HMP). The result showed that goji juice fermented by LAB increased the accumulation of dihydroxyacetone phosphate (DHAP), 3-phosphate-glyceraldehyde, 3-phosphate-glycerol, 4-phosphate-erythrose, lactate, succinate, and fumarate, while decreased malate, citrate (53.62–59.84%), and pyroglutamate (33.94–39.90%) (Fig. 6b). Evolved strain LP-4-3* exhibited additional depletions of citrate and pyroglutamate by 10.45% and 7.61%, respectively, indicating intensified citrate utilization and accelerated glutathione turnover under high-sugar conditions (Table 1 and Fig. 6b). Six heterofermentative LAB produced a significant amount of 28.55–33.09 g/L lactate, with 1.55–2.25 g/L acetate, 0.89–1.57 g/L fumarate, and 0.08–0.57 g/L succinate (Table 1 and Fig. 6b). Although the consumed sugar was predominantly converted into lactate, the result showed that the lactate yield (produced lactate/consumed glucose) in high-glucose-adaptive strains LPE-2*, LP-D*, and LP-4-3* decreased by 12.68, 25.00, and 35.16% relative to their parental strains (Fig. 6c), suggesting more carbon flux was directed into other pathways. For example, fumarate production by high-glucose-adaptive strains LPE-2*, LP-D*, and LP-4-3* increased by 14.62%, 41.08%, and 4.04%, compared to their corresponding parental strains (Table 1 and Fig. 6b).

Goji juice mainly contains 1.43 g/L L-proline, 1.10 g/L L-alanine, 0.70 g/L L-arginine, and 0.50 g/L L-serine (Table 2). After fermentation by LAB, marked depletions of L-serine, L-methionine, L-glycine, L-lysine, L-tryptophan, L-phenylalanine, and the branched-chain amino acids (L-valine, L-leucine, and L-isoleucine) were observed (Table 2 and Fig. 6b), with L-leucine and L-serine being the most extensively consumed, presumably to supply nitrogen for biomass synthesis and redox homeostasis. L-leucine consumption by high-glucose-adaptive LAB exceeded that of the parental strains by 1.30–2.03% (Table 2 and Fig. 6b). Concomitantly, L-aspartate and L-glutamate accumulated to 0.78–0.89 g/L and 0.62–0.76 g/L, respectively, with significantly higher concentrations detected in the high-glucose-adaptive strains (Table 2 and Fig. 6b). Strain LPE-2* produced 14.59% more L-aspartate and 22.72% more L-glutamate than its parental strain LPE-2 (Table 2 and Fig. 6b). L-Aspartate and L-glutamate could promote the synthesis of purine, pyrimidine, and functional derived amino acids. For example, γ-GABA, a derivative of L-glutamate with health benefits including anti-anxiety and blood pressure regulation, increased by 6.93% and 4.06% in high-glucose-adaptive strains LPE-2* and LP-D*, respectively (Fig. 6b).

In addition, diaminopimelate (DAP), the precursor of L-lysine, significantly increased by 19.9–46.3 fold after fermentation (Fig. 6b). DAP levels in goji juice fermented by high-glucose-adaptive strains LP-4-3* and LP-D* were elevated by 12.55% and 37.32%, respectively, compared to their parental strains (Fig. 6b).

In the aromatic amino acid metabolic module, after fermentation by LAB, L-tyrosine, L-tryptophan, and L-phenylalanine were significantly reduced. Compared with parental LAB, high-glucose-adaptive LAB enhanced the reduction of aromatic amino acids by 9.66%–89.58% (Table 2). Because aromatic amino acids are precursors of many important functional compounds, it was necessary to analyze the change of metabolic pathway of aromatic amino acids in the high-glucose-adaptive LAB.

Functional indole compounds, including indole-3-lactate and melatonin, were enriched in the downstream of the L-tryptophan metabolic pathway (Fig. 6b). LAB-derived indole-3-lactate is known to alleviate intestinal inflammation, correct microbial dysbiosis, and exert antidepressant effects through AhR signaling14. A significant increase in indole-3-lactate, ranging from 12.93 to 16.49 mg/L, was observed in fermented goji juice (Table 3). The increase was more pronounced than previously reported, where the indole-3-lactate level was up to 6.25 mg/L in goji juice fermented by Lactiplantibacillus plantarum6. Furthermore, the result showed that high-glucose-adaptive Lactiplantibacillus plantarum (strains LP-D* and LP-4-3*) transformed more L-tryptophan into indole-3-lactate in comparison with their parental strains (Fig. 6d). The production of indole-3-lactate by the high-glucose-adaptive strain LP-4-3* exceeded that of its parental strain LP-4-3 by 22.32% (Fig. 6d). Besides, Melatonin is recognized for its antioxidant and anti-inflammatory properties, capable of treating neurodegenerative disorders, epilepsy, and dysbiosis15. Melatonin levels in fermented goji juice showed an increase of 13.78–145.91%, with a 24.94% elevation in the high-glucose-adaptive strain LP-4-3* relative to its parental strain (Fig. 6b).

Phenylpropanoids, including cinnamate, 2-coumarate, 4-coumarate, methyl chavicol, caffeyl alcohol, and sinapaldehyde in the downstream of L-tyrosine and L-phenylalanine metabolic pathway, were increased after LAB fermentation (Fig. 6b). Sinapaldehyde, a phenylpropanoid with established effects on reducing reactive oxygen species (ROS) and mitigating the production of tumor necrosis factor-α and interleukin-616, was enriched by 10.4–17.6 times in fermented goji juice (Fig. 6b). The content of sinapaldehyde in the high-glucose-adaptive strain LP-4-3* increased by 13.77% compared to its parental strain (Fig. 6b).

Acetyl-CoA could be utilized for the synthesis of functional fatty acids, which could mitigate ROS induced by lipopolysaccharide, reduce immune dysfunction risk, and boost antitumor immunity17,18. Palmitate, linoleate, 18-hydroxyoleate (a hydroxylated product of oleate), and their derivatives were accumulated through fermentation by LAB. 18-hydroxyoleate and linoleate increased by 1.7–4.8 times and 158.6–261.1 times, respectively (Fig. 6b). The result also showed that high-glucose-adaptive LAB enhanced the production of functional unsaturated fatty acids. 13-oxooctadeca-9, 11-dienoic acid production by high-glucose-adaptive strains LPE-2* and LP-4-3* exceeded that of their parental strains by 6.29% and 38.35%, respectively (Fig. 6b). Linoleate production by high-glucose-adaptive strains LPE-2* and LP-4-3* was 12.02% and 16.37% higher, respectively, and octadecadienoic acid production was 10.65 and 11.97% higher, compared to their parental strains (Fig. 6b).

Adequate polyamines have been reported to possess anti-inflammatory effects, and their dietary supplementation aids in preventing aging and memory loss19. LAB fermentation increased the accumulation of N-acetyl-aminobutanal (10.4–13.2 fold), N-acetyl-spermidine (23.7–28.8 fold), spermine (32.2–68.7 fold), and triferuloyl spermidine (3.3–8.9 fold) by consuming spermidine and trihydroxyferuloyl spermidine (Fig. 6b). High-glucose-adaptive strain LP-4-3* exhibited a 64.50 and 91.72% increase in spermine and triferuloyl spermidine accumulation, respectively, compared to parental strain LP-4-3 (Fig. 6b).

Evolved strains accumulate more compatible solutes in response of glucose stress

Accumulating intracellular compatible solutes is a key strategy for coping with various stresses like freezing, heat, and drying20. These uncharged, polar, and hydrosoluble solutes, including sugars, polyols, and amino acids, protect proteins and regulate osmotic pressure by reverting water concentration gradients. Here, various compatible solutes were found to be up-regulated in goji juice fermented by high-glucose-adaptive LAB.

Trehalose and galactitol are known to act as compatible solutes that help modulate osmotic pressure under high salinity and high-glucose stress20–22. In sugar conversion module, after goji juice fermentation by LAB, trehalose, maltose, and galactitol levels increased, while 6-phosphate-glucose levels reduced (Fig. 6b). Especially, trehalose levels in high-glucose-adaptive strain LP-4-3* were elevated by 13.53%, maltose levels in strain LPE-2* increased by 19.89%, and galactitol levels in strain LP-D* were heightened by 15.29% in comparison with their parental strains (Fig. 6b). These results suggested that the higher level of these compatible solutes likely serves as a defensive mechanism for these high-glucose-adaptive strains to alleviate hyperosmotic stress induced by high glucose. To verify this hypothesis, 4 mM trehalose, galactitol, or maltose was separately added to MRS plates containing high glucose concentrations (20, 150, 320, and 350 g/L), and the growth of high-glucose-adaptive and parental LAB strains was assessed (Supplementary Fig. 6). The result showed that maltose enabled LAB to maintain robust growth at 320 g/L glucose, whereas galactitol sustained visible growth even at 350 g/L glucose (Supplementary Fig. 6).

Moreover, compared with parental LAB, L-proline and L-glutamate increased in goji juice fermented by high-glucose-adaptive LAB, which play an important role in adjusting osmotic pressure20. Strains LPE-2* and LP-4-3* increased the content of L-proline by 13.20% and 27.84%, respectively (Fig. 6b). It was suggested that accumulated L-proline could provide osmoprotection against high-sugar stress.

Evolved strains store glucose as glucosides to alleviate glucose stress

Through fermentation by LAB, significant compositional changes in glycosylated phenolic compounds were observed. Phenylpropanoid glucosides such as 4-O-glucosyl-4-coumarate, 4-O-glucosyl-4-coumaryl alcohol, 1-O-glucosyl-ferulate, and 1-O-glucosyl-sinapate increased, while 2-O-glucosyl-2-coumarinate, 1-O-glucosyl-cinnamate, and anthocyanin glycosides like 3,5-O-glucosyl-cyanidin decreased (Fig. 6b). A higher accumulation of phenolic glucosides was shown in high-glucose-adaptive strain LP-4-3*. Production of 1-O-glucosyl-ferulate and 1-O-glucosyl-sinapate in strain LP-4-3* was 26.27 and 34.64% higher than those of parental strain LP-4-3, respectively (Fig. 6b). Compared to strain LP-4-3, consumption of 2-O-glucosyl-2-coumarinate and 3,5-O-glucosyl-cyanidin was reduced by 1.9 and 1.4 times, respectively, in strain LP-4-3* (Fig. 6b). It was suggested that high-glucose-adaptive LAB could store excessive glucose as glucosides, which bind with phenolic compounds.

In addition, high-glucose-adaptive LAB showed an enhanced capacity to produce glycosylated terpenoids. Picrocrocin and crocetin diglucosyl ester are glycosylated apocarotenoids with improved bioavailability and anti-cancer effects23,24. They increased by 2.8–3.7 times and 3.6–6.9 times in fermented goji juice, respectively (Fig. 6b). Picrocrocin levels increased by 16.26% in high-glucose-adaptive strain LP-4-3*, and crocetin diglucosyl ester production increased by 29.24, 52.08, and 49.86% in high-glucose-adaptive strains LPE-2*, LP-D*, and LP-4-3*, respectively, compared with their parental strains (Fig. 6b).

In addition to alleviating high-glucose stress by storing glucose as glucosides, high-glucose-adaptive LAB also utilized glycosylation of phenolic compounds and terpenoids to protect the aglycones from degradation and oxidation. This dual function of glycosylation not only enhanced the nutritional bioavailability of fermented goji juice but also represented an evolved mechanism to cope with the unfavorable environment.

To investigate the extent of glycosylation in phenolic compounds, non-fermented and fermented goji juice were treated with β-glucosidase. β-glucosidase could hydrolyze glucosidic bonds, thus exposing the hydroxyl groups of aglycones and increasing the reducibility. The Folin-Ciocalteu method depends on the reducibility of phenols to measure total phenol content. Therefore, the change of total phenol content could reflect the extent of glycosylation in phenolic compounds partially.

After β-glucosidase treatment, total phenols in goji juice fermented by the high-glucose-adaptive strain LP-4-3* increased significantly by 433.41 mg/L, a 3.9-fold enhancement over parental strain LP-4-3 (Fig. 7a). This result confirmed the glycosylation of phenolics was prevalent in goji juice fermented by strain LP-4-3*. In contrast, after β-glucosidase treatment, total phenols in goji juice fermented by high-glucose-adaptive strain LP-D* and its parental strain LP-D decreased by 4.26% and 12.30%, respectively (Fig. 7a). These observations indicated that the change in reducibility of fermented goji juice after β-glucosidase treatment was species/strain-dependent and might be influenced by the stability of aglycones and redox balance. Meanwhile, although flavonoids were not significant differential metabolites, their presence in glycosylated forms was also confirmed by an increase in total flavonoids after β-glucosidase treatment. Discussion

Although a previous study has underscored the advantage of goji juice fermented by LAB on improving flavor and mitigating liver injury7, the high residual sugar (mainly fructose and glucose) in goji juice (exceed 100 g/L even after fermentation) contravened sugar-control trends and raised health concerns of customers5. In this study, three high-glucose-adaptive LAB strains with high-sugar tolerance and efficient sugar metabolism were developed by ARTP mutagenesis and high-glucose screen (Fig. 1a). These evolved LAB were applied in goji juice fermentation and consumed 16.80–33.06% more glucose than their corresponding parental LAB (Fig. 3a). Besides, these high-glucose-adaptive LAB exhibited better performance in reducing residual sugar levels of goji juice (consumed by 45.69% and down to 89.82 g/L) (Fig. 3a) than previous reported LAB (consumed by 10.71–35.85%)5. In addition, high-glucose-adaptive LAB increased the biomass by 321.43% (up to 2.56*109 CFU/mL) (Fig. 3b) and improved the sensory characteristic of parental LAB-fermented goji juice (reducing sweetness and increasing fruity aroma and sourness) (Fig. 2). These findings suggested that high-glucose-adaptive LAB enhanced metabolic activity and optimized the fermentation of goji juice.

Furthermore, comprehensive metabolomics analysis based on HPLC and UPLC-MS/MS was conducted to investigate the metabolic shift in high-glucose-adaptive LAB. The results revealed that the majority of sugar was converted into lactate (28.55–33.09 g/L) (Table 1); however, the lactate yield of high-glucose-adaptive LAB was 12.68–35.16% lower than that of parental LAB (Fig. 6c). This indicated that the excess glucose consumed by the evolved strains was not solely channeled into lactate, but was systematically redirected toward a range of health-relevant secondary metabolites via reprogrammed metabolic pathways. KEGG enrichment analysis revealed that the high-glucose-adaptive LAB up-regulated five functional-metabolite-related modules, including central carbon metabolism, aromatic amino acid metabolism, fatty acid metabolism, polyamine metabolism, and sugar conversion in comparison with parental LAB, underpinning the superior sugar-utilizing capacity of the high-glucose-adaptive LAB (Fig. 6b). (1) In the central metabolic module, the TCA cycle in high-glucose-adaptive LAB elevated fumarate by 14.62% and, via an anaplerotic route, increased L-aspartate by 14.59% relative to the parental LAB. Glutamate rose by 22.72% in the high-glucose-adaptive LAB, driving a 6.93% increase in γ-GABA production relative to the parental LAB. DAP, which cross-links the glycan strands of peptidoglycan25, was 37.32% higher in the high-glucose-adaptive LAB than in the parental LAB. Accumulated DAP by the evolved strain might reinforce cell-wall stability, thereby enhancing resistance to high-sugar-induced osmotic stress. (2) In the aromatic amino acid metabolic module, tryptophan in the high-glucose-adaptive strains was preferentially diverted to indole-3-lactate (increased by 22.32% versus the parental LAB and up to 16.49 mg/L) and melatonin (increased by 24.94% versus the parental LAB). Besides, L-tyrosine and L-phenylalanine in the high-glucose-adaptive strains were transformed into phenylpropanoids, such as sinapaldehyde (increased by 13.77% versus the parental LAB). (3) In the fatty acid metabolic module, the high-glucose-adaptive strain directed elevated acetyl-CoA flux into linoleate, yielding a 16.37% increase over the parental strain. Additionally, in the high-glucose-adaptive LAB, unsaturated fatty acids such as 13-oxooctadeca-9, 11-dienoic acid and octadecadienoic acid rose by 38.35% and 11.97%, respectively, in comparison with the parental LAB. An increase in octadecenoic acid has also been observed in an osmotic-tolerant mutant of Lactobacillus paracasei26. Therefore, these functional unsaturated fatty acids might be synthesized for the up-regulation of cell membrane fluidity in response to hyperosmosis induced by high glucose. Metabolomics analysis of reprogrammed metabolic pathways demonstrated that high-glucose-adaptive LAB could transform sugar-rich fruit matrices into low-sugar and high-value beverages.

Behind the reprogrammed metabolism, the adaptive mechanism underlying glucose tolerance was also investigated. High sugar has previously been characterized as a non-ionic hyperosmotic stress. The key subject of the adaptive mechanism was osmotic protection, including (1) accumulation of compatible solutes such as trehalose in Gluconobacter oxydans at 400 g/L glucose27 and (2) membrane homeostasis through regulated glycerophospholipid and fatty acid metabolism in Lactobacillus paracasei at 248 g/L glucose26. Compared with previous reports, two complementary strategies, including classical compatible-solute accumulation and specific phenolic glycosylation, were identified in the evolved LAB. Firstly, compatible solutes such as maltose and galactitol were transformed from glucose and accumulated inside the cell to help counteract hyper-osmotic stress induced by high sugar without perturbing enzyme function. Maltose and galactitol levels in high-glucose-adaptive LAB were increased by 19.89% and 15.29%, respectively, in comparison with their parental strains (Fig. 6b). Their protective role under high glucose was confirmed (Supplementary Fig. 6). Secondly, excess glucose was specifically diverted into glycosylated phenolics and terpenoids, simultaneously lowering residual sugar and stabilizing labile aglycones. The production of 1-O-glucosyl-ferulate and crocetin diglucosyl ester was increased by 26.27% and 52.08%, respectively, in the high-glucose-adaptive strain, compared with the parental strain (Fig. 6b). While picrocrocin and crocetin diglucosyl ester levels were 16.26% and 49.86% higher, respectively, in the high-glucose-adaptive strain (Fig. 6b). The extensive glycosylation of phenolics and flavonoids was further demonstrated (Fig. 7). These adaptive strategies not only corroborate previous findings but also broaden the view of high-glucose adaptation beyond osmotic protection. Operating in concert, the two complementary mechanisms account for the enhanced sugar tolerance of the evolved strains.

To clarify the genetic basis of enhanced performance, detailed mutations of three well-performed high-glucose-adaptive LAB were investigated, compared with their corresponding parental strains (Supplementary Data 7). In the evolved strain LPE-2-1F (LPE-2*), the 62nd nucleotide upstream of the gene of phosphoenolpyruvate synthase (PpsA) was mutated from C to A. PpsA catalyzes the conversion of pyruvate and ATP into PEP and AMP in gluconeogenesis. The accumulation of PEP enhances sugar uptake via the PEP-dependent phosphotransferase system28. In the evolved strain LP-D-4G (LP-D*), the 112th nucleotide upstream of the gene of NAD+-dependent aldehyde/alcohol dehydrogenase (AdhE) start codon was mutated from T to C. AdhE channels the phosphoketolase-derived acetyl-CoA to ethanol, regenerating NAD+ and sustaining the redox balance of central metabolism29. In the evolved strain LP-4-3-10D (LP-4-3*), the start codon of the gene of nucleotide-binding universal stress protein (UspA) was mutated from ATG to ATA, resulting in an N-terminal methionine-to-isoleucine substitution. Because growth arrest under diverse stresses was invariably linked to UspA overexpression30, it was postulated that the variant attenuates UspA synthesis, thereby relieving its glucose-triggered inhibitory burden. Consistently, strain LP-4-3-10D exhibited a 3.2-fold higher biomass than the parental strain (Fig. 3b). Collectively, these mutations appear to target genes involved in sugar transport, redox balance, and stress response, suggesting a potential genetic basis for the observed high-glucose adaptation; however, their causative roles require definitive validation through allelic replacement or other functional studies in future work. While genomic mutations provide a static map of potential adaptive changes, the association between the genome and the metabolome during the complex juice fermentation remains unclear, which might be ascribed to the lack of information on transcriptional regulation, protein abundance, and post-translational control. Therefore, integrating transcriptomics and proteomics with genome-scale metabolic modeling is required to link genotype to phenotype. Future research should focus on exploring the adaptive mechanisms by multi-omics.

Parental Lactiplantibacillus pentosus and Lactiplantibacillus plantarum were isolated from fruit-and-vegetable fermentations. Both species are listed by the European Food Safety Authorities as qualified presumption of safety (QPS) microorganisms31. Their high-glucose-adaptive derivatives, obtained by physical ARTP mutagenesis without acquisition of foreign DNA, retain the QPS framework. Comparable applications of ARTP mutagenesis in food-grade yeast have been reported without compromising safety32. Nonetheless, a full safety assessment for acquired antimicrobial resistance and toxigenic potential of the high-glucose-tolerant LAB is still required before any subsequent food application.

This study developed the high-glucose-adaptive LAB by ARTP that simultaneously reduced residual sugar by 45.69% and enriched multiple functional metabolites (indole-3-lactate, linoleate, and spermine) in goji juice fermentation. The identification of classical compatible-solute accumulation and specific phenolic glycosylation provides new insights into high-sugar adaptation mechanisms of LAB. The evolved strains can be exploited to ferment other high-sugar and precursor-rich substrates, such as tryptophan-rich banana juice and oat-milk bases. Moreover, enabled by robust high-glucose tolerance, these evolved strains can sustain high-cell-density fermentation under elevated osmolarity and function as food-grade chassis for synthesizing value-added sugar alcohols and indole-derived nutraceuticals, broadening LAB application from conventional food fermentation to industrial biomanufacturing.

Methods

Materials and reagents

Goji juice samples were sourced from Red Power Goji Co., Ltd (Ningxia, China). Reagents, including glycerol, calcium carbonate, trichloroacetic acid, sodium chloride, sodium acetate, sodium dihydrogen phosphate, sodium carbonate, sodium nitrite, aluminum nitrate, sodium hydroxide, sulfuric acid, triethylamine, and indole-3-lactate, were procured from Sigma Aldrich Co. (St. Louis, MO, USA). 2-chloro-L-phenylalanine was acquired from Aladdin (Shanghai, China). Glucose, fructose, organic acids, and amino acids standards (all > 97.0% purity), β-glucosidase, trehalose, maltose, galactitol, and gallate were also obtained from Aladdin (Shanghai, China). Folin-Ciocalteu reagent was obtained from Merck (Darmstadt, Germany). LC-MS-grade solvents, including water, methanol, ethanol, acetonitrile, formate, tetrahydrofuran, and ammonium formate, were purchased from Fisher Scientific (Loughborough, UK). All reagents and standards were prepared using HPLC-grade solvents prior to use.

Strains and culture conditions

The LAB strains used in this study were isolated and identified in laboratory, including, Lactiplantibacillus pentosus LPE-2 (LPE-2), Lactiplantibacillus plantarum LP-D (LP-D), Lactiplantibacillus plantarum LP-4-3 (LP-4-3), Lactiplantibacillus plantarum LP-1-21 (LP-1-21), Lactiplantibacillus plantarum LP-4 (LP-4), Lactiplantibacillus plantarum LP-2-41 (LP-2-41), Lactobacillus fermentum LFE-JN9 (LFE-JN9), Lactobacillus helveticus LH-HE (LH-HE), Lactobacillus harbinensis P1-1 (P1-1), Streptococcus thermophilus ST-AR3 (ST-AR3), Lacticaseibacillus rhamnosus LR (LR), Lacticaseibacillus rhamnosus JN-4 (JN-4), and Pediococcus acidilactici PL-DR1 (PL-DR1). Cultivation of LAB strains occurred in de Man, Rogosa, and Sharpe (MRS) medium at 37 °C. Overnight cultures were mixed with 50% (v/v) glycerol and stored at −80 °C. Strains were activated in MRS medium at 37 °C for 12 h with two passages.

Tolerance test under high-glucose stress

Overnight cultures (1% v/v) were inoculated into MRS medium with glucose concentrations ranging from 20 to 420 g/L and incubated at 37 °C for 24 h. The statically cultured cell suspension was vortex-mixed, and the OD600 was measured with a microplate reader (Tecan Spark, Austria). To remain within the valid range (OD600 0.2–0.8), samples exceeding the upper limit were diluted 5- or 10-fold with sterile water before analysis. The growth status of LAB strains was estimated by OD600.

Glucose and fructose concentrations in supernatants were determined by HPLC using a Bio-Rad Aminex HPX-87H Ion Exclusion column (7.8 × 300 mm; Hercules, CA, USA) equipped with a refractive index detector, at 50 °C with a mobile phase of 5 mM sulfuric acid at a flow rate of 0.5 mL/min. Sugar identities were confirmed by retention time, and concentrations were determined by calibration with a standard curve (Supplementary Table 1).

The pH of the samples was measured by a calibrated pH meter. Total acids in goji juice were examined according to the Chinese National Standard GB/T 12456-2008.

Evolution of strains for high-glucose adaptability

Strains LPE-2, LP-D, and LP-4-3, serving as parental strains, underwent mutagenesis via ARTP. Cultures in the logarithmic phase were adjusted to OD600 = 0.6 and resuspended in 50 g/L glycerol. Aliquots of 10 µL bacterial suspensions were applied to sterile metal coupons. The system was activated with a pressure regulator output of 0.1–0.15 MPa and UV irradiation for 15–20 min, followed by plasma jet treatment of the metal coupons with bacterial suspension for 30, 60, and 90 s. Subsequently, the metal coupons were vortexed in 1 mL sterile water for 5 min and diluted by a factor of 1000.

After mutagenesis, diluted bacterial suspensions were spread on MRS-agar plates containing 300 g/L glucose. Isolated colonies were inoculated into MRS medium with 20 g/L glucose and incubated for 12 h. Cultures, after recovery, were then (1% v/v) inoculated into MRS medium with 150 g/L glucose and incubated at 37 °C for 24 h. Evolved mutants were screened based on OD600 and glucose consumption.

The evolved strains and corresponding parental strains were cultured in MRS medium with 20 g/L glucose for 12 h. Following recovery, cultures (1% v/v) were inoculated into MRS medium containing 150 g/L glucose and 50 g/L sterile calcium carbonate, and incubated at 37 °C for 24 h. The high-glucose-adaptive ability of evolved strains was compared with that of their parental strains based on OD600 and glucose consumption.

Fermentation of goji juice

The high-glucose-adaptive LAB and corresponding parental LAB cultured in MRS medium were harvested by centrifugation at 1100 g for 2 min, washed with 8.5 g/L NaCl solution, and then resuspended in the same solution. The 5% (v/v) of cell suspensions were inoculated into goji juice with sterile 50 g/L calcium carbonate and statically fermented at 37 °C for 38 h in a sterile environment. Sugar consumptions were measured according to 2.3.2. Cell counts were measured by the plate count method. A 100 μL aliquot of the fermented goji juice was transferred into 900 μL sterile water, mixed thoroughly, and subjected to a 10-fold serial dilution. The resulting suspension was further diluted sequentially to 10−5, 10−6, and 10−7. From each dilution, 100 μL was spread evenly on MRS agar plates, which were then incubated statically at 37 °C for 24 h. Three biological replicates and three technical replicates were performed for each sample. A plate carrying 30–300 distinct colonies was selected, and the colony count was used to calculate the viable cell counts in the original sample. The viable counts of LAB were expressed as colony-forming units CFU/mL.

Sensory evaluations

The sensory panel was selected from thirty individuals who were proficient in the quantitative descriptive analysis (QDA) scale for fruit juice and were able to accurately reflect the relative intensity of descriptors. Seven professional panelists (four females and three males) became familiar with goji juice and fermented goji juice during three training sessions, without knowledge of the composition of ingredients, before proceeding to the final descriptive evaluation. These sessions were conducted on separate days. During the three training sessions, eleven attributes related to goji juice and fermented goji juice were determined, including aroma and taste. Each reference standard and intensity score of the eleven attributes was listed in Supplementary Table 2.

In the final descriptive sensory evaluation, each sample (including non-fermented goji juice and the fermented goji juice by different LAB) was accurately measured at 10 mL and placed in a plastic cup. All samples were presented to the panelists in random order, twice each. Each sample was labeled with a unique three-digit code. The serving temperature was 25 °C. Firstly, the panelists evaluated the samples on the aroma attributes. Subsequently, each sample was tasted to assess the taste. Evaluation scores were within the range of 0–10. The average of the QDA data results was calculated and presented using a radar chart.

The training and evaluation were organized according to the International Organization for Standardization (ISO, 1993) and conducted in a sensory laboratory that complied with the American Society for Testing and Materials criteria. The study was conducted in accordance with the ethical standards of the Declaration of Helsinki and its later amendments. Ethical approval (JNU202509RB028) was obtained from the Medical Ethics Committee of Jiangnan University. All participants provided their informed consent prior to participating in this study.

Identification of metabolites by targeted metabolomics

The free amino acid profile was analyzed following a modified protocol6. Samples (500 µL) were mixed with trichloroacetic acid, vortexed for 2 min, and centrifuged at 9500 g for 2 min to collect the supernatant. The supernatant was analyzed by HPLC on an Agilent Hypersil ODS column (5 μm, 4.0 mm × 250 mm, Agilent Inc., CA, USA) with a diode array detector at 338 nm, with L-proline detected at 262 nm. The mobile phase was composed of (A) 27.6 mM sodium acetate: triethylamine: tetrahydrofuran (500:0.11:2.5, v/v/v, pH = 7.2) and (B) 80.9 mM sodium acetate: methanol: acetonitrile (1:2:2, v/v/v, pH = 7.2). Gradient elution was as follows: 0 min, 8% B; 17 min, 50% B; 20 min, 100% B; 24 min, 0% B, with a flow rate of 1 mL/min and column temperature at 40 °C. Amino acid identities were confirmed by retention time, and concentrations were determined by calibration with a standard curve (Supplementary Table 1).

Organic acids were determined by HPLC with a modified method6. Supernatants of samples (1 mL) were prepared for HPLC analysis on a Waters Atlantis T3 column (5 μm, 4.6 mm × 250 mm) with a UV detector at 210 nm. The analysis was conducted at 40 °C with a mobile phase of 20 mM sodium dihydrogen phosphate (pH 2.7) at a flow rate of 0.7 mL/min. Organic acid identities were confirmed by retention time, and concentrations were determined by calibration with a standard curve (Supplementary Table 1).

Indole-3-lactate was detected by LC-MS with a modified method6. Samples (1 mL) were mixed with methanol (4 mL), vortexed for 2 min, incubated for 12 h, and centrifuged at 9500 g for 2 min to collect the supernatant. The supernatant was analyzed by LC-MS on an InertSustain C18 column (2.1 × 150 mm, 2 μm) (GL Science Inc., Tokyo, Japan). The mobile phase was composed of (A) 1 g/L phosphoric acid in water and (B) acetonitrile. Gradient elution was as follows: 0.1–18 min, 10%–90% B; 18.1–25 min, 90% B; 25.1–28 min, 90%–10% B; 28–40 min, 10% B, with a flow rate of 0.2 mL/min and column temperature at 30 °C. Indole-3-lactate was identified by comparing its retention time and mass spectrometry data with those of the authentic standard. Quantification was performed using an external calibration curve constructed with 2, 5, 10, 20, and 25 mg/L indole-3-lactate (Supplementary Table 1).

Identification of metabolites by non-targeted metabolomics

Supernatants from non-fermented and LAB-fermented goji juice were collected via centrifugation at 15,000 g for 10 min. Aliquots of 100 µL were mixed with 400 µL of 80% (v/v) methanol-water, vortexed for 1 min, and centrifuged at 9500 g for 10 min at 4 °C. The supernatants were then freeze-dried, and 150 µL of 2-chloro-L-phenylalanine (4 mg/L) in 80% (v/v) methanol-water was added to dissolve the samples. Samples were filtered through a 0.22 µm membrane for UPLC-MS detection. Each group included three biological replicates, and quality control (QC) samples were prepared by pooling samples to correct analytical biases and instrumental errors.

Non-volatile metabolites were analyzed using an ultra-high-performance liquid chromatography-electrospray tandem mass spectrometry (UPLC-MS/MS) system, with separation on a Vanquish UHPLC System coupled to an ACQUITY UPLC® HSS T3 column (2.1 × 100 mm, 1.8 µm) at 40 °C, at a flow rate of 0.3 mL/min and an injection volume of 2 µL. Mobile phases for positive mode LC-ESI-MS were (A1) 1 g/L formate in acetonitrile and (B1) 1 g/L formate in water, with a gradient elution of 0–1 min, 10% A1; 1–5 min, 10%–98% A1; 5–6.5 min, 98% A1; 6.5–6.6 min, 98%–10% A1; 6.6–8 min, 10% A1. For negative mode, mobile phases were (A2) acetonitrile and (B2) 5 mM ammonium formate, with a gradient elution of 0–1 min, 10% A2; 1–5 min, 10%–98% A2; 5–6.5 min, 98% A2; 6.5–6.6 min, 98%–10% A2; 6.6–8 min, 10% A2.

Metabolite detection was performed on a Q Exactive Focus mass spectrometer, operating in Full MS-ddMS2 mode. ESI settings were: sheath gas pressure at 40 arb, aux gas flow at 10 arb, spray voltage at 3.5 kV (positive) and −2.5 kV (negative), and capillary temperature at 325 °C. The MS1 scan range was m/z 100–1000, with resolving powers of 70,000 FWHM for MS1 and 17,500 FWHM for MS/MS, and three data-dependent scans per cycle.

Raw mass-spectrometric data files were converted to the mzXML format by means of the MSConvert utility contained in the Proteowizard package (v. 3.0.8789). Peak picking, filtering, and alignment were subsequently performed with the R XCMS package under the following settings: bw = 2, ppm = 15, peakwidth = c(5, 30), mzwid = 0.015, mzdiff = 0.01, and method = “centWave”, yielding a quantitative feature matrix. Systematic drift was removed by support-vector regression-based correction against quality-control (QC) samples. Only those features exhibiting a coefficient of variation (CV) < 30% across the QC injections were retained for downstream analysis. Metabolite identification was performed by searching the acquired features against the public repositories HMDB, MassBank, LipidMaps, mzCloud, and KEGG, together with the in-house standard library. The mass tolerance was set to 30 ppm. The accurate mass of the precursor ion observed in the MS spectrum was used to deduce the molecular weight. The molecular formula was then predicted on the basis of the mass deviation (ppm) and adduct information, and subsequently matched against the databases. Features for which MS/MS spectra were available were subjected to additional secondary identification by comparing their fragment-ion patterns with those stored in the above libraries.

Verification of protective effect of galactitol, maltose, and trehalose

The spot assay method referred to a recent study of osmotic stress tolerance of Gluconobacter oxydans to high glucose27. High-glucose-adaptive LAB and parental LAB in the logarithmic phase were centrifuged at 1000 g for 2 min, washed with 8.5 g/L sodium chloride, and diluted to OD600 = 0.2 with 8.5 g/L sodium chloride. Aliquots of 2 µL of bacterial suspensions were spotted onto MRS-agar plates with varying glucose concentrations (20, 150, 320, and 350 g/L) and 4 mM potential protective compounds (galactitol, maltose, or trehalose) and then incubated at 37 °C for 48 h to assess cell growth.

Determination of total phenols and total flavonoids

Supernatants (500 µL) from non-fermented and LAB-fermented goji juice were treated with 500 µL of 1 mg/L β-glucosidase at 37 °C and 220 r/min for 1 h. Samples (before and after β-glucosidase treatment) were prepared for the analysis of total phenols and flavonoids.

Total phenol concentrations were measured using the Folin-Ciocalteu method with minor adjustments33. Diluted samples (1 mL) were mixed with 2 mL of Folin-Ciocalteu reagent (1:9, v/v) and 2 mL of sodium carbonate (75 g/L), then incubated in the dark for 30 min before absorbance at 760 nm was recorded. Gallic acid solutions of 0.01, 0.02, 0.03, 0.04, and 0.05 g/L were prepared and subjected to the above reaction procedure to construct a concentration-absorbance calibration curve. Total phenolic contents of the samples were calculated as gallic acid equivalents (mg/L).

Total flavonoid concentrations were determined by the sodium nitrite-aluminum chloride colorimetric method with minor modifications34. Diluted samples (1 mL) were mixed with 0.3 mL of 5% (w/v) sodium nitrite and incubated for 10 min, followed by the addition of 1 mL of aluminum nitrate (100 g/L) and further incubation for 5 min. The reaction with 2 mL of sodium hydroxide (1 M) was finalized to a total volume of 10 mL, allowed to stand for 5 min at room temperature with periodic shaking, and the absorbance at 510 nm was measured. Rutin solutions of 0.08, 0.16, 0.24, 0.32, and 0.4 g/L were prepared and subjected to the above reaction procedure to construct a concentration-absorbance calibration curve. Total phenolic contents of the samples were calculated as rutin equivalents (mg/L).

Statistical analysis

All experiments were carried out with biological replicates for each sample. Data analysis was performed using GraphPad Prism 8.34 software (CA, USA). One-way ANOVA and Duncan’s New Multiple Range Test were applied for significance using IBM SPSS Statistics 21.0 (IBM Inc., USA), with p-value < 0.05 considered statistically significant. Principal component analysis (PCA) and orthogonal projections to latent structures discriminant analysis (OPLS-DA) were utilized to discern differences between experimental groups. The influence of each metabolite on group discrimination was quantified by combining statistical significance (p-value by Student’s t test), variable importance in projection (VIP) derived from the OPLS-DA model, and fold change between classes. Features with p-value < 0.05 and VIP > 1 were considered statistically significant and were retained to assist in the selection of differential metabolites.

Time frame of experiments

The main experiments were conducted at Jiangnan University in Wuxi, China, and the non-targeted metabolomics was finished by PANOMIX Biomedical Technical Company in Suzhou, China. The time frame of each experiment was listed as follows: the first screen of candidate strains, 1 month; the second screen of high-glucose-adaptive strains, 3 months; goji juice fermentation, 1 month; sensory evaluations, 1 month; identification and quantification of metabolites by targeted metabolomics, 1 month; identification and semi-quantification of by non-targeted metabolomics,2 months; the verification of the protective effects of galactitol, maltose, and trehalose, 2 weeks; the verification of glycosylated total phenols and flavonoids, 2 weeks.
