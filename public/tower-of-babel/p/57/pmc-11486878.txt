Exploring the interaction mechanisms between cervical carcinoma in situ and antibody-mediated immune responses through Mendelian randomization analysis
Authors: Junfei Li, Lihuang He, Qun He, Kaihong Xie, Hui Xie
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
AbstractObjectiveThis study aims to investigate the causal relationship between cervical carcinoma in situ and antibody-mediated immune responses, providing a scientific basis for the prevention and treatment of cervical carcinoma in situ.MethodsA bidirectional Mendelian Randomization (MR) approach was utilized, leveraging two Genome-Wide Association Studies (GWAS) related to cervical carcinoma in situ and antibody-mediated immune responses to collect Single Nucleotide Polymorphism (SNP) data. Multiple statistical methods, including the inverse-variance weighted (IVW) method, MR-Egger regression, weighted median, and weighted mode, were utilized. Antibody-mediated immune response-related SNPs were used as instrumental variables (IVs) for a forward MR analysis of cervical carcinoma in situ, while cervical carcinoma in situ-related SNPs served as IVs for a reverse MR analysis of antibody-mediated immune responses.ResultsThe forward MR analysis revealed significant causal associations between two SNPs, GCST90006901 (P = 0.012, OR (95%CI) = 1.167(1.034–1.317)) and GCST90006909 (P < 0.001, OR (95%CI) = 1.805(1.320–2.467)), within antibody-mediated immune responses and the occurrence of cervical carcinoma in situ. The reverse MR analysis demonstrated that cervical carcinoma in situ exerts influence on multiple SNPs associated with antibody-mediated immune responses. Specifically, GCST90006891 (P = 0.018, OR (95%CI) = 1.164(1.027–1.319)) and GCST90006894 (P = 0.048, OR (95%CI) = 1.074 (1.001–1.153)) showed positive effects, while GCST90006899 (P = 0.022, OR (95%CI) = 0.935(0.882–0.990)) and GCST90006911 (P = 0.0193, OR (95%CI) = 1.226(1.034–1.454)) exhibited distinct trends of influence.ConclusionThe Mendelian Randomization analysis indicates a clear causal relationship between antibody-mediated immune responses and the prevalence of cervical carcinoma in situ, with cervical carcinoma in situ also exerting a certain degree of influence on antibody-mediated immune responses. This finding provides important insights into the interaction mechanism between the two and suggests avenues for developing effective prevention and control strategies.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1007/s12672-024-01456-5.Keywords: Cervical carcinoma in situ, Antibody-mediated immune responses, Mendelian randomization, Genome-wide association studies

Introduction

Cervical cancer (CC) ranks fourth both for incidence and mortality among women worldwide [1]; in contrast, in China, CC ranks seventh for incidence and eighth for mortality [2]. Despite the recent positive trend of declining incidence rates in cervical squamous cell carcinoma (CSCC), thanks to rapid advancements in medical technology and intensified efforts in CC screening, an alarming reversal has surfaced: the incidence of cervical adenocarcinoma (CAC) and its precursor lesion, cervical adenocarcinoma in situ (CAIS), is on the rise [3]. This phenomenon serves as an urgent call to action for the medical community to delve deeper into its pathogenesis, early diagnosis, and effective intervention strategies.

Cervical carcinoma in situ (CCIS), as a precancerous lesion lurking within the cervical glandular epithelium, is characterized by a distinct pathological feature: the neoplastic epithelium replaces the normal tissue of the cervical canal and its internal glands [4]. However, due to its asymptomatic nature in the early stages, which often leads to delayed diagnosis, the subsequent treatment becomes more complex and risky. Fortunately, the cancerous cells in CCIS have not yet breached the basement membrane, meaning there is no invasion or metastasis, hence it is classified as a “preinvasive cancer” or “Stage 0 cancer,” offering a valuable window for early intervention [5]. Achieving precise diagnosis and effective treatment at this stage can potentially lead to cure for the majority of patients, thereby preventing the progression of the disease into more severe invasive cervical cancer.

The role of human papillomavirus (HPV) infection as the primary culprit in CC and its precursor lesions cannot be overstated. Research indicates that over 90% of patients with CCIS test positive for HPV [6], with a particularly high prevalence of high-risk HPV types (HPV18 and HPV16) [7]. Furthermore, various external factors such as organ transplantation [8], rheumatic and immunologic diseases [9], the use of immunosuppressants [10], and smoking [11] can exacerbate the risk of HPV infection and the development of CCIS. Notably, long-term use of oral contraceptives is also suspected to be associated with HPV infection and the occurrence of CCIS, further underscoring the intricate complexity of CC pathogenesis [12].

Given the pivotal role of HPV infection in driving CCIS and the potential involvement of other viral infections in CC, a thorough investigation into how antibody-mediated immune responses participate in and influence the pathogenesis of CC and its precursors has emerged as a critical research area in medicine. This endeavor not only facilitates a more comprehensive understanding of the multifaceted etiology of CC but also holds the promise of unlocking new avenues for prevention, early diagnosis, and effective treatment. However, traditional observational studies are often constrained by confounding factors, making it difficult to accurately establish direct links between exposure and disease.

To address this limitation, the study introduces the innovative approach of Mendelian randomization (MR), which simulates the random allocation of alleles during natural meiosis, effectively controlling for confounding factors and creating a research environment akin to a randomized controlled trial [13]. By leveraging genetic variants as instrumental variables (IVs), the MR method enhances the detection of causal relationships and provides robust support for the quantitative analysis of the risk association between CCIS and antibody-mediated immune responses.

The present study employs a bidirectional MR strategy, with the overarching goal of comprehensively dissecting the potential linkages between CCIS and antibody-mediated immune responses. Our endeavor aims to furnish scientific evidence and identify novel intervention targets for the prevention, early diagnosis, and personalized treatment strategies of CC. We are firmly convinced that through this research, we will take significant strides towards lifting the veil on the intricate mechanisms underlying CC pathogenesis. Ultimately, we aspire to contribute meaningfully to the reduction of CC incidence and mortality rates, thereby enhancing the health and well-being of women globally.

Materials and methods data

Data sets and study design

In our pursuit of delving deeper into the potential associations between CCIS and antibody-mediated immune responses, we have meticulously chosen the FinnGen GWAS Summary Statistics database (https://www.finngen.fi) [14] as the cornerstone of our research endeavor. FinnGen, a landmark large-scale genomic and health data research initiative in Finland, boasts a dataset of unparalleled breadth and depth, offering formidable support for our investigation. Through FinnGen’s R9 browser, we leveraged the specific phenotype code `finngen_R9_CD2_INSITU_CERVIX_UTERI_EXALLC` to precisely retrieve the GWAS dataset pertinent to CCIS. This dataset comprehensively includes detailed information on 2236 female patients with CCIS. For specific details, please refer to the Supplement Table 1.

Selection of instrumental variables

Amidst the vast expanse of data from Genome-Wide Association Studies (GWAS), we meticulously sieve through to identify genetic variants strongly associated with specific exposure factors, with P-values significantly less than 5 × 10⁻⁶, ensuring a high degree of correlation between these variants and the exposure. To harness these genetic variants for MR analysis, we rigorously adhere to three core assumptions: (1). Relevance Assumption: Firstly, we establish a robust statistical association between the SNPs and the exposure factor, ensuring that the SNPs serve as valid proxies for the exposure. (2). Independence Assumption: We further refine our selection to ensure that the chosen SNPs are independent of any confounding factors that might influence the “exposure-outcome” relationship. By setting the linkage disequilibrium parameter r2 < 0.001 and limiting the genetic distance to 10,000 kb, we maintain complete linkage equilibrium among the SNPs, ensuring their random distribution genetically and effectively mitigating potential biases from linkage disequilibrium (LD). (3). Exclusion Restriction Assumption: Although fully validating this assumption poses challenges, we strive to select SNPs that primarily influence the outcome through the exposure factor and not via alternative pathways, through meticulous biological analysis and literature review, thereby adhering to the exclusivity requirement as closely as possible.

Based on these stringent criteria, we extract SNPs from the GWAS data that fulfill the three assumptions of MR and integrate them with relevant datasets for exposure and outcome. In this process, we meticulously eliminate palindromic SNPs to negate potential biases introduced by genotyping technologies. Ultimately, we curate an optimal set of IVs for the exposure factor, comprising SNPs that are not only tightly linked to the exposure but also meet the independence and exclusion restriction criteria of MR analysis, laying a solid foundation for delving deeper into the causal relationship between exposure and outcome.

Bidirectional MR scheme

This study was carefully planned as two interrelated and complementary parts to provide an in-depth analysis of the potential causal relationship between antibody-mediated immune responses and CCIS.

Focusing on antibody-mediated immune responses as the exposure factor

In this phase, we leverage significant SNP data related to antibody-mediated immune responses from GWAS as the exposure variable, while setting the GWAS data for CCIS as the outcome variable. To comprehensively and accurately assess the causal relationship between the two, we employ multiple MR analysis methods, including Egger regression to detect potential biases, inverse-variance weighting (IVW) to provide robust effect estimates, median-weighted (MW) method, and Simple mode and Weighted mode approaches. Among them, the results from IVW serve as the primary basis, while the others function as supplementary and validating evidence to IVW. We calculate the causal effect size (β-value), odds ratio (OR), and 95% confidence interval (CI) through univariate MR analysis.

Focusing on cervical carcinoma in situ as the exposure factor

In this section, the GWAS data for CCIS are utilized as the exposure variable, while the GWAS data related to antibody-mediated immune responses become the outcome variable. This reversal serves as a validation step that not only helps us exclude potential reverse causality but also delves deeper into the intricate associations between the two. Similarly, we once again applied various MR analysis methods, including Egger regression, median-weighted method, IVW, and others, to calculate and compare the causal effect estimates obtained from different approaches. This approach ensures a more holistic understanding of the relationship between CCIS and antibody-mediated immune responses.

Sensitivity analyses

To rigorously assess the homogeneity among the IVs employed in our MR analysis, we utilized Cochran’s Q test as a detection tool. A significant P-value < 0.05 in this test indicates the presence of heterogeneity among the genetic variants (SNPs). In the event of such heterogeneity, we proceed with additional validation steps to ensure the robustness of our findings. Firstly, we applied the MR-Egger regression method, which is designed to be robust against pleiotropy—a scenario where a genetic variant influences multiple outcomes beyond the primary exposure-outcome relationship of interest. An MR-Egger P-value less than 0.05 suggests the likelihood of pleiotropy, casting doubt on the direct causality inference and indicating that the results may not be entirely reliable.

To further validate and identify the potential source of pleiotropy or heterogeneity, we employed the leave-one-out (LOO) method. This method involves sequentially excluding each SNP from the analysis and reassessing the effect estimates. This iterative process helps us identify any individual SNP that may disproportionately influence the overall results, potentially due to pleiotropic effects or outliers. By evaluating the effect of each SNP on the overall outcome, we can gain insight into the robustness of our findings and the potential influence of any specific genetic variant.

Q test (Cochran's Q) is used to detect whether there is heterogeneity among the IVs. A P-value < 0.05 indicates the existence of heterogeneity; MR-Egger regression and LOO method are used to verify the polymorphism; a P-value < 0.05 indicates the existence of polymorphism, and the results are unreliable. The rule of remaining one is to eliminate individual SNPS to determine the impact of each SNP on the result.

Result

Preliminary analysis of the causal relationship between antibody-mediated immune responses and cervical carcinoma in situ

Leveraging the robust analytical capabilities of R software, we delved deep into GWAS data pertaining to antibody-mediated immune responses, uncovering remarkable findings. Specifically, the significantly positive causal effect sizes (β) observed for GSCT90006901 and GSCT90006909 have fortified the hypothesis of a positive causal relationship between antibody-mediated immune responses and CCIS. Furthermore, these results suggest that certain specific genetic variations, while facilitating antibody-mediated immune responses, may also subtly elevate the risk of developing CCIS, shedding light on the intricate interplay between immune regulation and tumorigenesis.

On the other hand, the discovery of negative causal effect sizes (β) for GSCT90006923 and GSCT90006924 opens up a new avenue, showcasing the potential protective role of antibody-mediated immune responses within specific genetic contexts. This finding challenges conventional frameworks by indicating that enhanced antibody-mediated immune responses can, in certain scenarios, act as a formidable defense against the onset of cervical carcinoma in situ, offering fresh perspectives on prevention and treatment strategies for CC.

Preliminary analysis of the causal relationship between cervical carcinoma in situ and antibody-mediated immune responses

Through a rigorous analysis of the MR associations between GWAS data for CCIS and antibody-mediated immune responses, we have unveiled a complex and intriguing pattern of causality between the two, as depicted in Fig. 2. Specifically, the positive causal effect estimates (β-values) observed in studies GCST90006891, GCST90006894, and GCST90006911 strongly support the hypothesis of a positive causal relationship between CCIS and antibody-mediated immune responses. This finding suggests that genetic risk factors for CCIS may, to some extent, contribute to the enhancement of antibody-mediated immune responses, highlighting the immune system's proactive engagement in response to this type of cancer.

However, it is crucial to note that the results from GCST90006899 and GCST90006904 present a stark contrast, with negative causal effect β-values indicating a potential negative causal relationship between CCIS and antibody-mediated immune responses (Fig. 2). This discovery challenges conventional understanding by suggesting that, within specific genetic contexts, genetic variations associated with CCIS may inhibit or diminish antibody-mediated immune responses, thereby influencing the progression and outcomes of the disease.

Evaluation of instrumental variables and heterogeneity analysis

When delving into the statistical properties of exposure-outcome data, we utilized the R2 and F-statistic calculations to quantify the goodness-of-fit and statistical significance of each pair of datasets. The total R2 values obtained for each exposure-outcome pair reflect the model's ability to predict changes in the dependent variable, while the F-statistic, as indicators of the overall model significance, are all greater than 10 (Supplementary tables 2 and 3). This outcome conclusively demonstrates that all IVs selected are strong, exhibiting high efficacy and robustness in unraveling the causal relationship between exposure and outcome.

Further scrutinizing the statistical information in Table 1, we uncover an intriguing detail: apart from GCST90006924 (P = 0.001), GCST90006923 (P < 0.000), and GCST90006904 (P = 0.032), which achieved statistical significance in the heterogeneity test (P < 0.05), the remaining data points did not exhibit significant heterogeneity (P > 0.05). This finding suggests that, in most cases, the causal effect estimates across different exposure-outcome datasets are relatively consistent, devoid of notable differences or variations. Even more reassuringly, all datasets displayed non-significance in the evaluation of horizontal pleiotropy (P > 0.05), indicating that the selected IVs did not exert significant impacts on factors other than the target exposure, thereby enhancing the purity and credibility of our causal inference.

Moreover, in the MR-Egger regression analysis, the intercept values for all data points adhered closely to zero, a notable finding that effectively excludes potential pleiotropic influences in the overall direction, reinforcing our confidence in the direct causal link between exposure and outcome. During the examination of Table 1, we observed a notable phenomenon: with the exception of the SNPs (whose heterogeneity P-values were less than 0.05, the I2_GX values for the remaining SNPs were relatively low. This further strengthens the credibility of our study, as lower I2_GX values generally indicate lower heterogeneity among different studies or estimates, suggesting relatively consistent effect estimates and thereby reducing the risk of bias arising from inter-study differences. Additionally, the LOO sensitivity analysis (Fig. 3) revealed that upon the sequential exclusion of each SNP, the error bars of the overall results remained relatively stable without significant fluctuations, further confirming the independence of individual SNPs in elucidating causal relationships and the robustness of the overall findings. This comprehensive assessment not only validates the reliability of our research conclusions but also underscores the formidable explanatory power of the selected IVs.

Discuss

This study, leveraging extensive public databases of GWAS, employed a two-sample bidirectional MR methodology to delve into the potential causal link between antibody-mediated immune responses and CCIS. Through rigorous data analysis and validation, the research revealed that, at the genetic level, specific antibody-mediated immune responses, identified as GCST90006901 and GCST90006909, are significant risk factors for the development of CCIS. This finding not only underscores the potential role of immune system dysfunction in the pathogenesis of CCIS but also offers a fresh perspective on the immunopathological mechanisms of cancer. Notably, in the reverse MR analysis, the study also observed abnormal expression of multiple genetic variations (GCST90006891, GCST90006894, GCST90006899, and GCST90006911) related to antibody-mediated immune responses in patients with CCIS. This observation further strengthens the complex interplay between disease and immune responses, suggesting that the onset of CCIS may not only impact local tissues but also trigger systemic alterations in immune responses.

In our current research endeavor, we have delved into the potential associations between multiple viral infections and the progression of CCIS, with a particular focus on two indicators: GCST90006901 (Epstein-Barr virus ZEBRA antibody levels) and GCST90006909 (Human herpes virus 7 U14 antibody levels). Our findings reveal a significant correlation between the elevation of these antibody levels and the development of CCIS. Although the specific molecular mechanisms remain elusive, existing research has outlined several plausible scientific scenarios.

Epstein-Barr virus (EBV), a prevalent γ-herpesvirus infecting nearly 90% of the global population, displays complex infection patterns [16]. Its preferential targeting of B lymphocytes, along with its ability to infiltrate epithelial cells, T cells, and natural killer (NK) cells, underscores its wide-ranging influence [17]. While studies suggest that the ZEBRA protein, a key regulator of the EBV lifecycle, may independently initiate the transition from latency to lytic phase, potentially aiding in viral reactivation and dissemination [18], the precise mechanisms underlying these processes remain under investigation. Similarly, the reported synergy between ZEBRA-induced viral factors and human IL-10 in evading immune detection and elimination of infected cells [19] involves complex interactions that are not fully elucidated. This proposed immune evasion strategy, which could involve disruption of immune response mechanisms, attenuation of NK/NKT cell lytic activity, and suppression of IFNγ-mediated signaling pathways, is intriguing but requires further validation [20, 21]. While these potential mechanisms may contribute to the long-term persistence of EBV in the host, their direct impact on the development of CCIS remains speculative and warrants cautious interpretation.

On the other hand, elevated U14 antibody levels of Human Herpesvirus 7 (HHV-7) (GCST90006909) indicate an intensified immune response to the virus. Given HHV-7's high prevalence and potential pathogenicity, particularly in immunocompromised individuals such as bone marrow or organ transplant recipients and HIV-infected individuals, its infection characteristics deserve attention [22–25]. HHV-7 primarily targets T-cells, disrupting the expression of HLA molecules and compromising T-cell recognition functions, potentially contributing to the formation of a concealed “viral reservoir” linked to precancerous changes in cervical cells [26, 27]. However, the direct link between HHV-7 and CCIS (assuming it stands for cervical cancer in situ or a similar condition, though not explicitly stated in the original text) remains speculative. This study explores the potential association between Chlamydia trachomatis tarp-D F1 antibody levels (GCST90006891) and CCIS. Despite limited direct evidence, the known correlation between Chlamydia trachomatis infection and cervical health, as well as its overlap with risk factors for cervical cancer, suggests that this relationship merits further investigation [28]. Analysis of Cytomegalovirus (CMV) pp28 antibody levels (GCST90006894) indicates intersections with HPV in the context of cervical cancer (CC). CMV may indirectly promote cervical cancer by modulating immune functions, stimulating cell proliferation, or cooperating with HPV [29]. The assessment of EBV EBNA-1 (GCST90006899) and HHV-6 IE1A (GCST90006911) antibody levels underscores their importance as biomarkers of immune responses to viral infections. While the roles of these viruses in the initiation and progression of CCIS are still under investigation, caution should be exercised in interpreting these results.

The study ingeniously embraced the MR strategy, leveraging SNPs as precise IVs, a move that significantly distinguishes it from traditional epidemiological research. By effectively sidestepping the intricate web of confounding factors, the study ensures the purity and precision of its conclusions. Moreover, it adeptly capitalized on the vast resources of public GWAS databases, amplifying the sample size, bolstering statistical power, and dramatically curtailing research timelines and financial expenditures. Despite the inherent heterogeneity among the IVs in both forward and reverse MR analyses, the research team adeptly addressed these challenges through rigorous screening and exclusion criteria, maintaining the clarity of the analytical path and reinforcing the reliability of the findings. Notably, the adoption of the IVW random-effects model not only enhances the adaptability of the results to intricate genetic structures but also fortifies the robustness and credibility of the analytical conclusions, offering the scientific community a stronger and more compelling evidence base.

However, it is imperative to acknowledge several limitations inherent in this approach. Firstly, the confinement of MR analyses to a European population highlights the urgent need for multi-ethnic and global studies. Such endeavors would mitigate ethnic biases and enhance the generalizability of causal insights across diverse populations. By embracing a truly global perspective, we can better capture the complexity and variability of human genetics, leading to more robust and universally applicable findings. Secondly, the potential for confounding biases stemming from genetic polymorphisms emphasizes the importance of adopting rigorous statistical methods and conducting validation studies. Future experiments should incorporate advanced techniques like multi-omics integration, sensitivity analyses, and multi-variant testing to disentangle the true causal signals from spurious associations. These efforts will strengthen the precision and reliability of MR-derived causal conclusions. Moreover, the intimate link between the efficacy of instrumental variables (IVs) and the sample size of GWAS underscores the critical role of large-scale studies in uncovering a wider array of genetic variants. The pursuit of even larger GWAS, leveraging the ever-growing repositories of genetic and phenotypic data, is imperative to identify more powerful IVs and refine our causal models. Crucially, the importance of future experiments cannot be overstated. They serve as the cornerstone for validating and refining the insights gained from MR analyses. By experimentally testing the functional relevance of genetic variants and their impact on biological pathways and disease outcomes, we can bridge the gap between statistical associations and mechanistic understanding.

Conclusion

In summary, while this study innovatively posits CCIS and antibody immune responses as exposure factors and employs a bidirectional MR analysis to explore their potential causal relationship, it is crucial to explicitly acknowledge the limitations of our findings and emphasize the necessity for future experimental validation. Our results, which identify GCST90006901 and GCST90006909 as significant risk factors for CCIS onset, and reveal notable genotype variations in GCST90006891, GCST90006894, and GCST90006899 among CCIS patients, offer valuable insights into the pathological mechanisms of CCIS. However, it is imperative to note that these findings are preliminary and require rigorous in vitro and in vivo experiments for further validation. Specifically, the preventive and therapeutic potential of targeting GCST90006901 and GCST90006909, and their ability to alleviate symptoms in CCIS patients, remain unproven and necessitate well-designed interventional trials. The limitations of our study, including its focus on a specific population, potential confounding biases from genetic polymorphisms, and the reliance on GWAS sample size for IV efficacy, underscore the need for broader, more comprehensive investigations. Future experiments will not only help to unravel the underlying biological mechanisms at play but also provide a solid scientific foundation for the development of targeted treatment strategies for CCIS. Such studies will significantly enhance our understanding of the disease's essence and potentially lead to improved therapeutic options, bringing much-needed relief to CCIS patients. By acknowledging these limitations and emphasizing the importance of future validation, we aim to strengthen the persuasiveness and impact of our findings.
