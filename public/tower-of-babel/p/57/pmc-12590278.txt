Design and systematic evaluation of novel chemical modification patterns enable the identification of a potent siRNA targeting PCSK9
Authors: Ze-Ao Huang, Shuo Yang, Guang-Shen Ji, Zhong-Cai Gao, Zhen-Min Li, Jie Wang, Fei Yu, Geng-Shen Song
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractFully modified small interfering RNAs (siRNAs) have demonstrated remarkable advantages in medicine development since they enable the knockdown of the majority of disease-related proteins. However, unmodified siRNAs usually cannot be used for medications due to their biochemical instability. siRNA modification patterns are engineered to enhance both structural stability and pharmacodynamic efficacy, thus being vital in the development of potent siRNA drugs. However, most modification patterns of approved or clinically investigated siRNAs are protected by patents, significantly restricting their broader application. Therefore, the development of proprietary modification patterns has been essential for siRNA therapeutics companies. Here, based on a comprehensive analysis of existing studies, we designed novel modification patterns by altering 2′-F moiety positions at critical sites on sense or antisense strands to enhance siRNA efficacy. Experiments indicate that these modification patterns improve the in vitro activity of an approved siRNA drug targeting proprotein convertase subtilisin/kexin type 9 (PCSK9) mRNA, which lowers plasma low-density lipoprotein cholesterol (LDL-C) levels. Furthermore, by optimizing sequence modification combinations, we identified a promising siRNA drug (YK1597-DV29PG5) targeting PCSK9, which has advanced to clinical trials with favorable preclinical efficacy and safety. This study offers valuable insights for designing novel, efficient modification patterns for siRNA drugs.Keywords: small interfering RNAs, chemical modification patterns, PCSK9, pharmacodynamics, safety assessment

Introduction

The use of small interfering RNA (siRNA) has proven to be an effective approach for inhibiting the expression of target proteins by suppressing their corresponding mRNA.1,2,3,4,5 It is well established that approximately 1.5% of the human genome encodes around 20,000 proteins, among which 2,000 to 3,000 are estimated to be disease associated; thus, their corresponding mRNAs are potentially targetable by siRNA therapeutics.6 This highlights the significant advantages of siRNA therapeutics over approved traditional medicines, which target no more than 700 proteins.6 However, existing studies have shown that due to the biochemical instability to nucleases, unmodified siRNAs can be too vulnerable to be used in medicines to treat diseases.7 Therefore, chemically modified nucleotides were incorporated into siRNAs to enhance their stability and further improve efficiencies.8,9,10 Additional benefits also exist; for example, replacing the 2′-hydroxyl of each residue with 2ʹ-O-methyl (2′-OMe) or other groups could largely reduce innate immune response.11,12 Moreover, appropriate phosphorothioate modifications could enhance the cellular uptake of siRNA by adjusting its binding with proteins.13

With the remarkable development of chemical modification techniques over the past decade, a series of heavily modified siRNA medicines have been developed and subsequently approved by the US Food and Drug Administration (FDA). These achievements strongly highlight the significance of these chemical modifications in the realm of siRNA-based therapeutics.10 Notably, chemical modification patterns are typically regarded as the core technology of siRNA therapeutic companies and are thus typically safeguarded by stringent intellectual property protections to prevent their unauthorized use by competing entities.14 As a result, the developing effective chemical modification patterns has become a top priority for most biotech companies which focus on siRNA therapy.

It is well established that siRNAs typically consist of approximately 40 nucleotides. Notably, 2ʹ-fluoro (2′-F) and 2′-OMe are the two most common chemical modifications. Potential combinations of these modifications at each position along a sequence render the optimization of their placement a highly intricate and challenging task. However, previous studies have shown that for siRNAs targeting different genes, specific positions—including positions 2, 6, 14, and 16 of the antisense strands (AS) and positions 7, 9, and 11 of the sense strands (SS)—prefer less bulky residues.15,16,17 This finding has simplified siRNA modification design and enabled the efficient, rational design of diverse siRNA drug “scaffolds.” Based on such discoveries, Alnylam was the first to systematically evaluate the modification preference at most siRNA sites.17 Subsequently, by leveraging the enhanced stabilization chemistry (ESC) platform, Alnylam developed its proprietary design variants (DV) modification patterns.17 The application of such modification patterns has been instrumental in the highly successful design of givosiran, lumasiran, inclisiran, and vutrisiran.18 This success ultimately led to their approval by the US FDA. Among them, inclisiran has exerted a profound influence on the field of oligonucleotide therapeutics. Specifically designed to target the mRNA of proprotein convertase subtilisin/kexin type 9 (PCSK9),19 it effectively treats individuals with elevated plasma low-density lipoprotein cholesterol (LDL-C) levels.20 In 2024 alone, inclisiran had achieved an impressive revenue of $754 million, reflecting a year-on-year growth of 114%.21 In the foreseeable future, siRNA drugs such as inclisiran will become an important supplement to statin drugs in LDL-C-lowering therapies.22 Currently, statin drugs are used by more than 145 million people worldwide.23

The triumph of inclisiran vividly illustrates the promising future of siRNA drugs in managing chronic diseases, suggesting that oligonucleotide therapeutics could revolutionize the treatment landscape for patients with long-term health conditions.24 Currently, numerous siRNA candidates have been investigated in preclinical and clinical trials, achieving significant progress.25 These studies covered a range of diseases, including chronic hepatitis B, dyslipidemia, and hypertension.26 With the rapid advancement of siRNA drug research, the rational design and optimization of novel modification patterns have emerged as critical determinants for unlocking the full therapeutic potential of these next-generation therapeutics.

In this study, we have designed five novel modification patterns (DV25–29; Figure 1A) and applied them to the sequence of an approved siRNA drug, which was noted as the positive control (PC) in our research. In vitro functional assays evaluated their gene-silencing efficacy. Through the systematic optimization of modification patterns and oligonucleotide sequences, we identified the potent siRNA agent YK1597-DV29PG5 (Figure 5), which targets PCSK9 mRNA and demonstrates excellent preclinical pharmacology efficacy and safety profiles. This agent has obtained investigational new drug approvals from both the US FDA and the National Medical Products Administration of China and is currently undergoing a phase 1 clinical trial in China. Our findings offer valuable insights for developing novel, efficient modification strategies for siRNA-based therapeutics.

Results

Chemical modification pattern designs and their in vitro evaluation

Although 2ʹ-OMe and 2ʹ-F are two of the most common modifications used to significantly increase the stability of siRNAs and subsequently enhance their efficacy in inhibiting target mRNA,27,28 they differ in potency. It has been found that the more 2′-OMe is added, the better the RNA stability benefits.17 Whereas certain key positions in both the SSs and ASs of siRNA prefer 2′-F nucleotide, the substitution of those positions with 2′-OMe can significantly impair siRNA efficiency. Alnylam has reported several modification patterns that balance both stability and efficiency. Their modification patterns have been applied to their siRNA drugs, such as inclisiran and givosiran.10,29

Here, we aim to design novel modification patterns with superior performance. Since previous reports did not clearly reveal the effects of 2′-F nucleotide modification at positions 3, 4, and 5 of the AS or positions 16 and 17 (separately) of the SS (5′ to 3′), and because the proposed preference for position 10 of the SS was inconsistent with its actual application in Alnylam drugs such as inclisiran and givosiran,10,29 we designed modification patterns DV25–29 (Figure 1A). These patterns were intended to evaluate the performance of 2′-F nucleotides at the specific positions mentioned above. Previous research by Alnylam has shown that, in addition to positions 2, 6, 14, and 16, 2′-F may also be preferred on positions 3, 4, and 5 of ASs.15 A recent study conducted by Davis et al. also designed a modification pattern with 2′-F at positions 4 and 5, similar to that of inclisiran.10,15 Givosiran’s design included an AS with 2′-F at positions 3 and 4.29 Based on these findings, we have designed our modification pattern DV25∼29 by adjusting the 2′-F modification at positions 3, 4, and 5 of the AS. For the SS, Alnylam’s research has shown that 2′-F at position 16 alone has a negative effect on the efficacy of siRNA, while the use of 2′-F at positions 16 and 17 together has slightly improved its inhibition efficiency. This finding suggests that 2′-F on position 17 alone may benefit the efficacy of siRNAs.17 Therefore, SSs with 2′-F on 17th residues are designed for all of our modification patterns (DV25–29). This preference for a less bulky residue on the equivalent position 17 of SSs was also supported by a systematic study on “bulky” residues at all siRNA positions.16 Furthermore, although previous research has claimed that position 10 of SSs (5′ to 3′) prefers 2′-F residues, neither inclisiran nor givosiran adopted this modification, which suggests that this modification may not benefit siRNA efficacy.10,29 Therefore, we have designed modification patterns DV28 and DV29 without using 2′-F at position 10 of SSs. Notably, these novel modification patterns have been successfully used for the development of the potent siRNAs targeting angiotensinogen mRNA in our recent report,26 in which we disclosed their development details.

Modification patterns DV25–29 were incorporated into the sequence of an approved siRNA agent (PC) to create five chemically modified siRNAs (Figure 1B). This PC targets PCSK9 mRNA to inhibit PCSK9 in circulation, thereby reducing both LDL-C levels and the incidence of cardiovascular events.4 Both PC-ESC (incorporated with the modification pattern ESC, which was developed by Alnylam) and PC served positive controls. All these siRNAs were not conjugated with the N-acetylgalactosamine (GalNAc) moiety, which was used for liver-targeted delivery. Phosphorothioate was incorporated at the ends of both ASs and SSs, as previously described.30

Next, an in vitro test was conducted using the Hep3B cell line to evaluate the efficacy of the new modification patterns. After transfection with different siRNAs (see Figure 1B), the level of PCSK9 mRNA was measured via qPCR. The results demonstrated that PCs with its own modification pattern and with the ESC pattern exhibited inhibition rates of 80.53% and 80.06% on PCSK9 mRNA, respectively, whereas the same sequence with the new modification patterns DV26, DV28, and DV29 showed significantly improved inhibitory rates of 87.71%, 90.50%, and 88.15% (p < 0.05, Figures 1B; Table 1) respectively. Additionally, the siRNAs adopting patterns DV25 and DV27 also showed superior inhibitory rates of 84.41% and 86.91% (Table 1). These results indicated that the new modification patterns DV25–29 exhibit superior—or at least equivalent—performance when compared to the patterns of ESC and the PC.

Basic siRNA sequence design and evaluation

To develop siRNAs with distinct sequences from clinically used PC, we designed and identified potent basic sequences following a similar protocol to that disclosed in our recent report.26 All siRNAs featured an ESC pattern (Figure 1) and are labeled with “ESC” in their names (Figure 2; Table 2). In vitro qPCR testing was conducted in Hep3B cell lines to evaluate their efficacy. Among them, siRNAs YK1596-ESC, YK1597-ESC, and YK1599-ESC outperformed PC-ESC, with average inhibitory rates of PCSK9 mRNA at 87.21%, 87.59%, and 85.94%, respectively, when compared to 80.53% for PC-ESC. The inferior siRNAs, YK1588-ESC and YK1598-ESC, exhibited inhibitory rates of 60.76% and 73.58%, respectively.

Screening of combinations of sequences and modification patterns

With potentially superior modifications (DV25–29; Table 1) and several newly designed good or moderate basic sequences (Table 2), we then investigated the combinations of sequences and modification patterns. Notably, the basic sequences of YK1588-ESC and YK1598-ESC were selected to determine whether well-designed modification patterns could significantly improve the efficacy of inferior sequences. The new modification patterns DV25–29 were incorporated into the five new sequences mentioned above, thereby generating a total of 25 new siRNAs. The PC sequence, with its inherent modification patterns (Figure 3A), served as the PC.

A subsequent round of in vitro testing in Hep3B cell lines at 50 pM was conducted (see results in Figure 3A; Table S1). When substituted with its own modification patterns, PC did not exhibit improved efficacy when compared to its ESC counterpart (PC-ESC). Similar to this result, sequences with sufficiently good efficacy, such as YK1596-ESC, YK1597-ESC, and YK1599-ESC, showed only limited efficacy improvements after incorporating the new modification patterns. Among these, the most notable improvement (4.83%, p < 0.01) occurred in sequence of YK1596-ESC when modified with the new pattern DV29, while a 4.91% (p > 0.05) decrease in efficacy was observed in sequence of YK1597-ESC upon incorporation of the new pattern DV25.

Notably, substantial increases in efficacy were observed for the inferior sequences of YK1588-ESC and YK1598-ESC. Specifically, YK1588-ESC exhibited significant improvements of 9.86% (p < 0.01) and 13.34% (p < 0.01) when modified with the new patterns DV28 and DV29, respectively. Moreover, YK1598-ESC showed significant improvements of 8.22% (p < 0.05), 8.10% (p < 0.05), 8.16% (p < 0.05), and 10.61% (p < 0.01) when modified with the new patterns DV26, DV27, DV28, and DV29, respectively.

In light of the aforementioned results, all five newly designed modification patterns were able to maintain or enhance the efficacy of the superior sequences YK1596-ESC, YK1597-ESC, and YK1598-ESC. For each superior sequence, two modified variants with the highest efficacy were selected (Table S1, bolded sequences) and subsequently conjugated with the liver-targeting moiety GalNAc cluster G5 for further in vivo investigation.

In vivo studies in PCSK9 transgenic mice

To assess the ability of our newly modified siRNAs to reduce serum PCSK9 levels, in vivo experiments were performed using a humanized PCSK9 knockin mouse model (hPCSK9-KI). The clinically used PC, conjugated with its GalNAc cluster L96, served as the PC in this case. The mice were administered a single subcutaneous (s.c.) injection (n = 6 per group) at a dose of 6 mg/kg. Serum PCSK9 levels were measured by ELISA at 3 days pre-dosing and 7, 14, 21, 28, and 35 days post-dosing.

Although these siRNAs demonstrated comparably strong in vitro efficacy, their GalNAc cluster (G5) conjugates exhibited strikingly different in vivo performance (Table S1; Figure 3). Among them, YK1599-DV27G5 showed negligible inhibition of PCSK9 (Figure 3D). Moreover, YK1596-DV27G5, YK1596-DV28G5, and YK1599-DV25G5 achieved maximal inhibitory rates comparable to PC but were followed by rapid rebound effects (Figures 3B and 3D). In contrast, YK1597-DV27G5 and YK1597-DV29G5 displayed significantly better efficacy persistence. Notably, YK1597-DV29G5 maintained comparable efficacy and duration to PC throughout the entire observation period (Figure 3C). Specifically, PC demonstrated a maximal inhibitory rate of 94.1% on day 14, with an endpoint inhibitory rate of 68.7% on day 35. In parallel, YK1597-DV29G5 exhibited a maximal inhibitory rate of 89.7% on day 14 and an endpoint inhibitory rate of 67.6% on day 35.

Incorporation of 5′-E-vinyl phosphonate to enhance in vivo efficacy

Previous reports have established that the 5′-phosphate moiety of the siRNA guide strand is critical for RNA-induced silencing complex (RISC) recognition.31,32 Notably, E-vinyl phosphonate (E-VP) modifications can improve the in vivo efficacy of GalNAc-conjugated siRNAs by enhancing their loading efficiency into the RISC.33 To further optimize in vivo performance, E-VP modifications (denoted by the suffix “P” in their nomenclature) were introduced at the 5′-end of the ASs of YK1597-DV27G5 and YK1597-DV29G5, thereby generating the new siRNAs YK1597-DV27PG5 and YK1597-DV29PG5.

A subsequent in vivo screening was performed in a humanized PCSK9 knockin mouse model, with the mice receiving a single s.c. injection at a dose of 2 mg/kg (n = 8 per group). Serum PCSK9 levels were measured by ELISA on day 3 pre-dosing and on days 7, 14, 21, 28, and 35 post-dosing (Figure 4). At the lower dose of 2 mg/kg, YK1597-DV27PG5 and YK1597-DV29PG5 maintained comparable efficacy (Figure 4A), being consistent with results observed at the 6 mg/kg dose (Figure 3C). As hypothesized, both siRNAs exhibited significantly improved efficacy when compared to their non-5′-E-VP counterparts. They achieved maximal inhibitory rates of 88.9% (YK1597-DV27PG5) and 87.4% (YK1597-DV29PG5) on day 21, with endpoint inhibitory rates of 63.4% and 63.6%, respectively, on day 35. Correspondingly, relative mRNA expression levels were reduced to 0.733 (YK1597-DV27PG5) and 0.489 (YK1597-DV29PG5) (Figure 4B). In contrast, PC demonstrated a maximal inhibitory rate of 74.8% on day 21 and an endpoint inhibitory rate of 44.7% on day 35, which were statistically inferior to those of both YK1597-DV27PG5 (p < 0.001) and YK1597-DV29PG5 (p < 0.01).

Given the comparable efficacy of YK1597-DV27PG5 and YK1597-DV29PG5 in inhibiting PCSK9 in humanized PCSK9 knockin mice, selecting a preclinical candidate between these two agents remained challenging. To resolve this, a pharmacodynamics evaluation was performed in healthy cynomolgus monkeys. Male monkeys (n = 2 per group) received a single s.c. injection at a dose of 6 mg/kg. Plasma PCSK9 and serum LDL-C levels were measured by ELISA and a clinical chemistry analyzer, respectively, on day 10 pre-dosing and every 7 days up to 84 days post-dosing (Figure 4C). The maximal PCSK9 inhibitory rates by YK1597-DV27PG5 and YK1597-DV29PG5 were 89.6% and 89.3%, respectively, while the terminal inhibitory rates were 65.0% and 65.5%, respectively. Nevertheless, the inhibitory rate of LDL-C, as the main curative effect index, exhibited an increase from YK1597-DV27PG5 to YK1597-DV29PG5, with maximal inhibitory rates of 63.5% and 85.0%, as well as terminal inhibitory rates of 57.0% and 74.0%. Notably, YK1597-DV29PG5 exhibited superior efficacy in lowering LDL-C levels when compared to YK1597-DV27PG5. Consequently, YK1597-DV29PG5 was confirmed as the preclinical candidate and advanced to further efficacy and safety profile studies.

Efficacy of the preclinical candidate siRNA agent in cynomolgus monkeys

In this study, 24 cynomolgus monkeys (12 males and 12 females) were randomly assigned into four groups (three males and three females per group) based on plasma PCSK9 protein levels at 3 days pre-dosing: a vehicle control group and YK1597-DV29PG5 low-dose (3 mg/kg), middle-dose (6 mg/kg), and high-dose (10 mg/kg) groups. Animals received a single s.c. injection of YK1597-DV29PG5 or vehicle at the dorsal neck. The results demonstrate that YK1597-DV29PG5 (3, 6, 10 mg/kg) significantly reduced plasma PCSK9 protein levels in healthy cynomolgus monkeys in a dose-dependent manner (Figures 5A and 5B), with no statistically significant differences between groups. Concomitant with PCSK9 inhibition, serum LDL-C and total cholesterol (TC) levels were also significantly reduced (Figures 5C–5F). At the 6 mg/kg dose, YK1597-DV29PG5 reduced serum LDL-C and TC levels with maximal inhibitory rates of 56.0% and 33.3% and endpoint (day 182) inhibitory rates of 25.91% and 18.5%, respectively.

Safety assessment

The toxicity profile of YK1597-DV29PG5 was evaluated in cynomolgus monkeys. In this study, 40 healthy cynomolgus monkeys were randomly allocated into four groups: a vehicle control group (received 0.9% NaCl injection) and low-dose (30 mg/kg), middle-dose (100 mg/kg), and high-dose (300 mg/kg) YK1597-DV29PG5 groups (n = 5 per sex in each group). All of the groups were administered by s.c. once every 2 weeks for 4 consecutive weeks (three doses in total; i.e., D1, D15, and D29), followed by an 8-week recovery period. The first dosing day is denoted as D1, and the end of the recovery period is D85. Histopathological examinations were performed on six animals (three per sex) from each dosing group at the end of the dosing period (D30) and on four animals (two per sex) at the end of the recovery period (D85; Tables S2 and S3). There were no test article-related deaths, moribundity, or other significant toxicities in any YK1597-DV29PG5 groups. No test article-related toxicologically significant changes were noted in clinical observation, body weight, food consumption, ophthalmology examination, hematology, coagulation, urinalysis, lymphocyte/lymphocyte subsets, complements, immunoglobulin, cytokines, gross necropsy, or organ weight/ratios in any dose groups. Pathological findings included minimal-to-mild vacuolation and basophilia in macrophage cytoplasm within the sinuses of lymph nodes at multiple sites, minimal-to-mild mixed cell inflammation in subcutis of the administration site, and minimal basophilia in Kupffer cell cytoplasm of the liver (Figure 6). These pathology findings were considered typical for siRNA-GalNAc conjugates.34 All of the aforementioned pathological alterations had partially or completely reversed during the recovery period (Tables S2 and S3). The no observed adverse effect level (NOAEL) for s.c. administration of YK1597-DV29PG5 in cynomolgus monkeys was identified to be 300 mg/kg.

Discussion

The chemical modifications to siRNAs significantly affect their efficacy and safety through multiple mechanisms, including enhancing sequence stability against nucleases, regulating base-pairing strength to reduce off-target effects while maintaining on-target activity, and altering pharmacokinetics for improved target tissue distribution.29 As typical chemical modifications of siRNAs, 2′-F and 2′-OMe nucleosides can be introduced at any position in siRNA sequences to regulate on-target efficacy and off-target effects.27,28 However, typical siRNAs, which consist of over 40 nucleotides, allow the design of over 100 million modification patterns. Although modifications often exhibit positional preferences, particularly in the seed region (positions 2–8) of the ASs, we have shown that reported modification patterns are not universally preferred for all sequences. Therefore, better-performing variants can be designed.

Our study focuses on positions 3–5 of the ASs and 16–17 of the SSs, which were underexplored in prior research.10,17 As shown in Figure 1A, new modification patterns (DV25–29) were designed. Compared with the reported ESC pattern, these new patterns had eliminated the 2′-F modifications at AS positions 8, 10, 18, 20, and 22 and SS positions 1, 3, 5, 13, 15, 19, and 21 (numbered from the 5′ to 3′ end). Consequently, the total number of 2′-F modifications in SSs for DV25–29 was reduced to five or fewer, compared to 12 in the ESC pattern, whereas the total 2′-F modifications in ASs were reduced from nine to six or fewer. Following their application to the PC sequence, these new patterns were evaluated in vitro using Hep3B cells, while the PC itself and an siRNA with the sequence of the PC but modified with the ESC pattern served as positive controls. All new patterns exhibited comparable or superior performance to that of the PC (Figure 1B). Among them, DV26, DV28, and DV29 demonstrated significantly improved efficacy, with PCSK9 inhibitory rates of 87.71%, 90.50%, and 88.15%, respectively (p < 0.05, Table 1). These findings indicate that reducing 2′-F modifications in SSs is critical. Compared to other new patterns with six 2′-F modifications in AS, DV25 (with four 2′-F modifications) showed relatively inferior performance, suggesting that the further reduction of 2′-F modifications in ASs may be detrimental to siRNA efficacy.

Using the methods we previously disclosed,26 several potent basic sequences targeting PCSK9 were readily obtained (Table 2). The incorporation of new modification patterns (DV25–29) has led to the generation of 25 new siRNAs, which were tested in vitro using the Hep3B cell line (Figure 3). The results showed that for highly potent siRNAs (e.g., YK1596-ESC, YK1597-ESC, and YK1599-ESC), only limited efficacy improvements were achieved with the new modification patterns, whereas more significant enhancements were noted for inferior sequences (e.g., YK1588-ESC, YK1598-ESC; Figures 2 and 3; Tables 2 and S1). Notably, even with their best modification patterns, inferior sequences did not outperform ESC-modified siRNAs with highly potent basic sequences. These findings highlight that prioritizing the potency of basic sequences is more critical, while effective modification patterns can further enhance siRNA activity or provide stable, robust target silencing effects.

Certain potent modified siRNAs (Table S1, bolded) were conjugated with GalNAc cluster G5 to generate six new siRNA-GalNAc conjugates, and the in vivo efficacies of these new conjugates were evaluated in a humanized PCSK9 knockin mouse model (Figure 3). Notably, siRNAs demonstrating significantly superior in vitro performance when compared to the PC did not consistently exhibit equivalent superiority in in vivo efficacy. For example, YK1596-DV27, YK1596-DV28, and YK1599-DV25 showed 9.95%, 11.98%, and 10.06% superior in vitro inhibitory rates vs. the PC, respectively. However, when tested in transgenic mice, their GalNAc-conjugated counterparts (YK1596-DV27G5, YK1596-DV28G5, and YK1599-DV25G5) exhibited target inhibitory rates that rebounded above baseline by day 35 post-dosing, whereas the PC maintained a 79% inhibitory rate (Figures 3B and 3D). These results highlight the critical need for systemic in vivo screening of candidate siRNAs due to significant discrepancies between in vitro and in vivo performance.

Moreover, minor alterations in the AS seed region proved detrimental to specific siRNAs. For instance, substituting 2′-F modifications at AS positions 4–5 in DV27 with 2′-OMe (generating pattern DV25) resulted in negligible target inhibition for YK1599-DV25G5 when compared to YK1599-DV27G5 (Figure 3D). Similarly, subtle changes in the SS—such as removing the 2′-F modification from SS position 10 in DV27—significantly delayed the onset of action when compared to the inhibition of YK1597-DV27G5 with YK1597-DV29G5. Collectively, these findings underscore the complex relationship between siRNA sequences and modification patterns. These results are consistent with our conclusion that minor alterations can have significant influences on efficiencies of siRNAs and further corroborate the findings in the previous report, noting that the modification pattern known as “2′-OMe-rich scaffolds” with 2′-F at positions 2, 4, 5, and 6 of the AS has also retained the inhibition efficiency of siRNA.15 Additionally, our findings on 2′-F at position 17 of the SS are also consistent with another study by O’Reilly et al., although their study did not explicitly analyze the modification pattern of 2′-F and 2′-OMe.16 By systematically assessing “bulk” residues at each position of both strands of siRNAs, they concluded that the bulky 2′-MOE (2′-methoxyethyl) residues are less preferred at position 5 of the “passenger strand” (counted in the 3′-5′ direction).16 This corresponds to position 17 (counted 5′-3′) in our 21-residue SS, where the smaller 2′-F modification is used instead. Additionally, they demonstrated that bulky 2′-MOE residues are also disfavored at positions 2 and 14 of the AS. This is consistent with our use of the 2′-F modification at positions 2, 6, 14, and 16 of ASs across all modification patterns.

The E-VP modification at the 5′ end of the AS is critical for enhancing siRNA efficacy.33 YK1597-DV27PG5 and YK1597-DV29PG5, which incorporated this modification, demonstrated over 15% improvement in in vivo target inhibitory rates (Figure 4A) and significantly outperformed the PC (p < 0.01). Notably, while both conjugates showed comparable serum PCSK9 protein inhibition in cynomolgus monkeys, YK1597-DV29PG5 exhibited LDL-C-lowering effects with faster onset and greater robustness, whereas YK1597-DV27PG5 showed delayed action (Figure 4C). The extended efficacy evaluations in cynomolgus monkeys showed that YK1597-DV29PG5 achieved a significant PCSK9 protein inhibition effect with LDL-C and remarkable TC reduction (Figure 5) across all dosing groups. In preclinical safety assessments, YK1597-DV29PG5 exhibited pathological alterations typical of siRNA-GalNAc conjugates,34 with a NOAEL of 300 mg/kg. Combined with the efficacy data above, these results highlight the successful application of the new modification patterns in siRNA therapeutic development.

In summary, we have systematically described the development process of potent novel modification patterns for siRNA therapeutics. These patterns (DV25–29) enabled the successful development of YK1597-DV29PG5, an siRNA-GalNAc conjugate targeting PCSK9. In preclinical studies, this conjugate demonstrated acceptable efficacy and safety in lowering circulating LDL-C levels. Currently, this agent is undergoing clinical trials in China. However, the current understanding still cannot precisely elucidate the relationship between specific modification patterns and sequences, thus underscoring the critical need for comprehensive in vitro and in vivo screening. Our research provides a valuable reference for the development process of siRNA therapeutics.

Materials and methods

Care and use of laboratory animals

All studies were conducted using protocols consistent with applicable local regulations and approved by the Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee. The test articles were diluted with 0.9% NaCl or PBS to achieve appropriate dosing concentrations and were administered s.c. to C57/BL6 mice (6–8 weeks old) or cynomolgus monkeys (3–5 years old). Randomization was performed to avoid bias. Investigators were not blinded to group allocation during the experiment or when assessing the outcome.

siRNA design and synthesis

siRNAs targeting PCSK9 were designed based on the mRNA sequence NM_174936.4 obtained from the NCBI. A total of 19 siRNA sequences targeting PCSK9 were designed using a method we had previously introduced. Firstly, all possible siRNA sequences were generated, and sequences with immunostimulatory motifs were filtered using Designer of Small Interfering RNA. The efficacy of sequences was then predicted using different rule sets or models, as introduced in a previous study.26 Thereafter, the sequences used in screening were chosen manually. The difference lies in the fact that the ESC pattern-modified siRNAs with optimal efficacy—rather than the alternating 2′-F/2′-MeO modifications—were identified through sequence composition screening in this study. The modification patterns with one or more 2′-F modifications at positions 3, 4, and 5 of the AS were designed and named DV26–29, while the modification pattern without 2′-F at these positions of the AS is known as DV25. For the SS, position 17 with 2′-F modification was designed for all patterns, while position 10 with 2′-F modification is only used for DV25–27 (Figure 1A). All oligonucleotides were synthesized on a Tsingke 192P synthesizer according to the protocol we reported.26 Phosphoramidite monomers of 2′-F or 2′-OMe were obtained from commercial sources. The 5′ end of GalNAc G5 was protected by 4,4′-dimethoxytrityl. This protecting group can be removed at the start of nucleotide assembly, thereby allowing GalNAc G5 to conjugate with the 3′ end of the SS during solid-phase synthesis. After solid-phase synthesis was completed, the AS and SS were cleaved from the synthesis support, respectively. This cleavage was achieved by subjecting them to a 28% ammonia solution at 85°C for a duration of 2 h. Thereafter, the system was cooled to room temperature. The mixture was then transferred to a filter press and eluted with a purified water and ethanol solution. The filtrates were combined, passed through a chromatography column, desalted, and concentrated using a 1-kDa ultrafiltration tube to obtain a single-stranded solution. The purified SSs and ASs were mixed in equimolar amounts, heated to 95°C, held at this temperature for 3 min, and then slowly cooled to room temperature to form double-stranded siRNA. After lyophilization, the siRNA was obtained as a white powder. The identities and purities of all oligonucleotides were confirmed using liquid chromatography-mass spectrometry (LC-MS) and high-performance liquid chromatography, respectively. Before testing in vitro or administration to the animals, the oligonucleotide solution was prepared to a certain concentration using PBS buffer.

Cell line culture and dosing

Hep3B cells (BNCC, BNCC342189) were grown in DMEM (Macgene) supplemented with 10% fetal bovine serum ([FBS] Vistech) and 1% penicillin-streptomycin (Gibco). Reverse transfections were performed in a 24-well plate using Lipofectamine RNAiMAX, according to the manufacturer’s instructions. Briefly, test articles were dissolved in water, diluted using Opti-MEM (Gibco), and mixed with equal volumes of Lipofectamine RNAiMAX dispersed in Opti-MEM. The mixture was incubated for 15 min at room temperature to form siRNA-Lipofectamine RNAiMAX transfection complexes. Cells were detached and diluted in Opti-MEM such that 500 μL contained 75,000 cells. Thereafter, 100 μL of transfection complexes was added to each well and 500 μL of diluted cells was added to a final volume of 600 μL. The plates were gently mixed by rocking them back and forth.

Analysis of hPCSK9 knockdown in transgenic mice

Male hPCSK9 transgenic mice (C57BL/6J) were dosed with GalNAc-siRNAs via s.c. injection at concentrations of 2 and 6 mg/kg (n = 8 and 6 animals per group, respectively). According to calculations based on body surface area, these doses are equivalent to 9.77 and 29.3 mg doses for a 60-kg human, respectively, which are the same doses used in a previous non-clinical study of inclisiran.35 Since female mice are usually considered to be more sensitive to environmental factors, only male transgenic mice were chosen for this experiment to avoid any possible uncertainty. Mice were maintained under a 12:12-h light-dark cycle, and the temperature was ∼18°C–23°C with 40%–60% humidity. A 0.15-mL blood sample was collected from each mouse 3 days before dosing and every 7 days after dosing. Blood samples were centrifuged at 3,000 rpm at 4°C to separate plasma, and hPCSK9 levels in plasma were determined using ELISA (Proteintech, KE00181). The LDL-C and TC content in blood were determined using a clinical chemistry analyzer (Beckman Coulter, AU680) with reagents purchased from Beckman Coulter. Randomization was performed to avoid bias. Investigators were not blinded to group allocations during the experiment or when assessing the outcome.

qPCR tests

For cell line samples, total RNA was extracted using the MiniBest Universal RNA Extraction Kit (Takara, 9767). For animal tissue samples, total RNA was extracted using the Eastep Super Total RNA Extraction Kit (Promega, LS1040) following the manufacturer’s instructions. RNA was reverse-transcribed using PrimeScript RT Master Mix (Takara, RR036B). qPCR was performed with TB Green Premix Ex Taq (Takara, RR420W) according to the manufacturer’s instructions using an ABI 7500 with the following primers: hPCSK9-F: 5′-CCTGGAGCGGATTACCCCT-3′; hPCSK9-R: 5′-CTGTATGCTGGTGTCTAGGAGA-3′; hGAPDH-F: 5′-AGTATGACAACAGCCTCAAG-3′; hGAPDH-R: 5′-TCATGAGTCCTTCCACGATA-3′; mGAPDH-F: 5′-GGGTGTGAACCACGAGAAATA-3′; mGAPDH-R: 5′-GTCATGAGCCCTTCCACAAT-3′. Data were analyzed using ΔΔCt normalized to the control group (cells treated with Lipofectamin RNAiMAX alone or animals treated with PBS).

Analysis of PCSK9 knockdown and LDL-C-lowering effects in cynomolgus monkeys

Cynomolgus monkeys aged 3–5 years were randomized and allocated to different groups (n = 6 per group, three males and three females). Four groups of animals were involved in this experiment, including animals that were s.c. dosed with 3, 6, or 10 mg/kg YK1597-DV29PG5 and a group that was dosed with vehicle (saline). Blood samples were collected every 7 days. Serum PCSK9 levels were determined using ELISA (Proteintech, KE00181). LDL-C and TC content were determined using a clinical chemistry analyzer (Beckman Coulter, AU680) with reagents purchased from Beckman Coulter.

Clinical pathology and histopathology

Whole venous blood was collected into serum separator tubes (BD Microtainer). Serum chemistry was analyzed using an automated chemistry analyzer (Hitachi, 7180). Blood cell counting was performed using a Sysmex XN 1000V hematology analyzer, and blood coagulation characteristics were analyzed with a Sysmex CS-5100. Urinalysis was performed using Sysmex UN-2000. Differences between group means were evaluated for statistical significance using one-way ANOVA. All animals involved in the clinical pathology and histopathology study were sacrificed according to the standard operating procedures, and tissues of interest were collected. All tissues were fixed in 10% neutral buffered formalin for 72 h. Tissues were trimmed, embedded in paraffin blocks, sectioned at 4 μm, stained with H&E using Tissue-Tek Prisma A1D (Sakura), and coverslipped using Tissue-Tek Glass g2 (Sakura). Two sections were examined microscopically from each liver in an unblinded fashion, followed by a blinded assessment to confirm subtle findings. The range of severity grade for each histologic finding was graded on a scale of 1–5, with 1 indicating minimal severity and 5 indicating severe severity.

Statistical analysis

Data are represented as means ± SD. For multiple comparisons between groups, one-way ANOVA was applied, multiple comparisons were conducted between one control group and the other groups, and p values were adjusted using Dunnett’s method. siRNAs with significantly higher inhibition efficacy were labeled on the graph. Statistically significant differences were expressed as follows: ∗p < 0.05, ∗∗p < 0.01, ∗∗∗p < 0.001. Statistical analysis was performed using GraphPad Prism software (version 10.4).
