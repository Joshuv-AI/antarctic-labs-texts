Performance evaluation of a reflexive algorithm for suspected heparin-induced thrombocytopenia
Authors: Sean G Yates, Christopher B Webb, Lisa M Scheid, Sajjad Afraz, Ibrahim F Ibrahim, Jimmy Lee, Ravi Sarode
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundThe diagnostic workup of heparin-induced thrombocytopenia (HIT) involves a heparin–platelet factor 4 complex-dependent screening test, such as an enzyme-linked immunosorbent assay (ELISA) or a rapid latex immunoturbidimetric assay (LIA), followed by a heparin-dependent platelet activation assay. Because of the high false-positive rates observed with LIA alone in our hospital, our laboratory adopted a reflex algorithm in which LIA-positive results are confirmed by an immunoglobulin G–specific ELISA before evaluation with serotonin release assay (SRA).ObjectivesTo evaluate the diagnostic performance and operational impact of an LIA-first reflex ELISA algorithm for suspected HIT compared with ELISA or LIA alone.MethodsWe retrospectively compared ELISA alone, LIA alone, and LIA with reflex ELISA confirmation across 3 diagnostic eras, with performance assessed against SRA-confirmed HIT. Sensitivity, specificity, positive predictive value (PPV), negative predictive value, and accuracy were calculated for each era, and associations between the magnitudes of screening-assay reactivity and SRA positivity were assessed.ResultsPPV improved from 31.3% with ELISA alone and 25.3% with LIA alone to 61.2% with reflex testing, while accuracy improved from 39.8% and 33.3% to 63.5%, respectively. Reflex ELISA confirmation reduced the number of patients classified as screening positive from 127 of 804 to 49 of 804, corresponding to a 61.4% reduction in potential SRA send-outs and a decrease from 15.8 to 6.1 SRA send-outs per 100 patients evaluated. Higher combined LIA and ELISA reactivities were associated with higher probability of SRA positivity.ConclusionReflex ELISA confirmation improved the diagnostic efficiency of HIT evaluation by increasing PPV and accuracy while reducing SRA send-outs. Combined screening-assay strength may help stratify the likelihood of HIT and guide the need for confirmatory testing, potentially improving time to HIT diagnosis and management in certain scenarios.Keywords: heparin, heparin-induced thrombocytopenia, immunoassay, platelets, thrombocytopenia

Introduction

The prevailing diagnostic approach for evaluating clinically suspected heparin-induced thrombocytopenia (HIT) involves using immunoassays to detect antibodies to platelet factor 4–heparin (PF4) complexes. The solid-phase enzyme-linked immunosorbent assay (ELISA) is widely used in this setting. Although ELISAs (polyspecific [immunoglobulin G {IgG}/immunoglobulin A/immunoglobulin M] and IgG-specific) have excellent sensitivity (97%) and negative predictive value (NPV; 99%), they demonstrate suboptimal specificity (82% and 87%, respectively) and corresponding high false-positive rates (18% and 13%, respectively) [1]. Additionally, ELISA testing takes approximately 4 hours; therefore, these tests are frequently batched in laboratories due to limited time and resources. The high false-positive rates associated with ELISA also have clinical consequences, as patients often remain on alternative nonheparin anticoagulants while awaiting results from functional platelet activation tests such as the serotonin-release assay (SRA) or the heparin-induced platelet aggregation assay. Nonheparin anticoagulants are considerably more expensive than heparin and confer greater bleeding risks, and confirmatory testing delays prolong a patient’s length of stay [2].

Several rapid immunoassays (RIAs) are available for diagnostic screening of HIT, with reported analytical turnaround times of 20 to 30 minutes. Since these tests are automated, resource utilization and technical requirements are minimized [3]. As a result, RIAs have the potential to significantly shorten diagnostic turnaround times, enhance patient outcomes, and reduce constraints on laboratory resources. In a clinical study, an RIA, the latex immunoturbidimetric assay (LIA), demonstrated diagnostic sensitivity and specificity for HIT of 97.4% and 94%, respectively [4].

However, upon retrospectively evaluating the performance characteristics of the LIA in our hemostasis laboratory, we observed relatively low specificity rates of 14.9% (95% CI, 6.2% to 28.3%), likely due to nonpathologic immunoglobulin A and immunoglobulin M antibodies binding to PF4–polyvinyl sulfonate complexes in the LIA reagent. Given the high rate of false-positives when using LIA alone, our laboratory adopted a diagnostic algorithm in which positive LIA results are reflexively confirmed by a positive IgG-specific ELISA result before being evaluated by SRA.

Although prior studies have evaluated RIAs individually and in combination, to our knowledge, no prior study has specifically assessed an LIA-first, reflex IgG-specific ELISA confirmation strategy prior to functional SRA testing. Therefore, the objective of this study was to retrospectively assess the performance of a diagnostic algorithm for HIT, where samples initially testing positive with the LIA were confirmed by an IgG-specific ELISA and then sent for functional SRA testing. These performance metrics were then compared with those of 2 retrospective cohorts tested with either ELISA or LIA alone.

Methods

Study design and setting

This retrospective study was conducted at the University of Texas Southwestern Medical Center, Clements University Hospital, an academic tertiary care hospital in Dallas, Texas. This study was approved by the local institutional review board (STU-2021-0673) and conducted in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki. Where applicable, diagnostic accuracy data were reported in accordance with published Standards for Reporting of Diagnostic Accuracy Studies suggestions [5].

Study population

Our study population included consecutive adult patients (aged ≥18 years) undergoing laboratory evaluation for suspected HIT. Patients were assigned to 1 of 3 cohorts according to the institutional screening strategy in place at the time of evaluation. Cohort 1 underwent IgG-specific ELISA alone (PF4 IgG assay; Immucor GTI Diagnostics), with positive results (≥0.4 optical density [OD] per manufacturer) further evaluated by SRA (Versiti Diagnostic Laboratories); cohort 2 underwent LIA alone (HemosIL, HIT-Ab [PF4-H], Instrumentation Laboratory), with positive results (≥1 U/mL per manufacturer) further evaluated by SRA; and cohort 3 underwent initial LIA testing, with positive LIA results reflexively confirmed by IgG-specific ELISA and further evaluated by SRA. In all testing eras, completed laboratory results were released to the electronic medical record and were immediately available to treating clinicians. In cohort 3, the initial LIA result was available upon completion. Positive LIA results triggered reflex IgG-specific ELISA testing, and the ELISA result was also released upon completion. Thus, the reflex algorithm did not prevent clinicians from seeing the rapid LIA result, but the final laboratory interpretation of screening positivity required both a positive LIA and a positive IgG-specific ELISA result. Patients were considered evaluable for diagnostic performance analyses if they had complete cohort-specific screening data and a corresponding SRA result. While SRAs were typically performed on samples with positive PF4-dependent screening results, SRAs may have also been performed in some cases with high clinical suspicion of HIT despite negative screening results.

Laboratory testing

The IgG-specific ELISA was performed on serum samples using a QUANTA-Lyser 3000 analyzer (Werfen) according to the manufacturer’s instructions. ELISA results were considered positive when the OD was ≥0.4 units and inhibition by high-concentration heparin was ≥50%. The LIA was performed on plasma samples using an ACL TOP 500 CTS instrument (Instrumentation Laboratory) according to the manufacturer’s instructions, with results ≥1.0 U/mL considered positive. SRA testing was performed by Versiti Diagnostic Laboratories. SRA results were considered positive with ≥20% serotonin release with the addition of 0.1 U/mL of heparin and with ≥50% inhibition in a paired-high-concentration heparin control (100 U/mL heparin). A negative LIA result was generally considered sufficient to exclude HIT in patients with low or intermediate clinical probability. However, when clinical suspicion remained high, particularly in patients with LIA results near the positive cutoff, additional testing with IgG-specific ELISA and/or SRA could be pursued. There was not an automated reflex workflow for negative LIA results; additional testing was provider-directed and contingent on continued clinical concern for HIT. Because our hemostasis laboratory also serves as a reference laboratory, complete clinical information and provider rationale for additional testing were not uniformly available.

Data collection

When available, clinical data for patients with SRA were retrospectively collected by reviewing electronic medical records. As the hemostasis laboratory at the University of Texas Southwestern Medical Center serves as a reference laboratory, this information was not always accessible. Data were collected using standardized forms to document variables including patient age, sex, medical or surgical clinical setting, platelet nadir, percent platelet decrease, 4Ts score, SRA results, and HIT-associated thrombosis or related sequelae. Sex was defined according to the sex recorded in the electronic medical record at the time of laboratory testing. All data underwent independent review by 2 investigators (S.G.Y. and C.B.W.), and discrepancies were resolved through consensus.

Statistical analysis

All statistical analyses were performed using R, version 4.5.2 (R Core Team, R Foundation for Statistical Computing). Diagnostic performance of each cohort-specific screening strategy was assessed relative to SRA-confirmed HIT among unique patients with complete cohort-specific screening data and a corresponding SRA. Sensitivity, specificity, positive predictive value (PPV), NPV, and accuracy were calculated for each cohort. Because confirmatory SRA testing was not performed uniformly across all screened patients in each diagnostic era, performance estimates should be interpreted as characteristics of the SRA-verified cohort rather than as characteristics of a specific assay.

To characterize the operational impact of each screening approach, we summarized the relationship between the number of unique patients screened and the number undergoing SRA testing in each era. For cohort 3, the estimated reduction in potential SRA send-outs was calculated by comparing the number of LIA-positive patients under a hypothetical LIA-only approach, with the number categorized as positive following reflex ELISA confirmation.

Exploratory analyses were performed to assess the relationship between the magnitude of the screening assay and observed SRA positivity. LIA and ELISA magnitude categories were defined empirically based on observed assay values and corresponding SRA positivity in historical single-assay cohorts (cohorts 1 and 2). The magnitude of LIA results was classified as follows: negative (<1.0 U/mL), weakly positive (1-2.99 U/mL), moderately positive (3-5.99 U/mL), and strongly positive (≥6 U/mL). The magnitude of ELISA results was classified according to historical results as negative (<0.4 OD units), weakly positive (0.4-0.99 OD units), moderately positive (1-1.49 OD units), or strongly positive (≥1.5 OD units). Scores were assigned to each category, and a composite LIA/ELISA score was calculated by summing the component scores for patients in cohort 3. The percent SRA release (median and IQR) at low-dose heparin was also analyzed as a continuous variable across combined ELISA/LIA score groups. Differences across groups were assessed using the Kruskal–Wallis test. The association between the combined score and the magnitude of SRA release was assessed using Spearman correlation.

Among patients with SRA testing and available clinical data, baseline characteristics were compared across cohorts. Continuous variables were compared using the Kruskal–Wallis test, and categorical variables were compared using the chi-square test.

Diagnostic performance of screening strategies

The diagnostic performance of each screening approach relative to the SRA-verified cohort is summarized in Table 2. Among the small number of screening-negative patients who underwent SRA testing, no SRA-positive cases were observed. However, because most screening-negative patients did not undergo SRA testing, sensitivity and NPV could not be reliably estimated for the overall screened population. Within the SRA-verified cohort, the reflex algorithm improved PPV to 61.2% compared with 31.3% for ELISA alone and 25.3% for LIA alone, while accuracy increased to 63.5% compared with 39.8% and 33.3%, respectively. Consistent with this observation, the proportion of patients with positive SRA results was higher in the reflex cohort (58.5%) relative to the ELISA-only (27.5%) or LIA-only (20.3%) cohorts.

Estimated reduction in SRA send-outs with reflex screening

Within cohort 3, a hypothetical LIA-only strategy would have classified 127 of 804 unique patients as screening positive, whereas only 49 of 804 patients remained positive after reflex ELISA confirmation. Thus, 78 patients were reclassified as negative by the reflex algorithm—a 61.4% reduction in potential SRA send-outs. When normalized to the number of patients evaluated in the reflex era, this translates to a decrease from 15.8 to 6.1 potential SRA send-outs per 100 patients.

Relationship between screening-assay magnitude and SRA positivity

The frequency of SRA-positive cases increased progressively with increasing ELISA and LIA reactivity (Table 3). Weakly positive results in both assays were associated with a low incidence of SRA positivity, whereas progressively stronger reactivity was associated with higher probabilities of SRA positivity.

Discussion

In this retrospective evaluation of 3 diagnostic eras for suspected HIT, we found that an LIA-first reflex algorithm, in which positive LIA results were confirmed by IgG-specific ELISA before SRA testing, improved diagnostic efficiency compared with either ELISA or LIA alone. The reflex approach reduced the proportion of patients classified as screening-positive, improved PPV and overall accuracy within the SRA-evaluable cohort, and reduced the estimated number of potential SRA send-outs compared with a hypothetical LIA-only strategy. Importantly, this approach preserved the operational advantage of LIA as a rapid first-line assay, allowing negative results to provide timely diagnostic information while limiting additional ELISA and functional testing to a smaller subset of patients.

The principal concern when using LIA alone is the incidence of false-positive results; among SRA-evaluable patients in our LIA-only cohort, 56 of 75 LIA-positive patients were SRA-negative, corresponding to a false-positive proportion of 74.7% among screening-positive patients. This rate of false positivity with LIA alone is consistent with reports in the medical literature [4,6]. In contrast, we observed that reflex ELISA confirmation decreased the number of LIA-positive patients progressing to SRA testing, with 78 of 127 LIA-positive patients being reclassified as negative after ELISA. This led to a 61.4% reduction in potential SRA send-outs and lowered the rate from 15.8 to 6.1 per 100 patients assessed during the reflex era. The reflex approach also appeared to enrich the screening-positive population for patients with platelet-activating antibodies, with SRA positivity among screening-positive patients increasing to 61.2% compared with 31.3% in the ELISA-only cohort and 25.3% in the LIA-only cohort, corresponding to absolute increases of 29.9 and 35.9 percentage points, respectively. These findings suggest that LIA can be useful as an initial rapid screening test but may be most effective when incorporated into a stepwise algorithm rather than used as a standalone screening assay.

We also observed that the strength of the screening assay was associated with SRA positivity rates. Increasing ELISA and LIA reactivities were each associated with progressively higher observed rates of SRA positivity, and higher combined LIA and ELISA scores in the reflex cohort were associated with greater observed rates of SRA positivity and higher quantitative SRA release. This observation suggests that quantitative or semiquantitative assay strength may provide clinically useful postscreening risk stratification. This finding is consistent with previous literature reporting an association between stronger immunoassay reactivity and SRA positivity and platelet activation as reflected by percent release and suggests that combined reactivity may correspond to disease probability, guide the need for confirmatory functional testing, and similarly predict clinical outcomes such as thrombosis [4,7,8].

Our findings are consistent with prior literature showing that RIAs offer practical advantages in HIT evaluation because they are automated, operationally simpler than ELISA, and provide substantially shorter turnaround times [9]. However, several questions remain regarding how these assays should be optimally incorporated into diagnostic algorithms. Accordingly, recently published guidelines from the American Society of Hematology on HIT emphasize the need for studies evaluating the performance of algorithms that use RIAs to diagnose suspected HIT [10]. Prior studies have evaluated RIAs individually and in combination, demonstrating that complementary assay strategies can improve diagnostic classification by leveraging distinct assay characteristics rather than relying on a single positive test result [11,12].

While our algorithm differs in that it uses LIA followed by an IgG-specific ELISA rather than 2 RIAs, the underlying principle is similar: the initial assay (LIA) provides rapid triage, while a second assay (ELISA) refines the overall posttest probability.

A strength of this study is that it evaluates a real-world institutional diagnostic algorithm across 3 distinct diagnostic eras, enabling comparison of ELISA alone, LIA alone, and reflex testing in routine clinical practice. The study also quantifies diagnostic performance and operational impact, including the estimated reduction in potential SRA send-outs. In addition, we evaluated the relationship between assay magnitude and SRA positivity, including a combined LIA/ELISA reactivity score, which provides exploratory data on how assay strength may further stratify HIT probability among patients with positive screening results. To our knowledge, this is the first reported evaluation of an LIA-first, reflex IgG-specific ELISA confirmation strategy before functional SRA testing.

Our study has limitations. First, this was a retrospective, single-center study, which limits the generalizability of our findings. Second, confirmatory SRA testing was not applied across all screened subjects but was performed in those with positive screening results or clinical concern for HIT, thus predisposing our study findings to verification bias; therefore, estimates of specificity, NPV, and likelihood ratios should be viewed as relative performance characteristics within an SRA-verified cohort rather than assay properties observed in consecutive patients. Although SRA was used as the reference standard, it is not a perfect diagnostic gold standard. False-negative SRA results have been described, particularly in selected clinical contexts, and false-positive results are also possible. Therefore, SRA-based classification may have misclassified a small number of patients. In addition, because our cohorts were derived from different timeframes, differences in patient selection, test ordering patterns, and the use of confirmatory testing may have contributed to between-cohort differences. Finally, because our hemostasis laboratory also serves as a reference laboratory, complete clinical information was not available for all patients, including 4Ts scores and the rationale for provider-directed additional testing following negative or high-negative LIA results. Therefore, we could not systematically assess how often negative or high-negative screening results prompted additional testing or whether such testing was driven by high clinical pretest probability.

Our data support the use of LIA as a first-line screening assay when coupled with a reflex ELISA. This approach preserves the operational advantages of a rapid, low-resource immunoassay, including timely negative results that can inform immediate management, limit reliance on more resource-intensive ELISA testing, and reduce the downstream burden of functional send-out testing, while improving the PPV and accuracy of screening-positive classifications. Given the increased cost of direct thrombin inhibitors and the increased bleeding risk in this setting, future studies should evaluate the cost-effectiveness of this diagnostic strategy, both with regards to laboratory testing and resource utilization, as well as downstream costs associated with the use of nonheparin anticoagulants.
