Tailored management of chronic bacterial prostatitis with sequential fluoroquinolones and adverse effect mitigation strategies: A case report
Authors: Thao D Nguyen
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractChronic bacterial prostatitis (CBP) presents diagnostic and therapeutic challenges, particularly in patients with drug allergies and atypical symptom profiles. This case describes a retired male healthcare professional with exclusive penile pain and urinary symptoms, diagnosed with CBP. Management involved sequential fluoroquinolone therapy—ciprofloxacin followed by moxifloxacin—over 6.5 weeks, guided by clinical condition and pharmacokinetic/ pharmacodynamic (PK/PD) considerations. To mitigate adverse drug reaction (ADR) risks, the patient incorporated probiotics, tendon support measures, and QT monitoring. The individualized approach resulted in significant symptomatic improvement without complications. Follow-up focused on prostate-specific antigen (PSA) monitoring, symptom tracking, and referral to pelvic floor physical therapy. This case’s approach combining sequential fluoroquinolone therapy with structured ADR-prevention strategies and a patient-engaged, shared decision-making model underscores the importance of individualized, multidisciplinary care in CBP.Keywords: Chronic bacterial prostatitis, Fluoroquinolones, Probiotics, Tendinopathy, QT, Prolongation, Pelvic floor therapy

Patient case presentation

A 69-year-old retired male healthcare professional, physically active and a regular swimmer, presented with severe penile pain during urination that had progressively worsened over two months. His history includes well-controlled hypertension on valsartan, benign prostatic hyperplasia (BPH), suboptimally managed with saw palmetto due to intolerance to alpha-blockers, and multiple drug allergies (penicillin, sulfonamides, IV contrast). Initial symptoms included nocturia (4–5 times nightly), urgency, and burning pain during urination.

Initial labs revealed a cloudy urine sample with pH 8.5, but otherwise negative urinalysis. PSA was elevated at 6.3 ng/mL (free PSA 1.6 ng/mL), compared to 3.4 ng/mL one year prior. No fever or systemic signs of infection were noted. Ultrasound of the bladder showed minimal urinary retention.

Diagnosis and treatment overview

The clinical picture suggested chronic bacterial prostatitis (CBP) [1]. Ciprofloxacin 500 mg BID was initiated empirically by a referred urologist. After 1 week, the patient noted good symptom improvement, but symptoms rebounded even though PSA declined to 3.92 ng/mL and UA was normalized (pH 7, clear). Ciprofloxacin was discontinued at 2.5 weeks, and therapy was switched to moxifloxacin 400 mg daily for 2 weeks by the urologist due to limited response, concern for anaerobic or biofilm-producing pathogens, and moxifloxacin’s higher concentration in the prostatic fluid, which is considered the site of infection for CBP [2]. After further discussion, the patient’s PCP extended moxifloxacin to a total of 4 weeks to ensure adequate coverage, as symptoms—though improved—were not fully resolved. The total fluoroquinolone treatment duration was 6.5 weeks. This approach was guided by pharmacokinetic and pharmacodynamic profiles of both agents, as detailed in Table 1.

PK/PD considerations

Moxifloxacin has much higher prostatic secretion concentration compared to that of ciprofloxacin and enhanced anaerobic coverage (see Table 1), supporting its use in persistent or atypical cases of CBP [2], [3].

Some evidence also suggests anaerobic organisms may be underrecognized contributors in CBP, despite negative cultures [4]. Switching between fluoroquinolones mid-therapy can be effective if guided by clinical response and tissue PK/PD considerations [5], [18] (Table 2).

Adverse event mitigation

Fluoroquinolones are associated with serious adverse effects during prolonged use, including Clostridioides difficile infection, tendinopathy and tendon rupture, and QTc interval prolongation [6]. The following measures may, to some extent, mitigate these risks (Table 3).

Clostridioides difficile infection prophylaxis

To mitigate Clostridioides difficile risk, the patient decided to alternate 2 probiotic products: Saccharomyces boulardii (Florastor®, 250 mg BID) and Lactobacillus rhamnosus GG (Culturelle®, BID, taken ≥2 h apart from moxifloxacin). His typical high-fiber plant-based diet, that provided approximately 30–35 g/day, should support the recovery of colonic microbiota.

Saccharomyces boulardii (strain CNCM I-745, Florastor®), a non-pathogenic yeast, has demonstrated efficacy in preventing antibiotic-associated diarrhea and C. difficile infection (CDI) in clinical trials [7], [8]. It acts by modulating inflammatory pathways and neutralizing toxins [7].

Although evidence on Lactobacillus rhamnosus GG (LGG) effectiveness is limited in adults, probiotics may support gut flora restoration and reduce risk of antibiotic-associated diarrhea [8], [9]. The patient spaced LGG doses and moxifloxacin by ≥ 2 h to limit antibiotic inactivation of the probiotic [10].

High-fiber, plant-based diets enhance microbiome diversity and short-chain fatty acid production, further reducing CDI risk. The impact of dietary fiber on gut microbiota is discussed by Makki et al. [11].

Tendon injury prevention

Fluoroquinolones carry an increased risk for tendinopathy and tendon rupture, especially of the Achilles, exacerbated by high-impact physical activities [12].

To mitigate this risk, the patient maintained a schedule of moderate swimming and progressive resistance-based gym workouts, activities that may help support tendon strength while minimizing strain on tendon structures [13]. The patient also took hydrolyzed bovine collagen (10 g) in combination with the exercises. A systematic review by Brueckheimer et al. [14] suggests that collagen type I supplementation, when combined with exercise, may offer potential benefits for musculoskeletal health, particularly by improving joint functionality and supporting tendon integrity. Patient education was emphasized on watching for early symptoms—pain, swelling, crepitus—and avoiding activities like high-impact jumping or uneven ground walking during therapy.

QT interval prolongation caution

Moxifloxacin carries a higher risk of QTc interval prolongation than ciprofloxacin, largely due to its more potent blockade of the IKr, the rapid component of the delayed rectifier potassium channel and a key player in the repolarization phase [15]. In healthy volunteers, a 7-day course of moxifloxacin increased QTc by approximately 6 ms from baseline, whereas ciprofloxacin produced no significant change. [16] In other QT studies, moxifloxacin 400 mg was often used as a positive control and typically prolonged QTc by about 7.5–12.5 ms. [15]

The patient had no known risk factors for QTc prolongation and reported no related symptoms. Prior ECGs showed no evidence of QTc prolongation. His low resting heart rate (50–60 bpm) was physiologically normal for an endurance-trained swimmer [17].

As such, a conservative approach with symptom-based monitoring was deemed appropriate. No ECG monitoring or intervention was required during treatment, and the patient reported no palpitations, dizziness, or other arrhythmic symptoms throughout the course.

Outcomes and follow-up with differential diagnosis

Following 6.5 weeks of sequential fluoroquinolone therapy—initially with ciprofloxacin for 2.5 weeks followed by moxifloxacin for 4 weeks—the patient experienced substantial symptomatic relief. Penile pain during urination improved by approximately 80–90 % from the peak intensity during exacerbation. In parallel, urinary urgency showed meaningful improvement, with better control and reduced need for immediate voiding. Nocturia frequency also declined, from 4 to 5 nightly episodes at baseline to 2–3 episodes per night. The patient did not experience any systemic signs of infection, such as fever or malaise, throughout the course of illness.

Post-treatment, pain remained mild but persistent, localized primarily at the distal penile shaft during urination, which represented a residual but significantly attenuated symptom profile. These outcomes are consistent with the clinical course observed in chronic bacterial prostatitis (CBP), where symptomatic reduction rather than complete eradication is often reported even after guideline-concordant antibiotic therapy [18], [19].

Laboratory monitoring showed normalization of urinalysis parameters, including a return of urine pH to neutral and clearance of turbidity. The patient’s symptoms improved back to baseline levels prior to the flare, with a stable resting heart rate of 50–60 bpm. No other side effects were observed. The patient’s total PSA decreased from 6.3 ng/mL at presentation to 3.92 ng/mL after 2 weeks of ciprofloxacin, and then increased to 4.63 ng/mL one month after 4 weeks of moxifloxacin, with free PSA decreasing from 25 % to 22.6 %.

PSA Interpretation and antibiotic duration

PSA fluctuations are common in the setting of prostatitis, which need follow-up monitoring rather than immediate intervention [19]. After 6.5 weeks of sequential fluoroquinolone therapy (2.5 weeks ciprofloxacin, 4 weeks moxifloxacin), the probability of bacterial clearance is high, given sufficient antibiotic duration, moxifloxacin’s strong intraprostatic penetration, and its broad in vitro coverage of atypical and anaerobic organisms [2], [3]. Additional antibiotics are usually not recommended once a guideline-concordant 4–6 week course has been completed. Further antibiotic exposure may increase resistance risk rather than clinical benefit [20].

Role of post-treatment cultures

The Meares–Stamey 4-glass test is considered the gold standard for confirming CBP, but its clinical utility after adequate therapy remains debated. Recurrence of symptoms, rather than negative cultures, defines treatment failure [20]. In this case, stable improvement and lack of systemic findings make clinical follow-up a reasonable strategy.

Non-antibiotic interventions

While the diagnosis of CBP (NIH Category II) remains most consistent with the initial presentation and antibiotic response, the persistence of penile-only pain raises the question of chronic pelvic pain syndrome (CP/CPPS, Category IIIb) partly manifested as referred pain [20]. The absence of systemic features, normal urinalysis, and sterile cultures also align with CP/CPPS [20].

Given residual mild penile pain and urinary irritative symptoms, supportive approaches are appropriate. Pelvic floor dysfunction is increasingly recognized as a contributor to pain syndromes. Referral to a certified pelvic floor physical therapist may provide both diagnostic clarity and symptomatic benefit [20].

Future management considerations

If symptoms flare again, next steps would include repeat urinalysis, urine culture, and consideration of localized culture testing if available. Non-antibiotic therapies—pelvic floor physical therapy, multimodal pain management, and phytotherapy supplements such as quercetin and pollen extract—may play a role. Ultimately, management should be individualized, balancing infection-directed therapy with supportive strategies to optimize quality of life. Should pain remain stable or improve under conservative management, advanced imaging like TRUS (transrectal ultrasound) or MRI is typically not necessary [20].

Conclusion

This case illustrates a tailored, evidence-based approach to managing chronic bacterial prostatitis, particularly in patients with complex medication intolerances and atypical symptom localization. When initial ciprofloxacin therapy provided only a limited response, a switch to moxifloxacin—chosen for its favorable PK/PD profile and higher prostatic fluid concentration—led to substantial symptom improvement.

Preventive strategies, including probiotics to reduce Clostridioides difficile risk, supplements and activity adjustments for tendinopathy, and QT monitoring, enabled over six weeks of antibiotic therapy without complications. For ongoing urogenital pain, such as localized penile pain, the differential diagnosis should include non-infectious etiologies like referred pain from underlying pelvic floor tension.

Long-term recovery is supported by ongoing monitoring with PSA follow-up, symptom tracking, and referral to certified pelvic floor physical therapy. This case underscores the value of individualized care, multimodal management, and shared decision-making in addressing the complexity of chronic prostatitis.
