Signal Peptide Engineering and Codon Optimization to Enhance α‐Gal A Activity for rAAV Gene Therapy of Fabry Disease
Authors: Siwu He, Li Yang, Ke Wan, Zhaoyue Zheng, Mi Leng, Chunmei Gan, Yu Liu, Lin Xiao, Jingya Ye, Zhian Chen, Jiao Zhou, C. Alexander Valencia, Aliah Abuammah, Hoi Yee Chow, Biao Dong, Yucheng Chen
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTFabry disease is an X‐linked lysosomal storage disorder caused by mutations in the GLA gene, leading to deficient α‐galactosidase A (α‐Gal A) activity and pathological accumulation of globotriaosylceramide (Gb3) and globotriaosylsphingosine (Lyso‐Gb3) in various organs. To enhance α‐Gal A expression and secretion for recombinant adeno‐associated viral (rAAV) gene therapy, the signal peptide was engineered in HepG2 cells. Among 22 heterogeneous signal peptides, sp21 was identified and validated for this purpose. The enzyme activity of GLA with sp21 in conditioned media was 2.3 times higher than that with the wild‐type signal peptide. Furthermore, the human GLA gene was codon optimized (hGLAco), aiming to achieve increased hepatic expression and prevent gene silencing. The secreted α‐Gal A activity of hGLAco increased approximately 4‐fold in vitro. Finally, the hGLAco transgene with sp21 driven by a liver‐specific promoter was packaged in rAAV8 vectors and injected into Fabry mice. Compared with the wild‐type cDNA, the engineered GLA gene resulted in a 6.9‐fold increase in secreted α‐Gal A activity in Fabry mice, and toxic Lyso‐Gb3 accumulation in plasma and key tissues was effectively cleared. These findings demonstrate a promising strategy to develop a more efficient rAAV gene therapy for Fabry disease.

Introduction

Fabry disease (OMIM: 301500) is a rare, X‐linked recessive disorder caused by mutations in the human GLA gene encoding alpha‐galactosidase A (α‐Gal A), resulting in systemic accumulation of globotriaosylceramide (Gb3), the primary substrate of α‐Gal A. Its deacylated derivative, globotriaosylsphingosine (lyso‐Gb3), is usually used as a disease biomarker. Deficiency of α‐Gal A leads to the pathological accumulation of Gb3 and Lyso‐Gb3 in various organs, especially resulting in progressive cardiac, renal, and cerebrovascular dysfunction [1, 2]. The two primary clinical subtypes of Fabry disease are classical and nonclassical (late‐onset) phenotypes. Males with classic Fabry disease exhibit little to no α‐Gal A activity (< 3% of normal), leading to markedly elevated levels of Lyso‐Gb3 in both plasma and urine. In contrast, males with late‐onset Fabry disease retain some residual α‐Gal A activity, resulting in lower Lyso‐Gb3 accumulation relative to classic Fabry disease [3]. In heterozygous females, the clinical manifestations range widely due to random X‐chromosome inactivation and epigenetic factors [4].

The estimated prevalence of Fabry disease ranges from 1 in 40 000 to 1 in 11700 [5]. However, newborn screening suggests a higher incidence of late‐onset Fabry disease in males with estimates of 1 in 3000 in Italy, 1 in 1200 in Taiwan and 1 in 6264 in China [3, 6]. Therefore, the incidence of Fabry disease may be significantly underestimated in clinical settings due to the limitations of earlier diagnostic techniques and the wide variability in patient phenotypes [7].

Enzyme replacement therapy (ERT), including agalsidase α (Replagal), agalsidase β (Fabrazyme), and pegunigalsidase alfa (Elfabrio), is currently available for the treatment of Fabry disease. ERT can alleviate symptoms but has limitations, including a short half‐life of enzymes, the need for biweekly intravenous injections, and the development of neutralizing antibodies that reduce efficacy over time [8, 9]. The pharmacologic chaperone migalastat (Galafold) has also shown efficacy, although only in a subset of patients with specific mutations [10]. Therefore, there is a need for more effective, durable, and broadly applicable therapeutic strategies for Fabry disease.

Gene therapy has emerged as a promising approach for Fabry disease, utilizing viral vectors such as retroviruses [11], adenoviruses [12], and recombinant adeno‐associated viruses (rAAVs) [13, 14, 15, 16, 17, 18]. More recently, lentivirus (LV)‐mediated approaches have advanced to first‐in‐human application in Fabry disease [19, 20, 21], building on their clinical success in trials for human β‐hemoglobinopathy [22]. However, it requires ex vivo isolation and transduction of patient cells followed by reinfusion, which makes the process complex, costly, and associated with transplantation‐related risks. rAAV vectors are widely used because of their safety and long‐term efficiency [23, 24, 25], but challenges such as immune responses, hepatotoxicity from high AAV doses, rare integration events, and cytokine‐mediated toxicities remain [26, 27, 28, 29]. Five rAAV‐mediated gene therapies for Fabry disease are in clinical stages, in which different rAAV serotypes, promoters, and transgenes are used to overexpress α‐Gal A (NCT04046224/NCT05039886 [ST920], NCT04519749/NCT05629559 [4D‐310], NCT06207552 [BBM‐F101], NCT06819514 [EXG110] and CTR20244539 [ZS805]). Current research observations indicate that systemic delivery of α‐Gal A expressed from rAAV‐transduced hepatocytes can increase the circulating enzyme levels and promote substrate reduction. However, as Fabry disease is a slowly progressive disorder, long‐term studies are required to fully determine clinical efficacy across different affected organs.

Signal peptides are short N‐terminal sequences that direct proteins through appropriate processing and secretory pathways within the trans‐Golgi network. Accordingly, the choice of signal peptide in a transgene can significantly influence protein secretion levels [30]. α‐Gal A is a lysosomal enzyme with a signal peptide, but its secretion efficiency is limited in cells [31]. Therefore, the signal peptide of α‐Gal A was engineered to enhance its secretion in the liver, thereby allowing it to function and correct the disease in targeted organs. Codon optimization has been shown to enhance the therapeutic gene expression of rAAV‐mediated gene therapy for genetic diseases while maintaining safety [32, 33]. More importantly, the deletion of potential CpG islands, methylation, and the resulting silencing signals are believed to be key to maintaining long‐term expression [34, 35]. Therefore, in this study, we investigated these two strategies to optimize rAAV‐based gene therapy for Fabry disease, aiming to enhance the efficacy of transgene expression and protein secretion.

Results

Selection of Signal Peptides to Enhance α‐Gal A Secretion In Vitro

To enhance the secretion and thus the activity of the α‐Gal A gene transfer, we replaced the native signal peptide of the human GLA with 22 heterogeneous signal peptides (Figures 1A and S1A). Most of them were predicted to be able to improve the secretion score by using SignalP 5.0 (Figure S1B,C). The engineered human GLA transgenes were cloned and inserted into an expression cassette driven by a chicken beta‐actin (CB) promoter and transiently transfected into human hepatoma HepG2 cells. In comparison with the wild‐type counterpart, five signal peptides, namely, sp1, sp4, sp20, sp21, and sp22, were able to increase α‐Gal A activity in media by approximately 1.2‐, 1.9‐, 1.2‐, 2.3‐, and 1.3‐fold, respectively (Figure 1B).

Moreover, Western blot analysis revealed that these five modified human GLA transgenes showed increased expression and secretion in the media (Figure 1C), consistent with the increase in α‐Gal A activity observed (Figure 1B). These findings indicated that the engineered human GLA transgenes with heterogeneous signal peptides were able to enhance the secretion and activity of α‐Gal A in vitro compared with the wild‐type human GLA.

Validation of the Ability of the Selected Signal Peptide to Enhance α‐Gal A Overexpression In Vivo

The transgene with sp21 was chosen for validation due to its highest capability to enhance the secretion of α‐Gal A. Two rAAV8 vectors were generated that carried human GLA with either a wild‐type signal peptide (wtsp) or sp21 under a liver‐specific promoter (LSP). Six‐ to eight‐week‐old Gla knockout (Gla KO) mice were injected intravenously using rAAV8 vectors at doses of 2 × 1012, 5 × 1012, or 5 × 1013 vg/kg (Figure 2A,B). The results indicated that hGLA DNA and mRNA copy numbers increased in a dose‐dependent manner for both transgenes at 12 weeks after rAAV8 vector administration (Figures S2A and S2B). These results confirmed that liver‐expressed α‐Gal A was secreted into the bloodstream and remained active, as indicated by analysis of α‐Gal A activity in mouse plasma (Figure 2C). All treated groups, except the low‐dose group receiving ssAAV8.wtsp‐hGLA, exhibited supraphysiological α‐Gal A expression levels with a dose‐dependent increase in plasma enzyme activity. Twelve weeks post‐treatment, the mean plasma activity levels for the low‐, mid‐, and high‐dose groups of ssAAV8.sp21‐hGLA were 14.0, 22.0, and 360.0 nmol/h/mL, respectively, whereas they were 0.5, 1.4, and 9.3 nmol/h/mL for the three different doses of ssAAV8.wtsp‐hGLA. Compared with that of ssAAV8.wtsp‐hGLA, the plasma enzyme activity increased 28.0‐, 15.7‐, and 38.7‐fold at the three doses (Figure 2C). Significant increases in hepatic α‐Gal A activity, of 8.6‐, 19.4‐, and 65.6‐fold, respectively, were also observed (Figure S3A). To examine enzyme uptake by other organs, α‐Gal A activities were measured in the heart, kidney, and spleen. In the high‐dose ssAAV8.sp21‐hGLA group, the mean α‐Gal A activity levels in the heart, kidney, and spleen were 1647.5, 821.8, and 10230.5 pmol/h/mg of total protein, respectively, representing 6.8‐, 1.5‐, and 2.5‐fold increases relative to those observed in wild‐type controls. Immunohistochemical analysis confirmed that α‐Gal A expression was higher in the high‐dose ssAAV8.sp21‐hGLA group than in the ssAAV8.wtsp‐hGLA group (Figure 2D). These results suggested that α‐Gal A expressed in the liver was able to be taken up by secondary tissues, although the activity levels in these tissues were approximately an order of magnitude lower than those in the liver (Figure S3A–D). Moreover, both the plasma and tissue α‐Gal A activities in the ssAAV8.sp21‐hGLA groups were positively correlated with the vector dose (Figures 2C and S3).

Glycosylation is an essential post‐translational modification for protein function, so the glycosylation profiles of α‐Gal A expressed by these two vectors were assessed. The results demonstrated that α‐Gal A overexpressed in Fabry mice retained intact glycosylation sites, consistent with those observed in recombinant human α‐Gal A (Rec.hAGA) (Figures 2E and S4A,B).

To assess whether the circulating α‐Gal A enzyme could clear the accumulated substrates, Lyso‐Gb3 in the plasma, a more specific indicator of Fabry disease, was quantified [36, 37]. Results showed that Lyso‐Gb3 accumulation in plasma was reduced across all treated groups. Specifically, in the ssAAV8.sp21‐hGLA‐treated groups, the mean plasma Lyso‐Gb3 levels were reduced to 9.1%, 7.1%, and 2.8% of those in the untreated Fabry mice at doses of 2 × 1012, 5 × 1012, and 2 × 1013 vg/kg, respectively, while they were 60.5%, 26.4%, and 17.7%, respectively, for the three different doses of ssAAV8.wtsp‐hGLA‐treated groups (Figure 2F). A dose‐dependent reduction of Lyso‐Gb3 was observed, indicating a positive correlation between α‐Gal A activity and Lyso‐Gb3 levels. Compared with the ssAAV8.wtsp‐hGLA group, the ssAAV8.sp21‐hGLA group presented lower Lyso‐Gb3 accumulation. Together, these data indicated that ssAAV8.sp21‐hGLA‐mediated expression of α‐Gal A remained sustained in plasma and tissues for 12 weeks and effectively reduced the plasma Lyso‐Gb3 levels in Fabry mice.

Codon Optimization of the Human GLA

To improve α‐Gal A expression, the human GLA cDNA sequence was codon‐optimized for frequent usage in mammalian cells. Using several online tools, all CpG islands were removed to reduce the risk of gene silencing in vivo, GC content was adjusted to 0.59, and potential alternative splicing sites were eliminated. The optimized sequence was further evaluated to avoid negative CIS elements and repeat elements, and to increase the codon adaptation index (CAI) to 0.95. These combined strategies were used to generate the codon‐optimized GLA (hGLAco) gene with improved α‐Gal A expression.

To evaluate the effect of codon optimization, plasmid vectors were constructed in which wild‐type human GLA (hGLA) or codon‐optimized human GLA (hGLAco) transgenes with the native signal peptide were cloned under a CB promoter and transiently transfected into human hepatoma HepG2 cells. Codon optimization significantly increased α‐Gal A expression, resulting in a 1.4‐fold increase, as demonstrated by Western blot analysis (Figure 3A,B), which was further confirmed by immunofluorescence staining (Figure 3C). Additionally, enzyme activity assays showed that α‐Gal A activity in the media of the pAAV.CB‐hGLAco group reached 2810 pmol/h/mL, representing a 4‐fold increase compared to the pAAV.CB‐hGLA group (Figure 3D). Overall, this codon‐optimized human GLA effectively enhanced α‐Gal A expression and activity.

rAAV Gene Therapy Using hGLAco With an Engineered Signal Peptide

To maximize α‐Gal A expression, signal peptide 21 was combined with the codon‐optimized human GLA gene, and the expression cassettes under the same liver‐specific promoter were packaged in AAV8 capsids as self‐complementary vectors (scAAV8) for fast and high‐level expression (Figure 4A). The four rAAV8 vectors used were scAAV8.wtsp‐hGLA, scAAV8.sp21‐hGLA, scAAV8.wtsp‐hGLAco, and scAAV8.sp21‐hGLAco.

The results revealed that HepG2 and Huh7 cells transduced with scAAV8.sp21‐hGLAco efficiently secreted α‐Gal A in a dose‐dependent manner, suggesting that the vector was functional (Figure S5A,B). The rAAV8 vectors were subsequently administered intravenously to 8–12‐week‐old Fabry mice at a dose of 5 × 1012 vg/kg. Eight weeks post‐administration, the mean plasma α‐Gal A activity levels in the scAAV8.wtsp‐hGLA, scAAV8.sp21‐hGLA, scAAV8.wtsp‐hGLAco, and scAAV8.sp21‐hGLAco groups were 442.6, 1551.5, 1833.3, and 3069.9 nmol/h/mL, respectively (Figure 4B). All these values exceeded the 39.8 nmol/h/mL observed in the ssAAV8.sp21‐hGLA group at a dose of 5 × 1012 vg/kg 8 weeks post injection (Figure 2C), in which α‐Gal A activity in the plasma, liver, heart, and spleen achieved wild‐type mice levels (Figure S3A–D). Notably, the scAAV8‐wtsp‐hGLAco group exhibited a 4‐fold increase in plasma α‐Gal A activity compared with the scAAV8‐wtsp‐hGLA group, demonstrating that codon optimization could enhance plasma enzyme activity in vivo. A similar conclusion could also be drawn from the result that the scAAV8‐sp21‐hGLAco group presented a 2.0‐fold increase in mean plasma α‐Gal A activity over the scAAV8‐sp21‐hGLA group, reaching 3070 nmol/h/mL. The α‐Gal A activity in the scAAV8‐sp21‐hGLA group was higher than that in the scAAV8‐wtsp‐hGLA group, and the scAAV8‐sp21‐hGLAco group outperformed the scAAV8‐wtsp‐hGLAco group, indicating that signal peptide 21 enhances α‐Gal A expression and secretion. Taken together, the transgene encoding the sp21 signal peptide and codon‐optimized human GLA (hGLAco) represented the most effective strategy for rAAV‐mediated gene therapy of Fabry disease in our mouse model, resulting in a 6.9‐fold increase in enzyme activity compared to the wild‐type human GLA gene.

α‐Gal A activity in various tissue types was also assessed 14 weeks post‐treatment. Significant increases in α‐Gal A activity were observed in the liver, heart, kidney, and spleen of all treated mice. The mean enzyme activities in the liver, heart, kidney, and spleen were 9.3‐, 34.9‐, 10.4‐, and 15.8‐fold higher, respectively, in the scAAV8‐sp21‐hGLAco group than in the scAAV8.wtsp‐hGLA group. Specifically, the enzyme activities in the heart and spleen in the scAAV8.sp21‐hGLA group were 2.5 and 1.4 times higher than those in the scAAV8.wtsp‐hGLAco group, respectively (Figure 4C). α‐Gal A expression was determined via immunohistochemical analysis of liver, heart, and kidney tissues, consistent with the results of enzyme activity analysis (Figure 4D). The α‐Gal A glycosylation profiles of these four vectors were also assessed, in which α‐Gal A exhibited intact glycosylation sites (Figure S6), consistent with our previous result (Figure 2E).

The biological function of overexpressed α‐Gal A was assessed via the reduction of Lyso‐Gb3 in plasma and peripheral tissues, serving as an indicator. The findings revealed a significant reduction in Lyso‐Gb3 levels across all treated groups, with levels decreasing to less than 5% of those in the control group (Figure 4E). Specifically, the mean plasma Lyso‐Gb3 levels were reduced to 2.2%, 2.7%, 1.3%, and 0.7% of those in untreated Fabry mice in the scAAV8‐wtsp‐hGLA‐, scAAV8‐sp21‐hGLA‐, scAAV8‐wtsp‐hGLAco‐, and scAAV8‐sp21‐hGLAco‐treated groups, respectively. Corresponding reductions in Lyso‐Gb3 levels were observed in the liver (0.8%, 0.7%, 0.2%, and 0.1%) and kidney (4.3%, 2.7%, 0.8%, and 0.3%). In the heart, Lyso‐Gb3 levels in most treated mice fell below the limit of quantification. The reduction of Lyso‐Gb3 in plasma and peripheral tissues showed a trend toward being greater in the scAAV8‐sp21‐hGLAco group (Figure 4E), which is consistent with the corresponding enzyme activity measurements (Figure 4C), although a definitive conclusion could not be drawn due to the low absolute Lyso‐Gb3 levels for most groups. Nevertheless, tissue α‐Gal A activities differed across groups, and vectors achieving higher enzyme levels may potentially allow therapeutic efficacy to be reached at lower doses.

Previous studies have demonstrated that high‐dose systemic AAV administration can cause acute and subacute liver injury [29, 38]. To evaluate the potential toxicity of the rAAV vectors, liver tissues were harvested for pathological evaluation 14 weeks post‐administration. Hematoxylin and eosin staining revealed no signs of inflammation or damage in the liver (Figure S7).

α‐Gal A Produced in Hepatocytes Is Taken Up by Kidney Cells via the M6PR Pathway

Given the therapeutic regimen, rAAV‐mediated human GLA is expressed primarily in the liver and is subsequently taken up by secondary tissues, particularly renal and cardiac tissues, through intercellular crosstalk, as demonstrated in gene therapies for several diseases [39, 40]. To assess whether α‐Gal A secreted by hepatocytes can be efficiently internalized by human GLA‐deficient cells, human GLA knockout (KO) 293T cells, which are kidney‐derived cells, were generated via the CRISPR/Cas9 technique (Figure 5A,B).

Enzyme replacement therapy for lysosomal storage diseases relies on mannose‐6‐phosphate (M6P) receptor‐mediated endocytosis and subsequent trafficking into the lysosomes of targeted organs [41, 42]. In this process, the M6P residues of therapeutic enzymes bind to the cation‐independent mannose 6‐phosphate receptor (CI‐M6PR) expressed on the surface of targeted cells [43]. Having demonstrated that liver‐expressed α‐Gal A was detectable in the heart and kidneys of Fabry mice (Figures 2D and 4D), we investigated whether the cellular uptake of hGLAco‐expressed α‐Gal A was mediated via CI‐M6PR. HepG2 cells were transfected with pAAV.CB‐hGLAco, and enzyme activity assays revealed a significant increase in α‐Gal A activity in the culture supernatant 48 h post‐transfection (Figure 5C). The α‐Gal A‐enriched supernatant from transfected HepG2 cells was incubated with human GLA KO 293 T cells for 24 h. In the absence of exogenous M6P, intracellular α‐Gal A activity and expression significantly increased, indicating efficient α‐Gal A uptake by the human GLA KO 293 T cells. Meanwhile, α‐Gal A uptake was markedly reduced with the addition of 5 mM M6P, confirming that the cells internalized α‐Gal A via the M6PR pathway (Figure 5D–F).

Discussion

In this study, we reported that engineering the signal peptide increased the expression and secretion of α‐Gal A both in vitro and in vivo settings. The incorporation of a codon‐optimized human GLA further enhanced therapeutic efficacy in Fabry mice, leading to improved tissue distribution of α‐Gal A and effective clearance of Lyso‐Gb3 from key organs. These findings provide additional preclinical evidence supporting the potential clinical application of rAAV‐based therapy for Fabry disease.

Gene delivery to the liver has been proposed as a treatment strategy for Fabry disease [15, 17, 44]. The liver is thought to have the unique ability to induce immune tolerance rather than immunity [45], and α‐Gal A produced in the liver can be secreted into the circulation, thereby providing a steady supply of the enzyme for “cross‐correction” in peripheral tissues. Therefore, rAAV gene therapy targeting the liver was chosen for this study.

Although a high dose of rAAV vectors often improves therapeutic efficacy, it also raises significant safety concerns. In clinical treatments for Duchenne muscular dystrophy (DMD), for example, high‐dose AAV administration has led to patient fatalities [46]. Recent research has also shown that high‐dose AAVs can cause endothelial damage, thrombocytopenia, and complement activation [29]. To reduce the dosage and address safety concerns associated with rAAV gene therapy, we proposed increasing the expression and secretion of α‐Gal A through signal peptide engineering and codon optimization.

Signal peptide engineering, which is commonly used in protein expression studies [47, 48, 49], has been employed in preclinical studies and clinical trials (ClinicalTrials.gov: NCT04093349). In some proteins, heterogeneous signal peptides increase both expression and secretion [48], whereas in others, they primarily increase secretion by promoting protein export, resulting in reduced intracellular levels [49]. In the present study, the enhanced protein secretion could be attributed to the SP's general structure, specifically its positive‐charged N‐region and hydrophobic H‐region [50]. It has been shown that altering the native signal peptide sequence may increase its binding affinity for the signal recognition particle (SRP), facilitating efficient translocation into the Endoplasmic Reticulum (ER) lumen, which is a key rate‐limiting step [51]. However, SP efficiency is highly context‐dependent; a heterologous SP may be ineffective if it is incompatible with the mature protein sequence (the “passenger”) [52]. In this study, the increased production in both lysates and media could come from two factors. First, the optimized N‐terminal codons of certain heterologous SPs may enhance translation initiation rates [51]. Second, efficient translocation facilitated by these SPs prevents the accumulation of precursors in the cytoplasm, thereby reducing ER stress and subsequent proteolytic degradation, leading to higher overall protein yields [53]. Our study demonstrated that multiple heterogeneous signal peptides effectively enhanced α‐Gal A activity, and that the selected sp21 was able to increase its expression and secretion both in vitro and in vivo (Figures 1B,C, 2C, and 4B).

Codon optimization of the human GLA (hGLAco) significantly improved α‐Gal A expression in HepG2 cells, resulting in a 1.4‐fold increase in the cell lysate and a 4‐fold increase in α‐Gal A activity in the media compared with hGLA (Figure 3A,B,D). The difference between the increases in protein expression and enzyme activity suggested that more α‐Gal A was secreted from the cells. Our study also revealed that codon optimization of the therapeutic gene enhanced its in vivo efficacy at lower doses. The mean plasma α‐Gal A activity in the scAAV8.sp21‐hGLAco group was 3069.9 nmol/h/mL at 8 weeks post‐administration (Figure 4B), which was 6.9 times higher than the wild‐type human GLA gene. While specific codon frequencies differ slightly between humans and mice, the overall codon preference trends remain highly consistent. This conservation likely explains why human‐optimized sequences yielded improved expression in our human cell line study and mouse study (Figures 3A and 4D).

Previous studies have reported that AAV‐expressed lysosomal hydrolases are taken up by cells via the M6PR pathway [15, 40]. In a patient with Fabry disease, the highest concentration of toxic Gb3 was observed in the kidneys, followed by the heart and liver [1]. In this study, we conducted a similar experiment using the GLA‐KO human cell line 293T, a kidney‐derived cell line, which more accurately models the disease phenotype in vitro. The results aligned with those of prior studies, indicating that α‐Gal A with codon optimization was internalized by cells through the M6PR pathway (Figure 5C,D). Our study revealed that α‐Gal A, expressed in hepatocytes, was successfully taken up by kidney cells, which may explain the ability of the vectors to clear accumulated Lyso‐Gb3 in other organs (Figure 4D,E).

Currently, in all clinical stage rAAV gene therapy for Fabry disease, GLA cDNAs were codon optimized, although the sequences were different due to the use of different parameters. Regarding promoter selection, liver‐specific promoters were chosen in ST920, FLT190, and AMT‐191 to restrict expression in hepatocytes. In contrast, a ubiquitous CAG promoter is used in 4D‐310, while a synthetic HC7 promoter targeting both liver and muscle tissues is used in EXG110. The choice of AAV capsids is equally heterogeneous: AAV6 (ST920), 4D‐C102, an engineered heart‐tropic capsid (4D‐310), AAV9 variant (EXG110), AAVS3 (FLT190), and AAV5 (AMT‐191). In our study, robust Lyso‐Gb3 reduction was achieved to less than 5% of Fabry mouse levels at a dose of 5 × 1012 vg/kg (Figure 4E), demonstrating competitive potency with EXG110 (2 × 1012 vg/kg) and FLT190 (2 × 1012 vg/kg) [17, 54]. It was shown that a significantly higher dose (5 × 1013 vg/kg) was required to obtain comparable cardiac substrate clearance in another study [15], underscoring the importance of α‐Gal A with high secretory efficiency. Recent studies have also shown that rAAV5 has a low prevalence of neutralizing antibodies [55, 56], and even the presence of such antibodies may not necessarily affect transgene expression [57]. Therefore, in clinical applications, the AAV5 capsid is also an option for developing rAAV gene therapy for Fabry disease. In our study, AAV8 was chosen because it has been shown to transduce mouse hepatocytes with high efficiency, which is important to evaluate α‐Gal A with engineered signal peptide in mouse models [58]. Some studies on repeated rAAV administration have shown that repeated dosing can be achieved through the modification of rAAV, presenting a potential option for re‐administration if the therapeutic effect weakens [59, 60].

Several limitations exist in this study. First, in the second animal study, Lyso‐Gb3 levels in most treatment groups were near or below the detection limit (Figure 4E), preventing a definitive comparison of Lyso‐Gb3 clearance among vectors. Second, only plasma Lyso‐Gb3 levels were reported, while Gb3, the primary substrate of α‐Gal A, was not quantified due to technical constraints in establishing a validated LC–MS/MS method at the time of the study. However, Lyso‐Gb3 is widely recognized as a more sensitive and reliable biomarker for monitoring disease progression and therapeutic efficacy in Fabry disease [36]. Third, current Fabry mouse models do not fully recapitulate the complex cardiac pathology observed in human patients [17, 61]. Fourth, current mouse models fail to identify enough Lyso‐Gb3 within the brain [62], which does not adequately simulate this clinical phenotype. A recent study utilized magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) to non‐invasively assess Gb3 levels in the brains of Fabry patients, providing quantitative neuroimaging evidence of Gb3 deposition [63]. Fifth, dysfunction of the autophagy‐lysosomal pathway is a significant pathological feature in patients [64]; however, their primary characteristics in mouse models are currently limited to small‐fibre neuropathy and hypertrophic cardiomyopathy [65, 66]. Sixth, although we assessed the safety of rAAV8 vectors only through histological analysis of the liver (Figure S7), we did not measure the plasma levels of alanine aminotransferase (ALT), aspartate aminotransferase (AST), anti‐α‐Gal A antibodies, or AAV8‐neutralizing antibodies during the first month post‐injection, all of which are important for safety evaluation in mouse models. However, a separate evaluation of the same expression cassette packaged in AAV5 showed minimal immunogenicity: no anti‐α‐Gal A antibodies were detected at 2 × 1012 vg/kg, and only low‐titer responses were observed in a minority of subjects at higher doses (6 × 1012 and 2 × 1013 vg/kg). Seventh, the degree of integration of the rAAV vectors was not measured in this study. rAAV also exhibits a degree of integration in non‐human primates (NHPs) and humans, which raises important safety considerations for the use of rAAV as a gene therapy vector [28].

In conclusion, our study highlights the potential of liver‐targeted rAAV gene therapy for Fabry disease, particularly when using an engineered signal peptide and a codon‐optimized transgene. The efficacy of the rAAV8 vector in reducing Lyso‐Gb3 levels and maintaining sustained α‐Gal A activity over extended periods provides a strong foundation for clinical translation. This gene therapy approach addresses key limitations of ERT and holds substantial potential to improve the therapeutic efficiency of rAAV gene therapy for Fabry disease. The present study provides proof‐of‐concept for improving α‐Gal A expression and substrate reduction, which are key determinants of clinical benefit in Fabry disease. In addition, a related AAV‐based therapeutic strategy using this hGLAco with engineered signal peptide is being evaluated in a clinical trial (CTR20244539).

Materials and Methods

Construction of rAAV Vector Plasmids

rAAV vector plasmids encoding human GLA expression cassettes fused with various signal peptides were constructed for secretion comparisons. All heterogeneous signal peptides replaced the native signal peptide of the human GLA gene (amino acids 1 ~ 31). Native human GLA cDNA was obtained via reverse transcription polymerase chain reaction (RT‐PCR) using RNA extracted from HKE293 cells as the template. The codon‐optimized human GLA (hGLAco) gene was synthesized by TSINGKE Biological Technology (TSINGKE, Beijing, China). The sequence was designed according to several optimization strategies to enhance α‐Gal A expression. First, rare human codons were replaced with frequently used synonymous codons. Second, the GC content was adjusted to enhance mRNA stability. Third, the sequences that could form strong secondary structures or cryptic splice sites were avoided using the program http://wangcomputing.com/assp/. Fourth, CpG islands were removed using the program http://www.bio‐soft.net/sms/cpg_island.html.

Cell Culture, Transfection, and Viral Infection

HEK293 cells, HepG2 cells, and Huh7 cells were obtained from ATCC and cultured in DMEM supplemented with 10% FBS (PAN Biotech, Aidenbach, Germany) and 1× penicillin/streptomycin (Solarbio, Beijing, China) at 37°C with 5% CO2.

For transfection, cells were seeded in 12‐well plates and grown overnight to approximately 70% confluence. Each well received a transfection mixture containing 0.75 μg of plasmid DNA and 1.5 μL of PEIpro (Polyplus, Illkirch‐Graffenstaden, France), which was prepared according to the manufacturer's instructions. Each plasmid was transfected in triplicate, and the experiments comparing α‐Gal A expression and secretion were independently repeated three times. Forty‐eight hours post‐transfection, both the cells and culture supernatants were collected for analysis.

For the rAAV8 infection assays, HepG2 and Huh7 cells were seeded in 96‐well plates at 2 × 104 cells per well and infected with rAAV8 vectors at multiplicities of infection (MOI) of 1 × 104, 1 × 105, and 1 × 106, respectively. Forty‐eight hours post‐infection, the media were harvested for measurement of α‐Gal A enzymatic activity.

rAAV Vector Production

rAAV vectors were produced by suspending human embryonic kidney (HEK293) cells following a triple plasmid co‐transfection method as described previously [67]. Briefly, a vector plasmid, an AAV helper plasmid, and an adenovirus functional helper plasmid pFΔ6 were co‐transfected into HEK293 cells cultured in a bioreactor at a 1:1:1 ratio. The transfected cells were harvested 3 days post‐transfection. rAAV vectors were purified via two rounds of cesium chloride gradient ultracentrifugation. After purification, the rAAV vectors were dialyzed in PBS containing 5% D‐sorbitol for 24 h with two buffer changes and then stored at −80°C until use. Purity was assessed using silver staining, and genome titers were quantified via qPCR. The primer pairs used for the human GLA gene (hGLA) were 5′‐GCCAGATTCCTGCATCAGTG‐3′ and 5′‐CTTCCAGCCTTCTGAGACCA‐3′, while the primers for the codon‐optimized human GLA (hGLAco) were 5′‐TTCGAAGTGTGGGAGAGACC‐3′ and 5′‐ACTCGTAGAAGCCCAGCTTT‐3′.

Animal Studies

Fabry mice (Gla KO; B6;129‐Glatm1Kul/J, stock no. 003535) were purchased from the Jackson Laboratory and were originally generated by Ohshima T and colleagues [65]. The mice were maintained under specific pathogen‐free (SPF) conditions with a 12‐h light/dark cycle, following the animal care protocols of Ebond Bio‐Medical Technology (Ebond, Chengdu, China). rAAV vectors were administered intravenously via the tail vein. The vectors were diluted in formulation buffer and injected at a volume of 200 μL per mouse into male mice aged 6–12 weeks. Blood samples were collected via retro‐orbital bleeding into EDTA K2‐coated tubes, and plasma was isolated by centrifugation at 2500×g for 10 min. At the designated time points, the mice were euthanized via PBS perfusion through the left ventricle under pentobarbital anesthesia. A portion of each tissue sample was snap‐frozen in liquid nitrogen and stored at −80°C for future use. Tissue samples frozen in liquid nitrogen were homogenized in radioimmunoprecipitation (RIPA) buffer (Beyotime, Shanghai, China) supplemented with 1% protease inhibitor cocktail (Bimake, Houston, TX). Protein concentrations were determined via the Enhanced BCA Protein Assay Kit (Beyotime, Shanghai, China).

Genomic DNA Purification and Quantification of the Vector Genome

Genomic DNA was extracted from liver tissues (20 mg per sample) via the TIANGEN Blood/Cell/Tissue Genomic DNA Extraction Kit (Tiangen Biotech, Beijing, China) following the manufacturer's protocol. DNA concentrations were measured via a Nanodrop One spectrophotometer. Viral genome (vg) copy numbers were quantified via qPCR via the ABI PowerUp SYBR Green Kit (Thermo Fisher Scientific, Waltham, MA) via the human GLA forward primer (5′‐GCCAGATTCCTGCATCAGTG‐3′) and reverse primer (5′‐CTTCCAGCCTTCTGAGACCA‐3′). PCR amplification was performed under the following cycling conditions: 2 min at 50°C, 10 min at 95°C, followed by 40 cycles of 30 s at 95°C and 1 min at 60°C. Amplification was detected via a QuantStudio 3 real‐time PCR system (Thermo Fisher Scientific, Waltham, MA). The genome copy number from liver samples was determined using a standard curve based on a linearized plasmid of known concentration, which served as the reference standard. The data were analyzed via QuantStudio Design and Analysis software.

Quantification of Human GLA mRNA in the Livers

Total RNA was extracted from liver tissues via an Animal RNA Isolation Kit (Foregene, Chengdu, China) following the manufacturer's instructions. cDNA was synthesized via the PrimeScript RT Reagent Kit with gDNA Eraser (Takara, Tokyo, Japan) according to the manufacturer's protocol. qPCR was performed via the ABI PowerUp SYBR Green Kit with custom primer targeting the human GLA cDNA transgene (forward primer: 5′‐GCCAGATTCCTGCATCAGTG‐3′, reverse primer: 5′‐CTTCCAGCCTTCTGAGACCA‐3′). A standard curve was generated via serial dilutions of the linearized plasmid.

α‐Gal A Enzymatic Activity Assay

α‐Gal A activity was measured via a fluorescence‐based enzymatic assay as previously described with slight modifications [68]. Briefly, 10 μL of diluted media, cell lysate, murine plasma, or tissue lysate was incubated with 20 μL of 2 mM 4‐methylumbelliferyl alpha‐D‐galactopyranoside (4MU‐α‐Gal; Santa Cruz, Dallas, TX) dissolved in assay buffer (0.1 M sodium citrate buffer, pH 4.6), along with 50 mM N‐acetylgalactosamine (Macklin, Shanghai, China) to inhibit α‐Galactosidase B activity [69]. After 30 min of incubation at 37°C, the reactions were stopped by adding 150 μL of 0.5 M Glycine‐NaOH buffer (pH 10.5). The amount of 4‐methylumbelliferone (4‐MU) produced was quantified by measuring fluorescence with a Biotek Synergy H1 microplate reader. α‐Gal A activity in conditioned media or plasma was expressed as nanomoles of 4‐MU produced per hour per milliliter (nmol/h/mL), while activity in tissues or cell lysates was expressed as nanomoles of 4‐MU produced per hour per milligram of total protein (nmol/h/mg).

Western Blot Analysis

Frozen liver tissue (~20 mg) was homogenized in 500 μL radioimmunoprecipitation (RIPA) buffer (Beyotime, Shanghai, China) supplemented with 1% protease inhibitor cocktail (Bimake, Houston, TX) via a High‐Speed Cryogenic Tissue Grinder (Servicebio, Wuhan, China) for two rounds (30 s pulse time, 60 Hz frequency). The crude liver lysates were centrifuged at 13000×g for 10 min at 4°C, and protein concentrations were measured via the Enhanced BCA Protein Assay Kit (Beyotime, Shanghai, China). Liver lysates were then digested with Endo H (endoglycosidase H) or PNGase F (peptide‐N‐glycosidase F) (NEW England Biolabs, Ipswich, MA) and analyzed via Western blot to assess the glycosylation pattern of the liver‐expressed α‐Gal A enzyme, following previously established methods.

Ten microliters of supernatant protein from HepG2 cells or 20 μg of total protein from liver homogenates were separated by SDS‐PAGE on 10% polyacrylamide gels. After transfer, the membranes were blocked with 5% skimmed milk (Sangon Biotech, Shanghai, China) and incubated with anti‐α‐Gal A antibodies (R&D Systems, Minneapolis, MN, or Proteintech, Wuhan, China), along with anti‐HRP‐β‐actin (ABclonal, Wuhan, China) or anti‐HRP‐GAPDH (Glyceraldehyde 3‐Phosphate Dehydrogenase) (Proteintech, Wuhan, China) as loading controls. The membranes were washed and then incubated with the corresponding secondary antibody (ABclonal, Wuhan, China), followed by visualization via a chemiluminescence imaging system (Bio‐Rad, Hercules, CA).

Analysis of the Cellular Uptake of the a‐Gal A Enzyme

Human GLA‐knockout HEK293T cells were generated via the CRISPR/Cas9 genome editing technique. Three different guide RNAs (guideRNA1: 5′‐CACC GCTAGCTGGCGAATCCCATG‐3′, guideRNA2: 5′‐CACC GACCCTCAGCGCTTTCCTCA‐3′, guideRNA3: 5′‐CACC GTCTGCCTTCTGAATCTCTT‐3′) targeting the first exon of the human GLA gene were designed via the web‐based tool CRISPOR (http://crispor.gi.ucsc.edu/). Human GLA‐knockout HEK293T cells were cultured for 24 h in the presence of α‐Gal A‐enriched HepG2 cell supernatant, with or without 5 mM mannose 6‐phosphate (M6P; Aladdin, Shanghai, China). The cell supernatants and lysates were collected and analyzed for α‐Gal A activity and expression via the described protocols. The supernatant from mock‐transfected HepG2 cells served as a negative control.

Immunohistochemistry and Histopathology

The animals were perfused with PBS before organ collection. The tissue samples were fixed in 4% paraformaldehyde overnight, processed for paraffin embedding and then sectioned at a thickness of 6 μm. After antigen retrieval, the sections were incubated with anti‐α‐Gal A antibody (5 μg/mL; R&D Systems, Minneapolis, MN) for 60 min in PBS containing 1% BSA and 0.01% Triton X‐100, followed by an additional 60‐min incubation with anti‐sheep HRP secondary antibody. Signal detection was performed using the 3,3′‐diaminobenzidine (DAB) ChromoMap kit, and the slides were counterstained with hematoxylin II and blue reagent before dehydration. For the immunofluorescence staining of α‐Gal A, the cells were first fixed with 4% paraformaldehyde and blocked in 0.01 M PBS buffer containing 0.1% Triton X and 1% BSA. The primary antibody was a mouse anti‐α‐Gal A monoclonal antibody (Proteintech, Wuhan, China), and the secondary antibody was a goat anti‐mouse IgG conjugated with Alexa Fluor 488 (Thermo Fisher Scientific, Waltham, MA). Imaging was performed using a Nikon AXR microscope. For hematoxylin and eosin (H&E) staining, the tissue sections were dehydrated through a graded ethanol series, cleared in xylene, embedded in paraffin, and cut into 6‐μm sections, followed by staining according to standard protocols.

Plasma and Tissue Lyso‐Gb3 Quantitation

The levels of Lyso‐Gb3 were quantified via liquid chromatography‐mass spectrometry (LC–MS) as described previously [15, 70, 71]. Tissue and plasma Lyso‐Gb3 concentrations were determined at XPiscoric Inc. (XPiscoric, Chengdu, China). A total of 20 μL of tissue lysates or plasma samples were diluted in methanol (MeOH) and mixed with internal standards, including glucosyl (β) sphingosine (GSG) and Lyso‐Gb3, for 3 min on an orbital shaker. The mixture was then centrifuged at 15000 rpm for 10 min. A volume of 190 μL of the processed tissue or plasma sample was injected into an integrated LC–MS/MS system. The analytes were separated via an Ultimate XB‐C18 column and detected in positive ionization mode.

Statistical Analysis

All the data presented in this study are reported as the means ± SEM. Statistical analyses were conducted via GraphPad Prism 8 software. Data sets were analyzed by one‐way ANOVA with Tukey's or Dunnett's post hoc correction and multiple t‐tests with Sidak‐Bonferroni post hoc correction for parametric tests. For non‐parametric tests, unpaired two‐tailed Student's t‐tests were applied. The statistical analyses performed for each data set are indicated in the figure legends. For all figures, statistical significance is denoted as follows: *p < 0.05, **p < 0.01, ***p < 0.001, ****p < 0.0001. A significant cutoff of p < 0.05 was applied for all tests.
