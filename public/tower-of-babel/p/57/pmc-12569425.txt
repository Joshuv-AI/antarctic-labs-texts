Gut Microbiota and CADASIL: Emerging Insights and Future Directions
Authors: Jay Chol Choi
Published: 2025

A recent study that explored the gut microbiota of cerebral autosomal dominant arteriopathy with subcortical infarcts and leukoencephalopathy (CADASIL) patients found clear differences relative to healthy controls.1 While the overall microbial diversity was similar among the samples, with no significant variations in α-diversity, β-diversity differed significantly between CADASIL and controls (p=0.015). In particular, specific bacterial taxa were altered; for example, Bacteroides dorei was more abundant in CADASIL patients. The authors discovered metabolic changes alongside these compositional shifts, with the bacterial metabolite indole being significantly elevated in CADASIL fecal samples compared with controls. Notably, the indole level was positively correlated with overrepresentation of Streptococcus species (including S. mutans and S. parasanguinis) in CADASIL patients. These findings replicate and extend earlier observations of gut dysbiosis in CADASIL, now linking microbial changes to functional consequences (tryptophan metabolite levels).2,3 The authors point out that both indole and Streptococcus species have been implicated in atherosclerosis, suggesting that CADASIL-associated dysbiosis contributes to vascular pathology via proatherogenic or proinflammatory mechanisms. This raises the intriguing possibility that the gut microbiome is an environmental modifier in a disease classically defined by a single gene mutation.

CADASIL is caused by pathogenic NOTCH3 mutations, yet patients with the same mutation often show markedly varying severities and ages at onset.4,5 This phenotypic heterogeneity implies that factors beyond the genome—including environmental influences—modulate disease expression.6 Indeed, recent CADASIL studies have highlighted roles for vascular risk factors and systemic exposures. A meta-analysis showed that intracerebral hemorrhage was associated with hypertension and a heavy cerebral microbleed burden, underscoring that modifiable factors such as blood pressure can affect CADASIL outcomes.7 Even acute infections and inflammation may act as triggers, since there are reports of SARS-CoV-2 infection precipitating ischemic events or unmasking CADASIL in previously undiagnosed individuals.8,9 Within this broader landscape, the gut microbiome constitutes a newly recognized contributor to CADASIL pathophysiology. There is increasing evidence linking gut dysbiosis to systemic inflammation and vascular dysfunction in various neurologic diseases. Liu et al.3 demonstrated that CADASIL patients harbor an altered gut microbial community with functional consequences, with CADASIL-associated microbes showing depleted vitamin metabolic pathways accompanied by elevated inflammatory cytokines. Intriguingly, that multiomics study identified a patient-derived bacterium (Fusobacterium varium) that worsened neurobehavioral deficits and induced systemic inflammation in a CADASIL mouse model. These findings reinforce the concept of a brain-gut-microbiome axis in CADASIL. The novel report in the Journal of Clinical Neurology of the discovery of increased indole (a microbiota-produced tryptophan metabolite) and Streptococci species of oral origin in CADASIL aligns with this paradigm. Indole and its derivatives (e.g., indoxyl sulfate) can promote vascular inflammation and atherosclerosis, and S. mutans and S. parasanguinis have been found within atherosclerotic plaques.10,11 Together these data suggest that alongside the NOTCH3 mutation, chronic vascular inflammation fueled by gut-derived metabolites and translocated oral bacteria can contribute to small-vessel damage in CADASIL. This hypothesis is consistent with recent insights that traditional risk factors and inflammatory processes significantly influence the clinical course of CADASIL patients.

These novel insights reveal exciting avenues for research and potential clinical applications. First, the gut microbiome and its metabolic products—could serve as a source of biomarkers for CADASIL. For example, the consistent elevation of fecal indole in CADASIL patients may represent a noninvasive marker of disease activity or progression if this is validated in larger cohorts. More broadly, machine-learning models integrating microbiota profiles with immune or metabolic indicators have shown promise in distinguishing CADASIL patients from controls (with one study achieving a classification AUC of 0.89).3 Such approaches might eventually complement genetic testing by providing insight into disease states, such as by identifying patients at a higher risk of stroke or cognitive decline based on their microbiome-inflammatory signatures. Second, these findings support the need to consider microbiome-targeted interventions. If certain bacteria or metabolites are confirmed to aggravate CADASIL pathology, strategies such as dietary modification, probiotics, and selective antibiotics could be explored for mitigating those effects. Nonetheless, several limitations should be acknowledged, including the small, single-center, and cross-sectional design with the use of family controls without functional validation. These constraints highlight the need for larger, longitudinal, and mechanistic studies to confirm the role of the microbiome in CADASIL.

In conclusion, studies of the gut microbiota in CADASIL patients provide a fresh perspective on how a monogenic small-vessel disease can be modulated by the microbial milieu. This aligns with a growing recognition that the clinical course of CADASIL patients is influenced not only by genes but also by environmental and systemic factors. Understanding the interactions between gut microbes and cerebral small vessels would facilitate the more-holistic management of CADASIL, whereby gene-based diagnoses could be complemented by greater attention being paid to the “second genome” in the gut.
