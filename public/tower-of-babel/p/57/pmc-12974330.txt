A Hybrid Nanosystem for Prostate Cancer Therapy: Codelivery of Enzalutamide and Curcumin via Selenium‐Embedded Mesoporous Silica and Chitosan Nanoparticles
Authors: Zahra Tavakoli, Khosro Khajeh, Bijan Ranjbar
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractProstate cancer is the second most common cancer globally, causing ≈396,792 deaths in 2022. Early diagnosis and advanced drug delivery are vital to prevent its progression. This research leverages the anticancer properties of selenium nanoparticles and enzalutamide, a leading prostate cancer drug, by coencapsulating them within mesoporous silica nanoparticles (MSNPs). MSNPs offer advantages for drug delivery, including high surface accessibility and a tunable porous structure. The results indicated that MSNPs synthesized via solvent extraction, yielding a specific surface area of 1017.4 m2/g, a pore volume of 0.2531 cm3/g, and an average pore size of ≈10 nm, were superior to those obtained by calcination, which yielded a smaller pore size (≈3 nm). Enzalutamide was loaded into these selenium‐embedded MSNPs, achieving a drug loading efficiency of 76.3 ± 0.5%. Separately, curcumin was encapsulated in chitosan nanoparticles with high efficiency (83.2 ± 0.7%). The combined nanosystem enables pH‐responsive, gradual drug release that mimics the tumor microenvironment. MTT assays confirmed the drug‐loaded system exerts significantly stronger, time‐ and concentration‐dependent anticancer effects than the free drug. Furthermore, curcumin plays a vital role in enhancing anticancer efficacy and inducing apoptosis. This research demonstrates that the designed dual‐nanoparticle system is a promising candidate for targeted prostate cancer therapy.Keywords: curcumin, enzalutamide, mesoporous silica, prostate cancer, selenium

Introduction

Prostate cancer is the second most common cancer in men and age is the most significant risk factor. The extended latency period characteristic of prostate cancer makes it particularly amenable to innovative treatment approaches and the early detection of the disease can increase the survival rate substantially [1]. Current therapeutic interventions span multiple modalities, including surgical therapy (invasive approach), radiation therapy, androgen deprivation therapy (hormone‐based treatment), and systemic therapies (chemotherapy, immunotherapy, or targeted agents). The androgen receptor (AR), in addition to its established anabolic and reproductive functions, plays a critical oncogenic role in tumorigenesis and metastasis across multiple malignancies, including prostate, bladder, kidney, lung, breast, and liver cancers. As a member of the nuclear receptor superfamily, AR contains three functional domains, the N‐terminal domain (NTD), the conserved DNA‐binding domain (DBD), and the conserved ligand‐binding domain (LBD) [2, 3]. Mechanistically, testosterone and dihydrotestosterone bind to the LBD, inducing structural conformational changes, facilitating nuclear translocation, DNA interaction, and modulation of prostate‐specific antigen levels. Current therapeutic strategies employ AR antagonists that disrupt this signaling cascade, with several nonsteroidal antiandrogens (NSAAs) receiving clinical approval for advanced prostate cancer treatment. Enzalutamide, a second‐generation antiandrogen, represents significant advancements in prostate cancer therapy due to its high affinity to AR compared to first‐generation antagonists, strong efficacy, convenient administration, and ability to delay disease progression across various stages of prostate cancer [4, 5, 6]. Conventional therapies face critical limitations, including poor solubility, lower bioavailability, restricted cellular uptake, and rapid drug degradation, which hinder their clinical efficacy and prevent the achievement of definitive therapeutic outcomes. Nanotechnology has emerged as a transformative solution in oncological management, with particular promise in cancer treatment [7, 8]. Various nanostructures can effectively reduce drug toxicity (side effects), enhance bioactivity, improve drug loading capacity, control release, surface modification, and facilitate smart targeting [9, 10, 11, 12]. Among different candidate materials, nanoparticles made of selenium, mesoporous silica, chitosan, and curcumin are particularly promising due to their unique characteristics. Selenium‐based nanoparticles (Se NPs) represent a promising biocompatible material with antimicrobial, anti‐inflammatory, and anticancer properties (Table 1). As an essential trace element incorporated into selenoproteins, selenium plays a crucial role in maintaining physiological redox balance [19, 20]. While selenium offers numerous benefits, its therapeutic application faces challenges due to toxicity at high doses. Nanotechnology provides a potential solution to this limitation [21, 22]. However, the Se NPs aggregate into dark orange precipitates and coating with mesoporous silica can overcome this obstacle. The unique properties of mesoporous silica include flexibility in modifying various surfaces (via available silanol functional groups), distinct structural properties such as mesopore size, surface area, pore volume, and controllable morphology. These nanosystems can encapsulate hydrophilic and hydrophobic molecules with similar loading efficiency due to the large number of pores [23]. In recent years, mesoporous silica‐based nanoparticles (MSNPs) have attracted increased attention from researchers due to their low toxicity, high therapeutic efficacy, and ability to accumulate at the site of disease [7]. Therefore, this research investigated the enzalutamide loading in Se‐embedded mesoporous silica NPs (Se@MSNPs). To enhance the oral bioavailability of the insoluble drug enzalutamide, Lee et al. developed a self‐nanoemulsifying drug delivery system (SNEDDS). The most effective formulation, which prevented rapid drug recrystallization, consisted of Labrafac PG, Solutol HS15, and Transcutol P. This system maintained a stable nanoemulsion and showed excellent drug release across different pH environments. In animal studies, the pharmacokinetic data revealed that this formulation achieved superior oral absorption compared to both unformulated enzalutamide powder and a commercially available product [24]. Researchers also created a folic acid‐coated NPs system for enzalutamide. This design leverages the fact that many cancer cells overexpress folate receptors. The formulation demonstrated significantly greater stability and efficacy in killing cancer cells in vitro compared to the non‐encapsulated or pure drug [25].

In addition, it is expected that this designed nanosystem, along with curcumin‐loaded chitosan nanoparticles (Cur‐CS NPs), will increase cancer cells’ sensitivity to the drug and enhance its effectiveness. Several studies have explored curcumin‐loaded nanocarriers, particularly those based on amphiphilic copolymers [26, 27]. For instance, Chuah et al. developed chitosan nanoparticles containing curcumin as an adhesive carrier for colon cancer treatment [28]. Rai et al. investigated the oral administration of chitosan‐bound curcumin nanoparticles in mice infected with a lethal strain of Plasmodium yoelii. Their results demonstrated that plasma and red blood cell concentrations of curcumin were significantly higher in mice treated with chitosan‐curcumin nanoparticles compared to those receiving pure curcumin [29]. Moreover, the nanoparticle formulation enabled sustained curcumin release over 8 h, whereas pure curcumin levels sharply declined after 3 h and became undetectable by 6 h. In prostate cancer research, Riviero et al. developed an antibody‐conjugated mesoporous silica nanosystem for docetaxel delivery, demonstrating 25% higher cellular uptake in LNCaP cancer cells than free docetaxel, along with a twofold increase in cytotoxicity [30]. Meanwhile, Li et al. revealed that curcumin enhances bicalutamide's antitumor effects in androgen‐independent prostate cancer by suppressing ERK1/2 and SAPK/JNK‐mediated p65 signaling, subsequently downregulating MUC1‐C expression [31]. Further innovations include Chen et al.'s glutathione‐responsive nanoparticles codelivering docetaxel and enzalutamide, achieving over 70% cancer cell uptake and superior growth inhibition compared to nontargeted formulations [32]. For breast cancer treatment, Lin et al. developed PEG‐coated mesoporous silica nanoparticles for simultaneous paclitaxel and curcumin delivery, enabling efficient intravenous administration of hydrophobic drugs and demonstrating synergistic cytotoxic effects on cancer cells [33]. The findings underscore nanotechnology's transformative potential in developing precision cancer therapies with improved efficacy and safety profiles.

Herein, we used two different chemical methods (solvent extraction versus calcination) to synthesize MSNPs and develop optimized Se@MSNPs with larger pore sizes using cetyltrimethylammonium bromide (CTAB), tetraethylorthosilicate (TEOS), sodium selenite as the Se precursor, and ascorbic acid as a reductant. Then, dynamic light scattering (DLS), Fourier transform infrared (FTIR), X‐ray diffraction (XRD), scanning electron microscopy (SEM), and transmission electron microscopy (TEM) were used to characterize the properties, functional groups, and surface morphologies of the synthesized NPs. In the next step, Se@MSNPs are loaded with enzalutamide. Additionally, to improve anticancer activity and increase curcumin bioavailability, a chitosan‐based nanosystem containing curcumin was prepared via ionotropic gelation with sodium tripolyphosphate. Subsequently, the studied cell lines (DU145 and LNCaP) were treated with the synthesized nanoparticles, and the optimal drug concentration and exposure time in the culture medium were determined using the MTT assay and Hoechst staining was performed to assess apoptosis.

Material and Methods

Sodium selenite (Na2SeO3), ammonium fluoride (NH4F), ascorbic acid (C6H6O6), acetic acid (C2H4O2), triphenyl tetrazolium chloride (TTC), thiazolyl blue tetrazolium bromide (MTT), and low molecular weight chitosan (CS) with a molecular weight of 50 kDa and 85% deacetylation degree were purchased from Sigma–Aldrich. Additionally, curcumin, sodium tripolyphosphate (TPP), cetyltrimethylammonium bromide (CTAB), tetraethyl orthosilicate (TEOS), sodium chloride (NaCl), potassium chloride (KCl), di‐sodium hydrogen phosphate (Na2HPO4), potassium dihydrogen phosphate (KH2PO4) for synthesis phosphate‐buffered saline (PBS), acetic acid glycial (99%), and sodium acetate (CH3COONa) for synthesis acetate buffer were also obtained from Merck. Deionized water was used for preparing all solutions during the synthesis process. For cell culture, fetal bovine serum (FBS) and DMEM medium were obtained from Gibco.

The Fabrication and Optimization of Selenium‐Embedded Mesoporous Silica

In summary, the synthesis was initiated by dissolving 0.38 g of CTAB, 1g of ascorbic acid, and 0.5 g of ammonium fluoride (NH4F) in 100 mL of deionized water. The resulting solution was stirred at 1500 rpm, and the temperature was increased to 80°C. Subsequently, 4.16 mL of an aqueous solution of Na2SeO3 (0.25 g/mL) was added dropwise to the mixture, which was stirred for 1 h. Upon adding Na2SeO3, the solution changed color from colorless to yellowish–orange. Concurrently, 2 mL of TEOS is added dropwise to the mixture, which is stirred for 2 h. The solution is then cooled to room temperature, and the resulting particles are collected by filtration [34, 35]. Then, two different approaches were used to ensure complete surfactant removal from the pores and to form mesoporous structures. In one manner, a series of solvent extraction processes is employed to ensure thorough washing of the product structures. Distilled water and ethanol are used to wash the products three times each. Subsequently, the particles were dispersed in a methanol/hydrochloric acid solution (1:8 v/v) three times (HCl concentration ≈0.6 M) [36]. Finally, distilled water and ethanol are used once more to neutralize the remaining acid. Ultrasonic cleaning is performed for 4 min, followed by a centrifugation step at 12,000 rpm for 10 min [33].

As an alternative method for the construction of selenium‐embedded mesoporous silica, the synthesized nanoparticles were dried at 80°C for 10 h and calcined at 600°C for 6 h. These processes were employed to remove CTAB and promote the formation of mesoporous structures (Figure 1). After this, the mesoporous silica structures formed by both methods were analyzed using BET to determine optimal pore accessibility, size, and volume [23].

Nanoparticles Characterization Methods

A series of analytical tests was conducted to ascertain the successful synthesis of the NPs. First, data obtained from the BET method were analyzed to determine the surfactant removal strategy for MSNPs. The BET system operates by measuring the volume of nitrogen gas adsorbed and desorbed by the material's surface at liquid nitrogen temperature (77 K). After placing the sample cell in a liquid nitrogen bath, the adsorbed gas volume is calculated by gradually increasing the nitrogen pressure in incremental steps. Subsequently, the desorbed volume is measured as pressure decreases, ultimately generating an adsorption/desorption isotherm plot based on relative pressure at constant temperature [38]. This linear BET plot, also called an adsorption/desorption isotherm, is used to derive the material's effective surface area. The following equation defines the specific surface area (SBET):

In this equation, N represents Avogadro's number, a is the effective cross‐sectional area of an adsorbed molecule (in mm2), m denotes the mass of the tested sample (in grams), and 22,400 refers to the volume occupied by 1 mol of adsorbed gas under standard conditions (in mL). Adsorption is typically described by isotherms, which quantify the amount of adsorbed substance as a function of pressure (for gases) or concentration (for liquids) at constant temperature. Conversely, the desorption isotherm is derived by measuring the volume of gas released during desorption. These isotherms, adsorption and desorption, characterize the material's surface properties and pore structure, enabling the calculation of critical parameters, such as specific surface area, for optimizing drug delivery systems [39].

Adsorption isotherms can be classified into six groups. Among these, Type IV isotherms are particularly significant as they are characteristic of mesoporous structures. This type of isotherm is commonly observed in mesoporous materials, where capillary condensation occurs in the mesopores, leading to a distinct hysteresis loop in the adsorption–desorption curve. After using BET analysis (BELSORP MINI II), the synthesized Se@MSNPs were characterized for their physicochemical properties. First, hydrodynamic diameter and surface charge were determined by DLS and zeta potential measurements (Zetasizer Nano ZS). Subsequently, Fourier‐transform infrared (FTIR) spectroscopy (Nicolet IR 100) was used to identify characteristic functional groups. The crystallographic structure was elucidated by X‐ray diffraction (XRD; Philips X’Pert MPD). Finally, morphological features, size distribution, and porous architecture were directly imaged using field‐emission scanning electron microscopy (FE‐SEM; Mira 3‐XMU) and transmission electron microscopy (TEM; Zeiss‐EM10C).

Enzalutamide Loading Into Se‐Embedded Mesoporous Silica Nanoparticles

Se@MSNPs were soaked in a concentrated drug mixture containing 0.05 g of enzalutamide and chloroform to load the drug molecules into the pores. After shaking at 130 rpm at 37°C for 19 h in a shaker incubator, the drug‐loaded nanoparticles and free drug were separated by centrifugation at 13,000 rpm for 10 min. FTIR analysis was then performed to confirm the presence of drug agents [25].

Preparation of Cur‐CS NPs

Cur‐CS NPs were prepared using an ionotropic gelation method with sodium tripolyphosphate (TPP) as a cross‐linker [40]. Briefly, 10 mL of an aqueous TPP solution (1 mg/mL, pH 5) was added dropwise to 17.5 mL of a CS solution (1 mg/mL in 1% acetic acid). For Cur‐CS NPs, curcumin (4 mg/mL) was incorporated into the CS solution before cross‐linking.

The formulation yielded nonaggregated NPs, which remained stable overnight under magnetic stirring. The particles were then isolated by centrifugation (8000 rpm, 4°C, and 15 min). The resulting pellet was washed three times with 10% aqueous ethanol to remove unreacted reagents.

The residual enzalutamide and curcumin in the supernatant were quantified using UV spectrophotometry at a wavelength of 236 and 430 nm, respectively [41, 42, 43]. Drug Release Studies

In vitro drug release assessment of enzalutamide and curcumin was performed according to the previously reported methods. Equal amounts of Se@MSNPs‐Enz or Cur‐CS NPs were dispersed in 3 mL of pH‐specific buffer (acetate buffer, pH 5 or PBS, pH 7.4, each containing 0.1% w/v Tween 80) and loaded into the dialysis bag (12 kDa). The sealed bags were immersed in 100 mL of a release medium (30% ethanol: 70% buffer) and agitated at 180 rpm and 37°C. At predetermined time intervals, 1 mL of the release medium was collected. After each sampling, the volume was replaced with fresh medium (the sink condition was maintained throughout the drug release, ensuring that the drug concentration in the release medium never exceeded 10% of its saturation solubility). The absorbance of the collected samples was measured at 236 nm for enzalutamide and 430 nm for curcumin using a UV spectrophotometer. The concentration of the released drug was determined from a pre‐established standard curve. The cumulative drug release was plotted as a function of time [24, 41].

Cell Culture Preparation of Prostate Cancer Cells

Prostate cancer cell lines (LNCaP and DU145) were cultured in DMEM medium supplemented with 10% FBS, penicillin, and streptomycin. The cells were maintained in a humidified incubator at 37°C under 5% CO2 to ensure optimal growth conditions.

Analysis of Cell Survival

The MTT method was used to evaluate the nanostructure's toxicity against cancer cells. The cancer cells were cultivated in a cell incubator maintained at 37°C with 5% CO2. These cells were seeded into 96‐well plates at a final volume of 100 μl during the logarithmic growth phase. Following a 1‐night incubation period, the cells were exposed to varying concentrations of the NPs to determine the appropriate IC50 (dose‐ or time‐dependent). The MTT assay evaluated two prostate cancer cell lines (DU145 and LNCaP) treated with enzalutamide (ranging from 5 to 30 µg/mL) [45], Se@MSNPs‐Enz (ranging from 5 to 30 µg/mL) alone or in combination with Cur‐CS NPs (15.41 µg/mL) [46] over 24–48 h of incubation. The culture medium itself was utilized as the control group. Subsequently, 31.5 μL of a 5 mg/mL MTT solution was added to the PBS and incubated for an additional 4 h. Subsequently, the culture medium was replaced with 200 μL of DMSO, and a Bio‐Rad microplate reader was used to measure absorbance at 570 nm [47]. Hoechst Staining

Before this step, based on the results of the previous step, the two cell lines, DU145 and LNCaP, will be treated with the studied nanostructures for 48 h. This technique is beneficial for studying apoptosis (programmed cell death) and for detecting cell nuclei in microscopic studies. Hoechst dyes are fluorescent dyes that bind to DNA. These dyes belong to the bisbenzimide class and exhibit a high degree of specificity for adenine‐ and thymine‐rich (A–T) regions of DNA. Two prevalent types of these dyes are Hoechst 33342 and Hoechst 33258. These dyes exhibit blue or purple fluorescence when exposed to UV or blue light, thereby enabling the visualization of cell nuclei under a fluorescent microscope. Apoptosis, a natural, programmed process of cell death, plays a role in the growth, development, and maintenance of homeostasis [48]. Hoechst staining can reveal morphological alterations in the nuclei of cells undergoing apoptosis. Distinct changes in the cell nucleus that can be detected by Hoechst staining occur during apoptosis. The nucleus of normal cells is uniformly and homogeneously stained, and its shape is round or oval. In contrast, the nucleus of apoptotic cells undergoes significant alterations, resulting in a distinct staining pattern. In such cases, the nucleus undergoes a series of morphological changes, including condensation and fragmentation. These alterations manifest as minute fluorescent dots or fragments within the nucleus. In this method, after a 24‐hour culture in a 96‐well plate, the cells are washed with PBS, and then Hoechst dye dissolved in the buffer is added. Images are then captured using a fluorescent microscope at 40x magnification, enabling the examination of cell apoptosis [49].

Results and Discussion

Optimization and Characterization of Nanoparticles

Mesoporous silica has emerged as an ideal drug‐delivery candidate due to its exceptional biocompatibility, nontoxicity, and chemical inertness. In this study, we systematically evaluated two distinct surfactant (CTAB) removal approaches (solvent extraction vs. calcination) to optimize the mesostructure for enzalutamide loading. The uniform pore architecture, high surface area, and tunable pore size of mesoporous silica make it particularly suitable for drug delivery applications [50]. In the process of drug delivery to target sites, the accessible surface area and pore size of synthesized nanoparticles play a crucial role in determining efficacy.

For the synthesis of mesoporous silica nanoparticles optimized for drug delivery applications, a high specific surface area is unequivocally superior to a low one. This is because the primary mechanism for drug incorporation into mesoporous silica is adsorption onto the extensive internal surface of the pore walls. A high specific surface area provides a vast number of binding sites for drug molecules. This directly translates to a higher drug‐loading capacity, allowing more therapeutic agent to be carried per unit mass of the carrier, which is crucial for efficacy and minimizing the dose of inert material administered.

However, a high specific surface area alone is insufficient if the pores are inaccessible. This is where pore size becomes critical. Large pores are overwhelmingly preferred over small pores. While small pores may offer a high surface area, they pose a significant risk of pore blockage, hindering both the loading of drug molecules and, more importantly, their controlled release. Larger pores, typically 4–10 nm or larger, are essential for several reasons. Firstly, they can accommodate larger drug molecules, such as proteins, antibodies, or genetic material, which are increasingly important in modern therapeutics. Secondly, large pores facilitate better diffusion of drug molecules into the depths of the pores during the loading process and out of the pores during the release phase, ensuring favorable release kinetics and preventing unwanted burst release. Furthermore, a related and vital parameter is pore volume. A high pore volume (often >1.0 cm3/g) is desirable, as it defines the physical space within the carrier available to host drug molecules. A combination of high specific surface area and large pore size naturally leads to a high pore volume, creating a spacious reservoir for drug storage.

So, in this study, the BET method was employed to calculate SBET from adsorption data over the relative pressure range 0.18–0.35. The results revealed that the calcination method yielded a specific surface area of 78.31 m2/g for mesoporous silica nanostructures [51]. The nitrogen adsorption–desorption isotherm matched the expected Type IV pattern for mesoporous materials (Figure 2). BJH (Barrett–Joyner–Halenda) analysis further determined a pore volume of 0.1843 cm3/g and an average pore size of ≈3 nm. In contrast, the solvent extraction surfactant removal method produced a significantly higher specific surface area (101.74 m2/g), along with a larger pore volume (0.2531 cm3/g) and pore size (≈10 nm). Given the higher specific surface area, larger pore size, shorter synthesis time, and enhanced surface/porosity metrics, the solvent extraction method was selected as the optimal synthesis approach. The high accessibility, tailored porosity, and suitable pore dimensions suggest effective drug loading both on the surface and within the nanoparticles, promising improved delivery efficiency.

Nitrogen adsorption–desorption isotherms and pore size distribution of mesoporous silica nanoparticles. (A) Calcination method, (B) solvent extraction method.

Also, the solvent extraction mechanism involves protonating the cationic surfactant's headgroup, converting it into a neutral, alcohol‐soluble species that can be washed out of the silica pores. In contrast, calcination requires temperatures between 400°C and 550°C to combust the organic template completely. In the calcination method, the selenium core would melt well before the surfactant is removed, leading to nanoparticles coalescing into large, bulk aggregates. This would completely negate the benefits of their nanoscale size and properties. Furthermore, at high temperatures in air, elemental selenium would oxidize to form selenium dioxide (SeO2), a volatile, toxic compound. This process would not only degrade the core but also pose a significant safety hazard. Therefore, calcination is not a viable option. The imperative is to use a low‐temperature surfactant‐removal strategy that preserves the integrity of the selenium nanoparticle, which is why solvent extraction is the preferred alternative.

Following the optimized synthesis of Se@MSNPs, DLS and zeta potential measurements revealed a hydrodynamic diameter of 156.7 nm and a surface charge of +29.4 mV. SEM analysis further confirmed the presence of silica surrounding the Se NPs, showing spherical morphologies with a homogeneous size distribution. The system's optimal nanoscale dimensions, positive surface charge, and uniform coating suggest that the Se@MSNPs platform has significant potential for enhanced drug delivery applications (Figure 3A,B).

(A,B) SEM analysis of Se@MSNPs and (C–F) TEM analysis of Se@MSNPs.

TEM analysis (Figure 3) confirmed the successful formation of ordered mesoporous structures with a well‐defined core–shell architecture, in which a mesoporous silica shell surrounded Se NPs. The TEM images revealed uniform NPs with an average diameter of ≈85 nm. Further verification was provided by FTIR spectroscopy, which revealed distinct vibrational fingerprints for each component of the nanocomposite system. As shown in Figure 4, the characteristic silica absorption bands at 1080 cm−1 (Si—O—Si asymmetric stretching), 1635 cm−1 (OH bending), 960 cm−1 (Si—O—Si symmetric stretching), 693 cm−1 Si—O—Si bending, and hydroxyl stretches (3200–3800 cm−1) from surface silanol groups (Si—OH) and adsorbed water molecules, while importantly demonstrating the absence of C—H stretching vibrations (2800–3000 cm−1), confirming complete removal of the CTAB template [52, 53]. These results provide compelling evidence for the successful synthesis of template‐free, well‐ordered Se@MSNPs. Selenium nanoparticles showed diagnostic bands at 3440 cm−1 (O—H stretching), and 1639 cm−1 (O—H bending), with additional weak Se—O stretching vibrations (800–900 cm−1) appearing after silica coating (Figure 4A) [54]. Enzalutamide's molecular signature was identified through its functional group vibrations, a nitrile peak at 2223 cm−1, carbonyl stretches 1694 cm−1, C—H stretches 3040 cm−1, N—H stretches 3342 cm−1, C—F stretches 1596 cm−1, sulfonyl asymmetric/symmetric vibrations 1331 and 1135 cm−1, respectively. In the final drug‐loaded nanocomposite (Se@MSN‐Enz), the coexistence of silica matrix vibrations (Si—O—Si, Si—OH), selenium signatures, and enzalutamide's characteristic peaks provided conclusive evidence of successful drug loading while maintaining the structural integrity of both the mesoporous silica carrier and therapeutic payload (Figure 4A) [25].

FTIR spectra of (A) enzalutamide, Se NPs, Se@MSNPs, and Se@MSNPs‐Enz. (B) FTIR spectra of Cur, CS, and Cur‐CS NPs.

Also, the FTIR spectra of curcumin exhibited characteristic vibrational bands at 3450 cm−1 (phenolic —OH stretching), 2950 cm−1 (methyl C‐H stretching), 2372 cm−1 (aromatic C—H stretching), 1614 cm−1 (C=O vibration), and 1450 cm−1 (aromatic C=C ring stretching), while chitosan displayed its signature peaks at 3435 cm−1 (hydroxyl stretching), 2848 cm−1 (CH stretching), 1650 cm−1 (amide I, C=O vibration), 1500 cm−1 (amide Π, N‐H bending), 1420 cm−1 (OH and CH2 bending) and 1078 cm−1 (C—O—C glycosidic linkage) [55, 56]. Upon encapsulation, the Cur‐CS NPs showed distinct peak shifts. The O—H stretch broadened and shifted to 3414 cm−1, indicating hydrogen bonding between curcumin's phenolic groups and chitosan's hydroxyl/amine moieties. The C=O vibration in free curcumin merged with chitosan's amide I band, appearing at 1690 cm−1, suggesting dipole–dipole interactions, and the aromatic C=C stretch shifted from 1450 to 1550 cm−1, demonstrating π–π stacking between curcumin's phenyl rings and chitosan's pyranose rings. Crucially, the preservation of all major functional group vibrations alongside these systematic peak shifts confirms successful curcumin encapsulation and reveals multiple molecular interactions, including hydrogen bonding, electrostatic forces, and hydrophobic effects, that stabilize the nanoparticle structure (Figure 4B) [57, 58].

Figure 5 presents the XRD pattern of Se NPs following the incorporation of mesoporous silica and enzalutamide. The characteristic peaks observed at 2θ angles of 21.73° and 25.13° confirm the presence of Se NPs. As shown in Figure 5, MSNPs exhibit a broad peak in the 2θ range of 19°–25°, which corresponds to their characteristic periodic electron density variation [59]. This diffraction pattern results from the highly ordered pore arrangement in mesoporous silica nanoparticles, reflecting a two‐dimensional hexagonal mesostructure. Furthermore, the obtained XRD patterns indicate a structural transition in mesoporous silica from amorphous to crystalline phases in both Se@MSNPs and enzalutamide‐loaded Se@MSNPs [60].

Encapsulation Efficiency, Drug Loading Efficiency, and Drug Release Studies

The drug loading efficiency of enzalutamide in the Se@MSNPs‐Enz was determined via a standard calibration curve at 236 nm, revealing a drug loading efficiency of 76.3 ± 0.5%. Additionally, the encapsulation efficiency of curcumin in Cur‐CS NPs was found to be 83.2 ± 0.7%.

In addition, the release of enzalutamide and curcumin from the NPs was evaluated under simulated physiological (pH 7.4) and acidic (pH 5) conditions. The release profiles exhibited a controlled, sustained pattern, devoid of an initial burst, indicating successful and homogeneous drug encapsulation within the nanoparticle core (Figure 6). Analysis over 48 h revealed a pH‐dependent release. At pH 5.0, 38.6% of the enzalutamide and 47% of the curcumin were released, compared to 31.2% and 33.4%, respectively, at pH 7.4. For curcumin, this disparity is due to the pH‐responsive swelling of the chitosan matrix. At a lower pH, the enhanced protonation of amine groups facilitates polymer hydration and swelling, promoting medium penetration and a transition to a rubbery state that accelerates drug diffusion. The superior release at pH 5.0 may be further enhanced by curcumin's greater stability in acidic media. Therefore, it is anticipated that these nanoparticles can deliver an adequate concentration of curcumin within the acidic cancer microenvironment to inhibit cell growth. Similarly, the release of enzalutamide demonstrated highly pH‐sensitive behavior, with the release rate inversely correlating with pH. At lower pH, the mesoporous silica structures became protonated, enhancing drug release by weakening interactions, including the potential cleavage of siloxane (Si—O—Si) bonds. Conversely, at a higher pH, drug release was hindered by stronger electrostatic forces, which act as a barrier. This mechanism was supported by the observation that the nanoparticles’ zeta potential decreased progressively with increasing pH. Given the acidic pH of the tumor microenvironment, the designed dual‐nanoparticle system comprising Se@MSNPs‐Enz and Cur‐CS NPs could serve as a promising delivery platform to enhance the efficacy of enzalutamide in prostate cancer treatment.

MTT Assay Results

The absorbance at 570 nm was measured using an ELISA reader, with all experiments performed in triplicate. Cell viability was calculated by comparing the absorbance of cells treated with various drug formulations to untreated control cells. As shown in Figure 7, the MTT assay evaluated two prostate cancer cell lines (DU145 and LNCaP) treated with enzalutamide, Se@MSNPs‐Enz alone or in combination with Cur‐CS NPs over 24–48 h of incubation. In DU145 cells, free enzalutamide and Se@MSNPs‐Enz showed IC50 values of 20.3 and 13.7 µg/mL at 24 h, decreasing to 15.2 and 9.6 µg/mL at 48 h, respectively. Also, for LNCaP cells, free enzalutamide and Se@MSNPs‐Enz showed an IC50 of 20.1 and 18.8 µg/mL at 24 h, decreasing to 13.5 and 10.7 µg/mL at 48 h, respectively. In DU145 cells, the combination therapy with Se@MSNPs‐Enz and Cur‐CSNPs showed even stronger effects, with IC50 values of 10.5 µg/mL at 24 h and 4.1 µg/mL at 48 h. Similar patterns were observed in LNCaP cells, where the combination reduced the IC50 to 4.6 µg/mL at 48 h. These results demonstrate significant synergistic effects between enzalutamide and Se@MSNPs/Se@MSNPs‐Enz and Cur‐CS NPs, time‐dependent cytotoxicity, where prolonged exposure improves efficacy, and concentration‐dependent responses. The findings suggest that this combined nanoformulation could be a promising strategy for enhanced prostate cancer therapy, particularly with prolonged exposure periods. The improved efficacy compared to single‐drug or nanoparticle therapy highlights the potential of this combinatorial approach in cancer treatment.

Results of Hoechst Staining

Apoptosis, or programmed cell death, is a natural and controlled process in the body that eliminates damaged, aged, or unnecessary cells. While it can occur in cancer cells, genetic mutations and disrupted signaling pathways often allow them to evade this mechanism, leading to uncontrolled growth. In healthy cells, apoptosis is mediated by proteins such as caspases and by nuclear changes (e.g., chromatin condensation and fragmentation). However, in cancer cells, this process may be impaired by mutations in apoptosis‐regulating genes (p53) or by overexpression of antiapoptotic proteins (Bcl‐2) [61, 62].

Cancer therapies often aim to induce apoptosis in tumor cells. Chemotherapy and radiotherapy, for instance, trigger apoptosis by causing DNA damage or activating cell‐death pathways. Thus, apoptosis in cancer cells is a key therapeutic target, and understanding its regulatory mechanisms can improve treatment strategies. Apoptotic cells show densely packed, brightly stained chromatin clusters (unlike healthy cells, which show uniform distribution). In later stages, the nucleus breaks into smaller fragments, appearing as bright spots under fluorescence microscopy [37]. So, based on the IC50 values obtained from the MTT assay, the apoptotic effects of the drug and nanoparticles on DU145 and LNCaP cell lines were evaluated using Hoechst staining, with cells exhibiting condensed or fragmented chromatin identified as apoptotic (all experiments performed in triplicate). These results highlight the synergistic effects of the nanoparticles and enhanced cancer cell sensitivity to enzalutamide when coadministered with curcumin. As illustrated in Figure 8, a significant increase in apoptosis and DNA fragmentation was observed in the LNCaP cell line compared to the DU145 cell line following treatment with enzalutamide, either alone or in combination with Se@MSNPs. The underlying mechanism is as follows: in LNCaP cells, enzalutamide potently antagonizes the androgen receptor, a primary driver of cancer cell growth and survival. By inhibiting this pathway, enzalutamide effectively induces programmed cell death in this cell type. In contrast, in DU145 cells, a model for advanced androgen‐independent prostate cancer, the primary target of enzalutamide, the functional androgen receptor, is largely absent [63]. Furthermore, in both cell lines, an additive effect was observed when enzalutamide was formulated within Se@MSNPs. Additionally, the increased DNA fragmentation in both cell lines (particularly in DU145) following concurrent application of Se@MSNPs‐Enz and Cur‐CS NPs can be attributed to curcumin's significant anticancer properties and its synergistic effect.

Fluorescent staining with Hoechst dye (33342) and evaluation of apoptosis in cancer cells treated with nanoparticles containing enzalutamide and curcumin.

Multiple anticancer mechanisms of curcumin have been identified. Inhibition of inflammatory signaling pathways by suppressing inflammatory factors like NF‐κB and COX‐2, which play key roles in prostate cancer progression. Induction of apoptosis through activation of caspase pathways and modulation of Bcl‐2 family proteins. Inhibition of cell proliferation by disrupting cell‐cycle regulators such as cyclins and kinases. Antiangiogenic effects through reduced VEGF expression. Metastasis suppression via inhibition of metalloproteinases and adhesion molecules. Modulation of androgen signaling pathways is crucial for prostate cancer growth. Antioxidant activity that neutralizes free radicals and reduces oxidative DNA damage, and epigenetic regulation through DNA methylation and histone modifications that influence cancer‐related gene expression [64, 65].

This study investigates a combination of two distinct nanocarriers, Se@MSNPs‐Enz and Cur‐CS NPs, designed for synergistic action and not previously evaluated. The Se@MSNPs‐Enz consist of a core of biocompatible and inherently bioactive Se NPs, a coating of highly porous MSNPs that provide a vast surface area to load the drug, and the drug enzalutamide, which is a second‐generation antiandrogen that blocks the androgen receptor (AR) [66]. The Cur‐CS NPs use chitosan nanoparticles as a carrier, a natural, biocompatible, biodegradable, and mucoadhesive polymer that enhances cellular uptake and loaded with curcumin, a natural polyphenol with potent anti‐inflammatory, antioxidant, and multitargeted anticancer properties. This combination is a strategic attack on prostate cancer, designed to enhance efficacy and overcome drug resistance. This design can offer critical benefits over conventional enzalutamide therapy (160 mg/day) and decreases its side effects in patients. Its primary advantage is overcoming treatment resistance, as many patients develop resistance to enzalutamide. Several studies have investigated the anticancer effects of NPs for prostate cancer. For instance, previous research evaluated the activity of pH‐dependent fungal metabolite (TM2)–CS NPs against the PC‐3 cell line, where the drug entrapment efficiency and loading capacity were 50.97 ± 0.12% and 24.68 ± 0.64%, respectively [67]. In another study, researchers developed CS NPs and chitosan–octanoate (CS–OA) NPs for curcumin delivery. The reported encapsulation efficiencies for these systems were 61.37 ± 1.70% and 60.20 ± 3.17%, respectively [46]. Cellular uptake of the empty NPs led to concentration‐dependent antiproliferative effects. Incorporating curcumin into the NPs substantially increased their cytotoxicity. Furthermore, researchers used a codelivery system of docetaxel and enzalutamide based on PSMA‐targeted, glutathione‐sensitive NPs. This formulation exhibited loading capacities of 6.3 ± 0.7% and 6.1 ± 0.6%, and entrapment efficiencies of 90.9 ± 2.7% and 89.5 ± 2.9% for docetaxel and enzalutamide, respectively. These NPs achieved a maximum cell growth inhibition of 79%, demonstrating greater efficiency than both single‐drug‐loaded NPs and free drug formulations [32]. So, this study provides a new opportunity to develop a highly efficient nanosystem with a loading efficiency of 76.3 ± 0.5% and an encapsulation efficiency of 83.2 ± 0.7% for enzalutamide and curcumin, respectively. This hybrid nanosystem benefits from the synergistic anticancer effects of Se NPs, enzalutamide, and Cur‐CS NPs, a combination that, to the best of our knowledge, has not been extensively synthesized or explored.

Curcumin can attack multiple pathways that drive this resistance (NF‐κB, AR variants), resensitizing cancer cells to enzalutamide [64]. These nanocarriers enhance the solubility and bioavailability of both enzalutamide and curcumin. This is particularly important because both drugs are hydrophobic, a property that leads to poor water solubility and, consequently, low bioavailability [68]. Furthermore, it enables passive tumor targeting via the EPR Effect. Prostate tumors have leaky vasculature and poor lymphatic drainage, allowing nanoparticles in this size range (≈50–200 nm) to preferentially accumulate in tumor tissue, thereby increasing local drug concentration and reducing systemic side effects. This active targeting approach minimizes exposure to healthy tissues, thereby reducing severe side effects associated with enzalutamide (such as fatigue and seizure risk) and potentially enabling higher, more effective doses [69]. The nanosystem holds significant potential for prostate‐specific targeting. For instance, the NP surface can be functionalized with ligands, such as antibodies targeting PSMA, enabling active, specific targeting of prostate cancer cells and further enhancing treatment precision and therapeutic efficacy. Sonkusre's histopathology research demonstrated that ingesting selenium nanopar­ticles at 50 mg Se/kg body weight resulted in significantly lower toxicity than L‐selenomethionine (5 mg Se/kg) [70]. Also, Shahverdi et al. discovered that folic acid surface‐coated selenium nanoparticles exhibited in vivo benefits for breast cancer. They found that Se NPs could inhibit prostate cancer growth by reducing IKK‐ε and SMAD2 mRNA levels in xenograft mice, suggesting that Se NPs could be used for antitumor purposes. The effectiveness of Se NPs dropped notably in mice treated with miR‐155‐5p shRNA, confirming the crucial role of miR‐155‐5p in facilitating Se NPs’ antitumor properties [71]. In addition, Chen et al. developed a novel NP system that simultaneously delivers two drugs, curcumin and cabazitaxel (CTX), for the treatment of prostate cancer. Their lipid‐polymer hybrid nanoparticles provided a sustained release of both compounds. When these NPs were coated with an aptamer (creating APT‐Cur/CTX‐LPNs), they demonstrated excellent anticancer properties, including effective inhibition of cell growth, precise tumor accumulation, and significant tumor growth suppression, with an optimal drug ratio of 2:5 (Cur to CTX) [72]. Similarly, Yan et al. created a different targeted system using EGFR‐binding GE11 peptides to decorate nanoparticles for the codelivery of docetaxel and curcumin. Their results confirmed that these ≈167 nm particles leverage both active (GE11) and passive (EPR effect) targeting mechanisms to deliver both drugs simultaneously into cancer cells. Collectively, these studies underscore a promising trend in oncology: the use of multifunctional nanoparticles to enhance drug efficacy, improve specificity, and minimize harmful side effects [73].

Based on the studies, we imagine the mechanisms of the Se@MSNPs‐Enz begin with a primary AR pathway blockade, where released enzalutamide performs its primary function with higher efficiency by inhibiting androgen binding to the AR, preventing AR nuclear translocation, and inhibiting AR‐DNA binding to directly shut down the primary driver of prostate cancer cell proliferation [74]. The selenium‐mediated activity from the Se NP core can induce oxidative stress specifically in cancer cells, pushing them into apoptosis; directly activates key apoptotic pathways, such as the caspase cascade; and inhibits angiogenesis to starve the tumor [75]. Simultaneously, the mechanisms of Cur‐CS NPs involve curcumin targeting cancer cells’ resistance mechanisms and survival pathways. It inhibits the crucial NF‐κB pathway, a transcription factor that promotes inflammation, cell survival, and chemotherapy resistance, which is hyperactive in CRPC, thereby sensitizing the cell to enzalutamide‐induced death. It suppresses AR Variants like AR‐V7, a significant cause of enzalutamide resistance, induces apoptosis by upregulating proapoptotic proteins (Bax) and downregulating antiapoptotic ones (Bcl‐2), and inhibits growth signals by downregulating key survival pathways like AKT/mTOR and MAPK that cancer cells use to bypass AR blockade [64, 76]. This multipronged strategy makes it extremely difficult for the cancer cell to adapt and survive, effectively overcoming the hallmark treatment resistance of advanced prostate cancer and leading to potent, synergistic cell death (Figure 9).

Conclusion

Prostate cancer is the second leading cancer among older men and accounts for a substantial number of deaths worldwide. Timely detection and innovative drug‐delivery methods, particularly nanoparticle‐based systems, are critical for controlling disease progression. SeNPs, given their role as a vital micronutrient, possess notable antimicrobial, antiviral, and antitumor effects. Meanwhile, mesoporous silica nanostructures have emerged as promising drug carriers due to their large surface area and porous architecture, allowing for efficient drug encapsulation. In this research, the BET technique was employed to refine the production of MSNPs. The results indicated that thermal calcination for surfactant elimination produced a surface area of 78.31 m2/g, pore volume of 0.1843 cm3/g, and average pore diameter of ≈3 nm. However, solvent extraction proved more effective, yielding a larger surface area (101.74 m2/g), increased pore volume (0.2531 cm3/g), and wider pore size (≈10 nm), making it the preferred synthesis method. Enzalutamide was successfully incorporated into Se@MSNPs, achieving a drug loading efficiency of 76.3 ± 0.5%, with a particle size of ≈85 nm. This pH‐sensitive nanosystem, when combined with Cur‐CS NPs, enhances cancer cell susceptibility to treatment, potentially lowering the required drug dose. MTT assays confirmed that the nanoparticle‐encapsulated drug had more potent anticancer activity than free enzalutamide, with effects varying based on exposure duration and nanoparticle concentration. Finally, our findings demonstrate that coadministration of Se NPs, enzalutamide, and curcumin enhances the anticancer effect on prostate cancer cell lines by inducing apoptosis more effectively than conventional drug treatment, thereby preventing the growth of prostate cancer cells. Therefore, this combinatorial nanosystem could reduce the required enzalutamide dosage and mitigate its adverse effects.
