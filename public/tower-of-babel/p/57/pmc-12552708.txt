Direct FFT oversampling without zero-padding
Authors: Codrin Donciu, Marinel Costel Temneanu, Elena Serea
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractThe Fast Fourier Transform (FFT) is the most powerful and widely used method for transforming signals from the time domain to the frequency domain, being employed in cutting-edge research fields. Achieving high-accuracy FFT involves increasing the number of frequency points through frequency oversampling, typically by extending the time-domain signal with zeros (zero-padding) and applying the FFT to the extended signal. While increasing the number of zeros enhances the precision of the results, it also substantially raises the computational load. To address the high computational demands associated with traditional frequency oversampling, this paper proposes a direct oversampling method. By employing a non-integer number of cycles within the FFT kernel, the method achieves spectral analysis results identical to those obtained with zero-padded oversampling but with a significantly lower computational cost. Specifically, it reduces the complexity from O(Mlog2M) to O(Mlog2N), where N is the original signal length and M is the oversampled frequency resolution. This approach eliminates zero-padding altogether, providing a mathematically equivalent and more efficient alternative for applications requiring high spectral resolution.Subject terms: Electrical and electronic engineering, Techniques and instrumentation

Introduction

The Fast Fourier Transform (FFT) is a powerful tool in signal and image processing, widely employed in fields such as neuroscience1,2, genetics3, nanomaterials4, biological camouflage5, particle physics6 and astronomy7. Initially, the Discrete Fourier Transform (DFT) has not aroused much interest due to its high computational demand but the development of the FFT algorithm by Cooley and Tukey8 provided researchers with an efficient tool to compute DFT, greatly expanding its applicability. Shortly thereafter, the two fundamental limitations of their canonical versions, the spectral leakage and the picket fence effect, were identified, prompting extensive research aimed at mitigating these issues.

The spectral leakage is caused by the algebraic operation performed by FFT onto signals with non-integer number of periods and can be fully eliminated through coherent sampling. In practice, achieving coherent sampling is often challenging, making spectral leakage nearly unavoidable. As a result, the windowing technique is used to reduce leakage. Numerous window functions have been developed. A general overview of different window proprieties is presented in9, while the influence of windows on detection of closely spaced spectral components are provided in10 where twelve window types were investigated.

The picket fence effect (PFE) arises from the discrete nature of frequency spectrum, where only binary frequencies (bins) can be found and become observable when the spectral components are not matching the bins. The parameters of the spectral component are defined by the peak of its Discrete Time Fourier Transform (DTFT). Taking into account that FFT is computed only in bins, the information regarding peak coordinates will not be exactly achieved. This effect is similar to looking at a fortress through a picket fence: only in particular circumstances, the fortress flag will be visible. But if the picket fence is properly shifted then is possible to observe it.

Two methods are available for reducing the picket fence effect: FFT interpolation (IpFFT) and frequency-domain oversampling.

IpFFT is a parametric method based on two or three adjacent FFT bins evaluation in order to match the peak of DTFT. The interpolation technique is effective only if spectral components are well separated, ensuring that the interpolation bins lie on either side of the sought peak without significant energy overlap. If overlap occurs, the bins may fall on the same side of the sought peak, potentially resulting in erroneous interpolation and the detection of a false peak.

FFT oversampling is a robust method aimed to obtain a higher number of points in frequency (bins) than the number of points of the time sequence, so as one of the bins get closer to the maximum power. This is achieved by extending the signal through zero-padding, which involves appending zeros to the end of the time-domain sequence prior to applying.

FFT. Although this approach does not increase the resolution in the meaning of detection of closely spaced spectral components, FFT oversampling is widely used11–15.

For a time sequence of N points, oversampling generates M frequency bins by extending the sequence with M–N zeroes. The resulting M points can be computed via several methods: the Z-Transform, requiring M*N operations16; the Chirp Z algorithm, requiring (M + N)log(M + N) operations17; or the FFT, which achieves this in MlogM operations. Over the years, numerous methods have been proposed to reduce the number operations associated with zero-padding in FFT computations. These optimization techniques are collectively known as pruning18–20.

In this context, we propose an FFT algorithm able to perform M points oversampling without the need to append M–N zeros (padding) and without relying on pruning techniques. The algorithm computes the M frequency points (bins) in MlogN operations while yielding identical spectral results to those obtained via conventional zero-padding. The proposed FFT method is built on the concept of DFT with non-integer argument.

Non-integer argument method

The DFT is a mathematical tool used to determine the spectral parameters (magnitude, phase and number of cycles) of a discrete signal sequence (a set of discrete values obtained by sampling a time domain continuous signal).

Let us consider a discrete signal x(n) sequence of N points. The DFT is computed by evaluating the inner product between the signal sequence and a set of complex exponential basis functions, known as the DFT kernel. For each integer frequency, where k = 0, 1 , …, N-1, the DFT at bin k is given by:

In the DFT, the output components Xk are indexed by k = 0, 1, …, N-1, where each k corresponds to a frequency bin representing k complete cycles of the complex exponential kernel over the length N of the input sequence.

This formulation provides spectral estimates only at integer multiples of the fundamental frequency resolution. In the following, we extend this framework to allow non-integer frequency arguments, enabling the evaluation of the DFT at arbitrary frequencies between the standard bin locations.

The DFT kernel consists of two discrete orthogonal signals (sin and cos), both containing the same number of points as the original signal sequence. In this context, the index k corresponds to the number of cycles the kernel completes across the N points of the sequence. Thus, for a given bin k, the DFT kernel oscillates k times over the duration of the signal. Kernel signals are easily observable in DFT rectangular form, where the cosine and sine functions define the real and imaginary parts of the kernel, respectively:

In the conventional DFT, the k argument is restricted to integer values, resulting in kernel signals composed of an integer number of cycles across the N-point sequence. But what happens if kernel multiplication is performed for a non-integer number of cycles (if we use non-integer values for k)? That is, can the DFT be meaningfully computed at fractional cycle rates? If signal sequence x(n) is not a coherent signal (the number of periods is not an integer) why kernel signals must be coherent ones?

Consider a signal containing 4.5 cycles sampled over N = 100 points (Fig. 3a). To determine the number of cycles using the DFT, we compute the frequency spectrum by multiplying the signal sequence with kernel functions corresponding to frequencies ranging from 0 to 99 cycles. For illustrative purposes, the kernel functions are represented solely by cosine waves. Specifically, to obtain spectral value at the 4th frequency bin, the signal sequence is multiplied by a cosine wave completing 4 full cycles over the 100 samples; similarly, for the 5th bin, it is multiplied by a 5-cycle cosine wave. However, since the actual signal contains 4.5 cycles, it is not optimally aligned with either of these kernels—resulting in energy being split across adjacent bins and the true frequency component being less accurately represented.

Comparative illustration between classical FFT (a), zero-padding FFT (b) and the proposed method FIN-FFT (c).

Similarly, the remaining frequency bins are computed using the same approach, each using a cosine kernel corresponding to its respective integer number of cycles. Figure 3a. displays only the region of interest near the spectral peak, which is essential for accurately estimating the number of cycles. It can be easily observed that the integer spaced frequency bins fail to precisely capture the true position and magnitude of the peak. Thus, the estimation of the number of cycles is subjected to significant error.

To more accurately determine the value of the maximum power, we can oversample in the frequency domain by increasing the number of the DFT bins. For example, to double the number of bins, the number of points in time domain must also be doubled, commonly by zero-padding. Thus, the time sequence of 4.5 cycles and N = 100 is increased by M–N = 100 zeroes. As illustrated in Fig. 3b, doubling the length of the time sequence effectively duplicates the frequency range prior to rescaling, resulting in a denser bin spacing i.e. the frequency resolution Δf is halved. In these conditions, the 9th bin is obtained by multiplying the sequence with kernel signals which contain an integer number of cycles (9 cycles). After rescaling (multiplying the frequency axis by 1/2), the maximum bin is observed at the kernel corresponding to 4.5 cycles. Hence, zero-padding enables the DFT to better localize spectral peaks that fall between the original integer-spaced bins.

But why should we extend the time sequence with zeroes which have useless information, when we can calculate directly, achieving the same spectrum, by using kernel signals with a non-integer number of periods (non-integer argument for DFT) which leads directly to fractional division of the frequency spectrum?

As shown in Fig. 3b, when the time sequence is multiplied by the kernel functions, the resulting nonzero values are confined to the portion of the signal that contains actual data (the original 4.5 cycles). All multiplications made between the zeroes of the time sequence and kernel signals have no contribution to the average of the signal obtained from multiplying. But what is the contribution of the zero-padding method? Fundamentally, it increases the density of frequency bins by allowing the kernel to complete a greater number of cycles across the extended sequence. Thus, the number of cycles is extended from 100 to 200 values, enabling the computation of 200 bins and a finer sampling of the frequency domain.

Focusing on the active portion of the signal (the region containing non-zero values), can be seen that it spans only the original, unpadded sequence. Within this region, the kernel functions used in the frequency domain do not complete an integer number of cycles; instead, they match the actual signal content, exhibiting 4.5 cycles. This active segment, highlighted in Fig. 3b is extracted and used to implement a method to calculate the DFT with non-integer argument, which develops the same frequency spectrum as the zero-padding, but without increasing the number of operations determined by the zeroes. By evaluating the kernel functions over just the non-zero portion of the sequence and aligning them to fractional cycle counts, we can reconstruct the same frequency spectrum as that obtained via zero-padding—but without the computational overhead introduced by multiplying with zeros. This concept is illustrated in Fig. 3c, which outlines a method for directly computing the oversampled spectrum while avoiding redundant operations.

The implementation of this DFT with non-integer argument into a FFT with non-integer argument (FIN-FFT) will allow obtaining a frequency spectrum with M frequency points, using only MlogN operations, instead of MlogM operations required by the traditional zero-padded FFT methods. Here, N denotes the number of points of the original time-domain sequence and M–N corresponds to the number of appended zeroes in the zero-padding approach. This efficiency gain is achieved by avoiding unnecessary computations involving zero-valued data, while still preserving the full spectral resolution afforded by oversampling.

Implementation

To illustrate the operation of the FFT with non-integer argument, we will compare it with the classical FFT approach under conditions of frequency oversampling. Consider a time-domain sequence consisting of N points, for which we aim to obtain M frequency bins. The ratio M/N represents the oversampling rate, indicating the factor by which the frequency resolution is increased. Conversely, the reciprocal N/M represents the oversampling step.

Consider a time-domain signal sequence consisting of N = 128 points. To perform frequency oversampling, we aim to compute, M = 256 frequency bins, effectively doubling the spectral resolution. In the classical approach, this is achieved by zero-padding the time-domain sequence with M − N = 128 zeros, resulting in a time-domain sequence of M = 256 points. An FFT is then applied to this extended sequence to obtain the desired oversampled spectrum. In this case, the oversampling rate is M/N = 2 and the oversampling step is N/M = 0.5.

During the first iteration of the outer loop, the index r is initialized to 0. In this case, the number of cycles generated by the kernel function is (k + 0), resulting exclusively in the generation of integer cycles (0, 1, 2, 3, …).

During the second iteration, r is incremented by N/M, equivalent to 0.5. Consequently, the number of cycles generated by the kernel function becomes (k + 0.5), producing non-integer cycles (0.5, 1.5, 2.5, 3.5, …).

By alternating the fractional shift r in each iteration, this approach effectively interpolates additional bins between the standard FFT frequencies, doubling the frequency resolution without zero-padding the time-domain signal. Discussions

Frequency domain oversampling remains a fundamental tool in high-precision spectral analysis. It reduces the picket fence effect and improves the alignment between discrete frequency bins and true spectral components. Classical oversampling techniques, based primarily on zero-padding the time domain sequence, are straightforward to implement but impose a substantial computational burden. This burden grows with the oversampling factor and becomes a significant constraint in real-time processing, large-scale data throughput or resource-limited platforms.

The method proposed in this work demonstrates that direct frequency oversampling can be achieved directly, without explicit zero-padding. The classical FFT was extended to allow non-integer kernel arguments. As a result, a zero-padded spectrum with M frequency bins can be computed directly from an N-point sequence without inserting M–N zeros. The computational complexity is thereby reduced from O(Mlog2M) to O(Mlog2N), compared to the classical zero-padding approach, yielding a computational gain that grows with the oversampling ratio M/N. For M ≫ N, this leads to a significant efficiency advantage.

The practical implications of this efficiency gain are illustrated in Fig. 7, which plots the percentage reduction in operational count for signal lengths N = 16, 128, 1024 and 16,384. As shown, the percentage gain increases with the oversampling rate, highlighting the scalability of the method.

Percentage drop in computation across increasing signal’s size for proposed method against zero-padded.

Zero-padding is widely adopted for its implementation convenience and compatibility with standard FFT routines. It effectively approximates a finer frequency grid by artificially extending the time-domain signal. In contrast, the present approach leverages FFT evaluations at non-integer frequency indices to achieve the same spectral resolution without signal modification. This preserves mathematical equivalence and provides a more direct formulation of spectral sampling. It also offers an analytically transparent and computationally efficient alternative.

Quantitative results confirm that the proposed method consistently reduces computational effort compared to the traditional zero-padding approach. This reduction grows with higher oversampling rates, underscoring the scalability and efficiency of the proposed technique. Across a range of signal lengths, the percentage savings in operation count scale favorably with the oversampling factor. At the same time, the method maintains identical spectral accuracy, making it well-suited for performance-critical applications where resources are limited.

Feature comparison of oversampling techniques.

Interpolated DFT (IpDFT) estimates spectral peaks between FFT bins by fitting a model (usually sinusoidal) to a few neighboring DFT samples. It requires at least 2 or 3 bins around a peak and works best when components are well separated.

Advantages: Low computational cost. Accurate for single-tone or sparse spectra.

Disadvantages: Not robust in crowded or noisy spectra. Requires good bin localization before interpolation.

Difference vs. FIN-FFT: IpDFT is an a posteriori interpolation after FFT. FIN-FFT performs a priori computation directly at fractional frequencies, without relying on peak detection or curve fitting.

Zoom FFT Zoom FFT focuses on a narrow frequency band using preprocessing (mixing, decimation, filtering) followed by a standard FFT on the decimated signal.

Advantages: Very efficient for zooming into narrow frequency ranges. Reduces number of FFT bins needed.

Disadvantages: Requires pre-filtering and careful design to avoid aliasing. Not suitable for full-spectrum applications.

Difference vs. FIN-FFT: Zoom FFT sacrifices spectral coverage to gain local resolution. FIN-FFT provides full-spectrum oversampling without filtering, suitable for wideband signals.

Chirp-Z Transform (CZT) CZT generalizes the DFT to arbitrary frequency contours (linear, spiral, etc.). It can compute spectral values at nonuniform or fractional frequencies using convolution-based techniques.

Advantages: Flexible frequency grid. Can target arbitrary frequency ranges.

Disadvantages: More complex implementation. Higher memory and operation count (especially for large FFT sizes).

Difference vs. FIN-FFT: CZT computes fractional frequencies via convolution; FIN-FFT uses recursive kernel shifts. FIN-FFT is simpler and faster for uniform fractional oversampling.

Pruned FFT Pruned FFT algorithms reduce computation by skipping unnecessary operations on zero-padded regions or on bins that are known to be irrelevant. They are typically used when only a portion of the FFT output is required, such as in narrowband analysis.

Advantages: Reduces operation count when processing sparse input or when only partial spectrum is needed. Avoids some multiplications with zero after zero-padding is introduced.

Disadvantages: Still relies on a full-size FFT (with zero-padding). Only skips operations after the signal has been artificially extended.

Difference vs. FIN-FFT: Pruned FFT still assumes zero-padding has occurred and then avoids operating on the padded zeros. The proposed method (FIN-FFT) does not introduce zero-padding at all; it computes fractional frequency bins directly. Thus, FIN-FFT prevents computational waste at the source.

The proposed method offers a unique balance: full-spectrum fractional resolution with low computational complexity and without requiring signal modifications or narrowband filtering. Unlike IpDFT or Zoom FFT, it does not rely on signal sparsity or band pre-selection. Compared to CZT, it is algorithmically simpler and computationally more efficient for uniform oversampling scenarios.

While spectrograms were not explicitly discussed, the proposed method integrates seamlessly with STFT-based spectrogram computation. Each FFT window processed with this approach yields higher frequency resolution, without increasing time duration or padding overhead. This enables sharper time–frequency plots in low-latency systems.

Limitations and future work

The present study establishes the foundation of the FIN-FFT method, demonstrating its ability to achieve oversampling without zero-padding and with reduced computational complexity. At this stage, the method is formulated for integer oversampling factors, which ensures conceptual clarity and ease of implementation, but may limit flexibility in applications requiring arbitrary frequency resolution. A natural next step will be to extend the framework toward rational or adaptive oversampling schemes, which would broaden its applicability. In addition, a systematic analysis of numerical stability under finite-precision conditions, particularly in high-resolution or real-time implementations, will further strengthen confidence in real-time deployment. Finally, while initial benchmarking has been conducted through FPGA synthesis, expanding the evaluation to CPUs, GPUs and embedded platforms will provide a comprehensive performance landscape. These directions are expected to enhance the method’s robustness and versatility, supporting its adoption in a wide range of practical systems.

Conclusions

This study presents FIN-FFT, an alternative formulation of frequency-domain oversampling that eliminates the need for zero-padding by directly evaluating the Discrete Fourier Transform at non-integer frequency indices. The approach performs repeated FFT computations with fractional frequency shifts, producing an oversampled spectrum without extending the time-domain signal.

Theoretical analysis demonstrates that zero-padding and non-integer frequency FFT are mathematically equivalent and both sample the same underlying spectrum, ensuring identical spectral resolution. The proposed method achieves this without performing operations on zero-padded data, thereby reducing computational overhead. Its compatibility with the standard FFT structure facilitates integration into real-time and resource-constrained systems.

These findings suggest that FIN-FFT provides a computationally efficient and accurate alternative for spectral analysis tasks, particularly in applications where resolution and processing efficiency are both critical.
