Imaging the distribution and drainage of fluid in the human olfactory regions using intravenous Gadolinium-based contrast agents (GBCA) enhanced MRI
Authors: Xinyi Zhou, Sofía García del Barrio Cervera, Yuanqi Sun, Yinghao Li, Wei Li, Licia Pacheco-Luna, Haris I Sair, Adrian Paez, Linda Knutsson, Peter C M van Zijl, Vidyulata Kamath, Arnold Bakker, Bryan K Ward, Jun Hua
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundCerebrospinal fluid (CSF) clearance via the olfactory pathway is well-documented in animal models. However, results from in vivo human studies appear inconsistent. Studies using intrathecal (IT) Gadolinium-based contrast agents (GBCA) enhanced MRI showed minimal tracer pass-through from intracranial to extracranial olfactory regions such as the nasal mucosa. Conversely, human imaging studies using intravenous (IV) tracers showed significant enhancement in the nasal mucosa, suggesting CSF drainage through the cribriform plate. This research seeks to clarify these conflicting results from imaging studies using intrathecal and intravenous tracers, and to provide a better understanding of intravenous GBCA distribution in intracranial and extracranial olfactory regions, an important issue for studies using intravenous-GBCA-enhanced-MRI to investigate CSF clearance.MethodsDynamic-susceptibility-contrast-in-the-CSF (cDSC) MRI was applied to measure GBCA distribution in the CSF immediately and 4 h after intravenous administration in 25 healthy volunteers (48.9 ± 19.5 years; 14 females). A region-of-interest (ROI)-based and a voxel-based analysis were performed to measure GBCA concentration in intracranial and extracranial olfactory regions. Paired t-tests were used to compare pre- and post-GBCA signal changes.ResultsGBCA-induced signal changes were detected in all olfactory regions immediately and 4 h after intravenous GBCA administration. GBCA concentration was significantly greater (P < 0.01) in extracranial olfactory regions than intracranial olfactory regions. At 4 h post-GBCA, GBCA concentration decreased in extracranial olfactory regions compared to the immediate post-GBCA period, while it was comparable at both time points in intracranial olfactory regions.ConclusionsIntravenous-GBCA-enhanced-MRI can detect GBCA distribution in the CSF space of olfactory regions in healthy subjects. The GBCA-induced CSF signal changes in intracranial olfactory regions were substantially smaller compared to extracranial olfactory regions. GBCA concentration in the CSF of intracranial olfactory regions was comparable to other intracranial regions. The significant GBCA-induced signal changes in extracranial olfactory regions may largely originate from peripheral blood supply when using intravenous tracers, which reflects lymphatic fluid circulation in the extracranial lymphatic system, and are not directly related to CSF clearance from the brain. Therefore, when using intravenous tracer-based imaging methods, it is critical to separate intracranial and extracranial regions in the analysis due to their different vascular supply.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12987-025-00710-4.Keywords: Glymphatic, Intracranial, Extracranial, Olfactory mucosa, Lymph node, Clearance

Introduction

Cerebrospinal fluid (CSF) circulation and clearance from the brain serves a critical physiological role for the central nervous system. The olfactory pathway has been proposed as one of the primary CSF clearance routes from the brain. Extensive studies in animal models —including rodents, sheep, dogs, minipigs, and non-human primates — have demonstrated that CSF in the subarachnoid space (SAS) can travel along the olfactory nerve, cross the cribriform plate, and enter the olfactory lymphatics within the nasal mucosa [1–24]. Nevertheless, the extent to which these animal results can extrapolate to the human olfactory system remains unclear. In humans, post-mortem examinations provided some evidence for the existence of a similar CSF clearance route through the cribriform plate [10, 25–30]. However, CSF and vascular dynamics can be very different under living physiological conditions. Only a few in vivo human studies have been performed to investigate CSF drainage via the olfactory regions. In human studies, imaging tracers can be injected directly into the CSF via intrathecal (IT) administration or can be injected into the blood first via intravenous (IV) administration, after which series of images are acquired to visualize the distribution of tracers in the brain. In two human MRI studies [31, 32], Gadolinium-based contrast agents (GBCA) were administered intrathecally in patients with various CSF and brain disorders. GBCA enhancement was significant in the olfactory bulb and straight gyrus regions superior to the cribriform plate, but was very limited or absent in the nasal mucosa regions inferior to the cribriform plate, suggesting limited CSF clearance across the cribriform plate. In contrast, two human studies using intravenously administered tracers seem to show the opposite. Using intravenous 18F-THK5117 and 11C-PiB tracers for Positron Emission Tomography (PET) imaging, de Leon et al. [33] found significant tracer accumulation in the superior and middle turbinates in the nasal mucosa. Sennfält et al. [34] reported significant GBCA-induced MRI signal enhancement around the cribriform plate. Both studies appear to support the hypothesis of a CSF drainage pathway through the cribriform plate.

When using IV imaging tracers to study CSF circulation, it is important to consider the vascular supply pathways in different regions for the tracers in the blood pool to enter the CSF space. Furthermore, tracer permeability can be vastly different in intracranial and extracranial areas. The olfactory bulb is the first intracranial structure along the human olfactory pathway. It is located superior to the cribriform plate of the ethmoid bone, and is supplied by branches of the internal carotid artery (ICA), including the olfactory artery, accessory olfactory artery, and anterior and posterior ethmoidal arteries [35]. On the other hand, the extracranial olfactory regions in the nasal mucosa located inferior to the cribriform plate are supplied by the sphenopalatine artery arising from the external carotid artery (ECA) as well as the anterior and posterior ethmoidal arteries arising from the ICA [35]. In healthy human subjects, intracranial blood vessels are protected by the blood-brain barrier (BBB), which is largely impermeable to IV GBCAs. On the other hand, the blood vessels in extracranial regions do not have a BBB [36].

IV GBCA based MRI approaches remain the only FDA-approved contrast enhanced MRI methods and have been routinely used for clinical examinations. As IV GBCAs can enter CSF circulation via the choroid plexus, dura mater and several other brain regions even in healthy human subjects [37–40], it may be used to study CSF circulation and clearance in a similar way to IT GBCA based MRI methods. However, the distribution of IV GBCAs in the CSF can differ significantly from IT GBCAs. In this study, we seek to better understand the distribution of IV GBCAs in the fluid space in intracranial and extracranial human olfactory regions, and attempt to explain the inconsistent findings reported in previous IT- and IV tracer-based imaging studies on this topic. The recently developed dynamic susceptibility contrast in the CSF (cDSC) MRI approach [41] was adopted to measure GBCA-induced MR signal changes in the olfactory regions immediately and 4 h after IV GBCA administration in healthy subjects. In cDSC MRI, a three-dimensional (3D) turbo spin echo (TSE, also known as fast spin echo or FSE) MRI sequence with a sub-millimeter spatial resolution, a temporal resolution of ~ 10 s and a whole-brain coverage was optimized to measure GBCA-induced MR signal changes in the CSF. The key difference between cDSC MRI and many MRI methods adopted in existing studies on this topic is the use of an ultra-long echo time (TE >1 s) [41]. As the T2 relaxation time of CSF (~ 1400 ms) is much longer than blood and tissue T2 (~ 50–200 ms), when such a long TE is applied, parenchymal and blood signals are effectively suppressed due to T2 decay (i.e. they are in the noise range, not significantly different from the background, and therefore are effectively zero), and thus, a pure CSF signal with minimal partial volume effects from the blood is acquired. This is particularly relevant for IV GBCA as the intravascular GBCA concentration can be substantially greater than that from CSF. All participants in this in vivo study were healthy human subjects without any known brain diseases. GBCA-induced MR signal changes were measured in the intracranial and extracranial olfactory regions separately. In the current study, fluids in the extracranial olfactory regions that exhibit T1 and T2 relaxation properties similar to those of intracranial CSF are referred to as “CSF-like fluids” (to distinguish them from blood in this IV study). These may include interstitial and lymphatic fluids, as well as nasal glandular and mucosal secretions.

Methods

Study participants

Twenty-five healthy subjects (age 48.9 ± 19.5 years, range 20–85 years; 14 females and 11 males) participated in this study. There was no history of neurological or psychiatric disorders in any of the participants. The Johns Hopkins Institutional Review Board reviewed and approved the study protocol. Each subject provided written informed consent. A subset of participants (10 subjects, age 41.5 ± 17.1 years, range 20–85 years; 6 females and 4 males) completed the additional 4 hour post-GBCA MRI scans (see below).

MRI and GBCA contrast

All participants were scanned on a 3.0 Tesla (3T) Philips human MRI scanner (Philips Healthcare, Best, The Netherlands) equipped with a 32-channel phased-array head coil and a dual-channel body coil. Standard IV administration of a GBCA contrast agent (ProHance, or Gadoteridol; 279.3 mg/mL or 0.5 mmol/mL; Bracco S.p.A., Milan, Italy; dosage = 0.1 mmol/kg calibrated by body weight; injection rate = 5 mL/s) was performed during the MRI scans for each subject.

The following MRI scans were performed for each subject:

Pre-GBCA FLAIR MRI for anatomical reference: repetition time (TR)/inversion time (TI)/echo time (TE) = 6000/2000/180 ms, voxel = 0.8 × 0.8 × 0.8 mm3, 188 slices, 8 min and 36 s.

Pre-GBCA FLAIR MRI for anatomical reference: repetition time (TR)/inversion time (TI)/echo time (TE) = 6000/2000/180 ms, voxel = 0.8 × 0.8 × 0.8 mm3, 188 slices, 8 min and 36 s.

cDSC MRI during GBCA administration: TR/TE/echo spacing (ES) = 10,000/1347/3.2 ms, voxel = 0.8 × 0.8 × 0.8 mm3, and 188 slices (same coverage as FLAIR MRI) [41]. A total of 60 cDSC volumes were acquired during 10 min and 20 s, and the GBCAs were administered at the 29th volume.

cDSC MRI during GBCA administration: TR/TE/echo spacing (ES) = 10,000/1347/3.2 ms, voxel = 0.8 × 0.8 × 0.8 mm3, and 188 slices (same coverage as FLAIR MRI) [41]. A total of 60 cDSC volumes were acquired during 10 min and 20 s, and the GBCAs were administered at the 29th volume.

Post-GBCA FLAIR MRI: the same FLAIR scan was repeated for each subject at approximately 6 min after GBCA injection.

Post-GBCA FLAIR MRI: the same FLAIR scan was repeated for each subject at approximately 6 min after GBCA injection.

A subset of participants (see Study participants) paid a second visit approximately 4 h after their first MRI session, during which the same FLAIR and cDSC MRI scans were acquired.

Data analysis

Image analysis was performed using the statistical parametric mapping (SPM) software package (Version 12, Wellcome Trust Centre for Neuroimaging, London, United Kingdom; http://www.fil.ion.ucl.ac.uk/spm/) and MATLAB (MathWorks, Natick, MA, USA). The realignment routine in SPM was used for motion correction in the cDSC images. FLAIR images were coregistered to the mean cDSC images. The ROIs were manually delineated on all slices using the ITK-SNAP software (Version 3.8; http://www.itksnap.org) by five experienced investigators (X.Z., W.L., L.P., H.I.S., and A.P.) independently. Discrepancies among different raters were reviewed and resolved during group meetings. All ROIs except for facial lymph nodes were identified in all participants (n = 25). Facial lymph nodes could only be identified in 10 subjects (age 50.9 ± 19.2 years, range 22–85 years; 6 females and 4 males). In the 4 hour post-GBCA scans, the inferior turbinate and facial lymph node could not be robustly identified, which were excluded from the analysis. All the ROIs were then overlaid onto all MRI scans from the same subject for further analysis.

In dynamic cDSC MRI scans, two types of analysis were performed. First, the ROI based analysis was performed in which signals from the entire ROI were averaged without voxel selection. Voxel-wise relative signal change (∆S/S) was calculated as the difference between pre- and post-GBCA signals divided by the pre-GBCA signal. The GBCA concentration in the CSF ([GBCA]) was calculated from cDSC ∆S/S using the theory described in our previous work. Note that an increased GBCA concentration in the CSF corresponds to a decreased cDSC signal change [41]. When comparing MR signals across multiple scanning sessions (pre-GBCA, post-GBCA, and 4 hour post-GBCA), the MR signal in each image was normalized using the mean corpus callosum signal of the corresponding scan to correct for potential intersession signal drift as described in previous studies [37].

Second, the voxel-based analysis was performed in which signals were averaged over voxels that showed significant post-GBCA changes compared to pre-GBCA in each ROI. A one-tailed two-sample t-test was employed to compare the pre- and post-GBCA signals in cDSC MRI. Voxels with an adjusted p-value of 0.05 or less were considered to have a significant post-GBCA signal change. Voxels that showed significant changes in dynamic cDSC images during the immediate and 4 hour post-GBCA periods were analyzed separately at each time point to reflect the actual signal changes in each ROI (more in “Discussion”). All the other procedures are the same as in ROI-based analysis.

Statistics

Statistical analyses were performed using paired t-tests to compare (1) relative signal changes and GBCA concentrations across ROIs at each time point, and (2) relative signal changes and GBCA concentrations between immediate and 4 hour post-GBCA periods within each ROI. For the voxel-based analysis, a one-tailed two-sample t-test was used to compare pre- and post-GBCA cDSC signals. Multiple comparisons were corrected by controlling the false discovery rate (adjusted P < 0.05) when identifying significant voxels within each ROI.

Results

Region of interest (ROI) based analysis

Signal changes over the entire ROI were averaged in this analysis. Immediately after IV injection, no significant signal changes were detected in the intracranial regions (olfactory bulb and straight gyrus) and the cribriform plate. Among the extracranial regions, significant post-GBCA signal changes were detected in the turbinates and nasal septum (Table 1), but not in the ethmoid bone region below the cribriform plate and the facial lymph nodes. Figure 2 presents the averaged time courses of relative signal changes (ΔS/S) measured from cDSC MRI in each ROI. Following IV GBCA injection, significant cDSC signal changes were observed in most extracranial ROIs (turbinates and nasal septum) from 10 to 20 s, reached a plateau around 50–70 s, and remained at the plateau for the remainder of the dynamic cDSC MRI scan (5 min). Note that a negative cDSC signal change is expected when GBCAs enter CSF [41].

Voxel-based analysis

Voxels with significant GBCA-induced signal changes were detected in all ROIs immediately following IV administration (Table 3). Among intracranial ROIs, the olfactory bulb showed a larger percentage of significant voxels (P < 0.01) than the straight gyrus. Among the extracranial olfactory ROIs, all the turbinates and the nasal septum showed similar percentage of significant voxels (P > 0.1). On average, extracranial ROIs showed a greater percentage of significant voxels compared to the intracranial ROIs and cribriform plate (P < 0.01). As shown in Fig. 3a, many significant voxels in the turbinates are close to the boundary of the region where abundant lymphatic vessels are located [30]. In the facial lymph node (Fig. 3c), most significant voxels were in the surface area of the lymph node where lymphatic channels are located, while the central region of the lymph node where blood vessels are located showed few significant voxels [43]. All intracranial and extracranial regions showed similar signal changes (ΔS/S) and GBCA concentrations from the significant voxels, respectively. On average, extracranial regions showed the greatest magnitude of ΔS/S and GBCA concentration, followed by the cribriform plate (P < 0.05), and intracranial regions showed the smallest magnitude of ΔS/S and GBCA concentration (P < 0.01) immediately after GBCA injection (Table 3).

Discussion

In this study, the distribution of CSF and CSF-like fluids (interstitial and lymphatic fluid, and nasal glandular and mucosal secretions) in human olfactory regions were examined using cDSC MRI immediately and 4 h after IV GBCA administration in a group of healthy subjects. GBCA-induced fluid signal changes were detected in all olfactory regions during both the immediate and 4 hour post-GBCA periods. However, the GBCA-induced fluid signal changes were more spatially extensive and greater in magnitude in extracranial olfactory regions such as the turbinates and nasal septum than in intracranial regions such as the olfactory bulb and straight gyrus. When comparing the immediate and 4 hour post-GBCA periods, extracranial olfactory regions showed lower GBCA concentration with a smaller spatial extent at 4 h, whereas intracranial regions showed comparable GBCA concentration and spatial extent at both time points.

Compared to existing in vivo human studies on CSF clearance via the olfactory route [31–34], the current study has a few unique features. First, an optimized MRI approach with minimal partial volume effects from blood and parenchyma was employed. Previous studies on this topic used FLAIR [32, 34] or Black Blood MRI [31] or PET [33]. When tracers are injected intravenously, they may be present in both blood and CSF, even after a few hours [44–46], which makes the origin of tracer induced signal changes ambiguous. The cDSC MRI method adopted in this study uses an ultra-long TE to suppress blood and tissue signals, so we are confident that the measured ∆S/S reflects GBCA-induced signal changes in the CSF and CSF-like fluids only, even when the spatial resolution is not sufficient to distinguish blood vessels and CSF space. This has been validated using phantoms in previous technical studies [41]. In the current study, the absence of cDSC signals in the central area of lymph nodes where most blood vessels are found further demonstrates that blood signals have been effectively suppressed in cDSC MRI. Second, all participants in the current study were healthy subjects without known diseases associated with BBB dysfunction or other brain diseases. Previous studies on this topic were performed in Alzheimer’s disease patients [33], individuals with tentative CSF circulation disorders [31], patients with various conditions [32], or mild stroke patients [34].

Most importantly, GBCA-induced fluid signal changes were examined in intracranial and extracranial olfactory regions separately in this study. Blood vessels in extracranial regions such as the nasal mucosa have fenestrated capillary systems [47], which permits IV GBCAs to enter the nearby CSF-like fluids [48] from the blood pool directly in these areas. This is indicated in our data, as extracranial olfactory regions showed more widespread spatially and more intense GBCA-induced signal changes than intracranial olfactory regions after IV administration. The lymph node, which is supplied by branches of the ECA, exhibited GBCA-induced signal changes similar to those observed in the extracranial olfactory regions, supporting the extracranial origin of the IV GBCAs detected in these regions. At 4 h post-GBCA, extracranial olfactory regions showed decreased GBCA-induced signal changes compared to the immediate post-GBCA period, while the GBCA-induced signal changes in intracranial olfactory regions persisted, which aligns with a generally faster lymphatic fluid circulation via the extracranial lymphatic system compared to CSF circulation in the brain. Tracer accumulation can also occur in the nasal glandular and mucosal secretions in these extracranial olfactory regions. Our results are consistent with previous studies that showed substantial tracer enrichment in the nasal mucosa area using IV tracers for PET [33] or MRI [34] with a highly similar spatial distribution and a comparable percentage of significant voxels in the area [33]. Nevertheless, as most of these tracers may come from peripheral blood supply after IV injection and thus do not pass through the brain, the distribution and clearance of these tracers in extracranial regions may not be relevant to CSF circulation and clearance from the brain but rather reflect lymphatic fluid drainage through the extracranial lymphatic system. The GBCA enhancement in the nasal mucosa resulting from intracranial CSF drainage after IV injection may be very limited, which could explain the seemingly conflicting results reported in studies using IT injection [31, 32], where little GBCA enhancement was detected in the extracranial olfactory regions below the cribriform plate.

In the intracranial regions, our data showed significant GBCA-induced CSF signal changes in the olfactory bulb and straight gyrus. When averaging over the entire ROIs, the signal changes were not significant. When a voxel-based analysis was conducted, sub-regions covering at least 30% of all voxels in each ROI showed significant post-GBCA signal changes. Both the olfactory bulb (an intracranial olfactory region) and the straight gyrus (an intracranial region outside olfactory regions) showed comparable signal changes that are similar to other intracranial regions reported in previous IV GBCA MRI studies [37, 39–41, 49–58]. In healthy human subjects, IV GBCAs are not expected to cross the BBB in intracranial regions. However, IV GBCAs can enter the intracranial CSF space at several locations in the brain with more permeable barriers between blood and CSF even in healthy subjects [38], such as the dura mater [39, 40] and choroid plexus [37]. The GBCA-induced signal changes detected in the intracranial olfactory regions, especially at 4 h post-GBCA, may come from the SAS that connects to these regions with weaker barriers. Therefore, IV GBCA distribution in the intracranial olfactory regions can be interpreted as an indicator of CSF circulation and clearance in this brain area.

The cribriform plate, the boundary between intracranial and extracranial olfactory regions, as well as the ethmoid bone region, the area immediately inferior to the cribriform plate both showed similar GBCA-induced signal changes to the intracranial olfactory regions. This is in agreement with previous studies using both IV [34] and IT [31] GBCA MRI.

Two analysis methods were performed in this study. First, the ROI-based analysis was carried out in which MR signals in the entire ROI were averaged. This is a straightforward approach which provides an overall evaluation of signal changes in the whole ROI. Nevertheless, most ROIs in the olfactory regions are highly heterogeneous. It is difficult or even impossible to visually separate small CSF structures within the ROIs. When averaging over the entire ROI, the underlying GBCA-induced signal changes may be significantly underestimated. Also, some ROIs, such as the ethmoid bone and facial lymph node, showed slightly positive average post-GBCA signal changes. However, these remained within the noise range and were possibly due to motion-related signal fluctuations. Some previous studies placed pre-defined circular or rectangular shapes in each ROI to identify sub-regions [31–34]. Here, we performed a second analysis method by selecting voxels that showed significant post-GBCA signal changes in each ROI, similar to typical functional MRI analysis. These voxels are more likely to correspond to CSF space within the ROIs with GBCA distribution, and therefore provide a more accurate measurement of the GBCA-induced signal changes in the CSF. When comparing the immediate and 4 h post-GBCA periods, the spatial distribution of these significant voxels differed slightly. We interpret this as a spatial drift of GBCA distribution within the ROIs over time, and therefore chose to calculate signal changes from significant voxels at each time point. The numbers of common voxels that showed significant post-GBCA signal changes at both time points were reported. However, we feel that the underlying physiological meaning of these common voxel is unclear. For instance, this spatial drift can be the result of tracer redistribution or motion. Thus, the common voxels were not used in further analysis.

The GBCA concentration in CSF calculated from the voxel-based analysis in intracranial olfactory regions was comparable to other intracranial regions measured in previous IV GBCA MRI studies [37, 41], i.e. approximately 5–10% of the typical steady-state GBCA concentration in blood after IV injection. The voxel-based GBCA concentration in extracranial olfactory regions were about 30–100% higher than the intracranial regions. The GBCA concentration calculated from the ROI-based analysis were much smaller, possibly due to the dilution effects from sub-regions in each ROI that do not contain CSF and CSF-like fluids and/or GBCAs. Interestingly, the GBCA concentration in most extracranial olfactory regions were about 0.02–0.04 mmol/L (~ 15–30%) higher than the lymph nodes. It is tempting to interpret this difference as an indicator that the nasal mucosa may receive GBCA distribution from both the intra- and extra-cranial pathways. However, this speculation warrants further investigation with longitudinal data.

The T1 and T2 values of CSF-like fluids in the extracranial regions can differ significantly from those of intracranial CSF. This may have important implications for interpreting GBCA -induced MR signal changes in these regions. First, in order to convert cDSC signal changes to GBCA concentration, the T1 and T2 values of the underlying fluid without GBCA need to be determined [41]. In the current algorithm, intracranial CSF T1 and T2 from the literature are adopted [41]. However, as shown in Figure S1 in the Supplemental Material, if the T1 of CSF-like fluid in the extracranial regions is significantly shorter than intracranial CSF T1, the GBCA concentration in the fluid can be overestimated using the current algorithm. This may be another factor contributing to the higher apparent GBCA concentration in the extracranial olfactory regions than in the intracranial olfactory regions and lymph nodes. Second, the different T1 and T2 values of CSF-like fluids can also alter the relationship between MR signal changes and GBCA concentration. For instance, Melin et al. [31] reported very limited GBCA enhancement in the nasal mucosa regions inferior to the cribriform plate using a T1-weighted Black Blood (T1-BB) MRI sequence after intrathecal GBCA administration. In Figure S2 in the Supplemental Material, we show that the GBCA-induced signal changes in a typical T1-BB sequence can diminish significantly when the underlying fluid has a shorter T1 than intracranial CSF. This may be an alternative consideration when interpreting the lack of GBCA enhancement in these regions. Similarly, the T1 and T2 relaxivity (r1 and r2, respectively) of GBCA in CSF-like fluids may also differ from those in CSF, as r1 and r2 are medium dependent. These considerations underscore the importance of examining the MR parameters when interpreting GBCA-induced signal changes across different regions and in different MRI sequences. The absence of substantial GBCA enhancement does not necessarily indicate a lower GBCA concentration. Therefore, approaches that can quantify GBCA concentration are strongly preferred.

Our IV GBCA data did not provide direct evidence for whether the olfactory route remains a primary CSF drainage pathway in humans. The smaller IV GBCA-induced CSF signal changes in the intracranial olfactory regions compared to extracranial regions should not be interpreted as evidence for limited olfactory CSF egress. Instead, this difference between the intracranial and extracranial olfactory regions may be primarily driven by the differing barrier permeability, tissue perfusion, and tracer kinetics in these regions. On the other hand, the fact that the GBCA concentration in the CSF of intracranial olfactory regions was comparable to other intracranial regions such as the choroid plexus and dura mater [37, 41] suggests that CSF clearance through the cribriform plate may play a similar role as the other CSF outflow pathways in the human brain. However, with the current data, we cannot determine how much tracer accumulation in the extracranial olfactory regions came from CSF drainage through the cribriform plate. Given that CSF production and circulation is a relatively slow process in the human brain and that CSF can drain from the brain through multiple pathways, GBCAinduced MR signal changes in the olfactory regions are expected to be small even if the olfactory pathway is still a major CSF outflow route in humans.

Technical limitations

The current study is a part of our initial effort to evaluate whether IV GBCA based MRI can be used to investigate CSF distribution and clearance in the human brain in a similar way as IT GBCA based MRI. Therefore, our sample size is quite limited, although all the results we report here were statistically significant. Furthermore, we only had two post-GBCA time points up to 4 h after IV injection, which is insufficient to assess the relatively slow CSF circulation and clearance in the human brain. Based on these initial results and the experience we gained from the pilot studies, we will perform subsequent longitudinal studies to monitor GBCA-induced signal changes in the CSF using the established methodology in a larger cohort. We will also extend the coverage of MRI scans to include the cervical lymph nodes in future studies. In image analysis, we were not able to co-register and normalize the olfactory ROIs into a common space due to considerable inter-subject variability in these small regions. We are currently working on deep-learning based methods for normalization, which will enable us to provide group-level maps that can improve reproducibility and interpretability. Finally, it should be noted that studying CSF drainage is most effective when tracers are introduced directly into the CSF space. The drainage process is better visualized using macromolecular tracers, as low-molecular-weight compounds tend to pass from extracranial tissues into blood vessels rather than reaching lymphatic pathways [5]. The entry points and clearance pathways for IV GBCAs in the CSF circulation have not been completely mapped. Further studies are warranted to address these important questions.

Conclusion

We showed that GBCA-induced CSF and CSF-like fluid signal changes can be detected in the olfactory regions in healthy human subjects using IV GBCA based MRI approaches. The intracranial olfactory regions showed smaller GBCA-induced CSF signal changes compared to extracranial olfactory regions. The GBCA concentration in the CSF of intracranial olfactory regions was comparable to other intracranial regions. GBCA-induced signal changes in the CSF-like fluids of extracranial olfactory regions were substantial, but they may primarily originate from peripheral routes after IV injection which reflects lymphatic fluid circulation in the extracranial lymphatic system, and are not directly related to CSF distribution and clearance from the brain. When using IV GBCA based methods, it is important to separate intracranial and extracranial regions in the analysis and to consider the blood supply to each region. Longitudinal studies using the established methodology are underway to further clarify the CSF clearance pathways in the human brain.
