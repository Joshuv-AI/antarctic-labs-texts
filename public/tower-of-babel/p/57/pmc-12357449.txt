Clinical efficacy of immune checkpoint inhibitors for cancer of unknown primary: a multi-center retrospective study
Authors: Haowen Wang, Siqi Song, Yuzhou Nie, Jinting Wu, Linyue Pan, Qizhen Zou, Gaizhen Kuang, Chen Chen, Xiaochen Zhang, Wenfeng Li, Shirong Zhang
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
BackgroundPatients diagnosed with cancer of unknown primary (CUP) have limited treatment options, resulting in a dismal prognosis. The efficacy of immune checkpoint inhibitors (ICIs) in these patients is still unclear.MethodsWe collected the clinical data of these patients retrospectively and multicentrically. In total, 190 patients diagnosed with CUP in 6 hospitals were collected, of which 58 patients received immunotherapy.ResultsThe median overall survival (OS) was 17.3 months. The OS of the ICIs-treated group was significantly longer than that of the non-ICIs group in unfavorable subgroup, 29.27 months and 10.43 months, respectively (HR = 0.435, 95% CI 0.267–0.709; p = 0.0006). In the first-line systemic treatment, the OS and progression-free survival (PFS) in the ICIs group were significantly longer than that of the non-ICIs group. The OS was 45.53 months versus 12.03 months (HR = 0.431, 95% CI 0.259–0.717; p = 0.004), and the PFS was 11.33 months versus 5.43 months (HR = 0.558, 95% CI 0.351–0.889; p = 0.021). The objective response rate (ORR) and disease control rate (DCR) of ICIs group were higher too. Finally, a predictive nomogram model was developed to accurately forecast the response of patients with CUP to ICIs.ConclusionsIn this cohort of unfavorable subgroup, immunotherapy appears to extend the survival of CUP patients who received chemotherapy or systemic therapy. One model could predict the time to recurrence following immunotherapy in the cohort.Trial registrationRetrospective registration of local ethics committees.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12885-025-14778-6.

Introduction

Cancer of unknown primary (CUP) is characterized by the detection of metastatic cancer without a discernible primary tumor, despite a standard and comprehensive diagnostic work-up [1]. CUP comprises 3%−5% of all diagnosed cancers, with an incidence ranging from 5.3 to 19 cases per 100,000 people worldwide [2, 3]. CUP ranks as the seventh to the eighth most prevalent malignant disease globally, and is the fourth leading cause of cancer-related mortality [4]. CUP tumors can be classified into favorable subgroup and unfavorable subgroup [5]. The favorable subgroup included: patients with neuroendocrine carcinoma, squamous carcinoma limited to cervical lymph nodes (LNs) (Head and neck cancer–like), adenocarcinoma restricted to axillary LNs in females (breast cancer–like), extragonadal germ cell tumor syndrome (GCT-like), peritoneal carcinomatosis in females (primary peritoneal cancer–like), squamous carcinoma limited to inguinal LNs (anal canal carcinoma–like), completely resectable by surgery single metastatic carcinoma. The remaining patients with CUP were classified as belonging to the unfavorable subgroup [1].

In current years, with the progress of radiological and molecular assessments, the recognition rate of primary tumor sites has gradually increased, but its carcinogenesis and progression mechanism are still mysterious. Therefore, CUP carries a dismal prognosis, with a median overall survival (OS) of less than 1 year [6]. Patients with CUP commonly undergo treatment with empiric chemotherapies, including platinum-taxane regimens [7]. In the unfavorable subsets, the median OS of patients receiving chemotherapy ranges from 1.6 months to 13.6 months [8], and the first-line treatment only yields a progression-free survival (PFS) of 2–4 months [9, 10]. For the targeted therapy based on profiling gene expression, the positive rate of targets with better curative effect is not high, only 5.2% are specific epidermal growth factor receptor (EGFR) mutations, which is clinically operable gene targets [11]. While the most found are TP53 (46.4%), KRAS (19.6%) and CDKN2A (18.6%), which are not available clinically [11]. The median OS was 9.8 months for site-specific treatment which based on gene expression profiling versus 12.5 months for empirical paclitaxel and carboplatin [12]. A meta-analysis also showed that site-specific therapy did not improve PFS (HR = 0.93, 95% CI 0.74–1.17; p = 0.534) and OS (HR = 0.75, 95% CI 0.55–1.03; p = 0.069) compared with empirical therapy [13]. Obviously, the results of site-specific treatment in CUP patients showed unsatisfactory.

Immune checkpoint inhibitors (ICIs) have become an indispensable choice for the treatment of many tumors. Not all patients are sensitive to ICIs. The Food and Drug Administration (FDA) has granted approval for the use of ICIs in patients with high tumor mutational burden (TMB), which also applies to CUP [14, 15]. Recently, an open-label phase II trial has demonstrated the clinical advantage of nivolumab and pembrolizumab in CUP patients [3, 16]. The objective response rates (ORRs) were 22.2% and 20.0%, respectively. Nivolumab had a median PFS of 4.0 months and a median OS of 15.9 months, while pembrolizumab had a median PFS of 4.1 months and a median OS of 11.3 months. Nivolumab showed better clinical efficacy in tumors with high programmed death-ligand 1 (PD-L1) expression, high tumor mutation burden, and microsatellite instability-high. Pembrolizumab was more effective in tumors with high PD-L1 expression and intense tumor infiltrating lymphocytes (TIL) infiltration. Besides, in second-line treatment of nivolumab and ipilimumab, the high TMB group exhibited longer median PFS (18.3 months vs. 2.4 months) and median OS (18.3 months vs. 3.6 months) [9]. However, there remains a paucity of data regarding patients with CUP who have undergone immunotherapy compared to those who have not. Especially the efficacy of immunotherapy in patients who have not previously undergone treatment remains largely undetermined.

Through the retrospective multi-center collection of CUP patients, we here reported the prognostic outcomes of CUP patients who received immunotherapy or not. We analyzed the efficacy of immunotherapy in the first-line treatment. Ultimately, we developed a predictive model for CUP patients to forecast the effectiveness of immunotherapy.

Clinical data collection

Clinical data: Patient data such as age, gender, height, weight, smoking, blood routine, LDH, Eastern Cooperative Oncology Group (ECOG) performance score, TMB, microsatellite instability (MSI), PD-L1 expression, pathology, status of lymph node metastasis, surgical treatment of tumors, therapeutic regimen, and time of progression and death were gathered.

Therapeutic regimen: Therapeutic regimens include systemic therapy, other treatments such as radiotherapy, local treatment, traditional Chinese medicine treatment, and no treatment. Systemic therapy includes chemotherapy, targeted therapy, and immunotherapy.

Treatment group refers to the patients receiving any therapeutic regimen. Non-Treatment group refers to the patients who have never received any treatment. ICIs group was defined as patients who received immunotherapy no matter which line in the treatment group. Non-ICIs group was defined as patients who had never received immunotherapy in the treatment group.

Pathology types: Tumor pathology types are divided into 5 categories according to the guidelines [1]: well and moderately differentiated adenocarcinomas; squamous cell carcinomas; carcinomas with neuroendocrine differentiation; poorly differentiated carcinomas (including poorly differentiated adenocarcinomas); undifferentiated neoplasms. Some pathology in our study could only be classified as malignant tumors due to sample size limitations, and we classified these patients into other groups.

Evaluation of efficacy and immune-related adverse reactions (AE): The efficacy assessment is conducted in accordance with the RECIST v1.1, which categorizes responses as complete remission (CR), partial remission (PR), stable disease (SD), or progressive disease (PD). ORR = (CR + PR)/(CR + PR + SD + PD) * 100%. DCR = (CR + PR + SD)/(CR + PR + SD + PD) * 100%.

Endpoints

This study employed a hybrid approach including both inpatient and outpatient hospital EHR systems. The follow-up includes therapeutic efficacy, treatment-related AE, and time of progression and death. PFS and OS were the main efficacy measures in this study. PFS is the duration from treatment to disease progression, or the last follow-up if progression has not occurred, or the time of death attributable to various causes. OS is the period from treatment to death or last follow-up, or the time of death attributable to various causes. In this study, PFS and OS were assessed monthly, with the follow-up concluding on December 31, 2023.

Statistical methodology

Data were analyzed using SPSS 26.0. Measurement data were presented as mean ± standard deviation if it followed a normal distribution, and as median (interquartile range) if it did not. Groups were compared based on data distribution, using t-test or rank sum test. Categorical data were presented as frequency (%) and compared using χ2 test or Fisher exact probability method. Post hoc multiple tests were performed using the Bonferroni test. The Kaplan-Meier method described patient survival. Log-rank tested differences, and calculated the HR with a 95% confidence interval (CI). Cox regression was used for univariate analysis and multivariate analysis. Cox regression analysis was conducted using backward stepping (Wald). GraphPad Prism 9 was employed for plotting. Α = 0.05, P < 0.05 was significant.

A nomogram was constructed using significant predictors based on multivariable Cox regression analysis (backward stepwise variable selection procedure) by R software (version 4.3.1) with the survival and rms packages. The 3-month, 6-month and 1-year recurrence-free survival predictions were established.

Results

Baseline characteristics

From October 2012 to December 2023, a total of 204 patients were treated in 6 hospitals according to the guidelines, 14 patients were excluded due to lack of survival data or treatment data, and 190 CUP patients were ultimately incorporated into the cohort. The baseline characteristics of the patients are depicted in Table 1. The median age is 60 years old, from 29 to 88 years old. There were 79 (41.58%) females and 111 (58.42%) males. There were 68 (35.79%) patients in the favorable subgroup and 122 (64.21%) in the unfavorable subgroup. 72 (37.89%) patients presented with tumors confined to the lymph nodes, whereas 118 (62.1%) patients exhibited tumors involving multiple organs. Among patients with non-only lymph node metastases, 75 (63.56%) also had metastases in other organs like bone, liver, lung, peritoneum, and brain. Additionally, 21 (17.8%) had multiple metastases within a single organ, such as bone, and 22 (18.64%) had metastases in multiple organs (excluding lymph nodes), like bone and liver or peritoneal and pelvic organ metastases. Adenocarcinoma was the most prevalent type, comprising 33.16% of cases, followed by poorly differentiated carcinoma and squamous cell carcinoma. 26 (13.68%) patients did not receive any treatment, while 164 (86.32%) patients were treated. A total of 58 cases of immunotherapy were documented, of which 39 (67.24%) were administered as first-line treatment. Immunotherapy encompasses a variety of pharmacological agents, with Sintilimab being the most commonly utilized among them. The expression levels of PD-L1 and tumor mutational burden (TMB) remain largely uncharacterized (Table 1).

Survival analysis

Our data revealed that the median OS of the 190 CUP patients was 17.3 months (Fig. 1A). The patients who received treatment exhibited a significantly prolonged survival time compared to those who did not receive any treatment, 24.87 months versus 4.35 months (HR = 0.357, 95% CI 0.178–0.718; p < 0.0001;Fig. 1B). Among the 26 untreated patients, the reasons for giving up treatment are as follows: 3 cases (11.54%) due to being too ill to qualify, 5 cases (19.23%) with systemic multiorgan metastasis, 4 cases (15.38%) aged over 80 years, and 14 cases (53.85%) attributable to educational background and familial considerations. To analyze treated patients further, 164 CUP patients were investigated. Univariate analysis showed that gender, ECOG score, LDH, only lymph node, classification, operation and neutrophil-to-lymphocyte ratio (NLR) were closely related to prognosis (Table S1, survival curve was shown in Fig. S1). When multivariate analysis was performed, only LDH, classification and NLR were associated with prognosis (Table S1). ECOG performance status and LDH are related to the prognosis of CUP patients [1, 9, 18], and our study is in line with these results. The OS and PFS of the unfavorable subgroup were significantly shorter compared to the favorable subgroup. Specifically, the OS was 15.87 months for the favorable subgroup versus 37.27 months for the favorable subgroup (HR = 2.617, 95% CI 1.446–3.247; p = 0.0003; Fig.S1A), while the PFS was 6.27 months compared to 13.6 months (HR = 1.722, 95% CI 1.202–2.468; p = 0.0041; Fig.S1B), respectively. Patients with only lymph node metastasis had a higher survival time compared to those with metastasis in multiple sites, which aligns with our findings [19].

Immunotherapy was analyzed in 164 patients with CUP, and the patients were categorized into two distinct groups, with and without ICIs (Table S2). However, there was no significant difference between the two groups in the treatment patients (25.73 months versus 16.17 months; p = 0.209; Fig. 1C). Cox proportional hazards analysis was conducted within the unfavorable subgroup, revealing that ICIs and the NLR were significantly associated with OS (Table S3). In the unfavorable subgroup, patients who received ICIs had a significantly longer median OS compared to those who did not, 29.27 months versus 10.43 months (HR = 0.435, 95% CI 0.267–0.709; p = 0.0006; Fig. 1D). The baseline characteristics of unfavorable subgroup CUP with or without ICIs were listed in the table (Table S4).

Systemic treatment options include chemotherapy, targeted therapy, and immunotherapy. Table 1 showed 7 patients receiving other treatments: 3 local radiotherapy, 2 interventional therapy (tumor local chemo perfusion via digital subtraction angiography), 1 surgical resection to achieve complete remission, and 1 Chinese medicine treatment. Subsequently, the patients who received first-line treatment were analyzed. Among the 157 patients diagnosed with CUP who received first-line systemic treatment, those with ICIs have longer survival than those without ICIs. But, there was no statistically significant difference observed in OS or PFS between the two groups (Fig. 2A, B). However, in the unfavorable subgroup, median OS was statistically significantly improved in patients receiving immunotherapy compared to those who did not receive it, 45.53 months and 12.03 months, respectively (HR = 0.431, 95% CI 0.259–0.717; p = 0.004; Fig. 2C). PFS yielded similar results between the two groups, 11.33 months versus 5.43 months (HR = 0.558, 95% CI 0.351–0.889; p = 0.021; Fig. 2D).

Effect analysis and adverse reactions

Then the effectiveness of immunotherapy in the treated patients were analyzed (Fig. 4). Among 164 treated patients, 58 patients received ICIs while 106 patients received non-ICIs. The ORR of the ICIs group was higher compared to the non-ICIs group, 53.4% vs. 40.6%, p = 0.002. The ICIs group had a higher disease control rate (DCR) compared to the non-ICIs group, 81% vs. 72.7%, p = 0.003 (Fig. 4A). Out of the patients receiving first-line systemic therapy, 39 were treated with ICIs while 118 did not receive ICIs. The ORR and DCR in the ICIs group exhibited statistically significant increases compared to the group that did not receive ICIs. The ORR was 69.3% vs. 42.4%, p = 0.015; The DCR was 94.9% vs. 76.3%, p = 0.007 (Fig. 4B).

The predominant adverse reactions associated with immunotherapy were rash, followed by blood routine changes, hepatic impairment, then were interstitial pneumonia, renal dysfunction, thyroid function decreased. Several patients experienced more than one adverse reaction. Most patients had no adverse reactions and tolerated the immunotherapy well (Table S5).

Predictive model

Subsequently, an analysis was conducted on 58 patients who underwent immunotherapy. Cox regression analysis revealed that ECOG, BMI, platelet-to-lymphocyte ratio (PLR), and ICIs treatment lines were identified as the main factors influencing PFS in the context of immunotherapy. The cut-off values of ECOG, PLR, ICIs-lines and PLR were identified using X-Tile software. The findings indicated that the survival risk was higher for ECOG ≥ 1 compared to ECOG = 0 (HR = 1.663, 95% CI 1.114–2.481; p = 0.013; Table S6), for BMI falling within the abnormal range compared to within the normal range (HR = 2.316, 95% CI 1.059–5.063; p = 0.035), and for ICIs used as a third-line or later treatment compared to within 2-lines treatment (HR = 1.827, 95% CI 1.292–2.584; p = 0.001). High PLR ratio poses a higher survival risk than low ratio (HR = 0.996, 95% CI 0.991-1.000; p = 0.041). Ultimately, a prediction model was constructed utilizing the aforementioned four indicators within the R software (Fig. 5). Each variable on the nomogram is allocated a score based on the rank order of the effect estimates. By summing these scores and assessing the cumulative score of all variables using a composite “total points” scale, one can extrapolate the estimated probability of recurrence time at 3 months, 6 months, or 1 year by drawing a vertical line downwards.

Discussion

Currently, there are limited studies on ICIs for CUP patients, with a focus on phase II clinical trials and predicting immunotherapy sensitivity through programmed death-ligand 1 expression (PD-L1), tumor mutation burden (TMB), microsatellite instability (MSI), and tumor infiltrating lymphocytes (TILs) [3, 9, 16, 20]. To our knowledge, this study represents the initial attempt to compare the effectiveness of with or without immunotherapy in unfavorable subgroup as a retrospective analysis. CUP can be divided into two clinicopathologic subtypes: favorable subgroup with prognosis of 12–36 months, and unfavorable subgroup with prognosis of < 1 year [21], and our results were close to previous reports.

In a retrospective study of 219 CUP patients, Log-rank testing showed longer OS in ICIs-treated CUP patients (p < 0.05), and 77% of ICIs-treated CUP patients (28 patients) achieved 12-month survival compared with 53% for non-ICIs treated CUP patients (128 patients) and 52.5% for non-systemic therapy treated CUP patients (35patients) [22]. However, the graph showed intersecting curves after 12 months. Our data indicated that the treated group had higher OS compared to the untreated group. Although the data volume of the two groups is quite different, the follow-up data of the untreated group is as complete as the treatment group, except for the therapeutic regimen. Excitingly, in the unfavorable subgroup of treated patients, the survival of patients treated with ICIs was significantly better than that of patients without ICIs treatment. Immunotherapy was used in 41 cases (41%) of the unfavorable subgroup and 17 cases (26.56%) of the favorable subgroup, with a higher proportion in the unfavorable group. This may explain the longer survival time for immunotherapy in the unfavorable subgroup, a trend not observed in the favorable subgroup. Other possible explanation for this phenomenon could be that patients in the favorable subgroup may respond well to conventional treatments such as chemotherapy, whereas those in the unfavorable subgroup may exhibit resistance to chemotherapy. Immunotherapy may improve survival more in the unfavorable subgroup than in the favorable subgroup. Immunotherapy served as a viable alternative for the unfavorable subgroup. CUP patients experience genetic changes and chromosomal instability (CIN) as the disease progresses [8]. It is recognized that CIN promotes cancer aggressiveness through the enhancement of tumor heterogeneity and genetic diversity among the tumor cell population, which allows to the therapy resistance such as conventional chemotherapy, but it is beneficial for immunotherapy [23, 24]. In our investigation, 72 patients in the unfavorable subgroup (72%) exhibited multiple organ metastasis, and a smaller proportion (28%) presented solely with lymph node metastasis. Conversely, a smaller percentage of patients in the favorable subgroup (37.5%) displayed multiple organ metastasis, while a larger proportion (62.5%) had only lymph node metastasis. Like all retrospective series, our study is subject to limitations inherent to this type of analysis, such as selection bias, reporting bias (including missing data), and referral bias.

In the unfavorable subgroup, patients who received systemic treatment including ICIs in the first line had longer OS and PFS compared to those without ICIs. The primary approach of systemic treatment typically consists of chemotherapy, leading us to investigate the effects of combining immunotherapy with chemotherapy. Out of 58 immunotherapy cases, 39 (67.24%) were first-line treatments. Among these, there were 18 clinical predictions: 6 for lung cancer, 4 for head and neck cancer, 3 for skin-derived cancers, 2 for gastric cancer, and 1 each for liver, prostate, and kidney cancers, along with 2 metastatic melanomas. TMB was identified in 2 patients, while the remaining 17 were evaluated by multidisciplinary team (MDT). Our results suggest that the addition of immunotherapy to chemotherapy substantially improves PFS and OS of patients in unfavorable subgroup. It indicated that the OS and PFS are extended compared to previous reports on immunotherapy alone. The median PFS and OS of nivolumab were 4.0 months and 15.9 months, and pembrolizumab were 4.1 months and 11.3 months [3, 16]. CUPISCO is a phase II randomized trial which evaluate the efficacy and safety of targeted therapy or immunotherapy as compared to platinum-based chemotherapy in patients diagnosed with CUP [25]. The results showed that in the unfavorable subset CUP patients, 326 (75%) were in the molecularly guided therapy (MGT) group and 110 (25%) were in the chemotherapy group. 235 patients received atezolizumab plus chemotherapy in MGT group. The PFS was 6.1 months in the MGT group versus 4·4 months in the chemotherapy group. The findings are proximity with our results. There is also an ongoing study of pembrolizumab in patients with poor-prognosis CUP (NCT03391973). The outcomes are eagerly anticipated.

Immunotherapy can improve ORR and DCR of other tumors. For example, in extensive-stage small-cell lung cancer, durvalumab with chemotherapy showed an ORR of 68% and a DCR of 75%, compared to 58% and 73% with chemotherapy alone [26]. In esophageal cancer, sintilimab with chemotherapy achieved an ORR of 66% and a DCR of 90%, compared to chemotherapy alone, which had an ORR of 46% and a DCR of 85% [27]. The ORR and DCR of chemotherapy in CUP patients were 15% and 50%, respectively [28]. And those of immunotherapy were 36% and 60%, respectively [16]. In our study, the ORR and DCR in ICIs-treated group were 53.4% and 81%, respectively. In the first-line systemic therapy containing ICIs, the ORR and DCR in containing ICIs were 69.3% and 94.9%, respectively. The elevation of effectiveness is primarily due to the integration of immunotherapy with additional treatment modalities.

NLR is currently reported to be linked to the prognosis of various types of tumors [29–31]. Our data showed that NLR was not independent prognostic factors by COX multivariate regression analysis (Table S1). We assessed the factors influencing the efficacy of immunotherapy. Biomarkers relevant to ICIs were identified in 28% of CUP patients, including a high TMB in 11.8%, microsatellite instability high status in 1.8%, and tumor PD-L1 expression in 22% [32]. The expression of PD-L1 can predict the efficacy of immunotherapy [33], 22% of patients with CUP have PD-L1 expression in their tumors [19, 34]. TMB is also an indicator of immunotherapy, and the more the better [20, 35]. However, only 15 patients with PD1/PD-L1 expression were collected in our data. Only 5 patients had TMB data. Most patients did not have detection because of the high cost. Therefore, alternative indicators must be utilized in order to forecast the effectiveness of immunotherapy in the case of insufficient data. We identified ECOG, BMI, therapy line of ICIs (ICIs-line), and PLR as independent prognostic variables for immunotherapy using Cox multivariate regression analysis, and subsequently developed a nomogram incorporating these variables. Our results showed that elevated PLR was associated with better immunotherapy outcomes. PLR has been documented in numerous scholarly articles pertaining to tumor immunotherapy [36, 37]. Previous study indicate that a high PLR correlates with reduced OS, PFS, and response rates in metastatic NSCLC patients treated with nivolumab [38]. While different tumors exhibit distinct characteristics and are not directly comparable, further research on the PLR remains necessary. The nomogram illustrates that for CUP patients with an ECOG score of 1, a normal BMI, receiving immunotherapy in the first line, and a PLR exceeding 132.5, the probability of no recurrence at 3 months, 6 months and 1 year were about 89%, 83% and 65%, respectively.

Our data currently exhibit certain limitations, including variability in systemic treatment protocols, a diverse array of immunotherapy drugs, a relatively small cohort of patients undergoing immunotherapy, and insufficient assessment of PD-L1 expression, TMB, and microsatellite instability. These aspects require further data collection in subsequent phases to enhance the robustness of our research.

Conclusions

In this cohort of CUP, specifically the unfavorable subgroup, immunotherapy appears to improve the efficacy of chemotherapy or systemic therapy, thereby prolonging survival. One model could predict the time to recurrence following immunotherapy for patients with CUP in our cohort.
