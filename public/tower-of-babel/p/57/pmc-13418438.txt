Quantitative measurements for acetabular cartilage delamination on MRI
Authors: Yichuan Zhu, Guanying Gao, Yan Xu
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractPurposeTo introduce the delamination width and delamination length for the quantitative description of the delamination between cartilage on MRI, and to determine the diagnostic value for acetabular cartilage delamination (ACD).MethodsData between January 2018 to December 2020 was reviewed. Patients diagnosed femoroacetabular impingement syndrome (FAIS) and underwent hip arthroscopy were included. Exclusion criteria were prior hip surgery and concomitant hip conditions. The delamination width and delamination length were measured at the coronal slice on magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) where the low-signal area on the fat-saturated proton density (FSPD) sequence was most apparent between the cartilage. The delamination width was measured along the direction of the radial line originating from the center of the femoral head. The delamination length was measured as the curve across the endpoints of delamination. The area under curve (AUC) for delamination width and length predicting ACD was 0.8677 (95% CI: 0.8113 to 0.9240) and 0.8113 (95% CI: 0.7528 to 0.8698), respectively. The cut off value of delamination width was 0.95 mm, with the sensitivity, specificity, positive predictive value (PPV), and negative predictive value (NPV) to be 89.9%, 74.8%, 70.0%, and 91.9%, respectively. The cut off value of delamination length was 2.80 mm, with the sensitivity, specificity, PPV, and NPV to be 43.0%, 94.2%, 90.2%, and 48.4%, respectively. Male sex and BMI were positively associated with delamination width and delamination length (all with P < 0.05).ConclusionThe delamination width and delamination length demonstrated good diagnostic value for ACD. Male sex and BMI were positively associated with these measurements. ACD may occur when the delamination width exceeds 0.95 mm and delamination length exceeds 2.80 mm in patients with FAIS.Level of evidenceLevel III. Cohort study.Keywords: Acetabular cartilage delamination, Femoroacetabular impingement, Magnetic resonance imaging

Introduction

Acetabular cartilage delamination (ACD) is a typical type of acetabular cartilage lesion that occurs when full-thickness cartilage separates or ‘‘debonds’’ from the underlying subchondral bone plate and forms an unstable cartilage flap, which is commonly caused by cam deformity [1]. The prevalence of ACD ranges from 31.5% to 86.5% in patients with femoroacetabular impingement syndrome (FAIS) [2–4].

The unstable cartilage flap may enlarge due to shear forces, posing risk of complete detachment from the adjacent cartilage, resulting in full-thickness defect and further impairing clinical outcomes [2, 5]. Therefore, it is important to recognize and adopt appropriate management for ACD. Currently, ACD is mainly diagnosed through arthroscopic inspection, which is determined by the presentation of “wave sign” or “carpet sign” [2, 3, 6, 7]. Preoperatively, magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) and magnetic resonance arthrography (MRA) are valuable examinations demonstrating diagnostic potential for ACD [6, 8]. However, due to different subjective diagnostic criteria and the experience in reading images, the diagnostic performance of MRI and MRA varies significantly across different studies [6, 9]. We have noticed that these diagnostic criteria consisted of qualitative descriptions of the delamination between cartilage. However, the thin space between the cartilage of the femoral head and that of the acetabulum, which is created by the vacuum of the hip joint, may pose challenges in making an accurate diagnosis [8].

The purpose of the present study is to introduce the delamination width and delamination length for the quantitative description of the delamination between cartilage on MRI, and to determine the diagnostic value for ACD. It is hypothesized that the delamination width and the delamination length could give proper quantitative representation of the ACD.

Methods

Patient selection

The patients’ MRI images and surgical reports were retrospectively reviewed from January 2018 to December 2020 at our institution. The study was approved by The Ethics Committee of Peking University Third Hospital (No. M2019193). Informed consent was obtained from all individual participants included in the study. All methods were carried out in accordance with the guidelines and regulations of the Ethics Committee of Peking University Third Hospital.

The inclusion criteria were: patients were clinically diagnosed with FAIS based on persistent hip or groin pain, positive physical examination (flexion adduction internal rotation test and/or flexion abduction external test), and radiographic findings (alpha angle [AA] > 55 degrees and/or lateral center-edge angle [LCEA] > 35 degrees); [10] failure of conservative treatment for at least 3 months; and underwent primary hip arthroscopic treatment for FAIS. The exclusion criteria were: prior hip surgery history; hip osteoarthritis with Tönnis grade > 1; concomitant hip conditions including synovial chondromatosis, pigmented villonodular synovitis, and developmental dysplasia of the hip (DDH, LCEA < 18 degrees).

Surgical technique

All surgeries were performed by a senior surgeon (Y.X.). The surgical process adopted a standard supine approach, as described in a previous study [11]. In summary, following the administration of anesthesia and the application of traction, the anterolateral, anterior, and mid-anterior portals were established. A comprehensive inspection of the central compartment via interportal capsulotomy was carried out to evaluate the intraarticular structures including acetabular rim, acetabular labrum, articular cartilage, and ligaments. ACD was diagnosed according to the presentation of “wave sign” or “carpet sign”, which means an area of degenerated cartilaginous surface at the chondrolabral junction, or a partial detachment of the articular cartilage from the subchondral bone without exposing it and no interruption of the articular surface [4]. After inspection, acetabuloplasty was performed when pincer deformity was present. Labral repair was carried out when a labral tear was found. Subsequently, chondroplasty was performed if partial-thickness cartilage lesions or chondral flaps were present. After treating the central compartment, the arthroscope was moved to the peripheral compartment, and femoroplasty was performed when cam deformity was identified. Finally, the capsule was routinely repaired.

Data collection

Patient demographics, including age, sex, height, weight, and body mass index (BMI), were retrieved from medical records. Preoperative radiographic examinations and CT scan were performed. The AA was measured on the 45-degree Dunn view and LCEA was measured on the anteroposterior (AP) view by one observer (Y.Z.), as previously described in relevant studies [12–14].

Preoperative non-contrast magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) with a 3.0-T scanner (Magnetom Trio with Tim system, Siemens Healthcare, Erlangen, Germany) was performed for the unilateral hip joint. Fat-saturated proton density (FSPD) and T2-weighted sequences were applied to obtain the axial, coronal, and oblique sagittal planes. The delamination width was measured along the direction of the radial line originating from the center of the femoral head. The delamination length was measured as the curve across the endpoints of delamination. As shown in Fig. 1, the delamination width and delamination length were measured at the coronal slice where the low-signal area on the FSPD sequence was most apparent between the cartilage, indicating the maximum values of delamination width and length.

Measurement of the delamination width and length of a right hip on MRI. A The white dot line is the radial assistant line. The red line demonstrates delamination width; B The yellow curve demonstrates delamination length

MRI measurements were independently performed by two sports medicine surgery surgeons (Y.Z. and G.G). The mean values of measurements obtained from both observers were used for further analysis. Interobserver and intraobserver (Y.Z., interval of 2 weeks) reproducibility was assessed using the intraclass correlation coefficient (ICC) to determine the absolute agreement of the MRI measurements with all patients included for analysis. The two observers were blind to the results of the arthroscopic inspection during the measurement.

Statistical analysis

All data were analyzed using SPSS Statistics (version 27.0; IBM, NY). Continuous parameters were presented as the mean ± standard deviation (SD). Categorical variables were presented as the number of patients (%). The Mann–Whitney U test and the independent t-test were applied to compare the skewed variables and normally distributed variables between patients with and without ACD. Spearman rank correlation analysis was conducted to determine the relationship between factors and delamination width and length. The receiver operating characteristic (ROC) curve analysis was performed to evaluate the predictive ability of delamination width and length depending on the area under the curve (AUC), and to determine the optimal cutoff value based on the maximum Youden index. The level of statistical significance was set at P < 0.05.

Results

As shown in Fig. 2, a total of 176 patients were included in the study. Detailed patient characteristics are presented in Table 1. The mean age at surgery was 36.9 ± 9.8 years. The mean AA of the study cohort was 56.1 ± 7.2 (range: 46.6 to 82.7, median: 56.2) degrees, and the mean LCEA was 31.8 ± 5.2 (range: 18.4 to 43.1, median: 32.1) degrees. The arthroscopic findings and procedures were presented in Table 2. A total of 69 (39.2%) patients were diagnosed with ACD under arthroscopy.

For MRI measurement, both delamination width and length demonstrated good interobserver and intraobserver reproducibility (Table 3, all with P < 0.001 and ICC > 0.85). Patients with ACD demonstrated significant larger delamination width (1.30 ± 0.48 mm [95% CI: 1.19 to 1.42] vs. 0.55 ± 0.50 mm [95% CI: 0.45 to 0.64], P < 0.001) and delamination length (8.08 ± 4.02 mm [95% CI: 7.11 to 9.04] vs. 4.19 ± 3.84 mm [95% CI: 3.46 to 4.93], P < 0.001) compared to patients without ACD. In regression model, both delamination width (Beta: 22.844 [95% CI: 8.762 to 59.557], P < 0.001) and delamination length (Beta: 1.276 [95% CI: 1.166 to 1.397], P < 0.001) demonstrated significant correlation with ACD (Table 4). In ROC analysis, the area under curve (AUC) for delamination width and length predicting ACD was 0.8677 (95% CI: 0.8113 to 0.9240) and 0.8113 (95% CI: 0.7528 to 0.8698), respectively (Fig. 3). The cut off value of delamination width was 0.95 mm, with the sensitivity, specificity, positive predictive value (PPV), and negative predictive value (NPV) to be 89.9%, 74.8%, 70.0%, and 91.9%, respectively. The cut off value of delamination length was 2.80 mm, with the sensitivity, specificity, PPV, and NPV to be 43.0%, 94.2%, 90.2%, and 48.4%, respectively.

Discussion

In the present study, we found that delamination width and delamination length demonstrated good diagnostic value for ACD. Male sex and BMI were positively associated with these measurements. ACD may occur when the delamination width exceeds 0.95 mm and delamination length exceeds 2.80 mm.

According to previous studies, different diagnostic performances were reported when using MRA. Anderson et al. [2] defined ACD when both met: at least two consecutive slices in the same plane or in the same location in two different planes showed focal discontinuity of cartilage and fluid located between the articular cartilage and subchondral bone plate, and the area of abnormal cartilage was not completely detached from the adjacent cartilage. It was found that this method demonstrated sensitivity of 22% and specificity of 100%. Pfirrmann et al. [8] defined ACD as the presence of a layer of fluid between the acetabular cartilage and the subchondral bone plate. This method demonstrated respective sensitivity, specificity, and accuracy of 22%−30%, 95%, and 57%−61% by two readers. Konstantinidis et al. [15] reported the respective sensitivity, specificity, PPV, and NPV of 6%, 98%, 27%, and 91% based on the radiologist report. These studies revealed that MRA demonstrated a relative low sensitivity (6%−22%) but high specificity (91%−100%). We propose that the insufficient contrast of the delamination and the strong contrast of the joint fluid led to the generally low sensitivity when using MRA to diagnose ACD.

Compared to MRA, MRI was a less invasive examination with higher sensitivity for diagnosing ACD. Gao et al. [9] applied the similar diagnostic criteria of Anderson et al. [2] and reported the respective sensitivity, specificity, PPV, and NPV of 83.7%, 82%, 74.2%, and 89.1%. Neumann et al. [16] defined ACD as the presence of a linear area of bright intensity along the acetabular subchondral bone, and/or presence of an area of darker tissue at the surface of the acetabular cartilage. This method demonstrated sensitivity of 73.0% and specificity of 71.0%. It was concluded that these subjective diagnostic criteria lie in the discovery of the signs of “delamination” between the cartilages. According to our clinical experience, such signs are easily confused with the space between the normal acetabular cartilage and the femoral head cartilage. The normal cartilage space can be observed on MRI without the application of additional traction force in some patients. When MRI was performed with additional traction force, the cartilage space would be enlarged, which may be helpful in distinguishing ACD from this space [17, 18]. However, considering that traction may cause additional pain to patients, we performed standard MRI in the present study and introduced two quantitative measurements.

As ACD often occurs at the junction of the acetabular labrum cartilage at the anterosuperior acetabulum, the coronal plane was selected as the measurement plane [2]. Although the oblique sagittal plane can better observe the anterior cartilage damage, it is not suitable for observing superior location. According to the study conducted by Mechlenburg et al. [17] on measuring the thickness of hip joint cartilage, we chose the radial direction through the center of the femoral head as the most ideal direction for measuring the delamination width. The delamination width demonstrated high sensitivity of 89.9%. Interestingly, the delamination length demonstrated relatively high specificity of 94.2%. The sensitivity of the delamination width and the specificity of the delamination were superior than the reported studies (sensitivity: 77.7%−83.7%; specificity: 66.7%−82%) [9, 16, 19]. We proposed that the delamination length reflected the lesion area of ACD, and the delamination width reflected the severity of ACD. These measurements added more quantitative descriptions in the two dimensions to the previously reported “wave sign” on MRI. Therefore, combining the two measurements to diagnose ACD on MRI may enhance accuracy. These measurements may also assist surgeons in preoperatively recognizing ACD and planning for appropriate cartilage lesion management, thereby achieving a more comprehensive clinical pathway where imaging-based findings guide treatment decision-making for patients with FAIS [20, 21].

We found male sex and increasing BMI was positively related to these two measurements. Beaulé et al. [22] found that male sex (OR value of 2.00) was risk factors for ACD. These two factors are also risk factors for hip cartilage damage. Obesity can cause degeneration of articular cartilage, while male sex was related with larger amount of physical activity and a higher proportion of cam deformities, which was the most important mechanism causing ACD in finite element analysis [23, 24]. These factors associated with delamination width and delamination length were also risk factors of ACD, which further suggested the validity of these measurements.

Limitations

Several limitations must be acknowledged. First, the study was retrospectively designed. Therefore, selection bias was inevitable. The selection bias also existed when subjectively choosing the measurement MRI slice since we were not able to measure delamination width and length at all slices to find the maximum one. Second, the diagnostic cut-off values of the delamination width and delamination length were accurate to the 0.01 mm level, which might cause difficulties in applying them in clinical practice. Using these measurements with a 1 mm precision might be viable in routine clinical practice. Third, the measurements were taken on the coronal plane, which was limited in determining the ACD that occurred at the anterior location. Last, the results and cut-off value were based on our single-center cohort. The diagnostic values may not generalize to populations with different prevalence. All patients were diagnosed with concurrent FAIS and ACD and underwent hip arthroscopy. This situation increased prevalence and may inflate diagnostic metrics. Therefore, the external validity to general hip pain populations is limited, and caution should be exercised when generalizing the results to the general hip pain population.

Conclusions

The delamination width and delamination length demonstrated good diagnostic value for ACD. Male sex and BMI were positively associated with these measurements. ACD may occur when the delamination width exceeds 0.95 mm and delamination length exceeds 2.80 mm in patients with FAIS.
