Force-sensor-integrated soft microfinger for mechanical stimulation and generated force evaluation of zebrafish larvae
Authors: Tomohiro Nakatsuka, Hiromi Hirata, Moeto Nagai, Satoshi Konishi
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractZebrafish exhibit specific locomotor patterns during the early embryonic and larval stages, which has established them as a widely used vertebrate model for locomotor and behavioral research. In movement- and motor disorders-related analyses, touch-evoked response experiments are commonly used to trigger reflex behaviors in a zebrafish. Traditionally, these experiments have been performed manually using metal instruments such as tweezers or needles. However, this method carries a risk of physical injury to the larvae and poses considerable limitations in terms of stimulation repeatability and quantitative control. Recently, automated systems for touch response experiments have been developed to overcome these limitations; however, metal instruments are still predominantly used. To address this gap, this study introduces a novel microrobotic platform for stimulus delivery and response evaluation, featuring a microfinger equipped with integrated force sensors that enable flexible and safe contact. For tail stimulation using the microfinger, only the head of the zebrafish larva required immobilization to ensure efficient transmission of the applied force, which necessitated the development of a head restraint chamber device. The resulting touch response experimental system, comprising the microfinger and the fixation apparatus, effectively elicited reflex responses. Introduction

Robotic technologies play essential roles in enabling automation and precise manipulation in biomedical research and applications1,2. Microrobots and micro-manipulators have been developed to automate scientific experiments and analytical tasks3,4. For instance, robotic systems for cell manipulation that integrate microrobots within microfluidic chips have been demonstrated5. Soft micro-actuators and microrobots have been developed because of their inherent safety when interacting with delicate biomedical objects such as embryos and cells6,7. Among them, elastic fluidic microactuators are not only safe but also capable of generating high force and power density8.

Zebrafish are widely used as vertebrate model organisms for drug screening and the investigation of biological mechanisms owing to their genetic homology with humans and the similarity of organ pathology17,18. In particular, embryos and larvae at early developmental stages are used in developmental biology and neuroscience because their transparent bodies enable high-resolution imaging19–21. Zebrafish embryos and larvae exhibit stage-specific locomotor patterns and can respond and adapt to mechanical stimulation22,23. Disease models can be established through chemical treatment or genetic manipulation, making zebrafish valuable for studying motor disorders, such as Parkinson’s disease24. Accordingly, the touch response test plays a crucial role in elucidating the mechanisms underlying locomotion, motor dysfunction, learning, and memory25,26.

In previous touch response assays of zebrafish larvae, mechanical stimuli were typically delivered manually using metal tweezers or needles27,28. Although manual stimulation requires only a simple experimental setup, it is difficult to precisely control the location, angle, contact duration, and force of stimulation29. In addition, this method carries the risk of damaging the fragile bodies of the larvae. The behavioral response has traditionally been evaluated in terms of the presence or absence of a reaction, swimming distance, swimming velocity, and latency. However, these metrics make it difficult to quantify the input-output relationship, that is, how much behavioral response is generated by a given level of mechanical stimulus.

In previous studies, a robotic system has been introduced to enable high-throughput experimentation and quantitative assessment of both stimulation and behavioral responses29. The system automated and quantified the touching location, direction (angle) and speed of stimulation. In addition, the response attitudes are quantified using curve-fitting method applied to image data. However, the system still relied on metal tweezers or needles as the contact interface, indicating the need for soft devices that can provide safer mechanical interaction. Furthermore, immobilization is essential for detailed imaging and efficient transmission of mechanical stimuli during touch response experiments in zebrafish larvae30.

Herein, we developed a soft microfinger integrated with a force sensor to enable safe mechanical stimulation and quantitative response evaluation in zebrafish larvae, replacing conventional tools such as needles and tweezers used in touch response experiments. In addition, we developed a head restraint chamber to ensure effective force transmission between the microfinger and the zebrafish larva while enabling clear observation. Agarose gel was used as the restraint medium based on previously reported microfluidic devices designed for larval trapping, chemical stimulation, and response evaluation31–34.

Previously, we developed a soft microfinger robot driven by a pneumatic balloon actuator (PBA). PBA converts applied pressure into bending motion through a balloon channel structure formed between two Polydimethylsiloxane (PDMS) thin films with different rigidities9. Owing to its small size, softness, and inherent safety, we have actively promoted the PBA application in the biomedical field10–12. We have also developed flexible tactile sensors, including force and temperature sensors, for integration with PBA13,14. Herein, a liquid metal strain gauge was incorporated as a force sensor to evaluate larval locomotion and responses. The liquid metal strain gauge was fabricated by filling PDMS microchannels with a low-toxicity liquid metal (Galinstan) to maintain the softness and safety characteristics inherent to PBA15,16. This sensing approach was chosen because the microchannel structure required for gauge formation can be fabricated using the same materials and processes as those used for the PBA, facilitating seamless integration. In our previous work, the microfinger was successfully applied to the manipulation of cellular spheroids in PBS solution12. Based on this result, we considered microfinger to be well-suited for delivering controlled mechanical stimulation to zebrafish larvae in water.

Touch response experiments in zebrafish larvae successfully elicited behavioral reactions, demonstrating the effectiveness of the force‑sensor‑integrated microfinger and the restraint chamber. These results indicate that the microfinger enables safe direct contact, controllable stimulation and quantitative evaluation of behavioral responses. Furthermore, the force-sensing capability allows both the applied stimulus and the resulting response to be quantified as forces, suggesting the potential to advance touch-response assays from simple behavioral observation to quantitative analysis of the input–output relationship.

Device design

Experimental system for touch response testing in zebrafish larvae

The experimental system for the zebrafish larval touch response test comprises a microfinger and a head restraint chamber, as shown in Fig. 1(a). First, we attempted to integrate PBA and the liquid metal strain sensor into a single finger structure. However, the increased film thickness and the driving requirements of the PBA resulted in higher structural rigidity, which reduced force sensitivity35. High sensitivity is essential for detecting locomotor forces at the micronewton (µN) scale in zebrafish larvae. Therefore, we redesigned the microfinger to include two separate fingers with distinct functions: an actuator finger incorporating PBA for mechanical stimulation and a sensor finger integrating the force sensor for locomotion measurement (Fig. 1(b)). In this study, we used zebrafish larvae older than 5 dpf which are commonly employed in touch response assays. The finger was designed with dimensions of 3 mm in length (L) and 1 mm in width (W) to match 5 days postfertilization (5 dpf) zebrafish larvae. At 5 dpf, the total body length of the larvae is approximately 4 mm, and the head width is 660 μm (n = 3). Therefore, the microfinger can accommodate larvae across a range of sizes, as long as their tail width allows them to fit between the fingers. The restraint chamber is a device designed to embed and restrain only the larvae head using agarose gel, thereby facilitating observation and enabling the microfinger approach. The chamber dimensions are tailored to the size of the larvae.

Soft microfinger for mechanical stimulation and generated force evaluation of zebrafish larvae. (a) Zebrafish heads are embedded and restrained using a custom chamber with agarose gel. The restraint chamber allows the tail chamber to be removed, providing access for the microfingers. It is designed to accommodate microscope lenses, similar to conventional zebrafish locomotion assessment setups. (b) The microfinger consists of two fingers with distinct functions: an actuator finger incorporating a PBA for mechanical stimulation, and a force-sensing finger incorporating a liquid metal strain gauge for locomotion evaluation.

PBA design of the microfinger

The right actuator finger of the microfinger incorporates a PBA. Herein, the force generated by the PBA is a critical parameter because it directly determines the magnitude of mechanical stimulation applied to the larvae. The stimulation force can be adjusted to any force smaller than maximum output by controlling the applied pressure (P). Therefore, a PBA design capable of producing the highest output force was selected, prioritizing the maximum achievable stimulation force. The PBA comprises two PDMS films: a thin membrane layer and a thicker beam layer. The PBA converts the applied pneumatic pressure in the balloon channel structure formed between these two layers into bending motion and force generation (Fig. 2(a)). Although previous studies have examined the relationships among PBA dimensions, stiffness, and bending displacement, the relationship between these parameters and the generated force has not been sufficiently investigated36. Therefore, two thickness values and two Young’s modulus values were applied to the membrane and beam layers, and the optimal configuration was selected from 16 combinations in this study. The membrane layer thicknesses were 150 and 60 μm, whereas the beam layer thicknesses were 350 and 100 μm. For elasticity (Young’s modulus), two values were adopted for each layer: 1.542 and 0.762 MPa. The Young’s modulus of PDMS was adjusted by controlling the mixing ratio of the base polymer and curing agent. The optimal configuration for achieving the maximum stimulation force was determined to be a membrane layer with a thickness of 60 μm and a Young’s modulus of 0.762 MPa, combined with a beam layer thickness of 350 μm and a Young’s modulus of 1.542 MPa. The stimulation force (Fstimuli) was measured and compared under the condition of a bending displacement (δa) of 500 μm, corresponding to the 500 μm gap between the two fingers.

It converts the pressure P applied to the balloon channel formed between the layers into bending motion. From a total of 16 combinations of tm, tb, Em and Eb, the optimal design was selected. (b) By modeling the liquid metal strain gauge as a cantilever beam, it functions as a force sensor, converting external forces applied in the bending direction into changes in gauge resistance through strain induced in the liquid-metal-filled channel. (c) From a fabrication perspective, setting the lower limits for the channel layer thickness and elasticity (hc = 60 μm and Es = 0.762 MPa) enabled the determination of the flat layer thickness (hf = 120 μm) that provides maximum sensitivity.

Design of the head restraint chamber for zebrafish larvae

The dimensions of the head restraint chamber were designed based on the body size of 5 dpf zebrafish larvae. The measured head width and tail width were 664 and 299 μm (n = 3), respectively. Accordingly, the narrowest region of the chamber, which is critical for effective larval restraint, was designed with a width of 400 μm (Fig. 3(a)). This dimension allows reliable immobilization of individuals with a head width that exceeds 400 μm while their tail width remains below 400 μm. The device restrains the larval head by embedding it in agarose gel. To enhance mechanical interlocking between the agarose gel and the chamber structure, multiple anchor holes (φ 400 μm) were incorporated based on previous studies (Fig. 3(a) and (b))24. The tail chamber was designed to be removable to allow access by the microfingers. In addition, the head chamber maintains a 2-mm distance between the larva and the bottom of the dish, enabling direct and centered contact with the microfinger.

The narrowest part of the chamber, critical for larval restraint, was designed to be 400 μm, allowing restraint of all individuals with a head width that exceeds 400 μm while their tail width remains below 400 μm. (b) After embedding in agarose gel, the tail chamber is designed to be removable, providing access for the microfingers. To enhance adhesion between the agarose gel and the device, multiple anchor holes (φ 400 μm) are incorporated.

Materials and methods

Animals

Zebrafish (Danio rerio) AB strain was purchased from Zebrafish International Resource Center in the University of Oregon, USA. Zebrafish were reared and maintained in 1.7 L tanks in a recirculating Meito System at 28 ± 1 °C under a 14 h light and 10 h dark photoperiod according to the standard protocol. Zebrafish larvae were raised in 90 mm dishes until 7 days post-fertilization. This study was approved by the Animal Care and Ethics Committee of Aoyama Gakuin University and carried out according to the Animal Research Reporting of In VIVO Experiments (ARRIVE) guidelines and relevant regulations.

Microfinger fabrication

The microfinger comprises a right finger integrating a PBA and a left finger integrating a force sensor. First, photolithography was performed using a photoresist (SU-8, Nippon Kayaku Co., Ltd., Japan) to pattern a silicon substrate and fabricate molds for the PBA and the sensor channel. Next, PDMS (Silpot 184, Dow Corning Inc., USA) with a base-to-curing agent mixing ratio of 18:1 was spin-coated onto the fabricated PBA and sensor channel molds, followed by thermal curing. After curing, the PDMS films were carefully released from the molds. The film thickness was controlled to meet the design specifications by adjusting the spin-coating speed and duration. The two PDMS flat films, corresponding to the PBA beam layer and the sensor flat layer, were fabricated separately by spin-coating PDMS onto silicon substrates and subsequently thermally curing them. Second, the fabricated PDMS thin films were bonded using vacuum ultraviolet (VUV) treatment to form the enclosed channel structures for both the PBA and the liquid metal strain gauge. Third, the PBA finger, the sensor finger, and a spacer to maintain the required gap between the two fingers were bonded together using VUV. Fourth, a PDMS connector was attached to create through-holes for pneumatic pressure application and liquid metal injection. The openings on the bottom surface were sealed with polyimide tape. Fifth, liquid metal (Galinstan, Zairyo-ya.com, Japan) was injected into the sensor channel, and stripped electrical wires were inserted into the through-holes to establish electrical connections. Finally, the wiring was secured using an adhesive (Super X2, Cemedine Co., Ltd., Japan). Figure 4 shows the fabrication results of the force sensor-integrated microfinger. Figure 4(a) confirms proper actuation of the microfinger, while Fig. 4(b) verifies successful filling of the liquid metal into the sensor channel structure.

Fabrication results of the force-sensor-integrated soft microfinger. (a) Top view of the fabricated microfinger. Proper actuation of the PBA is confirmed. (b) Side view of the fabricated microfinger. Filling of liquid metal into the sensor channel is confirmed.

Characterization of the soft microfinger

Characteristics of the PBA and force sensors were measured using a loadcell (LVS-5GA, Kyowa Electronic Instruments Co., Japan) mounted on a positioning stage (KZL06075-N1-C, Suruga Seiki Co.,Ltd., Japan). The positioning stage was controlled by a controller (DS102, Suruga Seiki Co.,Ltd., Japan) via a LabVIEW program.

Fabrication of the larvae head restraint chamber

The head restraint chamber was fabricated using a mold produced by a 3D printer (Agilista, Keyence Co., Japan). Acrylic AR-M2 was used as the printing material. PDMS was poured into the mold, degassed to remove air bubbles, and then thermally cured in an oven at 60 ℃ for 24 h. After curing and demolding, VUV treatment was applied to the chamber surface to enhance hydrophilicity.

Positioning zebrafish larvae in the head restraint chamber

Touch response assay used wild-type zebrafish larvae at 6 days postfertilization (6 dpf). Their body size is slightly larger than that of 5 dpf larvae. However, the restraint chamber can still accommodate 6 dpf larvae because they fall within the acceptable size range defined by the chamber dimensions.

Figure 5 shows the restraint protocol using the chamber device. First, a larva was placed into the chamber with a pipette. Water was then gently aspirated from the tail chamber side, guiding and securing the larval head in the inclined channel (Fig. 5(a)). Next, a drop of 2 wt% agarose solution (Agarose XP, Nippon Gene Co., Ltd., Japan) maintained at 40 °C was added to the head chamber. The larval head functioned as a plug, preventing the agarose solution from leaking into the tail chamber (Fig. 5(b)). After a few minutes, once gelation of the agarose was confirmed, the tail chamber was removed, thereby releasing the tail while maintaining head fixation (Fig. 5(c)).

The larvae tails are then inserted into the tail channel. (b) Add low-melting-point agarose solution (2 wt% at 40 °C) to the head-side chamber. The solution gels within minutes, embedding the larval head. (c) Remove the tail chamber and add water to the tail section.

Experimental system setup

Herein, the experimental setup constructed for the touch response assay is shown in Fig. 6. First, the head-restrained zebrafish larva was placed on the stage of a microscope (BX51WI, Evident Co.) and aligned so that the tail was positioned at the center of the field of view. The microfinger, mounted on a manual positioner (OYM-90, The Oyama Company, Ltd., Japan), was carefully adjusted to clamp the larval tail. Alignment along the Z-axis was performed by fine-tuning the manual positioner while monitoring the focus through the microscope.

In this model, the generated force of the zebrafish Fg is assumed to be sufficiently smaller than the actuator’s stimulation force Fstimuli and is therefore considered negligible. The PBA converts the applied pressure P into a driving force, which is defined as the output force Fout. This output force is subsequently distributed into the bending force Fbend required to achieve the desired displacement and the stimulation force Fstimuli exerted on the target. Accordingly, Fstimuli can be expressed as a function of the applied pressure P and the bending displacement δ, as shown in Eq. (9).

To quantify the stimulation force Fstimuli, Fout and Fbend must be determined.

First, the output force Fout is a distributed force generated across the entire balloon; however, owing to the difficulty of theoretical derivation and measurement, it was approximated as a concentrated force at the microfinger tip. According to Eq. (9), when δa = 0. Therefore, the output force Fout can be obtained by measuring Fstimuli using a load cell (Fig. 7(b)). The experimental results (Fig. 7(b)) were fitted with a cubic function.

Second, when no object exists to apply force to (Fstimuli = 0), the entire output force Fout is converted to the bending force Fbend so that . Thus, the Fout obtained in Fig. 7(b) is equivalent to Fbend. Under the condition Fstimuli = 0, the relationship between displacement δa and applied pressure P was measured (Fig. 7(c)). Using the data from Fig. 7(b) and (c), the applied pressure P can be eliminated to determine the bending force Fbend because each data point corresponds to the same applied pressure (Fig. 7(d)). As shown in Fig. 7(d), the bending force Fbend varies linearly with displacement δa and was therefore approximated by a linear function. Based on this, the stimulation force Fstimuli (P, δa) can be derived from the applied pressure P and displacement δa by substituting the experimental results for Fout and Fbend into Eq. (9).

The generated force (Fg) of zebrafish larva was calculated from the sensor output using the experimentally determined force sensor characteristics shown in Fig. 7(e). Figure 7(e) shows the calibrations of the fabricated force sensors. As shown in Fig. 7(e), performance of sensors is highly sensitive to fabrication variations. However, all fabricated sensors exhibited high linearity. Therefore, they can be used effectively. In this work, we used device 1 which showed the closest agreement with the theoretical characteristics.

Touch response assay by soft microfinger

As shown in Fig. 8, the touch response test was performed to evaluate mechanical stimulation and behavioral responses in zebrafish larvae at 6 dpf. In this experiment, generated forces were measured over a 400-s period under nonstimulation and stimulation conditions using the actuator (Fig. 8(a)). A high‑pass filter with a cutoff frequency of 5 Hz was employed to suppress low‑frequency noise arising from actuator operation. Figure 8(b) shows the sensor signal extracted over a 10-s interval without mechanical stimulation. As shown in Fig. 8(b), signal spikes with amplitudes of 0.005% or less were detected, coinciding with tail movements confirmed in the recorded video. Figure 8(c) shows the sensor signal extracted over a 10-s interval during mechanical stimulation. Even under mechanical stimulation, small spikes with amplitudes of 0.005% or less—similar to those observed in the absence of stimulation—were detected between 378 and 383 s. By contrast, at approximately 385 s, a pronounced locomotor response with an amplitude of approximately 0.02% was observed (Fig. 8(c)). Figure 8(e) shows a video frame captured at the moment when the sensor signal reached its maximum amplitude approximately 385 s. These results indicate that stimulation with the microfinger successfully elicited a strong locomotor response that was clearly distinguishable from spontaneous movement. Figure 8(f) shows the generated forces calculated for two 6 dpf zebrafish larvae using the force sensor calibration shown in Fig. 7(e). As shown in Fig. 8(f), under stimulated conditions, the microfingers clamped the larval tail, allowing greater forces to be transmitted to the sensor finger compared with the nonstimulated condition. Moreover, under mechanical stimulation, occasional locomotor forces approximately 3–15 times greater than those observed during spontaneous movement were recorded.

Next, we derived the stimulation force Fstimuli at the peak response at 385 s (Fig. 8(c)). During this event, the applied pressure to the PBA was P = 89 kPa and the actuator displacement measured using the microscope camera software (CellSens, Evident Co.) was δa = 799 μm. By substituting these values into Eq. (9), together with the experimentally obtained relationships shown in Fig. 7(b) and (d), the stimulation force was calculated to be Fstimuli = 495 µN. This magnitude (495µN) is sufficiently greater than the measured generated force Fg (0.2 ~ 3 µN), thereby validating the assumption used in the mechanical model. These results demonstrate that the stimulation force Fstimuli can be successfully quantified using the proposed approach.

Discussions

This study has two main objectives. First, we aimed to quantify both the applied mechanical stimulus and the resulting behavioral response, which was successfully achieved as demonstrated in the Results section. Second, we sought to ensure safe physical contact with zebrafish larvae, and no injuries were observed throughout the touch response assays. These findings demonstrate that the microfinger provides a novel platform that integrates safe direct contact, controlled delivery of stimulation forces, and quantitative evaluation of behavioral responses as generated forces.

In previous manual touch-response assays using metal tools, the applied stimulus force could not be precisely controlled, and the resulting behavioral responses were evaluated only qualitatively27,28. Moreover, direct contact with rigid tools carries a risk of damaging the fragile larval body. These methodological limitations have hindered the quantitative characterization of mechanosensory responses. In contrast, the soft microfinger developed in this study enables safe and repeatable stimulation while allowing both the stimulus force and the generated response force to be quantified. In addition, unlike previous robotic systems29, the microfinger enables force-based evaluation of both stimulation and response while avoiding the risks associated with metal tweezers or needles.

Despite these advantages, the present system has limitations in positioning accuracy and throughput. The stimulation and locomotor forces can be affected by variations in larval size and by the relative positioning of the device. Rapid adjustment of the microfinger position to match larval size will be important for improving consistency. In addition, the current restraint protocol requires manual pipetting, which reduces throughput. Therefore, future work should focus on automating larval restraint and positioning as well as increasing the number of samples and experimental conditions that can be tested.

Overall, the progress demonstrated in this study highlights the potential of the soft microfinger system as a new experimental platform for more precise mechanosensory-behavioral assays. In future work, we will pursue quantification of stimulus thresholds, detailed analysis of force-based input-output relationships, application to the disease models and drug-treated groups, and automation of larval restraint and positioning. Structural optimization based on Finite Element Analysis (FEA) will also be an important future step to ensure mechanical reliability and improve system performance.
