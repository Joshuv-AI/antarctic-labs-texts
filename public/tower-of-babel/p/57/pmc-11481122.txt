ABO blood types and head and neck cancer: a systematic review with meta-analysis of observational studies
Authors: Hanbo Shi, Jingjing Yao, Chenchen Jiang, Wenying Xu, Zhongliang Jiang, Yan Chen, Yun Qian
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
AbstractObjectivesThis research aims to explore the correlation between ABO blood groups and neoplasms in the head and neck region, specifically investigating the susceptibility of different blood groups to tumours.DesignSystematic review and meta-analysis.Data sourcesCNKI, WANFANG DATA, PubMed, Web of Science and Embase databases were systematically searched from inception to January 2024.Eligibility criteriaAll studies of ABO blood type and head and neck tumours will be included in this study.Data extraction and synthesisA systematic literature review was performed using digital platforms on CNKI, WANFANG DATA, PubMed, Web of Science and Embase databases, following the Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Reviews and Meta-Analyses guidelines for meta-analysis. Two authors independently extracted the data and assessed the quality of included studies. The Newcastle-Ottawa Scale was used to assess the quality of the included studies. Comparisons were made between blood types A, B, AB and their combined group versus O, along with subgroup analyses. Systematic analysis was performed by using Review Manager V.5.4 and Stata V.18 statistical software.Results30 articles were included, involving 737 506 subjects, among which 21 382 were patients with head and neck tumours. The overall analysis indicated a significant association between type AB blood (OR 0.762, 95% CI 0.637 to 0.910) and a reduced risk of head and neck tumours. In the Caucasoid race, type A blood is significantly linked to an elevated likelihood of head and neck tumours (OR 1.353, 95% CI 1.076 to 1.702), while in the Mongoloid race, type AB blood is significantly linked to a reduced likelihood of developing tumours in the head and neck area (OR= 0.732, 95% CI 0.588 to 0.910). No significant associations were found in the subgroup analysis by gender. Regarding different types of cancer, type A blood is significantly associated with an increased risk of salivary gland tumours (OR 1.338, 95% CI 1.075 to 1.665), and type AB blood is significantly linked to a lower likelihood of nasopharyngeal carcinoma (OR 0.590, 95% CI 0.429 to 0.812).ConclusionA correlation exists between ABO blood groups and tumours in the head and neck region. Introduction

Globally, malignancies in the head and neck region constitute about 5% of all cancer cases, positioning as the sixth most prevalent form of cancer worldwide.1 2 Head and neck cancers encompass malignancies occurring in the oral cavity, sinonasal cavity, pharynx, larynx and other related regions.3 The major risk factors for these cancers include tobacco use, alcohol consumption and oncogenic viruses such as human papillomavirus (HPV) and Epstein-Barr virus. Statistics indicate that in 2020, 1 518 133 new cases of head and neck tumours were diagnosed, leading to 510 771 deaths. The incidence and mortality rates of these tumours rank sixth and eighth, respectively, among all cancer incidence and mortality rates.4 5 Early detection and intervention can typically lead to a cure. Principal risk elements for tumours in the head and neck region include smoking, alcohol consumption, HPV infection and familial cancer history.69 Additionally, lifestyle, environmental factors, poor oral hygiene, race and host genetic susceptibility are often considered factors contributing to the onset of head and neck tumours. These elements influence a person’s vulnerability to developing head and neck tumours.

The ABO blood group system represents one of the earliest and most prevalent blood typing systems,10 comprising three carbohydrate antigens: A, B and H. Populations with blood types A, B and AB are capable of expressing the catalytic action of glycosyltransferases that are produced by the ABO gene, which converts transforms the H antigen into A or B antigens, while the type O blood (H) group lacks this activity.11 These antigens influence coagulation functions and are extensively involved in cellular functions and pathological activities.12 Following Karl Landsteiner’s identification of the ABO blood types in 1900, a substantial amount of research has been conducted on human diseases and transfusion complications related to the ABO blood types.1315 Consequently, research into the significance of the ABO blood types in disease development susceptibility has continued, covering various health conditions.16 17

In the 1950s, Aird et al18 first discovered that compared with the general population, individuals with type A blood were more susceptible to gastric cancer, while those individuals with type O blood showed reduced susceptibility. Since then, the exploration of the link between ABO blood groups and disease incidence has been a hot research topic, with numerous clinical studies accumulating a significant body of relevant evidence and data. This has set the stage for comprehensive meta-analyses and evaluations, such as a notable correlation between type A blood and the occurrence of gastric cancer19 and pancreatic cancer,20 and the lower susceptibility of individuals with type B blood to hepatitis B virus infection.21 Past studies indicate that people possessing type A blood are at a higher risk of head and neck cancer.22 Sheng et al discovered that persons with type A blood exhibit an increased likelihood of nasopharyngeal carcinoma (NPC) relative to individuals possessing blood type O,23 while Lin et al reported no significant correlation between ABO blood types and NPC. Furthermore, many studies have reported conflicting results.222427 This inconsistency may be due to variations in the prevalence of ABO blood groups in various nations, regions and ethnicities.2831 The relationship between these variations and the aetiology of head and neck tumours in different regions, as well as genetic backgrounds and environmental factors, is complex. Therefore, studies conducted among populations with different genetic backgrounds and environmental settings may yield divergent conclusions.

Although numerous studies have explored the connection between ABO blood groups and head and neck tumours, the question of whether blood type influences the incidence of tumours remains unresolved, and its pathogenic mechanisms are largely misunderstood. Previous research has investigated the relationship between ABO blood groups and various forms of cancer, including studies by Singh et al32 on oral cancer and Shao et al33 on nasopharyngeal cancer. However, these studies were limited to specific subtypes within the broader category of head and neck cancers. In this study, we aim to perform a comprehensive and systematic analysis of the association between ABO blood groups and head and neck cancers. Our focus will encompass both overall head and neck tumours and their subtypes, with particular attention to variations across different races and genders. In this study, we collected relevant research data and established a comparison model with type O blood as the reference, along with several extended comparison models, aiming to comprehensively explore their independent effects. We expect that the findings of this research will highlight the link between ABO blood groups and the occurrence of head and neck tumours, which will be greatly beneficial for the screening and counselling of head and neck tumour patients.

This study is registered with PROSPERO, registration number: CRD42024510176.

Method

Inclusion criteria

Before conducting the literature search, specific criteria for inclusion and exclusion were defined. Studies were required to encompass content related to the association between the ABO blood types and head and neck tumours, with publications in formal journals and a definite publication date. The language of the article is Chinese or English. Cases and controls had to be clearly sourced, providing relevant original data, and subjects were confirmed via pathological examination. The exposed risk factor was the ABO blood types of the case and control groups, necessitating precise blood typing methods or reliable data records. Literature that was difficult to extract data from, including grey literature, reviews, case reports, case series and animal study publications, was excluded. As well as the double counting of cohorts, such as cross-referencing authors and study populations, were excluded.

Literature search

This meta-analysis followed the Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Reviews and Meta-Analyses guidelines.34 35 A computerised search was conducted in CNKI, WANFANG DATA, PubMed, Web of Science and Embase databases, with the search period extending from the inception of each database to January 2024. Chinese search keywords included blood type; cancer; tumor. English search keywords were ABO blood types; Tumor; Neoplasm; Neoplasia; Neoplastic; Cancer; Carcinoma; Malignancy; Malignant; Oncology; Adenocarcinoma. Additionally, the references of the retrieved articles were reviewed to avoid missing other relevant literature.

Literature data extraction and processing

This study involved two researchers independently screening literature based on the defined inclusion and exclusion criteria, by reading titles and abstracts to eliminate ineligible publications. Subsequently, both researchers read the full texts of the papers that met the criteria, conducted a second screening and cross-checked their findings. In cases of disagreement, issues unresolved by discussion were referred to a third party with relevant disciplinary expertise for judgement. Data extraction was performed on the literature included in the final study, collecting the ensuing details: lead author, publication year, study’s country or region, ethnicity of the study population, type of cancer, total number of participants in the study population, number of cases and controls for different ABO blood types, proportions of different genders within ABO blood types and the source of control groups. When required data were missing or difficult to extract from the eligible literature, we attempted to contact the corresponding authors via email to obtain the necessary information. If complete data remained unattainable, the literature was excluded. Microsoft Excel software was used for this process.

Quality assessment of literature

The Newcastle-Ottawa Scale (NOS) is a frequently used tool for evaluating the quality of case–control studies, which includes the evaluation of exposure and outcome, comparability, and the selection of the population.36 For each piece of literature included in the study, we assessed its quality using the NOS, considering studies with scores of 5 or above as acceptable. Disputes were resolved through discussion; if an issue could not be resolved in this manner, a third party was consulted for judgement.

Statistical analysis

Systematic reviews were performed with Review Manager V.5.4 statistical software, with the statistic denoted as Peffect. Additionally, despite the fixed nature of blood types in individuals, the global distribution of blood types exhibits significant regional differences,2831 making it inappropriate to employ a fixed-effect model to evaluate the association between ABO blood groups and head and neck tumours straightforwardly. The Cochran Q test was employed to identify heterogeneity among studies,37 with Pheterogeneity representing the statistic from the Cochran Q test. The proportion of variability due to heterogeneity in risk assessments was indicated by the I2 value, with low heterogeneity at I² (0%–25%), moderate heterogeneity at I² (26%–50%) and high heterogeneity at I² (≥51%).38 In cases of excessive heterogeneity, such as I²>75%, it may be necessary to explore the sources of heterogeneity. Therefore, we applied the random-effects model analysis, with Peffect<0.05 indicating statistically significant differences. For analyses including no less than ten studies, commands were written using Stata V.18 software to assess publication bias through funnel plots and Egger’s test, with Pegger representing the statistic from Egger’s test. Pegger>0.05 suggests no publication bias.39 Sensitivity analysis was performed using a one-study-removed approach to evaluate the impact of individual studies on the overall results. Forest plots will be used to describe the overall study outcomes, and subgroup analyses will be conducted based on predefined variables.

Research plan

Based on the anticipated data set, we constructed three reference models comparing A to O, B to O and AB to O, using type O blood as the reference. Furthermore, to more comprehensively describe the relationship between the ABO blood types and head and neck tumours, four additional comparison models were constructed: A versus non-A (B+O+AB), B versus non-B (A+O+AB), AB versus non-AB (A+B+O) and O versus non-O (A+B+ AB). Considering the variations in blood type distribution and disease incidence rates among different ethnicities, subgroup analyses based on ethnicity were also conducted. Additionally, subgroup evaluations were conducted according to gender and type of cancer, assuming the dataset contained detailed data on gender and cancer types.

Result

Results of literature search and characteristics of the literature

The initial screening identified 5088 related articles, and after removing duplicates or irrelevant ones, 870 articles were obtained. After additional full-text reviews, a total of 30 articles, all observational studies, were included in the final analysis. Figure 1 depicts the methodology and outcomes of the literature search.

Flow chart of literature search and study selection.

As of January 2024, guided by the established inclusion and exclusion criteria, a total of 37 studies focusing on the connection between ABO blood groups and head and neck tumours were chosen. Online supplemental table 1 displays the search strategy and online supplemental table 2 displays the full-text review list. In the final review, 7 articles were excluded due to control groups and datasets not meeting the study requirements, thus 30 articles were ultimately included in the meta-analysis. This research included 737 506 participants, of which 21 382 were individuals diagnosed with head and neck tumours, covering different forms of cancer including oral and maxillofacial cancer (OMC), laryngeal carcinoma, salivary gland carcinoma (SGC) and NPC. While some studies that do not specify cancer types will be incorporated into the overall analysis, they will be excluded from subgroup analyses. Pertaining to the ethnic background of the participants, there were 22 articles on Mongoloid and 6 on Caucasoid populations, with 2 articles being unclassifiable. Online supplemental table 3 displays the attributes of the literature that was included. The included literature scored 5 or above on the NOS scale, indicating that the quality of the literature analysed was generally acceptable. The quality assessment of publication is shown in online supplemental table 4.

In the subgroup analyses, some studies could not be included due to a lack of necessary data for analysis. Moreover, in the subgroup analyses of different cancer types, studies containing multiple types of cancer but with data that could not be categorised were excluded from the subgroup analyses. Additionally, because some individual studies contained detailed data on multiple types of cancer, the total number of datasets in the cancer-type subgroup analyses exceeded the total number of studies. In the subgroup analyses of gender groups, since blood type genes are not located on sex chromosomes, the distribution of blood types is unrelated to gender,40 hence control groups were not differentiated by gender.

Results

Type O blood reference model

In the overall analysis, we found no significant association between type A blood (OR 1.019, 95% CI 0.917 to 1.132) or type B blood (OR 1.097, 95% CI 0.952 to 1.265) and the risk of head and neck tumours. However, type AB blood was significantly linked to a decreased risk of developing head and neck tumours (OR 0.762, 95% CI 0.637 to 0.910).

Further analyses by different ethnicities, genders and cancer types revealed that in the Caucasoid population, type A blood was markedly linked to an increased risk of head and neck tumours (OR 1.353, 95% CI 1.076 to 1.702), whereas in the Mongoloid population, type AB blood was markedly linked to a diminished risk (OR 0.732, 95% CI 0.588 to 0.910). No significant associations were found in the different gender subgroup analyses. Regarding different types of cancer, type A blood was markedly linked to an increased risk of salivary gland tumours (OR 1.338, 95% CI 1.075 to 1.665) and type AB blood with a decreased risk of nasopharyngeal cancer (OR 0.590, 95% CI 0.429 to 0.812).

Extended comparison models

In the comprehensive analysis, no significant correlation was identified between A and non-A (B+O+AB) (OR 1.00, 95% CI 0.913 to 1.104), B and non-B (A+O+AB) (OR 1.125, 95% CI 0.986 to 1.283) or O and non-O (A+B+AB) (OR 0.977, 95% CI 0.880 to 1.085). Nonetheless, a notable correlation was found between AB and non-AB (A+B+O) (OR 0.735, 95% CI 0.633 to 0.853), in alignment with the findings of the models using type O blood as a reference.

In subgroup analyses, among the Caucasoid population, AB compared with non-AB was significantly linked to a lower risk of developing head and neck tumours (OR 0.657, 95% CI 0.475 to 0.907) and O compared with non-O was notably linked to a lower risk (OR 0.808, 95% CI 0.690 to 0.946). In the Mongoloid population, AB compared with non-AB was markedly linked to a diminished risk (OR 0.724, 95% CI 0.604 to 0.868). Regarding different types of cancer, A compared with non-A was markedly linked to an increased risk of OMC (OR 1.168, 95% CI 1.010 to 1.350), B compared with non-B was markedly linked to an increased risk of laryngeal cancer (OR 1.238, 95% CI 1.032 to 1.485), O compared with non-O was significantly linked to a lower likelihood of SGC (OR 0.802, 95% CI 0.653 to 0.983), and AB compared with non-AB was significantly associated with a decreased risk of nasopharyngeal cancer (OR 0.603, 95% CI 0.459 to 0.794). No significant associations were found in the different gender subgroup analyses in the extended comparison models.

Discussion

The ABO blood group system’s antigens (A, B and H) consist of intricate carbohydrate molecules situated on the exterior surface of the red blood cell membrane.41 The antigen genes are situated on the long arm of chromosome 9 (9q34.2), but the chromosome does not directly encode the antigens.42 ABO antigens are processed from a common precursor substance, with the H gene product being L-fucosyltransferase-1. The precursor substance forms the H antigen under the catalysis of L-fucosyltransferase-1. In individuals with the O blood type, the lack of catalytic enzymes encoded by the A and B genes results in the absence of A and B antigens. When different groups are added under the action of their respective catalytic enzymes, A and B antigens are formed.43 Thus, under general circumstances, the blood type antigens of non-O individuals are structurally more complex than those of O-type individuals, which may lead to different physiological activities and pathological functions.

This meta-analysis, which included 30 studies, indicates a correlation between ABO blood types and head and neck tumours. Previous studies have often considered type O blood as a protective factor against tumours, while non-O types are seen as risk factors.44 Our research reflects this pattern, attributing it to the absence of the 261G nucleotide in the O allele, which leads to the lack of production of the catalytic enzymes required to generate A and B antigen substances. This structural and functional difference in the ABO gene may be one of the bases for the entirely opposite relationships that O and non-O blood types have with cancer. Furthermore, in the overall analysis, as well as among Mongoloid ethnic groups, and nasopharyngeal cancer groups, type AB blood showed positive results, suggesting that type AB blood might be another protective factor against head and neck tumours. This is consistent with the findings of Liang et al,45 Mo et al46 and Wang et al,47 who observed similar results in other types of cancer.48 The comparison model of AB versus non-AB confirmed this result. The A and B alleles are distinguished by the substitution of four nucleotides, leading to differences in the amino acids at positions 266 and 268. Specifically, the enzymes catalysing A antigens have Leu and Gly, while those for B antigens have Met and Ala, accountable for the functioning of A and B antigens, respectively. The cooperative effect of A, B antigens or their catalytic enzymes might effectively reduce the likelihood of developing head and neck tumours in individuals.

In the group with type A blood, the maxillofacial cancer group, the SGC group and the Caucasoid ethnic group all showed varying degrees of positive results. Helicobacter pylori, identified as a carcinogen for gastric cancer, can adhere to A antigens, this could elucidate the heightened risk of gastric cancer in individuals possessing blood type A.49 Given that HPV is a risk factor for head and neck tumours,5053 it raises the question of whether people with type A blood have a higher vulnerability to HPV infection. Studies have shown that the rate of high-risk HPV infections within the oral cavity is notably more prevalent in men compared with women,54 theoretically making men more susceptible to head and neck tumours. However, the findings of this research do not corroborate this hypothesis, suggesting that the A/B gene may play a larger role in individual susceptibility to tumours.

Regarding the association between type B blood and laryngeal cancer, although positive results were shown in extended comparison models only, the credibility of these results might be constrained because of the limited number of samples; therefore, they are not discussed further. We anticipate an increase in studies investigating the correlation between laryngeal cancer and blood type, with the objective of addressing the current knowledge gap in this area.

The extended analysis results are mostly consistent with the reference model analysis results using blood type O. However, positive outcomes were observed exclusively in the type O blood reference model or the extended comparison model. Given that blood type distribution is independent of gender, the subgroup analysis stratified by gender yielded negative results, aligning with our initial expectations. Additionally, ethnicity was identified as non-negligible factors affecting the association. However, due to the lack of sufficient data, such as cultural factors, socioeconomic status, eating habits, further in-depth studies could not be conducted.

A/B antigens serve a crucial function in the advancement of cancer, facilitating both coagulation and angiogenesis55 56 and serving as crucial receptors and ligands for significant immune proteins.57 58 Additionally, A/B antigens are capable of altering cellular motility,59 while H antigens influence resistance to cell apoptosis. The expression of A/B antigens also aids in evading immune surveillance,6062 potentially explaining the role of blood types in the development and spread of tumours biologically. It is important to note that the presentation of these antigens is not static; it varies with cell differentiation, disease progression and tumour development, with reductions in A/B transferase messenger RNA associated with the absence of antigen expression in solid tumours.63 Furthermore, variations in the ABO gene can influence the circulating amounts of several inflammatory molecules and markers, such as tumour necrosis factor α (TNF-α)64 and soluble intercellular adhesion molecule 1,65 66 soluble P-selectin6668 and E-selectin,69 70 all of which can enhance tumour cell migration, adhesion and the host inflammatory response to tumour cells. Moreover, plasma levels of von Willebrand factor, a key regulator in angiogenesis and cell apoptosis, vary among individuals with different blood types, with the lowest average levels observed in individuals with blood type O, and it is most elevated in those possessing type AB.7173 The reduced incidence of cancer in individuals with blood type AB may also be attributable to this phenomenon.

Several limitations of the study need to be highlighted. First, due to the limited amount of data collected, we were unable to conduct more in-depth stratified analyses. Second, our cancer categorisation is based on published literature, which may not have comprehensively covered all cancer types. Additionally, our study only included publicly available literature in English and Chinese, and unpublished studies and language biases might also affect the outcomes. Lastly, to maximise the sample size, we were unable to consider various possible confounding variables including age, gender, smoking habits and HPV infection. We recommend conducting further studies that control for these risk factors to elucidate the relationship between ABO blood group and the risk of head and neck cancer.

In summary, the aim of our research was to address the knowledge gap between the ABO blood types system and head and neck tumours, providing relevant information and evidence. The outcomes suggest a link between ABO blood groups and head and neck tumours, which contributes to a more comprehensive insight into the connection between ABO blood groups and susceptibility to cancer. Blood type identification methods combined with cytokine detection could serve as a supplementary tool in the tumour screening and prevention counselling process. However, the connection between blood group and tumours in the head and neck area requires further verification through more prospective studies.
