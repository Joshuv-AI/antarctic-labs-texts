Physiologically-based pharmacokinetic/pharmacodynamic modeling of meropenem in critically ill patients
Authors: Yujie Yang, Yirong Wang, Wei Zeng, Jinhua Zhou, Min Xu, Ying Lan, Lvye Liu, Jian Shen, Chuan Zhang, Qin He
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
AbstractThis study aimed to develop a physiologically based pharmacokinetic/pharmacodynamic model (PBPK/PD) of meropenem for critically ill patients. A PBPK model of meropenem in healthy adults was established using PK-Sim software and subsequently extrapolated to critically ill patients based on anatomic and physiological parameters. The mean fold error (MFE) and geometric mean fold error (GMFE) methods were used to compare the differences between predicted and observed values of pharmacokinetic parameters Cmax, AUC0-∞, and CL to evaluate the accuracy of the PBPK model. The model was verified using meropenem plasma samples obtained from Intensive Care Unit (ICU) patients, which were determined by HPLC-MS/MS. After that, the PBPK model was combined with a PKPD model, which was developed based on f%T > MIC. Monte Carlo simulation was utilized to calculate the probability of target attainment (PTA) in patients. The developed PBPK model successfully predicted the meropenem disposition in critically ill patients, wherein the MFE average and GMFE of all predicted PK parameters were within the 1.25-fold error range. The therapeutic drug monitoring (TDM) of meropenem was conducted with 92 blood samples from 31 ICU patients, of which 71 (77.17%) blood samples were consistent with the simulated value. The TDM results showed that meropenem PBPK modeling is well simulated in critically ill patients. Monte Carlo simulations showed that extended infusion and frequent administration were necessary to achieve curative effect for critically ill patients, whereas excessive infusion time (> 4 h) was unnecessary. The PBPK/PD modeling incorporating literature and prospective study data can predict meropenem pharmacokinetics in critically ill patients correctly. Our study provides a reference for dose adjustment in critically ill patients.Keywords: Meropenem, PBPK/PD, Critically ill patients, PK-SimSubject terms: Diseases, Medical research

Introduction

Severe infection is a life-threatening infection, and is characterized by shortness of breath, rapid onset of fever, and other systemic toxic symptoms, including severe hypoxia and sepsis1. The main principle in severe infection is antimicrobial drug therapy. Meropenem belongs to the class of carbapenem antibiotics, which is the first choice for mixed infection mainly caused by gram-negative bacilli, multidrug-resistant bacteria, and extended-spectrum β-lactamase-producing bacteria. Meropenem is of great significance in treating severe infection, but only rational drug use can ensure the clinical efficacy of meropenem2. However, physiological changes during severe infection with or without sepsis may influence the pharmacokinetics (PK) of meropenem, resulting in high variability in meropenem exposure in vivo. Meropenem cannot effectively kill pathogenic bacteria when the concentration of meropenem in vivo is insufficient. Moreover, it will accelerate the accumulation of drug-resistant mutants under the “pressure” of antibiotic selection3. On the other hand, the concentration of meropenem in vivo is too high to cause adverse reactions4. Therefore, optimizing the dosing regimen according to the characteristics of critically ill patients is necessary.

Physiologically based pharmacokinetic model (PBPK) can simulate the process of drug disposal in the body based on anatomy, physiology, biochemistry and physical chemistry5. In recent years, with the development of PBPK software and in vivo drug research technology, the application of the PBPK model in various fields has made significant progress, which can be used to predict drugs in special populations, different disease states, and drug-drug interaction6. The PBPK/PD model is to correlate the drug exposure levels in the target tissue predicted by the PBPK model with the PD attainment evaluation. For meropenem treatment, the drug concentrations of a free fraction remain above the minimum inhibitory concentration (%fT > MIC) is known as the PKPD index associated with antibacterial activity7, which is used as an output to establish the relationship between drug exposure and effects. The PBPK/PD model can compensate for the fact that the classical PKPD model cannot extrapolate the drug effect in different situations according to specific internal and external factors8. Furthermore, the PBPK/PD model can provide the most accurate prediction and the lowest risk of deviation for clinically rational drug use. No studies have reported the PBPK/PD model of meropenem in critically ill patients. Only the PBPK/PD model of meropenem plus fosfomycin in children has been established to explore the anti-infective treatment plan for multidrug-resistant bacteria9.

The current investigation aimed to develop a PBPK model of meropenem for critically ill patients. In the next step, the model was verified using meropenem plasma samples obtained from the ICU patients, which were determined by high-performance liquid chromatography-tandem mass spectrometry (HPLC-MS/MS). The PBPK model was after that combined with a PKPD model, developed based on f%T > MIC. In addition, we performed a Monte Carlo simulation to calculate the probability of target attainment (PTA) in patients.

Materials and methods

Workflow

In this study, a ‘bottom-up’ strategy was used to facilitate the PBPK/PD model of meropenem for critically ill patients (Fig. 1). Firstly, a PBPK model of meropenem was developed and evaluated in a healthy population for i.v. administration. Afterward, the established healthy population PBPK model of meropenem was extrapolated to the critically ill patients by adjusting anatomical and physiological parameters changes, such as glomerular filtration rate (GFR), albumin and hematocrit, etc. In this stage, the physicochemical and ADME properties of meropenem remained the same. Changes in physiologic parameters of critically ill patients were obtained from literature searches. The PBPK model wascombined with a PKPD model, developed based on f%T > MIC. Monte Carlo simulation was utilized to calculate the PTA in patients.

Schematic representation of the workflow of PBPK modeling development.

The physicochemical and ADME properties of meropenem, and reported PK profiles following i.v. administration of meropenem were obtained by searching Pubmed, Web of Science, Medline database, or “Drug bank” database using keywords ‘meropenem’ and ‘pharmacokinetics’. The particular keywords for patients with critically ill patients were ‘severe infection’, ‘critically ill’, or ‘sepsis’. The collected literature were organized according to the experimental data (plasma concentration-time profile) and dosing regimen, and we excluded the study on the determination of meropenem plasma concentration by the microbial method.

All simulations were performed using the PK-Sim (version 11.2, https://www.open-systems-pharmacology.org/) and Oracle Crystal Ball (version 11.1.2.4.0). Plasma concentration–time profile data in published literature were obtained with GetData Graph Digitizer (S. Fedorov, version 2.25.0.32). The observed PK parameters were obtained from literature or calculated using the non-compartment analysis by DAS software (version 2.0). Graphics creation and editing were using GraphPad Prism (version 6.02).

Development the PBPK model for meropenem

The PBPK model comprising 18 tissue/organ compartments was developed using PK-Sim model software, where human physiological parameters were obtained using the virtual population. The physicochemical and ADME parameters of meropenem were shown in Table 1. Relevant algorithms meeting the requirements of this model were selected from PK-Sim to establish the healthy adult model of intravenous meropenem according to the guidance-based workflow. On the basis of the healthy adult PBPK model, the physicochemical properties of all drug parameters (molecular weight, log P or Kp values, etc.) were fixed, and the physiology parameters (GFR, albumin, and hematocrit, etc.) were modified in order to match the physiological changes of critically ill patients. Sensitivity analysis was used to represent the effect of perturbations of individual physiological parameters on drug exposure levels (expressed as AUC0-∞, Eq. 1). A change in sensitivity value of + 1.0 indicated that a 10% increase in the test parameter resulted in a 10% increase in the simulated AUC0-∞, and Sensitivity ≥ 0.45 was considered the critical factor. The Monte-Carlo algorithm in the ‘Parameter Identification’ module was used to estimate the relevant parameters, so that the individual prediction curve and parameter values were consistent with the observed data. Evaluation of the PBPK model for meropenem

For PBPK model evaluation, wecompared simulated with observed data after i.v. administration following various dosing regimens. The predicted and observed concentrations versus time curves were compared based on visual inspection, and the goodness of fit of plasma drug concentration was calculated. The representative observed values were obtained from predicted meropenem concentrations versus the time curve for population PK studies. PK parameters of observed values were directly obtained from geometric or arithmetic means reported in the literature. If no reported PK parameters were reported, the intercepted plasma drug concentration values were used to fit by non-compartment analysis. The mean fold error (MFE) and geometric mean fold error (GMFE) methods were used to compare the differences between predicted and observed values of pharmacokinetic parameters Cmax, AUC0-∞, CL to evaluate the accuracy of PBPK model (Eq. 2–3). When the MFE and GMFE of all PK parameters were less than 2, we considered the PBPK model establishment successful.

Clinical data collection

Plasma samples for PBPK model evaluation were collected in critically ill patients from January 2022 to December 2022, treated in the ICUs of the Chengdu Third People's Hospital. The study was approved by the ethics committee of Chengdu Third People's Hospital and registered in the National Health Security Information Platform. In addition, the signed informed consent form was obtained from the patient or the guardian of the patient before admission to the study. The study was carried out by the Declaration of Helsinki. Inclusion criteria were as follows: (1) severe pneumonia patients with or without sepsis (18 years of age) as defined by the American College of Chest Physicians/Society of Critical Care Medicine criteria10; (2) necessity for treatment in an ICU; (3) indication for intravenous treatment with meropenem for a confirmed or suspected pathogenic infection. Patients were excluded if they had preexisting cirrhosis or any form of liver damage. Moreover, Patients who experienced renal failure were also excluded.

The dosing regimen of meropenem ranged between 0.5 and 1 g, and the dosing frequency was administered two to three times a day via different intravenous infusion times (0.5, 4 and 6 h). Blood samples for meropenem were collected at 5 min before administration (trough concentration, predose), and 1, 2, 4, and 6 h after the start of infusion. All venous blood samples were collected into heparin tubes and centrifuged to obtain plasma within 6 h of sampling. Plasma samples were stored at -80 °C until analysis of meropenem.

HPLC-MS/MS method for plasma concentration determination

Meropenem plasma concentrations were determined using a modified HPLC-MS/MS method. In brief, 25 µL of plasma was mixed with 75 µL acetonitrile, which contained the 0.75 µL deuterated forms of meropenem (internal standard). After incubation, the mixture was centrifuged, and the supernatant was transferred and extracted with 100 µL UP water. After centrifugation, 2 µL of the upper aqueous layer was injected into the column (Phenomenex Kinetex C18 2.6um, 100 × 3 mm). Analytes were measured in the multiple reaction monitoring (MRM) mode. The optimized MRM, cone voltage, collision energy, and dwell time are listed in Table S3.

The mobile phase consisted of a mixture of solution A (2 mM ammonium formate in water) and solution B (2 mM ammonium acetate in methanol) with an initial 5% solution B composition. The mobile phase composition changed from 5% B at 0.8 min to 95% at 1.5 min. The total runtime was 3.0 min Linear calibration curves were obtained in the concentration range of 0.4–22 mg/L for meropenem.

Monte carlo simulations

Monte Carlo simulations were performed on 10,000 virtual patients with pseudomonas aeruginosa infection to identify the pragmatic dose adjustment of meropenem by using oracle crystal ball (V11.1.2.4.0). The ratio of the time at which the plasma concentration was more significant than the MIC to the interval of administration (f%T > MIC) has been shown to be the PK/PD index for meropenem. The pharmacokinetic parameters of meropenem (Vd, CL, f) were obtained from the PBPK model. The distribution frequency of the MICs value of meropenem against pseudomonas aeruginosa was obtained by referring to the implementation standard document of antimicrobial susceptibility test developed by Clinical and Laboratory Standards Institute. The inter-individual variability in Monte Carlo simulations is derived from assumed pharmacokinetic parameters. It is assumed that the pharmacokinetic parameters Vd and CL follow a log-gaussian probability distribution, MICs follow discrete distribution, and f follows a uniform distribution. The confidence interval was set to 95, and 40%fT > MIC was used as the target threshold. The probability of target attainment (PTA) of f%T > MIC was calculated for each dosing regimen (1 g q12 h, 1 g q8 h) via different intravenous infusion times (0.5, 4 and 6 h), combined with various MICs (1, 2, 4, 8, 16 mg/L). PTA > 90% was the target for dose selection in our study.

Results

PBPK model establishment and evaluation

We developed a healthy adult PBPK model of meropenem based on the average physiological parameters of a typical 30-year-old European male individual. Parameters were randomly optimized to obtain best-fit parameters using observed values from 5 studies of a single i.v. administration of 1 g meropenem (infusion time 5 or 30 min) in Europeans. The established adult meropenem PBPK model is shown in the Fig. 2, which fits a single i.v. administration well at a dose of 1 g meropenem on visual inspection. The GMFE of AUC0-∞, Cmax, and CL were 1.11, 1.11, and 1.13 in the established adult meropenem PBPK model, respectively (Table S4). For healthy adult PBPK model evaluation, 12 adult meropenem PK studies following various dosing regimens were identified and used (Fig. 3). The goodness of fit diagram of plasma drug concentrations showed that more than 91.12% of the predicted drug concentration values were within twofold error range of the observed values, about 74.11 and 60.41% of the predicted values were within 1.5-fold and 1.25-fold margin of error, respectively (Fig. 4). The MFE average and GMFE of all predicted PK parameters were within 1.25-fold error range in both PBPK Models of meropenem i.v. administration (Table 2).

Prospective analysis of meropenem

This study assessed 92 samples from 31 critically ill patients. The mean serum creatinine (Scr) was 87.06 µmol/L, the mean patient weight was 57 kg, and the mean age was 66 years. Six patients (19.35%) with acute renal failure were on CRRT during their treatment. The physician-selected dose of meropenem was 0.5–1 g, and the patients received the drug every 8 or 12 h with or without extension of the infusion (Table S5).

We made the PBPK models fit to the observed clinical data. Out of the 31 patients, 16 patients (51.61%) used meropenem as targeted therapy by culture; all other patients lacked the microbiological evidence. The identified microorganisms based on meropenem sensitive culture were shown in Table S5, and most of them were pseudomonas aeruginosa and acinetobacter baumannii. The drug sensitivity results showed out of the 16 patients, 12 patients (75%) were resistant to meropenem. As for the treatment outcome, 15 of them (48.39%) got control of inflammation, and their condition improved after using meropenem. The rest of the patients were not so lucky, with 5 dying and 11 still unhealed. As shown in Fig. 7, Out of the 92 blood samples from 31 ICU patients, 71 (77.17%) blood samples were consistent with the simulated value (the solid line), which was within range of 5% to 95%. As shown in Table S5, 8 patients (25.81%) were under 60 years old, 6 patients (19.35%) were over 75 years old, and the remaining patients were between 60 and 74 years old. Meropenem exposure was higher in older adults (> 60 years old) than in younger adults but was generally within the predicted 5th to 95th percentile range. In sum, meropenem PBPK modeling is well simulated in critically ill patients.

Monte carlo simulations

The PBPK model was integrated with Monte Carlo analysis to evaluate the dosage regimen. The Vd, CL, and f were defined as 23.21 L, 12.07 L/h, and 0.98 in Asians with severe infection from PBPK model, respectively. The Asians were likelier to achieve PTA ≥ 90% than White Americans, depending on lower Vd. The PTA for the different dose regimens of meropenem at each MIC is shown in Fig. 8. All simulated regimens had a PTA (40%fT > MIC) of 90% at MICs ≤ 4 mg/L, except for 1 g q12 h with no extended infusion. No regimen achieved optimal PTA at MIC = 8 mg/L for 1 g q12 h. For severe infections caused by pseudomonas aeruginosa with MIC = 8 mg/L, a high frequency and extended infusion time of meropenem might be possible for bacterial eradication. That is to say, the dosing interval of 8 h had higher PTA achievement than the dosing interval of 12 h with the same single dose. The MIC that can achieve PTA ≥ 90% at 40%fT > MIC was 8 mg/L for 1 g q8 h or 4 mg/L for 1 g q12 h in the case of extended infusion, whereas excessive prolongation of the infusion time was unnecessary. There was no significant difference in PTA between 4 and 6 h infusion time, so excessive infusion time (> 4 h) was superfluous . Moreover, meropenem is not recommended because no regimen can achieve optimal PTA at MIC = 16 mg/L.

Discussion

Our study successfully developed the PBPK modeling of meropenem in critically ill patients, which could predict the disposition of meropenem quantitatively. Although a tight therapeutic range does not characterize β-lactams, TDM remains a useful tool for guiding drug dosing for critically ill patients in the ICU. Recently, TDM was reported to identify the need for β-lactam dose changes in 35% of critically ill patients undergoing CRRT.In particular, 24% of TDM sample values suggested excessive doses, while a dose increase was required in the remaining 11%11. However, in the absence of TDM, the PBPK model illuminates the way for clinical dose adjustment. The drug dosing based on the PBPK model can reduce the dose adjustment for critically ill patients. The PBPK model can maximize the use of existing data and improve and enrich the drug information of the special populations in different disease states through data extrapolation. We made the PBPK models fit to observed clinical data, and the results also confirm that our PBPK model can be used for dose design in critically ill patients. But it's worth noting our predicted blood concentration starts from time 0 on the steady state conditions, but the starting time of observations may not be. So, some differences between the predictions and observations are caused by different time points.

Many studies have shown that the plasma drug concentration of water-soluble antibiotics decreases in patients with severe infection12, and the dose should be increased appropriately. This is mainly due to the larger apparent distribution volume (Vd) of water-soluble antibiotics in critically ill patients. The parameters of Vd were defined as 23.21 L and 31.02 L in Asians and White Americans with severe infection from our PBPK model, respectively. The Vd predicted by our PBPK model is consistent with that reported in eight ventilator-associated pneumonia (VAP) patients with a mean body weight of 73 kg13 and nine Thai VAP populations with lower mean body weight (only 54.2 kg)14. It can be seen that the body weight of different populations greatly affects the vd. The PBPK model was integrated with Monte Carlo analysis to evaluate the dosage regimen. The Monte Carlo Simulations showed that Asians were more likely to achieve PTA ≥ 90% than White Americans depending on lower Vd. No regimens achieved optimal PTA at MIC = 8 mg/L in White Americans, even in the case of extended infusion. However, there was no significant difference in PTA between White Americans and Asians at the rapid infusion.

In general, extreme age (the elderly and infants) has an essential effect on pharmacokinetics. Interestingly, meropenem exposure was higher in older adults (> 60 years old) than in younger adults but was generally within the predicted 5th to 95th percentile range. Our research showed that when the meropenem dose decreased from 0.5 to 0.35 g in the elderly population, the plasma exposure levels of meropenem in the elderly population were consistent with those in younger adults (unpublished). Our prospective analysis also confirmed this conclusion and showed that most blood drug concentrations of the elderly population were within the predicted 5th to 95th percentile range under conventional treatment.

Understandably, our study showed that extended infusion and frequent administration were necessary to achieve the target of 40%fT > MIC for critically ill patients, which was consistent with previous research showing that extended infusion was judged to be the most appropriate dosing regimen for β-lactams15. However, excessive prolongation of the infusion time was unnecessary, and there was no significant difference in PTA between 4 and 6 h infusion time in our study. Considering the stability of the meropenem solution, we tend to choose a 4 h infusion time when it is necessary to prolong the infusion time for treating meropenem-nonsusceptible bacterial strains. In addition, it is worth noting that an administration interval of 40%fT > MIC is recommended for carbapenem to ensure the standard efficacy of antibiotics. In contrast, a dose interval of 100%fT > MIC is optimal in immunocompromised patients16. The efficacy was further improved when the antibiotic concentration was 4–5 times that of the MIC. Some studies have suggested that 50%fT > 5MIC is recommended as an optimal PD target in respiratory infections, such as pneumonia, bronchitis, or pleural infections17. Taccone et al. reported an obese patient with sepsis caused by pseudomonas aeruginosa pneumonia who was treated with meropenem to fight infection. The patient's target PD (40%fT > 4MIC) was achieved at a regimen of 3 g q6 h via 3 h intravenous infusion time, and the clinical outcome was improved18. If 100%fT > MIC is chosen as the PD target in our study, the regimen of 1 g q8 h via extended infusion can achieve optimal PTA ≥ 90% at MICs ≤ 2 mg/L in Asians (Figure S1). As for MICs ≥ 16 mg/L, some studies found that meropenem as a monotherapy was still competent under an optimized two-step-administration therapy but not the currently widely traditional simple prolonged-infusion17. The results were consistent with our study that no regimen can achieve optimal PTA at MIC = 16 mg/L even with the extended infusion. The treatment of MICs ≥ 16 mg/L is still a clinical challenge, and it is worth exploring and seeking solutions for this treatment dilemma.

Conclusion

The PBPK/PD modeling incorporating literature data and prospective study data is able to correctly predict meropenem pharmacokinetics in critically ill patients. Extended infusion and frequent administration are equally important to achieve the curative effect of meropenem for critically ill patients. The PBPK/PD modeling provides a reference for realizing clinically personalized medication for critically ill patients.
