Integrative experimental and computational dataset for repurposed antibiofilm drug candidates targeting quorum sensing
Authors: Abdelsattar M. Omar, Khadijah A. Mohammad, Mohammed Almalki, Khalid O. Abulnaja, Sabrin R.M. Ibrahim
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
ObjectivesThis data article presents an integrated dataset combining experimental in vitro biofilm assays and in silico analyses for repurposed compounds tested as antibiofilm agents.Data DescriptionA library of 29 small-molecule compounds (including in-house synthesized analogs and pharmacologically active drugs) was screened for antibiofilm efficacy against Staphylococcus aureus using a 96-well microtiter crystal violet assay. The dataset includes quantitative measurements of biofilm inhibition at multiple concentrations, along with minimum inhibitory concentrations (MICs) against planktonic cultures of S. aureus, Escherichia coli, and Candida albicans. In parallel, computational data comprise molecular docking results of the active compounds with two biofilm-related protein targets (a quorum-sensing regulator and a biofilm-forming enzyme), 100 ns molecular dynamics simulation trajectories for selected compound–target complexes, and predicted ADMET properties (Absorption, Distribution, Metabolism, Excretion, Toxicity) of the lead compounds.Reuse PotentialThese data can be used to explore structure-activity relationships for antibiofilm activity, to validate new computational models of biofilm inhibition, or to inform further drug repurposing efforts. All raw data (biofilm assay measurements, MIC values, docking poses and scores, simulation files, and ADMET profiles) are provided to facilitate reuse and reproducibility. No conclusions or interpretive analysis are included, making this a purely descriptive report of the dataset.

Background

Bacterial biofilms are structured communities of microbes attached to surfaces and encased in a self-produced extracellular matrix [[1], [2], [3]]. In this sessile state, cells exhibit extreme tolerance to antibiotics and host immune defenses, leading to persistent infections that are notoriously difficult to eradicate [1]. Biofilms are implicated in roughly 65–80 % of chronic and recurrent microbial infections in humans [1], underscoring their clinical significance. Examples include device-related infections and chronic wounds, where biofilm formation protects pathogens from antibiotics and immune clearance. Conventional antibiotics, developed to kill planktonic (free-living) bacteria, often fail to eliminate biofilm-based infections [1,4,5]. Currently, there are no approved therapeutics that specifically target or eradicate bacterial biofilms [[6], [7], [8]]. Clinicians must frequently resort to aggressive measures – for example, surgical debridement or removal of infected implants – alongside prolonged antibiotic therapy to manage biofilm-associated infections [9,10]. Even then, relapses are common. This lack of effective interventions highlights an urgent need for novel antibiofilm strategies. One emerging approach is to disarm pathogens by targeting biofilm formation processes (“anti-virulence”) rather than killing bacteria outright [9,11]. By interfering with biofilm-specific functions such as surface adhesion, extracellular polymer production, or quorum sensing, these strategies aim to prevent biofilm development and render bacteria more susceptible to treatment [[11], [12], [13]]. Quorum sensing (QS), a cell-density-dependent communication system, is a particularly attractive target, as it globally regulates biofilm maturation and bacterial virulence factor expression [14,15]. Accordingly, quorum-sensing inhibitors (QSIs) have been shown to significantly attenuate biofilm formation by silencing bacterial communication signals [14]. Notably, because QSIs and other antivirulence agents do not directly kill microbes, they impose less selective pressure for resistance and can synergize with conventional antibiotics [16,17]. Another promising strategy for rapid antibiofilm drug discovery is drug repurposing, which involves screening existing drugs or compound libraries for new antibiofilm activity [17,18]. Repurposing offers a faster, cost-effective route to therapy since candidate compounds often have known pharmacological and safety profiles, reducing the time needed for development [19]. In addition, many approved drugs have unrecognized antibiofilm or anti-QS properties that can be leveraged. For example, certain non-antibiotic molecules have been found to inhibit bacterial QS and biofilm formation without affecting planktonic growth. Building on this concept, the present work integrates in vitro biofilm assays with in silico molecular modeling to identify and characterize novel antibiofilm agents from a library of pharmacologically active compounds [20]. The overall goal was to discover repurposed molecules that effectively disrupt biofilms, potentially by targeting QS or other key pathways, thereby addressing the critical need for new strategies against biofilm-associated infections. Notably, any anti-quorum sensing mechanism proposed in this work (via LasR targeting) is based solely on computational predictions and was not confirmed by in vitro quorum-sensing assays.

Data Description

Antimicrobial activity (MIC data): The minimum inhibitory concentrations of each compound against planktonic bacteria and yeast are summarized in Table 1. The MIC values (in µg/mL) were determined for E. coli, S. aureus, and C. albicans using a standard broth microdilution assay. As shown in Table 1, most of the tested compounds did not inhibit growth up to the highest concentration tested (250 µg/mL), indicated by “>250″ entries. Two compounds stood out with measurable antimicrobial activity: CAB-15 and KAUS-29 each exhibited MICs of approximately 125 µg/mL across all three organisms (i.e. they showed growth inhibition of E. coli, S. aureus, and C. albicans at 125 µg/mL). Another compound, CAB-29, showed a selective effect, inhibiting S. aureus at ∼125 µg/mL but no inhibition of E. coli or C. albicans at 250 µg/mL. All other compounds had no observable MIC (no complete growth inhibition) up to 250 µg/mL. For comparison, the reference antibiotic ciprofloxacin had potent activity (MIC ∼0.2 µg/mL against E. coli and ∼3.4 µg/mL against S. aureus), and the antifungal fluconazole (Diflucan) had MIC ∼0.25 µg/mL against C. albicans. These reference values underscore that the test compounds are considerably less potent than standard antimicrobials. Biofilm inhibition efficacy: The ability of each compound to prevent biofilm formation was evaluated using a microplate crystal violet assay against S. aureus. Table 2 lists the percentage inhibition of biofilm biomass at three concentrations (125, 250, and 500 µg/mL) for each compound. Positive inhibition values indicate a reduction in biofilm relative to the untreated control, whereas negative values indicate that biofilm biomass was higher than the control (i.e., the compound enhanced biofilm formation). As shown in Table 2, the compounds exhibited a wide range of effects, from strong inhibition to substantial promotion of biofilm: approximately one-third of the compounds (around 9–10 compounds) showed notable biofilm inhibition at the highest concentration (500 µg/mL), while several others had minimal effect or even negative values. Notably, two compounds – KAUS-31 and KAUS-38 – achieved 100 % inhibition of S. aureus biofilm at 500 µg/mL, meaning no detectable biofilm formed in the presence of these compounds at that dose. Importantly, neither KAUS-31 nor KAUS-38 showed any planktonic growth inhibition in standard MIC assays (MIC > 250 µg/mL for both), indicating that their antibiofilm effect is not due to general bacterial killing. Similarly, other potent antibiofilm hits such as KAUS-1, KAUS-5, NPK-208, and CIN—OX15 demonstrated high biofilm inhibition percentages (>70–80 % at 500 µg/mL) despite no measurable planktonic MIC, underscoring a specific anti-biofilm action. Even at lower concentrations (125 µg/mL), they still inhibited about 62 % of biofilm formation. Other high performers included KAUS-1 and KAUS-5, which showed >80 % inhibition at 500 µg/mL and maintained roughly 48–65 % inhibition at 125 µg/mL. These results indicate that a subset of the tested molecules can significantly suppress biofilm development in a dose-dependent manner. In contrast, some compounds caused negative inhibition (biofilm promotion). For example, CAB-9 and NPK-210 at 500 µg/mL led to biofilm levels more than double those of the control (−132 % and −247 % inhibition, respectively, meaning the biofilm biomass was over twice as high as the untreated baseline). Several other compounds (e.g., KAUS-11, KAUS-16, KAUS-6) also increased biofilm formation to varying degrees at certain concentrations. This outcome underscores that certain chemical treatments can unintentionally stimulate biofilm production, possibly via stress responses or other compensatory mechanisms in the bacteria. It highlights the importance of screening not only for biofilm inhibition but also for any biofilm-enhancing effects. Most compounds fell in between these extremes, with modest or negligible effects. Roughly 10 compounds showed moderate inhibition (in the range of ∼20–80 % reduction in biofilm at the highest concentration), and many had near 0 % (no significant change) at lower doses. The biofilm inhibition dataset thus provides a differentiated profile of each compound’s antibiofilm activity. Computational modeling data: In addition to the experimental results, this article provides in silico data predicting how the active compounds may interact with molecular targets relevant to biofilm formation. Specifically, molecular docking simulations were performed targeting two proteins: LasR, a quorum-sensing receptor from P. aeruginosa, and Sortase A, an enzyme involved in cell-surface adhesion in S. aureus. These targets were chosen due to their well-documented roles in biofilm development in Gram-negative and Gram-positive bacteria, respectively. LasR in P. aeruginosa orchestrates quorum-sensing signals that drive biofilm maturation, whereas sortase A in S. aureus is essential for anchoring surface proteins during biofilm formation. For each compound that showed significant antibiofilm activity in the assays, docking was used to predict binding affinity and pose within the active site of LasR and sortase A. The docking outputs (provided in the supplementary data) include the top-ranked binding poses and Glide docking scores for each compound–target pair. For example, one of the repurposed drugs, 5-fluorouracil (5-FU), was predicted to bind in the LasR ligand pocket with a favorable docking score, consistent with literature reports that 5-FU can attenuate quorum sensing in P. aeruginosa (note: this anti-QS activity was not verified experimentally in our study). Similarly, another compound, ebselen, showed a high docking score for sortase A and was found to interact with the enzyme’s catalytic cysteine residue in the model, suggesting a potential mechanism for inhibiting S. aureus biofilm assembly (detailed interaction diagrams are included in the dataset). To complement the rigid docking results, 100 ns molecular dynamics (MD) simulations were conducted for selected compound–protein complexes. This helps evaluate the stability of the docked pose when the protein and ligand are allowed to flex. The MD trajectory data (included as coordinate files) indicate that in most cases the lead compounds remained stably bound in the target site over the simulation. For instance, the compound NPK-208 had a very low docking score in the initial rigid docking (implying poor fit to LasR); however, when receptor flexibility was introduced via induced-fit docking and MD simulation, a stable binding conformation emerged for NPK-208 in the LasR pocket. This suggests that NPK-208 can indeed bind LasR after the protein undergoes slight conformational adjustments – an insight that static docking alone might miss. Such cases, captured by our MD data, illustrate the importance of dynamic modeling for accurate prediction of ligand–receptor interactions. All MD simulation files and analyses (e.g. RMSD plots) are provided, allowing others to inspect the behavior of each complex. Lastly, the dataset includes in silico ADMET profiles for the key compounds. These were generated using QikProp software (Schrödinger) and summarize predicted physicochemical and pharmacokinetic properties such as log P, solubility, permeability, likely metabolic liabilities, and toxicity risk indicators. The ADMET results suggest that the majority of the active antibiofilm compounds fall within drug-like chemical space. For example, most compounds obey Lipinski’s “Rule of Five” for oral drug-likeness , with molecular weights, lipophilicity (cLogP), and hydrogen bond counts in ranges favorable for bioavailability. No compound was flagged for extreme insolubility or severe predicted toxicity; several compounds had low predicted toxicity and acceptable oral absorption scores. In silico ADMET profiling was performed here even for these previously approved compounds to assess their suitability for the new antibiofilm indication and to identify any potential pharmacokinetic or toxicity liabilities early on. These predictions, while requiring experimental confirmation, imply that the repurposed antibiofilm hits have reasonable pharmacokinetic profiles for potential further development. The full ADMET parameter tables are provided as supplementary material for researchers who may wish to compare these compounds’ properties or filter them against additional criteria. In summary, the computational data (docking models, MD simulations, and ADMET predictions) complement the experimental findings by providing hypotheses about each compound’s mode of action and its drug-like characteristics. This integrated dataset can be reused to guide structure-based design of improved antibiofilm agents or to prioritize compounds for in vivo testing. However, no strong correlation was observed between a compound’s in vitro biofilm inhibition efficacy and its docking scores or MD stability metrics – in other words, high predicted binding affinity did not always translate to high antibiofilm activity (and vice versa).

Experimental Design, Materials and Methods

This section outlines the in vitro microbiological assays and in silico computational studies performed to evaluate novel antibiofilm agents. All methods were designed for reproducibility, with clear controls and parameters specified for each step.

Microbial Strains and Culture Conditions

A panel of clinically isolated microorganisms was used, including Staphylococcus aureus (Gram-positive bacterium), E. coli (Gram-negative bacterium), and Candida albicans (yeast). Each strain was verified by standard microbiological methods and stored on agar slants at 4 °C until use. Prior to experiments, cultures were refreshed by sub-culturing onto appropriate media to ensure viability and purity.

Mueller–Hinton Broth (MHB) was the primary growth medium for bacterial assays, as recommended by Clinical and Laboratory Standards Institute protocols. MHB supports robust growth of both S. aureus and E. coli, and it was also used for C. albicans (with longer incubation for the slower-growing yeast). Mueller–Hinton Agar (MHA) plates were used for maintaining stock cultures and confirming inoculum purity. All incubations were at 37 °C; bacterial cultures were grown for ∼24 h and C. albicans for ∼48 h to reach the appropriate cell density.

Test Compounds and Preparation

A library of 29 compounds (comprising in-house synthesized coumarin-derived molecules and selected repurposed drugs with known pharmacological uses), Table S1, was screened for antibiofilm activity. Each compound was dissolved in an appropriate solvent (typically DMSO or water) to prepare stock solutions. Working solutions were prepared in MHB for bioassays. The final DMSO concentration in any biological assay did not exceed 3 % v/v, a level confirmed to have no effect on microbial growth or biofilm formation.

Reference Controls Ciprofloxacin (a broad-spectrum antibiotic) served as a positive control for bacterial assays, and fluconazole (Diflucan) for C. albicans. These were prepared per manufacturer instructions and tested in parallel with the novel compounds to validate assay performance. The expected reference MIC values (e.g., sub-µg/mL MIC for E. coli with ciprofloxacin) were observed, confirming that the assay conditions were appropriate and the test strains responded as expected.

Antimicrobial Activity Assay (Broth microdilution MIC)

Each test organism was grown overnight and then diluted in fresh broth to a standardized cell density (∼5 × 105 CFU/mL in the final well). For bacteria, an optical density (OD) at 600 nm corresponding to the desired CFU was used; for C. albicans, cells were counted using a hemocytometer or OD reading calibrated to CFU. In a sterile 96-well flat-bottom plate, 100 µL of MHB containing the test compound was added to the first well of a row (typically at 250 µg/mL or higher). Two-fold serial dilutions were performed across the row to achieve a range of concentrations (e.g., 250, 125, 62.5, 31.3 … down to ∼1 µg/mL). Then 100 µL of the standardized microbial inoculum was added to each well (final volume 200 µL). This yielded the target ∼5 × 105 CFU/mL in each well. Each plate included:

Sterility control: Media + solvent without inoculum (to confirm no contamination and account for any background signal).

Growth control: Inoculum in media without any compound (to represent 100 % growth).

Reference drug control: Inoculum with ciprofloxacin (for bacteria) or fluconazole (Diflucan) at known effective concentrations, to ensure the assay detects growth inhibition as expected.

Plates were incubated at 37 °C for 24 h (S. aureus, E. coli) or up to 48 h for C. albicans, under static conditions.

After incubation, each well was examined for visible growth (turbidity) against a dark background. The MIC was recorded as the lowest compound concentration with no visible growth (a clear well). To improve accuracy, optical density at 600 nm was also measured for each well using a microplate reader; a well was considered “no growth” if its OD600 was comparable to the sterile control and significantly lower than the growth control. Each MIC determination was performed in triplicate on separate days to ensure reproducibility. MIC values were accepted as final only if replicates were consistent; any anomalous result prompted a repeat assay.

Biofilm Inhibition Efficacy (Crystal violet assay)

The ability of each compound to inhibit biofilm formation was assessed using a 96-well microplate crystal violet assay, following standard protocols for biofilm quantification . We focused on Staphylococcus aureus as a model biofilm-forming organism for these experiments. An overnight culture of S. aureus was diluted into fresh MHB to an initial cell density of approximately 106 CFU/mL. Aliquots of 100 µL of this bacterial suspension were dispensed into sterile flat-bottom 96-well plates, and 100 µL of test compound solution (prepared in MHB) was added to each well. Compounds were tested at fixed concentrations of 500 µg/mL, 250 µg/mL, and 125 µg/mL (in separate wells) to evaluate dose-dependent effects. Control wells included bacteria without any compound (negative control for 0 % inhibition) and blank wells with media only (for background subtraction). The plate was incubated at 37 °C for 24 h under static (no shaking) conditions to allow biofilm formation on the well surfaces.

After incubation, the planktonic (free-floating) culture medium was carefully aspirated from each well without disturbing the adherent biofilm. Each well was then gently rinsed by adding and removing sterile phosphate-buffered saline (PBS) to remove any loosely attached cells. The remaining surface-attached biofilm was fixed and stained by adding 125 µL of 0.1 % (w/v) crystal violet solution to each well and incubating for 15–20 min at room temperature. Excess stain was then rinsed off by thoroughly washing the wells with water (the wash was repeated multiple times until no further unstained residue remained). The plate was air-dried, and the bound crystal violet (staining the biofilm biomass) was solubilized by adding 150 µL of 33 % acetic acid to each well. After 10 min of solubilization, the absorbance of the resulting solution was measured at 570 nm (OD570) using a plate reader. For each sample well, the OD570 reading (after subtracting the average value of blank wells) was used as an index of biofilm biomass. The percent biofilm inhibition for a treated well was calculated as:Percentageinhibition(%)=100×(1−ODtreatedODcontrol)

Thus, a value of 100 % indicates no detectable biofilm (complete inhibition), while a negative value indicates that the treated well had higher biofilm biomass than the untreated control (i.e., biofilm promotion). Each compound was tested in at least two independent experiments (each with triplicate wells per condition) to ensure data reproducibility. Results are reported as mean ± standard deviation, and statistical significance of differences between treated and control biofilms was determined by a two-tailed Student’s t-test (p < 0.05 considered significant). The biofilm inhibition data obtained were tabulated as shown in Table 2, and representative images of stained wells were documented for qualitative confirmation of the results

Molecular Docking and Simulation Insights

Molecular Docking Procedure: Computational docking studies were carried out to predict the interaction of the compounds with key biofilm-related protein targets. Two target proteins were selected for virtual screening: (1) Pseudomonas aeruginosa LasR, a quorum-sensing transcriptional regulator, and (2) Staphylococcus aureus Sortase A, a cell wall-anchoring enzyme. These targets were chosen based on their pivotal roles in biofilm formation in Gram-negative and Gram-positive bacteria, respectively. The docking simulations were performed using the Schrödinger Maestro molecular modeling suite. Protein structures were prepared from published crystallographic or homology models (retrieved from the Protein Data Bank or literature) by assigning protonation states, adding missing side chains, and minimizing the structure. All ligands (compounds) were energy-minimized and prepared with proper protonation using Schrödinger’s LigPrep. Glide (Grid-based Ligand Docking with Energetics) was used for the docking, employing the extra-precision mode for scoring. The binding site for LasR was defined around the natural autoinducer binding pocket, and for sortase A around the active site cysteine. Each compound was docked into both targets, and the top scoring poses were saved. For cases where the initial Glide docking score was poor despite experimental activity of the compound (suggesting a possible induced-fit scenario), an induced-fit docking protocol was applied. In induced-fit docking, the receptor side-chains were allowed to adjust in response to the ligand. This was especially used for compounds like NPK-208 that appeared to not fit the rigid LasR structure initially. Induced-fit docking often resulted in improved docking scores and more plausible binding poses when compared to the rigid receptor approach. All docking scores (Glide XP score in kcal/mol) and key ligand–residue interactions are provided in the supplementary tables. We also include images of representative docked poses (e.g., Supplementary Figure S1 shows the docking pose of compound CAB-19 in the LasR binding site, highlighting hydrogen bonds to the LasR active site residues).

Molecular Dynamics Simulations: To further validate the docked complexes, molecular dynamics (MD) simulations were performed using Desmond (Schrödinger). Selected ligand–protein complexes (generally those with highest docking affinity or particular interest) were embedded in an orthorhombic water box (explicit solvent model using TIP3P water) with appropriate neutralizing ions and 0.15 M NaCl to simulate physiological conditions. Each system was minimized and equilibrated, then subjected to a production MD run for 100 ns at 300 K and 1 atm (NPT ensemble). The simulation timestep was 2 fs, and coordinates were saved at regular intervals for analysis. During the simulation, the stability of the ligand within the binding site was monitored via root-mean-square deviation (RMSD) of the ligand and key protein residues. In most cases, the compound remained stably bound, with only minor fluctuations in pose, suggesting a stable interaction. For example, in the 100 ns simulation of the LasR–NPK-208 complex, the ligand RMSD stabilized after an initial adjustment, indicating that LasR’s binding pocket accommodated NPK-208 after a conformational shift. This aligns with the induced-fit docking result. Full trajectory files and analysis scripts are provided, so users may visualize the dynamic behavior or calculate other parameters (like binding energy estimates via MM-PBSA). The MD results underscore which docked poses are likely to be biologically relevant (stable) and also illustrate dynamic protein flexibility that might not be captured in static docking.

In silico ADMET Analysis: To assess drug-likeness and safety profiles of the lead compounds, in silico ADMET (Absorption, Distribution, Metabolism, Excretion, Toxicity) predictions were conducted. We utilized QikProp (Schrödinger) to compute various descriptors and ADMET indices for each compound. These include molecular weight, lipophilicity (Log P), water solubility (Log S), Caco-2 cell permeability, blood–brain barrier penetration potential, CYP450 enzyme binding alerts, predicted toxicity classes, and compliance with Lipinski’s Rule of Five. The ADMET results are compiled in a supplementary table. In general, the compounds in this study were within reasonable ranges for oral drug candidates: for instance, most had Log P values between 1 and 4, and none exceeded 500 Da in molecular weight or 10 rotatable bonds, which suggests good oral bioavailability potential. A few compounds showed slightly lower predicted solubility, but still within tolerable limits for likely formulation. Importantly, no compound was predicted to be an extreme outlier for toxicity; all fell outside the high-risk range for parameters like HERG channel inhibition or rodent LD50, based on the prediction models. These predictions should be interpreted with caution, but they provide an initial safety screen indicating that the antibiofilm leads do not have obvious “red flag” properties. By sharing these ADMET data, we enable other researchers to quickly identify if any compound might have issues (e.g., if a follow-up study wants to choose a lead compound for animal testing, the ADMET profile can guide the selection). All experimental protocols described above were carried out with appropriate controls and replicates to ensure data quality. The resulting dataset combines these wet-lab and dry-lab methods to give a holistic view of each compound’s profile as a potential antibiofilm agent. This integrated approach is similar to other recent efforts in anti-pathogenic drug discovery, and the data can be re-analyzed or mined to generate new hypotheses for combating biofilms. No conclusions are drawn here about efficacy or mechanisms; rather, the data are presented to support further independent analysis and reuse.

Ethics Statement

This study did not involve human participants or live vertebrate animals, and thus no specific institutional ethical approval was required. All experimental work was performed using microbial cultures (S. aureus, E. coli, and C. albicans) obtained from existing laboratory strain collections. The use of these microorganisms complied with institutional biosafety guidelines and standard ethical practices in microbiology. No human-derived materials or personal data were used. Because the bacterial and fungal isolates were de-identified and sourced from laboratory stocks, there were no issues of patient consent. In summary, the research was carried out on microbial cells in vitro, posing no ethical concerns regarding human or animal subjects.
