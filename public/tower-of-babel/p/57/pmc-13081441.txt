From early smoking to COPD and other chronic respiratory disease: unraveling the roles of chemical pollutants and underlying toxicological mechanisms
Authors: Rui Zhao, Huihui Zeng, Yan Chen
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundAlthough the association between cigarette smoking and respiratory disorders is well established, the long-term impact of early initiation of smoking on overall health and respiratory diseases remains insufficiently characterized.MethodsUsing data from 157,699 participants in the UK Biobank (UKB) and 21,727 participants in the National Health and Nutrition Examination Survey (NHANES), we systematically evaluated the associations between early smoking initiation and a broad range of health outcomes.ResultsParticipants who initiated smoking at an early age exhibited multiple adverse health outcomes. Early smoking was associated with a substantially increased risk of chronic lower respiratory diseases, accelerated decline in lung function, and elevated mortality. Further analyses highlighted the potential contribution of chemical pollutants in cigarette smoke to these detrimental effects. Notably, early smoking predominantly affected airway epithelial integrity and inflammatory pathways, and this association was partly mediated by cadmium exposure, a toxic metal commonly present in cigarette smoke.ConclusionsThe observed associations between early smoking initiation and a spectrum of unfavorable health outcomes underscore the urgent need for increased attention from healthcare professionals and society as a whole. Strengthened efforts to prevent adolescent smoking may play a critical role in reducing the future burden of chronic obstructive pulmonary disease (COPD) and other smoking-related diseases.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12890-026-04229-8.

Introduction

Chronic lower respiratory diseases are a group of non-infectious disorders affecting the lower airways and represent a major cause of global morbidity and mortality. Among these conditions, asthma and chronic obstructive pulmonary disease (COPD) are the two leading contributors to disease burden and premature death [1]. COPD is a common chronic airway disorder characterized by persistent and progressive airflow limitation [2, 3] and is associated with substantial morbidity, mortality, and healthcare utilization. Despite advances in prevention and treatment, COPD remains a major public health challenge for clinicians in the 21st century [4]. Globally, COPD is estimated to affect more than 380 million individuals [5], with projected cases rising to approximately 592 million by 2050 [6]. In parallel, the cumulative economic burden of COPD is expected to reach USD 4.326 trillion between 2020 and 2050, underscoring its profound societal impact [7]. Tobacco smoke exposure is the predominant cause of COPD, including both active smoking [8] and secondhand smoke exposure [9]. Notably, tobacco smoking remains the single most important risk factor for the development and progression of COPD [10]. Asthma, in contrast, is a heterogeneous and multi-factorial respiratory disorder characterized by variable respiratory symptoms and airflow limitation, including wheezing, shortness of breath, chest tightness, and cough [11, 12]. As the second most common chronic respiratory disease worldwide after COPD, asthma affects more than 300 million individuals globally [13]. Increasing evidence further indicates that tobacco smoking also increases the risk of developing asthma [14].

Tobacco smoking is widely recognized as one of the leading causes of premature death worldwide, contributing to increased mortality from both somatic and mental disorders [15]. Of particular concern, smoking initiation during adolescence has emerged as a critical global public health issue. Recent studies report that nearly 40% of adolescents who currently smoke exhibit tobacco dependence worldwide [16], and the prevalence of current adolescent smoking remains high and continues to increase in many regions [17]. A large proportion of adolescents had high-intensity and different kinds of tobacco use [18]. A research reported that at least 43.8 million adolescents aged 13 to 15 years use some form of tobacco, globally [19]. Beyond its immediate social, educational, and economic consequences, adolescent smoking adversely affects physical growth and biological development, increasing long-term disease risk and potentially exerting lifelong health impacts. Epidemiological studies have shown that adolescent smoking is associated with reduced lung function and an elevated risk of COPD later in life [20]. Consistently, cohort evidence suggests that reducing childhood smoke exposure may mitigate lung function impairment in early adulthood [21]. Importantly, susceptibility to smoking-related lung injury cannot be fully explained by adolescence as a legal or social construct. Instead, lung vulnerability is more accurately understood within the framework of the alveolar development stage (ADS), a prolonged period of alveolar growth and maturation. During ADS, which extends from birth through early adulthood to approximately 24 years of age, alveoli increase in number and progressively mature [22]. ADS is a complex biological process that begins as early as 35 weeks of gestation and continues for several years after birth, during which alveolar surface area expands by nearly twenty-fold [23]. This developmental process is essential for meeting the body’s bioenergetic oxygen requirements. Accumulating evidence highlights the heightened vulnerability of the lung to smoking during ADS. Clinical studies have demonstrated that COPD patients who initiate smoking at an earlier age experience worse lung function, more frequent acute exacerbations, and a higher long-term risk of death, emphasizing the disproportionate harm of smoking during this developmental window [24]. Furthermore, experimental and epidemiological studies suggest that smoking during ADS may not only reduce alveolar number but also induce persistent structural and functional abnormalities of the lung, which are central to the pathogenesis of COPD [25].

Based on this biological framework, we define early smoking as smoking initiation during the alveolar development stage (≤ 24 years of age), whereas individuals who initiate smoking after this period are classified as late smokers. Despite growing awareness of early-life susceptibility, relatively few studies have systematically evaluated the health risks of early smoking using an ADS-based definition, and the potential harms of smoking during this critical developmental period warrant further investigation.

Beyond its effects on lung development, tobacco smoking influences health through exposure to a complex mixture of toxic substances. Cigarette smoke contains more than 7,000 chemical compounds, including polycyclic aromatic hydrocarbons (PAHs), toxic metals such as arsenic and cadmium, nitrosamines, aromatic amines, and carbonyl compounds [26]. Early initiation of smoking may alter the absorption and internal burden of these harmful environmental pollutants, potentially amplifying their long-term biological effects. Therefore, we were particularly interested in evaluating the impact of early smoking on chemical pollutant exposure.

Accordingly, the present study aimed to systematically evaluate the health consequences of early smoking in large, nationally representative populations and to assess its potential contribution to chronic lower respiratory diseases. We leveraged data from the UK Biobank (UKB) and the National Health and Nutrition Examination Survey (NHANES), representing populations from the United Kingdom and the United States, respectively. In addition, we conducted a network toxicology analysis using NHANES data to identify potential early smoking–chemical pollutant–downstream target pathways, providing mechanistic insights into the biological effects of early smoking (Fig. 1).

Materials and methods

Study population

UK Biobank (UKB) is a large-scale prospective biomedical cohort comprising more than 500,000 community-dwelling adults in the United Kingdom. Detailed information on study design, recruitment, and data access has been described elsewhere and is publicly available (http://www.ukbiobank.ac.uk) [27]. All participants provided written informed consent, and UKB was conducted in accordance with the principles of the Declaration of Helsinki. Ethical approval was obtained from the North West Multi-centre Research Ethics Committee. The present analysis was conducted under UKB application ID 105,139.

Among UKB participants, we first identified individuals who reported ever smoking cigarettes (n = 298,425). Of these, participants who reported the age at which they started smoking cigarettes regularly were eligible for inclusion (n = 22,999). To minimize reverse causation and ensure sufficient follow-up time, analyses were restricted to participants aged ≥ 40 years at baseline. After excluding individuals with missing data on body mass index (BMI; n = 119), alcohol consumption (n = 9), or follow-up time (n = 305), a total of 22,566 ever-smokers were included in the final analysis. Participants who reported never smoking cigarettes were selected as a negative control group (n = 200,653). After excluding individuals with inconsistent or incorrectly reported smoking-related information (n = 63,489), age < 40 years (n = 3), or missing data on BMI (n = 797), alcohol consumption (n = 115), or follow-up time (n = 1,116), a total of 135,133 never-smokers were included in the final analysis (Fig. 2A).

The National Health and Nutrition Examination Survey (NHANES) is a nationally representative, continuous survey of the U.S. civilian, non-institutionalized population, employing a complex, multistage, stratified, and clustered probability sampling design. Data are collected in 2-year cycles to reflect the health and nutritional status of the U.S. population.

We included participants from six NHANES cycles (2007–2018), comprising a total of 59,842 individuals. Participants who reported the age at which they started smoking cigarettes regularly were identified (n = 15,313). After excluding participants aged < 40 years (n = 4,373) and those with missing data on respiratory disease diagnosis (n = 63), mortality status (n = 17), or BMI (n = 593), 10,266 ever-smokers were included in the final analysis. Participants who reported never smoking cigarettes were selected as a negative control group (n = 20,251). After excluding individuals aged < 40 years (n = 8,057) and those with missing data on respiratory disease diagnosis (n = 24), mortality status (n = 49), or BMI (n = 660), 11,461 never-smokers were included in the final analysis (Fig. 2B). The final analytic sample included 3,751 participants from 2007 to 2008, 3,938 from 2009 to 2010, 3,343 from 2011 to 2012, 3,624 from 2013 to 2014, 3,517 from 2015 to 2016, and 3,554 from 2017 to 2018.

The study protocol was approved by the Ethics Review Committee of the Second Xiangya Hospital, Central South University (approval number LYF20250266), with a waiver of informed consent granted due to the use of publicly available, de-identified data.

Smoking status and definition of early smoking

Smoking initiation age was assessed using the questionnaire item: “How old were you when you first started to smoke cigarettes fairly regularly?” Early smoking was defined as smoking initiation at or before 24 years of age, corresponding to the alveolar development stage. Participants who reported smoking initiation after 24 years of age were classified as late smokers. Individuals who reported never smoking cigarettes were categorized as never smokers and served as the negative control group.

Ascertainment of chronic lower respiratory diseases

In NHANES, chronic lower respiratory diseases included COPD, asthma, and chronic bronchitis, defined based on self-reported physician diagnosis using standardized respiratory questionnaires [28].

In UKB, chronic lower respiratory diseases included COPD, asthma, chronic bronchitis, bronchiectasis, interstitial lung diseases, and pneumoconiosis. Incident COPD was identified using the first occurrence of health outcomes defined by the International Statistical Classification of Diseases and Related Health Problems, 10th Revision (ICD-10), code J44 (field ID 42017). Diagnoses of respiratory diseases were ascertained through linked primary care records, hospital inpatient data, and other health-related registries.

Covariates

Potential confounders were selected a priori based on previous literature. Demographic covariates included age, sex, ethnicity, and socioeconomic status. Socioeconomic status was assessed using the Townsend Deprivation Index in UKB and the family income-to-poverty ratio in NHANES.

Lifestyle and health-related covariates included BMI, alcohol consumption, smoking pack-years, physical activity, and depression status. BMI was categorized as underweight (< 18.5 kg/m²), normal weight (18.5–<24.9 kg/m²), overweight (25–<29.9 kg/m²), and obesity (≥ 30 kg/m²). Alcohol consumption was categorized by drinking frequency in UKB and by average daily alcohol intake in NHANES. Smoking pack-years were calculated as the number of cigarettes smoked per day multiplied by years of smoking. Physical activity was assessed using the International Physical Activity Questionnaire (IPAQ) in both cohorts [29]. Depression status was determined based on self-reported questionnaire data.

Identification of disease- and pollutant-related target genes

Target genes associated with chronic lower respiratory diseases (including COPD, asthma, and chronic bronchitis) were retrieved from the GeneCards database (https://www.genecards.org/) using disease-related keywords. Target genes related to chemical pollutants were obtained from the Comparative Toxicogenomics Database (CTD) (https://ctdbase.org/).

Protein–protein interaction network and MCODE analysis

Overlapping target genes between chronic lower respiratory diseases and chemical pollutants were analyzed using the STRING database (https://cn.string-db.org/), restricted to Homo sapiens. A minimum interaction score of 0.7 was applied to construct the protein–protein interaction (PPI) network. Network visualization and module detection were performed using Cytoscape (version 3.10.3) with the MCODE and CytoNCA (version 2.1.6) plugins to identify key functional clusters.

Gene ontology enrichment analysis

Gene Ontology (GO) enrichment analysis was conducted using the DAVID online platform (https://david.ncifcrf.gov/). The top 10 enriched GO terms were visualized using an online bioinformatics visualization platform (http://www.bioinformatics.com.cn/).

Molecular docking

Molecular docking was performed to evaluate potential interactions between chemical pollutants and key protein targets by calculating CDOCKER energy and CDOCKER interaction energy. Protein structures were obtained from the Protein Data Bank (PDB) (https://www.rcsb.org/) and processed using Schrödinger’s Protein Preparation Wizard. Docking simulations and structural analyses were conducted using Discovery Studio.

Statistical analysis

All statistical analyses were conducted using R software (version 4.2.1). Two-sided P values < 0.05 were considered statistically significant. NHANES analyses incorporated sampling weights, primary sampling units, and strata to account for the complex survey design. Environmental subsample weights (WTSA2YR) were applied according to NHANES analytical guidelines (https://wwwn.cdc.gov/nchs/nhanes/tutorials/Weighting. aspx).

Baseline characteristics were summarized by smoking status. Continuous variables are presented as mean ± standard deviation (SD) and compared using t tests or ANOVA, while categorical variables are presented as counts and percentages and compared using chi-square tests. Kaplan–Meier survival curves and Cox proportional hazards models were used to estimate mortality risk across smoking groups. Multivariable models were sequentially adjusted: Model 1 (unadjusted), Model 2 (age, sex, ethnicity, socioeconomic status), and Model 3 (additional adjustment for BMI, alcohol consumption, smoking pack-years, physical activity, and depression). These covariates, including age and BMI, have normal distribution in both the UKB and NHANES databases (Supplementary file).

Associations between early smoking and lung function were evaluated using multivariable linear regression. Proteomic analyses were performed to identify differentially expressed proteins across smoking groups. Finally, an integrated network toxicology framework was applied to investigate potential mechanisms linking early smoking to chronic lower respiratory diseases via chemical pollutant exposure, incorporating pollutant association analysis, GO enrichment, and PPI network analysis.

In UKB, a total of 3,665 prevalent COPD cases were identified at baseline, with 1,330 incident COPD cases and 13,881 all-cause deaths recorded during follow-up. In NHANES, 1,872 prevalent COPD cases and 2,931 all-cause deaths were observed over the follow-up period.

In UKB, participants in the early-smoking group initiated smoking at a mean age of 16.38 years, whereas those in the late-smoking group initiated smoking at a mean age of 31.45 years. Similarly, in NHANES, the mean age of smoking initiation was 16.08 years in the early-smoking group and 30.52 years in the late-smoking group.

Across both cohorts, compared with never smokers, participants in the early- and late-smoking groups were more likely to be older, male, have a higher BMI, and exhibit worse socioeconomic status. Compared with late smokers, early smokers had significantly higher smoking pack-years in both UKB and NHANES.

Association between early smoking and chronic lower respiratory diseases

The associations between early smoking and chronic lower respiratory diseases were assessed using multivariable logistic regression models (Table 2). In NHANES, participants in the early-smoking group exhibited the highest prevalence of chronic lower respiratory diseases, including COPD, asthma, and chronic bronchitis, compared with both late smokers and never smokers. Late smokers also showed a significantly higher prevalence of these conditions compared with never smokers. In UKB, both early- and late-smoking groups demonstrated a higher prevalence of COPD, chronic bronchitis, and interstitial pulmonary diseases compared with never smokers. However, no statistically significant differences were observed for asthma, bronchiectasis, or pneumoconiosis (Supplementary Table 1). When directly comparing early and late smokers, early smoking was associated with a higher prevalence of chronic bronchitis, interstitial pulmonary diseases, and pneumoconiosis, whereas the prevalence of COPD was comparable between the two groups. In addition, mortality outcomes were evaluated. In both UKB and NHANES, participants in the early-smoking group exhibited higher all-cause mortality and higher chronic lower respiratory disease–related mortality compared with both late smokers and never smokers (Supplementary File 2).

Association between early smoking and lung function

Among UKB participants, lung function measurements were available for 144,288 individuals, including 123,945 never smokers, 1,981 late smokers, and 18,362 early smokers. Baseline lung function indices, including forced vital capacity (FVC), forced expiratory volume in 1 s (FEV₁), and FEV₁/FVC ratio, were analyzed using multivariable linear regression models (Table 3). Early smokers exhibited significantly lower FVC, FEV₁, and FEV₁/FVC compared with late smokers at baseline and during follow-up. Participants were further stratified according to GOLD classification criteria (GOLD 2025) [30]. Longitudinal analyses demonstrated that early smokers experienced more rapid declines in FVC and FEV₁ than late smokers in GOLD 0, GOLD 2, and GOLD 3–4 categories during follow-up (Table 4). No statistically significant differences in lung function decline were observed between early and late smokers in the GOLD 1 group.

Proteomic alterations associated with early smoking

To explore potential biological mechanisms underlying the greater health burden associated with early smoking, proteomic data from UKB were analyzed. After adjustment for age, sex, ethnicity, education level, Townsend deprivation index, BMI, smoking pack-years, alcohol consumption, physical activity (MET), and depression status, 326 proteins were identified as differentially expressed in association with early smoking (Fig. 3A; Supplementary Table 2). Among these, 134 proteins were upregulated and 192 proteins were downregulated.

The result of proteomics assays. A Volcano plots of differential expression proteins associations with early smoking (B) Molecular Function Analysis (C) Biological Process Analysis (D) Cell Component Analysis

Using the GeneCards database, disease-related targets were identified for COPD, chronic bronchitis, and interstitial pulmonary diseases. Among the 326 differentially expressed proteins, 142 overlapped with COPD targets, 72 overlapped with chronic bronchitis targets, and 92 overlapped with interstitial pulmonary disease targets (Supplementary Table 3). A total of 56 shared targets were common to all three diseases, including HMOX1, NOTCH3, and EGFR.

Gene Ontology (GO) enrichment analysis of these 56 shared targets revealed significant enrichment in receptor ligand activity, signaling receptor activator activity, and cytokine activity within the Molecular Function category (Fig. 3B). In the Biological Process category, enrichment was observed in cytokine-mediated signaling pathways, leukocyte cell–cell adhesion, response to corticosteroids, and positive regulation of epithelial cell proliferation (Fig. 3C). Cellular Component analysis indicated predominant localization to the external side of the plasma membrane, membrane rafts, and membrane microdomains (Fig. 3D).

Association between early smoking and chemical pollutant exposure

To further investigate the role of environmental toxicants, levels of chemical pollutants were analyzed across smoking groups. Compared with late smokers and never smokers, blood levels of lead and cadmium were significantly higher in early smokers (Fig. 4). Additional chemical pollutants, including mono-n-octyl phthalate and polyfluoroalkyl substances (e.g., perfluorooctanoic acid), also differed significantly in early smokers after multivariable adjustment (Supplementary Table 4).

Network toxicology analysis of cadmium-related mechanisms

Given the observed mediation effect of cadmium, network toxicology analyses were conducted to explore potential molecular mechanisms. Using the Comparative Toxicogenomics Database (CTD), 6,126 cadmium-related target genes were identified. Intersection analyses revealed 34 shared targets among cadmium-related genes, differentially expressed proteins, and chronic lower respiratory disease–associated targets (Supplementary Table 6).

Protein–protein interaction (PPI) network analysis of these 34 targets was performed using the STRING database (Fig. 5A). MCODE clustering identified two functional modules associated with cadmium-related toxicity (Fig. 5B and C). Module 1 comprised seven genes (HMOX1, BCL2, ESR1, MMP9, EDN1, BDNF, and EGFR) and was enriched in biological processes related to cellular response to cadmium ion, epithelial cell proliferation, and epithelial cell apoptotic processes (Table 6). Module 2 included ten genes (NOTCH2, ITGAM, NOTCH3, NCAM1, IL15, IL7R, IL7, FASLG, NOTCH1, and ICAM1) and was enriched in processes related to epithelial cell development and immune-associated pathways.

Discussion

In this large population-based study integrating data from the UK Biobank and NHANES, we systematically examined the health consequences of early smoking, defined according to the alveolar development stage. Our findings demonstrate that smoking initiation during this critical developmental window is associated with a substantially increased risk of chronic lower respiratory diseases, accelerated decline in lung function, and elevated mortality. Importantly, our analyses further suggest that these adverse outcomes may be partially mediated by altered exposure to chemical pollutants, particularly cadmium, and by downstream effects on epithelial cell integrity and inflammatory pathways.

Consistent with previous epidemiological evidence, we observed that individuals who initiated smoking earlier in life exhibited a higher prevalence of COPD, chronic bronchitis, and interstitial pulmonary diseases, as well as increased all-cause and respiratory disease–related mortality. Notably, the mean age of smoking initiation in the early-smoking group was approximately 16 years in both cohorts, whereas late smokers initiated smoking at around 30 years of age. This pronounced difference in exposure timing may contribute to the observed disparities in respiratory outcomes. However, Most prior studies have defined early smoking using legal or social constructs of adolescence rather than biological development stages [31]. In contrast, our study adopted an alveolar development–based definition, aligning with previous work from our group demonstrating that smoking initiation during this period is associated with adverse cardiopulmonary outcomes and increased mortality among patients with COPD [32]. These findings are further supported by earlier reports showing higher mortality among individuals who began smoking at younger ages [33].

The biological plausibility of these associations is supported by evidence that the alveolar development stage represents a period of heightened vulnerability. During this stage, lung growth and maturation are ongoing, and disruption may result in long-lasting structural and functional impairment. Experimental and clinical studies suggest that smoking during alveolar development induces systemic inflammation and interferes with normal lung maturation, potentially predisposing individuals to chronic respiratory diseases later in life [34]. Given that alveoli constitute the primary interface between inhaled toxins and lung tissue, early exposure to cigarette smoke may exert particularly deleterious effects during this window.

In line with this hypothesis, we observed that early smokers exhibited lower baseline lung function and more rapid declines in FVC and FEV₁, particularly among individuals with more advanced disease (GOLD 3–4). While previous studies have reported differences in lung function between young smokers and non-smokers [35], few investigations have focused on the rate of lung function decline associated with early smoking. Our findings highlight the importance of close monitoring of lung function among individuals who initiate smoking early, with potential implications for early intervention and prevention of COPD progression.

At the molecular level, proteomic analyses revealed widespread alterations in protein expression associated with early smoking. A substantial proportion of differentially expressed proteins overlapped with known targets of COPD, chronic bronchitis, and interstitial pulmonary diseases, with shared targets enriched in pathways related to inflammation and epithelial cell proliferation. Persistent airway inflammation is a well-established hallmark of chronic lower respiratory diseases [36], and epithelial cell injury and abnormal repair are increasingly recognized as central mechanisms in disease initiation and progression [37]. Together, these findings suggest that early smoking may exacerbate chronic respiratory disease risk by amplifying inflammatory responses and impairing epithelial homeostasis.

Beyond biological signaling pathways, our study provides novel evidence that early smoking is associated with altered internal exposure to chemical pollutants, a dimension that has received limited attention in previous research. We observed significantly higher levels of lead, cadmium, and perfluoroheptanoic acid among early smokers compared with late smokers and never smokers. Lead and cadmium are well-established components of cigarette smoke [38, 39] and have been consistently shown to accumulate at higher concentrations in smokers than in non-smokers [40]. Inhaled cadmium, in particular, may exacerbate lung injury given that the respiratory tract serves as the first line of defense against toxic substances [41], and elevated cadmium and lead levels have been linked to increased COPD risk [42]. Mechanistic studies suggest that these metals may promote lung dysfunction through oxidative stress and immune dysregulation, including impairment of antioxidant pathways such as NOX4/Nrf2/GCLC/GST signaling [43].

A key finding of our mediation analyses was that cadmium partially mediated the association between early smoking and both COPD risk and respiratory disease–related mortality. One plausible explanation is that the alveolar barrier is not fully developed during the alveolar development stage, rendering the lung more permeable to inhaled toxicants [44]. Following completion of alveolar maturation, enhanced barrier integrity may reduce systemic absorption of cadmium and related pollutants. Supporting this hypothesis, integrated network toxicology and proteomic analyses identified 34 shared targets linking cadmium exposure to chronic lower respiratory diseases, with enrichment in pathways related to epithelial cell proliferation, development, and apoptosis. Molecular docking further demonstrated strong interactions between cadmium and epithelial-related targets such as NOTCH1, HMOX1, and EGFR, lending additional mechanistic support to our proposed pathway.

Taken together, these findings suggest a biologically coherent model in which early smoking increases cadmium exposure during a vulnerable developmental window, thereby partially disrupting epithelial cell function and promoting inflammatory processes that contribute to the development and progression of chronic lower respiratory diseases.

Conclusion

Smoking initiation during the alveolar development stage is associated with a broad spectrum of adverse health outcomes, including increased risks of COPD, chronic bronchitis, and interstitial pulmonary diseases, accelerated lung function decline, and higher all-cause and respiratory disease–related mortality. Our findings suggest that these effects may be partially mediated by enhanced cadmium exposure and subsequent disruption of epithelial cell proliferation, development, and survival. These results underscore the critical importance of preventing smoking initiation during early life. Coordinated efforts involving families, educational systems, healthcare providers, and policymakers are essential to reduce youth smoking, alleviate the burden of chronic lower respiratory diseases, and improve long-term population health.
