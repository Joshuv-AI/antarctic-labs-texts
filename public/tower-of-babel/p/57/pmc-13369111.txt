Untargeted metabolomics reveals distinct metabolic profiles in MUT-type methylmalonic acidemia
Authors: Shuruq Alsuhaymi, Reem H AlMalki, Maha Al Mogren, Ahamd Alodaib, Abdul-Hamid Emwas, Majed Dasouki, Ahmad Alfares, Mariusz Jaremko, Anas M Abdel Rahman
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundMethylmalonic acidemia (MMA), the most prevalent type of inherited metabolic disorder, is inherited in an autosomal recessive pattern due to MUT gene mutations that impair methylmalonyl-CoA mutase (MCM) enzyme activity. These mutations lead to the toxic accumulation of methylmalonic acid, which causes mitochondrial dysfunction, metabolic disruptions, and multisystem damage. Newborn screening followed by confirmatory biochemical and genetic tests, such as acylcarnitine analysis and urine organic acid profiling, are widely accepted and routinely used in biochemical genetics laboratories. However, these conventional methods have limited ability to detect novel, clinically relevant biomarkers that may offer deeper insights into the pathophysiology of MMA. This study highlights the importance of untargeted metabolomics to identify candidate biomarkers that could potentially predict long-term prognosis or represent novel therapeutic targets.MethodsLC-HRMS was used to analyze serum samples from patients with genetically confirmed MUT-type methylmalonic acidemia (n = 27) and healthy controls (n = 27). Differential metabolite and pathway analyses were performed to identify metabolic alterations associated with MMA.ResultsA total of 267 dysregulated metabolites were identified in patients with MMA, including 185 upregulated and 82 downregulated metabolites. Downregulated metabolites included glutamine, isoleucine, and deamido-NAD+, whereas upregulated metabolites included acylcarnitines, succinyladenosine, and leukotriene B4. These dysregulated metabolites were primarily associated with key altered pathways, including arachidonic acid metabolism, nicotinate and nicotinamide metabolism, sphingolipid metabolism, glutathione metabolism, and purine metabolism. Notably, potential biomarkers such as 11,12-epoxyeicosatrienoic acid (AUC = 0.964) and MG (PGF2alpha/0:0/0:0) (AUC = 0.953) are implicated in pathophysiological mechanisms of MMA and its standard treatment through their association with inflammation, oxidative stress, and altered fatty acid metabolism.ConclusionsThese findings identify candidate disease-associated metabolites and provide insight into the metabolic disturbances associated with MMA. However, these results should be considered exploratory and require validation in larger, independent, age-matched, and longitudinal cohorts before clinical application. Introduction

Inborn errors of metabolism (IEMs) are rare genetic disorders caused by defects in specific enzymes, cofactors, or transport proteins. Over 1,400 IEMs have been identified, with the remaining untreatable and their underlying mechanisms yet to be fully elucidated [1–4]. The incidence of IEMs exhibits significant regional variability, often influenced by high consanguinity rates. For example, in Saudi Arabia, where consanguinity rates range from 50% to 80%, the incidence of IEMs is significantly higher than global averages, with some studies estimating it to be as high as 1 in 569 live births [5].

Among the many identified IEMs, methylmalonic acidemia (MMA) represents one of the most common organic acidemias [6]. Methylmalonic acidemia (MMA) is a rare inherited metabolic disorder caused by defects in the methylmalonyl-CoA metabolic pathway, resulting in the accumulation of methylmalonic acid and related toxic metabolites in blood and tissues. This toxic accumulation arises from enzymatic deficiencies, typically inherited in an autosomal recessive (AR) pattern. The genetic basis of MMA is primarily associated with deficiencies in the enzyme methylmalonyl-CoA mutase (MCM) or impairments in the metabolism of its cofactor, adenosyl-cobalamin (a bioactive form of vitamin B12). MCM, encoded by the MUT gene, plays an important role in the catabolism of certain amino acids (valine, isoleucine, methionine, and threonine) and odd-chain fatty acids by catalyzing the conversion of methylmalonyl-CoA to succinyl-CoA, a key intermediate in the tricarboxylic acid (TCA) cycle [7]. Mutations in the MUT gene, such as deletions, insertions, or missense, can result in MCM deficiency or dysfunction, disrupting this conversion and leading to the toxic accumulation of methylmalonic acid. These genetic mutations account for approximately 60% of MMA cases [8]. Moreover, MUT gene defects can affect mitochondrial function, reduce ATP generation, and increase oxidative stress. These mitochondrial dysfunctions contribute to long-term complications, including chronic kidney disease, neurodevelopmental delays, cardiomyopathy, pancreatitis, and bone marrow suppression [9–11].

The MMA clinical manifestations, mainly in early infancy, range widely from moderate to severe and life-threatening, depending on the type and degree of enzyme deficiency. Common symptoms in affected infants include recurrent vomiting, dehydration, reduced muscle tone (hypotonia), excessive fatigue (lethargy), and poor growth or failure to thrive. Moreover, metabolic acidosis, marked by an excess of acid in the blood, is a defining feature of MMA and, if untreated, can cause serious health complications, including acute metabolic decompensations (AMD) that may lead to coma or death. The underlying pathophysiology of AMD in isolated MMA remains incompletely understood. However, recent studies of MMA patient metabolomes suggest that metabolic acidosis during AMD is driven by the accumulation of acidic intermediates of branched-chain amino acid (BCAA) metabolism rather than methylmalonic acid levels. These findings emphasize the complexity of MMA and the need for further study to elucidate its acute and chronic mechanisms [12–15].

Diagnosing MMA can be challenging due to its nonspecific clinical presentation and biochemical overlap with other inborn errors of metabolism. Although newborn screening (NBS) using tandem mass spectrometry (MS/MS) enables early detection by measuring more than 40 metabolites in dried blood spots, including propionylcarnitine (C3), the C3/acetylcarnitine (C2) ratio, and methionine, metabolite-based screening may be less reliable in clinically unstable or stressed newborns [16]. Confirmatory testing for MMA typically involves urine organic acid analysis by GC-MS to detect elevated methylmalonic acid, 3-hydroxypropionate, and 2-methylcitrate, followed by molecular genetic testing to identify causative mutations such as those in MUT, with regional genetic variation affecting clinical presentation [7, 17–19].

Emerging technologies like untargeted metabolomics offer a valuable complement to targeted metabolic assays by providing a comprehensive perspective of metabolic pathways. This approach could enhance our understanding of MMA and other IEMs, potentially leading to new therapeutic strategies. Untargeted metabolomics extends the investigation range and provides a deeper insight into disorder mechanisms and disrupted pathways [16, 20]. However, challenges remain regarding its clinical application, particularly in cost, speed, accuracy, and reproducibility. Studies in specific populations, such as Saudi patients, have determined metabolic biomarkers and pathways in IEM disorders. For instance, a study on medium-chain acyl-CoA dehydrogenase deficiency (MCADD) disorder revealed disruptions in phenylalanine, tyrosine, and tryptophan biosynthesis pathways, with potential biomarkers such as PGP (a21:0/PG/F1alpha) and glutathione, demonstrating the importance of metabolomics in promoting our understanding of IEMs [21].

Despite advances in understanding MMA, further research is needed to enhance personalized disease management and patient outcomes. Accordingly, this study aims to conduct metabolic profiling of patients with MUT-confirmed MMA to identify candidate, clinically relevant biomarkers and provide deeper insights into the disease’s pathophysiology. This study demonstrates the value of untargeted metabolomics in identifying these candidate biomarkers and disrupted metabolic pathways, with potential applications in predicting long-term prognosis and suggesting novel therapeutic strategies. It also examines the impact of genetic and regional variations on disease expression.

Results

Sample demographic and clinical data

A total of 54 serum samples were analyzed, including 27 from patients with MMA and 27 from healthy controls (HCs). All patient samples were confirmed to carry MUT gene mutations through the standard clinical whole-genome analysis protocol. The HC group was selected to approximate the patient group in sex distribution; however, age differences between the groups were present. Female representation was similar between the MMA and control groups (51.85% vs. 44.44%; Fisher’s exact test, p = 0.79).

The demographic characteristics of the cohort are summarized in Table 1. The average age of the MMA group was 15.63 ± 1.49 years, while the healthy controls had an average age of 26.85 ± 0.91 years. Patients with MMA exhibited significantly elevated serum methylmalonic acid levels (744.00 ± 141.02 µmol/L), the primary biochemical marker of MMA.

It is worth noting that all study patients were on the MMA standard dietary plan and received L-carnitine, Vitamin B12, and hydroxocobalamin. Renal conditions were reported in many patients and were excluded before conducting our study to reduce confounding in this small cohort. Among patients with available renal function data (n = 4), estimated glomerular filtration rate (eGFR) values ranged from ≤ 15 to 59 mL/min/1.73 m², indicating varying degrees of potential renal impairment. These patients were excluded from this cohort due to the unavailability of similar data for the other participants, to reduce coverability in the metabolomic analyses.

Untargeted LC-MS metabolomics profiling in patients with MMA

The detected compound ions were 29,685 in both positive (n = 16,623) and negative (n = 13,062) ionization modes, respectively, and archived on the Metabolomics Workbench (ST003690). A total of 26,945 ion features remained after excluding age-dependent metabolites (n = 2194 ion features) using a moderated t-test, p ≤ 0.05, FC 2 (Figure S1). The dataset was normalized using median, log-transformation, and Pareto scaling to reduce major variances and verify the normal distribution.

In addition, unsupervised principal component analysis (PCA) showed partial separation between MMA and healthy control samples, with some overlap. The PCA showed partial separation between MMA and healthy control samples, with some overlap. The first two principal components explained 37.9% of the total variance (PC1 = 23%, PC2 = 14.9%) (Figure S2).

A partial least squares discriminant analysis (PLS-DA), a supervised tool, demonstrated a distinct clustering of samples and clear differentiation between MMA patients (green) and healthy controls (red) (Fig. 1A), and quality control QC (blue) (Figure S3). Furthermore, orthogonal partial least squares discriminant analysis (OPLS-DA) confirmed the significant separation between the groups, and the robustness of the created models was evaluated using 100-permutation test, yielding a model fitness (R2Y = 0.977) and predictive ability (Q2 = 0.814) indicating excellent model performance (Fig. 1B).

Random Forest classification was additionally performed to evaluate sample classification performance. The model showed an out-of-bag (OOB) error rate of 3.85%, with all healthy control samples correctly classified (27/27) and 92% of MMA samples correctly classified (23/25) (Figure S4).

Then, a frequency filter was applied to the dataset (n = 26,945 ions) using an 80% cutoff across all samples to remove features with excessive missing values. As a result, 13,608 features remained for further analysis. These features were statistically evaluated in patients with MMA and healthy controls (Ctrl) using a volcano plot (moderated t-test; FDR p ≤ 0.05; fold change ≥ 2). A total of 692 features were significantly dysregulated, with 431 upregulated and 261 downregulated in the MMA group compared to controls (Fig. 2). Among these, 536 features were annotated using the Human Metabolome Database (HMDB), Metlin MS/MS, LipidBlast, lipidMap, and KEGG. After excluding exogenous compounds, including dietary components, environmental contaminants, and drug-related metabolites, 267 features were classified as human endogenous metabolites (Table S1). Among these, 17 metabolites were identified using the library we built from standard materials (Table S1). These annotated metabolites were then used to investigate affected pathways and interpret their relevance to the pathophysiology of MMA.

Age-adjusted metabolite analysis

To assess whether age-related variation influenced the observed metabolomic alterations, an analysis of covariance (ANCOVA) was performed using age as a covariate. Following age adjustment, 219 of the 267 metabolites remained significantly associated with methylmalonic acidemia (MMA), as detailed in Table S2. Furthermore, a forest plot (Figure S5) displays the top 20 age-adjusted coefficients at 95% confidence intervals, including pyroglutamic acid, succinyladenosine, gamma-glutamylglycine, gamma-glutamylvaline, N2-methylguanine, glutamylisoleucine, and 11,12-epoxyeicosatrienoic acid.

To further evaluate the robustness of the age-adjusted findings, additional sensitivity analyses were performed using spline-based ANCOVA to account for potential non-linear age effects and by repeating the analyses using only samples within the overlapping age range between MMA patients and healthy controls. The principal discriminatory metabolites showed largely consistent effect sizes and directions across these analytical approaches (Figure S6; Table S3). In addition, Spearman correlation analyses between methylmalonic acid abundance and individual metabolites (n = 267) within the MMA cohort, providing complementary evidence that these metabolic alterations (n = 131, Table S4 and illustrated in Figure S7) that directly associated with disease severity based on the elevation of the MMA. Nevertheless, because of the limited age overlap between the study groups, residual age-related confounding cannot be completely excluded.

In addition, a heatmap analysis using Pearson distance measures displayed the dysregulation of 267 endogenous metabolites, including 185 upregulated and 82 downregulated in patients with MMA. Red and green color intensities represented the relative increase and decrease in metabolite regulation, respectively (Fig. 3).

Metabolic pathway analysis

We also investigated the most affected metabolic pathways in the patients with MMA based on the dysregulated endogenous metabolites (n = 267) using MetaboAnalyst software and the KEGG pathway library. Arachidonic acid metabolism, purine metabolism, and nicotinate and nicotinamide metabolism are examples of the most affected pathways in the MMA group (Fig. 4). Other pathways were also affected, including glutathione metabolism, sphingolipid metabolism, and steroid hormone biosynthesis (Fig. 4 and Table S5).

Biomarker analysis

Lastly, we performed receiver operating characteristic (ROC) curve analysis of the significantly dysregulated metabolites (n = 267) to identify potential biomarkers that may differentiate MMA samples from healthy samples. A PLS-DA method was applied to create a multivariate exploratory ROC analysis to classify and rank features (Fig. 5A). The ROC curve features identified using PLS-DA and cross-validation (CV) exhibited area under the curve (AUC) values ranging from 0.95 to 0.996, with confidence intervals (CI) of 0.818-1 and 0.975-1, respectively (Fig. 5A). A frequency plot was created to display the 15 metabolites with the highest VIP scores in the OPLS-DA model, based on their levels in the MMA and control groups (Fig. 5B). For example, the upregulated endogenous metabolites in MMA compared to the control group included 11,12-epoxyeicosatrienoic acid, 11-Hydroperoxydocosahexaenoic acid, and prostaglandin A1, and the downregulated metabolites involved 5-hydroxydec-7-enedioylcarnitine, UDP-N-acetylmuraminate, and N-arachidonoyl tryptophan (Fig. 5B). The AUC of the ROC curve for the upregulated biomarker leukotriene B4 in the MMA patient group was 0.936 (Fig. 5C). For the downregulated biomarker, MG (PGF2alpha/0:0/0:0) was 0.953 (Fig. 5D). These AUC values (> 0.9) indicate that the identified metabolites are strong candidate biomarkers for distinguishing patients with MMA from healthy controls.

Discussion

Methylmalonic acidemia (MMA) is the most prevalent inherited metabolic disorder caused by mutations in the MUT gene that impair methylmalonyl-CoA mutase (MCM) enzyme activity. Most laboratories report a reference interval for methylmalonic acid of ≤ 0.40 µmol/L [22]. Clinically, individuals with classical isolated MMA often experience failure to thrive, vomiting, neurological problems, metabolic acidosis, and hyperammonemia. Although extensive research has been conducted on MUT dysfunction, the metabolic disruptions and underlying pathological mechanisms of MMA remain poorly understood, and no curative treatments are available [23, 24]. MMA can arise from genetic and non-genetic causes. Genetic causes include pathogenic variants in MMUT, MMAA, MMAB, and cobalamin-related genes (LMBRD1, ABCD4, MTRR), which contribute to MMA accumulation and further complicate the disease’s clinical and biochemical heterogeneity [25, 26]. Non-genetic causes include maternal vitamin B12 deficiency, which can result in transient elevation of MMA in neonates [27].

The chronic accumulation has also been associated with secondary complications, including chronic kidney disease and neurological impairments [28]. Therefore, this study investigated the metabolic profiles of patients with MMA carrying MUT mutations to identify candidate biomarkers and altered pathways that may improve our understanding of MMA pathophysiology and support future diagnostic applications.

This study’s untargeted metabolomics analysis of serum samples revealed distinct metabolic profiles in patients with MMA compared to healthy controls, reflecting the extensive metabolic perturbations characteristic of this disorder.

A wide range of dysregulated metabolites, including amino acids, lipid species, acylcarnitine, and oxidative stress-related metabolites, was identified. These alterations are consistent with disruptions in amino acid and lipid metabolism and suggest contributions from inflammation, oxidative stress, and mitochondrial dysfunction to the pathophysiology of MMA.

Notably, methylmalonic acid was identified exclusively in the patient group, supporting its role as a characteristic confirmatory biomarker in this MUT-confirmed cohort. This finding highlights the value of untargeted metabolomics in capturing the expected biochemical signature of MMA and suggests that broader metabolite profiling may provide complementary information alongside conventional biochemical testing and confirmatory diagnostic approaches.

A significant reduction in several amino acids was observed in the MMA group, including L-isoleucine, a branched-chain amino acid (BCAA), and L-glutamine, a non-BCAA amino acid involved in anaplerotic replenishment of the TCA cycle. Reduced BCAA levels in patients with MMA most likely reflect chronic protein-restricted dietary management and MMA-specific medical formula intake, although secondary mitochondrial dysfunction may also contribute to altered amino acid availability and utilization in MMA [29, 30].

Reduced glutamine levels, in particular, may reflect deficient anaplerosis of the TCA cycle, as reported in related organic acidemias such as propionic acidemia [31]. In this context, low glutamine availability may further compromise energy metabolism and biosynthetic capacity, with potential downstream effects on NAD+ biosynthesis and glutathione metabolism, as discussed below. Additionally, reduced levels of N-arachidonoyl phenylalanine and N-arachidonoyl tryptophan were detected in the MMA group. These metabolites are N-acyl amino acid conjugates of arachidonic acid and belong to a broader class of endocannabinoid-like lipid mediators involved in lipid signaling and inflammatory regulation. Their reduction may reflect disrupted arachidonic acid-related lipid signaling and broader alterations in lipid–amino acid metabolism in MMA [32].

Nicotinic acid adenine dinucleotide (NAAD; deamido-NAD+), an intermediate in NAD+ biosynthesis, was significantly reduced in patients with MMA compared with healthy controls. NAAD is converted to NAD+ by glutamine-dependent NAD+ synthetase, directly linking glutamine availability to NAD+ biosynthesis [33, 34]. Given the reduced glutamine levels observed in the MMA group, lower NAAD may reflect impaired glutamine-dependent NAD+ synthesis rather than a primary defect in NAD⁺ metabolism. Because NAD+ is essential for cellular redox homeostasis and mitochondrial energy metabolism, reduced NAAD may indicate a broader disruption of these processes in MMA [35–39].

One of the most important findings of this study was that glutathione metabolism remained significantly disrupted after adjustment for age, supporting oxidative stress and redox imbalance as candidate disease-associated metabolites of MMA.

The disruption of glutathione metabolism in patients with MMA. Glutathione plays a central role in redox homeostasis and antioxidant defense, and its dysregulation has important implications for MMA pathophysiology. Previous studies have shown altered thiol status and reduced glutathione availability in patients with organic acidemias, including MMA and PA [40–44], alongside evidence of elevated reactive oxygen species and impaired antioxidant capacity [45, 46]. These findings support oxidative stress as an important contributor to secondary mitochondrial dysfunction in MMA.

Glutathione synthesis requires adequate availability of its direct amino acid precursors: glutamate, cysteine, and glycine. In our cohort, reduced glutamine levels may indirectly affect glutathione synthesis by limiting the availability of glutamate. Although methionine was not identified in the present dataset, it is a propiogenic essential amino acid commonly restricted in MMA dietary management and is relevant to sulfur amino acid and thiol metabolism [47]. Hence, the observed reduction in glutamine, together with the broader nutritional context of MMA, may contribute to impaired glutathione homeostasis and antioxidant capacity. Consistent with this interpretation, after accounting for the age correction, reduced S-formylglutathione levels were observed in the MMA group, suggesting altered glutathione-dependent aldehyde detoxification and a reduced capacity to neutralize reactive aldehydes, potentially contributing to oxidative damage, redox imbalance, and mitochondrial stress.

Conversely, elevated pyroglutamic acid, a byproduct of glutathione metabolism, was observed in the MMA group, reflecting increased glutathione turnover and impaired recycling under oxidative stress conditions [48], further supporting the profound redox imbalance in MMA. Together, these results reveal glutathione metabolism as a critically disrupted pathway in MMA, warranting further investigation into its potential as a therapeutic target.

In addition, we observed a significant elevation of succinyladenosine in the MMA group following age adjustment, suggesting altered purine metabolism and mitochondrial energy homeostasis in MMA. Succinyladenosine is a byproduct of adenylosuccinate (S-AMP) conversion to adenosine monophosphate (AMP) within the purine nucleotide cycle. Succinyladenosine accumulation has been reported in inborn errors of metabolism that disrupt purine metabolism and mitochondrial function, such as adenylosuccinate lyase (ADSL) deficiency and fumarase deficiency [49, 50], and its consistent upregulation in MMA may reflect secondary effects on nucleotide metabolism or mitochondrial energy balance, representing an underexplored aspect of MMA pathophysiology.

Lipid dysregulation represented a notable component of the metabolic profile in the MMA cohort. Because all patients followed a protein-restricted diet with MMA-specific medical formula, some lipid alterations could reflect dietary treatment rather than the primary disorder. Among the detected lipid mediators, a cytochrome P450-derived eicosanoid, was significantly upregulated. Eicosanoids are bioactive lipids derived from arachidonic acid and play key roles in inflammatory regulation and vascular responses. EETs are known to increase under oxidative stress conditions and have been associated with anti-inflammatory activity [51–53]. The chronic inflammatory and oxidative stress state in MMA may shift arachidonic acid metabolism toward the cytochrome P450-derived EET pathway, contributing to the observed elevation. In contrast, MG (PGF2α/0:0/0:0), an oxidized monoacylglycerol derived from prostaglandin F2α involved in apoptosis, inflammation, and membrane remodeling, was significantly downregulated, suggesting altered glycerolipid metabolism and impaired membrane-associated processes in MMA. signaling integrity. Its reduction may indicate altered glycerolipid metabolism or impaired membrane-associated processes in MMA [54].

Several acylcarnitine species, including undec-8-enoylcarnitine, tridec-6-enoylcarnitine, and octadecenoylcarnitine, were elevated in the MMA group compared to healthy controls (as expected). Patients with MMA routinely receive L-carnitine supplementation to promote the removal of accumulated acyl groups and to support acyl-CoA/CoA balance [55, 56]. Accordingly, part of the elevated acylcarnitines is likely attributable to the effect of L-carnitine supplementation, which increases circulating acylcarnitine species. Even so, elevated medium- and long-chain acylcarnitines have been documented in metabolic conditions involving impaired mitochondrial β-oxidation, including medium-chain acyl-CoA dehydrogenase (MCAD) deficiency and other fatty acid oxidation defects, and have also been reported in cellular models of MMA [57, 58]. Consistently, the elevated serum acylcarnitine levels in our MMA cohort, particularly in patients with MUT mutations, may reflect a combination of treatment-related acylcarnitine export and underlying disturbances in fatty acid oxidation and mitochondrial homeostasis.

Furthermore, elevated levels of neuromedin N, fructose-6-phosphate, and estrone glucuronide in MMA also suggest that mitochondrial dysfunction can disrupt neuroendocrine, glycolysis, and steroid metabolism [59, 60], supporting the systemic nature of this disorder.

This study identified several significantly altered metabolic pathways in MMA. Arachidonic acid metabolism was most prominently affected, with upregulation of prostaglandins and leukotrienes suggesting active inflammatory signaling in the pathophysiology of MMA [61]. In addition to leukotriene B4, several other metabolites related to the arachidonic acid cascade were detected in the MMA cohort, including HETE-related metabolites such as 5-HETE, 5-oxo-12-HETE, and 17-HETE; leukotriene-related metabolites including leukotriene A4, 20-oxo-leukotriene E4, 20-dihydroxyleukotriene B4, and 20-trihydroxy-leukotriene B4; and multiple prostaglandins, including prostaglandin A1, prostaglandin E2, prostaglandin G1, prostaglandin G2, and prostaglandin H1. Collectively, these findings suggest broader perturbation of arachidonic acid metabolism and eicosanoid signaling in MMA, supporting the involvement of inflammatory and oxidative stress-related mechanisms in disease pathophysiology.

Glutathione metabolism was also significantly disrupted, as reflected by elevated pyroglutamic acid and glutamyl-amino acids alongside reduced glutamine, consistent with impaired antioxidant defense and redox imbalance. Additionally, the nicotinate and nicotinamide metabolism pathway was affected, evidenced by reduced NAAD levels, most likely as a downstream consequence of glutamine deficiency and deficient anaplerosis of the TCA cycle [35, 62].

This study patients received standard clinical management, including dietary protein restriction, specialized medical formula, L-carnitine supplementation, and vitamin B12/hydroxocobalamin therapy, the individual contributions of treatment and disease to the observed metabolic alterations cannot be completely distinguished. Therefore, some dysregulated metabolites, particularly those involved in amino acid, lipid, and acylcarnitine metabolism, may reflect a combination of disease-related metabolic disturbances and therapeutic interventions. Similarly, although age-adjusted analyses and additional sensitivity analyses yielded largely consistent findings, the limited age overlap between the MMA and healthy control groups means that residual age-related confounding cannot be entirely excluded. Consequently, the reported metabolites should be interpreted as candidate disease-associated metabolites that provide insight into MMA pathophysiology and generate hypotheses for future investigation rather than definitive disease-specific biomarkers.

In sum, this exploratory study provides additional insight into the metabolic consequences of MUT deficiency by identifying alterations in amino acid, lipid, acylcarnitine, redox, and energy metabolism in a Saudi cohort with genetically confirmed MMA. Nevertheless, several limitations should be considered. In particular, the potential confounding effects of protein-restricted dietary treatment and medical formula intake on the observed metabolomic profiles should be carefully addressed in future studies, in which enrolling newly diagnosed individuals before starting the formula, followed by a series of samples, should help mitigate this limitation. Renal dysfunction, or reduced kidney function, is a common complication of MMA and may alter circulating metabolite profiles, particularly in pathways involved in arachidonic acid metabolism, redox homeostasis, mitochondrial energy metabolism, and NAD+ homeostasis. Because detailed renal function data were not available for inclusion within the approved dataset, the contribution of renal involvement to the observed metabolic signatures could not be fully assessed.

Finally, because this retrospective study used samples collected over multiple years, the effects of long-term storage and pre-analytical variability cannot be entirely excluded, particularly for labile lipid mediators such as eicosanoids, despite standardized processing and storage at -80 °C.

Future prospective studies with well-designed, larger independent, age matched cohorts including additional Saudi and regional MMA cohorts, detailed clinical phenotyping, renal function assessment, and standardized sample handling, are needed. Integration of metabolomics with genomic and proteomic approaches may further refine the understanding of MMA mechanisms and support the development of targeted therapeutic strategies.

Materials and methods

Ethical statement

This study was conducted in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki. The Institutional Review Boards at King Faisal Specialist Hospital and Research Center (KFSHRC) in Riyadh, Saudi Arabia, reviewed and approved the procedures for this study (RAC No. 2160 027). Consent was waived for leftover samples submitted for routine clinical testing.

Sample information and collection

Fifty-four serum samples were obtained from the Metabolomics Lab at the Genomic Medicine Center of Excellence at KFSHRC. The cohort included twenty-seven samples from patients confirmed to have MUT-deficiency MMA, along with twenty-seven healthy controls.

Serum samples were collected between 2019 and 2023 during routine clinic visits. Samples were obtained in a non-fasting state during periods of metabolic stability. Patients with MMA were managed according to standard clinical care, including dietary protein restriction, MMA-specific metabolic formula where documented, and routine metabolic therapy such as L-carnitine supplementation. Additional adjunctive therapies, including sodium bicarbonate, vitamin B12, or vitamin D, were used in some patients as clinically indicated.

All study samples were handled using our standardized lab procedure, processing, and storage workflow to minimize pre-analytical variability. In details, serum separated, aliquoted and stored at -80 °C within 1 h of venipuncture. A sample aliquot with the same cycle were thawed immediately at RT before metabolite extraction and analysis.

Metabolites extraction

The chemicals used for LC-MS, including methanol, acetonitrile, deionized water (dH2O), and formic acid, were sourced from Fisher Scientific Company (Ottawa, ON, Canada). Serum metabolites were extracted from 50 µL of samples collected from patients with MMA and healthy controls using a 50% acetonitrile-methanol mixture. Samples were prepared randomly and processed simultaneously to reduce variability. Quality control (QC) samples were prepared by aliquoting from each sample to ensure data reliability. The samples were vortexed in a ThermoMixer (Eppendorf, Hamburg, Germany) at 600 rpm and 25 °C for one hour. After vortexing, they were centrifuged at 16,000 rpm and 4 °C for 10 min. The resulting supernatants were transferred to new Eppendorf tubes and dried using a SpeedVac concentrator (Thermo Fisher, Christ, Germany) for the LC-MS [63].

LC-MS untargeted metabolomics analysis

For LC-MS sample analysis, the dried extracted samples were reconstituted in a mobile phase containing 50% solution (A: 0.1% formic acid in dH2O and (B: 0.1% formic acid in 1:1 (v/v) MeOH and ACN). In the beginning, 5µL of the reconstituted sample was injected into the LC column for polar metabolite separation using reversed-phase liquid chromatography on an ACQUITY UPLC XSelect column (100 × 2.1 mm × 2.5 μm, Waters Ltd., Elstree, UK). The mobile phase flowed at 300µL/min while maintaining the column temperature at 55 °C and the sample temperature at 4 °C. The gradient mode used to pump mobile phases A and B into the column is as follows: 0–16 min 95 − 5% A, 16–19 min 5% A, 19–20 min 5–95% A, and 20–22 min 5–95% A. The LC eluates were ionized by electrospray ionization (ESI) in both positive and negative modes and separated by m/z using an Xevo G2-S QTOF mass spectrometer (Waters Ltd., Elstree, UK). The MS source temperature was fixed at 150 °C, the desolvation temperature was set at 500 ◦C, and the capillary voltages were kept at 3.20 kV or 3 kV for ESI+ and ESI− modes, respectively. The cone gas flow was 50 L/h, the desolvation gas flow was 800 L/h, and the cone voltage was 40 V. The collision energies for the low and high functions were set to off and 10–50 V, respectively, in the MSE data-independent acquisition (DIA) mode. As recommended by the vendor, the mass spectrometer was calibrated with sodium formate (100–1200 Da) in both ionization modes. The lock spray mass compound, MS leucine-enkephaline (an external reference to the ion m/z 556.2771 in positive mode and 554.2615 in negative mode), was constantly injected, which is responsible for switching between the sample and the reference for every 45 and 60 s in both modes, scan time was 0.5 s, the flow rate was 10 µL/min, and collision energy was 4 V and 30 V for the cone, respectively. The DIA data were gathered in continuum mode with Masslynx™ V4.1 Software (Waters Inc., Milford, MA, USA).

QCs were introduced into the instrument at random to validate the system’s stability. After that, they were analyzed following the routine protocol. The acceptance criteria were to have all the QC samples separated from the other study groups, clustered together, and use their Relative standard deviations (RSD%) ≤ 30% [64].

Data processing and statistical analyses

The raw MS data were processed using Waters’ Progenesis QI v.3.0 software (Milford, MA, USA), including spectral alignment based on m/z and retention time, peak detection, and peak-quality filtering. In addition, features present in at least half of the samples were retained for further analysis. Age and sex were evaluated as potential covariates to assess their influence on serum metabolite distributions, and were excluded from the raw data during data processing. For age-related effects, we calculated the average age of the healthy control (HC) group and identified metabolites below the average age (BAA) or above the average age (UAA) threshold. We then analyzed these using a moderated t-test ([BAA] vs. [UAA], fold change ≥ 2.0, p ≤ 0.05). This assessment helped determine whether age-dependent trends could confound the primary MMA-control comparison, and the major discriminatory metabolites were not driven by age-related variation. To further evaluate the potential influence of age on metabolite differences between MMA patients and healthy controls, analysis of covariance (ANCOVA) was performed with disease status (MMA versus control) included as the fixed factor and age as a covariate. Prior to analysis, metabolite intensities were median normalized and log10 transformed. Adjusted group means with corresponding 95% confidence intervals (CIs) were estimated, and statistical significance was defined as a two-sided p < 0.05. The adjusted effect sizes and 95% CIs for the top 20 metabolites were visualized using forest plots. To assess the robustness of the findings, additional sensitivity analyses were performed using spline-based ANCOVA with cubic regression splines to accommodate potential non-linear relationships between age and metabolite abundance. The analyses were further repeated using only samples within the overlapping age range between the MMA and healthy control cohorts. Finally, to investigate disease-related metabolic alterations independently of comparisons with healthy controls, Spearman correlation analyses were performed within the MMA cohort between methylmalonic acid abundance and individual metabolite levels. All statistical analyses were performed using Python (version 3.11.0).

Metaboanalyst version 6.0 (McGill University, Montreal, QB, Canada) (http://www.metaboanalyst.ca, accessed on 3 August 2024) was used for multivariate statistical analyses [65]. The imported datasets underwent median normalization, log transformation, and Pareto scaling. It was utilized to perform the partial least squares discriminant analysis (PLS-DA) and orthogonal partial least squares discriminant analysis (OPLS-DA) models. Model fitness (R2Y) and predictive ability (Q2) were used to evaluate the OPLS-DA model [66, 67]. Pathway and biomarker analyses, MMA-linked biomarkers, and receiver operating characteristic (ROC) curves were generated utilizing the PLS-DA technique in Metaboanalyst software for broad analysis to identify potential biomarkers.

Mass Profiler Professional (MPP) software v15.0 (Agilent, Santa Clara, CA, USA) was employed for univariate analysis. A volcano plot was applied to determine the significant features with a moderated t-test, a fold-change (FC) cutoff of 2, and FDR p ≤ 0.05. A Venn diagram was also generated using MPP. At the same time, the heatmap analyses of altered features (in MMA patients compared to the controls) were conducted using Pearson distance measures based on the Pearson similarity test [68].

Metabolite identification

The statistically significant features from each dataset were selected and annotated using Progenesis QI v.3.0 software (Waters Technologies, Milford, MA, USA). For peak annotation, the precursor and product ions were annotated based on accurate mass, fragmentation pattern, and isotopic distributions utilizing databases such as the Human Metabolome Database (HMDB) with a mass error ≤ 5 ppm, METLIN MS/MS (www.metlin.scripps.edu), LipidMap, Lipid Blast, KEGG [69]. All metabolite identifications were assigned as putative (MSI Level 2), in accordance with the Metabolomics Standards Initiative (Sumner et al., 2007), as authentic standards were not analyzed under matched chromatographic and instrumental conditions. No MSI Level 1 identifications were reported. Exogenous compounds, including drugs, food additives, and environmental substances, were excluded from the results.

Conclusion

Our exploratory untargeted metabolomics analysis of serum samples from patients with MUT-confirmed MMA revealed distinct metabolic profiles compared with healthy controls, reflecting widespread biochemical disruption across multiple metabolic pathways. Significantly downregulated metabolites included glutamine, isoleucine, deamido-NAD+, and MG (PGF2α/0:0/0:0), while upregulated metabolites included acylcarnitines, succinyladenosine, and leukotriene B4. These alterations suggest contributions from impaired anaplerosis, inflammation, oxidative stress, and mitochondrial dysfunction to the pathophysiology of MMA. In addition, pathway analysis identified arachidonic acid, glutathione, and nicotinate/nicotinamide metabolism as among the most significantly affected pathways. Together, these findings expand our understanding of the metabolic consequences of MUT deficiency and identify candidate disease-associated metabolites that may contribute to our understanding of MMA pathophysiology. However, because of the retrospective study design and the potential influence of age differences, dietary management, standard treatment, and renal dysfunction, these findings should be considered hypothesis-generating rather than definitive. Validation in larger and longitudinal cohorts is required to determine their clinical utility. Future studies integrating metabolomics with genomic and proteomic approaches may further clarify disease mechanisms and support the development of more targeted therapeutic strategies for MMA.
