Childhood adversity, stress reactivity, and structural brain measures in stress‐related/neurodevelopmental disorders, and their comorbidity: A large transdiagnostic cross‐sectional study
Authors: Nelleke van der Weerd, Peter Mulders, Janna Vrijsen, Jasper van Oort, Rose Collard, Philip van Eijndhoven, Indira Tendolkar
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
AbstractChildhood adversity (CA), including childhood adverse life events, increases the risk for development of psychiatric disorders later in life. Both CA and psychiatric disorders are associated with structural brain changes and dysfunctional hypothalamic–pituitary–adrenal‐axis. However, many studies investigated single diagnostic and single regions of interest of the brain, and did not take stress reactivity into account. We investigated associations of CA and cortisol levels with gray matter volume and cortical thickness, in a whole‐brain manner. Primary analysis constituted of a transdiagnostic approach, followed by a moderation analysis to investigate the influence of diagnosis. Patients with stress‐related and/or neurodevelopmental disorders and matched healthy controls underwent an magnetic resonance imaging scan, next to assessing hair cortisol levels and CA/life events. CA was reported by 62–72% of the patients versus 33% of the controls. Primary transdiagnostic linear regression analyses revealed that CA was not associated with gray matter volume, while childhood life events were associated with lower right thalamic volume. Hair cortisol was not associated with any lobe volume. None of the associations were moderated by diagnosis. In conclusion, CA is a risk factor that needs to be taken into account when investigating psychiatric disorders. Yet the relationship with structural brain changes and stress reactivity is less clear than postulated on the basis of more seed‐based studies.Keywords: childhood adversity, neurodevelopmental disorders, stress reactivity, stress‐related disorders, structural brain development

INTRODUCTION

Childhood adversity (CA) can have severe impact on behavioral, emotional, physical, and cognitive functioning as it hits the developing brain (Carr et al., 2013). CA is defined as any form of physical, psychological or emotional abuse or neglect that results in actual or potential harm to a child (Teicher et al., 2003). Frequent occurrence of CA increases the risk for psychiatric disorders and their comorbidity (Hovens et al., 2010; Vrijsen et al., 2017). CA can be differentiated to threat and deprivation, where threat includes sexual violation, physical harm, and actual or threatened death, and deprivation includes institutionalization, poverty, and neglect. Furthermore, environmental experiences—that is, adverse childhood life events—are important as well for the developing brain, although less directly than other CA (McLaughlin et al., 2014). Further, different types of CA/life events (CA/life events diversity) seem to have some unique effects on structural brain development. Verbal abuse and sexual abuse specifically affect the auditory and somatosensory cortex, respectively (Teicher & Samson, 2016), whereas exposure to poverty/neglect affects neural functioning related to language abilities (McLaughlin et al., 2014).

CA events are thought to exert its impact by affecting the developing brain including changes in the hypothalamic–pituitary–adrenal (HPA)‐axis (Khoury et al., 2019). The challenge of the HPA‐axis may have larger impact on the modulation of stress reactivity later in life (Miller et al., 2007; Nelson et al., 2020). CA in the early development is thought to decrease stress reactivity later in life. Contrary, CA later in development likely increases stress reactivity (Koenig et al., 2011). Indeed stress reactivity, measured by hair cortisol level as a read‐out of the HPA‐axis, is lower when abuse occurred around the age of 3. However, hair cortisol levels have also been shown to differ across psychiatric disorders, thus complicating interpretation (Koumantarou Malisiova et al., 2021; Schalinski et al., 2019). Excessive activity of the HPA‐axis—and thus high cortisol levels—due to CA/life events, stimulates glucocorticoid receptors in the brain. Regions with high concentration of this receptor—including amygdala, hippocampus, and prefrontal cortex—are prone to structural and functional changes upon activation by cortisol (McLaughlin et al., 2014). However, the influence on stress reactivity resulting from CA is often not considered when investigating neural correlates of CA, generally only the presence of CA itself.

Several studies suggest that adults with a history of CA have lower volumes in gray matter regions relevant for emotion regulation, such as the amygdala, hippocampus, and prefrontal cortex, compared with adults without a history of CA (Paquola et al., 2016; Pollok et al., 2022). Volume changes in these brain regions are also commonly seen when comparing psychiatric patients with controls (Liao et al., 2013; Vythilingam et al., 2002; Yang et al., 2017). A study investigating women with major depressive disorder showed smaller hippocampal volumes in those who experienced CA compared with those who did not experience CA and healthy controls (Vythilingam et al., 2002). Further, subjects with susceptibility for depression showed reduced hippocampal volumes related to CA (Frodl et al., 2010; Rao et al., 2010). Yang and colleagues found that CA severity/frequency, measured as childhood trauma questionnaire score, was associated with reduced volumes of the fronto‐limbic areas and cerebellum in patients with depression, but not in healthy controls (Yang et al., 2017). Another study by Liao and colleagues showed increased left thalamic volume to be associated with CA in patients with generalized anxiety disorder, and not in healthy controls (Liao et al., 2013). Also patients with other psychiatric disorders such as psychosis had lower hippocampal and amygdala volumes, which were correlated to CA (Aas et al., 2012; Armio et al., 2020; Hoy et al., 2012).

Although extensive research has been done, several aspects remain unclear. First, most studies investigated the association of CA in the brain in a single diagnostic group. However, as about 28% of psychiatric patients have two or more diagnoses, there might be shared underlying brain changes (Kessler et al., 2005). A transdiagnostic approach might provide a more clinically relevant insight in the effects of CA on structural brain changes and how they relate to stress reactivity across different psychiatric disorders. Second, although some brain structures are affected by CA across psychiatric disorders, it remains unclear if structural brain differences related to CA are similar across patients and controls and whether the differences in psychiatric patients are independent of diagnosis and comorbidity. Third, several studies and reviews on structural brain changes related to CA show a lack of studies investigating CA type specifically in relation to the structural brain changes (Paquola et al., 2016; Pollok et al., 2022). This also concerns severity and frequency of CA, and the inclusion of adverse childhood life events. Fourth, many of these studies did not investigate recent stress reactivity, as proxy for HPA‐axis disturbances. Finally, an important limitation of previous work has been the focus on predefined regions‐of‐interest. It has become increasingly clear that brain changes in psychiatry are rarely focal in nature. Besides the well‐known regions affected by CA, more structures are possibly affected by stress early in development (Teicher et al., 2002) so that a whole‐brain approach is warranted.

In light of the above‐mentioned findings, we investigated the association of CA/childhood life events as a potential shared contributing factor for structural brain changes in patients with stress‐related and/or neurodevelopmental psychiatric disorders. Given the large transdiagnostic cohort of this study, we add new insight to the current literature by investigating the main influence of CA and childhood life events with a whole‐brain approach to focus on global changes, as a complementary approach to ROI approaches applied in literature thus far. Therefore, we aimed to assess the association between CA/childhood life events and (sub)cortical gray matter volume and cortical thickness, in a whole‐brain manner. Our primary analysis involved a transdiagnostic approach, after which a secondary analysis was done to investigate any influence of diagnosis with a moderation analysis. We assessed this association for CA and childhood life events separately (as an indirect measure for adversity), across the psychopathological continuum and non‐psychiatric controls matched for age, gender, and level of education. Finally, we relate CA and structural brain changes to individual stress levels over the last 3 months as measured by hair cortisol to include the possible role of the HPA‐axis reactivity.

METHODS

Subjects

This study is part of the cross‐sectional MIND‐Set study (Measuring Integrated Novel Dimensions in Neurodevelopmental and Stress‐related Mental Disorders), conducted at the department of Psychiatry of the Radboud university medical center and the Donders Institute in Nijmegen, the Netherlands (van Oort et al., 2020). In the MIND‐Set study, outpatients with a stress‐related disorder (mood/anxiety disorder and/or addiction) and/or a neurodevelopmental disorder (ADHD/ASD) were included. All patients were ≥18 years old and had at least one of these diagnoses according to the diagnostic and statistical manual of mental disorders (DSM)‐5. Diagnoses were confirmed by DSM‐based semi‐structured interviews, conducted by trained clinicians. Exclusion criteria were: a current psychosis, sensorimotor handicaps, a full‐scale IQ estimate <70, inadequate command of the Dutch language, or being mentally incompetent to sign an informed consent. A control group was included, with participants without a current or past psychiatric disorder. Possible eligible individuals were approached via databases of the department's previous studies, advertisement in newspapers, social media, and websites, and via the research participation system of the Radboud University Faculty of Social Sciences (SonaSystem). The absence of lifetime psychiatric diagnoses is assessed via a telephone screening interview. Additional exclusion criteria for control participants were disorders of the central nervous system, malignancies or rare somatic disorders, use of ≥15 units of alcohol per week, use of benzodiazepine or drug once or more per week. For the magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) MIND‐Set session, additional exclusion criteria were: metal objects in the body, ferromagnetic implants, pacemaker, brain surgery in the past, epilepsy, claustrophobia, and pregnancy. The study was approved by the local medical ethical committee and participants gave written informed consent.

MRI acquisition and analysis

Imaging was performed with a 3.0 T MRI scanner (MAGNETOM Prisma, Siemens, Germany). High‐resolution structural T1‐weighted images were acquired using a MPRAGE sequence with sagittal T1 3D turbo field echo (voxel size 1 × 1 × 1 mm, echo time 8.5 ms, repetition time 3.9 ms, acquisition time 7 min).

FreeSurfer (version 6.0; Martinos Center for Biomedical Imaging, Harvard‐MIT, Boston, MA; http://surfer.nmr.mgh.harvard.edu/) was used for cortical reconstruction and volumetric segmentation. FreeSurfer produces reliable values for cortical and subcortical measurements corrected for motion and normalized for intensity through validated algorithms and automated segmentation. First, an initial surface is generated by tiling the outside of the white matter mass for each hemisphere. This initial surface is adjusted for intensity gradients between the gray and white matter (white surface) and for the gradient between the gray matter and the CSF (pial surface). The distance between the white and pial surface represents the cortical thickness at any chosen point chosen. Volumes are generated through registration with a stereotaxic coordinate system. Quality control consisted of visual inspection of the subcortical structures and cortical regions, in line with a protocol developed by the ENIGMA consortium (http://enigma.ini.usc.edu/protocols/imaging-protocols/). Total estimated intracranial volume was used to adjust for individual differences in brain sizes.

Cortisol samples

Scalp hair samples (5 mm thick) with a length of 3 cm were taken to measure cortisol levels. Then, 3 cm hair corresponds with HPA axis activity over the last 3 months. Cortisol was extracted from hair samples using methanol. Then, cortisol values were determined by LC–MS/MS (Staufenbiel et al., 2015).

Childhood adversity

A CA questionnaire (NEMESIS) was used to assess adverse life experiences before the age of 16 (De Graaf et al., 2004). Four types of CA were distinguished: (1) physical abuse, (2) psychological abuse, (3) sexual abuse, and (4) emotional neglect. For each type, a frequency score was given from 0 (not) to 5 (very often). Then, the outcome was divided into three frequency groups: (1) not, (2) once/sometimes, and (3) regular/often/very often, according to the method developed previously (Hovens et al., 2010). A CA index was calculated by taking the sum of the frequency scores of the four types of adverse events, as a continuous measure that represents both the diversity and frequency of adverse events (Hovens et al., 2010). Further, life events before the age of 16, reflecting a potential indirect source of adversity, were defined as (1) divorce of parents, (2) death of a parent or sibling, and (3) adoption. Similarly to CA, a diversity and frequency score was determined for these childhood life events.

Statistics

To investigate differences in presence of CA (yes/no) and childhood life events (yes/no) between groups (stress‐related disorders, neurodevelopmental disorders, comorbidity of stress‐related and neurodevelopmental disorders, and controls), we used Chi‐square tests. ANOVA was used to investigate differences in hair cortisol levels between the groups. In our initial transdiagnostic analysis, we used linear regression for total gray matter volume/thickness and CA and childhood life events (diversity and frequency score), adjusting for age, antidepressant use (yes/no), sex, and total intracranial volume. First, the association between gray matter volume/thickness and CA and childhood life events (diversity and frequency score) were examined per lobe. The significant lobes were then investigated per region in the corresponding lobe. All analyses were corrected for multiple testing using the Benjamini–Hochberg method, with a false discovery rate (FDR) of 5% (q < 0.05).

A moderation analysis was performed to investigate any influence of diagnosis (stress‐related/neurodevelopmental/comorbidity/controls as moderator) on the correlation of gray matter volume/thickness (dependent variable) and CA/childhood life events (independent variable). Here, the same adjustments were made as for linear regression, namely for age, antidepressant use (yes/no), sex, and—in case of volume measures—total intracranial volume. Mediation analysis was done to test for mediation of cortisol on any association of CA/childhood life events (independent variable) and gray matter volume (dependent variable). Both the moderation and mediation analysis were done using PROCESS v4.2 in SPSS. Statistical tests were performed in SPSS software, version 25.

CA DISTRIBUTION

More patients with stress‐related disorder(s) or a comorbidity reported a history of CA compared with control participants without any psychiatric disorder (p < .001) (Figure 1). Psychological abuse was reported by 56% of the patients with a stress‐related disorder (particularly anxiety disorder) in contrast to 42–49% by patients with other disorders (p < .05) and 26% in controls (p < .001). Frequency of psychological abuse however, was more often reported as “sometimes” than “regularly—often” by patients with anxiety disorder compared with the other disorders (48/52 vs. 36/64, p < 0.01). Sexual abuse was more often reported in patient with stress‐related disorders (19–22%) compared with control participants (8%, p < .01). Frequency of indirect adversity caused by childhood life events did not differ between any of the groups.

Although for patients with depression or addiction this difference was driven by those who reported psychological abuse to occur regular‐often (p = .012 and p = .033, respectively), this difference in patients with anxiety disorders was already present when psychological abuse occurred sometimes (p = .002). CA and life events

CA frequency/diversity was not related to gray matter volumes in any lobe (lowest p = .068) (Table 2). Life events frequency/diversity, however, were significantly related to reduced gray matter volumes in the subcortical lobe (β = −.088, p = .005, FDR corrected q = 0.035), corrected for age, gender, total intracranial volume, and medication use (Supplementary Table 2). Further analysis of the subcortical regions showed that the right thalamus volume was negatively associated with childhood life events (β = −.103, p = .005, FDR corrected q = 0.035). This association was not mediated by cortisol levels measured in hair. Volume of the left thalamus (β = −.098, p = .018) and right hippocampus (β = −.103, p = .020) were associated with childhood life events; however, these results did not survive FDR correction (Table 3). All other lobes and total gray matter volume were not association with childhood life events. Further, cortical thickness was not associated with CA or life events (both when corrected and uncorrected for TIV). We found no interaction effect of gender and CA and childhood life events (data not shown).

Hair cortisol and CA

Hair cortisol levels were not associated with CA in patients (β = −.114, p = .152) or controls (β = −.118, p = .379). However, hair cortisol showed a trend for a negative association with childhood life events in patients (β = −.163, p = .057), but not in controls (β = .085, p = .515).

Hair cortisol and brain volumes

When using a transdiagnostic approach, hair cortisol level was not related to any lobe volume (lowest p = .313) or cortical thickness per lobe (lowest p = .231) (Supplementary Table 3).

Moderation by diagnosis

The association of CA with cortical thickness were moderated by diagnosis in the temporal lobe (p = .039) and occipital lobe (p = .016) (Supplementary Table 4). For both lobes, post hoc analysis showed significant association (p = .002) with neurodevelopmental disorders (Supplementary Table 5). Similarly, diagnosis moderated the association of hair cortisol with temporal lobe volume (p = .017) and total gray matter volume (p = .046) (Supplementary Table 6). Post hoc analysis showed temporal lobe was moderated by stress‐related disorders (p = .005), and total gray matter moderation by stress related‐ and neurodevelopmental disorders (p = .056 and p = .066, respectively) (Supplemental Table 7). However, none of these survived FDR correction. None of the other correlations (CA/childhood life events/cortisol with gray matter volume/cortical thickness) were moderated by diagnosis.

DISCUSSION

We investigated CA and childhood life events as a potentially shared associated factor with structural brain changes in patients with stress‐related and developmental psychiatric disorders and their comorbidity. While we found no association between CA and gray matter volumes, adverse childhood life events were associated with lower right thalamus volume. CA, childhood life events, and hair cortisol levels were not associated with cortical thickness. Hair cortisol levels were not associated with gray matter volume or cortical thickness. Finally, we found no moderation of diagnosis on any of these associations. First, we will discuss the CA and childhood life events distribution in our cohort, then go further into the neural correlates in relation to CA and childhood life events.

CA distribution across diagnoses

In our study, about two‐thirds of patients and one third of controls reported a history of CA. CA is thus an important factor that needs to be taken into account when investigating psychiatric disorders and in relation to the comparison group.

While all patients with stress‐related disorders reported a history of psychological abuse more often than controls, it was reported most often by patients with an anxiety disorder. In line with two previous studies that showed that psychological abuse was related to social anxiety disorder, we found psychological abuse might indeed be related to anxiety disorders, although we did not look into social anxiety disorder specifically (Kuo et al., 2011; Lochner et al., 2010).

We found that patients with multiple stress‐related disorders reported more CA, mostly with emotional neglect and psychological abuse. Furthermore, we found that both CA and childhood life events were less often reported by patients with neurodevelopmental disorders compared with patients with stress‐related disorders, or patients with comorbidity of both. Possibly, patients with neurodevelopmental disorders have experienced less CA and adverse life events. Another possibility however, is a difference in bias for reporting CA and negative live events. Previous research by Vrijsen et al. has shown that patients with stress‐related disorders have a memory bias for negative information (Vrijsen et al., 2017). However, previous research showed adults report less adversity, retrospectively, compared with findings of prospectively reported adversity (Sheikh, 2018; Williams, 1994). This research suggests retrospective results are rather an underestimation than an overestimation of CA. Further, a recent study suggested a vulnerability‐stress‐coping model as an explanation for development of anxiety disorders (Schiele et al., 2020). Coping constitutes behavioral strategies to face stress of traumatic experience. An avoiding coping strategy, for example, is related to development of psychiatric disorders, while problem‐solving oriented coping styles are correlated with high quality of life. The vulnerability‐stress‐coping model builds on the vulnerability‐stress model where genetic vulnerability and stress are considered underlying cause of development of psychiatric disorders (Zubin & Spring, 1977). Using the vulnerability‐stress‐coping model, the authors showed how specific coping mechanisms in combination with CA underlies anxiety levels. We might be able to use this theoretical model, interaction of coping mechanism with CA, for a broader range of psychiatric disorders as coping has been shown to be important in development of psychiatric disorders (Santarnecchi et al., 2018). Differences in coping mechanisms might lead to CA having different effects. Further, this might explain why CA can be also evident in otherwise healthy controls who, according to this model, would have positive coping mechanisms eliminating the potential effect of CA.

CA and neural correlates

Previous studies showed reduced volumes of hippocampus, amygdala, and prefrontal cortex in different psychiatric diagnoses (Paquola et al., 2016; Pollok et al., 2022). However, in our transdiagnostic whole‐brain approach, we neither found a correlation between CA and these three regions, nor did we find any moderation of stress‐related disorders, neurodevelopmental disorders, or a comorbidity of the two types of disorders. However, previous studies did find some common structural differences across psychiatric disorders (Gong et al., 2019; Li et al., 2020). One study, being a meta‐analysis, had more power to find these commonalities compared to our study (Li et al., 2020). The other study finding common structural differences included other types of psychiatric disorders—that is, posttraumatic stress disorder, obsessive–compulsive disorder, and bipolar disorder—and is therefore not directly comparable (Gong et al., 2019). Of the three regions, only right hippocampal volume was negatively correlated to childhood live events, before FDR correction. Although being a small effect in our analysis, this is in line with a previous study that specifically showed lower hippocampal volume based on a ROI approach was associated with CA in MDD (Opel et al., 2014), and the ENIGMA study that showed a negative correlation for hippocampal volume across several diagnoses (Opel et al., 2020). Although we found no correlations for CA and regional brain volumes, we did find childhood life events were associated with lower right thalamic volume across psychiatric disorders. This effect was not mediated by cortisol, in line with our finding that childhood life events might be associated with lower hair cortisol levels. Perhaps, the impact of CA on brain structures is exerted indirectly, rather than being a direct result of cortisol levels. Following stress, cortisol stimulates inflammation (Leite Dantas et al., 2021). This inflammation response might be an indirect route inhibiting neurogenesis, therewith affecting brain development (Baune et al., 2012). Further, the previously mentioned vulnerability‐stress‐coping model might be another explanation for the impact of CA on brain structures. Although evidence is scarce, coping mechanisms have been associated with changes in regional structural brain volumes (Holz et al., 2016). Thus, differences of brain structures after CA might be due to an interaction of CA with coping mechanisms. Whether the effect of adversity on brain structures is related to a psychodynamic effect or is manifested through the stress hormone axis remains difficult to say. Timing of adversity is of essence to the effect it could have on the stress hormone axis (Teicher & Parigger, 2015). As we have no information on this aspect of adversity, it remains unclear which is more likely to be the case in our study.

Although we found no correlation between CA and neural correlates, we found childhood life event frequency/diversity to be negatively related to thalamic volume. This association was independent of sex, and was not moderated by diagnosis. Previous research showed reduced thalamic volume was related to childhood physical abuse in a voxel‐based MRI study (Duarte et al., 2016). Further, animal studies showed high stress levels were associated with reduced thalamic volume in a voxel‐based MRI study as well (Yoshii et al., 2017). However, we only found reduced thalamic volume to be related to childhood life events rather than CA. Childhood life events might form a more indirect adversity compared with CA, and thus form an interesting addition to CA alone (Tendolkar et al., 2022).

Cortical thickness was not associated with CA or cortisol levels. Previously, studies showed some specific cortical thickness changes related to specific types of CA or events (Bounoua et al., 2020; Teicher & Samson, 2016; Yang et al., 2023). Possibly, we did not find any association due to the fact we did not look into specific types of CA, but rather looked into a frequency and diversity measure of CA in relation to differences in cortical thickness. Further, those studies used a region of interest approach, whereas we used a whole‐brain approach.

Hair cortisol levels and their neural correlates

Hair cortisol levels were only significantly related to the volume of the left transverse temporal gyrus. This region is in line with a study investigating MDD specifically, finding hair cortisol to be related to lower temporal region volumes (Green et al., 2021). We found no correlation with hair cortisol levels and CA, in line with previous research (Oresta et al., 2021). Similar to this study, we investigated CA before the age of 16. However, these findings on hair cortisol level might be due to the wide time window for CA in this study. A previous study investigating hair cortisol levels after CA occurring during development, found specifically reduced hair cortisol levels with CA occurrence at the age of 3 (Schalinski et al., 2019). Unfortunately, we do not have any data on the specific timing of CA and the childhood life events. Timing of both are likely to be of importance, as adversity of any kind will have a different effect on the stress‐system during a different time of development. This is highlighted as well by the Maltreatment and Abuse Chronology of Exposure (MACE) scale (Teicher & Parigger, 2015). We suggest the MACE scale should be used more often in future studies to further investigate the effect of timing of CA and childhood life events.

Further, the hair cortisol measurements were gender‐biased. Hair cortisol measurements require 3 cm of scalp hair, which was often not possible in male participants. Results might therefore not be representative for the whole cohort.

Our study has some limitations. First, due to limited group size we could not distinguish between the four types of CA. Second, recall bias is likely to occur as we asked participants about possible adversity in their childhood. However, findings from retrospective reports correlate with prospective findings on CA, where CA experiences are minimized on retrospective report (Sheikh, 2018; Williams, 1994). Third, the impact of CA on cortisol levels might depend on specific time windows. As we investigated adversity before the age of 16, we have a very broad time window which lacks some specificity. Fourth, hair cortisol levels were gender‐biased. However, this will be a problem in other studies as well, especially for stress‐related disorders where there are more women than men. Last, our analytic approach demanded multiple comparison correction, thus changes need to be large to come out as significant. However, when not taking multiple testing correction into account, most of our results remain similar. Our study also has some strengths. First, we used a naturalistic cohort of psychiatric patients, by allowing comorbidities in our cohort. Second, we had a transdiagnostic approach, gaining a broader, more general insight of CA in psychiatric disorders. Third, we included cortisol measurements to distinguish effects from CA and cortisol response on brain structures.

CONCLUSION

CA is an important risk factor that needs to be taken into account when investigating psychiatric disorders. In addition to CA, childhood life events may be an important indirect adversity to take into account as well. Often, CA and childhood life events are not yet considered, especially in relation to neurobiological changes. Yet, the relationship is less clear than postulated on the basis of more seed based studies.
