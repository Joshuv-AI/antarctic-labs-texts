The top 100 most cited peer-reviewed publications on global neurosurgery: Mapping research trends and gaps over four decades
Authors: Siddharth Venigalla, Tirone Young, Bahie Ezzat, Shrey Patel, Alexander J. Schupper, Zerubabbel K. Asfaw, Addison Quinones, Michael B. Lemonick, Kevin V. Nguyen, Saadi Ghatan, Ernest J. Barthélemy, Roger Hartl, Tanvir F. Choudhri, Franco Servadei, Isabelle M. Germano
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundThis study aimed to highlight the landscape of global neurosurgery (GNS) literature through a bibliometric analysis of the 100 most cited peer-reviewed publications. The objectives were to identify publication trends, citation patterns, authorship, and collaboration networks within GNS research over the past four decades.MethodsA comprehensive search was conducted using Google Scholar and the Web of Science (WoS) database to identify articles related to GNS published within the last 40 years. The top 100 most cited articles were selected for analysis. Bibliometric evaluation was performed using VOSviewer and the Bibliometrix package in RStudio to assess trends in publication, authorship, citation, and country contribution, including those from lower-middle-income and low-income countries (LMIC/LIC).ResultsThe 100 most-cited GNS publications, spanning 2000–2023, included 811 co-authors from 61 countries and were published across 40 journals, with 71% appearing in nine leading journals. Publication output after 2012 accounted for 68% and showed a significant increase compared to the previous period (χ2 = 12.96, p = 0.00032) The cumulative growth in publications was also significant (χ2 = 27.93, p = 0.00049), driven largely by authors based in the US (χ2 = 92.59, p = 3.7 × 10⁻19) and other high-income countries (χ2 = 44.23, p = 5.7 × 10⁻⁹). 50% of publications originated from the US, while only 9% were from LMIC/LIC. Subspecialty-focused research comprised 76% of papers, with trauma (31%) and pediatric neurosurgery (21%) being the most frequent topics. Among general GNS themes, surgical access (11%) and workforce/education and training (11%) predominated. Co-authorship analysis demonstrated extensive international collaboration, with an overall collaborative rate of 81%.ConclusionsThis study provides a comprehensive overview of the evolution and distribution of leading GNS research. The steady rise in publications underscores increasing world-wide engagement in addressing disparities in neurosurgical care. However, persistent underrepresentation from LMIC/LIC highlights a growth opportunity. Strengthening inclusive global partnerships and research capacity in resource-constrained settings will be essential to advance sustainability and overall GNS goals.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1007/s00701-026-06897-1.

Introduction

Global neurosurgery (GNS) has emerged from the recognition that neurological diseases and conditions transcend geographical boundaries and encompass multiple medical disciplines [42]. Situated at the intersection of health equity, global surgery, and public health, GNS addresses conditions requiring neurosurgical interventions that affect individuals of all ages and backgrounds worldwide [11, 27, 42, 89]. The field seeks to bridge gaps in access, expertise, and resources to ensure that safe, timely, and high-quality neurosurgical care is available to all who need it. Despite significant progress over the past decades, major disparities in neurosurgical care persist both within and across nations.

Contemporary, transnational efforts to address these disparities began in the 1930s–1940s in Asia and in the 1960 s in Africa, driven by pioneering individuals and organizations [37]. These initiatives were further advanced through regional and international efforts by the World Federation of Neurosurgical Societies (WFNS) and the establishment of the Foundation for International Education in Neurological Surgery (FINS) in 1969 [13, 37, 64]. Nonprofit organizations, including Neurosurgery Outreach Foundation, Mission: BRAIN, and CURE International, have played a critical role in addressing the shortage of neurosurgical resources and expertise in low-middle-income and low-income countries (LMIC/LIC) [64, 82]. Through surgical missions, training programs, and partnerships with local healthcare providers, these organizations aim not only to improve immediate patient care but also to empower local professionals to provide neurosurgical services independently, thereby fostering sustainable long-term impact [59, 91, 121]. In 2023, the American Association of Neurological Surgeons established a Global Committee to serve as a catalyst for advancing GNS initiatives in the US and abroad [1, 6].

Research and collaboration remain central to advancing the goals of GNS [76]. Bibliometric analyses offer a systematic approach to understanding trends in research output, collaborations, and citation patterns, particularly when comparing contributions from LMIC/LIC with those from high-income countries (HIC) [87, 135]. This study aims to identify the 100 most cited GNS publications over the past 40 years. By examining citation patterns, prolific authors, and research networks, this analysis seeks to provide insights that can guide future investigations, strengthen collaborations, and identify opportunities for further GNS development.

Methods and materials

Study design, search strategy, eligibility criteria

In this bibliometric analysis, relevant publications that represent the broad scope of GNS literature were identified across multiple databases. We queried a four-decade period (October 11th, 1985—October 19th, 2025) across Web of Science (WoS) and NIH iCite (Supplementary File 1). Search records were exported into a separate file, and duplicate records were identified and removed programmatically using Python version 3.12.0 based on article DOI. Article citation counts were then retrieved from Web of Science and Google Scholar and averaged to rank the articles in descending order. Three independent reviewers evaluated articles for relevance; conflicts were resolved by a fourth author.

Inclusion criteria were: (1) the primary focus of the article was GNS with the search terms provided in the supplementary file; (2) the article was an original research or review paper; (3) full-text access was available; (4) the article was published in a peer-reviewed, indexed journal; and (5) the article was written in English. Exclusion criteria were: (1) the article’s primary focus was not related to GNS; (2) abstract-only or poster presentations without full-text manuscripts; (3) non–peer-reviewed publications; and (4) non-English language articles.

Data extraction

Following identification of the 100 most cited GNS articles, bibliographic information was collected for each, including the article title, first and corresponding authors, PMID, year of publication, number of co-authors, journal, corresponding author’s institutional affiliation and country, study keywords, subspecialty classification, and total number of citations. Network analysis of author information was conducted with VOSviewer (Version 1.6.16), RStudio (version 4.2.2), and the Bibliometrix Package. Country income tiers were determined based on World Bank designations [135].

To identify relevant thematic trends, included articles were dichotomized into either (1) neurosurgical subspecialty–focused or (2) general GNS–focused papers. The subspecialty cohort was further divided into trauma, pediatrics, cerebrovascular, tumor, functional, and spine. Papers in the general GNS cohort were categorized by subtopics, including surgical access, workforce, education and training, economics, and research. In addition to this organization, keyword frequency and total were analyzed using Bibliometrix.

Statistical analysis

Descriptive statistics were reported as mean ± standard deviation for continuous variables and as frequencies and percentages for categorical variables. Chi-square (χ2) tests were used to evaluate differences in publication distribution across time periods, countries, regions, and institutional affiliations. A Mann–Whitney U test was performed to compare annual publication counts between early (2000–2012) and later (2013–2023) periods. Linear trendlines were generated to visualize cumulative publication growth over time, with coefficients of determination (R2) calculated to assess the strength of fit. Statistical significance was defined as p < 0.05. All analyses were conducted using Python version 3.12.0 (scipy.stats) and Microsoft Excel (Microsoft Corporation, Redmond, WA).

These publications spanned from 2000 to 2023 (Fig. 1a). The majority of articles (76%) focused on neurosurgery subspecialties, while the remaining addressed overarching GNS topics. Among the subspecialty-focused cohort, trauma (31%) and pediatrics (21%) were the most common (Fig. 2). Discussion

This study provides key insights into the evolving field of GNS, highlighting its major contributors, publication trends, and recent advancements. By analyzing a broad range of scientific publications and citation data, we offer a comprehensive overview of the GNS research landscape. The analysis demonstrates substantial growth over the past two decades, characterized by a steady increase in highly cited publications and annual citations, reflecting increasing engagement from researchers, clinicians, and other stakeholders.

Our findings on the persistent underrepresentation of LIC/LMIC authors in global neurosurgery align with broader patterns observed across global health and surgery research. Bibliometric analyses over the past three decades have documented a similar concentration of research output in high-income countries, despite the disproportionate disease burden in low-resource settings [106]. Scholars have highlighted how structural inequities in funding, mentorship, and publication practices perpetuate what has been termed epistemic injustice, marginalizing voices and expertise from the regions most affected [12, 86]. Systematic reviews have further shown that collaborative research often positions LMIC investigators in middle-author roles, limiting their visibility and leadership opportunities [47]. Efforts to grow global surgery research with a focus on equity—including intentional capacity building, LMIC-led collaborations, and inclusive authorship policies—have demonstrated promise but remain incomplete [48]. Our bibliometric analysis underscores that similar structural challenges persist in neurosurgery, reinforcing the need for strategies that prioritize equitable participation, recognition, and leadership for LIC/LMIC researchers as part of the broader movement to decolonize global health and surgery.

Shifting trends in global neurosurgery

The growing momentum in GNS research over the past decade reflects a broader international recognition of the importance of equitable neurosurgical care [40, 87]. A previous bibliometric study documented a marked rise in GNS publications beginning around 2010, a trend consistent with our findings [85]. This expansion likely corresponds to the increasing global health emphasis within neurosurgery and the establishment of formal academic collaborations, training initiatives, and policy frameworks supporting surgical system strengthening [39, 44, 98]. The proliferation of highly cited work in recent years suggests that GNS has evolved from a niche interest into a defined academic discipline with sustained scholarly engagement [76, 98].

Although the growth of GNS continues, the distribution of research output remains uneven. Contributions from HIC continue to dominate the literature, while representation from LMIC is comparatively modest [10, 104, 107]. This imbalance has been noted in prior analyses and underscores structural barriers, such as disparities in research funding, infrastructure, mentorship, and access to publication networks, that constrain participation from LMIC constituents [9, 87]. While we acknowledge that the disproportionate representation of authors from HIC is an inevitable byproduct of most GNS initiatives being driven by philanthropic efforts, addressing inequity requires intentional capacity building which may lead to equitable authorship practices, and institutional partnerships that prioritize local leadership and relevance to regional health priorities.

Global neurosurgery care access, education, and training

Access to surgical care and the development of education and training programs remain central themes within GNS. Our analysis supports this observation, with 16% of the 100 most-cited articles addressing these topics. The most cited paper in this category quantified the global burden of neurosurgical disease and emphasized the present significant deficit in the neurosurgical capacity required to meet worldwide needs [26]. Similarly, the second most cited paper highlighted the extensive unmet need for neurosurgical services, reinforcing the field’s growing focus on addressing disparities in access to care [89]. Together, these landmark studies underscore the neurosurgical community’s commitment to characterizing and reducing inequities in care delivery across diverse settings, especially where services remain limited.

To meet the expanding global demand for neurosurgical services, recent initiatives have focused heavily on workforce development, emphasizing education and training abroad. The most-cited article in this category identified innovative digital platforms capable of expanding global access to neurosurgical education [84]. Complementarily, another highly cited study described the impact of the WFNS Rabat Training Center in strengthening neurosurgical training across Sub-Saharan Africa [62]. Collectively, these efforts highlight the diversity of strategies being employed to enhance neurosurgical capacity worldwide, including the establishment of regional training centers, international partnerships, and the adoption of digital education technologies. Future opportunities for engagement include the utilization of virtual reality surgical simulators or AI-assisted technologies to augment education.

Neurosurgical subspecialties in focus: Trauma and pediatrics lead global efforts

The prevalence of TBI within trauma-related studies on our list of most cited articles reflects a sustained global concern regarding this condition’s burden and management. These investigations explored multiple dimensions of TBI, including workforce requirements for effective treatment, inequities in research capacity across regions, and the impact of treatment delays on patient outcomes in low-resource settings [18, 115, 118]. Within pediatrics, the most cited study (fifth overall) stratified global hydrocephalus incidence and identified substantial disparity in neurosurgical care, workforce, and infrastructure, particularly in regions with high birth rates and postinfectious etiologies [28]. Several other pediatric neurosurgery investigations examined pediatric surgical need, training capacity, and education initiatives aimed at reducing these deficiencies [7, 16, 23, 78, 90].

Together, the prominence of trauma and pediatric neurosurgery within the most cited literature reflects a collective effort to address conditions that disproportionately affect low- and middle-income regions. Both subspecialties exemplify the dual priorities of GNS: quantifying the burden of disease and implementing sustainable strategies to expand surgical capacity, education, and research equity. These focal areas highlight where global collaboration has been most active and where continued investment is likely to yield the greatest impact in improving neurosurgical outcomes worldwide.

Shaping global neurosurgery: The role of leading journals

This bibliometric analysis highlights the pivotal role of journals in shaping the discourse and direction of GNS. The JNSPG accounted for nearly one-third of all articles, underscoring its influence in disseminating high-impact research. Three landmark studies published in the same April 2019 issue of the Journal of Neurosurgery, all ranking among the ten most-cited articles in this analysis, quantified the global neurosurgical workforce deficit, the worldwide incidence of traumatic brain injury, and the epidemiology of hydrocephalus [26–28]. Neurosurgical Focus has also been particularly instrumental, publishing three articles in its October 2018 special issue, “Reducing Inequities in Global Neurosurgery Delivery.” [4, 23, 84] Furthermore, a dedicated collection of five GNS-related articles appeared in Journal of Neurosurgery, April 2020 [25, 26, 34, 79, 101]. All of these publications emphasize the growing recognition of GNS as a defined academic discipline and the commitment of leading journals to advancing global equity in neurosurgical care.

World Neurosurgery also ranks among the foremost contributors to GNS scholarship. However, its transition from a subscription-based model to an author publication fee system may present financial barriers for researchers in LMIC/LIC [2, 65, 107, 119, 136]. This shift underscores the importance of fostering equitable publishing opportunities, including fee waivers and open-access pathways, to ensure inclusive dissemination of scientific knowledge and to strengthen international collaboration in the field [9, 65, 102].

Limitations

This study has several limitations. First, the search terms “global neurosurgery” and “international neurosurgery” have only recently gained widespread use; consequently, earlier relevant articles addressing GNS under different terminology may have been overlooked. Second, the analysis was restricted to English-language, peer-reviewed publications, which may have excluded relevant work published in other languages or non-indexed regional journals. Third, while citation counts provide a functional proxy for scholarly influence, they do not fully capture an article’s impact on policy, clinical practice, or health system development.

Our article inclusion methodology, while designed to balance differences between databases, remains subject to variation in citation coverage, update frequency, and indexing criteria across platforms. Furthermore, duplicate records were removed programmatically using Python, and while this automated process improved consistency, it may not have fully eliminated all database inconsistencies or classification errors.

Finally, reliance on WoS, Google Scholar, and NIH ICite as primary indexing platforms may have excluded influential studies indexed elsewhere, such as Scopus or PubMed Central. As a descriptive bibliometric analysis, this study cannot assess the qualitative impact, methodological rigor, or real-world implementation of included research. Future studies incorporating additional databases, non-English literature, and alternative impact measures may assist in providing a more comprehensive understanding of GNS.

Conclusion

GNS has evolved into a distinct and rapidly expanding discipline that reflects the growing global commitment to equitable surgical care. This bibliometric analysis highlights significant progress over the past two decades, with increased scholarly engagement, diversification of research subjects, and leadership from established institutions driving much of the field’s expansion. Despite these gains, persistent underrepresentation of LIC/LMIC authors highlights ongoing inequities in authorship, resources, and research visibility. While initiatives such as international partnerships, training programs, and LMIC-led research networks have contributed to growth, structural barriers—including funding disparities, limited research infrastructure, and citation biases—continue to constrain equity. Strengthening collaborations, expanding access to education and training, and fostering inclusive publishing remain critical to advancing global neurosurgery. Continued, intentional engagement with LMIC partners will be essential to ensure that the benefits of neurosurgical innovation are shared worldwide.
