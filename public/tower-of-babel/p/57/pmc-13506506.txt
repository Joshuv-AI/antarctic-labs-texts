Safety and feasibility of tubeless PCNL without urinary catheter or retrograde intubation under paravertebral block
Authors: Guangyuan Yang, Fuchao Liang, Xiaosong Sun, Linghui Qin
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractObjectiveTo evaluate the feasibility and safety of omitting postoperative urinary catheter placement in patients undergoing percutaneous nephrolithotomy (PCNL) under paravertebral block (PVB) anesthesia without retrograde catheterization.MethodsThis retrospective study analyzed 197 eligible patients selected from an initial cohort of 248. All patients received PCNL under PVB. They were divided into two groups based on postoperative catheterization: Group 1 (without urinary catheter, n = 97) and Group 2 (with urinary catheter, n = 100). To minimize confounding factors, propensity score matching was performed based on the number of access tracts, resulting in two groups: Group A (without urinary catheter, n = 90) and Group B (with urinary catheter, n = 90). Preoperative baseline characteristics, perioperative parameters, and postoperative complications were compared between the groups.ResultsNo significant differences were observed in age, gender, body mass index (BMI), comorbidities, stone characteristics, or preoperative laboratory findings between the two groups (P > 0.05), indicating comparability. Regarding surgical efficacy and safety, there were no statistically significant differences in operative time, blood loss, number of access tracts, postoperative hemoglobin drop, inflammatory marker changes, or initial stone-free rate (P > 0.05). Group A showed superior outcomes in several aspects: lower visual analog scale (VAS) pain scores (P < 0.001), shorter postoperative hospital stay [3.00 (3.00, 4.00) vs. 4.00 (3.00, 4.00) days, P < 0.001], lower incidence of postoperative urinary tract infection (2/90 vs. 10/90, P = 0.017), and lower overall postoperative complication rate (9/90 vs. 25/90, P < 0.002).ConclusionFor appropriately selected patients undergoing PCNL under PVB without retrograde catheterization, omitting the postoperative urinary catheter is a safe practice. This approach is associated with reduced postoperative pain, shorter hospitalization, and merits clinical consideration under standardized perioperative management.Keywords: Percutaneous nephrolithotomy, Paravertebral block, Urinary catheter, Tubeless, Safety

Introduction

Percutaneous nephrolithotomy (PCNL) is a widely adopted minimally invasive surgical procedure for the treatment of renal and proximal ureteral stones [1]. This technique offers the advantages of minimal invasiveness and a high stone-free rate, demonstrating significant efficacy in the management of large-volume and staghorn stones [2, 3]. Conventional PCNL typically relies on retrograde catheterization to establish artificial hydronephrosis, a procedure that may carry risks such as ureteral injury, perforation, and infection [4, 5]. In response, the technique of PCNL without retrograde catheterization has been developed. By abandoning both retrograde catheterization and the lithotomy position, and instead performing ultrasound-guided puncture directly in the prone position, this approach effectively reduces related complications and offers a superior surgical pathway for patients with anatomical abnormalities or those unsuitable for the lithotomy position.

With the widespread adoption of the enhanced recovery after surgery (ERAS) concept [6], the practice of “tubeless” PCNL has emerged as a significant area of development [7, 8]. Extending beyond the conventional "tubeless" PCNL (no nephrostomy tube) [9], the procedure now also eliminates the urinary catheter. This advancement targets reduced postoperative pain, shorter hospitalization, and improved comfort, goals achievable through precise surgery and optimized anesthesia.

The choice of anesthetic technique is a key determinant influencing both the PCNL procedure itself and postoperative recovery [10]. Paravertebral block (PVB), a regional anesthetic technique, provides extensive unilateral somatic and sympathetic blockade. This results in effective intraoperative and early postoperative analgesia, reduces the need for general anesthetics and opioids, and promotes early ambulation and recovery [11, 12]. Together, PVB anesthesia, meticulous surgical technique, and strict perioperative management create the physiological basis and safety profile that makes urinary catheter-free PCNL feasible.

There is limited research on the combined approach of paravertebral block anesthesia, non-retrograde catheterization, and postoperative omission of a urinary catheter. Its safety and impact on recovery quality require further validation. Therefore, guided by the ERAS concept, this retrospective study analyzed 197 patients who underwent non-retrograde catheterization PCNL under PVB. By comparing clinical outcomes between groups with and without a urinary catheter, we aimed to systematically evaluate the feasibility and safety of omitting the catheter within this comprehensive model.

Materials and methods

Study subjects

A retrospective analysis was conducted on 197 patients who underwent PCNL at our institution from January 2024 to January 2025. "Tubeless PCNL" specifically refers to being both catheter-free and nephrostomy tube-free. After being fully informed of the advantages and disadvantages of both approaches (with or without a urinary catheter) in the context of their agreed-upon PCNL procedure, patients made an autonomous choice regarding postoperative catheterization. This study obtained informed consent from all participants, and the study protocol was approved by the Institutional Ethics Committee of our hospital (Approval No.: 2025–036).

Inclusion criteria were: (1) meeting the surgical indications for PCNL as outlined by the European Association of Urology (EAU) guidelines; (2) preoperative CT confirming a visible collecting system in the target kidney, providing suitable anatomy for percutaneous renal access; and (3) a maximum renal stone diameter not exceeding 3 cm.

Exclusion criteria included: (1) active urinary tract infection (positive preoperative urine culture without adequate control); (2) significant coagulation dysfunction (international normalized ratio (INR) > 1.5 or platelet count < 50 × 10⁹/L) or uncontrolled cardiopulmonary disease (American Society of Anesthesiologists (ASA) physical status ≥ IV); (3) history of previous ipsilateral renal surgery (e.g., renal cyst decortication, partial nephrectomy); (4) conditions commonly associated with voiding dysfunction, such as neurological disorders, benign prostatic hyperplasia, or urethral stricture; (5) presence of urinary tract anomalies (e.g., horseshoe kidney, transplanted kidney); and (6) patients requiring rescue catheterization for urinary retention (6 patients).

The criteria for rescue catheterization: The criteria were determined based on the patient's first sensation of voiding, strong desire to void, and maximum bladder capacity. When patients experienced the initial sensation of voiding, the bladder volume was approximately 150–250 mL. When they reported a strong desire to void, the bladder volume was approximately 300–500 mL. During this period, patients were instructed to attempt urination spontaneously without the aid of alpha-blockers. If the patient was unable to void spontaneously and the bladder volume reached the maximum capacity (approximately 400–600 mL) or the patient reported intolerable distension, rescue catheterization was performed. All measurements were conducted via bladder scanning using ultrasound.

Surgical technique

All procedures were performed by the same surgeon. The puncture site was selected according to the SVOF principle [13].

Group A: Following successful anesthesia, patients were placed in the prone position with elevation of the chest and hips. The target calyx was localized using intraoperative ultrasound (BK200) in conjunction with preoperative CT imaging. The puncture site was typically selected at the 11th intercostal space or subcostal below the 12th rib. Under continuous ultrasound guidance, a two-stage puncture technique was employed: the needle was first advanced from the skin to the renal fascia layer, where its position could be finely adjusted with respiration, and then inserted into the target calyx. After withdrawing the stylet, a J-tipped stiff guidewire was inserted, and its coiling within the collecting system was confirmed by ultrasound. The needle was then removed, and the tract was sequentially dilated up to 20F, over which a 20F negative-pressure access sheath was placed. Stone fragmentation was performed using a holmium laser or an electromedical systems (EMS) lithotripter. If significant residual stones were identified on ultrasound, additional tracts were established under ultrasound guidance for further lithotripsy. A double-J stent was placed upon clearance of ureteral stones. Prior to concluding the procedure, the absence of active bleeding was confirmed before withdrawing the access sheath and suturing the skin incision. Postoperatively, bladder urine volume was measured immediately, and spontaneous voiding was closely monitored.

Group B: After anesthesia induction, a 16F double-lumen silicone urinary catheter was inserted. Patients were then positioned prone, and all subsequent surgical steps were identical to those in Group A. The urinary catheter was maintained in a continuously open drainage state throughout.

Outcome measures

Definition of standardized management

In our center, perioperative management was strictly standardized between the two groups to ensure maximum consistency. The postoperative antibiotic prophylaxis regimen was standardized for all patients. Following PCNL, patients received intravenous antibiotics, primarily second-generation cephalosporins, once daily. If the patient was afebrile and laboratory parameters (e.g., serum white blood cell count) normalized by 48 h post-surgery, antibiotics were discontinued. Urine cultures were obtained at the time of the first postoperative voiding. Postoperative urinary tract infection was defined as the presence of clinical symptoms—such as fever (axillary temperature > 37.3℃), urinary urgency, frequency, dysuria, or suprapubic tenderness—along with at least one of the following laboratory findings: WBC > 5/HPF on urinalysis, positive nitrite, positive leukocyte esterase, bacterial colony count ≥ 103 CFU/mL on urine culture, or a positive urine culture for a pathogenic organism. Discharge criteria included stable vital signs, recovery of bowel function, ability to void spontaneously and ambulate, no requirement for analgesia, and absence of complications.

Primary outcomes

The overall complication rate, graded using the Clavien–Dindo classification system, was compared between groups. Specific complications monitored included postoperative fever, postoperative antibiotic therapy, pain requiring pharmacological intervention, persistent hematuria, nausea and vomiting, postoperative urinary tract infection, and sepsis.

Secondary outcomes

Secondary outcomes compared between the groups included operative time, initial stone-free rate, postoperative pain assessed by the visual analog scale (VAS) within 12 h, postoperative hospital stay, postoperative hemoglobin drop, and postvoid residual urine volume. Operative time was defined as the period from initial puncture localization to the withdrawal of the access sheath and completion of skin closure. Postoperative hospital stay was calculated from the day of surgery until the day the patient met discharge criteria and was actually discharged. Stone clearance was evaluated primarily by plain kidney-ureter-bladder radiograph or computed tomography within 48 h postoperatively, with residual fragments < 4 mm in maximum diameter defined as clinically insignificant residual stones. For patients in Group A, postvoid residual urine volume was measured immediately after the procedure using bedside ultrasound to objectively assess early voiding efficiency. A complete blood count was routinely checked on the postoperative day to monitor changes in hemoglobin and inflammatory markers. All patients had a double-J stent placed intraoperatively, which was removed via outpatient cystoscopy 2–4 weeks postoperatively.

Statistical analysis

Statistical analyses were performed using SPSS software (version 27.0). Continuous data conforming to a normal distribution are presented as mean ± standard deviation and were compared using the independent samples t-test. Continuous data with a non-normal distribution are expressed as median (M) and interquartile range (P25, P75) and were compared using the Mann–Whitney U test. Categorical data are presented as number (percentage) and were compared using the Chi-square test or Fisher's exact test, as appropriate. A two-sided P-value of < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Discussion

The postoperative management of PCNL is evolving towards a "tubeless" approach. While omitting the nephrostomy tube has been extensively explored [14], most PCNL procedures still routinely involve urinary catheterization. There is no consensus on whether the urinary catheter can be routinely omitted. The rationale for maintaining a urinary catheter is grounded in its ability to provide continuous urine drainage. This helps to reduce renal pelvic pressure, minimizes the risk of postoperative urine leakage and urinary obstruction, and allows for close monitoring of urine output in the early postoperative period [15]. However, the potential drawbacks of an indwelling urinary catheter cannot be overlooked. These include an increased risk of urinary tract infection, the occurrence of bladder irritative symptoms, and negative impacts on postoperative patient comfort and the overall recovery experience. Furthermore, such issues may adversely affect public safety reporting and healthcare reimbursement [16, 17]. The duration of urinary catheterization is a significant risk factor for catheter-associated urinary tract infection (CAUTI). Therefore, the early removal of the catheter serves as a cornerstone in reducing the incidence of CAUTI [18].

In this study, no statistically significant differences were observed between the two groups in core surgical outcome measures, including operative time, stone-free rate, and blood loss. However, patients in Group A demonstrated superior outcomes regarding postoperative pain, hospital stay, incidence of urinary tract infection, and overall complication rate. This observation provides new clinical grounds for re-evaluating the necessity of routine postoperative catheterization and suggests that omitting the urinary catheter under specific conditions may offer distinct advantages. Furthermore, the practice of avoiding routine catheterization has been established as safe and feasible in other surgical disciplines, such as orthopedics, gynecology, and thoracic surgery. This approach not only reduces postoperative urethral pain and urinary discomfort but also effectively lowers the risk of urinary tract infections and shortens the length of hospital stay [19–21].

PVB anesthesia is instrumental in achieving this goal. PVB targets the area where spinal nerves exit the intervertebral foramina. By blocking somatic and sympathetic nerve fibers across several adjacent dermatomes above and below the injection site, it effectively controls surgical site pain [22]. The significantly lower postoperative VAS scores in Group A of this study directly confirm the analgesic superiority of this approach. Although a small proportion of patients (5.8%) required rescue catheterization for urinary retention, the overall incidence remained manageable. This suggests that, under PVB anesthesia, the bladders of the vast majority of suitably selected patients can adequately adapt to a catheter-free state in the early postoperative period. Furthermore, the application of the non-retrograde catheterization technique inherently avoids the physical stimulation and potential injury to the ureteral orifice and bladder associated with retrograde intubation. Combined with precise puncture, this creates a prerequisite for omitting the urinary catheter. Finally, maintaining continuous negative-pressure suction during lithotripsy minimizes irrigation fluid influx into the bladder. The timely intraoperative placement of a double-J stent after addressing ureteral stones allows urine to reflux into the renal pelvis when bladder volume increases, thereby reducing bladder distension. Additionally, the preemptive placement of the double-J stent helps mitigate the risk of stone migration during the procedure.

The present study aimed to investigate the effect of a tubeless protocol on promoting enhanced postoperative recovery. The results demonstrated a significantly shorter postoperative hospital stay in Group A, a positive outcome attributable to the synergistic effect of multiple factors. Chief among these was the reduction in postoperative pain, which was markedly improved by avoiding the continuous urethral irritation caused by an indwelling catheter. Effective pain management not only directly reduces the need for opioid analgesics and their associated adverse effects, such as nausea, vomiting, and ileus, but also establishes a crucial foundation for early patient ambulation [23]. In this regard, PVB demonstrates distinct advantages. Compared to general anesthesia, PVB effectively minimizes intraoperative hemodynamic fluctuations, reduces the incidence of postoperative nausea and vomiting, and further decreases opioid consumption, thereby enhancing overall postoperative comfort [10]. Furthermore, as PVB provides only unilateral blockade, it exerts minimal impact on the circulatory system while preserving lower limb muscle strength, thereby allowing patients to resume ambulation shortly after anesthesia recovery [24]. Early ambulation, a cornerstone of ERAS, prevents deep vein thrombosis, promotes the recovery of gastrointestinal function, and enhances patient confidence in recovery, thereby establishing a virtuous cycle that accelerates rehabilitation.

Studies have shown that proper catheter management is crucial for preventing urinary complications [25, 26]. Omission of the urinary catheter reduces catheter-associated complications, improves patient comfort, and shortens hospital stay [17]. However, it must be emphasized that omitting the urinary catheter is not suitable for all PCNL patients. We suggest that ideal candidates are those with ASA physical status I–III, normal preoperative voiding function (particularly for males, without significant benign prostatic hyperplasia), moderate stone burden, and an uncomplicated intraoperative course. Conversely, a more cautious approach is warranted for patients with anticipated complex or prolonged surgery, advanced age, or a history of urinary retention; in these cases, catheter placement remains a crucial safety measure. Furthermore, a robust postoperative monitoring protocol is essential, such as the routine assessment of postvoid residual urine volume in this study, to ensure timely intervention for urinary retention and to minimize potential risks.

In conclusion, for strictly selected patients, non-retrograde catheterization PCNL performed under paravertebral block by an experienced urologist, followed by omission of the urinary catheter, can be considered a safe and feasible postoperative management strategy.

Limitations

This study is a single-center retrospective analysis with certain limitations: 1. As a single-center study involving a single institution and a single surgeon, the generalizability of the findings is limited; 2. Group allocation was based on patient preference (“voluntary choice”), which may introduce selection bias in data collection; 3. The sample size is relatively limited, and the follow-up period is short, which may lead to inaccuracies in assessing long-term complications, such as ureteral stricture. We hope to conduct prospective, multi-center, large-sample randomized controlled trials in the future to perform more in-depth research and provide higher-level clinical evidence.
