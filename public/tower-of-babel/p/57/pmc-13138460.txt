Cerebral Foreign Body Granulomatous Reaction Secondary to Hydrophilic Polymer Embolization Post-Mechanical Thrombectomy: A Case Report and Overview of Differential Diagnosis
Authors: Kaiying Wang, Sam White, Esther Quick, Stephen Bacchi, Rebecca Scroop, Rudy Goh, Karyn Boundy
Published: 2026

A 56-year-old male presented with left upper limb focal motor status epilepticus. His relevant medical history included a right middle cerebral artery (MCA) territory infarct 5 months previously secondary to an M1 segment MCA occlusion (Figure), which had been successfully treated with intravenous thrombolysis and mechanical thrombectomy. Aspiration thrombectomy was performed with transfemoral access using a CAT 6 aspiration catheter (CNS, Stryker Corporation, Kalamazoo, MI) via an 8F Infinity AXS guide catheter (CNS, Stryker Corporation), achieving modified Treatment in Cerebral Infarction score 2c reperfusion after 3 passes. Catheter navigation to the lesion was uneventful, and there was no reason to suspect catheter damage. The modified Rankin Scale score on discharge was 0. The cause of the stroke was defined as an embolic stroke of undetermined source.

B, Axial diffusion weighted magnetic resonance (MR) image performed 20 hours post-thrombectomy demonstrating diffusion restriction indicative of acute ischemia within the right MCA territory, including involvement of the caudate and lentiform nuclei. C, Axial T1-weighted fat-saturated gadolinium-enhanced MR image performed 5 months following mechanical thrombectomy, demonstrating multiple enhancing lesions within the right frontal lobe deep white matter, associated with marked vasogenic edema and resulting in regional sulcal and right lateral ventricular effacement. Following seizure onset, brain magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) demonstrated a 71×51 mm cluster of centrally T2 hyperintense lesions with peripheral T2 isointensity to hypointensity and strong postgadolinium enhancement within the right frontal lobe deep white matter and extending into the supramarginal gyrus and posterior superior temporal gyrus. These lesions were associated with marked surrounding vasogenic edema resulting in regional sulcal effacement and effacement of the right lateral ventricle without midline shift (Figure). Extensive serological testing for infectious and autoimmune causes was negative, and cerebrospinal fluid analysis (including serial culture) was unremarkable. The man developed refractory focal epilepsy, and serial brain MRIs over the subsequent month revealed slow increases in both the size and number of lesions, which remained confined to the right MCA territory.

Stereotactic biopsy was performed, demonstrating necrotizing granulomata and a foreign body giant cell reaction to basophilic foreign material, consistent with hydrophilic polymer originating from the catheter used during the prior thrombectomy (Figures S1 and S2).

The patient was treated with a 3-day course of high-dose intravenous methylprednisolone followed by transition to oral prednisolone. Ongoing immunosuppressive therapy with azathioprine (50 mg twice daily), methotrexate (15 mg weekly), and low-dose prednisolone was administered over the subsequent 4 years before prednisolone was tapered and ceased. The patient’s symptoms resolved shortly after commencing therapy, and serial brain MRIs demonstrated a significant reduction in both the burden of enhancing cerebral lesions and associated edema.

Discussion

Hydrophilic polymer coatings are widely used on endovascular devices to reduce friction and facilitate vessel navigation. Previous studies have described hydrophilic polymer detachment and distal embolization inciting cerebral granulomatous inflammation in neuroendovascular procedures such as thrombectomy and deployment of flow diversion devices for aneurysm treatment.1–3 The proposed mechanism involves polymer fragmentation during catheter manipulation followed by microembolization to distal arterioles.4,5 Once deposited within the neuroparenchyma, hydrophilic polymers elicit a delayed foreign body reaction (type IV hypersensitivity response) characterized by macrophage activation and giant cell formation, development of necrotizing granulomata, and surrounding reactive astrocytosis.6 The delayed onset of symptoms, occurring months after neurointervention in this and other reported cases, likely reflects the slow evolution of granuloma formation. Ishikawa et al1 and Meiers et al2 reported symptom onset 6 to 12 months post-thrombectomy, whereas Geisbush et al3 reported symptom onset 2 months following intervention. Granulomatous inflammation in hydrophilic polymer embolization typically remains confined to the specific vascular territory/territories navigated (eg, treatment of a right M1 MCA lesion involves catheter navigation through the right internal carotid artery and can, therefore, result in right anterior cerebral artery and MCA territory involvement).

Neurological deterioration following mechanical thrombectomy should prompt consideration of hydrophilic polymer embolization, as well as several other potential mimics.

Delayed posthypoxic leukoencephalopathy: often has a more acute onset, typically manifesting 1 to 3 weeks following hypoxic insult. MRI features include white matter T2 and fluid-attenuated inversion recovery hyperintensity (confined to the specific vascular territory/territories affected by vessel occlusion) with sparing of cortex. Enhancement is uncommon.

Cerebral abscess: typically acute to subacute onset (2 weeks to 4 months).7 MRI features include ≥1 enhancing lesion associated with vasogenic edema; however, unlike cerebral granulomatous inflammation, abscesses demonstrate central diffusion restriction and are usually associated with clinical/biochemical evidence of systemic inflammation and cerebrospinal fluid pleocytosis.

Conclusions

Neurological deterioration in the weeks to months following mechanical thrombectomy should prompt consideration of procedure-related complications such as hydrophilic polymer embolization or mimic conditions. The time course of symptom onset, coupled with imaging findings (particularly MRI), can be helpful in narrowing the list of differential diagnoses.
