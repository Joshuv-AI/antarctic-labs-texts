PDIA6 exacerbates hepatocellular carcinoma via enhancing proteasome-dependent degradation of AKAP12
Authors: Rucheng Yao, Yuhan Yin, Xiaosong Li, Xinyu Yang, Wenzhi He, Yufeng Yuan, Jun Hu
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractThe continuously rising incidence and persistently high mortality of hepatocellular carcinoma (HCC) have created an urgent need for a deeper understanding of the molecular mechanisms underlying this disease. In the present study, leveraging multiple HCC transcriptome databases, we employed expression analysis, correlation analysis, Gene Set Variation Analysis (GSVA), and cox regression analysis to identify that aberrant upregulation of PDIA6 represents a promising prognostic biomarker associated with adverse clinical outcomes in HCC. Using a series of in vitro oncology research approaches, as well as nude mouse models of subcutaneous tumor formation and lung metastasis, we experimentally validated that PDIA6 promotes the proliferation and metastasis of HCC cells. Through transcriptome sequencing analysis and subsequent rescue experiments, we further confirmed that PDIA6 enhances HCC cell proliferation and migration by activating the Wnt signaling pathway. Combined analysis via immunoprecipitation-mass spectrometry and proteomics revealed that PDIA6 significantly downregulates the expression of the tumor suppressor gene AKAP12. Subsequent rescue experiments demonstrated that PDIA6 drives HCC progression in a manner dependent on the reduced expression of AKAP12. Utilizing protein half-life assays, ubiquitination assays, and co-IP experiments, we uncovered the underlying mechanism: PDIA6 competitively binds to the UCH domain of USP24, which impairs the deubiquitinating activity of USP24 towards AKAP12. This ultimately leads to enhanced K48-linked ubiquitination of AKAP12 and its subsequent proteasomal degradation. We further verified, using specific activators, that AKAP12 inhibits the Wnt signaling pathway in a PKA-dependent manner. In vivo, targeted inhibition of PDIA6 exhibited a more potent therapeutic effect on Wnt-positive HCC tumors.Conclusion Collectively, our study demonstrates the HCC-promoting mechanism of the PDIA6-AKAP12-Wnt signaling axis and highlights its great potential for the development of therapeutic targets in HCC.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at https://doi.org/10.1007/s13402-026-01258-7.Keywords: Hepatocellular carcinoma, PDIA6, Wnt signaling pathway, Ubiquitination

Introduction

Hepatocellular carcinoma (HCC) stands as the most prevalent form of primary liver cancer worldwide, characterized by high incidence and mortality rates that pose a significant threat to human health [1, 2]. Owing to its insidious onset, the majority of patients are diagnosed at intermediate or advanced stages, resulting in extremely poor prognoses [3, 4]. In recent years, the clinical application of molecular targeted therapies and immune checkpoint inhibitors has led to improved survival rates in some advanced HCC patients [5–7]; however, the overall therapeutic efficacy remains limited, with most patients failing to achieve long-term benefits. Thus, in-depth exploration of the molecular mechanisms underlying HCC initiation and progression, along with the identification of novel potential therapeutic targets, continues to be a key focus in basic research on liver cancer.

The development and progression of HCC is a multi-step, multi-factorial process, typically accompanied with the inactivation of tumor suppressor genes and the activation of oncogenes [8]. Within this process, the aberrant gene expression arises not only from gene mutations, deletions, or amplifications but also from close associations with protein degradation and post-translational modifications [9]. For instance, SCF-SKP2 E3 ubiquitin ligase complex promotes the ubiquitination and degradation of p27 and p21, thereby facilitating the proliferation of HCC cells [10]. USP8 can deubiquitinate and stabilize β-catenin in HCC, which in turn drives cancer progression [11]. Within the immunosuppressive microenvironment of HCC, the ubiquitin-proteasome system also exerts a crucial function. Specifically, USP14 enhances the uptake of glucose by tumor cells through deubiquitinating and stabilizing the glucose transporter GLUT1; this metabolic advantage allows tumor cells to “starve” CD8 + T cells in the tumor microenvironment [12]. Thus, the role of post-translational regulatory systems in controlling protein stability during the initiation and progression of HCC has gained increasing recognition.

Protein disulfide isomerase family A member 6 (PDIA6) has garnered increasing attention in recent years. Existing studies have demonstrated that PDIA6 is overexpressed in various tumors, including pancreatic cancer, bladder cancer, gastric cancer, and glioblastoma, and is involved in regulating tumor cell proliferation, migration, invasion, and apoptosis [13–16]. However, its cryptic mechanism in liver cancer remains elusive, particularly whether it participates in signal pathways regulation through specific downstream target proteins, which has not been systematically elucidated. Meanwhile, studies have reported that overexpression of PDIA6 can promote the upregulation of β-catenin in pancreatic cancer [15]. Nevertheless, whether the regulatory effect of PDIA6 on liver cancer depends on the Wnt/β-catenin signaling pathway requires further investigation.

A-kinase anchor protein 12 (AKAP12) is a key member of the cyclic adenosine monophosphate-dependent kinase anchor protein (AKAP) family [17]. Previous reports have established multiple well‑defined links between AKAP12 and Wnt/β‑catenin signaling. As a scaffold protein, it can assemble and localize various signaling molecules such as protein kinase A (PKA), protein kinase C (PKC), and Src, thereby maintaining the spatial and temporal stability of cellular signal transduction [18]. In a variety of solid tumors, AKAP12 broadly exhibits low expression, and its deletion or downregulation is closely associated with uncontrolled cell cycle, enhanced migration, and malignant progression, making it recognized as a typical tumor suppressor [19, 20]. Notably, AKAP12 can affect downstream signal balance by regulating the activities of PKA and Src [21]. Previous studies have confirmed that Src can promote the nuclear translocation of β-catenin through phosphorylation and activate the transcription of Wnt target genes [22]; while PKA can influence the stability of β-catenin by regulating the activity of GSK3β [23]. However, how AKAP12 regulates the Wnt/β‑catenin signaling pathway during the initiation and progression of HCC remains unknown.

In this study, we initially demonstrated that the elevated expression of PDIA6 serves as a potential biomarker indicative of poor prognosis in HCC, and PDIA6 facilitates HCC proliferation and metastasis in vitro and in vivo. Mechanistically, PDIA6 promotes HCC cell proliferation and metastasis through activation Wnt/β-catenin signaling pathway. Through competitive interaction with USP24, PDIA6 enhances the K48-linked ubiquitin-mediated degradation of AKAP12, thereby relieving the inhibition on the Wnt/β-catenin pathway. Moreover, PDIA6 targeted siRNA shows more profound amelioration effects on Wnt-positive HCC in vivo. This finding not only clarifies the mechanism of action of the PDIA6/AKAP12/β-catenin axis in the occurrence and development of liver cancer but also suggests that PDIA6 may serve as a new molecular therapeutic target for HCC.

Materials and methods

Clinical specimens

Paraffin-embedded, RNAlater-preserved, and − 80 °C frozen HCC tissues with paired normal liver tissues were obtained from the Yichang Central People’s Hospital, China Three Gorges University. Patients who received preoperative anticancer therapies (e.g., chemotherapy, targeted therapy) were excluded. All specimens were collected with written informed consent, and the study was approved by the Ethics Committee of Yichang Central People’s Hospital‌ (2021-086-01). Detailed patients’ information is listed in Table S1.

Transcriptome data

Transcriptomic data for HCC were obtained from The Cancer Genome Atlas (TCGA) and public cohorts (including GSE14520, GSE109211, and others noted in figures) via the Gene Expression Omnibus (GEO). These datasets combined transcriptomic profiles and partial clinical follow-up records, covering tumor tissues and their matched adjacent normal liver tissues, generated through RNA-seq and microarray platforms.

Initial steps included evaluating expression distributions across datasets, with normalization applied to address significant batch effects. Principal component analysis (PCA) assessed overall sample clustering, and outliers were removed to ensure analytical robustness. For datasets with clinical data, variables such as demographics, histopathological grades, tumor stages, and survival outcomes were curated, extracted, and standardized to support correlation analyses and prognostic modeling. Normalized matrices were then used for downstream differential expression analysis, correlation analysis, and survival studies to investigate the expression patterns, clinical significance, and potential biological functions of PDIA6 in HCC.

RNA‑seq and data analysis

Hep3B cells transfected with PDIA6 overexpression plasmid or empty vector were harvested 48 h post‑transfection. Total RNA was extracted using TRIzol reagent, and library construction was performed using the VAHTS mRNA‑seq Library Prep Kit. Sequencing was carried out on the DNBSEQ‑T7 platform (MGI Tech) with 150‑bp paired‑end reads. Raw reads were filtered to remove adapter sequences and low‑quality reads using fastp. Clean reads were aligned to the human reference genome (GRCh38) using HISAT2, and gene counts were quantified with featureCounts. Differential expression analysis was performed using DESeq2, with |log2(fold change)| ≥ 1 and adjusted p < 0.05 considered statistically significant. Gene Ontology and KEGG pathway enrichment analyses were conducted using clusterProfiler. All experiments were performed with three biological replicates.

GSVA analysis

Gene Set Variation Analysis was conducted using the GSVA R package (v1.46.0). The normalized expression matrix, filtered for protein-coding genes with standardized symbols, was analyzed against standard gene sets downloaded from MSigDB. Significance of group differences was assessed via t-tests with Benjamini-Hochberg correction. Results were visualized using pheatmap and ggplot2.

Cell culture

The HCC cell lines (MHCC97L, HCCLM3, Hep3B, SNU-387, and HepG2), the HEK-293T cell line, and the immortalized normal liver cell lines (MIHA and THLE-2) were obtained from the China Center for Type Culture Collection (CCTCC) in 2023. All cell lines were authenticated via short tandem repeat (STR) profiling and routinely tested negative for mycoplasma contamination.

MHCC97L, HCCLM3, Hep3B, HepG2, and HEK-293T cells were cultured in high-glucose Dulbecco’s Modified Eagle’s Medium (DMEM, 4.5 g/L D-glucose). SNU-387 cells were maintained in RPMI-1640 medium. MIHA and THLE-2 cells were cultured in a dedicated medium according to the manufacturer’s instructions. All culture media were supplemented with 10% fetal bovine serum (FBS; Vazyme; Cat. No. F101-01) and 1% penicillin-streptomycin solution. All cell lines were incubated at 37℃ in a humidified atmosphere containing 5% CO2. Cells were subcultured upon reaching 80–90% confluence using 0.25% trypsin-EDTA. All experiments were performed with cells within 6 months of resuscitation or fewer than 20 passages to ensure stable genetic characteristics.

Cell counting kit-8 (CCK-8) assay

Cell number was assessed using the CCK-8 assay, in accordance with previously established protocols [24]. Briefly, HCC cells were inoculated into 96-well microplates at a density of 3 × 103 cells per well and allowed to adhere overnight under standard culture conditions. Following the indicated treatments, 10 µL of CCK-8 reagent (Dojindo Laboratories) was introduced to each well, and the plates were incubated for an additional 60 min at 37 °C. Optical density (OD) was subsequently quantified at 450 nm using the microplate reader. Relative OD was normalized to the vehicle control group, with data representing the mean of no less than three independent biological replicates.

Colony formation assay

Tumor cells in the exponential growth phase were harvested and suspended as single cells. They were subsequently inoculated into 6-well plates at the density of 1000 cells per well and maintained at 37 °C in a 5% CO2 environment. The growth medium was replenished every three days. Following a culture period of 10 to 14 days, the resulting colonies were fixed and visualized by staining with 0.1% crystal violet. Clusters comprising more than 50 cells were identified as colonies and quantified manually or via imageJ. The colony formation efficiency was derived from the ratio of countable colonies to the initial seeding density. Data were obtained from three independent experimental trials.

Western blot

Western blot was performed as previously reported [24]. Briefly, cells were lysed in RIPA buffer supplemented with protease and phosphatase inhibitor cocktails. Lysates were clarified by centrifugation at 12,000 × g for 15 min at 4 °C, and protein concentrations were determined using a BCA assay (Pierce). Equal amounts of protein (20–50 µg) were separated by SDS-PAGE (8–12% gels) and transferred onto PVDF membranes (Millipore). Membranes were blocked with 5% non-fat milk in TBST for 1 h at room temperature and then incubated with the indicated primary antibodies overnight at 4 °C (typical dilution, 1:1,000). After washing with TBST, membranes were incubated with HRP-conjugated secondary antibodies for 1 h at room temperature (typical dilution, 1:5,000). Signals were developed using enhanced chemiluminescence (ECL) reagents and captured with a ChemiDoc imaging system (Bio-Rad). Band intensities were quantified using ImageJ and normalized to β-actin. Detailed information for all antibodies and key reagents used in this study is provided in Supplementary Table S2.

Transwell assay

Transwell invasion and migration assays were performed as previously described [25]. To assess metastatic potential, Transwell assays were conducted. Cells were first deprived of serum overnight to synchronize growth. For invasion analysis, the upper compartments of 8-µm pore inserts (Corning) were coated with Matrigel (BD Biosciences; 1:8 dilution) and incubated for 30 min to form a matrix barrier. Migration assays were performed identically but without the Matrigel coating. Cells were resuspended in serum-free medium and seeded at a density of 1 × 104 cells per insert (200 µL volume). Complete culture medium (600 µL) was added to the lower reservoir to induce chemotaxis. After 24 h of culture, non-migrating cells were wiped from the top of the membrane. The cells on the lower surface were then fixed (4% paraformaldehyde) and visualized via crystal violet staining (0.1%). Migrated or invaded cells were imaged and quantified in five random microscopic fields per well. All assays were conducted in triplicate.

Immunohistochemical scoring

IHC staining was quantified using ImageJ software (NIH, Bethesda, MD, USA). For each tissue section, five randomly selected high‑power fields (×400) were captured. The H‑score was calculated as: H‑score = Σ(percentage of positive cells × staining intensity), where staining intensity was graded as 0 (negative), 1 (weak), 2 (moderate), or 3 (strong), and the percentage of positive cells ranged from 0% to 100%. The H‑score thus ranged from 0 to 300. To facilitate comparison across groups, all H‑scores were normalized to the mean value of the control group, which was set to 1. All scoring was performed independently by two pathologists blinded to the sample groups.

Quantitative PCR (qPCR)

RNA extraction was performed using TRIzol reagent (Invitrogen). Following quantification via a NanoDrop instrument (Thermo Fisher Scientific), RNA integrity was confirmed based on A260/A280 ratios (1.8–2.0). First-strand cDNA synthesis was then conducted using a cDNA Reverse Transcription Kit (Vazyme), with 1 µg of total RNA input per 20-µL reaction. For transcriptional analysis, qPCR assays were carried out using the SYBR Green Mix (Vazyme) on a Novogene Real-Time PCR platform. Each 20-µL reaction incorporated cDNA template (2 µL), specific primers (0.5 µL each at 10 µM), Master Mix (10 µL), and water. The amplification protocol involved a 10-minute activation step at 95 °C, followed by 40 cycles of denaturation (95 °C, 15 s) and annealing/extension (60 °C, 1 min). Specificity was validated through melting curve analysis. Relative expression levels were determined via the comparative cycle threshold (2−ΔΔCt) method, with GAPDH serving as the normalization reference. All assays were performed in triplicate across three independent experiments.The specific primers used for qRT-PCR were provided in Supplementary Table S3.

Plasmid construction and cell transfections

The Flag-PDIA6, GST-AKAP12, pCDH-vector, pCDH-PDIA6, pCDH-AKAP12, and pCDH-CTNNB1 plasmids were designed and constructed by Jima (China). The Flag-PDIA6 and HA-PDIA6 plasmids, a series of Flag-tagged PDIA6 truncation mutants (Flag-ΔThioredoxin1, Flag-ΔThioredoxin2, and Flag-ΔC), HA-AKAP12 and Flag-AKAP12 plasmids, ubiquitin-related plasmids (His-Ub, His-Ub-K48O, His-Ub-K63O, His-Ub-K6O, His-Ub-K11O, His-Ub-K27O, His-Ub-K29O, and His-Ub-K33O), as well as HA-tagged deubiquitinase plasmids (HA-USP24, HA-GATase, HA-M, HA-UCH, HA-DUF3517, and HA-ΔUCH) were designed and constructed by Jikai (China). All constructs were verified by DNA sequencing. Transient transfections were performed using JetPRIME® (Polyplus-transfection S.A., Illkirch, France) according to the manufacturer’s instructions.

RNA interference, RNA isolation, and real-time PCR

The siRNA oligonucleotides targeting PDIA6 and AKAP12 (siPDIA6#1, siPDIA6#2, and siAKAP12), together with the negative control siRNA (siNC), were designed and synthesized by the indicated vendors. Lentiviruses carrying shRNAs against PDIA6 (shPDIA6#1, shPDIA6#2, and shPDIA6#3) and the negative control (shNC) were obtained from Genechem (China). All siRNA and shRNA sequences used in this study are listed in Supplementary Table S4.

Co-IP and LC-MS

Plasmid-transfected cells were harvested and lysed in IP buffer (20 mM Tris-HCl, pH 7.4; 150 mM NaCl; 1 mM EDTA, pH 8.0; 1% NP-40; 1× protease and phosphatase inhibitor cocktail). Cell lysates were clarified by centrifugation at 12,000 × g for 10 min at 4 °C, and the protein concentration of the resulting supernatant was quantified using a BCA assay kit (Beyotime, P0010S). To reduce non-specific binding, the lysates were pre-cleared with 20 µL Protein A/G magnetic beads (MCE, Shanghai) for 30 min. Subsequently, fresh Protein A/G magnetic beads (20 µL) were incubated with immunoprecipitation-grade antibodies at a ratio of 2 µg antibody per 1 mg total protein, and the mixtures were rotated overnight at 4 °C. Beads were captured using a magnetic rack and washed three times with high-salt wash buffer (20 mM Tris-HCl, pH 7.4; 300 mM NaCl; 1 mM EDTA, pH 8.0; 1% NP-40) for 5 min each, followed by three washes with low-salt wash buffer (20 mM Tris-HCl, pH 7.4; 150 mM NaCl; 1 mM EDTA, pH 8.0; 1% NP-40) for 5 min each. Bound proteins were eluted by adding an appropriate volume of 1× SDS loading buffer and boiling at 96 °C for 10 min. The eluates were then briefly centrifuged at 12,000 g for 2 min at 4 °C, and the supernatants were collected for downstream analyses. For LC-MS/MS, samples were resolved by SDS-PAGE, stained with Coomassie Brilliant Blue, destained, and gel bands were excised and submitted to Biotree Biotech (Shanghai, China) for mass spectrometric identification.

GST pull-down assays

Purified GST-AKAP12 and Flag-PDIA6 proteins were mixed at equal amounts (0.5 mg each) and incubated on ice for 3 h with gentle rotation. The reaction mixtures were then loaded onto glutathione Sepharose 4B resin columns to capture GST-tagged complexes. After five washes with wash buffer to remove unbound proteins, the retained proteins were eluted using wash buffer supplemented with 15 mM reduced glutathione. Eluted fractions were resolved by 12% SDS-PAGE, transferred onto PVDF membranes, and immunoblotted with the indicated antibodies. GST alone was included as a negative control in parallel. Each pull-down experiment was independently repeated three times.

Xenograft tumor model

Subcutaneous xenograft tumor model construction was performed as previously reported [26]. All animal models were conducted in compliance with institutional ethical guidelines and approved by the IACUC of Wuhan University. Male and female BALB/c nude mice were obtained from Shanghai SLAC Laboratory Animal Co., Ltd. and maintained under SPF conditions.

Six-week-old male mice were subcutaneously injected with 5 × 106 tumor cells resuspended in 100 µL of Matrigel-supplemented serum-free medium (1:1 ratio). Tumor dimensions were recorded every three days, and volumes were estimated as volume = (length × width2)/2. Mice were euthanized upon reaching the ethical limit (1,500 mm3) or at 2 weeks post-injection. Excised tumors were weighed and processed for downstream histological or molecular examinations.

To evaluate metastatic potential, 6–8-week-old female mice were acclimated for one week prior to experimentation. HCCLM3 cells (1 × 106 cells) were injected into the lateral tail vein. Four Weeks post-injection, lungs were dissected, and the number of surface metastatic nodules was enumerated to assess tumor spread.

SiRNA-loaded liposomes for intratumoral injection

Lipids (DOPC: DOTAP: cholesterol = 5: 3: 2, molar ratio) dissolved in chloroform: methanol (9: 1, v/v) were added to a round-bottom flask. Organic solvents were evaporated via rotary evaporator (37 °C, 150 rpm, 30 min) and residual solvent removed under vacuum overnight to form a lipid film. Sterile PBS (pH 7.4) was added to reach 10 mg/mL lipid concentration; the mixture was incubated at 37 °C for 30 min (100 rpm) to form multilamellar vesicles (MLVs). MLVs were sonicated (30% amplitude, 5 s on / 10 s off, 5 min, ice) to form small unilamellar vesicles (SUVs) with siRNA, then incubated at room temperature for 20 min. The suspension was centrifuged (12,000 × g, 15 min, 4 °C) to remove aggregates; supernatant was ultrafiltered (10 kDa MWCO, 5,000 × g, 10 min, 4 °C) twice with PBS to remove free siRNA. 10 µL complex was injected intratumorally every 3 days once the volume of subcutaneous tumors reaches 100 mm3.

Statistical analysis

Data are presented as mean ± SD and analyzed with SPSS Statistics. Student’s t-test compared two normally distributed groups; one-way ANOVA with Bonferroni (homogeneous variance) or Tamhane T2 (heteroscedastic) post hoc tests analyzed multiple groups. Non-parametric tests were used for non-Gaussian data. Statistical methods and p values are detailed in figure legends. Data are presented as mean ± SD. Statistical significance is denoted as ***p ≤ 0.001, **p ≤ 0.01, *0.01 < p ≤ 0.05, and n.s. for p > 0.05.

Results

Elevated PDIA6 serves as the indicator of poor prognosis in HCC

We initiated our study by mining transcriptomic data from large-scale public repositories (TCGA, ICGC) and multiple GEO series (e.g., GSE6764, GSE10143). As shown in Fig. 1A, PDIA6 was consistently identified as being overexpressed in tumor tissues compared to non-neoplastic controls. To validate these in silico findings, we assessed PDIA6 expression in vitro and in vivo. Consistent with the database analyses, both qRT-PCR and Western blotting revealed generally elevated PDIA6 expression in most HCC cell lines and patient-derived tissues (Fig. 1B-D). Moreover, tissue microarray IHC staining confirmed distinct PDIA6 accumulation in tumor regions (Fig. 1E).

Further investigation into the clinical relevance of PDIA6 revealed a significant positive correlation with Ki67 expression (Fig. 1F) and GSVA scores related to metastasis and cell cycle regulation (Fig. 1G). Survival analysis (Kaplan‑Meier) of the TCGA cohort demonstrated that elevated PDIA6 expression conferred a worse prognosis, evidenced by shorter overall and disease‑free survival times (Fig. 1H and I). Additionally, PDIA6 exhibited high diagnostic accuracy for HCC, achieving an AUC of 0.83 (95% CI: 0.790–0.870) in ROC analysis (Fig. 1J). Taken together, these results establish PDIA6 as a potential proliferation-related biomarker with diagnostic and prognostic utility in HCC.

PDIA6 promotes HCC cell proliferation, invasion

We next sought to determine whether PDIA6 is essential for HCC aggressiveness. Following the successful generation of stable PDIA6-knockdown MHCC97L and HCCLM3 sublines (Fig. 2A), we observed a profound inhibition of cellular viability and clonogenicity (Fig. 2B and C). Similarly, the invasive capability of these cells was significantly blunted upon PDIA6 suppression (Fig. 2D). At the molecular level, this phenotypic shift was accompanied by the restoration of the epithelial marker E-cadherin and the repression of mesenchymal and invasion-related proteins (N-cadherin, MMP-2, MMP-7) (Fig. 2E). Furthermore, PDIA6 deficiency also increased the proportion of apoptotic cell death from approximately 1% in controls to a range of 2–3%, as indicated by an increase in TUNEL‑positive cells (Fig. 2F) and the activation of the intrinsic apoptotic cascade (BAX, cleaved-caspase 3/9), alongside the suppression of Cyclin D1 and CDX2 (Fig. 2G).

To corroborate these findings in vivo, nude mice were inoculated with PDIA6-silenced HCCLM3 cells. As anticipated, tumor volume and weight were significantly suppressed in the knockdown group (Fig. 2H-J), with a concurrent decrease in PCNA proliferation index within the xenograft tissues (Fig. 2K). Moreover, the metastatic potential of HCC cells was markedly abrogated by PDIA6 silencing, as evidenced by a reduction in lung colonization in the tail vein injection model (Fig. 2L). TUNEL staining of subcutaneous tumors (Fig. 2M) and pulmonary metastatic lesions (Fig. 2N) revealed that the apoptotic rate in vivo was higher than that observed in vitro; however, PDIA6 knockdown did not significantly affect apoptosis levels. Collectively, these findings indicate that PDIA6 promotes the proliferation and migration of HCC cells.

PDIA6 promotes activation of the Wnt/β-catenin signaling pathway

Given the generally elevated PDIA6 expression in HCC, we next sought to characterize the PDIA6-regulated transcriptome via RNA-seq. PCA and heatmap visualizations demonstrated a profound shift in the gene expression landscape upon PDIA6 overexpression (Fig. 3A and B). Differential expression analysis identified 650 genes (446 upregulated; 204 downregulated) significantly altered by PDIA6 status (Fig. 3C). Crucially, bioinformatics interrogation—combining internal data with external public databases—unanimously implicated the Wnt signaling pathway as a critical target of PDIA6 (Fig. 3D-G). This regulatory link was further supported by the significant positive correlation between PDIA6 levels and Wnt pathway signatures in large-scale clinical datasets (TCGA/GEO) (Fig. 3H and I).

Mechanistically, we hypothesized that PDIA6 exerts its pro-tumorigenic effects through the Wnt/β-catenin axis. To test this, we conducted “rescue” assays. As expected, the suppression of cell viability, colony growth, and invasive potential induced by PDIA6 knockdown was significantly mitigated by the overexpression of β-catenin in MHCC97L and HCCLM3 cells (Fig. 3J-M). These data provide compelling evidence that PDIA6 promotes HCC proliferation and metastasis via the activation of Wnt/β-catenin signaling.

AKAP12 identified as an interactor of PDIA6 in HCC

To identify the specific effectors mediating PDIA6 function, we integrated Co-IP/MS screening with quantitative proteomics (Fig. 4A). Proteomic profiling demonstrated that PDIA6 overexpression induces a significant shift in the cellular protein landscape (PCA analysis; Fig. 4B), resulting in 298 differentially expressed proteins (Fig. 4C). Intersection analysis of the physical interactome (Co-IP/MS) and the functional proteome identified AKAP12 as the sole overlapping candidate, suggesting it is both a binding partner and a regulatory target of PDIA6 (Fig. 4D).

We subsequently verified this interaction in cultured cell lines and using purified recombinant proteins. Endogenous Co-IP assays demonstrated robust binding between PDIA6 and AKAP12 in HCC cell lines (Fig. 4E and F), and immunofluorescence confirmed their cytoplasmic colocalization (Fig. 4G). Furthermore, GST-pulldown assays validated a direct molecular interaction (Fig. 4H).

Investigating the clinical relevance, we found that low AKAP12 expression is a predictor of poor prognosis in HCC (Fig. 4I and J). Crucially, molecular assays revealed that PDIA6 negatively regulates AKAP12 at the protein level but not the transcriptional level (Fig. 4K–N). Collectively, these data support a model where PDIA6 promotes HCC progression by directly binding to and destabilizing the tumor suppressor AKAP12.

PDIA6 enhances HCC through decreasing AKAP12 expression

We next verified the functional relationship between PDIA6 and AKAP12. In functional assays, AKAP12 acted as a potent tumor suppressor; its overexpression dramatically reduced cell viability and clonogenicity. Crucially, the introduction of exogenous PDIA6 was unable to counteract the suppressive effects of AKAP12 overexpression (Fig. 5A-C), a pattern mirrored in both subcutaneous tumor growth and lung metastasis models (Fig. 5D-F). This suggests that high levels of AKAP12 can override PDIA6-driven signaling.

To further substantiate this regulatory axis, we performed a “rescue” experiment by overexpressing AKAP12 in PDIA6-overexpressing cells. Whereas PDIA6 overexpression alone markedly enhanced cell proliferation and colony formation, concomitant AKAP12 overexpression effectively reversed these malignant phenotypes (Fig. 5G-I). In vivo experiments further supported this mechanism: the increases in tumor volume and the PCNA proliferative index observed in the PDIA6-overexpression group were significantly mitigated when AKAP12 was simultaneously overexpressed (Fig. 5J-L). Collectively, these results demonstrate that AKAP12 can counteract the PDIA6-driven enhancement of HCC proliferative and metastatic capacities in both in vitro and in vivo models, whereas PDIA6 is unable to override the tumor-suppressive effects of AKAP12 in HCC.

PDIA6 destabilizes AKAP12 via ubiquitination-proteasome dependent manner

We next investigated the molecular mechanism by which PDIA6 induces AKAP12 downregulation. Protein stability assays showed that loss of PDIA6 prolongs the half-life of AKAP12 (Fig. 6A). Using domain deletion mutants, we identified the Thioredoxin1 domain of PDIA6 as the critical region required for binding to AKAP12 (Fig. 6B); notably, overexpression of PDIA6 lacking this domain (Δ-Thioredoxin1) failed to promote AKAP12 degradation (Fig. 6C). In addition, treatment of both PDIA6-si and NC-si cell lines with the proteasome inhibitor MG-132 markedly increased AKAP12 expression in NC-si cells. Notably, after MG-132 treatment, AKAP12 levels became comparable between NC-si and PDIA6-si cells, with no significant difference observed. In contrast, treatment with the lysosomal inhibitor chloroquine (CQ) did not produce these changes (Fig. 6D), indicating that PDIA6 promotes AKAP12 degradation through the ubiquitin–proteasome pathway.

PDIA6 downregulates AKAP12 expression via ubiquitination-proteasome system. (A) PDIA6 destabilizes AKAP12 protein. (B) Immuoprecipitation analysis between AKAP12 and PDIA6 truncation mutants. (C) △Thioredoxin 1-deleted mutant loses the ability to decrease AKAP12 levels. (D) PDIA6 decreases AKAP12 levels in a proteasome-dependent manner. (E-F) PDIA6 silencing decreases exogenous AKAP12 ubiquitination level in MHCC97L (E) and HCCLM3 (F) cells. (G) PDIA6 interference increases exogenous AKAP12 K48-type ubiquitination level in MHCC97L cells. (H-I) AKAP12 interacts with USP24 supported by in vivo immunoprecipitation. (J) PDIA6 adding disrupts the interaction between AKAP12 and USP24. (K) USP24 accounts for the ubiquitination-enhancing effects of PDIA6

Mechanistically, PDIA6 promoted the accumulation of ubiquitinated AKAP12 species (Fig. 6E and F) and specifically enhanced K48 linked ubiquitin chains (Fig. S1A, 6G). Because PDIA6 is not an E3 ubiquitin ligase, we hypothesized that it may act by modulating a deubiquitinase (DUB). Interactome analysis identified USP24 as a specific binding partner of PDIA6 (Fig. 4A). We found that USP24 directly binds PDIA6 via its UCH domain (Fig. S1B) and also interacts with AKAP12 (Fig. 6H and I). Importantly, PDIA6 functions as a competitive inhibitor: its presence disrupts the USP24–AKAP12 interaction (Fig. 6J), and AKAP12 specifically binds to the UCH domain of USP24 (Fig. S1C). Consequently, PDIA6 impairs USP24 dependent deubiquitination of AKAP12, leading to increased ubiquitination and accelerated degradation (Fig. 6K). Collectively, PDIA6 drives AKAP12 degradation by blocking the protective function of USP24.

AKAP12 mediates the suppressive effect on Wnt/β-catenin signaling

Examination of clinical samples highlighted the pathological relevance of the PDIA6-AKAP12 axis. We observed a marked reduction in AKAP12 expression in HCC tissues relative to matched controls (Fig. 7A). Notably, a strong negative correlation was established between PDIA6 and AKAP12 protein levels in patient samples (Fig. 7B), supporting the hypothesis that PDIA6 destabilizes AKAP12 in vivo.

AKAP12 suppresses Wnt/Catenin signaling via activating PKA. (A) AKAP12 IHC staining of HCC and paired non-tumor adjacent tissue. (B) AKAP12 protein level shows negative correlation with PDIA6 protein levels in HCC samples. (C-D) AKAP12 shows negative correlation with Wnt signaling inTCGA-LIHC (C) and GSE14520 (D) cohort. (E-F) AKAP12 suppresses the expression of targets downstream β-catenin. (G) IHC staining of subcutaneous tumors. (H) The PKA activator cw008 reversed the activation of the Wnt/β-catenin signaling pathway induced by AKAP12 knockdown. (I) PKA inhibitor promoted the expression of targeted genes of Wnt/β-catenin signaling pathway. (J) The PKA inhibitor H89 reversed the suppression of the Wnt/β-catenin signaling pathway induced by AKAP12 overexpression

Furthermore, bioinformatic analysis linked AKAP12 deficiency to hyperactive Wnt signaling (Fig. 7C and D). We validated this functional link in vitro: ectopic expression of AKAP12 downregulated the β-catenin target genes c-Myc and Cyclin D1 (Fig. 7E and F). To confirm the hierarchy of this signaling axis, we performed a final rescue assay. The upregulation of Wnt targets induced by PDIA6 was significantly reversed by AKAP12 overexpression (Fig. 7G). Meanwhile, the PKA activator cw008 reversed the AKAP12 knockdown induced upregulation of the Wnt/β-catenin pathway components c-Myc and Cyclin D1; concurrently, phosphorylation of GSK3β was examined as a readout of PKA activation (Fig. 7H). In contrast, treatment with the PKA inhibitor H89 decreased GSK3β phosphorylation but increased c-Myc and Cyclin D1 expression (Fig. 7I). H89 also abrogated the suppressive effect of AKAP12 overexpression on these Wnt/β-catenin targets (Fig. 7J). In summary, our results demonstrate that, as a downstream effector of PDIA6, AKAP12 suppresses the Wnt/β-catenin pathway via PKA, thereby exerting a tumor suppressive role in HCC.

In vivo validation of the PDIA6–AKAP12–β-catenin axis

We next evaluated whether PDIA6 represents a viable therapeutic target in vivo, specifically in the context of Wnt pathway activation. Liposome-encapsulated PDIA6-siRNA was administered to mice bearing either control or β-catenin-overexpressing xenografts. While β-catenin overexpression drove aggressive tumor growth in the absence of treatment, it also created a distinct dependency on PDIA6.

Strikingly, PDIA6 knockdown exerted a stronger inhibitory effect on Wnt-high tumors than on basal controls. Quantitative analysis showed that PDIA6-siRNA reduced the volume of β-catenin-overexpressing tumors by 40% (relative to their NC-si controls), whereas control tumors were reduced by only 21% (Fig. 8A-D). This differential sensitivity indicates that hyperactive Wnt signaling exacerbates the tumor’s reliance on PDIA6. After setting a common endpoint at day 24, we found that PDIA6‑si treatment reduced tumor volume by approximately 55% in the control group and by 70% in the β‑catenin overexpression group, further supporting its therapeutic efficacy (Fig. 8E-G).

(E-G) Tumor image, tumor weight analysis and tumor growth curve, assessed till day 24 post-subcutaneous injection. Mechanistically, this tumor regression was accompanied by the downregulation of key Wnt targets at the mRNA/protein level (Fig. 8H and I) and a significant decrease in Ki67 positivity and c-Myc intensity (Fig. 8J). These findings suggest that PDIA6 inhibition effectively disrupts the Wnt signaling axis in vivo, making it a potent therapeutic approach for Wnt-driven liver cancer.

Discussion

The Protein Disulfide Isomerase family is well-documented in tumor progression, typically relying on its enzymatic disulfide isomerase activity [27–29]. For example, PDIA4 drives lung cancer via the STAT3 axis [30]. PDIA3P1 promotes esophageal cancer by preventing MARCH8-mediated HK2 degradation [31]. PDIA4 binds to TAX1BP1 and inhibits the activation of c-Jun N-terminal kinase (JNK), which in turn blocks the degradation of Tax1-binding protein 1 (TAX1BP1) and is associated with radiotherapy resistance in triple-negative breast cancer [32]. However, emerging evidence suggests that PDI proteins can also function as non-enzymatic scaffolds or chaperones. A notable example is nuclear PDIA3, which guides T cell differentiation independently of its catalytic site [33]. In alignment with this non-canonical paradigm, our study reveals that PDIA6 promotes HCC not through direct enzymatic catalysis, but by destabilizing the tumor suppressor AKAP12. We demonstrate that PDIA6 amplifies Wnt/β-catenin signaling pathway in HCC cells by reducing the stability of AKAP12 protein, thereby promoting HCC progression. Clinically, this defines a therapeutic window: PDIA6 inhibition is particularly potent in Wnt-hyperactive HCC, suggesting it could serve as a precision target for this subtype.

Dysregulated ubiquitination is a hallmark of HCC pathogenesis [34–36]. USP14-mediated deubiquitination of TUBA1A at K370 enhances the stability of TUBA1A and induces the proliferation and metastasis of HCC cells [37]. It has been discovered that the E3 ubiquitin ligase FBXO7 promotes the degradation of PRMT1 through ubiquitination, thereby impairing the arginine methylation and activation of PHGDH. This leads to impaired serine synthesis, accumulation of reactive oxygen species (ROS), and ultimately inhibits the growth of HCC cells [38]. USP13 can remove K29- and K48-linked polyubiquitin chains from WWP1 and stabilize it, thereby activating the Akt/mTOR signaling pathway in liver cancer [39]. Meanwhile, some adaptor proteins can also affect the progression of liver cancer by interfering with the ubiquitination-proteasome degradation process. In liver cancer, PIP5K1A competitively binds to the Kelch domain of Kelch-like ECH-associated protein 1 in a kinase-independent manner, causing NRF2 to escape ubiquitination and degradation, thus promoting NRF2-dependent transcription and inhibiting ferroptosis [40]. OTUB1 can also affect the ubiquitination level and protein stability of RACK1 through its non-canonical E3 ligase activity [24]. Here, we identify a novel competitive mechanism: PDIA6 acts as a molecular “interceptor.” Through its first Thioredoxin domain, PDIA6 binds to the UCH domain of the deubiquitinase USP24. This interaction sterically hinders USP24 from binding to AKAP12, thereby preventing its deubiquitination. Consequently, AKAP12 accumulates K48-linked polyubiquitin chains and undergoes proteasomal degradation, fueling HCC progression.

The link between AKAP12 and Wnt signaling has been underappreciated. As a scaffold protein, AKAP12 compartmentalizes PKA and PKC [41, 42]. However, there is report indicating that PKA acts as a negative regulator of Wnt/β-catenin signaling. PKA can phosphorylate β-catenin at Ser45, priming it for degradation independently of the Axin complex [43]. Additionally, PKA suppresses Wnt signaling in adrenal tumorigenesis [44]. In contrast, literature reports rarely mention that PKC directly regulates the Wnt/β-catenin signaling pathway; instead, it is more commonly recognized that the Wnt/β-catenin signal can activate the PKC signaling pathway [45, 46]. In the present research, we demonstrated that PKA activation reversed the promoting effect of AKAP12 knockdown on the Wnt/β-catenin signaling pathway. Therefore, we hypothesize that PDIA6-AKAP12 may regulate the activity of the Wnt/β-catenin signaling pathway in a PKA-dependent manner.

In conclusion, we identify PDIA6 upregulation as a prognostic biomarker for poor survival in HCC. Functionally, PDIA6 drives tumor growth and metastasis by activating Wnt/β-catenin signaling pathway. Mechanistically, PDIA6 functions as a competitive inhibitor of USP24, blocking the deubiquitination and stabilization of AKAP12. This leads to AKAP12 degradation and the subsequent release of Wnt signaling inhibition. Crucially, our in vivo data suggest that PDIA6 targeting offers enhanced therapeutic efficacy in Wnt-driven HCC, highlighting its potential for stratified cancer therapy. A limitation of this study is the use of intratumoral siRNA injection, which does not directly model clinically translatable systemic delivery. Future studies should explore targeted delivery vehicles, such as lipid nanoparticles or antibody-conjugated systems, for potential clinical application.
