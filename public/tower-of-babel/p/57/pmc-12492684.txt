Comparison of teacher-standardized patients and peer role-play in assessing medical students’ history-taking skills
Authors: Yingying Wei, Na Deng, Hongmei Zheng, Haijun Guo, Huiling Zhang, Bing Li, Daiqin Wu, Li wei, Xiaoyun Si
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
BackgroundStandardized patients (SPs) are widely utilized in the training of medical students’ history-taking skills. However, due to certain limitations, many institutions still rely on teachers and students to act as SPs. Despite this practice, there is limited research comparing the teaching effectiveness of these two models. Therefore, this study aims to examine the differences between the teacher standardized patient (TSP) and peer role play (pRP) models in the development of students' history-taking abilities, with a further analysis of the variations between scripted and non-scripted pRP approaches.MethodsFrom May to June 2023, 630 undergraduate third-year medical students majoring in clinical medicine participated in the study. Students were randomly divided into either the TSP or pRP group (both scripted pRP (spRP) and non-scripted pRP (non-spRP)). All groups participated in the same half-hour theory lecture before practicing history taking. After the course, we assessed students' classroom performance and documented their performance in medical record writing skills as well as their clinical history-taking scores during consultations with real patients. Additionally, students' satisfaction with the course was evaluated through a questionnaire. Pre- and post-course self-assessment forms enabled understanding of students’ cognition and self-perceived confidence in their interview skills.ResultsThere were statistically significant differences in the scores of the TSP, spRP, and non-spRP groups in group-based consultation assessment (p < 0.05), whereas there were no statistically significant differences in the scores of medical record writing (p > 0.05). The mean scores of students' overall attitudinal evaluation of the course had no statistically significant differences (p > 0.05). The mean scores of students' attitudes toward clinical skills development had no statistically significant differences (p > 0.05); the mean scores of students' attitudes towards teamwork had a statistically significant difference (p < 0.05). The pre-simulation self-assessment of all three groups was higher than their post-simulation elf-assessment, and the differences were statistically significant (all p < 0.05).ConclusionRP is more suitable for a history-taking practical course for junior students, which can mobilize the classroom atmosphere, increase students' interest and motivation, and develop students' sense of teamwork; spRP’s performance on the interrogation skills test was higher. Students' self-confidence and attention to the course had an impact on their self-assessment evaluation.

Introduction

The history-taking course is mandatory for medical students before entering clinical practice and includes skills training for history taking and doctor-patient communication. Obtaining a comprehensive patient history and possessing good doctor-patient communication skills can help in diagnosing the disease and establishing a harmonious doctor-patient relationship, improving patient satisfaction and compliance [1, 2]. Previous studies have confirmed that doctor-patient communication skills and history-taking skills can be taught and enhanced as part of the curriculum [3]. However, most medical schools in China currently do not pay enough attention to training medical students in doctor-patient communication skills, and students are not very motivated to learn on their own [4]. Existing studies suggest that educational programs frequently prioritize theoretical knowledge acquisition at the expense of cultivating applied communication competencies [5]. In a study on the organization of humanistic medical education teaching in 81 independently established medical schools across China, it was found that only 61.7% of the institutions offered courses in doctor-patient communication, of which only 27. 1% made these courses mandatory for students [6]. The teaching quality of doctor-patient communication training courses varies and the class hours are short; therefore, we need to find the best way to enhance students'motivation and attention and improve their doctor-patient communication skills within the limited teaching time [7].

Various studies have previously demonstrated that students are able to learn history-taking skills through different teaching methods, but there is no consensus as to which method is more effective and for which student population. Both standardized patients (SPs) and role play (RP) have previously been shown to have positive facilitative effects in teaching history taking [8–13]. SPs are individuals systematically trained to simulate clinical symptoms and conditions with high fidelity. While extensively employed in developed nations, the implementation and evaluation of SP programs in developing countries'medical institutions remain underexplored [14]. A 2016 national survey across 25 Chinese provinces (n=183 SP educators from 80 medical centers) documented mean hourly compensation rates of 12.60 for teaching activities and 18.82 for physical examination simulations, with a median annual training frequency of 12.9 sessions [15]. Comparative cost analyses in traditional Chinese medicine education revealed significantly lower expenditures for teacher standardized patients (TSPs: $7,590.00) versus conventional SPs ($17,415.60) [16]. Furthermore, SPs face their own set of challenges. Chinese standardized patient educators (SPEs) have noted that it is difficult for SPs to remember key points and effectively portray emotions that match the case scenario. Training SPs to provide appropriate feedback to medical students also presents a challenge. As a result, approximately 50% of standardized patients reported difficulties in aligning their feedback with the educational goals of medical students during clinical interactions [15, 17, 18]. Moreover, there is a shortage of SP trainers in the market, with most trainers being clinical doctors or nurses. This shortage is one of the main factors hindering the widespread use of SPs in educational activities [19]. The training and utilization of SPs involve considerable time and financial expenditures. Consequently, there is a continued focus on developing training models that are both feasible and cost-effective, while maintaining practical applicability. TSPs are an evolution of SPs, with the distinction that the patient role is assumed by clinicians or nurses possessing professional expertise. For students who have not yet encountered clinical practice, a knowledgeable SP, such as a TSP, may be more advantageous, as they can offer immediate feedback tailored to the student's performance and inquiries. TSP have been shown to improve students'doctor-patient communication skills and clinical skills [20]. Previous studies have demonstrated the feasibility, practicality, and cost-effectiveness of this approach, suggesting its potential as a viable alternative to SP simulation [16]. In a study involving senior residents as SPs, 72% of medical students reported that their performance as SPs was not influenced by their faculty status, 68% claimed the ability to perceive faculty members as authentic patients, and 96% indicated that this pedagogical assessment method facilitated their professional development [21]. However, TSPs exhibit limitations and fail to fully address learners'needs. Our previous research revealed that medical students demonstrated greater interest in student SPs (SSPs) and perceived SSPs as more effective for honing physician-patient communication skills [22]. Although faculty members possess advantages in knowledge transfer, their involvement may inadvertently create a tense teaching atmosphere, potentially contributing to reduced student satisfaction [22]. In contrast, SSPs integrate more seamlessly into peer groups, engage actively in discussions, and foster a relaxed learning environment that mitigates anxiety. This approach enables students to promptly identify knowledge gaps and enhance learning outcomes [23–25]. RP effectively enhances clinical communication skills, teamwork competencies, and empathic capacity in medical students by simulating authentic healthcare scenarios where participants assume roles such as physicians, nurses, or patients. Previous studies suggest that peer-role-play (pRP) may cultivate greater empathy toward patient concerns compared to standardized patient (SP) methodologies, supporting its integration into medical curricula [12]. However, current evidence remains inconclusive regarding the optimal strategy for training history-taking skills, with no definitive superiority established between these approaches [26]. While SP-based training demonstrates proven efficacy, curriculum planners must weigh its benefits against the modest cost reduction achievable through pRP in residency education [27]. Consequently, our study systematically compares TSP simulations with RP in history-taking instruction. Notably, RP inherently involves clinical scenario dramatization, raising pedagogical questions about script provision. Although prior research predominantly tasked students with self-developed scripts based on thematic guidelines, this approach poses significant challenges for learners lacking foundational knowledge in internal medicine [28]. To address this, we stratified participants into script-provided and non-script-provided cohorts to evaluate differential educational outcomes.

In this study, we set up TSP and RP teaching groups (RP was divided into scripted peer role play (spRP) and non-scripted peer role play (non-spRP)) to assess students'history-taking skills and investigate students'attitudes toward different teaching methods and their self-assessment ratings pre- and post-course to explore the more suitable teaching method for training students in history-taking skills. We hypothesize that the application of both RP and TSP could effectively enhance medical students'history-taking competence, communication skills, and self-assessment scores. Specifically, the RP approach may demonstrate superior efficacy in stimulating students'learning motivation and engagement compared to TSP. However, subgroup analysis suggests that the non-spRP cohort might exhibit lower satisfaction levels than the spRP group, while the spRP intervention appears to yield better performance outcomes in structured history-taking assessments.

Methods

Participants and grouping

The study cohort comprised 633 third-year clinical medicine students enrolled across five classes at Guizhou Medical University. Participants were recruited through the university's history-taking laboratory course administered during the May–June 2023 academic term. The 633 participants were stratified into 24 experimental clusters through a computer-generated randomization protocol. Each cluster was subsequently subdivided into eight working units comprising 5–6 participants. Group allocation followed these experimental conditions: TSP groups (assigned to clusters numbered 1 + 3n, n ≥ 0) received chief complaints without diagnosis. They were required to complete preliminary learning on how to conduct clinical consultations beforehand by self-directed study or collaborate in peer discussions. spRP groups (assigned to clusters numbered 2 + 3n, n ≥ 0) received case scripts including chief complaints, scenarios, doctor-patient conflict settings, patient's background information and clinical history information without diagnosis; non-spRP groups (assigned to clusters numbered 3 + 3n, n ≥ 0) received chief complaints and diagnosis without scripts, which were created by students. In the RP group, both of them would take turns playing the roles of patient, doctor, and evaluator to simulate clinical decision-making processes. They would continuously refine their performance throughout the role-playing process. All participants were informed consent. in accordance with Declaration of Helsinki guidelines prior to study initiation. This study was approved by the ethics committee of the Guizhou medical University(No. 266 (2023)).

Teaching arrangement

A total of 633 students from five third-year clinical programs participated in the study, with each class rotating through separate sessions. Students in each class were divided into three major groups (TSP group, spRP group, and non-spRP groups). Each major group was then randomly subdivided into eight small groups consisting of 5–6 students. All students had received prior training in medical interviewing, history-taking, doctor-patient communication, and diagnostic theory before the study began, but had not yet been exposed to internal medicine content. The course comprised 8 h in total: the first 4 h were dedicated to practicing medical interviews, while the remaining 4 h focused on summarizing interview techniques, medical record writing, and clinical reasoning. Eight instructors participated in the course. All participating instructors were senior attending physicians specializing in cardiology, respiratory medicine, gastroenterology, endocrinology, nephrology, hematology, and rheumatology/immunology. These physicians were fully dedicated to teaching, held qualifications as examiners for the National Medical Licensing Examination, and possessed extensive experience in mentoring medical interns and resident trainees. The TSPs in the study were selected from these eight instructors. Prior to the research, all participating physicians underwent TSP training conducted by the head of the internal medicine teaching and research group, an expert with extensive experience in medical education and simulated standardized patients. Given the instructors'strong clinical expertise, the training primarily focused on avoiding medical jargon and realistically mimicking patients'expressions and physical mannerisms. All instructors demonstrated satisfactory performance and passed the post-training assessment. Each instructor developed case scenarios and scripts tailored to their specialty. For each class, two instructors were randomly selected from the eight to serve as TSPs, while the remaining six were assigned to supervise four student groups each during interview practice sessions, with random allocation across classes. Each half-hour theoretical lecture was taught by the same instructor, as classes were held separately. Except for the two TSPs, the remaining six instructors were involved in the students’ interview practice, with each instructor assigned to supervise four student groups during the final practice stage. Teaching effect evaluation

The assessment of teaching effectiveness comprised three domains: comprehensive abilities, satisfaction with the course, and self-assessment of history-taking skills.

Medical record writing and comprehensive abilities

Comprehensive abilities included medical record writing skills, patient interview skills and formative assessment. Medical record writing and patient interview skills were conducted during the final phase of the study, where students were assessed based on their performance in clinical interviews with real patients and on the final medical record submitted by students. The comprehensive ability assessment was conducted on a group basis, while medical record writing was assessed on an individual basis. The evaluation of patient interview skills integrates the Arizona Clinical Interview Rating (ACIR) criteria, with additional scoring components for self-introduction and clear explanation of the consultation purpose to the patient [29]. The formative assessment evaluated students’ competencies in three areas: in-class performance, clinical reasoning, and team collaboration. The formative assessment was derived from two components: (1) a 10-min patient interview conducted by each group during the initial study phase, and (2) the first submitted medical record. Instructors rated students based on their fulfillment of three distinct roles: physician, evaluator, and team collaborator. Notably, in the TSP group, the"patient"role was performed by instructors rather than students; to ensure fairness, the performance of student actors in the patient role was excluded from scoring. The assessment of clinical reasoning was based on a comprehensive evaluation of the diagnosis, diagnostic rationale, and treatment plan sections within the comprehensive medical records submitted by group. Formative assessment in this context employs group-level scoring, where all members receive identical grades. The pedagogical rationale centers on developing collaborative competencies and institutionalizing peer-driven deliberative learning practices. Scoring is implemented as follows: group-based weighting applies to the patient interview and formative assessments, while medical record documentation undergoes individual scoring. Students’ satisfaction with the course

Satisfaction with the course was evaluated on a 5-point Likert scale, ranging from 1 (strongly dissatisfied/disapproved) to 5 (strongly satisfied/strongly approved), based on the following three dimensions: ① evaluation of the overall attitude toward the course (6 items); ② evaluation of attitude toward clinical skills development (3 items); ③ evaluation of attitude toward teamwork (4 items). All are described by mean scores, with 3.5 or more indicating positive attitudes.

Questionnaire assessment results

Questionnaire

The questionnaire was filled out anonymously by 630 students. After excluding three invalid questionnaires, 627 questionnaires (99.5% recovery rate) were used for analysis, including 207 in the SP group, 212 in the spRP group, and 208 in the non-spRP group. The reliability of the questionnaire was good (α = 0.962). The KMO test showed that the questionnaire variables were strongly correlated and could be used for factor analysis (KMO value = 0.939), and Bartlett's sphericity test showed that the correlation coefficient was not a unit array and was suitable for factor analysis (p < 0.001).

Discussion

International researches have compared SP and TSP teaching models for social populations and showed that students benefit more through TSP [30]. Previous studies have shown that different teaching methods, including RP and TSP, are effective in developing students'history-taking skills and doctor-patient communication skills [9, 31–34].Therefore, this study introduced TSP into history-taking training as a control to further analyze its benefits for students. However, fewer studies have directly compared the differences between RP and TSP teaching methods. This study is one of the few randomized controlled studies comparing the differences between RP and TSP. Moreover, we divided RP into scripted pRP and non-scripted pRP to further analyze which teaching model is more applicable to medical students. The initial study design implemented only the non-spRP condition, wherein participants were tasked with collaboratively developing RP scripts through evidence synthesis of assigned clinical scenarios (diagnoses and presenting complaints). This approach aimed to foster self-regulated motivation and team-based competencies. However, given participants’ limited foundational knowledge in internal medicine, the non-script development posed risks of cognitive overload. To mitigate frustration-induced disengagement, a modified intervention arm (spRP group) was incorporated, utilizing pre-scripted clinical narratives. This adaptation strategically reduced extraneous cognitive load, thereby optimizing task feasibility and learner receptivity. Our study indicates that the spRP group demonstrated the best performance in terms of medical history collection completeness, doctor-patient communication skills, and formative assessment, and achieved the highest overall score. No significant differences were observed in medical record writting. While there were no notable differences in teamwork scores across the groups, the spRP group reported higher satisfaction than the TSP group in terms of attitude toward teamwork and cooperation; however, no significant difference was observed between the spRP and non-spRP groups.

Students can achieve higher assessment scores through scripted role play

The results of this study showed that students in the spRP group achieved better assessment scores on learning outcomes than the students in the TSP group, which is in line with the findings of Julia and Bosse et al. [12, 13, 31]. RP can increase interaction between students, foster their sense of teamwork, and make them more involved and therefore more interested and motivated to learn, helping students understand the evolution of the disease and the characteristics of the patient’s medical history through the given script, which develops their clinical thinking skills. This was also confirmed by the study findings as students in the spRP group scored significantly higher than the TSP group on clinical thinking assessment and subjective attitudes toward the course concerning enhancing learning autonomy and motivation, increasing interest in learning, enhancing classroom interaction, and fostering team spirit.

Furthermore, the spRP group scored significantly better than the non-spRP group on the overall group-based assessment of interrogation skills. The scripts used in RP teaching are mostly prepared by teachers and fewer scripts are written by the students themselves. There is a lack of studies directly comparing the differences between the two RP teaching methods. This study is the first randomized controlled study comparing two different script sources for RP pedagogy. Students can directly rehearse if given a script and can learn about the evolution of the disease through the interpretation of the script with higher participation and motivation. However, writing good case scripts requires more specialized theoretical knowledge. Since the subjects of this study were all junior college students when undertaking the diagnostic laboratory course, they lacked knowledge of diseases and clinical thinking skills. At this time, if they were asked to write the scripts, it would invariably make the course more difficult and the students subconsciously might develop a resistance to learning and respond negatively. This is the reason why the non-spRP group scored lower in clinical thinking assessment and satisfaction with the course in terms of enhancing interest and motivation in learning. Therefore, attention should be paid to difficulty stratification in the teaching process, and different teaching objectives should be developed for students in different grades. For example, the primary stage should focus on history taking and case writing skills training, when a scripted RP model can be used, while the advanced stage should develop students'clinical thinking skills and their ability to deal with complex real-life scenarios, where an unscripted RP model is more applicable [30]..

Unscripted role play is as effective as teacher standardized patient model

A systematic review including 22 studies assessed the effectiveness of RP in improving medical students'doctor-patient communication skills and showed that pRP is as effective as other simulated patient teaching methods (including real patients and professional actors playing patients), both of which can improve students'doctor-patient communication and have lower financial costs [11]. This result is similar to the findings for the non-spRP group in this study, whose performance on interrogation skills assessment and case writing was not significantly different from that of the TSP group. This suggests that RP is valuable for students’ doctor-patient communication skills training, as it is as effective as SP, is even better than SP in post-training evaluation, and is less costly [13]. In terms of students'subjective attitudes toward different teaching methods, there was no significant difference in the overall mean scores of all three dimensions, and further refined analysis showed that the TSP group scored higher on"This teaching method has been helpful in learning and mastering the course knowledge,"whereas the non-spRP group scored higher on"This teaching method has developed my team spirit."This is because TSPs are professionals and can answer students'questions and give advice, whereas RP is conducted in a more relaxed atmosphere and requires group work to complete tasks. Although RP is gradually being used in clinical skills training and has achieved better teaching results, there are significant differences in implementation, which is one of the reasons for the current disagreement about which teaching method, RP or SP, can achieve better teaching results.

Importance of history-taking courses for students should be stressed

The results of this study showed that students'pre-course self-assessment was significantly higher than post-course self-assessment, which is inconsistent with the findings of Keifenheim KE et al. where students'self-assessment was higher after the teaching intervention than before the courses [35]

Moreover, some studies do not show any significant difference in the self-assessment of the participants before and after the courses [36]. Self-assessment is one of the most common methods of evaluating students'abilities [37]. Students'assessment and analysis of their performance according to the given criteria helps them ascertain their level of learning ability, leading to self-reflection and self-correction in the learning process, which can have an impact on their learning interest, process, and outcomes.

However, the self-assessment results are strongly influenced by students'subjective consciousness. However, Since the finding in history-taking study was not supported by previous literature, we interviewed some of the students from each class outside of the course and the initial analysis showed that the students'blind confidence and indifference before courses were responsible for the high pre-course self-assessment. The results of the study did not match our expectations, which can be tied to the Dunning-Kruger Effect [38].

The results of the study did not match our expectations, which can be tied to the Dunning-Kruger Effect [38].The model demonstrates a disproportional gap between subjective confidence in topic mastery and objective competency assessments, following a nonlinear pattern that amplifies significantly within populations exhibiting minimal domain knowledge [39, 40]. Since the score of the history-taking course does not account for a high percentage of the final exam, and the students had already received lecture training in history-taking and doctor-patient communication courses before participating in the study, in their opinion, they had basic theoretical knowledge about the subject and could obtain high scores without much effort; therefore, they appeared to be very confident in their pre-course self-assessment. However, the gap between theory and practice became evident during the training and final assessment. Although students'post-course self-assessments were significantly lower than their pre-course self-assessments, it showed that the students realized their shortcomings and would not take the course lightly, which is a kind of self-correction in the learning process, and only when they have the right mindset can they achieve good grades. Therefore, it is necessary for medical schools to stress the importance of history-taking courses by increasing class hours, giving the course more weightage in the examination results, and reforming the assessment methods. In addition, medical schools in China also need to pay more attention to the development of students'doctor-patient communication skills [37].

Clinical roles participants exhibit higher levels of satisfaction

Our investigation revealed that students assuming clinical roles (doctors and patients) consistently demonstrated higher evaluations across all metrics compared to evaluators, with no statistically significant differences observed between doctor and patient roles. This pattern aligns with the psychological profile of preclinical students who, lacking clinical exposure, exhibit heightened engagement with simulated clinical scenarios, whereas evaluator roles primarily reflect examination-oriented contexts. Notably, patient role participants provided equivalent satisfaction ratings to doctor counterparts, thus justifying role-playing as an effective instructional strategy. However, the comparatively diminished appraisal from commentators necessitates pedagogical adjustments in future implementations, including reduced evaluator allocations and optimized group configurations to ensure all students gain exposure to clinical role immersion.

Limitations

First, this study was mainly conducted in groups, which not only assessed students'learning but also teamwork among students. However, this could not reflect the real level of consultation skills of each student as there were 5–6 students in each group and only two students played the role of the doctor in the assessment and the rest acted as reviewers. Moreover, since students were randomly selected for the role of doctor, all the students needed to prepare equally before the assessment and it would not affect the students'learning outcomes. Second, we did not track the long-term learning outcomes of the students, such as their scores on the National Medical Licensing Examination and Objective Structured Clinical Examination (OSCE), or their learning during the clinical internship phase. Therefore, this study can only illustrate the benefits in the short term, and further research is needed to confirm the benefits in the long term.

Conclusion

The spRP group achieved the highest overall scores, demonstrating superior performance in history-taking completeness, physician–patient communication skills, and formative assessments. However, no significant differences were observed across groups in medical record writing proficiency. Notably, while all groups showed comparable teamwork scores, the spRP cohort reported greater satisfaction with teamwork attitudes than the TSP group. Interestingly, we found that students'self-assessment scores were significantly lower after the course compared to their pre-course evaluations. This initial analysis may be related to students'blind confidence in their skills before course, but more in-depth studies are needed to further elucidate this association.
