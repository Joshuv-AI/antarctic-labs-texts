A Medical Resident’s Dilemma
Authors: Anas Hashem
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
AbstractAs an international medical graduate doing internal medicine residency in the United States, the challenges faced by residents aspiring to secure highly competitive fellowships, particularly in the field of cardiovascular disease, are highlighted in this correspondence. There is a delicate balance between maintaining clinical proficiency and building a robust research portfolio, emphasizing the unique struggles encountered by individuals in similar situations. Throughout my residency journey, I have navigated the intricate path of managing the demands of medical education, clinical responsibilities, and research pursuits.Key Words: clinical proficiency, education, internal medicine resident, research publication

As an international medical graduate (IMG) hailing from the recently founded University of Sharjah in the United Arab Emirates (established in 2006), I am currently an internal medicine (IM) resident at Rochester General Hospital in Rochester, New York, USA. My aspirations, however, extend beyond the confines of residency, reaching toward a highly competitive fellowship specialty—cardiovascular disease in the United States. To contextualize the formidable journey ahead, a glance at the 2023 fellowship match report reveals 1,152 available cardiovascular fellowship positions, attracting a pool of 1,685 applicants.1 However, the stark reality is that 498 (29.6%) of these applicants were unsuccessful in securing a position. Delving deeper into the statistics, only 28.8% of those who did secure positions were non-U.S. IMGs like myself—a mere 332 individuals.1 This realization struck me with particular significance upon entering my IM residency. The challenge demands an early initiation of efforts to boost my curriculum vitae (CV) to strive for a position among highly qualified non-U.S. IMGs in a pool limited to approximately 332 positions. An additional layer of complexity emerged from the match report data revealing that successful non-U.S. IMGs possessed an average of 4.8 research experiences and approximately 18.0 publications (including abstracts, presentations, and publications).2

These figures, even before the beginning of my residency, were concerning and prompted me to establish a strategic approach. I found myself compelled to create a plan, cognizant of the limited time and ample challenges that lay ahead. In pursuit of enhancing my CV, a myriad of possibilities lay before me. The most promising avenue seemed to be: 1) foster my clinical knowledge acquisition and skills, while 2) establishing connections and networking with cardiologists through the research pathway, which would in turn help me to, 3) develop a strong academic CV. However, I needed to acknowledge the time-consuming nature of research pursuits: approximately 177 hours (7.3 days) is spent on a publication (from writing to submitting).3 Balancing this endeavor with the imperative of becoming clinically competent as an IM physician within a 3-year timeframe presented a daunting task.

Some colleagues have said, “the time invested in research can come at the expense of building medical knowledge, with potential repercussions on patient care.” How could I navigate the dual responsibilities of being clinically proficient as an IM resident and simultaneously cultivating a research-heavy CV to compete with competent cardiovascular disease fellowship applicants? This dilemma resonated not only with my personal journey but with that of many IM residents aspiring to highly competitive fellowships such as cardiovascular disease, gastroenterology, and pulmonology/critical care (Figure 1).

A Medical Resident's Dilemma: Clinical Proficiency vs Research Publication

Acquisition of clinical knowledge and skills

In the initial phase of my residency, my focus was on dedicating my time in developing my clinical knowledge and skills given my direct patient care. I have thoroughly evaluated the available resources such as NEJM Knowledge+ for IM board review, NEJM Resident 360, the Medical Knowledge Self-Assessment Program (MKSAP) question bank, MKSAP books, and UWorld question bank. These resources, primarily designed for basic and clinical medical knowledge, formed the core of a comprehensive IM resident curriculum. However, the residency journey encompasses far more than textbook knowledge. It is a dynamic process of improving communication and bedside clinical skills, proficient use of electronic medical records, and practices essential to being a competent resident capable of addressing patient needs and prompt decision-making capability. Although these skills could be cultivated during patient encounters on floors, medical intensive care unit, and clinic, the initial period was the most crucial to establish strong clinical basis.

Seniors’ perspectives: incorporating research to education

Six months into residency marked a pivotal juncture where I began to explore meaningful research opportunities. I sought guidance from my senior colleagues at our IM residency program who are interested in cardiology, and I observed 3 distinct perspectives. The first perspective emphasized quantity over quality, advocating for working on as many publications as possible to bulk up the CV, investing most of their residency time mainly on research. The second perspective championed high-quality research with fewer publications, focusing on specific cardiology topics with a deeper level of knowledge while keeping some time to enrich their medical knowledge. The third perspective, intriguingly, prioritized strengthening medical knowledge and clinical skills, involving rigorous problem solving and extensive reading, all while establishing relationships with cardiology attendings in-house, without substantial engagement in research.

Observing these 3 groups at our program illuminated distinct patterns. 1) The quantity-focused group often displayed weaknesses in medical and clinical expertise, with a relatively superficial cardiovascular knowledge related to their research work. They have primarily relied only on big national databases (ie, national inpatient sample, national readmission sample, and STS/ACC TVT registry databases). Their timeline from conceptualization to paper submission ranged from 2 to 4 weeks, leading to publications in low-to-mid impact factor journals. An acceptable number of interviews typically ensued, predominantly from mid-tier academic cardiology programs. 2) In contrast, the quality-focused group exhibited profound cardiovascular knowledge and engaged in some high quality national database studies, and well-established single or multicenter research projects. These endeavors, often demanding 4 to 6 months per project and approved by institutional review boards, resulted in papers submitted to high-impact factor journals such as JACC, JAHA, and JAMA. Consequently, this group garnered a high number of interviews from mid-to-high tier academic programs. 3) The clinically focused group is characterized by knowledge strength not only in cardiovascular disease but also in other subspecialties. They cultivated robust relationships with in-house cardiology attendings without a notable number of publications. While securing few interviews from community programs, their chances of matching in-house increased. However, the challenge lay in obtaining limited interviews from academic programs, a significant setback for those hoping to continue their education in well-established academic centers.

Personal approach: a middle ground

The challenge lies in maximizing residency time for medical knowledge and clinical skill enhancement while recognizing the imperative of research for fellowship applications. This dilemma is further complicated by varying future career interests—be it in-house fellowship, mid-tier academic/community programs, or high-tier academic programs. To initiate this path, I actively connected with senior residents, fellows, and academic mentors across various residency/fellowship programs. The guidance and expertise generously shared by these mentors were instrumental. Many offered assistance in suggesting research ideas, refining study methodologies, and crafting study proposals. Setting realistic expectations and surpassing them became a hallmark of my approach, emphasizing both the quality and quantity of clinically relevant research projects. My goal was always to be underpromising and overdelivering.

A desire to strike a balance between medical education, patient care, and CV enhancement motivated me to embark on a journey that encompassed clinical electives at an academic program (West Virginia University Heart and Vascular Institute) and diverse research endeavors. The outcome was a spectrum of research work—case reports, case-control, retrospective, prospective, national database, and clinical trial studies. The culmination of these efforts materialized in the presentation of research work as posters and oral abstracts, earning nominations for international research young investigator awards and winning the first runner-up for original research category at our residency research (Roshan Jha) competition. Meanwhile, I took pride in being nominated for and was awarded the title of Internal Medicine Resident of the Year 2023 among the second-year residents by our program. As my residency drew to a close, I was honored by our program director, Dr Nagesh R. Jadhav, to receive the esteemed ‘Dr U.C. Nigwekar Scholarship Award,’ which is granted to a resident for their academic excellence and contributions to scholarly endeavors throughout their three years of residency. Now, I have started my new role as an Chief Medical Resident at our program, Rochester General Hospital.

Navigating future horizons for IMGs

Yet, the journey continues, and the next milestone awaits—the application for the cardiovascular fellowship match during my chief year. The outlook is optimistic, and the hope for favorable outcomes fuels my aspirations. In a reflective note to my IM resident colleagues, especially those who, like me, are IMGs navigating the challenges of a foreign medical education, I will support and encourage you. The journey is arduous, marked by leaving your families to gain knowledge in the United States. The counsel is clear—focus on strengthening medical experience and skills, leverage the abundance of available resources, and savor the journey. If research opportunities unfold, approach them with a commitment to contribute, lead, and publish without undermining the essence of the residency experience. The intersection of research and clinical acumen shapes the narrative of a successful career in medicine, each step a testament to the commitment to lifelong learning and patient-centered care. Finally, the journey toward a competitive fellowship is one laden with complexity and nuance. The delicate balance between academic pursuits and practical skills defines the trajectory. Striking this balance is an ongoing pursuit, essential for success in the dynamic landscape of medical education and practice.
