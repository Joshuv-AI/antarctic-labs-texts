Towards a framework for implementing artificial intelligence in clinical medicine
Authors: Arjun Mahajan, Avery H LaChance, David W Bates
Published: 2026

Clinical artificial intelligence (AI) is proliferating rapidly. Across specialties, new diagnostic aids, predictive risk models and decision support systems are emerging at an accelerating pace, most developed and tested within research settings.1 2 In this perspective, we argue that as their number and complexity grow, they may exceed what the average clinician can reasonably remain apprised of and effectively use—a concern with emerging evidentiary support.3 Each model may carry distinct input structures, interpretation requirements and failure modes, creating a landscape that is increasingly difficult to navigate. For clinicians, the challenge may soon shift from scarcity of reliable AI tools to the practical impossibility of maintaining informed awareness of available tools, appropriate use contexts and their relative strengths and limitations.

While substantial effort has focused on developing new models, comparatively less attention has been given to building the structures organisations need to manage them and enable clinicians to identify, assess and incorporate them into practice. These challenges operate at multiple levels: individual clinicians face the cognitive burden of remaining informed, while health systems must develop infrastructure to support effective and safe adoption. Care delivery systems need approaches to catalogue and track model performance, and clinicians will need to understand which models exist, the evidence supporting them and the circumstances in which they should be applied.4 5 Without a connecting or discovery mechanism, providers may soon encounter a translation gap or ‘tool blindness’, where even rigorously validated tools fail to achieve meaningful uptake, resulting in wasted innovation. The downstream consequences of such a gap extend beyond underuse.6 Many models may remain confined to narrow development settings, limiting refinement opportunities in response to real-world clinical needs and diverse patient populations.4 5 Health systems may struggle to prepare for safe deployment when tools are developed and disseminated fragmentarily, with limited clarity about how their inputs, outputs and workflow demands intersect with existing processes.5 7 Collectively, these gaps widen the divide between innovation and implementation, constraining the extent to which AI can improve patient outcomes.

One potentially helpful conceptual strategy is the creation of a clinical AI interface layer, an organising structure that bridges the expanding array of tools and tracks how they are used and presented. This layer could take several forms, technological and organisational, each offering a practical path towards greater transparency and adoption.

For instance, a centralised AI discovery platform or ecosystem could offer clinicians a consolidated view of available models relevant to a given clinical task and the evidence supporting them. Such a platform could help clinicians identify models for their clinical context by indexing available tools according to criteria that matter to practice, including the types of inputs they require, the conditions or problems they address and the populations for which they were developed. For each deployed algorithm, it might offer structured, clinically interpretable evaluations or require reporting of critical factors such as external validation, known limitations and biases, performance drift monitoring and other safety considerations (table 1).1 6 An emergency medicine physician, for example, might use such a platform to compare validated sepsis prediction tools to use for their patient population. Alternatively, this platform might mirror the structure of a hospital formulary, in which a curated set of vetted algorithms is reviewed, approved and monitored for use within a given health system. Any interface platform must be thoughtfully implemented to avoid the risk of inadvertently contributing to the very fragmentation it seeks to resolve, adding complexity rather than streamlining clinical AI adoption. Other implementation considerations may include establishing governance and institutional ownership, clarifying accountability when tools underperform or cause harm and developing standardised evaluation frameworks (online supplemental file 1).

A complementary solution is the emergence of dedicated expertise and roles for clinicians trained to bridge AI development and practice.8 These individuals might focus on model translation and deployment rather than construction or broader health information technology infrastructure. Their responsibilities could include interpreting model outputs in the context of individual patient cases, clarifying limitations and appropriate use cases and guiding safe integration as tools evolve.

A more transparent discovery ecosystem, though not without limitations, may help reduce utilisation variability by making information about tools and their constraints accessible across institutions, regardless of size or technical capacity. It may also encourage standardisation, promoting more reliable comparisons of model quality and external performance.

Clinical AI will not realise its potential until physicians can reliably identify, understand, use and trust the tools that are being developed. Establishing a dedicated interface layer, through discovery platforms, specialised clinical roles or related mechanisms, is urgently needed. In building this infrastructure, medicine may better align the rate of innovation with the rate of meaningful clinical adoption.
