Clinical Efficacy of Narrow‐Diameter Tissue‐Level Implants for Molar Replacement: A 1‐Year Randomised Controlled Trial
Authors: Hyun‐Woo Park, Seung Ha Yoo, Seung‐Hyun Park, Jae‐Kook Cha, Ui‐Won Jung, Mariano Sanz
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTAimTo compare 1‐year clinical and radiographic outcomes of narrow‐ versus wide‐diameter tissue‐level implants for single molar replacement.Materials and MethodsThis two‐arm randomised controlled trial enrolled 20 patients (23 implants) in one sextant. Participants received either 3.5‐mm narrow‐diameter implants (NDI, n = 12) or 5.0‐mm wide‐diameter implants (WDI, n = 11) using a tissue‐level design with computer‐guided surgery. The primary outcome was marginal bone loss (MBL) at 1 year assessed by periapical radiographs. Secondary outcomes included operation time, pre‐allocation analysis of augmentation requirement, clinical peri‐implant parameters, hard‐ and soft‐tissue dimensions, mucosal recession and buccal soft‐tissue contour and implant stability. Non‐inferiority margin for MBL was 0.5 mm.ResultsAll 23 implants achieved 100% survival. Mean MBL was 0.39 ± 0.29 mm for NDI and 0.48 ± 0.31 mm for WDI (upper 95% confidence interval [CI]: 0.176 mm), confirming non‐inferiority of NDI. Operation time was shorter in this cohort for NDI (20.3 ± 9.3 min) than WDI (28.8 ± 15.3 min; one‐sided p = 0.049). Peri‐implant clinical parameters were comparable.ConclusionsNDI showed non‐inferiority to WDI in MBL at 1 year while reducing operative time, supporting NDIs as a viable and efficacious alternative for anatomically restricted posterior sites.Trial RegistrationKorean ClinicalResearch Information Service Number: KCT00094155

Introduction

Dental implant therapy has become the treatment of choice for replacing missing teeth, with long‐term success rates exceeding 95% according to systematic reviews (Jung et al. 2012; Pjetursson et al. 2012). Implant diameter selection is guided by residual bone volume, prosthetic requirements and occlusal loading (Buser et al. 2017). Standard‐diameter implants (≥ 3.75 mm) and wide‐diameter implants (WDI) (≥ 4.5 mm) have both been extensively documented as reliable in the posterior region due to favourable bone‐to‐implant contact and biomechanical stability (Renouard and Nisand 2006).

However, alveolar bone resorption following tooth extraction—particularly pronounced in the bucco‐lingual dimension—frequently results in insufficient bone width for standard or wide‐diameter implant placement without augmentation (Araujo and Lindhe 2005; Schropp et al. 2003). Guided bone regeneration (GBR) procedures increase treatment complexity, duration and cost while introducing risks of membrane exposure, graft resorption and delayed healing (Benic and Hammerle 2014; Urban et al. 2019). These considerations have motivated interest in narrow‐diameter implants (NDI, < 3.75 mm) as a minimally invasive alternative that may reduce augmentation needs in sites with limited bone width (Klein et al. 2014; Sohrabi et al. 2012).

Early concerns regarding the biomechanical performance of NDIs in posterior sites, particularly under high occlusal loads, have been addressed through advances in implant materials, surface modifications and connection designs (Renouard and Nisand 2006). Modern surface treatments enhance osseointegration even with reduced implant–bone contact area (Buser et al. 2017). Systematic reviews have reported favourable survival rates for NDIs in the posterior region (Ortega‐Oller et al. 2014; Sohrabi et al. 2012), although the quality of evidence remains limited by the heterogeneity in implant systems, follow‐up duration and outcome definitions (Klein et al. 2014; Sohrabi et al. 2012).

A critical gap in the existing literature is a lack of randomised controlled trials (RCTs) directly comparing NDIs with standard or WDIs for posterior single‐tooth replacement. Most available evidence derives from retrospective case series or prospective cohort studies without concurrent control groups. To date, no prospective studies have rigorously evaluated whether NDIs can reduce the need for augmentation, possibly owing to differences in baseline ridge morphology. Based on pre‐allocation planning, which applies both NDI and WDI within the same narrow ridge, the anticipated need for augmentation can be evaluated in advance.

The present RCT was designed to address these gaps by comparing two diameters of tissue‐level implants (3.5 mm [narrow] vs. 5.0 mm [wide] diameter) for single molar replacement. The primary objective was to assess the non‐inferiority of NDI relative to WDI with respect to marginal bone loss (MBL) at 1 year. Secondary objectives included buccal bone thickness (BBT), surgical parameters and clinical peri‐implant parameters. A pre‐allocation cone‐beam computed tomography (CBCT)‐based planning analysis was conducted to quantify the theoretical reduction in the bone augmentation requirement achievable with NDI placement. We hypothesised that the use of NDIs would maintain marginal bone stability within the prespecified non‐inferiority margin and reduce the need for bone augmentation and operative time, consistent with a more efficient alternative compared with WDIs.

Materials and Methods

Study Design and Ethical Approval

This prospective, single‐centre, parallel‐group RCT was conducted at the Department of Periodontology, Yonsei University Dental Hospital, Seoul, Republic of Korea. The protocol was approved by the Institutional Review Board of Yonsei University Dental Hospital (No. 2‐2023‐0070) and was registered at the Clinical Research Information Service (No. KCT00094155; https://cris.nih.go.kr/cris/search/detailSearch.do?seq=27121&search_page=L). The trial was designed and reported in accordance with the Consolidated Standards of Reporting Trials (CONSORT) statement for randomised trials (Hopewell et al. 2025). All participants provided written informed consent after receiving detailed information about the study procedures, risks and benefits (Figure 1).

Inclusion and Exclusion Criteria

Patients were recruited between August 2024 and January 2025 from individuals seeking implant therapy. Eligible participants were adults (≥ 20 years) requiring one or two implants in a posterior edentulous site (first or second molar) in the maxilla and/or mandible, with the site healed for ≥ 2 months after extraction. Participants were required to be generally healthy, including those with stable, well‐controlled medical or dental conditions, and to have adequate bone volume to accommodate either a 3.5‐mm or 5.0‐mm diameter implant at the prosthetically ideal position (with or without simultaneous GBR).

Exclusion criteria were severe alveolar bone deficiency unsuitable for implant placement, uncontrolled systemic disease (e.g., uncontrolled diabetes mellitus), history of head and neck radiation therapy and/or chemotherapy, pregnancy or breastfeeding, heavy smoking (≥ 20 cigarettes/day) or prior tumour resection in the same region.

The implants used were tissue‐level external‐hex fixtures (Bright Tissue‐Level Implant [BTLI], Dentium, Suwon, South Korea) with machined transmucosal collars. The narrow‐diameter configuration incorporates coronal reinforcement intended to improve resistance to bending and cyclic loading in posterior sites.

Randomisation and Allocation Concealment

Eligible patients were randomly allocated to one of two groups using a computer‐generated, blocked randomisation list created with web‐based software (sealedenvelope.com). Permuted‐block randomisation (block size 2, 1:1 allocation) was applied without stratification. Randomisation was implemented at the patient level; if two implants were planned in the same patient, both sites received the same allocation.

Participants were randomly assigned to one of the following groups: NDI Group: 3.5 mm Dentium BTLI implant;WDI Group: 5.0 mm Dentium BTLI implant.

Allocation was concealed using sealed, opaque envelopes opened immediately before surgery. Because of the differences in implant size, the operators, the investigator in charge of data collection and participants were unblinded from the time of surgery.

Implant Planning Prior to Group Allocation

All participants underwent CBCT imaging (Q‐FACE, HDXWILL, Seoul, South Korea; 85 kV, 8 mA; exposure time 24 s) for pre‐allocation planning. DICOM (Digital Imaging and Communications in Medicine) data were imported into implant planning software (Dentium Digital Guide Software, Dentium, Suwon, South Korea) for three‐dimensional assessment of site‐specific bone dimensions and prosthetically driven virtual planning.

For each site, two different types of implant placements were simulated at the same location: (i) a 3.5‐mm NDI and (ii) a 5.0‐mm WDI. In both cases, the implants were positioned prosthetically ideally, and measurements were obtained from cross‐sectional images perpendicular to the implant axis.

For each simulation, BBT was measured using the implant shoulder plane as the reference (Braut et al. 2011). Sites with BBT < 1.5 mm were categorised as thin buccal bone (Chappuis et al. 2017; Ferrus et al. 2010; Monje et al. 2019). Horizontal bone defects were further classified as described by Benic and Hammerle (2014).

Sites were considered to require augmentation when thin buccal bone (< 1.5 mm) and/or an intrabony defect was anticipated (Ferrus et al. 2010; Monje et al. 2019; Spray et al. 2000).

Surgical Procedure

On the day of surgery, participants were randomly allocated to one of the two groups. All procedures were performed under local anaesthesia using a static, fully guided surgical approach. The choice of surgical access (flapless vs. flapped) and augmentation was based on ridge width, bone conditions, soft‐tissue thickness and the presence of a deficient buccal contour. Augmentation was performed as required and categorised as follows: (1) GBR using a barrier membrane with graft material; (2) minor autogenous bone grafting without a membrane; (3) minor autogenous bone grafting with soft‐tissue augmentation; or (4) no augmentation. Detailed decision criteria and surgical procedures are described in Figure 2 and Appendix S1.

Follow‐Up Examination

Patients were evaluated at scheduled follow‐up visits: 2 weeks, 1 month, 3 months, 4 months, 10 months and 12 months post‐surgery. At 4 months, definitive restorations were delivered as either screw—cement‐retained prostheses (SCRP) or cement‐retained crowns, fabricated in zirconia or porcelain fused to metal (PFM). When two single‐molar sites were treated in the same patient, splinted crowns were used for prosthetic reasons.

At each visit, clinical assessments including peri‐implant soft‐tissue evaluation, probing depth and signs of inflammation or complications were conducted. Implant stability was assessed at all follow‐up visits. Periapical radiographs were obtained at 4, 10 and 12 months, with additional CBCT imaging at 12 months. Mechanical or biological complications were recorded and managed accordingly.

Outcome Measures

Primary Outcome

The primary outcome was MBL derived from periapical radiographs. Images were scaled using the known fixture length to correct magnification/distortion, and measurements were obtained using dedicated Picture Archiving and Communication System (PACS) software (Infinitt PACS; Infinitt, Seoul, South Korea).

MBL was defined as the distance from the implant shoulder (reference point) to the first bone‐to‐implant contact, measured at the mesial and distal aspects; the mean of the two measurements was calculated. All measurements were performed by a single examiner using a standardised protocol.

Secondary Outcomes

Secondary outcomes included hard‐ and soft‐tissue dimensional changes (Figure S1), surgical parameters (total operation time and type of adjunctive procedures), clinical peri‐implant parameters (probing depth, bleeding on probing (BOP), plaque index, and keratinised tissue width), implant stability, implant survival and success and aesthetic outcomes (mucosal recession and buccal soft‐tissue contour); detailed measurement protocols are provided in Appendix S2.

Statistical Analysis

Sample size was calculated based on the primary outcome of MBL at 1 year, using a non‐inferiority design. Assuming a mean MBL of 0.5 mm with a standard deviation (SD) of 0.25 mm in both groups, a non‐inferiority margin of 0.5 mm, one‐sided α of 0.025 and power of 0.95, a minimum of eight patients per group was required. Accounting for a 20% dropout rate, 10 patients per group (20 patients total) were targeted. The final sample included 23 implants (12 NDI, 11 WDI) in 20 patients with no dropouts.

MBL was analysed using linear mixed‐effects models with treatment group as a fixed effect, accounting for clustering of implants within patients. Non‐inferiority was concluded if the upper bound of the 95% CI for the mean difference (NDI − WDI) was below the prespecified margin of 0.5 mm (one‐sided α = 0.025). For secondary outcomes, pre‐allocation planning analysis was performed using McNemar's test. Continuous secondary outcomes, including operation time, soft‐tissue dimensions and mucosal recession, were analysed using linear mixed‐effects models with a patient‐level random intercept to account for multiple implants per patient. Categorical variables were compared using Fisher's exact test. BOP was analysed using generalised linear mixed‐effects models with a binomial distribution, accounting for clustering at the patient level.

All analyses were performed using SPSS (version 28.0; IBM Corp.). Statistical significance was set at two‐sided α = 0.05, except for non‐inferiority (one‐sided α = 0.025) and operation time (one‐sided α = 0.05).

Among the 22 patients screened, two declined, resulting in 20 enrolled patients, all of whom completed follow‐up.

Actual Implant Placement, Augmentation and Operation Time

BBT was comparable between groups (NDI: 1.86 ± 1.75 mm vs. WDI: 1.57 ± 0.76 mm; p = 0.787), as was the proportion of the thin (< 1.5 mm) sites (33.3% vs. 27.3%; Fisher's exact p = 1.00).

Augmentation was performed in seven NDIs (58.3%) and four WDIs (36.4%), with no significant between‐group difference (p = 0.414). Detailed augmentation types are presented in Table 2.

The mean baseline ridge width at the planned implant shoulder level was smaller in the NDI group (7.31 ± 3.54 mm) than in the WDI group (8.59 ± 2.10 mm), without statistical significance (p = 0.310) (Table 2).

Mean operation time was 20.3 ± 9.3 min in the NDI group and 28.8 ± 15.3 min in the WDI group. In a linear mixed‐effects model analysis, the NDI group showed a shorter operation time in this cohort compared to the WDI group (mean difference [NDI − WDI] −10.2 min, 95% CI: −22.5 to 2.0 min; one‐sided p = 0.049) (Figure 3).

Marginal Bone Level

All 23 implants survived over a 1‐year follow‐up (100% survival rate). Marginal bone level was assessed at 4, 10 and 12 months (Figure 4).

After 1 year, the mean MBL was 0.39 ± 0.29 mm in the NDI group and 0.48 ± 0.31 mm in the WDI group. In the linear mixed‐effects model, the estimated mean difference in MBL (NDI − WDI) was −0.086 mm (95% CI: −0.348 to 0.176 mm; one‐sided p < 0.001 for non‐inferiority) (Table S1a).

For the non‐inferiority assessment with a prespecified margin of Δ = 0.5 mm, the upper bound of the 95% CI (0.176 mm) was below the non‐inferiority margin, supporting non‐inferiority of NDI relative to WDI. The estimated intra‐class correlation coefficient (ICC) at the patient level was negligible (≈0).

Within‐group comparisons showed stable MBL in the NDI group from 4 months onwards (p = 1.000), while the WDI group showed continued bone level change beyond 4 months (Table S1b).

Aesthetic‐related soft‐tissue outcomes are summarised in Table S2. Minimal mucosal displacement was observed in both groups, with no significant implant diameter effect (p = 0.332). Buccal soft‐tissue contour scores did not differ significantly between the groups (p = 0.461).

Regarding longitudinal implant stability, damping capacity (The Trust, Genoss) showed a transient dip at 2 weeks, that is, slightly earlier than the commonly reported 3–4‐week nadir, followed by gradual increases at subsequent follow‐ups, with mean values ≥ 65 thereafter. Stability patterns were comparable across groups (Figure S2).

During follow‐up, one complication occurred in each group: abutment screw fracture in NDI (at 8 months post loading, managed by screw replacement) and inadequate primary stability in WDI (during fixture placement, implant left in place and managed according to the study protocol without additional surgical intervention). Implant stability improved from 72 at 1 month to 77 at 3 months (successfully loaded per protocol). No biological complications occurred. The overall complication rates were 8.3% (NDI) and 9.1% (WDI) (p = 1.00, Fisher's exact test).

Discussion

This RCT shows that 3.5 mm NDIs achieved non‐inferior marginal bone stability compared with 5.0 mm WDIs for single molar replacement at 1 year, with 100% survival in both groups. In addition, pre‐allocation planning analysis suggests that NDI placement significantly reduces the theoretical requirement for bone augmentation. A shorter operation time was also observed in the NDI group in this cohort, supporting procedural efficiency as an alternative treatment approach. Clinical peri‐implant parameters, soft‐tissue dimensions and aesthetic outcomes including mucosal recession and buccal soft‐tissue contour were all comparable between the groups. The negative values observed for mucosal recession indicate minimal coronal soft‐tissue displacement following prosthesis delivery, likely reflecting peri‐implant soft‐tissue maturation and stabilisation.

The absence of statistically significant differences in marginal bone resorption, implant survival and clinical parameters is consistent with previous reports indicating that NDIs can serve as a predictable alternative in posterior load‐bearing regions when sufficient primary stability is achieved (Klein et al. 2014; Shi et al. 2018). Although reduced implant diameter has raised biomechanical concerns, such as compromised fatigue and fracture resistance, accumulating evidence suggests that such limitations may be mitigated through advances in implant design and surface characteristics. Notably, while most existing evidence on NDIs is derived from retrospective studies or case series (Klein et al. 2014; Sohrabi et al. 2012), the present parallel‐group RCT provides more robust evidence by eliminating carryover effects inherent to split‐mouth designs (de Souza et al. 2018).

The comparable marginal bone stability observed between groups may be explained, at least in part, by contemporary implant design features. In particular, the tissue‐level configuration, in which the implant–abutment junction is positioned supracrestally, may contribute to reduced crestal bone remodelling compared with bone‐level designs (Lazzara and Porter 2006). Radiographically, marginal bone levels predominantly stabilised near the implant shoulder rather than progressing apically, suggesting physiological remodelling rather than pathological bone loss (Berglundh and Lindhe 1996). At 1‐year follow‐up, MBL magnitude remained within the range of early remodelling considered acceptable under classical success criteria (≤ 1.5 mm during the first year) (Albrektsson et al. 1986). Notably, MBL stabilised after prosthesis delivery in the NDI group with no further significant change beyond 4 months, while the WDI group showed continued remodelling up to 1 year, suggesting earlier crestal bone stability with NDIs.

A novel contribution of this study is the incorporation of a pre‐allocation planning analysis in which both implant diameters were simulated at identical anatomical sites. This within‐site comparison isolates implant diameter as the primary variable, minimising anatomical confounding inherent in conventional between‐group comparisons. Under these conditions, NDI placement was associated with a markedly lower estimated need for bone augmentation (26.1% for NDI, 52.2% for WDI). Actual ratios of bone augmentation were comparable (58.3% for NDI and 36.4% for WDI). The discrepancy between planned‐based prediction and actual augmentation rates in the randomised phase likely reflects a chance imbalance in ridge morphology between groups, as randomisation with a small sample size does not guarantee perfect baseline equivalence. Although the difference in ridge width between groups was not statistically significant (p = 0.310), the numerically narrower ridges in the NDI group may have contributed to higher actual augmentation rates. Nevertheless, this pre‐group‐allocation planning analysis may provide more robust evidence of the bone‐preserving potential of NDI because it controls for anatomical variability through within‐site comparisons.

The shorter operation times observed for NDIs (20.3 ± 9.3 min for NDI, 28.8 ± 15.3 min for WDI) may reflect procedural efficiency, primarily attributable to a simplified drilling protocol, reduced osteotomy volume and minimised patient discomfort (Lambert et al. 2015; Zhang et al. 2022).

Implant stability analysis showed the expected early stability dip followed by recovery, indicating favourable osseointegration. Although the stability dip appeared at 2 weeks, slightly earlier than the commonly reported 3–4‐week period, the limited temporal resolution and sample size preclude a definitive interpretation of this finding. The earlier timing of this stability dip warrants further investigation, as it may reflect differences in healing dynamics related to implant design, surface characteristics or patient‐specific factors. Future studies with more frequent early‐phase stability measurements and histological validation would help elucidate this temporal pattern.

Mechanical complications were limited, with one abutment screw fracture observed in the NDI group at 8 months post loading and no fixture fractures in either group. This suggests that over 1 year, the structural integrity of the implant body was preserved under posterior loading conditions. The reduced‐diameter implants in the present cohort may have benefited from design‐driven reinforcement at the transmucosal/tissue‐level portion. Despite the narrow platform, the implant incorporates a relatively wide (~3.8 mm) tissue portion with a 20° external taper connection, which preserves thickness at the coronal fixture region and may enhance resistance to bending and fatigue under posterior occlusal loading. Accordingly, these structural features could partly mitigate long‐standing biomechanical concerns for narrow implants (i.e., fixture‐level fracture and bending‐related complications), at least within the 1‐year observation period. Future studies with longer follow‐up periods and larger sample sizes are needed to confirm these observations and to evaluate the long‐term mechanical performance of NDIs under varying loading conditions.

Several limitations need to be addressed in the present study. Given the relatively short follow‐up, longer term follow‐up (≥ 5 years) is necessary to evaluate the durability of marginal bone stability and implant survival, as well as long‐term complication rates and fatigue resistance of the NDIs under sustained posterior loading. Moreover, the present study enrolled only 20 patients (23 implants), and considering this limited sample size and single‐centre setting, larger multi‐centre RCTs with adequate statistical power are needed to confirm these findings and evaluate subgroup effects (e.g., maxilla vs. mandible, immediate vs. delayed placement). Larger sample sizes may help minimise the chance of baseline imbalance in bone anatomy and, in turn, limit its impact on augmentation decisions and the interpretation of bone‐preservation outcomes. Although McNemar's exact test accounted for the paired within‐site structure of the pre‐allocation planning analysis, patient‐level clustering among participants contributing more than one implant site was not additionally modelled, which should be considered when interpreting the pre‐allocation analysis results. Outcome assessment was unblinded because of radiographically distinct implant diameters. Finally, because the present study evaluated a single NDI system, comparative studies evaluating different NDI systems are warranted to identify optimal design features.

In conclusion, the findings of this study support the use of 3.5 mm NDIs as a viable alternative to 5.0 mm WDIs for single molar replacement in anatomically restricted sites. NDIs offer several clinical advantages, including non‐inferior marginal bone stability compared to WDIs, substantial reduction in the estimated requirement for bone augmentation, shorter operation time reflecting procedural efficiency and comparable clinical peri‐implant parameters and soft‐tissue outcomes. Long‐term studies with extended follow‐up periods and larger sample sizes are warranted to confirm the durability of these results and further establish clinical guidelines for NDI selection in posterior applications.
