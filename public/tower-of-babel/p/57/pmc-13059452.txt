Barriers to optimal central venous catheter training: a qualitative study of physician experiences
Authors: Joy Abou Farah, Kipum Lee, Jin E Kim-Mozeleski, Alexia El Khoury, Zainab Albar, Christopher Ladikos, Demetra Yianas, Noah Carty, Tina M Lewis, Kendra Marianne Gardiner, Elie Saade

Background

Central venous catheter (CVC) placement is a high-stakes medical procedure that requires both precision and expertise due to the substantial risk of complications. In the United States alone, more than five million CVCs are inserted annually, highlighting the critical role of the procedure in clinical practice [1]. While ultrasound guidance has substantially reduced these risks, decreasing complication rates from 16.9% to 4.6%, the need for advanced training remains critical to ensuring patient safety [2]. Prior systematic reviews have demonstrated that simulation-based education for central venous catheterization is associated with improvements in learner performance and selected procedural outcomes, reinforcing the clinical relevance of structured procedural training [3].

Traditional training methods, including lectures and mannequin-based simulations, provide foundational knowledge and differ in their ability to replicate the dynamic, high-pressure environment encountered in real clinical settings. Simulation-based training (SBT) is commonly used to support procedural training and skill development [4]. SBT allows trainees to develop both technical and cognitive skills in a controlled setting with immediate feedback and repeated practice. However, the transition from simulated practice to independent performance in clinical settings is complex. From an educational theory perspective, procedural training can be conceptualized using Miller’s Pyramid, which describes progression from knowledge acquisition (“knows”) to demonstration of competence (“shows how”) and ultimately independent clinical performance (“does”) [5]. Within this framework, variability in supervised clinical exposure may influence how trainees translate simulated competence into independent practice.

Although advancements have been made in simulation and ultrasound-guided techniques, variability persists in how training for central venous catheterization (CVC) is structured and accessed across clinical settings. Prior literature has described challenges related to the availability of training opportunities, such as access to equipment and instructors, and the extent to which training environments capture communication of ethical considerations, and other contextual aspects of real-world practice. These contextual and ethical dimensions may reflect elements of the “hidden curriculum,” defined as the organizational structures, cultural norms, and implicit values that shape learning beyond the formal curriculum [6]. This variability raises ongoing questions about how existing training approaches are experienced by physicians and how well they align with the demands of clinical care [7].

National survey-based studies in internal medicine have documented variability in procedural training requirements, supervision, and assessment practices across residency programs, suggesting that opportunities for structured procedural learning are not uniform [8]. To better understand these challenges, we aimed to gain insights into the lived experiences of physicians to capture dimensions of training often overlooked by quantitative research. Consequently, we conducted a qualitative, formative study designed to explore physicians’ perceptions of gaps in central venous catheter (CVC) training. Utilizing interviews with physicians who routinely perform CVC placement across diverse specialties, this study sought to examine experiences and perceived barriers related to CVC training.

Methods

An exploratory qualitative study was conducted using semi-structured interviews in May 2024 with six physicians from University Hospitals, Cleveland, Ohio: five attendings (two with training roles) and one resident. Recruitment followed a purposive sampling strategy aimed at ensuring representation from specialties that routinely perform central venous catheter (CVC) placement, including Emergency Medicine, Radiology-Vascular and Interventional Radiology, Anaesthesiology, Internal Medicine, Pulmonary and Critical Care, allowing us to capture a diversity of perspectives on both the technical and educational dimensions of training. The participants were contacted by email and asked to participate without incentives or honorarium. The sample size and composition were shaped by availability, resulting in a cohort composed predominantly of attending physicians. Although the sample size was small, the study addressed a focused aim centred specifically on physicians’ experiences with CVC training. The in-depth, one-hour interviews and independent thematic analysis by three researchers contributed to the richness and rigor of the data. In alignment with the concept of information power, these characteristics supported the adequacy of the sample for this exploratory qualitative study [9]. Each interview lasted approximately one hour and followed a structured guide with 20 open-ended questions (see Supplemental Digital Appendix 1) exploring experiences with central venous catheter (CVC) training. The interview guide was developed by the multidisciplinary research team in alignment with the study objectives and structured around key domains of procedural training and clinical education. Questions were designed to explore technical workflow, educational preparation, feedback processes, patient safety considerations, and perceived training gaps. The guide was reviewed collaboratively prior to study initiation to ensure clarity and domain coverage and was applied consistently across interviews. The interviews were conducted either in-person or via Zoom, depending on participant availability. Two trained members of the research team were present for each interview: one led the semi-structured questioning and maintained interview flow, while the second documented structured notes in real-time to ensure comprehensive data capture. Audio recordings were not obtained. Three interviewers rotated across sessions to facilitate scheduling flexibility and reduce interviewer fatigue while maintaining consistency through use of a standardized interview guide. All interviewers had healthcare and research backgrounds, received prior training in qualitative interviewing and review of the study protocol before study initiation, and had no prior professional or personal relationship with the participants. An inductive thematic analysis was performed by three researchers (JAF, DY, CL), one of whom conducted interviews and another who had prior CVC placement experience. The researchers coded the data independently using the software Nvivo, identified patterns, and refined themes through conceptual clustering. Major themes were determined based on both recurrence (frequency and consistency of patterns across interviews) and conceptual relevance (the extent to which codes meaningfully addressed the study aim and contributed to understanding physician’s experiences with CVC training). Discrepancies were resolved through discussion and consensus to ensure analytic rigor.

This study was reviewed by the University Hospitals Institutional Review Board (UH IRB) and determined to be exempt under Category 2(iii) (STUDY20231382). All participants provided informed consent prior to participation. The study is reported in alignment with SRQR. Clinical trial number: Not applicable.

Results

Six physicians participated in this study, including five attending physicians and one resident. Participants represented Emergency Medicine, Radiology-Vascular and Interventional Radiology, Anaesthesiology, Internal Medicine, Pulmonary and Critical Care. Two attending physicians held formal training or supervisory roles within their departments. Thematic analysis of the interviews revealed three major themes with accompanying subthemes: Perceived insufficient preparation due to inadequate prior training; missed training opportunities due to time, patient volume, and equipment limitations; and a desire for feedback, standardization, and access to resources.

Theme 1: Perceived insufficient preparation due to inadequate prior training

Participants reported a perceived lack of preparedness prior to performing their first central venous catheter placement on a patient, despite prior observation of procedures and training with manikins.

Subtheme 1a: Shortcomings of manikin-based training

During the interviews, participants acknowledged the essential role of manikin-based training in medical education but emphasized its significant limitations. Although manikins provide a structured and controlled environment, they are regarded as inadequate for replicating the technical nuances and anatomical variability encountered in real patients. Participants described these limitations as leaving trainees insufficiently prepared for the complexities of the clinical practice.

“When the environment deviates from your expectations; how will you adapt your approach?” (MS, clinical instructor in the Emergency Department).

“Even if you do 100 [CVC Placement] on 100 manikins, seeing someone’s blood or poking them for the first time is always going to be hard to do.” (MO, Internal Medicine Resident).

Subtheme 1b: Limited exposure to real-life scenarios

Participants highlighted that their training did not address all aspects of CVC placement, particularly the patient-centred approach, which is both a critical and challenging component. They emphasized that the absence of training in patient interactions, including communication and ethical considerations, leaves trainees unprepared for these critical aspects of clinical practice.

“We deal with a lot of cancer patient and end of life care. We were not trained on it at all. Elderly, palliative, ethical discussions all the time; sometimes doing a procedure is not the right thing to do. People are not trained well enough on that.” (JD, Radiology-Vascular and Interventional Radiology).

“Most people do the first one on a patient. Devices can’t replicate a real person. It’s not the same. We don’t have anything that simulate the entire experience. Phantoms and simulator lab, but not the same feel as a real patient. And it’s clunky.” (JD, Radiology-Vascular and Interventional Radiology).

Additionally, participants pointed out that critical aspects of patient communication, such as obtaining informed consent, were not explicitly covered in training, but rather acquired informally over time.

“About consent: they or their caregiver have to understand why the central line is needed, what can go wrong, what discomfort they might feel- insertion or being draped […] Was taught by nurses- not in training.” (MS, clinical instructor in the Emergency Department).

Subtheme 1c: Inadequate training on sterile protocols

Sterility training was described as limited, with many trainees receiving minimal instruction on maintaining a sterile field. Instead, they relied on prior exposure to sterility concepts from surgical rotations without structured training on CVC related precautions and infection prevention.

“General sense of sterile process and prepping introduced in medical school.” “Hadn’t heard of CLABSI until residency. Unsure if those concepts are being taught in medical schools today.” (CM, Anaesthesiologist).

“Prep/sterility: no hands-on training. Techs do it, so we’re not trained to do it. As residents, you are exposed to it.” (JD, Radiology-Vascular and Interventional Radiology).

“Would focus on sterility with central line specifically outside of the surgical world in medical school.” (CM, Anaesthesiologist).

Theme 2: Missed training opportunities due to time, patient volume, and equipment limitations

Participants described several constraints, including time limitations, restricted procedural opportunities, and inadequate access to equipment and financial resources. These factors collectively reduced hands-on experience, impacting skill development.

Subtheme 2a: Time constraints in clinical settings

The fast-paced nature of clinical environments, especially emergency rooms and critical care units, where CVC placement is frequently performed, often limits opportunities for trainees to practice procedures. High patient volume, workload demands, and urgency of time-sensitive lifesaving procedures reduce the availability of supervised training.

“Time is a major barrier to improvement- leadership, trainees pulled in multiple directions.” (CM, Anaesthesiologist).

“Need more manpower to provide more training.” (SR, Pulmonary and critical care).

Subtheme 2b: Variability in patient volume

The availability of cases significantly influences the number of procedures a trainee can perform, which leads to variable exposure. Some rotations provide structured, high-volume learning experiences, whereas others offer fewer opportunities, resulting in uneven skill development.

“More structure, more guaranteed exposure—versus luck of the draw in the ICU.” (MO, Internal Medicine Resident).

“Rotations need to be more regimented/formalized. Requires time/money as well.” (JD, Radiology-Vascular and Interventional Radiology).

Subtheme 2c: Equipment and training tool limitations

Access to high-quality tools, including manikins, procedural kits, and ultrasound machines, is inconsistent, impacting the training quality. Financial constraints further limit investment in high-quality resources.

“Manikins, kits, and ultrasounds are expensive.” (MO, Internal Medicine Resident).

“Lack of investment in training.” (JD, Radiology-Vascular and Interventional Radiology).

Theme 3: Desire for feedback, standardization, and access to resources

This theme captures participants’ expressed desire for feedback, consistent protocols, and improved access to training resources.

Subtheme 3a: Desire for constructive feedback

Trainees emphasized the importance of real-time structured feedback in enhancing procedural skills. They highlighted the need for immediate case-based feedback, followed by structured debriefing sessions to reinforce learning.

“Feedback is critical. Multiple forms. Procedural-based feedback- direct either while procedure is happening or immediately following it.” (CM, Anaesthesiologist).

“A large role- should be immediate real-time feedback and debrief after. Writing component–included in the formal evaluation. Feedback forms every shift.” (JP, Emergency Medicine).

“Crucial- feedback at every part of it. Almost like a coaching would be helpful- in addition to feedback, a built-in system of continuously improving technique procedures- how do we go up a notch from current level?” (MS, Emergency Medicine).

Subtheme 3b: Need for standardized training protocols

Participants stressed the need for formalized and consistent training protocols across programs to ensure equitable learning experiences. Establishing standardized guidelines would create uniform expectations and help bridge the gaps in procedural competency.

" Would really like to have formal training- would be useful for the residency program.” (MO, Internal Medicine Resident).

Subtheme 3c: Access to high-quality resources

Trainees highlighted the necessity for reliable access to advanced training tools, including high-fidelity manikins and high-resolution ultrasound machines, to enhance procedural learning. The cost of these resources varies widely, creating disparities in training opportunities across institutions.

“Limited to having ultrasound. Can have bad ultrasound and high resolution. Can cost anywhere from 150K.” (SR, Pulmonary and Critical Care).

“At [Another health system]- all fellows used to get dedicated day per month just for teaching & learning from people. Attendings had to carry the pager and do all the work that day. We do not have that here, which could affect patient care. The [Another health system] has more faculty so can do this.” (SR, Pulmonary and Critical Care).

“National workshops that focus on CMEs for attendings who may or may not do any procedure very often. Nothing at UH.” (CM, Anaesthesiologist).

Discussion

Despite the increasing integration of simulation-based training (SBT) into medical education, physicians in this sample still described recurrent and significant deficiencies in their preparation for central venous catheter (CVC) placement. The interviews revealed three major areas of concern: perceived insufficient preparation due to prior training, missed training opportunities related to time, patient volume, and equipment limitations, and a desire for feedback, standardization, and access to resources. These findings align with growing evidence that while simulation provides foundational technical instruction, trainees may continue to experience difficulty navigating the complexity, variability, and decision-making demands encountered in clinical practice [10].

Participants frequently described their initial experiences performing CVC placement on patients as difficult, despite prior simulation exposure. While manikin-based training was viewed as valuable for introducing procedural steps, participants emphasized that it did not fully capture the variability, uncertainty, and contextual demands encountered in clinical settings, such as anatomical differences, time pressure, and the emotional weight of performing procedures on patients. The findings align with Chow et al., [11] who found manikin training increased procedural familiarity by 16.7% but achieved only 29.6% comfort after SBT training. Similarly, Suliman et al. [12] identified deficits in anatomical recognition among anaesthesia residents in atypical scenarios, concerns echoed by participants. Meta-analysis by Ma et al. [3] similarly notes that while simulation improves technical performance, trainees may continue to report variability in their preparedness when transitioning to clinical practice.

In addition to technical limitations, participants highlighted a lack of structured training in patient-centred communication, particularly in complex or emotionally charged situations. These elements of care were often described as learned informally or not addressed within formal curricula. This aligns with critiques of medical education that emphasize its overreliance on technical performance while underrepresenting the relational and reflective dimensions of clinical competence [13]. Without opportunities to engage in uncertainty, make and learn from errors, or navigate emotionally complex conversations, trainees may feel less prepared in developing non-technical yet essential skills related to ethical and patient-centred care.

Structural challenges further shape participants’ experiences of these educational experiences. Participants highlighted constraints such as limited time, variable patient volume, and insufficient access to essential equipment, including ultrasound machines and procedural kits, as primary obstacles to skill development. These concerns reflect findings by Mifflin et al., [14] who documented inequities in procedural exposure and advocated for more standardized, equitable pathways to ensure all learners achieve competency. The variability described by participants, often characterized as “luck of the rotation,” underscores how structural factors may shape access to procedural learning opportunities across clinical assignments. Participants’ reflections on technicians performing key procedural steps further illustrate how task redistribution within clinical workflows may influence trainees’ hands-on exposure. While delegation may support efficiency and patient flow, it may also shape how learners develop autonomy and procedural confidence over time. Prior work has shown that structured simulation-based mastery learning can influence procedural training processes, providing context for participants’ interest in more consistent and structured learning experiences [15].

The importance of timely and structured feedback emerged as a central theme across interviews. Participants consistently emphasized the need for real-time guidance during or immediately following procedures, as well as regular debriefing sessions to foster reflective learning. These preferences align with prior research suggesting that structured, high-quality feedback plays a role in supporting skill development and enhancing learner confidence [16]. Participants’ emphasis on feedback highlights interest in clearer mechanisms for tracking progress and expectations within training programs.

The results also highlight a broader ethical obligation in medical education. As Ziv et al. [17] note, educators must both prepare trainees adequately and protect patients from avoidable harm. Simulation may support this responsibility by allowing learners to practice procedural skills before performing procedures on patients. In doing so, it acts not only as a pedagogical tool but also as an ethical safeguard that reduces preventable patient suffering caused by procedural errors or complications. This perspective echoes the patient safety framework by Pronovost et al., which integrates technical training with system-level safeguards [18] and aligns with the “Zero Harm” vision that frames safety as the elimination of avoidable suffering [19].

For areas of CVC training that participants described as difficult to replicate through existing educational approaches, extended reality (XR) represents an even more immersive and emerging training modality for procedural education. Unlike manikin-based sessions, XR may provide a standardized platform for deliberate practice, simulating patient variability, complications, and communication [20].

This study is limited by its small sample of physicians from a single institution, which narrows the range of perspectives represented. Attending physicians reflected on both their own procedural experiences and their observations as instructors; however, because trainees were less represented in the sample, the findings may not fully capture the direct lived experiences of learners early in training. Because interviews were not audio recorded, some nuances tied to verbal tone and cues may not have been captured. The findings reflect how these participants experience and perceive CVC training, rather than offering objective measures or broadly generalized claims. As with any qualitative work, interpretation is shaped by both participants’ accounts and the researchers’ analytic lens.

Conclusions

The findings from this qualitative study underscore how physicians perceived persistent gaps in central venous catheter (CVC) training, including limited exposure to realistic scenarios, insufficient standardization in training and curriculum, and inadequate preparation for patient-centred communication. While manikin-based approaches offer a structured environment for acquiring technical skills, they often fail to capture the variability and complexity of real clinical encounters, particularly in high-stakes or emotionally nuanced situations. These limitations raise concerns not only about skill acquisition but also about the consistency and equity of training experiences across institutions and specialties. While centred on central venous catheter placement, these findings underscore challenges and opportunities that are broadly relevant to the training of other complex clinical procedures. Future research could explore how different training modalities are experienced across diverse clinical contexts and learner populations, and how these experiences evolve over time.
