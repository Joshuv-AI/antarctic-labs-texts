Semaglutide use in people with obesity and type 2 diabetes from real-world utilization data: An analysis of the All of US Program
Authors: Craig S. Mayer, Paul Fontelo
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
Aim:To analyse data from the All of Us Research Program to evaluate the real-world application and long-term effectiveness of semaglutide in treating type 2 diabetes and obesity patients in a large population.Materials and Methods:We identified patients prescribed semaglutide and analysed differences in route of administration and the time on semaglutide. For individuals diagnosed with obesity, we measured changes in body mass index (BMI) and weight from baseline, while measured changes in HbA1c for those patients with type 2 diabetes. We also examined the occurrence of newly diagnosed common adverse events from taking semaglutide.Results:For 3739 semaglutide patients, those on injectable semaglutide (3364 patients) averaged 301.54 days on the medication, with 20.36% having no end date, while those on oral semaglutide (435 patients) averaged 172.48 days, with 24.60% having no end date. We found average decreases of 1.54 kg/m2 in BMI, 4.65 kg in weight and 0.75% in HbA1c for semaglutide users. The decreases were larger in participants taking injectable formulation, probably because of higher starting values. Over time, improvements in these outcomes diminished, but the values remained significantly lower than baseline levels. Approximately only 1.0% of patients reported newly diagnosed common adverse events.Conclusions:Consistent with clinical trial findings, this real-world data analysis showed that semaglutide was well tolerated and that, for a large population, it effectively reduced BMI, body weight and HbA1c, albeit to smaller magnitudes than observed in clinical trials. These findings provide valuable insights into real-world experience and the long-term effectiveness of semaglutide.

INTRODUCTION

Public interest and that of the medical community has surged surrounding the use and effectiveness of glucagon-like peptide-1 (GLP-1) receptor agonists, particularly semaglutide. Many clinical studies have evaluated the effectiveness of these drugs for patients with type 2 diabetes and obesity, and whether initial positive outcomes translate into sustained improvements over the long-term.1 Worldwide, type 2 diabetes affects nearly 0.5 billion people (~6.28%), while obesity affects 2.5 billion people (~16%).2,3 GLP-1 receptor agonists are generally well tolerated, but they can cause some side effects, such as nausea, vomiting, diarrhoea and gastroparesis, with more significant adverse events being pancreatitis, gall bladder disease, hypoglycaemia, vision loss and allergic reactions.4–6 More research is needed to understand the long-term safety of these drugs, and the ability for prolonged usage may rely on the identification of certain adverse events and the development of new conditions relating to the use of these drugs.4 Further studies also seek to understand the effects of each of the injectable semaglutide drugs, such as Ozempic and Wegovy, as well as oral semaglutide, such as Rybelsus.7 Additional consideration of the cost of medications may also be a factor in their continued use.

Current existing publications on semaglutide draw primarily on data derived from randomized clinical trials, often leaving out real-world data.4,8 Clinical trial data are limited to a structured usage of the intervention by regulating time on drug and limiting the analysis to the period of the clinical trial. Even existing real-world analysis may limit the scope of analysis to a set time frame or delivery route. This study endeavours to bridge this gap by shedding light on real-world use of both injectable and oral forms of semaglutide without limiting the time of analysis. The investigation utilized electronic health records (EHRs) in the All of Us (AoU) Program, offering a comprehensive view of the drug’s performance in diverse clinical settings.9

The AoU Program is a large-scale, US-based research programme that combines comprehensive EHRs from multiple sites around the United States with surveys, sociological, genomic and wearable device data.9,10 The AoU research program began enrolment in 2018, but includes legacy EHR data for many of its participants, with the oldest record dating back to 1981. In this analysis, we used the EHR data in the AoU research workbench-controlled tier access released in April 2023.

MATERIALS AND METHODS

Semaglutide cohort

First, we identified all those individuals taking semaglutide. An individual was considered as someone who had taken semaglutide if they had a single instance of the drug in their EHR data. We then characterized the cohort of individuals who took semaglutide based on their demographic information and underlying clinical indication. These included the two most common conditions leading to semaglutide usage, obesity and type 2 diabetes, and identifying if any semaglutide users had either of these conditions. Participants with no condition data, or who were never diagnosed with either obesity or type 2 diabetes, were excluded from the analysis, even if they had taken semaglutide. These participants had inadequate information to properly assess the effect of the medication on the outcomes of the underlying condition. For individuals included in the cohort we analysed the amount of recorded follow-up time available in the data. This was carried out by calculating the time between the date of initiating semaglutide and the last recorded date in their EHRs of any record type.

Drug route

Together with the overall identification of semaglutide users, we analysed the distribution of participants based on the type of semaglutide used, either injectable or oral tablets. For each route as well as the entire semaglutide population combined, we evaluated the time and distribution of individuals based on how long they were on the medication. If no end date was listed, we considered the individual to be still taking the drug. For this analysis the start date was considered to be the first time the patient filled a prescription for semaglutide, while the end date (when listed) was the last date the patient was instructed to take the medication, as recorded in their EHRs.

Analysis of laboratory measurements

To assess the effectiveness of semaglutide, we examined laboratory values for all participants in this study. A participant was included in the analysis if they had at least two of the specified measurements, at least one before and one after starting semaglutide. We identified those who were lost to follow-up as defined as individuals with no recorded value after starting the medication. For analysis of laboratory results, we divided the overall cohort into two, based on the underlying condition present in the participant prior to starting semaglutide (type 2 diabetes and obesity). Many of the individuals were included in both cohorts because they were diagnosed with both conditions prior to using semaglutide. Each analysis was also performed with stratification by demographic criteria. For the analysis, R was used including several packages, such as the cor and dplyr packages. All results were calculated to the 95% significance level.

For the specified laboratory measurement, we considered the overall change from the measurement prior to starting semaglutide (baseline) to the last recorded value after starting semaglutide. This was carried out regardless of the time interval and without accounting for when an individual stopped taking the drug. In addition, we calculated the change from baseline over time by taking the value at a given length of time after starting semaglutide and calculating the change from baseline. This was conducted based on the amount of time between when the measurement was recorded and when semaglutide was initiated. Because not all individuals had measurements recorded on the same schedule, the number of individuals captured at a given point varied.

As part of our analysis, we also conducted a correlation study between the initial and final values, assessing the change from baseline in each participant. We further segmented our analysis into the distinct drug administration routes, injection and oral, to examine the overall change for each measurement.

For individuals with obesity, we looked at both body mass index (BMI) and weight. For individuals with type 2 diabetes, we considered HbA1c test results. All these tests were performed for most participants who had both conditions. We also assessed the final recorded HbA1c as a comparison with published target values for people with type 2 diabetes.

New condition occurrence post semaglutide

To understand the potential adverse effects of semaglutide usage, we identified the occurrence of various conditions after the initiation of semaglutide usage. This was carried out by calculating the number of individuals taking semaglutide who were diagnosed for a specific condition at any point after starting semaglutide and in follow-up visits thereafter. We calculated the number of people with a specific condition reported for the first time after starting semaglutide (new occurrence) as a percentage of the total cohort. We also looked at how common a new condition was relative to the condition in the cohort (total cases).

RESULTS

Cohort size

In AoU, we found 3739 participants who used semaglutide. Of these, 2151 (57.53%) had both type 2 diabetes and obesity prior to starting semaglutide, while 644 (17.22%) only had type 2 diabetes and 620 (16.58%) were diagnosed only with obesity and not type 2 diabetes; 324 semaglutide users did not have sufficient information to determine whether they belonged to any of these groups and were excluded from analysis.

Of the 3739 semaglutide users, 3364 received the medication via injection, while 435 took the tablet form; 89 reported using both routes (injection and oral tablets). The injectable semaglutide formulations included Ozempic (3154 participants) and Wegovy (210 participants), while all oral tablet participants were listed as taking Rybelsus. The population consisted of patients who initiated semaglutide from 2018 to 2022, with most starting in 2021 (1503 patients; 40.20%).

Cohort characteristics

The semaglutide users consisted of 2330 women (62.32%) and 1316 men (35.20%), while 93 were identified by another gender (2.49%). The overall AoU population is composed of 59.85% women. The average age at the start of using semaglutide was 57.63 (standard deviation [SD] 12.78) years, with ages ranging from 19 to 91 years.

For the population of semaglutide users, there was an average of 472.26 days of observation in the data following the initiation of semaglutide. The average length of included follow-up time in the data was larger for men (502.65 days) than for women (454.57 days). It was also noticeable that semaglutide users in their 30s (414.63 days) and 40s (442.02) had less observed follow-up time than those in their 50s (502.44 days) and 60s (502.91 days).

Although the cohort was observable for the follow-up time described above, not all individuals included in the cohort had a second measurement value after starting semaglutide, meaning that the change in measured values could not be determined. Also, 2864 (76.60%) had an analysed measurement value after starting semaglutide, while 875 (23.40%) people did not have a follow-up measurement and were excluded from the analysis. Of those lost to follow-up, 607 (69.37%) were women and 243 (27.77%) were men. Those lost to follow-up consisted of a higher percentage of women (69.37%) than that of the entire semaglutide cohort (62.32%). The average age of those lost to follow-up was 56.69 (SD 13.13) years, which is approximately 1 year younger (57.63 years) than that of the overall semaglutide cohort.

Time on semaglutide

The overall average time on semaglutide was 296.07 days; however, there was a significant difference between those on injectable semaglutide compared with those on oral tablets. For those on injectable semaglutide, the average length of time on the medication was 301.54 days compared with 172.48 days for those on oral semaglutide. The reason individuals stoppped taking the medication was not indicated in the data. Figure 1 shows the percentage of participants still taking semaglutide after a certain number of months.

As Figure 1 shows, 20.36% (685 participants) of injecting and 24.60% (107 participants) of oral semaglutide users did not have information on a medication end date and were considered to still be on the medication; 2148 participants (57.45% of overall semaglutide users) stopped taking the medication within 1 year of starting the medication. In addition, there was a difference in average time on semaglutide between men (336.69 days) and women (270.02 days). From an age perspective, the average time on the medication increased progressively as individuals started the drug at an older age, ranging from people in their 30s to those in their 70s.

BMI and weight changes

For the 2771 individuals who had an obesity diagnosis and took semaglutide (including both those with and without a type 2 diabetes diagnosis), we found that the average change from baseline for BMI was −1.54 (first quartile: −3.05, third quartile: −0.35) kg/m2, while for body weight it was −4.65 (first quartile: −8.30, third quartile: −0.15) kg. This shows that most individuals on semaglutide saw a decrease in both BMI and weight from baseline. Furthermore, 1878 participants (67.77%) of the 2771 semaglutide users with an obesity diagnosis experienced decreases in BMI from baseline, while 1989 participants (71.78%) had decreases in weight. Separating out by gender, male participants on semaglutide had an average change in BMI of −1.23 kg/m2, while women had an average change in BMI of −1.18 kg/m2 from baseline. For weight, the values were −5.79 kg for men and −4.40 kg for women. There was no statistically significant correlation between change from baseline for either BMI or weight in relation to age of starting semaglutide.

One factor that affected the amplitude of the final change from baseline for these measurements was the starting value. In general, for both BMI and weight, we found that the higher the baseline value for each measure, then the larger the change in each of BMI and weight from the baseline to the ending value. For BMI, we found that a 1 kg/m2 increase in baseline BMI equated to an additional change of −0.31 (95% confidence interval [CI]: −0.37 to −0.25) kg/m2 in change from baseline to final value. For weight, every additional kilogram of starting weight equated to an additional −0.24 (95% CI: −0.28 to −0.20) kg change from baseline to final value.

Figure 2 shows the change in BMI (part A) and weight (part B) over time from the baseline date.

Figure 2 shows that, after a large decrease initially, both the average BMI and weight began to stabilize, then showed some variability. The average weight began to decrease again after 3 years; however, this only represents 71 participants who were still on the medication and had a recorded value for that time frame. The last recorded values in both BMI and weight were significantly lower than the baseline BMI and weight.

We also observed a difference in average change for both BMI and weight based on the medication route (injectable compared with oral tablet). On average, the net change from baseline in BMI was −1.25 kg/m2 for those taking injectable semaglutide compared with −0.71 kg/m2 for those on oral tablets (P = .025). A similar difference was seen for weight: the average change from baseline was −4.81 kg for those on injections compared with −3.64 kg for those on oral tablets (P = .016). This difference may be related to the starting point because, as previously mentioned, the starting value has a significant effect on net change for both weight and BMI, and those on injectable semaglutide had, on average, a higher starting value for both weight and BMI compared with those on oral tablets. The average starting values for BMI were 34.35 kg/m2 for injections and 33.27 kg/m2 for oral tablets (P = .0061). For weight, the difference was 108.86 kg for injections and 105.31 for oral tablets (P = .008). Another contributing factor may be the previously mentioned difference in time on medication, which is, on average, 301.54 days for injections and 172.48 days for oral tablets.

HbA1c changes for people with type 2 diabetes

Overall, there were 2795 participants who took semaglutide who had type 2 diabetes (including both those with and without an obesity diagnosis). For these individuals, we found that the average change from baseline for HbA1c was −0.75% (first quartile: −1.50%, third quartile: −0.10%). For men, the average change from baseline was −0.70%, while for women the average change was −0.75%. We observed that there was a statistically significant positive correlation between the age of starting semaglutide and the final average change from the baseline value of HbA1c. The correlation coefficient between age and HbA1c was 0.07 (95% CI: 0.02 to 0.12; P = .003).

Overall, 1892 participants (67.69%) had an overall decrease in HbA1c from the baseline value, with an average decrease of 0.75%. From a starting value perspective, every percentage point increase in starting HbA1c was equal to an additional −0.57% (95% CI: −0.60% to −0.54%) percentage point change in the final value. Figure 3 shows the change in HbA1c (%) from the baseline value in months from the start of taking semaglutide.

As shown in Figure 3, there was a significant decrease in HbA1c from the baseline initially that reached its lowest point approximately 9 months after starting semaglutide. After that point, the average HbA1c value began to increase back towards the baseline value, correlating with the time when approximately one-half (50.26%) of individuals had stopped taking the medication.

The median endpoint for HbA1c was 7.10%, with 1310 participants (48.87% of individuals on semaglutide) achieving an HbA1c of less than 7%; 824 participants (29.48%) had even more effective results by reaching an HbA1c of less than 6.5%.

Those on injectable semaglutide (−0.78%) had a larger average change from baseline in HbA1c than those on oral tablets (−0.56%) (P = .024). Similar to the BMI and weight changes, those on injectable semaglutide also had a significantly higher average baseline HbA1c value (8.21%) than those on oral tablets (7.85%) (P = .022).

DISCUSSION

Real-world data

In contrast to most assessments of semaglutide performed on data generated from controlled clinical trials, our assessment is based on real-world data from EHRs. This allowed us to better understand the effects and usage of semaglutide in everyday life. For example, we observed the variability in time on drug that is not seen in clinical trials. There was a significant decrease in percentage of patients still on semaglutide after the first year. The available data were also able to show the change in associated measurement values even after stopping semaglutide.

Similar to the results in the clinical trials leading to US Food and Drug Administration approval (Pivotal Trials) associated with semaglutide (NCT02054897 for Ozempic and NCT02906930 for Rybelsus) for people with type 2 diabetes,11–14 our evaluation also showed decreases in HbA1c, but to a lesser extent than in trials. Specifically, our results showed an average decrease of 0.75% from baseline, in contrast to 1.52% observed in the Ozempic trial and 0.9% in the Rybelsus trial.

In contrast to the real-world data in this study, the clinical trials have a set time frame (30 and 26 weeks, respectively, for the trials mentioned above), potentially limiting their ability to fully capture the comprehensive spectrum of outcomes observed. Notably, our findings reveal prolonged monitoring for longer than 1 year, a duration not accounted for in the clinical trials, showcasing additional insights, particularly concerning increases in values towards baseline.

Other studies of the real-world application of semaglutide have also shown outcomes related to the medication’s usage. While in some cases these studies were limited in time scope or route of administration, they showed the safety and effectiveness of semaglutide in line with the conclusions in this study.15–18

Lasting effectiveness still needs validation

Following initiation of semaglutide, significant decreases were observed in measured values for both obesity and type 2 diabetes patients. While many individuals experienced increases in their BMI, body weight and HbA1c values upon discontinuing the medication, our findings indicated that final values remained significantly lower than baseline levels, indicating the potential efficacy of semaglutide. However, further studies are needed to ascertain the lasting improvements of semaglutide.

Differences between injectable and oral semaglutide

Using real-world data also enabled a better comparison of patients on injectable and oral semaglutide. Overall, our analysis showed that parenteral semaglutide was more frequently prescribed to individuals with higher laboratory values and more severe conditions. The medications appeared more effective in those with more severe conditions, as more significant reductions in BMI, weight and HbA1c were shown by parenteral semaglutide than by the oral route. An early favourable response and increased effectiveness may have led to patients being kept on the medication longer than those on the oral tablets, as a more pronounced drop-off in medication usage was shown in those taking oral tablets than in those on injections.

Secondary treatment option

Semaglutide is frequently a secondary treatment option for type 2 diabetes patients with suboptimal response to metformin and who have other health conditions.19,20 In many cases, shown by the cohort in AoU, individuals on semaglutide have already used either metformin (1532 AoU participants in the semaglutide type 2 diabetes cohort; 54.81%) or insulin (625 participants; 22.36%), with an unsatisfactory response. The decrease in HbA1c shown in the results indicates the effectiveness of semaglutide in these patient populations.

Limitations

One limitation of our analysis is the exclusion of co-morbidities and lifestyle factors when evaluating the impact of semaglutide. In assessing the effects of semaglutide on individuals with obesity, we only considered BMI and weight. Recent American Medical Association guidelines discourage the use and reliance on BMI alone for evaluating patients for obesity.21,22 Other measures of risk (visceral fat, body adiposity index, body composition, relative fat mass, waist circumference, and genetic or metabolic factors) were not available.22 There was only a limited sample size for the other relevant measurements in AoU participants.

Along with the limitations stated above, the current study has several limitations that are associated with real-world analysis as opposed to controlled clinical trials. These include differences throughout the cohort in follow-up time, the timing and number of recorded measures, as well as changes in the drug regimen, such as dose changes and the stopping of the medication. All of these factors contribute to differences in results associated with real-world data compared with those from clinical trials. It is also possible in the real-world setting that other factors may have contributed to the measured outcomes beyond the usage of semaglutide. While AoU includes wearable device data to measure movement and exercise, an insufficient number of participants in this semaglutide analysis had wearable device data present to include in our analysis. We also observed 355 individuals who were taking semaglutide and metformin. Although we did not separate these individuals and analyse them separately, the results may have been affected by the inclusion of metformin in the treatment. The same may be true for other medications given together with semaglutide for the studied cohort. While co-morbidities may impact the outcomes of taking semaglutide, because of the volume of different co-occurring conditions (> 2000 different conditions), we did not stratify by co-morbidity in the analysis.

CONCLUSION

In conclusion, the utilization and efficacy of GLP-1 receptor agonists, particularly semaglutide, have generated considerable interest in the medical community and the public in general. While existing literature has validated the safety and efficacy of semaglutide, it primarily relied on data derived from clinical trials, and this may lead to gaps in understanding its real-world utilization and effectiveness. Addressing this gap, our study leveraged real-world data sourced from EHRs within the AoU Program, contributing valuable insights. Our findings corroborate the effectiveness of semaglutide in reducing BMI (1.54 kg/m2 from baseline) and weight (4.65 kg) among individuals with obesity, as well as lowering HbA1c levels (0.75%) in those with type 2 diabetes. Moreover, our analysis showed a significant decrease in use within the first year; however, one-fifth of participants continue to use it for 5 or more years. Our results also indicate minimal occurrence of adverse events, with the most common side effects affecting less than 1.0% of the cohort. Overall, our study provides a comprehensive understanding of the real-world utilization and effectiveness of semaglutide.
