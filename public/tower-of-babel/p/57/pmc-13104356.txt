A simple and reliable method for the qualitative and quantitative analysis of nano- and microplastics in organs for controlled preclinical studies
Authors: Soyeon Jeon, Gyuri Kim, Jun Hui Jeon, Sung Ik Yang, Kyuhong Lee, June-Woo Park, Wan-Seob Cho
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundToxicity studies of microplastics increasingly emphasize the importance of particle retention, transformation, and physicochemical properties within biological systems, as these factors critically influence hazard identification and dose–response interpretation. However, quantitative and qualitative analyses of microplastics in biological matrices remain technically challenging, and many existing digestion-based methods can alter particle properties, thereby confounding toxicological outcomes. Herein, we propose a novel methodology to provide quick, easy, non-destructive, cost-efficient, and reliable outcomes by offering a particle collection method for microplastics as-present in organs using proteinase K (PK) digestion and a simple quantification method based on ultraviolet-visible (UV-Vis) spectrophotometry.ResultsThe test microplastic samples comprised three spherical polystyrene (0.1, 1, and 100 μm), two fragmented polystyrene (1 and 100 μm), one spherical polyethylene (10 μm), one fragmented polyethylene (100 μm), and two fragmented polypropylene (1 and 100 μm). Particles as-present in organs were successfully collected by conducting PK tissue digestion at 5 µg PK per mg dried tissue. The collected particles were then quantified using a standard curve, with absorbance measured at 750 nm. Spiking experiments with microplastics in mouse lung and mussel tissues showed > 90% accuracy and recovery for all test particles. Because the PK-based particle collection method is non-destructive, the collected particles can be further used to evaluate their physicochemical properties, such as size, shape, composition, and oxidative potential. However, the plastics treated with both acids and alkalis exhibited significant morphological, optical, and chemical changes, indicating that these methods are destructive to particles.ConclusionTherefore, this simple, non-destructive approach enables quantitative and qualitative analyses for laboratory toxicological studies and qualitative identification of microplastics in unknown biological samples, providing a valuable tool for future toxicity and environmental research.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12989-026-00677-6.Keywords: Microplastics, Proteinase K, Quantitative analysis, Qualitative analysis, Biological tissues, Biokinetics

Background

Plastic waste accumulates in various environmental matrices, including water, soil, and air, due to the exponential increase in production, limited biodegradability, and insufficient recycling capacity [1, 2]. These materials undergo fragmentation into smaller particles through environmental weathering processes such as abrasion, heat, and ultraviolet (UV) irradiation [3, 4]. Based on size limits, microplastics are defined as solid plastic particles that are insoluble in water and have dimensions between 1 μm and 5000 μm [5]. Meanwhile, nanoplastics are defined as particles smaller than 1 μm [6]. In recent years, microplastics have become a public concern due to their adverse effects on humans and ecosystems [7, 8].

Accurate quantitative and qualitative analyses of microplastics deposited in organs are essential for assessing their biological effects and determining biologically effective doses and biokinetics. Poorly soluble particles, such as microplastics, tend to accumulate, potentially causing overload and chronic toxicity [9–11]. Furthermore, in vivo studies to evaluate the biological effects of microplastics on humans are urgently needed for risk assessment and risk communication [10, 12]. Although quantitative organ burden data are essential for interpreting the toxicological significance of nano- and microplastic exposure, previous inhalation toxicity studies (e.g., polystyrene (PS) [13] and polypropylene (PP) [14]) and gavage studies (e.g., PP [15]) did not provide such data. Recent studies have started to address this limitation, but the currently available analytical approaches remain technically complex, time-consuming, and resource-intensive [16, 17].

Current analytical methods, such as Raman spectroscopy, Fourier transform infrared spectroscopy (FT-IR), and pyrolysis-gas chromatography/mass spectrometry (py-GC/MS), face significant limitations, including high costs, labor-intensive, time-consuming procedures, destructive analyses, and matrix-related interference in complex biological samples [18–23]. These limitations restrict the practicality of these methods for processing the large numbers of tissue samples typically required in animal exposure studies, particularly in resource-intensive research such as inhalation toxicology. Therefore, a rapid, cost-effective, and non-destructive quantification approach is urgently needed to support robust and reproducible evaluations of microplastic toxicity.

Proteinase K (PK) is a broad-spectrum serine protease commonly used for digesting proteins in various biological samples. Due to its high enzymatic activity and ability to efficiently break down proteins, PK effectively removes biological tissues while preserving the integrity of other substances [24]. This enzymatic digestion approach significantly mitigates major drawbacks of traditional chemical digestion methods, such as acidic or alkaline treatments, which often induce chemical degradation via hydrolysis and structural alterations. Here, we introduce a novel combined approach that utilizes PK digestion and ultraviolet-visible (UV-Vis) spectrophotometry for the qualitative and quantitative analysis of microplastics in tissues from mice and mussels. This proposed method facilitates precise and reproducible quantification, thereby advancing microplastic toxicology research and enabling robust evaluations of biological effects.

Methods

Selection of test microplastics

In this study, we used various plastic types, including PS, PP, and polyethylene (PE). Both spherical primary and fragmented secondary plastics were tested. Spherical PS particles with 0.1, 1, and 100 μm in diameter were purchased from Polysciences Co. Ltd. (Warrington, PA, USA). The fragmented PS particles (1 and 100 μm) and the fragmented PP particles (100 μm) were prepared using the in-house pulverization method [25, 26]. Briefly, the expanded PS (also known as EPS or Styrofoam) particles with a 1 μm diameter were prepared from EPS food containers by ball milling (K-One Nano; Seoul, Korea), 500-mesh brass sieving (Chunggye Sieve, Seoul, Korea), and membrane filtration [25]. The fragmented PS and PP particles with 100 μm diameter were prepared by cryogenic high-speed milling (Retsch GmbH, Haan, Germany) and sieving (Retsch GmbH) of PS and PP plastics (Lotte Chemical Co., Seoul, Korea) [26]. Furthermore, the fragmented PP particles (1 μm), the spherical PE particles (10 μm), and the fragmented PE particles (100 μm) were prepared using a previously described method [27, 28]. The abbreviations of test microplastics are as follows: SPS0.1, 0.1 μm-sized spherical PS; SPS1, 1 μm-sized spherical PS; SPS100, 100 μm-sized spherical PS; FPS1, 1 μm-sized fragmented PS; FPS100, 100 μm-sized fragmented PS; SPE10, 10 μm-sized spherical PE; FPE100, 100 μm-sized fragmented PE; FPP1, 1 μm-sized fragmented PP; FPP100, 100 μm-sized fragmented PP.

Physicochemical characterization of test particles

The morphology and primary sizes of test particles were evaluated using field-emission scanning transmission electron microscopy (FE-STEM; Talos F200X; Thermo Fisher Scientific, MA, USA) and scanning electron microscopy (SEM; JEM 2010; JEOL, Waltham, MA, USA). The average particle size was calculated by measuring 300 particles using the built-in software of TEM or SEM system. Hydrodynamic size and dispersion stability were assessed using the dynamic light scattering (DLS) method using the ZetaSizer NanoZS (Malvern Panalytical, Malvern, UK). Particles larger than 10 μm were excluded from the DLS measurement because they exceeded the measurable size range.

Optimal dispersion of test particles

The dispersion of test particles was a critical step for successful treatment and quantification, as agglomerations increase the inaccuracy of particle-originated toxic effects and optimal analysis. In this study, a dispersion protocol was developed for quantification by UV-Vis spectrophotometry and for in vivo experiments. Briefly, test particles were dispersed in 100% serum and sonicated for 10 min using a bath sonicator (Saehan-Sonic, Seoul, Korea). Serum proteins improve dispersion stability by forming a protein corona on the plastic surface, thereby increasing electrostatic repulsion, enhancing hydrophilicity, and providing steric hindrance [25, 26]. Sera for in vivo and in vitro studies were collected from the Institute of Cancer Research (ICR) mice and fetal bovine serum (FBS) (Corning, Corning, NY, USA), respectively. The particle suspensions were then diluted 1:9 (v/v) with distilled water (DW) and further sonicated for 10 min using a bath sonicator (Saehan-Sonic). After dispersion in DW, suspensions were centrifuged at 21,000 × g for 30 min at 4 °C, and the supernatants were discarded to remove unbound proteins.

At this step, the resuspending medium was selected differently for small particles (i.e., 0.1, 1, and 10 μm) and large particles (i.e., 100 μm) based on the best dispersion stability. The particles with 0.1, 1, and 10 μm were dispersed in DW, while particles with 100 μm were dispersed in a 75% (v/v) glycerin solution in DW to prevent sedimentation, as sedimentation limits the accuracy of optical absorbance-based quantification. The percentage of glycerin was selected by testing 25%, 50%, and 75% solutions (Fig. S1, see Supporting Information). Furthermore, the 75% glycerin solution did not exhibit optical interference at 750 nm because it showed a similar absorbance value to DW.

Quantitative assessment of collected particles based on UV–Vis absorbance measurements

The particles collected from mice and mussels were quantified using the standard curves generated by UV-Vis spectrophotometer. The standard curve fit and accuracy test were performed with test microplastics. Briefly, the absorbance of particle samples in the dispersion medium (i.e., DW for SPS0.1, SPS1, FPS1, SPE10, and FPP1; 75% glycerin for SPS100, FPS100, FPE100, and FPP100) with 8 concentrations was measured at 750 nm to set the standard curve fit. The absorbance of standard samples was measured using the UV-Vis spectrophotometer (Lambda 365, PerkinElmer, Waltham, MA, USA) at 750 nm. This wavelength was selected based on a full-spectrum evaluation to minimize matrix-derived absorption while retaining sufficient particle-induced scattering sensitivity. Compared with the UV region, where biological chromophores exhibit strong absorbance, the 750 nm region provides a more stable baseline with reduced background interference from tissue components [29–31]. The concentrations for the standard curve were selected based on the measurable values (e.g., absorbance value between 0.027 and 0.438), which differed by particle types: 125–1500 µg/mL for SPS0.1; 3.1–37.5 µg/mL for SPS1; 468.8–5000 µg/mL for SPS100; 5.9–62.5 µg/mL for FPS1; 156.3–1875 µg/mL for FPS100; 62.5–750 µg/mL for SPE10, 625–7500 µg/mL for FPE100; 7.8–93.8 µg/mL for FPP1; 234.4–2500 µg/mL for FPP100. Once the standard curve was prepared, an accuracy test was performed with three independent concentrations. The low concentrations for the accuracy test were selected above the 8th concentration of the standard curve, and then medium and high concentrations were selected at three- and six-fold multiples of the low concentration.

Collection of particles spiked in the mouse organs and mussels

Spiking experiments were performed on biological tissues because method validation requires controlled testing under known exposure conditions. Six-week-old female ICR mice were obtained from Hana Biotech (Gyeonggi-do, Korea) and acclimatized for 7 days before experimentation. The animals were handled and maintained according to procedures approved by the Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee of Dong-A University (# DAU-IACUC23-08). After acclimatization, the animals were sacrificed by incising the vena cava under deep anesthesia with isoflurane (Piramal Critical Care, Bethlehem, PA, USA) using a rodent anesthesia system (VetEquip, Pleasanton, CA, USA). Brains, hearts, intestines, kidneys, livers, lungs, and spleens were dissected, cut into small pieces, and dried at 60℃ for 2 days to maintain the PK digestion conditions, as fresh tissue can alter the buffer’s pH [32]. To recover microplastics from biological tissues, samples were homogenized using a tissue homogenizer (Thomas Scientific, Swedesboro, NJ, USA) under mild conditions, which are known to preserve particle morphology [33, 34]. Marine mussels were selected as test species for the quantification of microplastics in aquatic organisms. The marine mussel Mytilus galloprovincialis was collected from Yeosu-Si, Jeollanam-do (Korea). The collected mussels were cut into small pieces, dried at 60℃ for 2 days, and then pulverized using a tissue homogenizer (Thomas Scientific).

The dried powder of lungs and mussels was used to quantify nine types of microplastics, while other organs were used to quantify FPS1, assessing the feasibility of microplastic quantification across different organs. To ensure reproducibility and comparability, the test particles were spiked into pulverized tissue powder and digested with PK using our previously validated protocol [35, 36], which has been shown to achieve optimal digestion efficiency and particle recovery in biological tissues. The detailed methods were described in Supplementary Text S1 (see Supporting Information).

Proof of concept study using the mouse pharyngeal aspiration model

As a proof-of-concept for in vivo quantification, we applied the developed method to a mouse pharyngeal aspiration model and measured the deposition of microplastics in lung tissue following controlled pulmonary exposure. This approach supports lung burden analysis in inhalation toxicity studies. The collection and quantification methods were tested in mice at 0 and 24 h after the pharyngeal aspiration of microplastics with inhalable particles (i.e., SPS0.1, SPS1, and FPS1). Briefly, six-week-old female ICR mice (Hana Biotech) were acclimatized for 7 days and anesthetized with isoflurane using a rodent anesthesia system (VetEquip). The microplastic suspension dispersed in DW according to the dispersion protocol described in 2.3 was redispersed in phosphate-buffered saline (PBS) after centrifugation at 21,000 × g for 30 min at 4°C. The aspiration volume was 50 µL, and the treatment doses were selected based on the detection ranges of the standard curve: 500 µg/mouse for SPS0.1, 12.5 µg/mouse for SPS1, and 23.4 µg/mouse for FPS1. The pharyngeal aspiration was performed according to our previously described method [37]. The initial deposition concentration and retention fraction were evaluated at time 0 (immediately after aspiration) and 24 h post-aspiration, respectively. Euthanasia, lung dissection, and tissue digestion were performed as described above. Furthermore, the morphology of test microplastics was evaluated using FE-STEM (Thermo Fisher Scientific) after quantification by UV-Vis spectrophotometry to assess whether homogenization affected particle morphology. In addition, representative images of the recovered particles were obtained to visually confirm effective matrix removal and preservation of particle integrity.

Comparison of the PK digestion method with conventional methods

Collection of particles from lung tissue spiked with microplastics using acids and alkalis

The PK digestion method was compared with conventional tissue digestion methods, such as acid and alkaline digestion, in terms of recovery rate and preservation of microplastics’ intrinsic properties [38]. The tested microplastic samples were SPS0.1, SPS1, and FPS1. For acid digestion, 5 mL of 69% HNO3 (Thermo Fisher Scientific) was added to 20 mg of dried mice lung spiked with 500 µg microplastics (i.e., SPS0.1, SPS1, and FPS1) and incubated for 5 h at 80°C using a hot plate (Eppendorf; Hamburg, Germany). Then, 45 mL of DW was added, and the mixture was centrifuged at 21,000 × g for 30 min to collect the particle pellet. The supernatants were discarded, and the pellets were resuspended in 1 mL DW. For alkali digestion, 1 mL of 10% KOH (Sigma-Aldrich, ST. Louis, MO, USA) was added to 20 mg dried mouse lung spiked with 500 µg microplastics and incubated for 72 h at 60°C using the hot block (Eppendorf). After incubation, the digestion solution was diluted 1:9 with DW, and the suspension was centrifuged at 21,000 × g for 30 min to collect particle pellets. The supernatants were discarded, and the pellets were resuspended in 1 mL DW. To evaluate the potential background interference from the digestion protocols, blank control experiments were conducted by processing non-spiked lung tissues under the same conditions used for each digestion method (i.e., acid, alkali, and PK digestion). After digestion, the resulting suspensions were analyzed by UV-Vis spectrophotometry at 750 nm to confirm the absence of background absorbance signals in blank samples (data not shown).

Evaluation of physicochemical properties of collected particles

The particles collected using three different digestion methods were compared for oxidative potential, chemical properties, optical properties, and morphology. The intrinsic oxidative potential of microplastics was measured using the 2′, 7′-dichlorofluorescein diacetate (DCFH-DA) assay, as previously described [25]. The chemical properties of particles collected using three different digestion methods were evaluated by X-ray photoelectron spectroscopy (XPS; K-Alpha, Thermo Fisher Scientific) and Raman spectroscopy (UniRAM-3200 Micro Raman system, Uni nano Tech; Yong-In, Korea). All spectra in the XPS analysis were aligned to the standard with a binding energy of 284.6 eV for the C-C bond. The test particles for Raman analysis were irradiated by a 532 nm laser. The optical properties were measured using the absorbance at 750 nm. Finally, particle morphology was evaluated using FE-STEM (Thermo Fisher Scientific).

Statistical analysis

Statistical analysis and graphs were prepared using GraphPad Prism (version 9.0; La Jolla, CA, USA). The data were presented as the mean ± standard deviation (SD). Linear regression was applied to the standard curve fit. One-way ANOVA was used to compare different groups, after which a Tukey test was conducted as a post-hoc test.

Results

Physicochemical properties of test particles

The physicochemical properties of test particles are presented in Fig. 1. The test particles showed an expected morphology in terms of spherical and fragmented forms when observed with TEM or SEM (Fig. 1). The primary size measured by TEM or SEM was within the intended ranges as 100.2 nm for SPS0.1, 1.02 μm for SPS1, 91.1 μm for SPS100, 1.02 μm for FPS1, and 94.5 μm for FPS100, 15.9 μm for SPE10, 70.3 μm for FPE100, 0.86 μm for FPP1, and 95.3 μm for FPP100 (Table S2, see Supporting Information). The primary sizes of fragmented particles showed a wider, more heterogeneous distribution than those of spherical particles. The hydrodynamic sizes of the test particles showed good dispersion, with values within 30% of the primary particle sizes.

Preparation of standard curves using the absorbance at 750 nm wavelength

Prior to quantification, full-spectrum scanning (200–900 nm) was performed to identify an appropriate analytical window. The spectra confirmed that intrinsic polymer absorbance was primarily confined to the UV region, whereas the 600–900 nm range was largely free of molecular absorption and dominated by particle-induced light scattering (Fig. S2, see Supporting Information). Based on these results, absorbance at 750 nm was used for quantification. Furthermore, 75% glycerin was used to measure the 100 μm-sized microplastic because it did not cause absorbance interference at 750 nm and settled more slowly compared to other concentrations (Fig. S1, see Supporting information). The standard curve fit of all microplastics exhibited a linear regression with R2 values over 0.996 (Fig. 2). Because particles have unique absorbance values depending on their specific physicochemical properties [33], each particle type showed different detection ranges. Among test particles, 1 μm-sized particles (i.e., SPS1, FPS1, and FPP1) were more sensitive than others, and the detection limit of SPS1, FPS1, and FPP1 was approximately 3.1, 5.9, and 7.8 µg/mL, respectively. The SPS0.1, FPS100, SPE10, FPE100, and FPP100 showed similar detection ranges, and SPS100 showed the lowest sensitivity (Fig. 2). The accuracy test of this method using three independent concentrations from the standard curve fit showed that this method has more than 91% accuracy in estimating the target concentrations (Table 1).

Recovery rate of test particles spiked in the mouse and mussel tissue

Recovery rates of test particles were evaluated with the mouse lung and mussel tissue spiked with all microplastics, and recovery rates in various organs, including the brain, heart, kidney, intestine, liver, and spleen, were evaluated using FPS1. All test particles showed a recovery rate higher than 90% from the mouse lung tissue and mussels compared to the nominal concentrations (Fig. 3A and B). Furthermore, FPS1 showed a recovery rate higher than 91% in mouse soft tissues (Fig. 3C). The MDLs and LOQs for the nine tested particles in lung tissue were determined according to the EPA and ICH guidelines, ranging from 0.4 to 194 µg/mL and 1.1 to 618 µg/mL, respectively (Table S3, see Supporting Information). These parameters were established to evaluate the method’s sensitivity in detecting and quantifying each particle type.

Lung burden analysis of test particles using the mouse pharyngeal aspiration model

The concentration of microplastics was measured at times 0 and 24 h after the pharyngeal aspiration into the mouse lungs to test the developed quantification method in animal studies. The lung burden at time 0 (i.e., immediately after instillation) showed that the deposition rate of test particles was more than 94% compared to the nominal dose (Fig. 4A). The retention rates of microplastics at 24 h post-aspiration were 94–98% compared to time 0, which suggests that the test particles are hardly cleared within 24 h after treatment (Fig. 4B). Moreover, no apparent morphological alterations of the test microplastics were observed following homogenization and PK digestion (Fig. S3, see Supporting Information). In addition, the visual appearance of the post-digestion suspension was comparable to that of the primary particle suspension (Fig. S4, see Supporting Information).

Advantages of PK digestion compared to conventional digestion methods, such as acids and alkalis

Because the particle collection method using PK is non-destructive, the collected particles can be further evaluated for their physicochemical properties, such as size, shape, composition, and oxidative potential. The particles collected by the PK digestion showed no morphological changes in any test materials (Fig. 5A and C). However, the microplastics collected after acid and alkali digestion exhibited marked morphological alterations, indicating that these digestion methods can modify particle morphology (Fig. 5D and I). The XPS analysis showed that the ratio of elemental bonds was not changed in all tested digestion methods compared to raw materials (Fig. S5, see Supporting Information). The recovery rate of collected microplastics quantified by UV-Vis spectrophotometry at 750 nm was 35–84% and 27–77% for acids and alkalis, respectively (Fig. 6A). Furthermore, the oxidative potential of test particles was significantly increased by the digestion with acids and alkalis, while that of collected microplastics by the PK digestion method was similar to primary particles (Fig. 6B). In line with these changes in morphology and reactivity, the Raman spectra of particles collected after acid and alkali digestion no longer matched those of the pristine particles, showing altered features in the asymmetric CH2 stretching area near 2900 cm− 1 and aromatic CH stretching area of nearby 3100 cm− 1 (Fig. 6C and E). In contrast, the Raman spectrum of the particles collected by PK digestion was identical to that of pristine particles.

Discussion

Toxicity studies of microplastics using rodents and environmental organisms have increased exponentially, not only for the oral exposure scenarios [39] but also for the inhalation exposure scenarios, as plastic particles are found in indoor and outdoor air [39, 40]. Although toxicity studies have been performed, many studies did not report the organ burden or distribution data for microplastics due to difficulties in quantifying microplastics in biological matrices [33, 40]. Quantitative analysis of microplastics in prevalence studies is more complicated than hazard identification studies because prevalence studies need to quantify different particle types and shapes. In contrast, hazard identification studies usually use a single plastic type, and particles are relatively homogeneous. In this regard, this study proposes a novel methodology to quantify a homogeneous single type of plastic particles in biological matrices, such as mice and mussels. This methodology can provide quick, easy, non-destructive, cost-efficient, and reliable outcomes for collecting particles as-present in organs and their qualitative and quantitative analysis.

Recent studies suggested that nanoparticles can transform into new particle types through physical and/or chemical reactions in the body [41, 42]. Thus, the precise characterization of the physicochemical properties of collected particles as-present in organs and comparison with the test particles is needed to evaluate the biotransformation or biodegradation. For this purpose, biological tissues must be digested in a way that removes organic matrices without altering the physicochemical integrity of the particles. PK, a broad-spectrum serine protease, is widely used due to its substrate specificity. PK cleaves peptide bonds adjacent to the carboxyl groups of aliphatic and aromatic amino acids, and its enzymatic activity remains stable over a broad pH range (4–12), even in the presence of denaturing agents such as SDS and urea [43, 44]. This enables efficient digestion of organic proteinaceous materials in tissue samples while preserving non-proteinaceous particulates. This study demonstrated that the PK enzymatic digestion method can collect microplastics from organs with high yield and as-present state, while tissue digestion with acids and alkalis damaged the physicochemical properties of test particles.

Among various tissue digestion methods, acid solutions (e.g., nitric, perchloric, hydrochloric, and hydrofluoric acid) and base solutions (e.g., potassium hydroxide) in combination with oxidants (e.g., sodium persulfate) are most widely used in aquatic organisms and vertebrates for collecting microplastics in organs [45–47]. However, some microplastics are damaged or destroyed by acid solutions, oxidants, and high temperatures during processing (e.g., > 60°C) [45, 48–50]. Strong acid or alkaline digestion chemically interacts with microplastic polymers, typically occurring through the hydrolysis of ester bonds, which causes chain scission, melting, or fusion of particles, and changes in surface morphology across different polymer types [24]. In addition, oxidizing agents such as Fenton’s reagent and hydrogen peroxide have been reported to induce oxidative modifications on plastic surfaces, introducing hydroxyl, carbonyl, and carboxyl groups and increasing surface roughness and hydrophilicity [51–53]. Such alterations may significantly change the native surface chemistry of microplastics, thereby compromising their use in subsequent toxicological evaluations.

Biodegradable microplastics are more susceptible to destruction by contact with acids and bases; thus, enzymatic digestion (e.g., pepsin, pancreatic enzymes) has been suggested as an alternative tissue digestion method [54, 55]. For example, trypsin digestion of mussels showed a better recovery rate of about 88% compared to collagenase and papain, which had recovery rates below 80% [56]. Moreover, although lipase has been applied as a mild digestion enzyme, its recovery efficiency is highly dependent on tissue type and experimental conditions, with reports of incomplete removal of proteinaceous residues in bivalve tissues and residual proteins persisting after treatment, necessitating additional enzymatic steps [57, 58]. In this study, we demonstrated that PK alone enabled an effective recovery rate of over 90% for nano- and microplastics, suggesting that additional enzymatic treatment or repeated applications of PK would further improve recovery efficiency. Therefore, the digestion solution was selected based on the type of tissue to be digested completely.

In contrast, PK effectively digests biological tissue without causing such chemical degradation to the plastics, thus preserving particle integrity. However, it should be noted that certain biodegradable polymers, such as polylactic acid (PLA), have a glass transition temperature near 56°C, which could cause softening or deformation under the conditions used in this study. Similarly, polyhydroxyalkanoates (PHAs) are known to exhibit thermal sensitivity due to their crystallinity. Furthermore, polymers containing polar functional groups or heteroatoms, such as polyvinyl chloride (PVC), polyamide (PA), and polyethylene terephthalate (PET), may be hydrolyzed in proteinase K buffer at a weak alkaline pH [59–61]. Therefore, caution may be necessary when using this protocol with heat- or pH-sensitive microplastics, and further research is needed to determine the optimal enzymatic conditions for these materials [62, 63].

This study also suggested that some heterogeneous samples, such as fragmented particles, are acceptable for quantification. These considerations indicate that, although the 200–300 nm region has been widely used to detect plastics due to polymer-specific electronic transitions that generate strong intrinsic absorbance signals [64, 65], its strong susceptibility to solvent and matrix interference limits its quantitative reliability [64]. To overcome these inherent limitations associated with UV-region absorbance, alternative wavelength regions less susceptible to matrix interference must be considered. In contrast, for the plastic particles tested in this study, the 600–900 nm region is essentially free of intrinsic polymer absorbance, and the measured signal is dominated primarily by particle-associated scattering [66, 67]. Significantly, tissue chromophore absorption (e.g., hemoglobin, melanin) decreases markedly above 600 nm, thereby reducing background interference in this range [68, 69]. Consistent with this optical window, measurements at 750 nm provided stable baselines and reliable quantification in the present biological matrices [35]. Furthermore, the results of this study showed that absorbance in the UV region varied with the physicochemical properties of the plastics; however, absorbance in the visible region remained comparable across all test particles. Accordingly, this approach is most suitable for controlled experimental systems containing known particle types, rather than for direct quantification of unknown heterogeneous particulate mixtures. This optical behavior explains why smaller plastic particles often appear more transparent, as their reduced size markedly limits light scattering in the visible range [70, 71]. Conversely, fragmented or irregularly shaped particles exhibit stronger scattering than smooth spherical particles, making them appear less transparent and more easily detected in optical analyses [72, 73].

Particles collected by the PK tissue digestion can be analyzed by optical methods such as Raman spectroscopy, FT-IR, and UV-Vis spectrophotometry, while application to py-GC/MS may require additional consideration of matrix-related interference. Although UV-Vis spectrophotometry has relatively poor sensitivity, it remains a useful option for quantifying homogeneous particles in biological samples at measurable concentrations. Furthermore, UV–Vis spectrophotometry offers practical advantages such as rapid analysis, simpler sample preparation, significantly lower instrumentation and operational costs, and compatibility with high-throughput workflows while maintaining adequate sensitivity for microplastics quantification at environmental concentrations, especially when combined with PK digestion [74]. While PK digestion offers clear advantages—such as high recovery rates, accuracy, low cost, rapid processing, and non-destructive particle isolation compared to advanced instrumental methods (e.g., Raman, FT-IR) [24, 33]—practical considerations remain. These include strict control of experimental conditions (temperature, buffer composition, cleanliness) [33] and potential constraints for very large-scale or high-throughput sample analyses [75]. In particular, because PK digestion primarily targets proteinaceous tissue components, additional matrix-specific cleanup may be needed when lipid-rich biological samples are subjected to thermal analytical methods, as lipid-derived interference has been reported in Py-GC-MS-based quantification of polymers such as PE in animal tissues [76]. Laboratories should thus carefully consider these operational aspects when implementing PK digestion.

Given the challenges of dispersing microplastics in complex biological matrices, verifying quantification accuracy in simplified systems is a critical step prior to in vivo application. In this study, we demonstrated that the applied protocol resulted in accuracy rates exceeding 90% across all test particles, supporting its suitability for further biological applications. To address sedimentation of large or dense particles, glycerin was used to prevent the settling of 100 μm-sized microplastics, offering a practical solution to maintaining suspension stability. Furthermore, this method quantifies microplastics using a UV-Vis spectrophotometer based on absorbance standards, which makes it convenient for routine microplastic research. However, a key limitation is its inability to quantify microplastics in unknown environmental samples because it relies on pre-established standard curves. Nonetheless, enzymatic digestion enables the qualitative identification of microplastics in unknown matrices without altering their physicochemical properties, thereby supporting downstream analysis. Therefore, the decision tree shown in Fig. 7 provides a better understanding of how to perform quantitative analysis of microplastics and nanoplastics in biological samples. To our knowledge, the combination of PK tissue digestion and UV-Vis spectrophotometry for quantitative and qualitative analysis of nano- and microplastics described in this study has not been previously reported and may facilitate related studies.

Conclusions

This study provides a novel methodology for the quantitative and qualitative analysis of nano- and microplastics deposited in biological tissues for pre-clinical toxicity assessment. This technique is applicable only to spherical primary particles and fragmented secondary particles when the test particles are poorly soluble, have good absorbance at 600–900 nm, are non-destructive during PK digestion, are separable by centrifugation, and are well-dispersible in a water-based solution. For particles meeting these criteria, this methodology provides quick, easy, non-destructive, cost-efficient, and reliable outcomes for collecting in situ particles in organs and for their quantitative and qualitative analysis. Furthermore, these results suggest that PK digestion, unlike acidic or alkaline processes, does not damage microplastics and can also enable accurate qualitative identification of unknown samples. This addresses a critical technical gap in microplastic toxicology research and can facilitate a more comprehensive assessment of biological effects and biokinetics.
