Efficacy and safety of haemostatic agents for patients receiving antiplatelet therapy before coronary artery bypass grafting: a protocol for a systematic review and network meta-analysis of randomised controlled trials
Authors: Chao Chen, Xinguang Wei, Yunfei Han, Xiao Ren, Qiubei Xiao, Ying Xiong, Ruirui Mao, Yan Liu
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractIntroductionMost patients undergoing coronary artery bypass grafting (CABG) receive preoperative antiplatelet therapy to reduce ischaemic events but this increases perioperative bleeding and transfusion risk. Tranexamic acid, aminocaproic acid, aprotinin and desmopressin are commonly employed to reduce such risk; however, robust direct head-to-head comparative evidence remains limited in this specific population. Therefore, we will conduct a network meta-analysis (NMA) of randomised controlled trials (RCTs) to compare their relative efficacy and safety.Methods and analysisWe will systematically search PubMed, Embase, Web of Science, Scopus and Cochrane Central Register of Controlled Trials from inception to 24 March 2026 for RCTs that compare these four agents versus placebo/standard care or head-to-head in adults receiving preoperative antiplatelet therapy for CABG. Two reviewers will independently screen studies, extract data and assess the risk of bias using the Risk of Bias Instrument for Use in SysTematic reviews-for Randomised Controlled Trials tool. Primary outcomes will be the total perioperative volume of blood loss and the proportion of patients receiving red blood cell transfusions. Secondary outcomes will include blood product transfusion volumes, reoperation rates for bleeding or cardiac tamponade, rates of composite thromboembolic events, all-cause mortality and incidence of acute kidney injury. Pairwise random-effects meta-analyses will be performed first followed by Bayesian NMA. Surface under the cumulative ranking curve for the outcomes of interest will be used to rank the competing interventions. The Confidence in Network Meta-Analysis framework will be used to assess the quality of evidence.Ethics and disseminationEthical approval is not required. Findings will be submitted to a peer-reviewed journal and disseminated to support clinical decision-making.PROSPERO registration numberCRD420251235248.

Introduction

Coronary artery disease remains a leading cause of death and disability worldwide,1 2 and coronary artery bypass grafting (CABG) is a well-established revascularisation strategy for patients with high coronary complexity, which significantly improves long-term survival.35 However, these patients remain vulnerable to perioperative ischaemic events, prompting current guidelines to continue to recommend preoperative antiplatelet therapy.6 7 This strategy, while cardioprotective, concurrently confers a heightened risk of bleeding and subsequent transfusion requirements.810

To balance ischaemic and bleeding risks, systemic haemostatic agents are commonly administered to CABG patients with preoperative antiplatelet therapy, among which three antifibrinolytic agents—tranexamic acid (TXA), aminocaproic acid (EACA) and aprotinin—along with desmopressin (DDAVP) are the most frequently used in clinical practice.

TXA, a synthetic lysine analogue, which inhibits plasminogen-to-plasmin conversion to reduce fibrin degradation,11 has been demonstrated to reduce perioperative blood transfusion requirements in patients receiving either single antiplatelet therapy (SAPT) or dual antiplatelet therapy (DAPT),1216 but is linked to an increased risk of convulsive seizures.1720 EACA, which has a similar mechanism of action to TXA but is only one-tenth as potent, has been demonstrated to be effective in reducing blood loss and transfusion in cardiac surgery patients,21 but concerns have been raised regarding its potential risk of postoperative renal dysfunction.22 Aprotinin, a broad-spectrum serine protease inhibitor, exerts a potent antifibrinolytic effect by inhibiting plasmin and kallikrein.23 Although efficacy in reducing bleeding and transfusion requirements is well established,2428 the Blood conservation using Antifibrinolytics in a Randomised Trial (BART) study demonstrated increased mortality.29 Consequently, the European Medicines Agency (EMA) temporarily suspended its clinical approval in 2007.30 The Committee for Medicinal Products for Human Use judged the BART study to have methodological flaws (eg, inadequate heparin dosing, inappropriate data exclusion) and recommended lifting the suspension of aprotinin, limiting its use to prophylaxis in adults at high risk of major bleeding undergoing isolated CABG.31 The European Commission later approved this recommendation through Commission Regulation (EU) No 907/2013, reinstating aprotinin’s marketing authorisation under these strict conditions.32 DDAVP, a vasopressin analogue, enhances platelet aggregation by releasing factor VIII and von Willebrand factor.33 34 It may effectively reduce blood loss and allogeneic transfusion requirements in subgroups such as patients on antiplatelet therapy or with platelet dysfunction,3538 but may cause severe hypotension requiring haemodynamic support.39

However, head-to-head comparative trials among these four agents in CABG patients receiving antiplatelet therapy are scarce, and existing meta-analyses have three critical limitations: first, they aggregate patients regardless of antiplatelet therapy status (thus failing to address the specific needs of the CABG antiplatelet-exposed subgroup)16 17 39 40; second, they restrict inclusion to aspirin-only patients41; and third, no study has simultaneously compared all four agents within this population.1736 4245 To address this evidence gap, we will conduct a Bayesian network meta-analysis (NMA) of randomised controlled trials (RCTs), generating the treatment efficacy and safety ranking to inform perioperative haemostatic decisions and guideline development.

Methods and analysis

Design

This protocol follows the Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Review and Meta-Analysis Protocols (PRISMA-P) 2015 guideline.46 The final systematic review will adhere to the Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Reviews and Meta-Analyses (PRISMA) extension for NMA.47 It is registered with PROSPERO (CRD420251235248). Any protocol amendments will be documented with dates, rationales and updated in the PROSPERO registry. The PRISMA-P checklist of this study is included in online supplemental table 1.

Eligibility criteria

Inclusion criteria

(1) Population: adult patients (≥18 years) who received preoperative antiplatelet therapy and underwent primary isolated or combined CABG; (2) intervention: perioperative haemostatic agents (TXA, EACA, aprotinin or DDAVP); (3) comparators: another study agent, placebo or standard care; (4) outcomes: studies reporting at least one of primary or secondary outcomes listed below; (5) study design: RCTs.

Exclusion criteria

(1) Duplicate publications or multiple reports of the same study cohort (only the most comprehensive or recent version will be retained); (2) studies with unavailable full texts, or conference abstracts, trial registry records or other summary reports insufficient for eligibility assessment and risk-of-bias evaluation (ie, lacking study design, participant characteristics, intervention details or key outcomes); (3) studies with incomplete, unextractable or irreparably erroneous outcome data that could not be supplemented or clarified after contacting the corresponding authors, despite full-text accessibility.

Information sources and search strategy

We will systematically search PubMed, Embase, Web of Science, Scopus and the Cochrane Central Register of Controlled Trials from their inception to 24 March 2026, with no restrictions on language, year or publication status.

Additional studies will be identified by: (1) searching clinical trial registries (eg, ClinicalTrials.gov, the WHO International Clinical Trials Registry Platform (ICTRP)); (2) hand-searching reference lists of included studies and relevant reviews; (3) browsing official websites of cardiovascular societies (eg, American Heart Association, European Society of Cardiology, European Association for Cardio-Thoracic Surgery); (4) contacting corresponding authors for unpublished data or information clarification.

The search strategy, developed following the Peer Review of Electronic Search Strategies (PRESS) guideline,48 combines controlled vocabulary including Medical Subject Headings (MeSH) for PubMed and Emtree for Embase, as well as free-text terms covering: (1) CABG and related procedures; (2) antiplatelet therapy and specific antiplatelet agents; (3) TXA, EACA, aprotinin and DDAVP; (4) RCTs and related study designs. Full search strategies are presented in online supplemental table 2.

Study selection

Records retrieved from all aforementioned sources will be collated and deduplicated using EndNote and manual verification, then two independent reviewers will screen titles and abstracts for eligibility using Rayyan.49 Full texts of potentially eligible records will then be independently assessed against the eligibility criteria by the two reviewers.

Discrepancies at any stage will be resolved by consensus, or a third independent reviewer will make the final decision if consensus is not reached. Study selection will be documented in a PRISMA flow diagram, and reasons for exclusion at each stage will be recorded. Inter-reviewer agreement will be quantified using Cohen’s kappa.

For transfusion outcomes, we will document the transfusion protocols and trigger thresholds reported in each study. For thromboembolic events and AKI, we will record the specific definitions and diagnostic criteria used (eg, risk, injury, failure, loss, end-stage renal disease, acute kidney injury network (AKIN) or kidney disease: improving global outcomes (KDIGO) criteria for AKI; clinical diagnosis vs imaging-confirmed for thromboembolic events). Data extraction and management

Two independent reviewers will use a standardised form to collect: study characteristics (design, sample size), participant demographics and clinical features (including age, sex, body mass index, EuroSCORE, renal function, emergency vs elective status and baseline haemoglobin), antiplatelet regimen details (including cessation timing), intervention specifics (drug, dose, timing), surgical details (eg, on/off-pump), transfusion-related details (eg, haemoglobin or haematocrit thresholds for allogeneic RBC transfusion; criteria for fresh frozen plasma and platelet concentrate transfusion, including coagulation-parameter thresholds, bleeding-volume triggers, platelet-count thresholds or physician-directed protocols) and outcome data. For each outcome, the precise definition, measurement method and timing reported in the original trial (eg, blood loss assessment technique and time points, transfusion trigger criteria, AKI diagnostic criteria such as KDIGO stage and thromboembolic event definitions) will be recorded to assess cross-study standardisation. Continuous outcomes comprise means, SDs and sample sizes; dichotomous outcomes comprise the number of events and the total number of patients. Intention-to-treat data will be prioritised when available. Discrepancies will be resolved by consensus or a third reviewer.

Missing data

For missing or unclear data, we will contact the corresponding authors. If data are presented only in figures, we will use WebPlotDigitizer for extraction.50 For studies reporting medians and ranges, we will estimate means and SDs using the method described by Wan et al.51 If data remain unavailable after these attempts, the study will be excluded from the relevant analysis, and the reason will be documented.52

Risk of bias assessment

We will assess the risk of bias using the Risk of Bias Instrument for Use in SysTematic reviews-for Randomised Controlled Trials (ROBUST-RCT) tool—a newly developed, user-centred instrument designed to balance methodological rigour and practical usability.53 ROBUST-RCT will evaluate six core domains: (1) random sequence generation, (2) allocation concealment, (3) blinding of participants, (4) blinding of healthcare providers, (5) blinding of outcome assessors and (6) missing outcome data. For each domain, we will apply a standardised two-step process: step 1 will assess whether the methodological safeguard was implemented (eg, ‘Was allocation concealed using sequentially numbered opaque sealed envelopes?’); step 2 will judge the resulting risk of bias (rated as definitely low, probably low, probably high or definitely high). Overall risk of bias for each trial will be classified as low if all six domains are rated definitely low or probably low; otherwise, it will be classified as high. Two reviewers will independently perform the assessments; discrepancies will be resolved by consensus or consultation with a third reviewer.

Data synthesis

All statistical analyses will be performed using R software (V.4.4.3) with the following packages: metafor (for pairwise meta-analysis), gemtc and rjags (for Bayesian network meta-analysis), dplyr and tidyr (for data management). For pairwise meta-analyses, a two-sided p value <0.05 will be considered statistically significant. For Bayesian network meta-analyses, a statistically significant effect will be defined as a 95% credible interval (CrI) that excludes the null value (risk ratio (RR)=1 for dichotomous outcomes or mean difference (MD)=0 for continuous outcomes).

Pairwise meta-analysis

For direct comparisons with ≥3 RCTs, pairwise meta-analyses will be conducted using a random-effects model with a restricted maximum likelihood estimator.52 Continuous outcomes will be reported as MDs with 95% CIs; the standardised MD will be used instead if outcomes are measured on different scales. Dichotomous outcomes will be reported as RRs with 95% CIs.

Heterogeneity will be assessed using I² (substantial heterogeneity defined as I²>50%) and τ², with τ² estimates and 95% CIs reported.52

Network meta-analysis

Transitivity and network geometry

The transitivity assumption (ie, the distribution of effect modifiers is similar across comparisons) will be assessed by comparing key clinical and methodological characteristics across studies grouped by comparison.54 Specifically, we will systematically examine the distribution of: (1) baseline clinical profiles (eg, age, sex, body mass index, EuroSCORE, renal function, baseline haemoglobin and proportion of urgent or emergency procedures); (2) antiplatelet regimen (aspirin monotherapy, P2Y12 inhibitor monotherapy or dual antiplatelet therapy) and preoperative cessation timing; (3) surgical technique (on-pump vs off-pump CABG); and (4) transfusion-related factors (restrictive vs liberal RBC transfusion thresholds; algorithm-guided vs physician-directed transfusion protocols for fresh frozen plasma and platelet concentrates). Meaningful imbalances in these effect modifiers across comparison-specific study groups will be identified and addressed through subgroup analyses, meta-regression (if ≥10 studies are available) and sensitivity analyses restricted to more homogeneous subsets, and will be explicitly discussed as potential sources of indirect confounding. Network plots will be generated to visualise the available evidence,55 with each trial annotated by sample size and overall risk-of-bias judgement to visualise evidence quality.

Model specification

A contrast-based Bayesian network meta-analysis will be conducted within a Markov chain Monte Carlo framework, implemented in the gemtc and rjags packages in R software.56 57 Non-informative or weakly informative prior distributions will be used for all model parameters, in line with current Bayesian NMA guidelines. Model convergence will be assessed using the Brooks-Gelman-Rubin (BGR) statistic (convergence defined as BGR <1.05) and visual inspection of trace plots. Model fit will be evaluated using the deviance information criterion (DIC), with a lower DIC indicating a better fit. For rare binary events (event rate <5%), a continuity correction or a penalised binomial likelihood model will be applied to avoid biased effect estimates.

Relative treatment effects and ranking

Relative treatment effects for all outcomes will be reported as RRs with 95% CrIs for dichotomous outcomes and MDs with 95% CrIs for continuous outcomes. All treatment interventions will be ranked according to their efficacy and safety using mean ranks and the surface under the cumulative ranking curve (SUCRA) (range: 0–100%); a higher SUCRA value indicates a more favourable treatment effect for the corresponding outcome.58 SUCRA values will be reported as an adjunct to, not a substitute for, relative effect estimates. Clinical decisions should consider the magnitude and precision of effects rather than rank order alone. For safety outcomes (composite thromboembolic events and AKI), SUCRA-based rankings will be treated as purely exploratory, with primary interpretation guided by absolute risks and numbers needed to treat or harm.

Assessment of inconsistency

Consistency between direct and indirect comparisons will be assessed via node-splitting analysis.59 Where significant inconsistency (p<0.05) is detected, we will prioritise direct evidence and treat indirect estimates as supportive only; such inconsistency will be further explored via subgroup analyses/meta-regression (if ≥3 studies contribute data).

Subgroup analyses and meta-regression

Subgroup analyses will be conducted by patient factors (age: ≥65/<65 years, diabetes, renal function), antiplatelet factors (regimen: aspirin monotherapy/P2Y12 monotherapy/dual therapy, cessation timing: such as clopidogrel ≥5/<5 days preoperatively), drug factors (TXA/aprotinin dose: low/high), surgical factors (heparin exposure, urgency, on/off-pump CABG), transfusion-related factors (restrictive vs liberal RBC transfusion thresholds; algorithm-guided vs physician-directed transfusion protocols for fresh frozen plasma and platelet concentrates, if reported) and publication era (pre-2008, 2008–2012, post-2013) to account for temporal changes in aprotinin use and regulatory status.

Meta-regression will be performed if ≥10 studies are available and there is sufficient heterogeneity to explore, to assess the impact of study-level covariates on NMA results, using the R gemtc package.52 To avoid overfitting, we will prioritise the following core covariates that are most likely to confound the comparison of haemostatic agents: mean or median EuroSCORE, mean baseline haemoglobin, proportion of urgent or emergency procedures, mean cardiopulmonary bypass duration and publication year. Additional covariates (eg, mean age, proportion of female patients, mean body mass index, proportion with renal dysfunction) will be explored in univariable analyses if data permit, but we will avoid multivariable meta-regression with more than one or two covariates simultaneously to prevent spurious findings. This will help determine whether observed treatment effects are confounded by systematic differences in baseline clinical profiles between comparison groups.

Sensitivity analyses

We will perform several sensitivity analyses to test the robustness of our findings, including: (1) excluding studies at high risk of bias as judged by ROBUST-RCT; (2) using a fixed-effects model; (3) different imputation strategies for missing data (eg, best-worst and worst-best case scenarios); (4) excluding crossover trials (if any); (5) excluding studies that employ non-standard or non-comparable outcome definitions (eg, blood loss measured by non-validated visual estimation rather than quantitative drainage, or thromboembolic and AKI definitions that deviate substantially from contemporary consensus criteria such as KDIGO or AKIN); and (6) excluding studies published before 2008 or before 2013 to evaluate temporal bias related to aprotinin regulatory changes.

Publication bias assessment

For pairwise comparisons with ≥10 studies, comparison-adjusted funnel plots and Egger’s test will assess small-study effects (publication bias significant at p<0.05).60 Comparison-adjusted funnel plots will assess publication bias across the entire NMA network.61

Quality of evidence assessment

The CINeMA framework will be used to assess evidence quality for NMA findings, with within-study bias evaluated specifically using the ROBUST-RCT judgements (ie, trials rated as high overall risk of bias will contribute to downgrading the confidence rating), alongside reporting bias, indirectness, imprecision, heterogeneity, and incoherence.62 63

Patient and public involvement

Patients and the public were not involved in the design, conduct, reporting or dissemination of this research.

Discussion

To the best of our knowledge, this will be the first NMA comparing TXA, EACA, aprotinin and DDAVP exclusively in CABG patients receiving preoperative antiplatelet therapy (including SAPT and DAPT)—a high-risk subgroup facing the clinical dilemma of balancing perioperative thrombotic and bleeding risks. Existing meta-analyses have pooled mixed populations (with or without antiplatelet exposure), precluding definitive guidance on agent selection. The Bayesian network meta-analytic approach offers several advantages over conventional pairwise meta-analyses, including the ability to rank treatments and estimate comparative effectiveness even when head-to-head trials are unavailable.

The data generated in this study will be analysed and interpreted with caution in light of several limitations.

First, between-study heterogeneity is inevitable, arising from variations in patient characteristics, antiplatelet regimens, haemostatic agent dosing and administration timing and surgical techniques across included RCTs.13 14 64 65 To mitigate this, we will use random-effects models, conduct extensive subgroup and meta-regression analyses, and assess heterogeneity with I² and τ². We explicitly acknowledge that differences in antiplatelet regimens, variable preoperative discontinuation timing, distinct surgical techniques (on-pump vs off-pump) and non-standardised transfusion protocols—including heterogeneous thresholds for RBC transfusion and variable criteria for fresh frozen plasma and platelet concentrate transfusion—may violate the transitivity assumption and compromise the validity of indirect inferences. To address this, we will present comparison-specific tables detailing the distribution of these effect modifiers and will interpret indirect comparisons cautiously when transitivity is judged to be threatened.

Second, the choice of haemostatic agent may reflect distinct baseline clinical profiles (eg, higher bleeding risk for aprotinin), which can introduce indirect confounding bias. We will explore this via meta-regression and transitivity assessment, but residual confounding from unmeasured covariates cannot be fully excluded.

Third, the definitions and measurement methods for perioperative blood loss, thromboembolic events and AKI may vary across trials (eg, timing and methods of blood loss assessment, diagnostic criteria for AKI and composite thromboembolic event components). Furthermore, transfusion-related factors such as restrictive versus liberal RBC transfusion thresholds and algorithm-guided versus physician-directed transfusion protocols for fresh frozen plasma and platelet concentrates may differ widely across institutions, acting as important effect modifiers that influence the standardisation and comparability of haemostatic and transfusion outcomes. This variability may introduce outcome heterogeneity and compromise the standardisation of pooled estimates.

Fourth, we focus on perioperative outcomes (up to 30 days), so longer-term events will not be formally analysed. We will discuss long-term outcomes qualitatively and suggest directions for future research.

Fifth, potential publication bias and missing data are unavoidable; we will use funnel plots, Egger’s test and sensitivity analyses to assess their impact and conduct best-worst/worst-best case sensitivity analyses to evaluate the impact of missing data on study results.

Sixth, our findings rely on the assumptions of transitivity and consistency. Despite assessment via statistical/visual inspection and node-splitting, residual confounding from unmeasured covariates cannot be fully excluded.

Seventh, aprotinin’s regulatory history—specifically the EMA suspension in 2007 and conditional re-approval in 2013—may introduce temporal bias, with older studies potentially differing in patient selection, dosing practices and safety monitoring standards. We will address this through era-stratified subgroup analyses and sensitivity analyses excluding studies published before 2008 or 2013, but residual temporal confounding cannot be fully excluded.

Eighth, SUCRA-based treatment rankings may be misleading if small differences are over-interpreted. We will present rankings alongside absolute effects and emphasise that they do not necessarily reflect clinically important differences.

Ninth, where node-splitting reveals significant inconsistency, we will prioritise direct evidence and treat indirect comparisons as supportive only. Readers should therefore interpret indirect findings with appropriate circumspection.

In addition to the methodological limitations above, we emphasise that safety outcomes—specifically thromboembolic and renal endpoints—will be interpreted with particular caution. Any apparent differences in these risks require confirmation in large, dedicated trials and should not drive clinical decisions without consideration of absolute effect sizes. This caution reflects both the anticipated rarity of these events in the perioperative setting and the limited power of existing RCTs to detect clinically meaningful differences in harm.

Despite its limitations, this systematic review and NMA will provide the most comprehensive evidence to date regarding the comparative efficacy and safety of TXA, EACA, aprotinin and DDAVP for patients receiving antiplatelet therapy before CABG. The results will inform the development of evidence-based clinical guidelines, enhance patient safety and optimise blood management strategies in this high-risk surgical population.

Ethics and dissemination

Ethical approval is not required for this study, as all data will be extracted from anonymised, peer-reviewed published RCTs that have obtained ethical approval from their respective institutional review boards. No individual patient data will be used, and patient confidentiality will be strictly maintained.
