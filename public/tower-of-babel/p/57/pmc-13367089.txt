Factors associated with willingness to receive an adult tetanus booster in China: A cross-sectional study incorporating behavioral factor analysis
Authors: Wei Jiang, Ying Li, Yangyujing Li, Qinghao Guo, Di Liu
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTTetanus is vaccine-preventable but potentially fatal. In China, routine adult booster vaccination remains uncommon, and tetanus prevention continues to rely largely on post-injury prophylaxis. Evidence on willingness to receive an adult tetanus booster and its behavioral correlates is limited. We conducted a cross-sectional online survey of 1,522 adults in China to assess willingness to receive a tetanus booster and to examine cognitive, psychological, and service-related factors associated with willingness. The questionnaire collected sociodemographic characteristics, tetanus-related knowledge, and responses to 16 items covering potential promoting factors and barriers. Exploratory factor analysis identified three promoting dimensions (information clarity, service convenience, and opportunity/timing) and two barrier dimensions (accessibility barriers and psychological/cognitive barriers). Multivariable logistic regression was used to examine factors associated with willingness to receive a tetanus booster within the next 6 months under a convenient-vaccination scenario. Overall, 857 participants (56.3%) reported willingness to receive a booster. Information clarity (OR = 1.55, 95% CI: 1.29–1.86), opportunity/timing (OR = 1.21, 95% CI: 1.03–1.49), knowledge of the recommended 10-y booster interval (OR = 1.91, 95% CI: 1.42–2.63), and knowledge of where vaccination could be obtained (OR = 1.44, 95% CI: 1.16–1.84) were positively associated with willingness. Higher psychological/cognitive barrier scores were negatively associated with willingness (OR = 0.67, 95% CI: 0.56–0.79). Accessibility barriers showed a small positive association (OR = 1.16, 95% CI: 1.01–1.34), which warrants cautious interpretation. Basic sociodemographic characteristics were not independently associated with willingness in the adjusted model. In this sample, willingness to receive an adult tetanus booster was more closely related to cognitive, psychological, and service-related factors than to basic sociodemographic characteristics. These findings identify communication and service-delivery targets for future evaluation in adult tetanus booster programs in China.KEYWORDS: Tetanus, vaccination, booster immunization, vaccine acceptance, health behavior, factor analysis, adult immunization, China

Introduction

Tetanus is a severe, vaccine-preventable neurologic disease caused by Clostridium tetani, an environmental spore-forming bacterium that can enter the body through even minor or unnoticed wounds and produce a potent neurotoxin affecting the central nervous system.1,2 Although widespread childhood immunization has substantially reduced the global burden of tetanus, adult protection gaps remain in many settings where routine booster vaccination is not well established. Global incident tetanus cases decreased by 88%, from 615,728 in 1990 to 73,662 in 2019.3 Nevertheless, adult tetanus cases, including fatal cases, continue to be reported; a recent systematic review identified 182 adult cases from 36 countries between 1990 and 2024.4

To maintain protection across the life course, the World Health Organization recommends completion of a six-dose tetanus toxoid-containing series, including three booster doses after infancy.5 The U.S. Centers for Disease Control and Prevention further recommends that adults receive a tetanus-containing booster every 10 y.6,7 Despite these recommendations, adult booster coverage remains incomplete. In the 2018 U.S. National Health Interview Survey, for example, 62.9% of adults aged 19 y or older reported receipt of any tetanus toxoid-containing vaccine within the preceding 10 y, whereas tetanus-diphtheria-acellular pertussis vaccine coverage was 31.2%.8 In China, tetanus prevention is still addressed more often through post-traumatic management and post-exposure prophylaxis than through routine adult preventive vaccination.9,10 Many adults may therefore be unfamiliar with the need for booster vaccination before injury, and tetanus prevention is often considered only after trauma has occurred.

Adult vaccination decisions are commonly linked to cognitive factors, emotional responses, and vaccine-related health literacy.11–13 Vaccine hesitancy is often conceptualized using the WHO-SAGE 3C framework – confidence, complacency, and convenience – and the expanded 5C model, which further distinguishes confidence, complacency, constraints, calculation, and collective responsibility. The Health Belief Model similarly emphasizes perceived susceptibility, perceived severity, perceived benefits, perceived barriers, and cues to action as constructs relevant to preventive health behavior.14,15 Recent Chinese studies of vaccination intention also indicate that vaccine-related decision-making reflects multiple cognitive, attitudinal, and contextual constructs rather than knowledge alone.16,17 Although most tetanus research has focused on childhood immunization, wound management, or seroepidemiologic immunity patterns, adults’ willingness to receive tetanus booster vaccination has received much less attention. This gap is particularly relevant in China, where disease visibility is low, public reminders for adult boosters are limited, and post-injury prophylaxis is more familiar than pre-injury active booster vaccination.9,10

The promoting and barrier items used in this study were not designed to constitute a validated 3C/5C or Health Belief Model instrument. Rather, they were developed to represent behaviorally relevant constructs, including confidence-related information clarity, uncertainty about need or complacency-related perceptions, convenience or constraints related to access and service delivery, and cues to action created by vaccination opportunities in routine care. We therefore analyzed these items as exploratory behavioral factors associated with willingness to receive an adult tetanus booster. Using exploratory factor analysis and multivariable modeling, this study examined adult willingness to receive a tetanus booster in China and identified cognitive, psychological, and service-related factors associated with willingness.

Methods

Study design and participants

This cross-sectional survey was conducted to assess willingness to receive an adult tetanus booster in China and to examine cognitive, psychological, and service-related factors associated with willingness. The survey was administered online through Wenjuanxing, a widely used Chinese web-based survey platform. Rather than being posted as an unrestricted public advertisement, the questionnaire link was distributed through targeted clinical, academic, workplace, and community networks. Recruitment used convenience and chain-referral sampling. The link was initially shared through outpatient settings and affiliated academic, workplace, clinical, patient, and family networks. Data were collected between May 2025 and October 2025.

Before accessing the questionnaire, participants were required to read an electronic informed consent statement indicating that the study was intended for adults aged 18 y or older who could read Chinese. Only those who provided consent could proceed to the survey. The questionnaire included only adult age categories and did not provide a response option for individuals younger than 18 y. To reduce duplicate responses, Wenjuanxing was configured to allow only one submission per account. All study variables were mandatory, and questionnaires could not be submitted unless all required items were completed.

Because recruitment used convenience and chain-referral sampling and the survey link could be forwarded within participants’ networks, the number of individuals who received or viewed the survey invitation could not be determined; therefore, a conventional response rate could not be calculated. During the study period, 1,522 respondents completed the questionnaire and were included in the final analysis. Although the eligibility statement and questionnaire settings were intended to limit participation to eligible adults, eligibility was self-reported and could not be independently verified in this link-based online survey. The findings should therefore be interpreted as completed responses from a non-probability convenience and chain-referral online sample rather than as estimates from a probability-based sample with a defined sampling frame.

Participants were also asked whether they belonged to a consensus-defined high-risk group for tetanus exposure or vulnerability. This item was developed with reference to a 2024 Chinese expert consensus on the use of tetanus vaccines in high-risk populations.18 Predefined categories included military, police, or firefighting work; construction or other manual labor; agricultural, forestry, or fishery work; outdoor work or sports; frequent minor injuries or needle-stick exposure; pregnancy or planned pregnancy within 12 months; age 65 y or older; and not applicable. Participants selecting any predefined risk category were coded as “yes,” whereas those selecting “not applicable” were coded as “no.”

Questionnaire development and measures

The questionnaire was developed specifically for this study. Initial items were generated from the study objectives, relevant literature, and clinical experience in tetanus prevention and post-exposure management. Six experts from trauma surgery, emergency surgery, animal injury clinics, and immunization-related clinical practice reviewed the draft questionnaire. Content validity was assessed quantitatively using item-level content validity indices (I-CVI) and the scale-level content validity index average (S-CVI/Ave) based on expert ratings. I-CVI values ranged from 0.83 to 1.00, and the S-CVI/Ave was 0.92. The revised questionnaire was then pilot-tested with 20 frontline clinicians to improve wording clarity, comprehensibility, and contextual appropriateness before final administration. Because the instrument was developed for this study rather than adopted from a previously validated scale, it should be considered a study-specific exploratory questionnaire. Although content validity and internal consistency were assessed, full psychometric prevalidation was not performed.

The questionnaire comprised four domains: (1) sociodemographic characteristics; (2) clinical history and potential tetanus exposure; (3) knowledge and perceptions related to tetanus and tetanus vaccination; and (4) behavioral items related to adult tetanus booster vaccination. The behavioral section included eight items on potential promoting factors (Q21) and eight items on potential barriers (Q22). Internal consistency of the dichotomous promoting and barrier item sets was assessed using Kuder-Richardson Formula 20 (KR-20), yielding coefficients of 0.794 and 0.694, respectively.

The primary outcome was willingness to receive a tetanus booster within the next 6 months under a standardized hypothetical scenario in which vaccination would be conveniently available in community or primary care settings. In the primary analysis, “yes” responses were classified as willing, whereas “no” and “uncertain” responses were classified as unwilling/uncertain. As a sensitivity analysis, the original three-category outcome (willing, uncertain, unwilling) was modeled using multinomial logistic regression.

The questionnaire also assessed awareness of the recommended 10-y booster schedule, awareness of the difference between tetanus vaccine and tetanus antitoxin or immunoglobulin, knowledge of where tetanus vaccination could be obtained, childhood tetanus-containing vaccination history, tetanus-containing vaccination within the past 10 y, tetanus antitoxin or immunoglobulin use within the past 5 y, perceived severity of tetanus, recent immunosuppressive therapy, and wound or skin injury requiring management in the previous 12 months.

Exploratory factor analysis of promoting and barrier items

Separate exploratory factor analyses (EFA) were conducted for the promoting items (Q21) and barrier items (Q22). Suitability for factor analysis was assessed using the Kaiser-Meyer-Olkin (KMO) measure of sampling adequacy and Bartlett’s test of sphericity. A KMO value greater than 0.60 and a statistically significant Bartlett’s test (p < .05) were considered acceptable.

Factor extraction used maximum likelihood estimation with varimax rotation. The number of factors was determined using parallel analysis together with the interpretability of the resulting factor structure. Because the questionnaire was exploratory and study-specific rather than a formal scale-reduction instrument, factor loadings were interpreted alongside conceptual coherence and content coverage rather than by applying a purely mechanical exclusion threshold. Several items with loadings between 0.30 and 0.40 were retained because they contributed to the interpretability of the factor-level constructs and preserved substantive content within the promoting or barrier domains.

Standardized factor scores were calculated for each derived dimension and incorporated into the multivariable models. These scores were used as summary measures of the promoting and barrier dimensions related to adult tetanus booster willingness.

Statistical analysis

Participant characteristics were summarized descriptively. Categorical variables are presented as frequencies and percentages. In Table 1, willingness outcomes are reported as row percentages. Bivariate associations between participant characteristics and willingness were examined using Pearson’s chi-square tests, and Holm-Bonferroni adjustment was applied to account for multiple comparisons.

The primary multivariable analysis used logistic regression to estimate adjusted associations with willingness to receive a tetanus booster. The model included selected sociodemographic variables, clinical characteristics, EFA-derived factor scores, and key knowledge variables. Variable selection for the primary model was theory-informed and was not based solely on bivariate statistical significance. Adjusted odds ratios (ORs) and 95% confidence intervals (CIs) are reported.

Nested multivariable logistic regression models were additionally fitted to examine the incremental explanatory contribution of the sociodemographic variables, factor-analytic domains, and knowledge-related variables. The results of these nested models are presented in Supplementary Table 1.

A sensitivity analysis used multinomial logistic regression with the original three-category outcome (willing, uncertain, and unwilling) and the same covariates as the primary model. These results are presented in Supplementary Table 2.

Figures, including bar charts and factor score comparisons, were produced using ggplot2 in R. All analyses were conducted in R (version 4.1; R Foundation for Statistical Computing). All statistical tests were two-sided. Holm-adjusted P values are reported for bivariate comparisons; for other analyses, P < .05 was considered statistically significant.

Ethical approval

The study protocol was reviewed and approved by the Ethics Committee of The Central Hospital of Wuhan (approval number: WHZXKYL2024-184). Participants provided electronic informed consent before completing the questionnaire. No directly identifying personal information was collected for analysis. IP address information was available only in the raw export for data-quality checks, such as identifying potential duplicate submissions, and was removed before formal statistical analysis. IP addresses were not used to identify participants in the analytic dataset or in manuscript reporting.

Results

Participant characteristics, awareness, and overall willingness

A total of 1,522 adults were included in the final analysis. Of these participants, 38.4% were aged 18–29 y, 42.6% were aged 30–44 y, 14.6% were aged 45–59 y, and 4.3% were aged ≥ 60 y. More than half were female (57.9%). Most had a college or university education (69.5%), and 70.6% lived in urban areas. Overall, 27.0% were classified as belonging to high-risk groups for tetanus. Most respondents had not received recent chemotherapy or long-term immunosuppressive therapy (96.7%), and 21.8% reported a wound or skin injury requiring management in the previous 12 months.

Awareness of adult tetanus booster vaccination and related concepts was limited. Only 26.0% of participants knew that adults are recommended to receive a booster approximately every 10 y, 34.2% knew the difference between tetanus vaccine and tetanus antitoxin or immunoglobulin, and 50.3% knew where vaccination could be obtained. In addition, 47.6% reported childhood tetanus-containing vaccination, 9.7% reported tetanus-containing vaccination within the previous 10 y, and 13.8% reported receipt of tetanus antitoxin or immunoglobulin within the previous 5 y.

Under the hypothetical scenario of convenient vaccination in community or primary care settings, 857 participants (56.3%) reported willingness to receive a tetanus booster within the next 6 months, whereas 665 (43.7%) were unwilling or uncertain (Table 1). Bivariate results are shown in Table 1 using row percentages. Willingness differed by age group and education level and was higher among respondents with greater tetanus-related knowledge. A higher proportion of respondents reporting recent immunosuppressive therapy expressed willingness to receive a tetanus booster; however, this unadjusted difference should be interpreted cautiously because the subgroup was small. Sex, residence, occupation type, high-risk group status, and wound history did not show significant bivariate associations with willingness.

Factor structure of promoting and barrier items

The promoting items (Q21) were suitable for factor analysis (KMO = 0.81; Bartlett’s test of sphericity: χ2 = 1592.213, df = 28, p < .001). Parallel analysis supported a three-factor solution, which explained 34.3% of the variance. Based on item content and loading patterns, the three promoting dimensions were interpreted as information clarity, service convenience, and opportunity/timing (Table 2A). Information clarity included items on clear explanations of adverse events or contraindications, reminders, and active recommendations by healthcare professionals. Service convenience reflected simplified procedures and easier access to vaccination services. Opportunity/timing was centered on vaccination being offered during a clinical encounter.

The barrier items (Q22) also showed acceptable suitability for factor analysis (KMO = 0.70; Bartlett’s test of sphericity: χ2 = 1174.838, df = 28, p < .001). Multivariable analysis of factors associated with willingness

To estimate adjusted associations with willingness to receive an adult tetanus booster, we fitted a multivariable logistic regression model including sociodemographic variables, high-risk group status, EFA-derived factor scores, and key knowledge variables. Full model results are presented in Table 3. Nested multivariable models showed that explanatory power increased most after the factor-analytic domains were added, followed by the knowledge-related variables (Supplementary Table 1). In the sensitivity analysis using the original three-category outcome (willing, uncertain, unwilling), the main correlates showed broadly similar directions, particularly information clarity, psychological/cognitive barriers, and knowledge of the recommended 10-y booster schedule. However, some variables, including knowledge of vaccination sites, service opportunity, and access barriers, appeared to be more consistently related to uncertainty than to firm unwillingness (Supplementary Table 2).

In the adjusted model, information clarity was positively associated with willingness to receive a tetanus booster (OR = 1.55, 95% CI: 1.29–1.86, p < .001). Opportunity/timing was also positively associated with willingness (OR = 1.21, 95% CI: 1.03–1.49, p = .023). Psychological/cognitive barriers showed a negative association (OR = 0.67, 95% CI: 0.56–0.79, p < .001). Accessibility barriers showed a small positive association (OR = 1.16, 95% CI: 1.01–1.34, p = .041), a counterintuitive finding that should be considered exploratory. Service convenience was not independently associated with willingness (OR = 1.08, 95% CI: 0.92–1.29, p = .358).

Knowledge of the recommended 10-y booster schedule and knowledge of where tetanus vaccination could be obtained were independently associated with willingness. Participants who knew that adults should receive a tetanus booster approximately every 10 y had higher odds of willingness than those without this knowledge (OR = 1.91, 95% CI: 1.42–2.63, p < .001). Knowing where tetanus vaccination could be obtained was similarly associated with willingness (OR = 1.44, 95% CI: 1.16–1.84, p = .002). By contrast, knowledge of the difference between tetanus vaccine and antitoxin was not independently associated with willingness after adjustment (OR = 1.01, 95% CI: 0.77–1.35, p = .940).

Discussion

In this cross-sectional online survey of Chinese adults, just over half of respondents reported willingness to receive an adult tetanus booster under a hypothetical convenient-vaccination scenario. In adjusted analyses, willingness was more closely related to cognitive, psychological, and service-related factors than to basic sociodemographic characteristics. Information clarity, opportunity/timing cues, knowledge of the recommended 10-y booster schedule, and knowledge of where vaccination could be obtained were positively associated with willingness, whereas psychological/cognitive barriers were negatively associated with willingness.

The derived dimensions are broadly consistent with established vaccine-hesitancy frameworks. Information clarity maps most closely onto the confidence component of the 3C/5C frameworks because it reflects understanding of vaccine-related information, adverse events and contraindications, and the role of professional recommendation. Psychological/cognitive barriers overlap with uncertainty about the need for vaccination and with perceived barriers in Health Belief Model terms. Opportunity/timing, particularly vaccination offered during a medical encounter, can be understood as a cue to action. This interpretation is consistent with theory-informed vaccination research based on the Health Belief Model and the 5C framework.14,15 Recent Chinese studies of vaccination intention have likewise suggested that vaccine-related decisions are linked to multiple cognitive, attitudinal, and contextual constructs rather than knowledge alone.16,17

The positive association between accessibility barriers and willingness deserves particular caution. In most vaccination-intention studies, perceived accessibility barriers would be expected to reduce willingness. In the present study, however, the outcome was defined under a hypothetical scenario in which vaccination would be conveniently available, rather than as actual vaccination behavior. This factor may therefore have captured sensitivity to access-related conditions or potential responsiveness to improved service delivery, rather than a true facilitating effect of barriers themselves. The multinomial sensitivity analysis was compatible with this interpretation, as access barriers appeared more consistently related to uncertainty than to firm unwillingness. This counterintuitive finding should therefore be regarded as exploratory.

Opportunity/timing cues were also relevant. Respondents were more willing when vaccination could be linked to an existing healthcare encounter, such as an outpatient visit or another routine contact with the healthcare system. This pattern is consistent with the broader literature on opportunistic vaccination, which suggests that integrating vaccination into routine clinical workflows may reduce the practical and cognitive effort required for uptake.19,20 In settings where adult preventive vaccination is not yet highly visible, service-linked opportunities may be especially important.

Psychological/cognitive barriers showed a strong negative association with willingness. Concerns about adverse events, fear of injection, and uncertainty about the need for vaccination may be salient obstacles to adult tetanus booster uptake. This pattern suggests that hesitancy toward adult tetanus boosters may reflect unfamiliarity, low salience, and unresolved uncertainty more than overt distrust.21–23 Addressing these barriers may require not only general public information but also individualized communication during healthcare encounters.

Several limitations should be acknowledged. First, the cross-sectional design precludes causal inference, and the observed associations should not be interpreted as evidence of causation. Second, the online recruitment strategy limits generalizability. Respondents were disproportionately younger, urban, and highly educated compared with the broader Chinese adult population, whereas older adults and rural residents were underrepresented. Because these groups may differ in vaccine access, provider contact, trust in the healthcare system, and booster-related knowledge, the present findings may not fully reflect willingness among populations at higher risk of under-vaccination or tetanus exposure. Future studies should specifically target older and rural populations.

Third, the questionnaire was developed specifically for this study and should be considered exploratory. Although expert consultation, pilot refinement, internal consistency assessment, and factor analysis supported its use, full formal psychometric prevalidation was not performed. Internal consistency was acceptable for exploratory use, but psychometric evaluation remained limited. The total explained variance of the factor solutions was modest, indicating that the measured promoting and barrier items did not capture a substantial proportion of variation in adult tetanus booster willingness. The retained factors should therefore be interpreted as dominant conceptual patterns rather than a strict simple structure. Additional psychosocial and contextual influences, including social norms, trust in the healthcare system, prior vaccination experiences, and broader health motivation constructs, may not have been fully captured and should be incorporated in future research. Recent Chinese studies of vaccination intention and other theory-informed intention models similarly suggest that vaccine-related behavior is associated with a wider set of cognitive, attitudinal, and contextual constructs than can be represented by a brief exploratory instrument.16,17,24

Fourth, the primary analysis combined “uncertain” and “unwilling” responses into a single category. Although the three-category sensitivity analysis showed broadly similar directions for the main correlates, dichotomization may still have obscured differences between uncertainty and firm unwillingness. Future studies should distinguish these groups more explicitly to identify subgroup-specific barriers and intervention targets.

Despite these limitations, this study provides one of the first large-sample analyses of adult tetanus booster willingness in China to combine behavioral factor analysis with multivariable modeling. The findings suggest that adult tetanus booster willingness may be more closely linked to information clarity, practical opportunity, and psychological readiness than to basic sociodemographic characteristics alone. These results point to communication and service-delivery approaches that should be evaluated in future studies of adult tetanus booster uptake in China.

Conclusion

In this online sample of Chinese adults, willingness to receive an adult tetanus booster was more strongly associated with cognitive, psychological, and service-related factors than with basic sociodemographic characteristics. Information clarity, opportunity-related cues, knowledge of booster schedules, and knowledge of vaccination access were positively associated with willingness, whereas psychological/cognitive barriers were negatively associated.

These findings support further evaluation of communication- and service-based approaches to adult tetanus booster promotion in China. Clearer public communication, stronger provider recommendation, better awareness of vaccination sites, and integration of booster vaccination into routine healthcare encounters may be practical directions for future implementation studies that measure actual uptake as well as willingness.

Biography

Qinghao Guo is an Associate Chief Physician and Deputy Director of the Trauma Center at The Central Hospital of Wuhan, Tongji Medical College, Huazhong University of Science and Technology (Wuhan, China), where he also leads the Emergency and Trauma Surgery service. As a frontline trauma clinician, he specializes in the management of severe and critical injuries and the prevention of injury-related complications. His academic work focuses on infection prevention and emergency preparedness, with particular interest in tetanus prevention, adult immunization awareness, and implementation strategies to improve vaccine uptake. Dr. Guo serves on several professional committees, including the Hubei Medical Doctor Association committees in Trauma Surgery and Hand and Microsurgery, the Wuhan Medical Association Trauma Surgery Committee, and the Emergency Rescue Working Group of the Chinese Medical Doctor Association Trauma Surgery Branch. He has served as principal investigator for two multicenter GCP projects and has extensive experience in clinical research and trial implementation.
