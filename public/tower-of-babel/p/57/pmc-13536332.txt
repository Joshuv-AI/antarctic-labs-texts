Cephalometric and soft tissue determinants of esthetic outcomes after single-jaw mandibular setback surgery in skeletal class III patients: A retrospective study

Introduction

Orthognathic surgery is performed to correct dentofacial deformities, malocclusion, and facial asymmetry by surgically repositioning the maxilla, the mandible, or both, thereby restoring occlusal function and improving facial esthetics. In patients with skeletal Class III deformity, the optimal treatment decision depends on the patient’s age, severity of deformity, and treatment objectives. Orthognathic surgery can produce more substantial skeletal changes, including maxillary advancement, mandibular setback, an increased subspinale-nasion-supramentale (ANB) angle, and clockwise rotation of the mandibular plane.1

Cephalometric parameters are important factors in surgical decision-making. Patients undergoing bimaxillary surgery usually present with a more negative ANB angle and a smaller sella turcica-nasion-subspinale (SNA) angle, whereas those treated with isolated mandibular surgery tend to have a larger sella turcica-nasion-supramentale (SNB) angle. Important decision-making parameters include overjet ≤ −4.73 mm, Wits appraisal ≤ −11.18 mm, lower incisor angle ≤80.8°, maxillomandibular ratio ≤65.9%, overbite ≤ −0.18 mm, and gonial angle ≥120.8°. The ANS—Menton distance and nasolabial angle should also be considered to achieve optimal esthetic correction.1 In some cases, the treatment plan for mandibular prognathism may be determined not only by clinical examination and diagnosis, but also by the patient’s preference and financial considerations. Therefore, orthognathic treatment may involve camouflage or single-jaw surgery.

Mandibular setback surgery in single-jaw orthognathic procedures produces relatively predictable soft tissue changes, with soft-to-hard tissue ratios of approximately 0.9–1.0:1 at the chin and B-point; however, esthetic outcomes are influenced by the amount of setback, the occlusal plane angle, and changes in the cervical region.2–4 The relationship between hard and soft tissue changes after mandibular setback is highly predictable. A systematic review and meta-analysis reported soft-to-hard tissue movement ratios ranging from 0.915 to 1.051 for pogonion, point B, and lower incisor positions.2–4 More specifically, the soft tissue B-point and pogonion were shown to follow their corresponding hard tissue landmarks by approximately 97%. 3 In contrast, the lower lip (labrale inferius) follows the lower incisor by approximately 77%, indicating greater variability and lower predictability than chin movements.3

The amount of setback significantly influences esthetic outcomes, particularly in the submental region. Setbacks of ≥5 mm significantly reduce cervical length, whereas smaller setbacks (<5 mm) do not significantly affect cervical length.5 Such cervical shortening may adversely affect chin esthetics, with significant differences in the lip-chinthroat angle and lip length between groups.5 The occlusal plane angle is another key determinant of chin projection after mandibular setback. An occlusal plane angle of at least 15.45° has been reported to be necessary for proportional changes in the SNB angle and the soft tissue facial plane angle.6 This parameter may help determine whether single-jaw mandibular surgery or bimaxillary surgery is more appropriate in borderline cases.

Compared with isolated mandibular setback, bimaxillary surgery provides superior patient satisfaction and esthetic outcomes. Patients treated with two-jaw surgery report significantly greater satisfaction with mouth appearance, smile, and overall treatment results than those treated with mandibular setback alone.1 In addition, maxillary advancement has been associated with more favorable esthetic parameters than mandibular setback, particularly with respect to cervical length, which increases after maxillary advancement but decreases after mandibular setback.7

Mandibular setback surgery may also affect transverse facial dimensions. Studies using sagittal split ramus osteotomy (SSRO) have shown significant increases in interramus posterior width (1.8 mm) and intergonial width (2.2 mm).8 However, when intraoral vertical ramus osteotomy (IVRO) is used, hard tissue width increases immediately after surgery, whereas soft tissue changes are not significant at long-term follow-up, suggesting that IVRO does not significantly affect the transverse facial profile.9

The aim of this study was to analyze adult skeletal Class III patients who underwent single-jaw mandibular setback surgery, and to evaluate the associations among cephalometric variables, soft tissue parameters, the amount of setback, and postoperative esthetic outcomes, in order to provide reference guidelines for selecting single-jaw surgery.

Materials and methods

This retrospective study was conducted at the Department of Oral and Maxillofacial Surgery, Chung Shan Medical University Hospital, and included adult patients with skeletal Class III malocclusion subspinale-nasion-supramentale (ANB) angle <0° who underwent single-jaw mandibular surgery between 2016 and 2021. A total of 258 patients who met the inclusion criteria were enrolled. Preoperative and postoperative lateral cephalometric radiographs were collected and analyzed, as illustrated in Fig. 1. The study protocol was approved by the institutional review board (IRB) of Chung Shan Medical University Hospital (CS2-23015).

Indications for isolated mandibular surgery included a well-positioned maxilla with predominant mandibular prognathism. Patients with facial asymmetry type II (deviation of the mandibular body and ramus toward the same side, with greater ramus width discrepancy than chin deviation), as well as those with moderate-to-severe anterior open bite combined with mandibular prognathism, were considered candidates. Patient preference for single-jaw surgery and understanding of esthetic limitations were also considered.1,10

Inclusion criteria: patients aged ≥18 years diagnosed with skeletal Class III malocclusion, with no history of orthognathic surgery, facial trauma, cleft lip/palate, or maxillomandibular tumors, and with clearly interpretable pre- and postoperative lateral cephalometric radiographs.

Exclusion criteria: patients who did not meet the above criteria or had incomplete or poor-quality imaging data.

Patients were categorized into three groups: bilateral vertical ramus osteotomy (BVRO), genioplasty alone, and BVRO plus genioplasty groups. Cephalometric landmarks are shown in Fig. 2. Five variables were analyzed: nasolabial angle, upper lip to E line, lower lip to E line, vertical height change, and occlusal plane change.

Inter-examiner reliability was assessed by two examiners using the Wilcoxon signed-rank test and showed no significant difference. Statistical analyses were performed using R software (version 4.3.0; R Core Team, Vienna, Austria), including descriptive statistics, scatter plot analysis, multiple regression analysis, and receiver operating characteristic (ROC) curve analysis. The area under the ROC curve (AUC) was used to evaluate predictive accuracy, with higher AUC values indicating better model performance.

Results

A total of 258 adult patients with skeletal Class III malocclusion were included and categorized into three surgical groups: bilateral vertical ramus osteotomy (BVRO) group (n = 99), genioplasty alone group (n = 30), and BVRO plus genioplasty group (n = 129) (Fig. 1).

Postoperative lip position relative to the Ricketts E line showed that esthetic criteria were achieved in 47.76% of patients in the BVRO group (male: 24.13%, female: 65.78%), 50% in the genioplasty alone group (male: 25%, female: 87.5%), and 48.27% in the BVRO plus genioplasty group (male: 24.39%, female: 69.56%).

Hard tissue changes are summarized in Table 1. Significant differences were observed between preoperative and postoperative values in SNA, SNB, SND angles and Wits appraisal in both sexes (P < 0.05), indicating effective mandibular setback and improvement in sagittal skeletal relationships.

Soft tissue and surgical changes are presented in Table 2. The BVRO group demonstrated the greatest mandibular setback (approximately 11 mm). Changes in the nasolabial angle were more pronounced in females, except in the genioplasty alone group. The genioplasty alone group exhibited the greatest changes in upper lip position relative to the E line. No significant differences in vertical height change were observed among the three groups. All groups showed notable changes in occlusal plane angle. The genioplasty alone group showed smaller skeletal changes but greater lower lip displacement, particularly in females, whereas the BVRO plus genioplasty group demonstrated intermediate skeletal correction with balanced soft tissue responses.

Multiple regression analysis (Table 3) revealed that vertical height change was a significant positive predictor of setback in females in the BVRO group (β = 19.860, P = 0.018). No significant predictors were identified in the genioplasty alone group. In the BVRO plus genioplasty group, vertical height change remained a significant predictor in females (β = 15.893, P = 0.033), while upper lip to E line was a significant predictor in males (β = 1.700, P = 0.003).

ROC analysis (Table 4) demonstrated moderate predictive performance. Wits appraisal showed the highest predictive value in males (AUC = 0.67), while upper and lower lip positions demonstrated moderate predictive ability in females (AUC = 0.60–0.64).

Discussion

This study evaluated the impact of different surgical modalities (BVRO, genioplasty alone, and BVRO plus genioplasty) on postoperative esthetic outcomes in skeletal Class III patients. Consistent with previous studies emphasizing the importance of soft tissue response in orthognathic surgery, our findings demonstrate that both skeletal and soft tissue variables play distinct roles in determining optimal surgical approaches.11,12

Mandibular setback surgery resulted in significant improvement in Class III facial profiles. In the present study, postoperative ANB values became positive in both males and females, indicating substantial skeletal correction. The reduction in SND suggests posterior repositioning and rotation of the mandible, while postoperative Wits appraisal values returned to the normal range. These findings are consistent with previous studies reporting ANB improvements of approximately 3.6°–3.7°, along with soft tissue changes such as increased facial convexity (5.9°), lower lip retrusion (3.3 mm), and improved labiomental fold depth.13 The procedure effectively improves both skeletal alignment and soft tissue harmony.4

BVRO produced the greatest mandibular setback, confirming its primary role in correcting skeletal discrepancies. Soft tissue changes following vertical ramus osteotomy generally followed hard tissue movements, with ratios varying by anatomical region and gender (pogonion ratio approximately 1:1 in females and 1:1.1 in males).14 Three-dimensional analyses further demonstrate that sagittal skeletal movements are consistently reflected in the chin and perioral soft tissues, with high correlation at the pogonion (~95%).15

In contrast, genioplasty alone group resulted in more pronounced lower lip changes, suggesting its effectiveness in soft tissue refinement rather than skeletal correction. Previous studies have shown that setback genioplasty exhibits soft-to-hard tissue ratios of approximately 0.7:1 at pogonion and 1:1 at menton, with postoperative soft tissue thickening.16 However, prediction accuracy remains limited, with reported discrepancies of ±2.6 mm between predicted and actual outcomes.17 The combined surgical approach provided balanced improvements in both skeletal and soft tissue parameters, supporting its indication for comprehensive correction.18,19

Multivariable regression analysis revealed that vertical height change was the only significant predictor in the BVRO and genioplasty alone groups, highlighting the importance of vertical facial proportions in treatment planning. This finding aligns with previous evidence that vertical skeletal patterns influence surgical outcomes, particularly in high-angle patients who may require adjunctive genioplasty.20,21

Conversely, upper lip position relative to the E-line was a key determinant in the BVRO plus genioplasty group, indicating that soft tissue profile plays a critical role when genioplasty is incorporated. The E-line has been reported to have high sensitivity and specificity in esthetic evaluation, and upper lip position correlates strongly with skeletal movement.22,23 Predictive models incorporating both skeletal and soft tissue variables can explain up to 80–94% of soft tissue response variability.24

The ROC analysis in this study demonstrated only modest predictive performance, with AUC values ranging from 0.60 to 0.67. According to commonly accepted criteria, such values indicate limited discriminative ability, suggesting that these variables alone may not provide strong clinical predictive utility. Although statistically significant associations were identified, the relatively low AUC values imply that these parameters should not be used as standalone predictors for surgical planning or esthetic outcome assessment. Instead, they should be interpreted as part of a multifactorial framework that incorporates both skeletal and soft tissue considerations. Future studies incorporating additional variables or advanced predictive models may improve the accuracy and clinical applicability of prediction.

One important limitation of the present study is the relatively small sample size of the genioplasty-alone group, particularly after sex-based subgroup stratification. Subgroup analyses inherently reduce the effective sample size, which may compromise statistical power and increase the risk of type II error, thereby limiting the ability to detect true associations in regression models. Moreover, small subgroup samples may lead to unstable estimates and reduced generalizability of the findings. Therefore, the results derived from the genioplasty-alone group should be interpreted with caution. Future studies with larger and more balanced sample distributions are warranted to validate these findings. Another limitation of this study is the use of two-dimensional (2D) lateral cephalometric analysis to evaluate skeletal and soft tissue relationships. Although 2D cephalometry remains a widely used and clinically accessible tool, it is inherently limited by projection errors, superimposition of bilateral structures, and inability to capture volumetric and asymmetrical changes. In contrast, contemporary three-dimensional (3D) imaging techniques, such as cone-beam computed tomography and 3D facial scanning, provide more comprehensive evaluation of craniofacial morphology and soft tissue dynamics. Therefore, the findings of this study should be interpreted within the constraints of 2D analysis, and future studies incorporating 3D assessment are warranted to enhance diagnostic accuracy and predictive modeling.

In conclusion, surgical planning for skeletal Class III patients should integrate both skeletal correction and soft tissue considerations. Vertical height is a key determinant for mandibular setback procedures, whereas upper lip position is critical in deciding the need for combined genioplasty. Lower lip changes contribute primarily to soft tissue refinement. A comprehensive evaluation of multiple cephalometric and esthetic variables is essential to optimize treatment outcomes.
