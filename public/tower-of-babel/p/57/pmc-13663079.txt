Upregulation of osteopontin expression in prostatic stromal cells via the interaction with prostatic epithelial cells contribute to inflammation-related prostatic fibrosis
Authors: Xing-sheng Yao, Heng Zhang, Xiao-Hu Tang, Yan-Dong Wang, Yi-qi Ruan, Mu-xia Luo, Lei Tang, Ye Tian, Ying Cao
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ObjectiveThis study aimed to investigate the role of osteopontin (OPN) in promoting prostatic fibrosis through inflammation.MethodsOPN expression in the prostates of BPH patients was detected using immunohistochemistry, and its correlation with inflammatory grade, preoperative IPSS, and the percentage of collagen fiber was evaluated, respectively. RNA sequencing (RNA-Seq) identified differentially expressed genes (DEGs) in Spp1-OE WPMY-1 cells. A non-contact co-culture system of BPH-1 and WPMY-1 cells (with or without rhOPN treatment), Spp1-shRNA, or Spp1-OE WPMY-1 cells was established to assess WPMY-1 cells mRNA expression of PTGS2, CXCL1, CCL2, and Spp1 by RT-PCR.ResultsThe expression of OPN in prostate stromal cells was significantly higher in the symptomatic BPH (S-BPH) group, the IPSS 20–35 (severe symptom) group, and the Moderate + Severe inflammation group compared to the asymptomatic BPH (A-BPH) group, the IPSS 8–19 (moderate symptom) group, and the No + Mild inflammation group. Furthermore, OPN expression was positively correlated with preoperative IPSS and the percentage of collagen fibers in S-BPH patients. RNA-Seq identified some DEGs between Vector and Spp1-OE WPMY-1 cells, which are closely related to inflammation and fibrosis. In vitro experiments demonstrated that the expression of PTGS2, CXCL1, CCL2, and Spp1 significantly increased in WPMY-1 cells co-cultured with BPH-1 cells, particularly following treatment with rhOPN. Spp1-shRNA WPMY-1 cells co-cultured with BPH-1 cells exhibited reduced expression of PTGS2, CXCL1, and CCL2, whereas Spp1-OE WPMY-1 cells co-cultured with BPH-1 cells showed an increased expression of these genes.ConclusionsOPN may be involved in the occurrence of prostatic inflammation and fibrosis. The upregulation of OPN expression in prostatic stromal cells may contribute to prostatic fibrosis by interacting with prostatic epithelial cells to exacerbate inflammatory processes.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at https://doi.org/10.1186/s12894-026-02258-3.

Introduction

Bladder Outlet Obstruction (BOO) and its associated Lower Urinary Tract Symptoms (LUTS) are common health issues faced by elderly men. They mainly include a series of symptoms related to urination and storage, which seriously affect the quality of life of elderly men [1, 2]. These symptoms are typically attributed to an enlarged prostate and increased bladder outlet resistance. It is currently believed that Benign Prostatic Hyperplasia (BPH) is the most common cause of LUTS in men, and as a result, clinical terminology often uses LUTS and BPH interchangeably. Recent studies have found that prostate fibrosis may also play a significant role in the onset and progression of LUTS, in addition to increased prostate volume and smooth muscle tension [3–5]. Prostate fibrosis may be an important pathological factor that promotes the onset and progression of LUTS, either existing independently or coexisting with BPH. The study indicated that prostatic fibrosis diminishes the elasticity of the prostatic urethra, elevates the resistance at the bladder outlet, and contributes to male LUTS [6]. It may serve as a significant factor in symptom exacerbation, the reduced efficacy of standard medications, or drug resistance in patients experiencing LUTS. Current medical treatments for BPH/LUTS primarily focus on smooth muscle dysfunction and proliferation, with fibrosis in the prostate remaining clinically untargeted [7, 8]. Antifibrotic therapy is anticipated to emerge as another treatment strategy for LUTS. Therefore, research into the pathogenesis of prostate fibrosis and the identification of therapeutic targets for antifibrotic drugs will provide effective means for the treatment of BPH/LUTD, holding significant clinical application value and importance, particularly for patients who are unresponsive to conventional medications or have developed drug resistance.

The etiology of prostate fibrosis is uncertain, yet chronic inflammation is an apparent candidate. Studies have shown that prostate inflammation does induce an increase in collagen content, and several studies suggest a correlation between inflammation and fibrosis. Prostate fibrosis may potentially be a secondary result of inflammation [9]. Inflammatory cell infiltration in prostate tissue of BPH/LUTS patients is very common and is associated with the severity of symptoms and the risk of progression [10, 11]. Furthermore, patients with concurrent prostatitis experience not only glandular hyperplasia but also significant proliferation of fibrous tissue in the prostate [12]. The study by Wong et al. also indicated that the collagen content in the prostates of mice with E. coli-induced bacterial prostatitis was significantly increased, and fibrosis had developed. Furthermore, the degree of fibrosis could be reversed through anti-inflammatory treatment [13, 14]. Therefore, fibrosis is the result of a tissue repair response that becomes dysregulated after various types of tissue injury, particularly during chronic inflammatory disorders. Although prostatic inflammation can lead to fibrosis, the precise molecular mechanism behind this process has not yet been clarified.

BPH/LUTS primarily affects elderly men. Changes in circulating steroid hormones associated with age have been suggested as a factor in the etiology of BPH/LUTS. The prostate is a sex hormone-dependent organ. Both estrogens and androgens play roles in the growth, development, and proliferation of the prostate, and maintaining the balance between these hormones is crucial for the maintenance of the prostate’s physiological functions [15]. When this balance is disrupted, the biological behavior of prostate cells alters, resulting in disease [16]. The concentration and proportion of estrogen and androgen in the male body dynamically change with age. As men grow older, serum testosterone (T) levels gradually decrease, whereas estradiol (E2) levels tend to remain relatively stable, leading to an elevated estrogen/androgen ratio that disrupts the balance [17, 18]. Our research group’s preliminary studies have revealed that an imbalance in the estrogen/androgen ratio may affect prostatic inflammation and fibrosis [19]. We also conducted RNA-seq analysis on prostate tissue from rats treated with different sex hormone ratios to investigate the key molecules associated with prostatic inflammation and fibrosis. We discovered that the expression of secretory phosphoprotein 1 (Spp1), which encodes osteopontin (OPN), was significantly elevated. Moreover, the levels of OPN expression were positively correlated with various inflammatory and fibrotic factors, indicating that OPN might be implicated in prostatic inflammation and fibrosis [20]. Nonetheless, the precise mechanisms by which it functions necessitate furher study.

OPN is a secreted phosphoglycoprotein and was initially recognized as a matricellular protein of the extracellular matrix (ECM). Subsequent studies revealed that OPN may be involved in both physiological and pathological processes, including biomineralization, ECM and tissue remodeling and repair, immunity and the inflammatory response, cell death, and survival [21–23]. More recently, several studies have revealed that OPN has emerged as a cytokine with pro-inflammatory and pro-fibrotic actions. It plays a significant role in various cellular activities associated with inflammatory and fibrotic cascades, as well as wound healing [24–26]. Research has also reported that OPN is implicated in all prostate conditions, including prostatitis, BPH, and prostate cancer (PCa). In the present study, we designed an integrated approach that includes clinical tissue analysis of osteopontin (OPN) expression in patients with benign prostatic hyperplasia (BPH), transcriptomic profiling to identify the effects of OPN overexpression on downstream genes and pathways in prostate stromal cells, and co-culture experiments to assess how OPN expression in stromal cells impacts inflammatory chemokine production. This approach aims to elucidate the mechanistic link between OPN and stromal-epithelial crosstalk, specifically to explore the potential role of OPN in promoting prostatic fibrosis through inflammation.

Materials and methods

Prostatic samples of BPH patients and grouping

A total of 52 prostate samples were collected from elderly male patients with BPH who underwent transurethral resection of the prostate (TURP) at Guizhou Provincial People’s Hospital between October 2017 and April 2019. These patients were defined as the symptomatic BPH (S-BPH) group. Each patient with pathologically confirmed BPH had complete clinical information, including age, prostate volume, preoperative international prostate symptom score (IPSS), maximum urinary flow rate (Qmax) and postvoided residual urine (PVR). The age is from to 67 to 77 years (average age 71.25 ± 4.1 years), prostate volume is from to 40.82 to 124.58 ml, Qmax is from to 2.1 to 13.9 mL/s, and PVR is from to 0 to 306 mL. In addition, relevant comorbidities such as neurogenic bladder have been excluded. Based on the IPSS score, these patients were divided into moderate symptom group (IPSS 8 ~ 19) and severe symptom group (IPSS 20 ~ 35). Additionally, we collected prostate samples from 15 elderly male patients who underwent radical cystectomy for muscle-invasive bladder cancer (MIBC) during the same period. These patients had a preoperative IPSS score of 0, but were pathologically diagnosed with BPH postoperatively, defining them as the asymptomatic BPH (A-BPH) group. The age is from to 55 to 73 years (average age 66.46 ± 3.3 years). This study was approved by the Ethics Committee for Human Medical Experiments at Guizhou University (approval number: HMEE-GZU-2022-T005).

Cell lines

The human normal prostate stromal immortalized cell line (WPMY-1) and the human prostate epithelial cell line (BPH-1) were both obtained from the American Type Culture Collection (ATCC, VA, USA). The cells were cultured in DMEM medium (Gibco, USA) supplemented with 10% heat-inactivated fetal bovine serum (Invitrogen, USA) and 1% penicillin and streptomycin (Beyotime, China). All cells were cultured at 37℃, 5% CO2, 95% air and 95% humidity.

Construction of stable WPMY-1 cell with Spp1 interference and overexpression

To establish stable WPMY-1 cell lines for Spp1 interference and overexpression, we constructed lentiviral expression vectors carrying Spp1 cDNA and Spp1-targeting shRNA. The interference vector inserted the designed Spp1-specific shRNA sequence into the pLKO.1-puro vector (Addgene) (Spp1-shRNA), while the overexpression vector cloned the full-length coding sequence of human Spp1 into the pLenti-CMV-GFP-Puro vector (Addgene) (Spp1-OE), with non-targeting shRNA used as a negative control (NC and Vector). All constructs were validated by DNA sequencing. Lentiviral particles were obtained by co-transfecting the expression vector, packaging plasmid psPAX2, and envelope plasmid pMD2.G into HEK293T cells using Lipofectamine 3000 reagent (Thermo Fisher Scientific, USA), following the manufacturer’s instructions. After 48 and 72 h post-transfection, viral supernatants were collected, filtered through a 0.45 μm membrane, and concentrated by centrifugation at 25,000 × g for 2 h at 4 °C. WPMY-1 cells were seeded into 6-well plates and infected with concentrated virus in the presence of 8 µg/mL polybrene (Sigma-Aldrich, USA). After 24 h of infection, fresh medium containing puromycin (2 µg/mL) was added for selection, which lasted for 5–7 days. The interference and overexpression efficiency of Spp1 were verified by qRT-PCR analysis.

Non-contact co-culture of cells model establishment

The non-contact co-culture system of BPH-1 cells and WPMY-1 cells, both with and without treatment with 100ng/mL recombinant OPN (rhOPN), as well as Spp1-shRNA WPMY-1 cells and Spp1-OE WPMY-1 cells, were established using Transwell suspension culture chambers made of polyethylene terephthalate (PET) and six-well plates (Corning, NY, USA).BPH-1 cells were seeded in the upper chamber, while WPMY-1 cells, Spp1-shRNA WPMY-1 cells or Spp1-OE WPMY-1 cells were seeded in the lower chamber, respectively. Each group contained 5 × 104 cells per chamber. The cells were cultured in complete medium (Corning Life Sciences, USA) containing 5% CO2. After incubation at a constant temperature of 37 °C for 72 h, WPMY-1 cells from the lower chamber of each group were collected for RNA extraction.

Histopathological examination and inflammation grades in the prostate of BPH patients

The histopathological changes in the prostates of patients were observed, and inflammation was assessed according to the International Histological Classification of Prostatitis [27]. Briefly, Histological prostatitis is classified into four grades. No inflammation indicates that no inflammatory cells were observed in the prostate. Mild inflammation indicates scattered inflammatory cell infiltration in the prostate. Moderate inflammation indicates an accumulation of inflammatory cells in the prostate tissue, but without destruction of glandular epithelial tissue or the formation of lymphoid follicles. Severe inflammation indicates an accumulation of inflammatory cells in the prostate, accompanied by destruction of glandular epithelial tissue or the formation of lymphoid follicles.

Masson’s trichrome staining

Masson’s trichrome staining was employed to evaluate the percentage of collagen fibers, adhering to the manufacturer’s instructions (Solarbio, China). The staining results were observed using a Leica optical microscope, and the Image-Pro Plus 6.0 image analysis system was employed to evaluate the percentage of collagen fibers. The analysis of the relative area of collagen fibers was performed as follows. Collagen fibers and smooth muscle were stained blue and red, respectively. Under a 100× magnification field of view, 6 non-overlapping fields of view were randomly selected in the sections of prostate. The Image J software was employed to measure the area of the blue-stained regions between prostate acini and around the glands to calculate the relative area of collagen fibers. The average values are taken as the quantitative indicators for collagen fibers in the prostate of patients.

Immunohistochemical staining of OPN

Immunohistochemical (IHC) staining was performed to detect the expression of OPN in the prostate tissue of patients. Briefly, the sections were treated with blocking buffer (Dako Denmark A/S, Glostrup, Denmark) for 30 min at room temperature and thereafter incubated with anti-OPN antibody (1:100 dilutions; abcam; ab63856) overnight at 4°C. After thorough rinsing with Tris-NaCl buffer, the sections were incubated with biotinylated goat anti-rabbit IgG (1:200 dilution in Tris-NaCl buffer; Abcam, Cambridge, UK) for 60 min at RT. The sections were subsequently incubated with an avidin-biotinylated enzyme complex and 3,3’-diaminobenzidine (DAB). The optical densities (ODs) of OPN-like immunoreactivity (IR) were measured using a CM2000B Biomedical Image Analysis System (Beihang University, China). Under a 100× magnification field of view, the OD of OPN-like IR in prostatic stromal cell (the area between prostate acini and around the glands) were measured. Five random fields of interest were measured and the OD measurements were averaged. Immunohistochemistry staining was conducted under identical standards, including positive and negative controls in each batch. Positive controls were conducted on sections of human liver tissue known to exhibit positive OPN expression, and negative controls were incubated with nonimmune serum instead of the primary antibodies, resulting in no detectable staining.

RNA sequencing (RNA-seq) and bioinformatics analysis

Samples from the Vector and Spp1-OE WPMY-1 cell groups were collected, and total RNA was extracted using TRIzol reagent according to the manufacturer’s instructions. RNA integrity and concentration were assessed using the Agilent 2100 Bioanalyzer (Agilent Technologies, Santa Clara, California, USA). mRNA was then enriched using Oligo(dT) magnetic beads for cDNA synthesis. Polymerase chain reaction (PCR) and AMPure XP magnetic beads (Beckman Coulter, Brea, California, USA) were used to amplify the cDNA, and the PCR products were purified using AMPure XP magnetic beads to generate the final library. After quality control of the library, Illumina sequencing was performed. Transcriptomic data were analyzed using the “limma” package in R software (https://www.r-project.org/), with a threshold of adjusted p-value < 0.05 and |log2 fold change| > 1, to identify differentially expressed genes (DEGs) between the Vector control group and the Spp1 overexpression group. DEGs were visualized using the “pheatmap” and “ggplot2” R packages, generating volcano plots and heatmaps. Gene Ontology (GO) analysis (including biological process (BP), cellular component (CC), and molecular function (MF)) and Kyoto Encyclopedia of Genes and Genomes (KEGG) pathway enrichment analysis were performed using the DAVID database (https://david.ncifcrf.gov/). Gene Set Enrichment Analysis (GSEA) primarily involves three steps: calculating the Enrichment Score, estimating the significance level of the Enrichment Score, and performing multiple hypothesis testing (https://www.gsea-msigdb.org/).

Real-time PCR

The mRNA levels of PTGS2, CXCL1, CCL2 and Spp1 in WPMY-1 cells were detected by real-time PCR (RT-PCR). Total RNA was extracted from WPMY-1 cells using TRIzol reagent (Invitrogen, Carlsbad, CA). The RNA preparations were treated with three units of RQ RNase-free DNase (Promega) for 10 min at 37 °C to remove any traces of genomic DNA. The purity and quantity of the RNA were determined with a UV spectrophotometer, with an A260/A280 ratio between 1.8 and 2.0. Total RNA was reverse transcribed with MMLV reverse transcriptase and Oligo-dT primers. Forward and reverse primers for the selected genes were designed using Primer Express software (Applied Biosystems, Foster City, CA) and are listed in Table 1. Real-time quantitative RT-PCR was performed using the ABI PRISM 7300 Sequence Detection System (Applied Biosystems, Foster City, CA) and analyzed with GeneAmp7300 SDS software, with SYBR Green Master Mix (Applied Biosystems, Foster City, CA) used. The transcript levels were estimated using the formula 2−(△△Ct), where Ct represents the difference in Ct values between the target and the housekeeping gene GAPDH. The relative differences in expression between the groups are expressed as cycle time values, with the control group normalized to 100% for comparison.

Statistical analysis

Statistical analysis of the data was performed using SPSS software version 20.0 (IBM, Armonk, NY, USA). Data from the groups are expressed as the means ± standard deviations (SD). Group comparisons were conducted using t-tests, while comparisons among multiple groups were performed using one-way analysis of variance (ANOVA). Spearman correlation analysis was used for correlation studies. A p-value < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Results

OPN expression in the prostate of BPH patients and the correlation with inflammatory grade, preoperative IPSS and percentage of collagen fibers

The IHC images indicate that OPN staining is distributed across all cell types, including stromal, epithelial, and inflammatory cells. We found that the intensity of immunostaining for OPN in prostatic stromal cells was increased in the S-BPH group and the severe symptom group, and the ODs of OPN-like IR in prostatic stromal cells were significantly higher in the S-BPH group compared to the A-BPH group (p < 0.01) (Fig. 1A, B), and in the IPSS 20 ~ 35 group compared to the IPSS 8 ~ 19 group (p < 0.01) (Fig. 1C, D). Furthermore, we assessed the inflammatory grade and the percentage of collagen fibers in the prostates of patients with S-BPH. Our data also indicated that the OPN level was higher in the Moderate + Severe inflammation group compared to that in the No + Mild inflammation group (p < 0.01) (Fig. 1F), and the OPN expression was positively correlated with the preoperative IPSS (r = 0.6923, p < 0.01) and the percentage of collagen fibers in S-BPH patients (r = 0.7243, p < 0.01) (Fig. 1E). These results indicate that the upregulated OPN expression in prostatic stromal cells of BPH patients is closely associated with clinical symptoms, prostate inflammation, and the degree of fibrosis.

Identification of key genes and signaling pathways involved in inflammation and fibrosis in Spp1-OE WPMY-1 cells

To determine the impact of Spp1 overexpression on downstream genes or pathways in WPMY-1 cells, we conducted RNA-seq on Spp1-OE WPMY-1 cells to identify DEGs. A total of 673 DEGs were identified, with 263 genes upregulated and 401 genes downregulated (Fig. 2A, B). Among these DEGs, we identified several that were significantly upregulated and associated with inflammation, including IL1A, IL11, TSLP, and PF4. Similarly, genes linked to fibrosis such as MMP13, MMP3, TNC, and WNT3 also showed increased expression. Furthermore, Spp1, along with stress-related genes like HSPA5 and MANF, were upregulated (Supplementary Material Table 2). The GO enrichment analysis indicated that the DEGs were notably enriched in functional categories including “inflammatory response,” “collagen metabolic process,” “extracellular matrix binding,” and “growth factor activity” (Fig. 2C). Further KEGG pathway enrichment analysis revealed that the DEGs were predominantly involved in several classic inflammatory and fibrotic signaling pathways, such as “cytokine-cytokine receptor interaction,” “HIF-1 signaling pathway,” “IL-17 signaling pathway,” “TNF signaling pathway,” “Wnt signaling pathway,” and “complement and coagulation cascades” (Fig. 3A). Furthermore, to further validate the overall trends of these signaling pathways, we conducted GSEA. The results revealed that the gene expression profile in the Spp1-OE group was significantly enriched in several classical signaling pathways, including the IL-17 signaling pathway, the Toll-like receptor (TLR) signaling pathway, the JAK-STAT signaling pathway, and pathways related to ferroptosis.(Fig. 3B).

The effect of co-culturing with BPH-1 cells and rhOPN stimulation on inflammatory chemokine and OPN expression in WPMY-1 cells

To investigate the interaction between prostate epithelial cells and stromal cells, and whether OPN stimulates cytokine production in prostate stromal cells, we established a co-culture cell model using BPH-1 and WPMY-1 cells, with or without rhOPN treatment, to observe the effect of co-culture on the expression of inflammatory chemokines and OPN in WPMY-1 cells. The results indicated that, in comparison to WPMY-1 cells cultured in isolation, the expression of the inflammatory factor PTGS2, chemokines CXCL1 and CCL2, and Spp1 (which encodes OPN) was significantly elevated in WPMY-1 cells when they were co-cultured (p < 0.01) (Fig. 4).

Additionally, rhOPN was utilized to treat WPMY-1 cells both individually and in co-culture to observe the expression of inflammatory chemokines and OPN by the WPMY-1 cells. The findings revealed that, in comparison to WPMY-1 cells treated solely with rhOPN, those in co-culture with rhOPN exhibited a significantly higher expression of PTGS2, CXCL1, CCL2, and Spp1( (p < 0.05, p < 0.01) (Fig. 4). These findings support the notion that the interaction between epithelial cells and stromal cells within a co-culture environment can promote the expression of inflammatory chemokines and OPN in stromal cells. OPN stimulates the production of inflammatory chemokines and OPN in prostate stromal cells, a phenomenon that is more pronounced in the presence of epithelial cells.

The impact of OPN expression on the inflammatory chemokine expression of WPMY-1 cells in a co-culture setting

To further investigate the potential role of OPN in promoting inflammation during the interaction between epithelial and stromal cells, we constructed Spp1-shRNA and Spp1-OE WPMY-1 cells. We then established co-culture models with BPH-1 cells and either Spp1-shRNA or Spp1-OE WPMY-1 cells to observe changes in the expression of inflammatory chemokines in WPMY-1 cells. The RT-qPCR results indicated a significant decrease in the expression of PTGS2, CXCL1, and CCL2 in the co-culture group with Spp1-shRNA WPMY-1 cells, compared to the co-culture control group with NC WPMY-1 cells (p < 0.01). Conversely, when compared to the co-culture control group with Vector WPMY-1 cells, the co-culture group with Spp1-OE WPMY-1 cells exhibited a significant upregulation of PTGS2, CXCL1, and CCL2 (p < 0.01)(Fig. 5). The results suggest that the expression of OPN in stromal cells plays a crucial role in promoting inflammation during the interaction between epithelial and stromal cells, facilitating their interaction and exacerbating the inflammatory response.

Discussion

In the present study, we initially examined the expression of OPN in prostate tissues from patients with BPH. IIHC images revealed that OPN immunostaining was present in both epithelial and stromal cells. Inflammatory cells, including macrophages and lymphocytes, also demonstrated strong expression of OPN. We observed that the expression of OPN in both epithelial and stromal cells was weak in the A-BPH group, whereas in the S-BPH group, the expression of OPN in stromal cells was significantly increased. Subsequently, S-BPH patients were divided into two groups based on the IPSS. We also found that OPN immunostaining of prostate stromal cells was more abundant in the severe symptom group and was significantly higher than in the moderate symptom group. Furthermore, OPN expression was positively correlated with the patients’ IPSS scores. These results suggest that the upregulated expression of OPN in stromal cells is associated with the clinical symptoms of BPH patients, with higher OPN levels linked to more severe symptoms. Research indicates that OPN, as a pro-inflammatory and pro-fibrotic cytokine, may be involved in the pathogenesis of prostatitis and prostatic fibrosis. The study by Samara V. Silver et al. on an E. coli-induced prostatitis mouse model demonstrated that OPN expression and fibrosis-related genes were significantly increased in the prostates of wild-type mice. In contrast, the severity of E. coli-induced inflammation and fibrosis was significantly improved in Spp1-KO mice, suggesting that OPN may play an important role in inflammation-related prostatic fibrosis caused by bacterial prostatitis [28]. In this study, we also uncovered a positive correlation between OPN expression and the percentage of collagen fibers in the prostate tissue of S-BPH patients. Additionally, the OPN levels in the group with Moderate + Severe inflammation were higher than those in the group with No + Mild inflammation. These results support the association between OPN expression and prostate inflammation and fibrosis, leading us to speculate that OPN may be related to inflammation-associated fibrosis.

Prostate tissue is primarily composed of epithelial cells and stromal cells. Our immunohistochemistry staining of prostate tissue from patients with BPH revealed OPN expression in stromal cells, epithelial cells, and inflammatory cells, including macrophages and lymphocytes. However, we observed that the expression of OPN in the stromal cells of the prostate tissue significantly increased with the severity of clinlcal symptom and prostatic inflammation, whereas no significant changes in OPN expression were observed in the epithelial cells. The essence of fibrosis involves the excessive accumulation of collagen, which is the primary component of the extracellular matrix (ECM). It is widely believed that stromal cells may be the most significant contributors to prostate fibrosis, as they are the main producers of the ECM [29]. Based on this, we speculate that the increased expression of OPN in stromal cells may play a significant role in the development of prostate fibrosis in patients with BPH. To investigate the potential impact of increased OPN expression in stromal cells, we constructed a WPMY-1 cell line with overexpressed OPN and conducted transcriptome sequencing using RNA-Seq to identify downstream genes or signaling pathways affected by the upregulation of OPN expression. The results indicated that the overexpression of OPN significantly altered the transcriptome of the prostatic stromal cells WPMY-1, leading to significant changes in the expression of genes associated with inflammation, collagen synthesis and degradation proteases, growth factors and binding proteins, cytokines and chemokines, as well as cell surface molecules. Following the overexpression of SPP1 in WPMY-1 cells, the expression of fibrosis-related genes was enhanced, resulting in excessive ECM secretion [30]. Furthermore, by upregulating inflammatory factors, these cells can attract and chemotactically recruit inflammatory cells, thereby promoting the inflammatory response [31, 32]. GO and KEGG enrichment analysis of the DEGs also revealed significant enrichment in multiple processes closely associated with inflammation and immune response, including inflammatory response, cell chemotaxis, positive regulation of immune system processes, acute inflammatory response, interferon-γ production, and regulation of cytokine responses. In terms of MF, the analysis concentrated on cytokine activity, chemokine receptor binding, protein folding activity, and TNF receptor binding. These findings suggest that the overexpression of Spp1 in WPMY-1 cells is closely associated with inflammation and fibrosis. GSEA enrichment analysis also showed that Spp1-OE WPMY-1 cells significantly activated the IL-17 signaling pathway, JAK-STAT signaling pathway, Toll-like receptor signaling pathway, and ferroptosis pathway, which support the notion that Spp1 overexpression activates inflammatory-immune pathways, such as IL-17, JAK-STAT, and TLR, as well as the ferroptosis pathway, driving cells toward pro-inflammatory and pro-fibrotic phenotypes. Therefore, our bioinformatics analysis results further emphasize the crucial regulatory role of OPN overexpression in the pro-inflammatory and stress response networks.

Fibrogenesis is attributed to inflammation-associated dysregulation of tissue repair. Interactions between inflammatory cells and effector cells play important roles in tissue repair, and persistent inflammation and/or injury abnormally activate inflammation. It has been confirmed that the disruption of the stromal-epithelial cell relationship may lead to BPH, and this interaction is regulated by paracrine signaling molecules [33, 34]. Prostatic epithelial and stromal cells are not only regulated by inflammatory factors but also secrete cytokines involved in the development of fibrosis [35]. OPN, as a secretory multifunctional cytokine, plays a critical role in this complex network regulation process, which warrants further exploration. To observe the interaction between epithelial and stromal cells and the role of OPN in this process, we examined the impact of co-culturing epithelial and stromal cells on the expression of inflammatory factors and OPN. The results indicated that, in comparison to stromal cells cultured in isolation, the expression of the inflammatory factor PTGS2, as well as the chemokines CXCL1, CCL2, and OPN, was significantly elevated under the co-culture condition. PTGS2, as an inflammatory biomarker, is induced by various inflammatory signals. Its upregulation indicates the severity of the inflammatory response [36]. CXCL1 and CCL2, as chemokines, can attract inflammatory cells such as neutrophils, T cells, and mast cells, which express CXCR1 and CXCR receptors [37, 38]. The results suggest that the interaction between prostatic epithelial cells and stromal cells contributes to the expression of inflammatory chemokines in the stromal cells, thereby promoting the onset of inflammation. Moreover, compared to the co-culture group, the expression of PTGS2, CXCL1, and CCL2 was significantly more pronounced in stromal cells treated with rhOPN (possibly secreted by inflammatory cells or stromal cells with increased OPN expression). This indicates that OPN drives the expression of multiple cytokines and chemokines within prostate stromal cells, suggesting that OPN can exacerbate inflammatory processes in the prostate. Furthermore, to observe the interaction between epithelial and stromal cells and the role of increased OPN expression of stromal cells in this process, we developed a co-culture model involving Spp1-shRNA or Spp1-OE WPMY-1 cells alongside BPH-1 cells to explore the potential impact of stromal cell OPN expression on epithelial-stromal cell interactions. The results indicated that, in comparison to the Spp1-shRNA WPMY-1 co-culture group, the expression levels of PTGS2, CXCL1, and CCL2 in WPMY-1 cells were significantly reduced. Conversely, in the Spp1-OE WPMY-1 co-culture group, the expression of these genes was significantly elevated. This suggests that stromal cell OPN expression is pivotal in the interaction between epithelial and stromal cells and facilitates the promotion of inflammatory responses. Based on the above in vitro experimental results, we hypothesize that during prostate inflammation, inflammatory cells within the lesion (such as macrophages) secrete OPN, which promotes the excessive expression of inflammatory chemokines by stromal cells. Stromal cells with overexpressed OPN further enhance the secretion of inflammatory chemokines, continuously amplifying local inflammatory signals, recruiting more inflammatory cells to migrate to the site of inflammation, thereby exacerbating the local inflammatory response and promoting the development of fibrosis. In our previous studies, we confirmed that the expression of OPN in WPMY-1 cells is upregulated upon stimulation by inflammatory factors such as interleukin-1β (IL-1β) and interleukin-6 (IL-6). OPN acts on surrounding epithelial BPH-1 cells via paracrine signaling, further promoting the expression of inflammatory factors, including IL-1β and IL-6, in BPH-1 cells (data not shown). Based on these in vitro experimental results, we hypothesize that during prostate inflammation, inflammatory cells within the lesion secrete OPN, which in turn promotes the excessive expression of inflammatory chemokines and OPN in stromal cells. We further speculate that a paracrine feedback mechanism involving “OPN/inflammatory factors” may exist between prostate epithelial and stromal cells. This feedback loop continually amplifies local inflammatory signals, recruiting additional inflammatory cells to migrate to the site of inflammation, thereby exacerbating the local inflammatory response and promoting fibrosis development.

Studies have confirmed that OPN may act as a pro-fibrotic cytokine involved in the pathogenesis of various fibrotic diseases, and OPN is expected to become a new target for treating these fibrotic diseases. Elevated OPN expression is associated with the progression of cardiac fibrosis and the severity of heart failure [39]. OPN mediates lung fibrosis via EMT, indicating its potential as a therapeutic target and prognostic indicator for idiopathic pulmonary fibrosis (IPF), knockdown of OPN in bleomycin-induced lung fibrosis mice resulted in significantly reduced fibrosis, suggesting that OPN may serve as a target for the diagnosis and treatment of IPF [40]. In this present study, we found that OPN expression in stromal cells significantly contributes to the interaction between epithelial and stromal cells, facilitating their communication and enhancing the inflammatory response to stimuli. Targeting OPN may disrupt this “OPN/inflammatory factors” paracrine feedback mechanism, thus alleviating local inflammation and inhibiting prostate fibrosis. Effective therapies are needed to target not only inflammation but also fibrosis, which are key contributors to LUTS in prostate conditions. Our current study suggests that OPN may play a role in the development of prostatic inflammation and fibrosis. Further research is required to elucidate the precise mechanisms through which OPN contributes to prostatic inflammation and fibrosis and to determine whether OPN could serve as a novel therapeutic target for prostate fibrosis.

While this study offers valuable insights, it also has several limitations. First, our clinical specimens were limited in number, particularly in the asymptomatic BPH group, which included fewer patients and tended to be younger than those in the symptomatic BPH group, potentially influencing the results to some extent. Secondly, the non-contact co-culture system utilized here captures paracrine signaling; however, it does not reflect the full complexity of epithelial-stromal interactions involving ECM components or direct cell-cell communication. Finally, in vivo animal studies were not included to validate the proposed mechanisms, and such work will be an important focus of our future research. Although this study has the aforementioned limitations, its findings could potentially be impactful for understanding the pathophysiology of LUTS.

Conclusion

In summary, our study demonstrates that OPN expression is markedly upregulated in prostatic stromal cells of patients with symptomatic BPH and correlates positively with both lower urinary tract symptom severity (IPSS) and the extent of collagen deposition. Transcriptomic analysis of Spp1-overexpressing WPMY-1 cells revealed enrichment of pro-inflammatory and pro-fibrotic pathways, including IL-17, JAK-STAT, Toll-like receptor, and extracellular matrix remodeling cascades. In vitro co-culture experiments further showed that stromal OPN amplifies epithelial–stromal crosstalk, driving increased expression of PTGS2, CXCL1, CCL2, and Spp1 itself, thereby establishing a paracrine “OPN/inflammatory factor” feedback loop that exacerbates local inflammation and fibrosis. Together, these findings identify OPN as a critical mediator of inflammation-associated prostatic fibrosis and suggest that therapeutic targeting of OPN may offer a novel strategy to alleviate fibrosis-driven lower urinary tract symptoms in BPH.
