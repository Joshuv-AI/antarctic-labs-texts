Tele-ultrasound enables EMT-Intermediate providers to identify occult haemorrhage: A novel, scalable SUPER-YL framework for prehospital POCUS
Authors: Wen-Hung Yang, An-Fu Lee, Chao-Chun Huang, Tzu-Yun Hung, Wen-Chu Chiang
Published: 2025

To the Editor,

The widespread adoption of prehospital point-of-care ultrasound (POCUS) is hindered by significant logistical and training barriers, particularly for non-paramedic providers.1, 2 High-quality image acquisition and accurate interpretation in dynamic prehospital settings require intensive training and quality assurance, which are hard to scale.3 To address this, we developed the SUPER-YL (Smart Ultrasound for Prehospital Emergency Response – Yunlin) programme in Taiwan, integrating tiered, competency-based training with real-time tele-physician oversight to expand POCUS capabilities across the emergency medical services (EMS) workforce. We report a case that demonstrates its clinical efficacy.

A 20-year-old male involved in a motorcycle collision presented with benign clinical signs-alert, ambulatory, normotensive, with only a minor abdominal abrasion. As he met the American College of Surgeons’ field triage criteria for moderate-risk trauma,4 he was enrolled in the SUPER-YL protocol. In the ambulance, an EMT-Intermediate (EMT-I) trained via a focused four-hour module performed a three-view abdominal POCUS, revealing significant intraperitoneal free fluid. The video was sent via secure messaging to an on-call emergency physician, who confirmed suspected major intra-abdominal haemorrhage within two minutes (Supplementary Video: https://youtu.be/Ae6uO9LK9Z8). Despite reassuring vitals, this definitive finding led EMS crew to bypass the nearest community hospital and transport the patient to a trauma centre 30 min away. Computed tomography confirmed a grade IV liver laceration, successfully treated with embolization. The patient fully recovered. Written informed consent from the patient involved in this report was obtained before submission.

This case validates a new paradigm for prehospital diagnostics. The core innovation of the SUPER-YL model is separating image acquisition from interpretation. As detailed in Table 1, conventional POCUS models typically bundle these functions, requiring lengthy training for a limited number of high-level providers (e.g., paramedics or physicians) who must perform and interpret scans in the field. This creates a significant bottleneck to system-wide implementation. In Yunlin County, for example, there are 328 certified EMT-Is compared to only 60 paramedics (EMT-Ps). By training the larger EMT-I workforce on a simplified, focused acquisition protocol, the SUPER-YL model dramatically increases the potential reach of POCUS.

The integrated, real-time telemedicine component ensures safety and accuracy. This structure transforms the field provider's role from diagnostician to data acquirer, while leveraging remote expert-level interpretation for the critical decision-making process. This tele-supervision model is feasible and effective. Furthermore, the protocol's deliberate focus on moderate-risk, physiologically stable trauma patients maximises its clinical utility.4 POCUS is most likely to alter management in this “grey zone” population, where occult life-threatening injuries may be masked by a benign initial presentation, thereby addressing the well-documented problem of under-triage in blunt trauma.

In conclusion, the SUPER-YL model offers a feasible, safe, and scalable framework for expanding prehospital POCUS capabilities. By integrating tiered training with real-time telemedicine support, it empowers a broader range of EMS providers to identify occult life-threatening conditions, optimising triage and ensuring patients receive definitive care at the earliest opportunity.5 This approach provides a valuable blueprint for other EMS systems globally, particularly those facing resource or advanced provider workforce constraints.
