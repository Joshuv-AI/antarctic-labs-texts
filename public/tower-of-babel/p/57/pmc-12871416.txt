Changes in Burnout and Satisfaction With Work-Life Integration Among U.S. Residents and Fellows and the General U.S. Working Population Between 2012 and 2023
Authors: Tait D. Shanafelt, Liselotte N. Dyrbye, Christine Sinsky, Mickey Trockel, Michael Tutty, Hanhan Wang, Lindsey E. Carlasare, Colin P. West
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractPurposeThis study compares the prevalence of occupational burnout, depression, and satisfaction with work-life integration (WLI) among U.S. residents and fellows (trainees) with that of U.S. physicians and the general U.S. workforce and examines the current point prevalence of burnout and satisfaction with WLI among trainees relative to 2012.MethodBetween November 30, 2023, and January 2, 2024, U.S. trainees were surveyed using methods similar to a 2012 study. A sample of practicing U.S. physicians and a probability-based sample of the U.S. working population were surveyed at a similar timepoint. Burnout was assessed using the emotional exhaustion and depersonalization scales of the Maslach Burnout Inventory, depression using the Patient Reported Outcome Measurement Information System depression scale, and satisfaction with WLI with a previously used standardized item.ResultsSurveys were completed by 3,486 of 58,127 trainees (6.0%). Nonresponder analysis suggested participants were representative of U.S. trainees with respect to burnout and depression. A higher proportion of trainees than practicing physicians had burnout symptoms (1,376 of 3,486 [50.0%] vs 3,023 of 7,643 [45.2%]; P < .001). In pooled analysis of trainees and similarly aged U.S. workers in other fields, adjusting for age, gender, and relationship status, trainees were at higher risk for burnout (odds ratio [OR], 1.50; 95% CI, 1.27–1.77) but lower risk for moderate or severe depression (OR, 0.77; 95% CI, 0.63–0.93). In pooled multivariable analysis of trainees from the 2012 and 2023 surveys, adjusting for year in training, relationship status, and specialty, trainees in 2023 had lower odds of burnout than those who responded in 2012 (OR, 0.71; 95% CI, 0.61–0.81).ConclusionsBurnout among U.S. trainees was lower in 2023 than 2012. Despite this improvement, trainees remain at higher risk for burnout than workers in other fields. Continued efforts are needed to optimize trainee education, preparedness for practice, and well-being.

Awareness of occupational distress, as well as efforts to foster well-being among residents and fellows (hereafter trainees), has evolved substantially during the past 25 years. During this interval, the high prevalence of occupational distress1–5 and depression,6,7 as well as their association with quality of care,1–3,8–12 have been recognized, and contributing factors have been defined.4,13–26 Comprehensive efforts to improve residency and fellowship training have occurred, including substantive efforts by both health care organizations that educate trainees and the Accreditation Council for Graduate Medical Education (ACGME), which has implemented duty hour limitations and required holistic well-being improvement work by training programs as part of the accreditation process.27–29 Initiatives to drive progress have moved beyond personal resilience training and support for individuals in distress to include structural changes to enhance the learning environment and provide support during the formative postgraduate training years.30–35

Although extensive studies of trainees’ well-being have been conducted, few provide insight regarding how their experience compares to practicing physicians assessed at the same time point5,36 or the similarly aged working adults in the general population.36 In addition, few studies are able to provide perspective on the experience of trainees in today’s environment relative to the training experience of a decade ago.37–39 Such context is important to understand the progress that has been made and provide insights into continuing needs and opportunities.

In the current study, we compare the prevalence of occupational burnout, depression, and satisfaction with work-life integration (WLI) among U.S. trainees with that of both U.S. physicians and the general U.S. workforce. We also examine the current point prevalence of burnout and satisfaction with WLI among trainees relative to a similar study conducted in 2012.36

Method

The 2023 survey used methods similar to the survey of trainees in 2012.36 Surveys were administered electronically at both time points. The Stanford University, University of Illinois Chicago, and Mayo Clinic institutional review boards reviewed and approved the study.

Participants

Trainees.

The American Medical Association (AMA) Physician Professional Data (PPD), a nearly complete record of all U.S. physicians independent of AMA membership, included 136,668 trainees at the time of the study. All 62,988 trainees listed in the PPD who had given the AMA permission to contact them by email were emailed an invitation to participate on November 30, 2023, with reminder emails sent to nonresponders during the ensuing 3 weeks. Among the initial invitation emails, 4,861 were returned as undeliverable, yielding a total sample of 58,127. Surveys completed by January 2, 2024, were included in this analysis.

To estimate response bias, we also undertook a secondary survey of a random sample of 1,000 trainees who did not respond to the invitations to participate in the electronic survey. Ninety-one emails were undeliverable, yielding a final sample for the nonresponder survey of 909. These individuals were emailed an invitation to participate in an abbreviated survey on January 10, 2024, and offered a $20 gift card to do so. Two reminder emails were sent during the following 2 weeks. Nonresponder surveys completed by January 31, 2024, were included in this analysis.

Physicians.

As previously described,40 a contemporaneous sample of practicing U.S. physicians across all specialties was also assembled from the AMA PPD and surveyed during the same interval. The practicing physician survey included both an electronically administered survey and a separate mailed survey. A financial incentive for participation was offered to a subset of practicing physicians who received the online survey to increase participation rates. Surveys were completed by 7,643 physicians.

General U.S. population.

Similar to our 2012 study,36 we also surveyed a probability-based sample of employed adults from the general U.S. population between November 21, 2023, and November 30, 2023. This survey was conducted using the KnowledgePanel.41 Additional details related to the population survey have been previously reported.40 To provide a comparison group of similarly aged adults, the current analysis focuses on individuals in the general population aged 27 to 40 years who were actively working at the time of the survey.

Historical data.

Details regarding the survey methods, participation rates, and results for the survey of trainees and the similarly aged U.S. working population in 2012 have been previously published.36 As reported, a total of 1,509 trainees and 2,133 working U.S. adults aged 27 to 40 years participated in the 2012 study.36

Study measures

Personal and professional characteristics.

Surveys of both trainees and practicing physicians included items exploring demographic variables (e.g., age, gender, relationship status, parental status) and work characteristics (e.g., mean hours worked per week, specialty). Similar demographic characteristics were also collected from the population sample, along with information on weekly work hours, the highest level of education achieved, and current occupation.

Occupational burnout.

Burnout among trainees and practicing physicians was assessed using the full-length emotional exhaustion and depersonalization scales of the Maslach Burnout Inventory (MBI) used under license from Mind Garden Inc.42 Individuals were categorized as having high emotional exhaustion (score ≥ 27) or high depersonalization (score ≥ 10) according to established thresholds. Consistent with convention,1,43,44 physicians with high scores on the depersonalization and/or emotional exhaustion scales were considered to have at least one manifestation of professional burnout.42

Although the full-length subscales of the MBI are the standard for measuring burnout, its length limits use in larger surveys, such as those of the general population. Accordingly, as previously described,45,46 we assessed burnout in the general U.S. working population using 2 single-item measures adapted from the full MBI, which reliably stratify the risk of burnout. In previous studies, the areas under the receiver operating characteristic curve for emotional exhaustion and depersonalization for these single items relative to their full MBI domain scores were 0.94 and 0.93, respectively.45,46 The positive predictive values of these single-item measures for high levels of emotional exhaustion and depersonalization relative to the full-length MBI subscales are 88.2% and 89.6%, respectively.45,46

Depression and satisfaction with WLI.

Depression was assessed using the 4-item National Institutes of Health Patient Reported Outcome Measurement Information System (PROMIS) depression scale.47,48 The PROMIS depression score was calculated by the Health Measures Scoring Service using item-level calibrations. A T-score of 50 is the mean score for the U.S. general population with an SD of 10. PROMIS T-scores of 55, 60, and 70 are cut points for normal, mild, moderate, and severe depression, respectively. This tool has a high correlation (receiver operating characteristic curve > 0.8) with psychologist assessment of depression by the Diagnostic and Statistical Manual of Mental Disorders (Fifth Edition)49 criteria and with other depression assessments, such as the Patient Health Questionnaire-9.48 Satisfaction with WLI was assessed with the same standardized item used in prior years.36,50–53

Statistical analysis

Demographic and professional characteristics of trainees were summarized using descriptive summary statistics. Associations between variables were evaluated using the t test, Kruskal-Wallis test (continuous variables), or χ2 test (categorical variables) as appropriate. All tests were 2-sided with a type I error rate of 0.05.

To provide context for their current experience, trainees were compared with contemporaneous samples of practicing physicians, the general U.S. working population aged 27 to 40 years, and a similar group of trainees who participated in a nearly identical survey in 2012. To evaluate potential changes in burnout and satisfaction with WLI, trainees from both the 2012 and 2023 surveys were also pooled to evaluate the risk of burnout by participation year after adjusting for gender, year in training, relationship status, and specialty in a multivariable logistic regression. A similar pooled multivariable logistic regression was performed for WLI. Finally, a pooled multivariable logistic regression analysis of trainees and workers in other fields aged 27 to 40 years from 2023 was performed to identify demographic and professional characteristics associated with dependent outcomes. All analyses were completed using R, version 4.3.2 (R Foundation for Statistical Computing, Vienna, Austria).

Results

Surveys were completed by 3,486 of 58,127 trainees (6.0%) invited by email. To evaluate the representativeness of participants, we compared responders with all 136,688 U.S. trainees in the AMA PPD (Table 1) and conducted a secondary survey of nonresponders. The age of participants was similar to that of all U.S. trainees; however, participants were more likely to be women (1,291 of 24,655 [52.4%] vs 65,931 of 136,435 [48.3%]). The area of specialty training of participants was also aligned with all trainees in the AMA PPD (Table 1). Among the 909 individuals in the incentivized secondary survey of nonresponders (used to evaluate response bias), 141 of 909 (15.5%) responded. No statistically significant differences between responders to the primary and nonresponder surveys were observed in age, sex, mean weekly work hours, mean depression scores or depression T-scores, mean scores for the single item emotional exhaustion and depersonalization measures, or the percentage with symptoms of burnout (see Supplemental Digital Appendix 1 at http://links.lww.com/ACADMED/B775 for additional details). Participants in the nonresponder survey were less likely to be satisfied with WLI (30 of 136 [22.1%] vs 834 of 2,509 [33.2%]; P = .01). In aggregate, these findings suggest that participants in the primary survey were generally representative of the overall sample of residents with respect to level of burnout and depression but were more satisfied with WLI.

Comparison of trainees with U.S. physicians

A comparison of trainees who completed the 2023 survey with practicing U.S. physicians surveyed during a similar interval is given in Table 2. Practicing physicians were more likely to be men (3,831 of 6,538 [58.6%] vs 1,174 of 2,521 [46.6%]; P < .001) and, as expected, were older (median age, 53 vs 32 years; P < .001). As anticipated, the specialty profiles also differed modestly because trainees in the early years of training have not had a chance to fully differentiate into their final specialty discipline. Trainees worked a mean of 10 hours per week more than practicing physicians (60 vs 50 hours; P < .001), with 872 of 2,901 trainees (30.1%) and 799 of 7,178 practicing physicians (11.1%) working a mean of more than 70 hours per week.

No difference in mean emotional exhaustion scores on the full-length MBI was observed between trainees and practicing physicians (mean, 23.7 vs 23.2; P = .10). In contrast, the mean depersonalization score for trainees was higher than that of practicing physicians (mean, 9.2 vs 6.9; P < .001), with 1,125 of 2,755 residents (40.8%) compared with 1,917 of 6,696 practicing physicians (28.6%) scoring in the high depersonalization range. After adjusting for gender, relationship status, and weekly work hours, trainees had higher depersonalization (β = 1.32; P < .001) but lower emotional exhaustion (β = −2.52; P < .001) scores than practicing physicians. More trainees (1,376 of 2,754 [50.0%] vs 3,023 of 6,688 [45.2%]; P < .001) had at least 1 symptom of burnout. When similar comparisons between practicing physicians and trainees were made in 2012,36 mean emotional exhaustion (24.8 vs 22.7; P < .001) and depersonalization (10.4 vs 7.1; P < .001) scores were both statistically significantly higher among trainees than practicing physicians, as was the proportion of trainees with symptoms of burnout (1,021 of 1,694 [60.3%] vs 3,310 of 7,227 [45.8%]; P < .001).

As indicated in Table 2, trainees also had higher mean (SD) depression T-scores than physicians in practice (51.60 [9.19] vs 49.83 [8.52]), with 478 of 2,681 trainees (17.8%) and 811 of 6,181 practicing physicians (13.1%) having moderate or severe symptoms of depression. The difference in mean depression T-scores among trainees relative to practicing physicians was no longer statistically significant after adjusting for gender, relationship status, and weekly work hours. A similar comparison between practicing physicians and trainees in 2012 could not be performed because the 2012 survey used a depression screening tool rather than an instrument assessing the severity of depression.

Practicing physicians were more likely to be satisfied with WLI than trainees (2,732 of 6,479 [42.2%] vs 834 of 2,509 [33.2%]; P < .001). When similar comparisons were made in 2012, satisfaction with WLI was also higher in practicing physicians (3,512 of 7,244 [48.5%] vs 478 of 1,695 [28.2%]; P < .001).

Comparison of trainees with the age-matched U.S. working population

We next compared the experience of the 2,256 trainees aged 27 to 40 years in 2023 with 972 similarly aged U.S. workers in other fields (Table 3). Discussion

We report the findings of a large study comparing the experience of U.S. trainees with contemporary samples of U.S. physicians and similarly aged working U.S. adults as well as a sample of trainees studied in a nearly identical manner in 2012. Although trainees remain at higher risk for burnout than practicing U.S. physicians, the gap is smaller than observed in 2012. This finding appears to be due to the combination of improved emotional exhaustion among trainees and increased emotional exhaustion for practicing physicians in 2023 compared with 2012. Although depersonalization scores had improved for both groups in 2023 relative to 2012, depersonalization scores remained markedly higher for trainees than physicians in practice—a finding that persisted after adjusting for gender, relationship status, and work hours.

Insights are also provided by the comparison of trainees to similarly aged working adults. Higher mean scores for both emotional exhaustion and depersonalization were observed among trainees, with 40.1% of trainees and 30.8% of workers in other fields having symptoms of occupational burnout. This difference was smaller than in 2012, when 49.7% of trainees and 32.4% of U.S. workers in other fields had symptoms of burnout. On pooled analysis of trainees and similarly aged U.S. adults, adjusting for personal and professional factors, trainees had a 50.0% higher risk of burnout, although this difference was eliminated when work hours were added to this model. These analyses suggest that the increased risk of burnout among trainees is largely explained by the high number of work hours, a finding that stands in contrast to observations in practicing physicians who are at increased risk for burnout compared with working U.S. adults even after adjusting for weekly work hours.40

These higher rates of occupational burnout among trainees relative to the similarly aged working population stand in stark contrast to the findings regarding depression. No differences in mean depression scores (either raw scores or depression T-scores) were observed among trainees and U.S. workers in other fields. When depression scores were stratified into normal, mild, moderate, and severe categories using the established thresholds, trainees had a statistically significant more favorable profile than U.S. workers in other fields. On pooled analysis of trainees and similarly aged U.S. adults, adjusting for demographic characteristics, relationship status, and work hours, trainees remained at lower risk of experiencing severe or moderate to severe depression than similarly aged U.S. workers. These differences in findings for burnout and depression suggest that trainees are at increased risk for occupational forms of distress compared with similarly aged workers in other fields but are not at increased risk of depression.

The comparisons of trainees’ experiences of occupational burnout and satisfaction with WLI in 2023 relative to 2012 are also notable. Statistically significant lower emotional exhaustion and depersonalization scores were observed in 2023, and the proportion of trainees with symptoms of burnout was 10% lower in 2023 than 2012 (50.0% vs 60.3%). On multivariable analysis pooling trainees responding in 2012 and 2023 adjusting for personal and professional characteristics, 2023 responders had a 29.0% lower risk of burnout. In contrast to these improvements in burnout, no difference in satisfaction with WLI was observed by participation year.

Collectively, these data simultaneously affirm the efforts to improve the training experience of trainees during the past 2 decades and emphasize that there remains more work to do. Extensive structural changes to residency training, including substantial alterations in weekly work hours relative to several decades ago, have been implemented to mitigate fatigue.54–56 The ACGME Common Program Requirements and Clinical Learning Environment Review Recommendations have resulted in substantial adjustments to workload (e.g., caps on admissions, limitations in the number of consecutive hours on duty, greater protection for time dedicated to educational activities) and sought to create a learning environment where trainees feel valued.54–56 In addition, through the efforts of the ACGME and organizations that provide residency and fellowship training, more robust emotional, mental health, and other support has been developed, including regular check-ins and feedback from faculty and senior residents and, at many institutions, opt-out mental health appointments to reduce stigma and foster use.56–58 These and other changes have likely resulted in improvement in emotional exhaustion and depersonalization among trainees in 2023 relative to 2012. Despite this progress, approximately half of trainees continue to experience symptoms of burnout. In addition, no improvements in satisfaction with WLI were observed in 2023 relative to 2012, and trainees also remain at increased risk for burnout relative to similarly aged workers in other fields.

Where do we go from here? Despite the reductions in resident duty hours during the past several decades, it appears work hours remain a dominant variable contributing to burnout during training. Previous research also suggests that work hours contribute to resident depression.14 Residents and fellows worked a mean of 20 hours more per week than similarly aged workers in other fields, and in multivariable analysis, residents were no longer at increased risk for burnout or decreased satisfaction with WLI after adjusting for demographic characteristics and work hours. This observation creates a conundrum. Given the extent of the information to learn as well as the volume of experience required to develop and hone clinical judgment, concerns about the potential effect of further reductions of trainee work hours on the educational experience have been expressed.59,60 On the other hand, work demands resulting in inadequate sleep may attenuate learning and affect clinical performance.61 Sleep-related impairment in trainees is also associated with self-reported errors that cause patient harm.3,62 In addition, working more than 55 hours per week is associated with cardiovascular disease and stroke in the general population,63,64 suggesting that the current ACGME 80-hour limit may be far from optimal.

Although efforts to enhance the educational value of each hour worked have been pursued, these interventions are challenging to implement, can dilute trainee independence, and have limits. Although the concept of individualizing the duration of training based on achievement of competency milestones has been proposed (i.e., shortening it for some trainees and lengthening it for others),65 this approach remains controversial and has not been widely implemented. As with previous advances in physician training, solving this challenge will require innovation, courage, and experimentation coupled with rigorous assessment of outcomes.

Although weekly work hours appear to be a major contributor to the burnout and challenges with WLI among trainees, other opportunities remain to improve the training experience.32 Multiple studies have indicated that mistreatment of trainees by supervising physicians, peers, and other members of the clinical team remains prevalent and contributes to occupational distress.15,17 Low levels of self-compassion also fuel burnout and are cultivated by a professional culture of perfectionism and an expectation to be superhuman.66,67 A highly accomplished peer group, along with high personal expectations, leads to feelings of inadequacy and a lack of vulnerability that contribute to imposter phenomena.68–70 Social isolation and a lack of belonging are prevalent.23,24 Although program leadership is a tremendous source of support for many trainees, program directors and their faculty teams could be better supported and developed.19 Despite advances, opportunities to improve the learning environment persist. These and other dimensions provide substantive opportunities to improve aspects of training independent of hours that influence trainee well-being.71–73

The current study is subject to a number of limitations. First, similar to previous national surveys of trainees and practicing physicians, the participation rate is relatively low. Nonetheless, survey respondents appeared similar to the overall population of trainees. Data from a nonresponder survey also suggested that responders were generally similar to nonresponders with respect to levels of burnout and depression, although they were more likely to be women and more likely to be satisfied with WLI. Other studies using a variety of approaches to assess response bias in burnout surveys found that occupational burnout does not appear to increase or decrease the likelihood of participation51–53 and that the estimated prevalence of burnout is minimally impacted by nonresponse.74 Second, data on average weekly work hours were not collected in the 2012 study, so we are unable to directly compare self-reported work hours among residents in 2023 relative to 2012. Finally, the 2023 survey included a standardized assessment of depression severity, whereas the 2012 survey used a depression screening instrument that precluded comparison of depression among trainees in 2023 relative to 2012.

In conclusion, occupational distress among trainees appears to be lower in 2023 compared with 2012, and depression among trainees is similar to that of U.S. workers in other fields. Despite this improvement, trainees remain at higher risk for occupational burnout than workers in other fields, with approximately half of trainees experiencing symptoms of burnout. Given the well-established relationship between burnout and quality of care, professional behavior, and attrition from the profession, these findings have important consequences for the U.S. health care delivery system. Continued efforts are needed to optimize trainee education and well-being.
