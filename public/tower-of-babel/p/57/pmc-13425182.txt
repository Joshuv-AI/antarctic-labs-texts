Geographic Variation in Measles Vaccine Coverage Among Children Aged 7 Years in Northern California
Authors: Karen B. Jacobson, Kristin Goddard, Joan Bartlett, Sally C. Stephens, Helena I. Archer, Lauren D. Liao, Joshua T. B. Williams, Seohyun Kim, Ousseny Zerbo, Sophia R. Newcomer, Nicola P. Klein
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
This cohort study assesses the variation in measles-containing vaccine coverage at the county census tract level among children aged 7 years in Northern California.

Introduction

In 2025, the US surpassed its highest annual case count of measles in more than 30 years, mostly in unvaccinated children.1 Despite high vaccination coverage with measles-containing vaccine (MCV) at the county level in Northern California,2 recent cases and wastewater detection of measles have been reported regionally.3 Within this large geographic area are potential pockets of lower MCV coverage within counties. An immunization rate of at least 95% is generally considered necessary for herd immunity to measles.4 Undervaccinated children congregating in group settings (eg, schools) could increase the risk of measles transmission. We assessed variation in MCV coverage at the census tract level among children aged 7 years in Northern California.

Methods

This retrospective cohort study was performed within Kaiser Permanente Northern California’s (KPNC’s) integrated health system, which provides comprehensive medical care to approximately 4.4 million members, including 867 000 children and those with subsidized insurance. Members represent approximately 33% of the regional population and are sociodemographically representative of the geographic areas served.5 The KPNC Institutional Review Board approved the study and waived informed consent due to the use of only retrospective data. The study followed the STROBE reporting guideline for cohort studies.

The KPNC electronic medical record captures all health care encounter data, including demographics and vaccinations. Due to the brevity of this report, data on race and ethnicity were not collected. The study population included KPNC members who were aged 7 years between January 1, 2017, and December 31, 2024, and continuous KPNC members from age 4 to 6 years.

Measles vaccine coverage at the census tract level was calculated as the number of children who received 2 doses before age 7 years divided by the total number of children aged 7 years in each census tract. Census tracts were assigned from the most recent address before the 7th birthday.

We conducted an optimized clustering analysis using the Getis-Ord Gi* tool in ArcGIS Pro, version 2.7.1 (Esri) to detect local census tract clusters with significantly lower MCV coverage than expected based on overall KPNC coverage.3,6 A cluster identified at the 99% confidence level indicated that the observed spatial concentration of low-coverage tracts was highly unlikely (P < .01) to have occurred by chance. Local clusters were defined by an optimal fixed-distance band (8075 m) based on the average Euclidean distance to 30 nearest neighbors, excluding 63 locational outliers. Since cluster population size is not factored into hypothesis testing, we restricted analyses to census tracts with at least 30 children. A false discovery rate correction was applied to account for multiple testing and spatial dependence. We also conducted analysis using contiguity edges as an alternative spatial conceptualization. Statistical analyses were performed using SAS, version 9.4 (SAS Institute Inc).

Results

Of 248 048 children aged 7 years across 2995 census tracts, 239 236 (96.4%; 48.7% female and 51.3% male) resided in 2290 census tracts with at least 30 children and were included in the study (Figure 1). Measles vaccine coverage was 97.6% (mean, 97.5%; range, 75%-100%). There were 276 tracts with coverage less than 95%, encompassing 24 308 children (9.8%).

We identified 7 local clusters in which coverage was significantly lower than expected (Figure 2). Clusters included 242 census tracts with a combined MCV coverage of 94.6%, encompassing 25 803 children (10.8%). Clusters were noted in 8 counties across the North Bay (Sonoma and Napa counties), the Sacramento greater metropolitan area, and Fresno County. A separate analysis using an alternative spatial conceptualization yielded similar results.

Discussion

This cohort study found that overall coverage with at least 2 doses of MCV was high within KPNC and slightly above California’s statewide 2023-2024 rate of 96.2%.2 However, in more than 200 census tracts, MCV coverage in school-age children was below the 95% herd immunity threshold.4 While only El Dorado county was identified by the state as having MCV coverage less than 95%,2 we identified 8 counties with census tracts with less than 95% coverage. While MCV coverage in children aged 7 years does not equate to population immunity, undervaccination of school-age children congregating in group settings could increase the risk of measles transmission.7 Some census tracts with low MCV coverage (eg, San Joaquin county) did not meet our threshold for spatial clusters possibly because neighboring tracts did not share similarly low coverage, highlighting the importance of examining both individual tract coverage and spatial clustering as complementary approaches. Study strengths included a large population with high-quality longitudinal medical record data. Limitations included a lack of generalizability to rural census tracts and areas with non–KPNC-insured or uninsured children and that KPNC members may not represent the general population.

This cohort study found clusters of children aged 7 years undervaccinated for measles in geographic proximity, increasing the local risk of measles outbreaks. Measles prevention efforts should account for regional variation in MCV uptake to identify areas at risk, even in counties with seemingly high coverage.
