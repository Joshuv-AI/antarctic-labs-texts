Similarities and differences in the intestinal actions of metformin and imeglimin
Authors: Kenji Sugawara, Kaori Hozumi, Hiroshi Mizuma, Daisuke Sasaki, Masakazu Shinohara, Akihiko Kondo, Yasuyoshi Watanabe, Wataru Ogawa
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractAimsImeglimin is a novel antidiabetic drug that shares structural similarity with metformin. While the intestinal effects of metformin have attracted widespread attention, those of imeglimin remain underexplored.Materials and methodsC57BL/6J mice were treated with metformin or imeglimin for RNA sequencing of intestinal tissue. Gut microbiota composition was evaluated in KK‐Ay mice by 16S rRNA sequencing of faecal samples. To assess direct effects on the human microbiome, a gut simulator was used with faecal samples from healthy and diabetic individuals. Intestinal glucose dynamics were assessed in C57BL/6J mice following administration of [18F]fluorodeoxyglucose, with subsequent analysis of tissue distribution.ResultsBulk RNA‐sequencing of colonic tissue revealed that both drugs induced similar patterns of gene expression changes, including a prominent upregulation of Gdf15. However, single‐cell RNA‐sequencing uncovered distinct effects of the two drugs, with metformin having a pronounced impact on the functional properties of enterocytes and imeglimin increasing the proportion of IgA‐producing plasma cells. Metformin reduced gut microbial diversity and induced substantial changes in microbial composition, whereas imeglimin exerted less pronounced effects. Gut simulator analysis with human faecal samples showed that both drugs directly altered gut microbial populations but in different ways. Finally, metformin promoted glucose excretion into the intestinal lumen, whereas imeglimin had a minimal effect on this process.ConclusionsIn conclusion, although metformin and imeglimin exerted similar effects on gene expression in the colon at the whole‐tissue level, they showed distinct cell type–specific actions, and they differed in their influence on gut microbiota and intestinal glucose dynamics.

INTRODUCTION

Imeglimin is an antidiabetes medication recently launched in Japan that has attracted attention because of its novel mechanism of action. 1 Despite its marked structural similarity to metformin, 2 imeglimin is thought to lower blood glucose levels by stimulating insulin secretion from pancreatic β‐cells. 3 , 4 , 5 , 6 , 7 , 8 , 9 , 10 The underlying mechanisms of this effect appear to include activation of the NAD+‐cyclic ADP‐ribose‐Ca2+ signalling pathway, 7 enhancement of mitochondrial function and ATP production, 5 , 7 , 8 , 11 and stimulation of incretin secretion. 12 , 13

On the other hand, the structural similarity of imeglimin and metformin suggests that the two drugs may share certain pharmacological effects. Indeed, both drugs have been shown to inhibit mitochondrial respiration, 14 to suppress glucose production, 9 , 15 to stimulate AMP‐activated protein kinase (AMPK), 14 and to induce similar patterns of gene expression 14 in hepatocytes. Moreover, imeglimin enhances glucagon‐like peptide‐1 (GLP‐1) secretion, 12 , 13 an effect also apparent for metformin. 16 , 17 These findings collectively indicate that imeglimin and metformin exert both overlapping and distinct pharmacological actions.

While metformin has been thought to exert its primary antidiabetic effects in the liver, it also has various effects on the intestine. It thus stimulates the secretion of growth differentiation factor 15 (GDF15) 18 , 19 and N‐lactoyl‐phenylalanine 20 , 21 —both of which suppress appetite—in the intestinal tract. Metformin also modulates the composition and function of the gut microbiota, 22 , 23 which may contribute to the improvement of glucose tolerance. In addition, recent studies have shown that metformin promotes the excretion of glucose into the intestinal lumen, 24 , 25 , 26 thereby providing a substrate for short‐chain fatty acid (SCFA) production by gut bacteria. 26 However, whether imeglimin has similar intestinal actions has remained unknown.

In the present study, we investigated the effects of imeglimin on the intestine, with particular focus on gene expression, gut microbiota, and intestinal glucose dynamics. By comparing the actions of metformin and imeglimin, we aimed to elucidate their shared and distinct pharmacological effects.

MATERIALS AND METHODS

Study approval

Mice were handled according to the guidelines of and the study was approved by the Animal Experimentation Committee of Kobe University Graduate School of Medicine (Approval Nos. P180503 and P210104) and the Animal Care and Use Committee of RIKEN Kobe Institute (Approval No. A2021‐06). The protocol for the collection of stool samples from healthy individuals and individuals with type 2 diabetes was approved by the Ethics Committee of Kobe University Graduate School of Medicine (Approval Nos. 1902 and B200385).

Mice

C57BL/6J and KK‐Ay mice were obtained from CLEA Japan and maintained on a normal diet under a 12‐h light/12‐h dark cycle. Male mice at 8–12 weeks of age were studied. For examination of the effects of single‐dose drug administration, metformin (Sigma‐Aldrich, St Louis, MO) or imeglimin (provided by Sumitomo Pharma Co. Ltd., Japan) was administered by oral gavage at 600 mg/kg, and blood samples and intestinal tissues were collected 6 h after treatment. For examination of the effects of chronic drug administration, each drug was dissolved in drinking water at a concentration of 3 mg/mL and provided freely until the time of anaesthesia and sacrifice, with blood samples being obtained via cardiac puncture and intestinal tissues collected after 4 or 12 weeks. All in vivo experiments were conducted after a 6‐h fasting period. The intestine was excised from the pylorus to the anus and divided into four parts: upper small intestine (SI), lower SI, cecum, and colon. The upper and lower SI specimens were further subdivided into the duodenum, proximal SI, middle SI, and distal SI.

16S rRNA gene amplicon sequencing

C57BL/6J and KK‐Ay mice were treated with metformin or imeglimin for 4 weeks, after which faecal samples were collected for extraction of DNA with the use of a QIAamp PowerFecal Pro DNA Kit (Qiagen, Hilden, Germany). The extracted DNA was subjected to 16S rRNA gene amplicon sequencing by Rhelixa (Japan). The V3–V4 region was sequenced, and genus‐level taxonomic annotation and principal component analysis (PCA) were performed after quality control. SCFAs in faecal samples were extracted with water and analysed by ultra‐performance liquid chromatography.

RNA‐seq analysis

Total RNA was isolated from mouse intestinal tissues with the use of an RNeasy Mini Kit (Qiagen) and was subjected to RNA‐sequencing (seq) analysis by Macrogen Japan. The mRNA libraries were prepared with the use of a TruSeq Stranded mRNA Library Kit (Illumina, San Diego, CA) and were sequenced with a NovaSeq 6000 instrument (Illumina). Reads were aligned to the mm10 mouse genome. Genes with an expression level of >1 transcript per million (TPM) and a fold change of ≥2 were subjected to pathway analysis. Significance was defined as an adjusted p‐value of <0.05 after false discovery rate (FDR) correction using the Benjamini–Hochberg method.

Single‐cell RNA‐seq analysis

The colon was isolated from pooled samples of three C57BL/6J mice that had been treated with imeglimin or metformin for 12 weeks and was processed by Takara Bio (Japan) for single‐cell dissociation, library preparation, and sequencing. Libraries were generated with a Chromium Fixed RNA Profiling Kit (10x Genomics, Pleasanton, CA), and sequencing was performed on a NovaSeq 6000 instrument (Illumina). Data were processed with Cellranger and analysed with Scanpy. Analysis of differentially expressed genes (DEG) among clusters was performed using DESeq2, with DEGs being defined as those with a |log2(fold change)| > 1 and an adjusted p‐value <0.05 after FDR correction using the Benjamini–Hochberg method. Volcano plot was adopted to visualize the distribution of upregulated and downregulated DEGs across the comparison. Functional enrichment was performed with DAVID.

Gut microbiota simulation

The Kobe University Human Intestinal Microbiota Model (KUHIMM) was adopted to evaluate the effects of metformin and imeglimin on the human gut microbiota. Faecal samples from individuals with type 2 diabetes were treated with metformin or imeglimin at 10 or 25 mM, while those from control individuals were treated with 25 mM. These concentrations were selected based on previous studies. 23 , 27 , 28 All samples were cultivated in a Bio Jr.8 fermenter for 48 h at 37°C under anaerobic conditions using Gifu anaerobic medium. 29

Microbial DNA was then extracted from the cultures, and the V3–V4 region of 16S rRNA genes was sequenced with the use of MiSeq technology. Quality control was conducted with the DADA2 pipeline in QIIME 2, with taxonomic classification performed with the SILVA 138.2 database. The concentrations of SCFAs, including acetate, propionate, and butyrate, were also measured in the culture supernatants by high‐performance liquid chromatography.

[ 18F]FDG administration in mice

Intestinal glucose dynamics were measured in C57BL/6J mice that had been treated with metformin or imeglimin for 4 weeks, using [18F]Fluorodeoxyglucose (FDG), which was performed essentially as described in our previous study. 26 For ex vivo autoradiography, mice were sacrificed 1 h after [18F]FDG administration, and the gastrointestinal tract was then excised on ice. The tissue samples were placed on a chamber with a taut plastic film and were tightly contacted to an imaging plate (BAS IP SR 2040E, GE Healthcare Japan) for 60 min under shaded light. The autoradiogram was acquired by reading the imaging plate using a bioimaging analyser (FLA‐7000, GE Healthcare Japan) and was analysed with Image Gauge software (v4.21, Fuji Photo Film, Japan).

Statistical analysis

Statistical analysis was performed with the use of GraphPad Prism or R (R Foundation for Statistical Computing) software. Unless noted otherwise in the figure legends, comparisons between two groups were performed with Student's t test, and those among three or more groups by one‐way ANOVA followed by Tukey's post hoc test. A p‐value of <0.05 was considered statistically significant, also unless indicated otherwise.

RESULTS

Acute effects of metformin and imeglimin on gene expression in the colon

To assess the acute effects of metformin and imeglimin, we collected blood and colon samples at 6 h after a single oral dose (600 mg/kg) of these drugs in C57BL/6J mice. 18 Both metformin and imeglimin induced a decrease in blood glucose levels; however, neither drug caused any change in insulin levels (Figure S1A,B, Supporting Information). We performed RNA‐seq analysis on colon tissue, and PCA revealed a significant shift in gene expression for both drugs compared with the control, with a similar directional change along the component 1 axis (Figure 1A). A heat map of hierarchical clustering analysis indicated that the two drugs induced similar changes in a similar set of genes (Figure 1B). Volcano plots also revealed similarity in the genes highly upregulated or downregulated by the two drugs (Figure 1C). Pathway analysis of DEGs showed that several pathways, including mitogen‐activated protein kinase (MAPK) signalling and carbon metabolism, were influenced by both drugs (Figure S2). Furthermore, the slope of a scatter plot of metformin‐ or imeglimin‐induced changes in gene expression was close to 1 (Figure 1D), again indicating the similarities in the effects of the two drugs on gene expression. Changes in the expression of genes that were significantly influenced by both drugs were confirmed by RT‐qPCR analysis (Figure S3). Collectively, these results indicated that the overall effects of imeglimin and metformin on gene expression in the colon were similar.

Given that Gdf15, which has been implicated in the anti‐obese effect of metformin, 30 , 31 , 32 is markedly upregulated by both metformin and imeglimin (Figure 1C,D), we examined the serum concentration of GDF15. Both drugs significantly increased the serum concentration of GDF15 (Figure S1C). Evidence suggests that Gdf15 induction is mediated via the integrated stress response 18 ; indeed, both drugs increased the abundance of integrated stress‐related genes including Atf4 and Ddit3 (Figure S1D,E).

We further investigated whether the two drugs affect gene expression in intestinal regions other than the colon. RT‐qPCR analysis revealed that the expression of genes that were upregulated by each drug in the colon—including Gdf15, Chac1, Areg, Trib3, and Slc7a11—was not substantially affected by either drug in the duodenum or proximal, middle, or distal SI (Figure 1E), suggesting that the two drugs influence gene expression preferentially in the colon.

Chronic effects of metformin and imeglimin on gene expression in the intestine

We next investigated whether chronic treatment with metformin or imeglimin might also have similar effects on intestinal gene expression. RNA‐seq analysis was performed for the proximal, middle, and distal SI as well as for the colon of C57BL/6J mice treated with the drugs for 4 weeks. No significant differences in body weight were observed among the groups during the treatment period (Figure S4).

Scatter plot analysis of metformin‐ or imeglimin‐induced changes in gene expression revealed that the changes were relatively small in the proximal SI, with many genes clustering near the origin (Figure 1F). The changes in gene expression became more pronounced with progression toward the anal end of the intestine, reaching a maximum in the colon. A list of genes whose distance exceeds 3.5 in the scatter plots is provided in Table S1. The changes in the expression of these latter genes were also confirmed by RT‐qPCR analysis in the colon (Figure S5). Both PCA and hierarchical clustering analyses revealed that the two drugs induced similar changes in gene expression in the colon (Figure S6A,B). The effects of both drugs on gene expression in the intestine were thus also similar even after long‐term administration.

Although metformin and imeglimin induced similar changes in gene expression in the colon after either a single or long‐term administration, the genes affected by each drug differed depending on the duration of treatment. Scatter plots of the changes in gene expression induced by acute or chronic administration of each drug revealed that imeglimin affected a greater number of genes after a single administration than after long‐term treatment, whereas metformin affected more genes after long‐term administration (Figure 1G).

Cell type–specific responses to metformin and imeglimin in the mouse colon

To investigate further the effects of long‐term administration of metformin or imeglimin on gene expression and cellular adaptations, including changes in cell composition and cellular functions, we performed single‐cell RNA‐seq analysis for the colon of C57BL/6J mice that had been treated with the drugs for 3 months. Changes in body weight and random blood glucose levels over the treatment period are shown in Figure S7. UMAP visualization revealed distinct cellular heterogeneity within the colon as identified by Leiden clustering with annotated cell populations (Figure 2A,B).

Metformin treatment induced notable shifts in cluster distributions, particularly among enterocytes (Figure 2C,D). While the relative abundance of clusters 17 (Enterocyte_5) and 0 (Enterocyte_1) decreased after metformin treatment, that of clusters 6 (Enterocyte_4) and 2 (Enterocyte_2) increased (Figure 2E), suggesting that some enterocyte clusters had undergone transcriptional reprogramming, resulting in a shift toward other clusters. Given that clusters 17 and 6 showed the greatest decrease and increase, respectively, suggesting that a substantial proportion of cluster 17 cells had transitioned to cluster 6, we compared the transcriptional characteristics of these two clusters. Differential gene expression analysis identified 93 DEGs (Figure 2F and Table S2), among which Emp1 and Ndrg1 function in epithelial remodelling and cellular stress responses, 33 , 34 whereas Zg16 contributes to mucosal defense and epithelial barrier integrity. 35 Several genes related to solute transport—including Slc9a3 (sodium/hydrogen exchanger), Slc39a4 (zinc importer), and Slc40a1 (iron exporter)—showed altered expression, suggestive of transcriptional reprogramming of enterocytes toward the regulation of ion and metal transport. Of note, Slc37a2, which encodes a glucose‐6‐phosphate transporter, was significantly downregulated in cluster 6, suggestive of a dynamic shift in glucose metabolism within the enterocyte population. Consistent with these findings, pathway enrichment analysis revealed that oxidative phosphorylation, metabolic pathways, and mineral absorption were significantly enriched in cluster 6 (Figure 2G), supporting the notion of metabolic reprogramming of enterocyte state in response to metformin treatment.

In contrast, imeglimin reduced the number of B cells and markedly increased the proportion of immunoglobulin A (IgA)–producing plasma cells, an effect not observed with metformin (Figure 2D,E).

Chronic effects of metformin and imeglimin on the gut microbiota

We next compared the effects of metformin and imeglimin on the gut microbiota by performing 16S rRNA gene sequencing. Obese, diabetic KK‐Ay mice were orally administered these drugs for 4 weeks. There were no significant differences in body weight or random blood glucose levels among the groups (Figure S8). As expected, metformin notably reduced the Shannon index, a parameter of gut microbial diversity, 36 whereas imeglimin did not affect this parameter (Figure 3A). PCA showed that metformin induced substantial changes in the gut microbiota, whereas imeglimin had only minor effects (Figure 3B). Analysis at the genus level revealed that metformin induced a significant increase in the proportion of the genus Lactobacillus, an effect not apparent for imeglimin (Figure 3C,D). Similarly, the abundance of the genus Lachnospiraceae in the NK4A136 group was significantly reduced with both drugs, but the reduction was more pronounced with metformin. On the other hand, both drugs significantly reduced the proportion of the genus Alistipes, whereas neither drug affected that of the genus Muribaculaceae. The abundance of the Prevotella in the UCG‐001 group was significantly increased by both drugs, while Bacteroides showed a trend toward an increase (Figure 3C,D). These trends were also observed in wild‐type C57BL/6J mice treated under the same conditions. Specifically, metformin tended to increase the proportion of Lactobacillus, decrease the Lachnospiraceae NK4A136 group, and reduce Alistipes, although these changes did not reach statistical significance due to inter‐individual variability. In addition, the proportion of Bacteroides was significantly increased by both drugs, whereas Prevotella showed a trend toward an increase with metformin and a significant increase with imeglimin (Figure S9).

Furthermore, the SCFAs in faecal samples were measured following drug treatment. Although no statistically significant differences were observed, both metformin and, to a lesser extent, imeglimin tended to increase faecal concentrations of acetate and propionate (Figure S10). In contrast, butyrate levels tended to decrease with imeglimin treatment (Figure S10). These results indicated that, whereas both drugs affect the gut microbiota, their actual effects differ, potentially leading to distinct alterations in microbial metabolic output, such as SCFAs composition.

Direct effects of metformin and imeglimin on the human gut microbiota

We next investigated the direct effects of imeglimin and metformin on the human gut microbiota with the use of a gut simulator (KUHIMM). Faecal samples were obtained from healthy individuals (n = 10) and individuals with type 2 diabetes (n = 6), the inclusion and exclusion criteria for whom are shown in Table S3. Clinical characteristics of the donors with type 2 diabetes are provided in Table S4.

PCA analysis of faecal microbiota profiles before culture showed that the clusters differed slightly between the two groups of subjects (Figure S11), with the faecal samples from the individuals with diabetes showing a higher relative abundance of the genus Escherichia as well as Enterococcus and Fusobacteriaceae (Figure S12A,B). The cultivation system preserved the distinct gut microbiota profiles of the two groups (Figure S11). Culture with metformin significantly increased the proportion of the genus Bifidobacterium in samples from healthy individuals, an effect not observed with imeglimin (Figures 4A,B and S12A). Conversely, imeglimin significantly increased the relative abundance of the genera Enterococcus and Escherichia. The proportion of unclassified Clostridium also tended to be increased by imeglimin treatment, although this change was not statistically significant. Analysis of the faecal samples from individuals with type 2 diabetes revealed that, whereas metformin had no significant effect overall (Figures 4A,B and S12B), it tended to increase the relative abundance of the genus Bifidobacterium in a concentration‐dependent manner for donors (donors 3 and 6) with initially high levels of these bacteria (Figures 4C and S12B). In contrast, imeglimin induced a significant depletion of the genus Bifidobacterium. Imeglimin also significantly increased the proportion of the genera Enterococcus and unclassified Clostridium. Neither drug had any effect on the relative abundance of the genus Bacteroides. Together, these results using a gut simulator system indicate that both metformin and imeglimin can directly affect the composition of the human gut microbiota, although their effects differ.

Effects of metformin and imeglimin on intestinal glucose dynamics

We recently showed that metformin promotes glucose excretion into the intestinal lumen, but not glucose accumulation in the intestinal wall, in humans, 24 , 26 whereas it enhances both glucose excretion into the lumen and glucose accumulation in the intestinal wall in mice. 24 , 26 We next investigated whether chronic treatment of imeglimin affects such glucose dynamics in the intestine of mice.

Autoradiography revealed that metformin induced a marked increase in [18F]FDG accumulation in the distal small intestine, cecum, and colon, whereas imeglimin had more limited effects (Figure 5A). Quantitative analysis of radioactivity showed that both metformin and imeglimin significantly increased [18F]FDG uptake in cecal tissue, but the effect of imeglimin was again less pronounced (Figure 5B). Furthermore, metformin—but not imeglimin—increased [18F]FDG accumulation in the wall and lumen of the lower small intestine and colon, as well as in the lumen of the cecum (Figure 5B). These results indicated that metformin affects glucose dynamics in the intestine to a greater extent than does imeglimin, in particular with regard to the promotion of glucose excretion into the intestinal lumen. We also analysed changes in the expression levels of genes encoding various glucose transporters. While several genes were downregulated by metformin or imeglimin, GLUT1 was the only gene whose expression was clearly upregulated by both drugs, specifically in the colon (Figure S13A,B).

Exposures of metformin and imeglimin on intestine

Finally, we examined the extent to which the drugs reached the intestine. Mass spectrometric analysis of serum, intestinal tissue, and luminal contents revealed no significant differences between mice treated with metformin and those treated with imeglimin (Figure S14). The concentrations (ng/mg, mean ± SD) of metformin and imeglimin were 366.3 ± 175.8 and 304.6 ± 92.7 in colon tissue, and 537.2 ± 116.8 and 436.2 ± 98.3 in colonic luminal contents, respectively (Figure S14). These findings suggested that the observed differences may reflect inherent pharmacological differences between the two drugs, rather than differences in intestinal exposure. Notably, the serum concentrations of metformin and imeglimin were 4.2 and 3.4 ng/μL, respectively. These concentrations are comparable to those observed in humans receiving therapeutic doses of these drugs. 37 , 38

DISCUSSION

We here performed a comprehensive analysis of the effects of metformin and imeglimin on the intestine. While the overall impact of the two drugs on gene expression appeared similar, single‐cell RNA‐seq analysis revealed distinct cell type–specific effects. Moreover, their effects on gut microbiota composition and glucose dynamics in the intestine were also found to differ.

One of the most striking effects of metformin revealed by single‐cell RNA‐seq analysis was on the characteristics of enterocytes. Our analysis identified six distinct enterocyte clusters, of which two increased and three decreased in size in response to metformin, suggestive of a shift in enterocyte subpopulations toward the phenotypes of the expanding clusters. The most prominently increased enterocyte cluster showed enrichment of pathways related to oxidative phosphorylation, metabolic processes, and mineral absorption, possibly reflecting the metabolic effects of metformin on intestinal cells. Imeglimin, on the other hand, induced a decrease in the proportion of B cells and a concomitant increase in that of IgA‐producing plasma cells, which may reflect a differentiation shift from the former to the latter. IgA produced in the intestinal mucosa affects the composition and function of the gut microbiota 39 and contributes to maintenance of intestinal homeostasis. 40 Further studies are needed to experimentally validate the cellular and molecular changes indicated by the single‐cell RNA‐seq analysis.

Metformin, but not imeglimin, increased the proportion of the genus Lactobacillus in the mouse intestine and that of the genus Bifidobacterium in the gut simulator, with both of these types of bacteria being thought to protect against diabetes. 41 , 42 Imeglimin was found to specifically affect different bacterial genera in the gut simulator, including Escherichia, Enterococcus, and unclassified Clostridium. Variability in microbiota composition and drug response was observed in the KUHIMM experiments, likely due to the use of faecal samples from multiple human donors. Nevertheless, consistent trends across donors were observed for these genera, suggesting that the effects of both drugs on the gut microbiota may be at least partially generalisable.

Metformin has been shown to increase SCFA levels in humans, 23 , 43 which likely contributes to its beneficial metabolic effects. 44 In the present study using KK‐Ay mice, although no statistically significant differences were observed, acetate and propionate levels tended to increase with metformin treatment, and to a lesser extent with imeglimin (Figure S10). Lactobacillus is known as a heterofermentative lactic acid bacterium that produces acetate, while Bacteroides and Prevotella are major producers of both acetate and propionate. 45 , 46 In our analysis, metformin treatment significantly increased the abundance of the genus Lactobacillus, whereas imeglimin did not induce significant changes but showed an increase in some individuals. Furthermore, both drugs significantly increased the abundance of Prevotella and were associated with a trend toward increased Bacteroides levels. In addition, given that lactate serves as a substrate for propionate‐producing bacteria, the increase in Lactobacillus may have indirectly contributed to elevated propionate levels. Taken together, these microbial changes may underlie the alterations in SCFA levels, thereby contributing to the beneficial effects on glucose tolerance not only in response to metformin but also to imeglimin.

However, in our gut simulator experiments, metformin increased the proportion of the genus Bifidobacterium—which are known to produce SCFAs 47 —but did not significantly affect the concentration of SCFAs in the culture medium (Figure S12C). We recently showed that glucose excreted into the intestinal lumen is metabolised into SCFAs by the gut microbiota. 26 These findings suggest that both glucose excretion into the intestinal lumen and an increased abundance of SCFA‐producing bacteria may be required for metformin‐induced enhancement of SCFA production, which may contribute, at least in part, to the glucose‐lowering effects of metformin. To better understand the metabolic potential of the gut microbiota—particularly regarding SCFA biosynthesis and other metabolic pathways—and to establish a functional link between microbial changes and host glucose metabolism, future studies such as metagenomic analysis and targeted metabolomic profiling will be essential.

Using a newly developed positron emission tomography (PET)–magnetic resonance imaging (MRI)–based bioimaging technique, we recently showed that glucose (~0.41 g/h) is excreted into the intestinal lumen in humans, and that metformin augments this glucose flux approximately fourfold (to ~1.65 g/h). 26 In the present study, we also observed the enhancement of FDG accumulation in the intestinal wall and lumen of mice by metformin, whereas the effects of imeglimin on intestinal glucose dynamics were much more limited. These findings suggest that the increased FDG accumulation in the intestinal lumen under metformin treatment may reflect enhanced uptake in the intestinal wall, leading to increased glucose excretion. Although the precise mechanism remains unclear, several possibilities may account for the increased luminal accumulation of FDG under metformin treatment, including mass‐dependent diffusion, transcellular transport via glucose transporters, and paracellular routes. Among the various glucose transporter genes analysed, only GLUT1 showed increased expression in response to metformin. Although the upregulation was more pronounced with metformin, a slight increase in Glut1 expression was also observed with imeglimin. Therefore, the expression level of this transporter alone cannot fully explain the differences in glucose dynamics observed between the two drugs. These differences may also involve post‐transcriptional regulation or differences in membrane localisation.

Despite its structural similarity to metformin, a phase IIa trial of imeglimin found that gastrointestinal symptoms, including diarrhoea, were milder and occurred less frequently compared with those reported for metformin treatment, 48 with an association between changes in the gut microbiota and gastrointestinal symptoms being well established. Furthermore, an increased supply of glucose to the intestinal lumen may also influence both the composition and metabolic activity of the microbiome. Therefore, the gastrointestinal symptoms observed during metformin treatment may be attributable both to direct effects of the drug on the microbiota and indirect effects resulting from enhanced glucose excretion.

Although our study has provided important insight into the differential effects of metformin and imeglimin, it has several limitations. A primary concern is the lack of direct assessment of systemic metabolic outcomes, such as glycemic control and insulin sensitivity. Future comprehensive metabolic analyses will be necessary to determine the causal relationship between intestinal changes and systemic metabolic improvement. The second limitation is that the use of mouse models may not fully reflect human drug metabolism and intestinal physiology, with the exception of the gut simulator (KUHIMM), which utilises human‐derived faecal microbiota in a controlled ex vivo setting. The third limitation is that time‐dependent changes in gut adaptations between metformin and imeglimin remain unclear in this study. Further time‐course studies will be necessary to clarify how treatment duration affects gut responses to these drugs differently. Similarly, it would also be necessary to investigate dose‐dependency to define the pharmacological spectrum and threshold effects of each drug on the gut environment. Fourth, due to its design as an omics‐based comprehensive pharmacological analysis, detailed functional analyses of individual molecules or specific pathways contributing to the glucose‐lowering effects of metformin and imeglimin were not extensively performed. For example, further investigation is needed to elucidate how increased intestinal Gdf15 expression may contribute to these effects. Furthermore, recent studies have reported that both drugs exhibit anti‐inflammatory properties. 49 Chronic inflammation is a well‐recognised pathophysiological feature of various conditions characterised by elevated blood glucose levels, including type 2 diabetes, 50 prediabetes, 51 and metabolic syndrome. 52 Therefore, it is plausible that some of the observed improvements in metabolic parameters in our study may involve modulation of inflammatory pathways, which warrants further investigation. Fifth, although our gut simulator experiments demonstrated that both metformin and imeglimin can directly alter gut microbiota composition, the precise molecular mechanisms underlying these drug–microbiota interactions remain unclear. Further studies employing metagenomics, metabolomics, or targeted in vitro assays will be necessary to clarify how each drug directly influences gut microbial physiology.

In conclusion, our study has clarified similarities and differences in the effects of metformin and imeglimin. Our findings shed light on the distinct characteristics of imeglimin, a relatively new drug, and they provide important information for distinguishing imeglimin from other diabetes medications. Further exploration of the molecular mechanisms underlying the differences in the actions of metformin and imeglimin, particularly with regard to the differences in their chemical structures, may not only lead to a more complete understanding of the two drugs but also promote the development of further treatments for diabetes.
