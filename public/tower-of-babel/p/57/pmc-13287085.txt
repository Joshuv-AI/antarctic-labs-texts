Navigating the Evolution of Urothelial Carcinoma Treatment: From Chemotherapy to Immunotherapy
Authors: Ben Li, Ziqi Yang, Yihao Pei, Yan Wang, Yumin Chen
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTBackgroundThe urothelial carcinoma (UC) trial landscape has expanded over two decades, alongside a shift from chemotherapy to immunotherapy and targeted approaches. We characterized temporal patterns in trial phase, interventions, sponsorship, geographic footprint, and the uptake of biomarker specification and artificial intelligence (AI) terminology.MethodsWe conducted a trial registry‐based analysis of 823 interventional UC studies first posted on ClinicalTrials.gov during 2005–2024. Trials were classified by phase, recruitment status, and intervention, and further stratified by sponsor type and geographic region. Registry text was mined to flag biomarker‐specified and AI‐enabled registrations. Trends were summarized overall and for two periods: 2005–2014 and 2015–2024.ResultsAnnual registrations increased after 2015, peaking in 2018. Early‐phase studies predominated overall, with expansion of Phase 3 activity in 2015–2024. Drug interventions remained dominant as immunotherapy displaced chemotherapy; pembrolizumab was the leading agent in the later period. The sponsor mix shifted toward industry, and the geographic footprint broadened: the United States/Canada remained the largest contributor, with growth in multinational activity and increased participation from East Asia. Biomarker‐specified language rose to roughly one quarter of registrations in 2015–2024, whereas explicit AI terminology was rare. Early‐phase termination rates were notable.ConclusionsUC trials have scaled and globalized while consolidating immunotherapy and advancing genomically directed strategies. Priorities include precise preregistration of biomarker and computational endpoints, inclusive multinational accrual, and adequately powered comparative trials against current standards to accelerate durable clinical benefit.

Urothelial carcinoma (UC) is the most common malignant tumor of the urinary system, accounting for over 90% of bladder cancer cases. Among its histological subtypes, squamous differentiation and glandular differentiation are the most prevalent. UC is particularly prevalent in North America and Europe, where it is one of the most common malignancies in men. The incidence of UC increases significantly with age and is approximately three to four times higher in men than in women [1]. The pathogenesis of UC is multifactorial, involving factors such as smoking, exposure to chemical substances, poor drinking water quality, and genetic predisposition. Many patients are diagnosed at an advanced stage, often presenting with bladder dysfunction, renal impairment, and other complications that can be life‐threatening. Although surgical intervention remains the cornerstone of UC treatment, chemotherapy, which is primarily drug‐based, is also a viable first‐line therapeutic option. In recent years, immunotherapy has gained increasing attention, with immune checkpoint inhibitors such as pembrolizumab demonstrating notable efficacy in the treatment of advanced UC [2]. Despite the growing number of clinical trials for UC, the selection of appropriate therapeutic agents for UC patients remains a significant clinical challenge.

Data source and study periods. We queried ClinicalTrials.gov on April 5, 2025, using the condition term “Urothelial Carcinoma.” The dataset was restricted to interventional trials first‐posted between 2005 and 2024. In total, 823 records met eligibility; observational and expanded‐access studies were excluded. The raw export is available in the Supporting Information.

Classification and core variables. Trials were analyzed as recorded in the registry and classified by phase, recruitment status, and intervention type. Phases followed registry categories (Early Phase 1, Phase 1, Phase 2, Phase 3, and Phase 4). Recruitment status utilized labels that are reported in the Results, namely Completed, Terminated, Active, not recruiting, Withdrawn, and Unknown when present. Intervention types followed registry labels (Drug, Biological, Procedure, Radiation, Other); pharmacologic interventions refer to the registry‐defined Drug and Biological categories.

Sponsor classification, region mapping, and analytic summaries. Lead sponsors were assigned to Industry or Academic/Other via rule‐based parsing of the sponsor name. Trial locations were mapped to the United States and Canada, Europe, East Asia, Multinational, and Rest of world using country keywords in the registry entry. Trials were flagged biomarker‐specified when any registry text contained PD‐L1, PD‐1, CTLA‐4, FGFR, or FGFR3; trials were flagged AI‐enabled when the text contained artificial intelligence, machine learning, deep learning, radiomics, digital pathology, computer vision, or neural network. Descriptive summaries report annual counts, phase distribution with recruitment status, and intervention patterns, with temporal changes shown across two predefined study periods (2005–2014 and 2015–2024) and additional stratification by sponsor classification and region.

The overall status of UC clinical trials from 2005 to 2024 indicates a steady annual increase in the number of clinical trials, with a particularly significant growth observed after 2016. Specifically, from 2005 to 2014, the number of clinical trials remained relatively stable, with no major fluctuations. However, since 2015, the number of clinical trials has steadily risen, reaching a notable peak in 2018 (Figure 1A). We analyzed the clinical trial data from 2005 to 2014 (Figure 1B) and found that Phase 1 and Phase 2 trials dominated the research landscape. In Phase 1, there were 27 clinical trials, of which 18 (66.7%) were completed, 4 (14.8%) were terminated, and 2 (7.4%) were active but not recruiting. These results indicate that Phase 1 trials were primarily focused on safety assessment and preliminary efficacy verification, with most trials proceeding successfully. Nevertheless, the 14.8% termination rate highlights certain risks and challenges early in the trial phase, which may be related to the preliminary effects or safety concerns of the drugs. A further analysis of Phase 2 trials revealed a total of 96 studies, with 63 (65.6%) completed, 19 (19.8%) terminated, 2 (2.1%) active but not recruiting, 7 (7.3%) in an unknown status, and 5 (5.2%) withdrawn. Phase 2 trials are primarily focused on efficacy validation and monitoring side effects. While the majority of trials (65.6%) were completed successfully, the 19.8% termination rate suggests that there are challenges related to drug efficacy, clinical outcomes, or safety concerns. Overall, the dominance of Phase 1 and Phase 2 trials highlights the strong emphasis on safety and early efficacy evaluations. As trials progressed to later stages, especially Phase 3 and Phase 4, the number of studies decreased, indicating challenges in transitioning from clinical validation to long‐term safety monitoring.

We further analyzed the clinical trial data from 2015 to 2024, which revealed that in Phase 2, most trials were completed successfully (55.7%), but a significant proportion were terminated (23.8%). This suggests that while most therapeutic regimens progress to the next stage, there are still challenges in efficacy evaluation and monitoring side effects, particularly in ensuring efficacy while effectively controlling adverse effects. Compared to the previous decade, the number of Phase 3 trials has increased. This trend indicates a growing emphasis by developers and researchers on large‐scale clinical validation before new treatments reach the market.

Data from clinical trials conducted between 2005 and 2014 reveals a clear pattern in intervention types. As shown in Figure 1D, the top 5 intervention categories, ranked from largest to smallest, were: drug (59.8%), other (15.6%), procedure (12.4%), biological (8.5%), and radiation (3.7%). It is evident that drug interventions made up the majority of clinical trials. Moreover, to better understand the drug interventions, Figure 1E provides a detailed breakdown, highlighting the top ten therapeutic drugs. The top three drugs were Gemcitabine (21.9%), Cisplatin (19.0%), and Paclitaxel (15.2%), indicating that these three drugs remained commonly used for UC treatment during this period.

In contrast, clinical trials from 2015 to 2024 show some significant changes in intervention types. As shown in Figure 1F, the top 5 intervention categories for this period were: drug (69.6%), procedure (11.7%), biological (11.7%), other (4.8%), and radiation (2.2%). Drug interventions continued to dominate, with an increased proportion compared to earlier years, further emphasizing the central role of drug therapy in clinical trials. Figure 1G displays a detailed breakdown of drug interventions, with pembrolizumab (19.2%) becoming the most commonly used intervention, followed by Cisplatin (14.8%) and Gemcitabine (12.5%). This trend suggests that immunotherapy is gradually gaining ground in clinical research, with Pembrolizumab progressively replacing traditional chemotherapy drugs like Gemcitabine. This change reflects a shift in UC treatment toward more targeted and personalized therapies. Compared to conventional chemotherapy, immunotherapy may offer breakthroughs in treatment outcomes, marking an evolution in the UC treatment landscape and guiding future therapeutic trends.

Beyond trial counts, phases, and intervention profiles shown in Figure 1, we examined sponsor type, geographic footprint, and the uptake of biomarker‐specified and AI‐enabled designs (Figure 2). Sponsor composition shifted toward industry leadership in 2015–2024, with a larger share of industry‐led studies and a corresponding contraction in academic or other sponsors (Figure 2A). The geographic distribution broadened over time (Figure 2B): the United States and Canada remained the largest contributors, but the later period featured more multinational trials and greater participation from East Asia, whereas Europe declined in relative terms and the rest of the world remained small. Biomarker‐specified designs increased markedly across periods, rising from a low baseline in 2005–2014 to roughly one quarter of trials in 2015–2024 (Figure 2C). By contrast, AI‐enabled descriptors remained uncommon. Year‐by‐year series reinforce these patterns: biomarker‐specified trials rose sharply after 2015, peaking around 2019–2020, with proportion curves that track changes in the annual volume of registered trials (Figure 2D). AI‐enabled trials appeared only sporadically at very low counts, yielding near‐zero annual proportions (Figure 2E). Taken together, greater industry involvement, broader multinational execution, and increased use of biomarker specification are consistent with the therapeutic transition highlighted in Figure 1.

Our study found that in the past decade, many novel drugs have gradually replaced traditional chemotherapy agents. PD‐L1/PD‐L1 inhibitors, such as pembrolizumab, nivolumab, and atezolizumab, are representative of these new drugs [3, 4, 5, 6]. For metastatic UC that has failed platinum‐based chemotherapy, the use of immune checkpoint inhibitors targeting the PD‐1/PD‐L1 pathway has shown promising efficacy. Prior to this, there were no standard therapies for patients who failed platinum‐based chemotherapy. However, these emerging immune checkpoint inhibitors are rapidly becoming the standard of care in the current treatment landscape.

While our study provides a comprehensive overview of the clinical trial landscape for UC and highlights significant trends in therapeutic strategies, several limitations must be acknowledged. Firstly, our study deliberately excluded non‐interventional studies, such as observational cohorts and real‐world data analyses, to focus specifically on interventional clinical trials registered on ClinicalTrials.gov. This approach allowed us to concentrate on the evaluation of novel therapeutic interventions and their potential to become standards of care. However, it also limits our ability to capture the full spectrum of treatment approaches, including those that may offer complementary or alternative benefits. Non‐interventional studies, particularly those leveraging real‐world data, can provide valuable insights into the long‐term outcomes and safety profiles of treatments in diverse patient populations, which are often not fully captured in interventional trials. Additionally, the reliance on data from ClinicalTrials.gov may introduce biases related to the reporting and registration practices of different regions and institutions, potentially affecting the representativeness of our findings.

Moreover, despite the promising efficacy of immune checkpoint inhibitors observed in clinical trials, their long‐term safety and efficacy profiles are still evolving. The relatively high termination rates observed in Phase 1 and Phase 2 trials underscore the challenges in translating early‐phase successes into clinically viable treatments. These challenges may stem from unforeseen adverse effects, suboptimal efficacy in broader patient populations, or logistical issues in trial conduct. Furthermore, the increasing dominance of drug‐based interventions, particularly immunotherapies, may reflect a research bias toward pharmaceutical solutions, potentially overshadowing the development of other therapeutic modalities such as surgical innovations or multimodal approaches.

In addition to the modality trends summarized above, our registry‐based analysis identified structural shifts in the UC trial portfolio that help explain how immuno‐oncology moved from signal to standard. First, the sponsor composition changed, with a higher share of industry‐led trials in 2015–2024 and corresponding growth of multinational networks with increased participation from East Asia (Figure 2A,B). These patterns are consistent with the scale and tempo required for recent practice‐changing phase 3 programs, including avelumab first‐line maintenance after platinum and enfortumab vedotin plus pembrolizumab as superior to chemotherapy in the untreated metastatic setting, both of which demonstrated overall survival benefit and have reshaped first‐line management [7, 8], and are reflected in contemporary guidance [9].

Second, biomarker‐specified language in registrations increased markedly across periods (Figure 2C,D), aligning with the emergence of FGFR‐directed therapy as a validated precision pathway. Erdafitinib showed clinically meaningful activity in FGFR‐altered disease and improved overall survival versus chemotherapy after prior PD‐1/PD‐L1 therapy in the phase 3 THOR trial, reinforcing the role of genomic selection alongside checkpoint blockade [10, 11]. By contrast, AI‐enabled descriptors remained uncommon in registry text (Figure 2C–E) despite growing research interest in radiomics and digital pathology. This pattern suggests that computational methods are often deployed downstream in analysis pipelines rather than prespecified in protocols; clearer pre‐registration of such endpoints would improve transparency and reproducibility [12]. Taken together, greater industry involvement, broader multinational execution, and increased biomarker specification provide context for the rapid consolidation of immunotherapy‐ and antibody–drug conjugate‐based standards in advanced UC (avelumab maintenance; enfortumab vedotin plus pembrolizumab in the first‐line setting), now reflected in contemporary guidance [7, 8, 9].

Future progress in UC therapeutics will require trial designs that operationalize precision at scale. Priorities are to preregister biomarker strategies and explicitly distinguish eligibility enrichment from stratification, to broaden and diversify multinational accrual so that results generalize, and to conduct adequately powered, long‐term studies that test emerging regimens against contemporary standards. The consolidation of immunotherapy and antibody–drug conjugate combinations in the first‐line setting and maintenance strategies after platinum provides robust comparators for upcoming trials. In parallel, FGFR‐directed approaches for genomically defined disease should be prospectively integrated where indicated. As computational methods diffuse into imaging and pathology workflows, prespecified analytic endpoints and performance metrics at registration will improve transparency and reproducibility while keeping protocol documentation concise.

Our trial registry‐based analysis delineates the therapeutic shift toward immunotherapy and concurrent structural changes in sponsorship and geographic footprint, alongside greater use of biomarker specification, while explicit AI terminology remains uncommon. These observations, together with recognized limitations of registry text and the need for long‐term evidence, support a practical agenda: precise and transparent protocol registration, inclusive multinational execution, and rigorous comparative evaluation of rational combinations and targeted strategies. Meeting these goals should accelerate the translation of innovation into durable clinical benefit for patients with urothelial carcinoma.
