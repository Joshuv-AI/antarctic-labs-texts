Protocol for inducing cutaneous squamous cell carcinoma with chronic UV and treating with 5-aminolevulinic acid photodynamic therapy in SKH-1 mice
Authors: Wenbo Zhou, Keni Chang, Zhijie Ruan, Lunhui Chang, Xiaoying Gao, Xuan Liu, Guorong Yan, Xiuli Wang, Guolong Zhang, Qingyu Zeng
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
SummaryHere, we present a protocol for establishing cutaneous squamous cell carcinoma (cSCC) in SKH-1 hairless mice using a chronic ultraviolet irradiation regimen, followed by treatment with topical 5-aminolevulinic acid photodynamic therapy (ALA-PDT). We describe steps for a 28-week UV induction schedule, standardized preparation and application of ALA cream, and precise parameters for light irradiation. The model recapitulates key stages of human photocarcinogenesis, providing a physiologically relevant system for investigating cSCC pathogenesis and assessing PDT efficacy in vivo.For complete details on the use and execution of this protocol, please refer to Chen et al.1Subject areas: Cancer, Clinical Protocol, Model Organisms

Before you begin

Non-melanoma skin cancer ranks first in global incidence, with cSCC being the second most common subtype and a leading cause of mortality in this cancer category.2,3,4 Photodynamic therapy (PDT), with its advantages of minimal invasiveness, high lesion selectivity, low damage to surrounding tissues, and repeatability, has become an important non-surgical treatment option for cSCC.1,5 To enable physiologically relevant evaluation of PDT, this protocol is based on a chronic UV-induced SKH-1 mice model of cSCC. The model recapitulates the multistage progression from actinic keratosis to invasive carcinoma and closely reproduces key genetic (e.g., TP53, NOTCH) and epigenetic (e.g., DNA methylation) features of human cSCC. In contrast to chemically induced systems, it avoids obligate Ras-dependent signaling and marked inflammatory responses characteristic of chemical carcinogenesis, providing a translationally relevant platform for studying topical therapies such as ALA-PDT.6,7,8 Here we detail a reproducible, standardized procedure for performing ALA-PDT in this mouse model, offering a practical methodological reference for PDT application in cSCC research.

Innovation

This protocol details a standardized pipeline for establishing chronic UV-induced cSCC in SKH-1 mice and applying ALA-PDT, with key refinements that enhance physiological relevance, reproducibility, and translational utility. Its specific innovations include:

Employing chronic UV irradiation rather than chemical carcinogens, this approach recapitulates human photocarcinogenesis progression while avoiding obligate Ras-driven pathways and excessive inflammation.

Introducing a systematic minimal erythema dose (MED)-based escalation scheme that starts sub-erythemal and reaches a stable, chronically tolerated dose (1.625 MED), ensuring consistent tumor induction with minimal acute phototoxicity.

Defining optimized ALA-PDT parameters—including ALA cream pH (3.0–4.0), standardized skin pretreatment, a 3-h incubation, and precise light delivery (630 nm, 80–100 mW/cm2, 35–40 J/cm2)—to enhance treatment reproducibility.

Integrating clear tumor-monitoring criteria and volume-based humane endpoints (50–100 mm3), facilitating ethical, consistent lesion selection for therapeutic studies.

CRITICAL: Closely monitor mice for significant signs of photodamage, such as severe erythema or ulceration, during the irradiation period. If such symptoms are observed, consider pausing irradiation for a few days until recovery before resuming the schedule.

Note: Based on systematic dose-ranging pilot studies from our group, this irradiation protocol begins at a sub-erythemal dose and escalates progressively. This graded increase allows the skin to adapt gradually to UV stress, prevents acute phototoxicity from interfering with long-term carcinogenesis, and mimics the cumulative, progressive nature of human sun exposure. After 8 weeks, the dose is fixed at 1.625 MED. Pilot data defined this as the maximum chronically tolerated dose—it sustains mild but visible erythema, indicating continued biological activity, without causing significant weight loss, morbidity, or severe ulceration. Consequently, by applying a stable and tolerable irradiation level after escalation, the protocol maintains effective oncogenic pressure to drive tumor progression while minimizing severe photodamage.

Pause point: UV irradiation should be discontinued once tumors meet the histopathological criteria for cSCC, as they will continue to progress in size and severity over time.

CRITICAL: Euthanize animals immediately if the total tumor volume reaches that of a single 1.2 cm diameter lesion, or if ulceration, bleeding, or other signs of severe welfare compromise are observed.10 This threshold strictly follows the “early intervention” principle of animal welfare guidelines and accounts for the ulceration-prone nature of this cutaneous SCC model, ensuring action is taken before significant distress occurs.10 Perform post-mortem histopathological analysis on all tumors meeting these endpoints for final diagnosis.

Note: This protocol defines a tumor volume range of 50–100 mm3 for study inclusion. The lower threshold of 50 mm3 ensures selected tumors are clearly invasive. Prior evidence in this model shows that lesions ≥3 mm in diameter are predominantly squamous cell carcinomas,11 and lesions >4 mm often progress to invasive carcinoma.12 More recently, a systematic histopathological analysis in this model demonstrated that lesions with a volume ≥46.8 mm3 can be classified as cancer with 100% positive predictive value, providing direct pathology-based support for selecting 50 mm3 as the lower inclusion threshold.13 The upper limit of 100 mm3 is set to guarantee that tumors are sufficiently developed at baseline, exhibit consistent biological behavior, and are of a size amenable to standardized measurement, topical drug application, and light irradiation. This limit also prevents tumors from advancing too rapidly before treatment, avoiding ethical issues during animal welfare assessment.

Expected outcomes

UV-induced cSCC model

Upon initiating chronic UV irradiation, SKH-1 mice develop mild erythema within 48 hours, followed by skin thickening and edema. Continued exposure leads to progressive photoaging, with coarse wrinkles and hyperpigmentation typically evident by week 8. Approximately 30% of mice in our representative data develop actinic keratosis (AK) lesions from week 12 onward, visible as pinpoint to millet-sized papules. With cumulative UV exposure, these AK lesions progress sequentially. By week 28, most animals develop histologically confirmed cSCC, presenting as exophytic or ulcerated tumors, indicating successful model establishment (Figure 3B). The incidence of AK and cSCC at sequential time points is summarized in Table 2. Well-differentiated cSCC in this model is histologically characterized by keratin pearl formation (Figure 3D). Note that the timing of lesion onset and progression may vary between studies due to differences in experimental conditions and inter-individual variability. Tumor burden, including number and volume, also differs among animals and cannot be precisely predicted.

ALA-PDT treatment

Following ALA-PDT, assess the therapeutic response by measuring tumor size and documenting gross morphology. Treatment typically induces partial remission, characterized by tumor shrinkage and dark scab formation within days. Representative gross images show tumor morphology before treatment and 24 h after a single ALA-PDT session (Figure 4A). Histologically, H&E staining reveals residual epithelial disorganization and pyknosis in the superficial layers, interstitial vascular dilation with erythrocyte stasis, and extensive coagulative necrosis of tumor cells (Figure 4B). Ki67 immunohistochemistry shows fewer Ki67-positive cells in the PDT-treated group than in the untreated control group, and quantification confirms a significant reduction in the Ki67-positive cell ratio after treatment (Figure 4B), consistent with suppressed tumor cell proliferation. In this model, complete remission—defined as the absence of a palpable tumor—is rarely achieved with PDT monotherapy; lesions often stabilize or regrow after the initial response. These findings indicate that combining PDT with other modalities, such as immunotherapy, may improve durable response rates and represents a promising direction for further investigation in cSCC.1,15

Limitations

The extended duration of approximately 28 weeks required for tumor induction via chronic UV irradiation represents a major practical constraint. Phototoxic responses during irradiation necessitate careful monitoring and can introduce variability in progression timelines across individual animals. Furthermore, the reproducibility of the photo-carcinogenesis phase can be affected by variations in animal housing conditions and by operator-dependent factors during MED determination. The induced tumors are confined to the dorsal skin and naturally vary in size and differentiation, which must be considered during analysis. Lastly, the efficacy of the photodynamic therapy is dependent on the consistent preparation and stability of the ALA formulation, which constitutes a potential source of variability in treatment outcomes.

Troubleshooting

Problem 1

Severe phototoxicity observed during UV irradiation (e.g., intense erythema, ulceration, or crusting). (Related to Step 2 in “Chronic UV Irradiation for cSCC Induction”).

Potential solution

•This indicates excessive photodamage. Suspend UV irradiation for 3–5 days to allow for recovery. Upon resuming irradiation, do not increase the exposure time for the following week. Ensure accurate UV power measurement, as unexpectedly high output is a common cause.

Problem 2

Failure to form tumors after 16 weeks of UV irradiation. (Related to Steps 2 and 3 in “Chronic UV Irradiation for cSCC Induction”).

Potential solution

•Regularly verify the stability of the UV lamp output. Place each mouse individually to avoid mutual shielding and uneven irradiation. Strictly adhere to the percentage-based minimal erythema dose escalation scheme outlined in Table 1. If tumor induction remains suboptimal, the irradiation time per session may be slightly extended (e.g., by an additional 2–3 seconds per week) under close monitoring for phototoxicity. Clean the UV filters every two weeks and record the cumulative irradiation dose for each animal.

Problem 3

Mice show signs of awakening or pain during PDT (e.g., limb movement). (Related to Step 6 in “Topical ALA Application and PDT”).

Potential solution

•Check the isoflurane concentration and oxygen flow rate. Briefly increase the isoflurane concentration to 2% to rapidly re-establish a surgical plane of anesthesia. Ensure a proper seal of the nose cone. It is recommended to check the anesthesia equipment before each treatment session and to continuously monitor respiratory status throughout the procedure.
