Addressing connectomic constraints in early practice of awake-guided neurosurgery: a four-year prospective case-matched analysis
Authors: Sam Ng, Andrea Otamendi-Lopez, Basile Martin, Kevin Chalard, Vivien Szabo, Sylvie Moritz-Gasser, Guillaume Herbet, Hugues Duffau
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractPurposeAwake functional-guided resection (AFGR) is the gold-standard approach in glioma surgery. The learning curve associated with mastering white matter tracts (WMT) identification using direct electrostimulation (DES) mapping remains poorly characterized. The goal of this study was to compare subcortical DES mapping performance between early-career and expert neurosurgeons.MethodsIn this prospective, monocentric, case-matched study, patients undergoing AFGR (2022–2025) were operated on either by an early-career neurosurgeon during the first four years of independent practice (cohortA) or by a senior expert neurosurgeon with extensive experience in awake mapping procedures (cohortB). DES–elicited responses were compared using multivariable multinomial regression, adjusting for histology and anatomical location. WMT identification rates were analyzed using a benchmark-normalized Relative Detection Efficiency (RDE).ResultsOverall, 352 AFGR procedures were analyzed, including 101 in cohortA. Histology differed between cohorts, with fewer IDH-mutant grade 2 gliomas (46.5% vs 65.3%) and more glioblastomas (27.7% vs 6.4%) in cohortA. Postoperative tumor volume and functional outcomes were comparable. A total of 1,158 DES responses were recorded. Negative motor responses were more frequent in cohortA (+ 11.3%[95%CI:3.3–18.9], P = 0.006), whereas semantic paraphasia tended to be less frequent (− 15.4%[95%CI: − 32.3–0.6], P = 0.079). Identification of the inferior fronto-occipital fasciculus was reduced in cohortA, particularly in left frontal approaches (RDE:0.53[95%CI:0.31–0.81], P = 0.002), consistent with comparable overall extents of resection (EOR) except in frontal tumors.ConclusionBenchmarking early-career practice of AFGR against expert practice reveals selective, tract- and functional system–specific differences in DES mapping strategies, with minimal impact on EOR and similar functional outcomes. These findings suggest that AFGR is reliably implementable during early independent practice and support a dynamic connectomic constraint framework to guide training.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at https://doi.org/10.1007/s00701-026-06978-1.Keywords: Awake surgery, Glioma, Neurosurgical training, Functional mapping, Direct electrostimulation

Introduction

Awake functional-guided resection (AFGR) is considered the gold-standard neurosurgical strategy for optimizing the balance between extent of resection (EOR) and functional preservation in glioma surgery [4, 12], particularly in isocitrate dehydrogenase mutant (IDHm) lower-grade gliomas [11]. By relying on intraoperative direct electrical stimulation (DES) and real-time neuropsychological monitoring, AFGR allows individualized tailoring of resection boundaries based on functional networks rather than anatomical landmarks [6]. Despite this paradigm, substantial heterogeneities persist across neurosurgical centers [10]. Many teams continue to rely on “structural-guided” strategies derived from preoperative or intraoperative imaging modalities—such as neuronavigation, intraoperative magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) [14]—or tumor-oriented markers including Raman spectroscopy [30] or fluorescence-guided techniques [27]. While these technologies provide valuable complementary information to assist resections, several groups have demonstrated that a purely “functional-guided” approach, independent of imaging assistance, can achieve superior functional preservation [24], including cognitive outcomes [17], and quality of life [22], while maintaining benchmark oncological results. However, a major technical challenge of this functional philosophy lies in its reliance on continuous, multimodal, and flexible intraoperative monitoring, particularly when approaching deep white matter pathways [9]. Although awake cortical mapping is well described and reproducible across centers [19], axonal and subcortical mapping remain considerably more challenging [31]. Awake surgical approaches purely grounded in intraoperative functional responses require accurate anticipation of critical white matter tracts coursing within or adjacent to tumor tissue, together with a solid understanding of connectomic constraints [7], while also accounting for the practical limitations inherent to the tripartite interaction among the patient, the speech therapist/neuropsychologist, and the surgeon within the time-constrained environment of the operating theater. The learning curve associated with mastering subcortical DES functional mapping has rarely been systematically investigated. Available reports are limited and largely describe early experiences in awake glioma surgery [20, 21], without dedicated analyses of subcortical stimulation responses or user-dependent patterns in white matter tract identification. This gap may partially explain why many centers continue to favor structurally guided approaches, which are more readily implementable, less time-consuming, and more reliant on technological adjuncts, despite their inherent limitations when compared with AFGR [1, 2].

In this prospective, case-matched study conducted over four years at a single center, we compared the practices of two neurosurgeons—one in the early phase of independent practice following a specialized residency program, and the other an established expert in the field. Our primary objective was to quantify white matter tract–specific detection performance during early independent practice using a benchmark-based comparative framework. We further aimed to propose a pedagogical framework illustrating dynamic connectomic constraints, with the goal of facilitating learning curve progression and informing training strategies in AFGR.

Methods

Study design and participants

In this prospective descriptive study, patients were screened for inclusion according to the following criteria: surgery performed at the Montpellier University Medical Center (France), by either an early-career neurosurgeon (S.N.), or a senior neurosurgeon (H.D.) between January 2022 and December 2025. Eligible patients were required to undergo surgery using AFGR. All histological and molecular subtypes of brain lesions were included. It should be noted that data were collected in a routine clinical setting, and the protocol described below reflects our standard clinical practice. The study was approved by the Institutional Review Board of Montpellier University Medical Center (No. 202000557). All patients provided written informed consent to the intervention in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki. Patients operated on by the early independent practitioner constituted cohortA, whereas those operated on by the senior surgeon constituted cohortB.

Training and pre-requisite (CohortA)

The early-career neurosurgeon held the position of Assistant–Chef de Clinique and was considered an independent operator for selected cases in which a fully autonomous surgical approach was deemed appropriate. He completed a dedicated residency training program in the same center (2018–2022) with enfolded fellowship on white matter functional anatomy, including participation in approximately 150 procedures either as first surgical assistant or as a member of the neuropsychologist/speech therapist team involved in the analysis of intraoperative monitoring.

Interventions

Details on the training of the early-career neurosurgeon and the AFGR approach used in both cohorts are provided in the Supplemental Methods. Importantly, resection boundaries were systematically determined by intraoperative findings, without the use of adjunctive imaging-guided technologies. The same device was used for intraoperative DES application (Nimbus, Hemodia, 60 Hz, bipolar probe). The same multidisciplinary team—including anesthesiologists, neuropsychologists, and speech therapists—was involved in CohortA and CohortB.

Measures

Clinical and radiological data

The following variables were selected for analysis: age, sex, tumor location, histomolecular characteristics (WHO 2021 classification) [18], preoperative and 3-month postoperative tumor volumes based on MRI (including fluid-attenuated inversion recovery [FLAIR] and T1 gadolinium-enhanced sequences), obtained through manual segmentation using Horos (horosproject.org), extent of resection (EOR), neurological status, professional activity and ability to maintain professional engagement, and surgery-related complications. On FLAIR imaging, EOR was classified as supratotal (complete FLAIR resection with additional non-infiltrated tissue resection based on preoperative MRI), total (no residual FLAIR abnormality), subtotal (FLAIR residue < 10 mL), or partial (FLAIR residue ≥ 10 mL). On T1-weighted contrast-enhanced imaging, when applicable, EOR was classified as supratotal (no residual enhancement with resection beyond the enhancing margin), total (no residual enhancement), or non-total (residual or suspected enhancement).

Intraoperative functional responses

To characterize detection performance and further analyze subcortical responses, subcortical stimulation sites were identified intraoperatively and labeled with dedicated numerical tags. The speech therapist and neuropsychologist were systematically blinded to the timing and parameters of direct electrical stimulation (DES) application. Functional responses were considered valid only if they were reproducibly elicited in at least two of three stimulations in a non-consecutive manner. To minimize false-positive responses during intraoperative testing, all patients underwent preoperative neuropsychological evaluation using the same task battery planned for intraoperative mapping. Tasks or materials that could not be performed reliably preoperatively were excluded from intraoperative assessment. Tag locations and corresponding anatomical positions within the white matter were documented using intraoperative photographs.

The following intraoperative functional responses were selected for analysis, in accordance with previous reports in the field [25, 29]: speech arrest, positive motor responses (PMR), negative motor responses (NMR), stuttering, perseveration, anomia, semantic paraphasia, nonverbal semantic disturbances, phonological paraphasia, articulatory disorders, face-based emotion recognition disturbances, behavioral disturbances, visual field deficit and dysesthesia.

White matter characterization adjudication

White matter tract (WMT) identification was based exclusively on the surgeon’s intraoperative judgment and was included in the analysis only when explicitly documented in the operative report. Tract adjudication relied on direct anatomo-functional concordance between the location of the stimulation probe and the functional response elicited by DES. This determination was guided by established anatomical landmarks (e.g. stimulation beneath the inferior temporal sulcus for the inferior longitudinal fasciculus [ILF], or beneath the inferior circular sulcus of the insula and over the roof of the temporal horn of the lateral ventricle for the inferior fronto-occipital fasciculus [IFOF]). Diffusion tractography and neuronavigation tools were not used during surgery. Representative examples of anatomo-functional concordance are summarized in Table 1.

Statistical analysis

Clinical data comparisons

Continuous clinical variables were expressed as means ± standard deviation, and compared with the Wilcoxon nonparametric test. Categorical variables were expressed as counts and percentages and compared using the Fisher’s exact test.

Functional responses analysis

Differences in subcortical functional responses between cohorts were analyzed using multivariable multinomial logistic regression, with functional response type as a categorical outcome (the following functional responses were selected due to high iterations across both cohorts: NMR, phonological paraphasia, anomia, semantic paraphasia, perseveration, and anarthria) and cohort (A vs B) as the primary predictor. Models were adjusted for tumor laterality, histopathological grade, and anatomical location (frontal, parietal, temporal, insular, or central). Because multiple DES responses could be recorded within the same patient, inference was based on patient-clustered robust covariance estimators to account for within-patient correlation. Results were presented as adjusted predicted probabilities for each response by cohort, obtained through marginal standardization by computing counterfactual predictions under cohortA and cohortB while holding all other covariates at their observed values and averaging across the study population. Absolute adjusted differences with 95% confidence intervals (CI) were derived using simulation-based inference from the asymptotic multivariate normal distribution of the clustered parameter estimates. Model assumptions included mutually exclusive outcome categories, correct specification of the link function, and absence of problematic multicollinearity. Multicollinearity was assessed through inspection of the design matrices and evaluation of variance inflation factors. Adequate covariate overlap between cohorts was also verified.

Tract-level detection analysis

Detection of major white matter tracts, as adjudicated by the treating neurosurgeon, was treated as a binary outcome (identified vs not identified, in each patient) and modeled using binomial distributions within each cohort. DES identification rates were calculated as the proportion of resections in which each subcortical structure was identified. To constrain analyses to anatomically coherent regions, analyses were performed separately for frontal, temporal, insular, and parietal surgical approaches. To quantify detection performance in cohortA relative to the benchmark cohort (cohortB), we defined a Relative Detection Efficiency (RDE) as the ratio of identification probabilities between cohorts, computed as the detection rate in cohortA divided by that in the reference cohortB. Thus, an RDE reflects benchmark-normalized detection performance, with a value of 1 indicating equivalence to benchmark conditions. Uncertainty around detection rates for each cohort was estimated using binomial models. Posterior distributions for detection probabilities in each cohort were approximated using independent beta distributions parameterized as Beta(x + 1, n − x + 1), where x is the number of identifications and n the total number of resections. At each iteration, detection probabilities were sampled from these distributions, the RDE was recalculated, and this process was repeated 10,000 times. Statistical significance of differences in identification rates between cohorts was assessed using Fisher’s exact test.

All tests were two-sided, and a p-value < 0.05 was considered statistically significant. All statistical analyses were performed using R software (4.5.1, 2025, The R Foundation for Statistical Computing, Vienna, Austria).

Results

Clinical and oncological results

Overall, 352 AFGR procedures were analyzed, including 101 in cohortA. Clinical, radiological, technical and histopathological characteristics of the two cohorts are presented in Table 2. Tumor histopathological distribution differed substantially between cohortA and cohortB, with a lower proportion of IDHm grade 2 gliomas (47.5% vs 65.3%, P = 0.003) and a higher proportion of IDH-wild-type glioblastomas (27.7% vs 6.4%, P < 0.0001) in cohortA. Anatomical tumor distribution also differed between cohorts, with fewer insular tumors in cohortA compared with cohortB (12.9% vs 25.1%, P = 0.014). Despite these key differences, which could be expected to influence surgical complexity, preoperative tumor volume and postoperative residual tumor volume were comparable between the two cohorts, together with main early and late clinical outcomes. Of note, the proportions of tumors involving in the left perisylvian areas and the left and right perirolandic areas was similar between the two cohorts (43.6% vs 43.8%, P > 0.999; 4.0% vs 2.0%, P = 0.285; 3.0% vs 2.4%, P = 0.720, respectively). Overall, the EOR was similar between cohortA and cohortB, except for frontal tumors, where cohortB showed a higher proportion of cases with EOR > 95% (19/39 vs 63/92, respectively, P = 0.033, Table 3).

Intraoperative findings

Functional responses analyses

A total of 1,158 subcortical DES responses were recorded (349 in cohortA, 824 in cohortB). After adjustment for tumor laterality, histopathological grade, and anatomical location, multinomial modeling revealed significant differences in functional response profiles between cohorts (Global Wald test, P = 4.6 × 10⁻⁷1, see Table 4). NMR were more frequent in cohortA than in cohortB (adjusted probability 35.1% [95%CI, 30.7–39.6] vs 23.8% [95%CI, 20.5–27.1]; adjusted difference + 11.3% [95%CI, 3.3–18.9], P = 0.006). In contrast, semantic paraphasia was less frequent in cohortA (7.0% [95%CI, 5.0–9.1]) than in cohortB (22.4% [95%CI, 19.0–25.9]), with a large adjusted reduction that approached statistical significance (adjusted difference − 15.4% [95%CI, − 32.3–0.6], P = 0.079). No significant adjusted differences were observed for phonological paraphasia, anomia, or perseveration.

Across approaches, reduced detection efficiency in cohortA was most consistently observed for the IFOF, with significantly lower RDE values identified in frontal, temporal, and insular approaches (RDE range 0.52–0.58; all P ≤ 0.036). Decreased identification efficiency was also observed for the arcuate fasciculus in insular approaches (RDE:0.36; P = 0.004). Illustrative cases

To illustrate (1) variability in functional responses, (2) variability in major WMT detection, and (3) key steps of the learning curve during early experience with awake mapping, we selected six clinical vignettes highlighting both inappropriate and appropriate intraoperative DES mapping workflows (Fig. 1). Across these frontal and fronto-temporo-insular cases, surgical strategies underscored the critical role of dynamic connectomic constraints in achieving higher rates of WMT identification, particularly of the IFOF. Inadequate sequencing or management of intraoperative functional responses was associated with reduced mapping reliability, as early engagement of systems such as fronto-striatal fibers frequently interfered with the reproducibility and fine-grained mapping of more flexible cognitive networks, including semantic processing mediated by IFOF branches and speech production involving the superior longitudinal fasciculus/arcuate fasciculus (SLF/AF) complex.

Discussion

In this prospective, case-matched study, we investigated subcortical DES mapping performance during AFGR by comparing an early independent practitioner with a senior expert, using DES-induced functional responses and tract-specific detection efficiency as a benchmarked outcome. Beyond quantitative differences in WMT identification, our findings reveal systematic patterns in intraoperative functional responses and tract detection that are not fully explained by anatomical complexity or tumor characteristics alone. Instead, they point toward the existence of dynamic connectomic constraints—interaction-dependent limitations emerging during surgery that shape the feasibility, reproducibility, and sequencing of subcortical functional mapping.

AFGR has progressively shifted neurosurgical practice away from static anatomical boundaries toward individualized functional limits, enabling superior functional preservation while maintaining and optimizing onco-surgical efficacy (e.g. by allowing earlier preventive surgical attitude [16] and “supramarginal” resections [23]). This paradigm is grounded in the recognition that cognitive functions are supported by distributed, plastic networks rather than localized cortical epicenters [5]. However, while cortical mapping principles are now well codified [8, 28], subcortical mapping remains less standardized. Our results demonstrate that early independent practice is associated with selective reductions in detection efficiency for specific WMTs—most notably the IFOF. Importantly, these differences were not global but tract- and context-specific, suggesting that they arise not from insufficient technical execution per se, but from how functional systems are sequentially engaged and constrained during surgery.

Our functional response analyses further support this hierarchical organization. NMR, which are frequently associated with disruption of the FST or supplementary motor area–related circuits, were significantly more frequent in the early-practice cohort. In contrast, semantic paraphasias—hallmark responses of IFOF stimulations [13]—were significantly less frequent. This dissociation suggests that early activation of the negative motor network [26] may impose strong top-down constraints on cognitive performance, limiting the patient’s ability to sustain complex, flexible tasks required for semantic or higher-order cognitive mapping [15]. One of the most striking insights from both quantitative analyses and illustrative cases is the central role of surgical sequencing: cases in which distributed cognitive systems (e.g., IFOF, SLF/AF) were approached early—before extensive engagement of fronto-striatal or deep motor systems—were associated with higher-quality, reproducible responses and improved tract detection. Conversely, workflows that prioritized deep or segregated systems early often led to cascading functional instability, reinforcing the idea that surgical sequencing may be a determinant of mapping success.

Based on these observations, we define dynamic connectomic constraints as intraoperative limitations on functional mapping that emerge from the real-time interaction between (1) distributed neural systems, (2) task demands, (3) patient cognitive state, and (4) surgical sequencing. These constraints are not fixed anatomical barriers; rather, they evolve throughout resection as different networks are perturbed, recruited, or destabilized. Figure 2 synthesizes this concept by organizing functional systems along a gradient from distributed, flexible networks (e.g., language, semantic cognition, social cognition) to segregated, modality-specific systems (e.g., motor output, somatosensory, visual pathways). Crucially, the model emphasizes that early engagement of rigid or highly constraining systems can compromise subsequent access to more flexible cognitive networks, thereby reducing the reproducibility and interpretability of DES responses.

Importantly, our findings indicate that overall functional and oncological outcomes can be reproduced very early after dedicated training in AFGR. Even during the initial phase of independent practice, early practitioners achieved rates of EOR, postoperative functional preservation, and WMT identification that were largely comparable to those of an experienced surgeon. This observation held across a wide spectrum of tumor locations, including technically demanding cases such as insular gliomas. Together, these results support previous reports suggesting that awake DES-guided surgery is a robust and reproducible strategy that can be implemented reliably soon after specialized training [3], reflecting a relatively rapid and safe learning curve when appropriate multidisciplinary expertise and structured training are in place. Within this overall framework of comparable performance, our findings nevertheless suggest that early-career neurosurgeons may intuitively gravitate toward systems that produce robust, low-ambiguity responses (e.g., motor or visual pathways), while encountering greater difficulty with systems that require sustained cognitive stability and nuanced task performance. In practical terms, this translates into measurable effects on the EOR in specific anatomical contexts. In our cohort, this was illustrated by a lower rate of EOR > 95% in frontal approaches in the early-practice group, likely reflecting reduced ability to manage deep functional constraints, particularly those related to the IFOF in frontal regions. In the absence of an explicit framework emphasizing dynamic constraints and surgical sequencing, such biases may persist despite increasing case volume. By making these constraints explicit, the proposed model offers a strategy grounded in the intrinsic neuro-malleability of subnetworks rather than purely anatomical landmarks, enabling trainees to anticipate when and why mapping reliability deteriorates and, critically, how to prevent it in order to achieve an optimal EOR.

Limitations

Several limitations must be acknowledged. First, tract adjudication relied on surgeon-reported identification without diffusion tractography confirmation. While this approach reflects real-world functional practice and bypass direct limitations of this technology, it introduces subjectivity. Second, cohort differences in tumor histology and location, although adjusted for in analyses, may still contribute residual confounding. Third, this study reflects experience within a single high-volume center with a shared multidisciplinary team. The expert surgeon therefore served as an internal benchmark rather than an external reference. While this design minimized confounding related to institutional practices, operative protocols, and multidisciplinary support, it may limit the generalizability of the findings. Future multicenter studies would help determine whether the observed tract-specific learning patterns are reproducible across different surgical environments.

Conclusions

In summary, this study shows that awake functional-guided resection can be effectively implemented in early independent neurosurgical practice, yielding overall functional and oncological outcomes comparable to those of an experienced surgeon. Early practice was nevertheless associated with selective, tract- and functional system–specific differences in subcortical DES mapping strategies, with a minimal impact on EOR. These findings suggest that the learning curve of awake glioma surgery is driven less by global technical limitations than by system-specific mapping strategies and surgical sequencing. A dynamic connectomic constraint framework may therefore provide a useful basis for guiding training and standardizing functional mapping approaches in awake glioma surgery.
