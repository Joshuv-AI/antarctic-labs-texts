Ultrasound improves the low-sodium salt curing of sea bass: Insights into the effects of ultrasound on texture, microstructure, and flavor characteristics
Authors: Hengli Bai, Laihao Li, Yanyan Wu, Shengjun Chen, Yongqiang Zhao, Qiuxing Cai, Yueqi Wang
Published: 2023

ABSTRACT
Highlights•The role and quality impact of ultrasound-assisted fish curing is not yet clear.•Ultrasonic treatment increased the permeation rate and shortened marination time.•Ultrasound treatment reduced the hardness and chewiness of the fish.•Ultrasound-assisted curing improves the quality characteristics of sea bass.

Introduction

As a common, economically important fish, sea bass is preferred by consumers because of its tender flesh, limited number of spines, and high nutritional value. The primary production areas of sea bass are the coastal areas of Japan, South Korea, and China [1]. Curing is a common method for preserving seafood; however, traditionally curing sea bass has the disadvantages of long processing periods, high-salt content, and uneven salt distribution, which can cause fluctuations in product quality. Previous studies have demonstrated that the long-term intake of excessive salt increases the risk of hypertension and cardiovascular disease [2]. With increasing awareness of healthy eating among consumers, the demand for lightly salted fish products is increasing. Research has confirmed that light salting is beneficial for fish products, as it inhibits the growth of Aeromonas aeruginosa and reduces the production of biogenic amines during storage. Furthermore, low-salt cured fish retains strong elasticity and water-holding capacity after repeated freezing and thawing [3], [4], [5]. However, light-salt curing can pose alternative problems such as increased processing times and a greater likelihood of meat spoilage. Thus, there is an urgent need to reduce the amount of salt used in meat processing while improving the corresponding curing efficiency and ensuring that the quality of meat is not compromised following this process.

In recent years, the meat industry has been committed to improving product quality via the development of new processing methods such as ultrasonic technology, tumbling marination, high-pressure treatment, and pulsed electric-field treatment [6]. Ultrasonication refers to the process of mechanical waves converting electrical energy to acoustic energy at frequencies of up to 20,000 Hz, which is widely used in food processing, such as marination, defrosting, enzyme digestion, and sterilization, because of its high efficiency, low cost, and high safety [7]. In liquid, ultrasonic waves alternate between positive and negative pressure cycle phases of propagation. When strong enough, these sound waves can disrupt the integrity of the liquid under negative pressure; this results in the production of bubbles, which gradually expand with the continuous entry of gas into the liquid. In the positive pressure state, the bubbles contract owing to their surface tension. Therefore, these bubbles constantly expand and contract in the positive and negative pressure phases, respectively. This is commonly referred to as the “sponge effect”; in this process, the volume of gas entering the bubble is always greater than the volume of gas returning to the liquid. After several cycles, the bubble implodes owing to excessive internal pressure, releasing energy. This process ultimately leads to changes in the properties and microstructures of food treated with ultrasonic waves [8], [9].

Ultrasound can accelerate the penetration of NaCl and promote the Maillard reaction, lipid oxidation, and protein degradation, thus improving the texture, color, flavor, and taste of cured meat [8], [10]. Ultrasound has been shown to improve tenderness and enhance water retention by accelerating the degradation of myogenic fibronectin and inducing moderate oxidation of myosin [11], [12]. In addition, the Maillard reaction and lipid degradation can influence flavor compound release [13]. The cavitation effect of ultrasonic waves induces the rupture of water molecules, which generates hydroxyl radicals that accelerate the rate of lipid oxidation, and thus increase the relative levels of aldehydes, alcohols, and ketones [14].

Although ultrasonic-assisted curing technology is widely used in the meat industry, it is primarily used to improve the quality of meat from pigs, poultry, cattle, and sheep. Related research in the field of fish curing technology is limited; therefore, the role and quality impact of ultrasonic assistance in fish processing remain unclear. In this study, we aimed to investigate the effects of different ultrasonic conditions on the quality characteristics of sea bass meat. We specifically evaluated the effects of ultrasonic-assisted curing on texture, microstructure, free amino acids (FAAs), and volatile organic compounds in these fish. Overall, this study could provide a theoretical basis and new suggestions for the development of low-salt curing technologies for fish.

Materials and methods

Material preparation

Sea bass (weighing 400 ± 20 g) were purchased from Zhuhai, China. The live fish were transported to the laboratory and killed via heavy blows to the back of the head. Blood stains were rinsed with running water, after which the fish were scaled and gutted. The surface was wiped clean, the dorsal muscle was dissected, skinned, and cut into pieces (5 × 3 × 1 cm3; 20 ± 0.5 g). The pieces were randomly divided into four groups—three groups were set up as ultrasound-treatment groups (UL100, UL300, and UL500), and the remaining one group without ultrasound treatment was considered the control group (S). The samples were then stored in polyethylene bags at 4 °C until further use in the subsequent experiments.

Experimental methods

Ultrasonic routine performance measurement

The ultrasonic crusher (JY99-IIDN; Shanghai Hu Xi Industrial Co., Ltd.) used in this study had a maximum power of 1800 W, frequency of 20.5 kHz, and VARIO Rod diameter of 22 mm. The acoustic intensity of the sonication process was determined using the calorimetric method reported by Scudino et al. [15]. Briefly, 50 mL of pure water was into a 100 mL beaker; then, a probe was submerged 1 mL in the water and processed at different ultrasonic powers (100, 300, and 500 W) for 3 min. The temperature before and after ultrasonication was accurately read with an electronic thermometer (TP300, Chenzhou Island, Shenzhen), and acoustic power (AP) was calculated using the formula provided below. The determination was repeated three times at each power. Acoustic intensity (AI), power density (PD), specific energy (SE), and energy density (ED) were calculated using the following formulae.(1)AP(W)=mCpdTdt(2)AI(Wcm2)=4×APπD2(3)PD(Wcm3)=APV(4)SE(Jg)=AP×tm(5)ED(Jcm3)=UP×tVwhere, m is the mass of the sample (g), Cp is the specific heat capacity of pure water (Jg−1 °C−1), dT/dt is the rate of temperature change (°C/s), D is the diameter of the probe (cm), V is the sample volume (mL), t is the processing time (s), and UP is the ultrasonic power (J/s).

Brine curing treatment

The surface of the fish was wiped with kitchen paper and placed in a beaker with 5% brine at a mass ratio of 1:5. The curing treatment was then performed using an ultrasonic crusher. The distance between the sample and probe was 3–3.5 cm and the sample was placed perpendicular to the probe. The samples were processed for 30, 60, or 90 min with an output power of 100, 300, or 500 W with 4 s of sonication and 2 s of stoppage; the temperature was maintained at 4 °C throughout the sonication process. The temperature of the marinade was monitored with thermocouples. Alternatively, static marination was conducted to establish a control group. Following marination, the sensory evaluation of texture, color difference, and yield was performed within 24 h; the remaining samples were then stored at −18 °C. The nine treatment groups were named according to their output power and processing time: UL100-30, UL100-60, UL100-90, UL300-30, UL300-60, UL300-90, UL500-30, UL500-60, and UL500-90; the three control groups were named: S-30, S-60, and S-90.

Product yield

Before ultrasound treatment, the samples were weighed, with the corresponding measurement being recorded as m1. Following ultrasound treatment, the surface water on the samples was gently wiped off with kitchen paper. The samples were then reweighed, with the corresponding measurement being recorded as m2. Product yield was calculated using the following formula:(6)Product yield%=m2/m1×100

Texture

Texture analysis was performed as reported by Zhang et al. [16]. The treated fish samples were cut into 1 × 1 × 1 cm3 cubes; subsequently, sample texture was determined using a texture analyzer (QTS-25; CNS Farnell, UK). A cylindrical probe was selected as TA44 with a test speed of 0.5 mm s−1, 2 cycles, a trigger force of 5 g, and a downward pressure distance of 5.0 mm. Chewiness, hardness, resilience, and adhesion were measured with six repetitions for each sample.

Color difference

Color difference analysis was performed as described by Cropotova et al. [17]. The chromaticity parameters of the treated fish sample surfaces were determined using a colorimeter (CR-400; Konica Minolta, Inc., Japan) to determine their corresponding L*, a*, and b* color values. According to the manufacturer’s instructions, the colorimeter was standardized and calibrated using a calibration plate before use. Each sample was analyzed five times, and the average value was obtained.

Sodium content

Sodium content was determined by referring to the national standard GB5009.286–2016. The treated fish samples were minced using a meat grinder; then, 0.5 g of each sample was placed in a microwave digestion jar. Five milliliters of nitric acid was added to the samples, which were then stored at 25 °C for 2 h before microwave digestion. The samples were cooled and transferred to centrifuge tubes and were diluted to 50 mL with distilled water before passing through a 0.22 μm filter membrane. NaCl content of each fish sample was determined using inductively coupled plasma mass spectrometry (7900 ICP-MS; Agilent Technologies).

Based on the corresponding standard curve, the amount of Na+ retained in each fish was calculated using the following formula:(7)X=C×V×fm×10where, X is the sodium content (g/100 g), C is the sodium ion concentration calculated from the curve (mg/L), V is the solution volume (mL), f is the dilution factor, and m is the weight of the fish (g).

Microstructural observation of fish muscle

The treated dorsal muscle samples were prepared according to the method described by Zhou et al. [18] with modifications. The samples were cut into 2 × 2 × 0.5 cm3 slices and fixed in 2.5% glutaraldehyde solution at 25 °C overnight, and then subjected to gradient dehydration with 30%, 50%, 70%, 90%, and 100% ethanol solution for 20 min per incubation. The samples were then dehydrated and vacuum freeze-dried for 48 h. The surface microstructures of each fish piece were observed under a scanning electron microscope (Phenom Pure; Thermo Scientific) after ion sputtering with gold spray.

Sensory assessment

Ten sensory-trained students were selected to rate the fish on four parameters: texture, taste, color, and overall acceptability. According to the method of Peña-Gonzalez et al [11], the fish were packed in vacuum steaming bags, placed in a water bath, and steamed for 2 min until the temperature of the geometric center of the fish reached 72 °C. The temperature of the samples was maintained at 35 °C until sensory analysis. Tests were conducted in a room where the room temperature was maintained at 20 °C. In each test, the panelists randomly selected 20 g samples and assessed the fish characteristics in the following order: texture, taste, color, and overall acceptability, with each sample assessed on a seven-point intensity scale (Table S1) for the samples. The panelists rinsed their mouths with drinking water before tasting each sample, and each sample was analyzed three times.

Amino acid content and taste analysis

The FAA content in the fish samples was determined according to the national standard GB 5009.124–2016. Each sample (0.1 g) was placed in a sealed vial and 10 mL of 6 M HCl was added; the bottle was filled with nitrogen for 1 min and then sealed for hydrolysis at 110 °C for 22 h. The hydrolysate was cooled and fixed in a 50-mL centrifuge tube with distilled water. Thereafter, 1 mL of the sample was pipetted into a centrifuge tube and evaporated under a nitrogen blowing apparatus in a water bath at 95 °C. Subsequently, 1 mL of 0.01 M HCl was added to dissolve the residue in the centrifuge tube; the free amino acids in this sample were then identified using high-performance liquid chromatography (Agilent 1100; Agilent Technologies) after passing through a 0.22 μm filter membrane.

Taste activity value (TAV) has been expressed as the ratio of FAA concentration in the fish compared to its threshold value; if TAV was > 1, then the compound was considered to contribute to the taste of the fish. The formula used is as follows:(8)TAV=C/Twhere, C is the absolute concentration of the taste-contributing substance in mg/100 g of fresh fish and T is the threshold value of the taste-contributing substance in mg/100 g of fresh fish.

Volatile component analysis

To analyze the flavor components of fish qualitatively and quantitatively, the volatile flavor substances were examined in the headspace solid-phase microextraction mode combined with gas chromatography–tandem mass spectrometry (SPME-GC–MS). Sample pretreatment was conducted and instrumental conditions were maintained according to Zou et al.'s method with some modifications [14]. Initially, 5 g of sample was placed in a 20 mL extraction vial, and an internal standard (2-octanol) was added before sealing. The sample was then equilibrated for 20 min at 60 °C in a water bath. After equilibration, an activated extraction needle was inserted into the sample vial to conduct headspace extraction for 30 min. The extraction head was injected into the gas chromatograph, and the compounds were separated in the capillary tube by resolving the attachment at 250 °C without splitting mode for 20 min. The column chamber temperature was initially held at 40 °C for 5 min, before being increased to 240 °C at 5 °C/min and then increased further to 250 °C at 10 °C/min, where it was held for 6 min. The mass spectrometry component was run in the electron collision mode (70 eV) and full scan mode (29–550 Da). Each volatile substance was identified using the NIST 17 mass spectrometry database and characterized by deconvolution using AMDIS software (https://www.amdis.net), where the percentage peak area value indicated the relative content of each flavor substance.

Statistical analysis

All analytical tests were repeated three times. A one-way analysis of variance (ANOVA) was conducted using SPSS (version 26; IBM Corp., Armonk, NY, USA), with P < 0.05 indicating significance. All results are expressed as mean ± standard deviation. Plots were created using Origin 2021 (OriginLab, Northampton, MA, USA), clustering heat maps were generated using TBtools (https://github.com/CJ-Chen/TBtools-Manual), and principal component analysis was performed using MetaboAnalyst (https://www.metaboanalyst.ca).

Sodium content changes in fish

NaCl is an important ingredient in meat processing and imparts saltiness, which can affect product quality. The variations in NaCl content in fish subjected to different curing treatments are shown in Fig. 1A. The NaCl content of all sonicated groups following 30 min of treatment was significantly higher than that of the static marination group (P < 0.05). The same trend was observed after 60 and 90 min of treatment. The NaCl content of the UL300-30 treatment group was the same as that of the static marination groups after 60 min of treatment. Static marination for 90 min resulted in an NaCl content of 0.52 g/100 mg, whereas the UL500-30 treatment group had 1.3 times more NaCl content than the static marination group (0.67 g/100 g), indicating that ultrasonic marination reduced the time needed to achieve a specific NaCl content when compared to static marination. Overall, these results suggest that sonication significantly accelerated the diffusion of NaCl throughout the fish and enhanced curing efficiency. Some studies have demonstrated that the alterations in mass transfer induced by ultrasound can be attributed to an asymmetric implosion of cavitation bubbles on a solid surface; ultimately, this phenomenon generates microjets and microchannels that increase the mass transfer of NaCl [20], [21]. Similar results have been reported by Zhang et al. Product yield

Water retention can be used as measure of quality of cured fish and can be reflected by product yield, with a stronger water-retention capacity being positively associated with tender meat. The product yield of the static treatment group decreased with an increase in treatment time, whereas the yield of the ultrasonically treated products increased with increasing ultrasonic treatment time; the UL300-90 treatment group had the highest product yield (Fig. 1B). However, this change in product yield was not significant when the marination time was extended to 90 min (P > 0.05), which may be the result of more amount of NaCl penetrating the fish over this extended duration. Elevated NaCl levels can increase intermuscular ionic strength; this causes protein denaturation and results in charge changes and hydrogen bond breakage, which ultimately leads to a decrease in water-holding capacity [10]. The product yield of ultrasonically treated fish showed an increasing trend initially, and then decreased with increasing power for the same curing time. This decrease in product yield may be because the rate of salt-soluble protein dissolution is higher than the rate of water and salt permeation with high-intensity ultrasound [22]. Ultrasound treatment can improve the water-retention capacity of fish meat owing to the cavitation effect, which disrupts the muscle fiber structure and increases the void space in muscle fibers, thereby facilitating the rapid and uniform distribution of water and salt between muscles [16]. A study on ultrasound-assisted salt curing of pork determined that ultrasound treatment significantly reduced the centrifugal and drip loss of muscle, which could be attributed to ultrasound-induced moderation of oxidation that promotes protein hydration [23].

Color variation analysis

Fig. 2 shows the color variation of fish fillets under different treatments. The low-power ultrasound treatments (100 and 300 W) significantly increased the L* values of the fillets (Fig. 2A; P < 0.05); however, the high-power treatments decreased the L* values, except UL500-60, which may be related to the minimum water-holding capacity. Previous studies have shown that a decrease in water in muscle tissue results in a reduced surface refractive index, which ultimately leads to a decrease in L* values [24], [25]. Compared to fillets subjected to the static marination, all ultrasound-treated fillets (except fillets in the UL100-30 group) exhibited a reduction in a* values (Fig. 2B; P < 0.05), whereas the b* values (Fig. 2C) increased significantly (P < 0.05). The fillets in the UL100-30 group exhibited a reduction in b* value. Cropotova et al. [17] postulated that a reduction in a* value corresponds to a reduction in pigmentation, which could be attributed to the denaturation of fish hemoglobin induced by the pulsed electric field during the salting process. Guo et al. [26] reported that there was no significant difference in the a* and b* values between static and ultrasound-assisted marinated pork. However, Peña-Gonzalez et al. [11] found that ultrasound-treated beef exhibited lower a* values than the control group beef; nonetheless, no significant changes in b* values were observed. Texture

Texture is an important factor for evaluating the quality of fish meat. In the present study, the textural characteristics of marinated sea bass fillets subjected to different treatments were determined using the texture profile analysis. The effects of different curing treatments on the chewiness, hardness, springiness, and adhesiveness of sea bass fillets are shown in Fig. 3. Compared to static marination, ultrasonic treatment significantly reduced the hardness and chewiness of the fillets (Fig. 3B and D; P < 0.05), which was consistent with the results of previous studies, yao et al. [10] determined that low-power ultrasound may disrupt myogenic fibers in acoustically assisted brine-cured tuna, which effectively reduced the hardness and chewiness fish meat, with insignificant changes in hardness as ultrasonic treatment time and power increased (P > 0.05). Regarding adhesiveness and springiness (Fig. 3A and C), both low-power ultrasound treatments (100 and 300 W) improved the adhesiveness and springiness of the fish fillets (P < 0.05). In a previous study, the gel structure of chicken breast exhibited a more uniform and regular order under low-frequency ultrasound treatment; furthermore, this intricately ordered network structure increased the corresponding water-holding capacity of the gel [27]. Li et al. [28] also demonstrated that the springiness of ultrasound-treated chicken gizzards was significantly higher than that of the non-ultrasound-treated group, which was attributed to the cavitation effect of ultrasound that disrupts the integrity of the muscle, increases the interstices of the muscle fibers, and further improves the water-holding capacity of the fibers. However, higher-power ultrasonic treatment for an extended duration reduced the adhesiveness and springiness of the gizzards, which could be attributed to this treatment leading to the denaturation of proteins and deterioration of gelation properties, thus reducing the springiness. In summary, moderate sonication may be an effective strategy for improving the texture of fish fillets.Fig. 3Textural changes during the curing process. Microstructure

Fig. 4 shows the corresponding scanning electron microscopy images of fish dorsal muscle following different treatment strategies. The myogenic fibers in the static marination group were neatly arranged, with clear perimysium and small gaps; overall, these samples contained uniformly shaped and sized muscle fibers. The tenderness of meat depends on the degree of destruction of these myogenic fibers. When ultrasound treatment was performed, the mechanical effect of this process led to the disruption of myogenic fibers, resulting in fragmented fibers of varying lengths. In the 300 W treatment group, the border on the surface of myofibers became more blurred and the perimysium almost completely disappeared. Furthermore, this border was more significantly blurred and fragmented in the 500 W treatment group, with evident porosity being observed across the myofibers. Overall, these results were consistent with those of Li et al. [29], [30]. Peña-Gonzalez et al. [11] suggested that the cavitation effect of ultrasound causes disintegration of the connective tissue fibers; meanwhile, collagenases are activated and involved in the destruction of myogenic fibers, which ultimately promotes beef tenderization. Different ultrasound powers exerted significant effects on fish microstructure; however, brine immersion time had a negligible effect on fiber structure (Fig. 4). In a study on the effects of ultrasound treatment on beef microstructure, Wang et al. [31] revealed that samples without ultrasound treatment retained intact myofibril structure following 7 days of storage; however, the Z-lines of ultrasound-treated beef were disrupted, with misaligned myofibrils and an irregular banding pattern. In addition, the breakage and disintegration of myogenic fibers lead to the production of gaps, which reduce sodium transfer resistance and facilitate the osmotic diffusion of NaCl [16]. Sensory assessment

The results of the sensory evaluation of static-marinated and 300.W ultrasonically treated fish are shown in Fig. 5. There was no significant difference in the color scores between static-marinated and ultrasonically treated fish (P > 0.05); the panelists thought that ultrasonically treated fish tasted better than static-marinated fish, and in particular, the taste scores of the UL300-60-treated fish were significantly higher than those of the static-marinated fish (P < 0.05). However, with the increase in ultrasonication time, this change was not significant (P > 0.05), suggesting that ultrasonication improves the flavor of the fish. In terms of texture, ultrasonically treated samples were softer and juicier than the static-marinated fish, and their texture scores increased significantly with increasing ultrasonication time (P < 0.05), which is consistent with the results of hardness and water retention. Peña-Gonzalez et al. also concluded that ultrasonically treated beef was more tender and juicier than the control samples [11]. FAA and taste analyses

FAAs are important contributors to flavor and taste in fish and are obtained through the Maillard reaction and Strecker degradation. Fig. 6 shows the 23 FAAs identified from the sea bass samples assessed in this study. Moderate ultrasound treatment in the UL300-60 group significantly increased the total FAA content from 5.62 mg/g to 6.24 mg/g compared with that of the S-60 group. The application of ultrasound treatment in meat processing has been shown to accelerate the release of FAAs [32], [33]; however, in the present study, prolonged high-power treatment reduced the FAA content of fish samples. The FAA content of the UL500-90 treatment group reduced to 5.018 mg/g. This is consistent with results obtained by Wang et al. [34]. Fresh-tasting amino acids, Glu and Asp, are important substances responsible for imparting flavor to fish. In the current study, ultrasound treatment significantly promoted the release of Glu (Fig. 6B), especially at 300 W for 90 min (P < 0.05); specifically, the Glu content of the UL300-90 samples increased 1.4 fold compared with that of the S-90 samples. Similarly, ultrasound treatment promoted the release of Asp (Fig. 6A), with UL500-60 significantly increasing the Asp content (P < 0.05); however, UL100-60 treatment reduced the Asp content in sea bass samples. Gly, Hyp, Ser, Thr, Ala, Pro, and Arg are considered sweet amino acids with a pleasant taste. Fig. 6E and H shows that Hyp and Gly are the main sweet amino acids in the fish meat analyzed in this study. Ultrasound treatment increased the content of the sweet amino acids except Gly; additionally, the sweet amino content of the UL300-60 treatment group samples was 4.09 mg/g, which was significantly higher than that of the other treatment groups.

Cys, His, Lys, Val, Met, Phe, Ile, Leu, and Tyr are considered unpleasant bitter amino acids. In this study, short-term low-power sonication significantly reduced the content of Cys, His, and Val (P < 0.05). However, UL300-60 and UL300-90 treatments increased the content of Lys, Met, Phe, Ile, Leu, and Tyr. Interestingly, UL300-60 sonication increased the sweet, fresh, and bitter amino acid content. In a study on the effect of sonication and heating treatments on FAAs in dry-cured ham, Pérez-Santaescolástica et al. determined that sonication and heating treatments significantly increased sweet and bitter amino acid content [35].

Cathepsin B and cathepsin B + L cause excessive protein hydrolysis at higher levels [36], and Mehrabani et al. demonstrated that beef treated with sonication possessed a higher protease activity [37]. The cavitation effect of ultrasound induces the cleavage of water molecules into free radicals, which increases the level of degradation of meat proteins during the curing process [12]. This may be the reason that ultrasound treatment increased the level of degradation of fish proteins in the present study and lead to higher FAA content.

TAV greater than 1 is considered to contribute to the flavor of a product. Table S3 shows the TAVs of different compounds. In this study, the TAVs of Gly, Hyp, Asp, Glu, and His were calculated as > 1, and were therefore, considered to contribute to the taste of the product. Gly and Hyp impart sweetness, and their TAVs were the highest at 1.87 (S-30) and 1.41 mg/100 g (UL300-60), respectively. The TAVs of the fresh-tasting amino acids Asp and Glu were significantly increased after ultrasound treatment, at 1.55 (UL500-60) and 3.59 mg/100 g (UL300-90), respectively. This finding suggests that these fresh-tasting amino acids play an important role in the taste of a product after ultrasound treatment. The highest TAV in the static marination group was for the bitter amino acid, His (2.02 mg/100 g); however, the TAV for His was significantly reduced to 1.37 mg/100 g after ultrasound treatment (UL300-30). In conclusion, ultrasound treatment improved the taste of cured sea bass by enhancing freshness and sweetness while simultaneously reducing bitterness.

Analysis of volatile organic compounds

Flavor is the result of a combination of volatile organic compounds and water- or oil-soluble compounds. During fish fermentation, proteins and lipids are degraded and oxidized by endogenous enzymes and microorganisms; this results in the production of various flavor precursors such as esters, aldehydes, and ketones, which give the product its corresponding unique flavor [38]. In the current study, SPME-GC–MS was used to identify the volatile organic compounds in fish subjected to different curing treatments; 67 volatile substances were identified, namely, 14 alcohols, 10 ketones, 9 aldehydes, 10 alkanes, 9 aromatics, 9 esters, 3 furans, and 3 acids.

Aldehydes, which are primarily derived from lipid oxidative degradation or amino acid degradation induced by the Maillard reaction, have a low odor threshold and are important aromatic components in meat preparation [39], [53]. The overall distribution of aldehydes was significantly altered by ultrasound treatment (Fig. 7A). The UL300-60 treatment group contained significantly higher amounts of glutaraldehyde, hexanal, heptanal, octanal, nonanal, 3-furaldehyde, benzaldehyde, and trans-2-octenal. Among these components, hexanal, heptanal, octanal, and nonanal, which are straight-chain aldehydes with a higher content in cured fish, exhibited grassy, fatty, waxy, fruity, and floral aromas, respectively [40]. These straight-chain aldehydes are the primary volatile organic compounds detected in most other fermented fish, such as suan zuo yu [41] and dry-cured grass carp [42]. In contrast, the UL300-90 treatment group had significantly higher levels of 2-methylbutyraldehyde and 3-methylbutyraldehyde than the other treatment groups. Volatile organic compounds in meat products are affected by a combination of heating temperature, treatment time, and fat oxidation [43]; additionally, high levels of aldehydes in the sonication groups of this study may be a result of sonication increasing the rate of lipid oxidation and fatty acid degradation, which can lead to a significant increase in aldehyde content [44]. The results of this study were consistent with those of Zhang et al. on the effect of sonication on bacon flavor; their study found that aldehydes were most affected by sonication compared to other compounds [45]. In summary, sonication can promote the production of aldehydes with aromatic odors in wet-cured fish.Fig. 7Heat map of 67 volatile organic compound (VOC) clustering (A), changes in VOC content in fish under different curing treatments (B), changes in VOC quantities in fish under different curing treatments (C).

Alcohols are derived from lipid oxidation and amino acid metabolism, and are considered important volatile organic compounds because of their strong association with the characteristic fatty taste of meat [38]. Overall, alcohols were more abundant in cured sea bass, with 14 alcohols identified in total (Fig. 7C). Compared to static marination, sonication significantly increased the content of 1-heptanol, 1-decanol, linalool, eucalyptol, ethanol, 1-octanol, and 2-ethyl-1-hexanol. Among these compounds, linalool and ethanol were detected only in sonicated fish samples. Ultrasound has been reported to increase ethanol content [46]; this may be due to the local high temperature and pressure generated by ultrasound, which accelerates the rate of lipid oxidation, thereby promoting the production of alcohols [14]. Organic alcohol can be oxidized to aldehydes and esters via the thermal effect of ultrasound [47]. Under static marination treatment, 1-hexanol, 1-penten-3-ol, 1-pentanol, 6-undecanol, 1-octen-3-ol, and cyclooctanol content increased with increasing treatment time. of these compounds, 1-octen-3-ol is an oxidation product of unsaturated fatty acids, with a mushroom-like aroma that may contribute to the aroma profile of the marinated sea bass [48]. However, these compounds exhibited varying degrees of reduction under sonication, suggesting that sonication promotes the volatilization of alcohols and the production of other organic flavors.

Ketones are derived from amino acid degradation, lipid oxidation, and the Maillard reaction [49]. In this study, the ketones detected at high levels in the sonicated samples included 2-butanone, 2-heptanone, 2-nonanone, 3,5-octadien-2-one, and 3-hydroxy-2-butanone. The formation of these methyl ketones can occur via microbial metabolism or fatty acid oxidation, and they are considered to be key fatty aroma precursors in cooked meat [47]. However, the static marination treatment (S-90) resulted in higher contents of 2,3-pentanedione and 3,5-octadien-2-one. Most ketones confer fruity and creamy flavors that play a harmonizing role in the overall flavor of the meat product [50]; however, owing to the high threshold of ketones and their low content in the cured fish in our study (Fig. 7B), their contribution to the overall fish aroma was relatively low.

Esters are key flavor substances that provide cured fish with fruity and sweet flavors; these compounds are generated by esterification reactions between acids and alcohols. The cavitation effect of ultrasound promotes the reaction between alcohols and acids to generate esters such as methyl acetate, methyl propionate, and methyl hexadecanoate. Owing to the inhibitory effect of ultrasound on microbial growth, the metabolic rate of microorganisms is reduced during curing, which can result in lower ester abundance [48]. In the present study, the main acidic compounds in fish samples were determined to be n-decanoic, octanoic, and nonanoic acid, with the contents of n-decanoic and octanoic acids being highest in UL500-30 and that of nonanoic acids being highest in UL300-60 compared to those of the other treatment groups. These acids are primarily derived from the oxidation of unsaturated fatty acids, but contribute less to the flavor of cured sea bass due to their higher threshold; nonetheless, these acids are important precursors in the formation of esters [51].

To compare the intensity of compounds in several groups of samples, the effect of different curing treatments on the volatile organic composition of fish was explored via cluster analysis. Overall, the static marination and ultrasound treatment groups exhibited different distributions of flavor compounds, indicating corresponding differences in volatile organic compounds (Fig. 7A). This difference may be attributed to the ultrasound treatment increasing the level of lipid oxidation, thereby changing the flavor compound distribution [52].

Partial least squares discriminant analysis (PLS-DA) was used to confirm the correlation of key aroma compounds across treatments; these PLS-DA model parameters are shown in Fig. 8. The cumulative variance contributions of the 30, 60, and 90 min treatment groups were 99.4% (Fig. 8A), 99.5% (Fig. 8B), and 97.2% (Fig. 8C), respectively. This indicates that the flavor compounds were well differentiated at different ultrasonic intensities for the same treatment time, except for the 90 min treatment group, in which the flavor components at 300 and 500 W were similar. The contribution of volatile organic compounds to the aroma of cured sea bass was measured by calculating the variable importance in projection (VIP). Typically, volatile organic compounds with a VIP > 1 can be used as key markers of product flavor. Overall, we detected 16 compounds with VIP > 1 across all samples; this indicates that these compounds play a crucial role in the distinct odor differences observed in these sea bass samples. These compounds primarily included aldehydes and alcohols: octanal, nonanal, heptanal, pentanal, 3-methylbutanal, 1-hexanol, ethanol, 1-pentanol, 1-octen-3-ol, and 6-undecanol. The levels of all aldehydes were considered high in ultrasonicated fish, whereas the levels of the alcohols, except ethanol, were high in statically marinated fish. Conclusion

In the present study, we investigated the effects of different ultrasonic intensities and marination times on fish quality. Ultrasonic treatment significantly increased the permeation rate of NaCl and reduced marination time, with the effects of 300 W ultrasonic treatment for 30 min being comparable to those of static marination for 60 min. Owing to the cavitation effect of ultrasound, the texture of the meat also changed following ultrasonic-assisted marination, with a reduction in the hardness and chewiness of the fish, which was confirmed by the corresponding microstructure results. The content of FAAs and volatile organic compounds was also altered following ultrasonic-assisted marination, with the clearest changes being observed in the UL300-60 treatment group. Furthermore, ultrasonic treatment significantly increased the content of volatile organic compounds, especially that of aldehydes and alcohols. Overall, this study demonstrated that ultrasound-assisted curing technology can be applied as a new method to cure fish to reduce curing time and effectively improve the quality of fish meat. Additionally, this provides a theoretical basis for the ultrasound-assisted light salt curing and processing of seafood.
