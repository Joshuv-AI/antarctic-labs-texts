Infarction of parotid gland Warthin tumor following fine needle aspiration: A diagnostic mimicker of malignancy
Authors: Sree Manasa Puttamreddy, Lauren Worley, Samuel Borak, Viswanath Sai Puttagunta, Walter Bell, Andrew H. Ford, Bradley P. Thomas
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Fine-needle aspiration (FNA) induced infarction of Warthin tumor is a rare phenomenon with potential to mimic malignancy both radiologically and histologically. We report the case of a 55-year-old woman who initially presented with a painless left parotid mass. Pre-FNA contrast-enhanced CT demonstrated a well-circumscribed, homogeneously enhancing oval mass of 3 × 2.6 × 2.2 cm, and doppler ultrasound confirmed internal vascularity. FNA cytology confirmed a benign Warthin tumor. Fifteen days later, she developed painful swelling and trismus. A follow-up CT revealed complete loss of internal enhancement with no interval change in size, and doppler ultrasound demonstrated absent internal color flow, new from the pre-FNA exam. This constellation of findings was consistent with infarction. The patient was managed conservatively with marked clinical improvement within ten days. Subsequent surgical excision, performed approximately 4 weeks after FNA, confirmed infarcted Warthin tumor with no evidence of malignancy. This case emphasizes the importance of recognizing infarction as a benign post-FNA complication to avoid misdiagnosis and unnecessary aggressive treatment.

Introduction

Salivary gland disorders are broadly categorized as infectious, inflammatory, or neoplastic. When a neoplastic process is suspected, tissue diagnosis becomes essential to distinguish between benign and malignant lesions. Fine-needle aspiration (FNA) and ultrasound-guided core needle biopsy are standard, minimally invasive methods for establishing the diagnosis of salivary gland masses.

Warthin tumor (WT), more formally known as papillary cystadenoma lymphomatosum, is the second most common benign salivary gland neoplasm after pleomorphic adenoma. It almost exclusively affects the parotid gland and periparotid lymph nodes and occurs more frequently in men. It occurs bilaterally in 7-10% of cases and has a strong association with tobacco use. Smokers are up to 8 times more likely to develop this tumor than non-smokers [1,2].

The underlying pathogenesis of Warthin tumor is still debated. The most accepted theory proposes that WT originates from heterotopic salivary ductal epithelium trapped within lymphoid tissue during embryologic development. Immunohistochemical studies have demonstrated similarities between WT epithelial cells and those found in the striated and excretory ducts of normal salivary glands. Chronic exposure to tobacco-related carcinogens, such as benzopyrene and N-nitrosoguanidine, is believed to trigger oncocytic metaplasia and tumor formation.

Management of WT is usually surgical, often by superficial or total parotidectomy. However, in selected cases with characteristic imaging and cytology findings, particularly when asymptomatic, observation may be an appropriate alternative. FNA is widely considered safe and effective for diagnosis, though rare complications have been reported-including partial or complete infarction of the tumor following this seemingly minimally invasive approach to diagnosis.

Infarction of Warthin tumor after FNA is a rare event that can alter both the clinical presentation and radiologic features of the lesion. These changes can raise clinical concern for other complications such as inflammation, necrosis, or secondary infection. In addition to confusing clinical findings, post-resection pathology specimens can mimic malignancy. In this case report, we describe a patient with a cytologically confirmed Warthin tumor who developed increasing pain and swelling 2 weeks after FNA. Repeat imaging showed a striking change in enhancement pattern, which was later confirmed to be due to infarction by histopathology. Through this case, we discuss the radiologic evolution of infarcted Warthin tumor, explore the proposed mechanisms of post-biopsy infarction, and emphasize the importance of recognizing this rare but benign complication to avoid unnecessary interventions.

Case presentation

A 55-year-old woman initially presented with a painless firm swelling in the left parotid region. Intra-oral examination was normal. Clear saliva was expressed from her left parotid duct. Contrast-enhanced computed tomography (CT) of the neck revealed a well-circumscribed, uniformly enhancing, oval mass measuring 3 × 2.6 × 2.2 cm in the inferior aspect of the left parotid gland, with non-enlarged bilateral intraparotid lymph nodes (Fig. 1). Concurrent color Doppler ultrasound demonstrated a well-circumscribed, homogeneous mass with internal vascularity (Fig. 1). Fine-needle aspiration cytology (FNAC) demonstrated epithelial cells with oncocytic features and a background of lymphocytes, consistent with a diagnosis of Warthin tumor (Fig. 2). Fifteen days later, the patient re-presented with progressive pain and swelling in the same region (Fig. 3), now radiating to the left neck and behind the ear, impairing her ability to open her mouth and affecting daily activities including sleep. She reported alternating sensations of feeling hot and cold but denied any purulent discharge. On examination, she was afebrile with a blood pressure of 181/104 mmHg, heart rate of 96 bpm, respiratory rate of 16/min, and oxygen saturation of 98% on room air. Physical examination revealed a tender, nonfluctuant left parotid swelling with no overlying erythema extending into the mastoid and upper neck. No purulent ductal drainage was noted on the intra-oral exam. Laboratory workup revealed a mildly elevated white blood cell count of 12.2 × 103/L, which, while potentially consistent with an inflammatory response, raised concern for early infectious complication. Repeat contrast-enhanced CT of the neck showed a parotid lesion of similar size but with significant interval change in enhancement characteristics, now showing complete lack of enhancement, consistent with infarction (Fig. 1). Repeat color doppler ultrasound performed at the same visit confirmed the absence of internal color flow within the mass, in contrast to the vascularity demonstrated on pre-FNA imaging (Fig. 1). The CT showed no rim enhancement, or peri-lesional fat stranding to suggest abscess. The patient remained afebrile and clinically stable throughout, which was further evidence for a non-infectious process. Nevertheless, given the new onset of pain, the mildly elevated WBC, and the risk of developing complications such as post-FNA parotitis, parotid abscess, or deep neck space infection, empiric oral antibiotics were initiated alongside oral ketorolac for analgesia. Surgical excision was then recommended by ENT. At follow-up ten days later, she reported marked improvement in pain and swelling without new or worsening symptoms. Examination showed a reduction in the size of the parotid mass. Discussion

Warthin tumor is one of the more common benign tumors of the parotid gland, and it's usually straightforward to diagnose and manage. Patients often present with a painless, slow-growing lump, and fine-needle aspiration cytology is typically used to confirm the diagnosis. In this case, however, a rare phenomenon occurred- infarction of the tumor following FNA, which dramatically altered both the clinical picture and imaging appearance.

Although FNA is widely considered minimally invasive, there are a few documented cases where Warthin tumors have partially or even completely resolved after biopsy [3]. And on rare occasions, as seen in our patient, the tumor undergoes infarction, leading to pain, swelling, radiological and histological changes (Fig. 4). A study by Taylor et al. in 2008 reported a 9% incidence of infarction in Warthin tumors following FNA, suggesting that while rare, there is a poorly understood association [4].Fig. 4Histologic findings. There are several plausible explanations for this phenomenon. Warthin tumors are made up of 2 main components: oncocytic epithelial cells and a lymphoid-rich stroma. Oncocytic cells are packed with mitochondria, meaning they have high oxygen needs. The term papillary cystadenoma lymphomatosum was first coined by Aldred Scott Warthin, which led to its widely accepted eponym. Warthin noted that these tumors are not particularly well-supplied with blood; they have a limited arterial supply and thin-walled veins. It is hypothesized that this makes them more vulnerable to anything that might further reduce their blood flow. Therefore FNA can potentially disrupt the tumor’s already fragile vasculature, causing reduced oxygen supply and leading to ischemia and infarction [5,6].

The end result of this process is not only necrosis but also changes in the tissue that occur can mimic more serious pathology. When oncocytic cells die, the tissue can undergo squamous metaplasia which is a kind of transformation where the cells take on a more resilient, but less specialized form. Unfortunately, these changes can look more sinister, even malignant, under the microscope. This may confound the histologic appearance and mimic other entities such as mucoepidermoid carcinoma, invasive squamous cell carcinoma, or even necrotizing infections of the salivary glands. This can be particularly problematic during frozen section analysis, where decisions pertaining to surgical management need to be made quickly. This could potentially lead to overly aggressive treatment, including unnecessary lymph node dissections or more extensive surgery than needed. In the current case, the entirely submitted circumscribed, cystic tumor was predominantly necrotic with stromal reaction and small foci of squamous metaplasia exhibiting minimal atypia. No mucous or intermediate cells of a mucoepidermoid carcinoma, overtly infiltrative foci, perineural invasion, or lymph node metastasis to suggest a malignant invasive mimic was seen. In challenging cases, a p63 immunohistochemical stain may highlight an outer basal cell layer to the Warthin tumor, a contrast to the diffuse staining of neoplastic cells with p63 seen in mimics, although areas of squamous metaplasia in a Warthin tumor can also show diffuse staining, necessitating cautious interpretation. Negative testing for MALM2 gene rearrangement can also help support a Warthin tumor over a Warthin-like mucoepidermoid carcinoma, which may be positive [7]. In the setting of significant necrosis, caution and close follow-up is still warranted to ensure indolent behavior.

Radiologically, the transformation is equally striking. In our patient, the tumor initially appeared as a classic Warthin tumor with a well-circumscribed, homogeneously enhancing intrinsic parotid gland mass with internal vascularity on color Doppler ultrasound. Within 2 weeks following FNA, it evolved into a non-enhancing mass with absent internal color flow on repeat Doppler imaging (Fig. 1). This combination of new pain, rapid clinical change, and lack of enhancement prompted consideration of several diagnoses. Intratumoral hemorrhage was considered given the recent FNA, but argued against by the absence of high-density content on CT. Parotid abscess was a legitimate concern given the pain, trismus, and mildly elevated WBC; however, the patient remained afebrile, and no fluctuance on examination or CT findings to suggest infection. Aseptic inflammatory change or post-FNA parotitis was plausible, but the degree of enhancement loss was incongruent. Clinical stability of the patient, preservation of lesion margins, absence of worrisome features for infection or malignancy, and the temporal relationship to FNA suggested post-FNA infarction as the unifying diagnosis, which was subsequently confirmed with histopathology.

Overall, this case underscores the importance of considering infarcted Warthin tumor in the differential diagnosis of a painful, necrotic-appearing parotid mass following FNA. Familiarity with its pathophysiology, differential diagnosis of potential imaging changes after FNA, and subsequent histologic pitfalls can help clinicians avoid misdiagnosis and overtreatment, such as unnecessary lymph node dissection or radical parotidectomy.
