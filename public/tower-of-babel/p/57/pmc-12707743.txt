INTRODUCTION

Plasma cell dyscrasias (PCDs) are a group of plasma cell (PC) disorders displaying heterogeneous clinical presentation.[2] As PC is the sole source of antibody formation, most PCDs are characterized by the presence of serum monoclonal protein which is the hallmark of multiple myeloma (MM) – the most common PCD.[2] However, as a unique entity of PCDs, solitary plasmacytoma (SP) is an exception, as the serum monoclonal protein detection is not required according to the current guideline. As a spectrum, concurrence of MM with SP is usual. In recent guidelines, bone marrow examination is mandatory for most PCD spectrum, except in SP.[2]

SP can be presented either as extramedullary SP (EMP) (e.g., soft tissue) or as a solitary bone plasmacytoma. SP is a rare accounting, with a 0.15/100,000 incidence.[4] Unlike other PCDs, the SP presentation and clinical symptoms are commonly related to the mass compression to adjacent tissue. If SP is presented in dura mater, the clinical symptoms can mimic an extradural mass such as a meningioma. Thus, SP-MM coincidence may have MM clinical symptoms as well (e.g., anemia, bone pain, elevated creatinine, fatigue, and hypercalcemia).[2]

As commonly concurrence, with MM or other PCDs spectrum, an isolated SP presentation is rare. Fortunately, most isolated SP without MM has a good prognosis.[3] This case report describes an isolated EMP on the frontal region in a 20-year-old Javanese male who presented with a palpable rubbery frontal mass and visual disturbances.

CASE REPORT

In May 2023, a 20-year-old Javanese male presented to the neurosurgery clinic after being referred by the surgery department with a pronounced mass on his face, headache, and progressive visual disturbances. He already recognized the mass a year before, yet since the mass was small, it was neglected. However, the mass gradually enlarged over time, and within the month preceding presentation, his visual function began to deteriorate.

Physical examination revealed a firm palpable mass in the right frontal region with a diameter of approximately 8 cm × 6 cm with an indefinite margin. No motor weakness was observed in the extremities. Visual acuity of the right and left eye was 6/60 and 6/7.5, respectively. A contrast-enhanced computed tomography scan examination demonstrated an isodense, extra-axial mass in the right frontal region, measuring 7.7 × 5.2 × 8.5 cm [Figure 1], with heterogeneous enhancement suggestive of internal cystic or necrosis changes. A midline shifting of 0.8 cm to the left was noted. Intratumoral hyperostosis was observed, and no intra-axial abnormalities were identified. Laboratory findings were within normal limits, including red blood cell count of 4.36 × 106/μL, hemoglobin level of 9.9 g/dL, white blood cell count of 5.3 × 103/μL, neutrophils at 46.6%, lymphocytes at 43.5%, and serum creatinine at 0.66 mg/dL. With all available data, a provisional diagnosis of high-grade meningioma was made. Given the history of progressive mass enlargement and visual disturbances, surgical resection was planned per the patient’s consent.

Contrast computed tomography scan showed a an isodense extraaxial mass (red circle) on the right frontalis region with a diameter of 7.7 × 5.2 × 8.5 cm calculated with (a) axial, (b) coronal, and (c) sagittal section.

In June 2023, the patient underwent a large craniotomy under general anesthesia. An ~18 cm unilateral extended incision was made from the tragus, following the shape of the tumor circumferentially. The tumor was an extradural mass ~10 × 11.5 cm, with a pink-reddish appearance, soft, highly vascularized, and easily separated from the skull [Figure 2a]. Five burr holes were made to separate the tumor from the skull. The tumor was completely resected [Figure 2b]. The tumor was 10 × 11.5 × 1.5 cm [Figure 2c]. An allograft cranioplasty with titanium mesh was used for cranium closing.

(a) Tumor appearance after skin incision with pink-reddish appearance, soft, and highly vascularized. (b) The tumor completely removed. (c)The tumor’s gross appearance with a size of 10 × 11.5 × 1.5 cm.

The tumor sample was sent to the anatomical pathology department for confirmatory analysis since the tumor characteristics did not represent meningioma features and much more similar to a metastatic tumor. A day after the surgery, follow-up laboratory examination – thyroid function (free triiodothyronine [T3] and thyroxine [T4]), carcinoembryonic antigen (CEA), CA15-3, CA19-9, prostate specific antigen (PSA), and alpha fetoprotein (AFP) – was done due to suspicion of metastasis lesion. Nonetheless, none of these examinations were clinically significant – T3 of 1.34 pg/mL; T4 1 ng/dL; AFP 2.13 IU/mL; CEA 0.565 ng/mL; CA15-3 3.5 U/mL; CA19-9 3.44 U/mL; and PSA 0.46 ng/mL. Histopathological examination showed a PCs infiltration with atypical nuclei [Figure 3a] and positive MUM1 [Figure 3b] and CD138 [Figure 3c], suggesting plasmacytoma. A urine test for Bence Jones protein and serum protein electrophoresis were negative. Without any systemic symptoms and findings, isolated SP without MM was given with plasmacytoid meningioma as a differential diagnosis. Although plasmacytoma is a malignant neoplasm, in a multidisciplinary meeting, the committee decided that radiotherapy and chemotherapy were not necessary due to the complete removal of the tumor. Thus, recommended by the pathologist, isolated SP without MM was the final diagnosis for this case since the PC infiltration was within the bone without meninges involvement. Two years following the surgery, the patient was able to work normally without any complaint.

Histopathology examination with ×400 magnification of the tumor sample showed (a) multiple atypical cells with condensed nuclear components (red arrows) infiltrates surrounding soft-tissue (black arrowhead) stained with hematoxylin-eosin. Immunohistochemistry staining with (b) CD131, and (c) MUM1 showed a positive result suggesting plasmacytoma.

DISCUSSION

Isolated SP is a rare finding in daily practice, especially in the cranium and skull compartment.[1,7,9,11] Due to its expansive nature, SP is presented with signs and symptoms due to compression of adjacent structures. In some instances, a massive bulge mass the first sign as well. Hence, SP is usually misdiagnosed as the most common representative known tumor such as carcinoma nasopharynx, chordoma, meningioma, osteosarcoma, lymphoma, pituitary adenoma, metastatic carcinoma, eosinophilic granuloma, and MM.[12,13] Histopathological examination helps to determine plasmacytoma from the other possible tumors due to its distinct characteristics and positive staining with MUM1 and CD138.[10]

PCDs – including SP – originate from a reticulocyte, which is a resident of bone marrow. Imounga et al.[6] analysis in French Guiana population reported that concurrence of SS with MM is common either in male or female older than 50 years old, with a peak at 65–69 years old and accounting 1.5/100,000 deaths in 2010–2014 among men and 1.9/100,000 among women in 2010–2014. Its counterpart, EMP, has a lower incidence according to Huang et al.[5] analysis. Despite that, isolated SP without MM epidemiology is not recorded well, especially in cranium and skull base SP. In meta-analysis by Na’ara et al.,[8] most cases of skull base SP have an average age of 56.3 years old with equal gender distribution. Survival rates among skull base SP patients were 78% and 59% in 2 and 5 years, respectively.[8] Thus, patients with concurrent SP and MM have a lower survival rate than isolated SP. In this case, the patient was diagnosed with isolated SP of the frontal lobe and was relatively younger than Imounga et al.[6] and Na’ara et al.[8] analysis.

Whenever possible, SP shall be completely resected to prevent local recurrences as recommended by the guideline.[2] If complete resection is not possible, follow-up radiotherapy is necessary since plasmacytoma is a radiosensitive tumor. In this case, the complete tumor removal was done and since there were no systemic symptoms nor any evidence of MM, neither chemotherapy nor radiotherapy was given for the patient. As expected, the patient is functionally normal, fully active for daily activities without any complaint.

Cranial SP is a rare case. Fortunately, as the tumor is a space-occupying lesions, the clinical sign and symptoms would navigate the healthcare workers to recognize the abnormality; thus, time-consuming diagnostic exploring can be avoided. Nevertheless, due to its rarity, SP may misdiagnose at first. A common space-occupying lesion may be the first diagnosis given. In this case, meningioma and metastatic tumor were the first suspicion. Therefore, histopathological examination is vital for SP recognition. Once SP is recognized, MM screening is a must due to high probability of SP and MM concurrence. Moreover, comprehensive cancer screening may be needed to avoid undetected ongoing cancer. In this case, neither MM nor other cancers were detected; thus, chemo- and radiotherapy were not needed.

CONCLUSION

Cranial SP is a rare finding in daily practice. Early recognition of neurological symptoms due to a space-occupying lesion plays an important role in appropriate management. Thus, a comprehensive examination helps to identify cranial SP which can mimic another lesion such as meningioma. Although SP may seem harmless, it is important to do thorough cancer screening to avoid undetected ongoing developed cancer.
