Neoadjuvant chemoradiotherapy with capecitabine and irinotecan guided by UGT1A1 status in patients with locally advanced rectal cancer: 5‐year update of the CinClare trial
Authors: Zhen Zhang, Xinchen Sun, Anwen Liu, Yaqun Zhu, Tao Zhang, Luying Liu, Jianhui Jia, Shisheng Tan, Junxin Wu, Xin Wang, Juying Zhou, Jialin Yang, Chen Zhang, Hongyan Zhang, Xinjia He, Gang Cai, Chengyi Huang, Fan Xia, Juefeng Wan, Hui Zhang, Lijun Shen, Ling Wang, Wei Zhang, Sanjun Cai, Ji Zhu
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundThe optimal regimen and chemotherapy intensity are still under investigation for neoadjuvant treatment of locally advanced rectal cancer (LARC). The CinClare trial has demonstrated improved pathologic complete response (pCR) with the addition of irinotecan to neoadjuvant chemoradiotherapy (CRT) guided by uridine diphosphate glucuronosyltransferase 1A1 (UGT1A1) genotype in LARC. Here, we report the 5‐year follow‐up outcomes of the CinClare study.MethodsFrom November 2015 to December 2017, this randomized, open‐label, multicenter, phase III trial enrolled 360 patients with LARC and assigned them in a 1:1 ratio to CapIriRT (radiation with capecitabine combined with irinotecan followed by irinotecan and capecitabine) or CapRT (radiation with concurrent capecitabine followed by oxaliplatin and capecitabine). Irinotecan dosing was guided by UGT1A1 genotype (80 mg/m2 for *1/*1 and 65 mg/m2 for *1/*28). The endpoints, including local control (LC), distant metastasis‐free survival (DMFS), disease‐free survival (DFS), and overall survival (OS), were analyzed using the log‐rank test, Cox proportional hazards regression and restricted mean survival time (RMST) test at the data cut‐off date of June 2023.ResultsWith a median follow‐up of 60 months, the CapIriRT group showed numerically higher 5‐year LC (95.6% vs. 93.9%), 5‐year DMFS (83.9% vs. 77.9%), 5‐year DFS (77.7% vs. 70.6%), and 5‐year OS rates (82.9% vs. 76.1%) than the CapRT group. Further RMST test also showed a statistically significant difference in DFS (P < 0.05) and a borderline difference in OS (P = 0.050). Among the UGT1A1 *1/*1 population, the CapIriRT group had significantly improved 5‐year rates of DMFS, DFS, and OS (all P < 0.05). Patients achieving pCR also had significantly longer DFS and OS compared to non‐pCR patients (P < 0.05).ConclusionsThe addition of irinotecan guided by UGT1A1 genotype to a standard capecitabine‐based scheme brings clinical benefits with improved LC, DMFS, DFS, and OS. Patients with the UGT1A1 *1/*1 genotype derived notable benefit from irinotecan, with improved survival outcomes. Achievement of pCR is crucial as it is associated with improved long‐term survival. These findings support the integration of genomic testing into clinical practice to achieve a personalized irinotecan dosing regimen, which can optimize efficacy and safety.Trial registrationClinicalTrials.gov (NCT02605265).Keywords: antineoplastic combined chemotherapy protocols, capecitabine, capecitabine‐irinotecan combination, irinotecan, neoadjuvant therapy, Rectal neoplasms

BACKGROUND

In patients with locally advanced rectal cancer (LARC), treatment with curative intent during the initial therapeutic phase plays a pivotal role in improving survival outcomes. The widespread adoption of recommended sequential neoadjuvant therapy (e.g., chemoradiotherapy [CRT]), total mesorectal excision (TME), and postoperative adjuvant chemotherapy has greatly enhanced the pathologic complete response (pCR) rate and disease‐free survival (DFS) [1, 2], preserving organ function and enhancing patients' quality of life. Besides, patients who achieve a clinical complete response (cCR) following neoadjuvant therapy may benefit from a watch‐and‐wait (W&W) strategy while maintaining long‐term disease control [3]. In recent years, total neoadjuvant therapy (TNT), which shifts adjuvant chemotherapy to the preoperative setting, has gained increasing recognition for its potential to improve treatment adherence and reduce the risk of recurrence [4, 5]. However, optimizing TNT strategies, including the determination of treatment regimens and intensities, remains a critical area of investigation, especially in the context of precision medicine.

In the RAPIDO trial, the addition of consolidative CAPOX (capecitabine and oxaliplatin) or FOLFOX (leucovorin, fluorouracil and oxaliplatin) to short‐course radiation therapy in the preoperative setting for LARC significantly reduced the 3‐year treatment failure rate compared with standard neoadjuvant CRT, although overall survival (OS) remained similar [6]. Given the pursuit of improved disease control, the PRODIGE 23 trial, which incorporated 6 cycles of preoperative FOLFIRINOX (fluorouracil, leucovorin, irinotecan, and oxaliplatin) in the TNT strategy, achieved a primary endpoint of 3‐year DFS [7] and significantly improved 7‐year OS in patients with cT3 or cT4M0 rectal cancer [8]. Nevertheless, previous trials investigating irinotecan revealed notable occurrences of neutropenia and diarrhea, leading to poor tolerability and frequent treatment interruptions [9], which was also noted in the PRODIGE 23 trial using a fixed dose of irinotecan [7]. These findings suggest that irinotecan‐based treatment, while offering a promising strategy to improve surgical outcomes and prognosis of LARC, could be tailored to address these challenges.

The CinClare trial utilized a perioperative multi‐agent approach with an irinotecan‐based preoperative CRT regimen, one cycle of preoperative irinotecan‐based chemotherapy consolidation, and an adjuvant platinum‐based regimen. This approach could avoid possible chemotherapy resistance and ensure the tolerability of adjuvant therapy without cumulative platinum toxicity concerns. The dose of irinotecan in the neoadjuvant treatment was adjusted through a genotype‐guided dose adjustment scheme based on the association of uridine diphosphate glucuronosyltransferase 1A1 (UGT1A1) genotype with the maximum tolerated dose (MTD) of irinotecan [10, 11, 12]. The CinClare trial demonstrated that genotype‐guided irinotecan‐based neoadjuvant therapy significantly increased the pCR rate compared to platinum‐based regimens, with the majority of patients achieving TME [13]. Based on these findings, the Chinese Society of Clinical Oncology guidelines have recommended, since 2020, genotype‐guided irinotecan plus capecitabine in neoadjuvant CRT for LARC [14, 15]. However, long‐term benefits following genotype‐guided irinotecan treatment, TME, and platinum‐based adjuvant chemotherapy remain to be fully verified.

The present article reports the five‐year follow‐up results of the CinClare study, evaluating the long‐term clinical benefits conferred by irinotecan‐based neoadjuvant CRT guided by UGT1A1 genotype for patients with LARC. This study also assessed whether the observed improvement in pCR could translate into a long‐term survival benefit.

METHODS

Study design and patients

The CinClare trial was a multicenter, open‐label phase III study [13]. Key eligible criteria included patients with an age of 18‐75 years, Karnofsky performance status (KPS) score ≥ 70, histopathologically confirmed rectal adenocarcinoma located ≤ 10 cm above the anal verge, clinical stage T3‐4 and/or N+ disease, and a UGT1A1 genotype *1/*1 or *1/*28. The exclusion criteria included history of malignancy; previous chemotherapy or pelvic radiotherapy; pregnancy or lactation; known dihydropyrimidine dehydrogenase deficiency; and serious illness such as unstable angina or myocardial infarction within the previous 12 months.

Patients recruited from November 2015 to December 2017 were randomized 1:1 to the CapIriRT group or the CapRT group through a web interface (varied permuted block design with a block size of 2‐6). Randomization was stratified by UGT1A1 genotype, clinical T stage, and tumor distance from the anal verge. Masking was not performed for this open‐label study. Patients in the CapIriRT group received CRT, which included pelvic radiation at a dose of 50 Gy/25 fractions, with concurrent capecitabine (625 mg/m2 twice daily for 5 days per week) and irinotecan (80 mg/m2 weekly for patients with UGT1A1 *1/*1 genotype and 65 mg/m2 weekly for UGT1A1 *1/*28). CRT was followed by one cycle of XELIRI, which included irinotecan (200 mg/m2 on day 1) and capecitabine (1000 mg/m2 twice daily on days 1‐14). Patients in the CapRT group received CRT, which included pelvic radiation at a dose of 50 Gy/25 fractions with concurrent capecitabine (825 mg/m2 twice daily, 5 days per week). CRT was followed by one cycle of XELOX (oxaliplatin plus capecitabine), including oxaliplatin (130 mg/m2 on day 1) and capecitabine (1000 mg/m2 twice daily on days 1‐14). Eight weeks after completion of CRT, surgery was scheduled. Regardless of the pathologic result, adjuvant XELOX chemotherapy was administered in five cycles. Additional details of the study protocol are included in the Supplementary Materials .

The protocol of the original study was approved by the central ethics committee of Fudan University Shanghai Cancer Center (approval number: 1508151‐1) and the review boards of all participating institutions. Informed consent was obtained from all patients prior to participation. The study was registered at ClinicalTrials.gov (NCT02605265).

Endpoints

The primary endpoint, pCR, was previously reported [13]. The pCR rates were 30% (53/178) and 15% (27/178) in the CapIriRT and CapRT groups, respectively (risk ratio, 1.96; 95% confidence intervals [CI], 1.30 to 2.97; P = 0.001). The present study reports secondary endpoints, including local control (LC, defined as the proportion of participants without post‐surgery pelvic failure evaluated using Kaplan‐Meier Curve), DFS (defined as the time from randomization to documented tumor recurrence or death from any cause, whichever occurred first), and OS (defined as the time from randomization to the death from any cause). Distant metastasis‐free survival (DMFS) was included as a post‐hoc endpoint to offer additional insights, defined as the time from randomization to documented distant metastasis or death from any cause, whichever occurred first.

Statistical analysis

Survival differences were assessed using the log‐rank test, and the hazard ratios (HRs) with 95% CIs were calculated using the Cox proportional hazards regression. In the presence of potential non‐proportional hazards and a limited number of outcome events, restricted mean survival time (RMST) was also applied in this study to provide more robust insights into the survival comparisons. Long‐term endpoints were further analyzed in exploratory subgroup analyses stratified by different genotypes, tumor distance from the anal verge, clinical T stage, sex, age, and carcinoembryonic antigen (CEA). Elevated CEA levels were defined as higher than the respective laboratory thresholds of the participating research centers. Furthermore, patients who achieved pCR were compared with those who did not. P < 0.05 was considered statistically significant. SPSS (version 28.0, IBM Corp., Armonk, NY, USA, RRID: SCR_016479) was used for statistical analysis.

RESULTS

Patient characteristics

A total of 356 patients were included in this analysis, with a data cut‐off date of June 2023. The median follow‐up duration for all patients was 60 months (range, 1‐80). The patients' baseline demographic and clinical characteristics were well balanced, as shown in Supplementary Table S1 . Patients with the UGT1A1 *1/*1 genotype accounted for 74.7% of the cohort (n = 266), while patients with the UGT1A1 *1/*28 genotype accounted for 25.3% (n = 90). The baseline characteristics were also comparable between the two treatment groups in both genotype subgroups (UGT1A1 *1/*1 and UGT1A1 *1/*28), with no statistically significant differences. There were 157 and 154 patients undergoing surgery in the CapIriRT and CapRT groups, respectively. Among them, 31 (19.7%) patients in the CapIriRT group and 17 (11.0%) patients in the CapRT group received capecitabine/FU adjuvant chemotherapy, while 17 (10.8%) and 23 (14.9%), respectively, did not receive adjuvant chemotherapy. The pathologic stage and tumor regression grade were evaluated after surgery, and details of these pathologic results and adjuvant chemotherapy are shown in Supplementary Table S2 .

Despite a lack of statistical significance through log‐rank tests (all P > 0.05), the survival curves of these endpoints consistently show a clear separation between CapIriRT and CapRT groups (Figure 1), suggesting a long‐term clinical benefit of CapIriRT therapy. Long‐term outcomes in the UGT1A1 *1/*1 and UGT1A1 *1/*28 genotype subgroups

As shown in Table 1 and Figure 3 A‐D, in the UGT1A1 *1/*1 genotype subgroup, patients receiving CapIriRT demonstrated improved long‐term outcomes, with significantly prolonged DMFS, DFS, and OS compared to those undergoing CapRT treatment (all p < 0.05), as well as a numerical advantage in LC (p = 0.129). The 5‐year rates of LC, DMFS, DFS, and OS rates in CapIriRT and CapRT groups were 97.4% vs. 93.6%, 87.1% vs. 75.4%, 81.1% vs. 68.3%, and 87.0% vs. 74.5%, respectively.

On the other hand, in the UGT1A1 *1/*28 genotype subgroup (Table 1 and Figure 3 E‐H), no statistically significant difference was observed (p > 0.05). The 5‐year rates for CapIriRT vs. CapRT were 90.5% vs. 95.1% for LC, 74.9% vs. 85.6% for DMFS, 67.7% vs. 77.2% for DFS, and 70.9% vs. 80.2% for OS in patients receiving CapIriRT and CapRT, respectively.

Among patients with the UGT1A1 *1/*1 genotype, 36.8% (49/133) of those in the CapIriRT group experienced grade 3‐4 adverse events, compared to 6.8% (9/133) in the CapRT group (Supplementary Table S3 ). For patients with the UGT1A1 *1/*28 genotype, grade 3‐4 adverse events were reported in 42.2% (19/45) of the CapIriRT group versus 4.4% (2/45) in the CapRT group. Hematologic toxicities, including leukopenia and neutropenia, were predominant.

Prognosis of patients with or without pCR

In the modified intent to treat (mITT) population, patients achieving pCR had significantly longer DFS and OS compared to non‐pCR patients (Figure 4 ). The 5‐year DFS and 5‐year OS rates were 89.6% vs. 72.5% (p = 0.003) and 94.4% vs. 78.6% (p = 0.004), respectively. In addition, among patients who achieved pCR, outcomes were similar between CapIriRT and CapRT groups, with 5‐year DFS and 5‐year OS rates of 90.2% vs. 88.6% and 93.9% vs. 95.2%, respectively (both p > 0.05). Among patients who did not achieve pCR, those who received CapIriRT exhibited a better trend in terms of LC, DMFS, and DFS compared to those who received CapRT (all p > 0.05) (Figure 5 ).

DISCUSSION

This study presented updated evidence supporting the long‐term survival benefits of UGT1A1 genotype‐guided irinotecan‐based neoadjuvant regimen in LARC. Irinotecan plus capecitabine‐based CRT, followed by one cycle of consolidative irinotecan‐based preoperative chemotherapy, was associated with improved LC, DMFS, DFS, and OS. Particularly, RMST analysis indicated notable differences in DFS and OS. The 5‐year DFS, DMFS, and OS benefits from CapIriRT treatment were also achieved in the UGT1A1 *1/*1 genotype subgroup. Furthermore, patients who achieved pCR had favorable long‐term DFS and OS. These findings highlight the value of UGT1A1 genotype‐guided irinotecan‐based neoadjuvant regimen and contribute to the ongoing discussion regarding the personalization of the LARC treatment.

The optimization of the preoperative treatment strategy is critical for enhancing long‐term prognosis in LARC. Previous studies of TNT strategies mostly focused on moving postoperative chemotherapy cycles to the preoperative setting, such as consolidative CAPOX or FOLFOX following radiation in the RAPIDO trial [6], and preoperative FOLFIRINOX followed by capecitabine‐based CRT in the PRODIGE 23 trials [7, 8]. However, the role of chemotherapy as induction or consolidation in the RAPIDO trial [6] and the PRODIGE 23 trial varied [7, 8], raising the debate on the timing of chemotherapy. In addition, as a component of neoadjuvant therapy, the optimization of the chemotherapy backbone in CRT is also critical, particularly regarding the inclusion of a second agent. In light of reports suggesting improved outcomes with irinotecan over oxaliplatin [16], the combination of irinotecan plus capecitabine and radiation therapy was utilized in the CinClare trial using a UGT1A1 genotype‐guided dose adjustment scheme. The additional one cycle of preoperative irinotecan and capecitabine after CRT in the CinClare trial was supported by the recommendation of consolidation chemotherapy after radiation from the American Society of Clinical Oncology guideline based on CAO/ARO/AIO‐12 and OPRA trials [17, 18, 19, 20]. At the time of study design, the standard surgical interval after CRT was 6 to 8 weeks, with concerns that prolonged delays could increase surgical complexity. Our previous phase II study suggested that adding one cycle of chemotherapy within this interval was safe and effective, with the potential to enhance tumor regression [20]. While additional cycles of preoperative chemotherapy might theoretically offer greater tumor control, this must be weighed against the risks of increased toxicity, impaired tolerability, and reduced treatment compliance. Furthermore, the multi‐agent strategy with oxaliplatin used in the adjuvant setting instead of continuing the irinotecan‐based regimen, was designed to mitigate chemotherapy resistance and improve efficacy. Irinotecan is not recommended as part of standard adjuvant chemotherapy for colorectal cancer. This is supported by findings from randomized controlled trials including CALGB 89803 [21], PETACC‐3 [22], and a study by the Hellenic Cooperative Oncology Group [23], all of which demonstrated that adding irinotecan in the adjuvant setting led to increased toxicity without a substantial survival benefit. In this study, the perioperative multi‐agent strategy achieved a 5‐year OS rate of 82.9%, which is consistent with the 5‐year OS rates reported in the PRODIGE 23 (86.9%) [8] and RAPIDO (81.7%) [24] trials, although cross‐trial comparisons should be interpreted with caution due to differences in study design and populations. The observed OS rate difference of 6.8% between our study groups (82.9% vs. 76.1%) is clinically meaningful, particularly when viewed alongside the 5‐year OS rates of the PRODIGE 23 study (86.9% vs. 79.9%) [8]. Despite these promising findings, the study was powered to assess the primary endpoint of pCR rather than OS, which might explain the lack of statistical significance in the log‐rank test for OS. In recognition of this limitation, we performed an RMST analysis, which yielded a p value of 0.050, indicating a borderline difference in OS. Additionally, we evaluated the statistical power for detecting the observed difference in OS, which was insufficient, not meeting the conventional threshold of 80% for statistical power. However, the difference in 5‐year OS rates between the groups, coupled with the significant pCR improvement and the separation of DFS and DMFS curves, supported the survival benefit associated with CapIriRT. These findings contribute to the growing evidence base supporting genotype‐guided irinotecan‐based neoadjuvant therapy in LARC, particularly for patients with the UGT1A1 *1/*1 genotype. The observed long‐term benefits and manageable safety profile may help inform future updates of clinical practice guidelines, which should be further validated.

Several studies have indicated the potential role of UGT1A1 polymorphism in treatment outcomes and survival of patients with metastatic colorectal cancer [25, 26]. Given the limited pCR results with reduced dose of irinotecan in the RTOG 0247 trial [16], and the consideration of tolerability, the genotype‐based personalized dosing may be the solution. However, the long‐term outcomes observed in the UGT1A1 *1/*28 subgroup receiving CapIriRT raised important considerations. One potential explanation is the reduced irinotecan dosing in this subgroup, which was implemented to reduce toxicity risk. Prior studies have shown a dose‐response relationship between irinotecan exposure and therapeutic efficacy [27]. Clinically, this highlights the importance of UGT1A1 genotyping not only for toxicity risk mitigation but also for guiding treatment decisions. Nonetheless, due to the relatively small sample size of the UGT1A1 *1/*28 subgroup in this study, these findings should be interpreted cautiously and require further validation in larger, prospective cohorts. On the other hand, patients with the UGT1A1 *1/*1 genotype received a higher dose of irinotecan, since this UGT1A1 polymorphism was previously shown to be associated with less toxicity of irinotecan [28, 29]. In the analysis of the UGT1A1 *1/*1 subgroup, the 5‐year DMFS, 5‐year DFS and 5‐year OS rates of the CapIriRT group were higher than the CapRT group. In the meantime, no significant survival differences were observed in the UGT1A1 *1/*28 subgroup, although this might be attributed to the relatively smaller number of patients in this subset. Regarding the risk of severe toxicity, the incidence of grade 3 or 4 adverse events during CRT in the CapIriRT group was limited to 68 patients (38%) in our prior report, which contrasts with the rate observed in the CapRT group (6%) [13]. Despite this, the majority of patients in the CapIriRT group successfully completed surgery, and the surgical resection rate was similar to the CapRT group [13]. Furthermore, the proportion of patients in the CapIriRT group who had received adjuvant chemotherapy (89.2%) was slightly higher than that in the CapRT group (85.1%). The number of adjuvant chemotherapy cycles in the two groups was also comparable (median [range]: 5 [1, 2, 3, 4, 5, 6, 7, 8] versus 5 [1, 2, 3, 4, 5, 6, 7, 8]). These observations suggested that compared with the CapRT regimen, the toxicity of CapIriRT had no additional impact on the tolerability and compliance with surgery and adjuvant therapy. Additionally, the rate of grade ≥3 toxicity observed with CapIriRT is favorable when considering the findings of recent TNT regimens investigated in the trials, including PRODIGE 23 (37.2%) [7], CAO/ARO/AIO‐12 (36.4%) [18, 19], and RAPIDO (48%) [6]. Considering the substantial benefits in long‐term efficacy outcomes, the safety profile of CapIriRT is deemed acceptable. Accordingly, the CapIriRT regimen, with its biomarker‐guided design, could be reasonably considered an effective therapeutic option in addition to current TNT regimens for LARC.

The CapIriRT regimen was associated with increased pCR rates and favorable long‐term outcomes, which could be attributable to several key features of the treatment regimen: First, the CapIriRT group incorporated irinotecan into neoadjuvant CRT, employing a genotype‐guided dosing scheme that ensures safety and enhances patient tolerance to treatment. Second, the CapIriRT regimen included an additional cycle of chemotherapy in the interval between neoadjuvant CRT and surgery, administered using the same genotype‐guided dosing approach. This modification aligns with the recommendation of TNT strategies in LARC [4]. It is noteworthy that the GCR‐3 trial, which employed TNT with platinum‐based chemotherapy and CRT, did not demonstrate improvements in 5‐year DFS or OS compared to the standard of care [30]. This lack of benefit from platinum in the GCR‐3 trial highlights the potential utility of irinotecan‐based doublet chemotherapy in the CapIriRT regimen. Besides, the CapIriRT regimen used a multi‐agent approach in the perioperative periods to avoid possible chemotherapy resistance. Given that irinotecan is not recommended in adjuvant therapy, the use of preoperative irinotecan‐based treatment, followed by platinum‐based adjuvant chemotherapy, may reduce cumulative toxicity and improve tolerability to adjuvant therapy, while maintaining therapeutic intensity.

The relationship between pCR and OS remains under discussion. An individual patient data (IPD) meta‐analysis has shown that patients achieving pCR after neoadjuvant therapy have significantly better 10‐year OS and 5‐year DFS compared to those who do not achieve pCR [31]. However, another IPD meta‐analysis reported a poor correlation between pCR and 5‐year OS [32]. In this study, patients who achieved pCR had longer OS, suggesting that increased pCR rates observed with irinotecan‐based therapy might be associated with improved survival outcomes. Nevertheless, the observed improvement in pCR warrants continued follow‐up to determine whether these findings translate into sustained OS benefit.

This study has several limitations. Firstly, although UGT1A1*6 is a clinically relevant polymorphism in East Asian populations, present in approximately 15% of individuals [33, 34], the polymorphism of UGT1A1*6 was not assessed in this study. This was due to the limited clinical awareness and evidence regarding UGT1A1*6 at the time of trial design, when it was not yet part of routine clinical testing. Subsequent pharmacokinetic research by our team [35] found that increased mutation sites of UGT1A1*6 was associated with elevated plasma SN‐38 concentrations, increased toxicity and higher response rate to irinotecan. As a result, UGT1A1*6 genotyping has been incorporated into our ongoing prospective studies (e.g., CARTOnG‐2001 [NCT04443543] and APRAM [NCT05669092]), with irinotecan doses stratified accordingly. Although the lack of UGT1A1*6 data in this trial might introduce some uncertainty regarding toxicity and efficacy assessment, the randomized study design likely mitigated potential bias by balancing patient distribution across treatment arms. Secondly, the sample size was calculated based on the primary endpoint of pCR; thus, the study was not sufficiently powered to detect statistically significant differences in long‐term endpoints such as DFS or OS. Although RMST analysis offered additional interpretive value, the absence of statistically significant results from conventional log‐rank tests should be interpreted with caution.

CONCLUSIONS

The addition of irinotecan, guided by UGT1A1 genotype, to a standard capecitabine‐based regimen can provide clinical benefits by improving LC, DMFS, DFS, and OS for LARC. Among patients with the UGT1A1 *1/*1 genotype, the addition of irinotecan was associated with improved DMFS, DFS, and OS. The potential survival benefits associated with achieving pCR warrant further attention. Furthermore, the role of perioperative triplet chemotherapy in improving pCR rates and survival merits further investigation, particularly in large‐scale, real‐world studies.
