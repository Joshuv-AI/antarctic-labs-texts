The economic burden of outpatient chlamydia infections in Southern China: a cross-sectional study, 2021–2023
Authors: Jinshen Wang, Haiyi Li, Shujie Huang, Peizhen Zhao, Cheng Wang
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
Abstract AbstractObjectiveThe prevalence of chlamydia infections in China is rapidly increasing, which may lead to substantial economic burden. However, studies on the economic burden of chlamydia infections in China are limited. This study aims to measure the economic burden of outpatient chlamydia infections in Guangdong Province, China.DesignData on chlamydia outpatient costs were collected through outpatient medical record systems in healthcare facilities and a cross-sectional survey.SettingOutpatient departments of 15 hospitals in Guangdong Province, China.ParticipantsPatients diagnosed with chlamydia at the investigated medical institutions between 1 January 2021, and 31 December 2023.Main outcome measuresThe direct economic burden (medical costs+non-medical costs) was assessed using a bottom-up approach, productivity loss was evaluated through the human capital approach and the intangible burden was assessed by the contingency valuation method.ResultsThe annual per capita (per diagnosed outpatient case of chlamydia infection) medical costs were $37.74 (95% CI 35.38 to 40.10), with costs for patients with a co-diagnosis of pelvic inflammatory disease (PID) reaching $136.23 (95% CI 118.15 to 154.31). Annual per capita non-medical costs were $5.68 (95% CI 4.06 to 7.30) and productivity losses were $30.70 (95% CI 27.52 to 33.88). Ultimately, the per capita direct economic burden for outpatients with chlamydia was $43.42, and the total economic burden was $74.12. The per capita intangible cost was $132.49 (95% CI 110.12 to 154.86). Following the adjustment for covariates, medical costs were significantly associated with a co-diagnosis of PID (adjusted OR (aOR) = 8.07, 95% CI 5.23 to 12.31). The intangible cost was associated with urinary tract symptoms (aOR=1.56, 95% CI 1.01 to 2.42), abnormal vaginal bleeding (aOR=2.44, 95% CI 1.05 to 5.64), and high-risk sexual behaviour (aOR=5.63, 95% CI 1.63 to 17.63).ConclusionIn Southern China, chlamydia infections complicated by PID entail a markedly greater medical cost than uncomplicated infections. Effective prevention and treatment strategies are needed to prevent the progression of chlamydia infections to PID and thereby reduce the associated economic burden.Keywords: China, Cross-Sectional Studies, EPIDEMIOLOGY, Health Care Costs

Introduction

Chlamydia is a prevalent sexually transmitted infection (STI) that commonly affects adolescents and adults.1 In 2020, an estimated 128 million cases of chlamydia occurred worldwide among individuals aged 15–49 years.2 In recent years, China has witnessed a rapid increase in chlamydia incidence epidemic. According to the Chinese National STIs Surveillance System, the reported chlamydia incidence rate in China increased from 37.18 cases per 100 000 individuals in 2015 to 55.32 per 100 000 individuals in 2019, representing an average annual increase of 10.44%.3 Untreated chlamydia infection can lead to serious complications such as female infertility or male epididymitis,4 and may also elevate the risk of acquiring and transmitting HIV.5

In addition to the health burden, chlamydia infection also imposes a significant economic burden. A study conducted in the United States found that the lifetime medical costs associated with chlamydia infections amounted to $691.3 million in 2018, while productivity losses reached $528 million. The most recent research on the economic burden of chlamydia in China estimates that the total lifetime cost associated with chlamydia infections among individuals aged 18–49 years in Shandong Province amounts to approximately $41 million.6 However, to the best of our knowledge, current studies of the burden of chlamydia in China are limited to medical costs and lack data on direct non-medical costs, productivity losses and intangible costs, failing to comprehensively assess the economic burden of chlamydia.

Given the predominant role of outpatient care in the management of chlamydia infections,7 8 this study aims to evaluate the economic burden associated with outpatient visits for chlamydia infections in Southern China from a societal perspective,9 10 including direct medical and non-medical costs, productivity losses for both patients and their informal caregivers and intangible costs. The findings of this study will provide valuable data to inform future screening and intervention policies related to chlamydia.

Methods

Research sites

Guangdong Chlamydia Intervention Program is a large-scale programme aimed at improving the comprehensive prevention and treatment of chlamydia and developing a new model for prevention and control at both local and national levels. It was initiated by the Health Commission of Guangdong Province in 2022. Five cities or districts of different economic levels were selected as the implementation sites: Nanshan, Xiangzhou, Puning, Xinxing and Xinyi Districts, covering the Pearl River Delta as well as eastern, western and northern Guangdong. Based on previous research,11 three regions (Puning, Xinyi and Xinxing) with per capita disposable income below 76% of the national average in 2022 were classified as low-income regions, while two regions (Nanshan and Xiangzhou) with income above 130% of the national average were classified as high-income regions; no mid-income sites were designated in this phase. Detailed sociodemographic information on the surveyed regions can be found in online supplemental appendix table 1. We contacted all local medical institutions providing STIs diagnostic and treatment services. The selected institutions were required to meet the following criteria: (1) reported more than 50 cases of chlamydia from 2021 to 2022, and (2) maintained complete outpatient records from 2021 to 2022. Of the 27 facilities identified, 15 met these criteria and were included in the study (online supplemental appendix table 2). All 15 hospitals granted permission for the research team to extract and analyse de-identified patient data from their records.

Patient and public involvement

Patients were not involved in the design, implementation or in setting the research question or the outcome measures.

Indicator calculation and data collection

Our study received ethical approval from the Dermatology hospital of Southern Medical University (GDDHLS-2022110). The economic burden includes three parts: direct economic burden (including medical cost and non-medical cost), productivity loss and intangible cost.

Medical cost

Calculation

The bottom-up approach12 was applied to calculate the medical cost. Medical costs included the costs of registration, laboratory test, medication and treatment for chlamydia outpatient.

Data sources: hospital information system

The patient’s medical cost and annual times of medical visit originated from the Hospital information system of selected medical institutions. Patients with chlamydia at the genital anatomical site were identified from the medical system using International Classification of Diseases Tenth Revision (ICD-10) codes for chlamydia. Exclusion criteria for outpatient cases were under 16 years old and incomplete medical cost information. To ensure costs unrelated to chlamydia were not included, we included only costs of outpatient where a patient received a chlamydia diagnosis (chlamydia diagnosis with no other ICD-10 codes) or chlamydia co-diagnosis of pelvic inflammatory disease (PID) (code for both chlamydia and PID and no other ICD-10 codes) (refer online supplemental appendix table 3 for specific disease codes).

Non-medical cost, productivity loss and intangible cost

Calculation

The bottom-up approach12 was applied to calculate the non-medical cost. Non-medical costs include the fees for transportation, meals and accommodation. The calculation formulas for non-medical cost were:

Non-medical cost=non-medical cost per medical visit * average annual number of medical visits

Productivity costs included the loss of productivity caused by the absence of patients and their accompanying family members from work because of illness. The human capital approach was applied:

Productivity loss = (patient’s work time loss(year) * patient’s annual income+relatives’ accompanying time(year) * annual per capita disposable income in the local city) * average annual number of medical visits

The intangible cost refers to the psychological loss caused by the suffering of chlamydia-related illness, quantified by willingness to pay using the conditional assessment method. Participants were asked to indicate how much they would like to pay for the treatment of chlamydia-related diseases to avoid psychological stress and burden. Referring to previous studies,13 the questions were set as follows: If possible, how much were you willing to pay an amount of money for not being infected with chlamydia? Multiple-choice format was used: I don't want to pay, 1 day, 3 days, 1 week, 2 weeks, 1 month and other amounts of household income. Besides, given previous research13 suggesting that the willingness to pay results would be better reported in the median values than the means, we will present the intangible cost of chlamydia in both mean and median values in our results.

Data sources: questionnaire survey and statistical yearbook

Due to restrictions related to patient confidentiality and significant population mobility within the surveyed regions, it was challenging to conduct follow-up visits for patients who sought medical care between 2021 and 2022. Therefore, newly diagnosed and follow-up outpatients from 1 July 2023 to 31 December 2023, in surveyed medical institutions were investigated to get patient’s non-medical cost per medical visit, work time loss, annual income, willingness to pay and relatives’ accompanying time. The inclusion criteria were as follows: (1) aged ≥16 years and above and (2) positive chlamydia nucleic acid test, or positive chlamydia antigen test or positive cytological test result was confirmed by the healthcare facility where this study was conducted. Consecutive sampling was employed: outpatient physicians or nurses approached eligible patients in the order of their attendance, explained the study purpose and procedures and invited them to complete an online questionnaire until the target sample size was reached. Prior to starting the questionnaire, each participant provided informed consent by clicking an electronic consent box on the survey form. Only those who opted in by agreeing to participate could proceed with the survey.

The structural questionnaire used to collect data included three parts: (1) sociodemographic characteristics, including hospital, outpatient category, gender, birth, ethnicity, location, marriage, education and occupation, (2) information on medical visits and non-medical costs, (3) patients' willingness to pay. (4) Symptoms and reasons for seeking medical care (refer online supplemental appendix 4 for details). Following the categorisation outlined in the Chinese guidelines for the treatment of STIs, patients were classified into those presenting with chlamydia-related symptoms and those without symptoms (refer to online supplemental appendix table 5 for details). The questionnaire items were drafted with reference to previous research14 and modified according to the suggestions of experts. To test the feasibility of the questionnaire, pilot surveys were conducted among 20 chlamydia patients in Puning and Xinyi.

Preliminary survey data indicate that the direct non-medical costs average 6.92 dollars with a SD of 7.06; the indirect economic burden averages 15.91 dollars with a SD of 15.97 and the intangible cost averages 41.33 dollars with a SD of 43.35. Using 20% of the preliminary survey mean as the allowable error margin and accounting for an estimated non-response rate of 10%, the required sample size for each economic burden was calculated. The largest base size across outcomes was n0=106; allowing for 10% non-response yielded 118. To achieve comparable precision within each income stratum (high vs low), we targeted 118 per stratum (total 236). Because, participants were clustered within hospitals, we applied a conservative design effect=1.63,16 giving an operational target of ≈385 respondents. Allocation across strata was proportional to outpatient volumes observed in the pilot presurvey (high-income:low-income=3:2), resulting in targets of 231 and 154, respectively. Given the consecutive (non-probability) sampling, this planning size guides operational precision and does not confer a probability-based margin of error.

The annual per capita disposable income data of the investigation sites are from the Guangdong Statistical Yearbook.

Statistical analysis

Costs between 2021 and 2023 in this investigation were recorded in renminbi (RMB) and adjusted to the RMB value in 2022 according to the inflation rate. For comparison of values with studies abroad, we used the average exchange rate of 1 RMB (¥) = 0.1485 US ($) by the Bank of China in 2022. Descriptive statistics were used to summarise the distribution of population baseline characteristics in each survey year. In univariate analysis, the Mann-Whitney U test was used to compare differences between two groups, while the Kruskal-Wallis rank sum test was used in multiple groups. Multivariable logistic regressions were conducted to explore economic factors associated with chlamydia. In the multivariable models of medical costs, gender, age and day of visit were adjusted. In other multivariable analyses of other economic burdens, age, gender, ethnicity, education level and marital status were adjusted. The statistical significance test level was P<0.05. All analyses were performed using R 4.1.2.

Results

Medical cost

A total of 2288 patients diagnosed with chlamydia infection between 1 January 2021 and 31 December 2022 were included to estimate direct medical costs (figure 1). Among them, more than half were from the high-income region (56.3%, 1289/2288), over three-fourths were females (82.6%, 1892/2288) and over two-thirds were aged between 20 and 35 years (68.2%, 1558/2288). More than half of the patients sought care at maternal and child health hospitals (52.7%, 1206/2288). Nearly one-tenth of patients (9.0, 205/2288) had a co-diagnosis of PID. The average annual number of healthcare visits per infected individual was 1.4 times. Of these, patients from low-income region averaged 1.59 visits, significantly higher than the high-income region (1.25). Besides, patients seeking care at the centre for STIs and chronic disease control averaged 1.69 visits, significantly higher than other healthcare facilities (1.35~1.41). The average medical cost per patient was $37.74 (95% CI 35.38 to 40.10), with a median cost of $23.31. The medical costs in patients who were co-diagnosed with PID ($136.23, 95% CI 118.15 to 154.31) were much higher than in patients who were only diagnosed with chlamydia ($27.52, 95% CI 26.46 to 28.59) (P＜0.05) (table 1).

Direct non-medical cost, productivity loss and intangible cost

Between 1 July and 31 December 2023, the survey link was opened 834 times, and a total of 430 questionnaires were submitted. After eligibility screening, 418 patients were included in the analysis to assess the remaining components of the economic burden of chlamydia infection (figure 2). Among them, the demographic distribution in terms of geographical origin, gender ratio and age structure was similar to that of patients surveyed for medical cost. Additionally, the majority of patients were Han Chinese (97.4%, 407/418), with over half having a high school education or above (56.9%, 238/418), nearly two-thirds being registered residents of the local city (65.3%, 273/418) and married (71.5%, 299/418). More than half of the patients had a monthly income below $700 (52.1%, 218/418) and were employed as office workers or in commercial services (53.7%, 224/418), seeking care at gynaecology or obstetrics departments (50.2%, 210/418). The primary reasons for male patients seeking care were urethral symptoms (55.80%), partner diagnosed with an STI (19.20%) and ever engaged in high-risk sexual behaviour (15.40%). Female patients primarily sought care due to testing positive for chlamydia during routine examinations (37.9%), urethral symptoms (33.80%) and abdominal pain or dyspareunia. Over one-third of patients sought care without symptoms associated with chlamydia infection (36.12%, 151/418) (table 2).

Total economic burden

The direct economic includes the medical and non-medical cost, and the total economic burden typically includes only the direct economic burden and productivity loss.13 Therefore, the per capita direct economic burden for outpatient visits by chlamydia in Guangdong Province was $43.42, and the total economic burden was $74.12.

Factors associated with medical cost, direct non-medical cost, productivity loss and intangible cost

In the multivariable model adjusted for age, gender and year, medical cost was found to be associated with co-diagnosis of PID (adjusted OR (aOR)=8.07, 95% CI 5.23 to 12.31). After adjusting for age, ethnicity, marital status, education level, monthly income and gender, non-medical was found to be associated with occupation (office clerk/technical staff: aOR=2.03, 95% CI 1.01 to 4.07; business services: aOR=2.72, 95% CI 1.19 to 6.23). Productivity loss was found corrected with occupation was office clerk/technical staff (aOR=4.31, 95% CI 2.03 to 9.13), occupation was housewife (aOR=3.48, 95% CI 1.72 to 7.06), occupation was business services (aOR=6.18, 95% CI 2.56 to 14.97), occupation was unemployment/retirement (aOR=3.71, 95% CI 1.43 to 9.64), visits in the fields of gynaecology/obstetrics (aOR=9.01, 95% CI 2.62 to 30.99) or reproductive health and infertility care (aOR=6.34, 95% CI 1.82 to 22.09) (table 5).

Discussion

The escalating prevalence of chlamydia infection poses a growing economic burden on populations. Our study comprehensively assesses the economic burden incurred by outpatient visitors with chlamydia infection in Guangdong Province, China. We extend the existing literature by estimating the productivity losses and intangible costs resulting from chlamydia infection and analysing relevant influencing factors. These findings provide crucial baseline data for formulating and evaluating strategies for chlamydia prevention.

Our study estimated the average outpatient medical cost per case of chlamydia infection to be $37.74, with a cost of $30.15 for males. This cost is substantially higher than the lifetime medical cost for Chinese males with chlamydia, previously estimated at approximately $15.36 in 20186. The observed discrepancy is primarily attributable to the fact that the medical costs in our study were derived from field surveys, while prior estimates were based on standard treatment protocols in China, which included only the cost of polymerase chain reaction testing for chlamydia and the expenses for azithromycin. Additionally, we found that chlamydia patients co-diagnosed with PID incurred significantly higher medical costs, reaching $136.23, which aligns with the outpatient treatment costs for PID reported in other Chinese studies (approximately $131.00).17 PID is a common complication of untreated chlamydia, with an estimated 16% of untreated infections progressing to PID.18 Since most chlamydia infections are asymptomatic, detection poses a significant challenge.19 In countries such as the United Kingdom20 and the United States,21 widespread screening programmes for sexually active young people and high-risk populations have been implemented, leading to early detection and treatment of asymptomatic infections, thereby reducing the incidence of chlamydia-related complications such as PID. However, China currently lacks a national screening programme for chlamydia. This underscores the need to develop a tailored screening strategy in China to mitigate chlamydia-associated complications and reduce the economic burden on patients.

This study calculated the productivity loss of outpatients with chlamydia infection to account for approximately 41.4% of the total economic burden, amounting to $30.70, which is significantly lower than the $206 reported in the United States.22 This discrepancy can be attributed to two primary reasons. First, due to differences in healthcare systems,23 24 the productivity losses caused by chlamydia infections vary across countries. For instance, in our study, the average work time lost per patient was calculated to be 5.68 hours, which is lower than the previously reported 7.68 hours in the United States. Second, the difference is attributable to varying economic levels. The average hourly wage used in the US study was $27.30,22 substantially higher than the income levels of most participants in China surveyed for our study.

Our study found that the median intangible cost of outpatient chlamydia infections was $29.96, while the average intangible burden amounted to $132.49. Previous research13 suggests that using the contingent valuation method to assess the intangible burden of STIs is more reliable when reported through median values. In our study, the distribution of the asymptomatic burden of chlamydia was right-skewed, indicating that the median provides a more accurate representation of the data. Additionally, individuals with a history of high-risk sexual behaviour experienced higher intangible costs compared with those without such behaviours, likely due to more frequent medical visits, heightened concerns about STIs and the social stigma associated with these behaviours.25 26 In countries such as Australia27 28 and Sweden,29 the implementation of STIs risk assessment tools, promotion of self-sampling in non-clinical settings for chlamydia testing, and electronic notification systems have proven effective in increasing the willingness of high-risk individuals to seek sexual health services, while simultaneously reducing stigma. However, these measures are not widely adopted in China. As a result, future chlamydia prevention and control strategies in China should focus on developing approaches that enhance the willingness of high-risk populations to seek medical care and reduce the stigmatisation associated with such behaviours.

This study has several limitations that should be acknowledged. First, the analysis focused solely on the economic burden of outpatient chlamydia patients, excluding the financial impact of hospitalisation for complications such as ectopic pregnancy and infertility, which can arise from chlamydia infections. Second, the study relied on self-reported non-medical cost data from patients, which may introduce recall bias and affect the accuracy of the estimates. Third, the questionnaire component used a consecutive sampling strategy, which may introduce self-selection bias and precludes calculation of sampling error or response rate. Therefore, the estimates of non-medical costs, productivity losses and intangible costs should be interpreted with caution. Lastly, the data were drawn from two surveys conducted at different points in time, and any direct extrapolation of findings across time periods should be approached with caution. However, the use of the same survey locations and the similarities in the sociodemographic characteristics of respondents across both surveys help to mitigate the potential impact of this temporal discrepancy.

Conclusion

In conclusion, our study provides an estimation of direct medical costs, direct non-medical costs, productivity loss and intangible costs for outpatient chlamydia treatment in Southern China and explores associated factors. The research findings expand the scarce existing data on the economic burden of chlamydia infection, which contributes to allocating resources strategically to control the epidemics of chlamydia infection and inform cost-effectiveness analyses of chlamydia prevention programmes.
