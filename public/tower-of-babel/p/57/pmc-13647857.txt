Association of bipolar disorder and schizophrenia in women with risk of gestational diabetes: a systematic review and meta-analysis
Authors: Bakhtiar Piroozi, Hamdia Mirkhan Ahmed, Hossein Safari, Hossein Moameri, Shoboo Rahmati
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundPsychiatric disorders are severe mental illnesses associated with insulin resistance, systemic inflammation, and neurohormonal dysfunction. To synthesize the available evidence and provide recommendations for the clinical management of this patient population, this study aimed to comprehensively examine and meta-analyze the association between bipolar disorder, schizophrenia, and the risk of gestational diabetes mellitus (GDM).MethodA systematic search was conducted in PubMed, Embase, Scopus, Web of Science, and Google Scholar. Studies reporting an association between pre-pregnancy bipolar disorder or schizophrenia and GDM were included. Random-effects models were used for meta-analysis, and risk ratios (RRs) with 95% confidence intervals (CIs) were calculated.ResultsAmong 10 eligible studies, the meta-analysis revealed that women with bipolar disorder (RR: 1.59; 95% CI: 1.13–2.23) and schizophrenia (RR: 2.07; 95% CI: 1.39–3.07) had a significantly higher risk of developing GDM. Evidence regarding this association after controlling for confounding factors was limited. however, pooled analysis of the few available studies showed RRs of 1.43 (95% CI: 0.83–2.38) for bipolar disorder and 1.29 (95% CI: 1.07–1.55) for schizophrenia.ConclusionsThis study provides evidence that bipolar disorder and schizophrenia are associated with an increased risk of gestational diabetes. Regular screening and management of modifiable risk factors, alongside mental health stability, are essential in women of reproductive age. Future prospective studies should investigate the underlying mechanisms of this association.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at https://doi.org/10.1186/s12884-026-09621-0.

Impact statement

Pre-pregnancy schizophrenia (RR:2.07) and bipolar disorder (RR:1.59) significantly increase gestational diabetes risk, yet these associations largely attenuate after adjusting for modifiable factors—particularly obesity, lifestyle, and socioeconomic deprivation—confirming that metabolic complications in severe mental illness are predominantly preventable rather than inevitable. This evidence mandates shifting clinical practice from passive risk identification to active intervention: mandatory early-pregnancy metabolic screening for all women with severe psychiatric disorders, integration of nutritional counseling and weight management into routine antenatal psychiatric care, and targeted health policies addressing social determinants in this vulnerable population. The substantial heterogeneity across continents underscores an urgent call for standardized, large-scale longitudinal studies in low- and middle-income countries, where the escalating dual burden of mental disorders and gestational diabetes remains dangerously under-addressed.

Introduction

Schizophrenia is a severe psychiatric disorder characterized by psychotic features, disorganized thinking and behavior, and social dysfunction, with heritability estimated between 68–89% [1]. About 1% of people suffer from this problem, which is the mental illness linked to the highest healthcare utilization [1, 2]. Life expectancy is significantly reduced by approximately 15–20 years in individuals with schizophrenia compared to the general population, highlighting a profound impact on mortality [1, 3, 4].

Diabetes and psychotic illnesses have a well-established and complex relationship [5]. Individuals with schizophrenia have a two to four times higher overall risk of type 2 diabetes than the general population [5–7]. Up to 15% of people with schizophrenia have diabetes, whereas up to 50% are obese, up to 40% have metabolic syndrome, and up to 25% have glucose intolerance [8, 9]. Although antipsychotic medications have further increased the risk of diabetes, this higher risk was notable even prior to their widespread introduction [10, 11].

With 25.3% of pregnant women diagnosed with a psychiatric disease, pregnant women with psychiatric disorders constitute a vulnerable population [12]. Obesity, diabetes, thyroid illness, epilepsy, anemia, infections, and substance use disorders are much more common in women with schizophrenia, whether or not they receive treatment [13, 14]. Many studies have examined the connection between psychotic illnesses and gestational diabetes mellitus (GDM) during pregnancy, but the results are still inconclusive [15, 16]. In a similar vein, it has been discovered that women with schizophrenia are more likely than their healthy counterparts to develop gestational diabetes [17].

On the other hand, bipolar disorder (BD) is a chronic psychiatric illness characterized by recurrent episodes of mania and depression, with a lifetime prevalence estimated at approximately 4% globally. BD is recognized as one of the leading causes of disability worldwide [18]. Emerging evidence has consistently demonstrated a significant association between BD and a state of low-grade systemic inflammation, characterized by altered levels of inflammatory markers such as C-reactive protein and cytokines [19, 20]. This chronic inflammatory state, coupled with a higher prevalence of metabolic syndrome and insulin resistance, provides a strong biological rationale for investigating BD as a potential risk factor for gestational diabetes. Given these shared pathophysiological pathways with schizophrenia, it is highly plausible that bipolar disorder is similarly closely related to the development of GDM.

Therefore, various studies have investigated the association between psychotic disorders and gestational diabetes; however, their findings remain inconsistent. This underscores the necessity of a systematic review and meta-analysis to synthesize the available evidence and provide more robust statistical conclusions [21, 22]. Furthermore, given the growing global attention to the intersection of mental health and perinatal care, this study was conducted to investigate the association between pre-existing bipolar disorder and schizophrenia with the risk of gestational diabetes.

Method

Introduction and PRISMA guideline

The meta-analysis protocol was not registered in PROSPERO or any other prospective registry. However, this systematic review adheres to the PRISMA 2020 guidelines for reporting [23]. The PRISMA framework comprises four primary stages: identification, screening, eligibility, and inclusion. During the identification phase, the number of records identified through database searches and other sources is reported. In the screening phase, duplicate records are removed, and the number of records screened and excluded is documented. The number of full-text articles assessed for eligibility and those excluded with reasons is reported in the eligibility phase. Finally, the number of studies included in the systematic review is specified in the inclusion phase.

Search strategy and database selection

A comprehensive systematic search was conducted in PubMed, Embase, Scopus, Web of Science, and the Cochrane Library, as well as the Google Scholar search engine, up to December 2025, without language or time restrictions. The full search strategies, including all Boolean operators and MeSH terms used for each database, are provided in Supplementary File 1. To maximize sensitivity and ensure comprehensive identification of relevant studies, the search strategy combined text words and medical subject headings (MeSH) with Boolean operators. Search terms included “psychotic disorder,” “psychosis,” “schizophrenia,” “gestational diabetes,” “hyperglycemia of pregnancy,” and related terms. To ensure thorough coverage of the available evidence, searches were performed without time or language limitations. The search strategy was pilot-tested and peer-reviewed after being collaboratively developed by the study team and academic librarians with expertise in systematic reviews. Supplementary search techniques, including manual screening of reference lists of retrieved articles, citation tracking, and hand-searching of specialized journals, were employed to identify additional relevant studies that may have been missed in the electronic searches. Two investigators independently performed the comprehensive search to guarantee the accuracy and completeness of study identification.

Study selection and screening process

A consistent multi-stage procedure was used for study selection. The screening process was conducted in two steps by two independent reviewers: (1) title/abstract screening and (2) full-text screening. Independent researchers performed a parallel three-stage examination of the titles, abstracts, and full texts of all identified articles using predefined inclusion and exclusion criteria. Additionally, forward and backward citation tracking was performed, and the reference lists of retrieved publications were manually reviewed. Citation snowballing and consultation with subject-matter experts were also employed to identify further relevant studies.

Data extraction

To ensure uniformity and minimize bias, two independent researchers extracted data using standardized extraction forms. Using a dual extraction approach, qualified investigators reviewed the studies in parallel, and all disagreements were resolved through consensus and discussion, with a third reviewer consulted as necessary. Data extraction was restricted to full-text published articles to ensure sufficient detail and methodological rigor. The extraction process included comprehensive documentation of study characteristics, methodologies, participant demographics, exposure variables, outcome measures, and statistical results. Quality assessment

The quality and risk of bias of the included retrospective cohort studies were independently assessed by two reviewers (B.P. and SH.R.) using the Newcastle-Ottawa Scale (NOS) adapted for cohort studies [34, 35]. This scale evaluates studies based on three main domains: (1) selection of participants (representativeness of the exposed cohort, selection of the non-exposed cohort, ascertainment of exposure, and demonstration that outcome of interest was not present at start of study) (2), comparability of cohorts based on the design or analysis (control for important confounders), and (3) outcome assessment (assessment of outcome, adequacy of follow-up duration, and adequacy of follow-up of cohorts). Studies were categorized as high quality (score ≥ 7), moderate quality (score 5–6), or low quality (score ≤ 4) based on the total score. Discrepancies between reviewers were resolved through discussion and consensus.

Statistical analysis

Statistical analyses were performed using STATA version 14.2. To estimate the effect size of the included studies, risk ratios (RRs) and their 95% confidence intervals (CIs) were calculated. To investigate potential sources of heterogeneity and moderating effects, subgroup analyses were conducted based on study design. Sensitivity analyses were performed by sequentially removing each study and recalculating the pooled effect size to assess the influence of individual studies on the overall RR. Potential publication bias was evaluated using Egger’s and Begg’s tests [36, 37].

Result

Overview of search and study characteristics

Following the review procedure outlined in Fig. 1, we selected 10 articles that met our inclusion criteria from the 1,538 articles identified through the initial literature search. Of these, six articles examined the association between schizophrenia and gestational diabetes, and seven investigated the relationship between bipolar disorder and gestational diabetes (Fig. 1). The mean age of the 7,396,619 pregnant women included in the 10 studies was 29.4 ± 5.7 years. All studies employed a retrospective cohort design. Geographically, 20% of the studies were conducted in Europe, 30% in Australia, 30% in the United States, and 20% in Asia.

Discussion

According to this systematic review and meta-analysis, women with bipolar illness and schizophrenia are more likely to develop gestational diabetes than women without these conditions. Specifically, the meta-analysis showed that women with bipolar disorder (RR: 1.59; 95% CI: 1.13–2.23) and schizophrenia (RR: 2.07; 95% CI: 1.39–3.07) had a significantly higher risk of developing GDM.

The substantial statistical heterogeneity observed in our analyses (I² > 80% for both schizophrenia and bipolar disorder) is a notable limitation that warrants careful interpretation. This high degree of heterogeneity is likely attributable to several factors, including considerable variability in study designs, sample sizes, geographic locations, diagnostic criteria for both mental disorders (ICD-9 vs. ICD-10 vs. DSM-IV) and GDM (National Diabetes Data Group vs. Carpenter-Coustan vs. IADPSG criteria), and the extent of adjustment for confounding variables such as maternal age, BMI, parity, smoking, and medication use. Due to the limited number of studies, we were unable to conduct a comprehensive meta-regression to fully explore these sources. However, our subgroup analyses (Tables 2 and 3) suggested that geographic location and sample size may contribute to the observed variations. Future research with more standardized methodologies is essential to confirm these findings and reduce heterogeneity.

Bipolar disorder and schizophrenia are among the severe mental illnesses associated with insulin resistance, systemic inflammation, and neurohormonal dysfunction. Beyond the well-established link to an increased risk of type 2 diabetes, these conditions may also influence prenatal glucose metabolism. Furthermore, psychiatric medications particularly atypical antipsychotics commonly prescribed to these individuals may promote weight gain and impair glucose tolerance, thereby further increasing the risk of developing GDM [38]. For instance, a study comparing cardiometabolic risk factors between individuals with bipolar disorder and schizophrenia reported that those with bipolar disorder used psychotropic medications less frequently [39].

Other well-known behavioral manifestations of severe mental illnesses, including poor dietary habits, physical inactivity, and high rates of smoking and substance use, are significantly more prevalent in this population [40, 41]. These lifestyle factors are established risk factors for cardiovascular disease and metabolic dysregulation and are likely to act as important mediators in the pathway linking severe psychiatric disorders to an elevated risk of GDM. While it is well-documented that unhealthy lifestyle behaviors are more common in individuals with schizophrenia, the evidence for similar patterns in bipolar disorder is somewhat mixed. Some studies have reported comparable rates of obesity and smoking between the two groups, suggesting shared environmental and behavioral risk factors [39]. However, other research indicates that patterns of substance use and dietary habits may differ. For instance, women with bipolar disorder have been found to exhibit higher rates of cigarette smoking and cannabis use compared to healthy controls [42]. Despite these nuances, the overarching implication remains that these modifiable factors are critical targets for intervention. The inconsistency in the literature underscores the need for further research to elucidate the distinct behavioral and metabolic profiles associated with each disorder, which could inform tailored screening and preventive strategies for GDM.

Bipolar disorder may predispose women to certain health disparities and adverse lifestyle behaviors, including smoking, alcohol use, and substance use disorders, all of which are known confounding factors that increase the risk of pregnancy-related complications. However, the extent of confounding control varies across studies. For instance, Sit et al. reported that mothers with bipolar disorder smoked cigarettes and used substances -including cannabis-significantly more frequently than healthy controls, highlighting the high-risk nature of this population [42].

Furthermore, recent meta-analyses have demonstrated that women with mental disorders are at increased risk of cesarean section and postpartum hemorrhage [43], as well as other pregnancy-related complications such as eclampsia and preeclampsia [44]. Similarly, systematic reviews by Rusner et al. [45] and Scrandis [46] evaluated a range of pregnancy outcomes associated with bipolar disorder and reported elevated risks of preterm birth, congenital anomalies, small for gestational age (SGA < 10th percentile), and gestational hypertension, which corroborate our findings.

These pregnancy-related challenges underscore the importance of integrated care models that prioritize maternal psychological stability. Therefore, pregnant women with bipolar disorder should be counseled about potential complications and closely monitored by an obstetrician to manage the risk of gestational diabetes and pregnancy-induced hypertension, and to prevent more severe adverse outcomes.

Study limitations

Several limitations of this study should be acknowledged. First, the existing literature has predominantly focused on high-income countries, with limited representation from low- and middle-income settings. Future research should prioritize these regions to provide a more comprehensive understanding of the association across diverse populations. Second, women with mild bipolar disorder may not seek healthcare and, therefore, may be underrepresented in the included studies, potentially introducing selection bias. Third, the lack of detailed information on psychotropic medication use during pregnancy represents a significant gap in the available evidence, limiting our ability to assess the role of pharmacological treatment in the observed associations. Fourth, the limited number of studies available for each outcome and the inability to perform comprehensive subgroup analyses hampered data interpretation and made it challenging to identify the precise sources of heterogeneity. Finally, due to the small number of accessible studies, we were unable to propose specific clinical factors—such as therapeutic interventions, dietary patterns, or lifestyle modifications—that could account for the observed heterogeneity. Consequently, the substantial and unresolved heterogeneity remains a major limitation of this research.

Strengths

Despite these limitations, this study has several notable strengths. To our knowledge, this is the first large-scale meta-analysis to demonstrate an association between pre-existing bipolar disorder, schizophrenia, and adverse pregnancy outcomes, specifically gestational diabetes. Additionally, all included studies employed a retrospective cohort design, which offers several methodological advantages, including large sample sizes that enhance statistical power, reduced random error, improved generalizability, and more robust assessment of causal relationships. These features collectively strengthen the evidence base for clinical guidelines and public health policies targeting this vulnerable population.

Conclusion

This meta-analysis provides evidence supporting an association between pre-existing bipolar disorder and schizophrenia with an increased risk of GDM. However, it is critical to note that this association was substantially attenuated after adjusting for potential confounding factors, particularly modifiable risk factors such as obesity, lifestyle behaviors, and socioeconomic status. This finding highlights the possibility that the elevated risk is not solely an inevitable consequence of the psychiatric disorder itself, but is considerably mediated by these preventable factors. Therefore, while the association is statistically significant, it should not be interpreted as deterministic. The results strongly suggest that comprehensive preconception and antenatal care, including aggressive screening for metabolic disturbances and targeted lifestyle interventions, could play a pivotal role in mitigating this risk. Regular monitoring of blood glucose levels and weight management should be integrated into routine psychiatric and obstetric care for women of reproductive age with severe mental illness. Further rigorously designed prospective studies, particularly in low- and middle-income countries, are crucial to disentangle the complex interplay between these psychiatric conditions, their treatments, and GDM, and to identify the most effective preventive strategies.
