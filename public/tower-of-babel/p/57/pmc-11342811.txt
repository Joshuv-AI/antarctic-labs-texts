Literature review and case study of recurrent EPGA with elevated IgG4 and positive HBsAg successfully treated with rituximab
Authors: Junru Wang, Jun Wang, Nan Wang, Wei Wang, Ping Zhang, Yingying Lin, Guisen Li, Yurong Zou, Xiang Zhong
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundEosinophilic granulomatosis with polyangiitis (EGPA) is a rare form of autoimmune vasculitis. The involvement of IgG4 and HBsAg in EGPA is less common but can occur and may present unique challenges in management.Case presentation:We present a case study of a 70-year-old female diagnosed with EGPA confirmed via renal biopsy. She initially presented with recurrent purpura, diarrhea and progressive numbness in the hands and feet, accompanied by general weakness. Complete remission was achieved with a one-year course of prednisone acetate and cyclophosphamide treatment. However, upon discontinuation of self-medication, the disease relapsed, manifesting as a generalized rash and weakness in the extremities.Skin biopsy revealed eosinophil infiltration, with inflammatory cells predominantly surrounding blood vessels. Notably, during treatment, the patient’s hepatitis B markers transitioned from negative to positive for HBsAg. Subsequent administration of entecavir, along with monitoring for a decrease in HBV DNA levels, preceded the initiation of steroids and rituximab to attain remission once more. Among the remaining 15 patients analyzed, all exhibited elevated serum IgG4 levels, with none testing positive for hepatitis B. Notably, only one patient was diagnosed with immunoglobulin G4-related disease (IgG4-RD), suggesting that elevated IgG4 levels alone may not necessarily indicate IgG4-RD.ConclusionsOur case report highlights the first instance of recurrent EGPA accompanied by elevated IgG4 and positivity for hepatitis B, which was successfully treated with rituximab. In cases of concurrent hepatitis B, rituximab treatment may be considered once viral replication is under control. However, emphasis on maintenance therapy is crucial following the induction of disease remission.

Background

Eosinophilic granulomatosis with polyangiitis (EGPA), formerly known as Churg–Strauss syndrome, was initially described by Jacob Churg and Lotte Strauss in 1951 [1]. The reported prevalence of EGPA in the European population varies from 2–38 cases per million people, whereas data for the Asian population are less available [2,3]. A Japanese study reported a notable increase in EGPA incidence, increasing from 4.2 per million people in 2005 to 38.0 per million people in 2017 [4]. EGPA is categorized under antineutrophil cytoplasmic antibody (ANCA)-associated vasculitis (AAV) and is a rare autoimmune condition that affects multiple organ systems, including the sinuses, lungs, skin, nervous system, heart, gastrointestinal tract, and kidneys [5]. Approximately 30–40% of EGPA patients test positive for ANCAs, although this rate is typically lower than that of other AAVs [6,7]. In contrast to polyangiitis (GPA) and microscopic polyangiitis (MPA), EGPA is characterized by systemic necrotizing vasculitis (SNV) affecting small- and medium-sized vessels. Its primary clinical features include severe asthma and eosinophilia in the blood and tissues [1,5,8]. Despite its eosinophilic nature, EGPA shares certain characteristics with immunoglobulin G4-related disease (IgG4-RD). They share some pathological features (tissue eosinophilia, phlebitis, and IgG4+ plasma cells) and increased serum IgG4, but their clinical presentations are usually very different. This paper presents a recurrent case of EGPA accompanied by elevated IgG4 and positive HBsAg, which was successfully managed with rituximab therapy.

Case presentation

Clinical features and laboratory findings

A 70-year-old female presented with recurrent purpura, diarrhea, progressive hand and foot numbness, and generalized weakness. She had a history of asthma-like bronchitis episodes. During the disease course, multisystem involvement, including skin, nerve, muscle, gastrointestinal tract, kidney, and hematological system involvement, occurred. On presentation, her blood pressure was 167/46 mmHg, and her temperature was 36.5 °C. Physical examination revealed scattered red rashes on the abdomen and extremities that were partially fused into patches without rupture. The laboratory findings revealed hypoalbuminemia (serum albumin concentration: 24.8 g/L), mild renal impairment (serum creatinine: 50.7 μmol/L, BUN: 4.17 mmol/L, eGFR: 94.7 mL/min/m2), proteinuria (1+ on urinalysis, 0.66 g in 24 h), and occult blood (2+). The urine albumin-creatinine ratio (uACR) and urinary neutrophil gelatinase-associated lipocalin (NGAL) levels were within normal ranges. Hematology analysis revealed a total white blood cell count of 21.30*10E9/L, a percentage of eosinophils of 40.3%, a hemoglobin level of 84 g/L, a platelet count of 418*10E9/L, and a CRP level of 14.94 mg/L. In terms of humoral immunity, the patient presented an elevated serum IgG4 concentration of 12.00 g/L, which was significantly above the reference range of 0.08–1.40 g/L. Furthermore, the serum complement C3 level was decreased, at 0.605 g/L. Testing for myeloperoxidase (MPO)-ANCA and proteinase 3 (PR3)-ANCA yielded negative results. Hepatitis B markers, including HBsAg, anti-HBs, HBeAg, anti-HBe, and HBcAb, were negative. Biopsy and flow cytometry of the bone marrow revealed elevated eosinophils, accounting for 22.4% of the total count. Further detection of eosinophilia-associated genes returned negative results. Electromyography demonstrated significant neurogenic damage in the examined muscles.

Renal histology

Immunofluorescence microscopy of the renal biopsy revealed pauci-immune glomerulonephritis, with faint positivity of IgM deposition in the mesangial area. Light microscopy revealed sclerosed glomeruli (1 out of 10 assessed) and one segment of glomerular capillary loops displaying cellulolytic necrosis with peribulitis cell infiltration. The remaining glomerular structures exhibited mild segmental hyperplasia of mesangial cells and stroma. Additionally, granular degeneration of renal tubular epithelial cells, focal tubular atrophy, and mild interstitial fibrosis (less than 5%) were noted. Lymphocytic, plasma cell, and neutrophil infiltration (approximately 30%) were observed in multifocal areas of the renal interstitium (Figure 1A–B).

Treatment and follow-up

During the disease course, the patient presented with asthma-like symptoms, sinusitis, and polyneuropathy. Peripheral blood and bone marrow analyses revealed eosinophilia exceeding 10%, with the exclusion of hematologic disorders as the cause. The renal biopsy findings included capillary collaterals with fibrinoid necrosis. Immunohistochemical analysis of the renal biopsy revealed approximately 6 IgG4-positive plasma cells/HPF in the renal interstitium (Figure 1D), with an IgG4-positive plasma cell/IgG-positive + plasma cell ratio less than 40%. According to the 2019 American College of Rheumatology (ACR)/European League Against Rheumatism (EULAR) IgG4-RD classification criteria, the cumulative weight score was less than 20, indicating a diagnosis of IgG4-RD [9]. Following the 1990 classification criteria proposed by the American College of Rheumatology, the patient was ultimately diagnosed with eosinophilic granulomatous polyangiitis with elevated IgG4 levels. The patient subsequently received prednisone acetate at a dosage of 40 mg/day (0.7 mg/kg/day), which was gradually tapered by 5 mg/month, and maintenance therapy with prednisone acetate 5 mg/day was initiated. Alongside the irregular use of cyclophosphamide, a cumulative intravenous infusion of 1.0 g (2020/07/25 0.2 g, 2020/07/27 0.2 g, 2020/07/30 0.2 g, 2020/09/01 0.4 g) was given, followed by oral cyclophosphamide administration totaling 8.7 g due to inconvenient transportation. After one year of treatment, the patient’s rash markedly subsided, and her symptoms of wheezing and malaise significantly improved. Urinary protein and renal function returned to normal, with serum IgG4 levels decreasing to 2.98 g/L. The patient’s medication regimen was subsequently adjusted to maintenance therapy comprising prednisone acetate 5 mg/day and mycophenolate mofetil dispersible tablets 0.25 g twice daily.

One and a half years later, the patient generally felt well, with no particular discomfort. She independently discontinued all medications during the coronavirus disease 2019 (COVID-19) epidemic due to the inconvenience of visiting hospitals. She subsequently experienced a reappearance of rash all over her body, predominantly on the lower extremities and abdomen (Figure 4A), accompanied by numbness, weakness of the extremities and fingertips, and difficulty breathing with wheezing. Laboratory tests revealed a serum creatinine concentration of 113 μmol/L, an eGFR of 42.52 mL/min/m2, and a serum IgG4 level of up to 16 g/L. Further skin biopsy revealed degenerated lobulated nucleated cells and nuclear fragments in the dermis, leukocyte fragmentation, a small amount of eosinophil infiltration, and inflammatory cells predominantly distributed around the blood vessels (Figure 2), indicative of a relapse of eosinophilic granulomatous vasculitis. Additionally, the patient’s hepatitis B markers indicated a hepatitis B surface antigen level >250 IU/mL and positive E antigen and core antibody, with a high-precision hepatitis B DNA level of 6,820,000 IU/mL. The patient did not exhibit any symptoms of hepatitis. Initially, 0.5 mg/day entecavir was administered for 1 week, resulting in a decrease in hepatitis B DNA quantification to 170,000 IU/mL, with normalized liver function. Methylprednisolone 40 mg/day was subsequently prescribed for one week and then tapered to 4 mg/d over three months, in combination with rituximab 375 mg/m2/week for four doses. Throughout this treatment regimen, the patient continued taking 0.5 mg/day entecavir. Following treatment, the patient’s generalized rash subsided (Figure 4B), and symptoms of extremity weakness and tightness were completely relieved. Renal function monitoring during treatment revealed a decrease in the patient’s serum creatinine level to 73.5 µmol/L, an increase in the eGFR to 71.52 mL/min/m2, a decrease in the serum eosinophil count to 0.138*10E9/L, and normalization of the serum IgG4 level (0.99 g/L). The patient’s hepatitis B DNA concentration decreased to 5980 IU/mL (Figure 3).

Literature review

A systematic search was conducted across multiple databases, including PubMed, EMBASE, MEDLINE (Ovid), the Chinese National Knowledge Infrastructure (CNKI), the VIP Database for Chinese Technical Periodicals (VIP), and WanFang Data. The search spanned from the inception of each database until 1 January 2022, aiming to gather both international and domestic studies on “Eosinophilic Granulomatosis with Polyangiitis” (EGPA) and “IgG4”. The selection criteria included studies involving patients diagnosed with EGPA and reporting elevated IgG4 levels. The exclusion criteria were studies lacking specific values for serum IgG4, inaccessible data or untransformable full texts, and articles categorized as reviews or abstracts. Ultimately, the analysis included data from 15 patients [10–24], with 13 cases reported in Japan, 2 in China. (Table 1).

Among the 15 patients included in the review, seven were female and eight were male, resulting in a male-to-female ratio of 8:7. The majority of patients had a prior history of bronchial asthma and sinusitis. The mean age of the patients was 56.8 ± 12.2 years (mean ± SD), with an average blood eosinophil count of 39.8 ± 21.6% (mean ± SD). The median IgG4 titer was 457 mg/dl (interquartile range: 261–771).

The majority of the 15 patients initially presented with fever, rash, respiratory symptoms, abdominal pain, and weakness in both lower extremities. Most patients underwent tissue biopsy, revealing eosinophilic infiltration and fibrosis, whereas not all exhibited renal involvement. None of the reported patients had comorbid hepatitis B.

The majority of patients were treated with glucocorticoids alone, while a few received glucocorticoids in combination with immunosuppressive or biological agents, such as cyclophosphamide or benralizumab. None of the patients were treated with rituximab. Among these 15 patients, one experienced a relapse upon tapering of hormone therapy, and one developed secondary posterior circulation hemorrhage. However, all other patients exhibited clinical improvement, with reduced eosinophil counts and serum IgG4 levels.

Discussion

EGPA is a systemic vasculitis characterized by eosinophilia and extravascular granuloma formation that accumulates in multiple systems [25]. In the collected case reports, patients commonly presented with fever, extremity weakness, rash, abnormal sensation, and occasionally cholecystitis. Regarding renal damage, Durel CA et al. observed that the most frequent renal manifestation in ANCA-positive EGPA is necrotizing pauci-immune GN [26], and membranous nephropathy and membranoproliferative GN are mostly observed in ANCA-negative EGPA. Approximately 50% of cases display significant eosinophilic interstitial infiltration. Owing to the obvious geographical differences in the types of AAV and ANCA, PR3-ANCA and GPA predominate in Europe and the United States, whereas MPO-ANCA and MPA predominate in China, Japan, and other Asian countries, which differ from Europe and the United States in terms of clinicopathologic features, therapeutic response, and prognosis [27–30]. EGPA is rare in China, and the case presented herein is unique, ­featuring elevated IgG4 levels and positive HBsAg, adding complexity to the diagnosis.

EGPA and IgG4-RD share several similarities, often leading to confusion or overlap in diagnosis. Both patient groups commonly exhibit a history of allergy and present elevated levels of eosinophils, serum IgE and serum IgG4. In Kubo S’s study [31]. All EGPA patients had serum IgG4 levels that surpassed the diagnostic threshold of 135 mg/dL for IgG4-RD, indicating a lack of specificity for elevated IgG4 levels alone. However, the underlying pathogenes of these diseases differ. EGPA manifests as vasculitis, whereas IgG4-RD is characterized by immune-mediated chronic inflammation and fibrosis. Specific clinical and radiological features, such as mass-forming lesions, are indicators of IgG4-RD. Although elevated serum IgG4 and IgG4 infiltration are hallmarks of IgG4-RD, they can also occur in EGPA patients [32]. Kubo S identified differences in the immunophenotype between EGPA and IgG4-RD patients, suggesting that T-cell activation may play a crucial role in vasculitis development. The balance between factors involved in T-cell activation and those influencing B-cell differentiation and activation may contribute to the distinctions between IgG4-RD and EGPA.

According to the 2022 EULAR recommendations for the management of ANCA-associated vasculitis, induction therapy for new or recurrent EGPA with organ- or life-threatening manifestations typically involves a combination of high-dose glucocorticoids and cyclophosphamide. Alternatively, high-dose glucocorticoids in combination with rituximab may be used. For inactive patients with recurrent or refractory EGPA that poses organ- or life-threatening risks, mepolizumab is recommended for the induction of remission [33]. In our patient, EGPA affected multiple systems, including the respiratory and digestive systems, peripheral nerves, skin, and kidneys. Initially, the patient responded favorably to induction therapy with glucocorticoids and CTX. However, a relapse occurred after self-isolation, prompting another round of remission induction therapy with glucocorticoids and rituximab. At the three-month follow-up, the patient’s disease remained well-controlled. Given the recurrent nature of EGPA and the need for sustained disease control, maintenance therapy is imperative even after remission is reached.

Notably, initially, the patient tested negative for hepatitis B markers during the first admission; however, upon disease recurrence, the patient tested positive for HBsAg, HBeAg and HBcAb. This change in hepatitis B marker levels influenced the immediate use of immunosuppressants. To address this, we initiated treatment with entecavir for one week to reduce HBV DNA levels. Following confirmation of HBV DNA decline, we proceeded with steroid and rituximab therapy. Over the subsequent 18 months of follow-up, the patient maintained hepatitis B DNA levels below 100 and exhibited normal liver function. Many studies have reported that the use of immunosuppressive agents, biological agents, or chemotherapy drugs in patients with previous or current HBV infection is prone to hepatitis B virus reactivation and even severe hepatitis and liver failure, which may lead to death [34–36]. Patients are recommended to be evaluated for the risk of hepatitis B reactivation, and prophylactic use of antivirals is recommended for moderate/high-risk patients [34]. Notably, there was no abnormality in the regular detection of HBV DNA in our patient during treatment, but HBV DNA became positive after drug withdrawal. The prophylactic use of anti-hepatitis B virus drugs in hepatitis B-negative patients receiving immunosuppressive medications has not been fully studied until now [37]. Patients who are completely negative for hepatitis B markers may also carry low levels of virus [38,39]. Hepatitis B may be activated, especially following treatment with immunosuppressive drugs. Alternatively, patients may acquire hepatitis B during treatment. Therefore, it is crucial to regularly monitor hepatitis B markers and liver function in patients who are completely negative for hepatitis B markers, both during and after treatment, to prevent hepatitis B reactivation. On the basis of our case experience, we emphasize the importance of regular monitoring in the management of immunosuppressed patients. Additionally, while biologics are not absolute contraindications in HBV-positive patients, a thorough understanding of the patient’s condition and effective control of viral replication is essential.

In summary, following the diagnosis of EGPA, treatment should be tailored to the individual patient’s needs. For patients with concurrent hepatitis B, considering rituximab therapy after ensuring that viral replication is adequately managed could be an option. Vigilant monitoring of hepatitis B markers during treatment and cessation of immunosuppressive agents are imperative precautions.
