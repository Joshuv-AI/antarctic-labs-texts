TREM-1-dependent macrophage infiltration mediates visceral hypersensitivity via vlPAG GABAergic neuron activation in a mouse model of inflammatory bowel disease
Authors: Zi-ming Zhou, Wei Song, Miao-miao Guo, Yuan-qing Chu, Lu-bin Wang, Peng Cheng, Jing-wen Huang, Ting Su, Xu-Feng Huang, Yong-mei Zhang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundVisceral pain is a common symptom of inflammatory bowel disease (IBD). Although triggering receptor expressed on myeloid cells-1 (TREM-1) has been shown to induce infiltration of peripheral immune cells into the central nervous system, the specific contribution of peripheral monocytes/macrophages (Mo/MΦ) to visceral pain in IBD remains elusive. This study aimed to investigate the effect of TREM-1-dependent macrophage infiltration in the periaqueductal gray (PAG) on visceral pain and the underlying mechanisms in IBD.MethodsMale C57BL/6 J or Trem1−/− mice were fed 2% dextran sodium sulfate (DSS) for 7 days to induce colitis. Flow sorting, flow cytometry, and western blot were employed to isolate, transfer, and detect immune cells as well as TREM-1 expression. Immunofluorescence and in vitro brain slice electrophysiology were used to assess the activity of GABAergic neurons. Visceral pain was evaluated using the abdominal withdrawal reflex (AWR) score, visceral pain threshold, and electromyography (EMG).ResultsPeripheral Mo/MΦ infiltration into the PAG played a dominant role in enhancing ventrolateral PAG (vlPAG) GABAergic neuronal activity in mice with acute colitis, compared with the relatively minor contributions of resident microglia and peripheral neutrophils. This promoted acute visceral pain during intestinal inflammation with visceral pain persisting into the remission phase. To validate the role of peripheral TREM-1-expressing Mo/MΦ in visceral pain, microinjection of Ly6C⁺ monocytes into the vlPAG led to activation of vlPAG GABAergic neurons and an increase in visceral pain sensitivity. Pharmacological inhibition and gene knockout of TREM-1 both reduced Mo/MΦ infiltration, leading to decreased vlPAG GABAergic neuronal activity and mitigatory visceral pain.ConclusionsOur study identifies peripheral Mo/MΦ TREM-1 as a mediator promoting visceral pain via vlPAG GABAergic neuron activation. This finding presents a promising therapeutic approach for treating acute intestinal inflammation and visceral pain in colitis, while also offering a potential target for managing chronic visceral pain that persists in patients with IBD.Graphical Abstract Supplementary InformationThe online version contains Supplementary Material available at https://doi.org/10.1186/s11658-026-00980-2.Keywords: Monocytes/macrophages (Mo/MΦ), TREM-1, PAG, GABAergic neurons, Inflammatory bowel disease

Introduction

Inflammatory bowel disease (IBD), including ulcerative colitis (UC) and Crohn’s disease (CD), is a chronic relapsing nonspecific intestinal inflammatory disease [1–5]. Currently, the incidence and prevalence of IBD in the general population are increasing. Notably, up to 60% of patients with IBD experience persistent visceral pain, which can occur during both active inflammation and remission phases [6]. However, effective management strategies for visceral pain remain lacking [6], which highlights the urgent need for further research to investigate the specific mechanisms of persistent visceral pain [7].

There is growing evidence that neuronal sensitization induced by bidirectional communication between the gut and the brain, known as the gut–brain axis, plays a pivotal role in the development of visceral hypersensitivity. During colitis, activated proinflammatory intestinal macrophages release various cytokines, leading to gut vascular barrier (GVB) and blood–brain barrier (BBB) disruption [8–12], which provides a gateway for intestinal immune cells such as monocytes to migrate directly to the brain [13–15], further aggravating neuroinflammation, neuronal sensitization, and neuroplasticity [16]. On these monocytes/macrophages (Mo/MΦ), the triggering receptor expressed on myeloid cells-1 (TREM-1) is found to be highly expressed and to amplify proinflammatory immune responses of IBD [17–19]. Although TREM-1 has been shown to induce infiltration [19], its specific mechanism of regulating visceral pain via inducing neuronal sensitization in IBD remains elusive.

It is noteworthy that the periaqueductal gray (PAG), a central structure in the endogenous nociceptive core structure of the downstream inhibitory system of transmission [20], plays a key role in the modulation of nociception [21]. The changes in the activity of the structural and neuronal alterations within the PAG are reported to aggravate chronic visceral pain behavior [22]. Although the activation effect of microglia on visceral-pain-related neurons in the PAG has already been confirmed, peripheral-derived macrophages have rarely been studied so far, which may play an indispensable role in regulating neuronal activity.

In this study, we identified a more vital role of peripheral-derived Mo/MΦ in the PAG than resident microglia and peripheral neutrophils in raising ventrolateral PAG (vlPAG) GABAergic neuronal activity in acute colitis. This promoted acute visceral pain during intestinal inflammation, and these effects persisted into the remission phase. Besides, the microinjection of Ly6C⁺ monocytes into the vlPAG led to activation of vlPAG GABAergic neurons and an increase in visceral pain sensitivity, which validated the contribution of peripheral TREM-1-expressing Mo/MΦ to visceral pain. Both pharmacological inhibition and genetic ablation of TREM-1 indicated a beneficial effect in reducing peripheral Mo/MΦ infiltration into the PAG and vlPAG GABAergic neuronal activity, which correlated with alleviating acute and chronic visceral pain. Collectively, these findings highlight TREM-1 as a promising therapeutic target for the treatment and management of visceral pain in patients with IBD.

Methods and materials

Animals and reagents

The care and use of animals and the experimental protocols (202207S082) used in this study were approved by the Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee and the Officer of Laboratory Animal Resources of Xuzhou Medical University under the Regulations for the Administration of Affairs Concerning Experimental Animals in China (clinical trial number not applicable). Male wild-type C57BL/6 J mice were obtained from the Laboratory Animal Center at Xuzhou Medical University. Male Trem1 KO (B6/JGpt-Trem1­em1Cd60266026/Gpt) and Vgat-CRE mice in the C57BL/6 background were purchased from Gempharmatech Co., Ltd. (Nanjing, China). Male and female R26-LSL-DTR mice and CX3CR1CreERT2 mice were purchased from Shanghai Model Organisms Center, Inc. By crossbreeding R26-LSL-DTR mice and CX3CR1CreERT2 mice and genetic characterization, male CX3CR1CreERT2 × iDTR were obtained. All these mice were group-housed (three to five mice per cage) in a specific pathogen-free (SPF) environment with stable temperature (22 ± 1 ℃) and humidity (50 ± 10%) on a 12-h light/dark cycle. Except for the necessary modeling sessions, the mice had ad libitum access to food and water. All experimental reagents used in this study are listed in Supplementary Table 1.

Dextran sulfate sodium (DSS)-induced colitis

Acute colitis was induced in mice by drinking 2% (weight by volume [wt/vol]) DSS (36,000–50,000 MW, MP Biomedicals, USA) for seven consecutive days. The concentration and duration of DSS administration were described in previous studies [23]. During the entire period, mice were monitored for weight loss, stool consistency, and rectal bleeding.

Disease activity index (DAI) and scoring

The DAI was scored by combining the mice’s percentage of weight loss (0 for unchanged weight, 1 for 1–5%, 2 for 5–10%, 3 for 10–15%, and 4 for greater than 15%), stool consistency (0 for normal, 2 for loose stools, and 4 for diarrhea), and stool hemorrhage (0 for normal, 2 for occult blood positivity, and 4 for overt hemorrhage); the DAI score was obtained by dividing the total of the three results by 3.

Hematoxylin and eosin (H&E) staining and histological scoring

Mice were deeply anesthetized with 1% sodium pentobarbital (40 mg/kg, intraperitoneal [IP]; 0.01 mL/g body weight) and then euthanized, after which the distal colons were harvested. The intestines were washed in cold phosphate-buffered saline (PBS) and soaked in paraformaldehyde (4% in 0.1 mol/L phosphate buffer, pH 7.4) overnight. Then, we submerged the tissues in anhydrous ethanol through a graded alcohol series and embedded them in paraffin for sections. Colitis was histologically assessed on 5 μm sections stained with hematoxylin and eosin [24]. Briefly, crypt damage was rated on a scale of 0–4 as follows: 0 = no significant shortening or damage, 1 = mild changes, 2 = moderate changes, 3 = severe changes with an absence of surface epithelial cells, and 4 = complete destruction of crypts with the presence of surface epithelial cells, and inflammation score was rated on a scale of 0–3 as follows: 0 = no notable change, 1 = mild enlargement of lamina propria, 2 = moderate enlargement of the lamina propria with infiltrating inflammatory cells, and 3 = sharp increase of the lamina propria with severe inflammatory cell infiltration into the surrounding areas. The final score was obtained by averaging the values.

Visceral pain threshold and abdominal withdrawal reflex (AWR) score

A polyethylene balloon (1.5 cm × 1 cm) was prepared by affixing it to the end of PE-60 tubing with 4–0 suture thread in case of air leakage. We inserted the balloon into the colorectal region through a tube, which was connected to a mercury sphygmomanometer and a 20-mL syringe by a three-way tube. After the mice were calm for 10 min in a transparent organic glass box (10 cm × 5 cm × 5 cm), the three-way tube was opened, the syringe was pushed to inflate the balloon at a uniform speed by 10 mmHg per second, and the reaction of the mice was observed. The AWR score was used to assess the response of the abdominal wall to colorectal stimuli. AWR scoring standard was the following: 0, no evident behavioral changes; 1, immobile or only slight head movement; 2, contraction of abdominal muscles, but without lifting off the desktop; 3, contraction of abdominal muscles with lifting off the desktop; and 4, arching of abdominal wall and back. When the abdomen contracted and lifted off the desktop (AWR score ≥ 3), the barometric pressure shown on the sphygmomanometer was recorded immediately, which was the visceral pain threshold. Recorded three times with an interval of 5 min each time, the final results were averaged.

Electromyography (EMG)

The EMG response to colorectal distension (CRD) was applied to assess visceral hypersensitivity in mice. Mice were fasted and given water ad libitum for 12 h prior to the experiment. After mice were anesthetized with 3% isoflurane using anesthetic equipment (RWD, Shenzhen, China), they were fixed on a foam board, and a balloon connected to a sphygmomanometer with a 20-mL syringe was inserted into the colorectum of the mice. To minimize tissue injury, two 0.25-mm stainless steel wires were inserted into the external oblique muscle on one side of the abdomen, located 0.5 cm above the inguinal ligament and 0.5 cm lateral to the midline. The distal ends of the wires were connected to the positive and negative electrodes of the recording system. The electrodes were gently secured to the abdominal skin of the mice with adhesive tape. The mice were then placed in a transparent organic glass box (10 cm × 5 cm × 5 cm) and allowed to recover from anesthesia. After approximately 15 min, once the mice were awake and remained relatively immobile, the syringe was pushed at a uniform rate, maintaining the balloon pressure at 20, 40, 60, and 80 mmHg for 10 s, and the electromyogram was recorded using a BL-420N biological signal collection and processing system (BL-420N, Taimeng, China) at 5-min intervals. The high-frequency filter was set at 2 kHz, the time constant at 0.001 s, the sampling frequency at 40 Hz, the sensitivity at 500 μV, and the scanning speed at 250 ms/div.

Evans blue dye extravasation analysis

In total, 100 μL 2% Evans blue (cat. no. ST3273-1 g, Beyotime, China) was injected into mice with colitis at week 1 (W1) by tail vein. After 24 h, the mice were deeply anesthetized with isoflurane, followed by transcardial perfusion into the beating heart via the left ventricle with 50 mL of PBS to remove the intravascular dye. Different brain regions were sampled, including medial prefrontal cortex (mPFC), anterior cingulate cortex (ACC), nucleus accumbens (NAc), insular cortex (IC), paraventricular nucleus of the hypothalamus (PVN), and PAG, and then homogenized in 1 mL of 50% trichloroacetic acid and centrifuged (10,000 rpm, 20 min). The supernatant was diluted fourfold with ethanol. The concentrations of Evans blue were measured with a fluorescence reader (620 nm excitation; 680 nm emission) and presented as nanograms per milligram of brain tissue.

Stereotactic surgeries

All stereotactic surgeries were performed under sterile conditions. Firstly, mice were deeply anesthetized with 1% sodium pentobarbital (40 mg/kg, IP; 0.01 mL/g body weight) or with 2–5% isoflurane via an inhalation anesthesia machine (RWD Life Science Co., Shenzhen, China) and fixed in the stereotaxic frame (RWD Life Science Co., Shenzhen, China) by the incisor bar and ear bar to make sure that the skull plane was at a horizontal level. Secondly, after the head was shaved and sterilized, a vertical median incision was made to expose the skull, and 3% hydrogen peroxide was used to remove the periosteum to expose the bregma and lambda. Thirdly, we positioned a 1-µL Hamilton microsyringe at the bregma and set the instrument reading to zero. Then, we removed the Hamilton microsyringe above the target brain region to drill a hole with a dental drill and place it into the injection site (ventrolateral periaqueductal gray [vlPAG]: anteroposterior [AP], −4.5 mm; mediolateral [ML], ±0.25 mm; dorsoventral [DV], −3 mm). After leaving the needle in place for 5 min, we injected 200 nL viral vector bilaterally at a low and uniform speed and waited for 10 min. After the injection, we sutured and sterilized the wound and placed the mice on an insulating blanket, waiting for the mice to wake up. If repeated brain region administrations were required, stainless steel guide cannulas were bilaterally implanted into the vlPAG (AP, −4.5 mm; ML, ±0.25 mm; DV, −3 mm). The cannulas were secured to the skull with dental cement, and guide pins were inserted to maintain patency. Mice were allowed to recover for 1 week before subsequent experiments.

Intrathecal injection of naloxone

For intrathecal administration, mice were gently restrained, and naloxone hydrochloride injection (5 μL per mouse, cat. no. 2506071, Shandong Xinhua Pharmaceutical Company Limited, China) was injected into the L5–L6 intervertebral space using a microsyringe fitted with a fine-gauge needle. Correct needle placement was verified by a sudden tail-flick response. The control group received the same volume of vehicle. Following injection, mice were allowed to recover in their home cages, and subsequent behavioral assessment was conducted 1 h later.

Chemogenetic neuronal manipulation

To manipulate vlPAG GABAergic neurons via chemogenetic approaches, Vgat-Cre mice were used in this experiment. AAV-EF1α-DIO-mCherry (PT-0013, 5.35 × 1012 vg/mL, BrainVTA), AAV-hSyn-DIO-hM3D(Gq)-mCherry (PT-0019, 4.53 × 1012 vg/mL, BrainVTA), or AAV-hSyn-DIO-hM4D(Gi)-mCherry (PT-0020, 5.25 × 1012 vg/mL, BrainVTA) were bilaterally injected into the vlPAG of Vgat-Cre mice. A total volume of 200 nL was delivered at each injection site. Then, 4 weeks after viral injection, chemogenetic neuronal manipulation was achieved by intraperitoneal administration of clozapine-N-oxide (CNO; 3 mg/kg, dissolved in 2% DMSO and diluted in sterile saline; cat. no. HY-17366, MCE, USA) under baseline conditions, at the DSS W1 phase, and at the DSS W4 phase. Subsequently, 1 h after CNO injection, visceral pain threshold and visceral sensitivity could be tested.

Exhaustion of monocytes or neutrophils in peripheral blood

To deplete monocytes in peripheral blood, we administered clodronate liposomes (CLP; cat. no. 40337ES08, Yeasen, China) or control liposomes (vehicle; cat. no. 40338ES08, Yeasen, China). After mice were injected with 200 μL per 20 g body weight of CLP or control liposomes via the tail vein on days 2, 4, and 6 within 7 days of DSS consumption, monocytes in peripheral blood were cleared. To deplete neutrophils in peripheral blood, we administered biotin anti-mouse Ly-6G antibody (cat. no. 127604, Biolegend, China) or phosphate-buffered saline (PBS). After mice were injected with 200 μL per 20 g body weight of biotin anti-mouse Ly-6G antibody or PBS via intraperitoneal injection on days 2, 4, and 6 within 7 days of DSS consumption, neutrophils in peripheral blood were cleared. By the seventh day (W1), subsequent experiments were conducted.

Inhibition of peripheral monocyte infiltration into the vlPAG

RS102895 (40 μg in 600 nL; cat. no. HY-8611, MCE, USA), a selective C–C chemokine receptor type 2 (CCR2) antagonist, was continuously administered via a cannula implanted into the vlPAG for seven consecutive days during the period of DSS-induced colitis, while saline served as the vehicle control [25]. On the seventh day (DSS week 1 [W1]), subsequent experiments were performed.

Leukocyte peptide 17 (LP17) nasal administration

An antagonistic TREM-1 peptide LP17 (LQVTDSGLYRCVIYHPP) or a sequence-scrambled control peptide was chemically synthesized (GenScript, Nanjing, China). Mice were treated intranasally once daily with 200 μg LP17 or control peptide for 7 days during the period of drinking DSS. To confirm that LP17 could access the brain, it was labeled with rhodamine (cat. no. D10096, DuoFluor, China).

Region-specific depletion of microglia

CX3CR1creERT2 × iDTR mice were utilized to deplete microglia in the vlPAG [26]. In these mice, the induction of the diphtheria toxin receptor (DTR) expression in CX3CR1-expressing cells is activated by tamoxifen. Tamoxifen was dissolved in a mixture of 10% anhydrous ethanol and 90% corn oil (25 mg/mL; cat. no. T9010, Solarbio, China) and injected intraperitoneally into every mouse with about 0.1 mL tamoxifen solution for five consecutive days. After 4–6 weeks, monocyte turnover allowed for the presence of only resident microglia expressing DTR in the brain. To explore the influence of microglia in the vlPAG on visceral pain, we injected 200 nL of diphtheria toxin (DT; cat. no. D0564-1MG, Sigma-Aldrich, USA) working solution (3 ng/μL dissolved in PBS) into the vlPAG for three consecutive days to deplete microglia rather than peripheral-derived monocytes/macrophages, and the control group was injected with PBS.

Western blot (WB)

Whole protein samples were analyzed by western blot to identify the level of protein expression. Mice were deeply anesthetized and then euthanized. Targeted tissues such as the PAG region, colon, and spleen were quickly removed from the mice as protein samples into different precooled Eppendorf tubes (EP) tubes with radio immunoprecipitation assay (RIPA) lysis buffer (cat. no. P0013B, Beyotime, China) and phenylmethylsulfonyl fluoride (PMSF) solution (cat. no. ST507-10 mL, Beyotime, China), followed by ultrasonic lysis and centrifugation (12,000 rpm, 15 min, 4 ℃). The BCA protein measurement kit (cat. no. P0009, Beyotime, China) is used to detect the protein concentration of the supernatant protein solution and adjust the concentration of different groups to the same level. Then, equal amounts (30–50 μg) of protein samples were electrophoresed by 7.5–15% sodium dodecyl sulfate–polyacrylamide gel electrophoresis (SDS–PAGE) (cat. no. 20325ES62, Yeason Biotechnology, China) and transferred onto polyvinylidene fluoride (PVDF) membranes (cat. no. IPVH00010, Millipore, UK). After being blocked with 5% nonfat milk for 2 h at room temperature (RT), the membranes were incubated with the primary antibodies against TREM-1 (1:1000), occludin (1:1000), and ZO-1 (1:1000) overnight at 4 ℃. After lavage in Tris-buffered saline with 0.1% Tween-20 (TBST) (cat. no. 60145ES76, Yeason Biotechnology, China), the membranes were incubated with horseradish-peroxidase (HRP)-conjugated secondary antibody (1:2000) for 2 h at RT. The bands were visualized by an ultrasensitive ECL chemiluminescence kit (cat. no. P0018S, Beyotime, China). The gray-scale intensity of target protein bands was normalized to β-tubulin or GAPDH, and the control was set as 1. Data were analyzed with ImageJ software.

Enzyme-linked immunosorbent assay (ELISA) for endogenous opioids

The concentrations of dynorphin A (Dyn A) and methionine-enkephalin (MEK) in spinal cord tissues were determined using commercially available ELISA kits according to the manufacturers’ instructions (cat. nos. MM-49725M1, MM-44813M1, and MEIMIAN, China). Briefly, after deep anesthesia, mice were sacrificed, and spinal cord samples were rapidly isolated, weighed, and homogenized in ice-cold buffer on ice. After centrifugation at 5000g for 10 min at 4 °C, the supernatants were collected for analysis. Absorbance was measured at 450 nm, and peptide concentrations were calculated on the basis of standard curves and normalized to tissue weight.

Immunofluorescence (IF)

After being anesthetized with 1% sodium pentobarbital at a dose of 40 mg/kg by means of intraperitoneal injection, mice were perfused with normal saline (100 mL/100 g), followed by 4% polyformaldehyde (PFA; 100 mL/100 g). Whole brains were completely removed and post-fixed in 4% PFA for 6–8 h at 4 ℃, followed by immersion in 30% sucrose at 4 ℃ for 3 days. To better dehydrate the brains, we replaced the 30% sucrose on the second day. The brains were coronally serially sliced on a cryostat (cat. no. CM1800, Leica, Germany) at a thickness of 30 μm and stored in PBS at 4 ℃ prior to immunofluorescence staining. After being washed in PBS three times, selected brain slices were blocked with 1% bovine serum albumin (BSA) and incubated with primary antibodies overnight at 4 ℃ and secondary antibodies for 2 h at RT. Then, all brain slices were sealed with 4′,6-diamidino-2-phenylindole (DAPI) Fluoromount-G® (cat. no. 0100–20, SouthernBiotech, USA). Images were obtained with a fluorescence microscope (Olympus, Tokyo, Japan). For quantification of cells, the mean number of three consecutive sections around the PAG was counted automatically using ImageJ software. The sections used were at the same coordinates for each group.

In vitro brain slice electrophysiology recording

To record the firing activity of vlPAG GABAergic neurons, a Cre-dependent AAV vector (AAV-EF1α-DIO-mCherry; 5.35 × 1012 vg/mL; 200 nL per site; BrainVTA) was bilaterally injected into the vlPAG of Vgat-Cre mice. Approximately 4 weeks after viral injection, mice from different experimental groups underwent cardiac perfusion under deep anesthesia with 1% sodium pentobarbital (40 mg/kg, IP) with ice-cold high-sucrose cutting solution (mM), containing 80 NaCl, 4.5 MgSO4, 3.5 KCl, 0.5 CaCl2, 1.25 NaH2PO4, 90 sucrose, 25 NaHCO3, and 10 glucose. The brains were sliced horizontally at a thickness of 300 μm on a vibratome (cat. no. VT1200S, Leica, Germany) in the same cutting solution. Subsequently, the slices were quickly transferred to the artificial cerebral spinal fluid (ACSF) (mM), containing 126 NaCl, 1.2 NaH2PO4, 2.5 KCl, 1.2 MgSO4, 26 NaHCO3, 10 glucose, and 2.4 CaCl2, for 1 h at RT after incubation in high-sucrose cutting solution at 34 ℃ for 30 min. All solutions were saturated with 95% oxygen and 5% carbon dioxide to maintain a stable pH and continuous oxygenation. Under the observation of an upright microscope (cat. no. BX51WI, Olympus, Japan), vlPAG GABAergic neurons expressing red light were subjected to patch-clamp recordings with a standard borosilicate glass pipette (wall type) (cat. no. BF-150–86–10, SUTTER, USA) filled with intracellular solution composition (mM), containing 10 phosphocreatine–Tris, 10 HEPES, 2 ATP–Mg, 10 EGTA, 0.5 GTP–Na, 115 K gluconate, 20 KCl, 1.5 MgCl2, pH 7.25, 280–290 mOsm/kg. Whole-cell recordings were acquired using a MultiClamp 700B amplifier (Axon Instruments, Inc., USA), and the data were obtained and analyzed with Clampex and Clampfit software version 10.6 (Axon Instruments, Inc., USA).

Flow cytometry, analysis, and cell sorting

After mice were deeply anesthetized with 1% sodium pentobarbital (40 mg/kg, IP; 0.01 mL/g body weight) and transcardially perfused with 0.9% saline, PAG, spleen, and colon tissues were extracted, shredded, and incubated with DNAse I (cat. no. VIC115, Vicmed, China) and collagenase type II/IV (cat. nos. VIC080 and VIC082, Vicmed, China) in Roswell Park Memorial Institute (RPMI) 1640 under agitation (200 rpm) for 60 min at 37 °C. Then, all cells were filtered through a 100-μm cell strainer and suspended in PBS containing 2% (wt/vol) fetal bovine serum (FBS). In addition, blood was collected through the posterior orbital venous plexus of mice and preserved in 4 °C anticoagulation tubes. Then, incubated with ACK lysis buffer (cat. no. G2015-500ML, Servicebio, China) for 10 min to lyse red blood cells, blood cells were concentrated by centrifugation at 300g for 5 min at 4 ℃ and resuspended in PBS. All cells needed to be washed and labeled with fluorochrome-conjugated surface marker antibodies for fluorescence-activated cell sorting (FACS) analysis.

During the detection, forward scatter (FSC) and side scatter (SSC) were used to gate live cells instead of debris, cell aggregates, and doublets. The gate strategy was as follows: CD11b+CD45+Ly6G+Ly6C− neutrophils (PMN), CD11b+CD45hiLy6G−Ly6C+ monocytes/macrophages (Mo/MΦ), and CD11b+CD45intLy6G−Ly6C− microglia. In this process, we analyzed the data with FACSCanto II (BD Biosciences, USA) and examined the percentage of each cell population and median fluorescence intensity (MFI) with FlowJo version 10.8.1 software (TreeStar, Inc.). If it was necessary to collect cells, a FACSAria Fusion cell sorter (BD Biosciences, USA) was utilized to sort specific cells (CD11b+CD45hiLy6G−Ly6C+ monocytes in blood), which were then stereotaxically injected into the vlPAG region of mice (1 × 104 cells in 0.5 μL PBS, each side of vlPAG).

Statistics

All data are presented as mean ± standard error of the mean (SEM). The Shapiro–Wilk test was used to assess the normality of the data. Data that did not pass the normality test were analyzed using nonparametric tests. Normally distributed data with equal variance were analyzed using an unpaired two-tailed Student’s t-test. For multiple group comparisons, one-way or two-way analysis of variance (ANOVA) was performed, followed by Tukey’s or Sidak’s multiple comparisons test, as appropriate. For non-normally distributed data, the Mann–Whitney U test was used for comparisons between two groups, while the Kruskal–Wallis test was applied for multiple group comparisons, followed by Dunn’s multiple comparisons test. The specific statistical tests used for each experiment are listed in Supplementary Table 2, and the corresponding degrees of freedom and test statistics are reported. Statistical analyses were performed using GraphPad Prism 9 software (GraphPad Software, USA). Figures were generated using Adobe Illustrator 2023 (Adobe Systems Incorporated, USA). A value of *P < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Results

DSS induces colitis and visceral hypersensitivity in male mice

According to the previous modeling method [23], male C57BL/6J mice were given 2% DSS for 7 days followed by a 3-week recovery period. To characterize DSS-induced colitis, we assessed body weight, stool consistency, and the DAI score (Fig. 1A). Mice in the DSS group exhibited progressive weight loss and developed loose, bloody stools following DSS administration. Their body weight reached its lowest point on day 8 and gradually recovered thereafter (Fig. 1B, C). Similarly, the DAI score increased during the first week, peaked on day 8, and returned toward baseline during the recovery phase (Fig. 1D).

Histological analysis further confirmed these findings. H&E staining revealed marked epithelial erosion, disruption of the mucosal architecture, and extensive inflammatory cell infiltration in the colon at week 1 (W1), indicating an acute inflammatory phase. In contrast, by week 4 (W4), colonic morphology was largely restored, with minimal inflammatory changes, consistent with remission (Fig. 1E, F). Therefore, mice were categorized into three groups: control (CON), DSS week 1 (W1), and DSS week 4 (W4) [23]. Accordingly, W1 was defined as the acute inflammatory phase, whereas W4 represented the remission phase of colitis.

To evaluate visceral hypersensitivity, we measured AWR scores, visceral pain thresholds, and EMG responses across groups (Fig. 1G–K). Compared with control mice, both DSS W1 and DSS W4 groups exhibited significantly increased visceral sensitivity. Notably, visceral hypersensitivity was more pronounced at W1 than at W4, indicating that visceral sensitivity is highest during active inflammation but persists at a reduced level during remission.

The pathway of peripheral Mo/MΦ infiltrating the PAG during the inflammation phase of colitis

Previous studies have shown that the bidirectional communications within the gut–brain axis involve intestinal immune cells infiltrating the brain after both the GVB and the BBB integrity are compromised [13]. To verify the effects of DSS-induced colitis on intestinal and BBB permeability, we measured the expression of occludin and ZO-1 proteins in colon tissue and the cerebral cortex by western blot (Fig. 2A, C). The levels of occludin and ZO-1 were significantly reduced in both the colon and cerebral cortex at W1, but returned to normal levels at W4 (Fig. 2B, D). Evans blue dye extravasation analysis further indicated that the BBB was disrupted at W1, leading to increased permeability. This indicates that both structural and functional integrity were compromised at this stage, thereby creating a permissive environment for the infiltration of peripheral myeloid cells into the central nervous system (CNS) during the acute phase of colitis. At W4, although partial recovery was observed, BBB permeability remained elevated compared with normal levels (Fig. 2E). These findings suggest that, despite apparent structural recovery of the BBB and GVB, the functional integrity of the BBB had not fully returned to baseline. Notably, despite this residual increase in BBB permeability, we did not observe significant infiltration of peripheral myeloid cells at W4 (Fig. 3C, D), which may be attributable to the normalization of peripheral Mo/MΦ levels and the attenuation of central chemotactic signaling at this stage.

Owing to the complexity and specificity of brain regions for functions [27], we selected brain nuclei involved in regulating visceral functions and pain processing to investigate peripheral myeloid cell infiltration during the inflammation phase (W1) of mice with colitis, including the mPFC, ACC, NAc, IC, PVN, and PAG. In these nuclei, the infiltration of CD11b+CD45+Ly6G+ neutrophils (PMN) and CD11b+CD45hiLy6G−Ly6C+ monocytes/macrophages (Mo/MΦ) were assessed by flow cytometry (Fig. 2F, G). The results revealed no significant change in the number of peripheral PMN (Fig. 2H), whereas peripheral Mo/MΦ showed significant infiltration into the ACC, PVN, and PAG (Fig. 2I). The infiltration degree of peripheral Mo/MΦ in PAG was the most obvious. Besides, given that the PAG is the core structure of the endogenous nociceptive modulation system associated with pain behaviors [28], we selected the PAG as the primary focus of this study.

The origin of peripheral TREM-1+ Mo/MΦ in the PAG during the inflammation phase of colitis

In the PAG (Fig. 3A), TREM-1 expression was analyzed by flow cytometry on CD11b+CD45intLy6G−Ly6C− microglia and peripheral myeloid cells, including CD11b+CD45+Ly6G+Ly6C− PMN and CD11b+CD45hiLy6G−Ly6C+ Mo/MΦ during the inflammation and remission phase of colitis according to the previous gating strategy [17, 29] (Fig. 3B). The number of peripheral Mo/MΦ was significantly elevated at W1 and returned to baseline level at W4, whereas the number of peripheral PMN showed no significant changes throughout the process (Fig. 3C, D). Meanwhile, immunofluorescence showed a clear infiltration of Iba1⁺/Hexb⁻ peripherally derived Mo/MΦ into the vlPAG at W1, further supporting the above findings (Supplementary Fig. 1). Furthermore, TREM-1 expression on peripheral Mo/MΦ by flow cytometry was markedly increased at W1 and normalized at W4. In contrast, TREM-1 expression on microglia remained stable at W1 but increased at W4, while TREM-1 expression on PMN showed no significant change during this period (Fig. 3E, F). These differences in TREM-1 expression across various cell types and time points suggested that TREM-1 may play distinct roles in microglia and peripheral Mo/MΦ at different stages of colitis.

Simultaneously, we analyzed TREM-1 expression on Mo/MΦ in peripheral blood, spleen, and colon tissues, which were enriched with myeloid cells, by flow cytometry (Fig. 3G). At W1, TREM-1 expression on Mo/MΦ was significantly elevated in both the blood and colon tissues, consistent with Mo/MΦ in the PAG, whereas no significant change was observed in TREM-1 expression on Mo/MΦ in the spleen (Fig. 3H–M). Additionally, TREM-1 protein levels in the PAG, colon, and spleen were assessed by western blot to verify the results of flow cytometry, finding that TREM-1 expression was markedly increased in all three regions at W1 (Supplementary Fig. 2A–C). The inconsistency of results in the spleen may be owing to the presence of many other immune cells expressing TREM-1 in the spleen.

To further determine the origin of Mo/MΦ in the PAG, we selectively depleted monocytes in peripheral blood by administering CLP or control liposomes (vehicle) within 7 days of DSS consumption. This led to a significant reduction in the number of peripheral Mo/MΦ in the PAG and the blood at W1 (Fig. 3N–Q). Therefore, the consistency of TREM-1 expression and the monocyte clearance experiment by CLP suggests that the TREM-1+ Mo/MΦ in the PAG at W1 may predominantly originate from peripheral blood and colon tissues.

The activity of vlPAG GABAergic neurons mediates visceral pain of colitis

It is generally recognized that the PAG is divided into four subregions: dmPAG, dlPAG, lPAG, and vlPAG [30]. Among these, the vlPAG is particularly associated with pain regulation [28, 31]. To investigate the role of the vlPAG neurons in visceral pain, we co-stained c-Fos, GABA, and CaMKIIα by immunofluorescence in the CON, DSS W1, and DSS W4 groups to observe the activation of GABAergic and glutamatergic neurons (Fig. 4A). Under physiological conditions, approximately 65% of neurons in the vlPAG were GABAergic, while about 25% were glutamatergic (Fig. 4B). Therefore, regarding the GABAergic neurons in the vlPAG, there was a significant elevation in the number of c-Fos+ GABAergic neurons at W1, followed by a slight decrease at W4, while it remained above normal (Fig. 4C). However, there was no significant change in the number of c-Fos⁺ glutamatergic neurons at either W1 or W4 (Fig. 4D). Furthermore, to record the firing activity of vlPAG GABAergic neurons, a Cre-dependent AAV vector (AAV-EF1α-DIO-mCherry) was bilaterally injected into the vlPAG of Vgat-Cre mice. Approximately 4 weeks after viral injection, whole-cell patch-clamp recordings were then performed on identified vlPAG GABAergic neurons in brain slices. Compared with the CON group, the firing rates in both the DSS W1 and DSS W4 groups were increased, with the DSS W1 group exhibiting a higher firing rate than the DSS W4 group (Fig. 4F, G). However, the resting potential did not show significant changes across these groups (Fig. 4H). Moreover, the threshold current induced by step currents in vlPAG GABAergic neurons was significantly decreased in the DSS W1 group, and there was a decreasing trend in the DSS W4 group (Fig. 4I). This considerable increase in c-Fos expression, along with the decrease in threshold current in vlPAG GABAergic neurons of mice with colitis, suggests dramatic activation of these neurons. This activation aligns with the presence of acute visceral pain during the inflammation phase of colitis in mice, while the remission phase is still accompanied by chronic visceral pain.

To further clarify the relationship between the activity of vlPAG GABAergic neurons and visceral pain, we employed a chemogenetic approach in Vgat-Cre mice. AAV-EF1α-DIO-mCherry, AAV-hSyn-DIO-hM3D(Gq)-mCherry, or AAV-hSyn-DIO-hM4D(Gi)-mCherry were bilaterally injected into the vlPAG of Vgat-Cre mice. Subsequently, 4 weeks after viral injection, chemogenetic neuronal manipulation was achieved by intraperitoneal administration of CNO under baseline conditions, at the DSS W1 phase, and at the DSS W4 phase. Then, 1 h after CNO injection, visceral pain threshold and visceral sensitivity were tested. In normal Vgat-Cre mice, the activation of vlPAG GABAergic neurons decreased the visceral pain threshold significantly, consistent with the increased electromyographic responses to CRD at 60 and 80 mmHg. In contrast, inhibition of these neurons did not induce significant changes under normal conditions (Supplementary Fig. 3A–E). When vlPAG GABAergic neuronal activity was inhibited at W1, the visceral pain threshold was markedly elevated, accompanied by a significant reduction in electromyographic responses to CRD at 60 and 80 mmHg. Conversely, activation of these neurons at W1 did not produce notable effects (Supplementary Fig. 3F–J). At W4, both activation and inhibition of vlPAG GABAergic neurons altered the visceral pain threshold and sensitivity (Supplementary Fig. 3 K–O). Collectively, these findings indicate that the heightened activity of vlPAG GABAergic neurons in mice with colitis is closely associated with visceral pain, and that suppressing their activity effectively alleviates both acute and chronic visceral hypersensitivity.

Peripheral Mo/MΦ infiltration induces visceral pain via vlPAG GABAergic neuron activation

It has been found that circulating monocytes can infiltrate the diseased brain and function differently from microglia, while their effects on pain-related neurons remain unclear [32]. To determine whether peripheral Mo/MΦ infiltration contributes to the enhanced activity of vlPAG GABAergic neurons observed in mice with colitis, we first inhibited the infiltration of peripheral Mo/MΦ into the vlPAG by a method employed in previous studies [25, 33]. Specifically, the CCR2 inhibitor RS102895 (40 μg in 600 nL) or the control vehicle (600 nL PBS) was microinjected daily into the vlPAG during DSS administration (Fig. 5A). At W1, we assessed visceral sensitivity through a visceral pain threshold test and electromyography. The results showed a significant increase in the visceral pain threshold in the DSS W1 RS102895 group compared with the DSS W1 vehicle group (Fig. 5B), along with a marked reduction in visceral sensitivity at 60 and 80 mmHg during the CRD (Fig. 5C–E). Concurrently, immunofluorescence analysis revealed a significant decrease in the number of c-Fos+ GABAergic neurons in the vlPAG in the DSS W1 RS102895 group (Fig. 5F, G). Notably, at W4, chronic visceral pain in mice with colitis was also alleviated following RS102895 administration during W1 (Fig. 5H–K), accompanied by a corresponding decrease in c-Fos+ GABAergic neurons in the vlPAG (Fig. 5L, M). The efficacy of RS102895 was confirmed by flow cytometry, which demonstrated a significant decrease in the number of peripheral Mo/MΦ within the PAG at W1. This reduction was no longer evident at W4, as Mo/MΦ numbers had returned to baseline. Importantly, the microinjection of RS102895 into the vlPAG did not alter the number of Mo/MΦ in the colon (Fig. 5N–Q). These findings demonstrate that peripheral-derived Mo/MΦ in the PAG promote acute visceral pain via vlPAG GABAergic neuron activation during the acute phase of colitis. During the remission phase, although peripheral Mo/MΦ in the PAG have largely resolved, persistent activation of vlPAG GABAergic neurons is associated with sustained chronic visceral hypersensitivity.

According to previous studies, microglia play an important role in the generation and maintenance of pain behaviors [34, 35]. Here, to investigate the role of microglia in the vlPAG, we used CX3CR1CreERT2 × iDTR mice. Then, 4 weeks after gene expression was induced by tamoxifen, we specifically depleted microglia by targeted injections of DT working solution or PBS into the vlPAG on days 4, 5, and 6 during the 7 days of DSS-induced colitis (Supplementary Fig. 4A). Compared with the DSS W1 + PBS group, the changes in visceral pain threshold and visceral sensitivity in the DSS W1 + DT group were not significant (Supplementary Fig. 4B–E). Immunofluorescence staining of Iba1 and DAPI in the vlPAG confirmed a reduction in microglia numbers (Supplementary Fig. 4F, G). However, when DT was injected into the vlPAG for three consecutive days before W4, microglia depletion alleviated visceral sensitivity markedly (Supplementary Fig. 4H–K), and a reduction in microglia was observed (Supplementary Fig. 4L, M). The differential changes following the specific clearance of microglia in the vlPAG indicate that, in the inflammation phase of colitis, peripheral Mo/MΦ have a pronounced effect on the GABAergic neuron activation associated with visceral pain compared with microglia. However, in the remission phase, after the number of peripheral Mo/MΦ in the PAG normalizes, the influence of microglia on visceral pain becomes increasingly significant. This is consistent with the temporal differences in TREM-1 expression in these cells. We also confirmed that this method specifically depleted microglia in the vlPAG without affecting microglial counts in adjacent brain regions (Supplementary Fig. 5A, B).

In the above study, we highlighted the crucial role of peripheral Mo/MΦ in mediating visceral pain within the vlPAG of mice with colitis, particularly at W1. Although PMN infiltration in the PAG did not reach statistical significance, a modest upward trend was observed at W1 (Fig. 2G). To further assess the potential contribution of peripheral PMNs in the PAG, we administered anti-Ly6G, a neutrophil-depleting antibody, during W1 to reduce PMN infiltration (Supplementary Fig. 6A, B, G, H). However, neither the visceral pain threshold nor visceral sensitivity showed significant improvement at W1 (Supplementary Fig. 6C–F) or W4 (Supplementary Fig. 6I–L). Therefore, we excluded a major role of peripheral PMN infiltration in the PAG in mediating visceral pain associated with colitis.

Pharmacological inhibition of TREM-1 mitigates vlPAG GABAergic neuronal activity during the inflammation phase

As TREM-1 was expressed predominantly on infiltrating Mo/MΦ in the vlPAG during the inflammation phase of colitis (Fig. 3F), we proceeded to explore whether TREM-1 is involved in the infiltration of peripheral Mo/MΦ into the brain during the inflammation phase of colitis and its impact on visceral pain. In this part, LP17, an antagonistic peptide of TREM-1, was delivered intranasally as a noninvasive route that could enhance central delivery [36]. LP17 (25 μg/day) was administered intranasally for seven consecutive days during the DSS administration period, while a scrambled control peptide was given to the control group (Fig. 6A). At W1, visceral pain thresholds and electromyogram recordings revealed that the DSS W1 LP17 group had a significantly increased pain threshold compared with the DSS W1 control peptide group (Fig. 6B), and visceral sensitivity was notably reduced at 40, 60, and 80 mmHg of CRD (Fig. 6C–E). To verify whether LP17 reached the vlPAG, we labeled LP17 and Dextran 70 with rhodamine red fluorescent dye and administered them intranasally to mice. Dextran 70, with a molecular weight of 70 kDa, generally cannot bypass the BBB or enter the brain by intranasal administration. The results showed that no red fluorescence was detected in the Dextran 70 group (Supplementary Fig. 7A), whereas clear red fluorescence overlapping with cell nuclei was observed in the LP17 group. (Fig. 6F and Supplementary Fig. 7A). Further analysis of the PAG using western blot and flow cytometry revealed reduced expression of TREM-1 in the DSS W1 LP17 group (Fig. 6G, H), along with a decreased number of peripheral Mo/MΦ infiltrating the PAG (Fig. 6I, J) and lower TREM-1 expression on these cells (Fig. 6K, L). Immunofluorescence also indicated a reduction in the number of c-Fos+ GABAergic neurons in the vlPAG (Fig. 6M, N), aligning with the observed alleviation of acute visceral pain in mice with colitis.

Moreover, to specifically investigate the role of TREM-1 in the vlPAG, TREM-1 small interfering RNA (Trem1 small interfering RNA [siRNA]: 5′-GGUUCUUCAGAUGUGUUCATT-3′ and 3′-UGAACACAUCUGAAGAACCTT-5′, Generay, China) or negative control siRNA (NC siRNA) was microinjected into the vlPAG at day 4 during DSS administration. These findings collectively suggested that pharmacological inhibition of TREM-1 in the vlPAG attenuated the infiltration of peripheral Mo/MΦ and reduced the activation of vlPAG GABAergic neurons, thereby alleviating acute visceral pain in mice with colitis.

Genetic ablation of TREM-1 reduces vlPAG GABAergic neuronal activity during both inflammation and remission phases

After observing reduced Mo/MΦ infiltration by pharmacologically inhibiting TREM-1, transgenic Trem1−/− mice for systemic TREM-1 knockout and wild-type (WT) C57 mice were utilized to test the effect of genetic Trem1 ablation (Fig. 7A). Firstly, at W1, compared with the DSS W1 WT group, the DSS W1 Trem1−/− group showed a significant increase in visceral pain threshold (Fig. 7B), with a marked decrease in visceral sensitivity at 80 mmHg of CRD (Fig. 7C–E). After observing this phenomenon, we conducted western blotting, which confirmed a significant reduction in TREM-1 expression in both the PAG and colon tissue of the DSS W1 Trem1−/− group (Fig. 7F, G). From flow cytometry (Fig. 7H), the number of Mo/MΦ and the TREM-1 expression in the PAG, blood, and colon tissue were decreased in the DSS W1 Trem1−/− group (Fig. 7I–Q). Additionally, immunofluorescence indicated a reduced number of activated vlPAG GABAergic neurons (Fig. 7R, S), consistent with the alleviation of acute visceral pain. Secondly, we performed similar experiments at W4 to assess chronic visceral pain (Fig. 8A). The DSS W4 Trem1−/− group exhibited a higher visceral pain threshold (Fig. 8B) and decreased visceral sensitivity at 60 and 80 mmHg, mirroring the results at W1 (Fig. 8C–E). Furthermore, TREM-1 expression in both PAG and colon tissues by western blot remained reduced (Fig. 8F, G), and the number of activated vlPAG GABAergic neurons decreased by immunofluorescence, corresponding to the alleviation of chronic visceral pain (Fig. 8H, I).

Furthermore, similar experiments were performed in DSS W4 NC siRNA and DSS W4 TREM1 siRNA groups. Although no significant changes were observed in the number or TREM-1 expression of infiltrating peripheral Mo/MΦ in the vlPAG at W4 (Supplementary Fig. 8O–Q), visceral hypersensitivity was markedly alleviated in the DSS W4 TREM1 siRNA group (Supplementary Fig. 8 K–N), accompanied by a reduction in the number of c-Fos⁺ GABAergic neurons (Supplementary Fig. 8S, T). TREM-1-expressing peripheral monocytes mediate visceral pain via vlPAG GABAergic neurons and opioid-dependent descending modulation

To further investigate the role of TREM-1-expressing Mo/MΦ in the vlPAG, we next isolated Ly6C+ monocytes expressing TREM-1 from peripheral blood and stereotaxically injected them into the vlPAG of naïve mice to assess the effect on vlPAG neuronal activity (Fig. 9A). Firstly, we isolated Ly6C− and Ly6C+ monocytes from the peripheral blood of acute mice with colitis at W1 and sorted the cells on the basis of the surface expression of CD45, CD11b, Ly6G, and Ly6C by FACS. The naive WT mice were stereotaxically injected into the vlPAG with either PBS, Ly6C− monocytes, or Ly6C+ monocytes (1 × 104 cells in 0.5 μL PBS). After 24 h, compared with the naive + PBS and naive + Ly6C− monocyte groups, the naive + Ly6C+ monocyte group exhibited a significantly decreased visceral pain threshold (Fig. 9B) and increased visceral sensitivity at CRD pressures of 40 and 80 mmHg (Fig. 9C–E). Additionally, flow cytometry revealed an elevated number of peripheral Mo/MΦ in the PAG (Fig. 9F, G), and immunofluorescence also showed an increase in c-Fos+ GABAergic neurons in the vlPAG in the naive + Ly6C+ monocyte group (Fig. 9H, I). These findings provide robust evidence that TREM-1-expressing Mo/MΦ infiltration in the PAG mediates visceral pain by increasing the excitability of vlPAG GABAergic neurons.

Previous studies have established that the PAG is a critical hub in descending pain modulation, where GABAergic neurons exert inhibitory control over output neurons [37]. On the basis of this, we sought to determine whether endogenous opioid signaling in the spinal cord is involved in this circuit. We first measured endogenous opioid peptide levels in the spinal cord. Compared with the naive + PBS and naive + Ly6C⁻ monocyte groups, the naive + Ly6C⁺ monocyte group exhibited significantly reduced levels of Dyn A and MEK, indicating impaired endogenous opioid signaling (Supplementary Fig. 9A, B). This reduction was consistent with the increased activity of vlPAG GABAergic neurons observed under the same conditions (Fig. 9H). To further assess the functional role of opioid signaling, naloxone was administered intrathecally in the naive + Ly6C⁺ monocyte group (Supplementary Fig. 9C). At 1 h after administration, visceral pain threshold was significantly reduced, accompanied by increased visceral sensitivity at CRD pressures of 60 and 80 mmHg (Supplementary Fig. 9D–G), indicating that blockade of opioid receptors exacerbates pain responses. We next examined whether naloxone could reverse the analgesic phenotype following CCR2 inhibition. In DSS W1 mice, treatment with the RS102895 significantly increased spinal levels of Dyn A and MEK compared with vehicle-treated mice (Supplementary Fig. 9H, I), which was consistent with alleviation of visceral hypersensitivity (Fig. 5B–E) and reduced activity of vlPAG GABAergic neurons (Fig. 5F, G). Importantly, intrathecal administration of naloxone in RS102895-treated mice (Supplementary Fig. 9J) reversed the analgesic effect, as evidenced by reduced visceral pain threshold (Supplementary Fig. 9K) and increased visceral sensitivity at CRD pressures of 60 and 80 mmHg (Supplementary Fig. 9L–N). Collectively, these findings indicate that vlPAG GABAergic neurons regulate descending pain modulation through an opioid-dependent disinhibition mechanism. Increased activity of these neurons suppresses PAG output and facilitates pain, whereas their inhibition activates descending analgesic pathways via endogenous opioid signaling. Importantly, TREM-1-driven peripheral Mo/MΦ infiltration emerges as a critical upstream event that enhances vlPAG GABAergic neuronal activity and promotes visceral pain, highlighting a neuroimmune mechanism with potential therapeutic implications for the management of pain in IBD.

Discussion

In this study, we demonstrated that the infiltration of peripheral-derived Mo/MΦ into the PAG played a more vital role than resident microglia and peripheral neutrophils in enhancing the excitability of vlPAG GABAergic neurons during the inflammation phase of colitis. This promoted acute visceral pain during intestinal inflammation, with neuronal activation persisting into the remission phase and contributing to chronic visceral pain. By microinjection of Ly6C+ monocytes into the PAG, peripheral TREM-1+ Mo/MΦ was found to induce visceral pain by activating vlPAG GABAergic neurons. We also proved that pharmacological inhibition or genetic deletion of TREM-1 significantly reduced the infiltration of peripheral Mo/MΦ into the PAG, thereby diminishing GABAergic neuron activation. This intervention not only alleviated visceral hypersensitivity during the inflammation phase of colitis but also mitigated chronic visceral pain during the remission phase.

IBD is a chronic condition marked by recurrent intestinal inflammation [3]. Currently, dextran sodium sulfate (DSS, 2–5%) is widely used to damage the epithelial cells of the colon to trigger an abnormal immune response. It models clinical visceral pain owing to its affordability, ease of use, and ability to control both the induction and duration of disease [38]. In this study, mice experienced acute visceral pain associated with the acute inflammation phase of colitis after drinking 2% DSS for 7 days, while the colitis remission period was marked by chronic visceral pain, which closely resembles the changes observed in human UC. As such, this DSS model provides a reliable system for studying the mechanisms underlying visceral pain at various stages of disease and evaluating therapeutic efficacy [15, 23].

Up to 60% of patients with IBD experience abdominal pain in their lifetime regardless of disease activity, which significantly reduces the quality of life, while current opioid use can lead to addiction and other related side effects [6, 7]. As a result, researchers are actively exploring the underlying mechanisms of visceral pain to identify specific therapeutic targets. Visceral pain associated with colitis arises from a complex interplay of peripheral sensitization, neuroimmune interactions, spinal and central sensitization, as well as dysregulation of the gut–brain axis [6, 12, 39–41]. Currently, central sensitization and neuroplasticity are increasingly recognized as key contributors to visceral hypersensitivity [42, 43]. Therefore, IBD-related pain should not be viewed solely as a consequence of intestinal inflammation but rather as an extraintestinal manifestation involving CNS dysfunction [44]. Under pathological conditions (e.g., traumatic injury, chronic stress, infections), monocytes may permeate the blood–central nervous system (CNS) barriers via CCR2-dependent mechanisms, differentiate into macrophages, and modulate neuronal function by secreting inflammatory mediators, complementing the roles of resident microglia and border-associated macrophages [42]. Therefore, based on these, we concentrated on peripheral-derived Mo/MΦ in the brain and their role in modulating visceral pain, aiming to further investigate the gut–CNS connection. To examine the impact of peripheral-derived Mo/MΦ on visceral hypersensitivity, RS102895 was used to bind specifically to the β subunit of the CCR2 receptor with relatively high affinity, resulting in potent inhibition of CCR2 signaling [45], which could reduce monocyte recruitment to the brain in mice with colitis. Similarly, a neutrophil scavenger (anti-Ly6G) was also administered to deplete peripheral neutrophils during the period of acute colitis in mice to compare their impact on intracerebral infiltration [46, 47]. By comparison, we found that the reduction of Mo/MΦ infiltration in the brain of mice with colitis resulted in attenuation of visceral sensitivity, which was not greatly affected by peripheral neutrophils. Although we did not specifically investigate the exact source of peripheral Mo/MΦ in the brain in this part of the experiment, the findings demonstrated that peripheral blood and colon tissue are significant sources, through the observation of TREM-1 expression on Mo/MΦ via flow cytometry and the clearance of monocytes from peripheral blood using CLP injected into the tail vein.

The PAG, a ring-shaped layer of gray matter surrounding the midbrain aqueduct, is divided into four subregions: the dmPAG, dlPAG, lPAG, and vlPAG branches [28]. Of these, the vlPAG is most closely associated with pain modulation. It regulates not only physiological functions such as pain, breathing, vocalization, urination, and cardiovascular activity but also emotional responses such as anxiety and fear [28, 30, 31, 48, 49]. Notably, approximately 65% of neurons in the vlPAG of mice are GABAergic, and GABA-mediated neuronal activity within the vlPAG has been reported to play a pivotal role in regulating the endogenous descending pain-inhibitory pathway [20]. In our study, we further observed a marked activation of GABAergic neurons in the vlPAG during colitis, whereas glutamatergic neurons showed no significant changes. Given the prominent peripheral Mo/MΦ infiltration and the unique functional significance of the vlPAG, we therefore selected vlPAG GABAergic neurons as the primary focus of our investigation. Furthermore, we found that enhanced vlPAG GABAergic neuronal activity contributed to reduced spinal endogenous opioid levels and increased visceral pain. Conversely, reduced neuronal activity following CCR2 inhibition restored opioid signaling and alleviated visceral pain, which were reversed by naloxone. These findings indicate that vlPAG GABAergic neurons regulate descending pain modulation through an opioid-dependent disinhibition mechanism.

At present, research on visceral pain has largely focused on molecular mechanisms within the peripheral nervous system [50–52] and on resident immune cells in the CNS, such as microglia [23, 40, 53]. However, few studies have investigated the role of peripheral immune cells in the brain, particularly their interaction with microglia and neurons. Given that, we explored the relationship between the Mo/MΦ infiltration into the vlPAG and the alteration of GABAergic neuronal activity in the region. In this study, we also explored the role of microglia by administering DT to selectively ablate microglia in the vlPAG of CX3CR1CreERT2 × iDTR mice, without affecting microglia in adjacent regions. The results revealed that during the acute phase, peripheral Mo/MΦ play a more prominent role than microglia in the vlPAG. However, during the remission phase, microglia become key contributors to the persistence of chronic visceral pain. Thus, the findings demonstrate that peripheral Mo/MΦ and microglia in the vlPAG assume distinct dominant roles at different stages of colitis, aligning with the temporal differences in TREM-1 expression observed in these cells by flow cytometry.

TREM-1 is a key subtype of immune receptors, known for amplifying inflammatory responses by inducing signaling pathways via TLR and NLR [54]. Its role in regulating intestinal homeostasis and inflammation, particularly in macrophages, has gained significant attention [55]. Our findings in DSS-induced acute colitis, where the number of macrophages was markedly increased and the expression of TREM-1 was elevated, are consistent with previous studies in patients with IBD [56, 57]. Therefore, we investigated the role of TREM-1 in peripheral Mo/MΦ infiltration using both pharmacological inhibition and genetic knockout approaches. Although intranasal LP17 administration represents a noninvasive approach by which LP17 may access the CNS via olfactory and trigeminal pathways [36], this route also has important limitations, as intranasally administered molecules may be partially absorbed into the systemic circulation through the highly vascularized nasal mucosa [58, 59]. In parallel, TREM-1 knockout represents a global deletion model that affects both central and peripheral compartments. To further delineate the region-specific role of TREM-1, we performed local siRNA-mediated knockdown within the vlPAG, which enabled a more direct evaluation of TREM-1 function in this brain region. Collectively, these complementary approaches indicate that TREM-1 mediates the infiltration of peripheral-derived Mo/MΦ into the vlPAG, and it modulates visceral pain by enhancing the excitability of vlPAG GABAergic neurons. Furthermore, the experiment involving the stereotaxic injection of Ly6C+ monocytes into vlPAG offers compelling evidence of TREM-1-expressing-Mo/MΦ’s critical role in mediating visceral pain, highlighting a distinction from prior studies that primarily focused on either intestinal macrophages [60, 61] or resident microglia in the CNS [15, 23]. Together, these findings suggest that, during the active phase of colitis, targeting Mo/MΦ and developing drugs that specifically inhibit TREM-1 may offer a particularly effective strategy for alleviating visceral pain in patients with IBD. In addition, it could play a unique role in reducing central neuron sensitization during the acute phase, potentially preventing the onset and progression of chronic visceral pain.

This study has several limitations that should be taken into consideration. First, we did not comprehensively delineate the precise origin of peripheral-derived Mo/MΦ infiltrating the brain. Lineage-tracing approaches would provide more definitive evidence regarding their sources and migratory routes. Moreover, peripheral Mo/MΦ infiltration was also observed in other brain regions; however, the functional roles of infiltrating Mo/MΦ may differ across distinct neural circuits, which warrants further region-specific investigation. In addition, although we demonstrated that peripheral-derived Mo/MΦ enhance the excitability of vlPAG GABAergic neurons, the underlying cellular and molecular mechanisms mediating this effect were not examined in detail and remain to be elucidated. Furthermore, while our findings identify TREM-1-dependent Mo/MΦ infiltration as a critical mediator driving visceral pain via vlPAG GABAergic neuron activation, the precise mechanisms by which TREM-1 regulates peripheral Mo/MΦ infiltration into the CNS require further investigation. Finally, all experiments in this study were conducted in male mice to minimize variability associated with hormonal fluctuations during the estrous cycle [62, 63]. Although IBD shows a slight male predominance in some cohorts (approximately 50–60%) [64, 65], female individuals may experience a higher prevalence or greater severity of chronic visceral pain during remission of IBD [66]. Previous studies have also demonstrated sex differences in visceral pain sensitivity [66] and immune mechanisms involved in nociception [67]. Therefore, future studies including female mice will be necessary to determine whether the mechanisms identified here are conserved across sexes.

Conclusions

Peripheral Mo/MΦ TREM-1 is identified as a mediator of visceral pain via activation of vlPAG GABAergic neurons, complementing the prevailing view that microglia play a crucial role in the CNS. This finding may offer a promising therapeutic approach for treating acute intestinal inflammation and visceral pain in colitis, while also providing a potential target for managing chronic visceral pain that persists in patients with IBD.
