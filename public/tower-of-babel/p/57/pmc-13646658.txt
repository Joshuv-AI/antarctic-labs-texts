Growth Temperature Alters the Thermal Sensitivity of Mesophyll Conductance in Wild and Cultivated Tomato
Authors: Bar Ben Zeev, Adi Yaaran, Yotam Zait
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTMesophyll conductance (g m) is a key component of internal CO2 diffusion and can limit photosynthesis in C3 leaves. Temperature responses of g m are commonly treated as intrinsic properties of the leaf, yet it remains unclear whether they acclimate to growth conditions. Here, we tested whether growth temperature alters the thermal sensitivity of g m and whether this differs between wild and cultivated tomato. We compared the wild species Solanum pennellii and the cultivated Solanum lycopersicum cv. M82 grown under four temperature regimes (16°C/10°C to 34°C/28°C, day/night). Using combined gas exchange and chlorophyll fluorescence, we quantified net photosynthesis (A n), g m and photorespiration (R l ). Growth temperature strongly altered the thermal sensitivity of g m in both species. In M82, g m Q 10 increased from 0.92 to 1.29 across growth temperatures, and in S. pennellii from 1.03 to 1.44. Activation energy showed a similar shift, from values near zero under cold growth to 19.6 and 27.8 kJ mol−1 under warm growth. In contrast, species effects on g m were small, whereas differences in A n were larger and associated with variation in biochemical capacity. Thermal optima of g m shifted with growth temperature in both species, while R l remained consistently temperature sensitive with limited acclimation. The CO2 response of g m were conserved across growth temperatures. These results show that the temperature sensitivity of g m is not fixed, but acclimates to growth temperature, with a stronger effect than species identity. We conclude that g m thermal sensitivity acclimates to growth temperature, but differences in photosynthetic performance are driven mainly by biochemical capacity rather than g m.Keywords: activation energy, CO2 diffusion, mesophyll conductance, photorespiration, photosynthesis, Q 10 , Solanum pennellii, temperature response, thermal acclimation, tomato

Introduction

Photosynthesis is the primary process by which plants assimilate carbon. In C3 leaves, net CO2 assimilation (A n) depends on both biochemical reactions and the diffusion of CO2 from the atmosphere to the chloroplast (Farquhar and Sharkey 1982). Along this pathway, CO2 encounters several barriers, including stomatal conductance (g sw), and internal diffusion through the intercellular airspaces and mesophyll tissues, which together are described by mesophyll conductance (g m) and regulate CO2 transfer from the intercellular airspaces to the chloroplast stroma. Although the biochemical limitations of photosynthesis have been studied extensively, it is now clear that internal CO2 diffusion through mesophyll tissue can also impose a substantial constraint on carbon assimilation (Farquhar et al. 1980; Harley et al. 1992; Tomás et al. 2013; von Caemmerer and Evans 2015).

Mesophyll conductance is therefore a key component of the internal CO2 diffusion pathway. Early photosynthesis models assumed that g m was sufficiently large to impose little limitation on photosynthesis (Farquhar et al. 1980), but this view has changed over the past two decades (Harley et al. 1992; Tholen et al. 2012; Evans 2021). A growing body of evidence shows that g m strongly influences the CO2 concentration at the site of carboxylation. Mesophyll conductance reflects both structural and physiological properties of the leaf. Anatomical traits such as cell wall thickness, chloroplast positioning, and the surface area of mesophyll cells exposed to intercellular airspaces determine the internal diffusion pathway of CO2 (John et al. 1994; Terashima et al. 2011; Ellsworth et al. 2018). In addition, g m is closely associated with photosynthetic capacity, photorespiration and stomatal conductance (Flexas et al. 2008). At the molecular level, proteins such as carbonic anhydrases and aquaporins have been proposed to facilitate CO2 transport within the mesophyll (Gillon and Yakir 2000; Li et al. 2020; Momayyezi et al. 2020). Despite this progress, the mechanisms regulating g m under different environmental conditions remain poorly understood.

Temperature is a key environmental driver of photosynthesis (Sage and Kubien 2007). The response of net CO2 assimilation (A n) to temperature typically follows a parabolic pattern with a thermal optimum (T opt), above and below which assimilation declines (Berry and Bjorkman 1980). Many species can acclimate to growth temperature through shifts in T opt or changes in the temperature sensitivity of underlying processes, allowing plants to maintain performance across different thermal environments (Way and Yamori 2014; Yamori et al. 2014).

Mesophyll conductance (g m) is also temperature dependent. Within a physiological range, g m generally increases with leaf temperature, as observed in species such as tobacco and spinach (Bernacchi et al. 2002; Yamori et al. 2006), although both the magnitude and shape of this response vary widely among species (Scafaro et al. 2011; von Caemmerer and Evans 2015; Qiu et al. 2017). However, most studies have focused on short‐term, instantaneous responses to leaf temperature, whereas much less is known about how g m acclimates to different growth temperatures. This distinction is important because thermal acclimation of photosynthesis is often interpreted in terms of biochemical changes, even though CO2 assimilation depends on CO2 diffusion from the atmosphere through stomata and internal diffusion within the leaf to the chloroplasts.

In particular, quantitative characterization of g m thermal sensitivity, such as Q 10 (the change in rate per 10°C increase in temperature) or activation energy (ΔHa, describing temperature dependence based on Arrhenius kinetics), as biochemical processes typically show stronger temperature dependence than physical CO2 diffusion processes (Bernacchi et al. 2002).

Understanding g m acclimation is important because thermal acclimation of photosynthesis is typically interpreted through changes in A n, yet these responses integrate both biochemical capacity and CO2 diffusion. If g m is relatively conserved, then shifts in T opt of photosynthesis are likely driven primarily by biochemical processes. Conversely, if g m acclimates strongly, it may play a more active role in shaping the temperature response of photosynthesis. Mesophyll conductance also exhibits characteristic short‐term responses to intercellular CO2 concentration (C i), typically remaining relatively stable near ambient conditions while declining at both low and high CO2 levels (Flexas et al. 2007; Márquez and Busch 2024; Tholen et al. 2012). Although this behaviour has been consistently reported across species, its underlying mechanism remains unresolved. Current evidence suggests that g m responses to CO2 are only partially coordinated with stomatal conductance and may instead arise from internal processes within the leaf, including variable CO2 fluxes along the diffusion pathway and potential contributions from enzymatic facilitation (Mizokami et al. 2019; Márquez and Busch 2024). However, it remains unclear whether the CO2 dependence of g m is modulated by environmental factors such as temperature, or whether it reflects conserved properties of the diffusion‐assimilation system. Addressing this question is critical for distinguishing between intrinsic regulation of g m and emergent behaviour resulting from the coupling between CO2 diffusion and photosynthetic metabolism.

Wild relatives of crop species are widely used as sources of genetic variation for stress tolerance and physiological plasticity (Hajjar and Hodgkin 2007), although comparative studies show that domesticated cultivars can equal or even exceed their wild progenitors for some physiological traits in specific environments (Scafaro et al. 2011; Pelech et al. 2025). In tomato, wild species occupy diverse environments and display substantial variation in leaf structural traits that influence internal CO2 diffusion through the intercellular airspaces and mesophyll tissues (Muir et al. 2014). This raises the possibility that domestication has altered the regulation of g m (Barbour et al. 2010; Scafaro et al. 2011; Tomeo and Rosenthal 2017). Selection for high photosynthetic capacity under optimal conditions may favour higher or less variable g m, whereas wild species may retain greater flexibility to cope with environmental variability. However, such flexibility may involve trade‐offs with structural traits associated with stress tolerance, such as increased leaf thickness or density, which can restrict CO2 diffusion (Niinemets et al. 2009; Tomás et al. 2013).

Here, we compared the temperature responses of CO2 assimilation and mesophyll conductance in two tomato species representing contrasting evolutionary backgrounds: the wild species Solanum pennellii and the cultivated tomato Solanum lycopersicum cv. M82 (Torgeman et al. 2024). Plants were grown under four temperature regimes (16°C/10°C, 22°C/16°C, 28°C/22°C and 34°C/28°C day/night) to test how growth temperature modifies the responses of A n and g m. Using combined gas exchange and chlorophyll fluorescence measurements, we quantified CO2 assimilation, mesophyll conductance, stomatal conductance, photorespiration and biochemical parameters derived from CO2 response curves. This approach allowed us to assess the extent of g m acclimation and determine whether domestication influences the thermal plasticity of internal CO2 diffusion as represented by g m.

Materials and Methods

Plant Material and Growth Conditions

The study was conducted in a controlled Phytotron greenhouse facility equipped with four independent day/night temperature‐controlled rooms set to 16°C/10°C, 22°C/16°C, 28°C/22°C and 34°C/28°C. Two tomato species were used: the cultivated S. lycopersicum cv. M82 and the wild species S. pennellii. Seeds were germinated at 28°C/22°C and seedlings were grown for 2 weeks under the same temperature regime in 4‐L pots filled with a growth medium composed of volcanic tuff, coconut coir and peat in varying proportions (Tuff Marom‐Golan, Ram 6, Israel). Plants were then transferred to the four temperature treatments and acclimated for 3 weeks prior to measurements. Plants were irrigated to full pot capacity twice daily to ensure complete soil saturation. Plants were grown under a long‐day photoperiod of 16 h light/8 h dark. Photosynthetic photon flux density (PPFD) at canopy height ranged between approximately 800 and 1200 µmol photons m−2 s−1 across the day. Light conditions were similar among the four temperatures‐controlled rooms. The irrigation solution contained Ca (2.0 ± 0.1 mM), Mg (1.2 ± 0.2 mM), NH4 + (0.27 ± 0.04 mM), NO3 − (4.6 ± 0.1 mM), P (0.30 ± 0.02 mM) and K (2.3 ± 0.2 mM), as previously described (Zait et al. 2017).

Gas Exchange and Chlorophyll Fluorescence Measurements

To quantify the temperature response of net photosynthesis (A n), stomatal conductance (g sw) and electron transport rate (J t) across growth temperatures, gas exchange and chlorophyll fluorescence were measured using a LI‐6800F portable photosynthesis system (LI‐COR Inc., Lincoln, NE, USA) equipped with a 6 cm2 fluorescence leaf chamber. Measurements were performed on the youngest fully expanded leaves that developed under the respective growth temperature. Unless otherwise stated, measurements were conducted at a PPFD of 1200 μmol photons m−2 s−1, a reference CO2 concentration of 420 μmol CO2 mol−1 air and a flow rate of 400 μmol air s−1. Relative humidity inside the chamber was maintained between 50% and 60%. Measurements were conducted under ambient oxygen concentration (21% O2), unless indicated otherwise. Additional measurements at 2% O2 were performed to support photorespiratory analyses and are presented in Supporting Material S1. Chlorophyll fluorescence was measured with the LI‐6800F chlorophyll fluorometer using the multiphase‐flash protocol, with a background actinic light of 1200 µmol photons m−2 s−1. The multiphase saturating flash consisted of three consecutive 300 ms phases (total duration 0.9 s) with a peak flash intensity of approximately 10 000 µmol photons m−2 s−1.

CO2 Response Curves

To characterize the response of A n to intercellular CO2 concentration (C i) across growth temperatures, A/C i curves were measured under light‐saturating conditions (PPFD 1200 μmol photons m−2 s−1). CO2 concentration was initially set to 420 μmol mol−1 and then decreased stepwise to 300, 200, 150, 100 and 50 μmol mol−1, followed by stepwise increases to 420, 600, 800, 1000, 1200 and 1500 μmol mol−1. Measurements at each step were recorded once gas exchange parameters stabilized. V cmax and J max were estimated in R using the fitaci function from the plantecophys package (Duursma 2015), with the bilinear fitting method and fitted V cmax and J max were normalized to 25°C for comparison across treatments. The A/C i measurements were conducted inside the respective growth rooms without active control of leaf temperature, and leaf temperature (T leaf) recorded by the LI‑6800 at each CO2 step is provided for all treatments in Table S1.

Temperature Response Measurements

To characterize short‐term responses of leaf gas exchange to changes in leaf temperature, leaf temperature (T leaf) was controlled using the LI‐6800 temperature block. Measurements were initiated at 20°C and increased in 5°C increments up to 40°C. At each temperature step all parameters were recorded once steady conditions were achieved.

Estimation of Respiration in the Light, the Intercellular CO2 Compensation Point, Mesophyll Conductance and CO2 Photorespiratory Flux

To resolve internal CO2 diffusion and photorespiratory flux across leaf temperatures, respiration in the light (R d), the intercellular CO2 compensation point (C i * , the intercellular CO2 concentration at which net CO2 assimilation is zero in the light), mesophyll conductance (g m) and photorespiration (R l) were estimated from combined gas exchange and chlorophyll fluorescence measurements.

To quantify R d and C i *, Laisk curves were measured at three PPFD levels (400, 200 and 100 μmol photons m−2 s−1) (Figure S1). At each light level, CO2 concentration was decreased stepwise to 300, 200, 150, 100, 75, 50, 25 μmol CO2 mol air −1. Because the fitted Laisk curves did not intersect at a single point, C i * and R d were estimated using a least‐squares optimization approach. Linear regressions were fitted to the linear portion of each Laisk curve, and the coordinates of the common intersection point were obtained by minimizing the sum of squared vertical distances between the candidate point and all fitted lines simultaneously.

Light Response Curves

To assess the light dependence of A n and J t under photorespiratory and non‐photorespiratory conditions, light‐response curves were conducted in ambient (21% O2) and low oxygen (2% O2), while the CO2 concentration was fixed at 420 μmol CO2 mol−1 air. The measurement sequence involved light intensities of 2000, 1500, 1200, 1000, 800, 600, 400, 300, 200, 100 and 50 μmol photons m−2 s−1. Measurements at each light level were recorded once steady‐state gas exchange was achieved. These measurements were conducted inside the respective growth rooms without active control of leaf temperature, allowing leaf temperature to reflect the growth regime. These measurements were used to support the calibration of J t and R l (Figures S3, S4 and S5).

Thermal Response Modelling

To distinguish between long‐term acclimation to growth temperature and short‐term responses to changes in T leaf, thermal behaviour of A n, g m and R l was analysed using complementary empirical and thermodynamic approaches (Nobel 2009).

To determine the thermal optimum (T opt) of each parameter, second‐order polynomial models were fitted to the response of the parameter to T leaf, (y=a·Tleaf2+b·Tleaf+c). T opt was calculated as −b/(2a) for each Species × growth temperature combination. The standard error of T opt was calculated from the fitted model coefficients.

To quantify how the sensitivity of A n, g m and R l to temperature differed between species and growth regimes, activation energy (ΔH a) was calculated within the activation range (T leaf ≤ 35°C). Only the temperature range showing a monotonic increase was included to avoid the influence of high‐temperature deactivation.

For each parameter, ln(parameter) was regressed against the inverse of absolute temperature (1/T) according to the Arrhenius relationship. In this linearized form, the slope of the regression equals −ΔH a/R. Activation energy (ΔH a) was therefore calculated directly from the slope as −slope × R, where R is the universal gas constant (8.314 J mol−1 K−1) and T is leaf temperature in Kelvin. Separate slopes were estimated for each Species × growth temperature combination using an interaction model. Thermal sensitivity of g m was expressed as Q 10. Q 10 was estimated by fitting the model: gm=gm.25×Q10(Tleaf−25)/10directly to measured values between 20°C and 35°C using nonlinear least‐squares regression. The fitted Q 10 parameter and its standard error were extracted from the model.

Statistical Analysis

All statistical analyses were performed in R version 4.3.2 (https://cran.r-project.org). Data are presented as means ± standard error (SE) unless otherwise stated. For gas exchange parameters (A n, g m and R l), effects of Species and Growth Temperature were evaluated using linear models including their interaction. Species differences in parameters derived from A/C i curves (A max, V cmax, J max ; Table 1) were tested using two‐sample t‐tests with Benjamini–Hochberg adjustment for multiple comparisons. For thermal activation analyses, full‐factorial linear models were fitted to ln(parameter) as a function of inverse temperature (1/T), Species, growth temperature, and their interactions, and Type III ANOVA was used to test the significance of Activation (Arrhenius slope), Species, Growth Temperature and their interactions. Statistical significance was defined at p < 0.05.

Results

CO2 Response Curves and Derived Biochemical Parameters

Differences between species and growth temperatures were evident in the CO2 response curves (Figure 1) and the derived biochemical parameters (Table 1). A max varied with growth temperature and was consistently higher in S. pennellii, with the largest interspecific differences observed at intermediate growth temperatures. For example, at 28°C/22°C, A max was 33% higher in S. pennellii than in M82 (Table 1). V cmax and J max also varied with growth temperature, indicating adjustments in biochemical capacity (Table 1). This biochemical context is important for interpreting g m responses, as it separates diffusion limitations from changes in carboxylation and electron transport capacity. Mesophyll conductance (g m) exhibited a non‐linear response to C i in both species, increasing at low C i and declining at higher C i (Figure 1b), with peak values at 250–350 µmol mol−1. Differences between species were relatively small and decreased with increasing growth temperature, with g m converging at high C i across treatments. Photorespiration (R l) showed a distinct peak at low‐to‐intermediate C i (~150–250 µmol mol−1) and declined at higher C i (Figure 1c). Maximum R l increased with growth temperature in both species, with the largest interspecific difference observed at 28°C/22°C.

Response to Rapid Changes in Leaf Temperature (T leaf Curves)

Net CO2 assimilation (A n), mesophyll conductance (g m), stomatal conductance (g sw) and photorespiration (R l) all varied with changes in leaf temperature (T leaf) across growth conditions (Figures 2 and 3). Measurements were conducted between 20°C and 40°C. A n (Figure 3a) showed a parabolic response to increasing T leaf in both species, increasing from 20°C to an intermediate optimum and declining at higher temperatures. The thermal optimum shifted with growth temperature, generally occurring around 25°C–30°C. Across treatments, S. pennellii maintained higher A n than M82, particularly at warmer growth conditions. g m (Figure 3b) increased with T leaf from 20°C and reached a maximum at intermediate temperatures, followed by stabilization or a slight decline at higher temperatures. The position of the maximum varied with growth temperature and species, but overall patterns were similar between genotypes, with relatively small interspecific differences. g sw (Figure 3c) showed a more variable response to T leaf than A n and g m, with no consistent pattern in the position of the maximum across growth temperatures. In general, the cultivated genotype (M82) exhibited a stronger increase in g sw at higher temperatures, whereas S. pennellii maintained relatively high conductance across the temperature range. R l (Figure 3d) increased strongly with increasing T leaf in both species and reached its maximum at higher temperatures than A n and g m. Across all growth conditions, peak R l occurred at 35°C–40°C, indicating a stronger temperature dependence compared to photosynthesis and diffusion processes.

Thermal Optima (T opt) of Net CO2 Assimilation, Mesophyll Conductance and Photorespiration

Thermal optima (T opt) of A n, g m and R l were estimated from fitted temperature response models and increased with growth temperature in both species.

For A n, T opt in M82 increased from 25.6°C at 16°C/10°C to 30.9°C at 28°C/22°C, followed by a slight decline to 29.8°C at 34°C/28°C. In S. pennellii, T opt of A n increased from 21.8°C to 29.6°C across the same range and remained lower than in M82 at 16°C/10°C (≈3.8°C) and 28°C/22°C (≈2.8°C).

T opt of g m showed a similar upward shift with growth temperature in both species. In M82, it increased from 27.0°C to 30.7°C, while in S. pennellii it increased from 27.2°C to 31.2°C, with only minor interspecific differences (≤ 0.7°C) across all growth conditions.

In contrast, T opt of R l was consistently higher than that of A n and g m. In M82, T opt of R l increased from 34.1°C at 16°C/10°C to 39.2°C at 28°C/22°C and declined slightly to 37.6°C at 34°C/28°C. In S. pennellii, it increased from 34.3°C to 40.3°C across the same conditions, exceeding M82 at the highest growth temperature by ≈2.7°C.

Thermal Sensitivity of Net CO2 Assimilation, Mesophyll Conductance and Photorespiration (Q 10)

Thermal sensitivity was assessed using Q 10 calculated at 25°C. Q 10 of g m increased with growth temperature in both species. In M82, Q 10 of g m increased from 0.92 at 16°C/10°C to 1.29 at 34°C/28°C. In S. pennellii, Q 10 of g m increased from 1.03 to 1.44 across the same growth temperatures and was consistently higher than in M82 at 22°C/16°C, 28°C/22°C and 34°C/28°C (Figure 3 and 4). Under cold growth conditions, g m showed low thermal activation (Q 10 ≈ 1), whereas under warmer growth conditions Q 10 increased markedly (Figures 4 and 5), indicating enhanced temperature sensitivity of g m. Q 10 of A n showed moderate variation across growth temperatures, ranging from 0.82 to 1.30 depending on species and treatment. In contrast, Q 10 of R l remained consistently high across all growth temperatures (1.84–2.32), indicating a stronger short‐term temperature sensitivity compared to A n and g m.

Activation Energy and Arrhenius Model Effects

Activation energy (ΔH a; kJ mol−1) was estimated from Arrhenius models fitted separately for A n, g m and R l across species and growth temperatures. Positive ΔH a values indicate thermal activation, whereas negative values indicate a decline in activity with increasing temperature.

ΔH a varied strongly with both parameter and growth temperature. For A n, ΔH a in M82 ranged from −6.5 at 16°C/10°C to 19.9 at 28°C/22°C, before decreasing to 8.66 at 34°C/28°C. In S. pennellii, ΔH a values were lower overall, ranging from −15.0 at 16°C/10°C to 11.4 at 28°C/22°C and approaching zero (0.13) at 34°C/28°C. Positive and significant ΔH a values for A n were detected at 28°C/22°C in both species and at 34°C/28°C in M82.

In contrast, ΔH a of g m increased progressively with growth temperature in both species. In M82, ΔH a shifted from −6.0 at 16°C/10°C to 19.6 at 34°C/28°C, while in S. pennellii it increased from 2.17 to 27.8 across the same growth temperatures. ΔH a of g m was not significantly different from zero under cold growth conditions but became positive and significant at 28°C/22°C and 34°C/28°C, particularly in S. pennellii.

ΔH a of R l was consistently high across all growth temperatures and showed little variation between species. In M82, ΔH a ranged from 50.7 to 64.2 (declining to 48.3 at 34°C/28°C), while in S. pennellii it ranged from 49.0 to 62.5 (declining to 46.6 at 34°C/28°C). All ΔH a estimates for R l were highly significant, and interspecific differences were small relative to the magnitude of ΔH a.

Discussion

Growth Temperature Acclimates the Thermal Sensitivity of Mesophyll Conductance

This study shows that mesophyll conductance (g m) acclimates to growth temperature across a wide thermal range (Figures 1 and 2). Most previous work focused on instantaneous temperature responses of g m under one or two growth conditions (Bernacchi et al. 2002; Yamori et al. 2006; Warren 2008; Scafaro et al. 2011; Walker et al. 2013; von Caemmerer and Evans 2015). Here, we show that g m itself acclimates in its temperature sensitivity. Thermal optima (T opt) of A n, g m and R l increased with growth temperature in both species (Figure 3), consistent with the known shift in photosynthetic temperature optima with growth temperature (Berry and Bjorkman 1980; Yamori et al. 2014). Evidence for acclimation of g m to growth temperature is limited and mostly based on studies with one or two growth conditions (Yamori et al. 2006), with little information on how T opt of g m changes across a wider growth temperature range. In our study, T opt of g m closely followed that of A n and showed only minor differences between species (≤ 0.7°C), whereas differences in A n were larger. In contrast, T opt of R l was consistently higher than that of A n and g m across all growth temperatures. These results indicate that shifts in T opt represent a general acclimation response, but do not explain the differences in thermal sensitivity observed for g m, and therefore reflect a distinct component of temperature acclimation.

The thermal sensitivity of g m increased with growth temperature in both species (Figures 4 and 5). Under cold conditions, g m showed weak temperature dependence (Q 10 ≈ 1; Figure 4), whereas under warm conditions Q 10 increased to 1.29 and 1.44 in M82 and S. pennellii, respectively. Similarly, ΔH a increased from values close to zero or negative to 19.6 and 27.8 kJ mol−1 (Figure 5). These results show that acclimation affects the temperature response of g m, not only its magnitude (e.g., Q 10 and ΔH a). Previous studies have reported positive responses of g m to temperature, often following an Arrhenius relationship (Bernacchi et al. 2002), with some evidence for partial acclimation under contrasting growth conditions (Yamori et al. 2006). However, g m temperature responses vary widely among genera and environmental conditions (von Caemmerer and Evans 2015; Qiu et al. 2017; Evans 2021). Differences between species have often been linked to structural traits, such as mesophyll cell wall thickness (Scafaro et al. 2011). and more recently to chloroplast surface area exposed to intercellular airspaces (Huang et al. 2022). While our study did not quantify anatomical traits or protein activity, the selective acclimation of g m thermal sensitivity, with relatively small changes in its magnitude, and the similar responses observed in both species, suggests that these changes are driven by modifications in the properties of the diffusion pathway rather than by large structural differences (Shrestha et al. 2019). Candidate mechanisms include aquaporins, which can function as CO2 permeable membrane channels and influence CO2 diffusion (Uehlein et al. 2003; Hanba et al. 2004; Flexas et al. 2006), and carbonic anhydrase, which may modulate CO2/HCO3 − interconversion and thereby affect effective g m (Gillon and Yakir 2000; Momayyezi et al. 2020).

Despite similarity in the acclimation of g m thermal sensitivity in both species, the differences in net CO2 assimilation (A n) were much larger (Figure 2 and Table 2). S. pennellii maintained higher A n under several growth temperatures (Figure 2a), while g m remained relatively similar between species (Figure 2b). This indicates partial decoupling between diffusion capacity and photosynthetic performance as higher A n in the wild species was supported primarily by biochemical capacity (V cmax and J max; Table 1) rather than by large differences in g m. It is important to note that under cold growth (16°C/10°C) S. pennellii had lower g sw than M82 (Figure 2c), so additional stomatal limitation of A n in the wild species at low temperature cannot be excluded.

In soybean, Pelech et al. (2025) showed that a modern cultivated line exhibits approximately two‐fold higher mesophyll conductance than its wild ancestors, indicating that domestication and breeding have indirectly selected for increased g m and reduced internal CO2 diffusion limitation, while also revealing substantial genotypic variation in g m available to breeders. Together with studies showing genotype‐dependent trade‐offs between g m, photosynthetic capacity and water‐use efficiency in soybean (Tomeo and Rosenthal 2017), these results suggest that domestication can alter both the magnitude and plasticity of g m in some crops, whereas in tomato our data indicate that wild‐cultivated differences in thermal performance arise mainly from biochemical plasticity despite relatively similar g m across growth temperatures.

In contrast to g m, photorespiration (R l) remained strongly temperature sensitive across all growth conditions, with consistently high Q 10 and ΔH a (Figures 4, 5 and 5). This behaviour is consistent with the strong temperature dependence of Rubisco oxygenation and associated metabolic fluxes that contribute to photorespiratory CO2 release (Berry and Bjorkman 1980; Bernacchi et al. 2001). The contrasting patterns between g m and R l indicate that thermal acclimation differs between processes. g m showed clear changes in temperature sensitivity, whereas R l remained relatively stable. This highlights the need to separate diffusion from biochemical and respiratory/photorespiratory processes when interpreting thermal acclimation (Atkin and Tjoelker 2003; Way and Yamori 2014).

Decoupled Controls of Mesophyll Conductance Temperature and CO2 Responses

Mesophyll conductance exhibited a consistent peaked response to intercellular CO2 concentration across all growth temperatures (Figure 1b), remaining relatively stable at intermediate C i and declining at both low and high CO2 levels. Notably, this pattern was conserved despite strong acclimation of gm thermal sensitivity, indicating that the processes governing the g m/C i relationship are largely independent of those controlling its temperature response.

Previous work suggests that the CO2 dependence of gm reflects interactions between CO2 diffusion and internal carbon fluxes, including photorespiration and the balance between CO2 and HCO3 − along the diffusion pathway, rather than changes in the physical conductance itself (Flexas et al. 2007; Márquez and Busch 2024). The conservation of the g m/C i response across temperatures in our study supports this interpretation and suggests that it reflects intrinsic properties of the diffusion‐assimilation system.

In contrast, the increase in g m thermal sensitivity with growth temperature indicates that acclimation primarily affects the physical properties of the diffusion pathway, most likely membrane permeability and associated processes pathway (Bernacchi et al. 2002; Yamori et al. 2010). This suggests that CO2 and temperature responses of g m are controlled by different processes. Given the rapid nature of g m responses, such changes are unlikely to arise solely from structural modifications of the leaf, which occur over longer developmental timescales (Flexas and Diaz‐Espejo 2015). The underlying mechanisms remain unresolved.

Conclusions

Growth temperature modified the thermal sensitivity of mesophyll conductance across a wide thermal range. In both tomato species, g m became more temperature sensitive in warm‐grown plants, demonstrating acclimation of internal CO2 diffusion through changes in Q 10 and ΔH a. However, differences in photosynthetic performance between S. pennellii and M82 were much larger than differences in g m and were supported mainly by variation in biochemical capacity (V cmax and J max), indicating that g m alone does not explain species differences in thermal performance. These results show that thermal acclimation of photosynthesis involves coordinated adjustments among diffusion, biochemistry and photorespiration. The acclimation observed here acts on the temperature sensitivity of g m while leaving the g m/C i response conserved, suggesting partially independent controls of CO2 and temperature‐dependent behaviour of internal diffusion.
