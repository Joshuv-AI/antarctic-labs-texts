Treatment of iliac arteriovenous malformation associated with trisomy 21: a case report
Authors: Jinming Yang, Weichao Zhao, Liping Liang, Guoqing Chi
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
Both arteriovenous malformation (AVM) and trisomy 21 are rare diseases. Studies have shown that individuals with trisomy 21 may have potential vascular malformations, with the main site of onset being the portal system. This case involved an adult patient with trisomy 21 who presented with pelvic iliac arteriovenous malformations. The patient underwent interventional treatment, and the prognosis was favorable. It is important to enhance the screening for vascular malformations in patients with trisomy 21 syndrome. At the same time, interventional treatment for AVMs is effective and increasingly important for managing AVMs.

Background

Arteriovenous malformation (AVM) is characterized by an abnormal cluster of aberrant blood vessels termed a central nidus between arteries and veins resulting from the absence of a capillary bed. Congenital pelvic AVMs are exceptionally rare and typically present from birth, progressing throughout an individual’s lifetime. The clinical manifestations vary depending on the lesion’s location and commonly include symptoms such as genitourinary, reproductive, or gastrointestinal bleeding; pulsatile masses; pain; local compression symptoms; and systemic circulatory symptoms [1].

There has been only one reported case of a male patient with a major complex pelvic arteriovenous malformation primarily presenting with hematuria and associated with Down syndrome [2]. This case study describes the successful treatment of iliac arteriovenous malformation in an adult female patient with Down syndrome who initially presented with abdominal pain.

Case presentation

The patient is a 40-year-old female of Han ethnicity from Beijing, China. She visited our department with the main complaint of “lower abdominal pain for 2 months, aggravated for 20 days.” She presented with intermittent lower abdominal distension and pain, lower back pain, pain in the early morning, and relief upon standing and moving. Additionally, she had missed her menstrual period for more than 2 months. She had previously visited an external clinic, where an abdominal ultrasound showed a “curved tubular structure with a blood flow signal in the lower left abdomen,” suggesting the possibility of an arteriovenous malformation. Further evaluation, including complete contrast-enhanced CT angiography of the aorta, was conducted in our department. The CT scan revealed a tortuous and dilated vascular shadow on the left side of the uterus, raising suspicions of an arteriovenous malformation. Three-dimensional reconstruction revealed the presence of multiple dilated structures resembling aneurysm, indicating the presence of a coexisting iliac aneurysm. The patient had a previous medical history of Down syndrome.

Physical examination of the abdomen did not reveal any significant tenderness or rebound tenderness. The gynecological examination did not show any apparent abnormalities. After ruling out urinary and gynecological conditions associated with abdominal pain, further assessment of cardiac function was conducted, and no significant abnormalities were detected. Therefore, a plan was made to perform digital subtraction angiography (DSA) on the patient, with simultaneous treatment of the arteriovenous malformation and embolization therapy for the aneurysm. The patients received intravenous medications for active treatment and nonsteroidal anti-inflammatory drugs for symptomatic analgesia. After 3D reconstruction, three venous aneurysms with the following longest diameters were identified: 15.8 mm, 31.0 mm, and 52.1 mm (Fig. 1).

Operation procedure: Preoperative preparations, including skin preparation, urinary catheterization, and iodine allergy testing, were carried out. Heart rate and blood pressure were continuously monitored throughout the procedure. Angiography was performed at multiple angles to visualize the central nidus and the dilated structures (Fig. 2A). The venous aneurysm was accessed through the venous side, allowing visualization of the venous aneurysm cavity and multiple venous outflow tracts (Fig. 2B). Coil embolization was performed on the venous side of the aneurysm, which significantly reduced venous flow velocity (Fig. 2C). Angiography from the arterial side revealed multiple arterial inlets, and a second venous aneurysm was observed (Fig. 2D). Selective coil embolization was then carried out on the second venous aneurysm (Fig. 2E). During the procedure, the central nidus were supplied by branches of the uterine artery. However, due to the patient’s severe pain, which she could not tolerate, the embolization procedure was discontinued after using a small amount of 710–1000 μm gelatin sponge particle embolic agent (Alicon, Zhejiang, China) for embolization. Immediately after embolization, the iliac vein or significant aneurysm disappeared (Fig. 2F). The operation was complete. During the operation, we utilized a wide assortment of various models of Interlock fibered IDC occlusion systems (Boston Scientific, MA, USA) and Tornado Embolization Microcoils (Cook, IN, USA).

Postoperatively, the patient’s abdominal pain symptoms disappeared. Within one week after surgery, the patient experienced a low-grade fever, which was considered to be related to aneurysm thrombosis and absorption fever. On postoperative day 1, a CT scan showed that the position of the coil was satisfactory (Fig. 3A). Three months postoperatively, the patient had no abdominal pain, but her menstrual cycle remained irregular. Since discharge, she had experienced only one menstrual period. There was an improvement in her dysmenorrhea symptoms. CTA revealed that there was no opacification of the iliac vein during the arterial phase (Fig. 3B), confirming successful embolization. Additionally, there was a significant improvement in the patient’s pain. At six and twelve months postoperatively, the patient’s menstrual cycle became regular, and her abdominal pain was greatly relieved. The rechecked CTA image (Fig. 3C and D) also showed no opacification of the iliac vein during the arterial phase.

Discussion and conclusions

Congenital pelvic AVMs arise from the presence of multiple connections between the arterial and low-resistance venous systems. These malformations are considered undifferentiated vascular structures that occur due to developmental arrest at different stages, and they are clinically uncommon. Common symptoms or signs of pelvic AVM may include the detection of palpable pulsatile masses during abdominal, pelvic, or rectal examinations. Progressive sequelae of high-output congestive heart failure, such as orthopnea, edema, and fatigue, are also common manifestations of pelvic AVMs but are less frequent. Additionally, AVMs in different locations exhibit unique clinical presentations [3].

This can cause venous dilation in the bladder wall, vagina, and rectal wall, leading to hematuria, excessive vaginal bleeding, and gastrointestinal bleeding, which is rare in pelvic AVMs. Additionally, AVMs can erode adjacent structures. Bleeding can either be chronic or sudden and can be life-threatening. Patients with pelvic vascular malformations may also have a history of infertility or miscarriages. However, if the location of the lesion is favorable, these symptoms may not be present [4]. In the past, this disease was often misdiagnosed due to limited understanding. However, with recent advancements in diagnostic techniques, the rate of accurate diagnosis has increased, and researchers are continuously exploring suitable treatment methods [1].

When patients are admitted with acute or chronic abdominal pain, surgical and gynecological emergencies are usually the main considerations, and vascular diseases are not initially targeted for investigation, which may result in a greater likelihood of missed diagnoses. However, improved diagnostic techniques have led to increased accuracy of ultrasonography and other diagnostic methods. Ultrasonography can now be used to screen for typical vascular diseases, providing an initial overall assessment of the nature and extent of the disease. Subsequently, contrast-enhanced CT or CTA can be employed for targeted evaluation and detection of AVMs. Moreover, considering the limited pelvic space and close proximity of visceral organs, AVMs commonly affect neighboring organs. Hence, specialized examinations should be conducted to assess the extent of organ involvement based on the patient’s symptoms. DSA remains the gold standard for diagnosing AVMs due to its ability to provide a definitive diagnosis and guide treatment. It allows for dynamic assessment of blood flow perfusion, enabling precise treatment planning for the disease. Transcatheter embolization has emerged as the preferred treatment modality for AVMs [5].

Commonly used embolization agents include absolute ethanol, polyvinyl alcohol (PVA) particles, gelatin sponges, cyanoacrylate, Onyx, glue, etc [6]. In addition, coils, covered stents, and vascular plugs are also widely used. However, the use of PVA particles, gelatin sponges, stainless steel coils, and cyanoacrylate in large-caliber vessels, such as the internal iliac artery, may be associated with significant residual shunting and an increased risk of embolization agent displacement [7].

In the endovascular treatment of AVMs, completely blocking the central nidus and eliminating the abnormal drainage vein are the most accurate and thorough treatment plans. Treatment includes the use of absolute ethanol, coils and glue for venous embolization to achieve permanent occlusion of the direct drainage vein [8]. If the central nidus cannot be directly accessed through both the artery and vein, partial embolization of its branch vessels is also a commonly chosen treatment method.

In this case, the patient presented with abdominal pain and was diagnosed with an iliac arteriovenous malformation following ultrasound, CT angiography, and angiographic examinations. After undergoing embolization treatment, the patient experienced significant improvement in pain and had a good prognosis. During the follow-up of the patients, we chose to perform contrast-enhanced CT or CTA to understand the pelvic organs and to determine whether there was arteriovenous malformation through no enhancement of the iliac vein in the arterial phase. However, the evaluation of AVMs was insufficient because of many artifacts of coils, such as DSA, which could make the evaluation of AVM lesions more accurate and sufficient, but the mental retardation and pain tolerance of the patients were very poor. Therefore, we plan to choose DSA when the patient develop symptoms of AVMs or other imaging evidence of recrudescent AVMs.

Trisomy 21, also known as Down syndrome (DS), is characterized by various clinical features that arise due to the presence of an additional copy of chromosome 21. Down syndrome is a phenotype that affects multiple body systems, especially the musculoskeletal, nervous, and cardiovascular systems. Common symptoms associated with Down syndrome include congenital heart defects, duodenal atresia, cataracts, Hirschsprung disease, megacolon, choanal atresia, and anorectal malformations [9].

Genetic testing revealed several mutations associated with AVMs, including phosphatase and tensin (PTEN) in Bannayan-Riley-Ruvalcaba or Cowden syndrome and RASA-1 mutations in Parkes Weber and capillary malformation-arteriovenous malformation (CM-AVM) [4]. Mutations in ALK-1 and endoglin have been linked to hereditary hemorrhagic telangiectasia (HHT). However, the impact of chromosome 21 on these genes remains unclear. Current evidence suggests that congenital anomalies of the umbilico-portal system may be associated with abnormalities of chromosome 21, such as polysplenia, biliary atresia, or cardiac malformations. Some genetic associations have also been described. The most common chromosomal alteration associated with these anomalies is trisomy 21 [10].

Several reports have documented abnormal anatomy of the portal venous system in individuals with Down syndrome [2]. In another case report, a boy with Down syndrome was diagnosed with a combination of multiple vascular abnormalities, including extrahepatic portal hypertension, ileocecal AVMs, caval/iliac venous malformation, and rectal bleeding [11]. Only one published case of an adult with Down syndrome and pelvic AVM complicated by hematuria has been reported, resulting in death after embolization and cystectomy [2]. To our knowledge, our patient represents the only reported surviving adult with Down syndrome and pelvic AVM treated interventionally. Although the underlying mechanisms remain incompletely understood, this case further supports a potential association between vascular malformations and trisomy 21.

We believe that the main reason for the worsening abdominal pain symptoms after the patient lies flat for a long time at midnight is a decrease in iliac vein pressure and an increase in arteriovenous shunting. Additionally, the patient’s poor pain tolerance, as evidenced by reporting greater pain during local anesthesia for subcutaneous injection, contributes to discomfort. Importantly, the uterus is more sensitive to ischemia, as the patient experienced significant pain when a small amount of gelatin sponge was used for embolization of the left uterine artery during the procedure. This sensitivity further exacerbates abdominal pain symptoms when the patient remains in a supine position for an extended period at night.

When the patient assumes a sitting position, venous pressure increases, shunting decreases, and blood perfusion to distant organs increases, resulting in pain relief. Correcting the blood supply to the target organs and reducing cardiac pressure requires occluding abnormal vessels in the patient. Dense embolization was considered the most reliable technique for treating this patient, and as such, dense embolization was performed. The postembolization effect was significant. However, for most patients with a large venous aneurysm cavity, the economic burden of dense embolization is significant. It remains to be investigated whether dense embolization of the inflow and outflow tracts and incomplete embolization of the aneurysm are feasible.

Conclusion

We present a case study of a female patient with Down syndrome who complained of abdominal pain and was diagnosed with symptomatic AVM. We successfully treated her using minimally invasive endovascular embolization. The significance of interventional embolization therapy has been growing in the field of minimally invasive treatments, which offer more precise outcomes. Consequently, it has gradually become the preferred treatment option for symptomatic AVMs. Furthermore, it is essential to emphasize vascular ultrasound examination as part of the physical examination for patients with Down syndrome. In cases where necessary, contrast-enhanced CT scans can be performed to screen for the presence of an AVM.
