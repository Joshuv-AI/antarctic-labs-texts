Development, Construction, and Evaluation of an Alternative Dosimetry Phantom for Computed Tomography
Authors: Lukmanda Evan Lubis, Windi Dliya Najmah, Yuni Muliyanti, Ika Hariyati, Dea Ryangga, Terry Mart, Hilde Bosmans, Djarwani Soeharso Soejoko
Published: 2023

ABSTRACT
This article aims to present the development, construction, and evaluation of an alternative computed tomography dose index (CTDI) phantom. Epoxy resin was mixed with an iodine-based contrast agent to produce radiological characteristics resembling polymethyl methacrylate (PMMA) as a standard CTDI phantom. As a preliminary study, testing was carried out using computed tomography images (80 and 120 kVp) on 12 variations of epoxy-iodine resin mixtures to obtain relative electron density (ρe) values and effective atomic numbers (Zeff) of the samples. The alternative CTDI phantoms were then constructed with a resin-iodine mixture using iodine concentrations that yield on closest ρe and Zeff values to those of PMMA. The evaluation was carried out by comparing dose measurement results at various energies between the alternative phantom and the International Electrotechnical Commission-standard CTDI phantom. At a concentration of 0.46%, the epoxy resin has ρe and Zeff with a deviation against PMMA of 0.12% and 1.58%, respectively, so that composition was chosen for the alternative CTDI phantom construction. The average dose discrepancy values were 5% and 1%, respectively, for the head and body phantoms in the tested tube voltages of 80 kVp, 100 kVp, 120 kVp, and 135 kVp. The Student’s t-test result between the alternative and the standard phantoms also showed P < 0.05, indicating the comparability of the alternative CTDI phantom with the standard CTDI phantom.

INTRODUCTION

In recent decades, computed tomography (CT) has been the gold standard in medical imaging due to its capability to present cross-sectional images of an anatomy. Although it is diagnostically highly useful for nearly every pathological group, the risk included in each scan is deemed rather high when compared to planar, two-dimensional radiography. In 2000, the Conference of Radiation Control Program Directors through nationwide evaluation of X-ray trends survey disclosed that the patient surface dose from a typical abdominal CT is 200 to 300 times more than that of a typical chest radiograph, 20–30 times that of the mean glandular dose of a typical craniocaudal mammogram, and approximately 10–20 times that of typical abdominal radiography.[1] With the use of CT being projected to increase by 4% each year and with an estimated annual use of 300 million scans worldwide, the dose level requires special attention.[2]

Therefore, in most quality control protocols for CT, dose measurement is always an integrated part[3-6] with the dose measurement method following a standard by the International Electrotechnical Commission (IEC 6061-2-44) and the International Commission on Radiation Unit and Measurement.[7,8] Both IEC standard and technical dosimetry protocols describe the use of a specific phantom made of polymethyl methacrylate (PMMA) for the measurement of dose index.[9,10] The cast PMMA phantom serves to represent objects for head and body application with diameters of 16 cm and 32 cm, respectively. However, the phantom requires certain factory-level techniques to be manufactured, leading to a typically high price in most regions. Owing to this reason, the phantom is not always available in the clinical setting for in-house medical physicists to use. Unlike typical image quality check phantom, it is not commonly included in CT unit purchases. The situation becomes more challenging with the fact that the CT dosimetry phantom is too expensive for medical physicists to procure individually.

Efforts for making available alternative phantoms are performed by previous investigators. Sookpeng et al. produced a nylon-based CT dosimetry phantom with the largest measurement deviation of 14% against measurement in the standard CT dose index (CTDI) phantom.[11] Meanwhile, a more recent work by Fatichah et al. manufactured a polyester-resin phantom as an alternative for PMMA with the highest deviation of 7.5% in measurements.[12] While these previous works seem promising, efforts should be made to achieve less deviation to allow ease of use. Moreover, these previous works rely mainly on practical measurement results of attenuation coefficients, rather than more fundamental investigations of relative atomic density and effective atomic number in addition to basic Hounsfield Unit (HU) evaluation for theoretical approach.

This work aims to explore a new material for an alternative dosimetry phantom for CT. The development started with two-step iteration testing of a range of phantom material candidates to match with the target HU of PMMA. The HU similarity was confirmed by using the DEEDZ method to check their respective electron densities (ρe) and effective atomic number (Zeff) to ensure its similarity of physical properties with that of PMMA. Selected compositions will be used to construct the phantom by casting the solution into head and body CTDI phantom using prepared silicone molds. An evaluation was carried out by comparing CTDI measurement results in the constructed phantom and comparing them with that of the standard CTDI phantom.

MATERIALS AND METHODS

Composition determination

A preliminary test using an arbitrary selection of solution was performed to obtain a range of concentration to focus on. In this initial step, epoxy resin was mixed with iohexol 350 mg I/mL to form a total of 30 mL volume samples. Five variations of the contrast agent concentration against the total volume (in vol%), namely 0.0%, 0.3%, 3.3%, 10.0%, and 16.7% were used as preliminary solutions. The resulting solution was cast into small silicone molds and scanned together with a PMMA rod using a Toshiba Aquilion CXB-500B CT scanner (Tustin, California, USA) to get their respective HUs. Once a typical range of iodine concentration that makes up solutions with relatively close HU with PMMA was known, a new series of samples (called “phantom samples”) were constructed in the range. A total of 12 phantom samples were manufactured based on the preliminary test HU results, as presented in Table 1. These samples underwent subsequent testing using the DEEDZ method[13,14] to finally select the concentration based on their respective electron densities (ρe) and effective atomic number (Zeff).

The method starts with calculating the constant α that is a calibration factor correlating the ξ difference between two images (Δξ, which is equivalent to μˆh − μˆl) and the electron density ρe. Thus, obtaining the value α of only requires a correlation curve between Δξ and ρe acquired by scanning a phantom containing objects with known ρe, with higher and lower energies. With this α parameter measured, the ρe of the phantom material candidates can be collected by scanning them with two energy levels, which are identical to the ones used for calibration and then use.

In this work, calibration was performed using the CIRS 062M electron density phantom (CIRS, Norfolk, USA).

Phantom construction

Molds made of room temperature vulcanizing (RTV) 52 silicone [Figure 2] were constructed to serve as head and body size phantom molds. The molds were constructed using IEC standard CTDI phantom as cast to ensure size and shape reproducibility. Iodine concentration on epoxy resin chosen from the candidate will be made into an alternative phantom by pouring it into the mold. The heat gun was used in the material mixing and pouring process to eliminate air bubbles. The mixture was left to cure for at least 24 h before drilled to create housing for ionization chambers.

Evaluation method

After the alternative phantom is produced, the air kerma on every dosimeter position is measured and the CTDI weighted (CTDIw) value for head and body phantom is calculated to be compared with results from an IEC standard phantom. To observe the effect of energy on the measurement, measurements were performed in four variations of tube voltage of 80 kVp, 100 kVp, 120 kVp, and 135 kVp on 200 mA tube current and 1.5 s exposure. Axial scans were used throughout all measurements. Filtration, reconstruction algorithm, slice thickness, and pitch were kept the same for all exposures (FC70 reconstruction algorithm, 8 mm thick slices, and pitch = 1). Construction of the alternative phantom

The composition of epoxy resin and iodine was selected which produced radiological characteristics resembling PMMA in sample 9, namely the addition of 0.6 mL of iodine contrast to 150 g or 129 mL of epoxy resin or equal to 0.46 vol% iodine concentration. This variation has a mass density of 1.157, a relative ρe value of 1.148, and Zeff value of 6.57 and produces error values of 0.12% and 1.58%, respectively, from the reference values of PMMA relative ρe and Zeff. In the alternative phantom construction, 12 L of epoxy resin, 120 mL of antibubble solution, and 62 mL of iodine-based liquid contrast were used. The mixture was poured into silicone molds for head and body CTDI phantoms. The alternative CTDI phantom is shown in Figure 5, side by side with the IEC standard CTDI phantom for comparison. The alternative CTDI phantom has identical dimensions as the standard CTDI phantom and a total weight of 14.7 kg (i.e., 5.2 kg less than the standard IEC phantom).

For the head phantom, the average CF values [Table 6 and Figure 8b] are also approaching unity, but at lower energies (80 kVp and 100 kVp) the results indicate a larger standard deviation compared to those of higher energies (120 kVp and 135 kVp). Since this deviation is obtained in the results of both standard and alternative phantoms, it is suspected that this occurs due to the attenuation of X-rays through a more dominant photoelectric process. The hypothetically smaller intensity, therefore, causes the larger standard deviation of the X-ray detected by the ionization chamber. The average value of CF for the alternative body phantom is 1.01, which indicates a 1% discrepancy in dose measurement between the two body phantoms.

The results of dose measurements using the standard and alternative CTDI phantoms were compared by using the t-test, showing P = 0.000003 (P < 0.05). This means that the measurement values from the two phantoms are not significantly different. Based on Tables 5 and 6, it is known that the CF of the CTDIw has an optimal average value (close to unity) at tube voltages of 80 kVp and 100 kVp. The average CF for all dose measurements of head and body phantoms was 0.98 (2% difference against the standard IEC phantom). Therefore, it can be concluded that the alternative CTDI phantom constructed in this work is comparable with the standard IEC CTDI phantom.

Limitations of study and future development

While the development, construction, and evaluation of the alternative phantom have been described in this study, the evaluation requires subsequent studies on the material’s integrity over time. Studies on the HU uniformity, noise level and homogeneity, performance reproducibility, and mechanical robustness compared with standard CTDI phantom over a period are planned for future development.

CONCLUSIONS

The suitable composition to produce PMMA-equivalent materials, namely the addition of iodine with a concentration of 0.46% in epoxy resin, was obtained. The construction of an alternative CTDI phantom has been successfully carried out in this study. The CFs for standard CTDI phantoms were found to be at 0.95 and 1.01, which results in a discrepancy of 5% and 1%, respectively, for the head and body phantoms in the tested tube voltages of 80 kVp, 100 kVp, 120 kVp, and 135 kVp. From these results, it can be concluded that the constructed alternative CTDI phantom has comparable performance and is proposed as a candidate for dosimetry tools for clinical medical physicists.
