Bigger May Be Better Indeed?
Authors: Alvin H.Y. Ko, Ivan Wong
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Corresponding Author

Left atrial appendage closure (LAAC) has emerged as a cornerstone therapy for stroke prevention in patients with nonvalvular atrial fibrillation who are at high risk for bleeding on long-term anticoagulation.1, 2, 3 The WATCHMAN FLX device, a second-generation plug-type occluder, has demonstrated robust efficacy and safety in clinical trials and registries.4, 5, 6 However, one persistent challenge lies in device sizing, particularly for "borderline" left atrial appendage (LAA) anatomies where ostial dimensions straddle the manufacturer's recommended ranges for 2 adjacent sizes. In such cases, operators must balance the risks of inadequate sealing (leading to peridevice leaks [PDLs] and thromboembolism) against potential complications from oversizing (such as embolization or perforation).

Prior evidence underscores the clinical significance of PDLs following LAAC. In the PINNACLE FLX (Protection Against Embolism for Nonvalvular AF Patients: Investigational Device Evaluation of the WATCHMAN FLX LAA Closure Technology) trial evaluating the WATCHMAN FLX device, a significant 7.4% of patients had PDL ≤5 mm during device implantation and approximately 10% at 1 year.4 Combined analysis from the 3 studies (PROTECT AF [WATCHMAN Left Atrial Appendage System for Embolic PROTECTion in Patients With Atrial Fibrillation], PREVAIL [Prospective Randomized Evaluation of the Watchman Left Atrial Appendage Closure Device in Patients With Atrial Fibrillation Versus Long-Term Warfarin Therapy], and CAP2 [Continued Access to PREVAIL]) demonstrated that PDL ≤5 mm at 1 year was associated with heightened thromboembolic risk.7 Furthermore, data from the Japanese OCEAN-LACC (Optimized Catheter Valvular Intervention–Left Atrial Appendage Closure) registry indicated that even minor PDL ≤3 mm detected at implantation correlated with increased rates of transient ischemic attack, ischemic cerebrovascular accident (CVA), or systemic embolism (SE).8 Consequently, minimizing or eliminating PDL during device implantation is critically important to mitigate the risk of subsequent ischemic CVA or SE, and thereby optimize device efficacy.

In this issue of JACC: Asia, Kuwabara et al9 deliver a compelling substudy from the Japanese multicenter TERMINATOR (Transcatheter Modification of Left Atrial Appendage by Obliteration with Device in Patients with NVAF [Non-Valvular Atrial Fibrillation]) registry, shedding light on this dilemma by comparing outcomes of larger vs smaller WATCHMAN FLX devices in borderline LAA cases. Drawing from 1,190 consecutive LAAC procedures between May 2021 and September 2023, the study focused on 757 patients with borderline LAA sizes (ostial diameters of 16.8–18 mm, 18.9–21.6 mm, 21.7–24.3 mm, or 24.5–27.9 mm), where 2 device sizes are viable per manufacturer guidelines. Intraprocedural transesophageal echocardiography was used for measurement of ostial diameters at multiple angles. Device selection was at the operator’s discretion, considering the LAA morphology, ostial size, depth, distal LAA capacity for device accommodation, and the extent of pectinate muscle trabeculation. Patients were categorized into large (n = 390) or small (n = 367) device groups based on the implanted size. The median follow-up was 274 days, capped at 1.5 years for analysis uniformity. The primary outcomes included all-cause mortality, ischemic CVA or SE, major bleeding events, device-related thrombosis (DRT), and periprocedural complications.

The study's key finding is compelling: larger devices were associated with significantly fewer incidences of residual PDLs (1.54% vs 4.90%; P = 0.015), aligning with higher maximal compression rates (21.22% ± 4.44% vs 19.63% ± 4.35%; P < 0.001) and lower cumulative rates of ischemic CVA or SE (8.4%; 95% CI: 1.8%-34.1% vs 10.5%; 95% CI: 3.6%-28.4%; P = 0.036), without compromising procedural safety. The small device was identified as a predictor of cumulative ischemic CVA or SE with univariate Cox regression (HR: 2.87; 95% CI: 1.02-8.08; P = 0.045), alongside prior ischemic stroke (HR: 3.84; 95% CI: 1.37-10.79; P = 0.011) and left ventricular ejection fraction (HR: 0.96; 95% CI: 0.93-1.00; P = 0.042). LAA depths were comparable between the 2 groups in all zones.

Technical and procedural success rates were high and comparable (>96% in both groups), with no increases in complications like tamponade or embolization. The study also provided important information on postprocedural antithrombotic management, which could affect future events of DRT, ischemic CVA, or SE. Antithrombotic regimens evolved comparably in both groups, shifting from oral anticoagulation–dominant at discharge to single antiplatelet therapy by 12 months. DRT rates were similar (8.6% vs 3.2%; P = 0.603), as were all-cause mortality and major bleeding.

These results align with prior observations that PDLs, even small ones (0–5 mm), elevate thromboembolic risk, as evidenced by the NCDR (National Cardiovascular Data) registry and meta-analyses from PROTECT AF and PREVAIL trials.7,10,11 The authors attribute the benefits to higher compression rates in the large device group (21.22% maximum vs 19.63%; P < 0.001), which may enhance sealing and mitigate positive LAA remodeling observed in the subsequent phase. Intriguingly, real-world practice patterns revealed that larger ostial diameters favored bigger devices, whereas greater LAA eccentricity (a marker of irregularity) tilted toward smaller ones—highlighting the nuanced decision making beyond mere measurements.

The study's strengths are noteworthy. As a prospective, multicenter registry capturing consecutive cases, it reflects everyday clinical practice in a high-bleeding-risk population (median HAS-BLED score 3.0). The focus on borderline anatomies, which comprise a substantial portion of LAAC candidates, fills a void left by randomized trials like PINNACLE FLX, which did not specifically dissect sizing strategies. Moreover, the inclusion of temporal shifts in antithrombotic therapy from both groups provides context for postprocedural management.

Yet, limitations temper enthusiasm. As an observational study, selection bias looms: operators chose sizes based on nonstandardized assessments of morphology, depth, and trabeculation, potentially confounding results. Relying on site-reported transesophageal echocardiography without routine computed tomography follow-up might underestimate PDLs or miss subtle differences. The median follow-up is relatively short, and the exclusively Japanese cohort may limit generalizability. Univariate analyses identified small devices as a predictor of ischemic events (HR: 2.87; P = 0.045), but multivariable adjustment was absent, leaving residual confounders unaddressed.

Clinically, these findings nudge operators toward larger devices in borderline cases, provided LAA depth is adequate to accommodate the device without excessive protrusion. This could refine clinical practices, in which current recommendations emphasize adequate compression and minimal leak but offer little on device selection in borderline LAA sizing. However, caution is still advised: oversizing risks remain theoretical but real, especially in shallow or eccentric LAAs.

Looking ahead, randomized trials on broader populations, comparing sizing strategies, perhaps incorporating advanced imaging like 3-dimensional computed tomography or artificial intelligence–assisted planning and simulation, are essential to validate these observations. Longer-term data from TERMINATOR and global registries like NCDR will clarify durability. In an era of expanding LAAC indications, optimizing device in LAA closure is not just technical—it's pivotal for patient safety and efficacy. Kuwabara et al9 should be congratulated, as the current study provided a valuable compass, and the journey toward precision sizing in LAAC continues.
