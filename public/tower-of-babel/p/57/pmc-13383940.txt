Deferred cord clamping in twin pregnancies: benefit, risk, or missed opportunity? A narrative review
Authors: Aimann Surak, Graeme Polglase, Georg M Schmölzer
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractDeferred umbilical cord clamping enhances placental transfusion and is associated with improved outcomes in preterm infants. Following lung aeration and prior to umbilical cord clamping, the increase in pulmonary blood flow contributes to establishing blood volume within the pulmonary vascular bed, which constitutes the mechanism of physiology-based cord clamping. While the benefits of deferred cord clamping have been well described in singleton deliveries, evidence in twin pregnancies remains limited. This is particularly relevant for monochorionic twins, who share a placental circulation and may be at risk of inter-twin transfusion. This narrative review summarizes and critically appraises the available literature on deferred cord clamping in twin pregnancies, with particular emphasis on monochorionic twins. We highlight potential benefits, risks, and current knowledge gaps. Introduction

Deferred cord clamping (DCC), also referred to as delayed cord clamping, is recommended for at least 60 s in preterm infants born <37 weeks’ gestation who do not require immediate resuscitation at birth (strong recommendation, moderate-certainty evidence).1 Umbilical cord clamping strategies can be broadly categorized into time-based DCC, which involves delaying clamping for a predefined duration (typically 30 s to several minutes),2 and physiology-based cord clamping (PBCC), in which clamping is guided by the establishment of physiological stability, such as lung aeration and effective ventilation, rather than a fixed time interval.3 In PBCC, this approach allows pulmonary blood flow to replace umbilical venous return, thereby supporting a more stable cardiovascular transition.3 Although there may be overlap between these approaches in practice, they are conceptually distinct and defined by different criteria for timing of cord clamping.

In large meta-analyses such as the individual participant data (IPD) meta-analysis by Seidler et al.,4 comparisons of deferred versus immediate cord clamping are based on the timing of cord clamping rather than the physiological rationale guiding that timing. Notably, the majority of included studies employed time-based approaches to deferred cord clamping, with limited representation of PBCC strategies.

In preterm infants, an individual participant data meta-analysis demonstrated that DCC, compared with immediate cord clamping (ICC), significantly reduces mortality prior to discharge (OR 0.68, 95% CI 0.51–0.91) and the need for blood transfusion (OR 0.59, 95% CI 0.47–0.73).5 However, no significant differences were observed for several other outcomes, including intraventricular hemorrhage and the need for inotropic support.

Importantly, these benefits have been predominantly identified in singleton births with limited similar evidence in monochorionic twin births, commonly excluded in clinical trials.

While dichorionic/diamniotic twins have separate placental circulations and may be considered more suitable candidates for DCC, clinical practice remains variable and recommendations are largely extrapolated from singleton data. In contrast, monochorionic/diamniotic and monochorionic/monoamniotic twins share placental circulations and may require different cord management strategies. Due to concerns regarding potential inter-twin transfusion and altered hemodynamics, DCC is often avoided or individualized in monochorionic twin pregnancies.

The objective of this narrative review is to summarize the current evidence on DCC in twin pregnancies, with a particular focus on monochorionic twins, and to evaluate the potential benefits and risks in this population. We also aim to highlight existing knowledge gaps and areas requiring further research, including the need for adequately powered studies and individual participant data meta-analyses.

Methods

This narrative review was conducted to summarize and contextualize the available literature on deferred cord clamping in twin pregnancies. A structured literature search was performed using PubMed and MEDLINE databases for studies published up to [December 2025]. Search terms included combinations of “delayed cord clamping,” “deferred cord clamping,” “twins,” “multiple gestation,” “monochorionic,” “dichorionic,” and “placental transfusion”.

The included studies were selected based on relevance to the topic and their contribution to understanding the physiological mechanisms, clinical outcomes, and potential risks and benefits of deferred cord clamping in twin pregnancies. Overall, the literature identified for this review consisted predominantly of observational studies, retrospective cohorts, quality improvement initiatives, and a limited number of randomized trials. Most studies evaluating twin pregnancies included relatively small sample sizes, and monochorionic twins were underrepresented across nearly all study designs.

No formal inclusion or exclusion criteria were predefined, and studies not considered directly relevant to the scope of this review were not included. As such, the selection process may be subject to selection bias, and the possibility of selective citation of the literature cannot be excluded.

As a narrative review, no formal risk of bias assessment or grading of certainty of evidence was performed. In addition, the identification and selection of studies were not conducted using a formal systematic or scoping review framework and therefore may be subject to selection bias. Furthermore, the possibility of publication bias within the available literature cannot be excluded. Therefore, statements regarding certainty or quality of evidence throughout this review should be interpreted within the context of these methodological limitations. This narrative approach was chosen to provide a focused, physiologically informed synthesis of a heterogeneous and evolving body of literature, particularly in an area where high-quality evidence remains limited.

The available literature specifically addressing twin pregnancies was relatively limited and consisted primarily of observational studies, retrospective cohorts, and small quality improvement initiatives, with comparatively few randomized studies. Across studies, monochorionic twins were inconsistently represented and were frequently not analyzed separately from dichorionic twins.

Dynamics of blood distribution after birth

Pulmonary vascular resistance (PVR) remains high until the lungs are aerated. Following cord clamping, umbilical venous return is interrupted, resulting in a significant reduction in cardiac preload to both ventricles. This abrupt reduction in preload is often associated with bradycardia.3

In an observational cohort study of 610 term and late-preterm infants, Kc et al. reported higher risk of bradycardia with ICC compared to DCC (aRR 1.51; 95% CI; 1.01–2.26).6 Additionally, there is a coincident increase in systemic vascular resistance (SVR),7 which results in augmented afterload, an increase in arterial blood pressure (BP) within only a few heartbeats, and unstable fluctuations to cerebral blood flow (CBF).8, 9

Those changes in loading conditions are likely well tolerated by the myocardium in term infants, however, in preterm infants, this could potentially compromise stroke volume and cardiac output, and subsequently, a low cardiac output state.10, 11 Such risks in preterm infants are related to inherent characteristics of their myocardium, which is not well conditioned to handle substantial changes in loading conditions. Those inherent characteristics include immature myocardium, paucity of L-type calcium channels, underdeveloped sarcoplasmic reticulum, and less contractile elements.12, 13

Physiology of delayed cord clamping and its hemodynamic benefits. Following birth, continued umbilical venous return maintains right ventricular output and supports pulmonary blood flow, which in turn sustains left ventricular preload through pulmonary venous return. With the placenta still attached, the left ventricle continues to pump against a low-resistance circuit, contributing to a more stable cardiovascular transition. These mechanisms underpin the benefits of deferred cord clamping observed in clinical studies. While this model is primarily derived from singleton pregnancies, it provides a physiological framework for understanding cord clamping strategies; in twin gestations—particularly monochorionic twins—these processes may be altered due to shared placental circulation and inter-twin vascular connections.

While these physiologic mechanisms are well described in singleton pregnancies, their applicability to twin gestations—particularly monochorionic twins with shared placental circulation—remains uncertain.

Benefits of dcc in singleton infants

Most physiologic and clinical evidence supporting DCC is derived from singleton pregnancies, particularly preterm singleton infants. However, some outcomes have primarily been studied in term populations, whereas others are largely restricted to preterm cohorts. Although some mechanisms and outcomes may be applicable to twins, direct evidence in multiple gestations remains limited. Therefore, the benefits summarized below should primarily be interpreted within the context of singleton data and the population from which each outcome was derived.

Evidence from predominantly term and late-preterm infants suggests that DCC results in earlier stabilization of oxygen saturation, with infants reaching oxygen saturation values of 85–90% sooner than those undergoing immediate cord clamping. DCC may also reduce episodes of bradycardia and tachycardia during the first minutes after birth, although confirmation from randomized trials is awaited.27 Similarly, infants undergoing DCC for more than 60 s achieve higher oxygen saturation and heart rate values during the first 10 min after birth.28, 29 Evidence regarding cerebral oxygenation is less consistent. Badurdeen et al. reported no difference in cerebral oxygenation between early cord clamping and physiology-based cord clamping,30 whereas Pichler et al. observed reduced cerebral tissue oxygenation despite higher peripheral oxygen saturation following DCC.31

Hemodynamic benefits have also been described in preterm infants. DCC has been associated with attenuation of fluctuations in blood pressure and cerebral blood flow during the immediate postnatal transition.32 It has also been associated with higher superior vena cava (SVC) flow, a surrogate marker of systemic blood flow.33 Low SVC flow has been linked to intraventricular hemorrhage (IVH)34 and adverse neurodevelopmental outcomes,35, 36 and several observational studies have demonstrated higher SVC flow following DCC.37, 38

Studies conducted primarily in preterm infants have reported higher blood pressures during the first minutes after birth following DCC compared with ICC.39 In a systematic review by Rabe et al., authors reported that DCC was associated with higher mean arterial blood pressure at 4 h after birth compared to ICC (mean difference 3.52 mmHg [(95% CI) 0.60, 6.45]).40 However, interpretation of more recent studies requires caution because different cord management strategies have been evaluated. For example, whereas Rabe et al. compared deferred with immediate cord clamping,40 Badurdeen et al. compared early cord clamping with physiology-based cord clamping and reported no difference in mean blood pressure at 3–4 min after birth.30 Accordingly, these findings should not be interpreted as directly comparable.

In preterm infants, DCC has been associated with higher hemoglobin concentrations both at birth and near discharge, as well as reduced need for blood transfusion. For example, hemoglobin values were higher both at birth (15.9 vs. 14.9 g/dL, p = 0.001) and near discharge (10.7 vs. 10.1 g/dL, p < 0.001), with fewer transfusions required (39.4% vs. 54.9%, p < 0.001).41 Similar results have been confirmed in a recent network meta-analysis.5

Evidence from both preterm populations and large cohorts of term and late-preterm infants suggests that DCC may be associated with reduced mortality, although evidence strength differs across gestational groups. Mercer et al. demonstrated a protective effect of DCC in very low birth weight male infants, showing reduced risk of motor disability at 7 months corrected age.42 Furthermore, DCC might improve motor function at 18 months in preterm infants less than 32 weeks’ gestation.43 Similarly, other trials reported some long-term benefits upon follow-up at 4 months,44 and at 2 years of age.45

In a large retrospective cohort study of more than 12,000 term and late preterm infants, DCC until spontaneous breathing was established was associated with reduced mortality.46 This has been further supported by a recent network meta-analysis.5

Importantly, DCC has not been associated with an increased need for phototherapy despite slightly higher peak bilirubin concentrations.47 Similarly, DCC has not been associated with an increase in maternal complications. This includes no significant differences in the risk of postpartum hemorrhage (risk ratio 1.04, 95% CI 0.65–1.65), maternal hemoglobin values at 24–72 h after birth (mean difference 0.12 g/dL, 95% CI 0.30–0.06), need for uterotonics (RR 1.17, 95% CI 0.94–1.44), or prolonged third stage (>30 min: RR 1.18, 95% CI 0.55–2.52; or >60 min (RR 1.11, 95% CI 0.33–3.74), irrespective of mode of delivery (vaginal versus C-section).44

DCC in twin pregnancies: uncertainty and practice

Monochorionic twins are at higher risk for morbidities compared with singletons born at the same gestational age; this includes increased rates of prematurity, greater need for NICU admission, growth restriction, and potential increased mortality, particularly with higher-order multiples.48 Given the multiple benefits of DCC described earlier, monochorionic twins may likewise benefit from enhanced placental transfusion through DCC. Despite the established benefits of DCC in singleton pregnancies, its application in twin pregnancies remains variable and controversial. The main concern with performing DCC in monochorionic twins is the potential for differential perfusion between twins; however, this represents only one aspect of a highly complex and heterogeneous clinical scenario.

Several additional factors may influence the safety and efficacy of DCC or PBCC in twin pregnancies. These include variability in placental vascular architecture, such as twin-to-twin transfusion syndrome (TTTS) and twin anemia–polycythemia sequence (TAPS), the presence and completeness of prior interventions (e.g., fetoscopic laser ablation), and whether inter-twin transfusion is acute (peripartum) or chronic in nature. Differences in fetal condition, including growth restriction, chronic hypoxia, anemia or polycythemia, and cardiovascular adaptation, may also influence each twin’s physiological reserve and response to cord management strategies.

Additional practical considerations include the ability to reliably distinguish donor and recipient twins at birth, which may be particularly challenging during cesarean delivery. Delivery sequence, the interval between births, and maternal safety concerns—including postpartum hemorrhage risk and placental complications—may also influence decision-making. Collectively, these considerations highlight the substantial heterogeneity among twin pregnancies and underscore the challenges of applying uniform cord management strategies across this population.

Most randomized studies evaluating cord management strategies have either excluded multiple gestations entirely or included only small numbers of twins without subgroup analyses according to chorionicity. In current practice, DCC is generally avoided in monochorionic twins because of theoretical risks of acute inter-twin transfusion and unfavorable hemodynamic changes.

Recently, Rondagh et al. conducted a single-center retrospective cohort study evaluating acute peripartum TTTS in vaginally born monochorionic twin pregnancies. The investigators compared early cord clamping (<60 s) with DCC (>60 s). Acute peripartum TTTS was defined as a hemoglobin difference >8 g/dL within 12 h after birth in the absence of findings consistent with chronic TTTS. The prevalence of acute peripartum TTTS was higher in the DCC group than in the early clamping group (17% [3/18] versus 0% [0/17]).49 Based on these findings, the study authors advised caution regarding routine DCC in vaginal monochorionic twin deliveries.49

Additional concerns extend beyond acute inter-twin transfusion and include uncertainty regarding placental transfusion dynamics, neonatal hemodynamic effects, and potential maternal consequences in multiple gestations.

In a randomized controlled trial of pregnancies with multiples, including triplets, DCC neither enhanced placental transfusion nor improved neonatal hematocrit.50 In the subgroup of monochorionic twins, increasing placental perfusion to the first-born twin could theoretically deplete placental blood volume available to the second twin. Furthermore, the study found no improvement in SVC flow or other neonatal outcomes. Many of these clinically important variables are inconsistently captured or reported in existing studies. Consequently, interpretation of available evidence remains challenging, particularly in monochorionic twins where placental physiology and risk profiles differ substantially from other twin subgroups.

DCC in twin birth and potential benefits

Despite the aforementioned concerns and lack of strong guideline endorsement, some centers continue to perform DCC in twin births following individualized assessment.52, 53 However, the available evidence supporting this practice is limited, heterogeneous, and derived predominantly from observational studies. Much of the physiologic rationale underpinning DCC is derived from singleton pregnancies, and its applicability to twin gestations remains uncertain.

Current recommendations, such as those from the Canadian Pediatric Society, suggest that DCC may be considered in twins when not contraindicated, although this is based on low-certainty evidence.54 The optimal duration of DCC in twin pregnancies is also unclear, and a delay of 30–60 s is generally extrapolated from singleton data rather than being supported by robust twin-specific evidence.

Clinical consequences and potential benefits of DCC in preterm twins are reported in the literature. In a quality improvement initiative by Chiruvolu et al., DCC was performed on 16 preterm twin deliveries, 15 were dichorionic and only 1 monochorionic twin (who underwent an early laser ablation for TTTS). The authors reported that DCC was feasible and was not associated with apparent adverse neonatal outcomes, and the main benefit of DCC was a reduction in early RBC transfusions and a decrease in the hospital length of stay.55 Additionally, in a retrospective cohort of 300 dichorionic and 290 monochorionic preterm twin pairs by Lopriore et al., authors demonstrated that the second-born twins had higher hemoglobin values compared to the first-born twins, in both monochorionic and dichorionic pregnancies56; although cord management strategies were not specified in the cohort, authors postulated that once the umbilical cord of the first-born twin is clamped, the second-born twin may not only receive blood from its own placenta but also from the placenta-share of the co-twin through the patent vascular anastomoses.56 This is consistent with previous observations reported by Galea et al.,57 Wenstrom et al.,58 and Machin and Keith.59 In monochorionic twins, the hemoglobin difference may be explained by the presence of placental vascular anastomoses, which allow inter-twin blood flow. However, the direction and magnitude of this transfusion depend on the type and functional significance of these vascular connections, as well as the timing of cord clamping for each twin after birth. In dichorionic twins, who lack shared placental circulation, the higher hemoglobin levels observed in second-born twins are more likely attributable to differences in the timing of cord clamping between the first- and second-born infants, with a longer interval before clamping potentially allowing greater placental transfusion.60, 61

In a retrospective analysis of the Canadian Neonatal/Preterm Birth Network database, outcomes were compared between DCC >30 s versus early clamping within 30 s in 1597 twins of <30 weeks’ gestation. The primary composite outcome was death and/or severe neurologic injury (defined as severe IVH and/or periventricular leukomalacia). There was no significant difference observed between groups (aOR 1.07; 95% CI, 0.78–1.47). However, DCC was associated with reduced number of blood transfusions (adjusted coefficient, 0.49; 95% CI, 0.12–0.86) and a shorter NICU length of stay (adjusted coefficient, 4.17; 95% CI, 8.15–0.19).62 Although this analysis included monochorionic and dichorionic twins, the absence of stratification by chorionicity significantly limits interpretation, as these groups differ fundamentally in placental physiology and risk profile.

Available observational data have not demonstrated a clear increase in maternal bleeding complications associated with DCC in twin pregnancies. However, existing studies are limited by relatively small sample sizes and may be underpowered to detect uncommon maternal outcomes such as major postpartum hemorrhage. In a retrospective cohort study of twin pregnancies Ruangkit et al., 295 women who underwent early clamping (less than 30 s) compared with 154 women who underwent DCC (more than 30 s). No difference in maternal bleeding complications between groups was observed.63 Notably, the early clamping group included 58 (19.7%) women with monochorionic twins, while the DCC group included 32 (20.8%) women. The authors concluded that DCC may be feasible in multiple pregnancies without an apparent increase in maternal complications. However, these findings should be interpreted cautiously given the observational design and small sample size. Similarly, Chiruvolu et al. in a quality improvement initiative reported no increase in maternal hemorrhagic complications associated with DCC.55 Consistent findings were observed in a large retrospective study from Korea involving 705 twin pregnancies, in which DCC was performed in 225 cases (31.9%); in this cohort, DCC was not associated with higher rates of postpartum bleeding or maternal complications. However, the observational design limits causal interpretation.64 Importantly, many maternal and neonatal outcomes of interest are relatively uncommon, and currently available studies may not be adequately powered to exclude clinically meaningful differences in safety outcomes.

A major limitation in the available evidence on DCC in twins is the small representation of multiple gestations in published trials, as well as the marked heterogeneity within twin populations. Available evidence consists predominantly of small observational cohorts and retrospective studies, with relatively few studies including substantial numbers of monochorionic twin pregnancies. For example, assuming a baseline rate of death or severe neurologic injury of approximately 15–20% in very preterm infants, several thousand infants would be required to reliably detect a modest relative risk reduction (e.g., 20%) with adequate statistical power. Sample size requirements become even greater when subgroup analyses are performed according to chorionicity or specific clinical conditions. Therefore, most existing studies are likely underpowered for safety outcomes, and the absence of statistically significant differences should not be interpreted as evidence of equivalence or safety.

A major limitation of the current evidence base is inadequate stratification according to clinically important variables such as chorionicity, placental vascular anatomy, fetal condition, and prior fetal interventions. This limitation is particularly important in monochorionic twins, who remain underrepresented in existing studies despite having the highest theoretical risk profile. In the study by McDonnel et al., only four twin sets were included among 46 infants born <33 weeks’ gestation randomized to early versus deferred clamping.65 Similarly, a feasibility trial of 65 infants including only seven twin and one triplet pregnancies reported no difference in hemoglobin in the first hours of life. Even in the largest randomized controlled trial to date, the Australian Placental Transfusion Study (APTS) including 390 infants from multiple pregnancies (25% of a cohort of 1566 infants), no separate subgroup analysis for twins was performed.66

The available evidence presents conflicting findings regarding the safety and potential benefits of DCC in twin pregnancies, particularly in monochorionic twins. On one hand, some observational studies and quality improvement initiatives suggest that DCC is feasible and may be associated with benefits such as reduced transfusion requirements and shorter hospital stay, without an apparent increase in neonatal or maternal complications.55, 62, 63, 64 Importantly, much of the available evidence is derived from observational studies with inherent risk of bias, including non-randomized group allocation and residual confounding, and often involves sample sizes that may be insufficient to detect clinically meaningful differences in key outcomes. These limitations are further compounded by the potential for publication bias and the selective reporting of positive or clinically significant findings.

On the other hand, emerging data raise concerns regarding potential risks in monochorionic twins. For example, Rondagh et al. reported an increased prevalence of acute peripartum TTTS in infants undergoing DCC compared with early cord clamping (17% vs. 0%).67 Similarly, randomized data in multiple pregnancies have not consistently demonstrated improved placental transfusion or neonatal outcomes and have raised concerns regarding maternal complications in some settings.50 These contrasting findings highlight the uncertainty in the current evidence base and underscore the importance of interpreting results within the context of study design, population characteristics, and chorionicity.

Importantly, high-quality randomized controlled data in monochorionic twin pregnancies are extremely limited. Most randomized trials evaluating cord clamping strategies have either excluded multiple gestations or included only small numbers of twins without stratification by chorionicity.65, 66 As a result, current practice is largely informed by observational studies and extrapolation from singleton data, which may not adequately capture the unique hemodynamic considerations of monochorionic twins. Most available studies are retrospective and include relatively small sample sizes, limiting the ability to assess important outcomes such as acute inter-twin transfusion and long-term neurodevelopment. Consequently, reported benefits—including reduced transfusion requirements or shorter hospital stay—should be interpreted cautiously because residual confounding cannot be excluded.

Therefore, while some findings suggest potential benefits, conclusions should be interpreted cautiously because the available literature is dominated by observational studies, includes small sample sizes, lacks adequate subgroup stratification, and contains limited randomized data—factors that would likely contribute to low certainty of evidence if formally assessed. Extrapolation from singleton physiology and outcomes may not be appropriate in all twin pregnancies, particularly in monochorionic gestations where shared placental circulation introduces unique risks.

Conclusions

DCC is supported by strong physiologic rationale and high-quality evidence in singleton preterm infants, where it improves hemodynamic stability, reduces transfusion requirements, and is associated with improved survival. In contrast, in twin pregnancies—particularly monochorionic twins—the evidence base remains limited and heterogeneous.

What is known: Available studies in twin pregnancies suggest that DCC is feasible and may be associated with benefits such as reduced transfusion requirements and shorter NICU stay. However, these findings are derived predominantly from small observational studies and should be interpreted cautiously. These studies are susceptible to bias and confounding and often lack sufficient statistical power for important clinical outcomes. Emerging evidence in monochorionic twins raises concerns regarding potential risks, including acute inter-twin transfusion.

What remains unknown: There is insufficient high-quality evidence to determine the safety and efficacy of DCC in monochorionic twin pregnancies. The impact of DCC on inter-twin hemodynamics, optimal timing of cord clamping, and long-term neurodevelopmental outcomes remains unclear. Randomized controlled data in this population remain extremely limited. Uncertainty is further compounded by the heterogeneity and overall limited quality of the available evidence base. Interpretation of the existing literature is further limited by potential publication bias and the absence of systematically synthesized evidence.

Proposed clinical framework: Current evidence remains insufficient to support definitive practice recommendations regarding DCC in twin pregnancies, particularly for monochorionic twins. Until higher-quality evidence becomes available, decisions regarding cord management should be individualized using a structured assessment of chorionicity, gestational age, anticipated need for immediate resuscitation, fetal condition, delivery circumstances, and institutional expertise. In practice, DCC may be more readily considered in clinically stable dichorionic twins when no contraindications exist, and immediate resuscitation is not anticipated. In contrast, monochorionic twins warrant greater caution because shared placental circulation introduces theoretical risks related to acute inter-twin transfusion and altered hemodynamics. Therefore, extrapolation from singleton data should be undertaken cautiously, particularly in pregnancies with shared placental circulation or additional complications. The proposed framework to support individualized decision-making is presented in Fig. 3.

Future research priorities: Future research should prioritize study designs that account for key sources of heterogeneity, including chorionicity, placental vascular architecture, fetal condition, and prior fetal interventions. Given the substantial heterogeneity in twin pregnancies and the limitations of the current evidence base, reliance on secondary analyses alone is unlikely to resolve key questions regarding safety and efficacy. Individual participant data (IPD) meta-analyses may facilitate more granular analyses stratified by chorionicity, gestational age, and birth order. However, their utility will remain limited because the available literature is dominated by observational studies, includes small subgroup sample sizes, and contains relatively few randomized data in twin pregnancies. Therefore, adequately powered prospective studies specifically designed to address these subgroups are essential to meaningfully advance the evidence base.
