Extracorporeal Shock Wave Therapy Promotes Peripheral Nerve Regeneration Through ERK1/2‐Mediated Autophagy
Authors: Xingdong Xiang, Xiaoqiong Wu, Shen Ling, Junwen Zhu, Jian Zhang, Tengfei Fu
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTExtracorporeal shock wave therapy (SWT) has shown potential in promoting peripheral nerve regeneration, yet its underlying mechanisms remain incompletely understood. In this study, we explored the role of extracellular signal‐regulated kinase 1/2 (ERK1/2)‐mediated autophagy in SWT‐enhanced nerve repair using both in vivo and in vitro models. Application of SWT to transected sciatic nerves in rats led to increased ERK1/2 phosphorylation and upregulation of the autophagy marker LC3B, as confirmed by immunofluorescence and RT‐qPCR. In vitro, SWT‐treated Schwann cells exhibited enhanced autophagosome formation and accelerated autophagic flux, correlating with increased axonal outgrowth in cocultured dorsal root ganglion neurons. Pharmacological modulation further revealed that ERK1/2 activation enhanced, while inhibition suppressed, SWT‐induced autophagy and neurite extension. Moreover, SWT improved motor recovery in injured rats, as evidenced by enhanced gait performance, sciatic functional index, and electrophysiological recordings. These effects were amplified by cotreatment with the ERK1/2 agonist senkyunolide I and attenuated by the inhibitor SCH772984. Histological and ultrastructural analyses showed enhanced Schwann cell proliferation, axonal integrity, and myelin reorganization following SWT, alongside improved muscle morphology. Collectively, our findings identify ERK1/2‐activated autophagy in Schwann cells as a key mechanism mediating the therapeutic effects of SWT, supporting its translational potential for peripheral nerve repair.

Introduction

Peripheral nerve injury (PNI) remains a significant clinical challenge, often leading to debilitating sensory and motor deficits, muscle atrophy, and prolonged disability [1]. Despite the intrinsic regenerative capability of the peripheral nervous system, functional recovery is frequently incomplete, especially in cases involving nerve transection or delayed repair. Various therapeutic strategies—including microsurgical repair, nerve grafting, physical therapy, as well as medications—offer limited efficacy due to factors such as poor axonal alignment, slow regrowth rates, and hostile inflammatory microenvironments [2, 3, 4].

Extracorporeal shock wave therapy (SWT), a noninvasive mechanical stimulation technique initially used for lithotripsy, has emerged as a promising modality in regenerative medicine [1, 5]. SWT is now extensively applied in musculoskeletal disorders, wound healing, and, more recently, neurological conditions, including PNI [3, 6, 7, 8]. Mechanistically, SWT delivers high‐amplitude, short‐duration acoustic waves that stimulate cellular responses through mechanotransduction, promoting angiogenesis, modulating inflammation, and activating regeneration‐associated pathways [9, 10].

Accumulating evidence indicates that SWT can enhance nerve regeneration and improve functional outcomes in animal models [2, 3, 4]. Several potential mechanisms have been proposed, including the release of growth factors, the modulation of inflammatory responses, and the promotion of Schwann cell (SC) activity [5, 11]. However, despite promising findings, the precise cellular and molecular mechanisms underlying SWT's therapeutic effects on peripheral nerves remain incompletely elucidated.

The extracellular signal‐regulated kinase 1/2 (ERK1/2) is one of the best‐characterized members of the mitogen‐activated protein kinase (MAPK) family, playing a pivotal role in various aspects of SC biology, including differentiation, myelination, and remyelination [12, 13]. Low‐intensity extracorporeal SWT promotes SC proliferation via activation of ERK/MAPK signaling, and sustained ERK1/2 activation can lead to autophagy [14, 15, 16]. Concurrently, SC autophagy directly facilitates peripheral nerve regeneration by efficiently clearing inhibitory myelin debris and contributing to myelin compaction [17].

Here, we hypothesized that SWT enhances nerve repair through the activation of ERK1/2, which subsequently modulates autophagy in SCs, leading to axonal regrowth and functional recovery. To test this, we used a rat sciatic nerve transection‐suture model, complemented by in vitro SC–dorsal root ganglion (DRG) coculture assays, to examine the underlying mechanisms. Our findings provide novel insights into SWT‐driven nerve regeneration and support its further exploration as a therapeutic strategy for PNI.

Materials and Methods

Rat Model and SWT Treatment

In this study, animal experiments were conducted using a rat sciatic nerve transection and repair model. This model was established using adult rats (Sprague‐Dawley). Rats were anesthetized with isoflurane gas (2–3% inhaled). A skin incision was made along the lateral aspect of the thigh. The underlying muscles were bluntly dissected to expose the sciatic nerve trunk. Following transection, the two nerve ends were immediately realigned and repaired using microsurgical techniques with 10‐0 nonabsorbable sutures through the epineurium. The muscle and skin layers were subsequently closed. Rats were randomly assigned to various experimental groups. The pharmacological interventions commenced immediately after the surgical procedure. Rats received intraperitoneal (i.p.) injections of either SCH772984 (50 mg/kg, 1% DMSO in normal saline, MedChemExpress, USA) or senkyunolide I (36 mg/kg, 1% DMSO in normal saline, MedChemExpress) once every 24 h for 7 consecutive days. Animals in the control group received an equal volume of the vehicle solution (1% DMSO in saline) on the same schedule.

Based on established protocols from SWT literature, low‐energy settings were selected for treatment [4]. Unanesthetized rats received focused SWT initiated 24 h post‐surgery [18]. Pain was assessed immediately before, during, and for 5 min after each SWT session by a blinded observer monitoring for validated rodent pain behaviors (vocalization, aggressive avoidance, guarding, or licking of the treated limb). No animals exhibited pain‐related behaviors during or after SWT sessions. The electromagnetic shockwave generator (DUOLITH SD1 TOWER, Storz Medical, Switzerland) was vertically positioned over shaved skin at the distal portion of the injured sciatic nerve. The 1.6 mm circular applicator tip coupled with transmission gel (Antiseptic Ultrasound Coupling Gel, Yijie Medicine, China) ensured energy transduction. Each rat delivered 300 pulses at an energy flux density of 0.2 mJ/mm2 with a repetition rate of 3 Hz [2, 18]. Therapeutic interventions targeted the distal nerve stump through four treatment sessions administered over 7 postoperative days at 48‐h intervals. At 6 and 12 weeks post‐surgery, we employed the Catwalk Gait Analysis System to analyze the footprint mean intensity and footprint area of the hind limb. The experiment involved four groups (n = 5 rats each group), with the entire protocol repeated three times independently to ensure reproducibility. Rats were housed in standard cages under a 12‐h light/dark cycle with ad libitum access to food and water, in a temperature (22 ± 2°C) and humidity‐controlled (50 ± 10%) facility. All animal procedures were performed in accordance with the protocols approved by the Fudan University Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee (No. 2022–332).

The Usage of CatWalk XT

An automated quantitative gait analysis system, CatWalk XT (CWT; Noldus, Wageningen, Netherlands), was used to assess the motor function and coordination of rats. All tests were conducted in a quiet, dedicated behavioral testing room. Prior to formal data collection, all rats underwent a habituation and training protocol. Specifically, on the day before testing, each rat was placed in the CatWalk XT tunnel and allowed to explore freely until it could successfully complete one full traversal (from the start box to the end box and back again) without hesitation or interruption. This ensured consistent and voluntary locomotion during subsequent recordings. The CatWalk recordings were performed in a darkened testing room (with exclusion of ambient daylight) to ensure optimal contrast for the system's internal illumination, as per the manufacturer's guidelines. For each testing session, a minimum of three complete, uninterrupted runs were recorded per animal, with each run consisting of at least two full‐step cycles.

Data acquisition and initial processing were performed using the manufacturer's proprietary software (CatWalk XT 10.6.68, Noldus) in strict accordance with its standardized operational protocols [19]. The recorded runs were automatically classified by the software and then manually verified to ensure accuracy. The following key gait parameters were extracted and analyzed to assess coordination and functional recovery: print area, mean footprint intensity, print length, print width, stride length, step cycle, stand phase duration, and swing speed. This detailed protocol, which adheres to the platform's standardized operation, ensures the reliability and reproducibility of our gait analysis.

Cell Culture and SWT Treatment In Vitro

Primary DRGs were obtained from Sprague‐Dawley rats (3−5 weeks old), as previously described [20]. Cells were seeded in the lower chamber of transwell plates (0.4 µm pore size). Primary SCs were expanded in DMEM supplemented with 10% FBS. SCs were plated in the upper chamber of separate transwell systems. The applicator tip coated with ultrasound gel (Antiseptic Ultrasound Coupling Gel, Yijie Medicine) was coupled to the plate base. An electromagnetic shockwave generator (DUOLITH SD1 TOWER, Storz Medical) delivered 300 pulses per session at 0.4 mJ/mm2 energy flux density (3 Hz) through a 0.5 cm‐thick porcine tissue barrier mimicking biological attenuation. Four extracorporeal SWT treatments were administered at 48‐h intervals over 7 days. Treated SC chambers were then transferred to DRG‐containing systems. Biological interactions were assessed after 72‐h coculture under standard conditions (37°C, 5% CO2).

H&E, Masson's Trichrome, and Toluidine Blue Staining

Following completion of the in vivo experiments, nerve and muscle tissues were fixed in 4% paraformaldehyde (Servicebio, G1101, China) for 48 h at 4°C (except for frozen sections). Then, fixed muscles were sectioned (4 µm thickness), and Masson's trichrome staining kit (Freethinking, FH115100, China) and H&E dye (Freethinking, FS141500) were used to visualize myofibers and collagen fibers in the muscle tissue. Fixed nerves were sectioned, and toluidine blue dye (Freethinking, FH119100) was used to visualize the growth cones of regenerating axons and newly formed nerve fibers.

Electrophysiological Test

Rats were anesthetized with isoflurane gas (2–3% inhaled). For the electrophysiological assessment, compound muscle action potentials (CMAPs) were recorded from rats under anesthesia at 12 weeks post‐surgery. Following a standard protocol for sciatic nerve‐gastrocnemius muscle preparation, the sciatic nerve was surgically exposed at the mid‐thigh level. A bipolar stimulating electrode was placed in direct contact with the sciatic nerve proximal to the injury site. A unipolar recording needle electrode was inserted into the belly of the ipsilateral gastrocnemius muscle, with a reference electrode placed in the muscle tendon and a ground electrode positioned subcutaneously between the stimulating and recording sites. A single stimulus (0.1–1.0 mA, 0.1 ms) was applied using a biological signal acquisition system (MD3000‐C, Zhenghua Biological Instrument Equipment Co., Ltd., China), and the amplitudes of CMAP were recorded once supramaximal stimulation was reached by using progressively increasing stimulus intensity [21, 22].

Immunofluorescence

For tissue immunofluorescence analysis, paraffin sections were deparaffinized in xylene, hydrated in graded ethanol solutions, and subjected to heat‐induced antigen retrieval. Endogenous peroxidases and antigens were blocked with 3% H2O2 and 3% bovine serum albumin (BSA), respectively. The multiplex immunofluorescence protocol involved multiple rounds of staining. For each round, sections were incubated with the respective primary antibody diluted in 3% BSA overnight at 4°C, followed by incubation with appropriate fluorescent secondary antibodies for 1 h at room temperature in the dark. Finally, the nuclei were counterstained with DAPI. Antibodies against phospho‐ERK1/2 (28733‐1‐AP, Proteintech, China), LC3B (14600‐1‐AP, Proteintech), S100β (15146‐1‐AP, Proteintech), and NF200 (21471‐1‐AP, Proteintech) were used in this part of the study. Stained sections were observed under a fluorescence microscope (CKX53, Olympus, Japan).

Transmission Electron Microscopy

For transmission electron microscopy (TEM), control or SWT‐treated SCs were fixed with 2.5% glutaraldehyde in 0.1 M sodium dihydrogen phosphatase (G1102, Servicebio) for 4 h at 4°C before being fixed with 1% osmium tetroxide for 1 h at room temperature. After dehydration using graded ethanol solutions and gradual infiltration with EMbed 812 epoxy resin (SPI Supplies, 90529‐77‐4, USA), ultrathin sections (60–80 nm) were prepared using an ultramicrotome (Leica, EM UC7, Germany) and contrasted with 2% uranium acetate and 2.6% lead citrate. The sections were observed under a TEM (Hitachi, HT7800, Japan), and autophagosomes were identified as previously described [23]. Nerves were isolated immediately and immersed in 2.5% glutaraldehyde solution for 24 h. The sciatic nerves were stained with 1% osmium tetroxide and observed with TEM after dehydrating and embedding. Images of each sample were acquired from a randomly chosen area. The diameter of the myelinated axon, the thickness of the myelin sheath, the ratio of the myelin area to the cross‐sectional area of the nerve fiber, and the G‐ratio (quantified by dividing the axon radius by the myelinated fiber radius) were calculated from the TEM images.

SC Transfection

Lentiviral particles expressing shRNA targeting rat Atg7 mRNA (Hanbio, China) and a nontargeting control (scrambled) were used. Primary rat SCs were seeded in 6‐well plates at a density of 2 × 105 cells per well and cultured overnight. The next day, cells were transduced with lentivirus at a multiplicity of infection of 20 in the presence of 8 µg/mL Polybrene (H8761, Solarbio, China). After 24 h, the virus‐containing medium was replaced with fresh complete growth medium. To establish stable knockdown, cells were selected with 2 µg/mL puromycin for 72 h starting at 48 h post‐transduction. Knockdown efficiency was confirmed by qRT‐PCR analysis at 5 days post‐transduction. For subsequent experiments, stably transfected SCs were harvested or subjected to SWT treatment 48 h after reaching confluence.

Autophagy Flux Detection

After transduction with adenovirus expressing HBAD‐mcherry‐EGFP‐LC3 (HB‐AP2100001, Hanbio), SCs were treated with SWT or control conditions for 48 h. The cells were then fixed with 4% paraformaldehyde (G1101, Servicebio) for 30 min at room temperature, and the nuclei were counterstained with DAPI (G1407‐25ML, Servicebio). Finally, immunofluorescence was observed and captured using a Leica SP8 Confocal microscope, as previously described [23].

Total RNA Preparation and Quantitative Real‐Time PCR Analysis

Total RNA was extracted from cultured SCs using TRIzol reagent (Thermo Fisher Scientific, 15596026, USA), according to the manufacturer's instructions. Then, 400 ng of total RNA was reverse transcribed into cDNA using a reverse transcriptase kit (4368814, Thermo Fisher Scientific). qRT‐PCR was performed using a SYBR green mixture (A25778, Thermo Fisher Scientific) and a StepOnePlus Real‐Time PCR instrument (4376600, Thermo Fisher Scientific). Primers used for each transcript are listed in Table S1.

Western Blot

For Western blot analysis, SCs (4 × 106 cells/sample) were collected and lysed in ice‐cold RIPA lysis buffer (R0010, Solarbio) containing a protease inhibitor cocktail (04693124001, Merck, Germany) and a phosphatase inhibitor cocktail (04906845001, Merck). Cell lysates were then centrifuged at 12,000 × g for 15 min at 4°C, and the protein sample was diluted for denaturation. Subsequently, 50 µg of total sample protein was separated using 10% SDS‐PAGE and electrotransferred onto polyvinylidene fluoride membranes using a Bio‐Rad power supply (CA, USA). The membranes were blocked with 5% nonfat milk for 1 h at room temperature and then incubated with primary antibodies overnight at 4°C. After washing, the membranes were incubated with horseradish peroxidase‐conjugated secondary antibodies (A0208, Beyotime, China) for 1 h at room temperature. Protein bands were visualized using an ECL kit (34580, Thermo Fisher Scientific). Images were captured using an enhanced chemiluminescence imaging system (ChemiDoc XRS+, Bio‐Rad) with default parameters and without gamma adjustment. Subsequently, images were analyzed using the Image Lab analysis software (1709690, Bio‐Rad). GAPDH was used as the loading control. Antibodies against phospho‐ERK1/2 (28733‐1‐AP, Proteintech), ERK1/2 (11257‐1‐AP, Proteintech), ATG7 (10088‐2‐AP, Proteintech), LC3B (14600‐1‐AP, Proteintech), P62 (18420‐1‐AP, Proteintech), and GAPDH (60004‐1‐Ig, Proteintech) were used in this part of the study.

Statistical Analysis

Quantitative data are presented as the mean ± SD. Individual data points are displayed, and all experiments were performed with at least three replicates. In the animal experiments, each group consisted of five rats, and a random field of view was taken from each rat. Statistical analysis was performed using the unpaired Student's t‐test for comparisons between two groups. For comparisons of more than two groups, one‐way analysis of variance (ANOVA) was used, followed by a Bonferroni multiple comparisons post‐hoc test. The areas of fluorescent staining for muscle fibers and nuclei were quantified using ImageJ software. Data were analyzed using GraphPad Prism software (version 9.0, USA). A p value < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Results

SWT Enhances ERK1/2 Phosphorylation and Autophagy in Distal Nerve

To investigate the mechanistic basis of SWT in peripheral nerve repair, we administered SWT to the distal segment of sciatic nerves in rat transection‐suture models and analyzed localized protein expression changes. Immunofluorescence costaining with S100β and DAPI revealed significantly elevated LC3B expression and enhanced phosphorylated ERK1/2 signal intensity in SWT‐treated nerves compared to controls (Figure 1A–C). Concurrent RT‐qPCR analysis demonstrated upregulated transcription of Mapk3 and Map1lc3a in SWT‐treated neural tissue, confirming pathway activation at both translational and transcriptional levels (Figure 1D,E). These findings collectively suggest that when applied to peripheral nerves, SWT coordinated activation of autophagy flux and ERK/MAPK signaling cascades, providing mechanistic insights justifying further investigation.

SWT‐Induced ERK1/2 Phosphorylation Mediates SCs Autophagy

To investigate SWT's cellular effects, we established an in vitro model using SCs and primary DRG neurons in a transwell coculture system with 0.5 cm porcine muscle tissue simulating in vivo SWT attenuation (Figure 2A). Detected by TEM, SWT‐treated SCs exhibited a higher abundance of autophagosomes compared to the control group (Figure 2B). Activation of autophagic flux in SWT‐treated SCs was further confirmed by a decreased ratio of microtubule‐associated protein 1 light chain 3 beta (LC3B) yellow puncta to total LC3B red puncta in comparison to that of the control cells (Figure 2C,D).

To further investigate the relationship between SWT‐induced ERK1/2 phosphorylation and neural repair, SCs were subjected to SWT combined with pharmacological ERK1/2 activation (senkyunolide I) or inhibition (SCH772984) prior to DRG coculture. SCH772984, a potent and selective ATP‐competitive inhibitor of ERK1/2, was used to specifically block the pathway activated by SWT [24]. Senkyunolide I, a known activator of the ERK1/2 signaling pathway, was used to test the sufficiency of ERK1/2 activation in potentiating SWT effects [25]. SWT‐treated SCs demonstrated enhanced capacity to promote DRGs axonal extension compared to untreated controls, with the most pronounced neurite outgrowth observed in cultures combining SWT and ERK1/2 agonist pretreatment. Conversely, concurrent ERK1/2 inhibition attenuated the therapeutic effects of SWT, resulting in diminished axonal elongation relative to SWT‐only groups (Figure 2E,F). These functional outcomes correlated with sustained ERK1/2 phosphorylation dynamics in SCs, demonstrating the critical involvement of the MAPK/ERK pathway activation in mediating SWT‐enhanced neuron−glial interactions during peripheral nerve regeneration.

We performed shRNA‐mediated knockdown of the essential autophagy gene Atg7 in primary SCs to definitively test the necessity of autophagy for the therapeutic benefits of SWT. Scrambled control SCs showed the expected activation of autophagy, characterized by a decrease in P62 and an increase in LC3B‐II. Crucially, in Atg7 mRNA‐knockdown SCs, SWT failed to induce these autophagic changes. The levels of P62 remained high, and the conversion of LC3B‐I to LC3B‐II was significantly blunted, confirming that the autophagic flux was effectively blocked (Figure 2G). These results offered direct genetic evidence that an intact autophagic pathway was necessary for SWT to trigger autophagy in SCs.

To determine whether ERK1/2 mediates the activation of autophagic flux following SWT treatment, we employed the inhibitor SCH772984 or the agonist senkyunolide I in conjunction with SWT treatment. The results showed that SCH772984 strongly suppressed SWT‐induced phosphorylation of ERK1/2 and formation of LC3B‐II. In contrast, when treated with senkyunolide I alone, SCs maintained high levels of ERK1/2 phosphorylation and LC3B‐II production, which enhanced the autophagy‐promoting effect of SWT treatment (Figure 2H).

SWT Promotes Motor Functional Recovery via ERK1/2‐Activated Autophagy

To validate the in vivo relevance of the observed mechanisms, we performed Catwalk gait analysis at postoperative weeks 6 and 12 in sciatic nerve‐transected rats receiving SWT with or without pharmacological modulation (Figure 3A,B). SWT monotherapy significantly improved hindlimb functional recovery compared to untreated controls, as evidenced by enhanced gait stability, increased mean intensity of left hind footprints, and expanded contact area. The therapeutic synergy between SWT and ERK1/2 activation was further demonstrated by superior locomotor performance in rats cotreated with SWT and senkyunolide I, whereas concurrent SCH772984 administration attenuated the therapeutic efficacy of SWT (Figure 3C,D). The sustained therapeutic benefits of SWT intervention were further corroborated by sciatic functional index (SFI) assessments at week 12, which mirrored the earlier gait analysis trends demonstrating enhanced motor recovery in SWT‐treated rats compared to controls (Figure 3E). These functional improvements correlated with sustained ERK1/2 phosphorylation dynamics in nerve tissues, underscoring the translational potential of targeting MAPK/ERK signaling to amplify SWT‐driven nerve restoration.

Biological and histopathological analyses of innervated gastrocnemius muscles revealed that SWT‐treated specimens exhibited improved muscle architecture with enhanced bulk preservation (Figure 4A,B). Electrophysiological assessments demonstrated superior CMAP amplitudes in the SWT group, while combined SWT and senkyunolide I administration yielded synergistic therapeutic efficacy (Figure 4D,E). Conversely, pharmacological ERK1/2 inhibition via SCH772984 attenuated these structural and functional improvements, establishing concordance between neurophysiological recovery and muscle regenerative capacity through ERK1/2‐autophagy axis‐mediated neuromuscular functional restitution.

ERK1/2‐Activated Autophagy Mediates SWT‐Induced Nerve Regeneration

Immunohistochemical and immunofluorescence analyses of sciatic nerves across experimental groups revealed that SWT intervention significantly enhanced S100β expression, denoting SC proliferation, accompanied by elevated NF200 immunoreactivity, suggesting axonal integrity preservation (Figure 5A). The combined SWT and senkyunolide I treatment exhibited synergistic histopathological enhancements, whereas SCH772984 coadministration attenuated these therapeutic effects. Semi‐quantitative multifield analyses consistently validated these findings (Figure 5B,C), mechanistically linking ERK1/2‐mediated autophagic activation in SCs to SWT‐induced neurofunctional recovery.

Discussion

This study demonstrates that SWT promotes peripheral nerve regeneration through activation of the ERK1/2 signaling pathway and subsequent autophagy in SCs, ultimately contributing to enhanced structural and functional recovery following sciatic nerve injury. Through a combination of in vivo and in vitro models, we provide compelling evidence that ERK1/2 activation plays a central role in mediating autophagic flux, axonal elongation, myelin reorganization, and neuromuscular restoration under SWT.

The core contribution of this study lies in demonstrating that SWT regulates SC autophagy by activating the ERK1/2 pathway, a process now recognized as a critical step in multiple stages of Wallerian degeneration and subsequent regeneration [10]. Autophagy promotes myelin clearance, maintains metabolic homeostasis, and supports SC phenotype transitions necessary for axonal regrowth [26]. Although ERK1/2 signaling is known to coordinate SC plasticity by regulating dedifferentiation, myelin breakdown, and subsequent remyelination [13, 27], its role as an upstream regulator of autophagic flux in the SWT context remains unexplored. This study reveals a mechanistic bridge between biomechanical stimulation and intracellular degradation pathways, highlighting the adaptability of SCs to physical signals. This concept aligns with a broader shift in regenerative medicine, where mechanical stimuli are viewed not merely as external forces but as regulators of intracellular signaling networks with therapeutic relevance.

The significance of ERK1/2 activation in SCs extends beyond autophagy regulation. The ERK1/2 pathway integrates multiple signals, including growth factors, extracellular matrix deformation, metabolic cues, and inflammatory mediators [28]. Its activation following SWT suggests that shockwave‐induced mechanical stress may mimic or amplify endogenous regenerative signals that occur after PNI. This is consistent with the rapidly evolving framework of mechanobiology, where cells convert biophysical stimuli into transcriptional and metabolic responses [29]. Notably, recent studies in other tissues such as bone and tendon demonstrate that SWT enhances tissue repair via MAPK/ERK activation, suggesting a potentially conserved mechanism of mechanotransductive regeneration [30, 31]. Placing our findings in a cross‐tissue context suggests that SWT may function as a multitarget regulator of cellular mechanotransduction, with its specific effects on SCs shaped by the unique demands of nerve repair [32].

Convergent validation from behavioral, electrophysiological, and histological data strongly confirms the functional significance of this pathway. Improvements in gait parameters (CatWalk analysis) and SFI are not isolated phenomena but functional manifestations of cellular and structural restoration. Our results, showing enhanced myelin sheath organization (via electron microscopy) and axonal integrity (NF200), provide the structural basis for the improved CMAP amplitudes. Crucially, suppression of the ERK1/2 attenuates both functional and structural improvements, further confirming that this pathway is not just involved but is an indispensable component of the regenerative cascade initiated by SWT. This aligns with recent findings that MAPK/ERK pathway activation contributes critically to nerve regeneration by regulating SC plasticity, dedifferentiation, and myelination [13, 27].

From a translational medicine perspective, this study reveals several key opportunities and challenges. The demonstration that ERK1/2 inhibition attenuates SWT‐induced functional recovery suggests that maintaining ERK signaling integrity may be essential for clinical efficacy. This raises questions regarding patient populations with impaired ERK signaling—such as those with diabetes, aging‐related deficits, or metabolic diseases [33], whose response to SWT may be altered. Personalized adjustment of SWT parameters, including energy flux density, frequency, and pulse number, may be necessary to optimize responses in such populations.

Furthermore, the translational potential of SWT also depends on clarifying its dose–response characteristics. While SWT is attractive due to its noninvasive nature, inappropriate dosing may inhibit cell growth and interfere with repair potential [34]. Current clinical applications of SWT in musculoskeletal disorders provide valuable safety data [35, 36], but its application to nerve tissues will require tailored protocols given the sensitivity of neural structures. Larger‐animal studies are critical for establishing optimal energy parameters, evaluating long‐term efficacy, and assessing safety within anatomically relevant environments.

While our findings strongly support the regenerative role of ERK1/2‐activated autophagy in SWT‐mediated nerve repair, some limitations should be acknowledged. First, the use of a single nerve injury model may limit generalizability across different types or severities of PNI. Additionally, although we focused on ERK1/2 and autophagy, other mechanotransduction pathways such as YAP/TAZ or small extracellular vesicle‐mediated intercellular communication could also contribute and warrant further exploration [10].

In conclusion, our study provides compelling evidence that SWT promotes peripheral nerve regeneration through ERK1/2‐dependent autophagy in SCs. This mechanistic insight opens new avenues for optimizing regenerative strategies targeting SC plasticity and supports the broader integration of SWT into neurorehabilitation protocols for PNI.
