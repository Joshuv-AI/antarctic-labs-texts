Perioperative inflammatory and coagulation biomarkers for predicting venous thromboembolism after lower limb fracture surgery
Authors: Jing Tian, Lixin Cao, Ziyu Zhang, Guangyu Ren
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundVenous thromboembolism (VTE), encompassing deep vein thrombosis (DVT) and pulmonary embolism (PE), is a preventable but serious postoperative complication in patients with lower limb fractures. Despite prophylactic measures, incidence remains high due to the interplay of trauma, immobility, and systemic inflammation. Biomarkers such as D-dimer, C-reactive protein (CRP), interleukin-6 (IL-6),and neutrophil-to-lymphocyte ratio (NLR), along with clinical recovery parameters, may improve prediction but remain under-integrated in orthopedic trauma risk models.MethodsA retrospective observational study was conducted in 106 patients undergoing surgical fixation for lower limb fractures between 2021 and 2024. All patients received risk-stratified VTE prophylaxis per Caprini score and institutional guidelines. Perioperative blood samples were collected preoperatively and on postoperative days 1, 3, and 5 to measure inflammatory, hematologic, and coagulation parameters. Clinical and surgical data, including ambulation timing and hospital stay, were recorded. VTE diagnosis was symptom-triggered and confirmed via Doppler ultrasonography or computed tomography pulmonary angiography (CTPA). Statistical analyses included logistic regression with odds ratio (OR), calibration testing, and receiver operating characteristic (ROC) analysis.ResultsTwenty-one patients (19.8%) developed VTE. Significant risk factors included advanced age, hypertension, femoral fractures, delayed ambulation, and prolonged hospitalization. Biomarkers such as CRP, IL-6, tumour necrosis factor-α (TNF-α), D-dimer, NLR, and fibrinogen were consistently higher in VTE patients, particularly on postoperative Day 3. Multivariate analysis identified age (OR 1.05, 95% CI 1.01–1.10), hypertension (OR 2.41, 95% CI 1.01–5.76), delayed ambulation (OR 3.26, 95% CI 1.22–8.70), and elevated D-dimer (OR 1.32, 95% CI 1.08–1.63) as independent predictors. ROC analysis demonstrated strongest performance for D-dimer [area under the curve (AUC) 0.78], while a combined biomarker model integrating D-dimer, CRP, IL-6, NLR, and fibrinogen improved discrimination [AUC 0.86, sensitivity 81.0%, specificity 79.5%, negative predictive value (NPV) 93.2]. Calibration plots and decision curve analysis confirmed good model performance.ConclusionThis study demonstrates that perioperative biomarkers, particularly when integrated with recovery parameters such as ambulation delay, enhance prediction of postoperative VTE in orthopedic trauma patients. While findings are exploratory due to limited events and symptom-triggered detection, the results highlight the translational potential of combined biomarker–clinical models. Development and external validation of trauma-specific nomograms are warranted to enable individualized prophylaxis and improve patient outcomes.

Introduction

Venous thromboembolism (VTE), encompassing deep vein thrombosis and pulmonary embolism, remains one of the most serious yet potentially preventable complications following surgical treatment of lower limb fractures [1]. Tibial and femoral fractures are particularly high-risk due to their proximity to major venous structures, the extent of soft tissue injury, and prolonged postoperative immobilization [2, 3]. Although surgical fixation is essential for anatomical restoration and functional recovery, it simultaneously increases thrombotic risk by promoting venous stasis, endothelial injury, and systemic inflammatory activation [4, 5]. Consequently, combined pharmacological and mechanical thromboprophylaxis strategies have become standard practice [6, 7]. However, significant heterogeneity persists in the selection, timing, and duration of prophylactic regimens across institutions, contributing to variability in reported VTE incidence and underscoring the need for more individualized risk stratification approaches [8]. Increasing evidence highlights the close biological interplay between inflammation and thrombosis in trauma and postoperative settings. Inflammatory mediators such as C-reactive protein and interleukin-6 are not only markers of systemic stress but also active participants in endothelial dysfunction and coagulation cascade activation [9, 10]. The concept of “immunothrombosis” describes this bidirectional interaction between immune activation and clot formation, which is particularly relevant after orthopedic trauma [11]. Hematologic indices including the neutrophil-to-lymphocyte ratio, platelet-to-lymphocyte ratio, and fibrinogen have emerged as accessible surrogate markers of prothrombotic potential [12–14]. Elevated neutrophil-to-lymphocyte ratio reflects systemic inflammatory stress and immune dysregulation, whereas increased platelet-to-lymphocyte ratio suggests concurrent platelet activation and lymphocyte suppression, both conducive to thrombogenesis [15]. Among coagulation markers, D-dimer remains the most extensively studied; while highly sensitive for thrombosis, its specificity is limited in the postoperative setting due to physiological fibrinolysis [16–18]. These limitations have prompted growing interest in combining dynamic biomarker measurements to enhance predictive accuracy [19–22]. Despite advances in biomarker research, widely used clinical risk assessment tools such as the Caprini and Padua scores demonstrate inconsistent performance in trauma-specific populations [23–25]. These instruments were largely developed in general surgical or medical cohorts and do not adequately account for dynamic perioperative changes in inflammatory and coagulation markers or functional recovery variables such as ambulation timing [26]. Although emerging nomogram-based models incorporating laboratory parameters have shown preliminary promise, most remain underdeveloped, lack external validation, and are seldom evaluated in real-world orthopedic trauma settings where prophylaxis protocols and detection practices vary considerably [27–29]. Thus, while individual biomarkers and clinical factors have been studied extensively, their integration into cohesive and pragmatic predictive frameworks for lower limb fracture patients remains limited.

Accordingly, the present study sought to evaluate perioperative inflammatory, hematological, and coagulation biomarkers in patients undergoing surgical treatment for lower limb fractures and to explore their association with postoperative VTE. By integrating biomarker dynamics including C-reactive protein, interleukin-6, D-dimer, neutrophil-to-lymphocyte ratio, and fibrinogen with clinical recovery parameters such as ambulation timing and length of hospitalization, we aimed to identify independent predictors of VTE and assess the performance of a combined predictive model within a real-world orthopedic trauma population.

Materials and methods

Study design and ethical approval

This retrospective observational study was conducted in the Department of Orthopaedics at a tertiary care teaching hospital. The study protocol was reviewed and approved by the Institutional Review Board, and all procedures adhered to the ethical principles outlined in the Declaration of Helsinki. Patient data were anonymized prior to analysis.

Study population

Patients who underwent surgical fixation for lower limb fractures between January 2021 and December 2024 were screened for eligibility. A total of 106 patients met the inclusion criteria and were included in the final analysis.

VTE prophylaxis protocol

All patients underwent risk stratification using the Caprini scoring system in accordance with institutional guidelines. Prophylactic strategies were individualized based on risk category.

Mechanical prophylaxis included graduated compression stockings and intermittent pneumatic compression devices. Pharmacological prophylaxis with low molecular weight heparin or direct oral anticoagulants was initiated 12–24 h postoperatively, depending on hemostatic stability. Early mobilization, limb elevation, and adequate hydration were uniformly encouraged.

Blood sampling and laboratory measurements

Venous blood samples were obtained preoperatively (within 24 h before surgery) and postoperatively on days 1, 3, and 5. Inflammatory markers, including C-reactive protein and fibrinogen, were measured using immunoturbidimetric assays (Beckman Coulter AU5800, USA). Interleukin-6, interleukin-10, and tumor necrosis factor-α were quantified using enzyme-linked immunosorbent assay kits (R&D Systems, USA), with a detection range of 1–500 pg/mL and intra- and inter-assay coefficients of variation below 10%. Coagulation parameters including prothrombin time, activated partial thromboplastin time, international normalized ratio, and D-dimer were analyzed using an automated coagulation analyzer (Sysmex CS-5100, Japan). Hematological indices such as neutrophil-to-lymphocyte ratio, platelet-to-lymphocyte ratio, mean platelet volume, platelet count, and total leukocyte count were measured using an automated hematology analyzer (Sysmex XN-1000, Japan). Biochemical parameters including albumin, total protein, lactate dehydrogenase, creatinine, blood urea nitrogen, ferritin, and uric acid were determined using a standardized chemistry analyzer (Beckman Coulter AU5800).

All assays were performed in accordance with manufacturer instructions, and internal quality control procedures were conducted daily to ensure reliability.

Clinical and surgical variables

Demographic and clinical variables included age, sex, body mass index, smoking status, and comorbidities such as hypertension and diabetes mellitus. Surgical variables comprised fracture site, type of fixation, anesthesia modality, duration of surgery, intraoperative blood loss, and time from injury to surgery.

Postoperative variables included time to ambulation, duration of immobilization, length of hospital stay, and occurrence of complications such as surgical site infection or reoperation.

Diagnosis and monitoring of VTE

Patients were monitored clinically throughout hospitalization and followed for 30 days postoperatively. Evaluation for VTE was symptom-triggered. Clinical suspicion was based on signs such as limb swelling, calf tenderness, dyspnea, or chest discomfort.

Suspected deep vein thrombosis was assessed using compression Doppler ultrasonography. Suspected pulmonary embolism was confirmed using computed tomography pulmonary angiography. The timing of VTE diagnosis relative to surgery was recorded. As routine screening of asymptomatic patients was not performed, asymptomatic events may have been underdetected.

Statistical analysis

Data was entered into Microsoft Excel and analyzed using SPSS version 26.0 (IBM Corp., USA). Continuous variables were assessed for normality and expressed as mean ± standard deviation or median (interquartile range), as appropriate. Between-group comparisons were conducted using Student’s t-test or the Mann–Whitney U test. Categorical variables were presented as frequencies and percentages and compared using the chi-square test or Fisher’s exact test.

Variables demonstrating statistical significance in univariate analysis were entered into multivariate logistic regression to identify independent predictors of VTE. Odds ratios with 95% confidence intervals were calculated. Multicollinearity was evaluated using variance inflation factors.

Model discrimination was assessed using the area under the receiver operating characteristic curve, and calibration was evaluated using the Hosmer–Lemeshow goodness-of-fit test and calibration plots. Decision curve analysis was performed to assess clinical utility. To account for multiple comparisons, false discovery rate correction was applied.

Missing data (< 5% of the dataset) were handled using multiple imputation techniques. All statistical tests were two-tailed, and a p-value < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Reproducibility

To enhance transparency and reproducibility, anonymized datasets and statistical code (SPSS syntax and R scripts for regression and receiver operating characteristic analyses) will be made available upon reasonable request.

Results

Demographic and clinical characteristics

A total of 106 patients undergoing surgical treatment for lower limb fractures were included, of whom 21 (19.8%) developed postoperative VTE. Patients in the VTE group were significantly older than those without VTE (68.1 ± 10.8 vs. 59.5 ± 12.2 years, p = 0.004). Sex distribution did not differ between groups (p = 0.64). Hypertension was more prevalent among patients with VTE (57.1% vs. 30.6%, p = 0.02), while diabetes mellitus, smoking history, and cardiovascular disease were not significantly different between groups (all p > 0.05).

Femoral fractures were more frequent in the VTE group compared with the non-VTE group (57.1% vs. 34.1%, p = 0.05). No significant differences were observed in operative duration, type of anesthesia, or intraoperative blood loss. Postoperatively, patients who developed VTE had longer hospital stays (12.1 ± 3.8 vs. 9.2 ± 3.3 days, p = 0.002) and were more likely to experience delayed ambulation beyond three days (57.1% vs. 25.9%, p = 0.009).

Baseline demographic, clinical, and surgical characteristics of the study cohort are summarized in Table 1. The mean age of the overall sample was 61.3 ± 12.4 years, with patients who developed VTE being significantly older than those without VTE (68.1 ± 10.8 vs. 59.5 ± 12.2 years, p = 0.004). Although the male-to-female ratio was similar between groups (66.7% vs. 61.2%, p = 0.64), hypertension was notably more prevalent in the VTE group (57.1% vs. 30.6%, p = 0.02). Other comorbidities, including diabetes mellitus, smoking history, and cardiovascular disease, were more common in VTE patients but did not reach statistical significance. With respect to fracture characteristics, femoral fractures were more frequently associated with VTE events compared to tibial or ankle/fibular fractures (57.1% vs. 34.1%, p = 0.05). Surgical variables, including mean operative duration (126 ± 41 vs. 109 ± 37 min, p = 0.08), type of anaesthesia, and intraoperative blood loss, showed no statistically significant differences between groups. However, postoperative outcomes demonstrated clear disparities. Patients who developed VTE had significantly longer hospital stays (12.1 ± 3.8 vs. 9.2 ± 3.3 days, p = 0.002) and were more likely to experience delayed ambulation beyond three days post-surgery (57.1% vs. 25.9%, p = 0.009).

Inflammatory and immunological parameters

Postoperative inflammatory markers increased significantly, with peak levels observed on postoperative Day 3 (Table 2; Fig. 1). CRP, IL-6, TNF-α, NLR, and fibrinogen levels were significantly higher in patients who developed VTE compared with non-VTE patients, particularly on Day 3 (adjusted p < 0.05 for all). IL-10 increased postoperatively in both groups without significant between-group differences.

Hematologic indices demonstrated transient perioperative changes. WBC counts increased postoperatively and declined by Day 5, while hemoglobin levels decreased following surgery with partial recovery. Platelet counts and MPV did not differ significantly between groups. PLR and NLR peaked on Day 3, with significantly higher NLR values observed in VTE patients at postoperative timepoints (p < 0.05). Detailed biomarker values are presented in Table 2 and illustrated in Fig. 1. C-reactive protein (CRP) demonstrated a marked postoperative increase, rising from 15.1 mg/L (95% CI: 14.2–16.0) preoperatively to a peak of 26.8 mg/L (95% CI: 25.1–28.5) on Day 3, before partially declining to 19.4 mg/L (95% CI: 18.1–20.7) on Day 5. The difference between baseline and Day 5 remained significant after FDR correction (adjusted p = 0.008). Notably, CRP was consistently higher in patients who developed VTE, with significant between-group differences observed at Day 3 [29.7 vs. 25.9 mg/L, p = 0.004] and Day 5 [21.8 vs. 18.6 mg/L, p = 0.01]. A similar trajectory was observed for interleukin-6 (IL-6), which increased from 28.9 pg/mL (95% CI: 26.8–30.9) to 48.6 pg/mL (95% CI: 45.2–52.0) on Day 3, before declining to 36.1 pg/mL (95% CI: 34.0–38.2) on Day 5 (adjusted p = 0.012). IL-6 levels were significantly higher in the VTE group on Days 1 and 3 (p < 0.05 for both), highlighting its role as a mediator of the early systemic inflammatory response. Tumor necrosis factor-α (TNF-α) also rose significantly, from 19.3 pg/mL (95% CI: 18.1–20.5) to 31.5 pg/mL (95% CI: 29.4–33.7) on Day 3, then fell to 23.7 pg/mL (95% CI: 22.2–25.2) by Day 5 (adjusted p = 0.009), with VTE patients showing consistently higher values. Interestingly, the anti-inflammatory cytokine IL-10 also increased postoperatively, from 4.8 pg/mL (95% CI: 4.4–5.2) to 7.4 pg/mL (95% CI: 6.8–8.0) on Day 3, before stabilizing at 6.2 pg/mL (95% CI: 5.8–6.6) on Day 5 (adjusted p = 0.03).

Hematologic indices showed similar perioperative shifts. White blood cell (WBC) counts increased from 7.4 × 10⁹/L (95% CI: 7.0–7.8) to 10.3 × 10⁹/L (95% CI: 9.6–11.0) on Day 3, before declining to 8.2 × 10⁹/L (95% CI: 7.8–8.6) on Day 5 (adjusted p = 0.04). Hemoglobin fell significantly due to perioperative blood loss, from 12.7 g/dL (95% CI: 12.3–13.1) to 11.0 g/dL (95% CI: 10.7–11.3) on Day 3, with only partial recovery by Day 5 (11.4 g/dL, 95% CI: 11.1–11.7; adjusted p = 0.02). Platelet counts showed a non-significant increase from 275 × 10⁹/L to 290 × 10⁹/L (p = 0.18). Both the platelet-to-lymphocyte ratio (PLR) and neutrophil-to-lymphocyte ratio (NLR) peaked on Day 3, reflecting systemic stress and inflammatory activation. PLR rose from 152.6 (95% CI: 146.0–159.2) to 174.7 (95% CI: 166.0–183.4) on Day 3 before falling to 158.6 (95% CI: 152.0–165.2) on Day 5 (adjusted p = 0.03). NLR increased from 3.04 (95% CI: 2.8–3.3) to 3.61 (95% CI: 3.3–3.9) on Day 3, then decreased to 2.88 (95% CI: 2.6–3.2) on Day 5 (adjusted p = 0.02). Both indices were significantly higher among VTE patients at postoperative timepoints (p < 0.05). Mean platelet volume (MPV) remained stable throughout the perioperative period (9.4–9.5 fL, adjusted p = 0.52), suggesting no significant variation in platelet activation. Taken together, these findings confirm that the postoperative course is characterized by an early inflammatory surge with compensatory anti-inflammatory response, hematologic stress indicators, and changes in coagulation-related parameters. Importantly, CRP, IL-6, TNF-α, D-dimer, NLR, and fibrinogen consistently showed stronger elevations in VTE patients, suggesting potential predictive value for thrombotic events.

Coagulation and biochemical parameters

Table 3 presents the trends in coagulation and biochemical parameters observed preoperatively (Day 0), and on postoperative Days 3 and 5. D-dimer levels showed a significant increase from 0.42 ± 0.11 mg/L on Day 0 to 1.21 ± 0.38 mg/L on Day 3, followed by a partial decline to 0.88 ± 0.26 mg/L on Day 5, with the difference from baseline remaining statistically significant (p = 0.005) (Fig. 2). Fibrinogen levels also rose significantly from 298 ± 54 mg/dL preoperatively to 321 ± 64 mg/dL on Day 5 (p = 0.011), consistent with an acute phase response. LDH (p = 0.008), ferritin (p = 0.031), uric acid (p = 0.045), and BUN (p = 0.038) showed similar significant increases postoperatively. Serum albumin declined significantly from 3.8 ± 0.6 to 3.6 ± 0.4 g/dL by Day 5 (p = 0.049), possibly due to surgical stress and protein redistribution. In contrast, changes in prothrombin time (PT), activated partial thromboplastin time (aPTT), INR, total protein, and creatinine were not statistically significant (p > 0.05), suggesting that overall coagulation times and renal function remained stable during the early postoperative phase.

Surgical and postoperative parameters

The study cohort’s average operative time was 94.6 ± 23.5 min, with a range of 60 to 150 min (Table 4). The mean in this study falls within a safe clinical window, despite the fact that longer surgery times are typically thought to be a risk factor for VTE because they result in prolonged immobility and surgical trauma.

The intraoperative blood loss ranged from 100 to 500 mL, with an average of 223.4 ± 89.2 mL. Although this amount of blood loss is not significant for lower limb surgeries, it may nevertheless have an impact on fluid shifts, postoperative inflammatory reactions, and the requirement for transfusions—all of which are associated with an increased risk of thrombosis. The average postoperative time to first ambulation was 2.8 ± 1.1 days, with a minimum of 1 day and a maximum of 5 days. The length of hospital stay ranged from 5 to 16 days, with an average of 8.6 ± 2.7 days. Due to prolonged immobility and possibly undetected complications, extended hospitalization is a known risk factor for VTE. A sizable portion of patients took longer than usual to recover, which may indicate that complicated fractures or postoperative issues prolonged their hospital stay. In terms of anesthesia, 44.34% (n = 47) of cases involved general anesthesia, whereas 55.66% (n = 59) involved spinal anesthesia. Nine patients (8.49%) had surgical site infections, which is within the range of postoperative complications that are typical for orthopedic trauma surgery. These infections may increase the risk of thrombosis by exacerbating systemic inflammation. Reoperation was also necessary in 3 cases (2.83%), which is another factor that can postpone recovery and raise the risk of VTE. Reoperation is usually linked to either surgical failure or complications.

Comparison of VTE vs. non-VTE patients on key parameters (postoperative day 3)

On postoperative Day 3, patients who developed VTE had significantly higher levels of CRP, IL-6, D-dimer, NLR, and fibrinogen compared with non-VTE patients (all p < 0.05). Time to ambulation and length of hospital stay were also significantly longer in the VTE group (Table 5; Fig. 3).With a p-value of 0.013, the VTE group’s CRP levels were significantly higher (30.56 ± 7.91 mg/L) than those of the non-VTE group (25.89 ± 7.66 mg/L). This suggests that patients who experienced VTE had a more severe systemic inflammatory response. A prothrombotic state is known to be exacerbated by elevated CRP, which is also linked to elevated fibrinogen levels and platelet activation. Another important pro-inflammatory cytokine, IL-6, was also markedly higher in the VTE group (52.1 ± 11.7 pg/mL) than in the non-VTE group (47.3 ± 12.1 pg/mL) (p = 0.046). This reflects increased immune activation following surgery and supports the role of IL-6 as a biomarker and possible mediator of VTE. A well-known indicator of fibrin turnover and thrombosis, D-dimer levels were significantly higher in the VTE group (1.44 ± 0.40 mg/L) than in the non-VTE group (1.15 ± 0.33 mg/L), with high statistical significance (p = 0.004). The diagnostic and prognostic value of D-dimer in identifying patients at risk for VTE in the early postoperative phase is confirmed by this finding.

With a p-value of 0.018, the NLR, a measure of immune stress and systemic inflammation, was significantly higher in VTE patients (3.61 ± 1.11) than in non-VTE patients (2.92 ± 0.94). An imbalance between innate and adaptive immune responses that favors a thrombogenic and proinflammatory environment may be the cause of elevated NLR.

In line with a hypercoagulable state, fibrinogen levels were also significantly higher in the VTE group (362 ± 78 mg/dL) than in the non-VTE group (340 ± 68 mg/dL, p = 0.041). Higher fibrinogen, an acute-phase reactant and a crucial substrate in clot formation, reinforces the connection between inflammation and thrombosis. Functionally speaking, the VTE group’s time to ambulation was significantly longer (3.6 ± 0.9 days) than that of the non-VTE group (2.6 ± 1.0 days, p = 0.006). Early postoperative mobilization protocols are crucial because delayed mobilization increases the risk of VTE by prolonging venous stasis.

VTE either prolonged recovery or was a sign of a more complex perioperative course, as evidenced by the significantly longer length of hospital stay in the VTE group (10.2 ± 3.1 days) compared to non-VTE patients (8.2 ± 2.4 days, p = 0.009). Additionally, longer hospital stays put patients at greater risk for immobilization and procedures.

Multivariate logistic regression analysis and transparency

Multivariate logistic regression was performed to identify independent predictors of VTE among 106 patients, of whom 21 (19.8%) developed thromboembolic events. The limited number of events increases the risk of model overfitting; thus, results should be considered exploratory. Variables entered into the model included age, hypertension, femoral fracture site, delayed ambulation (> 3 days), postoperative D-dimer, CRP, IL-6, NLR, and fibrinogen. As shown in Table 6, older age (OR: 1.05 per year; 95% CI: 1.01–1.10; p = 0.02), hypertension (OR: 2.41; 95% CI: 1.01–5.76; p = 0.048), delayed ambulation (OR: 3.26; 95% CI: 1.22–8.70; p = 0.018), and elevated postoperative D-dimer (OR: 1.32 per µg/mL; 95% CI: 1.08–1.63; p = 0.006) were identified as independent predictors of VTE. CRP and IL-6 showed positive associations but did not remain significant after false discovery rate (FDR) correction. NLR and fibrinogen demonstrated modest trends without statistical significance. Multicollinearity diagnostics revealed all Variance Inflation Factor (VIF) values < 2.0, indicating no significant collinearity among predictors. Calibration was acceptable (Hosmer–Lemeshow χ² = 6.14, p = 0.52), and the calibration plot showed good agreement between predicted and observed risk, with slight underprediction in the highest-risk decile. Discrimination was fair, with the model achieving an area under the ROC curve (AUC) of 0.81 (95% CI: 0.72–0.90).

ROC curve analysis of postoperative markers for predicting VTE occurrence (day 3 values)

The diagnostic performance of key postoperative Day 3 parameters in predicting VTE is presented in Table 7, with corresponding ROC curves shown in Figs. 4 and 5. Among individual biomarkers, D-dimer demonstrated the highest discriminatory power with an AUC of 0.78 (95% CI: 0.69–0.87), optimal cut-off > 1.65 µg/mL, sensitivity of 76.2%, specificity of 71.8%, PPV of 43.8%, and NPV of 91.2 (p = 0.001). CRP also showed strong accuracy (AUC = 0.74, 95% CI: 0.64–0.84), with a cut-off > 20.5 mg/L yielding 71.4% sensitivity, 69.4% specificity, PPV 40.5%, and NPV 89.6 (p = 0.002). IL-6 achieved an AUC of 0.72 (95% CI: 0.62–0.82) with a threshold > 42 pg/mL, balanced sensitivity and specificity around 67%, and NPV of 87.4 (p = 0.003). Both fibrinogen (AUC = 0.70, 95% CI: 0.60–0.80) and NLR (AUC = 0.68, 95% CI: 0.58–0.78) demonstrated moderate predictive performance, with sensitivities and specificities above 60%. Notably, the clinical parameter of delayed ambulation (> 3 days) also emerged as a significant predictor, with an AUC of 0.66 (95% CI: 0.55–0.77), sensitivity 57.1%, specificity 68.2%, and p = 0.021, reinforcing the importance of early mobilization in postoperative care.

When biomarkers were combined into a multivariable predictive model (D-dimer, CRP, IL-6, fibrinogen, and NLR), the AUC improved to 0.86 (95% CI: 0.78–0.94), with sensitivity of 81.0%, specificity of 79.5%, PPV of 55.3%, and NPV of 93.2. Net Reclassification Improvement (NRI) analysis confirmed that the combined model significantly outperformed D-dimer alone, reclassifying 23.8% of patients into more appropriate risk categories (p = 0.01). Decision Curve Analysis (DCA) demonstrated that the combined model offered higher net clinical benefit across threshold probabilities of 10–50% compared with single biomarker use. It should be noted that the present analysis is constrained by several factors. First, the relatively small number of VTE events observed (n = 21) limits the statistical power of regression modeling and increases the risk of overfitting, despite attempts to mitigate this through careful variable selection and multicollinearity checks. Second, VTE detection in this study relied on symptom-triggered imaging rather than routine protocolized screening, which may have led to underestimation of true event rates and introduced detection bias. Finally, while the regression and predictive models demonstrated good discrimination and calibration, these results should be regarded as exploratory and hypothesis-generating. External validation in larger, prospectively designed cohorts with standardized screening protocols will be essential to confirm the reproducibility and clinical applicability of these findings.

Timing of VTE diagnosis

Although VTE events were documented during hospitalization and within 30 days postoperatively, the precise timing of biomarker peak values relative to confirmed VTE diagnosis was not systematically recorded for all patients. Consequently, exact temporal relationships between laboratory marker elevations and VTE onset could not be fully evaluated. Most clinically suspected events occurred within the early postoperative period; however, routine protocolized timing analysis was not performed.

Discussion

Major findings

In this retrospective cohort study of patients undergoing surgical treatment for lower limb fractures, we evaluated perioperative inflammatory, hematologic, and coagulation biomarkers alongside clinical recovery parameters to determine their association with postoperative venous thromboembolism (VTE). The overall incidence of VTE was 19.8%. Multivariate analysis demonstrated that advanced age, hypertension, delayed ambulation, and elevated postoperative D-dimer levels were independent predictors of VTE. Among laboratory biomarkers, D-dimer showed the strongest individual discriminatory performance, whereas inflammatory markers including CRP and IL-6 were significantly elevated in VTE patients but did not remain independent predictors after adjustment. Importantly, a combined predictive model integrating inflammatory biomarkers with clinical recovery parameters achieved superior predictive accuracy (AUC 0.86) compared with individual markers alone. These findings suggest that integrating dynamic inflammatory and coagulation biomarkers with clinical recovery indicators may improve perioperative VTE risk stratification in orthopedic trauma patients.

D-dimer and coagulation markers

Our findings support the role of D-dimer as a key biomarker for postoperative VTE detection. Patients who developed VTE demonstrated significantly higher postoperative D-dimer levels, and D-dimer emerged as an independent predictor in multivariate analysis. Similar findings have been reported in previous studies of orthopedic trauma populations. For example, Chen et al. reported that elevated D-dimer levels were strongly associated with postoperative deep vein thrombosis in patients with lower extremity fractures, highlighting its utility as an early screening marker [6]. Likewise, other previous studies demonstrated that postoperative D-dimer monitoring improves early detection of thrombotic events in trauma patients [30–33].

However, despite its high sensitivity, D-dimer has limited specificity in postoperative settings due to physiological fibrinolysis triggered by surgical trauma and tissue injury. Previous investigations have therefore suggested combining D-dimer with additional laboratory markers to improve predictive accuracy [34–36]. Our results support this approach, as the combined biomarker model significantly outperformed D-dimer alone in ROC analysis.

Inflammatory biomarkers and immunothrombosis

Postoperative inflammatory activation appears to play a critical role in thrombus formation following orthopedic trauma [37]. In the present study, CRP and IL-6 levels were significantly higher in patients who developed VTE, particularly on postoperative Day 3. These findings support the concept of immunothrombosis, in which inflammatory mediators promote endothelial dysfunction, platelet activation, and coagulation cascade amplification.

Similarly, elevated CRP and IL-6 have been described as predictors of adverse thrombo-inflammatory events in trauma and surgical patients, aligning with our observations of their strong association with postoperative VTE [38–42]. Previous studies have also demonstrated that IL-6 may promote thrombosis by stimulating hepatic fibrinogen synthesis and enhancing tissue factor expression in endothelial cells [42].

Other studies have reported comparable results. For instance, Ma et al. identified CRP and inflammatory markers as significant predictors of thrombosis in patients with lower limb fractures, emphasizing the link between systemic inflammation and thrombotic complications [5]. While these markers did not remain independent predictors in our adjusted regression model, their consistent perioperative elevation suggests that they contribute to the prothrombotic inflammatory environment observed after major orthopedic trauma.

Hematologic indices

Hematologic indices reflecting systemic inflammatory stress also showed associations with thrombotic risk. In our cohort, the neutrophil-to-lymphocyte ratio (NLR) was significantly elevated in VTE patients during the early postoperative period. Elevated NLR reflects neutrophil activation and relative lymphocyte suppression, both of which are associated with inflammatory and prothrombotic states.

Previous studies have reported similar associations between NLR and thrombotic events in trauma patients. For example, other previous studies demonstrated that inflammatory hematologic indices may provide useful adjunctive information for predicting thrombosis risk in fracture patients [10, 43]. Although NLR did not remain an independent predictor in multivariate analysis, its moderate predictive performance in ROC analysis suggests that it may contribute to risk stratification when combined with other biomarkers.

Integration of clinical and laboratory predictors

An important finding of this study is the strong predictive contribution of delayed postoperative ambulation. Patients who began ambulation more than three days after surgery had a significantly higher risk of VTE. Prolonged immobilization is a well-recognized contributor to venous stasis and thrombogenesis, particularly following major orthopedic trauma.

Few biomarker-focused studies have quantitatively incorporated functional recovery parameters such as ambulation timing into predictive models [44–47]. Our findings demonstrate that combining clinical recovery indicators with laboratory biomarkers substantially improves predictive performance, as reflected by the higher AUC of the integrated model (0.86). These results highlight the importance of incorporating both biological and clinical factors into risk prediction frameworks.

Comparison with current risk assessment tools

Current clinical practice commonly relies on risk assessment models such as the Caprini score to guide thromboprophylaxis strategies. While Caprini remains a validated risk assessment tool, its lack of biomarker integration and limited granularity in trauma populations constrains predictive accuracy [48–51]. These scoring systems were largely developed for general surgical or medical patients and may not fully capture the complex inflammatory and coagulation responses associated with orthopedic trauma [51].

Our findings suggest that biomarker-based predictive frameworks may complement existing risk assessment tools by incorporating dynamic perioperative changes in inflammatory and coagulation markers. Integrating such markers into clinical decision-making may help identify patients at particularly high risk for VTE and enable more individualized prophylactic strategies.

Strengths of the study

Several methodological strengths enhance the credibility of our findings, including standardized biomarker measurement, structured thromboprophylaxis protocols, and the use of comprehensive statistical modeling. Second, biomarkers were measured at multiple perioperative time points, allowing evaluation of dynamic biomarker trajectories rather than single static measurements. Third, rigorous statistical methods were employed, including multivariate regression modeling, false discovery rate correction, and evaluation of model discrimination and calibration. Together, these measures enhance transparency and translational value as reported by previous authors.

Limitations

This study also has several limitations that should be acknowledged. The retrospective single-center design may limit generalizability to other populations and healthcare settings. Additionally, VTE diagnosis was based on symptom-triggered imaging rather than routine screening, which may have resulted in underestimation of asymptomatic events. The relatively small number of VTE cases (n = 21) also increases the risk of model overfitting and limits statistical power. Furthermore, the predictive model was not externally validated, and therefore the findings should be considered exploratory. Future prospective multicenter studies with standardized screening protocols and larger sample sizes are necessary to validate the predictive performance and clinical utility of integrated biomarker-based risk models.

Conclusion

This study demonstrates that perioperative inflammatory and coagulation biomarkers, when integrated with clinical recovery metrics, may improve prediction of VTE in orthopedic trauma patients. D-dimer remains the strongest individual marker, but combining it with CRP, IL-6, fibrinogen, and NLR alongside functional parameters such as ambulation delay significantly improves predictive accuracy and calibration. Although limited by small event numbers and symptom-triggered ascertainment, the findings highlight the translational potential of biomarker–clinical integration. Developing and externally validating trauma-specific predictive nomograms should be prioritized to achieve individualized prophylaxis and improve postoperative outcomes.
