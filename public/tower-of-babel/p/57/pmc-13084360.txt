Combined Use of Pharmacological Treatment and Noninvasive Brain Stimulation for Smoking Cessation in Adult Smokers: A Systematic Review
Authors: Julia Díaz-Miravalls, Fátima Lorena Merchán-Molero, Ana Yarritu-Sánchez, Miguel Jiménez-Gómez, José Ignacio De-Granda-Orive
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractNon-invasive brain stimulation (NIBS) is emerging as a promising treatment for nicotine dependence. Several systematic reviews discuss the effectiveness of repetitive transcranial magnetic stimulation (rTMS) as monotherapy for smoking cessation; however, its effectiveness in combination with conventional pharmacotherapy has not been well studied.We performed a systematic review of randomized controlled trials (RCTs) that investigated the efficacy of a combination of NIBS and conventional pharmacotherapy for smoking cessation. The primary outcomes were smoking abstinence rates. Secondary outcomes were craving, withdrawal symptoms and adverse events.We included 2 RCTs with a total of 79 smokers. At week 12, abstinence rates were 82.4% vs. 30.7% in Ibrahim et al., who combined rTMS/sham stimulation respectively with varenicline, and 27.7% vs. 27.7% in Trojak et al., who combined rTMS/sham stimulation with nicotine replacement treatment (NRT). The latter group reported that continuous abstinence was significantly higher in the active group (p = 0.027) only at week 2. Active rTMS led to a significant reduction in the FTCQ-12 compulsive craving dimension (p = 0.011). Neither study reported serious adverse events related to the interventions.Adjunctive rTMS improves short-term smoking abstinence and reduces craving when combined with pharmacotherapy compared with pharmacotherapy alone, with good tolerability.Keywords: Tobacco cessation, Smoking cessation, Non-invasive brain stimulation, Transcranial magnetic stimulation, Varenicline, Nicotine replacement treatment

Introduction

Smoking is responsible for about 8.7 million annual deaths, so cessation is one of the most cost-effective public health interventions.1

According to data from the 2024 EDADES survey (Survey on Alcohol and Other Drugs in Spain), the prevalence of tobacco use among individuals aged 15–64 was consistently higher in men than in women. Past-year consumption reached 40.9% in men compared with 32.7% in women, while past-30-day use was 28.9% and 22.6%, respectively. Notably, 67.7% of daily smokers reported a desire to quit.2 However, each smoker requires an average of 6 attempts before achieving successful cessation,3 the difficulty being rooted in nicotine addiction and withdrawal syndrome.

Nicotine dependence is based on a complex neurobiological interaction involving activation of the mesolimbic dopaminergic system, particularly neurons in the ventral tegmental area (VTA) projecting to the accumbens nucleus (NAc). This pathway plays a central role in positive reinforcement and reward-related learning. Nicotine triggers multiple nicotinic acetylcholine receptor subtypes (nAChRs), with those containing the β2 subunit being particularly relevant due to their high affinity. Nicotine binding to these receptors depolarizes dopaminergic neurons and increases their activity, leading to enhanced dopamine release in the NAc and the pleasurable sensations associated with smoking. But nicotine not only activates dopaminergic neurons, it also modulates the activity of GABAergic and glutamatergic interneurons through different nAChRs subtypes: it enhances GABAergic inhibition and increases activation of presynaptic α7 receptors on glutamatergic terminals. This combination of reduced inhibition and enhanced excitation is crucial in the establishment of addictive behavior. Beyond its acute effects, chronic nicotine exposure induces upregulation of nAChRs, potentiation of glutamatergic synapses, alterations in GABAergic signaling, homeostatic dysfunctions, epigenetic changes, and modifications in the expression of dopaminergic receptors such as D2 in the NAc. After cessation, these adaptations contribute to withdrawal symptoms, characterized by affective and somatic manifestations.4, 5, 6, 7

The understanding of these mechanisms has driven the development of therapeutic strategies aimed at modulating reward system activity and restoring the functional balance of frontostriatal circuits. Currently, the main therapeutic tools available to increase long-term abstinence rates include behavioral interventions and pharmacotherapy (varenicline, cytisinicline, bupropion, and nicotine replacement therapy). The combination of these approaches demonstrates superior efficacy compared to monotherapy (15.6% vs. 8.6%). According to the randomized, double-blind EAGLES trial,8 abstinence rates were significantly higher with varenicline (21.8%), bupropion (16.2%) and nicotine patches (15.7%) compared with placebo (9.4%). Furthermore, recent studies suggest that combining agents with different mechanisms of action may constitute the most effective approach for maintaining 6-month abstinence.9 Nevertheless, given the magnitude of the public health burden, it remains a priority to investigate novel therapeutic approaches capable of achieving higher success rates.

In this context, non-invasive brain stimulation (NIBS) has emerged as a potential therapeutic intervention to modulate the activity of neural networks implicated in addiction. Among its modalities, the most widely studied is repetitive transcranial magnetic stimulation (rTMS), which uses magnetic fields to induce electrical currents in the cerebral cortex. Its effects primarily depend on stimulation frequency: low-frequency stimulation (≤1 Hz) exerts an inhibitory effect, whereas high-frequency stimulation (≥5 Hz) is excitatory. Although it acts directly on the targeted region—typically the dorsolateral prefrontal cortex (DLPFC)—it can also influence connected subcortical areas, such as the limbic system, and modulate dopaminergic and GABAergic neurotransmission.10 In a crossover study, Johann et al. (2003)11 demonstrated a significant reduction in craving and cigarette consumption within 6 h post-treatment. Similarly, Amiaz et al. (2009)12 evaluated the effect of rTMS applied to the left DLPFC over 10 consecutive days, observing both objective and subjective reductions in craving, as well as decreased cigarette consumption over a 6-month follow-up period.

Several systematic reviews have examined the efficacy of NIBS techniques for smoking cessation. Results have been heterogeneous: a meta-analysis by Kang et al. (2013)13 reported a reduction in dependence symptoms when stimulating the right DLPFC, although no hemispheric differences were observed regarding cigarette consumption, while Fecteau et al. (2014)14 observed a significant decrease in the number of cigarettes smoked. However, not all studies report positive outcomes; for instance, Falcone et al. (2016)15 conducted a randomized, placebo-controlled trial and found no significant benefits for transcranial direct current stimulation in smoking cessation.

The goals of the study were to determine whether the combined approach of pharmacotherapy for cessation and NIBS increases abstinence rates compared with pharmacotherapy intervention alone, to evaluate the impact of the combined approach on relapse rates and treatment adherence, and to examine the safety profile and adverse effects associated with combined therapy.

Material and methods

Study design

A systematic review of the literature was conducted in accordance with the PRISMA 2020 statement16 (Table II). The review protocol was prospectively registered in PROSPERO (CRD420251134593). Research question was structured using the PICO framework (Population, Intervention, Comparator, and Outcomes)

Study population

We included studies of adults (≥18 years) who were active smokers with nicotine dependence and motivated to quit smoking. Participants from any setting (outpatient, inpatient, community-based interventions) were eligible.

We excluded studies conducted in animals, adolescents, or in participants with other substance use disorders in which smoking cessation was not the primary outcome. Former smokers with any concurrent addiction were also excluded.

Intervention

Combination of rTMS with any approved pharmacological treatment for nicotine dependence (e.g., varenicline, cytisinicline or nicotine replacement treatment).

Comparators

The comparator was pharmacotherapy alone, against which the combined approach of pharmacotherapy and rTMS was evaluated.

Outcomes

Primary outcomes: smoking abstinence rates in the short term (≤3 months), medium term (3–6 months), and long term (≥6 months).

Secondary outcomes: relapse rates, treatment adherence, craving and withdrawal symptoms, and adverse events associated with the interventions. Patient-reported outcomes and quality of life measures.

Search strategy

A comprehensive literature search was conducted in 5 major databases – Web of Science, Scopus, Pubmed, Embase, Proquest – to identify relevant studies published without time limitation since all the studies were published from 2003 onwards. The search date was May 28, 2025.

Search protocol was: “(“Smok* cessation*” OR “Tobacco cessation*” OR “Tobacco use* cessation*” OR “Stop* Smok*” OR “Giving Up Smoking” OR “Giving smoking” OR “Quitting smok*”) AND (“magnetic* brain* stimulation*” OR “magnetic* transcran* stimulation*” OR “Noninvasiv* brain* stimulation*” OR “Non-invasiv* brain* stimulation*” OR “transcran* current* brain* stimulation*” OR “transcran* current* stimulation*” OR “transcran* direct* current* brain* stimulation*” OR “transcran* direct* current* stimulation*” OR “transcran* electric and magnetic* stimulation*” OR “transcran* electric* stimulation*” OR “transcran* electrother* stimulation*” OR “transcran* magnetic* stimulation*” OR “transcran* brain* stimulation*” OR “trans-cran* magnetic* stimulation*” OR “transcran* static* magnetic* stimulation*”).

All published trials evaluating NIBS combined with pharmacological therapy for smoking cessation were considered for inclusion. Randomized controlled trials using parallel-group or crossover design and clinical letters were included. Preprints or inaccessible full texts were excluded. Publication languages were limited to English or Spanish. No time or study design filters were applied in the search strategy.

The search results were exported to an Access database using our own Bibliometrics software, where duplicates were manually eliminated.

Subsequently, a screening process by title and abstract was carried out independently by 2 reviewers (JDM, FLM). Studies that did not meet the inclusion criteria were excluded at this stage. The full text of potentially eligible records was assessed to determine their final inclusion in the review. Any discrepancies between the reviewers were resolved by intervention of a third reviewer (MJG), and those that were deemed not valid from the full-text analysis were excluded. Only 2 studies met the inclusion criteria, reflecting the current scarcity of evidence on combined pharmacological and non-invasive brain stimulation strategies for smoking cessation rather than limitations of the search strategy. Since only 2 highly heterogeneous RCTs were eligible, a formal risk-of-bias assessment would have provided only limited interpretability and was therefore not performed.

Results

After the search for articles was carried out in the different databases, 613 studies met the selection criteria in accordance with the PRISMA statement. Duplicate studies were eliminated, and 167 articles were identified for manual review (Fig. 1). Two studies were finally chosen and included in the systematic review (Table 1).

Characteristics of the studies

Two randomized, double-blind, sham (placebo)-controlled clinical trials met the inclusion criteria and were included in this systematic review. Both trials evaluated the efficacy and safety of combining rTMS with approved pharmacological therapies for tobacco dependence.

The study by Trojak et al. (2015)19 was conducted in France and assessed the combination of high-frequency rTMS over the left dorsolateral prefrontal cortex (DLPFC) and nicotine replacement therapy (NRT). The study by Ibrahim et al. (2023),21 conducted in Canada, examined the combined use of rTMS targeting the insular cortex with varenicline treatment. Both trials used sham stimulation as the control condition and measured abstinence rates as the primary endpoint, confirmed by biochemical verification (expired carbon monoxide (CO) < 10 ppm or serum cotinine < 15 ng/ml).

Population

The 2 included trials enrolled a total of 79 adult smokers motivated to quit. In Trojak et al., 19 37 participants aged 18–65 years with severe nicotine dependence (Fagerström ≥7) and at least 2 prior unsuccessful cessation attempts were included. In Ibrahim et al. (21), 42 participants aged 18–65 years with moderate-to-high nicotine dependence (Fagerström ≥4), smoking ≥10 cigarettes/day, and CO ≥10 ppm were recruited.

Participants in both studies exhibited high motivation to quit, as assessed by validated readiness-to-change scales.

Intervention

In the study of Trojak et al.,19 participants received 10 sessions of high-frequency rTMS (1 Hz; left DLPFC) administered over 2 consecutive weeks in the intervention group (n = 18) vs sham (n = 19) in the control group, concurrently with transdermal NRT (21 mg/24 h). After the rTMS phase, NRT continued alone for 4 weeks with progressive dose tapering (14 mg/24 h for 2 weeks, then 7 mg/24 h for 2 weeks).

The sham-controlled trial study of Ibrahim et al.21 recruited 42 participants, randomized to receive either active (n = 24) or sham (n = 18) high-frequency rTMS directed to the insula. Participants received 20 sessions of high-frequency rTMS (10 Hz) targeting the insula, administered daily (5 days/week for 4 weeks), combined with varenicline following the standard 12-week dosing regimen (0.5 mg tablet once daily for the first 3 days, 0.5 mg twice a day for the next 4 days, and then 1 mg twice a day for the rest of the treatment course).

Outcomes

At week 12, active treatment vs sham treatment abstinence rates were 82.4% vs. 30.7% in Ibrahim et al.21 and 27.7% vs. 27.7% in Trojak et al.19

In the study by Trojak et al., follow-up assessments were conducted at weeks 2, 6, and 12. Continuous abstinence at week 2 was significantly higher in the active group than in the sham group (p = 0.027). No statistically significant differences were observed at weeks 6 or 12. Active rTMS led to a significant reduction in the compulsive craving dimension of the FTCQ-12 (p = 0.011), although this effect was not sustained beyond the treatment period.

In the study by Ibrahim et al., follow-up extended to 26 weeks, with assessments at weeks 4, 12, and 26. Continuous abstinence rates were significantly higher in the active group than in the sham group at week 12 (82.4% vs. 30.7%, p = 0.013). No significant differences were found at week 4 (66.8% vs. 64.8%) or week 26 (25.9% vs. 30.7%). Both groups showed progressive reductions in nicotine dependence and craving scores over time, although the time × treatment interaction was not statistically significant. Withdrawal symptoms peaked at week 2 in both groups and subsequently declined, with a more stable reduction being observed in the active rTMS group. The number of cigarettes smoked and exhaled CO levels decreased significantly over time in both groups, but no between-group differences were observed.

Adverse events

Neither studies reported serious adverse events related to the interventions. Minor side effects were mild and transient, typically including scalp discomfort, headache, nausea, and vivid dreams. These effects did not result in treatment discontinuation.

In Ibrahim et al.,21 nausea occurred in 33.3% of the active group versus 16.7% in the sham group; headaches were more frequent in the sham group (33.3% vs. 16.7%), but differences between groups were not statistically significant.

Discussion

Most studies to date have focused on evaluating rTMS as a standalone intervention. Earlier meta-analyses of standalone rTMS for smoking cessation have shown modest benefits with heterogeneous protocols. Benjamin Petit et al.17 performed a systematic review and metanalysis in 2022 to assess the effectiveness of NIBS (monotherapy) for long-term smoking cessation. The RR of sustained abstinence of NIBS compared to sham was 2.39 [95% CI = 1.26–4.55; I2 = 40%]. More recently, in 2025, Xue Shang et al.18 conducted another systematic review and meta-analysis evaluating the efficacy and safety of transcranial magnetic stimulation (TMS) for smoking cessation. They analyzed data from 967 smokers and found that participants who received rTMS showed a significant reduction in the number of cigarettes smoked [mean absolute difference = −6.72; 95% CI (−9.47, −3.97)].

Unlike previous systematic reviews focusing on TMS monotherapy, this review addresses the specific and clinically relevant question of whether combining TMS with pharmacological treatment provides incremental benefit beyond pharmacotherapy alone, and explores the potential additive or synergistic effects of a combined therapeutic strategy. Trials assessing the synergistic effects of NIBS with approved pharmacological agents such as varenicline, cytisinicline or NRT are still few in number, reflecting the early stage of clinical research in this field. This evidence gap underscores the need for further high-quality clinical studies to confirm the potential additive or synergistic benefits of combining rTMS with standard pharmacological treatments in tobacco dependence.

Clinically, adjunctive rTMS may represent a feasible, non-invasive option for smokers who fail conventional pharmacotherapy.19 The rationale for combining rTMS with pharmacotherapy lies in their complementary mechanisms of action within the neurobiological framework of nicotine addiction. Pharmacological agents primarily target nicotinic acetylcholine receptors (nAChRs).4 In contrast, rTMS acts by modulating cortical excitability and enhancing neuroplasticity in brain regions implicated in executive control, craving regulation, and reward processing. High-frequency rTMS over the left DLPFC may normalize hypoactivity in cortical regions associated with inhibitory control and diminish cue-reactivity in the nucleus accumbens.20 When combined, these interventions may produce additive or even synergistic effects. Specifically, rTMS appears to potentiate the therapeutic effects of pharmacotherapy (both nicotine replacement therapy and varenicline) by modulating neural circuits involved in craving and reward processing, particularly within the prefrontal cortex.19, 21

In this systematic review, we analyzed the evidence of combination of rTMS and conventional pharmacotherapy to quit smoking. Only 2 randomized controlled trials have been conducted combining rTMS with conventional pharmacotherapy to quit smoking.19, 21 A critical aspect in interpreting the present evidence is the marked heterogeneity between these 2 included randomized controlled trials, which likely accounts for the divergent outcomes observed. The most salient differences relate to the stimulation target, stimulation parameters, treatment schedule, and pharmacological backbone, all of which are known to influence the efficacy of rTMS in addiction-related interventions.

Despite the promising results, both studies were limited by small sample sizes, relatively short follow-up durations, single-center designs and variability in stimulation parameters and brain regions. The heterogeneity and the limited number of eligible studies precluded both a formal meta-analysis and subanalysis of predictors of treatment response. These factors restrict the generalizability of the findings.

Nonetheless, the consistency in improved abstinence outcomes across both trials provides preliminary evidence that rTMS may serve as a valuable adjunctive tool in tobacco dependence treatment. Future large-scale, multicenter trials with standardized rTMS protocols (including frequency, intensity, site) and longer observation periods (≥6 months) are warranted to validate these results and clarify the optimal parameters for clinical application and assess cost-effectiveness for integration into smoking cessation programs.

The most recent systematic review on the use of TMS for smoking cessation was published in 2025 by Shang et al. in General Hospital Psychiatry. This review included 15 randomized controlled trials involving 967 smokers and found that rTMS significantly increased smoking cessation rates (odds ratio 2.77; moderate-quality evidence), reduced daily cigarette consumption, and lowered nicotine dependence scores. Additionally, higher stimulation frequency (10 Hz), a greater number of treatment sessions (≥20), and specific population characteristics—such as adults with tobacco use disorder who had failed previous quit attempts and those with comorbid schizophrenia—were associated with improved outcomes.22

Regarding the clinical applicability of TMS, this technique is approved by the FDA and is widely used in the treatment of other psychiatric disorders. Based on the available literature, including the findings of this present review, TMS could be considered as an adjunctive strategy to pharmacotherapy for smoking cessation in individuals with at least 2 unsuccessful quit attempts despite optimized pharmacological treatment.

Conclusion

The evidence base is currently insufficient to draw firm conclusions. This systematic review, while preliminary, suggests that adjunctive rTMS may enhance the efficacy of first-line pharmacotherapies for smoking cessation. Adjunctive rTMS improves short-term smoking abstinence and reduces craving when combined with pharmacotherapy compared with pharmacotherapy alone, with good tolerability and without significant adverse effects. Larger multicenter trials are needed to define standardized stimulation protocols and to assess long-term relapse prevention.
