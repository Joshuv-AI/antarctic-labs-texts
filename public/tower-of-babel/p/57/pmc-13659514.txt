Emergency remote robotic right hepatectomy for spontaneous rupture of hepatocellular carcinoma
Authors: Fengwei Gao, Zhigui Li, Xin Zhao, Qingyun Xie, Zongtao Song, Bingran Zhang, Xiang Zhu, Zhenghao Su, Danzeng Laba, Taqing Suoba, Xiaoou Li, Wei Yuan, Jun Ma, Mengsong Wang, Tian Lan, Yaowen Jiang, Nian Li, Yunshi Cai, Jiayin Yang, Hong Wu
Published: 2026

Remote robotic surgery has emerged as a frontier of surgical innovation. Since the first transatlantic telesurgical procedure in 2001, advances in 5G networks, dedicated optical fiber transmission, and robotic teleoperation platforms have broadened the clinical reach of telesurgery, including applications in urology, hepatobiliary surgery, and pancreatic surgery (1,2). In a randomized controlled trial of 72 patients, Wang et al. showed that telesurgery was noninferior to local robotic surgery for urologic procedures (3). In hepatobiliary surgery, a prospective single-arm study of six patients demonstrated the feasibility and safety of remote robotic hepatectomy (4). Recently, Fan et al. reported five cases of remote hepatobiliary and pancreatic surgery, and Li et al. described two cases of remote robot-assisted day-case liver surgery (5,6).

Spontaneous rupture of hepatocellular carcinoma (HCC) is a life-threatening surgical emergency, occurring in approximately 3% to 15% of patients with HCC and in up to 10% to 15% of patients in some Asian regions. Reported acute-phase mortality ranges from 25% to 75% (7). Management focuses on prompt resuscitation and hemostasis, followed by individualized treatment according to hemodynamic status, hepatic functional reserve, and tumor resectability. Systematic reviews, meta-analyses, and recent retrospective studies generally support staged hepatectomy after hemostasis by transarterial embolization (TAE) or transarterial chemoembolization (TACE), a strategy associated with lower in-hospital mortality, reduced intraoperative blood loss, fewer transfusions, and possible long-term survival benefit (8). Therefore, when interventional radiology (IR) resources are immediately available, initial transarterial hemostasis followed by reassessment and delayed hepatectomy remains the preferred strategy for many patients with ruptured HCC. Emergency upfront hepatectomy should be reserved for highly selected patients who can be stabilized, have preserved hepatic reserve, and have technically resectable localized disease. For selected patients who stabilize after resuscitation, have localized disease, and retain adequate hepatic reserve, however, emergency hepatectomy can provide both definitive hemostasis and oncologic clearance, with survival outcomes that are not inferior to those achieved with interventional approaches (9).

Here, we describe what is, to our knowledge, the first emergency remote robotic right hepatectomy performed in a high-altitude region for hemorrhage caused by spontaneous rupture of HCC. Previous reports of remote hepatectomy have largely involved elective surgery. Ruptured HCC in an emergency setting introduces additional technical and logistical risks, including active intraperitoneal bleeding, time-sensitive decision-making, network-dependent operative control, and the need for immediate patient-side rescue. Accordingly, this case should be viewed as an exceptional, highly selected application of telesurgery rather than as evidence that emergency remote robotic hepatectomy should replace established staged or centralized pathways for ruptured HCC.

A 56-year-old woman was admitted with intermittent right upper abdominal pain of more than 1 year’s duration, which had worsened during the preceding 15 days. She had a long-standing history of hepatitis B virus (HBV) infection. On admission, her blood pressure was 108/64 mmHg, heart rate 68 beats/min, hemoglobin 78 g/L, platelet count 478×109/L, prothrombin time 11.6 seconds, albumin 41.6 g/L, total bilirubin 6.4 μmol/L, alanine aminotransferase 31.3 U/L, and aspartate aminotransferase 22.4 U/L. Liver function was Child-Pugh class A. HBV DNA was 6.67×101 IU/mL, and alpha-fetoprotein was 4,476.3 ng/mL. Admission contrast-enhanced computed tomography (CT) demonstrated a 9.5 cm × 7.6 cm mass in hepatic segments 5 and 6, suspicious for HCC and associated with intratumoral hemorrhage (Figure 1A). At that time, the patient had no hemodynamic instability or clinically evident hemoperitoneum. Prophylactic transarterial intervention, transfer to a tertiary hepatobiliary center, and elective resection after further optimization were potential management options. Because the patient was initially stable and no local IR specialist was available, the local team proceeded with close monitoring and remote specialist consultation. In retrospect, when feasible, early transfer to a center with both IR hemostasis and advanced hepatobiliary rescue capability should be considered for similar patients before rupture occurs.

One week after admission, the patient experienced abrupt worsening of abdominal pain. Diagnostic abdominal puncture yielded nonclotting blood. Repeat laboratory testing showed a hemoglobin level of 57 g/L; blood pressure was 101/62 mmHg and heart rate 75 beats/min, consistent with suspected spontaneous HCC rupture and hemoperitoneum. After rapid fluid resuscitation and anesthetic evaluation, hemodynamic stability was achieved. Remote expert consultation determined that the tumor was confined to the right liver, liver function remained Child-Pugh class A, and no definite distant metastasis was evident. At this point, staged treatment with emergency TAE/TACE followed by delayed hepatectomy was again considered the standard reference strategy; however, the high-altitude center lacked immediate IR capacity, and urgent transfer was judged less favorable after active rupture had occurred. The decision for immediate definitive resection was therefore made only after stabilization and multidisciplinary risk assessment. The patient was therefore considered suitable for emergency right hepatectomy with curative intent. The local high-altitude center had neither an experienced hepatobiliary surgeon able to perform complex hepatectomy nor an interventional specialist available for emergency hemostasis; however, it had an established bedside surgical team and reliable telesurgical communication infrastructure. Emergency remote robotic right hepatectomy was therefore performed. Although the tumor was centered in segments 5 and 6, right hepatectomy rather than isolated segment 5/6 resection was selected because of the large tumor size, active rupture, need for rapid inflow control, and concern that a parenchyma-sparing emergency resection would require more complex transection around bleeding tumor-bearing liver with potentially higher risk of uncontrolled hemorrhage and compromised oncologic margins. In a staged setting after successful hemostasis and reassessment, a more limited segmental or non-anatomic resection may be appropriate for selected patients to preserve functional liver volume. This study was approved by the Ethics Committee of West China Hospital, Sichuan University (ethics approval No. 20261164). Written informed consent was obtained from the patient for publication of this article and accompanying images. A copy of the written consent is available for review by the editorial office of this journal.

The operation was performed on April 8, 2026, using the domestically developed Toumai MT-400 robotic system. The remote surgeon operated from the master console in Chengdu (approximately 500 m above sea level), whereas the patient-side operating room was located in Lhasa (approximately 3,600 m above sea level) (Figure 1B). The straight-line physical distance between the two sites was approximately 1,250 km, and the network transmission path was approximately 2,200 km. A local open surgical team remained on standby throughout the procedure. The bedside team included surgeons experienced in emergency abdominal laparotomy, exposure, packing, suction, vascular compression, and conversion to open surgery, as well as anesthesiologists and nursing staff prepared for rapid transfusion and resuscitation; however, the team was not equivalent to an advanced hepatopancreatobiliary (HPB) unit capable of independently performing elective complex hepatectomy. The remote HPB surgeon was designated as the operative leader throughout the procedure.

Before incision, a written bailout plan was confirmed. In the event of robotic-system failure, network interruption, gas embolism, major hepatic venous or inferior vena cava (IVC) injury, or uncontrollable hemorrhage, the protocol required immediate cessation of robotic dissection, rapid undocking, conversion to open laparotomy, manual compression or packing, Pringle inflow control when feasible, activation of massive transfusion, and remote expert guidance for damage-control hemostasis. If definitive vascular control could not be safely achieved locally, the priority would have been temporization and transfer or staged management rather than insisting on completion of remote minimally invasive resection. These safeguards were regarded as mandatory prerequisites and do not negate the general principle that major hepatobiliary surgery is safest when experienced local rescue capability is available.

After pneumoperitoneum was established, approximately 1,500 mL of pre-existing intraperitoneal blood and clot was encountered. Following evacuation of the hemoperitoneum, exploration revealed a large actively bleeding tumor in the right liver involving segments 5 and 6. Cholecystectomy was performed first, followed by hilar dissection with ligation and division of the right hepatic inflow pedicle. After the ischemic demarcation line appeared, liver transection was carried out along this plane. During parenchymal transection, ultrasonic shears, bipolar coagulation, and a vascular closure device were used to control the hepatic veins and intrahepatic pedicle structures. The right hepatic vein was subsequently divided, completing en bloc right hepatectomy (Figure 1C-1G). The specimen was retrieved in a protective bag. The abdominal cavity was irrigated extensively with warm distilled water, and two drains were placed.

Operative time was 300 minutes, and additional intraoperative blood loss after evacuation of the hemoperitoneum was estimated at 100 mL. This value did not include the approximately 1,500 mL of pre-existing intraperitoneal blood and clot, which was evacuated and recorded separately at the beginning of the operation. The intraoperative estimate was based on suction volume after deducting irrigation fluid, gauze/sponge assessment, and the anesthetic record after right inflow control. Conversion to open surgery was not required. Network transmission remained stable throughout the procedure, with round-trip latency of 16 to 20 ms and no interruption, perceptible delay, or visual disruption. Final pathology confirmed HCC (Figure 1H). The postoperative course was uneventful. Oral intake resumed on postoperative day 1, abdominal drains were removed on postoperative day 3, and the patient was discharged on postoperative day 7. No Clavien-Dindo grade III or higher perioperative complications occurred. Follow-up contrast-enhanced CT showed no definite abnormality (Figure 1I).

This case indicates that, in carefully selected patients, emergency remote robotic right hepatectomy may achieve four objectives in a single procedure: rapid hemostasis, complete tumor resection, reduced operative trauma, and cross-regional access to specialist surgical care. This interpretation should remain conservative. The favorable outcome in one stabilized patient does not establish superiority over TAE/TACE followed by staged hepatectomy, nor should it be generalized to unstable patients, patients with poor hepatic reserve, multifocal disease, major vascular invasion, or centers without predefined rescue capability. This approach should not be generalized to all patients with ruptured HCC. For patients with persistent hemodynamic instability, poor hepatic reserve, multifocal disease, or lesions unlikely to be radically resected within an acceptable emergency timeframe, current evidence favors transarterial intervention or open hemostatic surgery, followed by staged hepatectomy after recovery of general condition and liver function.

Technically, emergency remote robotic hepatectomy requires communication safety and bedside rescue capacity to be treated as core prerequisites, alongside the remote surgeon's expertise. In a prospective study of remote hepatectomy published in 2025, all six procedures were completed without conversion; real-time round-trip latency ranged from 16.20 to 129.58 ms, and no frame loss was observed (4). In another complex procedure, Huang et al. reported successful remote robotic pancreatoduodenectomy with a median latency of 25 ms, supporting the technical feasibility of remote major pancreatic resection (10). Preclinical evidence further indicates that network latency and bandwidth directly affect operative safety and surgeon workload during telesurgery (2). Emergency remote major hepatectomy therefore requires redundant network support, explicit contingency protocols for immediate patient-side takeover, and a mature local team capable of anesthesia management, transfusion support, and prompt conversion to open surgery when required. For ruptured HCC, we believe candidate selection should require at least the following conditions: restoration of hemodynamic stability after resuscitation; Child-Pugh A or otherwise adequate hepatic reserve; localized resectable disease without radiologic evidence of IVC or main hepatic vein invasion; an experienced remote HPB surgeon; a validated low-latency network with redundancy; prepared blood products and anesthesia support; and a bedside team able to perform immediate open damage-control rescue. If any of these conditions is absent, transfer, transarterial hemostasis, or open damage-control surgery should take priority over remote definitive hepatectomy.

Oncologically, ruptured HCC represents not only an acute hemorrhagic event but also a potential source of intraperitoneal dissemination. Prior studies suggest that peritoneal tumor seeding may worsen long-term prognosis; nevertheless, with active treatment, outcomes in selected patients are not necessarily equivalent to those expected for conventional T4 advanced disease (7). Early or staged hepatectomy has been associated with better survival than nonsurgical treatment, and some data suggest that survival after staged resection may approach that of selected patients with nonruptured HCC (9). In the present case, the operative strategy emphasized early inflow control, en bloc resection, minimal tumor manipulation, specimen retrieval in a protective bag, and generous peritoneal lavage. These measures were intended both to secure hemostasis and to reduce the risk of peritoneal implantation.

In conclusion, emergency remote robotic right hepatectomy was completed in this patient after adequate resuscitation, stringent patient selection, and deployment of a robust network and bedside rescue system. The case is best interpreted as a hypothesis-generating demonstration of feasibility under exceptional circumstances, not as proof that emergency telesurgical hepatectomy is preferable to staged treatment or centralized HPB care. For critically ill hepatobiliary patients in remote regions, this model may offer a pathway to specialized minimally invasive surgery with curative intent when standard hemostatic resources are unavailable and all safety prerequisites are satisfied. Its safety, reproducibility, and generalizability, however, require validation through multicenter experience and long-term follow-up.
