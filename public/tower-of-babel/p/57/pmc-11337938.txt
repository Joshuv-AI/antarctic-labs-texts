Diffuse large B-cell lymphoma in the C8 root mimicking a schwannoma: illustrative case
Authors: Felipe Ramirez-Velandia, Mohit K Manchella, Anirudh Penumaka, Steven N Schwartz, Hemant Varma, Rafael A Vega, Ziev B Moses
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
BACKGROUNDPrimary lymphomas arising in peripheral nerves are extremely rare, with just a few case reports documented in the literature.OBSERVATIONSThe authors present the case of a 62-year-old female with C8 radiculopathy, initially suspected to have a schwannoma based on imaging but later diagnosed with primary C8 nerve root B-cell lymphoma. Following histopathological confirmation, the patient underwent 5 courses of chemotherapy, resulting in a decrease in the size of her lesion on magnetic resonance imaging. At her clinical follow-up 3 months later, her symptoms had improved. After performing a systematic review of the literature while adhering to the Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Reviews and Meta-Analyses reporting guidelines, the authors found only 3 similar cases, each with atypical presentations and treated with various chemotherapeutic agents and one of which experienced a central nervous system recurrence.LESSONSThese cases highlight the importance of early and accurate diagnosis for optimal management and long-term vigilance of primary lymphomas in the peripheral nerve. https://thejns.org/doi/10.3171/CASE24338

Spinal schwannomas, comprising 55% of spinal neoplasms, are frequently encountered intradural extramedullary tumors.1 These benign lesions, classified as World Health Organization grade I, are typically found incidentally in patients with radicular or myelopathic symptoms.2 Predominantly observed as foraminal, dumbbell-shaped, T2-hyperintense lesions, they primarily affect the cervical spine and are initially managed conservatively until symptomatic progression requires surgical intervention.3

In contrast, non-Hodgkin lymphoma (NHL), the most common hematological malignancy, accounting for 2.8% of cancer diagnoses, is typically treated with chemotherapy, often making debulking unnecessary.4 NHL rarely infiltrates peripheral nerves, which can result from direct extension of local lymph nodes or as part of systemic dissemination (neurolymphomatosis [NL]), with the latter occurring more frequently in B-cell disorders.5, 6 Primary lymphomas arising in the peripheral nerves have also been reported, but such occurrences are even rarer.7–9

Here, we present a case of primary lymphoma arising in the peripheral nerve and mimicking a schwannoma in its presentation. In order to compare this case with other similar cases reported in the literature, we performed a systematic review to highlight the clinical, radiological, and pathological features of reported cases of primary lymphomas of the spinal nerves.

Illustrative Case

A 62-year-old female was referred to the spine clinic due to an approximately 2-year history of worsening left-hand weakness and numbness, difficulty with fine motor movements, and recurrent episodes of shooting pain down her left arm. Despite multiple trials of oxycodone, tramadol, gabapentin, and tizanidine, she reported limited improvement in her symptoms. The initial physical examination revealed significant grip weakness (2/5 strength), atrophy of 1 dorsal web space, clawing of the last 2 digits, along with diminished sensation in digits 4 and 5 radiating up to the forearm.

Her magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) revealed a left isoenhancing C8 intraspinal mass with extraforaminal extension into the brachial plexus, raising concern for a malignant etiology (Fig.1A–D), along with multilevel cervical spondylosis (Fig.1E). No prior cervical imaging was available to evaluate the growth of this lesion. To exclude a potential malignant etiology, chest and abdomen/pelvis computed tomography (CT) studies were performed, which did not reveal evidence of occult lesions or sarcoidosis. Given her ongoing symptoms and MRI findings, her case was discussed in a weekly multidisciplinary meeting with faculty and attendings, and a consensus was reached to proceed with a biopsy with possible resection of the nerve root mass plus instrumentation. The surgical plan included performing a C5 to T2 posterior instrumented fusion with or without removal of the left C8 nerve sheath tumor.

During preoperative testing the day prior to the scheduled procedure, the patient noted worsening pain and weakness in her left upper extremity. In addition, she displayed acute binocular diplopia. She was sent to the emergency room, and brain MRI was done, which ruled out an acute ischemic event. She underwent repeat imaging, which revealed stability in the C8 lesion but significant T2 signal changes in the cord, for which she was started on steroids (Fig. 1F and G). Neurology was consulted, and a chest CT scan was obtained, which did not reveal evidence of sarcoidosis or other chest lesions.

The procedure was performed under neuromonitoring, including somatosensory evoked potentials, motor evoked potentials (MEPs), and electromyography. High-speed navigated drill pilot holes were made for the C5–6 bilateral lateral mass and T1–2 bilateral pedicle screws. On the left at C7–T1, a complete facetectomy was undertaken to expose the foraminal and extraforaminal C8 nerve. After opening the dura, the dorsal nerve rootlets appeared firm and enlarged. Further examination of the ventral rootlets demonstrated coalescence into a firm, expanded nerve structure that was tan-gray and irregular in appearance (Fig. 2A– C). The abnormal-appearing ventral nerve rootlets all fired at minimal stimulation thresholds of 2 mA upon nerve stimulation, while dorsal nerve rootlets appeared abnormal. Next, using sharp dissection, 2 specimens of abnormally enlarged and firm-appearing nerve rootlets were sent for analysis. The frozen specimen revealed lesional tissue and findings of numerous small, round blue cells, suggestive of a lymphoma; therefore, complete resection was not achieved. MEPs were stable after the biopsy. Regular closure of the dura mater was performed. Subsequently, titanium rods were cut and bent into the cervical curvature. The arthrodesis was completed with rods into the screws and tightened in place with threaded locking nuts, along with a bone graft of bone chips. The patient was subsequently transferred to the neurocritical care unit intubated and sedated. The histopathology evaluation revealed diffuse large B-cell lymphoma (DLBCL) with large, atypical B cells with an admixed subset of small CD3-positive cells (Fig. 2D– G). The estimated Ki-67 proliferation index was greater than 80%. Immunostains for cytokeratin cocktail (CAM5.2 and AE1/3) and synaptophysin were negative, but the cells displayed nuclear PAX-5 immunoreactivity. Further neurological evaluation evidenced limited abduction of her left eye, raising concern for leptomeningeal spread. Cerebrospinal fluid cytology did not reveal evidence of immunophenotypic features of involvement by leukemia/lymphoma, as the B cells were 9% of lymphocytes and were polyclonal, whereas T cells made up 70%, expressing mature lineage antigens (CD3, CD5, CD2, and CD7). Additionally, a minor subset of T cells exhibited dim or variably reduced CD7 expression, and the T cell population had a CD4/CD8 ratio of 9.9. Further investigations with [18F]fluorodeoxyglucose positron emission tomography did not reveal any evidence of avid disease elsewhere. Neuro-oncology recommended initiation of treatment for central nervous system (CNS) lymphoma with high-dose methotrexate (HD-MTX), with the addition of ifosfamide to the first cycle to provide systemic coverage.

A week later, a multidisciplinary oncology meeting suggested the addition of the modified rituximab, cyclophosphamide, hydroxydaunomycin, oncovin, and prednisone (R-CHOP) regimen, comprising 6 cycles of R-CHOP and 6 cycles of HD-MTX, followed by thiotepa-based high-dose chemotherapy and stem cell rescue. Treatment initiation was delayed due to a urinary tract infection and dehiscence of the surgical wound, which required revision, paraspinal muscle flaps, and placement of vacuum-assisted closure. In this context, she was started on vancomycin and ceftriaxone and was discharged to continue oral antibiotics with cefpodoxime and doxycycline as an outpatient until her drain was removed by plastic surgery.

The patient was followed up in the spine clinic 3 months later, having undergone 5 cycles of chemotherapy. She reported improvement in her left upper extremity strength and adequate healing of her wound, although she mentioned experiencing episodes of neuropathic pain around her brachial plexus. On physical examination, she exhibited weakness in shoulder abduction (3/5 deltoid strength; unable to hold above head) and mild distal upper-extremity weakness (4/5), but her sensation remained intact to light touch. Postoperative cervical MRI demonstrated a reduction in the size of the lesion (Fig.3), and she has since continued with her oncological treatment. She will continue to receive care from oncology, undergoing imaging surveillance to assess tumor response. Additionally, neurosurgery will follow up to assess hardware positioning with cervical radiographs at our spine clinic. Literature Review

To gather a better understanding of the available literature on primary lymphomas in the spinal nerves, we conducted a search of multiple databases, including PubMed, Embase, and Web of Science, adhering to the Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Reviews and Meta-Analyses (PRISMA) reporting guidelines.10 A search strategy used the following terms: ‘‘peripheral nerve” AND “lymphoma” AND “spine.” We screened titles and abstracts and included the case reports of patients with histopathological evidence of lymphoma in the peripheral nerves published in English or Spanish from database inception until October 26, 2023. We excluded lymphomas involving the cranial nerves, cauda equina, and distal nerves and with nerve involvement secondary to NL. Two reviewers (M.K.M., F.R.V.) independently reviewed the titles, abstracts, and, when necessary, the full texts regarding the inclusion and exclusion criteria. To maximize our chances of identifying all relevant cases, we also manually checked the reference lists of the included studies, performed a backward citation analysis, performed a forward citation analysis, and used the similar articles feature of a database. A detailed flowchart of the selection process is shown in Fig. 4. The primary outcomes to be collected were the clinical presentations, imaging features, pathological findings, treatment plans, and clinical outcomes. To assess the methodological quality of the included cases, an adapted version of the modified Newcastle-Ottawa Scale, which converges into 8 items, was used.11 FIG. 4.Flowchart of the selection process of the articles included in the review adhering to the PRISMA 2020 statement.

Results

From the initial list of 268 results of our search strategy, there were 3 other cases of spinal nerve primary lymphomas described in the literature and selected for this review. An outline of the details for each case is presented in Table 1. Two of the reported cases presented with C8 radiculopathy, while 1 case featured extramedullary cord compression in the lumbar spine.7–9 Two of the cases represented B-cell lymphoma, while the 1 case was a T-cell lymphoma. Common imaging features included hypointense T2-weighted lesions with mild enhancement on postcontrast imaging and foraminal invasion in cases affecting the cervical spine. Each case was managed differently: the 2 cases with cervical lesions underwent resection and received adjunctive radiation and chemotherapy, although with variations in delivery methods, frequencies, and regimens. The patient with lumbar spine involvement received HD-MTX therapy. All 3 patients achieved disease remission with symptomatic improvement, although 1 case had CNS recurrence. Discussion

Lymphomas are heterogeneous, abnormal, monoclonal proliferations of lymphocytes that aggregate to form masses, most commonly within the lymph nodes. Extranodal presentations can occur in 15%–30% of lymphomas, particularly for non-Hodgkin B-cell lymphomas.12 Among them, DLBCLs are probably the most common variant, accounting for 31% of all NHL cases in Western countries and 37% of B-cell tumors worldwide.13 It has been estimated that 40% of DLBCLs manifest with extranodal involvement, and the presentation, behavior, and prognosis are strongly influenced by the specific extranodal site of origin. 14

NL was initially described by Lhermitte and Trelles in 1934, establishing the idea that peripheral nerves could be infiltrated by either malignant or nontumor lymphocytes.15 The initial hypothesis suggested that NL resulted from subarachnoid invasion, exhibiting distinct clinical behavior compared to perineural and epidural lymphoma, which presented with cauda equina syndrome.16 In 2003, Baehring et al. described 4 distinct clinical patterns of presentation for NL, including painful involvement of nerves or roots, cranial neuropathy, polyneuropathy, or mononeuropathy.6

Unfortunately, NL remains quite unresponsive to treatment. Steroid therapy alone has been shown to provide symptom relief but only for short durations.6 Although rituximab has shown effectiveness in DLBCL,17, 18 it does not significantly improve outcomes in NL.19–21 Moreover, patients with NL who undergo treatment with rituximab and combination therapy relapse shortly after or during treatment. More promisingly, 1 group reported a patient who remained relapse-free at 1 year following ESHAP therapy (etoposide, methylprednisolone, cytosine arabinoside, and cisplatin) and BEAM therapy (carmustine [BCNU], etoposide, cytarabine, melphalan with stem cell transplantation).4 The sparse information available on this condition and the lack of standardized therapy for peripheral nerve lymphomas make treatment modality decisions for these cases difficult.

Observations

In the presented case, we highlighted a primary peripheral nerve lymphoma arising in a spinal root, a presentation even less common than NL, with limited information available in the literature regarding medical strategies. For our patient, we established treatment with HD-MTX due to the suspicion of CNS involvement, but we later included an adjunctive modified R-CHOP according to a multidisciplinary consensus. Our patient reported symptomatic improvement, and there was clinical evidence of enhanced motor and sensory function, indicating a favorable response to medical therapy. Similarly to our results, Al-Khayat et al. reported the case of a patient presenting with bilateral cervical radiculopathy, which improved after 4 cycles of ABVD (Adriamycin, bleomycin sulfate, vinblastine sulfate, dacarbazine) and radiotherapy.7 Moreover, in the case presented by Wachter et al., the patient exhibited right upper-hand weakness, which improved after resection of an initially suspected schwannoma.8 However, adjunct medical therapy was started due to evidence of T-lymphoblastic lymphoma. The patient's condition progressed to a more disseminated disease, with the initial manifestation of radicular symptoms becoming the first evident sign of such an advanced condition, ultimately resulting in recurrence and death 1 year after treatment. The other case reported by Kim et al. involved a B-cell lymphoma located in the lumbar spine.9 The patient experienced complete resolution of their symptoms after resection of the lesion, although HD-MTX was also administered. Compared to the other 2 cases, the one by Kim et al. has the most similar medical strategy as in our patient, likely due to the same histopathological diagnosis.

The uniqueness of the identified cases lies in their atypical presentation, as lymphomas rarely debut with radiculopathy. As shown, only 3 other cases of primary lymphoma in the spinal nerves have been documented in the literature, making each case a valuable addition to our understanding of this condition.7–9 Notably, our case represents the first primary DLBCL reported in the cervical spinal roots. Moreover, the successful achievement of disease remission and symptomatic improvement in all 3 identified cases is noteworthy and suggests that early diagnosis and appropriate medical treatment can lead to favorable outcomes.

However, it is important to highlight that the case reported by Wachter et al. experienced a CNS recurrence.8 In our case, we only had a short follow-up, thus limiting our understanding of the patient’s overall prognosis after her initial treatment. Nonetheless, the potential for recurrence, even after successful initial treatment, underscores the challenges in management and the importance of active surveillance for these patients.

Note that our review has several limitations that need to be recognized. The inclusion of a few case reports introduces a very low quality of evidence and high heterogeneity, not allowing us to fully analyze the overall prognosis of patients with primary spinal nerve lymphomas. However, we mitigated potential publication bias by using multiple databases, minimized language bias by including English- and Spanish-language studies, and tried to compare the handful of cases available in the literature.

From these observations and upon reflection on the illustrated case, our team recommends that the workup of a patient presenting with an isoenhancing foraminal dumbbell-shaped lesion at the exit of a peripheral nerve requires careful consideration of available imaging to characterize patterns of growth. If no prior imaging is available, as in our case, there is a need to assess the extent of invasion. Bilobed and encapsulated lesions should raise suspicion for a benign lesion such as a schwannoma or neurofibroma.22 Lesions with clear invasion of the brachial plexus, and those expanding beyond the neuroforamen or into the surrounding soft tissue, require additional workup, and key differentials include metastasis, primary malignant peripheral nerve sheath tumors, and systemic disorders.23–25 Such cases would benefit from biopsy to confirm the diagnosis and guide treatment.

Lessons

From this case, we have learned that dumbbell-shaped lesions are not exclusive to schwannomas and can also be seen in other malignant spinal tumors. Rather, a careful evaluation of the imaging features and patterns of extension needs to be performed, and a biopsy is ultimately necessary to confirm the diagnosis and guide treatment.

Nonetheless, the radicular syndrome illustrated in our case as the initial sign of lymphoma is a very unusual presentation, with only a few similar cases reported in the literature. Our case adds to the existing knowledge regarding this uncommon clinical presentation, emphasizing the importance of early recognition and treatment, which allows for targeted medical interventions. However, due to the limited number of reported cases and the scarcity of clinical evidence, the overall prognosis in patients with such symptoms remains inadequately understood. Based on the 3 previous cases and our own experience, disease remission is achievable with appropriate medical therapy, but the risk of recurrence underscores the need for ongoing vigilance and follow-up care.
