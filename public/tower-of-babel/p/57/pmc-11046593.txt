A rare case of filarial cervical abscess
Authors: Ramzeen Nilam Hameed, Geetha Philips, K N Sreelatha, Soumya John
Published: 2023

Introduction

Lymphatic filariasis affects an estimated 110 million people and is found throughout the tropics and subtropics. Filariasis is the most common cause of acquired lymphedema. It is characterised by peripheral and inguinal lymphadenopathy. Cervical lymphadenopathy is a rare manifestation. We present a case of filarial cervical abscess.

Case report

A 61-year-old woman presented with a right cervical swelling of 2 weeks' duration. There was no history of fever, cough or weight loss. On examination she was afebrile, with a right cervical lymph node palpable, firm to hard in consistency and mobile. Routine blood investigations were normal. She was treated with oral antibiotics for 1 week, but the swelling remained the same size. Ultrasound neck showed right cervical and supraclavicular significant nodes with loss of fatty hilum. Fine needle aspiration cytology was normal.

Histopathology sections from the larger lymph node showed an abscess filled with neutrophils and surrounded by epitheloid histiocytes, foreign body type of giant cells and dense mixed inflammatory infiltrate rich in eosinophils. Multiple cross sections of a degenerated worm were seen among inflammatory cells. The findings were consistent with parasitic abscess, probably filarial. Serum filarial antibody detection test was negative. Filarial antigen was not detected.

She was treated with single dose of diethylcarbamazine citrate and albendazole. The nodes disappeared and she has since been closely followed up.

Discussion

Lymphatic filariasis is considered globally as a neglected tropical disease (NTD) caused by Wuchereria bancrofti, Brugia malayi and Brugia timori. Humans are the only definitive host, while the mosquito is the intermediate host. Eosinophilia and microfilaremia are common in the acute phase. The chronic stage is characterised by lymphadenopathy, lymphedema, hydrocele and elephantiasis. The adult worm resides in lymphnodes, where the gravid female releases a large number of microfilariae. These larvae pass through the thoracic duct and pulmonary capillaries to the peripheral circulation. Adult worms can survive in the lymphatic system for 5–15 years. Microfilariae can be picked up in superficial locations by FNAC. Definitive diagnosis of microfilaria is made by demonstration of parasite in peripheral smear; however, filariasis can exist without microfilaremia.

This patient presented with cervical lymphadenopathy with no systemic symptoms. The provisional diagnosis was a granulomatous pathology. Tuberculosis was ruled out. FNAC showed no malignant cells. Histopathology showed parasitic abscess. Single doses of a two-drug combination of diethylcarbamazine with albendazole or Ivermectin are 99% effective in removing microfilariae from the blood for a full year after treatment.

Conclusion

Filariasis presenting as cervical lymph node enlargement is not often mentioned in literature. Our case shows that filariasis should be considered in patients with cervical lymphadenopathy.
