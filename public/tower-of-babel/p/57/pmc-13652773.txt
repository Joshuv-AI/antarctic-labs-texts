Quinolones as anti-Toxoplasma agents: a comprehensive scoping review of in vitro and in vivo efficacy
Authors: Maryam Hataminejad, Marzieh Asadi, Saeed Emami, Moozhan Mofidi, Mahbobeh Montazeri
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractToxoplasmosis is a globally prevalent parasitic disease with significant therapeutic limitations, especially in vulnerable populations. This scoping review assesses the efficacy of quinolones against Toxoplasma gondii in vitro and in vivo. PubMed, ScienceDirect, Web of Science, Embase, Scopus, ProQuest, and Google Scholar were searched from inception to August 2024. Original English-language studies evaluating quinolone effects on T. gondii were included. Screening and data extraction followed PRISMA 2020 guidelines. Of 5,252 initial records, 29 studies were shortlisted for final qualitative synthesis. In vitro analysis revealed a stark dichotomy: while clinical fluoroquinolones such as ciprofloxacin (IC50 = 3.44–20 µM) possessed weak-to-moderate activity, experimental scaffolds, particularly Hydroxyquinolone derivatives (e.g., Compound A, IC50 = 0.0004 μM) and endochin-like quinolones (ELQs) (e.g., ELQ-316, IC50 = 0.000007 μM) achieved sub-nanomolar potency by inhibiting the mitochondrial electron transport chain. Structural optimization was essential; for example, adamantane-conjugated ciprofloxacin (Adam-Cipro) was 31-fold more potent than the parent drug (IC50 = 0.64 µM vs. 20 µM). Combination regimens (e.g., gatifloxacin-pyrimethamine) maximized efficacy while also lowering resistance risks. Novel compounds such as ELQ-316 (0.08 mg/kg) decreased brain cyst burden by up to 88% in chronic models and provided complete protection in acute models. Translational challenges were identified, including the failure of decoquinate (DCQ) to suppress vertical transmission in pregnancy models and the emergence of resistance to ELQ-316. Key research gaps persist: the activity of quinolones against latent bradyzoites, efficacy in immunocompromised hosts, and safety in children and pregnant women remain unexplored. This review demonstrates that while conventional clinical quinolones are suboptimal against T. gondii, structure-optimized quinolones targeting the parasite mitochondria can yield outstanding potency. Efficacy is determined by precise structure activity relationships (particularly at the C-7 position) and advanced drug delivery systems. Introduction

Toxoplasma gondii is an opportunistic pathogenic protozoan belonging to the phylum Apicomplexa, responsible for zoonotic toxoplasmosis, which has a significant impact on human health and animal husbandry. It is estimated that approximately one-third of the global human population is infected with T. gondii (Dubey 2021; Flegr et al. 2014). This ubiquitous parasite exhibits a complex life cycle culminating in sexual reproduction exclusively within the intestines of felids, its definitive hosts, leading to the production of oocysts. Concurrently, T. gondii undergoes asexual replication in a variety of intermediate hosts (Blader et al. 2015). Transmission to humans primarily occurs via three routes: ingestion of undercooked or raw meat containing tissue cysts; accidental ingestion of oocysts shed by infected felids through contact with contaminated cat litter, soil (including gardening soil), unwashed fruits or vegetables, or contaminated drinking water (Shapiro et al. 2019); and congenital transmission from an infected mother to her fetus via the placenta (Dunn et al. 1999).

In individuals with a fully functioning immune system, infection with T. gondii usually remains in a latent state and does not cause noticeable clinical symptoms. The parasite often persists in a dormant form without triggering obvious illness. However, in people with weakened immune systems such as those with cancer or acquired immunodeficiency syndrome (AIDS) the infection may cause serious complications, including inflammation of the brain (encephalitis), eye damage, and involvement of other vital organs (Dian et al. 2023).

Currently, there is no approved vaccine or a definitive pharmacological treatment specifically targeting T. gondii infection. The clinical management of toxoplasmosis primarily relies on drugs such as pyrimethamine and sulfonamides, which are associated with significant side effects including gastrointestinal disturbances and, in some cases, teratogenic risks (Alday and Doggett 2017; Dunay et al. 2018). Although alternative treatment options exist, such as pyrimethamine combined with clindamycin, atovaquone, clarithromycin, or azithromycin, as well as monotherapy with trimethoprim-sulfamethoxazole (TMP-SMX) or atovaquone, none of these regimens have demonstrated superiority over the pyrimethamine-sulfadiazine combination. Moreover, no current treatment effectively targets the latent stage of T. gondii infection (Dunay et al. 2018). These limitations underscore the pressing need to develop safer and more effective therapeutic strategies to control and alleviate toxoplasmosis (Montazeri et al. 2018, 2017).

Quinolones are synthetic antibiotics widely used for bacterial infections due to their broad-spectrum activity and strong oral bioavailability. They exert their bactericidal effect by targeting bacterial DNA gyrase and topoisomerase IV enzymes, which are essential for DNA replication and cell division. By stabilizing enzyme–DNA complexes, quinolones induce DNA breaks that ultimately lead to bacterial cell death. Fluoroquinolones, a subclass of quinolones characterized by the addition of a fluorine atom, show enhanced potency and expanded activity against a wider range of bacteria, including some Gram-positive and anaerobic species (Del Cacho et al. 2006; Gouvea et al. 2013; Hooper 2001). Emerging research suggests that quinolones and fluoroquinolones may also be effective against T. gondii, by disrupting unique parasite DNA replication mechanisms, offering a promising avenue for therapeutic development with low toxicity (Awad et al. 2024; Emami et al. 2024; Rocha-Valderrama et al. 2025).

The current review aims to thoroughly assess the anti-T. gondii effects and the structure–activity relationships of quinolone compounds based on in vitro and in vivo studies. Owing to the considerable drawbacks of currently available treatments including toxicity, emerging drug resistance, and limited effect on latent cysts the review seeks to identify novel agents that target specific parasite pathways like the apicoplast. Compiling the existing data will support the rational design and development of more effective and safer anti-T. gondii therapies for future clinical application.

Methods

Literature search strategy

A systematic and rigorous literature search was conducted to identify all relevant studies examining the efficacy of quinolone compounds against T. gondii. Multiple electronic databases, PubMed, ScienceDirect, Web of Science, Embase, Scopus, ProQuest, and Google Scholar were searched comprehensively up to August 2024. The search combined controlled vocabulary (such as MeSH terms) and free-text keywords relating to T. gondii, toxoplasmosis, and specific quinolone/fluoroquinolone agents (e.g., Ciprofloxacin, Levofloxacin, Moxifloxacin). Boolean operators “AND” and “OR” were used strategically to construct search queries maximizing sensitivity and specificity. No initial language restrictions were applied, ensuring broad capture of potentially eligible studies.

Eligibility criteria

Inclusion criteria focused exclusively on original experimental research articles that assessed the effects of quinolones on T. gondii using in vitro cell culture or in vivo animal models. Only studies with full English texts and empirical data were considered for inclusion. Exclusion criteria eliminated review articles, case reports, conference abstracts, purely computational or theoretical studies, clinical human trials, investigations confined to ocular toxoplasmosis, and duplicate datasets. This ensured a homogeneous sample of experimental evidence pertinent to the antimicrobial evaluation of these compounds.

Study selection procedure

Following the compilation of search results, studies underwent a two-stage screening process performed independently by two reviewers. Initially, titles and abstracts were screened for relevance against inclusion criteria. Subsequently, full texts of preliminarily eligible articles were assessed to confirm eligibility. Any disagreements between reviewers were resolved through consensus discussion or, when necessary, adjudicated by a third senior reviewer to maintain impartiality and methodological rigor. Additionally, reference lists of included publications were manually reviewed to identify any further studies not captured during database searches.

Data extraction and management

A structured and dual-phase data extraction framework was developed to ensure comprehensive and standardized collection of relevant study parameters. Separate customized forms were utilized for in vitro and in vivo investigations. For in vitro studies, extracted data included year of publication, parasite strain, specific quinolone or fluoroquinolone agent, host cell type, culture methodology, assay techniques, principal findings, efficacy outcomes, and bibliographic details. For in vivo studies, variables captured encompassed publication year, drug compound, animal species and strain, inoculum size, treatment regimen, methods of efficacy assessment, key results, overall effectiveness, and study citations. Two independent reviewers performed data extraction, with discrepancies resolved by re-examination of source articles and discussion to ensure accuracy.

Quality assessment of included studies

Although formal risk of bias assessment is not mandatory for scoping reviews per PRISMA-ScR guidelines, we evaluated the methodological quality of included studies to contextualize the strength of the evidence. For in vitro studies (n = 23), we developed a customized 5-item quality checklist based on established criteria for cell-based antiparasitic assays: (1) clear description of parasite strain and host cell type; (2) inclusion of positive controls (e.g., pyrimethamine or atovaquone); (3) reporting of IC50/EC50 with measures of variability (standard deviation or confidence intervals); (4) dose–response curve with at least five concentrations; and (5) independent replication (≥ 3 experiments). Each item was scored as 1 (yes) or 0 (no/unclear), generating a total score out of 5. Disagreements between reviewers were resolved by consensus discussion. For in vivo animal studies (n = 15), two independent reviewers (M.H. and M.M.) assessed risk of bias using the SYRCLE's Risk of Bias tool, which covers 11 domains: selection bias (sequence generation, allocation concealment, baseline characteristics), performance bias (random housing, blinding of caregivers/investigators), detection bias (random outcome assessment, blinding of outcome assessors), attrition bias (incomplete outcome data), reporting bias (selective outcome reporting), and other sources of bias. The detailed quality assessment results are presented in Supplementary Table S1 and S2.

Reporting standards

All methodology reporting adheres strictly to the Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Reviews and Meta-Analyses (PRISMA) 2020 guidelines (Frost et al. 2022). This adherence guarantees transparency, reproducibility, and comprehensive documentation of the review process, facilitating critical appraisal and synthesis of the gathered evidence (Fig. 1). This review was approved by the Ethics Committee of Mazandaran University of Medical Sciences (IR.MAZUMS.REC.1403.461).

Results

Literature search, study selection, and methodological overview

Initial systematic searches across seven biomedical databases (PubMed, ScienceDirect, Web of Science, Embase, Scopus, ProQuest, and Google Scholar) yielded 5,252 records. Following removal of 1,009 duplicates, 4,243 unique records underwent title and abstract screening, resulting in exclusion of 4,201 records due to irrelevance or failure to meet predefined inclusion criteria. Subsequent full-text assessment of 42 articles led to exclusion of 13 studies, culminating in 29 articles for qualitative synthesis. The final corpus comprised 14 exclusively in vitro studies, 6 exclusively in vivo investigations, and 9 integrated studies employing both methodologies, as detailed in the PRISMA flow diagram (Fig. 1).

Methodological quality assessment (Supplementary Table S1 and S2) revealed that while most in vitro studies (18/23) scored ≥ 4/5 on our quality checklist, indicating robust experimental design, the majority of in vivo studies (12/15) had unclear risk of bias in allocation concealment and random housing, though outcome assessment and reporting were generally adequate. This heterogeneity should be considered when interpreting comparative efficacy.

In vitro profiling: compound efficacy, mechanisms, and structure–activity relationships

Analysis of in vitro studies revealed a dominant use of the virulent RH strain, with only one study employing the chronic-infection ME49 strain. Human foreskin fibroblasts (HFF) were the most frequently used host cells, followed by MRC-5 fibroblasts, Vero cells, and specialized BeWo trophoblast cells. Among these, Vero cells were used in three studies.

Experimental investigations primarily focused on quinolone derivatives (15 studies) compared with classical fluoroquinolones (8 studies), while several investigations (6 studies) also assessed drug combinations for synergistic potential.

The investigated compounds primarily belonged to three distinct chemical classes with shared quinolone cores, each with characteristic substitutions dictating their anti-Toxoplasma activity (Fig. 2). Classical fluoroquinolones (Panel A), such as ciprofloxacin and trovafloxacin, feature a fluorine atom at the C-6 position and a piperazinyl or pyrrolidinyl moiety at C-7, which are critical for antibacterial activity but confer only modest anti-parasitic potency. Hydroxyquinolones (Panel B), exemplified by HDQ and its derivatives, are characterized by a hydroxyl group at the N-1 position. This modification, along with specific side-chain optimizations, dramatically enhances potency by shifting the primary target from bacterial DNA gyrase to the parasite's mitochondrial electron transport chain. The most potent class, ELQs (Panel C), typically possess a lipophilic side chain at the C-3 position (e.g., phenoxy or chlorophenoxy groups) and lack the C-6 fluorine. This structure optimizes binding to the cytochrome bc₁ complex (Complex III) in T. gondii, leading to sub-nanomolar inhibitory concentrations.

Common structures and SARs of fluoroquinolones (A), hydroxyquinolones (B), and endochin-like quinolones (C)

Potency against T. gondii tachyzoites varied by over four orders of magnitude, with IC50/EC50 values ranging from nanomolar to milligram-per-milliliter levels. Among clinically used fluoroquinolones, ciprofloxacin (IC50 = 3.44–20 µM) and trovafloxacin (IC50 = 1.85–2.35 µM) showed moderate-to-weak efficacy compared with next-generation derivatives.

Structural optimization substantially enhanced activity when directly compared within the same experimental models. Adamantane-conjugated ciprofloxacin (Adam-Cipro, IC50 = 0.64 µM) was approximately 31-fold more potent than its parent drug ciprofloxacin (IC50 = 20 µM). Hydroxyquinolone scaffolds displayed outstanding potency: Compound A (IC50 = 0.0004 μM) was ~ 10.5-fold more potent than HDQ (IC50 = 0.0042 µM), and Compound B (IC50 = 0.0008 µM) was ~ 5.3-fold more potent than HDQ in the same assay. Endochin-like quinolones (ELQs) achieved even greater potency. In one head-to-head comparison, ELQ-316 (IC50 = 0.000007 µM) and ELQ-271 (IC50 = 0.0001 µM) were approximately 20,000-fold and 1,400-fold more potent than atovaquone (IC50 = 0.138 µM), respectively. In a separate study using a different parasite strain, ELQ-316 (IC50 = 0.004 µM) and ELQ-271 (IC50 = 0.005 µM) remained 5.5-fold and 4.4-fold more potent than atovaquone (IC50 = 0.022 µM). HQNO, another quinolone-related molecule, showed strong inhibition (EC50 = 0.995 µM).

Synergistic combinations further enhanced efficacy. The gatifloxacin-pyrimethamine combination reduced the IC50 by ~ 33% compared to gatifloxacin alone. Likewise, combining HDQ with atovaquone lowered the IC50 from 0.0003 μM (HDQ alone) to 0.00016 μM, representing a 1.9-fold increase in potency. In contrast, fluoroquinolone mixtures such as enrofloxacin + toltrazuril achieved additive but not superior activity compared to single agents.

Mechanistic assays (microscopy, qPCR, ELISA, and TEM) revealed mitochondrial electron transport chain inhibition (ELQs, HDQ), structural disruption of tachyzoite vacuoles, and suppression of bioenergetic metabolism (ATP depletion and ROS imbalance) as key mechanisms. Together, these studies underscore that targeted quinolone derivatives, particularly hydroxyquinolones and ELQs, markedly outperform conventional fluoroquinolones in potency and selectivity (Table 1).

In vivo efficacy: survival outcomes, parasite burden, and translational challenges

In vivo investigations consistently utilized murine models to assess the anti-Toxoplasma efficacy of quinolones. Most studies (10 of 15) employed the acute RH strain, while a smaller subset (5 studies) evaluated chronic infection using the ME49 strain. Tachyzoites were predominantly inoculated intraperitoneally (12 studies), whereas cyst-based infections were established orally in chronic models.

Dosing regimens varied widely, ranging from sub-milligram levels (ELQ-316: 0.08 mg/kg; ELQ-271: 0.14 mg/kg) to high-dose therapies such as gatifloxacin (400 mg/kg/day). Early-generation fluoroquinolones provided partial protection: trovafloxacin (100–200 mg/kg) yielded 100% survival in acute infection, while gatifloxacin combined with pyrimethamine (200 + 12.5 mg/kg) achieved 85% survival, confirming synergy.

Among modified fluoroquinolones, levofloxacin demonstrated dual significance: its pharmacokinetics were altered by T. gondii infection (reducing distribution half-life and increasing peak concentration), and when combined with garlic extract (90 mg/kg for 7 days), it reduced ME49 brain cyst counts by ~ 93% and improved immune responses (↑IL-12, IL-17, IFN-γ; ↓IL-10). Ciprofloxacin derivatives (Adam-Cipro, Et-Cipro, Bn-Cipro) extended survival to 13–25% after 60 days compared with complete mortality in ciprofloxacin-treated mice—while nanoparticle formulations of ciprofloxacin enhanced histopathological recovery but did not significantly improve survival.

Advanced delivery systems and structural optimization further boosted activity: niosomal norfloxacin reduced peritoneal tachyzoite counts by 84–91% compared with 45% for the free drug. In parallel, ELQs and hydroxyquinolone analogues achieved remarkable outcomes; ELQ-400 provided complete survival in acute RH infection, while ELQ-271/316 reduced brain cyst burdens by 76–88% and ELQ-334 decreased cyst numbers by ~ 67%. Notably, despite its sub-nanomolar in vitro activity, ELQ-316 failed to achieve complete parasite clearance in the brain, underscoring the existence of pharmacokinetic barriers that limit tissue penetration. Hydroxyquinolone derivatives (HDQ, Compounds A and B) also produced sub-nanomolar IC50 values in vivo, correlating with strong suppression of parasite loads in target tissues.

Conversely, decoquinate (DCQ) failed to prevent congenital transmission and adversely affected pup survival, whereas its derivative RMB060 significantly reduced adult infection despite incomplete prophylaxis. HQNO (2-heptyl-4-hydroxyquinoline N-oxide) demonstrated notable in vivo activity (20 mg/kg for 7 days), markedly decreasing peritoneal parasite burdens.

Collectively, these findings highlight the translational promise of optimized quinolone scaffolds particularly ELQ and hydroxyquinolone analogues over classical fluoroquinolones, though formulation-dependent efficacy and strain-specific resistance (as observed with ELQ-316) remain significant challenges (Table 2).

Discussion

This review integrates available evidence on the anti-T. gondii potential and structure–activity relationships (SAR) of quinolone-based compounds. The findings reveal a clear pharmacological disparity: clinically used fluoroquinolones such as ciprofloxacin, levofloxacin, and gatifloxacin display only moderate efficacy, whereas structurally optimized analogues, particularly hydroxyquinolones and ELQs exhibit exceptional sub-nanomolar potency. These compounds act primarily through inhibition of the mitochondrial electron transport chain, leading to ATP depletion, increased reactive oxygen species (ROS) generation, and irreversible ultrastructural damage observable under transmission electron microscopy (TEM). Structural modification markedly enhances efficacy; for instance, incorporation of an adamantane moiety or lipophilic side chains into the quinolone core dramatically increases potency compared to parent drugs, highlighting the critical role of rational design in overcoming intrinsic limitations of conventional fluoroquinolones (Bajohr et al. 2010; Doggett et al. 2012; Martins-Duarte et al. 2015; Mo et al. 2023).

Synergistic drug interactions substantially potentiate anti-Toxoplasma activity and may reduce the risk of resistance. Combinations such as gatifloxacin–pyrimethamine and HDQ–atovaquone have demonstrated enhanced efficacy in vitro and improved survival in acute in vivo models (Beshay et al. 2023; Saleh et al. 2007). Likewise, levofloxacin combined with garlic extract reduced ME49 brain cyst burden by 92.8% while enhancing immune responses (↑IL-12, IL-17, IFN-γ; ↓IL-10) without hepatorenal toxicity (Beshay et al. 2023). These results align with findings from Sadeghi et al. (2022), who reported supra-additive inhibition in NFQ-2/pyrimethamine combinations, and Emami et al. (2024), who observed strong synergy between quinolone-coumarin hybrids and novobiocin (Emami et al. 2024; Sadeghi et al. 2022). Conversely, Rashed et al. (2022) noted that ciprofloxacin–silver nanoparticles improved hepatic histopathology but failed to improve survival, underscoring that nanocarrier efficiency depends on bio-targeting rather than mere encapsulation (Rashed et al. 2022).

Despite remarkable in vitro potency, translating these effects into consistent in vivo efficacy remains challenging. Although next-generation quinolones, particularly ELQs and hydroxyquinolones, offer strong protection in murine models of both acute and chronic toxoplasmosis at extremely low doses, several biological and pharmacological barriers persist. The failure of DCQ to prevent congenital transmission, despite its efficacy in non-pregnant models, illustrates the influence of host physiology and tissue-specific barriers such as the placenta (Ramseier et al. 2021). Moreover, the emergence of resistance to highly potent ELQ-316 through cytochrome b mutations emphasizes the need for combinatorial strategies or drugs with higher genetic barriers to resistance (Alday et al. 2016; Doggett et al. 2020; McConnell et al. 2018; Ramseier et al. 2021).

A comparative analysis of the included studies reveals that direct quantitative correlation (IVIVC) between in vitro potency and in vivo efficacy is not consistently observed, and several pharmacokinetic obstacles underlie the translational discrepancies. The most striking example is ELQ-316, which, despite an exceptional in vitro IC50 of 0.000007 µM (Doggett et al. 2012), failed to completely eliminate brain infection in the chronic ME49 mouse model (McConnell et al. 2018). This failure can be attributed to multiple factors: (a) Blood–brain barrier penetration: Although ELQ‑316 reduced brain cyst burden by up to 88%, the effective concentration reaching the brain parenchyma is likely insufficient for complete eradication of intracellular bradyzoites, which may require a higher threshold concentration than that needed to inhibit tachyzoites in peritoneal fluid. (b) Systemic metabolism and bioavailability: The subsequent analog ELQ‑400, which incorporates structural optimization and prodrug strategies, achieved 100% survival in acute models and superior tissue clearance compared to ELQ‑316, indicating that improving metabolic stability and half-life is as critical as intrinsic potency (McConnell et al. 2018). (c) Placental and tissue‑specific barriers: The case of DCQ clearly demonstrates that high in vitro potency (IC50 = 0.5 µM) and efficacy in non‑pregnant adult mice do not translate to prevention of congenital transmission in pregnant mice, owing to high plasma protein binding and poor affinity for placental transporters (Ramseier et al. 2021). (d) Infection‑induced pharmacokinetic alterations: Yarsan et al. (2003) showed that T. gondii infection itself alters the distribution half‑life and peak concentration of levofloxacin, highlighting the dynamic interaction between host physiology, parasite burden, and drug disposition. Furthermore, heterogeneity in in vivo endpoints (survival vs. cyst burden reduction), dosing regimens, and inoculation routes precludes a simple linear IVIVC. Nevertheless, a qualitative trend is evident: compounds that achieve both high potency in tachyzoite‑based assays and favorable tissue distribution (e.g., ELQ‑400, niosomal norfloxacin) consistently outperform those with poor pharmacokinetic profiles.

Quinolone activity against T. gondii is significantly influenced by structural optimization targeting parasitic pathways. The higher selectivity indices of quinolone-coumarin hybrids (SI = 7.27–13.43) compared to pyrimethamine (SI = 3.05) indicate improved therapeutic windows through inhibition of parasite replication with minimal host cytotoxicity (Emami et al. 2024). Similarly, nitrofuran-conjugated quinolones (NFQ-2 and NFQ-5) inhibit tachyzoite growth through dual gyrase inhibition and redox interference (Sadeghi et al. 2022), congruent with earlier findings that C-7 side-chain optimization enhances apicoplast targeting (Anquetin et al. 2004; 2005; 2006). Critically, mitochondrial electron transport chain inhibition has emerged as a leading mechanism; DCQ derivatives (e.g., RMB060) and PPQ-6 cause irreversible mitochondrial matrix damage, although DCQ itself was ineffective in pregnancy models owing to poor pharmacokinetics (Awad et al. 2024; Ramseier et al. 2021).

Advanced drug delivery systems, such as niosomal formulations, show significant potential for enhancing bioavailability and targeting parasitic reservoirs, yielding superior outcomes compared to conventional administration. This indicates that optimizing pharmacokinetics and biodistribution is as critical as intrinsic potency for clinical success.

Despite promising preclinical results, the clinical translation of anti-Toxoplasma compounds faces three recurrent challenges. However, recent studies have begun to address each of these obstacles. (1) Model limitations: Overreliance on the acute RH strain in fibroblast-based assays fails to recapitulate drug efficacy against persistent bradyzoites or in neurotropic and placental contexts. To overcome this, a few studies have employed the ME49 cyst-forming strain (Doggett et al. 2012; Ramseier et al. 2021) and pregnancy-compatible models such as BeWo trophoblast cells (da Silva et al. 2017). Future efforts should expand to include organoid systems and chronic infection models that better mimic human disease. (2) Bioavailability barriers, especially placental penetration: The failure of DCQ to prevent congenital transmission (Ramseier et al. 2021) highlights poor tissue distribution. Recent advances in drug delivery have shown promise: niosomal norfloxacin achieved 84–91% reduction in peritoneal tachyzoites (Eid et al. 2023), and ciprofloxacin-loaded silver nanoparticles improved histopathological outcomes (Rashed et al. 2022). However, no study has yet applied targeted nanoparticles or liposomes specifically to overcome the placental barrier in Toxoplasma congenital infection representing a critical research gap. (3) Resistance management: Sub-nanomolar-potency compounds such as ELQ-316 are prone to resistance via cytochrome b mutations (Alday et al. 2017). Existing solutions include combination therapy (e.g., ELQ-316 + atovaquone, which showed additive effects) and the development of next-generation ELQ analogs such as ELQ-400, which exhibited no cross-resistance and achieved 100% survival in acute toxoplasmosis (McConnell et al. 2018). Continued structural optimization at the C-7 position and the use of hybrid pharmacophores represent additional strategies to raise the genetic barrier to resistance.

The methodological heterogeneity identified by our quality assessment (Supplementary Table S2), particularly the variability in in vivo experimental protocols and incomplete reporting of randomization and blinding, underscores the need for standardized reporting guidelines such as the ARRIVE guidelines in future animal studies to facilitate direct cross-study comparisons.

Conclusions

This review demonstrates that while conventional fluoroquinolones exhibit limited anti-T. gondii efficacy, structurally optimized quinolone derivatives–particularly ELQs and hydroxyquinolones – achieve exceptional sub-nanomolar potency by targeting mitochondrial electron transport and inducing irreversible parasitic damage. Future efforts must prioritize chronic infection models, hybrid pharmacophores merging quinolone scaffolds with immunomodulators, and advanced delivery systems (e.g., niosomes) to overcome tissue barriers. Ultimately, strategic molecular design, rational combination therapies, and biologically relevant models are essential to translate these promising compounds into clinically actionable anti-T. gondii agents.

However, several critical research gaps remain. First, the activity of quinolones against latent bradyzoites and tissue cysts has been scarcely investigated, as most in vitro studies relied on the acute RH strain. Second, nearly all in vivo efficacy data come from immunocompetent murine models; the pharmacokinetics and pharmacodynamics of these compounds in immunocompromised hosts (e.g., HIV/AIDS, cancer, pregnancy) are unknown. Third, no safety data exist for next-generation quinolones (ELQs, hydroxyquinolones) in special populations such as children and pregnant women, two groups that bear a high burden of congenital and reactivated toxoplasmosis. Addressing these gaps should be a priority for future research.
