The Relationship Between Anti–Cell Division Cycle and Apoptosis Regulator 1 Autoantibodies, Anti‐Sp4 Autoantibodies, and Cancer in Anti–Transcription Intermediary Factor 1γ–Positive Dermatomyositis
Authors: Christopher A Mecoli, David Fiorentino, Jemima Albayda, Julie J Paik, Eleni Tiniakou, Brittany Adler, Andrew L Mammen, Lisa Christopher‐Stine, Antony Rosen, Livia Casciola‐Rosen
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
AbstractObjectiveThe objective of this study was to describe the frequency, co‐occurrence, and cancer association of anti–cell division cycle and apoptosis regulator 1 (anti‐CCAR1) and anti‐Sp4 in two large independent adult dermatomyositis (DM) cohorts.MethodsAnti–transcription intermediary factor 1γ (anti‐TIF1γ)–positive patients with DM from two independent cohorts were studied to determine the serologic overlap of anti‐CCAR1 and anti‐Sp4 autoantibodies measured by enzyme‐linked immunosorbent assay. Associations between cancer‐associated myositis (CAM) and antibody‐defined subgroups within anti‐TIF1γ–positive patients with DM were determined.ResultsA total of 305 anti‐TIF1γ–positive patients with DM were studied: 169 patients from Johns Hopkins and 136 patients from Stanford. In each cohort, approximately one‐third of anti‐TIF1γ–positive patients with DM were anti‐Sp4 positive, one‐third were anti‐CCAR1 positive, 20% were positive for both, and half of patients were negative for both. There was a strong association with CAM in patients lacking both these antibodies (Johns Hopkins, odds ratio [OR] 12.9 [95% confidence interval (CI) 3.6–89.5]; Stanford, OR 4.5 [95% CI 1.8–13.2]). The strongest negative association with CAM was found in patients with anti‐Sp4 or anti‐CCAR1 (Johns Hopkins, OR 0.07 [95% CI 0.01–0.27]; Stanford, OR 0.22 [95% CI 0.07–0.55]).ConclusionBoth anti‐Sp4 and anti‐CCAR1 autoantibody subgroups are negatively associated with CAM. Although the magnitude of this association is substantial, cancer occasionally occurs in patients positive for either specificity. Conversely, approximately half of anti‐TIF1γ–positive patients with DM are negative for both antibodies (anti‐Sp4/anti‐CCAR1 negative), and thus this subgroup may warrant more intensive cancer surveillance.

INTRODUCTION

Recent data have demonstrated the presence of anti–cell division cycle and apoptosis regulator 1 (anti‐CCAR1) and anti‐Sp4 in patients with dermatomyositis (DM), 1 , 2 , 3 , 4 specifically patients with anti–transcription intermediary factor 1γ (anti‐TIF1γ) DM. In multiple cohorts, patients producing anti‐CCAR1 or anti‐Sp4 in isolation have demonstrated a lower risk of cancer‐associated myositis (CAM), potentially proving useful for cancer risk‐stratification in patients with new‐onset anti‐TIF1γ DM. However, it is unknown what proportion of patients with anti‐TIF1γ DM have only anti‐SP4, only anti‐CCAR1, or both. Furthermore, the relationship between each of these serologic subgroups and CAM remains to be defined. In this report, we have measured the frequency of these serologic subgroups and analyzed their association with CAM in two large DM cohorts.

PATIENTS AND METHODS

Study population

We applied the following inclusion criteria when selecting the patient populations for this study: (1) patients seen in the outpatient clinics at either Stanford University Medical Center or the Johns Hopkins Myositis Center with a diagnosis of DM according to classification criteria defined by Bohan and Peter 5 or the 2017 American College of Rheumatology and EULAR idiopathic inflammatory myopathy criteria 6 ; (2) patients aged >18 at DM symptom onset; (3) anti‐TIF1γ antibody positive by enzyme‐linked immunosorbent assay (ELISA; Medical & Biological Laboratories Co, Ltd [MBL]) using the >32‐unit cutoff defined by the manufacturer currently used for research purposes only; (4) banked serum samples available for research autoantibody analyses. Serum samples used in this study have been banked for a median of eight years (interquartile range 6–13 years).

DM onset was defined as the first patient‐reported symptom, including rash, weakness, myalgia, or dyspnea. CAM was defined as any malignancy diagnosed by performing a tissue biopsy, excluding nonmelanoma skin cancer, within three years of DM symptom onset. Cancer index date was defined as the date of cancer diagnosis, or in patients in whom the cancer was in clinical remission and later recurred, either the date of recurrence or original diagnosis was used, whichever was closest to the date of DM symptom onset.

Anti‐Sp4, anti‐CCAR1, and other autoantibody assays

Antibodies against CCAR1 and Sp4 were assayed by ELISA using serum samples at 1:400 dilution, as detailed previously. 2 , 3 The same anti‐CCAR1–positive serum, carefully selected with an optical density reading in the linear range of the assay, was included as a reference in every anti‐CCAR1 ELISA, and all absorbances were calibrated relative to it. Anti‐Sp4 ELISAs were standardized with an appropriate reference serum in the same way. The mean + 3 SDs of the values obtained by assaying serum samples from healthy controls was used as the cutoff for assigning antibody positivity (0.307 and 0.313 calibrated OD units for the CCAR1 and Sp4 ELISAs, respectively). Because the Sp4 used to coat the wells for the anti‐Sp4 ELISA was Gluthathione S‐Transferase (GST)‐tagged, all serum samples that were positive in this ELISA were tested in a subsequent ELISA in which GST was used to coat the wells. Samples that tested positive for both were assumed to be anti‐GST positive and were considered negative for Sp4 antibodies.

Anti‐TIF1γ antibodies were assayed by ELISA (MBL) using the >32‐unit cutoff defined by the manufacturer. We have previously demonstrated the anti‐TIF1γ MBL ELISA readout corresponds well with immunoprecipitation and other Clinical Laboratory Improvement Amendments–certified tests in the United States. 7 , 8 However, given concerns of cross‐reactivity between anti‐TIF1γ antibodies and anti–Mi‐2, 9 we assayed all 305 patients for anti–Mi‐2 antibodies using Euroimmun line blot and/or MBL ELISA. All clinical information and serum samples were collected under institutional review board–approved protocols, and all patients and healthy controls provided written informed consent to participate.

Statistical analyses

Descriptive statistics were reported for demographic and clinical characteristics. Student's t‐test, Fisher's exact test, and Wilcoxon's rank sum test were used to compare subgroups. All analyses were performed using R Statistical Software 10 (v4.3.1; R Core Team 2023).

RESULTS

Patient cohorts

A total of 305 anti‐TIF1γ–positive patients with DM met inclusion criteria. Of these, 169 patients were from Johns Hopkins and 136 patients were from Stanford. Individuals in the Johns Hopkins cohort were younger (median age at DM onset at Johns Hopkins was 48 years, compared to 52 years at Stanford; P = 0.02). Slightly more patients from Johns Hopkins were women (85% vs 77%; P = 0.09), and more were White (84% vs 66%; P < 0.001). There were 25 patients with CAM from Johns Hopkins (14.8%) and 34 patients with CAM from Stanford (25%) (Table 1). On testing both cohorts for anti–Mi‐2 antibodies, 9 of 169 (5%) were positive in the Johns Hopkins cohort, and 9 of 136 (7%) were positive in the Stanford cohort.

Co‐occurrence of anti‐CCAR1 and anti‐Sp4 and association with contemporaneous cancer

In both cohorts, anti‐TIF1γ–positive patients with DM with antibodies against CCAR1 alone, anti‐Sp4 alone, both, or neither were found (Figure 1). The two independent cohorts were strikingly similar: The frequency for anti‐Sp4 was 35% (59 of 169) in the Johns Hopkins cohort, and 30% (41 of 136) in the Stanford set. Antibodies against CCAR1 were found in 35% (59 of 169, Johns Hopkins) and 32% (44 of 136, Stanford) of patients. Twenty‐two percent (37 of 169) of patients in the Johns Hopkins cohort were positive for both anti‐Sp4 and CCAR1, compared to 21% (28 of 136) in the Stanford cohort. In both cohorts, this dual positivity was negatively associated with CAM (Table 2). The odds ratios (ORs) for CAM in patients with anti‐Sp4, anti‐CCAR1, and both from Johns Hopkins were 0.07 (95% confidence interval [CI] 0.00–0.35), 0.14 (95% CI 0.02–0.51), and 0.14 (95% CI 0.01–0.71), respectively. For Stanford, these numbers were 0.33 (95% CI 0.10–0.86), 0.28 (95% CI 0.09–0.75), and 0.44 (95% CI 0.12–1.29), respectively. The strongest negative association for CAM was found in patients who produced either anti‐Sp4 or anti‐CCAR1 (Johns Hopkins, OR 0.07 [95% CI 0.01–0.27]; Stanford, OR 0.22 [95% CI 0.07–0.55]). In patients negative for both anti‐Sp4 and anti‐CCAR1, there was a strong association with CAM (Johns Hopkins, OR 12.9 [95% CI 3.6–89.5]; Stanford, OR 4.5 [95% CI 1.8–13.2]).

Interestingly, despite a strong negative association with CAM, a few cancers were diagnosed in the anti‐Sp4 and anti‐CCAR1 subgroups. In the Johns Hopkins cohort, one patient with anti‐Sp4 and anti‐CCAR1 antibodies was diagnosed with melanoma in situ. In the Stanford anti‐Sp4–positive group with CAM, the cancers included breast cancer (stage IIA), endometrial adenocarcinoma (stage I), ovarian adenocarcinoma (stage IC with recurrence one year later), B cell lymphoma (stage IV), and cervical cancer (stage IV). Among the anti‐CCAR1–positive patients with CAM in the Stanford cohort, all cancers were the same as in the anti‐Sp4 group, with the exception of prostate cancer (stage IV) replacing ovarian cancer (Supplemental Table 1).

Given that several patients with either anti‐CCAR1 or anti‐Sp4 were diagnosed with cancer, we investigated whether anti‐CCAR1 and/or anti‐Sp4–positive patients with CAM differed systematically from anti‐CCAR1 and/or anti‐Sp4–negative patients with CAM. No variable—age at DM onset, sex, race, cancer type, or cancer stage—differed significantly (all P > 0.05, data not shown). In the six anti‐Sp4–positive patients with CAM, the median time from cancer diagnosis to Sp4 autoantibody detection was 1.75 years (range 0–5 years). Because this time is largely influenced by when the patient presented to the clinic and when the research serum sample was drawn, we subsequently explored the longitudinal nature of Sp4 antibody titers (Serial Sp4 titers section).

Serial Sp4 titers

Of the anti‐Sp4–positive patients from both cohorts, 16 had banked longitudinal serum samples available for study. The average time span of longitudinal serum sample collection on these 16 patients was 5 years (SD 3.8 years). Of the 16 patients, 6 (38%) normalized after an average time of 3.5 years. Of the six patients with normalized anti‐Sp4 titers, one (16%) achieved DM clinical remission. Of the 10 who did not normalize, 0 achieved DM clinical remission. Two patients initially negative for Sp4 antibodies became positive over time. Longitudinal bleeds were available on only three patients with CAM; over an average period of 7 years (range 1–14 years), all maintained high‐titer anti‐Sp4 antibodies and active DM.

DISCUSSION

In two large independent anti‐TIF1γ–positive DM cohorts, we demonstrate that approximately one‐third of patients are anti‐SP4 positive, one‐third are anti‐CCAR1 positive, and 20% are positive for both. Importantly, we found that both autoantibody subgroups are negatively associated with CAM. It is noteworthy that although the magnitude of this association is dramatic (OR < 0.50 for all subgroups), cancer at both low and high stages can still occur in patients with these autoantibodies.

This current study validates our prior work demonstrating a negative association between anti‐CCAR1 and CAM in two independent US cohorts. A negative association with CAM and anti‐SP4 has now been observed in four cohorts: Johns Hopkins, Stanford, Pittsburgh, and a Japanese cohort. 1 , 2 , 3 , 4 However, the current study of anti‐Sp4–positive patients is the largest to date; whereas the previous studies have a combined number of anti‐Sp4–positive patients equaling 52 across multiple rheumatic diseases, the current study includes 100 and exclusively focuses on the anti‐TIF1γ–positive DM subgroup. Our results reveal that as in anti‐CCAR1, patients with anti‐Sp4 can be diagnosed with CAM.

Because the overall numbers of patients with CAM were low in both anti‐Sp4 and anti‐CCAR1 subgroups, we could not confidently determine whether there was any synergy between the two antibodies regarding the negative association with cancer. However, several patients who were positive for both anti‐Sp4 and anti‐CCAR1 developed CAM. One possible explanation, other than the study being underpowered, is that these immune responses represent redundant antineoplastic mechanisms, and thus there is no observed additive effect. Alternatively, some forms of carcinogenesis may not be susceptible to the cancer‐protective mechanisms exerted by anti‐Sp4 or anti‐CCAR1. Future studies examining the tumor microenvironment and mutational burden are warranted to address these important issues.

On examination of serial anti‐SP4 titers, it was observed that some patients without cancer became negative for anti‐Sp4 autoantibodies over time, whereas others with cancer remained strongly positive over time. Our current hypothesis is that anti‐Sp4 and anti‐CCAR1 autoantibodies represent an antitumor immune response. If correct, one expected outcome is that there should be a positive correlation between anti‐Sp4 and absence of cancer at the time of blood draw (ie, anti‐Sp4 positivity correlates with the absence of cancer). For this hypothesis to hold, cancer status at the time of blood draw needs to be known. In the current work, cancer was defined as clinically detectable by conventional means (typically a mass on imaging with subsequent histopathologic confirmation). Although this is standard of care, this is a relatively crude, insensitive approach for detecting the presence of emerging cancer. Newer, more sensitive methods of cancer detection, such as free tumor DNA, may allow for the opportunity to better assess the relationship between longitudinal anti‐SP4 (and anti‐CCAR1) titers and emergence or disappearance of cancer. Thus, a patient seemingly without cancer might harbor a subclinical tumor that generates an anti‐Sp4 immune response that could conceivably eliminate the tumor. Without a continued antigenic (Sp4) source from the cancer, the anti‐Sp4 antibodies might decline over time. A second expected outcome is that an anti‐Sp4 immune response directed against the cancer may pressure the cancer to undergo changes to escape its anti‐tumor effects. This would effectively “decouple” the association between autoantibody titer and cancer protection. Studies to investigate the mutational environment of the cancer, paired with serum samples, would be informative and are currently underway.

There are several noteworthy limitations to our study. The frequency of CAM (15% in Johns Hopkins, 25% in Stanford University) is lower than that in other DM cohorts. Whether these findings are replicated in cohorts with higher frequencies of CAM remains to be studied. Given the low number of cancers in our anti‐Sp4 and anti‐CCAR1–positive subgroups, we were unable to confidently assess a synergistic relationship in terms of cancer association. Furthermore, based on our longitudinal data, patients can convert from positive to negative or negative to positive for anti‐Sp4. Thus, the associations may change depending on the serum collection date and its temporal relationship to DM onset, DM treatment, and cancer treatment.

In conclusion, one‐third of anti‐TIF1γ–positive patients with DM have anti‐Sp4, one‐third have anti‐CCAR1, and 20% have both. The presence of either autoantibody negatively associates with CAM, although cancers can still occur in patients who are anti‐Sp4 or anti‐CCAR1 positive. Conversely, approximately half of anti‐TIF1γ–positive patients with DM are negative for both antibodies (anti‐Sp4 and anti‐CCAR1 negative), and thus this subgroup may warrant more intensive cancer surveillance.
