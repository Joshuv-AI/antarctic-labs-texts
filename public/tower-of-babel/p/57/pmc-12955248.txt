Definition, Symptoms, Risk Factors, Epidemiology, and Autoimmunity of Long COVID
Authors: Muhammad Taufan Umasugi, Endah Fitriasari
Published: 2026

To the Editor,

We read with considerable interest the narrative review by Kozłowski et al., “Long COVID Definition, Symptoms, Risk Factors, Epidemiology and Autoimmunity: A Narrative Review.”1 The authors provide a valuable and timely synthesis of current knowledge, particularly in relation to autoimmune phenomena such as Guillain–Barré syndrome, antiphospholipid syndrome, and Kawasaki-like disease in the context of SARS-CoV-2 infection. Their figures summarizing the interactions between innate immunity, interferon signaling, inflammasome activation, and subsequent autoimmunity are especially instructive for clinicians who do not routinely work in clinical immunology.1

At the same time, we would like to offer 3 observations that, in my view, may help refine the conceptual and clinical implications of their work, and suggest priorities for future research.

First, the review understandably foregrounds autoimmunity as a unifying mechanistic explanation for long COVID. However, recent multidisciplinary syntheses emphasize that post-acute COVID-19 is better conceived as a heterogeneous syndrome arising from overlapping pathways, including persistent viral reservoirs, immune dysregulation, endothelial and microvascular injury, dysautonomia, coagulation abnormalities, and organ-specific damage, alongside autoimmune mechanisms.2,3 By focusing mainly on autoimmunity, the article risks inadvertently conveying a degree of causal certainty the present evidence base does not yet support. A more explicit acknowledgment that autoimmunity is one of several plausible, and possibly interacting, mechanisms would more closely align with recent high-level overviews and consensus pieces.2,4

Second, the strength of evidence underpinning an autoimmune etiology may merit a more critical appraisal. Kozłowski et al. rightly highlight the growing body of case reports, small cohorts, and mechanistic studies suggesting that autoantibodies and immune complexes can be detected in a subset of patients with long-lasting post-COVID symptoms.1 Yet, contemporary systematic reviews paint a more nuanced picture. For example, a recent Lancet Infectious Diseases review of autoantibodies in long COVID found that although most studies reported some association, there was substantial heterogeneity in design, timing of sampling, assay methodology, and case definitions, with frequent risk of bias and limited adjustment for confounding.5 Similarly, a systematic review of autoantibody persistence in post-COVID conditions concluded that the available data offer “restricted insight” into whether these antibodies are causal drivers, epiphenomena of tissue damage, or both.6

This distinction is clinically important. If autoantibodies and related immune signatures are partially epiphenomenal, then broad immunosuppression might not improve outcomes for many patients and may even be harmful. Recent work on autoimmunity in long COVID therefore emphasizes the need to move beyond presence/absence of antibodies toward rigorous phenotyping, longitudinal sampling, and functional characterization of immune responses.7 Integrating these perspectives more explicitly into the narrative review would help readers calibrate the evidential weight appropriately.

Third, the review’s disease-specific sections could benefit from stronger contextualization of absolute risk and competing explanations. Guillain–Barré syndrome (GBS) and pediatric inflammatory multisystem or Kawasaki-like syndromes are, as the authors note, well-recognized postinfectious autoimmune entities, and several mechanistic reviews have now summarized plausible links among SARS-CoV-2, molecular mimicry, and GBS.8, 9, 10 However, large-scale epidemiological analyses suggest that the incidence of GBS attributable to COVID-19 infection or vaccination remains low in absolute terms compared with the background population risk.11 Clarifying this balance would help clinicians counsel patients appropriately and avoid overattribution of diverse neurological complaints to autoimmune neuropathy in the absence of supportive clinical and electrophysiological findings.

A similar point applies to antiphospholipid antibodies and COVID-19–associated thrombosis. Kozłowski et al. highlight antiphospholipid syndrome as a key exemplar of post-COVID autoimmunity.1 Yet, recent systematic reviews and meta-analyses indicate that while antiphospholipid antibodies are frequently detected in patients with COVID-19, they are often low-titer or transient, and their presence does not consistently correlate with thrombotic events.12, 13, 14 Conversely, some observational work suggests that classical risk factors, critical illness, and COVID-associated coagulopathy may play a more dominant role in many thrombotic complications.12 A more granular discussion of antibody titers, persistence, and clinical correlation would therefore strengthen the inference that antiphospholipid autoimmunity is a central driver of long COVID rather than one contributory pathway in selected subgroups.

Perhaps the most pressing gap highlighted by the review, albeit only briefly, is the translational bridge from mechanism to intervention. As recent overviews of long COVID therapeutics have shown, there is currently a proliferation of small, heterogeneous trials testing antivirals, immunomodulators, anticoagulants, rehabilitation strategies, and neuromodulatory approaches, but very few studies stratify participants by underlying biology.15 The autoimmune framework articulated by Kozłowski and colleagues could provide a rational basis for stratification and trial design, but only if accompanied by concrete proposals: for example, prospective cohorts with serial immune profiling and autoantibody panels; nested case–control studies comparing patients with and without well-defined autoimmune phenomena; and biomarker-guided randomized trials of targeted immunotherapies.

In this regard, the diagrams presented in the review could be further leveraged as testable hypotheses rather than purely illustrative models. For instance, the proposed roles of persistent inflammasome activation, type I interferon perturbation, and ADAR-mediated RNA editing suggest specific candidate biomarkers (e.g., NLRP3-related mediators, interferon-stimulated gene signatures, ADAR activity) that could be integrated into multi-omic phenotyping frameworks now being piloted in large long COVID consortia.1,2 Explicitly linking these mechanistic schemata to concrete study designs would greatly enhance the review’s impact for both clinicians and translational researchers.

In summary, Kozłowski et al. have produced a timely and informative narrative overview of autoimmunity in long COVID, which will be of interest to clinicians seeking to understand the complex immunopathology of this condition. We respectfully suggest that future iterations of this work, or related reviews, might (1) situate autoimmunity more explicitly within the broader spectrum of long COVID mechanisms; (2) more critically appraise the causal inferences that can be drawn from the existing autoantibody literature; and (3) articulate clearer research and trial designs that would allow autoimmune endotypes of long COVID to be identified and targeted in a robust, biomarker-driven manner. Such developments would, we believe, further enhance the journal’s contribution to this rapidly evolving field and help bring much-needed clarity to patients and clinicians confronting the long-term consequences of SARS-CoV-2 infection.
