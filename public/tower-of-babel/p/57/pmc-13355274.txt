A propensity score-matched comparison of medium- to long-term outcomes between plasmakinetic enucleation of the prostate and transurethral resection of the prostate for benign prostatic hyperplasia
Authors: Penglei Mao, Zhangming Lei, Jianyong Zhu, Feng Ye
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundTransurethral resection of the prostate (TURP) remains the standard surgical treatment for benign prostatic hyperplasia (BPH), yet its the adoption of alternative techniques such as plasmakinetic enucleation of the prostate (PKEP). While PKEP has demonstrated favorable short-term outcomes, comparative evidence on mid- to long-term functional efficacy, safety, and the associated learning curve relative to TURP remains limited. This study aims to compare perioperative outcomes, mid-to-long-term functional efficacy, safety, and learning curves between PKEP and TURP in patients with BPH.MethodsA retrospective analysis was conducted on BPH patients treated between January 2017 and December 2022. Propensity score matching (PSM) with a 1:2 ratio and a caliper value of 0.10 was employed to balance baseline characteristics, resulting in 159 matched patients (53 in the PKEP group and 106 in the TURP group). Perioperative outcomes, along with International Prostate Symptom Score (IPSS), quality of life (QOL) score, maximum urinary flow rate (Qmax), post-void residual urine volume (PVR), and International Index of Erectile Function-5 (IIEF-5) scores were assessed at 3 to 24 months postoperatively. Complications were classified using the Clavien-Dindo grading system, and learning curves were analyzed using the cumulative sum (CUSUM) method.ResultsThe PKEP group demonstrated significantly shorter operative time, intraoperative irrigation fluid usage, postoperative bladder irrigation duration and volume, catheterization time, and hospital stay compared to the TURP group (all P<0.05). Additionally, the PKEP group experienced a significantly smaller reduction in postoperative hemoglobin levels (P=0.01). At 24 months postoperatively, the PKEP group achieved superior IPSS scores, Qmax, and IIEF-5 scores compared to the TURP group (IPSS: 6.00 vs. 8.00, P=0.01; Qmax: 24.06 vs. 22.46 mL/s, P=0.02; IIEF-5: 18.57 vs. 16.28, P=0.002). Furthermore, the PKEP group had a significantly lower incidence of erectile dysfunction and Clavien-Dindo II complications (both P<0.05). Learning curve analysis indicated that PKEP achieved surgical proficiency in efficiency (approximately 20 cases) and bleeding control more rapidly than TURP, with minimal or no initial learning phase required for bleeding control.ConclusionsPKEP demonstrates comparable mid-to-long-term efficacy to TURP but offers advantages in terms of reduced invasiveness, higher perioperative efficiency and safety, and a lower incidence of Clavien-Dindo II complications.

Highlight box

Key findings

• Compared with transurethral resection of the prostate (TURP), plasmakinetic enucleation of the prostate (PKEP) yielded shorter operative time, less hemoglobin loss, faster recovery, and fewer Clavien-Dindo grade II complications.

• At 24 months, PKEP produced superior International Prostate Symptom Score (IPSS) (6 vs. 8), maximum urinary flow rate (Qmax) (24.1 vs. 22.5 mL/s), and International Index of Erectile Function-5 (IIEF-5) scores (18.6 vs. 16.3), and a lower erectile dysfunction rate

• Cumulative sum (CUSUM) analysis showed PKEP reached operative proficiency at approximately 20 cases with stable hemostasis from the outset, while TURP exhibited continuous refinement.

What is known and what is new?

• TURP is the standard surgical treatment for benign prostatic hyperplasia but carries bleeding-related morbidity. PKEP shows favorable short-term results, yet long-term, propensity score-matched evidence comparing functional outcomes, safety, and learning curves remains limited.

• This matched study provides robust 24-month data demonstrating that PKEP maintains advantages in voiding, erectile function, and safety, and reveals a shorter, more predictable learning curve, even among surgeons already experienced in TURP.

What is the implication, and what should change now?

• PKEP can serve as an efficient, cost-effective first-line enucleation option where laser infrastructure is unavailable. Its compact ~20-case learning curve facilitates rapid incorporation into surgical training, enabling wider dissemination and long-term benefit for more patients.

Introduction

Benign prostatic hyperplasia (BPH) is one of the most prevalent urological conditions among middle-aged and elderly men, with its incidence increasing significantly with age. Studies indicate that over 40% of men in this demographic are affected by BPH. The primary pathological feature of BPH is an enlargement of the prostate gland, which leads to urethral compression and subsequent lower urinary tract symptoms (LUTS) (1). These symptoms include frequent urination, urgency, nocturia, and difficulty in urination, significantly reducing patients’ quality of life (QoL). If left untreated, BPH can progress to severe complications such as acute urinary retention, recurrent urinary tract infections, or even kidney dysfunction. As a result, timely and effective treatment is essential (2). Over the past few decades, transurethral resection of the prostate (TURP) has been widely regarded as the “gold standard” surgical treatment for BPH (3). TURP involves the transurethral removal of hyperplastic prostate tissue, which effectively alleviates obstructive symptoms and improves bladder function. However, despite its proven efficacy and cost-effectiveness, several limitations of TURP have become evident. First, because TURP involves piecemeal resection, residual adenomatous tissue may remain, increasing the likelihood of recurrence. Second, the use of monopolar energy in TURP can cause intraoperative and postoperative thermal damage, leading to complications such as urethral stricture and urinary incontinence (4,5). In recent years, new surgical techniques have been developed to address the limitations of TURP. Among these, plasmakinetic enucleation of the prostate (PKEP) combines principles from TURP and suprapubic prostatectomy. PKEP uses bipolar electrodes and conductive solutions to generate ionized plasma, and it employs saline as the irrigation fluid instead of glycine or sorbitol, significantly reducing the risks of intraoperative bleeding and transurethral resection syndrome (6,7). Although PKEP has demonstrated safety and efficacy in several studies, its long-term clinical value remains under investigation, particularly concerning the durability of symptom relief, reoperation rates, and its impact on sexual function (8,9). To address these issues, this study used propensity score matching (PSM) to control for confounding factors and conducted longitudinal data analysis to systematically compare the mid-to-long-term clinical efficacy and complications of PKEP and TURP in patients with BPH. We present this article in accordance with the STROBE reporting checklist (available at https://tau.amegroups.com/article/view/10.21037/tau-2026-0229/rc).

Methods

Clinical data

This study was conducted in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki and its subsequent amendments. The study was approved by the Ethical Committee of Quzhou People’s Hospital, and individual consent for this retrospective analysis was waived. This retrospective study analyzed patients with BPH who underwent TURP or PKEP at Quzhou People’s Hospital between January 2017 and December 2022. Following a screening process, 1,008 patients were included in the analysis. The detailed inclusion and exclusion process is presented in Figure 1.

Flowchart of patients. Inclusion and exclusion criteria

The inclusion criteria were as follows: (I) patients with persistent urinary dysfunction unresponsive to conservative medical treatment for over 1 year; (II) urodynamic evidence of severe bladder outlet obstruction with a maximum urinary flow rate (Qmax) <10 mL/s; (III) adequate bladder detrusor function with a post-void residual urine volume (PVR) ≥50 mL; (IV) preoperative International Index of Erectile Function-5 (IIEF-5) score ≥12; and (V) a diagnosis of BPH confirmed by digital rectal examination, imaging (e.g., ultrasound), and postoperative pathology.

Exclusion criteria included: (I) patients with bladder tumors; (II) a history of prostate tumors or prior prostate surgery; (III) severe systemic diseases; or (IV) long-term use of medications that significantly affect sexual function.

Surgical methods

All patients underwent continuous epidural anesthesia and were positioned in the lithotomy posture. A suprapubic cystostomy was performed, and low-pressure irrigation was maintained throughout the procedure. The TURP procedure was conducted using the Wolf resectoscope system manufactured by Wolf Medical (Germany). The energy settings were configured as follows: cutting power at 100–140 watts (W) and coagulation power at 60–80 W. A 5% glucose solution served as the bladder irrigation fluid during the procedure. The transurethral plasma enucleation of the prostate (PKEP) procedure was performed using the Gyrus bipolar plasma resection system manufactured by Gyrus Medical (UK). The energy settings were adjusted as follows: cutting power at 140–160 W and coagulation power at 80–100 W. During this procedure, 0.9% normal saline was used as the bladder irrigation fluid.

TURP group

The TURP procedure was conducted under video endoscopic monitoring. The surgeon assessed the morphology and degree of hypertrophy in the urethra, verumontanum, middle lobe, and bilateral prostate lobes. Key anatomical landmarks, including the ureteral orifices, verumontanum, and external urethral sphincter, were carefully identified. Resection was performed sequentially, starting with the middle lobe, followed by the bilateral lobes and the anterior commissure. A layered dissection approach was employed, progressively advancing down to the surgical capsule. Hemostasis was promptly achieved using electrocautery. Upon completion of the resection, the surgical field was meticulously inspected to confirm the absence of active bleeding or residual adenomatous tissue. Finally, a urinary catheter was inserted, and continuous bladder irrigation was initiated.

PKEP group

In the PKEP procedure, a circumferential incision was made at the deep urethral mucosa near the posterior surgical capsule behind the pubic symphysis. The middle lobe was enucleated retrogradely, starting from the distal mucosa and progressing toward the bladder neck. A plasma electrode was used to carefully dissect between the adenoma and the surgical capsule, creating a cleavage plane and extending it toward the bladder neck until the circumferential fibers of the bladder neck were exposed. Similarly, enucleation of the bilateral lobes began at the 5 o’clock or 7 o’clock position at the apex of the prostate and proceeded along the surgical capsule plane in a clockwise or counterclockwise direction until reaching the 12 o’clock position, effectively interrupting the adenoma’s blood supply. The enucleated adenomatous tissue was then excised using a plasma loop electrode and pushed en bloc into the bladder and subsequently fragmented using a mechanical tissue morcellator. The morcellated chips were then aspirated out of the bladder. Postoperatively, meticulous hemostasis was ensured, a urinary catheter was inserted, and continuous bladder irrigation was initiated.

Surgeons and technique adoption

All procedures in both groups (PKEP and TURP) were performed by the same two senior urologists. Both are board-certified with over 15 years of endoscopic urology practice and had each performed more than 500 TURP procedures independently before the study period. This establishes their profound proficiency with the resectoscope and standard BPH management. It is critical to clarify that PKEP was a newly introduced technique at our institution at the start of this study. The 53 PKEP cases analyzed represent the complete initial experience of these two surgeons with this modality. To mitigate the learning curve, both surgeons completed a structured transition program (simulation training and mentored cases under an experienced PKEP mentor).

Study indicators

Baseline data

Basic patient information, including age, height, body mass index (BMI), duration of BPH, prostate volume, comorbidities, and serum total prostate-specific antigen (tPSA) levels, was collected.

Perioperative indicators

Clinical parameters related to surgery were recorded, including operative time, total volume of intraoperative irrigation fluid, weight of resected tissue, and preoperative and 24-hour postoperative hemoglobin levels along with their decline. Additionally, the duration and volume of postoperative bladder irrigation, catheterization time, and length of postoperative hospital stay were documented. Prostate weight was measured via transrectal ultrasound and calculated using the following formula: prostate weight = transverse diameter × anteroposterior diameter × longitudinal diameter × 0.52 × 1.05.

Efficacy and functional outcome indicators

Efficacy was primarily evaluated using internationally recognized scales, including the International Prostate Symptom Score (IPSS), QoL score, Qmax, and PVR. Patients underwent regular follow-up assessments preoperatively and at 3 months, 6 months, 1 year, and 2 years postoperatively. Sexual function was assessed using the International Index of Erectile Function-5 (IIEF-5) score, with an IIEF-5 score below 12 indicating erectile dysfunction. Retrograde ejaculation was defined as the presence of an ejaculation sensation without semen exiting through the urethra during orgasm, accompanied by the detection of a significant number of sperm in the urine sample collected after sexual activity.

Postoperative mid-to-long-term complications

There is no universally accepted definition for the time frame of mid-to-long-term complications following TURP and PKEP procedures. Considering the extent of surgical trauma and recovery time, this study defined mid-to-long-term complications as symptoms persisting beyond 6 months postoperatively, diagnosed by clinical physicians or confirmed via ultrasound examination.

Statistical analysis

Data analysis was conducted using R software (version 4.5.2). Statistical inference employed two-sided tests, with a P value of less than 0.05 considered statistically significant. For continuous data, normality was evaluated using the Shapiro-Wilk test. Data following a normal distribution were reported as mean ± standard deviation (mean ± SD), and group comparisons were performed using the independent samples t-test. For data not following a normal distribution, results were presented as median and interquartile range [median (IQR)], and group comparisons were conducted using the Mann-Whitney U test. Categorical data were expressed as counts (percentages), with group comparisons performed using either the χ2 test or Fisher’s exact test, as appropriate. To adjust for baseline confounding, a generalized linear model (GLM) was used to calculate propensity scores, followed by nearest-neighbor matching in a 1:2 ratio without replacement. The caliper value was set to 0.10 times the standard deviation of the propensity score. The effectiveness of matching was assessed by calculating the standardized mean difference (SMD) for all covariates, with an SMD <0.1 indicating adequate balance between groups (10). Covariate balance after matching was visualized using a Love plot. For variables measured repeatedly over time, such as IPSS, QoL, Qmax, and IIEF-5, trends were analyzed using the Friedman test. Pairwise comparisons were adjusted using the Bonferroni correction method. To investigate the impact of surgical methods on postoperative complications, a conditional logistic regression model was fitted using the clogit() function from the survival package on the propensity-matched sample. Variables with an SMD >0.1 after matching were included as covariates for adjustment. Learning curve analysis was performed using cumulative sum (CUSUM) curves.

Comparison of IPSS, QOL, Qmax, PVR, and IIEF-5 between the two groups

Preoperatively, no statistically significant differences were observed between the two groups in any of the assessed scores (all P>0.05). At 24 months postoperatively, the median IPSS score in the PKEP group was significantly lower than that in the TURP group (6.00 vs. 8.00, P=0.01). However, no significant differences in QOL scores were found between the groups at any time point.

The Qmax values in the PKEP group were significantly higher than those in the TURP group at 6 months (25.04±4.03 vs. 22.80±4.71 mL/s, P=0.002) and 24 months (24.06±3.92 vs. 22.46±4.12 mL/s, P=0.02) postoperatively. Similarly, PVR values were significantly lower in the PKEP group compared to the TURP group during the early postoperative period (3 and 6 months, both P<0.05).

Moreover, IIEF-5 scores in the PKEP group were significantly better than those in the TURP group at 6 months (19.47±2.71 vs. 17.57±2.95, P<0.001) and 24 months (18.57±3.66 vs. 16.28±5.32, P=0.002) postoperatively (Table 4). Both groups demonstrated significant time-dependent improvements in IPSS, QOL, Qmax, and PVR scores postoperatively. However, the overall time-dependent trend for IIEF-5 in the TURP group did not reach statistical significance (Figure 3).

Comparison of postoperative complications or adverse events between the two groups

The overall incidence of postoperative complications did not differ significantly between the two groups (Table 5). However, the PKEP group had a significantly lower incidence of erectile dysfunction [5 (9.43%) vs. 36 (33.96%)] and Clavien-Dindo II complications [8 (15.09%) vs. 39 (36.79%)] compared to the TURP group (P<0.05).

To further corroborate the CUSUM findings and to statistically confirm the attainment of a technical plateau, we compared the early phase (first 26 cases) and late phase (subsequent 27 cases) within each surgical group. The full set of comparisons is presented in Table S1.

Within the PKEP group, no significant differences were observed between the early and late phases for either operative time (P=0.058, r=−0.26) or hemoglobin reduction (P=0.56, r=−0.08), with small-to-negligible effect sizes.

Discussion

Since its introduction, TURP has been considered the gold standard for surgical treatment of BPH due to its established efficacy (11). Nonetheless, inherent limitations such as perioperative bleeding, risks associated with monopolar current, and thermal tissue damage have driven the pursuit of more optimal surgical techniques (4,12). While numerous studies have compared TURP with other modalities, consensus on the optimal approach remains elusive (13,14). Our findings demonstrate that PKEP offers significant advantages over TURP, not only in perioperative efficiency and safety but also in long-term functional outcomes and surgical learnability.

The PKEP are largely attributed to its precise control over anatomical structures during surgery. PKEP employs an anatomically guided enucleation technique, following the natural adenoma boundary along the prostate’s surgical capsule. This approach ensures the anatomically more complete clearance of hyperplastic tissue while achieving efficient hemostasis by precisely managing the arterial blood supply to the prostate (14-16). Such anatomical precision significantly reduces the risk of diffuse bleeding, which can obscure the surgical field, and minimizes postoperative complications such as thrombus formation and increased irrigation requirements (17-20). The combination of vascular control and effective tissue removal forms the foundation for PKEP’s superior perioperative outcomes. In contrast, TURP employs a piecemeal, layer-by-layer resection technique. This method often results in an obscured surgical field due to bleeding, leading to incomplete hemostasis, longer operative times, and increased postoperative irrigation demands. PKEP’s anatomically precise handling and hemostatic techniques establish a “precision resection-immediate hemostasis” response chain, whereas TURP relies more heavily on the surgeon’s experience within a restricted and less visible operative field. This reliance on experience contributes to greater variability in surgical outcomes.

PKEP also offers advantages in long-term functional recovery, including improvements in Qmax and reductions in PVR, further emphasizing the benefits of complete adenoma enucleation (15). By thoroughly removing adenomatous tissue, PKEP not only alleviates urinary obstruction but also reduces the risk of postoperative complications such as chronic inflammation, tissue edema, or scar formation. These functional advantages help maintain long-term urethral morphology and ensure sustained urinary flow. Conversely, TURP’s piecemeal resection technique can result in irregular cavity morphology and residual adenomatous tissue, particularly at the prostate apex. These factors may limit urinary flow postoperatively and increase the risk of scar formation, thereby negatively impacting long-term functional recovery.

Although some studies suggest that enucleation techniques may affect sexual function due to proximity to the neurovascular bundle (21-23), PKEP mitigates this risk through the use of bipolar plasma energy. The clear surgical field and precise anatomical dissection in PKEP minimize unnecessary traction and thermal damage to the neurovascular bundle, particularly at the prostate apex. This minimally invasive approach likely reduces the risk of postoperative sexual dysfunction.

However, existing evidence regarding the effect of PKEP and TURP on postoperative erectile function (IIEF-5) is inconsistent. Systematic reviews and meta-analyses suggest that the overall change in IIEF-5 from baseline after TURP is not significant, whereas a mild decline in IIEF-5 may occur after PKEP. However, some singlecenter retrospective or cohort studies have observed higher IIEF-5 scores in the PKEP group compared with the TURP group, or no significant difference between the two. Therefore, the more clinically reasonable conclusion is that the net effect of the two procedures on erectile function is generally comparable, with considerable inter-individual variability and study-design heterogeneity (24-26). Some studies have also proposed that PKEP provides a clearer surgical field, less thermal injury, and a lower probability of nerve damage, which could theoretically reduce the negative impact on sexual function. However, the same meta-analysis still observed a decline in IIEF-5 in the PKEP group, suggesting that this “theoretical advantage” may not necessarily translate into a stable population-level benefit (24,25). Current evaluations of sexual function primarily use subjective scales, such as the IIEF-5. Future research should incorporate objective assessment methods, such as nerve conduction studies or functional magnetic resonance imaging (MRI), to better understand the biological mechanisms behind these differences and validate existing findings.

One of PKEP’s most significant advantages is its shorter learning curve, which facilitates its adoption and widespread use. Studies indicate that operative times for PKEP stabilize after approximately 20 cases, with hemoglobin levels during surgery remaining largely unaffected by the surgeon’s learning phase. This suggests that PKEP’s effective hemostasis depends more on the inherent technique and its anatomical consistency than on surgeon experience. This alignment between technique and anatomical principles makes PKEP outcomes more predictable and reduces variability among surgeons.

To statistically validate these CUSUM findings, we conducted a subgroup analysis, comparing the early phase (first 26 cases) and late phase (subsequent 27 cases) within the PKEP group. No significant differences were observed between the early and late phases for either operative time (P=0.058, r=−0.26) or hemoglobin reduction (P=0.56, r=−0.08), with small-to-negligible effect sizes (Table S1).

The rapid stabilization observed in our study is attributable to two factors. First, the inherent anatomical consistency of the PKEP reduces case-to-case technical variability. Second, and critically, both surgeons in this study possessed over 15 years of TURP experience and had each performed more than 500 TURP procedures before transitioning to PKEP. This extensive background in endoscopic prostate surgery provided highly transferable skills in endoscopic anatomy, hemostatic techniques, and fluid management, likely compressing the learning curve substantially. Consequently, our finding of a learning plateau at approximately 20–26 cases should be understood as specific to experienced endourologists undertaking a structured transition. For novice endoscopic surgeons without comparable TURP experience, the PKEP learning curve may be considerably longer.

In contrast, the TURP group in our study demonstrated a different temporal pattern: even though TURP was a mature technique for both surgeons, significant improvements were still observed between the early and late phases in operative time (P=0.01, r=−0.337) and hemoglobin reduction (P=0.03, d=0.607). This ongoing refinement, even in an already-established technique, stands in stark contrast to the early stabilization achieved with PKEP and further underscores it’s learnability advantage. The shorter, more predictable learning curve of PKEP facilitates its more rapid adoption across various levels of healthcare institutions, enabling a greater number of surgeons to master this advanced surgical technique and expanding its benefits to a broader patient population.

Furthermore, high-quality randomized controlled trial evidence indicates that both PKEP and holmium laser enucleation of the prostate (HoLEP) are effective and safe transurethral enucleation modalities for BPH, with comparable long‑term improvements in urinary symptoms and urodynamic parameters. However, in terms of perioperative outcomes, HoLEP appears to hold an overall advantage: it is associated with shorter total operative time, less blood loss, shorter bladder irrigation and catheterization durations, shorter hospital stay, and a lower recatheterization rate. Conversely, the primary advantages of PKEP lie in its quieter operation and the absence of additional disposable laser equipment costs. These considerations make PKEP a particularly attractive first-line enucleation option in healthcare settings where holmium laser infrastructure is not readily available or where procedural cost containment is a priority. The choice between PKEP and HoLEP should therefore be individualized based on institutional resources, surgeon expertise, and patient‑specific factors. Future prospective trials directly comparing PKEP with HoLEP and other emerging enucleation modalities are warranted to further define their relative merits (27).

Limitations

This study employed a retrospective design, which makes it impossible to completely eliminate the influence of unmeasured confounding factors. All surgeries were performed by experienced surgeons, potentially limiting the generalizability of the findings to less experienced practitioners or beginners. Additionally, the study lacks long-term follow-up data, which precludes an evaluation of long-term outcomes. Due to hospital-specific constraints on the implementation of PKEP, the number of patients undergoing PKEP was significantly smaller than that of TURP, potentially introducing selection bias.

Conclusions

In summary, for BPH patients requiring surgical treatment, PKEP and TURP demonstrate comparable mid-to-long-term efficacy. However, PKEP offers several advantages, including less trauma, greater perioperative safety, faster recovery, and superior outcomes in postoperative urinary function improvement and sexual function preservation. Furthermore, the relatively shorter learning curve of PKEP facilitates its adoption across a broader range of healthcare institutions. Despite these findings, there are several limitations in this study, including its limited sample size and single-center design. Therefore, further multicenter studies with larger sample sizes and extended follow-up durations are necessary to validate these results.
