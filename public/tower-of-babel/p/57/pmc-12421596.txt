Wearable device-measured circadian rest–activity rhythm with mortality risk in patients with cancer
Authors: Xionge Mei, Nana Zheng, Biao Li, Yue Liu, Lulu Yang, Tong Luo, Ngan Yin Chan, Joey Wy Chan, Yaping Liu, Xiao Tan, Christian Benedict, Yun Kwok Wing, Jihui Zhang, Hongliang Feng
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractObjectivesThe objectives were to examine the associations between accelerometer-measured circadian rest–activity rhythm (CRAR), the most prominent circadian rhythm in humans and the risk of mortality from all-cause, cancer and cardiovascular disease (CVD) in patients with cancer.Methods7456 cancer participants from the UK Biobank were included. All participants wore accelerometers from 2013 to 2015 and were followed up until 24 January 2024, with a median follow-up of 9.00 years. The multidimensional parameters of the CRAR were calculated using the 7-day accelerometer data collected under free-living conditions. Cox proportional hazard models were used to evaluate the associations between CRAR and all-cause, cancer and CVD mortality.ResultsAmong 7456 cancer patients (mean age: 65.7±6.87 years; 58.85% women) aged 44–79 years, 934 (12.5%) deaths occurred over 9.00 years (64 525 person-years). CRAR disruptions, including low amplitude, low mesor and high fragmentation, were significantly associated with an increased risk of all-cause mortality (adjusted HR range, 1.30–2.00), cancer (adjusted HR range, 1.46–1.83) and CVD mortality (adjusted HR range, 1.73–2.66) in patients with cancer.DiscussionThese associations were robust across various cancer types. In addition, CRAR disruptions, particularly low amplitude, exceeded multiple traditional risk factors such as poor sleep, smoking, alcohol consumption, obesity and unhealthy diet in predicting mortality.ConclusionCRAR parameters may serve as novel and robust predictors of mortality in patients with cancer.Keywords: Data Interpretation, Statistical; Health Information Management; Home Care Services; Patient Outcome Assessment; Outcome and Process Assessment, Health Care

Introduction

Cancer is a leading cause of death worldwide, accounting for nearly one-fifth of all deaths by 2022.1 Therefore, identifying modifiable risk factors for mortality in patients with cancer is of paramount importance.2 In addition to clinical risk factors such as late detection, diagnosis and metastasis,3 the role of modifiable factors, including socioeconomic status and lifestyle, is increasingly recognised.4 Considering the prevalence of circadian rhythm disruptions in patients with cancer and the potential impact of these disruptions on the efficacy of cancer treatment,5 it is imperative to investigate the influence of circadian rhythm disruptions on mortality risk in patients with cancer.

Animal studies have demonstrated that circadian rhythm disruption can facilitate tumour initiation, progression and metastasis.6 7 Key components of circadian physiology, such as melatonin, may improve survival in patients with cancer undergoing treatment.8 A randomised controlled trial found that survival and chemotherapy tolerance were enhanced when patients with metastatic non-small cell lung cancer were concomitantly treated with melatonin.9 This evidence provides a robust foundation that patients with cancer who have a strong circadian patterns may have better chances of survival than those with disrupted circadian patterns. However, owing to the obvious difficulties in optimally measuring circadian rhythms in free-living individuals,10 there is a lack of compelling evidence linking circadian rhythm disruptions to mortality risk in patients with cancer.

In this cohort study, we reported the largest sample to date (n=7456) of objective 7-day monitoring of activity data, which enabled the continuous measurement of CRAR, the most prominent circadian rhythm in humans.11 The primary purpose of this study was to investigate the relationships between multidimensional CRAR parameters (eg, amplitude, timing, day-to-day stability and fragmentation) and all-cause, cancer and CVD mortality in patients with cancer. Circadian rhythm disruption is associated with increased CVD incidence and mortality.12 CVD is the leading cause of non-cancer-related mortality in patients with cancer.13 Therefore, CVD mortality was included as a key outcome of this study. Moreover, comparative analyses were performed to rank the predictive capability of the CRAR parameters and nine traditional predictors of mortality.

Methods

Participants and accelerometer assessment

Between 1 June 2013 and 23 December 2015, 106 053 participants agreed to participate and were provided with an Axivity AX3 wrist-worn triaxial accelerometer (Axivity, Newcastle, UK). Accelerometer data were collected for continuous 24-hour activity data for 7 consecutive days. A total of 103 712 raw accelerometer datasets were received and 8023 participants with a history of cancer at baseline were included in the study. 567 participants were excluded due to the following exclusion criteria: (1) data problem indicator; (2) lack of quality wear time; (3) calibration of quality based on their own data; (4) interrupted recording periods; and (5) data recording errors. The details of the participant selection for the current study are presented (online supplemental figure 1). The values of certain covariates were obtained from the touchscreen questionnaires at the time point closest to the accelerometer wear (online supplemental figure 1).

Exposures

The CRAR parameters were determined using the well-established non-parametric method (‘nparACT’ package, V.0.8) and extended cosinor model (‘ActCR’ package, V.0.2.0).14 15 The non-parametric parameters included the mean activity level of the least active 5-hour period (L5), the start time of L5, the mean activity level of the most active 10-hour period (M10), the start time of M10, relative amplitude (RA: (M10−L5)/(M10+L5)), intradaily variability (IV: fragmentation of the rhythm) and interdaily stability (IS: day-to-day stability/regularity). The extended cosinor model yields the cosinor amplitude (difference in activity between the peak and trough of the fitted curve), mesor (midline estimation statistic of rhythm) and acrophase (cosinor analysis to denote the phase angle corresponding to the peak of the fitted rhythm).11 Additionally, the dichotomy index (I<O) was included because it is an independent prognostic factor for survival in patients with cancer.16 I<O measures the percentage of activity per minute spent ‘in bed’ (I), which is less than the median activity spent ‘out of bed’ (O), and an I<O of 100% indicates a very abundant and regular CRAR (online supplemental table 1). The correlation matrix for the CRAR parameters is presented in online supplemental table 2.

Covariates

Covariates were obtained using three distinct methodologies: accelerometers, self-report questionnaires and registry records. The age of the participants was assessed at the time the accelerometer was worn. Accelerometer wear data were categorised by season. Spring was defined as March–May, summer as June–August, autumn as September–November and winter as December–February. Self-report questionnaires at baseline were used to determine sex (female/male), ethnicity (white/non-white), region of recruitment (England/Wales/Scotland) and the Townsend deprivation index (continuous). The latter is a measure of socioeconomic status and was based on the postcode of residence and included data on unemployment, car and home ownership, and household overcrowding. Body mass index (BMI) categories were constructed from height and weight measurements obtained during the baseline centre visits. The categories included normal weight or underweight (BMI<25 kg/m2), overweight (BMI25–30 kg/m2) and obesity (BMI≥30 kg/m2). The education level (higher education, any other qualification or no qualification), smoking status (never smoked, former smoker or current smoker), frequency of alcohol consumption (none current, up to two times per week, three or more times per week) and diet-related factors were obtained from touchscreen questions. Previous diagnoses of hypertension, diabetes, depression and longstanding illness were obtained from self-report questionnaires, hospital records and the death registry. The values of certain covariates, including BMI, education level, smoking status, alcohol consumption, healthy diet score, and diabetes history were obtained from touchscreen questionnaires at the time point closest to the accelerometer wear (online supplemental figure S2).

Outcomes

The outcomes were all-cause, cancer (ICD-10 codes C00-C97), and CVD (ICD-10 codes I00-I99) mortality. We measured CVD mortality to strengthen the interpretation by providing a more complete picture of health risks and mortality pathway. At the time of analysis, Cox regression analyses were censored at the date of death or the date of available mortality data (25 January 2024), whichever occurred first (online supplemental table 3).

Statistical analysis

The number of mortality events was sufficient, with at least 10 events per variable.17 Missing data were minimal, with less than 5% missing data for each covariate, and dispersed throughout the dataset. Multiple imputation, using the ‘mice’ R package (V.3.13.0), was employed to address missing values and reduce inferential bias.18 Exposures, covariates, mortality outcomes and time to outcomes were included in the imputation process.19 20 Baseline characteristics of the full and complete case samples were similar (online supplemental figure 3 and online supplemental table 4).

Cox proportional hazard models were used to assess the associations between CRAR properties and mortality risk, with HRs and 95% CIs calculated for the different adjustment models. Model 1 was adjusted for age and sex; model 2 included ethnicity, deprivation index, recruitment centre, education, season of accelerometer use, BMI, diet, alcohol consumption, smoking, illness, depression, hypertension, diabetes and CVD comorbidities; and model 3 included cancer-related factors (prognosis, behaviour, type and duration). Proportional hazard assumptions were met based on Schoenfeld residuals and cumulative risk curves (online supplemental figures 4–9). Seven sensitivity analyses were performed, including restricting the sample to complete cases; censoring data at 31 December 2019 to account for the COVID-19 pandemic; excluding death events within the first year of follow-up; excluding participants with prevalent comorbidities (long-standing illness, depression, hypertension and diabetes); excluding participants with a history of shift work; excluding participants with less than 6 days of accelerometer wear and using the Fine-Gray subdistribution hazards model by incorporating all-cause death as a competing risk for cancer and CVD cause mortality. Multicollinearity was minimal, as supported by variance inflation factors.21 Restricted cubic splines (RCSs) have been used to visualise the relationship between continuous CRAR properties and mortality. Subgroup analyses were performed to explore significant interactions between variables, such as age, sex and cancer prognosis.

The XGBoost machine learning method was used to rank the importance of the CRAR parameters and nine traditional risk factors,22 including moderate-to-vigorous physical activity (MVPA), sleep efficiency, sleep duration, smoking, alcohol consumption and obesity. XGBoost calculates variable importance based on performance improvements at each splitting point, measured by the Gini index, followed by gradient boosting. Optimal model performance was achieved through 10-fold cross-validation. SHapley Additive exPlanations (SHAPs) were also applied to assess the relative importance of CRAR parameters and lifestyle factors for all-cause, cancer and CVD mortality.23

All statistical tests were two tailed, and p≤0.05 was considered significant. To limit the rate at which false positives occurred among all analyses and in cases where any type I error rate was included, all p values in the fully adjusted models were corrected using the false discovery rate method. All statistical analyses were performed using the R software (V.4.0.3).

Over a median follow-up of 9.00 years, 934 participants died, 714 died of cancer and 229 died of CVD. The baseline characteristics of the 7456 participants are presented in table 1. The mortality group categories exhibited comparable baseline profiles for ethnicity, Townsend deprivation index, recruitment region, educational level and season of accelerometer use. Furthermore, participants who died from CVD were more prone to obesity, a history of hypertension and diabetes at baseline assessment.

IV demonstrated a significant overall correlation with all-cause and cancer mortality (poverall<0.05), but not with CVD mortality. The associations between I<O and all-cause, cancer and CVD mortality risks were statistically significant (poverall<0.05). No significant associations were found among L5 start time, M10 start time, IS, acrophase all-cause, cancer and CVD mortality risk.

Associations between CRAR quartiles and mortality risk

The results of the Cox regression analysis indicated that low RA, low M10, low cosinor amplitude, low mesor and low I<O were significantly associated with an increased risk of all-cause mortality (Q1 vs Q4: adjusted HR range, 1.30–2.00), cancer (Q1 vs Q4: adjusted HR range, 1.46–1.83) and CVD mortality (Q1 vs Q4: adjusted HR range, 1.73–2.66) in patients with cancer (figure 4). In addition, high IV was significantly correlated with a higher risk of all-cause (Q4 vs Q1: adjusted HR=1.30, 95% CI, 1.08–1.56) and cancer mortality (Q4 vs Q1: adjusted HR=1.46, 95% CI, 1.17 – 1.81), but not with CVD mortality. Furthermore, high L5 demonstrated a significant positive association with CVD mortality risk (Q4 vs Q1: adjusted HR=1.55, 95% CI, 1.05–2.29). However, other CRAR parameters, such as the M10 start time, L5 start time, IS and acrophase, exhibited no significant associations with all-cause, cancer or CVD mortality risk (online supplemental table 5).

Sensitivity analyses

The results remained robust to sensitivity analyses conducted using complete cases and censored data up to 25 January 2024, excluding death events within the first year of follow-up, participants with a history of shift work, participants with less than 6 days of accelerometer wear, and using the competing risk regression model (online supplemental tables 6–11). The observed associations among RA, IV, M10, cosinor amplitude, mesor, I<O and all-cause mortality and the association between L5 and CVD mortality remained consistent across all sensitivity analyses. Nevertheless, the correlation between IV and CVD mortality was not statistically significant when the participants with prevalent comorbidities were excluded (online supplemental table 12).

Interaction and subgroup analyses

Interaction and subgroup analyses demonstrated that the relationships between CRAR parameters, including RA, IV, M10, cosinor amplitude, mesor, I<O and mortality outcomes were valid across multiple settings. Significant interaction effects were observed on the additive scale for the sex and CRAR parameters (online supplemental table 13). Furthermore, significant interaction effects were observed for RA and cosinor amplitude with age, as well as for cosinor amplitude and mesor with smoking status. M10 and cosinor amplitude showed additive interactions with cancer prognosis (online supplemental tables 14–16). No significant interaction effects were observed between CRAR parameters and alcohol consumption or depression (online supplemental tables 17 and 18). The results of the subgroup analyses were consistent with those of the interaction analyses (online supplemental tables 19–30). Moreover, the correlations between CRAR parameters and mortality risk remained robust across cancer types, such as breast cancer, prostate cancer, gastrointestinal cancer and lymphatic and haematopoietic cancer, although several associations became marginally non-significant (adjusted p value range: 0.05–0.06, online supplemental tables 31–35).

Predictive capacity of CRAR parameters and traditional risk factors

The comparison analyses, conducted using the SHAPs method, demonstrated that among the 11 CRAR parameters and nine traditional factors, low RA exhibited the strongest predictive capacity for all-cause, cancer and CVD mortality, followed by I<O or smoking (figure 5). Similarly, Cox regression analyses showed that low RA exhibited the strongest association (adjusted HR range: 1.52–2.15) with mortality outcomes (online supplemental tables 36–38). Furthermore, we evaluated the predictive capacity of the CRAR parameters and traditional risk factors in patients with different cancer types. In eight of the 15 comparison models, low CRAR amplitude (low RA or low cosinor amplitude) exhibited the greatest predictive capacity. Among the participants with breast cancer, M10 demonstrated the highest predictive capacity (online supplemental figure 10). Overall, the predictive capacity of the CRAR parameters was consistent across different cancer types (online supplemental figures 11–14).

Discussion

The present study showed that CRAR disruptions, characterised by low amplitude, low mesor and high fragmentation, were significantly associated with an increased risk of all-cause, cancer and CVD mortalities. These associations were robust following a series of sensitivity and interaction analyses. Notably, these associations remained valid after controlling for age, sex, cancer prognosis, smoking status, alcohol consumption and depression. Moreover, the associations remained robust across the different cancer types. Comparative analyses demonstrated that the CRAR parameters, particularly low RA, exceeded multiple traditional risk factors for mortality, such as poor sleep, smoking, alcohol consumption, obesity and unhealthy diet, in predicting mortality outcomes. These findings highlight the robust predictive capacity of CRAR disruption for mortality risk in patients with cancer.

The findings of this study provide substantial evidence that a low I<O is an independent predictor of increased mortality risk among patients with cancer (adjusted HR range, 1.47–1.81). I<O is a circadian physiological biomarker that has been previously shown to provide consistent prognostic information regarding the survival of patients with cancer. A negative correlation was observed between low I<O and quality of life and/or mortality risk in patients with colorectal cancer (n=77–436).24 25 A cohort study of 84 patients with lung cancer reported a significant correlation between low I<O and poor overall survival.26 This study contributes to the existing knowledge base by demonstrating the predictive value of I<O across different cancer types, including breast, prostate, gastrointestinal, lymphatic and haematopoietic cancers. Previous evidence has also revealed that circadian rhythm disruption accelerates the progression of breast27 28 and prostate cancers.29

In humans, circadian rhythm assessment encompasses the phase (timing), amplitude, central tendency (mesor), day-to-day regularity, fragmentation and robustness of the rhythms.30 Growing evidence suggests that disturbances in these circadian measures are the most common parameters that lead to diverse adverse health consequences, such as CVD, mood disorders and neurodegenerative diseases.11 31 However, the association between multidimensional CRAR parameters and mortality risk in patients with cancer remains unclear. In addition to I<O, this study demonstrated that multidimensional CRAR disruptions (ie, low M10, high IV, low amplitude and low mesor) predicted an increased mortality risk in patients with cancer (adjusted HR range, 1.30–2.00 for all-cause mortality and adjusted HR range, 1.46–1.83 for cancer mortality). Our findings also demonstrated that the CRAR amplitude exceeded low MVPA, abnormal sleep duration and decreased sleep efficiency in predicting mortality. Collectively, our findings led to the hypothesis that CRAR disruptions, particularly decreases in amplitude, are promising markers of cancer prognosis.

Several possible mechanisms may contribute to the association between CRAR disruption and an increased mortality risk in patients with cancer. First, human genetic studies have indicated that decreased CRAR amplitude is associated with mutations in ZF21, which is involved in cell migration, a key process in tumour invasion.32 Second, CRAR disruption induces dysregulation of the natural immune response and T-cell cytokine production, implying that an impaired immune response leads to metastasis in different cancer types.33 34 Third, the mutation or deletion of circadian genes, such as Per1/2, Clock, Bmal1 and Cry1/2, can accelerate the progression of liver, lung, colorectal and ovarian cancers.35 36 Fourth, light-at-night can suppress endogenous melatonin secretion, a hormone with well-documented antineoplastic, antioxidant and immune-modulating properties and promote nocturnal activity, both of which may disturb circadian homeostasis. Although melatonin levels and light-at-night exposure were not directly assessed in this study, their potential role as mediators of circadian disruption and cancer mortality warrants investigation in future research. A better understanding provided by this study of how the CRAR amplitude affects pathophysiological processes can be leveraged to design disease prevention and treatment strategies for cancers.

Implications

First, this study identified CRAR parameters as robust markers for predicting survival in patients with cancer, demonstrating greater efficacy than other traditional risk factors. CRAR parameters can be readily and accurately measured, offering a promising avenue for identifying individuals at an ultrahigh risk in clinical practice. Second, CRAR may serve as a potential safety endpoint in cancer management. Previous studies have demonstrated that chemotherapy cycles can disrupt CRAR.37 Third, the finding that RA showed the strongest predictive ability highlights the importance of integrating the health effects of physical activity and its timing. Previous studies have typically measured and accumulated physical activity across nights and days, thus neglecting the health effects of the timing of physical activity. In terms of RA, high daytime physical activity is considered to be protective, whereas high night-time physical activity is detrimental. Lastly, our study indicates that improving CRAR could be a useful means to minimise the mortality risk in patients with cancer. In addition to physical exercise and good sleep, previous evidence has shown that chronotherapeutic interventions such as melatonin and bright light can improve CRAR in multiple disease conditions.38 Further interventional investigations should be performed to clarify the effects of improving the CRAR on cancer prognosis.

Limitations

First, although 7-day monitoring periods are commonly used in circadian rhythm monitoring studies because of their ability to capture CRAR across both weekdays and weekends and their reliability for within-year repeated assessments,39 it remains uncertain whether a 7-day accelerometer measurement adequately reflects longer-term behavioural patterns. Second, the observational nature of this study precludes causal inferences. Although we made extensive efforts to minimise the risks of confounding and reverse causality, such as controlling for a range of covariates and excluding events that occurred during the first year of follow-up, residual or unmeasured confounding factors cannot be ruled out. Third, the most population was white ethnicity (97.96%), and this homogeneity can limit the external validity of the study. Future studies should prioritise more representative sampling or validate findings in more diverse cohorts. Fourth, some covariates, such as lifestyle variables, were collected mainly during physical visits to the assessment centres, nearly a few years before the present study baseline. However, because these covariates are generally stable over time,40 this time lag is unlikely to weaken our findings. Fifth, this study encompassed diverse cancer types and performed subgroup analyses for several cancer categories, enabling the exploration of the widespread impact of CRAR dysregulation on survival across various cancer types. However, additional research is warranted to elucidate the relationship between CRAR and mortality in specific cancer types. Furthermore, information on cancer aggressiveness, such as stage, treatment and morphology, which are critical factors influencing prognosis and mortality, has been incorporated into the UK Biobank. Future studies investigations should consider these variables.

Conclusions

CRAR disruptions, characterised by low RA, low M10, low mesor, low cosinor amplitude, low I<O and high IV, are associated with increased mortality risk in patients with cancer. Furthermore, low RA levels exceeded multiple traditional risk factors for mortality, such as poor sleep, smoking, alcohol consumption, obesity and unhealthy diet, in predicting mortality outcomes. Further studies are needed to clarify the impact of CRAR on mortality risk in patients with cancer.
