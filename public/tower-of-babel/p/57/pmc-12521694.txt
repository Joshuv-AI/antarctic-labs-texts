Transitioning from deceptive to nondeceptive placebo treatment to reduce pathological skin-picking
Authors: Anne Schienle, Johannes Wilhelm, Sandra Tanzmeister
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
BackgroundDeceptive placebos (DPs) have been shown to alleviate symptoms of several mental disorders. However, the willingness of patients to transition from DP treatment to nondeceptive placebos (NDPs) has not been systematically investigated.MethodThis study examined whether individuals with pathological skin-picking (n = 69), who were initially administered oral capsules labeled as N-acetylcysteine (DPs; one per day over two weeks), would consent to continue the same treatment regimen when the capsules were presented as NDPs. The DP and NDP treatments were compared in terms of their reported effectiveness in reducing skin-picking symptoms and difficulties in emotion regulation. The average duration between the start of the DP and NDP trials was 17.4 weeks. Additionally, participants indicated their preference for DP or NDP treatment in an open-ended format.ResultsFollowing debriefing after the DP trial, 20 participants (mean age = 32 years; male-to-female ratio 1:19) agreed to enter the NDP trial. On average, these participants had reported clinically meaningful symptom reduction during the DP phase. By the end of the study, only four participants expressed a preference for NDPs, which did not result in statistically significant symptom reduction.ConclusionThe benefits of DP treatment for pathological skin-picking did not carry over to NDPs. Future research could explore whether extended psychoeducation increases the number of NDP responders.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1007/s44192-025-00286-3.

Introduction

Deceptive placebos (DPs) have demonstrated efficacy in alleviating symptoms of mental disorders, particularly anxiety and depression [19]. Recent studies have also identified individuals who respond to placebos in disorders characterized by body-focused repetitive behaviors, such as skin-picking disorder (SPD) and trichotillomania [13], Schienle et al., 2025). SPD has a prevalence of approximately 2–5% in the general population [1] and is characterized by recurrent and excessive skin manipulation (e.g., scratching, rubbing, squeezing). Such behaviors result in psychological distress, physical complications (e.g., wounds, infections, scarring), and functional impairment in various domains [1].

Despite their demonstrated efficacy, DPs pose significant ethical challenges. Their administration compromises patient autonomy and informed decision-making. Furthermore, disclosing DP use after treatment has been linked to negative patient reactions, such as disappointment or embarrassment [2, 5]. The discovery of deception by the patients may erode their trust in healthcare providers, which is particularly concerning in psychiatric care, where a strong therapeutic alliance is a crucial determinant of treatment efficacy [11, 21].

Conversely, some evidence suggests positive outcomes following debriefing after DP treatment [5, 8, 16, 23]. For instance, in a study involving patients with depression, individuals who received a DP alongside cognitive behavioral therapy reported a positive response to post-treatment debriefing. Specifically, these patients expressed satisfaction upon learning that the pharmacological component of their treatment was a placebo, as it reinforced their sense of self-efficacy by demonstrating that therapeutic progress was attributable to their own efforts [23].

An ethically acceptable alternative to DP treatment is the use of non-deceptive placebos (NDPs: placebos administered with full transparency to patients). Two meta-analyses [4, 27] have shown that NDPs can produce moderate effects across a range of conditions, particularly in terms of self-reported symptom relief for pain and emotional distress, when compared to no-treatment controls. NDPs were not superior to treatment as usual (see meta-analysis by [3]).

Studies directly comparing the effects of DPs and NDPs remain limited and yield heterogeneous results. While some investigations into pain processing have found both treatments to be equally effective (e.g., [6, 7]), others have reported superior outcomes for DPs (e.g., [20]).

The present study examined whether individuals with pathological skin-picking would consider NDP treatment after receiving DP capsules for two weeks, followed by debriefing. Perceived symptom reduction and improvement in emotion regulation (as indicated by questionnaire scores; SPS-R: [10], DERS: [14]) were compared between the two phases of placebo treatment (DP, NDP). Additionally, participants who chose NDP treatment were compared to those who declined it in terms of symptom-related variables (e.g., baseline symptom severity, reported symptom reduction in the DP phase). Finally, participants were asked to express their preference between DP and NDP treatment and provide a rationale for their choice in an open-ended response. It was expected that NDPs would lead to symptom reduction and improved emotion regulation, particularly in patients who had benefited from DP treatment.

Method

Participants

The present investigation included 20 individuals who had participated in a previous DP trial on pathological skin-picking [24]. Participants of this DP trial (n = 69, 90% female, 7% male, 3% non-binary, 89% with a high school diploma; mean age = 31.07 years, SD = 9.25) were contacted to determine their interest in entering a trial with NDPs. Reported comorbid diagnoses of mental disorders included depression (19%), panic disorder (1%), generalized anxiety disorder (4%), social anxiety disorder (4%), obsessive–compulsive disorder (4%). Participants had been recruited through self-help groups for body-focused repetitive behaviors, social media platforms, and the outpatient clinic of the university with a specialized unit for SPD. Exclusion criteria included reported severe comorbid mental disorders (psychotic disorders, substance dependence, borderline personality disorder, depression with severe symptoms) and skin diseases as the main cause of skin-picking.

Of the participants in the DP trial, 19 females and one male agreed to enter the NDP trial (mean age = 32 years, SD = 7.8; 88% with a high school diploma). Reported comorbid diagnoses of mental disorders in this sample were depression (15%), and generalized anxiety disorder (5%). None of the patients were taking active medication for skin picking or enrolled in psychotherapy during the trial.

Questionnaires

The German version of the Skin Picking Scale-Revised [10] assesses symptom severity and impairment due to skin-picking. The scale contains eight items (e.g., “How strong was your urge to pick at your skin?”) which are answered on a 5-point Likert scale (0 = no urge,4 = very strong urge). Higher scores indicate greater difficulties with emotion regulation. This scale was selected based on findings from the DP trial, which indicated significant placebo effects.

Design and procedure

All participants started with the DP trial including two weeks of daily placebo treatment (capsules introduced as ‘N-acetylcysteine’, which has shown promise as a treatment for disorders with body-focused repetitive behaviors, [12]). The placebo capsules (size 00) were filled with starch. Before and after the two-week condition, participants completed a disorder-specific questionnaire (SPS-R, [10]) and a scale assessing difficulties in emotion regulation, DERS, [14]). After the DP trial, all participants were debriefed in form of a letter send via email that described the main findings of the study: a large reduction of skin-picking symptoms and a moderate improvement in emotion regulation after two weeks of the treatment. It was emphasized that participants achieved this outcome through self-regulation and not through medication (Supplementary Material S1).

In a second letter (Supplementary Material S2), all participants were asked if they were interested in treatment with an NDP. The concept of nondeceptive placebos was introduced, along with positive findings from NDP studies. The average duration between the start of the DP and NDP trials was 17.4 weeks (range: 13 – 22). Both the DP and NDP capsules were identical in appearance and supplied in similar containers, differing only in their labels (DP: N-acetylcysteine; NDP: Placebo). As in the DP trial, participants completed the questionnaires (SPS_R, DERS) before and after the two-week placebo treatment. At the end of the study, participants were asked to express their treatment preference (DP, NDP) and provide a rationale for their choice in an open-ended response.

The DP and NDP trials followed the Declaration of Helsinki and were approved by the local ethics committee (DP: GZ. 39/27/63 ex 2023/24; NDP: GZ. 39/223/63 ex 2023/24). All participants gave written informed consent.

Results

Results showed that 71% of DP participants declined NDP treatment after debriefing. Among those who refused, 29% briefly informed the experimenter of their decision via email while 42% did not respond to the invitation. The remaining 20 participants (29%) agreed to take NDPs.

Symptom reduction and improvement in emotion regulation: DP vs NDP

Data from the 20 participants who entered the NDP trial were used in an analysis of covariance (ANCOVA; Jamovi 2.6 [17]) to compare their SPS-R and DERS scores before vs. after the two-week placebo phases (factor: Time) between DP and NDP (factor: Placebo type). The covariate was the time (without treatment) between the DP and NDP trial (in weeks).

SPS_R: The ANCOVA revealed significant interactions between Placebo Type x Time x Covariate (F1,18 = 8.319, p = 0.010, η2 = 0.316) and Placebo Type x Time (F1,18 = 5.935, p = 0.025, η2 = 0.248). Symptom reductions (differences of SPS_R scores before vs. after the placebo treatment) for DP and NDP were not significantly correlated (r = -0.13, p = 0.583). Open comments: Among the 20 participants who continued treatment with NDPs, 15 (75%) expressed a preference for DPs by the end of the study, four participants (20%) preferred the NDP, and one did not respond (comments are listed in Supplementary Table S2). The majority believed that deception (i.e., believing they were receiving medication) was essential for the placebo effect to occur. A preference for NDPs was associated with the belief that this treatment fosters an active role in symptom management.

Discussion

This study examined whether participants in a DP trial on reducing pathological skin-picking would be willing to enter an NDP trial and whether this NDP intervention would be effective. The results indicated that 71% of DP participants did not continue with NDP treatment following DP debriefing.

Refusal of NDPs has been previously reported in studies involving both patients and healthy participants. In a study on patients with depression [22], 19% declined participation or dropped out of the study. Similarly, in a randomized controlled trial by Schienle and Unger [25] investigating NDP treatment to enhance relaxation training effects in healthy students, 15% of participants actively refused to take the placebo. In the present study, the refusal rate was even higher, with 29% actively opting out. Only four participants provided reasons for declining the NDP treatment: two cited insufficient effects from DP, one reported sufficient symptom reduction during the DP trial, making further treatment unnecessary, and one was unable to participate due to physical illness.

On average, individuals who agreed to take NDPs reported clinically meaningful symptom reduction by the end of the DP trial. The effect size for the DP associated symptom reduction (Cohen’s d = 0.64) was comparable to the overall effect of behavioral treatments for SPD reported in a meta-analysis (standardized mean difference 0.68; see [26]). However, placebo benefits disappeared in the NPD trial. Symptom reduction and improvements in affective control were not statistically significant anymore. In line with these findings were the open comments of the participants. The majority (75%) indicated a preference for deceptive placebo treatment after having experienced treatment with NDPs. This finding aligns with existing literature (e.g., [15]). In this survey, laypersons reported more positive outcome expectations for DPs and rated the administration of DPs as more acceptable than NDPs.

The findings of the present study challenge the optimistic view of an effective transition from DP to NDP for patients with skin-picking disorder. This contrasts with results from meta-analyses for clinical trials (e.g., [28]), which suggest a significant overall effect of NDPs. In these trials, patients often received active medication alongside the placebo. For instance, in Kaptchuk et al.'s [18] study on NDP effects in irritable bowel syndrome (IBS), 54% of patients in the placebo group were also taking IBS medication, while 24% were on antidepressants. The effects of NDPs alone may be smaller than those observed when they function as dose extenders. This should be investigated in a future study.

Notably, the present findings also allow for a more optimistic interpretation. SPS-R scores remained stable between the end of the DP trial and the beginning of the NDP trial, which, on average, occurred four months later. This suggests that participants maintained their treatment gains even after discontinuing DP intake. It is possible that no further reduction in self-reported symptoms could be achieved with placebo treatment alone. However, participants expressed considerable skepticism toward the NDP treatment, as reflected in their open comments, which challenges this more positive interpretation.

We must also acknowledge several limitations of the current study. First, the sample was predominantly female, limiting the generalizability of the findings to males. However, given that SPD has a higher prevalence among females, the uneven sex distribution is characteristic of the disorder and reflects its typical population profile [9]. Second, the sequence of interventions was not randomized, as the NDP treatment consistently followed the DP intervention. To enable a clearer comparison of symptom reduction over time, it would be beneficial to implement a parallel-group study design in which participants receive either DP or NDP treatment, rather than both in succession. Third, only a small number of participants agreed to take part in the NDP trial, resulting in a limited sample size. Fourth, participants could choose whether to continue or discontinue the NDP treatment, introducing a potential self-selection bias. However, even those who had responded positively to the DP intervention and agreed to enter the NDP trial were unable to transfer their learning gains to the NDP context. Future research could explore whether extended psychoeducation increases the number of NDP responders. Since many participants viewed deception as a key aspect of placebo effects, it is important to address this belief in greater detail while also reinforcing the concepts of placebo-induced self-regulation and self-healing effects.

Finally, this study did not include a treatment expectation questionnaire. Multidimensional self-report instruments (e.g., [27]) have been developed to assess expectations regarding treatment benefits, potential adverse effects, and their impact in the context of placebo interventions. Future studies should incorporate such measures to better understand the role of treatment expectations concerning NDPs.
