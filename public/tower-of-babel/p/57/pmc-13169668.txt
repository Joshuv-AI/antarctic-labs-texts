Influenza vaccination intention among emergency medical service personnel: examining the role of health perception within the theory of planned behavior
Authors: Demhat Şahin Uyar, Tezcan Şahin
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundInfluenza vaccination is a key preventive measure for healthcare personnel; however, vaccination uptake remains suboptimal despite the elevated occupational risk faced by emergency medical service (EMS) personnel. The Theory of Planned Behavior (TPB) has been widely applied to explain vaccination intention, yet the role of broader health-related self-evaluations within this framework remains insufficiently explored. This study aimed to examine influenza vaccination intention among EMS personnel by extending the TPB with health perception.MethodsA cross-sectional study was conducted among EMS personnel affiliated with the Muğla Provincial Ambulance Service in Türkiye. Data were collected from 474 participants using a convenience sampling approach. The survey included a demographic questionnaire, the Theory of Planned Behavior Scale for Influenza Vaccination, and the Health Perception Scale. Structural equation modeling using Partial Least Squares (PLS-SEM) was employed to examine the relationships among TPB constructs, health perception, and influenza vaccination intention, including the potential moderating role of health perception.ResultsAttitude toward influenza vaccination was positively associated with vaccination intention (β = 0.541, p < .001), while health perception showed a smaller but statistically significant positive association (β = 0.161, p < .001). Perceived behavioral control (β = 0.051, p = .234) and subjective norm (β = 0.026, p = .544) were not significantly associated with intention. Moderation analysis indicated that health perception significantly strengthened the relationship between attitude and vaccination intention (β = 0.204, p < .001), whereas its moderating effects on perceived behavioral control (β = 0.039, p = .142) and subjective norm (β = −0.058, p = .186) were not statistically significant. The structural model explained 45% of the variance in vaccination intention (R² = 0.454).ConclusionsInfluenza vaccination intention among EMS personnel appears to be primarily shaped by attitudinal factors, while health perception exerts both a direct and a moderating influence. These findings suggest that interventions aimed at increasing vaccination uptake among EMS personnel should prioritize strengthening positive attitudes toward vaccination while also considering how individuals’ perceptions of their own health shape preventive decision-making.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12889-026-27218-2.Keywords: Influenza vaccination, Vaccination intention, Theory of Planned Behavior, Health perception, Emergency medical service personnel, Structural equation modeling

Background

Throughout human history, efforts to understand and control disease have played a central role in shaping medical knowledge and public health practices. Advances in microbiology and the identification of infectious agents marked a turning point by enabling systematic preventive interventions, positioning preventive healthcare as a cornerstone of modern health systems [1]. These developments laid the foundation for contemporary public health strategies aimed at reducing disease burden through proactive, population-level measures.

Among preventive health interventions, immunization represents one of the most effective and sustainable strategies for disease prevention. The World Health Organization defines immunization as the process by which individuals are protected against infectious diseases through vaccination, emphasizing its role in reducing morbidity and mortality at both individual and population levels [2]. Vaccination not only protects vaccinated individuals but also limits the circulation of vaccine-preventable pathogens through herd immunity or eradication, thereby reducing exposure risk within communities [3]. Consequently, immunization is widely regarded as one of the most cost-effective public health interventions [4].

Extensive evidence demonstrates that vaccination programs generate substantial economic benefits by preventing disease-related healthcare expenditures and productivity losses. Historical examples, such as the global eradication of smallpox, illustrate that investments in immunization yield returns that far exceed their initial costs [5]. Comparative cost-effectiveness analyses consistently show that vaccination outperforms many other preventive measures in terms of cost per life-year saved, underscoring its contribution to the sustainability of healthcare systems [6–8].

Seasonal influenza, in particular, continues to impose a considerable health and economic burden worldwide due to its high transmissibility, recurrent outbreaks, and associated complications. Productivity losses related to absenteeism, together with direct medical costs, constitute a substantial share of this burden [9]. Influenza vaccination has been shown to reduce complications, hospital admissions, and intensive care utilization, thereby alleviating pressure on healthcare systems [10]. Despite these well-documented benefits, influenza vaccination coverage remains suboptimal across many populations.

The effectiveness of vaccination programs depends not only on scientific evidence and vaccine availability but also on public acceptance and participation. In recent years, growing vaccine hesitancy has highlighted the importance of psychosocial factors in shaping vaccination decisions [11, 12]. Vaccination decisions are increasingly recognized as complex behaviors influenced by individual health perceptions, social norms, and perceived competence to act [13, 14]. These dynamics are particularly salient among healthcare personnel, whose vaccination behaviors affect patient safety and public trust in immunization programs [15].

While vaccination programs are widely recognized as highly effective public health interventions, their success ultimately depends on individual-level decisions to accept or decline vaccination. Understanding these decisions therefore requires attention not only to epidemiological and economic considerations but also to how individuals act.

Social psychology–based behavioral models provide such frameworks by accounting for cognitive, normative, and control-related influences on behavior. Among these models, the TPB posits that behavior is primarily determined by behavioral intention, which is shaped by attitudes toward the behavior, perceived subjective norms, and perceived behavioral control [16, 17]. Intention is widely regarded as the most proximal predictor of behavior [18, 19]. TPB has been widely applied to vaccination research, with empirical studies consistently demonstrating that positive attitudes toward vaccination and supportive social norms are among the strongest predictors of vaccination intention, while the influence of perceived behavioral control varies across contexts [20–22]. Beyond vaccination, TPB has also been successfully applied to a broad range of health-related intentions and behaviors, including organ and body donation, medication adherence, and other preventive health behaviors, demonstrating its robustness and generalizability across diverse health contexts [23–25]. These findings support the use of TPB as a comprehensive framework for understanding complex health decision-making processes.

Despite its predictive success, TPB may not fully capture all determinants relevant to preventive health decisions. Although TPB provides a parsimonious and robust framework for predicting intention and behavior, it primarily focuses on behavior-specific evaluations (attitude), perceived social pressures (subjective norm), and perceived behavioral control. Broader self-evaluative constructs related to individuals’ overall health may therefore remain outside the scope of the core TPB components. Prior research has emphasized that TPB can be meaningfully extended through the inclusion of additional constructs, provided that these variables are conceptually distinct from the core TPB components and explain variance beyond attitudes, subjective norms, and perceived behavioral control [26].

Health perception represents one such construct. Rather than evaluating vaccination itself, health perception captures individuals’ generalized appraisal of their overall health status. Health is commonly defined as a state of complete physical, mental, and social well-being rather than merely the absence of disease [27]. Accordingly, health perception—individuals’ beliefs, feelings, and cognitions regarding their health—has been identified as a determinant of health-related decision-making [28]. Individuals may perceive themselves as healthy despite diagnosed conditions or, conversely, feel unhealthy in the absence of objective illness [29, 30], which may influence engagement in preventive behaviors such as vaccination [31].

Health perception is conceptually distinct from TPB constructs. Attitude reflects evaluations of the desirability or benefits of vaccination, and perceived behavioral control reflects perceived ease or difficulty in performing the behavior [16]. In contrast, health perception represents a generalized and relatively stable self-assessment of overall health status [27, 30, 31]. Whereas TPB constructs are behavior-specific and situational, health perception operates at a broader level of abstraction and exists independently of any single preventive action. Thus, it reflects a global self-evaluation rather than a disease-specific or behavior-specific judgment, and may shape how individuals interpret vulnerability, need for prevention, and the relevance of protective behaviors.

In the context of influenza vaccination, TPB-based studies have highlighted the importance of attitudes toward vaccine effectiveness and safety, perceived social expectations, and prior vaccination experience in shaping intention [32–35]. However, individuals’ broader evaluations of their own health may also influence vaccination decisions. Those who perceive themselves as generally healthy may underestimate their susceptibility to influenza and consequently report lower vaccination intention [36, 37].

Beyond functioning as an independent predictor, health perception may also act as a contextual factor that influences how TPB constructs translate into intention. TPB has been described as an open framework that allows the inclusion of additional constructs when theoretically justified [26, 38]. Given that health perception reflects a generalized appraisal of one’s overall health status [31], it is theoretically plausible that such evaluations may shape the relative salience of attitudes, perceived norms, and control beliefs in the formation of intention. Accordingly, the potential moderating role of health perception was examined within the structural model to provide a more nuanced assessment of its position within the TPB framework.

Against this background, the present study aimed to examine the role of health perception in shaping influenza vaccination intention among EMS personnel within an extended TPB framework. Specifically, a single structural model was tested in which health perception was examined both as a predictor of vaccination intention and as a moderator of the relationships between TPB constructs (attitude, subjective norm, and perceived behavioral control) and intention. This integrated modeling approach allows for the simultaneous assessment of the direct and moderating roles of health perception within the TPB framework. Methods

Study design and setting

This study employed a quantitative, cross-sectional design to examine relationships between health perception and influenza vaccination intention among EMS personnel within the framework of the Theory of Planned Behavior. The cross-sectional design enabled the assessment of psychological constructs and behavioral intentions within a single study period.

The study was conducted among EMS personnel employed by the Muğla Provincial Ambulance Service under the 112 Emergency Medical Services system in Muğla Province, Türkiye.

Study population and sampling

The study population consisted of 870 actively working EMS personnel within the ambulance service system, including physicians, paramedics, emergency medical technicians, ambulance drivers, and other support staff who operate within the same emergency response environment. In EMS systems, emergency care is delivered through team-based operations, and both clinical and non-clinical personnel may share the same confined working environment within ambulances, which can increase exposure to respiratory infections [39]. Sample size adequacy was evaluated based on established structural equation modeling (SEM) guidelines [40]. According to the “10-times rule,” the minimum required sample size should be at least ten times the maximum number of structural paths directed at a single endogenous construct. In the proposed model, the largest number of structural paths converging on one endogenous variable (vaccination intention) was four, indicating a minimum recommended sample size of 40.

In addition, a post-hoc power analysis was conducted using parameters equivalent to those implemented in G*Power for multiple regression with four predictors (α = 0.05, N = 474). The analysis indicated that the available sample size provides 80% statistical power to detect a small effect size (f² ≈ 0.025), suggesting that the study is sufficiently powered to detect small effects [41].

During the three-month data collection period, 500 questionnaires were distributed. Of these, 474 were returned completed, corresponding to a response rate of 94.8% and representing approximately 54.5% of the target population. Data screening indicated that no missing responses were present; therefore, all questionnaires were retained for analysis.

Data collection procedure

Data were collected between January and March 2025 corresponding to the influenza season. Questionnaires were administered face-to-face at EMS stations, the command control center, administrative units, and training facilities. Participants were informed about the study objectives, voluntary participation, confidentiality, and data protection procedures. Written informed consent was obtained prior to participation.

Measures

A researcher-developed form was used to collect information on sociodemographic characteristics, influenza vaccination history, and beliefs regarding vaccination. An English version of this instrument is provided as Supplementary File 1.

Health Perception Scale: Health perception was assessed using the Health Perception Scale developed by Diamond et al. [42] and adapted into Turkish by Kadıoğlu and Yıldız [43]. The scale consists of 15 items rated on a five-point Likert scale. Higher scores indicate more positive health perception. During the measurement model evaluation, ten items were removed due to low factor loadings, resulting in a final construct consisting of five indicators.

Theory of Planned Behavior Scale for Influenza Vaccination: TPB components and influenza vaccination intention were measured using the scale developed by Chu et al. [32]. The scale includes 17 items rated on a seven-point Likert scale and assesses attitude, subjective norm, perceived behavioral control, and intention. Translation was conducted using a translation–back-translation procedure. During the measurement model evaluation, three indicators were retained for the attitude construct in the final model. The remaining constructs retained their original indicators.

Statistical analysis

Data were analyzed using Partial Least Squares Structural Equation Modeling (PLS-SEM). The analyses were conducted using SmartPLS 4 [44]. PLS-SEM was selected because it is suitable for testing complex theoretical models involving multiple latent constructs and moderating relationships [40].

The analysis followed a two-step approach. First, the measurement model was evaluated to assess internal consistency reliability, convergent validity, and discriminant validity of the constructs. Internal consistency was examined using Cronbach’s alpha and composite reliability (CR). Convergent validity was assessed using standardized factor loadings and the average variance extracted (AVE). Discriminant validity was evaluated using the heterotrait–monotrait ratio (HTMT) and the Fornell–Larcker criterion [45].

Second, the structural model was evaluated to test the hypothesized relationships among the constructs. Collinearity among predictors was examined using variance inflation factor (VIF) values. Path coefficients were estimated using the PLS algorithm, and their statistical significance was assessed through bootstrapping with 5,000 resamples. In addition, the coefficient of determination (R²), effect size (f²), and predictive relevance (Q²) were examined to evaluate the explanatory and predictive performance of the model [40].

Moderating effects were tested using the product indicator approach by creating interaction terms between health perception and the TPB constructs (attitude, subjective norm, and perceived behavioral control). Furthermore, out-of-sample predictive performance was assessed using the PLSpredict procedure [40].

A significance level of p < .05 was adopted for all statistical tests.

Ethical considerations

Ethical approval was obtained from the Muğla Sıtkı Koçman University Health Sciences Ethics Committee (approval date: 22 December 2024; approval number: 159). Institutional permission was granted by the Muğla Provincial Health Directorate. The study was conducted in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki.

Common method bias (CMB)

Although data collection occurred over a three-month period, all constructs were measured using self-report questionnaires completed by the same respondents within a single survey administration. Therefore, the potential for common method bias (CMB) was considered.

Procedural remedies included voluntary participation, assurances of anonymity, and the use of previously validated measurement scales with clear wording. In addition, CMB was assessed statistically using the full collinearity variance inflation factor (VIF) approach [46]. Full collinearity VIF values ranged from 1.211 to 1.725, remaining well below the recommended threshold. These findings indicate that common method bias is unlikely to represent a serious threat to the validity of the results (Table 1).

Discussion

The present study examined influenza vaccination intention among EMS personnel by integrating health perception into the TPB. Overall, the findings support the applicability of TPB constructs in explaining influenza vaccination intention while highlighting the additional, albeit modest, contribution of health perception as a psychological determinant. Although participants reported relatively high levels of perceived health, their intention to receive the influenza vaccine remained comparatively low, underscoring the complexity of preventive health decision-making among EMS personnel. These dynamics may be particularly relevant in EMS settings, where personnel operate under demanding and time-constrained working conditions.

Consistent with TPB, attitude toward vaccination showed the strongest association with influenza vaccination intention. This finding aligns with a substantial body of literature demonstrating that individuals’ evaluations of vaccine benefits, safety, and necessity play a central role in shaping vaccination intention and decision-making processes [22, 32, 33]. Among EMS personnel, vaccination may be perceived as a discretionary preventive behavior rather than an obligatory professional practice, which may further amplify the role of personal attitudes in vaccination decision-making. The substantial effect size observed in this study suggests that interventions aiming to improve vaccination uptake should prioritize strategies that target attitudinal change, such as emphasizing both personal protection and patient safety.

In contrast, subjective norm was not significantly associated with influenza vaccination intention in the present study. Although previous research has identified social influence as a potential determinant of vaccination behavior [21, 51], findings across healthcare worker populations remain inconsistent. Several studies have reported weak or non-significant normative effects compared with attitudinal determinants [52, 53]. The absence of a significant normative influence in this study may reflect the relatively autonomous decision-making environment of EMS personnel or occupational characteristics of emergency care settings, where vaccination decisions may be perceived as individual rather than socially regulated behaviors. This pattern may also indicate that vaccination decisions among EMS personnel are influenced more strongly by individual evaluations than by perceived social expectations.

Perceived behavioral control was also not significantly associated with vaccination intention. Previous studies have reported mixed evidence regarding the role of perceived behavioral control in vaccination decision-making [54–56]. Within EMS systems, demanding work schedules, shift-based employment patterns, and operational pressures may shape perceptions of control over preventive behaviors such as vaccination. It is therefore possible that structural or organizational factors influence vaccination decisions beyond individual perceptions of behavioral control in this occupational context.

Health perception was positively associated with influenza vaccination intention, although the magnitude of this association was relatively modest. This finding indicates that individuals’ subjective evaluations of their health status may contribute to vaccination intention, supporting previous research linking health perception to preventive health behaviors [34, 57, 58]. However, the relatively small effect size suggests that health perception alone may not be sufficient to motivate vaccination among EMS personnel. In some cases, individuals who perceive themselves as healthy may still underestimate their susceptibility to infectious diseases [59–61]. Such perceptions may potentially reduce the perceived necessity of preventive measures such as vaccination.

An additional finding of this study concerns the moderating role of health perception in the relationship between attitude and influenza vaccination intention. The results indicated that health perception strengthened the association between positive attitudes toward vaccination and intention to vaccinate. Previous research suggests that the relationships proposed in the Theory of Planned Behavior may vary depending on contextual or individual-level factors [26, 38]. For example, prior studies have demonstrated moderating effects of variables such as gender, trust, and social norms on the associations between TPB constructs and vaccination-related outcomes [62–64]. Building on this perspective, the present findings suggest that individuals’ evaluations of their overall health status may also shape how attitudinal beliefs are translated into vaccination intention. In particular, EMS personnel with higher perceived health may be more likely to act upon favorable attitudes toward vaccination, thereby strengthening the attitude–intention relationship. Although the observed effect size was relatively small, this finding provides incremental evidence that broader health-related perceptions may function as contextual factors within TPB-based models of vaccination intention. To our knowledge, few studies have examined whether general health perception may play such a moderating role within TPB-based models of vaccination intention.

Descriptive findings related to influenza vaccination beliefs and vaccination history provide additional context for interpreting the study results. These variables were examined descriptively and were not included as predictors in the SEM model in order to preserve theoretical parsimony and maintain the conceptual focus on TPB constructs. More than half of the participants reported no prior influenza vaccination experience, and fewer than one-third reported repeated vaccination. Furthermore, less than half of the participants endorsed the belief that influenza vaccination contributes to the improvement of public health. Together, these findings may indicate that influenza vaccination has not been fully internalized as a routine or normative professional practice among EMS personnel. Previous studies have shown that limited past vaccination experience and weaker endorsement of collective benefits may hinder the development of stable vaccination habits and contribute to an intention–behavior gap [20, 33].

The structural model explained 45% of the variance in influenza vaccination intention (R² = 0.454), indicating a moderate level of explanatory power within the PLS-SEM framework. This level of explained variance is broadly comparable to values reported in several TPB-based studies of vaccination intention among healthcare workers and other populations [20, 32, 51, 58]. Differences in explanatory power across studies may be related to variations in study populations, contextual factors, and model specifications. For example, previous research has shown that additional variables such as prior vaccination behavior, anticipated regret, emotional responses, or perceived disease risk may further increase the explanatory capacity of TPB-based models. Nevertheless, given the limited number of studies specifically focusing on EMS personnel, the present findings provide valuable insights into influenza vaccination intention within this high-risk and relatively understudied occupational group.

Rather than proposing a major theoretical refinement, the present study provides incremental insight into how broader self-evaluations of health may operate alongside established TPB constructs in shaping vaccination intention among EMS personnel. Health perception demonstrated a modest but statistically significant association with intention, suggesting that it may function as a complementary determinant within this occupational context. Importantly, the coexistence of relatively high perceived health status and low vaccination intention highlights the complexity of preventive health decision-making even among healthcare professionals.

From a public health perspective, the findings of this study highlight the importance of addressing attitudinal determinants when designing strategies to improve influenza vaccination uptake among EMS personnel. Since attitude toward vaccination emerged as the strongest determinant of vaccination intention, public health campaigns targeting healthcare workers should emphasize the effectiveness, safety, and broader benefits of influenza vaccination for both personal protection and patient safety. In addition, the moderating role of health perception suggests that vaccination promotion messages may be more effective when they frame vaccination as a means of maintaining health rather than solely preventing disease. Such messaging strategies may help strengthen the translation of favorable attitudes into vaccination intentions among healthcare professionals.

At the organizational level, EMS systems may benefit from implementing strategies that facilitate easier access to vaccination within demanding emergency healthcare environments. Examples include offering vaccination during work shifts, integrating vaccination programs into routine occupational health services, and encouraging visible support from institutional leadership. At the individual level, educational initiatives that strengthen positive attitudes toward influenza vaccination and highlight its relevance for both personal and patient safety may further support vaccination uptake. Taken together, these approaches may contribute to fostering a stronger culture of preventive health behavior within EMS settings.

Strengths and limitations

This study has several strengths that should be considered when interpreting the findings. First, the study was conducted with a relatively large sample of EMS personnel, a high-risk and understudied professional group in the context of influenza vaccination. The inclusion of multiple professional roles within the EMS system enhances the internal diversity of the sample and allows for a more comprehensive examination of vaccination intention within emergency healthcare settings. Second, the use of well-established and previously validated measurement instruments strengthens the methodological rigor of the study. The measurement model assessment indicated acceptable levels of internal consistency reliability as well as generally adequate convergent and discriminant validity, supporting the suitability of the constructs used in the structural model. Third, integrating health perception into the TPB provides a theoretically grounded extension that offers additional insight into psychological determinants of vaccination intention.

Despite these strengths, several limitations should be acknowledged. First, vaccination intention does not necessarily translate into actual vaccination behavior. Although intention is conceptualized within the TPB as the most proximal determinant of behavior [16], future research should examine the relationship between intention and objectively measured vaccination uptake. Second, the cross-sectional design of the study precludes causal inferences between health perception, TPB components, and vaccination intention. Longitudinal studies are therefore needed to examine how these relationships evolve over time. Third, the data were collected using self-reported questionnaires, which may be subject to recall and social desirability bias, particularly within healthcare professional populations. In addition, the study was conducted within a single provincial EMS system, which may limit the generalizability of the findings to other organizational or regional contexts. Finally, the use of convenience sampling may have introduced selection bias, as voluntary participation could have resulted in the overrepresentation of individuals with greater interest in health-related issues.

Furthermore, although the structural model demonstrated moderate explanatory power, a substantial proportion of variance in vaccination intention remained unexplained. This suggests that additional factors—such as prior vaccination behavior, anticipated regret, emotional responses, perceived disease severity, or organizational influences—may also play an important role in shaping influenza vaccination intention among EMS personnel. Future studies incorporating these variables, as well as qualitative or mixed-method approaches, may provide a more comprehensive understanding of vaccination decision-making in this occupational group.

Conclusion

This study examined influenza vaccination intention among EMS personnel by integrating health perception into the TPB. The findings indicate that influenza vaccination intention is primarily shaped by attitudinal factors, while broader health perceptions play a complementary role in explaining preventive health decision-making. Although participants reported relatively high levels of perceived health, their intention to receive the influenza vaccine remained comparatively low, highlighting the complexity of vaccination decisions even among healthcare professionals.

Consistent with the TPB framework, attitude toward influenza vaccination emerged as the strongest predictor of vaccination intention. In contrast, subjective norm and perceived behavioral control were not significantly associated with intention in this study, suggesting that vaccination decisions among EMS personnel may be influenced more strongly by individual evaluations than by perceived social expectations or perceived behavioral control. Health perception showed a positive but modest association with vaccination intention and also moderated the relationship between attitude and intention, indicating that individuals’ evaluations of their own health may influence how favorable attitudes translate into vaccination intentions.

Descriptive findings further revealed limited influenza vaccination experience and relatively low endorsement of the collective benefits of vaccination among participants. These patterns suggest that influenza vaccination may not yet be fully internalized as a routine professional practice within this occupational group.

Overall, the findings provide incremental evidence that both attitudinal beliefs and broader health perceptions should be considered when designing strategies to improve influenza vaccination uptake among EMS personnel. Strengthening positive attitudes toward vaccination, addressing organizational barriers, and promoting a stronger culture of preventive health behavior may contribute to improving vaccination uptake and strengthening preventive health practices in emergency healthcare settings.
