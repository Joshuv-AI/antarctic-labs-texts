Background

Mucosal melanoma is a Malignant neoplasm arising from melanocytes in the mucosa. Sinonasal mucosal melanomas constitute 1% of all melanomas and about 4% of all sinonasal tumors [1, 2]. There is a wide patient age range, with an incidence peak in the seventh decade of life. There is no sex predilection [3]. Mucosal melanomas are biologically distinct from cutaneous melanomas. Etiological factors, including melanocytosis, remain speculative. Sinonasal mucosal melanomas most frequently arise in the nasal cavity or septum and rarely in the nasopharynx or maxillary sinuses [2, 3] with clinical presentations of nasal obstruction, epistaxis, and facial pain. They are frequently diagnosed late due to the presentation of nonspecific symptoms mimicking benign conditions. Locally aggressive and spread with bone/orbital involvement [1]. May also spread to distant areas such as lungs, liver, brain and lymph nodes. Immunohistochemical evaluation is necessary, particularly in amelanotic tumors. S100 protein and melanocytic markers (HMB45, tyrosinase, melan-A, MITF, and S0X10) show variable sensitivity depending on morphological type [4, 5]. S100 protein highlights > 95% of epithelioid/undifferentiated melanomas versus 85% of spindled mucosal melanomas [6, 7]. Similar variability has been noted in melanocytic Markers, highlighting 75–80% of melanomas with epithelioid morphology versus 65–70% of spindle cell melanomas [3]. Various treatment plans can be applied depending with the extent of disease and facility setting. Treatment includes surgical resection, which is often incomplete due to anatomy, radiotherapy to improve local control, immune checkpoint inhibitors in a more advanced setting or targeted therapy [2, 3]. They have poor prognosis with a 5 year survival for 20–45% of the patients owing to late detection and metastasis [4].

Case presentation

An 80-year-old African female presented with a 6 month history of progressive nasal obstruction, predominantly affecting the left nostril. This was accompanied by recurrent episodes of epistaxis, also prominent on the left side. She denied experiencing headaches, visual disturbances or episodes of loss of consciousness. On examination, the nose had a black polypoid lesion on the left nostril pushing the septum on the contralateral side and there was no bulging intraorally.

All laboratory investigations were within normal ranges. A computed tomography (CT) scan of the paranasal sinuses revealed an enhancing soft tissue Mass infiltrating the left nasal passage and ethmoid air cells measuring approximately 7.3 × 2.5 × 2.8 cm in size with associated nasal occlusion and obstruction of the ipsilateral osteomeatal complex and sphenoethmoidal recess leading to maxillary and sphenoid sinus opacification. There was destruction of the left medial maxillary sinus wall and medial aspect of the inferior orbital wall. Regional lymphadenopathy was not visualized. (Figs. 1 and 2). The patient was classed as stage IVA according to The American Joint Committee on Cancer (AJCC) system.

A CT scan of the chest and abdomen revealed no distant metastases. The electrocardiogram (ECG) and echocardiogram (ECHO) done were normal. The patient was scheduled for functional endoscopic sinus surgery (FESS).

Intraoperatively, a blackish mass in the left nasal cavity blocking the nostrils was noted, extending to the anterior third of the inferior turbinate, septum, middle turbinate and floor with mucoid secretions from the maxillary, ethmoid, sphenoid and frontal sinuses associated with normal mucosa. Debulking of the blackish mass was done and the sample was taken for histopathological review.

Histopathology reported it to be a mucosal melanoma Fig. 3, which was confirmed by Human Melanin Black (HMB45) immunohistochemistry (Fig. 4).

Treatment

The patient underwent surgical resection of the tumor, but due to the extent of the disease and its location, only debulking was possible. Discussion with the multidisciplinary team: adjuvant treatment with immunotherapy with immune checkpoint inhibitors was recommended. However, owing to limitations in accessibility and financial constraints, the patient was unable to proceed with the immunotherapy. Instead, opted for best supportive care with symptomatic management.

Discussion

Mucosal melanoma is a very aggressive Malignancy that arises from melanocytes located in mucosal membranes. It accounts for approximately 1% of all melanomas and has a significantly worse prognosis than cutaneous melanoma due to delayed diagnosis and its aggressive nature [8, 9]. The sinonasal tract is among the most common sites of involvement in the head and neck region, accounting for nearly 50% of all mucosal melanomas in this area [10, 11]. Unlike cutaneous melanomas, which are associated with ultraviolet (UV) radiation exposure, the etiology of mucosal melanomas remains unclear.

Genetic profiling and molecular pathogenesis

Mucosal melanoma exhibits molecular profile compared to cutaneous melanoma. Unlike the latter, which commonly harbor BRAF V600E mutations, mucosal melanomas frequently display mutations in the KIT gene, particularly in exons 11, 13 and making them potential candidates for tyrosine kinase inhibitor therapy (e.g. imatinib) in selected patients [12–14]. In addition, NRAS mutations are observed in a subset of cases, although less frequently than in cutaneous melanoma [12]. Notably, BRAF mutations are rare in mucosal subtypes, reported in fewer than 10% of the cases [13, 15].

Recent genomic studies have also identified SF3B1 and TP53 alterations and amplifications in CDK4, MDM2 and TERT which are implicated in the tumor’s pathogenesis and resistance to therapy. Moreover, mucosal melanomas generally exhibit a low tumor mutational burden (TMB) and low UV-induced mutation signatures, contributing to their poor responsiveness to immune checkpoint inhibitors [16, 17].

In clinical practice, genetic profiling using next-generation sequencing (NGS) is recommended where resource permit, to identify actionable mutations and stratify patients for a targeted or investigational therapies [18, 19]. Although not performed in this case due to logistical constraints, incorporating genomic profiling could have potentially uncovered KIT or NRAS mutations informing targeted therapy options and enrollment in clinical trials.

Histopathological and immunohistochemical (IHC) confirmation is critical in establishing the diagnosis of mucosal melanoma. The tumor cells in this case demonstrated typical melanocytic markers, including Human Melanin Black-45 (HMB45), a sensitive marker for melanocytic lesions [20]. Other commonly used markers include S100, Melan-A, and SOX10, which can help differentiate mucosal melanoma from other poorly differentiated malignancies such as sinonasal undifferentiated carcinoma (SNUC) or poorly differentiated squamous cell carcinoma. Advanced imaging plays a crucial role in determining the extent of disease and guiding surgical management. In this case, paranasal sinus CT imaging revealed an enhancing soft tissue mass with osseous destruction and involvement of multiple sinus cavities. This is consistent with previous studies highlighting that sinonasal mucosal melanoma frequently demonstrates aggressive local invasion with bone erosion and orbital extension. Further imaging of the chest and abdomen ruled out distant metastases, which are common in advanced cases [15, 21, 22]. The American Joint Committee on Cancer (AJCC) staging system is often used to classify mucosal melanoma, with a high tendency for local recurrence and distant metastases even after aggressive treatment. Most sinonasal melanomas present at an advanced stage, reinforcing the need for early diagnosis and intervention [23].

Management of sinonasal mucosal melanoma is challenging owing to its aggressive nature and poor response to conventional treatments. The mainstay of treatment involves surgical resection, preferably with clear margins, followed by adjuvant radiotherapy [13]. In this case, the patient underwent Functional Endoscopic Sinus Surgery (FESS) for tumor debulking. Although complete surgical resection is the goal, clear margins are often difficult to achieve due to the anatomical constraints of the sinonasal region [24–26].

Adjuvant radiotherapy has been shown to improve local control but does not significantly enhance overall survival [27–29]. For unresectable or metastatic disease, systemic therapies, including immune checkpoint inhibitors (ICIs) such as anti-PD-1 (nivolumab or pembrolizumab) and anti-CTLA-4 (ipilimumab), have shown promise [16, 16, 30]. Studies indicate that response rates to ICIs in mucosal melanoma are lower than in cutaneous melanoma, likely due to lower tumor mutational burden [16, 17]. Targeted therapies, such as tyrosine kinase inhibitors (TKIs), including imatinib and nilotinib, have been evaluated for mucosal melanoma harboring for KIT-mutant melanomas have also been explored with variable outcomes [14, 18, 19].

Despite aggressive multimodal treatment, the prognosis of sinonasal mucosal melanoma remains poor, with a 5-year overall survival rate ranging from 20 to 45% [31]. The high recurrence rate, early distant metastasis, and limited therapeutic options contribute to the unfavorable outcomes observed in these patients [32, 33].

Conclusion

Mucosal melanoma of the sinonasal tract is a highly aggressive malignancy with a poor prognosis owing to delayed diagnosis and limited treatment options. This case highlights the diagnostic and therapeutic challenges associated with this entity. Early recognition, multimodal management, and emerging systemic therapies offer hope for improving outcomes, but further research is needed to develop more effective treatment strategies.
