Impact of kynurenine metabolites on B and T cells in multiple sclerosis and allergies
Authors: Ivana Stanković Matić, Lara Saftić Martinović, Ines Benčik, Ana Novaković, Nada Starčević Čizmarević
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
Chronic inflammation is central to both multiple sclerosis (MS) and allergic conditions, though their pathological outcomes differ. In MS, inflammation leads to demyelination and neurodegeneration in the central nervous system, while in allergies, it causes localized tissue swelling and irritation. Emerging evidence suggests that heightened immune activity in allergies may influence or exacerbate inflammatory responses in MS, with some researchers proposing shared immunopathological mechanisms between allergic hyperactivity and the autoimmune processes underlying MS. The kynurenine pathway, a key route of tryptophan metabolism, has garnered attention for its role in the pathogenesis of neurodegenerative, psychiatric, vascular, and autoimmune diseases. Its metabolites are known to modulate neuroinflammation and immune responses, particularly through their effects on B and T lymphocytes, which are critical in adaptive immunity and autoimmune pathology. This article explores the influence of the kynurenine pathway on B and T lymphocyte function, aiming to determine its potential to provide deeper insights into immune dysregulation in chronic inflammation diseases. By understanding these interactions, we seek to evaluate the kynurenine pathway as a target for developing more effective therapeutic strategies for chronic inflammations and autoimmune conditions.

Introduction

Multiple sclerosis (MS)

Multiple sclerosis (MS) is a chronic autoimmune disease of the central nervous sys-tem (CNS), characterized by inflammation, demyelination, gliosis, and neuronal loss. The disease primarily targets myelinated axons within the CNS, resulting in varying degrees of damage to both the myelin sheath and the axons themselves (Haki et al., 2024). Globally, MS affects approximately 2.3 million individuals, with the majority of diagnoses occur-ring between the ages of 20 and 50 years. Notably, the condition exhibits a higher preva-lence in females compared to males, and can result in physical disability, cognitive dys-function, and a significant reduction in quality of life (McGinley et al., 2021). Recent research suggests that multiple sclerosis (MS) arises from a com-plex interplay between genetic predisposition and environmental factors encountered from early development to young adulthood.

Key factors implicated in influencing genetic susceptibility to MS and contributing to disease risk include vitamin D deficiency, season of birth, tobacco exposure, and infection with the Epstein-Barr virus (Gouider et al., 2024). Multiple sclerosis (MS) is an unpredictable disease characterized by significant variability in both its onset and clinical course. Three primary phenotypes have been identified: (1) relapsing-remitting MS (RRMS), which is marked by episodes of acute relapses followed by periods of complete or partial recovery, with stable clinical status between relapses.

At disease onset, most patients experience relapses with focal neurological deficits, followed by partial or full remission. RRMS is the most prevalent phenotype, affecting approxi-mately 85 % of individuals with MS (“New Insights into Multiple Sclerosis Clinical Course from the Topographical Model and Functional Reserve - ClinicalKey,” n.d.); (2) primary progressive MS (PPMS), which is characterized by continuous disease progression from the onset of clinically evident disease, typically without relapses but occasionally with pe-riods of disease plateau. PPMS affects around 15 % of MS patients (Antel et al., 2012); and (3) secondary progressive MS (SPMS), which is characterized by ongoing disease progres-sion with or without acute relapses, leading to the accumulation of disability in various functions, including ambulation, autonomic processes, and cognition. Approximately 5 % of patients with RRMS may transition to SPMS annually (Rovaris et al., 2006). The mam-malian central nervous system (CNS) has a limited capacity for self-repair and renewal, with inflammation often posing a threat to neuronal integrity. Historically, the presence of immune cells in the CNS was considered a marker of pathology, supported by several ob-servations: the lack of lymphatic vessels within the brain parenchyma (Sandrone et al., 2019), minimal or absent MHC II expression by resident microglia during homeostasis, the absence of antigen-presenting cells, and the protective role of the blood−brain barrier in restricting immune cell entry (“Revisiting the Mechanisms of CNS Immune Privilege - ClinicalKey,” n.d.). Consequently, the CNS was long regarded as an "immunologically privileged" organ. However, recent advances in neuroimmune interactions have reshaped our understanding of the peripheral immune system's role in maintaining CNS homeo-stasis and its involvement in disease (Rustenhoven and Kipnis, 2022).

Allergic conditions

Allergic diseases represent a significant burden on healthcare systems and arise from hypersensitivity, an inappropriate immune response to harmless environmental sub-stances known as allergens. These conditions are broadly categorized based on their un-derlying immunological mechanisms as either immunoglobulin E (IgE)-mediated or IgE-independent. IgE-mediated disorders include atopic asthma, allergic rhinitis (AR), al-lergic conjunctivitis, IgE-dependent atopic dermatitis (AD), and food allergies, while non-IgE-mediated conditions encompass non-atopic asthma, contact dermatitis, non-IgE-dependent AD, and non-IgE-mediated food allergies (Potaczek et al., 2020; Czar-nowicki et al., 2019).

Despite the heterogeneity and pathobiological differences between specific allergic disorders, certain shared mechanisms are recognized, particularly in IgE-mediated dis-eases. These disorders are characterized by chronic allergic inflammation punctuated by acute episodes. A variety of cells contribute to this process, including epithelial cells, anti-gen-presenting cells (APCs), T and B lymphocytes, mast cells, basophils, eosinophils, and neutrophils. The epithelial barrier plays a crucial role beyond serving as a mechanical de-fense, integrating innate and adaptive immune responses. Allergen-triggered epithelial mediators stimulate APCs, which subsequently activate allergen-specific T cells (Alashkar Alhamwe et al., 2020).

T cells fulfill multiple functions, including the orchestration of adaptive immune re-sponses, effector activities, and activation of B cells to differentiate into plasma cells, which produce allergen-specific IgE. Additionally, some functions traditionally attributed to T cells are also performed by innate lymphoid cells (ILCs). IgE molecules bind to high-affinity receptors (FcεRI) on basophils and mast cells, and allergen-induced cross-linking of these complexes triggers the release of bioactive mediators such as hista-mine. This cascade initiates immediate allergic reactions and sustains allergic inflamma-tion (Potaczek et al., 2020, Alashkar Alhamwe et al., 2020).

Both MS and allergic conditions exemplify diseases where chronic inflammation plays a central role, leading to progressive damage (neuronal in MS, epithelial or systemic in allergies) and significant impacts on patients' quality of life. Although distinct in their clinical manifestations and underlying immune mechanisms, multiple sclerosis and al-lergic conditions exemplify the diverse manifestations of immune dysregulation in chronic diseases. This article seeks to explore the shared principles of immune-mediated pathology by examining these two conditions, with a particular focus on the influence of kynurenine pathways on the immune cells involved. By comparing and contrasting their immunological underpinnings, the discussion aims to shed light on commonalities in disease processes and identify potential avenues for therapeutic intervention. This unified perspective not only deepens our understanding of each disease but also underscores the broader implications of immune system dysfunction in human health.

Immune cells, pathways and their function in MS pathology.

Th1 and Th17 cells play crucial roles in MS pathology by promoting inflammation, disrupting the BBB, recruiting other immune cells, and directly damaging CNS tissue. Their activities create a vicious cycle of inflammation and tissue destruction that underlies the disease. On the other hand, Tregs limit the activity of autoreactive T cells that mistak-enly target myelin components in the central nervous system (CNS) and this suppression is mediated through direct cell-cell contact and the release of inhibitory cytokines such as interleukin-10 (IL-10) and transforming growth factor-beta (TGF-β) (B. Arneth, 2021).

The balance between Th17 and Th1 cells plays a critical role in determining whether inflammation will be initiated and a demyelinating plaque will develop. Inflammation occurs when the ratio of Th17 to Th1 cells exceeds 1, indicating a disproportionate in-crease in IL-17 secretion, which is now regarded as a key regulator of autoimmune pro-cesses in the CNS (Murúa et al., 2022). The discovery of the involvement of TGF-β and IL-6 in the generation of proinflammatory Th17 cells is also surprising, given that TGF-β alone has anti-inflammatory effects and induces the activation of the FOXP3 transcription factor, which is essential for generating regulatory T lymphocytes (Tregs) that are crucial for immunosuppression and the establishment and maintenance of im-mune tolerance.

The functional antagonism between Th17 cells and Tregs, along with their shared differentiation factors, has become particularly interesting from the perspective of im-munomodulatory therapy for autoimmune disorders (Wang et al., 2023).

On other side B cells play a crucial role as professional APCs in MS, effectively recog-nizing and presenting central nervous system (CNS) antigens to T cells. This interaction facilitates T cell activation and amplifies inflammatory responses, a process underscored by the upregulation of MHC class II, CD40, and CD80/86 on B cells in MS patients, reflect-ing their heightened antigen-presenting capacity. Beyond their role in antigen presenta-tion, B cells contribute to the inflammatory milieu through the secretion of pro-inflammatory cytokines, including interleukin-6 (IL-6), tumor necrosis factor-alpha (TNF-α), and lymphotoxin-alpha (Häusser-Kinzel and Weber, 2019). They pay contribu-tion to both antibody-dependent and antibody-independent mechanisms of CNS injury.

In addition to antibody secretion by plasma cells, the mechanisms through which B cells act in pathogenesis are antigen presentation to T cells and driving proliferation of brain-homing T cells, production of inflammatory cytokines and soluble toxic factors, in-volvement in ectopic lymphoid aggregates in the meninges, and hosting Epstein-Barr vi-rus (Comi et al., 2021). Peripheral as well as CNS B cells from MS patients exhibit a chronic activation state with a shift toward antigen-experienced memory B cells (Häusser-Kinzel and Weber, 2019). These memory B cells show increased expression of CD40 and HLA-DR, suggesting enhanced ability to present antigens to T cells, thus amplifying the immune response (Kumar and Axtell, 2023). This heightened activity of memory B cells contributes to the sus-tained inflammation observed in MS, therefore these activated memory B cells contribute to the development of CNS lesions and promote the persistence of autoimmune inflam-mation (Cencioni et al., 2021). In addition, patients experiencing active relapses in MS have been shown to have a higher frequency of memory B cells in peripheral blood compared to patients in remission (Arneth, 2019). B cell memory plays a key role in the progression of MS. During disease relapses, there is an increase in the number of class-switched memory B cells, which are more likely to secrete pro-inflammatory cytokines such as IL-6 and TNF-α. These cytokines promote the activation of T cells, driving the inflammatory response that supports disease progres-sion in MS (Cencioni et al., 2021). The exacerbation of disease symptoms has been linked to an imbalance in the frequency of naïve versus memory regulatory B cells (Bregs), with MS patients experiencing a reduction in functional regulatory B cells during relapse (Häusser-Kinzel and Weber, 2019, Cencioni et al., 2021).

By comparison, Bregs diminish immune activation by producing anti-inflammatory cytokines such as interleukin-10 (IL-10), interleukin-35 (IL-35), and transforming growth factor-beta (TGF-β). In MS, the impaired function of Bregs results in diminished produc-tion of these regulatory cytokines, thereby exacerbating disease pathology and highlight-ing a critical imbalance in immune regulation. The dynamic interplay between pro-inflammatory B cells and Bregs is fundamental to maintaining immune homeostasis (Sakkas et al., 2018). Disruption of this balance, characterized by excessive production of pro-inflammatory cytokines and diminished regulatory functions of B cells, contributes to immune dysregulation and drives the progression of MS.

Therapeutic strategies that focus on enhancing the functional capacity of Bregs, par-ticularly their production of interleukin-10 (IL-10), or selectively targeting pathogenic B cell activities, represent promising approaches for the treatment of MS. Bruton’s tyrosine kinase (Btk) inhibitors, which modulate signaling through the B cell receptor, have demonstrated potential in promoting tolerogenic B cell responses while concurrently suppressing pro-inflammatory mechanisms (Crofford et al., 2016). Research has also highlighted the role of B cells in shaping the T cell response in MS. A subset of human memory B cells that express the receptors TIM1, CD43, and CD27 may have an-ti-inflammatory functions in autoimmune disease (Kumar and Axtell, 2023). B cells that secrete IL-10 and TGF-β suppress the activation of pathogenic Th1 and in some extent Th17 (Häusser-Kinzel and Weber, 2019) and secretion of cytokines IL-35 and TGF-ß addi-tionally redirects T cells toward Forkhead Box P3 (FoxP3 +) Treg phenotype and attenu-ates autoimmunity (Kumar and Axtell, 2023).(Fig. 1).Fig. 1Key signaling pathways and cellular interactions of adaptive immune cells in multiple sclerosis. figure obtained in reactome23.Fig. 1

B and T cells in allergic conditions

The major well-established contribution of B cells to allergies is through the produc-tion of antigen-specific IgE antibodies. These antibodies are crucial for the development of allergic reactions, as they bind to high-affinity IgE receptors (FcεRI) on mast cells and ba-sophils (Udoye et al., 2023). Upon subsequent exposure to the same allergen, the allergen crosslinks IgE-FcεRI complexes on these cells, triggering the release of in-flammatory mediators such as histamine, cytokines, and leukotrienes, leading to typical allergic symptoms such as itching, swelling, and bronchoconstriction.

The process of IgE production begins with the activation of B cells by T follicular helper (Tfh) cells, which produce IL-21, IL-4, and IL-13. These cytokines promote class switching of B cells to IgE production. Th2 cytokines, especially IL-4 and IL-13, are essen-tial for this process. In addition, IgE production is influenced by the B cell receptor (BCR) signaling and interactions with co-stimulatory molecules provided by Tfh cells (Allen, 2022). Recent studies have also highlighted the role of IL-21 in regulating IgE production (Yang et al., 2020). Interestingly, IgE memory B cells, once hypothesized as contributors to long-term allergen sensitization, are rarely found in circulation and are thought to play a limited role in allergic recall responses. Instead, the majority of IgE is derived from extra-follicular plasma cells, with only a small fraction originating from germinal centers, em-phasizing the rapid and transient nature of IgE production (Allen, 2022).

B cells with immunosuppressive capacity, Bregs, play an important role in re-straining inflammation. Bregs play a role in controlling inflammation through the secre-tion of anti-inflammatory cytokines, such as IL-10, TGF-β, and IL-35. These cytokines suppress allergen-specific immune responses, counteract type 2 inflammation, and pro-mote allergen tolerance (Kliem and Schaub, 2024, Satitsuksanoa et al., 2023). IL-10 is cen-tral to the function of Bregs, suppressing IgE-mediated allergic cascades and reducing airway inflammation. IL-10-producing Bregs are decreased in patients with allergic dis-eases, such as asthma and atopic dermatitis (AD).

In severe AD, the frequency of CD24hiCD38hi Bregs is reduced, and their IL-10 produc-tion is impaired, particularly in response to lipopolysaccharides (LPS) (Satitsuksanoa et al., 2023). Similarly, asthmatic patients exhibit lower levels of circulating IL-10-producing Bregs, and their ability to respond to LPS is diminished compared to healthy individuals (Kliem and Schaub, 2024, Satitsuksanoa et al., 2023).

Furthermore, Bregs have been shown to induce Treg differentiation, which further suppresses the allergic inflammatory response. Through the production of IL-10, Bregs inhibit T helper 1 (Th1) and Th17 cell differentiation while promoting the induction of FoxP3 + Tregs, thereby dampening the pro-inflammatory cascade (Kliem and Schaub, 2024, Piper et al., 2019). The role of Bregs in suppressing allergic inflammation is crucial, and therapies aimed at enhancing their function could offer new avenues for treating allergic diseases.

Bregs also contribute to allergen tolerance by promoting IgG4 production, which in-creases during allergen immunotherapy. IL-10 is instrumental in this process, as it sup-presses IL-4-induced IgE synthesis while enhancing IgG4 class-switch recombination (CSR). IgG4 captures allergens before they bind to FcεRI-bound IgE, thereby preventing mast cell and basophil activation (Allen, 2022, Satitsuksanoa et al., 2023).

The impairment of Breg function and IL-10 production is a hallmark of severe allergic diseases. For example, DOCK8-deficient Bregs in a murine asthma model exhibit reduced responses to TLR9 signaling, leading to impaired IL-10 production and exacerbated in-flammation. Similarly, reduced Breg populations and IL-10 production are observed in both adult and pediatric patients with allergic asthma, correlating with increased disease severity and poor immune regulation (Boonpiyathad et al., 2019).

Memory B cells play a critical role in the long-term maintenance of immune responses by persisting in a relatively quiescent state and rapidly reactivating upon allergen re-exposure. Upon reactivation, memory B cells can differentiate into plasma cells or ger-minal center B cells, further promoting IgE production (Kliem and Schaub, 2024). In aller-gic conditions, the role of memory B cells, particularly those contributing to IgE-mediated responses, has been a focus of investigation. However, studies have suggested that IgE memory B cells are relatively rare in circulation and that their role in the recall response to allergens may be minimal. Instead, most of the IgE produced in allergic reactions comes from short-lived plasma cells in the extrafollicular foci rather than from memory B cells in the germinal centers (“Human BCR Analysis of Single-Sorted, Putative IgE + Memory B Cells in Food Allergy - ClinicalKey,” n.d.).

Allergen- specific T cells are central to immune regulation, determining whether the immune system exhibits tolerance or pathology toward allergens. Specific subsets like Type 2a T helper cells (Th2a), Tregs, Type 1 regulatory T cells (Tr1), and follicular regula-tory T cells play key roles. Th2 cells produce IL-4, IL-5, and IL-13, promoting IgE synthesis, eosinophil recruitment, and mucus hypersecretion. These features define type I hypersen-sitivity reactions observed in asthma, rhinitis, and food allergies.

Th2 cells themselves may limit Th1 and Th17 activity through cytokine-mediated inhibition, but this balance is often dysregulated in allergies (Suhrkamp et al., 2023). Th2 and Th1 immune responses exhibit a mutually antagonistic relationship. Cytokines produced by Th2 cells, particularly IL-4, inhibit the production of IFN-γ by Th1 cells, thereby suppressing Th1 differentiation.

Conversely, IFN-γ secreted by Th1 cells actively inhibits Th2 polarization. In the con-text of allergic diseases, a predominance of Th2 responses effectively downregulates Th1 activity, leading to an immune system skewed toward type 2 inflammation. Although Th2 and Th17 responses are traditionally regarded as distinct pathways, emerging evidence indicates their coexistence in certain severe allergic conditions, such as chronic asthma. Under these circumstances, Th17 cells contribute to exacerbated tissue inflammation through their production of IL-17 and related cytokines, thereby establishing a complex cytokine network that amplifies pathological inflammation and disease severity (Suhrkamp et al., 2023; Bernaldo de Quiros et al., 2018).Recent research has called into question the traditional Th1/Th2 paradigm, high-lighting significant overlaps in immune mechanisms between allergic and autoimmune diseases (“The Th1/Th2 Paradigm: A Misrepresentation of Helper T Cell Plasticity - Clini-calKey,” n.d.).These overlaps include shared genetic susceptibility loci and environmental influences, such as microbiome dysbiosis and reduced biodiversity. Epidemiological evi-dence indicates that individuals with allergic conditions, including asthma, atopic ecze-ma, and allergic rhinitis, exhibit an elevated risk of developing autoimmune disorders such as systemic lupus erythematosus (SLE), rheumatoid arthritis, and inflammatory bowel disease. This co-occurrence points to common underlying pathways of immune dysregulation, with Th17 cells emerging as critical mediators in the pathophysiology of both allergic and autoimmune conditions (). As for tolerance to allergens, it is often mediated by Tregs, which suppress na-ive T cells, prevent Th2 differentiation, and induce anergy or deletion of allergen-specific effector T cells (Suhrkamp et al., 2023).

Tregs suppress allergic inflammation through mechanisms like cytokine secretion (IL-10, TGF-β), cytolysis, and metabolic control. These processes ensure tolerance to food antigens in non-allergic individuals (Piconese et al., 2021). Tregs, including thymus-derived (tTregs) and peripherally induced (pTregs), are crucial for maintaining peripheral tolerance. They control inflammation via FOXP3 expression, cytokine secretion, and interaction with APCs, particularly tolerogenic dendritic cells (DCs). These DCs facilitate Treg differentiation and function by producing factors such as indoleamine 2,3-dioxygenase (IDO) and retinoic acid (Tai et al., 2023).

Shared immunological mechanisms between multiple sclerosis and allergies

MS and allergy disorders, while clinically dissimilar, share underlying immunolog-ical pathways that emphasize a complex interaction between genetic predispositions, en-vironmental exposures, and immune regulation. Both diseases entail a dysregulated im-munological response, even though with different Th cell polarizations. MS is predomi-nantly caused by Th1 and Th17-mediated inflammatory responses that target self-antigens in the central nervous system, resulting in demyelination. Allergies, on the other hand, are dominated by Th2 responses, which are distinguished by increased IgE synthesis and eosinophilic inflammation (Bartůňková et al., 2009). The hygiene hypothesis offers a coherent paradigm for understanding the growth in MS and allergies in industrialized nations. Reduced early-life exposure to microbial antigens can compromise immune system development, causing a shift in the balance of pro-inflammatory and regulatory T cells. This shift increases the susceptibility to auto-immune illnesses like MS and hypersensitivity problems such as allergies (Okada et al., 2010). Early microbial exposure is necessary for the development of Tregs, which reduce both Th1-driven autoimmune and Th2-driven allergic reactions. Emerging studies indi-cate that Breg and especially memory B cells, are key players in MS due to their antigen experience, increased lifespan, and rapid response to stimulation. They may contribute to the disease's pathology through antigen presentation and cytokine produc-tion(Lipińska-Opałka et al., 2024; DiSano et al., 2021).

Genetic predispositions strengthen the relationship between these disorders. The HLA region, namely the HLA-DRB1*1501 allele, is highly linked to MS susceptibility. This allele is also involved in modifying immunological responses in allergy disorders, im-plying shared genetic pathways (Barcellos et al., 2003). Furthermore, cytokine gene poly-morphisms impacting IL-12 and IL-13 production are related with Th1/Th2 imbalances, which influence both autoimmune and allergy diseases (Bartůňková et al., 2009).

According to epidemiological research, allergic disorders can increase the likelihood of developing certain autoimmune ailments, such as MS. For example, a large cohort re-search indicated an elevated risk of autoimmune illnesses among persons with allergic rhinitis, asthma, or eczema, highlighting the immunological interplay between these con-ditions (Krishna et al., 2019). These findings call into question the classic Th1/Th2 para-digm, pointing to a more complex immune dysregulation caused by shared causes such as infections, microbiome changes, and genetic variants.

Both illnesses cause abnormalities in Treg and Breg function and cytokine signaling. In MS, Treg-mediated suppression is lost, resulting in unregulated Th1/Th17 activity, whereas in allergies, compromised Tregs fail to moderate Th2 responses. The kynurenine metabolic pathway is especially interesting in the context of MS and allergies because it connects immunological and metabolic control. Metabolites like kynurenic acid and quinolinic acid affect Treg activity and cytokine production, both of which are important in autoimmune and allergy diseases. Dysregulation of this pathway has been reported in MS and may potentially contribute to the pathophysiology of allergy illnesses, making it an attractive target for future research and treatment. The serotonin pathway is responsible for 1–2 % of dietary tryptophan metabolism, creating serotonin, a neurotransmitter important for mood regulation, and melatonin, a hormone implicated in circadian rhythm. Commensal bacteria in the gut can convert tryptophan into indoles, which influence host immunity and gut-brain axis connections via ligands for the aryl hydrocarbon receptor (AhR). The kynurenine pathway degrades the vast majority of tryptophan (around 95 %). This process begins with the oxidative cleavage of tryptophan to N-formylkynurenine, which is mediated by indoleamine-2,3-dioxygenase (IDO1 and IDO2) or trypto-phan-2,3-dioxygenase (TDO). These enzymes are activated by numerous stimuli, including pro-inflammatory cytokines such as interferon-gamma (IFN-γ), and are differently ex-pressed in tissues. IDO is mostly present in immunological and epithelial cells, while TDO is predominantly hepatic. N-formylkynurenine is then degraded into kynurenine, a key metabolite in the process. Kynurenine is a precursor to several downstream metabolites, including kynurenic acid, 3-hydroxykynurenine, anthranilic acid, and quinolinic acid. These metabolites have a wide range of biological characteristics, from neuroprotective (kynurenic acid) to neurotoxic (quinolinic acid), and they play an important role in maintaining homeostasis or contributing to disease when dysregulated. The last step in the route is the creation of nicotinamide adenine dinucleotide (NAD+), a key coenzyme in cellular respiration and energy production (Stone and Williams, 2023, Badawy, 2017).

In multiple sclerosis (MS), indoleamine 2,3-dioxygenase (IDO) enzymes and tryptophan metabolism play an important role in shaping B cell responses also. Both IDO1 and IDO2 can influence B cells in opposite ways: IDO1 tends to drive immunosuppressive effects, promoting regulatory B cells (Bregs) and IL-10 production, while IDO2 is more often linked to pro-inflammatory, autoreactive

B cell activity. Experimental models of MS have shown that IDO activity contributes to the expansion of Bregs and improvement of disease symptoms, but blocking IDO with inhibitors like 1-methyltryptophan reverses these protective effects (Dagenais-Lussier et al., 2021). Thus, in MS, dysregulated tryptophan metabolism may shift the balance between regulatory and pathogenic B cell functions. Considering this pathway is crucial for optimizing treatments and vaccination strategies, since fine-tuning Trp metabolism could enhance protective humoral responses while reducing autoimmune B cell activity (Dagenais-Lussier et al., 2021).

Immunological implications of the kynurenine pathway

The kynurenine pathway connects metabolic and immunological processes, primarily through the enzymatic activity of IDO1 and IDO2 and TDO. These enzymes control tryptophan degradation. Among downstream enzymes, kynurenine aminotransferases (KATs) and kynurenine 3-monooxygenase (KMO) are most relevant for immune regulation, generating either anti-inflammatory kynurenic acid or pro-inflammatory metabolites such as 3-hydroxykynurenine and quinolinic acid, while others mainly channel intermediates toward energy metabolism and are less directly involved in shaping immune regulation (Stone and Williams, 2023, Badawy, 2017).

One important aspect of IDO-mediated immunoregulation is its ability to alter T cell activity. IDO-driven tryptophan catabolism causes tryptophan depletion, which activates the GCN2 kinase pathway in T cells. This amino acid-sensing mechanism inhibits effector T cell proliferation while shifting the balance towards Treg differentiation (de Araújo et al., 2017). In experimental pulmonary paracoccidioidomycosis, IDO1 deficiency impaired the IDO1/AhR axis, resulting in diminished Treg expansion, enhanced Th17 responses, and loss of immune tolerance, which collectively aggravated disease severity and promoted autoimmunity (de Araújo et al., 2017). Clinical evidence also supports this concept. In a study of pediatric allergic asthma, reduced IDO activity was strongly correlated with a disrupted Th17/Treg balance, characterized by elevated IL-6 and IL-17 and decreased IL-10 and TGF-β levels (Hu et al., 2020). These findings reinforce the notion that IDO functions as a molecular switch promoting Treg differentiation while restraining Th17 responses.

Another enzyme, KMO plays also a central role in immunoregulation by guiding the metabolism of kynurenine to produce metabolites with pro-oxidative and pro-apoptotic qualities, such as 3-hydroxykynurenine and quinolinic acid. Afterwards, evaluated 3-hydroxykynurenine promotes oxidative stress and synergizes with pro-inflammatory cytokines, such as interleukin-1β, to induce cell death. This results in reduced numbers of pro-inflammatory effector T cells (Hayes et al., 2023). In addition, by depleting Th1/Th17 subsets, KMO activity indirectly shifts cytokine balance, reducing pro-inflammatory signals while permitting conditions that may support Treg differentiation (Hayes et al., 2023). Thus, KMO is a checkpoint that determines whether tryptophan metabolism leads toward an immunosuppressive/apoptotic outcome for effector T cells or a tolerogenic bias favoring Tregs. Importantly, pharmacological investigations identify KMO as a more selective immunometabolic target than IDO, because its inhibition reduces quinolinic acid while raising kynurenic acid, altering the balance away from pro-inflammatory Th1/Th17 responses and toward improved Treg activity (Stone and Williams, 2023). Current evidence does not indicate a direct role for KMO in B-cell biology; its effects on B cells are likely indirect, mediated by changes in T-cell activity and the broader inflammatory milieu.

Furthermore, kynurenine and its metabolites, particularly kynurenic acid, act as ligands for the aryl hydrocarbon receptor (AhR), sustaining the Treg phenotype while reducing inflammatory Th17 cell development. This dual mechanism highlights the KP's significance in immunological tolerance (Stone and Williams, 2023, Badawy, 2017).

Kynurenine metabolites also affect cytokine production. Kynurenic acid, an AhR agonist, has significant anti-inflammatory activities by reducing pro-inflammatory cytokines like TNF-α and IL-6. In contrast, quinolinic acid, a neurotoxic NMDA receptor agonist, worsens inflammation and contributes to tissue destruction. This duality highlights the kynurenine pathways complicated involvement in immune regulation, as the balance of its metabolites can decide whether an immunological response is protective or pathogenic (Stone and Williams, 2023, Badawy, 2017). The interactions between these metabolites result in a dynamic regulatory framework. In homeostasis, anti-inflammatory metabolites such as kynurenic acid and Tregs take precedence, preserving tolerance. Under pathological stress, pro-inflammatory metabolites such quinolinic acid and 3-hydroxykynurenine can activate the immune system and cause tissue damage. This balance is crucial in disorders like MS and allergy conditions, where either excessive inflammation or immune suppression can accelerate disease progression.

Relevance to multiple sclerosis and allergies

The kynurenine pathway is important in structuring immune responses and impacting the pathophysiology of MS and allergy disorders, which have differential immunological polarizations caused by Th1/Th17 and Th2 responses, respectively. Dysregulation of kynurenine pathway can worsen the inflammatory and immunosuppressive pathways that form the basis of these illnesses. Regulation of kynurenine pathway metabolites in multiple sclerosis and allergic diseases.

Impaired tryptophan catabolism is closely associated with immune regulation loss in MS. IDO1/IDO2 and TDO are the main enzymes that metabolize tryptophan, producing downstream kynurenine metabolites that have strong immunoregulatory effects (Correale 2020). Through the GCN2K and mTOR pathways, decreased activity of these enzymes restricts the formation of kynurenine in MS patients, which results in increased Th1/Th17 responses and decreased Treg counts. Experimental research indicates that while injection of the tryptophan metabolite 3-hydroxyanthranilic acid inhibits Th1/Th17 activity, promotes Treg differentiation via DC-derived TGF-β, and improves clinical outcome, IDO1 deficiency exacerbates the severity of the disease. In clinical settings, poor tryptophan catabolism leads to progressive demyelination and neurodegeneration through persistent neuroinflammation and loss of tolerance. This is the cause of relapses characterized by cumulative neurological impairment, motor and sensory deficiencies, cognitive deterioration, and optic neuritis.

In allergic disorders, the activity of IDO1/IDO2 is significantly correlated with alterations in the tryptophan degradation pathway (Esmaeili and Hajavi, 2022). A diminished kynurenine/tryptophan (KYN/TRP) ratio is indicative of impaired conversion of tryptophan into immunoregulatory kynurenine metabolites. This is due to reduced IDO1 activity. The reduced production of kynurenines, which typically promote regulatory T-cell differentiation and suppress excessive Th1/Th2 responses, favours Th2 polarization, a defining characteristic of allergic inflammation. In the clinical setting, this dysregulation is characterized by increased eosinophilia, elevated IgE levels, and intensified inflammatory responses. Conversely, individuals who develop tolerance or are asymptomatic atopic exhibit elevated IDO activity and increased KYN/TRP ratios, underscoring the enzyme's critical function in the preservation of immune tolerance. These results indicate that the targeted modulation of the IDO–kynurenine pathway may be a promising therapeutic approach for the restoration of tolerance in allergic diseases.This comparison demonstrates the kynurenine pathway's dual role in influencing immunological and inflammatory responses in different illness situations (Esmaeili and Hajavi, 2022).

Conclusion

The kynurenine pathway plays a crucial role in modulating immune responses, with its metabolites deploying significant effects on B and T cell function. Regulatory B cells and regulatory T cells (Tregs) emerge as essential players in maintaining immune tolerance, with their impaired function contributing to the progression of multiple sclerosis (MS) and allergic conditions. While Bregs suppress inflammation through cytokines like IL-10 and TGF-β, promoting allergen tolerance and reducing autoimmune responses, Tregs counterbalance pro-inflammatory Th1 and Th17 activity, critical in MS pathology. The interplay between these regulatory cells and the kynurenine metabolites provides valuable insight into the mechanisms of immune dysregulation.

Moreover, the evolving understanding of the Th1/Th2 paradigm highlights the increasing recognition of Th17 cells as central mediators of inflammation in both autoimmune and allergic diseases. The shift from the binary Th1/Th2 framework to a more subtle appreciation of Th1 and Th17 responses underscores the complexity of immune regulation and its pathological manifestations. In MS, the pathogenic role of Th17 cells and their interplay with Tregs offers promising avenues for targeted immunomodulation. Similarly, the presence of Th17 activity alongside Th2 responses in severe allergic conditions reflects the shared immune dysregulation across these diseases. Furthermore, in MS, memory B cells (Bmem) exhibit enhanced antigen-presenting capacity and directly contribute to CNS lesions, whereas in allergies, B mem cells contribute more transiently to the inflammatory milieu, with IgE production largely attributed to short-lived plasma cells.

The kynurenine pathway, with its ability to influence these immune subsets, offers a unique therapeutic target. By modulating the balance between pro-inflammatory and regulatory pathways, interventions aimed at this pathway could restore immune homeostasis, providing relief in both autoimmune and allergic conditions. Understanding these shared mechanisms not only enhances our knowledge of chronic inflammatory diseases but also drives the development of innovative and customized therapeutic strategies.
