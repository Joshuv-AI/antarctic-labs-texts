Redefining Calciphylaxis as a Uniquely Bone Forming Subcutaneous C5b-9–Mediated Microvascular Injury Syndrome Associated With Localized Subcutaneous and Systemic Complement Pathway Activation
Authors: Zachary Wolner, Luna Tello, Taylor Kalomeris, Robert Swerlick, Cynthia M. Magro
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
Background:Microvascular thrombosis is key to the pathogenesis of calciphylaxis. C5b-9–mediated microvascular injury reflective of complement pathway activation could be a key pathophysiologic event.Methods:We conducted a retrospective multicenter study of 24 patients who have had biopsy-supported calciphylaxis from the 2010–2022 data base from Emory where C5b-9 immunohistochemistry (IHC) had not been conducted and the 2019–2023 data base from Cornell where C5b-9 IHC was done as part of the routine calciphylaxis work up. IHC for C5b-9 on lesional biopsy specimens was assessed and correlated with routine light microscopic findings and clinical features.Results:Most of the patients in our study had uremic calciphylaxis associated with obesity, diabetes, dialysis, hypertension, hyperparathyroidism and elevated serum phosphorus. Most patients did not have defined procoagulant and/or hyperviscosity states. The vascular pathology was predominantly limited to the subcutaneous fat and ranged from a calcific intimal arteriopathy to microvascular thrombosis with endothelial injury with or without endothelial calcification. In most cases (ie, in excess of 80%), there was prominent deposition of C5b-9 within the vasculature including the microvasculature and arteries of the fat at least localized to injured vessels suggesting a causal association. In about 40% of cases, there was evidence of systemic complement pathway activation revealed by concurrent dermal microvascular C5b-9 deposition.Conclusions:Calciphylaxis is characterized by subcuticular vascular changes that reflect an interplay between complement triggered endothelial cell injury, resultant vascular thrombosis, and subsequent abluminal calcification. Complement inhibition therapy defines a potential intervention that should be explored.

INTRODUCTION

Calciphylaxis is a rare disorder affecting 1%–4% of patients with end-stage renal disease on dialysis but can occur in nonuremic diverse settings such as warfarin administration, malignancy, Crohn disease, liver disease, and hyperparathyroidism.1 The name designation of calciphylaxis was made by the famous Canadian endocrinologist Hans Selye in 1961 as a form of precipitous hypersensitivity in rodents characterized by the sudden onset of extravascular calcification temporally associated with hyperparathyroidism and hypervitaminosis D along with external triggers such as trauma and metallic salt exposure. Although Selye's rodent model emphasized extravascular calcification, shortly thereafter, the first article appeared describing the human equivalent but where the same underlying disease associations lead to ischemic injury attributable to a unique calcific microangiopathy.1–4

The pathogenesis of calciphylaxis is not completely understood. Pathogenetically, a subcutaneous obliterative intimal calcific small vessel arteriopathy frequently accompanied by subcutaneous capillary endothelial cell calcification and microvascular thrombosis, the latter reflective of a localized or potentially systemic procoagulant state, leads to cutaneous and subcutaneous ischemia. In essence, calciphylaxis reflects a severe ischemic dermopathy syndrome. Patients on dialysis and, in particular, patients with calciphylaxis mobilize calcium from bones, which then has a proclivity to deposit in the intima of arterioles and small arteries in the fat up to 600 μm in diameter and averaging around 100 μm resulting in an obliterative calcific arteriolopathy and arteriopathy.5,6 First-line treatment often attempts to normalize calcium phosphate levels through more intensive dialysis sessions, noncalcium phosphate binders, intravenous sodium thiosulfate, and supportive wound care, including hyperbaric oxygen therapy.7,8 Although no prospective studies have been done, intravenous sodium thiosulfate has been purported to improve morbidity from calciphylaxis.9

One recent case report by one of the authors of this current study highlighted deposits of C3d, C4d, C5b-9, and MBL-associated serine protease 2 within the microvasculature of the dermis and subcutaneous fat in a patient with calciphylaxis and COVID-19, findings indicative of activation of the mannan lectin and alternative pathways.10,11 That same author has been conducting the C5b-9 assay on routine clinical cases of calciphylaxis for the past 15 years as part of the diagnostic evaluation of the biopsy material and consistently reported significant microvascular deposition of C5b-9 (personal observations). This study formally explores the role of complement activation in the pathogenesis of calciphylaxis.

METHODS

A search of pathology specimens between July 2010 and September 2022 at 1 academic medical center identified 12 patients who have a clinical history and histopathologic features consistent with calciphylaxis. An additional 12 cases diagnosed with calciphylaxis, where biopsies had C5b-9 conducted as part of the routine work up, were uncovered between 2019 and 2023 in another academic institution. Clinical, laboratory, imaging, and pathology data were reviewed and recorded (Table 1). An acute presentation was arbitrarily defined as a lesion that was biopsied ≤30 days from clinical documentation of appearance, and a chronic presentation was defined as a case that was biopsied >30 days from clinical documentation. Calciphylaxis lesion distribution was categorized as local and multifocal. A local distribution designated those cases where 1 or multiple lesions were located in 1 anatomic region, whereas a multifocal distribution referred to multiple lesions distributed across 2 or more anatomic regions. In certain cases, some limited work up for a hypercoagulable state was conducted. Potential parameters assessed included deficiency of antithrombin III or proteins C and S, activated protein C resistance with follow-up genotyping for factor V Leiden mutation, prothrombin mutation (G20210A), elevated factor VIII level, presence of lupus anticoagulant, presence of anti-IgG or IgM cardiolipin antibodies, presence of heparin-PF4 antibodies, or elevated homocysteine levels.12 Markers of coagulation and hemostasis activation (from here on referred to as “activation of the coagulation cascade”) including elevated prothrombin fragment 1.2 (PF1.2), elevated PF1.2 and thrombin antithrombin complexes, or elevated thrombin antithrombin complexes with a normal PF1.2 were assessed in certain cases from one institution.13

Unstained slides from 15 cases from Emory University were evaluated for C5b-9 microvascular deposition via immunohistochemistry performed at Weill Cornell Medicine; hematoxylin and eosin (H&E) stained slides were available on each case to allow a correlation between the extent and pattern of microvascular C5b-9 and light microscopic findings. The remaining 12 cases were procured from the archival slide files of the division of dermatopathology, Weill Cornell School of Medicine where the light microscopic findings and C5b-9 results were detailed in the preexisting biopsy reports. Among the routine light microscopic features assessed in each case were features of acute calciphylaxis, characterized by epidermal, eccrine coil, and subcutaneous fat necrosis (nonlipomembranous variant) and features of chronic calciphylaxis as revealed by fibrosis, neovascularization, and hemosiderin deposition. In both acute and chronic cases, lipomembranous fat necrosis, a recognized form of ischemic fat necrosis, was not a morphologic determinant of an acute versus chronic designation. Dermal vascular ectasia and other dermal changes such as microvascular thrombosis were assessed. Within the fat, calcification of the vasculature was examined, primarily focusing on intimal calcification of arteries and arterioles and/or endothelial cell calcification typically at the capillary level. The presence or absence of subcutaneous thrombosis was determined.

Regarding the deposition pattern of C5b-9, systemic complement pathway activation was an evidence-based cutoff of at least 10 vessels within the dermis showing endothelial and subendothelial cell staining. C5b-9 deposition in the fat was evaluated as a separate morphologic parameter whereby the nature of the vessels showing the deposition pattern was determined. Three primary patterns were determined namely C5b-9 in arteries showing calcific change, C5b-9 in damaged thrombosed microvessels, and C5b-9 in structurally normal appearing vessels.14 As the end point for administration of anti-C5 therapy is the presence of C5b-9, additional complement studies on C3d, C4d, and MBL-associated serine protease 2 to explore other complement pathways (ie, classic, alternative, and mannan-binding lectin) were not evaluated.

The methodologies for C3d, C4d, and C5b-9 staining have been described in prior publications. Immunohistochemical staining of C3d (Ready to use polyclonal antibody, Cell Marque), C4d (1:50, monoclonal antibody, Quidel), and C5b-9 (1:250, clone aE11, Dako) was accomplished using the Bond III Autostainer (Leica Microsystems, IL). Formalin-fixed and paraffin-embedded tissue sections were first baked and deparaffinized. Antigen retrieval was followed by heating the slides at 99–100°C in Bond Epitope Retrieval Solution 2 for 20 minutes (for C3d and C4d) and at 37°C in Bond Enzyme solution for 10 minutes (for C5b-9).10,15–17

RESULTS

Clinical Features

Regarding the first patient cohort (Emory University School of Medicine), the patients' ages ranged from 33 years of age to 78 years of age at the time of biopsy, with a median of 57.5 and a mean of 56.8 years. There were 9 female patients and 3 male patients. Ten patients presented with 1 or more erythematous to violaceous focally ulcerated plaques, 1 presented with blistering skin, and 1 with hip pain (Figs. 1A, B). Six patients had multifocal disease, whereas it was localized in 6 patients. In most cases, lesions involved the distal lower extremity, whereas there was proximal lower limb involvement in 4, low back involvement in 1, and both upper and lower limb proximal involvement in 1 case. The overall duration of the eruption ranged from 2 days up to >365 days. Six of the cases had lesions of calciphylaxis for greater than 30 days, whereas in 5 cases, the duration was less than 30 days, and 1 case had an unknown duration. Comorbidities were prevalent, with most patients having a history of diabetes mellitus, hypertension, obesity, and hyperparathyroidism. Three individuals had elevated phosphorus levels, whereas 4 had a positive smoking history. Antiphospholipid antibody testing was conducted in 7 cases and was negative in all cases. Four of the patients had been on steroids chronically. None had known underlying autoimmune disease or antinuclear antigen (ANA) testing. All 12 cases were classified as uremic.

A, B, The clinical lesions of calciphylaxis reflect the occlusive nature of the subcutaneous vasculopathy that defines calciphylaxis. In (A), the patient presents with striking livedo reticularis without any discernible ulcers. Livedo reticularis typically reflects a deeper seated occlusive vasculopathy (A). In (B), the ischemic microenvironment has led to frank ulceration (B).

With respect to the second patient cohort (Weill Cornell Medicine), there was a total of 12 cases uncovered where C5b-9 was conducted as part of the diagnostic evaluation of the case over a time period of 5 years. The patients ranged from 50 years of age to 85 years of age. The median age at the time of biopsy was 74 and the mean age was 69.5 years. There were 10 female patients and 2 male patients. All patients presented with 1 or more erythematous retiform to violaceous focally ulcerated plaques. Six patients had multifocal disease, whereas it was localized in 6 patients. Lesions predominately involved the distal lower extremities. Three cases had involvement of the thigh, whereas 1 had involvement of the buttock. The overall duration of the eruption ranged from 1 day up to 395 days. Seven of the cases had lesions of calciphylaxis for greater than 30 days, whereas in 4 cases, the duration was less than 30 days. In 1 case, the onset of the lesion was unknown. Over half the patients had diabetes mellitus, hypertension, obesity, hyperparathyroidism, and elevated phosphorus levels and were on dialysis for chronic kidney disease. Four of the cases had been on steroids chronically. Three patients had a positive smoking history. Antiphospholipid antibody testing was conducted in 5 cases and was positive in 2 of the cases (cases 2 and 9). Regarding underlying autoimmune disease, 2 patients had Crohn disease and 1 had rheumatoid arthritis. Of the 5 patients who had an ANA test, 2 were positive (cases 9 and 11). Eight cases were classified as uremic and 4 were nonuremic variants of calciphylaxis. Warfarin use, pancreatic adenocarcinoma, and hyperthyroidism were seen in patients with nonuremic calciphylaxis.

Light Microscopic Findings

With respect to the first patient cohort (Emory University School of Medicine), there were a total of 15 cases received for integrated (H&E) and C5b-9 immunohistochemical assessment by Weill Cornell Medicine. Twelve of the cases (cases 1, 2, 3, 4, 5, 7, 8, 9, 10, 11, 12, and 15) had diagnostic features of calciphylaxis; 3 of the cases (cases 6, 13, and 14) were not held to have unequivocal diagnostic features of calciphylaxis on the material received by Weill Cornell Medicine for C5b-9 assessment. Of the diagnostic cases, 6 had classic features of chronic calciphylaxis characterized by the absence of acute necrosis and the presence of dermal and/or subcutaneous fibrosis with neovascularization in the dermis and variable dermal and/or subcutaneous hemosiderin deposition (Figs. 2A, B). Four had features of acute calciphylaxis without concurrent chronic features. The acute features were characterized by abrupt epidermal necrosis and/or acute nonlipomembranous subcutaneous fat necrosis with marked adipocyte disruption, fibrin deposition, and influx of scavenger macrophages into the acutely damaged fat (Fig. 2B). Two cases had features of both acute and chronic calciphylaxis; they showed a combination of abrupt epidermal and/or subcutaneous fat necrosis, fibrosis, hemosiderin deposition, and/or neovascularization.

Dermal and/or subcutaneous fibrosis with neovascularization in the dermis and variable dermal and/or subcutaneous hemosiderin deposition was a characteristic finding in cases of chronic calciphylaxis and reflects the natural reparative response to local ischemia. In this particular image, there is interlobular septal fibrosis and increased vascularity. In addition, there are concurrent features of acute calciphylaxis given the extent of fat necrosis heralded by the influx of lipid laden macrophages (A). In this particular photomicrograph, the dermis exhibits fibrosis and neovascularization. Although the diagnostic vascular changes are typically confined to the microvasculature of the subcutaneous fat, the chronic ischemic changes such as fibrosis are oftentimes present in the overlying dermis (B).

Calcific intimal arteriopathy was observed in 7 cases. Calcific arteriopathy could be seen in cases that fell into the acute, chronic, and/or overlapping acute/chronic category (Figs. 3A, B). Endothelial cell calcification within the subcutaneous microvasculature was seen in 5 cases and was observed in cases showing both acute and chronic calciphylaxis (Fig. 4). Subcutaneous microvascular thrombosis (Figs. 5A, B), with or without endothelial localization (Fig. 6) of calcium, was seen in 4 cases and, therefore, was a potential histologic finding encountered in the acute, mixed, and chronic calciphylaxis setting. Livedoid vascular ectasia was seen in 5 cases; 3 of which had chronic changes of calciphylaxis, whereas 2 had histologic features of acute calciphylaxis. Three of these cases had an obliterative calcific arteriopathy.

DISCUSSION

Calciphylaxis is a well-established prototypic ischemic dermopathy syndrome that can occur in both a uremic and nonuremic setting.18,19 There are aspects of the histopathology that are distinct for this entity and separate it from other catastrophic cutaneous occlusive vascular syndromes.

In our bi-institutional study, the light microscopic findings seen in our 24 cases were reproducible between cases. In particular, the confinement of the vascular pathology to the subcutaneous fat is one of the cardinal hallmarks of calciphylaxis and was well exemplified in our cases excluding 1 case of warfarin-induced calciphylaxis where there was concurrent dermal microvascular thrombosis. We have previously reported on calciphylaxis in the setting of severe/critical COVID-19 whereby the biopsy showed classic concurrent dermal features of COVID-19–associated retiform thrombotic purpura. In addition, 1 aspect of the vascular pathology that likely contributed significantly to the overlying cutaneous ischemia was an obliterative calcific arteriopathy, which resulted in severe luminal attenuation and, therefore, significantly restricted proximal blood flow. The result of this distal subcutaneous obstructive vasculopathy is an overlying ischemic cutaneous microenvironment. This finding was seen in most of our cases. Another characteristic histologic feature was a significant pauci-inflammatory thrombogenic vasculopathy with or without concurrent endothelial cell calcification. There was a reasonable correlation between the identification of certain histologic findings of chronicity namely in the context of fibrosis, neovascularization, and hemosiderin deposition and a more protracted duration of symptoms.5

In most cases, there were significant subcutaneous deposits of C5b-9 within the microvasculature, whereas in almost 60% of the Cornell cases and one-third of the Emory cases, there was evidence of systemic complement pathway activation based on the degree of dermal microvascular C5b-9 over and above the subcutaneous vascular deposition of C5b-9 as will be discussed at present. Particularly high levels of microvascular C5b-9 were localized to the injured and thrombosed microvessels (ie, at the capillary and venular level) within the fat to suggest a causal relationship between complement pathway activation and microvascular thrombosis. Potential risk factors associated with evidence of both complement pathway activation and a striking pattern of subcutaneous microvascular thrombosis included diabetes mellitus, dialysis, and antiphospholipid antibodies. As calcification of the endothelium was frequently seen, one might hypothesize that damage to endothelium mediated by complement activation may be a key event that could potentially perpetuate or even be causal of a microvascular bone forming phenotype as revealed by variable endothelial and intimal calcification. A similar pattern of endothelial C5b-9 could be seen in the larger vessels showing an obliterative calcific arteriopathy. It has been suggested in an earlier study that fibrin deposition as a sequelae of endothelial cell injury could be a critical impetus to local osteopontin production from either the endothelium or smooth muscle of the vasculature.20 In this regard, complement-mediated endothelial cell injury at the larger vessel arterial level could presage the calcific fibrosing intimal arteriopathy. With chronicity, changes typical for the chronic ischemic microenvironment such as fibrosis and neovascularization emerge and define a distinctive phase in the dynamic evolutionary morphology seen in calciphylaxis.

Patients with systemic and subcutaneous localized complement pathway activation not surprisingly had underlying diseases associated with systemic complement pathway activation specifically about diabetes mellitus and dialysis. Complement activation in hemodialysis is attributable to the binding of ficolin 2 and mannan binding lectin to the hemodialysis membrane with the end result being mannan binding lectin pathway activation.21–23 In diabetes mellitus, a different mechanism underlies complement pathway activation namely about the glycosylation of the C5b-9 regulatory molecule CD59 resulting in the unchecked formation of C5b-9 independent of the complement pathway activated.24

Another consistent association was hyperparathyroidism, which was likely not a factor causative of complement pathway activation but reflective of renal failure; in the same way, high phosphorus levels were likely not a factor contributing to complement pathway activation but rather was a sign of renal failure. Renal failure in turn leads to aberrations in calcium phosphate metabolism.

Some of the patients in our study were on warfarin before the development of calciphylaxis, suggesting a causal association between warfarin administration and the development of calciphylaxis. Warfarin is a critical inhibitor of a vitamin K–dependent matrix protein 1a ,which naturally attenuates arterial calcification. Furthermore, warfarin directly inhibits the ability of endothelial cells to elaborate protein S, a critical component of the natural anticoagulant system. Hence, although utilized for its anticoagulant properties, warfarin can promote both a paradoxical local procoagulant and calcium forming milieu within the microvasculature, creating the optimal microenvironment conducive for the development of calciphylaxis.25,26

Overall, it is reasonable to state that the subcutaneous arteriopathy and thrombogenic microangiopathy of calciphylaxis are associated with localized activation of the complement pathway in the fat with or without concurrent evidence of systemic complement pathway activation. The ubiquitous finding of microvascular complement deposition within the subcutaneous vasculature in patients with calciphylaxis is an important finding. Although prior studies have not emphasized the role of complement pathway activation in the pathogenesis of calciphylaxis, there is an anecdotal case report that highlights complement pathway activation in a biopsy of calciphylaxis.10,11

The aberrations in calcium phosphate metabolism as revealed by hyperparathyroidism and elevation in phosphorus are critical requisites for the endothelial and or intimal precipitation of calcium, an event that likely rests on an initial insult to endothelium such as endothelial injury mediated by the membranolytic attack complex (ie, C5b-9). Obesity is also clearly a risk factor as it was seen in most of our cases. The association between obesity and the localization of calciphylaxis to fat suggests that obesity is an important requisite for the development of calciphylaxis. Oxidative stress and reactive oxygen species are significant within the subcutaneous fat of obese patients. Nitrous oxide activity and endoplasmic reticulum stress in adipocytes increases reactive oxygen species, which in turn can stimulate the release of inflammatory cytokines.9,27 Inflammation and oxidative stress could contribute to vascular injury including their role in activating the classic and mannan binding lectin pathways, therefore, priming vessels for acquiring a bone forming phenotype.

Although our study emphasized the consistent findings of diabetes, dialysis, hypertension, obesity, high phosphorus, hyperparathyroidism, and hypertension as risk factors, the identification of specific hyperviscosity and objective laboratory data supportive of a hypercoagulable state were not identified in most patients although 2 patients had antiphospholipid antibodies. In contrast, a case control study in 2018 identified lupus anticoagulant, protein C deficiency, and combined thrombophilia in dialysis patients to be significantly associated with calciphylaxis development.12 Furthermore, King et al retrospectively reviewed 16 patients with calciphylaxis who had received concomitant direct oral anticoagulants. With a mean follow-up of 523 days, 5 (31%) had complete resolution and 9 were alive (56%).28 Critically, these studies linked calciphylaxis to hypercoagulability and anticoagulation with clinical improvement.

Complement pathway activation leading to C5b-9–mediated endothelial cell injury is the pathologic cornerstone of several thrombotic microangiopathies, such as atypical hemolytic uremic syndrome (aHUS) and catastrophic antiphospholipid syndrome.16,29 We would propose that calciphylaxis also falls under the umbrella of a potentially catastrophic thrombotic microangiopathy syndrome. In a prior study examining the role of the normal skin biopsy in diagnosing atypical hemolytic uremic syndrome, striking microvascular deposits of C5b-9 were observed within the dermis in patients with atypical hemolytic uremic syndrome. The study emphasized the role of the normal skin biopsy in establishing a diagnosis of systemic complement pathway activation and, therefore, providing quantitative C5b-9 vascular data that would predict the potential responsiveness to eculizumab and justification for its administration. Patients with atypical HUS are routinely treated with eculizumab, a monoclonal antibody that binds C5 prohibiting its division in C5a and C5b leading to rapid improvement in renal function and normalization of anemia and thrombocytopenia.30 Establishing this link between calciphylaxis and complement pathway activation could open a new therapeutic avenue with anti-C5 therapy, which to date has been very successful in abrogating thrombotic complications associated with aHUS, catastrophic antiphospholipid antibody syndrome and Kohlmeier–Degos disease.12 Anti-C5 therapy for calciphylaxis seems a very feasible therapeutic option that should be explored at least in select cases especially those with evidence of systemic complement pathway activation.
