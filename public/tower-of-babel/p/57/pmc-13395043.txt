A price check on the ketogenic diet for adults with epilepsy in Australia
Authors: Lillian King, Jessica Maccarrone, Ashwinpreet Kaur, Susanna Chen, Patrick Kwan, Terence J. O'Brien, Neha Kaul
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractObjectiveThe ketogenic diet is an established and effective treatment for drug‐resistant epilepsy and is an emerging therapy for other conditions. Affordability of therapeutic ketogenic diets for epilepsy has not been well studied. This study aimed to compare weekly cost of ketogenic diets with national dietary guidelines and a typical Australian diet.MethodSeven‐day meal plans were created for a reference male and female, aged 31–50 years, for five diets: classical ketogenic, modified ketogenic, specialty modified ketogenic, national dietary guidelines, and a typical Australian diet. Prices were obtained from three supermarkets.ResultsFor females, the classical ketogenic diet ($70.07AUD) and typical Australian diet ($75.91AUD) were cheaper than the national dietary guidelines ($99.23AUD). For males, all ketogenic diet types and the national dietary guidelines ($94.81–119.60AUD) were more expensive than the typical Australian diet ($82.98). Following the dietary guidelines, modified or specialty modified ketogenic diets increased food costs by 9%–31% for females and 26%–44% for males compared to current spending on a typical Australian diet.SignificanceOur study demonstrates that, for males aged 31–50 years, ketogenic diet therapy for epilepsy is more expensive than the typical Australian diet. Financial assistance may be considered to reduce barriers to dietary therapy for epilepsy.Plain Language SummaryA specialized ketogenic diet is an effective treatment for managing epilepsy when medication has been ineffective at controlling seizures. This study shows that some ketogenic diets for epilepsy cost more than a typical Australian diet. The higher costs may stop people from starting or staying on this helpful treatment. Providing financial support to help pay for these diets could make them easier to follow and allow more people to access this treatment. This could help more people manage their seizures and lower the long‐term costs of epilepsy for the healthcare system and society.

INTRODUCTION

The classical ketogenic diet and its variations are an effective treatment for people living with drug‐resistant epilepsy and some metabolic epilepsies. 1 , 2 Despite its effectiveness, sustained adherence to dietary therapy remains low, particularly among adults. 2 The perceived higher cost of ketogenic diet therapies has been reported as barriers to referrals, commencement, and continuation of dietary therapies from neurologists and people living with epilepsy, or their caregivers. 3 , 4 , 5

Previous studies 6 , 7 examining affordability of various specialized diets have reported the ketogenic diet to be among the most expensive. These studies evaluated the ketogenic diet for weight loss, and not the therapeutic high‐fat, low‐carbohydrate ketogenic diet used for epilepsy treatment. In this study, we compared the cost of ketogenic diet plans for epilepsy and the national dietary guidelines with the cost of a typical Australian diet.

METHODS

To determine the cost of dietary therapies for epilepsy, 7‐day meal plans were developed to meet the nutritional needs of a reference male and female across five reference diets: the Australian Guide to Healthy Eating diet, a typical Australian diet, and three ketogenic diets—the modified ketogenic diet, classical ketogenic diet, and specialty modified ketogenic diet that included commercially available specialty low‐carbohydrate products. The macronutrient breakdown of the reference diets is available in Table S1.

Seven‐day meal plans were created using FoodWorks Online (Xyris Pty Ltd., Australia). The characteristics of the reference individuals for this study, aged 31–50 years, were based on the reference individuals used to calculate the Australia and New Zealand 2005 Nutrition Reference Values. 8 An activity factor of 1.3 was applied to estimate energy requirements, and the recommended dietary intake was used for protein requirements. 8

The Australian Guide to Healthy Eating diet followed the recommended food group serves per day for reference individuals. 9 Data from the 2011 to 2012 Australian Health Survey showed less than 4% of adults met Australian Guide to Healthy Eating recommendations. 10 To address this, a fifth meal plan was created to reflect the typical Australian diet. This reflected 2022 to 2023 daily consumption patterns for all Australians based on data from the Australian Bureau of Statistics. 11 For the modified ketogenic diet and specialty modified ketogenic diet, the composition was based on 75% of energy from fat, 20% from protein, and 5% from carbohydrates. 12 The classical ketogenic diet was developed using a ratio of 2.5:1 (grams of fat:grams of protein and carbohydrate combined) to ensure the minimum daily recommended dietary intake for protein was met. The total daily energy for all meal plans was similar, ~2100 kcal for the male and ~1750 kcal for the female, based on the Australian Nutrient Reference Values. 8 Meal plans were designed to optimize cost‐effectiveness and minimize waste by using frozen products, repeating recipes, and consumption of leftover meals. The proportion of food products purchased but not utilized in the meal plans was similar across all diets. The meal plans are available in Table S2.

Data analysis

A cross‐sectional survey collected ingredient prices for each meal plan. Price data were obtained in July 2024 from Australia's three leading grocery retailers by market share: Woolworths, Coles, and Aldi. For each item, the lowest price per gram item was selected and the average price from the three supermarkets used in the analysis. Weekly per‐gram shopping costs were calculated as the total 7‐day diet cost in absolute grams (Table 1). Weekly costs were compared to the 2024 median income for Australian males and females and Disability Support Pension (DSP) to determine the percentage of income needed for the diet. 13 , 14 Weekly meal‐plan costs, in purchasable units, were also calculated and presented in Figure 1. Purchasable units were defined as the quantity of an item required to fulfill the weekly meal plans, rounded up to the nearest whole commercially available unit.

RESULTS

Cost—female

The classical ketogenic diet was the cheapest weekly, per‐gram, meal plan at $70.07 per week, followed by the typical Australian diet, the specialty modified ketogenic diet, and the modified ketogenic diet, with the Australian Guide to Healthy Eating diet being the most expensive at $99.23 (Table 1). The total weekly food shop costs, in purchasable units, showed similar results (Figure 1).

Cost—male

The typical Australian diet was the cheapest per‐gram meal plan at $82.98 per week, followed by the classical ketogenic diet, Australian Guide to Healthy Eating diet, and specialty modified ketogenic diet. The most expensive was the modified ketogenic diet at $119.60 (Table 1). Comparing the cost of the total weekly food shop in purchasable units showed that the Australian Guide to Healthy Eating diet was the most expensive, while the typical Australian diet remained the cheapest (Figure 1).

The contribution of each food group to the weekly per‐gram meal plan costs for both males and females can be seen in Figure 2. A key driver of the higher cost of the ketogenic meal plans and the Australian Guide to Healthy Eating diet among males was the cost of lean protein sources, which accounted for 36.8%–53.1% of costs in these diets compared to only 18.4% of costs in a typical Australian diet. Costs of specialty ketogenic grains, which were often higher in protein than non‐ketogenic grains and were used in the specialty modified ketogenic diet meal plan, were offset by reductions in the cost of lean protein.

In 2023, the median weekly taxable income was $1509 for males and $1130 for females. 13 The percentage of median weekly taxable income spent on the per‐gram cost of each meal plan ranged from 6% to 9% for females and 5% to 8% for males. 13

The weekly DSP payment was $558.15 in 2023. 14 The percentage of weekly income spent on the per‐gram cost of each meal plan ranged from 13% to 18% for females and 15% to 21% for males receiving the DSP (Table 1).

DISCUSSION

The weekly, per‐gram cost of the five meal plans ranged from $70.00 to 99.23AUD for the reference female and $82.98 to 119.60AUD for the reference male. The Australian Guide to Healthy Eating diet and the modified ketogenic diet were the most expensive diets, using per‐gram costs, for the reference female and male respectively.

For both females and males respectively, the typical Australian diet was the least expensive ($130.00–135.60AUD) and the Australian Guide to Healthy Eating diet was the most expensive ($206.10–211.88AUD) when comparing weekly diet costs in purchasable units (Figure 1). When comparing weekly diet costs in purchasable units, all ketogenic diet types were more expensive than the typical Australian diet for both males and females (Figure 1).

A barrier to dietary therapy for epilepsy, as reported by people with epilepsy and clinicians, is the perceived higher cost of the ketogenic diet. This may negatively impact referrals to ketogenic diet services as well as people with epilepsy's willingness to consider dietary therapy. 3 , 4 , 5 Our study highlights that the cost of ketogenic diets for epilepsy is not significantly more expensive than following the Australian Guide to Healthy Eating recommendations. This differs with other similar studies which reported the ketogenic diet to be more expensive. 6 , 7 Previous studies examining costs of the ketogenic diet designed ketogenic meal plans for indications other than epilepsy and often used costly products such as premium brand protein powders which may have increased prices. 7

As less than 5% of Australians currently meet the Australian Guide to Healthy Eating recommendations, comparing the costs of a ketogenic diet solely to the Australian Guide to Healthy Eating diet is not a true reflection of affordability. 9 This study addresses a gap in the literature by comparing the cost of specialized ketogenic diets to the cost of a typical Australian diet, making the results more generalizable.

When compared to a typical Australian diet the cost of following a ketogenic diet (classical ketogenic diet, modified ketogenic diet, specialty modified ketogenic diet) for epilepsy was found to be more expensive for males. For females, the specialty modified ketogenic diet and the modified ketogenic diet were both more expensive than the typical Australian diet. Following the Australian dietary guidelines, modified or specialty modified ketogenic diets increased food costs by 0.63–4.18 percentage points for females and 1.44–6.56 percentage points for males, representing a 9%–31% and 26%–44% increase in spending compared to the typical Australian diet for females and males respectively. Adding to this, the modified versions of the ketogenic diet are the most prescribed version of the diet in adults 12 and that the majority of people with epilepsy earn below the median weekly income, 15 the increased costs of these diets may make them unattainable, particularly in the current cost of living crisis which has impacted households in Australia and worldwide. 16

While not within the scope of this study, the “hidden costs” of following a specialized diet also need to be considered including additional time to complete the shopping, plan and prepare meals, and track the diet. Additional out‐of‐pocket monitoring costs related to ketogenic diet therapy such as blood tests and ketone testing products also need to be considered, as well as the cost of vitamin and mineral supplementation and ketogenic nutritional supplements as required. The frequency of required supplementation and monitoring would be determined by the treating clinician or dietitian on an individual basis, and their costs have therefore not been formally incorporated into this study. However, these additional costs, on top of the higher food shopping costs associated with following a therapeutic ketogenic diet, could further exacerbate the financial burden. While the cost of ketogenic nutritional supplements are covered on the Pharmaceutical Benefits Scheme in Australia, using these products primarily as a strategy to offset the increased grocery costs associated with following a therapeutic ketogenic diet for epilepsy falls outside their intended therapeutic scope and could be considered a form of cost shifting (from the individual to the healthcare system) rather than cost savings and represent an inappropriate use of clinical resources.

This study demonstrates that following the Australian dietary guidelines was also more expensive than the typical Australian diet, for both males and females. People with epilepsy are at significantly higher risk of morbidity and mortality due to cardiovascular disease than those without epilepsy. 17 For people with epilepsy who have been recommended a ketogenic diet but are unable to commence or maintain a therapeutic ketogenic diet, following a healthy diet such as the Australian dietary guidelines would be recommended and may reduce the risk of cardiovascular disease in this population. 18 However, based on the cost of following the Australian dietary guidelines (Figure 1), even following this diet may be unachievable without additional financial support.

Dietitians are well placed to provide individualized advice to adapt dietary recommendations to each person's budget while maintaining dietary efficacy as well as direct them to services, which can improve food security. 19 In several countries, there are dietary subsidies available to ease the economic burden for patients who are required to follow a specialized diet as a treatment for their medical condition (e.g., gluten‐free diet for celiac disease or low‐protein diets for phenylketonuria). 20 In Australia, patients with inborn errors of metabolism, including phenylketonuria, can receive a monthly grant to help with increased food costs if they maintain compliance with their recommended therapeutic diet. 21 Currently, no such subsidy is available for people requiring a therapeutic ketogenic diet for epilepsy.

The Australian public healthcare system subsidizes the cost of some anti‐seizure medications, neurostimulation devices, implantation and ongoing management, and epilepsy surgery, minimizing out‐of‐pocket expenses for the patient. Conversely, the financial requirements of maintaining a therapeutic ketogenic diet fall to the individual. This may present a socioeconomic barrier to treatment initiation and adherence with a therapeutic ketogenic diet, which is not present in surgical or pharmacological alternatives. If a ketogenic diet subsidy improved uptake and adherence to the ketogenic diet, this could significantly reduce the overall economic burden of epilepsy. A study in 2020 demonstrated that even modest improvements in seizure freedom rates of 5%–10%, which is well within the range that has been demonstrated in clinical trials of the ketogenic diet, could reduce GDP lost as a result of drug‐resistant epilepsy by $3.9–7.8 billion AUD, respectively. 22 Our findings could help to inform healthcare policies and to promote investment in programs designed to increase uptake and adherence to ketogenic diet therapy.

LIMITATIONS

A limitation of this study was the use of reference meal plans, which only included foods prepared in the home. Meal plans only covered a 1‐week timeframe, and the study prioritized the lowest absolute price per quantity of item required over the lowest cost per‐gram. These factors could have artificially increased the weekly costs. The reference meal plans were Western‐centric, lacking representation of diverse cultural cuisines, and were limited to classical and modified ketogenic diets. Consequently, the cost implications of other therapeutic diets for epilepsy, including the modified Atkins diet, low glycemic index treatment, and the medium chain triglyceride diet, were not evaluated. Costs of grocery items may vary based on seasonal availability, inflation, supply chain expenses, and geographical location. Costs were based on the aggregated average of minimum prices across three supermarkets; this approach ensured a complete data set in instances where specific items were unavailable. However, it may have unintentionally reduced the cost advantage of shopping at a budget supermarket. Future studies could be strengthened by prospectively evaluating real‐world food diaries with food expenditure data specifically from people with epilepsy from a range of geographical locations and cultural backgrounds, before and after commencing dietary therapy to evaluate the true cost impact.

CONCLUSION

Our study demonstrates that following a therapeutic modified ketogenic diet for epilepsy is more expensive than the typical Australian diet, resulting in increased household food expenditure for all people with epilepsy. The increased cost of therapeutic ketogenic diets for epilepsy could be a prohibitive factor leading to reduced utilization of this effective treatment. Policymakers and insurers should consider the role of a financial subsidy, similar to medication access schemes, to reduce barriers to dietary therapy for epilepsy.
