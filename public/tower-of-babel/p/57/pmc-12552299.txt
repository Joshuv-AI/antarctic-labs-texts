Successful repeated endoscopic balloon dilatation for esophageal stricture as chronic graft‐versus‐host disease
Authors: Erika Sekiguchi, Masatoshi Sakurai, Takahiro Inoue, Kyoko Masuda, Yusuke Kubota, Takahide Shindo, Tomohiko Tanigawa, Akihiko Chida, Jun Kato, Hirofumi Kawakubo, Keisuke Kataoka
Published: 2025

Dear Editor:

Chronic graft-versus-host disease (GVHD) is a serious and common complication in allogeneic hematopoietic stem cell transplantation (HSCT) recipients [1, 2]. Although upper esophageal stricture has been described as one of the diagnostic features of chronic GVHD in the National Institutes of Health (NIH) Consensus paper [3], there are limited reported cases [4, 5], and no standard treatment strategies have yet been established. We herein report a patient with esophageal stricture as chronic GVHD who was successfully treated with repeated endoscopic balloon dilatation.

A 50-year-old man was diagnosed with myelodysplastic syndrome with increased blasts-2. He underwent allogeneic bone marrow transplantation from a human leukocyte antigen allele-mismatched unrelated donor under a myeloablative regimen. GVHD prophylaxis consisted of tacrolimus (FK506) and short-term methotrexate (MTX). He did not develop acute GVHD and achieved complete remission. On day 68, he was diagnosed with cryptogenic organizing pneumonia (COP), and received systemic prednisolone (PSL; 0.5 mg/kg) in addition to FK506, and the COP improved immediately. While tapering PSL, he was clinically diagnosed with COP exacerbation and chronic oral GVHD (NIH Score 1), and the PSL dose was increased to 1 mg/kg. The lesions then rapidly improved, PSL was tapered and FK506 was discontinued. Approximately six years after HSCT, the patient was diagnosed with chronic GVHD of the skin (Score 3) and fascia (Score 2), and we commenced MTX (10 mg/week) in addition to PSL (2.5 mg/day). At the same time, the patient also complained of dysphagia and could not consume enough food, resulting in weight loss of more than 10%. Endoscopy revealed a stricture in the upper esophagus, and he was diagnosed with GVHD of the gastrointestinal tract (Score 2). PSL was increased to 10 mg/day, the skin sclerosis, fasciitis and dysphagia improved slightly, and the patient recovered his food intake and body weight. However, six years and ten months after HSCT, the dysphagia worsened and he developed aspiration pneumonia four times in six months, resulting in weight loss again. Endoscopic findings revealed a severe stricture in the upper esophagus, which could not be passed by transnasal endoscopy (Fig. 1a). Therefore, we performed endoscopic balloon dilatation, which allowed passage of the endoscopic scope (Fig. 1b). Immediately after the initial dilatation, the patient’s dysphagia improved significantly, and his food intake and body weight increased. After five dilatations at 3–6-month intervals, he remained free of aspiration pneumonia for more than a year (Fig. 1c), with body weight recovering to within 5% below baseline and no procedure-related adverse events.

Endoscopic findings and clinical course of the patient. a) Endoscopic findings showed salivary accumulation in the parietal fossa due to severe stenosis of the upper esophagus. b) Pictures of the initial endoscopic balloon dilatation. Severe stenosis was observed in the upper esophagus (1, 2), which was dilated with a 12–15 mm balloon at 8 atmospheres for 2 min (3, 4). Dilatation was relatively easy and the endoscope could pass (5, 6). c) Clinical course of the patient before and after the onset of esophageal graft-versus-host disease (GVHD). Approximately six years after transplantation, he lost more than 10% of his body weight (BW) due to dysphagia and was diagnosed with esophageal GVHD by esophagogastroduodenoscopy. Once he improved with increasing doses of prednisolone (PSL) in addition to methotrexate (MTX), he had a brief episode of aspiration pneumonia and again lost more than 10% of his BW. Following endoscopic balloon dilatation, he gained weight rapidly, and has since undergone a total of five regular dilatations at intervals of three to six months, with no episodes of aspiration pneumonia for over a year

To our knowledge, this is the first case that achieved sustained improvement of esophageal GVHD by repeated endoscopic balloon dilatation. Systemic glucocorticoids and other immunosuppressive agents remain the standard treatment for moderate or severe chronic GVHD but are associated with adverse effects and increased risk of infection [6–8]. Therefore, localized treatment is important in the long-term control of chronic GVHD [9, 10]. Such localized treatment avoids increasing systemic immunosuppressive agents, reduces treatment-related adverse effects and potentially does not reduce the graft-versus-tumor effect. Additional evidence is needed to clarify the efficacy and safety of repeated endoscopic balloon dilatation in these circumstances.
