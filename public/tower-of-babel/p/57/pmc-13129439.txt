Validation of NT-proBNP cutoff points for heart failure diagnosis in adults with CHD in the outpatient clinic and emergency department
Authors: Ahmed E Ali, Zeyad Kholeif, Marwan Ahmed, Samuel Saad, Alexander C Egbe
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundEmpirical data support the use of N-terminal pro-B-type brain natriuretic peptide (NT-proBNP) for heart failure (HF) rule-in and rule-out in patients with acquired heart disease, but similar data are lacking in adults with congenital heart disease (CHD). The purpose of this study was to assess diagnostic performance of conventional NT-proBNP cutoff points for HF diagnosis (rule-in and rule-out) in adults with CHD.MethodRetrospective study of adults with CHD and NT-proBNP measurement in the outpatient clinic (outpatient cohort) or in the emergency department (ED) (2003-2023). HF diagnosis was based on clinical assessment at the time of NT-proBNP measurement. Logistic regression was used to assess the diagnostic performance of conventional NT-proBNP cutoff points (<125 pg/ml for HF rule-out and ≥250 pg/ml for HF rule-in in the outpatient setting; <300 pg/ml for HF rule-out and ≥450 pg/ml for HF rule-in in the ED setting).ResultsThe outpatient cohort comprised 3961 patients (age 45 ± 16 years), and NT-proBNP <125 pg/ml ruled-out HF with 86% sensitivity, while NT-proBNP level ≥250 pg/ml ruled-in with 68% specificity. The ED cohort comprised 687 patients (age 49 ± 16 years) and NT-proBNP level <300 pg/ml ruled-out HF with 82% sensitivity, and NT-proBNP level ≥450 pg/ml ruled-in HF with 75% specificity.ConclusionsThese data support the use of these NT-proBNP levels for HF screening in the CHD population, which in turn would enable early detection and treatment of HF.Keywords: Heart failure diagnosis, Congenital heart disease, Cardiovascular biomarkers

Introduction

Heart failure (HF) is the leading cause of mortality in adults with congenital heart disease (CHD) [1,2]. The etiology of HF in CHD is multifactorial, and include valve disease, arrhythmias and conduction system disease, and myocardial dysfunction [1,2]. The clinical presentations of HF in CHD range from stage B HF (no current or prior history of HF symptoms), stage C HF (current or prior history of HF symptoms) to stage D HF (advanced or end-stage HF) [[3], [4], [5], [6], [7], [8]]. The risk of mortality correlates with HF stage, and hence the need for early diagnosis and treatment of HF [[3], [4], [5], [6], [7], [8]].

Pro-B-type natriuretic peptide (proBNP) is secreted by cardiomyocytes in response to hemodynamic stress, and then cleaved into active BNP and inactive NT-ProBNP, and NT-proBNP levels correlate with HF severity and mortality [9,10]. The guidelines for management of HF recommend the use of NT-proBNP assay for diagnosis and monitoring of HF in patients with acquired heart disease, and these recommendations are based on robust evidence demonstrating the prognostic role of NT-proBNP data in this population [[9], [10], [11], [12]]. As a result, several NT-proBNP cutoff points have been proposed for HF diagnosis (rule-in and rule-out) in the outpatient clinic and the emergency department (ED), thereby facilitating early diagnoses and treatment of HF [[13], [14], [15]]. However, these NT-proBNP cutoff point are not widely used for HF diagnosis in adults with CHD, because they have not been systematically tested in this population [16,17]. The current study aims to address this knowledge gap, by assessing the diagnostic performance of conventional NT-proBNP cutoff point for HF diagnosis (rule-in and rule-out) in adults with CHD.

Methods

Study population

This is a retrospective study of adults (age ≥18 years) with CHD biventricular physiology who underwent NT-proBNP measurement between January 1, 2003 and December 31, 2023. We excluded patients with Fontan palliation, and patients with unrepaired/palliated cyanotic heart disease. The patients were divided into 2 cohorts depending on the clinical setting where NT-proBNP was measured. The first cohort comprised patients who underwent NT-proBNP measurement in the outpatient clinic (outpatient cohort), and the second cohort comprised patients who underwent NT-proBNP measurement in the ED (ED cohort). For the outpatient cohort, the first clinical encounter with NT-proBNP measurement was considered the baseline encounter, and HF diagnosis (presence versus absence of HF) was based on clinical assessment during this encounter. For the patients in the ED cohort, the first ED presentation because of cardiovascular symptoms, and during which NT-proBNP was measured was considered the baseline encounter, and HF diagnosis (presence versus absence of HF) was based on clinical assessment during this encounter.

The chart of all the patients with NT-proBNP measurements were reviewed by medical data abstractors for the pre-defined diagnostic criteria (with CHD biventricular physiology who underwent NT-proBNP measurement between January 1, 2003 and December 31, 2023). In cases were diagnoses were not clear, the senior authors adjudicated diagnoses. The abstractors and authors were blinded to the actual value of NT proBNP measurement at the time of determining/adjudicating HF diagnoses.

Study objectives

(i)Assess the diagnostic performance of NT-proBNP cutoff points to detect HF (rule-in and rule-out) in the outpatient clinic. (ii) Assess the diagnostic performance of NT-proBNP cutoff points to detect HF (rule-in and rule-out) in patients presenting cardiovascular symptoms in the ED.

Assess the diagnostic performance of NT-proBNP cutoff points to detect HF (rule-in and rule-out) in the outpatient clinic. (ii) Assess the diagnostic performance of NT-proBNP cutoff points to detect HF (rule-in and rule-out) in patients presenting cardiovascular symptoms in the ED.

Study variables

HF diagnosis

HF diagnosis was based on the presence of these two criteria. (i) Clinical features consistent with HF (symptoms [dyspnea, orthopnea, paroxysmal nocturnal dyspnea, fatigue, and pedal edema), signs [elevated jugular venous pressures, crackles, and pedal edema], and evidence of congestion on chest x-ray [12,18]. (ii) Initiation or uptitration (>20% increase in dose) of loop diuretic because of clinical features of HF. A patient who met both criteria was considered to have HF.

NT-proBNP measurement

Based on the consensus statement by the Heart Failure Association of the European Society of Cardiology, the NT-proBNP cutoff point for HF rule-out in the outpatient clinic was NT-proBNP <125 pg/ml, while the cutoff for HF rule-in was NT-proBNP ≥250 pg/ml [10,13]. Similarly, the NT-proBNP cutoff point for HF rule-out in the ED was NT-proBNP <300 pg/ml, while the cutoff for HF rule-in was NT proBNP ≥450 pg/ml [10,13].

Statistical analysis

Data were presented as mean ± standard deviation, median (Q1, Q3), and count (%). Between-group comparisons were based on Fisher's exact test, unpaired t-test, or Wilcoxon rank sum test, as appropriate. Logistic regression analysis was used to assess the diagnostic performance of the different NT-proBNP cutoff points (predictor variable) to detect HF (outcome variable) and expressed as odds ratio and area under the curve (AUC), as well as sensitivity and specificity. The diagnostic performance of NT-proBNP cutoff points was assessed in the overall cohort, as well as subgroup analyses based on age (<50 years versus ≥50 years), body mass index (BMI) (<30 kg/m2 versus ≥30 kg/m2), and systemic ventricular ejection fraction (SVEF) (<50% versus ≥50%). All statistical analyses were performed with BlueSky Statistics software (version. 7.10; BlueSky Statistics LLC, Chicago, IL, USA), and JMP statistical software (version 17.1.0, JMP Statistical Discovery LLC, NC). P value < 0.05 was considered to be statistically significant for all analyses.

NT-proBNP levels

The median NT-proBNP levels for outpatient cohort was 187 (75, 552) pg/ml. NT-proBNP level was higher in patients ≥50 years of age compared to those <50 years of age (320 [115, 851] versus 136 [62, 397] pg/ml, p < 0.001), and in patients with SVEF <50% compared to those with SVEF ≥50% (397 [130, 1038] versus 161 [67, 466] pg/ml, p < 0.001), (Fig. 1). NT-proBNP level was not significantly different between patients with BMI ≥30 kg/m2 versus <30 kg/m2 (178 [72, 537] versus 210 [83, 609] pg/ml, p = 0.33), (Fig. 1).

NT-proBNP levels and HF diagnosis

Of 3961 patients, 306 (8%) had HF based on evaluation in the outpatient clinic. NT-proBNP level <125 pg/ml ruled-out HF with 86% sensitivity, 59% specificity (AUC 0.667), 87% positive predictive value, and 98% negative predictive value. NT-proBNP level ≥250 pg/ml ruled-in HF with 74% sensitivity, 68% specificity (AUC 0.699), 76% positive predictive value, and 71% negative predictive value. Of the 3961 patients, 287 (7%) had systemic right ventricle. Among patients with systemic right ventricle, NT-proBNP level <125 pg/ml ruled-out HF with 88% sensitivity and 51% specificity (AUC 0.667), while NT-proBNP level ≥250 pg/ml ruled-in HF with 72% sensitivity, and 63% specificity (AUC 0.638).The diagnostic performance of NT-proBNP cutoff points for HF rule-out and rule-in in the overall cohort was comparable to that observed on subgroup analysis based on age, BMI, and SV EF (Table 3).

Of 3961 patients in the Outpatient Cohort, 1283 (32%) had stage III-V chronic kidney disease. Among patients with stage III-V chronic kidney disease, NT-proBNP level <125 pg/ml ruled-out HF with 82% sensitivity and 56% specificity (AUC 0.651), in patients with stage III-V chronic kidney disease, and 88% sensitivity and 61% specificity (AUC 0.694), in patients without stage III-V chronic kidney disease.

Patients with atrial fibrillation

Of the 3961, patients 810 (21%) had current or prior history of atrial fibrillation. Among these 810, NT-proBNP level <125 pg/ml ruled-out HF with 92% sensitivity and 46% specificity (AUC 0.622), while NT-proBNP level ≥250 pg/ml ruled-in HF with 78% sensitivity, and 59% specificity (AUC 0.620).

NT-proBNP level ≥450 pg/ml ruled-in HF with 58% sensitivity, 75% specificity (AUC 0.657), 88% positive predictive value, and 36% negative predictive value. Of the 687 patients 158 (23%) had current or prior history of atrial fibrillation. Among these 158, NT-proBNP level <300 pg/ml ruled-out HF with 86% sensitivity and 49% specificity (AUC 0.631), while NT-proBNP level ≥450 pg/ml ruled-in HF with 53% sensitivity, and 79% specificity (AUC 0.667).

Discussion

In this study, we assessed the diagnostic performance of NT-proBNP cutoff points for HF diagnosis in a large cohort of adults with CHD and biventricular physiology. We observed that NT-proBNP <125 pg/ml ruled-out HF with 86% sensitivity, and NT-proBNP level ≥250 pg/ml ruled-in HF with 68% specificity in the outpatient setting. Similarly, NT-proBNP level <300 pg/ml ruled-out HF with 82% sensitivity, and NT-proBNP level ≥450 pg/ml ruled-in HF with 75% specificity in the ED cohort.

HF is a leading cause of cardiovascular mortality in adults with CHD, and the risk of mortality is highest in patients with advanced or stage D HF [[3], [4], [5], [6], [7], [8]]. Early detection of HF and timely initiation of GMDT halts or slows HF progression and cardiovascular mortality [[3], [4], [5], [6], [7], [8]]. HF is a clinical diagnosis that is based on symptoms, signs, and objective evidence of congestion [12,18]. Making this diagnosis is challenging and can sometimes lead to missed or delayed diagnosis with negative prognostic implications [12,18].

NT-proBNP is a cardiovascular biomarker that can be used for screening of HF [12,19]. The practice guidelines and consensus statements recommend use of NT-proBNP for HF diagnosis and risk stratification, and proposed NT-proBNP cutoff point for rule-in and rule-out of HF in the acquired heart disease population [10,12,13,18]. The use of NT-proBNP for HF diagnosis in adults with CHD is limited by the lack of empirical data about the diagnostic performance of these NT-proBNP cutoff points in the CHD population [16,17]. The current study addressed this knowledge gap by demonstrating that the NT-proBNP cutoff points derived from the acquired heart disease population, had robust diagnostic performance for HF diagnosis in the CHD population [10,12,13,18]. Furthermore, the diagnostic performance of these NT-proBNP cutoff points were consistent on subgroup analysis based on age, BMI, and SVEF.

NT-proBNP level is typically lower in patients with obesity, and higher in older patients and those with reduced SVEF, hence the proposal for different NT-proBNP cutoff points for different subgroups [9,10,13]. While we observed the expected higher NT-proBNP level in older patients, and those with reduced SVEF, there was no significant difference in NT proBNP levels based on BMI. We speculate that this may be related to the fact that only a small proportion of the cohort had class ≥ II obesity (BMI ≥35 kg/m2). We also observed that the diagnostic performance of the NT-proBNP cutoff points was consistent across subgroups, supporting the use of same cutoff point across subgroups.

Clinical implications and future directions

The current study provides empirical data to support the use of conventional NT-proBNP cutoff point for HF diagnosis in adults with CHD. The NT-proBNP cutoff points for rule-out HF had good sensitivity both in the outpatient and ED setting, suggesting that it can be used for rapid HF screening in these settings. Of note, the diagnostic performance of NT-proBNP was comparable in patients with versus without history of atrial fibrillation, as well as patients with versus without chronic kidney disease. These suggest that NT-proBNP can be used for HF evaluation across these subgroups. For patients with NT-proBNP levels that fall below these thresholds, clinical evaluation should be directed towards identifying non-cardiac etiology for the symptoms. Similarly, the NT proBNP cutoff points for rule-in HF had good specificity (albeit low sensitivity) both in the outpatient and ED setting. Of note, the specificity of NT-proBNP for rule-in HF was higher in the ED setting compared to the outpatient setting, likely due to higher pretest probability for HF in the ED setting. Patients with NT-proBNP levels above this threshold should undergo further cardiovascular evaluation to identify cardiac causes for the clinical presentation. Perhaps, the application of these NT-proBNP cutoff points for HF screening in adults with CHD would improve early detection of HF and timely initiation of GDMT to improve clinical outcomes as has been observed in the acquired heart disease population [10,12,13,18].

Limitations

This is a retrospective single-center study conducted in a tertiary CHD center, and it is therefore prone to selection and ascertainment bias. HF diagnosis was based on the assessment of the attending physician in the ED or the outpatient clinic, and these physicians were not blinded to the result of NTproBNP assay. There is also potential for misdiagnosis of HF in these settings. Although we presented diagnostic performance of NT-proBNP across the entire spectrum of CHD, it is important to emphasize that there is significant anatomic and physiologic heterogeneity in the CHD population, and this may not be adequately accounted for using a single diagnostic cutoff point. Furthermore, we excluded patients with Fontan palliation, and patients with unrepaired/palliated cyanotic heart disease and hence the results of this study cannot be extrapolated to this population. While the data abstractors and authors were not aware of the NT-proBNP-value when a diagnosis of heart failure was made, the physician caring for the patient in the ED or the outpatient clinic, was fully aware of the NT-proBNP-value measured at that point in time.

Conclusions

The conventional NT-proBNP level cutoff-point for HF diagnosis can be used for HF screening in adults with CHD in the outpatient setting, as well as ED setting. The diagnostic performance of the NT-proBNP levels was comparable to the results from the acquired heart disease population and was consistent across subgroups. These data support the use of these NT-proBNP levels for HF screening in the CHD population.
