Gastric biopsies with prominent eosinophils: Clinicopathologic features and response to therapy
Authors: Nigar Anjuman Khurram, Sanjay Kakar, Dana Balitzer
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractAimsThe use of the term ‘histologic eosinophilic gastritis’ (EoG) has been proposed for cases with ≥30 eosinophils/high power field (HPF) in five separate HPF and with no known associated causes of eosinophilia. This study evaluates the clinical presentation, pathologic characteristics of ‘histologic eosinophilic gastritis’.Methods and ResultsGastric biopsies with prominent mucosal eosinophils over 23 years were retrieved yielding 87 qualifying cases. Clinical information and follow‐up data were retrieved from medical records. Of the 72 cases with no discernible aetiology, 56 satisfied the criteria for ‘histologic eosinophilic gastritis’ (Group 1), while 16 had prominent eosinophils without satisfying these criteria (Group 2). There were no differences in clinical presentation, endoscopic findings, morphologic features or treatment response between these groups. The cases with a secondary aetiology (Group 3), included Crohn disease (n = 5), concurrent or prior history of Helicobacter pylori gastritis (n = 5), strongyloidiasis (n = 1), collagenous gastritis (n = 1), hyper‐eosinophilic syndrome (n = 1), autoimmune atrophic gastritis (n = 1) and systemic mastocytosis (n = 1). Of the 10 patients with histologic eosinophilic gastritis who were treated with steroids (with or without a combination of other therapies), seven (70%) had documented response to steroid‐based therapy, with three (30%) patients who were symptomatically steroid non‐responsive. No histologic differences were identified in patients with steroid‐responsive disease. Two patients developed relapsing disease characterized by severe abdominal pain dependent on steroid therapy.ConclusionsThe criteria for ‘histologic eosinophilic gastritis’ can miss cases with clinically significant eosinophilia and our study results argue against using this term for pathology diagnosis. The majority of patients with increased gastric mucosal eosinophils present with non‐specific GI symptoms and endoscopic findings. While a significant proportion of patients improve symptomatically without treatment or with anti‐acid therapy, a small proportion of patients develop steroid‐dependent, relapsing idiopathic disease that warrants additional clinical follow‐up.Keywords: histologic eosinophilic gastritis, mucosal eosinophilia, peripheral eosinophilia

Introduction

Eosinophilic gastritis (EoG) is part of a group of eosinophilic gastrointestinal disease (EGID) of unknown aetiology characterized by elevated gastrointestinal eosinophils and variable gastrointestinal symptoms. 1 There is a frequent association with autoimmune and allergic conditions such as food allergies, asthma, allergic rhinitis and atopic dermatitis. 2 , 3 , 4 Peripheral eosinophilia is observed in 23%–86% of patients, and many individuals may present with elevated serum IgE levels. 2 , 5 , 6 , 7 , 8 , 9 Rare cases have been reported in conjunction with familial Mediterranean fever. 10 , 11 , 12

Non‐EoE Eosinophilic Gastrointestinal Diseases (EGID) currently lack established diagnostic guidelines and patients often face long delays before diagnosis. 13 In 2011, Lewin et al. proposed the threshold of 38 eosinophils/mm2 and the term ‘histological eosinophilic gastritis’ for gastric biopsies with ≥30 eosinophils per high power field (HPF), in at least five HPFs in the absence of known associated causes of eosinophilia. The Food and Drug Administration (FDA) has accepted this threshold for the diagnosis of EoG 14 ; however, the diagnosis of eosinophilic gastritis (EoG) is complicated by a lack of standardized range for normal eosinophil counts in the gastric mucosa, with studies reporting averages between 4 and 11 eosinophils per high‐power field (HPF). 15 , 16 , 17 , 18 Moreover, the optimal number of biopsies and the eosinophil cut‐off required for diagnosis are unclear. 19 , 20

The diagnosis of EoG is also complicated by the heterogeneity of clinical presentation and response to a variety of therapeutic modalities. Disease symptoms and presentation are heterogeneous, and clinical presentation may vary with the depth of involvement. 5 , 21 While some patients experience spontaneous remission, others exhibit a relapsing course that necessitates long‐term maintenance therapy. 13 , 15 Given the putative role of T helper (Th)2 immunity, atopy and food allergy, dietary‐based therapy and immunomodulation with corticosteroids are common treatment options. 4 , 22 Other therapeutic options include other anti‐allergy drugs such as mast cell stabilizers or leukotriene receptor antagonists. 23 Clinical trials are currently investigating the role of monoclonal antibodies for the treatment of eosinophilic gastrointestinal disorders including EoG. 22 , 24 In some publications, steroid therapy appears to be the most successful treatment modality with symptomatic improvement in 55%–90% of patients. 5 , 25 While EoG has been previously reported to be highly responsive to dietary restriction in paediatric populations, other studies have reported that avoidance of specific food allergens did not resolve symptoms in EGID. 9 , 26 , 27

We examined the clinical presentation, pathologic characteristics and natural history of patients that satisfied the proposed definition of ‘histologic eosinophilic gastritis’ 28 as well as cases that showed ‘prominent eosinophils’ without qualifying for ‘histologic eosinophilic gastritis’.

Materials and Methods

Patient Selection

A retrospective review of cases spanning 23 years (2000–2022) was performed at the University of California San Francisco (UCSF, San Francisco, CA) via natural language search for gastric biopsies with ‘prominent eosinophils’ or ‘eosinophilic gastritis’ in the diagnosis or comment section of the report/electronic medical record. This study received approval from the Committee on Human Research, Department of Pathology, University of California, San Francisco (San Francisco, CA). Ninety‐four cases (n = 94) were identified; four cases (n = 4) were excluded due to unavailability of slides, one case was excluded due to sampling of the gastroesophageal junction (n = 1), one case was excluded based on an endoscopic finding of a polypoid lesion (n = 1), and one case was excluded due to a lack of clinical information (n = 1). The final study group comprised 87 cases (n = 87).

Clinical Evaluation

Demographic information, relevant medical history (including inflammatory bowel disease, celiac disease and atopic conditions such as asthma, eczema or allergic rhinitis), presenting symptoms, endoscopic findings at presentation and post‐endoscopy medications were extracted for all cases from the electronic medical record. Laboratory data, including peripheral eosinophilia, stool microbiology and food allergy testing, were also recorded when available. Therapies were categorized as gastroesophageal reflux disease (GERD)‐based (proton pump inhibitors and histamine‐2 receptor antagonists), steroid‐based (including topical and systemic therapies as well as patients on multimodal therapies) or other (such as dietary elimination, lifestyle modification, immunomodulatory drugs for inflammatory bowel disease or pathogen specific treatments). Therapeutic response was evaluated based on documented symptomatic improvement or resolution as noted in clinical follow‐up records.

Pathologic Evaluation

The peak eosinophil count (PEC) per high‐power field (HPF) was recorded in at least five separate HPFs. Peak eosinophil counts were scored initially by one pathologist, with re‐review by a second pathologist for concordance. Additional histologic features including eosinophil cryptitis (eosinophils involving epithelium of crypts), eosinophilic pit abscess (eosinophils extending into lumen of gastric crypts) and eosinophil sheets in the lamina propria (defined as aggregates of eosinophils with obliteration of gland architecture) were recorded. The background stomach was also evaluated for chronic gastritis, active gastritis and reactive gastropathy. The depth of involvement (mucosa or extension into muscularis mucosae) was documented.

Of the 87 cases, no secondary cause was discernible in 72 cases (82.8%) by clinical or pathologic evaluation. Fifty‐six of these 72 cases (77.8%) showed >30 eosinophils/HPF in at least five HPF (Group 1), which correspond to ‘histologic eosinophilic gastritis’ based on previously published criteria. 25 Sixteen of 72 cases (22.2%) without any discernible secondary aetiology did not meet this threshold of eosinophilia (Group 2). Most cases in Group 2 (15 of 16, 93.8%) had a peak eosinophil count of at least 30 eosinophils in a HPF but did not involve five separate HPFs, with an average peak eosinophil count of 41 eosinophils/HPF (range 19–67) and a mean eosinophil count of 22 eosinophils/HPF averaged over 5 high‐power fields. In the remaining 15 cases, a secondary aetiology for gastric eosinophilia was either already established (n = 10), identified concurrently (n = 4) or after initial work‐up (n = 1) (Group 3). The aetiologies included Crohn disease (n = 5), concurrent or prior history of H. pylori (n = 5), strongyloidiasis (n = 1), collagenous gastritis (n = 1), hyper‐eosinophilic syndrome (n = 1), autoimmune atrophic gastritis (n = 1), systemic mastocytosis (n = 1).

Statistical Analysis

A Shapiro–Wilk test was performed to determine the distribution of age and peak eosinophilic count (PEC), and both were below the 0.05 threshold (age W = 0.935, P < 0.001; PEC W = 0.489, P < 0.001), and therefore, the non‐parametric Kruskal–Wallis test with the corresponding effect size eta squared (η 2) reported was used to compare the Group with patient age and PEC. The magnitude of the effect size of η 2 was small between 0.01 and 0.06, medium between 0.06 and 0.14, and large beyond.

Pearson Chi‐squared test of independence (χ 2) with corresponding effect size (Cramer's V) and adjusted standardized residual was used to determine if there was a relationship between the proportion of pathologic and clinical features for each of the three groups (Groups 1, 2 and 3). Statistical significance was assumed at P < 0.05. Magnitude of effect size of Cramer's V was considered negligible between 0 and 0.05, weak association between 0.05 and 0.1, moderate association between 0.1 and 0.15, strong association between 0.15 and 0.25, and very strong association greater than 0.25. Adjusted standardized residuals above 2.0 or below −2.0 are the general cut‐off used to show greater discrepancy for an alpha of 0.05. Analyses were performed using IBM SPSS Statistics 30.0.0.0.

Of the patients with data available, blood eosinophil counts were elevated (>0.5 × 10E9/L) in 22.7% (10 of 44) patients in Group 1, 33.3% (1 of 3) of Group 2 and 16.7% (2 of 12) of Group 3, with no statistically significant difference between the groups (χ 2 (2) = 0.437, P = 0.804). Eosinophilic cryptitis was noted significantly more often in Group 3 (66.7%) compared to 6.3% of patients in Group 2 and 39.3% of patients in Group 1 (χ 2 (2) = 12.126, P = 0.002), with a very strong association (Cramer's V = 0.373). There was no significant difference in the presence of eosinophil sheets (χ 2 (2) = 5.206, P = 0.074) (Figure 2B), eosinophilic pit abscess (χ 2 (2) = 3.722, P = 0.156), reactive gastropathy (χ 2 (2) = 2.724, P = 0.256) or active inflammation between the groups (χ 2 (2) = 4.440, P = 0.109).

There was a significant difference in peak eosinophilic count (PEC) between the groups (H(2) = 7.944, P = 0.019, with a medium effect size η 2 = 0.09). The mean rank was highest at 53.9 in Group 3 (Mdn = 66.0, M = 68.8, SD = 24.8) followed by 45.6 for Group 1 (Mdn = 52.5, M = 85.5, SD = 109.4) and 29.3 for Group 2 (Mdn = 35.0, M = 43.1, SD = 29.0). The highest average peak eosinophil counts were observed in Group 3 cases (Figure 3A–D) including 160 eosinophils/HPF in Crohn's disease, 87.6 eosinophils/HPF in infectious aetiologies, and 169 eosinophils/HPF in cases with hematologic disorders.

An average of 3.4 tissue fragments (range 1–8 fragments) were sampled in Group 1, 2.7 tissue fragments (range 1–6 fragments) were sampled in Group 2, and 3.9 tissue fragments (range 1–10 fragments) were sampled in Group 3, respectively. There was no statistical difference in the number of tissue fragments sampled in Groups 1 and 2 (P = 0.11). Only a minority of cases (5.3% of cases in Group 1, 0% of cases in Group 2 and 6.7% of cases in Group 3) sampled at least eight tissue fragments.

A total of 9 patients had documented clinical symptomatic improvement to topical or systemic steroid therapy (with or without a combination of other therapies), and 12 patients responded to treatment with anti‐acid therapies.

Of the patients with topical or systemic steroid therapy (with or without a combination of other therapies), a favourable response was observed in 7/10 (70.0%) Group 1 cases and 2/3 (66.7%) of Group 2 cases (χ 2 (2) = 0.012, P = 0.913). Ten patients in Group 1 received glucocorticoid therapy (n = 5 with oral budenoside, n = 4 with oral budenoside in combination with prednisone and n = 1 with prednisone) for a presumptive diagnosis of eosinophilic gastritis (i.e., primary EGID). Two additional patients in Group 1 were prescribed Budenoside or Fluticasone in combination with anti‐acid therapy but did not take the glucocorticoid‐based treatment. Therapy for two patients in Group 2 was treated with glucocorticoids (n = 1 with oral budenoside in combination with prednisone and n = 1 with inhaled corticosteroids) for concomitant diagnosis of eosinophilic esophagitis.

Two patients in Group 1 developed multiple relapsing, steroid‐responsive disease characterized by recurrent flares of abdominal pain. Both patients had peripheral eosinophilia; one patient had elevated serum IgE and one patient tested positive for food allergens. Of the steroid non‐responsive patients (n = 3 in Group 1 and n = 1 in Group 2), two patients had persistent abdominal pain and one patient had persistent dysphagia attributed to concurrent eosinophilic esophagitis. One of the patients with steroid‐responsive disease in Group 2 also tested positive for food allergens with elevated serum IgE, while this was not tested in the second patient. The steroid non‐responsive Group 2 patient had persistent symptomatic dysphagia attributed to concurrent eosinophilic esophagitis.

Four patients treated with steroid therapy underwent follow‐up endoscopy with gastric biopsies. Two patients with relapsing steroid‐dependent disease underwent follow‐up biopsies demonstrating a persistent increase in gastric mucosal eosinophils (n = 1 with a decrease in PEC from 201 eosinophils/HPF to 61 eosinophils/HPF and n = 1 with similar PEC of 53 eosinophils/HPF and 54 eosinophils/HPF, respectively). The other two patients showed chronic gastritis (n = 2) without an increase in mucosal eosinophils.

Response to anti‐acid therapies was observed in 10/14 (71.4%) patients in Group 1 and 2/2 patients (100.0%) in Group 2 (χ 2 (2) = 0.762, P = 0.383). Other non‐steroid‐based treatments were pursued in 5 Group 1 patients (2 with dietary elimination, 2 with lifestyle changes, 1 with a motility agent) and 2 Group 2 patients (gluten elimination diet and sulfasalazine for inflammatory bowel disease). No treatment was given to 10 Groups 1 and 2 Group 2 patients.

Follow‐up endoscopy with gastric biopsy was performed in 11 patients (from Groups 1 and Group 2) treated with anti‐acid therapies. One patient (n = 1) showed a persistent increase in mucosal eosinophils (with similar peak eosinophil count of 62 eosinophils/HPF compared to 60 eosinophils/HPF on the prior biopsy) despite symptomatic improvement with PPI documented clinically. The remainder of the patients had follow‐up with normal biopsies (n = 3), chronic or chronic active gastritis (n = 5) or reactive gastropathy (n = 2).

Follow‐up data to assess treatment response were available for 12 of the 15 patients (80.0%) in Group 3. Of the five patients with Crohn disease, 4 were treated with Vedolizumab (80.0%). Of the 5 patients with concurrent or prior history of H. pylori infection, follow‐up data were available for 3 patients. One patient with active disease received triple therapy for H. pylori, one patient had remote infection and a gastric ulcer was responsive to anti‐acid therapy, and one patient received no treatment. One patient with strongyloidosis (diagnosed via serum antigen testing) was treated with Ivermectin. The remaining cases were 1 patient each with collagenous gastritis and possible celiac disease put on gluten‐free diet, hyper‐eosinophilic syndrome treated with mepolizumab and systemic mastocytosis treated with montelukast.

There was a statistically significant association between steroid or anti‐acid response and patient sex among Group 1 patients as well as all patients, with 85.7% of female patients in the steroid responsive group compared to 20% in the anti‐acid responsive group in Group 1 (Table 6A), χ 2 (1) = 7.137, P = 0.008, with a very strong association (Cramer's V = 0.648). A history of atopic disease was more common with steroid‐responsive group (77.8%) compared to anti‐acid responsive group (25.0%) (χ 2 (1) = 5.743, P = 0.017) with a very strong association (Cramer's V = 0.523). Discussion

The term ‘histologic eosinophilic gastritis’ has been proposed for cases in which gastric biopsy shows more than 30 eosinophils per high power field in five different HPFs without a discernible aetiology. In this study, there were no significant differences in clinical features, histologic features or endoscopic appearance between cases that satisfied the proposed criteria of ‘histologic eosinophilic gastritis’ (Group 1) and cases that showed ‘prominent eosinophils’ in gastric biopsies (Group 2) which either showed 30 eosinophils in at least one HPF, but not 5 HPF, or up to a mean eosinophil count of 22 per HPF. Using the proposed cut‐off for the definition of ‘histologic eosinophilic gastritis’ may potentially miss a significant subset of cases with clinically significant gastric eosinophilia.

The proposed definition of ‘histologic eosinophilic gastritis’ also requires that there is no discernible aetiology; however, the clinical work‐up for associated aetiologies often occurs after the gastric biopsy diagnosis, and the existence of discernible aetiology is often not known at the time of biopsy. The use of ‘histologic eosinophilic gastritis’ can be potentially misleading and we do not recommend this term for pathologic diagnosis. Rather, we consider it appropriate to refer to these cases as ‘gastric mucosa with prominent eosinophils’ and providing a mean count over 5 HPFs. This recommendation is in accordance with the approach followed for eosinophilic esophagitis, where a diagnosis of ‘histologic eosinophilic esophagitis’ is not made on biopsy but rather the increase in intraepithelial eosinophils is noted in the diagnosis and the number based on HPF is provided. The report should include a comment about secondary causes of mucosal eosinophilia such as H. pylori infection, Crohn's disease, parasitic infections, drugs and haematological or lymphoid disorders. Testing for food or other allergy and infectious workup should be recommended depending on the clinical setting.

It was recently noted that gastrointestinal eosinophilia is patchy and evaluation of multiple non‐overlapping eight gastric biopsies is necessary to identify biopsies meeting criteria for histologic eosinophilic gastritis. 29 None of the Group 2 cases in this series met the minimum sampling requirement. Therefore, endoscopists should also be aware of the necessity for adequate gastric sampling in cases with a clinical suspicion for EGID.

The highest eosinophil counts in our series were noted in cases with a secondary aetiology of gastric eosinophilia (Group 3 in our study). These cases were also significantly more likely to show infiltration of the muscularis mucosa. Of the five patients with known (n = 4) or suspected (n = 1) history of Crohn disease, all gastric biopsies showed more than 30 eosinophils in at least five HPF, and three cases were signed out at our institution with a top line diagnosis of ‘histologic eosinophilic gastritis.’ Crohn's disease had a peak count of 160 in an HPF in our series; high number of gastric eosinophils in Crohn disease have been previously reported. 28 Prominent eosinophils (defined as at least 30 eosinophils/high power field) have also been reported in 52% of biopsies with collagenous gastritis and 46.1% of cases of autoimmune gastritis. 30 , 31 Therefore, marked gastric mucosal infiltration by eosinophils does not indicate primary EGID and can be observed in secondary causes of eosinophilia.

Two patients in this study developed relapsing, steroid responsive disease characterized by recurrent flares of abdominal pain. Both patients had documented peripheral eosinophilia; one patient had elevated serum IgE and one patient had positive food allergens. These findings support prior reports that a small proportion of patients develop steroid dependent, relapsing idiopathic disease that warrants additional clinical follow‐up. 8 , 15 While no histologic features (including PEC) were predictive of steroid responsive disease in our study, a history of atopic disease or peripheral eosinophilia was more common in the steroid responsive group compared to anti‐acid responsive group. Therapeutic strategies for non‐eosinophilic esophagitis (EoE) EGIDs, including eosinophilic gastritis, are not well established, and no randomized controlled trials have been published to date. Two recent studies demonstrated histologic improvement in gastric eosinophils after budesonide 32 or swallowed fluticasone therapy in patients with concomitant eosinophilic esophagitis, 33 and in a retrospective analysis of 12 EGID patients with extra‐oesophageal disease, 50% of patients reported symptomatic improvement and 58% achieved remission following budesonide treatment. 34

There are several limitations of this study, which include its retrospective nature and limited follow‐up data. Clinical information such as food allergy data were available only in a subset of cases as the clinical evaluation had not been uniformly performed. Lastly, despite an archival search spanning 23 years at our institution, the sample size in Groups 1 and 2 is limited. The case search relied on the mention of increased eosinophils in the pathology reports, which may have missed some cases. Nonetheless, given that the finding of prominent eosinophils has been previously documented in the literature, additional controls (i.e., Group 3 cases) are unlikely to change our conclusions.

In conclusion, our study highlights the pitfalls with the use of the term ‘histologic eosinophilic gastritis’ and shows that patients with prominent gastric eosinophils below the proposed threshold have similar clinical and morphologic characteristics and can benefit from therapy. Currently, there are no clinical guidelines for routine allergy, infectious or laboratory testing in these patients. Prospective studies of gastric eosinophilia cases with uniform work‐up for allergic disease, infectious possibilities and secondary aetiologies are necessary to formulate guidelines for diagnostic criteria and treatment of these cases.
