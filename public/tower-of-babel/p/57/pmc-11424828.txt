Removal of a broken Kuntscher intramedullary nail for a femoral diaphyseal nonunion 17 years after injury
Authors: Akihiro Yamaji, Yohei Yanagisawa, Hajime Mishima, Masashi Yamazaki
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
AbstractIntroductionIn treating nonunion, the removal of a broken nail is often difficult. We report a technique for removing a broken Kuntscher intramedullary nail. In this technique, a chisel is inserted into the lumen of the intramedullary nail, and the nail is rotated.Case presentationA 57-year-old man presented to our hospital with femoral diaphyseal nonunion lasting 17 years after injury. The patient suffered a right femoral diaphyseal fracture at 40 years old and was treated with a Kuntscher intramedullary nail. The patient underwent two additional surgeries at another hospital for the nonunion; however, the nonunion did not heal, and the intramedullary nail broke. The patient was diagnosed with noninfectious hypertrophic nonunion and underwent surgery. We resected part of the lateral cortex of the femoral diaphyseal nonunion and reached the proximal end of the broken Kuntscher intramedullary nail. We inserted a chisel into the lumen of the intramedullary nail, held the chisel using pliers, and rotated the nail to separate it from the surrounding tissue. The proximal side of the broken intramedullary nail was easily removable. One year after surgery, the right femoral pseudarthrosis was healed with a bony callus.DiscussionThis procedure was possible because of the structural characteristics of the intramedullary nail, which is straight without curvature but has grooves.ConclusionWe removed a broken Kuntscher intramedullary nail without causing an iatrogenic fracture by fenestration of the cortex and rotation of the nail.Keywords: Kuntscher intramedullary nail, Broken nail

Introduction

Intramedullary nails are the established treatment for limb fractures [1]. However, nail breakage can occur due to trauma to the ipsilateral limb or in cases of delayed union or nonunion. Several methods have been reported for removing broken intramedullary nails; however, in many cases, removal is difficult [2].

In this case report, we describe the removal method of a Kuntscher intramedullary nail in a patient with femoral diaphyseal nonunion 17 years after the first surgery. This study was conducted according to the Surgical CAse Report (SCARE) Guidelines [3].

Case presentation

A 57-year-old man presented to our hospital with femoral diaphyseal nonunion lasting 17 years after injury. At the age of 40 years, the patient suffered a right diaphyseal femoral fracture and a right supracondylar fracture in a motorcycle accident and was treated with a retrograde intramedullary nail (Ace φ9 mm × length 320 mm, Stryker, USA, Portage Wisconsin). After surgery, the patient was referred to another hospital for treatment. The right femoral supracondylar fracture healed; however, nonunion of the right femoral diaphyseal fracture occurred. At the age of 45 years (5 years after injury), the patient underwent surgical removal of the distal cross-screw of the Kuntscher intramedullary nail for the nonunion. However, the nonunion did not heal. At the age of 54 years (14 years after injury), he was attempted as a second operation for the nonunion. The surgeon attempted to retract the top of the greater trochanter and drive the intramedullary nail anteriorly. However, removal of the Kuntscher intramedullary nail was challenging. In this surgery, an iatrogenic fracture occurred at the right basi-cervical femoral neck due to the force of striking the intramedullary nail. During surgery, the basi-cervical fracture was fixed using two screws. The Kuntscher intramedullary nail was placed, and the proximal cross-screw of the nail was removed. An autologous iliac bone graft was placed in the nonunion site.

However, the nonunion persisted after the second surgery. At the age of 57 years (17 years after the injury), the patient experienced increasing pain in his right thigh without any trauma. The right femoral diaphyseal fracture remained not united, and on X-ray, the Kuntscher intramedullary nail appeared to be broken. The patient was referred to our hospital for nonunion treatment.

At admission to our hospital, the patient used crutches and was partially weight-bearing on his right lower limb. The patient was diagnosed with hypertrophic nonunion due to instability (Fig. 1). We decided to replace the nailing on the right diaphyseal femoral nonunion. Preoperative imaging revealed that the diameter of the Kuntscher intramedullary nail was 9 mm, and the diameter of the medullary canal was 11 mm, indicating the presence of a space around the nail (Fig. 2).

Intraoperative photographs. a: Fenestration of the lateral cortex of the femoral nonunion. b: Insertion of a chisel into the groove of the Kuntscher intramedullary nail. c: Rotation of the Kuntscher intramedullary nail by holding the chisel using pliers and rotating it.

During surgery, the patient was placed in the left lateral decubitus position to remove the distal intramedullary nail. We developed a lateral parapatellar approach, removed the end cap, connected the Kuntscher intramedullary nail using an extractor, and removed the distal nail from the knee joint. The proximal Kuntscher intramedullary nail was approached from the lateral side of the nonunion. The nonunion area was filled with fibrous tissue and was unstable. The lateral side of the nonunion was fenestrated in the range of 1/2 of the femoral diameter.

We attempted to remove the proximal part of the broken Kuntscher intramedullary nail from the lateral side of the nonunion. We hammered chisels around the intramedullary nail to create space, inserted a chisel into the lumen of the intramedullary nail (the groove of the Kuntscher intramedullary nail), held the chisel using pliers, and rotated the Kuntscher intramedullary nail to separate it from the surrounding tissue. The proximal side of the broken intramedullary nail was removed from the fenestration (Fig. 3).

Schema made by inserting a chisel into the groove and rotating it.

After removing the intramedullary nail, the wound was closed, and the patient was moved to a traction table. After removing the two cannulated cancellous screws inserted in the basi-cervical femoral neck, an intramedullary nail (T2 φ 12 mm × length 340 mm, Stryker, USA, Portage Wisconsin) was inserted anteriorly. The nail was fixed by inserting three proximal and three distal cross-locking screws. Before inserting the intramedullary nail, cancellous bone was collected using a Reamer Irrigator Aspirator (reamer head φ 13 mm, DePuy Synthes, USA, Raynham Massachusetts), and the removed cancellous bone was transplanted to the nonunion (Fig. 4).

Removed implant.

After surgery, the patient was kept on bed rest for 1 week, after which weight bearing was initiated. Bone and soft tissue specimens obtained intraoperatively from the nonunion were negative for bacteria. A final diagnosis of noninfectious hypertrophic nonunion was made. One year after surgery, the right femoral pseudarthrosis was healed with a bony callus (Fig. 5).

Discussion

Intramedullary nails are often removed during surgery for nonunion; however, broken nails can be challenging to remove. When the intramedullary nail cannot be removed, nonunion treatment is extremely difficult.

Various extraction methods have been reported for removing broken intramedullary nails left in the medullary canal. Extraction techniques reported include using custom-made hooks [4,5], multiple guidewires [6,7], intramedullary nail push–pull using a window of bone and various instruments and proximal stacked wires [2,[8], [9], [10]], a custom-made extractor, a carbide drill [11], or a T-reamer [12].

However, the removal of a broken nail has various limitations. The use of a technique using multiple guidewires may be difficult because of bony ingrowth or a very tight isthmus–nail interface [7]. A technique using a T-reamer can be used only for undeforming hollow broken nails; however, this approach may increase the risk of distal migration of the fragment [12]. A technique using wires as hooks has been described as technically challenging because the wire can bend and even break within the nail [13].

In cases of broken Kuntscher intramedullary nails, removal of these nails may be challenging due to callus formation in the groove of the cross-screw and callus formation from the broken part of the intramedullary nail to the intramedullary nail cavity [14]. In this case, removal of the nail from the fenestration was difficult because of its strong continuity with the bone. Bone formation was observed in the groove of the Kuntscher intramedullary nail and in the hole of the cross-screw; therefore, we decided that cutting the continuity between bone formation and the Kuntscher intramedullary nail was necessary. Kuntscher nails are straight with no curvature. Therefore, we determined that the continuity between the new growth bone and the Kuntscher intramedullary nail could be cut by inserting a K-wire or chisel alongside the nail. We then drove a 5-mm-wide chisel around the intramedullary nail and inserted it into the groove of the nail. We rotated the chisel along with the intramedullary nail in the medullary canal. The nail was then removed manually from the fenestration in the nonunion. The diameter of the broken Kuntscher intramedullary nail was 9 mm; however, the diameter of the preoperative femoral medullary canal was 11 mm; therefore, there was a margin around the Kuntscher intramedullary nail. Hence, even if the Kuntscher intramedullary nail is rotated in the medullary canal, it can be removed relatively easily without causing a fracture. However, the absence of a space between the wall of the medullary canal and the nail may cause an iatrogenic fracture; therefore, gentle manipulation is required. In this case, because the femoral diaphyseal fracture did not achieve union for a long time, the intramedullary nail carried most of the load, and the diaphyseal femur was not highly loaded. Therefore, the medullary canal diameter may have increased over time due to the thinning of the bone cortex.

The intramedullary nail was straight; therefore, it could rotate within the medullary canal. Current femoral intramedullary nails often have lateral and lordotic curvatures. Rotation of such a curved intramedullary nail using this removal technique may result in an iatrogenic fracture.

This removal technique has the disadvantage of requiring soft tissue/bone invasion because the technique requires fenestration of the nonunion. One report suggested creating a bone window less than one-third of the circumference of the bone so that its replacement would cause no issues with stress fractures [7]. In this case, the nonunion was fenestrated to approximately one-half of the diameter of the femur; however, fixation was required because the nonunion treatment was performed after the removal of the intramedullary nail. If fenestration can be fixed with an implant, this technique is useful for removing a broken intramedullary nail.

Surgeries for nonunion with a broken intramedullary nail >17 years after the initial surgery have not been reported. This technique is useful when removing broken intramedullary nails.

Conclusion

In this study, we successfully removed a broken Kuntscher intramedullary nail by rotating it in the medullary canal in a patient diagnosed with diaphyseal femoral nonunion.
