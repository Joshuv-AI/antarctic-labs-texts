ROS-responsive nanoplatform-mediated targeted intranasal delivery of Piezo2 siRNA for the treatment of trigeminal neuralgia
Authors: Yangbin Wang, Li Zhao, Jiaming Chen, Peiqi Cao, Yuanyuan Li, Qingling Wang, Jingxia Xiang, Shaomei Chen, Shiling Liu, Haoxiong Zhang, Daoshu Luo, Mei Lin, Feng Wang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractTrigeminal neuralgia (TN) is one of the most severe neuropathic pain conditions, yet current pharmacological treatments are hindered by low bioavailability, systemic toxicity, and drug resistance. The mechanosensitive ion channel Piezo2 has been identified as a key mediator of orofacial mechanical allodynia in TN, making it a highly attractive but as yet clinically untargeted therapeutic target. To address this critical gap, we developed RLPSe nanoparticles, a microenvironment-adaptive nanoplatform composed of a polyvinylamine (PVAm)-L44 copolymer crosslinked via diselenide bonds and conjugated with rabies virus glycoprotein 29 (RVG29). The diselenide bond confers oxidative stress responsiveness, while RVG29 enables specific neuronal targeting. In vitro studies demonstrated that RLPSe nanoparticles exhibited good biocompatibility, oxidative stress responsiveness, and neuronal targeting efficiency; they effectively scavenged intracellular reactive oxygen species and delivered siRNA to knock down Piezo2 expression in neurons. Following intranasal administration in vivo, RLPSe nanoparticles were successfully internalized by trigeminal ganglion cells. Notably, this was associated with reduced neuronal activation in central pain-related regions, including the spinal trigeminal nucleus caudalis and primary somatosensory cortex. Collectively, this study presents a non-invasive, microenvironment-adaptive gene silencing strategy that combines intranasal delivery, oxidative stress responsiveness, and Piezo2 knockdown, representing a promising approach for further investigation in the context of trigeminal neuralgia.Keywords: Trigeminal neuralgia, Piezo2, Reactive oxygen species, Nanoparticles, Intranasal delivery

Introduction

Primary trigeminal neuralgia (TN) is among the most severe and debilitating neuropathic pain disorders. It is characterized by paroxysmal, electric shock-like pain in the orofacial region, which can be triggered by innocuous stimuli such as light touch, speaking, or chewing. These sensations often originate from specific trigger zones located on the upper lip, nostrils, or corners of the mouth [[1], [2], [3], [4]]. This condition frequently leads to a profoundly diminished quality of life, which is commonly accompanied by anxiety and depression. Under normal physiological conditions, orofacial nociceptive signals are transmitted via trigeminal afferent neurons through the trigeminal ganglion (TG) to the spinal trigeminal nucleus, with subsequent projections to cortical regions for central processing [5,6]. The pathogenesis of classic TN typically begins with neurovascular compression, which induces focal demyelination and nerve injury. This damage results in nerve hyperexcitability and the generation of ectopic action potentials. Furthermore, persistent injury triggers maladaptive plasticity in both the peripheral and central nervous systems, enabling nonnoxious stimuli to evoke painful responses, a phenomenon central to the disease's debilitating nature [[7], [8], [9]]. Although microvascular decompression remains the only potentially curative intervention, long-term follow-up studies have revealed significant limitations, including pain recurrence and incomplete relief [10,11]. Therefore, developing effective, sustained, and well-tolerated pharmacological strategies represents a major unmet need in TN management.

The mechanosensitive ion channel Piezo2 has recently emerged as a key player in sensory transduction, particularly in chronic mechanical pain. Structurally, Piezo2 is a large protein composed of more than 2000 amino acids and approximately 40 transmembrane domains, forming a nonselective cation channel. Upon activation, it preferentially permits Ca2+ influx (Ca2+ > K+ > Na+ > Mg2+) and engages calcium-sensitive signalling pathways to orchestrate downstream cellular responses [12,13]. Predominantly expressed in sensory neurons, Piezo2 is essential for light touch perception and noxious mechanical sensation [14]. In humans, it localizes to trigeminal and dorsal root ganglia, as well as to specialized mechanoreceptors such as Merkel cells, Meissner's corpuscles, and Pacinian corpuscles. It mediates a wide spectrum of processes ranging from tactile discrimination and proprioception to visceral mechanotransduction [15]. Ikeda's team demonstrated that capsaicin-induced facial tactile allodynia in rats was significantly attenuated by shRNA-mediated Piezo2 knockdown in whisker follicles, underscoring Piezo2's functional importance in orofacial pain pathways [16]. Our group has further validated and extended this finding. Direct injection of Piezo2 shRNA lentivirus into the TG significantly knocked down Piezo2 protein and mRNA expression and notably alleviated orofacial mechanical allodynia in a TN model rat, whereas scramble shRNA had no effect [17]. This result provides the direct evidence that Piezo2 knockdown in the TG is sufficient to attenuate mechanical hypersensitivity induced by trigeminal nerve compression injury. Collectively, these findings establish Piezo2 as a core therapeutic target for TN.

Advances in nanomedicine have enabled the design of endogenous stimulus-responsive nanoparticles. These so-called “smart” carriers react to pathological cues in the disease microenvironment, allowing targeted and on-demand drug release. This offers a minimally invasive paradigm for treating complex neurological conditions. The pathological niche of injured trigeminal nerves is defined by elevated reactive oxygen species (ROS), proinflammatory cytokines, and altered inhibitory tone, providing a unique set of triggers for such intelligent nanotherapeutics [[18], [19], [20], [21], [22]]. The integration of microenvironmental awareness with spatiotemporal control over drug release makes adaptive nanoplatforms promising strategies for achieving effective TN treatment.

Guided by this rationale, we developed a microenvironment-adaptive nanoparticle named RLPSe. As described in Scheme 1A, these nanoparticles were engineered using the neural-targeting peptide rabies virus glycoprotein (RVG29) and redox-sensitive diselenide bonds. We demonstrated that following intranasal delivery, RLPSe efficiently transfected trigeminal ganglion neurons, knocked down Piezo2 expression, and attenuated orofacial mechanical hypersensitivity (Scheme 1B), thereby offering a novel and targeted therapeutic strategy for trigeminal neuralgia.

Materials and methods

Animals and cells

Adult male Sprague–Dawley rats (160 ± 20 g) were obtained from the Experimental Animal Center of Fujian Medical University. The animals were housed on a 12:12-h light–dark cycle at constant room temperature (22 ± 2 °C) with food and water provided ad libitum. All experimental procedures were approved by the Animal Care and Use Committee of Fujian Medical University (Approval No. IACUC FJMU 2023-Y-1116) and followed the guidelines of the International Association for the Study of Pain (IASP). Only males were used to avoid estrous cycle-related variability. SD rats were randomly assigned to either the TN or Sham group using a random number table. Under anaesthesia with avertin (300 mg/kg, i.p.), an arc-shaped incision was made near the right orbit of rats. The infraorbital nerve was exposed via blunt dissection. In the TN group, a custom hollow steel catheter (OD: 0.4 mm) with a nylon filament (0.2 mm diameter) was inserted along the infraorbital nerve groove towards the trigeminal root entry zone (TREZ). The filament was then extruded to compress the nerve, after which the catheter was withdrawn. In the Sham group, the infraorbital nerve was only exposed without compression. Finally, the incision was sutured and topically treated with erythromycin ointment. After rats were anaesthetized with avertin, the nanoparticle preparation was instilled into the nasal cavity, which was inhaled into the nasal cavity along with the respiratory movement of the rats. The concentration of the nanoparticle preparation was 1.2 μg/ml, and the concentration of the siRNA was 1 μg/ml. Fifty microlitres/100 g of rat nanocomplex formulation was added at a rate of 10 μl per drop within 45 min. After each drop was applied, the rats were paused for 3 to 4 min to ensure that they inhaled the drop and maintained stable breathing. The nostrils of the rats were carefully observed for any signs of blockage or irritation.

HT22 cells were purchased from iCell Bioscience Inc. (Shanghai, China), while RSC96, B16F10 and BV2 cells were obtained from the Cell Bank/Stem Cell Bank of the Chinese Academy of Sciences (Shanghai, China). All the cell lines were cultured in DMEM supplemented with 10% FBS and 1% penicillin–streptomycin under normoxic conditions (5% CO2, temperature: 37 °C).

Preparation of the nanoparticles

Synthesis of L44-PVAm: The polyvinylamine (PVAm) used in this study was synthesized in-house according to the procedure reported by our group, with an average molecular weight (Mw‾) of 9.61×103 and a distribution (Mw‾/Mn‾) of 1.29 [23]. Vacuum-dried polyether L44 (Macklin, Shanghai, China) was reacted with benzenesulfonyl chloride in anhydrous pyridine under N2 at room temperature for 3 h. The crude product was sequentially washed with 3 M HCl, saturated NaHCO3, and saturated NaCl solutions and then purified by recrystallization (THF/diethyl ether) and vacuum-dried to obtain the activated L44 benzenesulfonate ester. L44-OBs and PVAm were added to the flask at a molar ratio of 1:1.5 and then incubated at 60 °C for 24 h. After the reaction was completed, dialysis was carried out for 3 days with a 2000 Da dialysis bag, during which time the deionized water was changed 9 times. After dialysis was completed, the product L44-PVAm was obtained through freeze-drying.

Synthesis of LPSe: After a specific amount of selenocystine was added to a beaker, a proportionate amount of 1-ethyl-3-(3-dimethylaminopropyl) carbodiimide (EDC) (Adamas, Shanghai, China) and N-hydroxysuccinimide (NHS) (Adamas, Shanghai, China) was added for activation for 12 h. L44-PVAm was mixed with activated selenocystine (Yuanye Bio-Technology, Shanghai, China) at a 10:1 M ratio and reacted for 24 h. The product was subsequently filtered through a 0.22 μm membrane and dialyzed for 3 days using a 2000 Da molecular weight cut-off membrane. During this process, the water was alternately changed 9 times with 0.1 M carbonate buffer solution and deionized water to remove the unreacted reactants. After dialysis is completed, the product LPSe is obtained through freeze-drying.

Synthesis of RLPSe: A specific amount of LPSe and activated RVG29 (GL Biochem, Shanghai, China) at a 10:1 M ratio was added to the flask and allowed to react for 24 h. The peptide sequence of RVG29-Cys was YTIWMPENPRPGTPCDIFTNSRGKRASNGC. Afterwards, the mixture was filtered through a 0.22 μm filter membrane and dialyzed for 3 days in a 2000 Da dialysis bag. During this process, the water was alternately changed 9 times with 0.1 M carbonate buffer solution and deionized water to remove the unreacted reactants. After dialysis was completed, the product RLPSe was obtained through freeze-drying.

Characterization of copolymers

For proton nuclear magnetic resonance (1H NMR) analysis, RLPSe, LPSe, L44-PVAm and PVAm powders were dissolved in D2O and gauged on a Bruker 250 MHz Ascend spectrometer (Bruker Biospin, Ettlingen, Germany). Dry RLPSe, LPSe, L44-PVAm and PVAm powders were mixed with potassium bromide for FT-IR spectrometry (PerkinElmer, Waltham, MA, USA) detection with scanning range between 4000 and 400 cm−1. When the concentration of the nanoparticle solution was approximately 10 μL, it was dropped onto the front side of the copper mesh. After 15 min, filter paper was used to absorb the remaining solution from the side of the copper mesh. The remaining surface liquid was left to dry at room temperature using a FEI TECNAI G2 transmission electron microscope (Thermo Fisher Scientific, Hillsboro, OR, USA) at 120 kV. The observation grid was pressed down, and the appearance and shape of the sample were recorded. To explore the particle size and potential value of the copolymer/RNA complex, the polymer and the RNA were dissolved in distilled water in specific amounts. The polymer and RNA dissolved in distilled water were mixed at different mass ratios to form nanocomplexes, which were incubated at room temperature for 30 min. The particle size and potential value were detected by a laser particle size analyser/potential metre (Litesizer 500, Anton Paar, Graz, Austria).

Agarose gel electrophoresis experiment

The binding ability of RLPSe to RNA was evaluated through agarose gel electrophoresis experiment. The RNA concentration in each well was kept constant. The polymers dissolved in distilled water were mixed with RNA at different polymer/nucleic acid mass ratios to form nanocomplexes. They were allowed to stand for 30 min. Complexes (5:1) and 5×loading buffers of RNA-bound loading buffer were added to the gel sample loading wells, and RNA was used as the negative control. The electrophoresis conditions included 50 ml of agarose gel (containing 0.5 μg/L HeaRed™ dye) for electrophoresis, a voltage of 100 V, and an electrophoresis time of 30 min. Imaging and photography were performed using a gel imager (Tanon 4600SF, Shanghai, China). The RNA was quantified according to the fluorescence intensity through the analysis from the software Image J.

To assess the RNA release profile of the RLPSe/RNA complexes under oxidative conditions, complexes at mass ratios ranging from 0 to 1.2 were exposed to H2O2 at final concentrations of 0, 1, and 2 mM and incubated for 8 h. Following incubation, loading buffer was added, and the samples were immediately loaded onto an agarose gel.

To determine the stability of RLPSe/RNA nanoparticles against ribonuclease A, the complexes (at a mass ratio of 1.2:1) were incubated with 5 ng/ml RNase A (Sigma, St Louis, MO, USA) at room temperature for varying periods (0, 6, 12, 18, 24 and 30 min). After incubation, the solution was mixed with 1.0 wt% of SDS and incubated at 4 °C for 3 min to terminate the reaction. Loading buffer was then added, and the sample were immediately analysed by agarose gel electrophoresis.

Experiment on hydroxyl radical scavenging ability

A 1 ml aliquot of RLPSe solution was placed in a 10 ml centrifuge tube. Afterwards, 1 ml of FeSO4 (6 mmol/L) and 1 ml of H2O2 (6 mmol/L) were added sequentially. The mixture was vortexed thoroughly and allowed to react in the dark for 10 min. Subsequently, 1 ml of salicylic acid-ethanol solution (6 mmol/L) was introduced. After mixing, the reaction was protected from light and maintained at 37 °C for 30 min. The absorbance of each sample was measured at 510 nm. A blank measurement was obtained by replacing the H2O2 solution with an equivalent volume of pure water in the above procedure.

Assessment of orofacial pain behaviour

Mechanical allodynia and spontaneous scratching behaviour in the orofacial region were evaluated in rats. For mechanical threshold testing, the animals were acclimated to the testing environment for 30 min prior to each session. The orofacial area was shaved one day before assessment. Using von Frey filaments, the mechanical withdrawal threshold was determined by applying filaments in ascending order of force; a positive response was defined as three or more aversive reactions out of five applications per filament, with 30-s intervals between applications. Spontaneous scratching behaviour was recorded by placing the rats individually in transparent acrylic chambers with minimal bedding after a 30-min acclimation period. One scratching bout was defined as three or more consecutive facial-scratching movements occurring within a 15-min observation period without human interference. Baseline measurements were taken before surgery and again on days 7, 14, 21 and 28 after the operation. Additional assessments were conducted before intranasal administration and at 4, 24, 48, 72, and 96 h after administration.

All behavioral tests were performed under blinded conditions. von Frey mechanical pain threshold tests and counting of scratching actions were conducted by an experimenter blinded to the group assignments. The group codes were disclosed after testing by another investigator who was not involved in the behavioral evaluation.

Tissue section preparation and histochemical staining

On postoperative day 28, the rats from each group were anaesthetized with avertin (300 mg/kg, i.p.) and transcardially perfused with precooled saline followed by 4% paraformaldehyde (PFA). The right TG and brain tissues were harvested, post fixed in 4% PFA at 4 °C for 12 h, and cryoprotected in 30% sucrose at 4 °C for 48 h. Serial sections were cut on a cryostat at 20 μm (TG) or 35 μm (brain), mounted on adhesive slides, and stored at −20 °C. Histochemical staining, including hematoxylin and eosin (HE) and luxol fast blue (LFB) staining, was performed using commercial kits (Solarbio, Beijing, China) strictly following the manufacturer's instructions.

For immunofluorescence, frozen sections were thawed, rinsed in 0.01 M PBS, and blocked with QuickBlock™ blocking buffer (Beyotime, Shanghai, China) at room temperature for 1 h. The sections were incubated overnight at 4 °C with the following primary antibodies: rabbit anti-Piezo2 (1:400; Novus, Cat# 78624, Centennial, CO, USA), chicken anti-NEFH (1:500; Abcam, Cat# ab82259, Cambridge, UK), mouse anti-NeuN (1:500; Abmart, Cat# M050661F, Shanghai, China), mouse anti-SOD2 (1:500; Proteintech, Cat# 83519-4, Wuhan, China), mouse anti-NSE (1:500; Proteintech, Cat# 66150-1, Wuhan, China), goat anti-CGRP (1:500; Abcam, Cat# ab36001, Cambridge, UK), and rabbit anti–c-Fos (1:1000; CST, Cat# 2250S, Danvers, MA, USA). After being washed with PBS, the sections were incubated for 1 h at room temperature with the following corresponding secondary antibodies: goat anti-rabbit Alexa Fluor Plus 555 (1:1000; Invitrogen, Cat# A32732, Waltham, MA, USA), donkey anti-rabbit Alexa Fluor Plus 488 (1:1000; Invitrogen, Cat# A21206, Waltham, MA, USA), goat anti-rabbit Alexa Fluor Plus 647 (1:1000; Invitrogen, Cat# A32733, Waltham, MA, USA), goat anti-mouse Alexa Fluor Plus 555 (1:1000; Invitrogen, Cat# A32727, Waltham, MA, USA), goat anti-mouse Alexa Fluor Plus 488 (1:1000; Invitrogen, Cat# A11001, Waltham, MA, USA), donkey anti-goat Alexa Fluor Plus 594 (1:1000; Invitrogen, Cat# A32758, Waltham, MA, USA) and goat anti-chicken Alexa Fluor 647 (1:1000; Invitrogen, Cat# A21449, Waltham, MA, USA). The nuclei were counterstained with DAPI (1:1000; Beyotime, Cat# C1002, Shanghai, China), and the slides were mounted with antifade medium. Images were acquired using a confocal microscope (Leica TCS SP5, Heidelberger, Germany).

For ultrastructural analysis, animals were anaesthetized by intraperitoneal administration of avertin and subjected to transcardial perfusion with ice-cold phosphate-buffered saline (PBS; 0.1 M, pH 7.2), followed by a fixative consisting of 3% glutaraldehyde and 1.5% paraformaldehyde in the same PBS buffer. Tissue segments containing the trigeminal nerve root region were dissected and immersed in fixative at 4 °C for 72 h. After being rinsed with PBS, the specimens were postfixed in 1% osmium tetroxide containing 1.5% potassium ferrocyanide at 4 °C for 2 h. After the PBS washed, en bloc staining was performed with uranyl acetate. Dehydration was conducted using a graded ethanol series, and the samples were embedded in epoxy resin. Ultrathin sections were prepared and examined using a transmission electron microscope (FEI Tecnai G2, Hillsboro, OR, USA).

All image analyses were performed under blinded conditions. For immunofluorescence staining and tissue fluorescence intensity, anonymized image files (file names containing only random numbers without grouping information) were quantified independently by two researchers. The final results were averaged for statistical comparison.

Primary culture of TG neurons

Adult SD rats (300 ± 20 g) were used for TG neuron culture. First, the rats were anaesthetized with avertin (300 mg/kg, i.p.), and then decapitated and sacrificed. After the brain tissue was separated, the TG tissue was removed with a microshear and temporarily placed in D-Hank's solution (BC BioTechnology, Guangzhou, China). Under a stereomicroscope, the vascular membrane and outer nerve membrane on the tissue sample were removed using cell tip forceps. After these tissues were removed, the high-glucose medium was aspirated, add 1 ml of 0.25% pancreatic enzyme-EDTA (Invitrogen, Waltham, MA, USA) and 1 ml of 1 mg/ml type I collagenase (Invitrogen, Waltham, MA, USA) were added, and the samples were digested in a 37 °C water bath for 20 min. The solution was removed every 5 min, inverted up and down to mix well, and then mixed with a 1000 μl pipette at the end of the last digestion. The tissue was made flocculent, and then add 2 ml of complete DMEM/F12 (Invitrogen, Waltham, MA, USA) supplemented with serum was added to terminate the digestion. The cell suspension was filtered through a 40 μm filter screen into a 50 ml centrifuge tube and centrifuged at 1000 rpm for 10 min. The supernatant was carefully discarded, an appropriate amount of complete DMEM/F12 medium was added to resuspend the cells, the cells were counted with a cell counter, the resuspended cell solution was diluted to an appropriate concentration as needed for the experiment, and the cells were seeded in a culture dish precoated with D-polylysine (Solarbio, Beijing, China). The cells were incubated at 37 °C in a 5% CO2 incubator. After 4 to 6 h, the medium was changed to Neurobasal Plus/B27 Plus neuronal conditioned medium (Invitrogen, Waltham, MA, USA) for continued culture. Afterwards, medium of the neurons was changed every 72 h.

In vitro delivery of RLPSe/siPiezo2

Primary neurons were inoculated onto 29 mm glass-bottomed culture dishes at a density of 15,000 cells and cultured for 3 days. RLPSe and siPiezo2 (Sense GCUCAGAAAUGGUGUGCUATT; antisense UAGCACACCAUUUCUGAGCTT) (Sangon Biotech, Shanghai, China) were mixed at a mass ratio of 1.2:1, after which a pipette was used to aspirate and mix them well to form a nanocomplex. The sample was allowed to stand at room temperature for 20 min. The old cell culture medium from the well plate was aspirated, the RLPSe/siPiezo2 nanocomplex (50 pmol/ml) and the neurobasal complete neuronal maintenance medium were mixed, and the cells were then added to the culture dish. Cellular uptake was allowed to proceed for 48h at 37 °C under 5% CO2.

Verification of mRNA transcription levels by RNA extraction and RT-qPCR

After the TG neurons were completely aspirated from the culture medium, TRIzol reagent (Beyotime, Shanghai, China) was added to lyse them on ice for 10 min. The culture dish was gently tapped to assist in cell lysis. The lysates from each group were drawn and transferred to sterile and enzyme-free centrifuge tubes. Chloroform was added and the mixture was shaken vigorously for 10 s and then allowed to stand at room temperature for 10 min to extract RNA. After centrifugation, the clear liquid on the upper layer was transferred it to a new sterile and enzyme-free centrifuge tube. After an equal volume of isopropyl alcohol was added and mixed well, the mixture was allowed to stand at room temperature for 10 min. The mixture was centrifuged again to obtain the RNA precipitate. The RNA precipitate was rinsed twice with 75% ethanol. After the ethanol completely evaporated, 10 μL of sterile and enzyme-free water was added to dissolve the RNA precipitate, and its concentration and mass were measured. HiScriptⅡQ RT SuperMix was subsequently used for reverse transcription into cDNA, after which the relative mRNA expression level of Piezo2 was measured by PCR. PCR amplification was performed at 95 °C for 30 s, followed by 40 cycles of thermal cycling at 95 °C for 10 s, 50 °C for 30 s, and 95 °C for 15 s. The relative expression level of mRNA was calculated by the relative quantification method (2-△△Ct method). The sequences of primers used for qPCR amplification were as follows: Piezo2(Forward primer 5′- ACACCATGCTGGTGCTCATC−3’; Reverse primer 5′- AGATGTCCAATCGGGTGGGA -3′) and β-actin (Forward primer 5′- GGCTGTATTCCCCTCCATCG -3’; Reverse primer 5′- CCAGTTGGTAACAATGCCATGT -3′) (Sangon Biotech, Shanghai, China).

Quantification of protein expression by western blot analysis

Protein samples were extracted from rat TG tissues. After two washes with cell culture-grade PBS, the cells were lysed on ice for 20 min using RIPA lysis buffer supplemented with a phosphatase inhibitor. For tissue samples, rats were anaesthetized with avertin and perfused with precooled saline. The harvested tissues were placed in prechilled homogenization tubes, to which 200 μL of RIPA buffer was added per 20 mg of tissue weight. The tissues were homogenized on ice using an automated homogenizer with precooled steel beads. All the lysates were then centrifuged at 12,000 rpm for 20 min at 4 °C. The resulting supernatants were collected, and the protein concentration of each sample was determined using a BCA assay kit (Beyotime, Shanghai, China). Subsequently, 5× loading buffer was added to the protein supernatants to achieve a final concentration of 1×, followed by denaturation at 100 °C for 5 min in a metal bath.

Protein samples (10 μg) were separated on 10% SDS–PAGE gels and transferred to polyvinylidene difluoride (PVDF) membranes (Merck Millipore, Burlington, MA, USA). The PVDF membranes were blocked with 5% (w/v) nonfat milk for 1 h at room temperature (24 ± 2 °C) followed by incubation overnight at 4 °C with the following primary antibodies: rabbit anti-Piezo2 (1:1000; Novus, Cat# 78624, Centennial, CO, USA), mouse anti-SOD2 (1:1000; Proteintech, Cat# 83519-4, Wuhan, China) and mouse anti-beta-Actin (1:1000; CST, Cat# 3700, Danvers, MA, USA). Next, the PVDF membranes were incubated with a horseradish peroxidase conjugated goat anti-rabbit IgG (H + L) (1:10000; Bioworld, BS13278, Nanjing, China) or goat anti-mouse IgG (H + L) (1:10000; Bioworld, Cat# BS12478, Nanjing, China) secondary antibody at room temperature for 2 h. Protein bands were visualized with Immobilon Western Chemiluminescent Reagent (Merck Millipore, Burlington, MA, USA) and a chemiluminescence image analysis system (Tanon, 4600SF, Shanghai, China). ImageJ software was used to analyze and normalize the band intensities.

Cytotoxic CCK8 detection

Before CCK8 detection, cells were seeded in 96-well plates at a density of 1 × 104 cells per well in 100 μL of DMEM and incubated at 37 °C in a 5% CO2 incubator. After 24 h, fresh DMEM containing different concentrations of polymer (0, 1, 5, 10, 50, 100, 500, or 1000 μg/ml) was added to each well. After 24 h of culture, the DMEM was replaced with normal DMEM, after which 10 μL of CCK8 solution (Servicebio, Wuhan, China) was added. After staining for 1 h, the absorbance at 450 nm was measured using an ELISA plate reader (Agilent, Hangzhou, China).

Hemolysis test

To prepare 2% erythrocytes, fresh rat blood was stored in anticoagulant vessels, the red blood cells ware washed with PBS, the samples were centrifuged at 2500 rpm for 5 min at room temperature, and the samples were washed three times. The supernatant did not appear red in colour. Afterwards, 200 μL of erythrocytes was taken and diluted to 10 ml. Moreover, the concentration of the RLPSe polymer was adjusted to 1, 5, 10, 50, 100, 500 and 1000 μg/ml with a PBS solution. Next, 0.5 ml of the polymer solution was mixed 0.5 ml of the 2% erythrocyte solution and allowed to stand at room temperature for 3 h. Afterwards, the samples were centrifuged at 12000 rpm for 3 min. One hundred microlitres of the supernatant was added to a 96-well plate, and the absorbance was measured at a wavelength of 570 nm using an ELISA plate reader (Agilent, Hangzhou, China). Distilled water and PBS were used as the positive control and negative control respectively.

Determination of the malondialdehyde (MDA) concentration in trigeminal nerve roots

Each group of SD rats was anaesthetized with avertin, after which the necks were removed and the rats were sacrificed. The heads were quickly decapitated and placed on ice. The brain tissues were removed on ice to obtain fresh TG tissues which were subsequently placed in centrifuge tubes. The MDA levels were measured using an MDA assay kit (Boxbio, Beijing, China), and all the experimental procedures were performed in strict accordance with the manufacturer's instructions.

Assessment of reactive oxygen species (ROS) production

The production of ROS in cells was evaluated after the respective treatments. The H2DCFH-DA (UElandy, Suzhou, China) was used for the detection of ROS in cells under the following conditions: blank, treatment with 50 μM H2O2 for 1 h, and treatment with 50 μM H2O2 for 1 h followed by RLPSe treatment. The cells were subsequently observed and analysed using a Nikon Eclipse Ti2 inverted fluorescence microscope (Nikon, Tokyo, Japan).

Flow cytometry analysis

Following 10 h of coincubation with FITC-labelled RLPSe and LPSe or RLPSe/5′-Texas Red-labelled siPiezo2, the HT22, RSC96, and BV2 cell lines were digested into single-cell suspensions using 0.25% trypsin without EDTA. The collected cells were subsequently washed three times. Finally, the cell suspension was evaluated using NovoCyte Opteon flow cytometry (Agilent, Hangzhou, China).

Quantification of nanoparticle uptake

Following the respective treatments, the HT22, RSC96, and BV2 cell lines were washed three times with phosphate-buffered saline (PBS) and subsequently detached using 0.25% trypsin without EDTA to form a single-cell suspension. The cell concentration was determined using an automated cell counter. Thereafter, the cells were pelleted and lysed with 100 μL of RIPA buffer. The concentration of RLPSe-FITC in the collected lysate was measured using an Evident FV4000 confocal microscope (Evident, Tokyo, Japan) equipped with a fluorescence correlation spectroscopy (FCS) module. Finally, the amount of RLPSe-FITC internalized per cell was calculated on the basis of the measured concentration and the predetermined cell count.

Analysis of single-cell RNA sequencing data

Our analysis incorporated the publicly available scRNA-seq dataset from Yang et al. which profiles the cellular heterogeneity of human and mouse trigeminal ganglia [24]. Processed data were retrieved from NCBI (accession: GSE197289) and were integrated into our analysis to determine the distribution of the Piezo2 protein in human and mouse TG cells. Standard preprocessing and cell type identification were performed as described in the original study.

RNA-seq data analysis

The dataset of differentially expressed genes (DEGs) was derived from our previous RNA-seq study on the trigeminal root entry zone of rats following compression injury, which measures transcript abundance [25]. Functional enrichment analysis, including Gene Ontology (GO) analysis (biological Process, cellular component, and molecular function) and Kyoto Encyclopedia of Genes and Genomes (KEGG) pathway enrichment analysis, was performed on this gene set using the online platform WeiShengXin (bioinformatics.com.cn) [26].

Statistical analyses

GraphPad Prism 9 (GraphPad Software, Inc.) was used for performing analyses and generating graphs. The Shapiro–Wilk test was used to check the normality of the data. A nonparametric Mann–Whitney U test or Wilcoxon matched-paired signed rank test was performed if the data were not normally distributed. We performed paired or unpaired two-tailed Student's t-tests to compare data between two groups. For experimental groups with multiple comparisons, we utilized two-way ANOVA followed by post hoc analyses. Significance levels are indicated as follows: ∗P < 0.05,∗∗P < 0.01,and ∗∗∗P < 0.001. P values less than 0.0001 are not provided as exact values. All the results are presented as the mean ± SD. All the statistical tests, significance analyses, and other relevant information for data comparison are specified in Supplementary Data 2.

Results

Transcriptomic profiling of trigeminal ganglia reveals Piezo2 upregulation and oxidative stress-related pathways activations in compressed nerves

We leveraged a public snRNA-seq dataset (GSE197289 from NCBI) comprising trigeminal ganglia from healthy C57BL/6 mice (11 males, 3 females) and human donors who died of nonneurological causes (1 male, 2 females). Our analysis of ∼100,000 nuclei revealed the cellular heterogeneity and Piezo2 transcript expression landscape across both species. Transcriptomic analysis revealed ten cell types in the human trigeminal ganglion, with robust mRNA expression of Piezo2 in neuronal, glial, and immune cell subsets (Fig. 1a). In mice, the corresponding clustering revealed 14 distinct types, with Piezo2 transcript expression mainly confined to neurons and low-threshold mechanoreceptor (Fig. 1b). Our previous bulk RNA-seq study of the compressed TREZ (n = 6 per group) [25] revealed pronounced differential expression of genes associated with Piezo2 function (including Ngf, Pik3cd, and Piezo2; Fig. 1c) and the oxidative stress-related pathway (including Nlrp3, Cybb, and Casp1; Fig. 1d). GO–KEGG enrichment analysis of the transcriptome data functionally linked Piezo2 function and oxidative stress-related pathways to the observed changes in gene expression. Specifically, GO analysis revealed significant enrichment in biological processes such as “response to mechanical stimulus” and “regulation of reactive oxygen species”, as well as in molecular functions such as “cation channel activity” (Fig. 1e). Concurrently, KEGG analysis revealed their involvement in 13 key signalling pathways, notably the “PI3K-Akt signalling pathway”, “MAPK signalling pathway”, and “NOD-like receptor signalling pathway” (Fig. 1f). These findings collectively establish that trigeminal nerve root compression triggers core pathological processes involving upregulation of Piezo2 transcripts and oxidative stress.

Increased Piezo2 expression and oxidative stress in the TG following chronic nerve compression

Next, we developed a rat model of chronic trigeminal nerve root compression (Fig. 2a). Successful induction was confirmed by behavioural assays, which revealed that compared with those in the Sham group, the rats in the TN group exhibited sustained mechanical hypersensitivity (Fig. 2b) and increased spontaneous facial scratching (Fig. 2c) indicating persistent neuropathic pain. Histopathological analysis confirmed structural injury at the compression site, with LFB staining and transmission electron microscopy (TEM) revealing loosened nerve fibres with diminished myelin staining and evident demyelination, respectively (Fig. 2d). Biochemically, malondialdehyde (MDA) levels were significantly elevated in the TGs of TN model rats (Fig. 2e), suggesting increased oxidative stress. We further investigated the expression of the mechanosensitive channel Piezo2 and the antioxidant response in the TG. Immunofluorescence staining revealed a significant increase in the proportion of Piezo2-positive neurons among both CGRP+ and CGRP− neurons (Fig. 2g–f). Moreover, the expression of superoxide dismutase 2 (SOD2) was markedly upregulated (Fig. S1). These increases were further confirmed at the protein level by Western blotting for both Piezo2 and SOD2 (Fig. 2h-j). In summary, our results indicate that chronic trigeminal nerve compression induces demyelination, oxidative stress, and upregulation of Piezo2 expression in the TG.

Synthesis and characterization of RLPSe

The RLPSe nanoparticles were prepared through a sequential synthesis route (Fig. 3a; Fig. S2). First, the base polymer material L44-PVAm was synthesized via free radical polymerization. LPSe was then formed by the grafting of selenocystine onto L44-PVAm. Finally, RLPSe was obtained by conjugating the neural-targeting peptide RVG29 to LPSe. RLPSe/siPiezo2 nanoparticles were formed by incubating RLPSe with siPiezo2 for 20 min.

The successful synthesis of RLPSe was confirmed by FTIR spectroscopy and 1H NMR spectroscopy. The FTIR spectrum showed characteristic peaks at 3430 cm−1 (N-H stretch of PVAm), 1580 cm−1 (C=C stretch of the vinyl group), 1650 cm−1 (C=O stretch from the newly formed amide bonds between PVAm and both selenocystine and RVG29), and 1110 cm−1 (C-O-C stretch of the ether bond from L44-PVAm coupling) (Fig. 3b). The 1H NMR spectrum in D2O showed a solvent peak at approximately 4.7 ppm. Although polymer quantification can be challenging because of signal broadening, successful synthesis was verified by the observed changes in the amide proton region (7.5–9.5 ppm), together with the results of other peak shift analyses and the FTIR data analysis (Fig. 3c).

The RNA binding ability of the RLPSe nanocarrier was evaluated by agarose gel electrophoresis. The complete retardation of RNA migration occurred at RLPSe/RNA ratios above 1.2 (Fig. 3f), indicating effective complexation and sufficient positive charge density. The hydrodynamic diameter and zeta potential of the RLPSe/RNA nanoparticles were measured by dynamic light scattering. At the optimal RLPSe/RNA mass ratio of 1.2, the nanoparticles had an average size of 142.5 nm and a positive surface charge (Fig. 3d and h), which is suitable for cellular uptake. Transmission electron microscopy confirmed that the nanoparticles were spherical and uniform in size (Fig. 3e). Additionally, The RLPSe/RNA nanogels remained relatively stable during the first 3 days of incubation in PBS at room temperature, but showed obvious changes in size and size distribution by day 7 (Table S1).

To examine the ability of RLPSe to release RNA in an oxidative environment, we investigated the oxidative stress-responsive release of RNA from the RLPSe/RNA nanocomplexes. Agarose gel electrophoresis demonstrated that RLPSe fully bound RNA at mass ratios ≥1.2 under normal conditions (0 mM H2O2), but released it upon exposure to 1 mM or 2 mM H2O2 (Fig. 3f and g). Consistent with this, the zeta potential of the nanocomplexes decreased significantly after H2O2 treatment at mass ratios ≥0.8, indicating dissociation of the complex and loss of surface positive charge (Fig. 3h and i). To assess nuclease protection, RLPSe/RNA complexes (at a mass ratio of 1.2) were incubated with RNase A. Over a 30-min period, RLPSe effectively protected the RNA from enzymatic degradation (Fig. 3j–l).

Biosafety evaluation of RLPSe

The biosafety of RLPSe was systematically evaluated through a series of in vitro and in vivo assays. Cytotoxicity assays demonstrated that RLPSe had negligible effects on the viability of the HT22, RSC96, and BV2 cell lines at concentrations up to 100 μg/ml (Fig. S3a–c). Hemocompatibility testing revealed a hemolysis rate of less than 5% at concentrations less than 100 μg/ml (Fig. S3d), indicating that RLPSe is a nonhemolytic material according to ISO 10993-4 standards. Furthermore, in vivo toxicity assessment revealed no signs of significant organ damage in histological sections of the heart, liver, lungs and kidneys from treated animals (Fig. S3e). Collectively, these results indicate that RLPSe exhibits excellent biocompatibility and presents no significant toxicity concerns within the tested concentration range.

Oxidative stress responsiveness and neurotropism of RLPSe

To evaluate the oxidative stress responsiveness of RLPSe, we examined its hydroxyl radical-scavenging capacity. The results showed that RLPSe effectively reduced the number of hydroxyl radicals generated by H2O2, as indicated by the decreased formation of 2,3-dihydroxybenzoic acid from salicylic acid (Fig. 4a; Fig. S4a). To assess the ability of RLPSe to regulate intracellular oxidative stress, we established an oxidative stress model in B16F10 cells and HT22 cells using H2O2 and then cocultured them with RLPSe. RLPSe significantly reduced H2O2-induced intracellular ROS levels (Fig. 4b and c; Fig. S4b).

We next evaluated the delivery efficacy of RLPSe/siPiezo2 in primary TG neurons in vitro. Intranasal administration of RLPSe/siPiezo2 effectively alleviates orofacial pain in TN model rats

To evaluate the therapeutic effect of RLPSe/siPiezo2 on orofacial pain, we intranasally administered RLPSe/siPiezo2 to rats 28 days after TN model was established, when pain behaviours had stabilized. Mechanical pain thresholds and spontaneous scratching episodes were assessed before and at 4, 24, 48, 72, and 96 h after treatment (Fig. 6a). Behavioural results across different groups before and after administration revealed that RLPSe/siPiezo2 effectively alleviated orofacial pain in TN model rats, as evidenced by a significant increase in the mechanical pain threshold and a concurrent reduction in spontaneous scratching episodes at 24- and 48-h post–treatment (Fig. 6b and c). Paired t tests confirmed that compared with pretreatment at baseline, intranasal administration of RLPSe/siPiezo2 significantly increased the mechanical pain threshold and reduced the scratching behavior in TN model rats at 24- and 48-h post-treatment (Fig. 6d and e). Unpaired t-tests further confirmed that the pain thresholds were significantly greater in the TN + RLPSe/siPiezo2 group than in both the TN + RLPSe/siNC and the TN + PBS group at 24 and 48 h. Similarly, the results of the intergroup comparisons revealed a significant reduction in scratching episodes in the TN + RLPSe/siPiezo2 group compared with all the control groups at the corresponding time points (Fig. 6f and g; Fig. S6a–b).

We next evaluated the targeting efficiency and protein knockdown efficacy of siPiezo2 in vivo. RLPSe/siPiezo2 effectively delivered siPiezo2 to TG neurons, with significantly greater delivery efficiency in the TN group than in the Sham group (Fig. 7a and b). Immunofluorescence staining and Western blotting confirmed that RLPSe/siPiezo2 reduced Piezo2 expression in the TG of TN model rats (Fig. 7c–e). Discussion

The management of TN remains a formidable challenge in clinical neurology, with a significant proportion of patients experiencing suboptimal outcomes from existing pharmacological and surgical interventions [1]. The development of more effective and minimally invasive treatments for TN remains an important goal in pain research. In recent years, nanomaterial-based targeted drug delivery systems have demonstrated substantial clinical translation potential, achieving notable success across various diseases [27,28].These systems, including those based on liposomes, dendrimers, and metal nanoparticles, provide distinct advantages for precise targeting, controlled release, and enhanced therapeutic efficacy [[29], [30], [31]]. In this context, our study addresses the unmet clinical need via the development and validation of RLPSe/siPiezo2—an intranasally administered, microenvironment-adaptive nanotherapeutic that precisely targets the mechanosensitive ion channel Piezo2, a key molecular driver of neuropathic pain.

Transcriptomic profiling of human and mouse trigeminal ganglia revealed Piezo2 expression across diverse cell populations, underscoring its broad potential to influence TG function. Critically, RNA-seq analysis of the compressed trigeminal root entry zone revealed the concurrent upregulation of Piezo2 and activation of oxidative stress-related pathways as core events in chronic nerve compression. These findings were experimentally confirmed, and revealed significant upregulation of Piezo2 expression in both CGRP+ and CGRP− neurons and elevated expression of oxidative stress markers. Concurrently, our previous work demonstrated that Piezo2 knockdown in the TG region alleviates orofacial pain [17]. Taken together, these findings provide a pathological rationale and position Piezo2 as a central amplifier in TN pathogenesis.

To translate this rationale into therapy, we engineered RLPSe nanoparticles with a multifunctional design. The conjugation of RVG29 conferred neurotropism, enabling preferential internalization by neuronal cells—a crucial feature for maximizing TG delivery and minimizing off-target effects [28,32,33]. The incorporation of diselenide bonds introduces oxidative stress-responsive release, ensuring that siRNA payloads are selectively unloaded in the pathological TG microenvironment [[34], [35], [36]]. Beyond serving as a release trigger, the diselenide bonds enabled RLPSe to directly scavenge radicals and reduce intracellular ROS, thus combining targeted delivery with intrinsic antioxidant activity. Furthermore, RLPSe exhibited excellent biosafety and siRNA protection, fulfilling key prerequisites for clinical translation. Under oxidative stress conditions, RLPSe/siPiezo2 outperformed commercial transfection reagents. Following intranasal administration, RLPSe was taken up by trigeminal nerve endings in the nasal mucosa and transported to the TG, with successful colocalization of RLPSe and siRNA within TG neurons confirming effective nose-to-brain delivery. This strategy achieved significant Piezo2 knockdown, which translated into sustained relief of orofacial pain behaviors.

Notably, within the time window of 4 to 48 h post-administration, the RLPSe/siNC group exhibited a slight but statistically nonsignificant trend toward pain relief compared with the TN + PBS group. This may reflect a limited, transient modulatory effect of the RLPSe carrier's intrinsic antioxidant activity on the TG oxidative microenvironment. Previous studies have confirmed that alleviating oxidative stress in this region can ameliorate TN. For instance, Liu et al. demonstrated that activation of the Nrf2/HO-1 pathway in the TG to scavenge ROS inhibits pyroptosis and relieves pain in a TN animal model [20]. Vasavda et al. reported that significant oxidative stress is present in both the cerebrospinal fluid of TN patients and in TN mouse models, and that activation of the NRF2 antioxidant network produces analgesic effects comparable to those achieved by TRPA1 inhibition [19]. These reports support the notion that antioxidant activity alone has therapeutic potential. However, in the present study, the weak relief trend observed in the TN + RLPSe/siNC group disappeared rapidly after 4 h (with no significant difference from the PBS group by 24 h), whereas the TN + RLPSe/siPiezo2 group maintained a significant and sustained analgesic effect at 24-48 h. Notably, this time window coincided closely with the peak of siRNA-mediated Piezo2 protein knockdown. We therefore conclude that the therapeutic effect of RLPSe/siPiezo2 is primarily attributable to specific gene silencing by Piezo2 siRNA; the carrier's antioxidant activity alone produces only a transient, limited effect insufficient to account for the sustained therapeutic outcome.

In addition to serving as a trigger for siRNA release, ROS may also influence Piezo2 activity through alternative mechanisms. Emerging evidence suggests that oxidative modification of the lipid bilayer can alter membrane tension, thereby potentially influencing Piezo2 gating, as Piezo2 is an intrinsically mechanosensitive channel that responds directly to changes in membrane stretch [37]. Thus, it is plausible that the ROS-rich pathological microenvironment in TN directly or indirectly sensitizes Piezo2, lowering its activation threshold and contributing to the vicious cycle of pain. However, we acknowledge that this hypothesis remains speculative, and direct experimental validation—such as measurements of membrane fluidity, lipid peroxidation, and Piezo2 channel activity under controlled oxidative conditions—is beyond the scope of the present study. These mechanistic questions represent important directions for our future research.

Our study integrates two rapidly advancing fields: the neurobiology of Piezo2 and smart nanotherapeutics. The central role of Piezo2 in pain is supported by growing evidence [15,[38], [39], [40]], and our study confirms this finding by demonstrating that its targeted knockdown reverses established pain. Notably, peripheral Piezo2 silencing in the TG was associated with reduced neuronal activation in the Sp5C and the primary somatosensory cortex. This observation raises a possibility of a “peripheral-to-central” silencing effect, although whether this reflects a direct causal circuit mechanism or an indirect consequence of pain relief remains to be determined.

Several limitations merit consideration. Although RLPSe was designed for TG delivery, off-target accumulation in peripheral tissues may occur. Achieving selective uptake specifically by trigeminal nerve endings rather than other peripheral nerves or tissues remains an open question for further investigation. Meanwhile, the TG contains heterogeneous sensory fibres—Aβ, Aδ, and C fibres—with distinct functional roles [41]. Although Piezo2 was broadly upregulated, its subtype-specific contributions to orofacial pain require further investigation. Transcriptional profiling has revealed at least eight neuronal subtypes in the TG [24], and elucidating the role of Piezo2 in these populations is essential for understanding TN pathogenesis and developing therapeutics for precision therapy.

These considerations suggest potential directions for next-generation targeting strategies. Emerging approaches such as intersectional genetics or optogenetics may enable cell-type-specific modulation of Piezo2, potentially inhibiting pathological signalling while preserving its physiological roles. Such advances could help guide the design of smarter nanomaterials and support the development of more precise TN therapies in the future.

Conclusion

In this study, a targeted nanotherapeutic was developed by functionalizing nanomaterials with RVG29 and diselenide bonds, enabling precise in vivo delivery of Piezo2 siRNA to trigeminal ganglion neurons. This strategy effectively alleviated orofacial pain in a rat model of TN. Notably, peripheral Piezo2 silencing was associated with reduced neuronal activation in central pain-related regions, but whether this reflects a direct causal mechanism remains to be determined. Our findings provide insight into trigeminal neuropathic pain mechanisms and introduce a versatile nanomaterial that may warrant further investigation for the treatment of TN and possibly other neurological conditions.
