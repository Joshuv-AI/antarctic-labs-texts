Viscoelastic Multiscale Mechanical Indexes for Assessing Liver Fibrosis and Treatment Outcomes
Authors: Zhuo Chang, Liqiang Zhang, Jiu-Tao Hang, Wenjia Liu, Guang-Kui Xu
Published: 2023

ABSTRACT
AbstractUnderstanding liver tissue mechanics, particularly in the context of liver pathologies like fibrosis, cirrhosis, and carcinoma, holds pivotal significance for assessing disease severity and prognosis. Although the static mechanical properties of livers have been gradually studied, the intricacies of their dynamic mechanics remain enigmatic. Here, we characterize the dynamic creep responses of healthy, fibrotic, and mesenchymal stem cells (MSCs)-treated fibrotic lives. Strikingly, we unearth a ubiquitous two-stage power-law rheology of livers across different time scales with the exponents and their distribution profiles highly correlated to liver status. Moreover, our self-similar hierarchical theory effectively captures the delicate changes in the dynamical mechanics of livers. Notably, the viscoelastic multiscale mechanical indexes (i.e., power-law exponents and elastic stiffnesses of different hierarchies) and their distribution characteristics prominently vary with liver fibrosis and MSCs therapy. This study unveils the viscoelastic characteristics of livers and underscores the potential of proposed mechanical criteria for assessing disease evolution and prognosis.Keywords: viscoelasticity, power-law rheology, liver fibrosis, self-similar hierarchical theoretical model, mechanical index

Soft tissues are complex, anisotropic, and dynamic materials that exhibit nonlinear and viscoelastic characteristics.1 Their mechanical properties play a vital role in a variety of physiological and pathological processes, such as development, homeostasis, and disease progression.2−5 The mechanical properties of livers, as one of the typical soft tissues, are determined by both intracellular and extracellular structural proteins. These proteins form a sophisticated hierarchical nano- to microstructure where resident cells are embedded and interacted with one another.4,6 Liver fibrosis, a wound-healing response to chronic liver injure and inflammation, represents a critical step toward cirrhosis and liver cancer.7 It is characterized by excessive extracellular matrix (ECM) accumulation and abnormal remodeling, leading to tissue structural failure and stiffening.7−11 In recent years, mesenchymal stem cells (MSCs) therapy has gained recognition as a promising approach to alleviate inflammation and liver fibrosis and promote liver repair due to its immunosuppressive and regeneration effects.12−14 Despite liver biopsy being the current clinical standard for liver fibrosis diagnosis, it is not applicable for screening, early detection, and quantitative assessment of therapeutic effectiveness.13 Tissue mechanics and mechanical profiling have provided valuable insights into subtle microenvironmental and cellular changes.15−17 For example, Desai et al.8 observed significant tissue stiffening associated with liver fibrosis development, while Sun et al.9 discovered increased tissue stiffness alongside altered mechanical heterogeneity during fibrosis progression. Tian et al.10 demonstrated the potential of elastic modulus distribution profiles as mechanical indicators to assess liver disease progression.

Although the static mechanical information on livers has been gradually studied, there is still limited knowledge regarding how their dynamic mechanical properties (e.g., viscoelasticity) associate with liver fibrosis progression and antifibrosis therapy.1,3,6 Despite certain approaches, such as shear wave elastography18 and magnetic resonance elastography,19 having been developed to measure the storage and loss moduli of livers, they suffer from significant limitations, including high variability, low accuracy, and inability to quantitatively reflect the dynamic processes of fibrogenesis and treatment. It is now well established from a variety of studies that atomic force microscopy (AFM) proved an important technique to disclose the viscoelastic properties of individual cells20,21 and soft tissues6,22−27 at micro- and nanoscales, especially for those with various disorders, such as cardiovascular diseases,23 osteoarthritis,15 and cancers.16,17,24 To date, however, there remains a paucity of robust mechanical markers that can accurately capture the fibrosis progression and evaluate the therapeutic outcomes.

To circumvent the challenges of in-depth understanding of dynamic mechanics of soft tissues, we recently developed an AFM-based microrheology to examine the viscoelastic properties of cardiac tissues during the progression of myocardial infarction.25 Here, we extend this methodology to investigate the creep responses of livers with varying statuses (healthy, fibrotic, and MSCs-treated fibrotic). Interestingly, we find that regardless of their status, the livers exhibit a universal two-stage power-law rheological characteristics across different time scales, which is rarely observed in single cells.20,26 Different from our previous study that exclusively focused on the averaged mechanical properties of diseased tissues, we demonstrate a marked transformation in both the distribution profile and rheological heterogeneity of these power-law exponents. Importantly, the mechanical characteristics of fibrotic livers after MSCs therapy appear to evolve toward the normal physiological states, accentuating their potential to capture therapeutic effects. Further, we utilize a self-similar theoretical model to decipher subtle variations in the viscoelastic behaviors of livers, and the obtained mechanical parameters along with their distribution profiles and heterogeneity exhibit noteworthy disparities among different livers. Our study highlights the potential of these viscoelastic mechanical indexes and their distribution characteristics in assessing fibrosis progression and therapeutic effects.

The mouse model of liver fibrosis and MSCs therapy were established, as illustrated in Figure 1A. After CCl4 treatment, the fibrotic liver displays a roughened appearance contrasting with the glossy surface of control (Figure 1B). Next, we evaluated the fibrous tissue accumulation in each group. Histological examination reveals significant fibrous tissue deposition in fibrotic livers (Figures 1C,D). The MSCs therapy shows a notable reduction in the collagen, suggesting the potential of MSCs to halt fibrosis progression and reverse its course toward cirrhosis and liver cancer.12,13 The fibrotic area (FA) of fibrotic livers displays a nearly 10-fold increase compared to the control, which is then significantly attenuated by two-thirds following MSCs (control = 1.35 ± 0.42%; fibrotic = 17.99 ± 5.54%; MSCs = 5.87 ± 1.15%), as shown in Figure 1E. Furthermore, we quantified the plasma concentration of aspartate aminotransferase (AST) to detect the liver damage. Notably, the AST level in fibrotic mice is 5-fold higher than control and dropped by half upon MSCs administration (control = 20.88 ± 0.54 IU/L; fibrotic = 98.42 ± 12.26 IU/L; MSCs: 51.09 ± 13.20 IU/L), as shown in Figure 1F. These results corroborate our histological findings and demonstrate the therapeutic efficacy of MSCs in ameliorating liver fibrosis.

Currently, it remains a significant challenge to characterize the dynamical mechanical changes of fibrotic livers, despite the interaction between alterations in static tissue mechanics and disease progression having been established.7,10 By using the AFM-based microrheology approach (Figures S1 and S2),25 we investigated the dynamical creep responses of different livers, as summarized in Figures 2A,B and Table S1. The time-dependent creep compliance J(t) is defined as the ratio of strain ε(t) to applied mechanical stress σ(t). Given the creep behavior of distinct liver tissues, we plotted the compliance over time on a log–log scale for three groups (Figure 2C). Interestingly, it can be clearly seen that the creep compliances of all three groups exhibit a universal two-stage power-law viscoelastic rheology (i.e., J(t) ∼ tα) at different time scales. A single power-law rheological feature has been intensively found in isolated cells and epithelial monolayers,27 and the power-law exponents generally fall into the range of 0.1–0.5.20,28 Only a few studies observed a double power-law rheological behavior in the nucleus29 and isolated cells.20,26 Specifically, the time-dependent creep deformation can be perfectly caught by these two scaling exponents αshort (time range: 10–2–10–1 s) and αlong (time range: 100–101 s). For three groups, the values of αlong are all smaller than those of αshort, indicating that all three tissues become stiffening in the long-time range (Figure 2C). More importantly, both power-law exponents and their distribution patterns for each group are different, indicating the potential of these exponents for clinical usage, namely, for distinguishing between healthy and fibrotic livers and assessing therapeutic effects, as discussed in the following.

As shown in Figures 2D–G, we investigated the differing power-law exponents αshort and αlong for three groups. The average of αshort and αlong and their heterogeneity (i.e., coefficient of variation (CV)) are summarized in Table S2. We find that αshort for fibrotic livers is considerably smaller than that of the control (Figure 2D and Table S2). Of note, the αshort is more variable and extensively distributed (i.e., greater heterogeneity) in fibrotic livers than in control. Plotting a histogram of αshort of fibrotic livers discloses a bimodal distribution, which significantly differs from the unimodal distribution profile of control (Figure 2F). However, there are no changes in the distribution profiles of creep displacements for the three groups (Figure S3). Furthermore, we discover a moderate effect of MSCs therapy on the alteration of fibrotic liver rheological heterogeneity. We observe that the higher rheology peak of αshort (HRP_αshort) for both fibrotic and treated livers resembles the single rheology peak found in healthy livers (HRP_αshort ∼ 0.9). Conversely, the lower rheology peaks of αshort (LRP_αshort) consistently decrease to an approximate value of 0.7 for both diseased and treated livers. Here, the HRP_αshort indicates the normal rheological characteristic of livers in the short-time scale, while the rheological change induced by fibrosis is effectively captured by the LRP_αshort (∼0.7). Therefore, the αshort and its distribution profile can be exploited as a valuable mechanical index for tracking liver disease, while tissue rheological heterogeneity better serves the evaluation of treatment response.

In addition, the αshort is in the range 0.8–0.9, which signifies that all liver tissues are more fluid-like during the initial response. At short time scales, correlating to high frequencies,20,28 single cell’s rheological behavior is thought to be governed by internal fluid redistribution throughout the cytoplasm30 and the dynamics of a single cytoskeleton filament.28,31 For the liver tissue where there is a high proportion of liver cells (∼80% of liver mass is hepatocytes),32 the intercellular and extracellular fluid phases are compressed and flow through the porous meshwork of the cytoplasm and ECM, respectively, during the instantaneous stress.30 Hence, we detected significant creep deformation with large power-law exponents at the initial subsecond time scale.

Beyond 1.0 s, we find significantly decreased power-law exponents in all groups (αlong = 0.15–0.2), implying a solid-like change of all tissues during time progresses. On this time scale, we observe that the average of αlong of fibrotic tissues is slightly smaller than that of control (Figure 2E and Table S2). Interestingly, as shown in Figure 2G, the αlong histogram of fibrotic livers reveals a bimodal distribution, while it is unimodal for control and MSCs groups. Strikingly, the higher rheology peak of αlong(HRP_αlong) remains consistently around 0.2 across all three groups. This rheological distribution pattern not only implies that MSCs therapy facilitates the regeneration of fibrotic progression and liver functions but also reinstates the original mechanical properties of healthy livers. In addition, the lower rheology peak of αlong (LRP_αlong) in Figure 2G can adeptly characterize the fibrosis-driven tissue stiffening with ease (LRP_αlong ∼ 0.1). Moreover, fibrotic livers exhibit greater rheological heterogeneity, indicated by the larger CV of αlong, than normal samples (Figure 2G). Additionally, we find that MSCs therapy reduces the mechanical heterogeneity of fibrotic tissues by 24% (Table S2). Thus, αlong and its distribution profile are likely to be utilized to assess fibrogenesis and therapeutic effects.

In this time range, the complete cellular cytoskeleton controls the viscoelastic behavior of cells rather than the cytoplasm or a single filament at shorter time scales. The power-law rheological exponent of isolated cells has been discovered to fall into the range 0.1–0.5.20,28 Similar to a single epithelial cell, the creep response of epithelial tissues was shown to follow a power-law rheology with an exponent of 0.15, although the rheological impacts of ECM were not involved.33 It was found that the ECM also follows a power-law rheological characteristic with a smaller exponent of 0.05–0.09 than that of isolated cells.34 In this study, the obtained αlong is comparable to that of isolated cells rather than the ECM network. We speculate that the differences in the power-law exponents of distinct liver tissues may be mostly attributed to changes in the whole cellular viscoelastic properties. Together, these findings not only substantiate the diagnostic potential of power-law exponents in tracking disease progression but also unveil the prospective applicability of the distribution profile and heterogeneity of these exponents for assessing therapeutic efficacy.

Based on the hierarchical structures of biological tissues, we have previously developed a self-similar mechanical model,25 as illustrated in Figure 3A. Using this theoretical framework, we can perfectly capture the dynamical mechanical characteristics of all liver tissues, as shown in Figure 3B. These new mechanical parameters of three groups are compared in Figures 3C–J and Table S3. We find that the viscosity and elastic moduli of different hierarchies demonstrate a consistent increase coupled with a notable alteration in their distribution profiles and mechanical heterogeneity with fibrosis progression. In comparison, MSCs therapy is found to significantly inhibit the development of live fibrosis and promote the recovery of liver function through normalizing fibrotic tissue viscoelasticity and heterogeneity. The changes in these mechanical parameters for different livers are discussed in detail as follows.

First, we evaluate the impact of fibrosis and MSCs therapy on cytoplasmic elastic stiffness E1 because the mechanical properties of cytoplasm vary dynamically in response to physiological or developmental circumstances.35 It is found that with fibrosis progression, E1 increases by approximately 75%, whereas MSCs therapy moderately reduces the cytoplasmic stiffness of fibrotic livers (Table S3). Our calculated cytoplasmic elastic moduli are in good agreement with that of other cells with tens of Pa.36,37 Moreover, the E1 is more variable with broadening in its distribution for fibrotic tissues (Figure 3C and Table S3). In addition, the cytoplasm exhibits different stiffness distribution profiles that are bimodal for the fibrotic group and unimodal for control and MSCs groups (Figure 3G). Specifically, as shown in Figure 3G, the higher elasticity peak of E1 (HEP_E1) of fibrotic livers signifies the elevated cytoplasm stiffness associated with fibrosis, presenting a 2-fold increase compared to the lower elasticity peak of E1 (LEP_E1) of normal livers (LEP_E1 ∼ 43 Pa; HEP_E1 ∼ 75 Pa).

Second, we investigate how fibrogenesis and MSCs therapy affect the elastic stiffness at the cellular level hierarchy (E2). As shown in Figure 3D, the E2 significantly increases with fibrosis progression; nevertheless, a notable reduction in E2 can be seen after the therapy. Moreover, compared to normal livers, the E2 variation of fibrotic tissues is greater, whereas therapy lowers this stiffness variation (Figure 3D and Table S3). The increased E2 is likely attributed to intracellular actin fiber orientation shifting from diffuse to consistent for healthy and fibrotic livers, respectively.38Figure 3H illustrates the presence of two elastic peaks of E2 in treated livers. The higher elasticity peak of E2(HEP_E2 ∼ 200 Pa) corresponds to the upregulated and reorganized cytoskeleton during fibrosis,39,40 while post MSCs therapy, a portion of the cytoskeleton remodels and regains normal mechanics (LEP_E2 ∼ 110 Pa). Third, we analyze the changes in elastic stiffness of the third tissue-level hierarchy (E3) for three groups. Comparing with the control group, the E3 increases 3-fold in fibrotic tissues, whereas the therapy dramatically decreases E3 by approximately 50% (Figure 3E and Table S3). It should be noted that the total elastic stiffness of the tissue can be calculated by our theoretical model: Etotaltheory = E1 + E2 + E3. To validate this, we measured the static elastic stiffness of three groups (Figures S4 and S5). The theoretically calculated stiffnesses and their distribution profiles of three groups (Etotaltheory) demonstrate excellent agreement with those obtained from static indentation experiments (Etotalexp) (Table S4 and Figure S5).

We last investigated the viscosity of livers from three groups. In comparison to control samples, we notice a prominent increase in the cytoplasmic viscosity η in fibrotic livers (Figure 3F and Table S3). Remarkably, with the development of fibrosis, the cytoplasmic viscosity heterogeneity increases more than 3-fold (Table S3). Meanwhile, MSCs therapy appears to reverse the process of fibrosis by restoring the viscosity homogeneity. It is worth noting that the η distribution of the fibrotic group displays a bimodal profile, in contrast to the unimodal profile of control and MSCs groups (Figure 3J). In addition, fibrotic livers exhibit both a low-viscosity peak (LVP) and a high-viscosity peak (HVP). Remarkably, MSCs therapy effectively restores the gene and protein expression levels to a normal range, successfully tuning η back to its physiological state (∼40 Pa·s). Our results are highly consistent with previously reported cytoplasmic viscosity (∼ tens of Pa·s),36,37 despite the cell type varies.

To understand the efficacy of these new mechanical indexes for evaluating liver status, we performed receiver operating characteristic (ROC) analysis (Figures 4A,B). The diagnostic abilities of these indexes are measured by the areas under the ROC curves (AUC) and are summarized in Table S5. We subsequently assess the correlations between these mechanical indexes in Figure 4C. Generally, the αshort, the elastic stiffnesses of different hierarchies (E1, E2, and E3) and the entire tissue (Etotaltheory and Etotalexp) are all found to have higher sensitivity and specificity in identifying fibrotic livers from control (Figure 4D, colored in pink). In addition, there are four mechanical indexes (E2, E3, Etotaltheory, and Etotalexp) with good performance in distinguishing MSCs treated samples from fibrotic livers (colored in red). We further investigate the effectiveness of these indexes with high discriminatory power (0.8 < AUC < 1), as summarized in Table S6. Our findings reveal that the indexes based on elastic stiffness are more suitable to evaluate the therapeutic effects of MSCs therapy, whereas αshort exhibits excellent diagnostic potential for liver fibrosis (Figure 4D).

The present study has thoroughly scrutinized the dynamic mechanical behavior of livers with fibrosis and MSCs therapy. We first conducted AFM-based microrheology tests on a series of livers to assess their dynamic creep responses. The most spectacular finding is that regardless of their status, all of the liver tissues exhibit a universal two-stage power-law rheology at different time scales, which is rarely observed in single cells. These two power-law exponents and their distribution profiles significantly alter with liver status, implying a possible rheological change during liver fibrosis and MSCs therapy. Another contribution is that we used a hierarchical theoretical framework for completely capturing the dynamic mechanical characteristics of different livers at cytoplasmic, cellular, and tissue scales. We demonstrate that the average values, mechanical profile, and heterogeneity of the new mechanical parameters remarkably vary with different livers. Taken together, the power-law exponents and hierarchical elastic indexes along with their data characteristics (i.e., mean, pattern, and CV) can serve as simple yet robust mechanical indexes for tracking dynamic mechanical changes in liver fibrosis progression and MSCs therapy.

To date, it has been challenging to quantitatively assess the development of liver fibrosis and therapeutic effects. Our results have significant implications for the understanding of how dynamical mechanical properties alter with liver fibrosis and MSCs administration and further complement those of earlier studies of hepatic static mechanics. This study substantiates and extends upon the trends elucidated in our prior research,25 as we develop a new viscoelastic multiscale indexes-based mechanical criteria for assessing liver fibrosis and treatment outcomes. This multidimensional assessment approach can be potentially applied to other biological tissues, providing insight into complicated disease evolution, prognosis evaluation, and guiding the biomedical material design.
