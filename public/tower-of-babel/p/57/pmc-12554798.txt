Application of a reverse consultation model for pediatric obesity care: A quality improvement initiative
Authors: Elizabeth Hegedus, Emily Sousa, Priya Patel, Ahlee Kim, Brenda Manzanarez, Anet Piridzhanyan, Paula Mrowczynski-Hernandez, Alaina P. Vidmar
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
ObjectiveIntegrating pediatric obesity care into primary care remains challenging, especially in underserved areas. To address this, the REACH-KIDS (Reverse Consult Care Model for the Comprehensive Treatment of Pediatric Obesity in Primary Care) program, which aims to empower primary care clinician through education and resources was developed. This study describes the design and early evaluation of a quality improvement initiative aimed at building PCP capacity in pediatric obesity care using a collaborative care model.MethodsThe REACH-KIDS model was implemented across two sites, involving didactic mini-series sessions designed to educate primary care clinicians on obesity care. The program's feasibility was evaluated by tracking attendance, engagement, satisfaction, and knowledge gained. Between November 2023 and May 2024, two mini-series were conducted across the sites, and feedback was gathered to assess shifts in clinical practice, particularly in anti-obesity medication use.ResultsAt Site one, 68, 67, and 67 attendees participated in September, October, and November 2023 sessions, respectively. At Site two, 42, 45, and 52 attendees participated in March, April, and May 2024. Most attendees (75%) reported a shift in clinical practice regarding obesity pharmacotherapy after attending the mini-series. Satisfaction with the sessions was high, and knowledge assessments significantly improved participants' understanding of obesity treatment options. Notably, participants reported increased confidence in prescribing anti-obesity medications, indicating a positive clinical impact of the educational intervention.ConclusionsThe REACH-KIDS model demonstrated feasibility and effectiveness in improving primary care clinicians' knowledge of comprehensive obesity care. This quality improvement initiative highlights the potential of education and collaboration to enhance obesity care in primary care settings. Future studies should focus on expanding this model and assessing its impact on clinical outcomes in underserved populations. Limitations include the restricted geographic scope and reliance on self-reported changes in clinical practice; future work will require objective measures of patient outcomes and broader implementation.

Introduction

Obesity is a growing public health concern, affecting approximately one in five children. Projections indicate that by 2050, over half of youth will experience obesity, exacerbating the risk of long-term health complications (Hales et al., 2018). In response to this, the 2023 American Academy of Pediatrics (AAP) guidelines emphasize a comprehensive approach, incorporating intensive health behavior and lifestyle treatment (IHBLT), anti-obesity medication, and metabolic and bariatric surgery (MBS) for those with severe obesity (Hampl et al., 2023). Despite these recommendations, significant gaps remain in integrating subspecialty obesity care with primary care, particularly for underserved populations (Hampl et al., 2023; Salam et al., 2020; Cho et al., 2022).

In Los Angeles, where 37% of youth aged 10 to 18 are affected by obesity, access to specialized obesity care is limited, creating an urgent need for scalable and sustainable solutions (Styne et al., 2017; August et al., 2008). Although most pediatric obesity care occurs in primary care settings, primary care clinicians report low confidence and limited training to implement obesity interventions, especially pharmacotherapy and MBS referrals. Moreover, marginalized communities often face structural barriers that restrict access to specialized services. To address this gap, Children's Hospital Los Angeles (CHLA) developed the REACH-KIDS (Reverse Consult Care for the Comprehensive Treatment of Pediatric Obesity in Primary Care) model. This innovative model aims to enhance the capacity of PCP to manage pediatric obesity, including the integration of obesity pharmacotherapy and bariatric surgery when necessary. By promoting a collaborative care approach, REACH-KIDS empowers primary care clinicians with the tools and knowledge needed to provide effective obesity management while ensuring access to specialized care when required.

REACH-KIDS is a quality improvement initiative that offers structured education, clinical tools, and access to specialist support (Fig. 1). The model draws upon established frameworks such as the Collaborative Care Model (CCM), Chronic Care Model (CCM), and the ECHO Model. These models emphasize shared responsibility, task-shifting, and knowledge dissemination between subspecialists and generalists. Specifically, REACH-KIDS integrates a multidisciplinary training curriculum, longitudinal support through toolkits and consultation, and clear protocols for shared care based on obesity severity and comorbidities.

Primary care clinicians receive a didactic curriculum focused on obesity treatment modalities, including IHBLT, pharmacotherapy, and bariatric surgery (Hampl et al., 2023; Kelly et al., 2016; Ioannides-Demos et al., 2005; Kühnen et al., 2022; Kelly and Fox, 2017; Singhal et al., 2021; Barlow et al., 2021). The curriculum is delivered through three 90-min virtual sessions, complemented by an annually updated toolkit. This toolkit provides resources on nutrition, physical activity, behavior modification, and pharmacotherapy prescribing protocols. Ongoing support is available through email or phone consultations with obesity medicine specialists, addressing case-specific inquiries.

The program targets youth aged 18 months to 21 years with Class II obesity (BMI >35 kg/m2 with comorbidities) or Class III obesity (BMI >40 kg/m2). Youth with Class I obesity or overweight are managed within their primary care setting, with primary care clincians offering lifestyle recommendations and anti-obesity medications. Regular monitoring through quarterly visits ensures ongoing coordination between primary and specialized care teams.

While previous research on pediatric obesity has focused primarily on tertiary care, there is a gap in evidence regarding effective integration between primary care and subspecialty obesity care, particularly for marginalized populations. Los Angeles County, with a high prevalence of pediatric obesity and limited access to comprehensive care, is a focal point for evaluating the feasibility and effectiveness of the REACH-KIDS model.

This manuscript outlines the design, rationale, and early implementation of the REACH-KIDS model, offering insights into its potential to improve care delivery and health outcomes in underserved communities. We hypothesize that this innovative, stakeholder-driven approach will be well-received by primary care clinicians and provide a foundation for broader adoption in managing pediatric obesity, particularly in underserved populations.

Materials and methods

REACH-KIDS team members and stakeholders

The REACH-KIDS model (Fig. 1) was developed through a quality improvement process, incorporating input from multiple stakeholders and key team members. These included primary care clinicians (n = 25), clinic administrative staff (n = 10), and key decision-makers (n = 10) across two different health systems. Together, these systems encompass over 240 pediatricians spread across 80 locations in six counties, serving diverse populations, including historically underserved communities. Stakeholders focus groups and surveys were conducted to ensure that the model would address the unique needs of primary care clinicians and the children they serve. This iterative engagement ensured that the model was tailored to the unique operational and clinical realities of participating sites. This study was reviewed and deemed exempt by the Institutional Review Board, as it involved the analysis of de-identified data collected for educational quality improvement purposes.

Needs assessment

A comprehensive needs assessment was undertaken in collaboration with the Quality Improvement and Healthy System Development and Integration teams to tailor the curriculum and chronic care model to each site's specific requirements. The needs assessment included providers from four sites across two large health systems serving predominantly publicly insured pediatric patients with similar demographic profiles. Site one and Site two, where REACH-KIDS was implemented, belong to different health systems with distinct geographic locations, patient demographics, and clinicians infrastructures, allowing for examination of variability in implementation. Data collected during the assessment included estimates of patient volume with obesity, clinician comfort levels with intensive health behavior and lifestyle treatment, anti-obesity medication prescribing, and metabolic and bariatric surgery referrals, as well as preferred training formats. Semi-structured interviews conducted by a team member (AV) provided qualitative insights into site-specific challenges and expectations, aligning these findings with existing resources and operational workflows.

Curriculum design

The REACH-KIDS didactic curriculum was delivered by a multidisciplinary team comprising pediatric endocrinologists, general pediatricians, nurse practitioners, dietitians, and psychologists. Participating clinicians attended three 90-min virtual sessions, spaced monthly. Sessions were delivered via a Health Insurance Portability and Accountability Act-secure web platform. Topic selection for each site's mini-series was informed by site-specific needs assessments and provider feedback. At Site one, a single team member (AV) delivered all three 90-min sessions, while at Site 2, sessions were delivered by three team members (PC, PP, AV). Each session included didactic content, interactive case discussions, and Q&A time. Lecture preparation required approximately 240 min per site, with three 30-min stakeholder engagement sessions conducted prior to delivery. Following sessions, additional time was allocated for addressing post-session questions submitted via email or phone. Clinicians were required to register in advance, after which the session link was sent to their preferred email address. Sessions combined didactic presentations with interactive case discussions and dedicated question and answer time. Any unanswered questions were followed up via email, and responses shared with all attendees to foster continuous learning. A designated team member (AV) provided ongoing clinical and technical support via email or phone. At program initiation, clinicians received a comprehensive primary care obesity care toolkit (Table 1). This toolkit included educational materials on intensive health behavior and lifestyle treatment, nutrition, physical activity, sleep hygiene, and behavioral modifications, as well as mental health service access. The toolkit also incorporated an obesity pharmacotherapy prescribing protocol adapted from the Pediatric Endocrine Society's Obesity Special Interest Group, a templated insurance appeal letter for denied medication coverage, and patient education materials available in five languages. The tool kit was provided electronically to all providers. Formative work for REACH-KIDS implementation

The first phase of REACH-KIDS implementation centered on educating primary care clinicians about obesity treatment within the primary care setting, with an emphasis on integrating the reverse care model into practice. The REACH-KIDS model is rooted in collaborative care principles, enhancing the delivery of team-based, patient-centered obesity care. The model's design enables primary care clinicians to manage patients with Class 1 (100–120 % of the 95th percentile BMI) and Class 2 obesity (120–140 % of the 95th percentile BMI) without comorbidities independently. The goal is for these patients to be seen quarterly by their primary care clinicians, who will initiate obesity pharmacotherapy when indicated, based on the AAP 2023 guidelines. For patients with Class 2 obesity with comorbidities or Class 3 obesity (BMI >140 % of the 95th percentile), primary care clinicians refer patients to CHLA's comprehensive obesity care program. These patients receive coordinated care from a multidisciplinary team, which includes dietitians, obesity medicine specialists, and mental health providers. They have quarterly visits with a dietitian, bi-annual visits with an obesity medicine specialist, and mental health assessments tailored to the needs of the patient and family. In line with the collaborative care model, patients also continue to follow up with their primary care clinicians every three months, ensuring continuous monitoring and coordination of care. This approach helps extend contact hours, ensuring patients receive adequate care while optimizing available resources.

Outcome measures

Primary outcomes included: (1) feasibility of implementing the didactic curriculum across two sites, operationalized using three metrics, attendance at didactic sessions, distribution and utilization of the primary care obesity toolkit, and provider-reported satisfaction with the curriculum; (2) clinician satisfaction with the educational content; and (3) moderator satisfaction and time investment in training delivery and follow-up. Attendance was recorded via virtual logs. Immediately post-session, participants completed online satisfaction surveys, which also served as a requirement for continuing medical education credit. Feedback from these surveys informed iterative curriculum refinement. Additionally, moderators tracked preparation and engagement time as a balance measure to assess the resource demands associated with sustained implementation of the REACH-KIDS model.

Results

Didactic mini-series summary

Two didactic mini-series were conducted between November 2023 and May 2024 across two sites from different large health systems. The first series at Site one occurred in September, October, and November 2023, with 68, 67, and 67 attendees, respectively (89.1 % MD, 7.3 % DO, 3.6 % residents). A total of 150 pediatricians were initially invited to participate, and 75 responded and consented to participate, registering for the miniseries. This indicates that approximately 50 % of the invited clinicians at Site one attended the sessions, providing an estimate of reach and demonstrating feasibility at this site. The topics covered in these sessions were: Session 1 – Injectable Obesity Pharmacotherapies; Session 2 – Topiramate and Phentermine; and Session 3 – All Other Oral Obesity Pharmacotherapies. Each session was conducted by one facilitator for 90 min as outlined in the Curriculum Design section. The second series at Site two was conducted in March, April, and May 2024, with 42, 45, and 52 attendees, respectively (78.1 % MD, 6.3 % residents, 12.1 % nurse practitioners). A total of 100 pediatricians were initially invited to participate, and 65 responded and consented to participate, registering for the miniseries. This indicates that approximately 65 % of the invited providers at Site 2 attended the sessions, providing an estimate of reach and demonstrating feasibility at this site. The topics in these sessions were: Session 1 – Primary Care Visits and HABITS Questionnaire; Session 2 – Topiramate and Phentermine; and Session 3 – Injectable Medications.

Post-didactic session satisfaction

Participants rated the effectiveness of the presentations in addressing specific learning objectives (LOs) for each session at both Site one and Site two (Table 2). Participants at both Site one and Site two rated the effectiveness of the sessions on a 4-point Likert scale (Outstanding, Good, Average, Poor), with no ratings of “Poor.” At Site one, in Session 1 on injectable obesity medications, the first learning objective received Outstanding ratings from 70.9 %, with 23.6 % rating it as Good. For the second objective, 74.5 % rated it as Outstanding. In Session 2 on Topiramate and Phentermine, 67.4 % rated the first objective as Outstanding, and 73.1 % rated the second objective as Outstanding. In Session 3 on other oral obesity medications, 66.7 % rated the first objective as Outstanding, with 74.0 % rating the second objective as Outstanding. Following these sessions, 100 % of providers at Site one found the content relevant to their practice, with 100 % indicating the sessions would influence clinical decision-making (92.7 % for Session 3). At Site two, in Session 1 on IHBLT and obesity care, 73.9 % rated the first two objectives as Outstanding, with 80.4 % rating the third objective as Outstanding. In Session 2 on Topiramate and Phentermine, 58.3 % rated the first two objectives as Outstanding. In Session 3 on injectable medications, 90.1 % rated the first objective as Outstanding, with 84.4 % rating the second objective as Outstanding. Following these sessions, 100 % of providers found the sessions relevant, and most affirmed they would impact clinical decision-making (Session 2: 97.1 %, Session 3: 100 %). Thematic feedback

Post-didactic session surveys were completed after each session at both Site one (42 responses) and Site two (65 responses, Table 4). Responses were categorized into themes using content analysis by two independent reviewers. The majority of comments from both sites (42 at Site one, 65 at Site two) focused on medication prescriptions and clinical decision-making. A key theme across both sites was a shift in providers' approach to prescribing obesity medications. At Site one, 11 comments highlighted increased confidence, with providers feeling more prepared to initiate pharmacologic treatments. One provider stated, “I now have the knowledge to comfortably start obesity meds.” Similarly, at Site two, 13 comments reflected growing comfort with medications like Topiramate and Phentermine. Another theme was the shift from hesitancy to confidence in prescribing weight loss medications. Providers who were uncertain before now felt more equipped to initiate treatments. One pediatrician from Site one shared, “I still don't feel comfortable due to lack of experience, but I plan to start prescribing weight loss medications to my patients.” At Site two, one provider noted, “I was never comfortable before, but am willing to utilize this as an adjunctive treatment.” A third theme was integrating pharmacotherapy into the broader management of obesity as a chronic disease. Providers emphasized combining medication with lifestyle changes as a comprehensive approach. At Site one, one provider remarked, “I will start discussions with patients regarding pharmacotherapies for weight management.” Similarly, Site two providers expressed intention to incorporate medications into treatment plans. Lastly, several providers noted the increased ability to tailor recommendations to individual patients. Discussion

This quality improvement initiative demonstrates that the REACH-KIDS model is a feasible and effective strategy to enhance primary care clinicians' knowledge and confidence in managing pediatric obesity, particularly regarding pharmacotherapy. The high attendance, satisfaction, and reported practice shifts underscore the model's acceptability and relevance to clinical workflows in diverse, underserved settings.

The REACH-KIDS model builds upon established frameworks, the Collaborative Care Model, Chronic Care Model, and ECHO model, leveraging their strengths to integrate subspecialty expertise into primary care (Yeoh et al., 2018, Tylavsky et al., 2020, Cloutier et al., 2015). Specifically, the model empowers primary care clinicians to manage overweight and class I obesity cases independently while facilitating specialist support for more complex patients, enhancing care coordination and continuity. By ensuring regular patient follow-up and multidisciplinary engagement, the model aligns with best practices for chronic disease management. This approach fosters greater collaboration between primary care providers and obesity medicine specialists, facilitating more comprehensive care for patients with obesity.

The reverse consultation model employed by REACH-KIDS contrasts with traditional referral models in which primary care clinicians often rely on subspecialists for guidance and management. In reverse consultation, primary care clinicians actively participate in virtual consultations with specialists, receiving guidance on challenging cases while also contributing their clinical perspectives. This model not only strengthens the primary care clinicians role but also promotes a more dynamic, reciprocal exchange of expertise. By facilitating this interaction, the REACH-KIDS model helps build clinicians competencies and confidence in managing pediatric obesity within their own practices. This collaborative model aligns well with the Collaborative Care and Chronic Care models (Epping-Jordan et al., 2004), which emphasize the importance of integrating multidisciplinary expertise and continuous, proactive care for chronic conditions like obesity (Casanova et al., 2021). These frameworks underscore the importance of ongoing clinician support and patient engagement, particularly in underserved or resource-limited settings (Smith et al., 2022).

The Chronic Care Model, designed by Dr. Ed Wagner, emphasizes proactive patient care through planned interactions between patients and their healthcare teams, which has been shown to improve outcomes for chronic conditions (Epping-Jordan et al., 2004). This model informed the REACH-KIDS model by ensuring patients would see healthcare clinicians at least once per quarter, with more frequent visits for patients with more severe obesity or comorbidities. By integrating regular visits and consistent follow-up, the REACH-KIDS model ensures that patients receive continuous care, which is crucial for managing chronic conditions like obesity.

The ECHO model, developed by the University of New Mexico, was originally created to extend the reach of specialists to rural and underserved communities by using videoconferencing to facilitate knowledge sharing among primary care clinicians. This model inspired the REACH-KIDS mini-didactic sessions, which utilized virtual platforms to share expert knowledge about pediatric obesity care. Just as the ECHO model has been successful in disseminating specialized knowledge in other fields, the REACH-KIDS model leverages this approach to educate primary care clinicians on obesity pharmacotherapy and management.

While preliminary, these findings suggest that the REACH-KIDS approach can bridge critical gaps in obesity care, particularly in resource-limited settings. However, there are several limitations to this study. Implementation was limited to two sites in Southern California, potentially limiting generalizability. Moreover, despite improved clinicians' confidence, financial and insurance barriers to pharmacotherapy access persist, posing obstacles to equitable care delivery. Further exploration of the financial sustainability of models like REACH-KIDS is warranted, especially with respect to billing and reimbursement. There is potential for the model to be reimbursed through time-based consultation codes (e.g., CPT 99446–99,449), which would allow for the virtual consultations between specialists and primary care providers to be reimbursed. Additionally, the model may align with care coordination and collaborative care frameworks, potentially making it eligible for reimbursement under these models as well. Future research should evaluate the model's impact on patient-centered clinical outcomes, cost-effectiveness, and scalability. Addressing systemic barriers, including medication affordability and insurance authorization processes, will be essential to maximize the model's benefits, and further studies should explore the feasibility of incorporating REACH-KIDS within broader reimbursement structures.

Conclusions

The REACH-KIDS model offers a novel, stakeholder-informed framework to improve comprehensive pediatric obesity care integration within primary care. Initial implementation shows promising feasibility, clinician satisfaction, and knowledge gains, particularly in obesity pharmacotherapy. By fostering collaboration between primary care clinicians and obesity medicine specialists, the model enhances care continuity and resource utilization, representing a scalable approach to addressing pediatric obesity in underserved populations. With further investigation into reimbursement opportunities and broader integration within health systems, REACH-KIDS has the potential to serve as a sustainable, impactful intervention. Further research is warranted to assess clinical outcomes and broader dissemination potential.
