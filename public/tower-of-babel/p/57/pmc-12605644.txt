Strongyloides stercoralis infection presenting as a surgical diagnosis, a rare but important consideration: Case series of two patients
Authors: Martin Infante Altamirano, Akshat Sanan, Gustavo A Rubio, Henry J Lujan
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractIntroductionStrongyloides stercoralis (S. stercoralis), is a parasitic helminth with variable gastrointestinal symptoms that can mimic inflammatory bowel disease or malignancy. Diagnosis is often delayed due to nonspecific presentation and low test sensitivity, especially in non-endemic areas.MethodsWe reviewed two cases of S. stercoralis infection presenting as chronic colitis. Clinical, endoscopic, and histologic findings were analyzed, and diagnosis was confirmed via serology testing.ResultsBoth patients presented with chronic abdominal pain and colitis on image test. Serological tests revealed immune responses consistent with S. stercoralis infection. One patient had a history of travel to an endemic area; the other had chronic alcohol use. Symptoms resolved with antiparasitic therapy.ConclusionS. stercoralis infection can produce chronic colitis and lead to misdiagnosis. Awareness of risk factors and histologic features is essential to avoid unnecessary surgical intervention.Keywords: Case series, Strongyloides stercoralis, Inflammatory bowel disease

Introduction

Strongyloides stercoralis (S. stercoralis), is a soil-transmitted helminth responsible for Strongyloidiasis, a parasitic disease prevalent in tropical and temperate regions [1]. Clinical presentations range from asymptomatic infection, occurring in up to 60 % of cases, to dermatologic, pulmonary or gastrointestinal (GI) symptoms that are often sporadic and chronic. In immunocompromised individuals, it may lead to hyperinfection or disseminated disease [2]. Risk factors include corticosteroid therapy, chronic alcoholism and HTLV-1 infection [3]. Diagnosis remains challenging due to variable clinical presentation, inconsistent eosinophilia, and the low sensitivity of standard stool microscopy [4].

We present two cases of Strongyloidiasis manifesting as chronic colitis with recurrent abdominal pain with inflammatory changes on colonoscopy. One patient had an identifiable epidemiologic risk factor, namely travel to an endemic region, while the other had a recognized risk factor of chronic alcohol use. This report aims to highlight the diagnostic challenges associated with Strongyloides infection, its potential to mimic surgical conditions and the possible long-term gastrointestinal complications of chronic infection.

This case series has been reported in line with the PROCESS guidelines [20]. Written informed consent was obtained from both patients for publication of this report and accompanying images. A copy of the written consent is available for review by the Editor-in-Chief of this journal on request.

Methods

Patient 1

This is a 28-year-old man, with recently diagnosed Bell's palsy, who presented to the emergency department with abdominal pain, cramps and flatulence. Patient was born in Colombia and immigrated to the United States during childhood. Six months prior, he reported travel to Puerto Rico. Patient reported moderate epigastric pain (6/10) radiating to the left lower quadrant, without nausea, vomiting, constipation or diarrhea. Three months prior, he experienced similar pain and an abdominal computerized tomography (CT) scan showed extensive pericolic stranding and sigmoid colon wall thickening suggestive of acute colitis of unclear etiology. On admission, vitals were normal. Examination revealed mild to moderate epigastric and mid abdomen tenderness without peritoneal signs. Laboratory tests were revealing for a calprotectin level of 6630 μg/g. Abdominal CT scan showed wall thickening in the distal descending and proximal sigmoid colon, with fat stranding, lymphadenopathy and splenomegaly (Fig. 1, Fig. 2). Colonoscopy revealed a 7 mm sessile polyp in the ascending colon and erythematous mucosa in the descending and sigmoid colon which was biopsied (Fig. 3, Fig. 4). Pathology demonstrated increased eosinophils in the sigmoid colon mucosa, without malignancy. Due to the presence of eosinophils on pathology results and the patient's history of travel, a blood workup was performed, which revealed positive S. stercoralis IgG antibodies. The surgical team was consulted based on the colonoscopy findings, but no urgent indication for surgical intervention was present. The patient was treated for Strongyloides colitis, which can resemble inflammatory bowel disease, with ivermectin (200 μg/kg for two days). He was discharged after symptom resolution and tolerance of oral intake.

Patient 2

This is a 76-year-old man with a past medical history significant for atrial fibrillation, stroke, syphilis and chronic alcohol use. Patient had megacolon and, seven years ago, underwent a partial sigmoid resection for volvulus, followed one year later by lysis of adhesions for bowel obstruction. Several months ago, he was hospitalized for recurrent sigmoid volvulus, which was successfully reduced endoscopically. During that evaluation, colonoscopy revealed an 8 (width) × 20 (length) cm infiltrative, non-obstructing, partially circumferential rectal mass; an 8 mm mucosal rectal nodule; chronic colitis; and multiple sigmoid ulcers. Biopsy of the mass was negative for malignancy. The patient was evaluated by the surgical team and managed non-operatively.

On this admission, he arrived in the emergency department complaining of abdominal pain and bloating for two days without nausea or vomiting. Vitals were normal. His abdomen was distended, tender, and bowel sounds were diminished. Non-bloody diarrhea was noted. Laboratory tests showed hemoglobin 8.2 g/dL, white blood cell counts of 6.8 × 109/L, neutrophils 59.4 % and eosinophils 8.3 %. Clostridium difficile and MRSA PCR were negative. Chest X-ray revealed right lower lung interstitial opacities and mild small bowel distension. Abdominal CT scan showed a dilated sigmoid colon (11.3 cm), with a 3.1 cm linear foreign body and circumferential rectal wall thickening (Fig. 5, Fig. 6). Colonoscopy was performed to decompress a recurrent volvulus, and the previously identified rectal mass was biopsied (Fig. 7, Fig. 8). Final pathology was consistent with inflamed granulation tissue. S. stercoralis IgG antibodies were positive. Due to recurrent volvulus episodes, history of megacolon and acute colonic obstruction episode, the patient underwent a total abdominal colectomy with end ileostomy, with preservation of the rectum. Intraoperative findings included a dilated descending colon (>15 cm) and cecum (>12 cm) with serosal tears. Pathology showed no carcinoma and benign lymph nodes. Patient received treatment with Ivermectin and after a long hospital stay was discharged. At 3-months follow up, patient was doing well, denied abdominal pain and had no complaints.

Discussion

S. stercoralis is a helminth endemic to tropical and subtropical regions and the leading cause of Strongyloidiasis in humans. Its prevalence is particularly high in areas with poor sanitation, reaching up to 70 % in countries such as Peru, Kenya or Namibia [5]. In the USA and Europe, most cases are considered to have originated abroad, primarily from South America and the Caribbean, where environmental conditions favor its transmission [6]. Socioeconomic disadvantages, institutionalization, human immunodeficiency virus (HIV) infection, alcoholism, and malnutrition increase the risk of infection [3,7]. The filariform larvae infect by penetrating the skin, then migrate via the venous system to penetrate the pulmonary alveoli or can migrate directly to the small intestine through the connective tissues [8,9].

The clinical presentation of Strongyloidiasis can be categorized into acute, chronic and hyperinfection. Acute infections are often oligosymptomatic, presenting with dermatitis in the invasion zone, by asthma symptoms, peripheral eosinophilia, heartburn or bloating [10]. Pulmonary manifestations on chest imaging may range from normal findings to lobar infiltrates, interstitial infiltrates, abscess/cavitation or bilateral opacities [5]. Chronic infection typically manifests with cough, rhonchi, dyspnea, abdominal pain, anorexia, diarrhea or constipation [11]. Hyperinfection involves disseminated disease with larvae spreading to multiple organs, including the liver, pancreas, kidneys, mesenteric lymph nodes, brain and muscles [12]. Both of our patients presented with abdominal pain and bloating, with patient #2 experiencing diarrhea. A chest x-ray obtained for patient #2 revealed right lower lung interstitial opacities likely due to atelectasis or an infiltrative process.

The gold standard for diagnosing Strongyloidiasis is the direct visualization of larvae. However, this becomes challenging due to the low sensitivity of a single specimen stool microscopy that is 21 % and only approaches 100 % with examination of seven samples [5,13,14]. In cases of hyperinfection, the number of worms increases and larvae may also be detected in sputum, bronchoalveolar or gastric lavage, urine, blood and biopsies of the lung or GI tract [15]. The number of stool samples is an important factor that influences the non-detection of the helminth in the feces, especially in chronic infection where larval output may be minimal and sporadic. For these reasons, serum Immunoglobulin G (IgG) assays are useful and valuable for infection diagnosis. In previous studies, serum IgG was found to have a diagnostic sensitivity of 88.2 % [16]. In our cases, both patients had positive IgG results that are related to the clinical and course presentation. No stool test was performed.

S. stercoralis infection can cause nonspecific alterations on image studies. These findings can be similar to inflammatory/infectious intestinal diseases [17]. Interestingly, radiologic signs of Strongyloidiasis have been described in most parts of the GI tract, except for the colon. Abdominal image studies were taken in our patients, patient #1 abdominal CT scan showed significant wall thickening in the descending and proximal sigmoid colon with fat stranding, lymphadenopathy in the retroperitoneum and splenomegaly. Patient #2 abdominal CT scan demonstrated distended sigmoid colon and rectum circumferential wall thickening. In our review of the literature, we only found one case report, from Yerra and Yarra, that described a patient with suspicion of chronic Strongyloidiasis and abdominal CT scan alterations described as mild circumferential enhancement and haziness of the wall of distal sigmoid colon and rectum [18].

During Strongyloidiasis, a range of colonoscopy findings can be seen, some non-specific like erythema and loss of vascular pattern, and some that may be characteristic like yellowish-white nodules [19]. In biopsy specimens, adult females, eggs and the rhabditiform larvae are typically found in the duodenum, while rhabditiform and filariform larvae may be present in the colon [19]. If the filariform larvae penetrate the colonic mucosa, it can produce eosinophilic infiltration surrounding that area giving a macroscopic appearance of yellowish-white nodules. In immunocompromised patients, the colon involvement is characterized by aphthous ulcers, severe erosions, serpiginous ulcerations, and stenosis in association with hyperinfection [19]. In our case series, patient #1 had erythematous mucosa in the descending and sigmoid colon and the pathology demonstrated increased eosinophils in the sigmoid colon mucosa. In patient #2, a colonoscopy performed months before, revealed partially inflamed colorectal mucosa with active chronic colitis. During the most recent colonoscopy, mucosal ulceration was also noted.

Some of the antiparasitic drugs effective against uncomplicated strongyloidiasis include ivermectin, benzimidazole compounds, and pyrvinium pamoate. For complicated cases, ivermectin at 200 μg/kg/day until resolution of clinical symptoms is the recommended approach [21]. Both of our patients received this regimen, and their symptoms resolved without further complications. In inflammatory bowel disease, treatment consists of aminosalicylates, immunomodulators, corticosteroids, biologic agents, and small molecules. The regimen depends on patient response, and escalation may be necessary [22].

Conclusions

There is a surgical tenet regarding careful diagnosis of rectal malignancy before surgical resection due to the presence of mimics such as colitis cystica profunda and solitary rectal ulcer syndrome. Similarly, in patient #2, endoscopic findings were highly suggestive of malignancy. However, initial nonoperative recommendations were to wait for the biopsy results and eventually the diagnosis of Strongyloides was obtained, thus avoiding a proctectomy. In a similar way, there were concerns about malignancy in patient #1, however, no surgical treatment was decided due to lack of pathological evidence of malignancy. S. stercoralis is a parasite that can involve the gastrointestinal tract, and in the colon the clinical and macroscopic presentation can resemble inflammatory bowel disease and even malignancy. It is therefore important for surgeons to consider this pathogen in their differential diagnosis to ensure appropriate surgical decision-making.
