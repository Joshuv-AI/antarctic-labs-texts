Bone stress injuries in elite runners: intrinsic and extrinsic risk factors in Italian track and field athletes
Authors: Gioi Spinello, Davide Grigoletto, Silvia Cerea, Nicole Casali, Marta Ghisi, Giuseppe Marcolin, Antonio Paoli, Tatiana Moro
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundBone stress injuries (BSIs) are overuse injuries that occur when a normal bone is no longer able to withstand repetitive mechanical loading. Given the high occurrence of BSIs in track and field athletes, this study aimed to evaluate risk factors associated with BSI development in elite athletes competing in different track events.MethodsIn this retrospective observational study, 172 Italian elite track and field athletes (mean age 23.7 ± 3.5) were interviewed using a closed-ended questionnaire that encompassed participant characteristics, history of BSI, and potential contributing factors. Female participants were also asked to answer the Low Energy Availability in Female Questionnaire (LEAF-Q).ResultsIn the total cohort, no sex differences were found in the presence or lifetime number of BSIs. Injured athletes had a different site distribution according to their event specialization, with endurance athletes experiencing more forefoot and more leg BSIs compared to sprinters. Among female athletes, those with menstrual irregularities reported a higher lifetime number of BSIs compared to those without irregularities, and athletes with higher LEAF-Q score had a higher number of BSIs. In regression analysis, age at menarche emerged as the only significant predictor, with each unit increase associated with a 20.1% increased risk of a higher number of BSIs.ConclusionThis study highlights the absence of sex differences in the presence and number of BSIs but reveals event-specific differences in BSIs distribution in specific anatomical sites in elite Italian runners. Among female athletes age at menarche appears to be a key factor associated with BSI burden, supporting its relevance as a marker to consider in the assessment of BSI risk.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s13102-026-01639-3.Keywords: Stress fractures, Running, Low energy availability, Menstrual cycle

Background

Bone stress injuries (BSIs) are caused by repeated submaximal stress that exceeds the bone’s normal ability to repair itself, resulting in alteration or disruption of the homogeneity of the bone [1].

BSIs are relatively rare in the general population, however they are more prevalent among athletes engaged in activities involving continuous repetitive loading such as marching or running [2]. Track and field athletes, in particular, are among those with the highest risk of BSIs due to the high training volumes and intensities required in these disciplines [3]. This increased load exposes the athletes to an increased probability of developing BSIs, which prevents training and forces withdrawal from competitions. Importantly, BSI are primarily related to prolonged exposure over time rather than acute competitive events [5].

The etiology of BSIs is multifactorial, involving both intrinsic factors, such as sex, physical conditioning, hormonal disorder, and extrinsic factors, such as type of activity, training intensity, and environmental conditions [3]. Notably, female athletes in leanness sports manifest a heightened incidence of these injuries, likely due to the combined effect of low energy availability, menstrual irregularities, and bone health [2–4].

Low energy availability occurs when energy intake is inadequate to meet the demands of exercise energy expenditure, and when the energy deficit causes physiological alterations can lead to a condition defined as relative energy deficiency in sport [5, 6]. In high-level athletes, multiple daily training sessions and elevated energy expenditure increase the risk of low energy availability, a condition known to adversely affect multiple physiological systems, including skeletal and reproductive function.

In this context, menstrual irregularities, such as amenorrhea and oligomenorrhea, are relatively common among athletes and have been consistently associated with an increased injury risk [7]. In particular, athletes with current or past menstrual dysfunction have been shown to be at increased risk of bone stress injury [8–10].

In recent years, the Italian track and field national team holds an important role in European and global contests, securing several medals in both youth and senior categories at major championship events. To our knowledge, detailed BSI epidemiology in elite cohorts remains limited, with existing studies often focused on a single sex, specific event discipline (i.e., long distance running), or a specific timeframe rather than an athlete’s entire career [11–14].

Despite increasing attention to BSIs in athletic populations, it remains unclear whether BSI occurrence and injury patterns differ across running disciplines and sexes in elite population, and which factors are most strongly associated with BSI risk.

Therefore, the aim of this retrospective study was to gather more information and investigate possible risk factors for BSIs, with the ultimate goal of preventing their occurrence in elite Italian track and field athletes specialized in running events (sprints, hurdles, and endurance). We also examined whether specific injury sites were more prevalent in certain disciplines. We hypothesized that endurance athletes would have a higher number and/or occurrence of BSIs compared to other running events, due to the more repetitive nature of the discipline.

Furthermore, considering that female athletes are generally more exposed to BSIs, we conducted a more thorough investigation within this subgroup, collecting additional information on low energy availability and menstrual irregularities. We expected our findings to align with the literature, aiming to clarify the relative importance of low energy availability, menstrual irregularities, and body mass index as potential risk factors associated with our cohort.

Methods

This study is an observational retrospective study involving high-level track and field running event specialists and was conducted between January and September 2024.

One hundred and seventy-two elite track and field athletes were recruited by means of social media and through advertisements placed in national sports clubs. Inclusion criteria for the study required athletes to be running event specialists in the top 50 athletes of the Italian ranking; notably, 91.9% of the athletes who met these criteria were involved in either the Italian youth or absolute national team. We divided running events into three categories: sprints (ranging from 60 to 400 m), hurdles (100/110 meters hurdles or 400 m hurdles), and endurance (ranging from 800 to 10,000 m and 3,000 m steeplechase).

All participants provided digital informed consent prior to the interview. This study involved human participants and was approved by the Human Ethical Committee (HEC-DSB/09–18). Athletes were assigned a unique identification code to ensure anonymity. Data were collected via a standardized telephone interview conducted by the same researcher (G.S.), lasting approximately 15 min and consisting of 26 closed-ended questions (yes/no, multiple choice, and numerical responses). The interview collected demographic and sport-related information, including age, sex (biological), event specialty, years of overall track and field practice and years of training in their current event. Participants were asked whether they had ever been diagnosed with a BSI confirmed by a physician verified through imaging (MRI or CT scan) and not attributed to trauma or occurred outside of sport participation. For athletes reporting a BSI, we recorded the specific fracture site and other potential factors associated with the injury, specifically the use of spikes and the training surface. Training surfaces were classified according to the prevalent surface on which each athlete trained at the moment of the injury. Surfaces were categorized as asphalt, unpaved surface, indoor track, treadmill, old track, modern track, and mixed terrain. Mixed terrain was defined as a training condition in which athletes regularly train on multiple surfaces rather than consistently on a single surface. The distinction between old and modern track was based on observable structural and surface degradation. The sites of fracture were divided in forefoot (metatarsal bones and sesamoids), mid-hind foot (calcaneus, talus, cuboid, cuneiform and navicular), leg (tibia and fibula) and other sites (vertebrae, meniscus, pelvic bones) and were defined respectively as BSIFF, BSIMH, BSIL and BSIO.

Questions used for the interview are shown in Supplementary Materials.

Female participants were later contacted again to fill the Low Energy Availability in Female Questionnaire (LEAF-Q) [15], a validated questionnaire that investigates the presence of low energy availability-related issues. Although all female athletes were invited to complete the LEAF-Q, data were obtained from only a subset of participants. Participants’ characteristics are shown in Table 1.

We used G*Power [16] to determine the statistical power achieved with our sample size. The total sample size (n = 172) provided us an estimated power of 83% to detect medium-sized effects (f = 0.25) in the one-way ANOVAs (used as parametric equivalents of the most complex group comparison) and 84% power to detect small correlations (r = 0.20). Additionally, the female subsample (n = 119) was enough to detect a medium effect size in the regression analysis with an estimated power of 98%.

Statistical Analysis

All statistical analysis were conducted using IBM SPSS statistics, version 28.

Data distribution was assessed using the Shapiro-Wilk test, and homogeneity of variance was evaluated using Levene’s test. As the assumptions of normality and/or homogeneity of variance were not consistently met across variables, non-parametric statistical methods were used for all analyses. Non-parametric Mann-Whitney U-test was used to study differences between-groups comparison (male vs. female and injured vs. non-injured athletes) in the overall sample. In addition, the Mann-Whitney U-test was also used in the female subsample to compare injured and non-injured athletes, athletes with and without menstrual irregularities, and at-risk or not at-risk of developing the Female Athlete Triad (LEAF score ≥ 8). Effect sizes were calculated using r (rank-biserial correlation) and interpreted according to conventional criteria, with values of 0.1 indicating a small effect, 0.3 a moderate one, and 0.5 a large effect [17, 18].

Chi-squared (χ²) analyses were used to investigate differences in menstrual cycle status (amenorrhea, oligomenorrhea, and regular cycles) between different events. The Kruskal-Wallis test was used to evaluate the most frequently affected anatomical site of BSI and to compare both the number and anatomical distribution of BSIs across different events. Effect sizes were estimated using epsilon squared (ε2), with values of 0.01, 0.08 and 0.26 interpreted as small, medium, and large effects, respectively [18]. When significant differences emerged, pairwise post-hoc comparisons were performed using Dunn’s tests with Bonferroni correction.

Spearman’s rank correlation analysis was conducted to explore associations between the number of BSIs and variables including age, age at first BSI, years of practice, years of event-specific practice, LEAF-Q score, and age at menarche.

Finally, a generalized linear model with a Poisson distribution was used to examine associations between number of BSIs and multiple predictors, including age, age at menarche, age at first BSI, years of practice, years of event practice, and LEAF-Q score.

Results

Total sample

We compared male and female athletes to observe possible sex differences, but we found no significant differences related to age (U = 2707.50; Z=-1.49; p = 0.14; r=-0.11) years of practice (U = 3107.00; Z=-0.16; p = 0.88; r=-0.01) years of event’s practice (U = 2647.50; Z=-1.69; p = 0.09; r=-0.13) and the number of BSIs (U = 2765.00; Z=-1.40; p = 0.16; r=-0.10).

Secondly, we observed differences between injured and non-injured athletes. No significant differences were observed related to age (U = 3070.50; Z=-1.93; p = 0.05; r=-0.15), years of practice (U = 3238.00; Z=-1.41; p = 0.16; r=-0.11), and years of event’s practice (U = 3152.00; Z=-1.68; p = 0.93; r=-0.13).

Considering different events (i.e., sprints, hurdles, and middle and long distance), no significant differences emerged for the presence of a BSI (χ2(2) = 2.70; p = 0.26). The Kruskal-Wallis test did not reveal a statistically significant difference in the number of BSI among different events H(2) = 5.14; p = 0.08; ε2 = 0.02).

In order to further analyse recurrent factors among injured athletes (n = 87), we observed associations with possible risk factors and whether specific anatomical sites of BSIs were most recurrent. The number of BSIs was not statistically influenced by spike’s use (U = 843.50, Z=-0.84, p = 0.40, r = 0.02), or insoles (U = 323.00, Z=-1.75, p = 0.08, r=-0.19) while it was influenced by the training ground H(6) = 14.34, p = 0.03, ε2 = 0.10. Post hoc analysis adjusted with Bonferroni correction for multiple comparisons indicated that running on a mixed training ground (Mdn = 2.00, IQR = 1.50-4.00) (p = 0.04) was associated with a higher number of BSIs than running on a new track (Mdn = 1.00, IQR = 1.00-1.75).

The regions of the body most subjected to BSIs were mid-hindfoot (BSIMH) (41.2%), forefoot (BSIFF) (29.1%), and leg (BSIL) (18.2%), other sites of the body (BSIO) were also involved (11.5%).

Among injured athletes, 31.00% were sprinters (n = 27), 25.30% were hurdlers (n = 22), and 43.70% were middle- and long-distance runners (n = 38). We observed that there were no differences in number of BSIs among different events and that some sites of fractures were more common in some disciplines than in others (Table 2). We observed a statistically significant difference for the variables number of BSIFF and number of BSIL. Post hoc analysis adjusted with Bonferroni correction for multiple comparisons revealed that endurance athletes had a statistically higher number of BSIFF compared to hurdlers (p = 0.004), but not to sprinters (p = 0.165), and no differences among hurdlers or sprinters emerged (p = 0.631). With respect to BSIL, post hoc analysis adjusted with Bonferroni correction revealed that endurance athletes showed a higher number of BSI compared to sprinters (p = 0.004) but not to hurdlers (p = 0.123), and no differences among hurdlers and sprinters emerged (p = 1.00). No significant differences were found in BSIMH or BSIO .

Female athletes

Among the 119 female athletes, 52.94% had experienced at least one BSI. We used Mann-Whitney U test to compare injured and non-injured athletes but we found no statistical significant differences in any of the observed variables age (U = 1430.50, Z=-1.79, p = 0.74, r=-0.16), years of practice (U = 1593.00, Z=-0.92, p = 0.36, r=-0.08), years of events’ practice (U = 1484.00, Z=-1.50, p = 0.13, r=-0.14) or age at menarche (U = 1404.50, Z=-1.82, p = 0.07, r=-0.16). Menstrual irregularities were differentially expressed among events: amenorrhea was more frequent among endurance athletes (χ2(3) = 20.22; p< 0.001).

In the female sample, 70 athletes (58.8%) answered the LEAF-Q. The questionnaire was administered at a later stage of the study, after the initial data collection, which explains why not all 119 female athletes participated. Among these respondents, athletes who had experienced at least one BSI had a higher LEAF-Q score (Mdn = 9.00, IQR = 5.00–13.00) compared to non-injured athletes (Mdn = 5.00, IQR = 2.00–9.00) (U = 339.5, Z=-3.22, p<0.001 r=-0.38). Among the athletes who completed the LEAF-Q, 42.9% were classified as at high-risk for developing the Female Athlete Triad (LEAF-Q score ≥ 8). High-risk athletes reported a higher number of BSI (Mdn = 1.00, IQR = 0.00–2.00) than athletes not at risk for developing the Triad (LEAF-Q score < 8) (Mdn = 0.00, IQR = 0.00–1.00), U = 799.00; Z = 2.55, p = 0.01, r = 0.30, and a lower BMI (ever reached) (Mdn = 17.76, IQR: 16.64–18.96) than athletes not at high-risk (Mdn = 18.73, IQR = 17.93–19.39), U = 369.00; Z=-2.74, p = 0.01, r = 0.32.

Considering female injured athletes only (n = 63), we observed that athletes who had age at menarche over 15 years old (n = 25; 21%) had no higher number of BSIs (Mdn = 2.00, IQR = 1.00–4.00) compared to athletes who had their menarche at a lower age (Mdn = 1.00, IQR = 1.00–2.00), (U = 485.00; Z = 1.88; p = 0.06; r = 0.23).

We observed significant differences in the number of BSIs among menstrual irregularities H(2) = 7.26 p = 0.03 ε2 = 0.09. Post hoc analysis adjusted with Bonferroni correction for multiple comparisons revealed that athletes who had amenorrhea showed a higher number of BSIs (Mdn = 2.00, IQR = 1.00–4.00) compared to athletes who had a regular menstrual cycle (Mdn = 1.00, IQR = 1.00–2.00) (p = 0.024), while no difference with athletes experiencing oligomenorrhea (Mdn = 1.00, IQR = 1.00–2.00) (p = 0.313).

Spearman correlational analysis revealed no significant correlations between number of BSIs and age (ρ = 0.14, p = 0.26), years of practice (ρ=-0.01, p = 0.95), years of events’ practice (ρ = 0.21, p = 0.10), age at first BSI (ρ=-0.19, p = 0.14), LEAF-Q score (ρ = 0.22, p = 0.22), and age of menarche (ρ = 0.23, p = 0.08).

A generalized linear model with a Poisson distribution was used to examine the number of BSIs in relation to age, age of menarche, age of first BSI, years of practice, years of events’ practice, and LEAF-Q score. Among these predictors, only age at menarche was statistically significant. Specifically, each one-year increase in age at menarche was associated with a higher expected number of BSI (B = 0.18; SE = 0.06) corresponding to a 20.1% increase in the BSI count (IRR = 1.20, p = 0.004; 95%CI = 1.06–1.36).

Discussion

Despite the high mechanical demands of track and field running, epidemiological data on BSIs across different running disciplines and sexes remain limited, especially in elite athletes and over the course of an athlete’s entire career. Consequently, discipline- and sex-specific injury patterns and associated risk profiles in elite populations are not well defined.

In this epidemiological study, we aimed to explore the possible associations between BSIs and potential risk factors in Italian elite track and field athletes specialized in running events. The main finding of our study was that no sex differences in BSI incidence or frequency were found within this cohort. Although unexpected, this finding should be interpreted within the specific context of an elite athlete population and lifetime injury occurrence. Previous studies have typically investigated BSI over a limited timeframe or examined only in youth or collegiate categories rather than across an athlete’s entire career [2, 12, 14]. Our findings are consistent with those of Kelly et al., who similarly reported no sex differences in BSI incidence in a prospective cohort study of Olympic athletes enrolled in the British Athletics World Class Programme [11]. This observation reinforces the well-established concept that track and field athletes are at high risk of developing BSIs [3, 4] due to the rigorous training and competition demands that make both males and females equally vulnerable to these injuries, especially when athletes are at the highest competitive level. Indeed, we observed that approximately 50% of the sample experienced at least one BSI during their career.

Although there are relatively few longitudinal studies examining long-term injury patterns in track and field, research on overuse injuries in elite cohorts suggests that elite athletes are more likely to develop these injuries, due to their high training volume and potential imbalance between internal and external workloads [19, 20].

Beyond intrinsic factors, extrinsic elements such as training equipment and surface characteristics may also influence BSI risk. Spike or insoles use was not associated with BSI occurrence; in contrast, training surface showed a statistically significant association with BSI occurrence and a medium effect size, suggesting a potentially meaningful relationship. The role of training surface in BSI development remains complex and inconsistently reported in literature, with harder surfaces often hypothesized to increase mechanical loading [21, 22]. The association observed in the present study should be interpreted in light of its retrospective design. In the Italian context, modern track surfaces have been adopted relatively recently and differences in surface exposure may therefore be confounded by training history and temporal factors rather than reflecting an isolated effect of surface material.

Considering the potential interplay of training conditions and biomechanical stress, we also examined whether anatomical distribution of BSIs varied according to event specialization. Consistent with existing literature, the foot and the lower leg were the most frequently affected anatomical sites [19, 23, 24]. Differences across running disciplines in anatomical distribution of BSIs in the forefoot and the leg were observed, with medium effect sizes suggesting a potentially meaningful relationship between injury location and event specialization. Endurance athletes tended to present higher forefoot BSIs compared with hurdlers, while for tibia and fibula BSI, endurance athletes had more BSIs compared with sprinters. Although these findings might be influenced by the relatively small sample size within each event, they may reflect potential differences in injury patterns that could be related to the biomechanical demands of specific running disciplines. Training and competing in running events expose athletes to continuous repetitive stress; with each foot strike, ground reaction forces from two to three times body weight are expressed [25] and these forces increase with higher running speeds [26] exposing higher risk factors for lower limb injuries [27, 28]. While recent studies on the biomechanics of hurdling are limited [29–31], it is reasonable to hypothesize that the takeoff and landing phases involved in hurdle clearance also produce high loading rate, i.e. the speed at which force is applied to the body upon ground contact, that stress the athlete’s body. Nonetheless, these interpretations remain speculative and should be confirmed by future studies.

Beyond biomechanical factors, sex-specific physiological characteristics and energy availability patterns may further modulate BSI risk, particularly in female athletes.

In our cohort, the first bone stress injury (BSI) in male athletes predominantly occurred in the under-18 category, whereas in female athletes, the frequency of first BSI was similar across under-18, under-20, and under-23 categories. While this observation should be interpreted with caution, this difference likely reflects sex-specific patterns of skeletal maturation. Males experience peak height velocity later than females, coinciding with rapid bone elongation and incomplete mineralization, creating a transient window of skeletal vulnerability. In addition, sex-related differences in bone architecture, geometry, and strength during adolescence may temporarily contribute to greater skeletal fragility in males [32, 33]. Although training loads and intensities in under-18 categories are generally lower than in older age groups, male athletes typically generate higher absolute forces due to greater strength and speed [34], which may further increase mechanical loading on the skeleton and contribute to BSI risk. In contrast, females typically reach skeletal maturity earlier, but peak bone mass continues to accrue into early adulthood. Variations in age at menarche, menstrual irregularities, and low energy availability can influence bone mineral density and structural integrity, maintaining a relatively constant risk of BSI across age categories.

Within this context of multiple interacting risk factors, no differences were observed between injured and non-injured athletes, while clear differences emerged among injured athletes. Specifically, we observed that the number of BSIs differed according to menstrual status, with athletes reporting amenorrhea exhibiting greater injury burden compared to athletes who had a regular menstrual cycle. In addition, athletes classified as being at higher risk of developing the Female Athlete Triad had a higher lifetime number of BSIs compared with those at lower risk. These results reflect the current literature, which indicates that female athletes with menstrual irregularities and low energy availability are at higher risk of BSIs [3, 8, 22, 35–38].

In regression analysis age at menarche emerged as the only significant predictor of the number of BSIs, whereas LEAF-Q score was not independently associated with injury occurrence. Delayed age at menarche has been associated with several physiological factors affecting bone health, which may predispose athletes to an increased fragility in later life, potentially leading to more BSIs [39].

The absence of an independent association between LEAF-Q score and BSI number contrasts the findings reported by Holtzman et al. [6] and Wilwand et al. [40] who found that higher LEAF-Q scores were associated with an increased incidence of BSI. However, these results were obtained respectively in a cohort of female athletes from various sports and young noncompetitive recreational runners, suggesting that the relationship between low energy availability and BSIs may differ according to competitive level, sport specialization and cumulative training exposure.

Our research focused specifically on running elite-level athletes, providing a remarkable perspective across all running disciplines, considering that sprinters and hurdlers subgroups are often underrepresented in the existing literature, which centers primarily on endurance athletes.

Limitations

Despite this research highlighted important aspects related to BSI incidence over the entire career of the Italian elite track and field running athletes, it is worth to mention some limitations, such as a relatively small sample size for the subgroup analysis, the retrospective nature of the data collection, and consequently potential inaccuracies from self-reported data. The retrospective nature of the study precludes causal inference and the identified relationship should be interpreted as associations rather than evidence of causation. Moreover, we decided to include only high-level athletes, but we cannot ensure that these findings can be generalized to athletes competing at a lower level.

Conclusions

Our results confirm that BSIs are a recurrent injury among track and field athletes, highlighting the importance of implementing preventive and tailored programs from the first phases of an athlete’s career. The anatomical distribution of BSIs varies by running discipline, with endurance athletes showing a higher burden of forefoot and leg BSIs, suggesting that prevention and monitoring strategies should be tailored to the specific demands of each discipline.

In particular, special attention should be paid to athletes with late menarche and menstrual irregularities, considering their established associations with both BSIs occurrence and the Female Athlete Triad. This supports the need for systematic screening, and the use of a simple tool, such as the LEAF-Q, may help assess health status and identify athletes at risk for low energy availability or menstrual dysfunction, allowing for early identification and intervention. Taken together, these findings suggest that BSI prevention in elite track and field athletes may benefit from a comprehensive approach that considers intrinsic, extrinsic and discipline-specific factors to support health and performance.
