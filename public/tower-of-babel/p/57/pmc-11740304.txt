Unveiling the challenges encountered by newly graduated nurses during night shifts: A descriptive qualitative study
Authors: Naomi Kisanuki, Miyuki Takase, Masako Yamamoto
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
ObjectivesNewly graduated nurses commence night shifts during a phase of heightened vulnerability to reality shock, exacerbating the challenges faced by these graduates. Therefore, this study aimed to identify the challenges experienced by newly graduated nurses when undertaking night shifts in order to help identify a strategy for supporting their adaptation to these shifts.MethodsSemi-structured personal interviews were used to collect data. Fifteen newly graduated nurses were selected to participate in the study by purposive sampling method from November 2021 to March 2022 in one of four general hospitals situated in the southwestern region of Japan. Semi-structured interviews were analyzed by using thematic analysis.ResultsThe analysis revealed five central themes and nine subthemes: challenges in task efficiency (task organization challenges, challenges in time-constrained task performance), challenges in responding to patients’ changing conditions (challenges in responding to patients’ irregular conditions, challenges in responding to nighttime changes in patients’ behaviors), challenges in independent practice (challenges in engaging in independent nursing practice, challenges in dealing with unexpected events), challenges in establishing collaborative relationships (challenges in working closely with other staff, challenges in establishing collaborative relationships during night shifts with limited resources), challenges in adapting to shift work (challenges in managing physical conditions for shift work).ConclusionsNewly graduated nurses often encounter challenges in performing their tasks during the transition period. When working night shifts, they face additional hurdles unique to nocturnal duties. The findings underscore the necessity for these graduates to develop night shift-specific readiness to effectively navigate the demands inherent in such work schedules.

Introduction

Reality shock is a persistent concern within healthcare institutions, particularly affecting newly graduated nurses. Typically arising three to six months after these nurses enter the profession, reality shock manifests as a perceived dissonance between their acquired practical skills and those demanded by the clinical environment. Recently, the concept of “transition shock” has emerged to encapsulate the experiences of disorientation, loss, and confusion among graduates as they grapple with the professional expectations, responsibilities, and performance standards inherent in the shift from student to practitioner [1]. The initial year of employment constitutes a critical period during which newly graduated nurses forge their career trajectories. Thus, this initial period profoundly influences their long-term professional satisfaction and development. Failure to address reality shock during this transitional phase not only jeopardizes the career progression of these nurses and increases their turnover rate [2,3], but also imposes significant organizational costs. Contributing factors to reality shock among newly graduated nurses encompass a lack of competence and self-efficacy [4], the weighty responsibility associated with patient care, insufficient communication skills for managing patients’ psychological needs [5,6], and challenges in balancing professional duties with personal life commitments [7]. Moreover, the commencement of night shifts, typically undertaken during a phase of heightened vulnerability to reality shock [8,9], exacerbates the challenges faced by these graduates [10]. Thus, it becomes imperative for newly graduated nurses to acclimate themselves to night shifts as a crucial strategy for mitigating reality shock.

There are various forms of nurse rosters, with one type of shift dividing the day into three shifts, each consisting of 8 h of work. Another type divides the day into two shifts, with nurses working 12 h shifts, or day shift workers working 8 h shifts while night shift workers extend their hours to 16 from the evening to the early morning [11]. Regardless of the type, nurses working night shifts are compelled to adapt to a different rhythm of life compared to their daytime counterparts. Consequently, shift workers commonly experience sleep disorders characterized by poor sleep quality and shortened sleep duration, attributed to irregular sleeping patterns and inadequate rest between shifts [[12], [13], [14]]. Notably, many newly graduated nurses, particularly those unaccustomed to night shifts, also encounter sleep disturbances due to disruptions in their sleep-wake cycle [10,15,16]. Consequently, night shifts often precipitate cognitive decline and reduced productivity among nurses [[17], [18], [19]]. Moreover, nurses assigned to night shifts typically face higher patient loads compared to those on daytime shifts. This heightened workload poses a particular challenge for newly graduated nurses, who must independently judge patient care needs and their priorities by themselves. Additionally, the reduced staffing during night shifts necessitates that newly graduated nurses discern which issues warrant reporting and discussion with senior colleagues. Thus, night shift duties represent enormous and demanding responsibilities for newly graduated nurses.

Various initiatives have been implemented to support newly graduated nurses during their transition, such as the introduction of preceptor to provide essential physical and psychological resources, and the provision of simulation training to enhance clinical skills. The implementation of such measures has been linked to increased competency, self-confidence, and job satisfaction [20], and a reduced turnover rate among newly graduated nurses [20,21], thus yielding benefits for both nurses and organizations. However, to date, there has been limited research on programs specifically tailored to assist newly graduated nurses in adjusting to night shifts. Some Japanese hospitals offer orientation shifts for graduates, where they work alongside senior nurses as extra staff on multiple night shifts, receiving step-by-step support until they can work independently. Nevertheless, the efficacy of such orientation programs remains undetermined, and the development of dedicated support programs for night shifts is lacking. The absence of such support programs leaves newly graduated nurses to resort to individual coping strategies to adapt to night shifts, which are often deemed ineffective in mitigating sleep disturbances and fatigue accumulation [9].

In order to build an effective support program to facilitate their seamless adaptation to the night shift, it is paramount to understand the specific challenges faced by newly graduated nurses during this period. However, to date, few studies have specifically examined the experiences and challenges confronted by newly graduated nurses when working night shifts. Identifying the challenges encountered by newly graduated nurses facilitates the determination of the requisite readiness for night shifts. Moreover, clarifying this readiness enables the development of supportive measures to enhance their adaptation to nocturnal work. Implementing such a program can alleviate the burden on newly graduated nurses during night shifts, consequently mitigating the risk of reality shock and turnover. Therefore, the aim of this study was to identify the challenges experienced by newly graduated nurses when undertaking night shifts.

Methods

Study design and participants

This study utilized a qualitative descriptive design employing semi-structured interviews to explore the challenges encountered by newly graduated nurses during night shifts. This methodology facilitated the generation of comprehensive descriptive summaries of participants’ experiences [22,23] enabling researchers to delve into their perceptions, feelings, thoughts, and associated emotions.

Using a purposive sampling method, newly graduated nurses employed at four general hospitals situated in the southwestern region of Japan were recruited for this study. These hospitals, encompassing both public and private sectors, varied in size from small to large, each with more than 150 beds. Recruiting nurses from these diverse hospital settings was intended to enhance the representativeness of the participant sample. However, because the hospital sizes varied, the number of newly graduates employed in these hospitals also varied. Thus, the participants were recruited on a pro rata basis in accordance with the hospital size. The following inclusion criteria were applied to recruit nurses capable of articulating their challenges during night shifts: 1) nurses with less than one year of clinical experience post-graduation, 2) those with a minimum of one month’s experience in night shifts, and 3) individuals capable of independently performing night shifts (i.e., those who had completed orientation shifts). Participant recruitment continued until data saturation was achieved, signifying no emergence of new information.

Recruitment and enrolment

After obtaining consent to conduct the study from the directors of nursing at each of the participating hospitals, researchers requested the directors to distribute an invitation letter detailing the study’s purpose and methods to individuals meeting the inclusion criteria. The directors then introduced potential participants to the researchers, and the researchers contacted these individuals to confirm their willingness to participate. At the onset of each interview, the researcher orally and in writing explained the study’s purpose and procedures, and then obtained written consent from the participants.

Ethical considerations

As mentioned above, participants were briefed on the purpose and procedures of data collection before commencement. They were assured that participation was voluntary, and declining to participate would not result in any work-related repercussions. Furthermore, participants were assured that their data would be anonymized using pseudonyms and would solely be utilized for the purpose of this study. This research was conducted with the approval of Yasuda Women’s University Ethics Review Committee (Approval No. 210003).

Data collection

Semi-structured interviews were carried out with the participants from November 2021 to March 2022. Interviews were conducted in a private setting involving only the female researcher and the participant, either face-to-face such as a conference room within the hospitals, ensuring a quiet and calm environment, or online using Zoom software upon participant request.

At the beginning of each interview session, participants completed a questionnaire gathering demographic information and details regarding their night shift status (e.g., night shift schedule, the start time of their night shifts, whether they underwent orientation shifts, and the commencement of independent night shift work). Following this, the researcher initiated the interview sessions based on the interview guide which was developed through a comprehensive literature review. 1) What were your challenges during the night shift? 2) What specific things have you found challenging on the night shift? 3) Why were you challenged about that during the night shift? 4) What readiness do you think you need to acquire before starting the night shift? 5) Why do you feel you need that readiness before starting the night shift? 6) What support did you receive in working the night shift? 7) What support would you have liked to have received for working the night shift?

All interviews were conducted by the first author, who possesses both interview skills and over a decade of expertise as a nurse unit manager, supporting and supervising graduate nurses. The researcher encouraged participants to elaborate on specific challenges they encountered and the reasons behind their challenges. With participants’ consent, interviews were recorded using a digital voice recorder, and non-verbal data, such as facial expressions and postures, were documented in an observation notebook. Each interview session was conducted once and lasted between 25 and 50 mins (Mean = 38.83, SD = 9.24).

Data analysis

Audio-recorded data were transcribed verbatim, and thematic analysis, following the method proposed by Merriam & Tisdell [24] was employed to identify common challenges faced by new nurses during night shifts. Initially, the researcher repeatedly reviewed the verbatim transcripts to develop an overarching understanding of the interview data. Subsequently, transcripts were deconstructed into smaller meaningful units, and codes were assigned to represent the challenges experienced by newly graduated nurses during night shifts. These coded data were then compared and contrasted with other data, with similar codes grouped into categories. Categories were further organized into themes based on their conceptual similarities.

After conducting an analysis of the initial case, the second case was sequentially examined using the same methodology. The resultant codes, categories, and themes from these initial two cases were amalgamated to create a master list, “containing a primitive outline or classification system reflecting the recurring regularities or patterns” [24]. Subsequent cases were then analyzed in alignment with this master list, using a continual process of comparing and contrasting raw data, codes, and categories to refine themes delineating the challenges encountered by newly graduated nurses during night shifts. This iterative analytical approach persisted until no further categories or themes emerged, signifying the attainment of data saturation. NVivo14 (Lumivero. Denver, US) software was used for data management.

Trustworthiness

To establish the trustworthiness of the study, several approaches were undertaken to ensure its credibility, confirmability, transferability, and dependability [25]. To establish credibility, or the truthfulness of the study findings, the researcher conducted interviews with compassion and without exerting any form of coercion, thereby encouraging candid responses from participants. Despite the first author’s background as a nurse unit manager, she had no prior working relationship with the participants, which is important to note. Additionally, member checking was conducted by returning the interview transcripts to the participants and asking them to verify that the transcripts accurately represented their perspectives [26,27].

To ensure the confirmability of the findings, which involves maintaining the neutrality of a researcher’s perspective [25], the first author maintained a neutral stance toward the participants’ narratives when analyzing the data. Furthermore, the analysis process and outcomes were scrutinized by the second author, who was proficient in qualitative research. To enhance the transferability of the findings to other populations, participants were recruited from multiple hospitals with different foundational bodies and varying in size. Finally, to assure the dependability, or the consistency of the findings [25], the appropriateness of the extracted codes, categories, and themes was rigorously evaluated through repeated readings of the data.

Results

All 15 graduates introduced by the directors of nursing participated in the study. The mean age of the participants was 22.87 ± 0.64 years, and all were female. The graduate nurses entered the hospital workforce in April 2021. Seven of them worked in medical/surgical units, two in surgical units, three in units for internal medicine, and three in the critical care units. All underwent orientation night shifts, which commenced in April (n = 1), May (n = 5), June (n = 4), July (n = 2), August (n = 1), and October (n = 2) in 2021. The average length of graduates’ experience of night shifts at the time of the data collection was 5.8 (SD = 2.3) months, ranging from two to nine months, with the majority having six months of experience (n = 5) (Table 1). As a result of the analysis, five themes emerged representing challenges encountered by newly graduated nurses during night shifts. Four of these themes comprise two categories each. One category pertains to challenges that arose due to the graduates’ inadequate preparation for night shifts (i.e., those challenges that would not have been experienced if the graduates had been equipped with the competence to perform their nursing duties independently before commencing night shifts), while the other category encompasses challenges resulting from the graduates’ lack of experience in night shifts. Theme 1: Challenges in task efficiency

This theme highlights the challenges faced by graduates in efficiently performing night shift duties. It comprises two categories: “task organization challenges” (challenges arising from inadequate preparation) and “challenges in time-constrained task performance” (challenges resulting from a lack of experience).

Task organization challenges

This category delineates the challenges encountered by newly graduated nurses in executing their nursing duties. These challenges encompass prioritizing multiple assignments and organizing tasks efficiently, a stark contrast to their student experience of caring for only one patient at a time. Graduates frequently found themselves overwhelmed by the volume of tasks, struggling to discern task priorities, and this resulted in work delays. Their inability to reorganize tasks effectively compounded the issue, leading to a negative cycle of delays. In particular, graduates faced difficulty managing multiple simultaneous tasks.“For instance, when a patient had a bowel movement, others under my care simultaneously pressed call bells. I needed to administer tube feeding to one patient, yet before that, I had to check her blood sugar level and prepare medications to inject via the gastric tube … If a patient was easily offended, I questioned where I should turn.” (ID = 6)“When I had a patient on the first day after surgery, I had to take her vital signs every two hours, while also responding to call bells. So it was difficult to prioritize and decide which task to do first.” (ID = 4)

I needed to administer tube feeding to one patient, yet before that, I had to check her blood sugar level and prepare medications to inject via the gastric tube … If a patient was easily offended, I questioned where I should turn.” (ID = 6)

“When I had a patient on the first day after surgery, I had to take her vital signs every two hours, while also responding to call bells. So it was difficult to prioritize and decide which task to do first.” (ID = 4)

Challenges in time-constrained task performance

This category clarifies the difficulties experienced by graduates in keeping pace with the distinctive workflow of night shifts. During these shifts, nurses must complete numerous tasks within the constraint of the limited window of patient wakefulness. The workload intensifies notably during the morning hours (6:00 a.m.–8:00 a.m.), requiring nurses to conduct patient observations, vital sign assessments, blood sampling, and assist with daily living activities. As mentioned above, graduates already struggle to manage multiple simultaneous tasks. However, when these tasks must be completed within a limited time frame, the pressure on graduates more than doubles. One graduate, in particular, experienced enormous pressure to complete multiple tasks concentrated in the morning, which led to significant challenges in task execution.“I have a challenging morning because I am so rushed. I have to updateintravenous injectionon patients and attend to numerous other tasks such as taking blood samples, transporting patients to the operating theater, assisting patients with dressing, and so forth.” (ID = 4)

“I have a challenging morning because I am so rushed. I have to updateintravenous injectionon patients and attend to numerous other tasks such as taking blood samples, transporting patients to the operating theater, assisting patients with dressing, and so forth.” (ID = 4)

A concentration of tasks also occurs in the evening, between dinner and bedtime. Within this narrow timeframe, graduates must complete patient observations, administer medication (or replace intravenous injection), and assist patients with bedtime activities such as toileting and brushing their teeth.“It’s difficult for me to organize my work to complete all the patients’observations before bedtime. I consider ‘how much time is needed to complete the observation of each patient,’ or ‘how many minutes I can spend for each patient’s observation to complete more than 10 patients’observations within a scheduled timeframe.’ But even then, there are still various things that have to be taken care of, such as attending to call bells, IV replacement, etc., so things don’t go as planned, and most of the time I end up running over time …” (ID = 13)

Theme 2: Challenges in responding to patients’ changing conditions

This theme encompasses the anxiety and difficulties associated with responding to changes in patients’ conditions and delivering nursing care accordingly. It consists of two categories: “challenges in responding to patients’ irregular conditions” (challenges arising from inadequate preparation) and “challenges in responding to nighttime changes in patients’ behaviors” (challenges resulting from a lack of experience).

Challenges in responding to patients’ irregular conditions

This category underscores the difficulties graduates encounter in assessing patients’ irregular conditions and providing appropriate care. Newly graduated nurses often face challenges in making timely clinical judgments based on various patient responses and family interactions. Additionally, they tend to struggle to recognize and assess irregularities in patients’ conditions, such as altered degrees of consciousness. For example:“When I went to the patient’s room in the morning, the patient’s level of alertness was somewhat low … A senior nurse happened to bring the patient breakfast and remarked, ‘He seems a bit dazed, doesn’t he?’ I couldn’t determine if his condition was worsening or if he was still sleepy.” (ID = 10)

“When I went to the patient’s room in the morning, the patient’s level of alertness was somewhat low … A senior nurse happened to bring the patient breakfast and remarked, ‘He seems a bit dazed, doesn’t he?’ I couldn’t determine if his condition was worsening or if he was still sleepy.” (ID = 10)

In addition, graduates grapple with adjusting and modifying their care approaches, when patients present irregularities. For instance, administering antipyretics to a febrile patient is straightforward for graduates. However, they are often uncertain about how to intervene when the same patient exhibits an irregular symptom, such as low blood pressure. One graduate recalled:“There was a patient with a body temperature above 38.0°C. I thought about administering antipyretics, but I wasn’t sure if they were appropriate for a patient with low blood pressure, as I wasn’t familiar with the criteria. So, I consulted with senior staff.” (ID = 8)

“There was a patient with a body temperature above 38.0°C. I thought about administering antipyretics, but I wasn’t sure if they were appropriate for a patient with low blood pressure, as I wasn’t familiar with the criteria. So, I consulted with senior staff.” (ID = 8)

Challenges in responding to nighttime changes in patients’ behaviors

This category encompasses the challenges of caring for patients who exhibit agitation or delirium during nighttime hours, behaviors the graduates had seldom encountered during daytime, leaving them in a state of confusion. Many graduates were startled by these disturbed behaviors. One newly graduated nurse recounted her challenge of caring for a patient with severe delirium. She reflected as follows:“I had a patient with severe delirium, and since I had never been in charge of her during the day and had not collected enough information, it was difficult for me to understand the patient’s condition and how to respond …” (ID = 15)

“I had a patient with severe delirium, and since I had never been in charge of her during the day and had not collected enough information, it was difficult for me to understand the patient’s condition and how to respond …” (ID = 15)

They were also unsure of how to respond to a confused patient effectively, which hindered their performance during night shifts. In particular, graduates struggled with ensuring the safety of disturbed patients while performing other duties. They described their experiences as follows:“When I had a disturbed patient, I had him in a wheelchair and made a round of other patients with him. I did so because I could not leave him alone. That’s the hardest part …” (ID = 9)“When I had a patient with postoperative delirium who had an IV line and an indwelling urethral catheter in situ, I was careful to prevent him from removing these tubes. So I visited the patient frequently and tried to keep the tubes out of his sight. Despite these efforts, he managed to remove them anyway.” (ID = 13)

Theme 3: Challenges in independent practice

This theme encapsulates the challenges faced by graduates in transitioning to independent nursing practice. These challenges stem from their perceived lack of competence and confidence compared to the significant responsibilities assigned to them. It consists of “challenges in engaging in independent nursing practice” (challenges arising from inadequate preparation) and “challenges in dealing with unexpected events “(challenges resulting from a lack of experience).

Challenges in engaging in independent nursing practice

This category clarifies the anxiety stemming from the gradual transition to independent nursing practice. At the time of the interviews, the majority of graduate nurses had begun practicing independently, with occasional support from senior nurses. As they assumed independent roles, they experienced a lack of confidence and uncertainty in engaging in independent nursing practice.“Since I often work independently, I have to prepare all the supplies by myself without excess or deficiency, and it is difficult to think about what procedures I should follow in order to get things done quickly while the patients are sleeping.” (ID = 1)“I often assist patients to the bathroom on my own. One of my peers accidentally caused her patient to fall, so when I have to transfer a patient from a wheelchair, I sometimes wonder if I am doing the right thing, which makes me a little anxious.” (ID = 12)

“Since I often work independently, I have to prepare all the supplies by myself without excess or deficiency, and it is difficult to think about what procedures I should follow in order to get things done quickly while the patients are sleeping.” (ID = 1)

“I often assist patients to the bathroom on my own. One of my peers accidentally caused her patient to fall, so when I have to transfer a patient from a wheelchair, I sometimes wonder if I am doing the right thing, which makes me a little anxious.” (ID = 12)

Simultaneously, they became acutely aware of the weight of responsibility placed upon them. The pressure intensified as they realized they were solely responsible for crucial patient information and for ensuring no vital details were overlooked.“Since I am responsible for the room, I have to report patients’information, which I am the only one who knows. For example in patient hand-over, I feel a heavy responsibility …” (ID = 3)

“Since I am responsible for the room, I have to report patients’information, which I am the only one who knows. For example in patient hand-over, I feel a heavy responsibility …” (ID = 3)

Challenges in dealing with unexpected events

This category describes the anxiety and tension associated with managing unexpected events without the assistance of senior nurses, which is inevitable during night shifts when staffing levels are significantly lower than during the day. In addition, graduates are even tasked with attending to unforeseen situations beyond their assigned patients, because the higher patient load on night shifts necessitates collaboration among staff. A graduate recalled a failure to attend to a patient not under her care as follows:“When a ventilator alarm suddenly went off for a patient who was not assigned to me, the senior nurse responsible for the patient was attending to another patient. But, I was unable to respond properly on my own, which was a bit of a problem.” (ID = 9)

“When a ventilator alarm suddenly went off for a patient who was not assigned to me, the senior nurse responsible for the patient was attending to another patient. But, I was unable to respond properly on my own, which was a bit of a problem.” (ID = 9)

In addition, unexpected nighttime events, such as responding to deteriorating patients, often place graduates in unfamiliar situations, thereby intensifying their anxiety about practicing independently. One graduate recounted an incident involving the abrupt deterioration of a patient, during which she became keenly aware of her lack of proficiency not only in assessing and responding to the patient’s condition but also in preparing essential medical equipment to intervene effectively. Such self-awareness might not have arisen had she exclusively worked day shifts, where closer collaboration with a senior nurse is typically available.“The most challenging part was dealing with a sudden change in a patient’s condition. If it happened during a day shift, a senior staff member would assist me. However, during night shifts, the senior nurse also had her own patients to attend to, leaving me to handle the situation alone. Everything was unfamiliar to me, so I felt panicked and couldn’t do much. It even took me some time to get a 12-lead electrocardiogram.” (ID = 11)

However, during night shifts, the senior nurse also had her own patients to attend to, leaving me to handle the situation alone. Everything was unfamiliar to me, so I felt panicked and couldn’t do much. It even took me some time to get a 12-lead electrocardiogram.” (ID = 11)

Theme 4: Challenges in establishing collaborative relationships

This theme delves into the challenges faced by graduates in fostering collaborative relationships with other staff, which are vital for effective performance of their duties. It consists of two categories: “challenges in working closely with other staff ”(challenges arising from inadequate preparation) and “challenges in establishing collaborative relationships during night shifts with limited resources” (challenges resulting from a lack of experience).

Challenges in working closely with other staff

This category encompasses challenges in establishing collaborative relationships with senior nurses. Graduates often struggled to forge close working ties with certain senior nurses who were perceived as hostile and intimidating, making them difficult to approach. One graduate stated:“There are seniors who are easy to talk to and those who are hard to talk to. If a senior responds like‘So what?’when we talk to her, that makes us feel intimidated and makes it difficult to speak. This is a bad thing.” (ID = 5)

“There are seniors who are easy to talk to and those who are hard to talk to. If a senior responds like‘So what?’when we talk to her, that makes us feel intimidated and makes it difficult to speak. This is a bad thing.” (ID = 5)

Another recounted the following:“At first, I was a bit worried about how I should address those who were difficult to talk to, and how I could best get my thoughts across.” (ID = 6)

“At first, I was a bit worried about how I should address those who were difficult to talk to, and how I could best get my thoughts across.” (ID = 6)

Such strained relationships hindered effective reporting and consulting with senior nurses, exacerbating interpersonal dynamics. One graduate hesitated to report a patient's condition to senior nurses with whom she felt uncomfortable due to their intimidating demeanor, resulting in ineffective collaboration.“When I checked the (systolic) blood pressure of a patient on antihypertensive medication, it wasn’t too high … When all the senior nurses are hard to approach, I wonder who I should ask, whether it’s okay to inquire (i.e., about withholding the medication), or if they’ll take offense.” (ID = 2)

Challenges in establishing collaborative relationships during night shifts with limited resources

This category highlights the heightened difficulty experienced by graduates in reporting to and consulting with senior nurses and physicians during night shifts, when human resources are scarce. A limited number of staff during night shifts means that graduates are often unable to avoid interaction with senior nurses with whom they feel uncomfortable. Moreover, a high nurse-to-patient ratio further complicates their ability to approach seniors who are overwhelmed with numerous tasks. This situation impedes graduates’ ability to communicate promptly and effectively with senior nurses.“Senior nurses are busy looking after their patients. So I felt conflicted about asking about this at this particular time. It may be something that doesn’t have to be done now. But, if I don’t ask now, it makes me feel anxious and afraid.” (ID = 14)

Interaction with physicians also poses a challenge for graduates, as not all attending physicians are available during night shifts. For an instance, on a night shift, one graduate needed to liaise with on-call physicians instead of attending physicians and articulate a patient’s condition comprehensively. She found it challenging to convey not only the patient's fever but also the associated concerns due to the patient's unique circumstances.“I think it’s a little difficult to communicate not only that the patient has a fever but also that the patient is concerned about having a fever because of the kind of patient he is.” (ID = 1)

Theme 5: Challenges in adapting to shift work

This theme addresses the challenges faced by graduates in adjusting their lifestyle to accommodate night shifts. It comprises one category: “challenges in managing physical conditions for shift work” (challenges resulting from a lack of experience).

This category highlights the struggles of graduate nurses in obtaining sufficient sleep before and after night shifts, as well as managing accumulated fatigue resulting from working during these hours. Night shifts necessitated a lifestyle contrary to their usual routine of waking up in the morning and sleeping at night, leading to disrupted sleep patterns and difficulties in adaptation to shift work.“In the beginning, I woke up in the middle of the night for a week or so, after I did a night shift” (ID = 7).“If I couldn’t sleep before the night shift and didn’t get enough rest, I would already be tired before I came (to work) … I also think it would be quite difficult to get back into the rhythm of my usual life. If I finish the night shift around 9:00 a.m. and then go to bed, I can’t sleep at night … then, the day shift becomes tough, as well.” (ID = 2)“In contrast to day shifts, managing my physical condition was difficult because my daily rhythm was completely reversed. Discussion

This study delved into the challenges faced by newly graduated nurses when undertaking night shifts. Through our investigation, we identified five key themes: “challenges in task efficiency,” “challenges in responding to patients’ changing conditions,” “challenges in independent practice,” “challenges in establishing collaborative relationships,” and “challenges in adapting to shift work.” Each theme encompasses difficulties experienced by novice nurses during night shifts, either because they were unprepared for independent practice due to a lack of nursing competence, or because they lacked experience dealing with night-specific incidents and duties. In essence, newly graduated nurses encounter hurdles unique to nocturnal duties in addition to the general challenges associated with performing their duties. The findings underscore the necessity for these graduates to develop night shift-specific readiness to effectively navigate the demands inherent in such work schedules.

Newly graduated nurses said they faced a challenge in organizing their tasks efficiently. Nurses are often exposed to two types of pressure: one is time pressure and the other is pressure to provide responsible care for patients [28,29]. These pressures are intensified during the night shift, which necessitates not only the seamless execution of tasks but also the efficient management of responsibilities within a condensed timeframe. Andrews asserts that newly graduated nurses experience significant stress due to both the volume of tasks and the responsibility for multiple patients simultaneously [28]. Furthermore, they grapple with apprehension regarding task prioritization [30]. Consistent findings were corroborated in the current study, wherein newly graduated nurses consistently expressed uncertainty and anxiety during task execution, hindering their ability to perform duties seamlessly. This challenge persists during night shifts. Effective time management emerges as a critical concern for these graduates, with some feeling overwhelmed by workload demands and struggling to provide comprehensive care to all assigned patients during their shifts [31,32]. On night shifts, this pressure intensifies as nurses must promptly prioritize and organize tasks, which are often more substantial than those encountered during daytime shifts and must be completed within a condensed timeframe. The participants in this study similarly reported difficulty in this respect, underscoring the necessity for them to enhance their time management skills to effectively navigate night shifts.

Newly graduated nurses also faced challenges in identifying changes in patient conditions during the night, while simultaneously delivering individualized nursing care adapted to these changes. Their limited capacity to identify changes in patient conditions using pattern recognition and intuition, attributed in part to insufficient knowledge and experience, is characteristic of graduate nurses [33]. Additionally, a lack of critical thinking and reasoning skills hinders their capacity to make sound clinical decisions, necessitating a prolonged period for training [5,34]. This limitation was evident during night shifts, where graduates faced difficulty in rendering clinical judgments based on the changes in patient conditions and intervening accordingly. Moreover, night-specific fluctuations in patient behavior further complicate nursing interventions. A systematic review [35] indicates that nurses often lack knowledge and education in dementia care, relying on learning from observing others manage delirious patients. However, graduates have limited opportunities to observe due to their short experience with night shifts and heavy patient loads. Caring for delirious patients also increases their already stretched workload by requiring additional safety interventions due to the unpredictable behavior of the patients [35]. These factors make it extremely challenging for graduates to care for patients with delirium.

Furthermore, alongside the responsibility of providing independent nursing care to their assigned patients, newly graduated nurses also encountered challenges in managing unforeseen events, such as addressing sudden patient deterioration during night shifts. Attending to abruptly deteriorating or critically ill patients necessitates advanced clinical judgment. However, graduate nurses seldom encounter such scenarios [36], rendering on-the-spot situational assessment and proactive decision-making challenging. In fact, responding to emergencies was identified as one of the procedures that the majority of new graduates felt uncomfortable performing independently [37]. Engaging in such advanced nursing practices can induce stress among graduate nurses if they are inadequately prepared [38], potentially contributing to transitional shock. Consequently, the initiation of night shifts significantly impacts graduate nurses.

In fulfilling their professional obligations, graduates must not only possess a sufficient level of nursing competence but also demonstrate the capacity to collaborate effectively with fellow staff members. Particularly during night shifts, where human resources are constrained, adept collaboration, initiative, and timeliness become imperative. However, recent graduates have been documented to encounter difficulties in developing interpersonal relationships with their colleagues [6,38]. Consistent with existing literature, the graduates under scrutiny in this study encountered obstacles in fostering an efficient collaborative relationship, often feeling apprehensive when interacting with intimidating nurses or displaying reticence toward senior nursing staff. Moreover, Song and McCreary posit that graduates frequently perceive limitations in their communication abilities concerning patient treatment [5]. Correspondingly, the participants in this study also struggled with organizing information and conveying the patient's status to physicians in a succinct and comprehensible manner. Forbes and Evans contend that graduates commonly experience adverse emotional states such as anxiety and nervousness during interactions with medical practitioners [39]. This phenomenon is especially pronounced during night shifts, where graduates may be required to report to an on-call physician rather than the primary attending physician, necessitating the articulation of the patient's historical progress and current condition. The findings of this investigation indicate that graduate nurses encounter impediments in establishing effective collaboration with senior nurses and physicians through proficient communication during nocturnal shifts.

Finally, night shifts necessitate not only the execution of duties and provision of nursing care but also the self-management of graduates’ physical well-being. However, the graduates examined in this study encountered difficulty in effectively managing their own physical health. It is customary for nurses to engage in shift work in hospitals that operate round-the-clock. This form of work schedule is widely recognized to be associated with an elevated risk of cardiovascular disease, gastrointestinal disorders, and medical complications, notably including sleep disturbances and heightened fatigue [[40], [41], [42]]. In this study, the graduates experienced sleep deprivation and increased fatigue upon commencing night shifts, aligning with the adverse effects of shift work documented in prior research. The failure to adequately address these risks compounds the challenges of adjusting to nocturnal schedules. Sleep disturbances and fatigue are additionally linked to various cognitive impairments, such as diminished concentration and memory, subsequently affecting executive function, including the ability to prioritize tasks and comprehend the broader context [9]. Put simply, the graduates in this study may have encountered heightened difficulty in fulfilling their already demanding night shift responsibilities when their physical well-being was insufficiently managed. This could lead to a further accumulation of fatigue, creating a detrimental cycle for new graduate nurses.

Implications for clinical practice

The findings of this study highlight the necessity for specific readiness among graduate nurses as they embark on night shifts. Key components of this readiness include enhanced time management skills, the ability to prioritize tasks, and flexibility in adjusting work schedules while fulfilling duties. Graduates must also demonstrate the critical thinking and reasoning abilities, and must possess the necessary skills and knowledge to manage patients experiencing physiological and psychological changes. Moreover, graduates should cultivate skills to foster collaborative relationships and effectively communicate patient progress to fellow staff members. Additionally, graduates need to develop strategies for effectively managing their physical well-being, including maintaining healthy sleep patterns [43].

To adequately prepare graduates for night shifts, organizations should implement programs aimed at helping them acquire the aforementioned skills, knowledge, and abilities. Furthermore, organizations should customize the commencement of night shifts for each individual graduate by assessing their readiness [44]. Senior nurses should recognize the multiple challenges faced by graduates, including meeting task demands and seeking assistance and guidance from seniors with whom they feel uncomfortable. In particular, being able to feel safe when putting questions to seniors is indispensable for allowing the graduates to cope with various challenges encountered in the transition phase [45]. Senior nurses should understand this imperative. Lastly, it is crucial to acknowledge that graduates may experience reality shock, and negative experiences during night shifts can intensify their inclination to leave their positions. Providing reflective feedback that enhances their confidence while identifying areas for improvement is essential in this regard.

Implications for nursing research

Newly graduated nurses experience various challenges in performing night shifts due to inadequate preparation for independent work and a lack of experience in night shifts. These findings suggest that graduates should be trained specifically for night shifts so they have readiness to cope with the demands placed on them. The results indicate that this readiness can be classified into two categories: readiness that can be developed before starting night shifts and readiness that can be acquired through practicing night shifts.

To support the independent practice of newly graduated nurses in night shift duties, it is necessary to first identify the readiness required for night shifts and then introduce night shifts while assessing the readiness of new graduate nurses. Providing individualized support aligned with each graduate nurse's night shift readiness is also essential. Therefore, a scale to measure night shift readiness is indispensable. By developing such a scale, it will be possible to gain insight into how the readiness of new graduate nurses to perform night shifts develops and to determine when and what kinds of support are necessary for the graduates. By understanding these factors, adequate support programs to facilitate a smooth transition to night shifts can be developed.

Limitations

An attempt was made to diversify the recruitment of graduates across multiple hospitals to bolster the transferability of the findings. However, the study’s geographical scope may limit the applicability of its conclusions to graduates in disparate regions and nations. Furthermore, this investigation delved into graduates’ perceptions of the challenges inherent in undertaking night shifts, aiming to discern the requisite preparedness for nocturnal duties and to offer implications for organizational support structures for newly graduated nurses. Nonetheless, relying solely on interviews with graduate nurses may not fully reveal the complete spectrum of essential skills and knowledge they require. Thus, future research endeavors should consider soliciting insights from preceptor nurses to devise comprehensive strategies for supporting graduate nurses as they embark on night shifts.

Conclusions

The exploration of challenges confronting newly graduated nurses during night shifts reveals a multifaceted landscape. Graduates, amidst their transition into professional nursing practice, grapple with a host of complex situations intensified by the nocturnal setting. These challenges encompass a spectrum of dimensions, encompassing physical, psychological, and operational realms. Key among these hurdles are the demands of managing a diverse array of tasks within a compressed timeframe, navigating the care of patients prone to agitation or delirium during nighttime hours, confronting unforeseen events with limited human resources, fostering collaborative relationships with peers, and effectively managing the physiological toll of nocturnal work patterns. This delineation underscores the critical imperative for healthcare organizations to furnish comprehensive support mechanisms tailored to empowering newly graduated nurses to navigate the rigors of night shifts. Such interventions, ideally instituted before the initiation of night shifts and sustained throughout the adaptation process, are indispensable in facilitating successful acclimatization and bolstering the resilience of these novice practitioners.
