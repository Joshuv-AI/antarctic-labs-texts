Giant Biatrial Myxoma With Few Symptoms—The Odd Between the Rare: A Case Report
Authors: Jeton Bytyci, Dragos Andrei Duse, Marcus ten Hoevel, Karsten Meuter, Dina Borcean, Alexander Albert, Reinhard Hoeltgen, Marcus Wieczorek, Ralf Erkens
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTCardiac myxomas are rare and mostly benign. Their thrombogenic surface can lead to embolization, and their size can impair cardiac hemodynamics. Here, we present a unique case of large biatrial myxomas despite mild symptomatology. A female patient (62 years, Caucasian) presented with aggravation of dyspnea and palpitations, with the first occurrence only four months prior to the current clinical presentation. Only moderate changes in standard laboratory markers, including inflammation markers, were observed. Clinical symptomatology and standard transthoracic echocardiography were sufficient for the diagnosis of multiple myxomas (68 × 52 mm in the left atrium and 56 × 53 mm in the right atrium) causing mitral and tricuspid valve inflow obstructions, which were confirmed by subsequent histopathology following surgical resection. The case emphasizes the need for standardized echocardiography at the initial presentation of every patient with dyspnea to exclude cardiac myxoma as a rare cause for the symptoms. The atypical presentation underlines the necessity for serial echocardiographic assessment during follow‐up due to possible recurrence.

Introduction

Cardiologists are not often confronted with heart tumors. These appear extremely rare (< 0.2% of all tumors found in humans), and, fortunately, primary left heart tumors are mostly (> 90%) benign [1]. The prevalence of cardiac tumors is rising due to improved imaging techniques and enhanced screening consultations [2]. Demographic and individual features offer initial information regarding the tumor's etiology. Echocardiography is a handy and widely available imaging technique for assessing cardiac tumors' location, morphology, and size.

Accounting for approximately 50% of the cases, the most common primary intracardiac tumors are myxomas [3]. Occurring with an incidence of 8–150 cases per million, these benign tumors appear predominantly in females aged 40–60 [4]. They originate mainly from the fossa ovalis and are commonly found in the left atrium [5]. Right atrium myxomas are less common, and tumors originating from the aorta, pulmonary artery, ventricles, or even other organs are even more rarely encountered. Multiple myxomas occur in about one‐fifth of cases [5]. Clinical presentation is heterogeneous, and symptoms evoked by the cardiac masses are nonspecific. Frequently, the first symptom is an embolus detached from a thrombus formed on the surface. With tumor growth, dyspnea and palpitations occur due to mechanical valve obstruction [3], and even cases of sudden cardiac death have been described [6]. In some cases, and more often in malignant forms, fever, fatigue, or even paraneoplastic symptoms might accompany the occurrence of the tumors [7]. Surgical resection is the therapy of choice to prevent further tumor growth due to its association with good short‐and long‐term outcomes [5, 7]. Recurrence rates of ~5% have been described [4]. Cardiac myxomas appearing in more cardiac chambers are extremely rare.

In the current report, we present a unique case of a mildly symptomatic female patient with biatrial myxoma from initial presentation to follow‐up.

Case History/Examination

A 62‐year‐old female patient was referred to our hospital by a local cardiologist due to progressive exertional dyspnea (NYHA class III) and palpitations, which began 4 months before presentation and worsened during a vacation in the mountains. No additional symptoms at presentation or in the prior months could be ascertained. The patient had no family history of heart disease and had never smoked. There were no previous diseases or cardiovascular risk factors in her medical history.

Clinical consultation showed a 3/6 holosystolic murmur at the left fourth intercostal space, midclavicular line. No signs of acute neurological impairment were present. The patient had normal blood pressure and a slightly elevated pulse (130/80 mmHg and 108 bpm), a normal peripheral oxygen saturation (97%), and a normal body temperature (36.5°C). The electrocardiogram showed sinus tachycardia with an average heart rate of 108 bpm without additional abnormalities. Only moderate changes in standard laboratory markers, including inflammation markers, were observed (moderate anemia with hemoglobin (Hb) from 10.8 g/dL, reference range: 12–15.5 g/dL, mean corpuscular volume 75 fl, reference range: 80–99 fl, mean corpuscular hemoglobin 22 pg, reference range: 27–33.5 pg, slightly elevated D‐dimer 0.7 μg/mL, reference range: 0–0.5 μg/mL, moderately elevated pro‐BNP 998 pg/mL, reference range: 0–125 pg/mL, slightly elevated C‐Reactive Protein (CRP) 7.4 mg/dL, reference range: 0–0.49 mg/dL).

Transthoracic echocardiography showed a 68 × 52 mm round, homogeneous, and isoechogenic lesion in the left atrium originating from the interatrial septum. In addition, a similar isoechogenic mass measuring 56 × 53 mm was visibly attached to the right interatrial septum of the right atrium (Figure 1 and Video 1). There was no visible connection between the lesions. Both tumors showed a round to slightly oval shape, a smooth surface, and a demarcated edge. There was a visible prolapse of both masses through the mitral and tricuspid valves, impairing valve inflow during diastole. Left‐ventricular systolic function was mildly reduced (left‐ventricular ejection fraction: 47%). Right ventricular function was normal (tricuspid annular plane systolic excursion of 18 mm). Doppler echocardiography displayed moderate mitral regurgitation and severe tricuspid regurgitation with eccentric jets and flow acceleration evoked by the protrusion of the masses. No significant stenosis of the mitral or tricuspid valve was observed. The initial expected diagnosis was cardiac myxomas.

Differential Diagnosis, Investigations and Treatment

Thoracic and abdominal computed tomography (CT) ruled out other tumors as a possible primary tumor for cardiac metastasis. Coronary CT angiography excluded relevant coronary stenosis as part of the preoperative screening. The CT scans showed hypodense masses in both atria (Figure 2).

Axial section thoracic computed tomography showing the two intra‐atrial myxomas (blue arrows).

After completing preoperative screening, the patient was transferred to the cardiovascular surgery department for open tumor‐resection surgery. This was performed without complications under cardiopulmonary protection granted by a heart‐lung bypass machine. Both masses were resected carefully to avoid manipulation and fragmentation of the tumor (Video 2). The left‐sided mass was extensive and involved part of the septum, necessitating partial septal removal, and a bovine pericardial patch was used to reconstruct the atrial septum. The tricuspid valve was reconstructed during the same procedure. Intraoperative transesophageal echocardiography confirmed complete resection.

Conclusion and Results

As expected preoperatively, a histopathological assessment of the masses confirmed the diagnosis of atrial myxoma. The postoperative course showed no complications. Echocardiography prior to discharge showed neither residual myxoma nor a significant regurgitation of the mitral or tricuspid valve (Figure 3 and Video 3). Resection of the myxoma did not affect left‐ventricular function, which remained slightly impaired at discharge.

Discussion

The presented case showed the development of two more likely than not independent myxomas in a middle‐aged woman from initial clinical presentation until hospital discharge. It is rare since multiple myxomas are present in only about 20% of all myxoma cases [5]. In addition, the size of the tumors adds to the impressive clinical presentation. From echocardiographic findings, the tumors appear to comprise more than half of both atrial volumes. The odd part of the case was the total absence of symptoms despite the dimension and consecutive filling of cardiac atria. However, several previous reports have described biatrial myxomas presenting with only mild or nonspecific symptoms [8, 9]. It is unclear how long the patient had been living with the tumorous formations since the growth rate of tumors remains controversial [10]. However, a range of several years would be expected. Despite the longer existence of the myxoma, the patient experienced relevant dyspnea and palpitations only two weeks prior to presentation, perhaps as hallmarks of the onset of valve inflow obstruction. Fortunately, there were no direct or indirect signs of neurological impairment or infarctions due to embolization originating from the myxoma. In fact, the smooth surface of myxomas predisposes them to thrombi formation, and the occurrence of consecutive emboli in the systemic circulation appears in up to one‐third of cases [11, 12]. On the other side of the heart, pulmonary embolism originating from a right‐atrial myxoma occurs in 3%–20% of the cases [13, 14]. An interesting finding reported before was that the size of myxomas might not contribute to the risk of embolization [15, 16]. Regarding laboratory findings, in most cases of myxoma, elevations of several infectious parameters, such as the C‐reactive protein or interleukins, were expected [17, 18]. The blood work analyzed at hospital admission found no evidence of active, systemic inflammation. Of note, no measurements of interleukin‐2, ‐6, or ‐12 were performed, as it is not part of standard laboratory assessment.

Echocardiography showed impairment of diastolic filling due to mechanical obstruction of mitral and tricuspid valves. Because inflow impairment is a typical problem in these patients, the current recommendations are to perform color and continuous Doppler assessment over the valves to establish whether turbulent flow and velocity elevations are present. However, one must remember that in extensive tumors, atrial filling has noticeable effects. Therefore, the classical echocardiographic projections, such as the apical four‐chamber view, might not capture the full extent of flow variations. This is particularly relevant for the measurement of the continuous Doppler, underestimating the impairments in cardiac hemodynamics caused by tumor formation. In some cases, transesophageal echocardiography can also provide additional diagnostic information and augment preoperative screening by providing enhanced image clarity. Similarly, cardiac magnetic resonance might provide helpful information on volumetry and hemodynamic changes. Unfortunately, we were unable to perform an MRI scan on our patient due to claustrophobia.

Some traits presented in the case are common and known for myxoma. For instance, both myxomas originated from the interatrial septum. This is the most common localization described [7, 19]. In addition, the morphological aspect of the tumors in B‐mode echocardiography is similar to the one described in the literature [19]. Furthermore, dyspnea, which also occurred in the presented patient, is typical in myxoma due to blood flow obstruction. In fact, location, morphology, and symptomatology were helpful in making the diagnosis of myxomas only by clinical presentation and echocardiography. While most myxomas appear sporadic, about 10% have been associated with the Carney complex, an autosomal dominant disorder characterized by cardiac myxomas in combination with spotty skin pigmentation and various endocrine tumors or manifestations of endocrine hyperactivity [20, 21]. Occurrence of multiple (cardiac) myxomas has also been associated with the Carney complex. Although the clinical presentation in our patient did not resemble the syndrome, genetic testing for inactivating mutations in PRKAR1A was not performed; therefore, the Carney complex could neither be confirmed nor excluded.

Our case shows the central role of echocardiography, a feasible, fast, and widely accessible method, in standard care assessment in patients with dyspnea and/or palpitations. It will be the only diagnostic imaging method needed in most myxoma cases. As it should be standard care, it is worth mentioning the impact of echocardiography in all patients presenting with dyspnea, as it is a feasible and easy method to exclude the rare differential diagnosis of myxomas. Additionally, serial echocardiographic follow‐up investigations should be performed after myxoma removal to exclude any recurring tumors since these can occur in up to 20% of cases, and laboratory markers do not necessarily show any reflection of tumor formation.
