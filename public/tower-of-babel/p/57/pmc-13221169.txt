A State-of-the-Art Review Exploring the Cardiovascular Outcomes of Renin-Angiotensin Inhibitors in Acute Kidney Injury
Authors: Farah Yasmin, Sharjeel Ahmad, Adina Jabeen, Abdul Moeed, Muhammad Sohaib Asghar, Afsana Ansari Shaik, Mohammed Mahmmoud Fadelallah Eljack, M. Chadi Alraies
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundRenin-angiotensin system (RAS) inhibitors, including ACE inhibitors, ARBs, and angiotensin receptor-neprilysin inhibitors (ARNIs), are essential in managing cardiovascular and renal diseases.SummaryThe purpose of this narrative review was to comprehensively evaluate the cardiovascular outcomes associated with the use of RAS inhibitors in patients who have experienced acute kidney injury (AKI). The study highlighted cardiovascular complications of AKI, role of renin-angiotensin inhibitors for cardioprotection post-AKI, reviewed clinical considerations for RAS inhibitor use post-AKI to prevent cardiovascular complications, and discussed mechanistic basis of RAS inhibitors in post-AKI cardioprotection as well as cardiovascular safety concerns of RAS inhibitors post-AKI.Key MessagesBy highlighting areas for future research, this review intended to stimulate further studies that will ultimately enhance patient care and outcomes.

Introduction

Renin-angiotensin system (RAS) inhibitors, including ACE inhibitors, ARBs, and angiotensin receptor-neprilysin inhibitors (ARNIs), are essential in managing cardiovascular and renal diseases. They target the RAS to counteract the harmful effects of angiotensin II (Ang II), such as vasoconstriction and fibrosis [1, 2]. Initially developed for hypertension, their uses have expanded to include heart failure, chronic kidney disease (CKD), and diabetic nephropathy, supported by numerous clinical trials showing their effectiveness [3, 4]. Acute kidney injury (AKI) is characterized by a sudden decline in renal function that not only portends adverse renal outcomes but also significantly elevates the risk of subsequent cardiovascular events [5, 6]. Mechanistic studies have implicated neurohormonal activation, systemic inflammation, and oxidative stress as key mediators linking AKI with cardiovascular complications [7, 8]. Epidemiological data indicate that patients with even transient AKI episodes have an increased risk of myocardial infarction, heart failure, and stroke, emphasizing that AKI is more than an isolated renal event, and it is a marker of systemic vulnerability [4, 9].

Cardiovascular Complications of AKI

AKI triggers a complex cascade of pathophysiological events that extend well beyond the confines of the renal parenchyma, with profound implications for the cardiovascular system [8]. One of the pivotal responses to AKI is the activation of the RAS, leading to elevated levels of Ang II [10]. While systemic signals in the setting of acute tubular injury, such as elevated chloride delivery to the macula densa and volume overload from oliguria, are expected to suppress systemic renin release, evidence indicates that intrarenal (local) RAS activation can bypass these inhibitory mechanisms [11]. Animal models of ischemia-reperfusion injury (a common model of AKI) demonstrate marked increases in intrarenal and urinary Ang II with little change in plasma levels, suggesting compartmentalized RAS upregulation within the kidney despite systemic suppression [11]. Ang II is not only a potent vasoconstrictor but also an important mediator of inflammation and oxidative stress, thereby exacerbating endothelial dysfunction and accelerating atherosclerotic processes [1, 7]. Experimental studies have demonstrated that the surge in Ang II following AKI increases the production of inflammatory cytokines such as interleukin-6 and tumor necrosis factor-alpha, setting the stage for vascular injury and subsequent myocardial stress [2, 8].

In addition, the hemodynamic perturbations that accompany AKI, namely, fluid overload, electrolyte imbalances, and neurohormonal dysregulation, can lead to increased cardiac preload and afterload, predisposing the myocardium to injury and remodeling [5, 9]. Neurohormonal activation, including the stimulation of the sympathetic nervous system and aldosterone secretion, further amplifies the deleterious effects on cardiac structure and function [6, 12]. Moreover, AKI-associated systemic inflammation has been implicated in the development of microvascular dysfunction, which can compromise myocardial perfusion and precipitate ischemic events [8, 13].

Animal models of ischemia-reperfusion injury, a common cause of AKI, have provided insights into the remote effects of renal injury on the heart. These models demonstrate that renal injury releases circulating mediators that induce myocardial apoptosis and fibrosis, thereby contributing to the progression of heart failure [14, 15]. Clinical observations have paralleled these findings, as epidemiological studies reveal a robust association between episodes of AKI and subsequent adverse cardiovascular events [4, 16]. Importantly, the bidirectional nature of the cardiorenal syndrome implies that cardiovascular disease can also exacerbate renal dysfunction, creating a vicious cycle that complicates management [17]. Collectively, these insights underscore the importance of early therapeutic intervention to interrupt the progression from AKI to chronic cardiovascular disease [18, 19]. Understanding these mechanisms is crucial to optimizing the use of RAS inhibitors in mitigating both renal and cardiac damage in this vulnerable population [20, 21].

Role of Renin-Angiotensin Inhibitors for Cardioprotection Post-AKI

The clinical evidence exploring the role of RAS inhibitors in AKI is both extensive and multifaceted. Numerous observational studies and randomized controlled trials (RCTs) have focused on evaluating how RAS inhibitors, including ACE inhibitors and ARBs, influence cardiovascular outcomes following an episode of AKI. Data from large cohort studies have consistently demonstrated that AKI is an independent risk factor for the development of long-term cardiovascular complications, such as myocardial infarction, heart failure, and stroke [4, 5]. In this context, RAS inhibitors have been evaluated for their potential to ameliorate these risks through mechanisms that extend beyond simple blood pressure control [1, 2]. Numerous studies have demonstrated that patients treated with RAS inhibitors post-AKI experience a reduction in adverse cardiovascular events, including myocardial infarction, stroke, and heart failure exacerbations [1, 4, 5]. The protective effects of these agents are believed to be mediated through both hemodynamic improvements and direct anti-inflammatory actions, which collectively attenuate the processes of cardiac remodeling and fibrosis [2, 7].

Several landmark studies have highlighted the anti-inflammatory and anti-fibrotic properties of ACE inhibitors and ARBs, suggesting that these agents might mitigate the maladaptive remodeling that occurs after AKI [3, 7]. For instance, trials have shown that patients who receive RAS inhibitors after AKI experience lower rates of rehospitalization for heart failure and reduced progression to CKD [9, 14]. Timing, however, appears to be critical; early initiation of RAS inhibitor therapy during the recovery phase of AKI may optimize outcomes, whereas delayed administration may limit benefits and potentially exacerbate renal injury in vulnerable subpopulations [12, 15]. The duration of therapy is a critical factor. Short-term outcomes typically focus on immediate hemodynamic stabilization and prevention of recurrent renal injury, whereas long-term outcomes are concerned with preventing chronic cardiovascular disease and progressive renal decline [13, 18]. Studies have demonstrated that continuous RAS inhibitor therapy is associated with sustained improvements in left ventricular function and a reduction in myocardial fibrosis, likely due to persistent anti-inflammatory and anti-oxidative effects [8, 14]. Moreover, evidence suggests that the interplay between renal and cardiovascular systems, commonly referred to as the cardiorenal syndrome, can be significantly modified by timely RAS inhibition [17, 19].

There is strong evidence that renin-angiotensin system inhibition (RASi) after AKI confers significant cardiovascular benefits. Meta-analyses and systematic reviews consistently demonstrate that the use of ACE inhibitors or ARBs in patients recovering from AKI is associated with lower cardiovascular mortality and reduced incidence of myocardial infarction and stroke, benefits that extend beyond blood pressure control [3, 9, 16]. RCTs further support these findings, showing that early and sustained RASi use can stabilize hemodynamics and decrease rehospitalization for heart failure [12, 15]. Moreover, a dose-response relationship has been described, with higher intensity of RASi therapy correlating with greater cardiovascular risk reduction in post-AKI patients [13, 16]. Observational registry data also indicate improved long-term survival and fewer cardiovascular events with continued RASi therapy, suggesting a durable protective effect beyond the acute phase of renal injury [17, 18]. Nevertheless, concerns persist regarding the potential for RASi to precipitate recurrent renal dysfunction, especially in patients with severe or unresolved kidney impairment [8, 19]. Mechanistic Basis of RASis in Post-AKI Cardioprotection

RASis exert their beneficial effects through a complex interplay of mechanisms that target both the cardiovascular and renal systems, as illustrated in Table 3. ACE inhibitors block the conversion of Ang I to Ang II, thereby reducing vasoconstriction and mitigating the pro-inflammatory and pro-fibrotic signals that contribute to myocardial and renal injury [2, 8]. ACE inhibitors and angiotensin receptor blockers (ARBs) have distinct mechanisms (ACE inhibition versus AT1 receptor blockade), leading to subtle physiologic differences. However, randomized trials and meta-analyses have not demonstrated a clear superiority of one class over the other for major cardiovascular endpoints [3]. ARBs are generally better tolerated, with fewer coughs and withdrawals, while clinical outcome differences are not established [3]. Therefore, the choice of ACEi versus ARB should be individualized based on the indication and tolerability rather than assumed class superiority [4]. More recently, ARNIs have been introduced. These agents combine AT1 receptor blockade with the inhibition of neprilysin, an enzyme responsible for the degradation of natriuretic peptides, thereby enhancing natriuretic, diuretic, and vasodilatory effects [5, 9].

ARNIs combine the blockade of AT1 receptors with neprilysin inhibition, enhancing the signaling of natriuretic peptides [26]. In heart failure populations, ARNIs have been shown to improve cardiovascular outcomes and do not appear to increase adverse renal outcomes compared to ACEi/ARBs [27]. Observational and post-hoc analyses suggest that ARNIs may preserve estimated glomerular filtration rate (eGFR) in certain cohorts, although specific evidence in AKI patients is limited and primarily indirect (based on post hoc analyses and animal models) [28]. Therefore, ARNIs are promising for cardiorenal protection, but more specific RCTs in patients with recent AKI are needed before recommending routine use after AKI [29].

Beyond their primary effects on the RAS, RAS inhibitors have been shown to modulate several molecular pathways implicated in cardiovascular disease. They reduce myocardial fibrosis, improve endothelial function, and decrease oxidative stress and inflammation [12, 13] Preclinical studies have demonstrated that RAS inhibition can attenuate the expression of pro-fibrotic markers and cytokines, thus preventing adverse ventricular remodeling [14, 15]. These effects are especially relevant in the context of AKI, where the resulting systemic inflammation and neurohormonal activation significantly elevate cardiovascular risk [16, 17].

Pharmacokinetically, these agents differ in their duration of action and dosing schedules. ACE inhibitors are generally administered once or twice daily, while ARBs and ARNIs offer greater dosing flexibility, which can be tailored to the patient’s renal function and cardiovascular status [18, 19]. The sustained receptor blockade achieved by these agents is critical for long-term cardiovascular protection, as evidenced by multiple clinical trials showing reduced mortality and lower rates of rehospitalization for heart failure [20, 21]. In summary, the mechanistic versatility of RAS inhibitors, ranging from hemodynamic modulation to direct anti-inflammatory and anti-fibrotic actions positions them as indispensable tools in the management of cardiorenal syndromes.

Cardiovascular Safety Concerns of RAS Inhibitors Post-AKI

While the RASis have been shown to offer cardiovascular protection in patients recovering from AKI, their use is not without risks [30] (Table 4). One of the primary concerns is the potential for hypotension, especially in volume-depleted individuals or those concurrently taking diuretics [31]. This may precipitate syncope, dizziness, or cardiac ischemia due to inadequate perfusion [31]. RASi can also impair renal autoregulation by dilating the efferent arteriole, thereby potentially exacerbating renal dysfunction in susceptible individuals [32]. Furthermore, the reduction in aldosterone secretion can lead to hyperkalemia, increasing the risk of life-threatening arrhythmias such as ventricular fibrillation or asystole, particularly in patients with existing cardiac conduction abnormalities [33].

Additionally, the blunting of the renin-angiotensin response may interfere with compensatory mechanisms necessary during episodes of hemodynamic stress, such as heart failure exacerbations or infections [34]. In some cases, underlying subclinical cardiac dysfunction may be unmasked with RASi initiation, presenting as bradyarrhythmia or heart block, especially in those with diabetes or pre-existing cardiac conditions [35]. Drug-drug interactions, particularly with NSAIDs or potassium-sparing diuretics, further compound the risk, heightening the potential for acute hemodynamic compromise or electrolyte imbalances [36]. Given these concerns, the decision to initiate or resume RASi therapy post-AKI should involve a comprehensive assessment of cardiovascular status, renal function, and electrolyte balance, ideally guided by close monitoring and individualized risk-benefit evaluation [37].

RAS Inhibitors in Sepsis

Sepsis is a leading cause of AKI in ICU patients and poses specific challenges for RAS blockade [38]. Observational and systematic review data show that pre-admission ACEi/ARB exposure is associated with more severe AKI at presentation but not consistently worse hospital mortality; some meta-analyses even report lower mortality with prior RASi exposure [39]. Nevertheless, during active vasodilatory shock or hemodynamic instability, RAS inhibitors are commonly held because they can worsen hypotension and interfere with vasopressor responsiveness (notably, they may alter Ang II response) [39]. Current expert reviews recommend individualized decisions: withhold RASi in patients with ongoing hypotension or vasopressor requirement and consider reintroduction once hemodynamics are stable and renal perfusion is recovered; document rationale and involve critical care nephrology/cardiology when needed [40].

Guidelines and Recommendations on RASi Management: When to Hold and When to Resume

Incorporating evidence-based guidelines is crucial for the safe and effective use of RASi in patients with AKI (Fig. 1). These guidelines, developed by leading health organizations, such as the kidney disease: Improving Global Outcomes (KDIGO), the American Heart Association (AHA), the American College of Cardiology (ACC), and the European Society of Cardiology (ESC) provide clinicians with recommendations to balance the therapeutic benefits of RASi against potential risks, ensuring optimal patient outcomes. The use of RASis, including ACE inhibitors and angiotensin receptor blockers (ARBs), in the setting of AKI requires careful consideration. Although RASis have well-established cardiovascular benefits, such as reducing hypertension, left ventricular hypertrophy, and preventing heart failure progression, their use in AKI must be individualized [41].

Major guideline-issuing bodies include KDIGO provides comprehensive recommendations on the use of RASi in patients with AKI and CKD. Current guidelines recommend temporarily withholding RASi during episodes of moderate to severe AKI, especially when there is evidence of volume depletion or hemodynamic instability. However, this pause should be reassessed frequently, as prolonged discontinuation may deprive patients of critical cardiovascular protection, particularly those with a history of heart failure or significant proteinuria. The 2012 KDIGO Clinical Practice Guidelines for AKI suggest that RASi use should be carefully monitored due to its potential to cause or exacerbate kidney dysfunction. While these agents are beneficial in CKD for slowing disease progression, their role in AKI remains controversial. KDIGO recommends considering reintroduction post-recovery based on renal function and potassium levels. The guidelines also emphasize regular monitoring of serum creatinine and potassium to prevent hyperkalemia and worsening renal function [41].

The AHA/ACC guidelines focus on the use of RASi in heart failure and cardiovascular disease, even in patients with concurrent kidney impairment. The 2022 AHA/ACC/HFSA Guideline for the Management of Heart Failure recommends continued use of RASi in patients with stable AKI when benefits outweigh the risks. However, in cases of severe AKI, hypotension, or hyperkalemia, temporary discontinuation is advised. The guidelines also stress the importance of blood pressure control, frequent renal function testing, and potassium monitoring in patients receiving RASi therapy. Alternative therapeutic options, such as SGLT2 inhibitors, are suggested for patients at high risk of AKI progression [42]. Recent clinical and observational studies indicate that SGLT2 inhibitors are kidney-safe and may reduce the risk of recurrent AKI and CKD progression when initiated after recovery from an AKI episode. Cohort studies and trial-derived analyses suggest lower rates of recurrent AKI and slower eGFR decline with post-AKI SGLT2i exposure. However, prospective randomized trials specifically in immediate post-AKI populations remain limited. Clinicians may consider SGLT2i for patients with indications (T2DM, CKD, heart failure) once hemodynamically stable and euvolemic, with close monitoring for volume status and creatinine [43].

Future Directions and Research Opportunities

While current guidelines provide a framework for RASi use in AKI, several gaps in knowledge remain. Future research should aim to identify patient subgroups that may safely benefit from continued RASi therapy during AKI, minimizing risks while preserving cardiovascular benefits. Large-scale RCTs are essential to determine the safety and timing of RASi reintroduction post-AKI, as well as their long-term impact on kidney and heart health. Advancements in precision medicine, including the use of biomarkers for early AKI detection and risk stratification, may help personalize RASi therapy [44].

Alternative or adjunctive therapies, such as SGLT2 inhibitors and ARNIs, require further investigation to assess their renal and cardiovascular effects in patients with AKI. Recent clinical and observational studies suggest that SGLT2 inhibitors are kidney safe and may reduce the risk of recurrent AKI and CKD progression when initiated after recovery from an AKI episode. Cohort studies and trial-derived analyses indicate lower rates of recurrent AKI and slower decline in eGFR with post-AKI SGLT2i exposure. However, prospective randomized trials specifically in immediate post-AKI populations remain limited. Clinicians may consider SGLT2i for patients with indications (type 2 diabetes, CKD, heart failure) once hemodynamically stable and euvolemic, with close monitoring for volume status and creatinine levels [45, 46]. Novel strategies, including nephroprotective agents and machine learning-driven predictive models, could refine treatment decisions and reduce the incidence of AKI-related complications. Ongoing clinical trials and real-world data analyses will further enhance our understanding of balancing the benefits and risks of renin-angiotensin-aldosterone (RASI) therapy in AKI, ultimately improving patient outcomes.

Conclusion

The bidirectional relationship between AKI and cardiovascular disease is driven by shared pathophysiological mechanisms, with RAS playing a central role. RAS inhibitors (RASis) offer cardiovascular benefits beyond blood pressure control, including reduced myocardial infarction, stroke, and mortality. However, concerns remain regarding their impact on renal function, particularly in AKI patients. Clinical evidence on RASi use in AKI is mixed, prompting guideline recommendations for cautious, individualized management with close monitoring. Future research should refine risk stratification, explore alternative therapies, and optimize treatment strategies to balance cardiovascular protection with renal safety.
