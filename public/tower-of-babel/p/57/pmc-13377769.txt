Association between creatinine-to-cystatin C ratio and ALSFRS-R across clinical phenotypes
Authors: Yoshihisa Fujiwara, Akihiro Hashiguchi, Shiori Yamashiro, Hiroshi Seno, Yuichiro Ohya, Daigo Yasutomi, Natsumi Fujisaki, Miwako Kido, Shugo Suwazono, Takashi Tokashiki
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundReliable and accessible biomarkers for amyotrophic lateral sclerosis (ALS) are scarce. Creatinine (Cre) reflects muscle mass, whereas cystatin C (CysC) may reflect neurodegeneration without being directly influenced by muscle mass; however, both have limitations. We aimed to investigate whether the creatinine-to-cystatin C ratio (Cre/CysC) was cross-sectionally associated with functional status in patients with ALS.MethodsWe retrospectively analyzed 30 patients diagnosed with ALS at the National Organization Hospital Okinawa Hospital between 2021 and 2024. Baseline ALS Functional Rating Scale–Revised (ALSFRS-R) scores and serum Cre and CysC levels were recorded. Associations with the ALSFRS-R were assessed using Spearman’s correlation, with subgroup analyses by sex, site of onset, age at diagnosis, body mass index (BMI), and diagnostic delay. Multivariable analyses were performed to examine the independent association between Cre/CysC and ALSFRS-R while accounting for relevant clinical covariates.ResultsCre/CysC showed a stronger cross-sectional correlation with ALSFRS-R (rs=0.648, p = 0.0001) than Cre alone (rs =0.427) or CysC (rs =–0.119). Exploratory subgroup analyses showed generally positive associations in several subgroups, although no statistically significant association was observed in the small bulbar-onset subgroup. In multivariable analysis adjusted for age at onset and diagnostic delay, Cre/CysC remained independently associated with ALSFRS-R (β = 20.1, 95% CI 6.41–33.9, p = 0.006). Given the small sample size and cross-sectional design, these findings should be interpreted as exploratory.ConclusionsCre/CysC showed a stronger cross-sectional association with functional status than either marker alone. Because it is derived from routine laboratory tests, Cre/CysC may represent a simple exploratory measure associated with functional status in ALS. However, the present findings do not establish prognostic utility or fully account for disease stage and biological heterogeneity. Prospective longitudinal studies incorporating disease progression measures and broader clinical and genetic characterization are warranted.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12883-026-04983-6.Keywords: Amyotrophic lateral sclerosis, Creatinine-to-cystatin C ratio, Neurodegeneration, Muscle mass reduction, Retrospective study

Background

Amyotrophic lateral sclerosis (ALS) is characterized by the degeneration of the spinal anterior horn cells and corticospinal tracts. ALS is reported to exhibit various onset and progression rates [1, 2]. Regardless of the clinical phenotype, the revised ALS Functional Rating Scale (ALSFRS-R) is widely used as a clinical assessment tool [3].

However, despite previous investigations into biomarkers such as neurofilament heavy and light chains, uric acid, and ferritin, no biomarker has yet been established for clinical use in predicting ALS progression or prognosis. Among these biomarkers, serum creatinine (Cre), a metabolic product of muscles, has been reported to be a potential prognostic marker for ALS [4, 5], because it reflects muscle atrophy. Lower Cre levels may indicate disease progression and poor prognosis. However, Cre levels exhibit sex-based differences in reference values and are influenced by factors such as renal function, dietary intake, tissue protein catabolism, and hepatic function, because Cre is synthesized in the liver and retained in the muscle.

Serum cystatin C (CysC) has also been proposed as an marker associated with ALS-related outcomes [6, 7]. Several studies have suggested that elevated CysC levels are correlated with poor prognosis, potentially reflecting neurodegeneration and neuronal damage.

Taken together, the present study aimed to examine whether the creatinine-to-cystatin C ratio (Cre/CysC) demonstrates a stronger cross-sectional association with ALSFRS-R compared to Cre or CysC alone.

Methods

Patients

This retrospective cross-sectional study included patients diagnosed with ALS at the National Organization Hospital Okinawa Hospital between January 1, 2021, and October 31, 2024, based on the revised El Escorial criteria. Patients who met the diagnostic criteria for clinically definite ALS, clinically probable ALS, clinically probable laboratory-supported ALS, and clinically possible ALS were enrolled. Patients were excluded if they had comorbid conditions with a clear impact on life expectancy, such as malignant tumors, or if they or their legal representatives declined to participate. No patients had chronic dialysis, acute kidney injury, clinically apparent infection at sampling, or systemic corticosteroid exposure.

Clinical data collection

The baseline clinical data included age at onset and diagnosis, site of symptom onset (bulbar or non-bulbar), diagnostic delay, and body mass index (BMI) at diagnosis. ALSFRS-R at the time of diagnosis, and Cre and CysC levels were measured within one month of diagnosis. Diagnostic delay was defined as the disease duration (in months) between initial symptom onset and confirmed diagnosis and was used as a proxy for disease duration. Disease stage at sampling was not formally assessed. ALSFRS-R scores were assessed by experienced neurologists. Blood samples were obtained as part of routine clinical care. Serum creatinine and cystatin C values were retrieved from the medical records. Measurements were performed in the institutional clinical laboratory using standard routine assays. Samples were analyzed as part of routine care and were not stored for research purposes before measurement. Because of the retrospective design, fasting status and detailed pre-analytical conditions, including exact handling times, were not uniformly available. Genetic testing for SOD1 was performed in a subset of patients. Age at diagnosis was used to describe baseline clinical characteristics at the time of assessment, whereas age at onset was included in regression analyses as a clinically relevant variable reflecting underlying disease biology and its potential association with functional status.

This study was conducted in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki (2024) and current ethical guidelines and was approved by the institutional hospital Ethics Review Board of NHO Okinawa Hospital (Approval No.2024-17). Written informed consent was waived because the study was retrospective, did not involve a clinical trial, and used anonymous data.

Statistical analysis

We first examined the relationships between the ALSFRS-R and Cre, CysC, and Cre/CysC using Spearman’s rank correlation coefficients. Subsequently, for subgroup analyses, we assessed the correlations between the ALSFRS-R and Cre/CysC according to sex, site of disease onset, age at diagnosis, BMI, and diagnostic delay. Age at diagnosis, BMI, and diagnostic delay were divided by the median. Subgroup analyses were considered exploratory, and no adjustment for multiple comparisons was applied given the limited sample size. We performed multivariable linear regression analyses with ALSFRS-R as the dependent variable. The primary independent variable was the Cre/CysC. The primary model (Model 1) was adjusted for age at onset and diagnostic delay. A sensitivity model (Model 2) was adjusted for age at onset, diagnostic delay, and cystatin C-based estimated glomerular filtration rate (eGFRcys). Regression coefficients (β) with 95% confidence intervals (CI) were reported. Adjusted R² was calculated to assess model fit. All statistical computations were two-tailed, and p-values < 0.05 were considered significant. Analyses were conducted using the JMPฏ18.2.2 software (SAS Institute).

As an exploratory supplementary analysis, receiver operating characteristic (ROC) curve analyses were performed to explore the discriminative performance of Cre, CysC, and Cre/CysC for lower ALSFRS-R status, using the median ALSFRS-R score as the cut-off (values below the median were defined as lower ALSFRS-R). The area under the curve (AUC) and 95% confidence intervals were estimated using the DeLong method. These analyses were considered exploratory and descriptive because of the small sample size and cross-sectional design.

Results

In this study, we included 30 patients with ALS who met the revised El Escorial criteria (possible or higher). Among them, 17 (56.7%) were male and 13 (43.3%) were female. The mean age at onset was 62.6 ± 13.1 years, and the mean age at diagnosis was 63.9 ± 13.1 years. The mean BMI was 20.8 ± 3.8. Bulbar onset was in eight (26.7%) patients and non-bulbar onset in 22 (73.3%) patients. The median duration from symptom onset to diagnosis (diagnostic delay) was 17 months (interquartile range (IQR): 12, 26). The median ALSFRS-R score was 34.5 (IQR: 29.8, 39.0). The median Cre and CysC levels were 0.57 mg/dL (IQR: 0.42, 0.71) and 0.90 mg/dL (IQR: 0.76, 1.05), respectively. The median Cre/CysC was 0.59 (IQR: 0.53, 0.80). We analyzed the cross-sectional association between Cre/CysC and functional status. Spearman’s correlation analysis revealed a positive correlation between Cre/CysC and ALSFRS-R (rs = 0.648, p = 0.0001), whereas Cre alone (rs = 0.427, p = 0.0186) and CysC (rs = − 0.119, p = 0.5311) showed weaker or no significant associations (Fig. 1). To evaluate whether Cre/CysC was independently associated with ALSFRS-R, we performed multivariable linear regression analysis adjusted for age at onset and diagnostic delay, with age at onset included as a clinically relevant variable reflecting disease biology. Cre/CysC remained associated with ALSFRS-R (β = 20.1, 95% CI 6.41–33.9, p = 0.006). Neither age at onset nor diagnostic delay was significantly associated with ALSFRS-R in the adjusted model. The adjusted R² of the model was 0.331 (Table 2). The subgroup analysis showed generally positive correlations between Cre/CysC and ALSFRS-R across most patient subgroups (Table 3; Fig. 2).

In exploratory analyses stratified by sex, positive correlations were observed in both females (rs = 0.841, p = 0.0003) and males (rs = 0.563, p = 0.0188), with a numerically higher correlation coefficient in females (Fig. 2a). Similarly, a positive correlation was observed in patients with non-bulbar-onset ALS (rs = 0.705, p = 0.0002), but not in those with bulbar-onset ALS (rs = 0.264, p = 0.5284) (Fig. 2b).

When stratified by age at diagnosis, Cre/CysC correlated significantly with ALSFRS-R in both the younger (< 64 years, rs = 0.751, p = 0.0013) and older (≥ 64 years, rs = 0.521, p = 0.0464) subgroups (Fig. 2c). The correlation was also maintained regardless of BMI (< 20.8, rs = 0.586, p = 0.0216; ≥20.8, rs = 0.713, p = 0.0029) (Fig. 2d) and diagnostic delay (< 17 months, rs = 0.617, p = 0.0110; ≥17 months, rs = 0.565, p = 0.0355) (Fig. 2e).

Exploratory ROC analyses are provided in Additional file 2 for completeness. Because these analyses used a median-based dichotomization of ALSFRS-R in a small cross-sectional cohort, they were interpreted descriptively and were not used to support diagnostic or prognostic conclusions.

Discussion and conclusion

To our knowledge, this study appears to be among the first to directly compare the cross-sectional association between Cre/CysC and ALSFRS-R with that of Cre and CysC alone. Cre/CysC showed a numerically stronger association with functional status among the examined markers (Cre, CysC, and Cre/CysC), and similar trends were observed across most subgroups; however, these analyses were exploratory and should be interpreted with caution. The relationship between Cre/CysC and ALSFRS-R remained significant after adjustment for clinically relevant variables, including age at onset and diagnostic delay (disease duration) in the primary model. In supplementary analyses additionally adjusting for eGFRcys, the association remained significant.

These findings suggest that the observed association was not fully explained by age at onset, diagnostic delay, or renal filtration function in the adjusted and sensitivity analyses. Nevertheless, given the cross-sectional nature of this study, these findings should be interpreted as associations at a single time point and cannot establish temporal or causal relationships with disease severity or progression.

The absence of a significant correlation in bulbar-onset ALS warrants careful interpretation. Patients with bulbar-onset disease often exhibit less prominent systemic muscle atrophy in early stages and may differ in disease characteristics [8], which could reduce the sensitivity of muscle-related markers such as Cre. Patients with bulbar-onset disease in our cohort were older on average than the overall study population. Given that older age at onset has been associated with more rapid functional decline [9], age-related disease dynamics may have attenuated the cross-sectional association between Cre/CysC and ALSFRS-R in this subgroup, although this interpretation should be confirmed in larger cohorts. This interpretation is biologically plausible, as evidence from clinical fields beyond ALS has demonstrated that Cre/CysC has been associated with skeletal muscle mass and clinical outcomes [10, 11].

Consistent with previous reports [4, 5], our study showed that lower Cre levels are associated with lower in ALSFRS-R scores. Furthermore, CysC has been reported as a marker associated with neurodegeneration, with elevated levels indicating poor prognosis [6, 7]. Notably, our study extended these previous findings by evaluating Cre/CysC, which showed a numerically stronger correlation with ALSFRS-R than did either Cre or CysC alone. Previous studies have suggested that Cre is related to muscle atrophy and that CysC may be associated with neurodegenerative processes or ALS outcomes [4–7]. In this context, Cre/CysC may serve as an exploratory index associated with functional status at a given time point, although the biological contribution of each component remains uncertain. Recent studies have recognized Cre/CysC as a surrogate marker of skeletal muscle mass, sometimes referred to as a “sarcopenia index,” and have reported its association with frailty and adverse outcomes across diverse clinical populations [12]. These observations provide additional biological plausibility for the relevance of Cre/CysC in ALS.

A notable strength of this study is the use of two inexpensive, widely available, and routinely measured laboratory parameters, making Cre/CysC a readily available exploratory index. As both Cre and CysC are part of routine laboratory testing, Cre/CysC can be calculated without additional cost or specialized assays. Neurofilament light chain (NfL) has emerged as a promising biomarker of ALS [13–15], reflecting axonal injury and disease progression. However, its clinical implementation remains limited by the need for specialized and costly immunoassays. Among other biomarkers, uric acid is easily measurable. Although baseline uric acid levels have been reported to be associated with longitudinal changes in ALSFRS-R [16, 17], no significant correlation has been observed between uric acid levels and cross-sectional ALSFRS-R scores. Thus, these findings suggest that Cre/CysC, based on routine laboratory testing, may represent a simple and accessible measure in research settings. However, its clinical utility should be interpreted cautiously, as the present findings are based on cross-sectional associations and do not directly inform its ability to predict disease progression or prognosis. Therefore, Cre/CysC should currently be considered an exploratory marker of functional status rather than a validated biomarker for clinical decision-making.

Despite these promising findings, this study has several limitations. First, the retrospective cross-sectional design precludes inference regarding disease progression, temporal changes, or prognostic utility, and may have introduced unmeasured confounding. Second, this was a single-center study with a relatively small sample size, which may limit generalizability and reduce the precision of the estimated effect sizes. In addition, formal disease staging was not incorporated at the time of sampling, which may affect the interpretation of biomarker levels across different disease phases. Third, although we adjusted for key clinical variables and performed sensitivity analyses, residual confounding remains possible, particularly with respect to skeletal muscle mass, nutritional status, and systemic conditions that may influence Cre/CysC. Fourth, genetic data were limited, as only a subset of patients underwent SOD1 testing, and comprehensive genetic characterization was not available. Fifth, because laboratory data were obtained retrospectively from routine clinical practice, detailed pre-analytical factors, including fasting status and sample handling conditions, could not be uniformly assessed. Finally, subgroup findings, particularly in patients with bulbar-onset disease, should be interpreted with caution and considered exploratory given the limited sample size. Future prospective multicenter studies with larger cohorts and longitudinal follow-up are warranted to validate these findings and to determine whether dynamic changes in Cre/CysC are associated with disease progression and survival.

In this study, we investigated the association between the Cre/CysC ratio and functional status in ALS, as assessed using the ALSFRS-R. Strictly, our findings reflect cross-sectional functional status rather than disease progression, as the rate of ALSFRS-R decline has been more closely associated with prognosis in ALS [18]. Longitudinal changes in Cre/CysC may also have potential prognostic relevance and warrant further investigation. As no single biomarker fully reflects the heterogeneity of ALS pathophysiology, complementary approaches are likely needed. Although neurofilament light chain (NfL) has been proposed as a marker of upper motor neuron involvement [19, 20], Cre/CysC incorporates Cre, which is related to muscle atrophy, and may therefore capture aspects of peripheral muscle wasting associated with ALS. These complementary features suggest that combined evaluation of NfL and Cre/CysC may be informative; however, this remains speculative and requires validation in longitudinal and genetically characterized cohorts. Overall, Cre/CysC may serve as an exploratory measure to complement clinical assessment and to support exploratory stratification in ALS clinical research.
