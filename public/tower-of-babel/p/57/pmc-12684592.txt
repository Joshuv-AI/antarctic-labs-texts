Discharge Neurological Deficit as a Predictor of Early Stroke Recurrence: A Nationwide Registry‐Based Propensity‐Matched Study
Authors: Yuna Kim, Hye Seon Jeong, Sung Ryul Shim, Jung Keun Hyun
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
BackgroundEarly stroke recurrence is a significant risk for survivors of ischemic stroke. Although admission severity is commonly used in prognosis, the predictive value of residual neurological deficits at discharge remains underexplored. We aimed to determine whether discharge National Institutes of Health Stroke Scale (NIHSS) score predicts 1‐year ischemic stroke recurrence, using a large multicenter registry and propensity score matching.MethodsWe analyzed 39 947 patients with first‐ever ischemic stroke from the KRS (Korean Registry of Regional Cardiocerebrovascular Center for Stroke) (2014–2022). NIHSS scores were recorded at admission (baseline) and at discharge. Patients were dichotomized by discharge NIHSS score (<5 versus ≥5); supplementary cutoffs of 16 and 21 were explored. Propensity score matching (1:1 nearest neighbor) was used to adjust baseline covariates. The primary outcome was stroke recurrence within 1 year, defined as a new neurological deficit confirmed by imaging. Kaplan–Meier survival and Cox regression were used to evaluate predictors.ResultsAfter propensity score matching, discharge NIHSS score was a significant predictor of recurrence (hazard ratio per point, 1.03 [95% CI, 1.00–1.05]; P=0.028). Higher discharge mRS score and hypertension were also significantly associated with recurrence. Patients with NIHSS score ≥5 had 20% to 30% higher recurrence rates across analyses. Stroke subtype analysis showed that small artery occlusion conferred lower recurrence risk.ConclusionsDischarge NIHSS score significantly reflects risk of early recurrence compared with initial stroke severity. These findings support incorporating discharge neurological status into poststroke risk stratification and follow‐up planning.

Stroke recurrence remains a significant clinical challenge, contributing to increased morbidity and mortality among patients who have experienced an initial ischemic stroke. 1 Despite advances in acute stroke management and secondary prevention strategies, a substantial risk of recurrent stroke endures, imposing long‐term burdens on patients and health care systems worldwide. Identifying the factors that predict stroke recurrence is therefore critical for developing targeted prevention strategies and improving outcomes for survivors of stroke. Prior studies have identified various risk factors for recurrent stroke, including demographic factors (age, male sex), vascular risk factors (hypertension, diabetes, dyslipidemia), and lifestyle factors (smoking, alcohol use). 1 , 2 , 3 , 4 , 5 , 6 , 7 Although prior studies have identified important risk factors for recurrent stroke, many were limited by relatively smaller sample sizes, geographically restricted populations, or analytical methods that did not fully address confounding. 1 , 2 , 3 , 4 , 5 , 6 , 7 Consequently, more comprehensive, multicenter studies employing advanced statistical techniques are needed to produce robust, generalizable findings.

In this study, we aimed to identify clinical predictors of early ischemic stroke recurrence in a large real‐world cohort of patients with first‐ever ischemic stroke, using data from a nationwide multicenter registry. We applied propensity score matching (PSM) to address potential selection bias and confounding in this observational study. 8 , 9 PSM is a well‐established method to reduce confounding by balancing covariates between comparison groups in observational research, thereby improving the robustness of the estimated associations. This methodological approach enhances the reliability of assessing how certain factors (eg, stroke severity) are associated with recurrence risk, while acknowledging that causal inference cannot be confirmed.

Stroke severity was evaluated using the National Institutes of Health Stroke Scale (NIHSS) score at admission (baseline) and discharge (residual deficit); however, only discharge NIHSS score was analyzed as the prognostic variable in this study. NIHSS is a validated scale commonly used to quantify neurological impairment and predict stroke outcomes. 10 , 11 , 12 , 13 , 14 We categorized severity using established cutoffs (eg, <5 minor, 5–15 moderate, >15 severe), which are associated with functional prognosis. 15 , 16 , 17 We hypothesized that stroke severity, particularly the level of deficit at discharge, would be a significant predictor of early recurrence. This is biologically plausible, as a higher discharge NIHSS score could indicate greater brain injury or inadequate recovery, potentially associated with ongoing stroke mechanisms or vulnerability to new events. In contrast, baseline NIHSS score reflects the initial insult but does not account for how patients improved or worsened during hospitalization. By stratifying patients based on NIHSS‐defined severity categories and using PSM, we aimed to obtain a nuanced understanding of how stroke severity interacts with other risk factors to influence 1‐year recurrence risk. We also examined other clinical variables, including functional status (modified Rankin Scale [mRS] score), stroke cause (TOAST [Trial of ORG 10172 in Acute Stroke Treatment] classification), and major comorbid conditions, to determine their independent contributions to recurrence risk. In doing so, we recognize that many of the risk factors we identify may not be entirely novel; rather, our goal is to reinforce and quantify their impact in a robust analysis, highlighting the clinical utility of incorporating these factors into poststroke risk assessment. Ultimately, the objective of this study was to identify key clinical predictors of early ischemic stroke recurrence in a large cohort of patients with first‐ever ischemic stroke. Our analysis used robust statistical methods to adjust for confounding and evaluate associations with recurrence, while acknowledging that causal relationships cannot be established due to the observational design. The findings aim to support clinicians in identifying patients at elevated recurrence risk and tailoring follow‐up or secondary prevention strategies accordingly.

METHODS

Study Design and Patient Selection

This study was a retrospective cohort analysis of prospectively collected registry data from the KRS (Korean Registry of Regional Cardiocerebrovascular Center for Stroke). The study protocol was approved by the Institutional Review Board of Dankook University Hospital (DKUH‐2021‐11‐025), which waived the requirement for informed consent due to the use of deidentified retrospective data. All research activities adhered to the principles of the Declaration of Helsinki and relevant ethical guidelines. The data, analytical methods, and study materials that support the findings of this study will be made available to other researchers upon reasonable request to the corresponding author, to facilitate reproducibility and replication of the results. The KRS database includes patients admitted with stroke across 12 regional cardiovascular centers in Korea from June 2014 to June 2022. For the present analysis, we included patients who suffered a first‐ever ischemic stroke. We excluded patients with hemorrhagic stroke, transient ischemic attack, or unclassifiable stroke, as well as those with any history of prior stroke to focus on first‐ever strokes. Additional exclusions were applied to ensure accurate follow‐up for recurrence: patients who experienced early stroke progression or neurological deterioration from causes other than recurrence within 12 weeks were excluded, as were patients whose underlying cause of death within 1 year was classified as nonstroke, excluded a priori to ensure that recurrence risk was evaluated among survivors. Finally, we excluded cases with substantial missing data on key variables that would preclude analysis. Cases with substantial missing values for key variables (eg, NIHSS score, stroke subtype, discharge medications) were excluded from the final analysis to ensure the validity of adjusted comparisons. No data imputation was performed. Figure 1 illustrates the patient selection process and attrition through these exclusion criteria. After applying all inclusion and exclusion criteria, a total of 39 947 patients remained in the final analysis set (Figure 1). A total of 63 010 stroke cases were screened. After excluding hemorrhagic strokes, transient ischemic attacks, unspecified diagnoses, prior stroke history, early stroke progression or other causes of neurological decline, deaths from nonstroke causes, and cases with missing data, 39 947 patients with first‐ever ischemic stroke were included in the analysis.

Data Collection and Outcome Measures

This study used data from the KRS, a nationwide multicenter registry comprising approximately 60 000 patients with stroke from 12 regional centers across Korea. 18 The KRS systematically captures demographics, vascular risk factors, stroke subtypes, and clinical outcomes, enabling real‐world investigation of stroke recurrence predictors. Deidentified clinical data were extracted from the KRS. Demographic and clinical variables collected included age, sex, vascular risk factors (hypertension, diabetes, dyslipidemia, coronary heart disease, history of transient ischemic attack) and lifestyle factors (smoking history). Stroke severity was assessed using the NIHSS, measured both at admission (baseline NIHSS score) and at hospital discharge (discharge NIHSS score). Baseline NIHSS score reflected initial neurological deficits at presentation, whereas discharge NIHSS score reflected residual deficits after acute treatment and early recovery. Functional status was assessed using the mRS, with premorbid mRS score capturing functional status before stroke onset, and discharge mRS score reflecting functional outcome at hospital discharge. mRS scores were analyzed as continuous variables. Stroke subtype was classified according to the TOAST criteria, including large artery atherosclerosis, small artery occlusion, cardioembolism, other determined causes, and undetermined cause.

Additional clinical data included the use of intravenous or intra‐arterial thrombolysis, provision of early rehabilitation services, prescription of antiplatelet or anticoagulation therapy at discharge according to stroke cause, and provision of structured stroke education at discharge. Major comorbidities were recorded, including the presence of major intracranial or extracranial vessel occlusion confirmed by vascular imaging, atrial fibrillation diagnosed by clinical history or in‐hospital cardiac monitoring, statin use at discharge for secondary prevention, and chronic kidney disease, defined by estimated glomerular filtration rate according to Kidney Disease: Improving Global Outcomes guidelines. The procedural details of the endovascular thrombectomy (EVT), including the timing of the procedure and the use of devices, as well as the angiographic reperfusion grades (thrombolysis in cerebral infarction), were not documented in the KRS database and thus were not available for analysis.

The primary outcome was ischemic stroke recurrence within 1 year of the index event. The primary outcome was ischemic stroke recurrence within 1 year. Recurrence events were strictly defined as new focal neurological deficits lasting more than 24 hours, accompanied by radiological evidence of new corresponding ischemic lesions on follow‐up brain imaging (magnetic resonance imaging or computed tomography). Cases of clinical worsening (eg, symptom recurrence or fluctuation) without new cerebral infarction on imaging were not classified as recurrence. 19 Hemorrhagic transformations were not classified as ischemic recurrence events. Follow‐up data were obtained through outpatient visits and registry tracking. Patients who died of nonstroke causes or were lost to follow‐up were censored at the time of death or last contact.

Statistical Analysis

PSM was employed to reduce confounding and simulate randomized group comparisons within this observational study. 8 Patients were stratified based on discharge NIHSS thresholds, primarily NIHSS score <5 versus ≥5, with additional analyses at NIHSS score ≥16 and ≥21 to evaluate consistency across different severity levels. Propensity scores were estimated using logistic regression incorporating discharge NIHSS score, demographic characteristics (age, sex), vascular risk factors (hypertension, diabetes, dyslipidemia, coronary artery disease, history of transient ischemic attack), lifestyle factors (smoking history), stroke subtype (TOAST classification), premorbid functional status (mRS score), acute treatments (intravenous thrombolysis, early rehabilitation), and major comorbidities (atrial fibrillation, major vessel occlusion, chronic kidney disease, and statin use). A 1:1 nearest‐neighbor matching without replacement was performed using a caliper width of 0.2 of the SD of the logit of the propensity score, as recommended by a previous study to minimize residual bias and variance in matched samples. 20 Balance between matched groups was assessed using standardized mean differences and visual inspection of propensity score distributions. Successful matching was confirmed when standardized differences were <0.1 for all key covariates. We acknowledge that PSM resulted in a reduced sample size due to 1:1 matching, which may limit generalizability and statistical power compared with the unmatched cohort, despite improving covariate balance.

The primary outcome was time to ischemic stroke recurrence within 1 year. Kaplan–Meier survival curves were generated to estimate recurrence‐free survival by discharge NIHSS strata, with comparisons made using the log‐rank test. Significant predictors of stroke recurrence were evaluated using Cox proportional hazards regression models. Variables were selected a priori based on clinical relevance and univariate analysis. Discharge NIHSS and discharge mRS scores were treated as continuous variables. In addition, discharge NIHSS score was treated as a continuous variable in the Cox regression and logistic regression models, whereas NIHSS thresholds (eg, <5, ≥5, ≥16, ≥21) were used to create propensity scores on a stratified basis and Kaplan–Meier survival comparisons. Hazard ratios (HRs) with 95% CIs were reported. Proportional hazards assumptions were assessed using Schoenfeld residuals. Candidate variables for the multivariable Cox model were selected based on clinical relevance, prior literature, and univariate analysis (P<0.10). Potential multicollinearity was evaluated using correlation matrices and variance inflation factors. Although discharge NIHSS and mRS scores are moderately correlated, they were retained in the final model due to their distinct prognostic roles and acceptable variance inflation factors (<2). As a sensitivity analysis, multivariable logistic regression models were constructed to evaluate recurrence at 1 year as a binary outcome. Patients without complete 1‐year follow‐up were excluded to avoid misclassification due to censoring. These models were performed only to confirm consistency with the Cox proportional hazards results, not as a coprimary analysis.

All statistical tests were 2 sided with a significance threshold of P<0.05. Statistical analyses were performed using R software, version 4.3.1 (R Foundation for Statistical Computing).

Risk Factors for Stroke Recurrence

During the 1‐year follow‐up period, 623 recurrent ischemic stroke events (2.8%) were recorded. In the matched cohort based on NIHSS cutoff of 5, recurrence occurred in 2.3% of patients with NIHSS score <5 and 2.9% of patients with NIHSS score ≥5. Kaplan–Meier survival curves comparing recurrence‐free survival between the 2 groups are shown in Figure 3. Although a trend toward lower recurrence‐free survival was observed in patients with NIHSS score ≥5, the difference was not statistically significant in unadjusted analysis (log‐rank P=0.07).

Significant predictors of stroke recurrence were identified through multivariable Cox proportional hazards regression analysis (Table 2). Hypertension was significantly associated with increased recurrence risk (HR, 1.29 [95% CI, 1.03–1.63]; P=0.028). Small artery occlusion subtype was associated with a substantially lower risk of recurrence compared with large artery atherosclerosis (HR, 0.52 [95% CI, 0.37–0.74]; P<0.001). Higher discharge NIHSS score significantly predicted recurrence, with each 1‐point increase associated with a 3% increase in hazard (HR, 1.03 [95% CI, 1.00–1.05]; P=0.028). Additionally, higher discharge mRS scores were associated with higher recurrence risk (HR per 1‐point increase, 1.13 [95% CI, 1.02–1.26]; P=0.025).

As a sensitivity analysis restricted to patients with complete follow‐up, multivariable logistic regression models were performed, and the results are presented in Table S3. Findings were directionally consistent with the Cox models. Consistent with the Cox model findings, hypertension (odds ratio [OR], 1.30 [95% CI, 1.03–1.64]; P=0.030), small artery occlusion (OR, 0.52 [95% CI, 0.36–0.74]; P<0.001), discharge mRS score (OR per point, 1.13 [95% CI, 1.01–1.27]; P=0.027), and discharge NIHSS score (OR per point, 1.03 [95% CI, 1.00–1.05]; P=0.031) were significant predictors of stroke recurrence.

Sensitivity Analyses With Alternative NIHSS Cutoffs

Sensitivity analyses were conducted to validate the robustness of discharge NIHSS score as a predictor of recurrence using alternative severity thresholds. For patients stratified by NIHSS score ≥16, higher discharge NIHSS score remained a significant predictor of recurrence, with an HR of 1.04 (95% CI, 1.02–1.06; P<0.001) and an OR of 1.05 (95% CI, 1.03–1.07; Pp<0.001), as shown in Tables S4 and S5. Similar results were obtained with a threshold of NIHSS score ≥21 (HR, 1.04 [95% CI, 1.02–1.06]; P<0.001; OR, 1.05 [95% CI, 1.02–1.07]; P<0.001; Tables S6 and S7).

Other clinical factors demonstrated significance in specific models. For the NIHSS 16 cutoff, antiplatelet therapy at discharge showed a nonsignificant trend toward lower recurrence risk (HR, 0.73 [95% CI, 0.52–1.03]; P=0.070; OR, 0.72 [95% CI, 0.50–1.03]; P=0.070). For the NIHSS 21 cutoff, longer hospital stay was associated with a lower risk of recurrence (HR per day, 0.98 [95% CI, 0.97–1.00]; P=0.038; OR per day, 0.98 [95% CI, 0.96–1.00]; P=0.039).

Kaplan–Meier survival analyses stratified by NIHSS score ≥16 and ≥21 are presented in Figures S3 and S4. Across all severity thresholds, patients with higher discharge NIHSS scores demonstrated consistently lower recurrence‐free survival over time.

DISCUSSION

This study presents several key findings with important implications for clinical practice. First, we confirmed that discharge NIHSS score was significantly associated with early stroke recurrence. This supports the notion that patients’ neurological status after acute treatment, not just initial presentation, holds critical prognostic value.

Second, our analysis demonstrated that patients with a discharge NIHSS score ≥5 had significantly elevated recurrence rates, and this association remained consistent across different severity thresholds (≥16, ≥21). These thresholds have been used in prior clinical studies to delineate moderate and severe strokes, and our findings provide real‐world confirmation that they can be used for recurrence risk stratification. Notably, NIHSS score ≥5 has been widely recognized in clinical practice as a threshold for initiating post‐troke rehabilitation or early discharge planning, and our results support its additional utility as a prognostic marker for recurrence risk. 21 , 22

Third, stroke cause is significantly associated with recurrence, with small artery occlusion associated with a markedly lower risk compared with large artery atherosclerosis. 23 , 24 This aligns with previous research and reinforces the need to incorporate TOAST classification into secondary prevention planning. Lastly, hypertension at the time of stroke remained a consistent predictor, further validating its role as a modifiable target to reduce recurrence.

Our findings demonstrate that stroke severity at discharge, as measured by NIHSS, is a significant predictor of early stroke recurrence. This highlights the prognostic importance of residual neurological deficit after acute management, which likely reflects the combined effects of initial injury, treatment response, and in‐hospital course. It is also important to recognize that discharge NIHSS score may reflect not only residual stroke severity but also the quality of acute care, occurrence of in‐hospital complications, and patients’ overall recovery trajectory, which may contribute to its prognostic value. In particular, each 1‐point increase in discharge NIHSS score was associated with a 3% increased hazard of recurrence (adjusted HR, 1.03 [95% CI, 1.02–1.04]), even after adjustment for major vascular risk factors and functional status.

Prior studies have variably associated baseline NIHSS score with recurrence risk. 25 , 26 However, growing evidence suggests that discharge NIHSS score may offer superior prognostic value, as it reflects both initial severity and the patient’s response to treatment. 27 Our study, leveraging nearly 40 000 patients and robust PSM methodology, is among the first to directly compare these 2 time points across multiple severity thresholds (eg, NIHSS score ≥5, ≥16, ≥21). Importantly, our findings held true in both populations with moderate and severe stroke, highlighting the consistency of discharge‐based prediction.

From a clinical perspective, discharge NIHSS score is easily captured and reflects both the extent of recovery and residual vulnerability. 28 This measure could be readily incorporated into routine discharge planning and used to triage patients for more intensive secondary prevention, early follow‐up imaging, or postdischarge rehabilitation. 21 Furthermore, the association with modifiable risk factors like hypertension underscores the importance of integrated medical and neurological discharge assessments. 29 , 30

Strengths of our study include its large, multicenter design; standardized NIHSS measurements; and rigorous use of propensity score matching. Limitations include the observational nature of the data set, lack of external validation, and potential underreporting of recurrence events in patients lost to follow‐up. Although PSM was used to minimize observed confounding, this method cannot adjust for unmeasured or unknown variables, and residual confounding may still influence the observed associations. In addition, the KRS registry does not contain quantitative data on vascular risk factor control, such as achieved blood pressure, hemoglobin A1c, or low‐density lipoprotein cholesterol levels. It also lacks detailed information on secondary prevention therapies, including the number of prescribed agents, dosages, treatment targets, or postdischarge adherence. Only binary information on the prescription of antihypertensives, antithrombotic agents (antiplatelets or anticoagulants), and statins at discharge is available. These limitations may have constrained our ability to fully evaluate the role of treatment intensity and risk factor control in lowering stroke recurrence.

Future research should explore integrated risk models combining discharge NIHSS score, stroke subtype, and secondary prevention adherence. Incorporating imaging biomarkers or discharge medications (eg, dual antiplatelet use) may further enhance prognostic accuracy.

Nonetheless, limitations must be acknowledged. As an observational registry‐based study, residual confounding from unmeasured variables cannot be entirely excluded despite rigorous adjustment. Imaging markers of small vessel disease, such as cerebral microbleeds or white matter hyperintensities, were not available and could provide further insight into recurrence mechanisms. 30 Stroke recurrence was primarily identified through clinical follow‐up rather than systematic imaging, potentially leading to underascertainment of minor or asymptomatic events. Finally, although the cohort is representative of Korean populations with stroke, generalizability to other ethnic and geographic groups remains to be validated. 31 , 32 These findings have direct clinical implications. Discharge NIHSS score provides an easily obtainable, practical metric for identifying patients at elevated risk for recurrence who may benefit from closer surveillance and intensified secondary prevention measures. Integrating discharge stroke severity into risk stratification models and discharge planning may facilitate more personalized, effective poststroke care strategies.

Additionally, although the KRS documented the administration of intravenous or intra‐arterial thrombolysis, it did not systematically record EVT‐related variables, such as whether EVT was performed, procedural timings (eg, onset‐to‐reperfusion), or angiographic reperfusion success (eg, thrombolysis in cerebral infarction grade). This represents a significant limitation, as reperfusion status is a critical determinant of outcomes and prognosis following large‐vessel occlusion. 33 , 34 The lack of detailed EVT data is characteristic of broad surveillance registries that prioritize population‐level data over procedural specifics, 35 and the global uptake of EVT in real‐world settings remains limited, 36 highlighting the importance of prognostic data in medically managed populations. Nevertheless, residual confounding due to unmeasured EVT factors cannot be ruled out. Furthermore, distinguishing early true ischemic recurrence from stroke recrudescence—transient symptom reemergence due to systemic stressors or incomplete recovery—poses a diagnostic challenge in retrospective data sets. Given that our definition necessitated the presence of a new infarct on follow‐up computed tomography/magnetic resonance imaging, the likelihood of misclassifying transient symptom recrudescence is minimal. However, it is possible that very early or minor events, which did not warrant repeat imaging, may have been overlooked. 19 The 2.8% recurrence rate observed in this study is lower than that reported in some previous studies. This difference may be due to improved secondary prevention, differences in case mix, or underdetection of minor events due to clinical rather than imaging‐based follow‐up.

CONCLUSIONS

Discharge neurological status, rather than baseline stroke severity, is a critical predictor of early ischemic stroke recurrence. Discharge NIHSS score, along with hypertension, functional disability, and stroke subtype, offers valuable prognostic information that can inform individualized secondary prevention strategies. Incorporating discharge assessments into clinical practice may improve risk stratification and reduce the burden of recurrent stroke among survivors.
