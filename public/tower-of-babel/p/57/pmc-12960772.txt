Inhibitory Effect of Curcuma xanthorrhiza Extract Against SARS‐CoV‐2 D614G Virus‐Like Particle (VLP): Implications for COVID‐19 Therapy Development
Authors: Miftahul Mushlih, Marlita Bemova, Hagar Ali Marzouk, Kavana Hafil Kusuma, Ilmiana Nurur Rohmah, Nur Fitriana, Alex Prima, Yuyun Ika Christina, Dinia Rizqi Dwijayanti, Dewi Mustikaningtyas, Nashi Widodo, Muhammad Sasmito Djati, Sri Widyarti, Muhaimin Rifa’i, Morteza Tayebi
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Abstract Curcuma xanthorrhiza, a commonly used Indonesian medicinal plant, has been widely utilized in traditional remedies, yet its antiviral potential against SARS‐CoV‐2 remains poorly defined. The current study was aimed at investigating the effect of C. xanthorrhiza ethanol extract on inhibiting viral entry through in silico and in vitro studies. The active compounds of C. xanthorrhiza were characterized using liquid chromatography–high‐resolution mass spectrometry (LC‐HRMS). The identified compounds were screened according to the Lipinski, Ghose, Veber, and Egan rules, and their antiviral activity was predicted using the PASS server. Molecular docking and dynamic simulations were employed to determine binding affinity and stability between the selected compounds and the SARS‐CoV‐2 Spike D614G protein. The virus‐like particle (VLP) of SARS‐CoV‐2 G614D was produced in HEK‐293T cells via cotransfection of plasmids encoding spike‐EGFP, membrane, and envelope proteins, and its morphology was verified by transmission electron microscopy (TEM). The IC50 value of C. xanthorrhiza extract on 16HBE14o‐ cells was analyzed using the WST‐1 method. 16HBE14o‐ cells were exposed to VLP and treated with C. xanthorrhiza at 2.5, 5, 10, 40, and 125 μg/mL for 24 and 48 h. The EGFP intensity of VLP‐internalized cells was assessed through fluorescent microscopy. Curcumin, curcumin II, piperine, (+)‐ar‐turmerone, and peruvinine showed the strongest binding affinities (−7.5 to −6.2 kcal/mol), while curcumin, curcumin II, and (+)‐ar‐turmerone exhibited the most stable binding interactions. TEM confirmed successful VLP formation with a mean diameter of 52.94 ± 27.32 nm, and the IC50 of the extract was 125 μg/mL. Furthermore, C. xanthorrhiza extract at 2.5 μg/mL significantly reduced VLP internalization after 24 h. These findings highlight C. xanthorrhiza as a promising natural candidate for SARS‐CoV‐2 inhibition, supported by consistent computational and experimental evidence.Keywords: Curcuma xanthorrhiza, RBD G614D, SARS-CoV-2, VLP

1. Introduction

An unusual outbreak of acute pneumonia was first detected by the end of December 2019. This illness was later confirmed to be caused by a novel coronavirus, officially named Severe Acute Respiratory Syndrome Coronavirus‐2 (SARS‐CoV‐2) [1]. The incident gained worldwide attention, and the disease was subsequently named Coronavirus Disease 2019 (COVID‐19). COVID‐19 soon disseminated worldwide, developing into a major global pandemic [2]. Each country has experienced fluctuating cases of COVID‐19 over time. The transmissibility of SARS‐CoV‐2 is closely related to its adaptive mutations [3].

SARS‐CoV‐2 is categorized as a member of the beta coronavirus group, belonging to the same cluster as MERS and SARS‐CoV, detected in 2003 and 2014, respectively. Based on its structure, SARS‐CoV‐2 is single‐stranded RNA with a positive sense and a crown‐like outer shell [4]. Individuals infected with beta coronavirus typically do not show symptoms or may only exhibit mild flu‐like symptoms. In contrast, COVID‐19 presents with a broad spectrum of symptoms, ranging from mild or asymptomatic cases to severe respiratory illness and death [5].

Among the mutations that have emerged, one of the most dominant and significant changes is the D614G mutation in the spike (S) protein of SARS‐CoV‐2 [3, 6]. This mutation leads to enhanced viral infectivity and transmissibility. Previous studies revealed that the D614G variant stabilizes the prefusion conformation of the spike protein, increasing its binding affinity to the angiotensin‐converting enzyme 2 (ACE2) receptor, which promotes viral entry into host cells [7]. Consequently, the D614G variant rapidly became the dominant strain globally due to its higher replication efficiency and competitive advantage over the original Wuhan strain [8].

In the effort to mitigate COVID‐19, herbal‐based therapies have gained attention as potential affordable alternatives [9–11]. Communities have used herbal medicines for generations to treat several diseases. Curcuma xanthorrhiza is one of the native Indonesian plants widely used as traditional medicine by communities and industries in Southeast Asian countries, especially Indonesia, Malaysia, Thailand, the Philippines, and Vietnam [12–14]. C. xanthorrhiza is usually boiled directly or mixed with several other spices, which is known as jamu [15, 16]. The antiviral potential of herbal remedies is linked to their bioactive components. Xanthorrhizol, curcumin, curcumin II, demethoxycurcumin, and alpha‐terpineol are some compounds considered as potential treatments for COVID‐19 [17, 18]. Despite the widespread use of C. xanthorrhiza, scientific evidence regarding its effectiveness in preventing and treating SARS‐CoV‐2 is still limited. Therefore, this study was aimed at exploring the inhibitory potential of C. xanthorrhiza ethanol extract against SARS‐CoV‐2 entry through an integrated in silico and in vitro analysis. The ability of C. xanthorrhiza bioactive compounds to inhibit the receptor‐binding domain (RBD) of SARS‐CoV‐2 D614G virus‐like particle (VLP) were investigated by in silico studies and then validated in a cell culture environment using constructed SARS‐CoV‐2 VLP.

2. Materials and Methods

2.1. In Silico Studies

2.1.1. Extraction and Identification of Active Compounds From C. xanthorrhiza

The dried rhizome of C. xanthorrhiza was obtained from the UPT. Balai Materia Medica, Batu, East Java, with Voucher Species Number 210509.TWK.L.R 004. Extraction was performed using the maceration method with ethanol, as described by Fitriana et al. [19]. The powdered C. xanthorrhiza rhizome was extracted using 96% ethanol (1:2, w/v) at room temperature for 3 days, followed by filtration with Whatman No. 41 filter paper. The filtrate was evaporated with a rotary evaporator (Buchi R‐114, PT. BUCHI Indonesia) at 55°C. The obtained extract was stored at 4°C in the refrigerator.

2.1.2. Liquid Chromatography–High‐Resolution Mass Spectrometry (LC‐HRMS) Analysis

The obtained extract was diluted with 0.1% ethanol to a final volume of 1500 μL, then homogenized, centrifuged at 6000 rpm for 2 min, and filtered using a 0.22‐μm syringe filter. The solution was processed via an autosampler (Thermo Fisher Scientific Inc., United States), and 100 μL was injected into a LC‐HRMS system (Thermo Scientific Dionex Ultimate 3000 RSLCnano with a micro flow meter). LC‐HRMS analysis was conducted at 30°C using a Hypersil GOLD aQ C18 column (50 × 1 mm, 1.9‐μm particle size). The mobile phase consisted of 0.1% formic acid in water (Solvent A) and 0.1% formic acid in acetonitrile (Solvent B). The flow rate was 40 mL/min with a 30‐min run time. Detection was performed in positive ion mode using a Q Exactive Orbitrap mass spectrometer (Thermo Fisher Scientific Inc.) [19]. The compounds were selected based on Z‐Cloud similarity (> 80%), relative abundance, and availability in PubChem (https://pubchem.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/).

2.1.3. The Prediction of Physicochemical Properties, Druglikeness, and Biological Activity From C. xanthorrhiza Extract

The structure of the SARS‐CoV‐2 spike model was obtained from RCSB PDB (ID = 6ZB5). The target position′s sequence (D614G mutation) was aligned, edited, and then modeled using the Swiss Model (https://swissmodel.expasy.org/interactive) [20]. Validation procedures involved calculating the root mean square deviation (RMSD) using PyMOL. The modeling results were validated using ProQ scores (https://www.sbc.su.se) and the Ramachandran plot. Furthermore, the RBD was selected and realigned with the RBD in 6ZB5.

Potential drug compounds were analyzed using SwissADME (https://www.swissadme.ch/) with the Lipinski, Ghose, Veber, and Egan parameters. Lipinski violation rule < 2 is still allowed [21]. The biological activity of the generated compounds is analyzed using the PASS server (http://www.pharmexpert.ru/passonline). The Pa value serves as a parameter in assessing biological activity.

2.1.4. Molecular Docking Analysis

The modeling proteins were prepared using Discovery Studio Version 16 (Dassault Systèmes BIOVIA, 2015) to remove ligands and water molecules. Before use, Kollman charges, hydrogen bond (H‐bond) optimization, and Gasteiger charges were added using AutoDock MGL tools. Ligands were prepared by minimizing energy and converting them to .pdbqt (protein data bank) format using Open Babel integrated into PyRx 0.9.5 [22], and docking was also performed using its software. Docking was performed at the binding site of the receptor, specifically targeting the active area of the RBD, using AutoDock Vina Version 1.1.2 integrated into PyRx 0.9.5. The analysis utilized a grid box with coordinates of −40.34, 30.85, and 6.61 (x‐, y‐, z‐) and dimensions of 35, 50, and 30 Å at the active area of the RBD [23]. The docking results were then visualized using the Proteins Plus webserver (https://proteins.plus/) [24].

2.1.5. Molecular Dynamic (MD) Simulation

The MD simulations were conducted using the Yet Another Scientific Artificial Reality Application (YASARA) Software Version 23.4.25 with an AMBER14 force field [25]. The molecules that passed the druglikeness screening were further selected down to five based on the lowest binding affinity and the presence of identified bonds on https://proteins.plus/ [26]. The utilized macro programs included md_run for executing simulations, while md_analyze and md_analyzeres were used to evaluate RMS value, RMSD ligand movement, the number of hydrogen bonds, and the radius of gyration. This analysis was performed on the ligand with the lowest binding affinity, which passed druglikeness and antiviral potential selection criteria. The simulations were conducted under physiological conditions, specifically at pH 7.4, 37°C, 1 atm, and 0.9% salt content, for 50 ns with autosaving every 25 ps [27].

2.2. In Vitro Studies

2.2.1. Plasmid Construct and Cell Culture

The SARS‐CoV‐2 G614D VLP was produced by expressing the recombinant spike (S), membrane (M), and envelope (E) proteins in human embryonic kidney 293T (HEK‐293T) cells. The sequences encoding these proteins were obtained from GISAID with the ID EPI_ISL_437188. To create a biological marker, an enhanced green fluorescent protein (EGFP) was fused to the carboxy terminus of the spike (S) protein via a five‐amino acid linker (PPVAT). The sequence for the EGFP protein was obtained from the NCBI GenBank database with Accession Number U55761. The sequences were then optimized for human codon usage using the GenSmart Codon Optimization tool (GenScript) and synthesized into pcDNA3.1 (+) expression plasmids by GenScript (Singapore), resulting in three recombinant plasmids: Spike‐EGFP_pcDNA3.1/Zeo(+), M_pcDNA3.1/Hygro(+), and E_pcDNA3.1/Zeo(+) which contain zeocin and hygromycin antibiotic resistance genes to facilitate the identification of successfully transfected cells.

The HEK‐293T cell line was purchased from Elabscience Biotechnology Inc. (United States). The cells were cultured in Dulbecco′s modified essential medium (DMEM) high glucose (Gibco, Thermo Fisher Scientific Inc., United States) supplemented with 10% fetal bovine serum (FBS) (Gibco, Thermo Fisher Scientific Inc., United States) and 1% antibiotics, that is, penicillin/streptomycin (10,000 U/mL), and incubated at 37°C with 5% CO2.

2.2.2. Transfection and VLP Isolation

Transfection using the electroporation method was performed when the HEK‐293T cell confluency reached over 90%. The cells were harvested by trypsinization, centrifuged at 1500 rpm for 5 min at 4°C, and washed with 500 μL of cold phosphate‐buffered saline (PBS). The cells were homogenized and centrifuged again. The three recombinant plasmids, each 10 μL, were then added to the cells, homogenized, and transferred into a cold electroporation tube. The mixture was incubated in a cold state for 5 min before undergoing five times pulse electroporation at 1800 V, followed by a 10‐min rest. Afterward, the sample was transferred to a petri dish and supplemented with medium. Two days posttransfection, the VLP‐containing culture supernatant was collected into a 2‐mL tube and subjected to a stepwise centrifugation. The first centrifugation was performed at 1000 rpm for 10 min at 4°C, followed by a second centrifugation at 2000 rpm for 10 min at 4°C. The supernatant was then filtered. The VLP was pelleted from filtered supernatant through a 20% (w/v) sucrose cushion at 39,000 rpm for 4 h at 4°C. The VLP‐containing pellet was resuspended in PBS. A nanodrop spectrophotometer was used to determine the VLP concentration.

2.2.3. Production and Characterization of SARS‐CoV‐2 G614D VLP

To assess the success of producing SARS‐CoV‐2 G614D VLP, transmission electron microscopy (TEM) and fluorescence microscopy analysis were used. The collected VLPs were placed on carbon‐coated grids for 1 min and underwent a 45‐s staining process with Uranyless (Electron Microscopy Sciences [EMS], Pennsylvania, United States). Subsequently, the sections were observed and imaged using a transmission electron microscope (TEM HT7700) operating at 100 kV. HEK‐293T cells were plated on a bottom glass dish in complete medium for fluorescence analysis. After 48 h, EGFP observation was conducted using the Bio‐Imaging Navigator FSX100 Olympus at 488 nm.

2.2.4. Determination of the Inhibitory Concentration (IC50) of C. xanthorrhiza Extract on 16HBE14o‐ Cells

The IC50 of C. xanthorrhiza extract on 16HBE14o‐ cells was analyzed using the WST‐1 method. A total of 8 × 104 cells was plated into a 96‐well plate. The cells were incubated for 24 h under conditions of 5% CO2 at 37°C. The cells were cultured in Alpha‐MEM (Gibco, Thermo Fisher Scientific Inc., United States) supplemented with 10% FBS (Gibco, Thermo Fisher Scientific Inc., United States), 2 mM L‐glutamine, and 1% penicillin/streptomycin (10,000 U/mL) (Thermo Fisher Scientific Inc., United States). After incubation for 2 days and achieving over 80% confluency, the medium was replaced with medium containing crude extract at different concentrations (350, 175, 87.5, 43.75, 21.875, and 0 μg/mL). The IC50 was determined with linear regression y = a x + b. The cell culture medium was replaced with the treatment medium and incubated for 24 h. The medium was replaced with a complete medium and 5% WST‐1 reagent and then incubated for 30 min before being analyzed using a microplate reader (Bio‐Rad model 680) at 450 nm.

2.2.5. Inhibitory Test of C. xanthorrhiza on SARS‐CoV‐2 G614D VLP Attachment

Preliminary inhibition testing of the extract was conducted to determine its ability to inhibit virus attachment to the host cell (16HBE14o‐). 16HBE14o‐ cells were seeded at a density of 6 × 104 cells per well in 24‐well plates containing glass coverslips. The cells were cultured in Alpha‐MEM medium supplemented with 10% FBS, 2 mM L‐glutamine, and 1% penicillin/streptomycin (10,000 U/mL) (Thermo Fisher Scientific Inc., United States). After 48 h (cell confluence over 60%), the cells were treated with a mixture of C. xanthorrhiza and SARS‐CoV‐2 G614D VLP. The extract was prepared as a serial stock solution in DMSO and diluted five times in culture medium to a final concentration range of 2.5–125 μg/mL, with a final DMSO concentration of < 1%. SARS‐CoV‐2 VLP was added to a final concentration of 200 ng/mL. The mixture was incubated under the same conditions as the cell culture for 60 min before addition to the cells. The existing culture medium was removed, and the mixtures were added. After 24 and 48 h, the culture was observed under a fluorescence microscope (Olympus Fluoview FV1000). Treatments were performed in triplicate, and each culture was analyzed in four different fields of view. To quantify the inhibition of SARS‐CoV‐2 VLP attachment to the cells, the reduction in the intensity of the EGFP fluorescence area in the VLP‐exposed cells was compared to the average intensity of EGFP in the C+ (VLP SARS‐CoV‐2, without extract) and the C− (without VLP SARS‐CoV‐2, without extract).

2.3. Data Analysis

Data are presented as mean values ± standard deviation (SD) obtained from three replicates. Statistical analysis was performed using SPSS Version 26 software. One‐way analysis of variance (ANOVA) with Bonferroni correction was used. EGFP cell counting was performed on each cell, excluding cells with morphological abnormalities (syncytia) from the analysis. A significance level of p > 0.05 was used to determine significant differences, indicated by “∗,” while differences with p < 0.05 were indicated by “∗∗∗.”

3.2. Molecular Docking Results

To provide a comprehensive overview of the antiviral potential and structural basis of interaction, the results were presented in terms of predicted antiviral activity and binding affinity of selected compounds (Figure 1), followed by detailed structural modeling, validation, and molecular docking analysis of the SARS‐CoV‐2 spike protein D614G mutant (Figure 2). The structural modeling of the SARS‐CoV‐2 spike protein D614G mutant was performed using the 6ZB5 template through Swiss‐Model (Figure 2a). After the trimming process, the RBD was selected as the test protein for molecular docking (Figure 2b). Validation of the model was conducted by redocking the native ligand N3, and the RMSD between the redocked ligand and the native ligand in the crystal structure was calculated using PyMOL, yielding a value of 0.758 Å (Figure 2c), indicating high accuracy. Further assessment of model quality showed that 98.3% of residues were in allowed regions according to the Ramachandran plot, while ProQ analysis indicated a predicted LGscore of 9.480 and a predicted MaxSub score of −0.544, confirming the reliability and stability of the modeled RBD.

Using the validated model of SARS‐CoV‐2 spike protein D614G, molecular docking results revealed that curcumin II, curcumene, (+)‐ar‐turmerone, dibutyl phthalate, zerumbone, and DEET exhibited antiviral potential against more than four viruses, as summarized in Figure 1b. Among these, curcumin, curcumin II, curcumene, and dibutyl Phthalate showed stronger predicted activity against HIV, rhinovirus, and picornavirus (Pa > 0.6, darker color). Additionally, Figure 1a shows that curcumin, curcumin II, piperine, (+)‐ar‐turmerone, and peruvinine demonstrated the lowest binding affinities toward the RBD, with values of −7.5, −7.4, −6.8, −6.3, and −6.2 kcal/mol, respectively, indicating favorable interactions with the target site.

From a total of 32 active compounds, 16 were selected for further analysis based on druglikeness screening criteria. However, these compounds passed the druglikeness screening: (‐)‐caryophyllene oxide, curcumene, nootkatone, dibutyl phthalate, zerumbone, 3‐methoxycinnamic acid, parthenium, bisacurone, ambrosic acid, DEET, and galaxolidone were not included in the MD simulation because the ligand–protein interaction analysis using Proteins Plus indicated no strong binding interactions or key stabilizing contacts with the target residues.

The molecular interaction analysis revealed that curcumin and curcumin II exhibited the most complex binding patterns with the target protein residues, primarily through conventional hydrogen bonds involving ASN501, ARG408, and TYR453, along with additional Pi‐donor hydrogen bonding with GLY496. Both compounds also engaged in electrostatic interactions with GLU406 via Pi‐anion bonding, further stabilized by hydrophobic Pi‐Pi T‐shaped interactions with TYR505. In contrast, piperine formed only an electrostatic interaction with GLN498 through Pi‐cation bonding and a single hydrophobic Pi‐alkyl interaction with TYR505. The ligand (+)‐ar‐turmerone predominantly interacted through hydrophobic contacts with key aromatic residues (TYR505, TYR495, and PHE497) and one conventional hydrogen bond with GLY496. Meanwhile, peruvinine exhibited a more balanced interaction profile, forming conventional hydrogen bonds with GLY496 and ASN501, as well as hydrophobic Pi‐alkyl interactions with TYR495, PHE497, and TYR505 (Table 2).

3.3. MD Simulation Results

MD analysis determined the bond stability between the active components of C. xanthorrhiza compounds and RBD D614G. RMSD of curcumin and peruvinine remained stable under 2 nm for 50 ns. Meanwhile, the other compounds showed RMSD values under 3 nm (Figure 2e). The RMSD ligand movement analysis indicated stability in all five compounds. The structure of peruvinine was changed from 30 to 45 ns, but this condition eventually stabilized. Based on SoluteHbonds and RadGyration, piperine, curcumin, curcumin II, (+)‐ar‐turmerone, and peruvinine all demonstrated similar stability. The stability of the hydrogen bonds produced by the five ligands indicated equally stable conditions.

3.4. SARS‐CoV‐2 G614D VLP Production and Characterization

SARS‐CoV‐2 G614D VLP was produced by cotransfecting recombinant plasmids expressing S‐EGFP, M, and E proteins into HEK‐293T cells. The success of SARS‐CoV‐2 VLP production was assessed by observing the transfected HEK‐293T cells using a fluorescence microscope. The presence of green fluorescence, resulting from EGFP fused to the spike protein, indicated the SARS‐CoV‐2 VLP formation (Figures 3b, 3c, 3d, and 3e). The green fluorescence in Figure 3a indicates SARS‐CoV‐2 VLP formation inside the cells, while the green fluorescence in the medium indicates that VLPs have been released into the medium. TEM analysis confirmed that the SARS‐CoV‐2 VLP exhibited the crown‐like morphology characteristic of SARS‐CoV‐2 virions, with an average diameter of 52.94 ± 27.32 nm (Figure 3e).

3.5. The Inhibitory Effect of C. xanthorrhiza in 16HBE14o‐ Cell Attachment and the Syncytia Formation by SARS‐CoV‐2 VLP

The fluorescence microscopy images showed strong EGFP fluorescence in the C+ group (VLP SARS‐CoV‐2, without extract), indicating VLP attachment to the 16HBE14o‐ cells. The C− group (without VLP SARS‐CoV‐2, without extract) showed minimal background fluorescence. At 24 h, 2.5 μg/mL of C. xanthorrhiza crude extract reduced the EGFP intensity to 30% compared to the positive control, suggesting inhibition of VLP attachment. This inhibitory effect was not observed at 48 h, with EGFP intensity increasing to 4.6 AU/cell. Furthermore, higher extract concentrations led to a dose‐dependent increase in the EGFP intensity (Figure 4a,b).

In the C+ group, prominent syncytia formation was observed at 24 and 48 h. Large and multinucleated cell masses characterized these syncytia with a strong, concentrated EGFP fluorescence signal, indicating successful VLP attachment and subsequent cell‐to‐cell fusion, especially shown in the phase‐contrast images (Figure 4c). The C− group exhibited normal, healthy cell morphology, with distinct individual cells and no evidence of syncytia formation. As expected, minimum background fluorescence was observed. The same phenomenon was found by treating 2.5 μg/mL of C. xanthorrhiza crude extract, which inhibits the SARS‐CoV‐2 VLP attachment to the cells. Cells in this group appeared similar to the C− group, different from those in the other groups, which were treated with a higher concentration of C. xanthorrhiza crude extract.

The IC50 value of C. xanthorrhiza extract on 16HBE14o‐ cells was determined to be 125 ± 17.4 μg/mL (Figure 4d). This value was considerably higher than the concentration exhibiting the strongest inhibition of VLP attachment and syncytia formation, confirming that the reduction in EGFP fluorescence in higher concentrations of C. xanthorrhiza extract was mainly due to the inhibition of VLP attachment, not cell death.

4. Discussion

The present study constructed the SARS‐CoV‐2 VLPs to investigate the potency of C. xanthorrhiza in inhibiting SARS‐CoV‐2 VLP attachment to 16HBE14o‐ cells. For both in silico and in vitro testing, the study involved modeling the SARS‐CoV‐2 Spike D614G protein and producing SARS‐CoV‐2 VLPs. The in silico analysis using Swiss‐Model and molecular docking with the RBD domain showed accurate results, validated by redocking the native ligand N3 (RMSD = 0.758 Å), Ramachandran plot (98.3% residues in allowed regions), and ProQ analysis (predicted LGscore: 9.480; MaxSub: −0.544), confirming the reliability of the protein model [20, 35].

For VLP production, the cotransfection of plasmids through the electroporation method offers advantages in ease of use and low operational costs [36]. The successful production of SARS‐CoV‐2 VLPs was confirmed by EGFP fluorescence under fluorescence microscopy and VLP particles by TEM analysis. The produced VLPs were corona‐shaped with crown‐like structures, characteristic of SARS‐CoV‐2, and had an average diameter of 52.94 ± 27.32 nm. This size was smaller than the 129 ± 32 nm reported by Swann et al. [37] but comparable to VLPs produced using the same cell line with cotransfection of S, M, E, and N genes (90.33 ± 32.45 nm) [38] and VLPs produced in insect cells (103.30 ± 27.44 nm) [39]. Importantly, the VLP size in this study falls within the range for the wild‐type SARS‐CoV‐2 virion (60–140 nm) [40]. This study corroborates previous work demonstrating the successful production of the SARS‐CoV‐2 spike protein using the same approach [41, 42]. In addition to confirming VLP characteristics, the generated VLP was able to induce syncytia formation in 16HBE14o‐ cells, consistent with a report by Bosch et al. [43], which showed syncytia formation by the exposure of cells to MHV‐SGFP, indicating the proper synthesis and folding of recombinant spike‐EGFP in the VLP structure. The produced SARS‐CoV‐2 VLPs were then used to investigate the efficacy of crude ethanol extract of C. xanthorrhiza as an anti‐SARS‐CoV‐2 agent. The use of SARS‐CoV‐2 VLPs offers the advantage of lower laboratory standard requirements [43]. Furthermore, the potency of C. xanthorrhiza as an anti‐SARS‐CoV‐2 agent was determined by its ability to inhibit the attachment of VLPs to cells. C. xanthorrhiza was selected as a drug candidate due to its widely believed role in treating and preventing SARS‐CoV‐2 [44]. The present study used C. xanthorrhiza compounds with a Cloud M/Z ratio greater than 80%. Then, all active compounds were used for molecular docking with the RBD of the SARS‐CoV‐2 D614G variant. The D614G variant is known for its efficiency in binding with ACE2 [3].

16HBE14o‐ cells can be used as an appropriate model for studying SARS‐CoV‐2 infection [45]. The current study revealed that the crude extract of C. xanthorrhiza exhibits inhibitory activity at low concentrations (2.5 μg/mL). The highest inhibitory activity was observed at 48 h, with viral attachment increasing as the concentration increased (> 5 μg/mL). At low concentration, specific active compounds in C. xanthorrhiza might bind optimally to VLP attachment receptors on the host cell. At higher concentrations, other less effective compounds, or even the active compounds themselves, might saturate other binding sites, interfere with the optimal binding of the most potent inhibitors, or cause steric hindrance, thus reducing the overall inhibitory effect [46].

Previous research analyzed the ability of xanthorrhizol, an active compound of C. xanthorrhiza, but no inhibitory activity was found on virus attachment [47]. Crude extract of C. xanthorrhiza contains several active components that inhibit virus attachment to ACE2 at low concentrations. However, this study has not determined the optimal concentration of crude extract of C. xanthorrhiza in inhibiting VLP attachment to the 16HBE24o‐ cells. A systematic investigation of a wider range of concentrations is necessary to identify the optimal dose for maximal antiviral efficacy. The higher concentration of more than 5 μg/mL increases virus entry into cells, so optimization is needed.

C. xanthorrhiza extract exhibits a dose‐dependent effect in inhibiting the entry of SARS‐CoV‐2 into cells. At low concentrations, bioactive compounds such as curcumin can inhibit the interaction of the virus with the ACE2 receptor, suppress inflammation, and demonstrate antiviral activity without toxicity to host cells [46]. However, at high concentrations, toxic effects may occur, causing cell membrane disruption that facilitates viral entry instead [48]. Although the mechanism of VLP entry in this study remains unclear, this statement is supported by the increasing intensity of EGFP as the extract concentration increases. Additionally, curcumin can influence ACE2 receptor expression, where increased doses may potentially enhance ACE2 expression and the risk of infection [49]. Thus, the effect of C. xanthorrhiza extract in inhibiting SARS‐CoV‐2 depends on the dosage used, highlighting the need for further research to determine the optimal concentration. Further research on the inhibitory effect of SARS‐CoV‐2 attachment using HBE cells has not been conducted yet. This study serves as an initial step in understanding the characteristics of these cells.

In addition, LC‐HRMS profiling revealed the presence of several unexpected compounds such as DEET, dibutyl phthalate, and tridemorph. These compounds are not biosynthesized naturally by plants and are likely derived from environmental or laboratory contamination, such as pesticide residues, plasticizers, or solvent impurities [50, 51]. Particularly, phthalate esters are known to accumulate in soil and plants in contaminated regions, as reported by Xing et al. [52], indicating potential absorption through agricultural or storage conditions. Although curcumin II showed relatively low similarity in the present analysis, several previous studies have reported its strong biological potential. Therefore, despite its low similarity score, curcumin II was still included in this study due to its reported pharmacological relevance and potential contribution to the observed bioactivity.

Based on their pharmacological relevance, five bioactive compounds were selected for molecular docking and MD simulations, including curcumin, curcumin II, (+)‐ar‐turmerone, peruvinine, and piperine. Some compounds with antiviral potential and passing druglikeness selection did not show specific interactions with the Proteins Plus web server. Curcumin, the primary curcuminoid in Curcuma species, has previously been reported to have antiviral capabilities against viruses, such as Zika, dengue, Japanese encephalitis, chikungunya, vesicular stomatitis, and pseudorabies virus [53–55]. Based on bioinformatics analysis, curcumin has a binding affinity of −7.5 kcal/mol with the RBD D614G. This value is lower than the binding of curcumin with the SARS‐CoV‐2 wildtype, which is −7.9 kcal/mol [56]. Curcumin II, a structural analog of curcumin, shares similar biological activities and contributes to the overall therapeutic potential of the extract and is also indicated to have strong binding abilities to the RBD D614G [12]. In this study, the binding affinity of curcumin and RBD D614G was −7.4 kcal/mol, slightly lower than curcumin. Previous research has shown that curcumin II exhibits higher efficacy compared to curcumin in inhibiting viral infection, primarily through blocking the interaction between the viral RBD and the host ACE2 receptor, thereby preventing viral attachment and entry into host cells [57, 58].

Furthermore, (+)‐ar‐turmerone, a major sesquiterpene in turmeric essential oil, possesses immunomodulatory and antiviral properties that support the host defense mechanism [59]. Piperine, commonly referred to as 1‐peperoyl piperidine, is an aromatic alkaloid synthesized by plants of the Piper genus [60]. Piperine is known for various biological activities, including its anti‐inflammatory effects and the enhancement of drug bioavailability, which might indirectly contribute to combating viral infections. However, specific research on anti‐SARS‐CoV‐2 activity remains limited [61]. Peruvinine, a sesquiterpene lactone classified under prenol lipids (ambrosanolides and secoambrosanolides), has a molecular formula of C15H20O4 and a molecular weight of approximately 264.32 g/mol. Peruvinine and piperine have little specific evidence related to anti‐SARS‐CoV‐2 activity.

Molecular interaction analysis indicated that curcumin and curcumin II formed the most extensive interactions, combining hydrogen bonds, electrostatic, and hydrophobic contacts, suggesting a strong binding affinity to the target protein. In contrast, piperine exhibited minimal interactions, while (+)‐ar‐turmerone and peruvinine showed moderate profiles dominated by hydrophobic and hydrogen bond interactions. These findings highlight the critical role of hydrogen bonding and hydrophobic contacts, particularly at TYR505 and GLY496, in stabilizing ligand–protein complexes, with curcumin demonstrating the highest binding potential.

MD analysis indicates a sufficiently high stability, as seen from the stable RMSD values during the 50 ns trial. This stability is reflected in the RMSD value of < 3 Å, indicating no significant protein conformational changes [62]. This study supports curcumin in C. xanthorrhiza, which has significant potential as an antiviral against SARS‐CoV‐2 [63]. This study suggests that curcumin, curcumin II, piperine, (+)‐ar‐turmerone, and peruvinine play a crucial role in inhibiting the attachment of SARS‐CoV‐2 VLP D614G to 16HBEo‐ cells [64]. The study specifically targets the D614G variant, but SARS‐CoV‐2 has evolved numerous other mutations (e.g., Delta and Omicron variants). The efficacy of the extract against other variants remains unexplored. The study did not include animal models or clinical trials to confirm the antiviral activity, pharmacokinetics, and safety profile of C. xanthorrhiza extract in physiological systems. Therefore, further study is required to verify the inhibitory activities of C. xanthorrhiza extract in animal models infected with SARS‐CoV‐2 VLP.

5. Conclusion

SARS‐CoV‐2 VLP G614D was successfully produced and confirmed by TEM visualization with a 90.33 ± 32.45 nm diameter. C. xanthorrhiza extract significantly impeded the entry of SARS‐CoV‐2 VLP G614D to 16HBE14o‐ cells at 2.5 μg/mL for 24 h of incubation. Curcumin, curcumin II, piperine, (+)‐ar‐turmerone, and peruvinine have the lowest binding affinity (−7.5, −7.4, −6.8, −6.3, and −6.2 kcal/mol, respectively). Curcumin, curcumin II, and (+)‐ar‐turmerone had the most stable interaction with the SARS‐CoV‐2 VLP G614D. These findings suggest that C. xanthorrhiza is a promising candidate for combating SARS‐CoV‐2, supported by in vitro and computational studies.
