The effect of implementing the workplace violence management training program on knowledge, attitude, and self-confidence regarding adaptation to patient violence among undergraduate nursing students
Authors: Fatemeh Heshmati Nabavi, Marzieh Namazi, Azadeh Saki, Saeed Vaghei, Milad Rezaei, Zohre Pouresmail
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundViolence management training is necessary for nursing students who are in the most contact with the patient and his/her companion. The aim of this study was to investigate the effect of implementing the workplace violence management training program (WVMTP) on knowledge, attitude, and adaptation to patient violence among undergraduate nursing students.MethodsThis semi-experimental study was conducted on 42 nursing students. Sampling was performed using the convenience sampling method and the samples were randomly assigned to the intervention (IG) and control groups (CG). The data collection tools included the Knowledge and Attitude toward Patient Violence Questionnaire, and the Self-Confidence in Adaptation to Patient Violence Questionnaire. The IG received training in the form of question/answer, lecture, and role-play, using slides and showing movies for 6 hours.ResultsNo statistically significant difference was found in the mean knowledge score of students in the IG and CG two weeks after the intervention. The Wilcoxon test results indicated no statistically significant difference in comparing the mean self-confidence score in adaptation to patient violence in the IG and CG two weeks after the intervention, but a statistically significant difference was observed in the IG before and after the training (p = 0.40).ConclusionAlthough the WVMTP for nursing students was able to increase student knowledge, attitude, and self-confidence in the short term, considering the instability of this learning within the next two weeks and the importance of violence management skills, this skill is recommended to be included in the curriculum and educational credits with continuous training.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12909-026-08898-1.

Introduction

The occupation of nursing has traditionally been recognized as a high-risk profession due to the inherent characteristics of the job, such as direct patient care and less-than-ideal clinical settings [1]. A global concern has been the increasing frequency of violence against nurses and nursing students in the workplace [2, 3]. “Any act or threat of physical violence, harassment, intimidation, or other threatening disruptive behavior” that takes place in the workplace is classified as workplace violence (OSHA, ND). According to recent evaluations, it is most often described as being committed by patients, and to a lesser amount, by their families and visitors [4, 5]. Verbal aggression against nurses in psychiatric facilities ranges from 7.8% to 89.4%, while physical violence against them is reported to occur in between 2.3% and 88.4% of cases [6].

Patients in a hospital setting commonly display a range of factors that increase their risk of violence, such as substance abuse, stress, pain, cognitive impairment, and altered mental status [7]. Although the evidence presented suggests that nursing personnel encounter workplace violence [8], there is a scarcity of research on the experiences of pre-registration nursing students in this regard. Nevertheless, there exist numerous justifications for investigating the experiences of students. The rate of attrition in training incurs significant costs in terms of both economic and other resources [9]. Additionally, workplace violence serves as a contributing factor for some students. Students are susceptible to workplace violence and may be at a higher risk due to their lack of experience [10].

Studies carried out in Turkey [11, 12], Iran [13, 14], and other Middle Eastern nations have revealed that nursing students face higher physical and verbal abuse, with patients and their companions mostly imposing this aggression on them [14, 15].

In addition, these studies found that the experience of violence at work can lead to psychological complications such as anxiety, depression, diminished self-esteem, irritability, feelings of distress and fear, as well as damage to the student’s physical health. Experiencing workplace violence can also reduce nursing student commitment, dismiss student values and professional identities, and, in some cases, lead them to abandon nursery care [16].

To prevent the detrimental aspects of experiencing violence in the workplace, developing anger management and empowerment programs is necessary and essential for the treatment staff [17, 18]. In this fact, anger management skills are essential for nursing students, as in clinical situations, various conflicts are common. Therefore, nursing education should be revised in a way that helps students improve their ability to control anger [12].

Students are not immune to workplace violence and may be more vulnerable due to their inexperience [19]. Research has focused more on clinical violence directed at nurses than nursing students, but related studies have found variation in the types, causes, and outcomes of clinical violence [19–21]. One review study conducted about workplace violence prevention programs has suggested that programs should be conducted using new teaching methods and with a control group [22]. The purpose of this study was to determine the impact of the Workplace Violence Management Training Program (WVMTP) on Knowledge, Attitude, and self-confidence regarding adaptation to Patient Violence among Undergraduate Nursing Students.

Method

Study design and participants

The present research was a semi-experimental study conducted from March to October 2021 in the Faculty of Nursing and Midwifery, Mashhad University of Medical Sciences. The research population consisted of nursing students. The inclusion criteria included the willingness to participate in the research, no history of mental disorders, and no history of taking neuropsychiatric drugs. The calculated sample size for each group was 22 and by considering 10% withdraw 25 samples at each group was assigned.

As the required sample size estimation could not be directly derived from the Solorzano Martinez (2017) study [23], a post-hoc calculation was performed to estimate the overall effect size of intervention from this study. So, the parameters used were a statistical power of 90%, a one-tailed α level of 0.050, and an estimated large effect size (Δ = 1). The result of this calculation indicated a necessary sample of 20 per group. Consequently, anticipating a 20% participant withdrawal, the final assigned sample size was set at 25 participants per group.

For sampling, the researcher first went to the research setting, selected the eligible students; the first 25 participants on the list were assigned to the intervention group, and the next 25 were assigned to the control group. The research sample encompassed 50 nursing students studying in the 6th semester who were willing to participate in the study and were randomly divided into two intervention (N = 25) and control (N = 25) groups. Two people from the intervention group and 1 person from the control group were excluded from the study because of absence at the time of completing the questionnaires. Moreover, after data collection, 1 person from the intervention group and 4 people from the control group were excluded from the study because of not completing the questionnaires correctly, and finally, 20 people remained in the control group and 22 people in the intervention group.

Data collection and measurements

Data collection tools included a demographic questionnaire, the Knowledge and Skill Questionnaire, the Violence Experience Questionaire, the Attitude toward Patient Violence Questionnaire, and the Self-Confidence in Adaptation to Patient Violence Questionnaire. The demographic questionnaire contained 6 questions (age, gender, grade point average (GPA), marital status, extent of interest in the field of nursing, and intention to change the field). The Knowledge and Violence Experience Questionnaire contained 5 questions related to knowledge and Skill and 15 questions related to violence experience which is extracted from Jonas-Dwyer et al.’ study (2017), Scoring of the knowledge section in this questionnaire is based on a Likert scale, where ‘Strongly agree’ receives a score of 4 and ‘Strongly disagree’ receives a score of 1. The maximum possible score is 20 and the minimum possible score is 4. To evaluate the patient violence experience section, a 15-item questionnaire was used before the intervention. Following the intervention, 5 items (questions 4, 7, 8, 9, and 20) were added to this section. The scoring for this section will be based on the calculation of frequency percentages [24]. The Attitude toward Patient Violence Questionnaire contained 20 questions which extracted from Collins (1994) [25] and Beech (2001) [26]. This 20-item instrument uses a Likert scale, with responses ranging from a minimum score of 1 (‘strongly disagree’) to a maximum score of 4 (‘strongly agree’). The total scores for this questionnaire range from a minimum of 26 to a maximum of 74.

The Self-Confidence in Adaptation to Patient Violence Questionnaire designed by Thackery (1987) consisted of 10 questions [27], This items scored on a scale from 1 to 11. A score of 1 represents the worst possible state, while a score of 11 indicates the best possible state. The total scores for this questionnaire range from a minimum of 10 to a maximum of 110. The validity was confirmed by the qualitative content validity confirmation method by experts and content validity confirmation method and content validity index (CVI) calculation and reliability was confirmed by calculating internal consistency and Cronbach’s alpha coefficient. The Cronbach’s alpha coefficient was calculated as 0.78 for the Knowledge and Violence Experience Questionnaire, 0.65 for the Attitude toward Patient Violence Questionnaire, and 0.95 for the Self-Confidence in Adaptation to Patient Violence Questionnaire.

The inter-rater reliability was calculated as an ICC of 0.85 for the Knowledge and Violence Experience Questionnaire, ICC of 0.85 for the Attitude toward Patient Violence Questionnaire, and ICC of 0.85 for the Self-Confidence in Adaptation to Patient Violence Questionnaire. The test-retest reliability was calculated as the correlation was (r = 0.82) for the Knowledge and Violence Experience Questionnaire, for the Attitude toward Patient Violence Questionnaire(r = 0.82), and for the Self-Confidence in Adaptation to Patient Violence Questionnaire(r = 0.82).

After obtaining permission from the Ethics Committee of Mashhad University of Medical Sciences and the Dean of the Faculty of Nursing and Midwifery and coordinating with the Faculty’s Graduate Education Unit, the researcher determined a class to bring all the research participants together. The researcher first introduced herself to the students and selected the eligible individuals based on the research course-taking checklist from among the students meeting the inclusion criteria using the convenience sampling method. After providing a group oral explanation (about 5 min) about the research purpose and method, written informed consent was obtained from the selected individuals. Having assigned the participants to the intervention and control groups, the researcher held a 6-hour WVMTP for the intervention group. This intervention was carried out by a co-author with a background in facilitating violence management workshops for nursing staff [28] for two sessions. The training material was prepared based on the topics outlined in Table 1, using content from journals and authoritative nursing textbooks such as “Workplace Violence in Healthcare Toolkit: a Guide to Establishing a Prevention & Training Program” and “Leadership Roles and Management Functions in Nursing”.

The first session was held for 3.5 h on the topic of workplace violence aiming to familiarize nursing students with workplace violence via lectures and playing videos. The second session was held for 2.5 h on the topic of workplace violence management methods aiming to familiarize nursing students with workplace violence management skills via lectures, playin videos, and role-play (Table 1). Data collection was carried out three times before the intervention, immediately after the intervention, and two weeks after the intervention.

Data analysis

We analyzed the data using SPSS software version 20. The Kolmogorov-Smirnov test was used to assess the normality of the data. Due to the non-normality of the data, non-parametric tests (the Mann-Whitney U test, Fisher’s exact test, and Wilcoxon test) were used to analyze the data. A significance level of p > 0.05 was considered.

Ethical considerations

In the study, the ethics committee of Mashhad University of Medical Sciences approved the ethical considerations (Ref No: IR.MUMS.NURSE.REC.1402.022).

Results

The mean age of the participants in the intervention group and in the control group was 22.05 ± 0.75 and 21.95 ± 1.96 years, respectively. The results demonstrated that the two groups were homogeneous regarding gender (p = 0.834), marital status (p = 0.187), and intention to change the field (p = 0.379) (Table 2). In terms of GPA, students had a mean score of 17.37 ± 1.00 in the intervention group and 17.11 ± 1.04 in the control group. A Pearson’s chi-square test revealed that there was no statistically significant difference between the two groups regarding their level of interest in their field of study (p = 0.299).

Discussion

This semi-experimental study was conducted aiming to determine the impact of the Workplace Violence Management Training Program (WVMTP) on Knowledge, Attitude, and self-confidence regarding adaptation to Patient Violence among Undergraduate Nursing Students. The results indicated the increased knowledge of nursing students in the intervention group about violence during the study, but the mean knowledge score in the intervention and control groups was not significantly different. In this regard, Martins (2017) concluded in his pilot study that the implementation of a simulated training program could give rise to a significant increase in the knowledge of students about violence [29]. In this study, the sample size was 15 nursing students and the simulation-based educational intervention was held in one day. Although this study was similar to our study in terms of sample size and number of sessions, the results were different. Concerning the effectiveness of this intervention compared to the current study, it can be pointed out that simulation is one of the educational methods used for training under conditions similar to real life. In this method, training is presented without the existence of real-life risks, which is one of the best educational methods to promote knowledge and skills [30]; however, a large part of the training presented in this research was carried out by the lecture method. One of the reasons for the non-significance of the knowledge score difference between the workshop and control groups can be attributed to the program implementation method and the use of traditional training methods. The results of another study by Brann and Hartley (2016) demonstrated that the National Institute for Occupational Safety and Health (NIOSH) workplace violence prevention program culminated in a sustainable increase in the knowledge of nursing students during the intervention and after the follow-up stage [31]. The intervention evaluated in this study included the implementation of the NIOSH violence prevention program online for nursing students. This online training course consisted of 13 sections in 20-minute modules [31] and a large part of this program was conducted through case studies and the application of student knowledge under conditions similar to real life. The same problem can be the reason for the different effectiveness of the intervention implemented in this study on the knowledge of students. Thus, it can be suggested that using a combination of educational methods that can both promote the knowledge of students and challenge the learned knowledge in practice can culminate in sustainable learning. In similar studies conducted on similar populations, it has also been specified that violence prevention training interventions can result in promoting participants’ knowledge. For example, Glass et al. (2017) concluded in their study that implementing a computer-based violence prevention training intervention led to promoting home care workers’ knowledge. In this study, two groups of “computer-based training intervention” and “computer-based training along with peer facilitation” received violence prevention training. In computer-based training, the training was conducted during two modules. In this study, the participants’ knowledge about violence prevention methods improved in both groups [32]. Overall, based on the results of the current study and other similar studies, it can be concluded that presenting violence prevention training culminates in promoting participants’ knowledge about violence prevention methods. Based on the research results, it can be concluded that the use of a combination of educational methods with a focus on interactive methods, such as simulation, role-play, and the use of scenarios and similar methods that can induce indirect experiences for learners, is more influential in promoting knowledge. Furthermore, considering that the students participating in the current research were taking a management course and they had received numerous materials in the management class, this issue can be another reason for the presence of a significant relationship in the level of knowledge in the two intervention and control groups.

In addition, the study results indicated that the attitudes of the students increased during the intervention but no significant difference was observed in the control group. However, similar studies on students denote the effectiveness of violence prevention training on the attitudes of the students. For example, in Brown et al.’s (2018) study conducted using the simulation method for workplace violence prevention training, students showed their better preparedness to encounter violent situations. Moreover, the evaluators of the student performance in the scenarios approved the improvement of students’ ability to respond to violent situations [33]. Simulation-based training can directly affect individuals’ attitudes and reinforce interprofessional collaboration. In line with the above research, another research by Liu et al. (2022) indicated that the use of a combination of behavioral economics and simulation-based training culminated in promoting the attitudes of the medical students toward violence prevention. In this research, the simulation group received about 5 h of training regarding violence prevention through the simulation method, and the other group received 3 h of training regarding workplace violence training behavioral economics and workplace violence simulation training [34].

The study results also demonstrated the increased self-confidence to deal with violence in the students in the intervention group, with no significant difference from the control group. Similar to the current study, the results of Jeong and Lee’s (2020) study investigating the effectiveness of the clinical violence prevention training program for nursing students indicated that this training program gave rise to promoting problem-oriented and emotion-oriented coping styles, and the ability of the students to deal with violence also increased. In this study, the training program was conducted using simulation, in such a way that two researchers and a special speaker held the sessions, and three professional performers were used as patients in the simulation. The preliminary program was implemented compactly for two days. Simulation of a violent situation encompassed verbal harassment and physical violence by a patient with acute pancreatitis referring to the emergency room with complaints of upper abdominal pain [21].

The results of Liu et al.’s (2022) study also indicated that the use of a combination of behavioral economics and simulation-based training led to promoting self-efficacy regarding violence prevention among medical students. Self-efficacy is an individual’s confidence person has in himself/herself to do something [34]. In similar studies investigating violence prevention training programs for nurses, such as Hemati-Esmaeili et al. (2018), reveal that After workplace violence prevention program implementation, verbal violence and mobbing/bullying against nurses significantly decreased, as well as their fear of violence and concerns. Also, In the nurses’ view, violent incidents can be prevented and managed [28]. The results of Ostram and Mayerlo’s (2008) study investigating the effectiveness of a violence management training program also indicated that the training program consisted of three 4-hour sessions. According to the results of this study, training results in stable changes in individuals’ knowledge and behavior; in addition, violence management training is effective in improving individuals’ insight toward violence and their ability to adapt to unfavorable working conditions [35]. Moreover, in a semi-experimental study conducted by Lamont and Brunero (2018) to determine the effectiveness of the violence prevention program for nurses, it was shown that implementing the violence prevention program could culminate in promoting nurses’ confidence in violence adaptation. In this study, 11 workshops were held, which had 3 objectives: (1) Formulating violence risk assessment programs and their management, (2) using stress-relieving techniques during aggression intensification, and (3) using detachment when responding to a violent person [36]. Overall, research results demonstrate that the use of methods such as simulation, role-play, and training methods being able to remind reality so that students can practice violence prevention skills frequently can culminate in promoting knowledge, attitude, self-efficacy, and confidence in the ability to cope with violent occurrences among students.

Conclusion

Although providing violence management training among nursing students could have favorable short-term educational impacts regarding knowledge, attitude, and confidence in adaptation in the intervention group, the impacts of this intervention were not significant compared to the control group during two weeks. This study highlights the necessity of implementing more comprehensive, long-term, and continuous WVMTP within nursing internship settings to achieve sustainable outcomes. Future research should prioritize longitudinal studies with larger sample sizes to further investigate the long-term impact and scalability of such comprehensive programs.

Limitations

This study has several limitations that should be considered when interpreting the results. First, the project was conducted as a Master’s thesis within a single academic semester at a nursing school, Due to these constraints, it was not feasible to design the study with a longer follow-up period. Consequently, a convenience sampling method was used from a single center, and blinding of participants and facilitators was not possible. These methodological limitations may affect the generalizability of our findings.

Implication to practice

The results of this study can be instrumental in designing programs to enhance the physical, mental, and psychological well-being, as well as the knowledge and attitudes, of nursing students. This can be achieved by preparing them to effectively and rationally handle potential violent incidents in clinical settings. Given that workplace violence is a growing and serious concern for healthcare systems, it is incumbent upon university administrators and officials to develop preventative programs for students.

Considering the documented statistics on violent incidents against students in clinical settings, the results of this research can serve as a valuable resource for future investigators. Furthermore, these findings can provide a foundation for further studies on the impact of implementing workplace violence prevention programs.
