Introduction

Cribriform morular thyroid carcinoma (CMTC) represents a rare malignant thyroid tumor with specific growth patterns and molecular features. CMTC is mainly seen in young Asian females, with an average age of 25 years at diagnosis[1]. CMTC is related to the activation of the WNT/β-catenin signaling pathway, typically ensuing from germline or somatic mutations within the APC gene, leading to the accumulation of β-catenin and the formation of CMTC[2,3]. Familial adenomatous polyposis (FAP) is a dominant hereditary disease caused by germline mutations in the APC 5q21 region, so CMTC is often closely related to FAP[4].

CMTC constitutes < 1% of all thyroid malignancies. Its unique histological features (cribriform architecture and morular cells), underlying genetic background (association with APC gene mutations), and clinical behavior (generally favorable prognosis but occasional lymph node metastasis) remain incompletely characterized in the medical literature.HIGHLIGHTSCribriform morular thyroid carcinoma (CMTC) is related to the activation of the WNT/β-catenin signaling pathway and familial adenomatous polyposis.This study reports the youngest case of CMTC.This review summarizes clinical features and the molecular mechanism of CMTC.

Case presentation

Patient history

The patient was a 13-year-and-11-month-old female who was admitted to the hospital due to the detection of a right neck mass for 1 week. Ultrasound examination indicated that the right lobe of the thyroid gland was enlarged to approximately 12 × 10 cm, with diffuse fine-punctate calcifications throughout the entire right lobe (Fig. 1). Within the left lobe of thyroid, there was a heterogeneous echoic area measuring approximately 1.7 × 0.7 cm, which also demonstrated diffuse fine-punctate calcifications; the corticomedullary structures of the lymph nodes in the bilateral neck III–IV regions were indistinct and some nodes contained fine-punctate hyperechoic foci. The patient denied any history of neck irradiation, a family history of thyroid carcinoma or FAP, medication use and drug allergies, etc. Subsequently, total thyroidectomy and bilateral lymph node dissection were performed. The postoperative recovery of this patient is satisfactory. No recurrence or complications were observed during the 10-month follow-up after surgery.Figure 1.Preoperative neck ultrasonography of the patient revealed that the right thyroid gland was nearly entirely occupied by the tumor, exhibiting a hypoechoic pattern with tiny punctate calcifications. Methods

Following surgical excision, the specimens were fixed with 4% neutral buffered formalin within 30 minutes and then maintained in fixative for 24 hours. Subsequently, the specimens underwent paraffin embedding and were stained with Hematoxylin-Eosin staining (HE) and immunohistochemistry.

This case report has been reported in line with the SCARE checklist[5].

A comprehensive literature search was conducted to identify relevant of CMTC. The databases including PubMed and Web of Science were searched from inception to January 1995.

Discussion

Epidemiological characteristics

Patients with CMTC are relatively more prevalent in the East Asian population, with an incidence rate of approximately 1.4%. The average age of first diagnosis was 25 years old, with a male-to-female ratio of approximately 1:61[3,6]. Cases of CMTC account for approximately 0.16-0.5% of all thyroid cancer cases[7,8]. About 60% of CMTC cases occur in the setting of previously diagnosed FAP, among which 40% of CMTC cases are the initial symptoms of FAP[9–11]. Some studies have reported that up to 80% of FAP patients may be associated with congenital hypertrophy of the retinal pigment epithelium. Therefore, ophthalmic examination can also assist in the clinical diagnosis of hereditary CMTC[12,13].

Clinical features

The initial symptom of patients with CMTC is usually a painless, gradually enlarging neck mass. Preoperative ultrasound often suggests a TIRADS-3 category nodular goiter or follicular tumor. In this case, the preoperative ultrasound showed diffuse fine-punctate calcification within the thyroid gland. After consultation in multiple hospitals, diffuse sclerosing variant of PTC could not be excluded, and bilateral cervical lymph nodes were suspected of metastasis. Therefore, preoperative imaging examinations for CMTC patients lack specificity.

Histological immunohistochemical and genetic characteristics

In terms of pathological features, CMTC is characterized by a typically intact capsule and is internally divided into lobules. Under the microscope, multiple growth patterns can be observed, including cribriform and morular structures, and these growth patterns do not have a fixed proportional composition. It has been reported that nearly 30% of tumors are associated with vascular invasion and 40% of tumors are associated with capsular invasion[1,6,14,15].

CMTC, due to its possession of typical nuclear features of PTC, was once considered as a pathological subtype of PTC. However, most literature reports that CMTC does not carry mutations in BRAF and RAS within the MAPK pathway, and generally lacks the expression of PAX8 and Tg. In this case, no mutation in the BRAF V600E gene was identified by molecular genetic testing, which is consistent with findings from previous studies. The only genetic alteration detected was a CCDC6-RET gene. The CCDC6-RET gene encodes a constitutively activated tyrosine kinase, resulting in aberrant activation of downstream signaling pathways, including but not limited to the MAPK/ERK and PI3K/AKT pathways. This leads to uncontrolled cellular proliferation and enhanced cell survival, and may ultimately contribute to oncogenesis. The CCDC6-RET fusion is a well-established oncogenic driver and is commonly observed in thyroid carcinoma.

In the cribriform areas, TTF-1, ER, and PR are expressed, while in the morular areas, CD5, CK5, and CDX2 are expressed with a lack of TTF-1 expression. In CMTC, β-catenin is mainly expressed in the nucleus or cytoplasm, whereas in PTC, β-catenin is mainly expressed on the plasma membrane[2,3]. In the 5th edition of the WHO classification of thyroid tumors, CMTC is classified as an independent thyroid neoplasm of uncertain histogenesis and is no longer regarded as being subordinate to PTC.

Molecular mechanisms

It is well known that the WNT/β-catenin signaling pathway plays a crucial role in maintaining the intestinal stem cell niche, and it is closely associated with intestinal disease. Activation of this signaling pathway through APC gene mutation is sufficient to induce excessive proliferation of the intestinal epithelium and polyposis[2,16], which also explains the molecular mechanism underlying the co-occurrence of FAP in CMTC patients.

FAP is characterized by the presence of numerous polypoid adenomas of varying sizes throughout the colon and rectum, with nearly 100% probability of progressing to colorectal cancer during middle age[16]. Approximately 53–60% of CMTC cases are associated with FAP, and CMTC patients with FAP often present with multifocality[13,17,18].

In CMTC, β-catenin is mainly expressed in the nucleus and cytoplasm, while in PTC, β-catenin is mainly expressed on the plasma membrane[2,19]. Under normal circumstances, APC protein forms a “destruction complex” together with β-catenin, scaffold protein Axin, glycogen synthase kinase GSK3β, and serine/threonine kinase CK1, which phosphorylates β-catenin and eventually promotes its ubiquitination and proteasomal degradation[19]. When the APC gene mutates, the WNT/β-catenin signaling pathway will be activated, and the truncated APC protein will be unable to form a complex with β-catenin, Axin, GSK3β, and CK1, resulting in the inability to phosphorylate β-catenin[20,21]. It will lead to the inactivation of GSK3β by activating different protein cascade reactions[22]. Excessive β-catenin accumulates in the cytoplasm and migrates ectopically to the nucleus as a transcriptional activator, where it binds to T-cell factor/lymphocyte enhancer factor family DNA-binding proteins and provides an uncontrolled permanent mitogenic signal, thereby enabling WNT gene expression and participating in proliferation, invasion, dedifferentiation, and tumorigenesis[1,23,24].

the origin of CMTC remains ambiguous. Boyraz et al suggested that CMTC lacks clear biomarkers of thyroid follicular cell differentiation, and the presence of morulae might represent thymic/ultimobranchial pouch-related differentiation[2]. Ana et al considered that CMTC originates from the endodermal progenitor lineage of follicular cells[16]. Regarding the prognosis of CMTC, it is generally believed that CMTC is more indolent than PTC. About 12% of cases have lymph node metastasis at the time of diagnosis, and 5% of cases present with distant metastases such as to the lung, bone, and brain. To date, only four cases of death caused by CMTC have been reported[1]. Therefore, the overall prognosis of CMTC patients can be relatively good.

Conclusion

In summary, CMTC, classified as an independent thyroid neoplasm of uncertain histogenesis, demonstrates distinctive cribriform and morular pathological characteristics. Due to its association with APC gene mutations and the activation of the WNT/β-catenin signaling pathway, it is closely related to FAP. Therefore, patients with CMTC should be vigilant regarding potential diseases in the intestinal tract.
