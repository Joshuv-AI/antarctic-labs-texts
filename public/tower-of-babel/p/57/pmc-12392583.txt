Optimal transplantation strategies for stem cell treatment in premature ovarian failure: a network meta-analysis
Authors: Guo Ziwei, Ni Shuang, Wu Di, ji Li
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
BackgroundThe incidence of POF has increasingly affected younger individuals in recent years, and stem cell therapy has gradually been incorporated in clinical practice. Given the wide variety of stem cell sources, there are inherent differences in their biological characteristics and functions. This article compares the efficacy of stem cells from different sources in the treatment of premature ovarian failure in an animal model, and provides a basis for their clinical application.MethodsA search was conducted for studies on stem cell therapy for POF animal models in PubMed, Cochrane Library, CBM, EMbase, CNKI, Wanfang, VIP, and the time limit for searching was from the date of the establishment of the respective databases to 2024-12-31. After screening the literature, two researchers independently extracted and analyzed the data. The quality of the included studies was assessed using the SYRCLE Animal Experimentation Risk of Bias Assessment Scale. The main outcome measures included estradiol, follicle-stimulating hormone, luteinising hormone, and the number of follicles at each level. After assessing the risk of bias in the included studies, a network meta-analysis, plotting, and tabulation were performed using Stata 15.0 software.ResultsThirty-three animal studies involving 877 experimental mice were finally included. The overall quality of the literature was considered medium. The results suggest that four stem cell therapies——UC-MSCs, AD-MSCs, HuMenSCs and OGSCs, may be the four most efficacious in the treatment of POF; and UC-MSCs may show superior therapeutic advantage in increasing the number of follicles. When comparing transplantation routes, Tail Vein Injection and Intraovarian Injection may have higher efficacy compared to other routes of administration.ConclusionsThis study systematically explores the therapeutic strategies for POF treatment using stem cell therapy. Further research is necessary to identify the optimal transplantation strategy and to advance both animal experiments and clinical research by improving and standardizing the design, implementation, and reporting of such studies.

Background

Premature ovarian failure (POF) is a multifactorial condition characterized by secondary amenorrhea, low estradiol (E2) expression and high follicle-stimulating hormone(FSH) expression in women under 40 years of age [1]. Studies indicate that the prevalence of POF increases progressively with age [2]. While the exact cause of POF remains unknown in most cases, it is generally associated with genetic factors, iatrogenic causes, infections, autoimmune disorders, and environmental influences. Current treatments primarily include lifestyle modifications and hormone replacement therapy. Although these interventions can alleviate symptoms and improve fertility, they cannot restore ovarian function and carry a potential risk of cancer [3]. Therefore, POF is a significant global public health issue, highlighting the urgent need for safer and more effective alternative therapies.

Recent advances in regenerative medicine have raised new hope for treating POF. Stem cells (SCs) are a cell subtype that remains undifferentiated in both embryonic and adult tissues. They have the ability to self-renew and differentiate when needed. In differentiated organs, stem cells help restore function by repairing tissue damage. SCs can be classified based on their source, including mesenchymal stem cell (MSC), human induced pluripotent stem cell (hiPSC), ovarian germline stem cell (OGSC), embryonic stem cell (ESC), and monocytes, among others. In recent years, stem cell (SC) therapy has shown significant success in treating conditions like spinal cord injury, neurodegenerative diseases, multiple sclerosis, heart disease, and diabetes. Some studies have also demonstrated the efficacy of stem cell-based therapies for treating POF [4, 5].

Methods

The study followed PRISMA statements, and the protocol was registered as CRD42025630342 in PROSPERO.

Search strategy

Study retrieval was performed using databases such as PubMed, Cochrane Library, CBM, EMbase, CNKI, Wanfang and VIP. The search terms and methods were as follows: (1)“stem cells” or “SCs”; (2)“premature ovarian failure” or “premature ovarian insufficiency” or “POF” or “POI”;(3)the first and second terms were combined for retrieval.

Study selection

Inclusion criteria: Female mice; successful animal models of POF or ovarian insufficiency; treatment with stem cells in the treatment group; control group treated with blank control, normal saline, physiological serum or PBS buffer; randomized controlled animal trials; literature published in Chinese or English up to December 31, 2024.

Exclusion criteria: Studies involving rats or other animals; ex vivo, in vitro, human studies or in silico studies; repetitive research; missing or unclear data; conference articles and review articles; studies without relevant outcomes.

Two investigators independently screened the studies by reviewing titles and abstracts. Then, they independently read the full texts and selected studies meeting the inclusion criteria. In cases of disagreement, the third investigator was consulted to resolve discrepancies.

Data extraction

The two investigators independently extract the following information. In case of disagreement, they resolved discrepancies through consultation with the third investigator: first author; year of publication; modeling methods and sample size; type, dosage, and transplantation route of stem cells; interventions used in the control group; Outcome indicators: E2, FSH, luteinizing hormone(LH), primordial follicle number, primary follicle number, secondary follicle number and atretic follicle. Quality assessment

The methodological quality of each study was assessed by the author using the Systematic Review Center for Laboratory Animal Experimentation (SYRCLE) tool to evaluate the risk of bias in animal experiments [6]. The toolkit includes 10 items: randomized sequence generation; comparable baseline demographics; concealed allocation; randomization of housing conditions; masking of care providers and researchers; random sampling for outcome evaluation; blinded outcome assessors; appropriate handling of missing outcome data; absence of selective outcome reporting; and no other sources of bias.

Statistical analysis

The numerical units of experimental outcomes varied across studies, and all data were converted into standardized units before analysis. The network meta-analysis (NMA) was performed using frequentist inference, with all results analyzed through a random-effects model. Continuous outcomes were represented as mean differences (MD) with 95% Confidence Interval (95% CI). When a dataset cannot be converted into standardized units, continuous variables should be expressed using the Standardized Mean Difference (SMD) and 95% CI. Statistical analyses and graphical representations were conducted using Stata 15.0 software. A network evidence graph was generated using the “network” command in Stata 15.0, where node sizes were proportional to sample sizes, and edge widths corresponded to the number of studies connecting the interventions. In the presence of closed loops among interventions, the “ifplot” command was used to assess loop inconsistency by evaluating the absolute differences between direct and indirect evidence. The inconsistency factor (IF) and its 95% CI were calculated for each closed loop. Consistency between direct and indirect comparisons was assumed when P > 0.05 and the lower limit of the 95% CI for the IF included zero; otherwise, significant inconsistency within the loop was indicated. The “SUCRA” command in Stata 15.0 was used to generate cumulative ranking probability curves and calculate surface under the cumulative ranking area (SUCRA) values, which were used to rank the efficacy of the interventions for each outcome measure. A higher SUCRA value indicated a more favorable treatment ranking. Publication bias and the robustness of the meta-analysis results were assessed using the “network funnel” command in Stata 15.0 to generate funnel plots.

Follicle-stimulating hormone (FSH)

A comprehensive analysis was conducted on 28 studies [7–14, 16−21, 24–30, 32−34, 36–39] involving FSH levels, which included a total of 765 murine subjects and 13 distinct intervention methods. The evidence network diagram for each outcome measure is shown in Fig. 2B. Among the interventions, twelve stem cell-based therapies were compared to control groups (blank/placebo), with additional comparisons made between UC-MSCs, AD-MSCs and HC-MNCs under different administration routes. Consistency evaluation results show no significant inconsistencies in the included studies, and a consistency model was applied to estimate the relative efficacy of various interventions. The SUCRA analysis indicated that OGSCs administered via Intraovarian Injection (IOI) intraovarian injection had the highest likelihood (96.2%) of being optimal in reducing FSH levels in the experimental model, as shown in Fig. 3B. Additionally, data suggest that for HC-MNCs, IOI (66.3%) may be superior to TVI (33.2%) in reducing FSH levels in mice. Discussion

This comprehensive NMA included 33 studies with 877 experimental murine subjects and systematically evaluate the therapeutic efficacy of various stem cell-based interventions for POF, comparing their administration routes. The results indicate that UC-MSCs, AD-MSCs, HuMenSCs and OGSCs may be more effective in treating POF. Among these, UC-MSCs may offer the greatest therapeutic advantage, particularly in increasing the number of follicles.

UC-MSCs, derived from extraembryonic tissues, offer several biological advantages, including abundant availability, reduced immunogenicity, and a lack of tumorigenic potential [39]. Notably, progenitor cells from the placental-umbilical junctional zone exhibit autonomous regenerative capacity independent of maternal influence, as demonstrated by their robust paracrine activity and differentiation potential [40]. A clinical study [41] showed that UC-MSC transplantation in patients with POI led to a significant increase in the number of follicles at each developmental stage, without adverse events. This finding aligns with the conclusions of the present study. Numerous studies have demonstrated that UC-MSCs can enhance follicle quantity and quality by reversing OGG and TIC apoptosis [42]– [43], inhibiting oxidative stress and promoting angiogenesis [44]. These effects result in a reduction in the abnormal accumulation of ROS [45] and a significant inhibition of fibrosis [46], thereby improving the function of damaged ovarian tissue [47].

In two clinical trials conducted by Zafardoust et al. (2018, 2023) [48]– [49], HuMenSCs demonstrated therapeutic efficacy in improving clinical manifestations of ovarian hypoplasia, along with a favorable safety profile following transplantation. Derived from endometrial tissue, HuMenSCs exhibit enhanced therapeutic potential for female reproductive disorders due to their anatomical proximity to ovarian tissues, which may facilitate targeted cellular interactions [50]. Complementary research by Noory [51] et al. revealed that HuMenSCs promote folliculogenesis through paracrine mechanisms, specifically by upregulating Bax and Bcl-2 expression, as well as exerting anti-apoptotic effects on granulosa cells.

In comparison, AD-MSC, primarily isolated from subcutaneous adipose tissue, offer advantages such as easy access, no immune rejection, and avoidance of ethical concerns [52]. Current evidence suggests that AD-MSCs exert therapeutic effects on POF primarily through cytokine-mediated paracrine signaling [53–55]. This signaling modulates gene expression patterns that regulate granulosa cell proliferation, differentiation, and apoptosis inhibition [56], thereby promoting follicular development. Given these advantages, AD-MSCs have gained increasing prominence prominence in experimental and clinical applications for managing POF.

OGSCs were first successfully isolated from murine ovarian tissue in 2009 [57], demonstrating remarkable self-renewal capacity and differentiation potential. These pluripotent cells can expand in vitro and regenerate functional oocytes when transplanted into ovarian tissues of infertile mouse models, ultimately achieving follicular maturation, successful fertilization, and live births. These properties have established OGSCs as a promising therapeutic candidate for POF treatment. The existence of OGSCs has been further confirmed by detecting characteristic markers of adult stem cells and germ cells, such as Oct-4, c-kit, vasa, and telomerase, in the surface epithelium of ovaries from various mammalian species [58–61]. Notably, Tilly et al. [62] (2012) extracted germline stem cells from functionally normal adult ovaries and implanted them into the ovarian tissues of humans and immunocompromised mice, both of which showed the formation of new primordial follicles. Despite these breakthroughs, OGSC research lags behind other stem cell disciplines, with substantial evidence gaps remaining in understanding their precise mechanisms of action and therapeutic efficacy in POF pathophysiology.

Collectively, TVI and IOI show higher efficacy compared to other administration routes. IOI, in particular, allows direct stem cell delivery, bypassing systemic clearance mechanisms (e.g., pulmonary/hepatic first-pass effects) [63] and presenting a relatively lower risk compared to Ovarian Artery Injection(OAI). Both TVI and Subcutaneous Injection(SC) have demonstrated a favorable safety profile, but TVI offers broader systemic immunomodulation and a faster onset of action. Comparative analysis of transplantation routes revealed that UC-MSCs administered via TVI had superior endocrine-modulating efficacy, with optimal E2 elevation and FSH suppression in ovarian insufficiency models. Notably, AD-MSCs and HC-MNCs showed enhanced therapeutic potential when delivered via IOI, suggesting that efficacy may vary depending on the administration route. Studies [64] have shown that MSCs significantly improve ovarian hypoplasia via IOI, which aligns with the findings of this study.

While these promising results highlight the potential of stem cell therapies, further challenges remain in their clinical application. Recent advancements in stem cell-based interventions for POF have moved into preclinical and early clinical investigation phases. Stem cell therapies have shown great promise, particularly for many difficult-to-treat diseases, but several challenges remain in their clinical translation due to factors such as individual patient variation and a certain percentage of non-responders. Firstly, stem cells may exhibit atypical pharmacokinetic characteristics in vivo. Wang Guangji et al. reported that MSCs initially accumulate in the lungs following peripheral intravenous injection and then gradually distribute to other organs over time, which is a distribution pattern very different from that of conventional drugs [65]. Secondly, the fate trajectory of stem cells in vivo involves a time-dependent clearance process, which shows significant variability in the persistence of MSCs under different experimental conditions. This variability may limit their therapeutic efficacy [66–69]. Additionally, the dose-response relationship remains a key translational barrier. Some studies have shown that the therapeutic effect of UCMSCs is dose-dependent, but it remains unclear how the optimal dose in animal models translates to human clinical applications [70]. Finally, the source of the stem cells and the method of injection may affect therapeutic efficacy to varying degrees. Therefore, the clinical application of stem cell therapy still needs to address these translational barriers to facilitate its wider use.

Limitations

Despite the promising findings, the current evidence base remains constrained by methodological limitations. While preliminary studies show therapeutic potential, they often involve small cohorts, highlighting the need for validation through multicenter trials and independent replication studies to ensure robustness. The present systematic analysis exhibits inherent constraints, including a literature search confined to major Chinese and English databases, thereby potentially omitting relevant research published in other linguistic domains. Experimental models predominantly utilize murine specimens, with insufficient data from alternative species precluding comparative interspecies analysis. Furthermore, differences in cell transplantation dosages may affect the results, but this study did not compare therapies with varying dosages. Additionally, most included studies lacked detailed descriptions of methodological safeguards, including sequence generation adequacy, allocation concealment, blinding of personnel (animal keepers and investigators), random outcome assessment, and blinding of outcome assessors. When assessed using the SYRCLE risk-of-bias tool, most studies were rated as ‘unclear’ in these areas, indicating a need for improved methodological reporting. Therefore, larger and more comprehensive studies are needed in the future to provide stronger evidence for stem cell therapy in POF.

Conclusion

Stem cells have become a prominent therapeutic tool in the treatment of premature ovarian failure, attracting significant attention in both experimental and clinical studies. They can significantly improve hormone levels and enhance ovarian reserve function. This study further confirms that stem cell transplantation treatments from different sources were superior to the blank/placebo group to varying extents, with different transplantation routes also showing varying effects on treatment efficacy. In summary, the four stem cell therapies——UC-MSCs, AD-MSCs, HuMenSCs, and OGSCs——are likely the most effective for treating POF, with UC-MSCs showing particular therapeutic advantages. TVI and IOI offer significant benefits over other administration routes, primarily due to enhanced cellular retention and targeted modulation of the ovarian niche. However, given the quality of the included studies and methodological limitations, more high-quality data from pilot studies are needed to further validate these findings.
