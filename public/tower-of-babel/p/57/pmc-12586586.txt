Decadal changes in wind speed have offset and then aggravated the impact of warming on maize production in China since 1980
Authors: Zongliang Zhang, Jialu Xu, Yuzhong Zhang, Huimin Zhuang, Shaokun Li, Fulu Tao, Zhao Zhang
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractWind is a critical meteorological factor for crop production, yet the effect of its long-term changes on crop yield has not been adequately discussed. Here, we assess the effect of wind on maize yield and production in China with statistical modeling of weather and crop data for 1980−2017. The analysis, applied in different regions and scales, consistently reveals that maize yield increases by ~9.4% for every 1 m·s−1 decrease in growing-season wind speed anomaly after controlling for temperature and precipitation. Three decades of decreasing wind speed between 1980 and 2010 have enhanced China’s maize production by 101.2 Mt, fully offsetting the loss due to warming temperatures. However, this compensatory effect has rapidly diminished in the last decade due to the reversal of wind trends, with expanding areas experiencing both increasing wind speeds and warming temperatures. The wind effect is substantially underestimated by crop models, exposing gaps in our current evaluation of climate risks to food security.Subject terms: Climate-change impacts, Agriculture, Agroecology

Introduction

Climate change is a growing threat to global food security1. Various climate and weather variables are projected to shift in differing directions and magnitudes across regional and global scales, impacting food production through diverse pathways. Understanding how food production responds to these changes is crucial for enhancing adaptation strategies. The effects of temperature and precipitation, along with their fluctuations, extremes, and spatiotemporal patterns, on crop production have been a topic of extensive studies for a long time2–6. More recently, climate-related factors such as atmospheric CO2 levels, snow cover, and clouds have also been found to play significant roles in crop production7–10.

Wind is another weather variable that can influence crop growth and yield formation. It can directly influence stomata conductance, leaf movements, light interception, and, consequently, key physiological processes including photosynthesis and transpiration11–13. Moreover, wind can also interact with the thermal and moisture environment of crops, affecting leaf temperature, heat stress, water loss, and drought stress12,14,15. Wind could cause mechanical damage such as sandblasting, leaf tearing, and lodging11,15–18, and lodging is a main cause of large-scale crop failure, among others19. Field experiments conducted across the world and crop model simulations have demonstrated that changes in wind can influence the yield and production of various crops, for instance, wheat production in Iran and Italy, corn and wheat yield in North China, and maize and soybean yield in the US20–23. A series of field observations in Australia has seen crop yield improvement through planting windbreaks around the fields, particularly in circumstances with large and damaging winds or drought events24. Furthermore, newly developed crop models also begin to incorporate processes that are dependent on wind in their simulation25–27.

Significant changes in near-surface wind speeds have been observed worldwide in recent decades28,29. Reports have documented both decadal wind stilling (a decrease in wind speed) and more recent wind reversals (an increase in wind speed)29–31. These changes have prompted investigations into their effects on various processes, such as evapotranspiration, plant seed dispersal, soil erosion, and solar radiation32–36. While it can be inferred that changes in wind might affect crop production through these processes20,37,38, there is a lack of direct empirical evidence to quantify the long-term and large-scale response of crop yields to changes in wind speeds.

Various approaches have been developed to investigate the impact of wind on crops. One such method involves plant- or field-level experiments, which replicate diverse wind patterns and plant exposures using fans, wind tunnels, and shelters39,40. However, there are large uncertainties associated with upscaling the findings from these experiments to larger regions over extended periods. Another widely used method for evaluating the effects of climate change is process-based crop models1,8,20. These models simulate physiological and physical processes related to crop growth, thereby linking crop yields to their growing conditions. For example, the Penman–Monteith (PM) equation, which incorporates all the factors that affect evapotranspiration from plant canopies and land surfaces, including the effect of wind31,41,42. However, current crop models may not account for all wind-related processes or factors, such as wind-induced lodging. Statistical models, another commonly-employed method to identifying the effects of climate change3,6,7, can provide direct empirical evidence by deriving the relationship between yields and wind speeds, although the underlying mechanisms are not thoroughly detailed.

In this study, we investigate the effect of wind speed on maize yield by analyzing the relationship between county-level yields and their corresponding weather conditions. The weather conditions are derived from observations in 2416 meteorological stations and 313 agro-meteorological stations across China from 1980 to 2017. Our analysis workflow is shown in Fig. S1. Briefly, we build an ensemble of statistical models across multiple spatial scales to establish the relationship between growing-season wind speed and maize yield. We then estimate the cumulative impact of wind changes in recent decades on China’s maize production and contrast it with the impact of warming temperatures. Finally, we compare the observation-derived wind-yield relationship with simulations by crop models. Our findings suggest that wind is an underappreciated element of climate change that affects crop production.

Results

Long-term wind speed trends in the maize growing season

The wind speed in the maize growing season showed a general slowing down and reversal in 1980–2017, with a turning point in 2011 (Fig. 1). In China’s maize-growing counties, the 38-year average wind speed during the maize growing season was 2.13 m s−1. More than 71% of maize-growing counties experienced a significant steady decrease in wind speed since 1980, and 40% of these counties with wind stilling went through a significant reversed trend in wind speed since 2011 (Figs. 1a and S2, specific statistical results for each county are shown in the Source Data file). These counties were mainly distributed in Northeast, North, Northwest, and Southwest China, largely coincident with the country’s maize-growing belt (Figs. 1b and S3). Averaging over all maize-growing counties, we found the growing-season wind speed had decreased significantly (slope = −0.013 m s−1 yr−1, 95% Confidence Intervals = [−0.016, −0.011], t(df = 35) = −11.9, and p < 0.05) from 2.41 to 2.00 m s−1 between 1980 and 2010, with its anomaly decreasing from 0.30 to −0.11 m s−1; and then rebounded significantly (slope = 0.05 m s−1 yr−1, 95% Confidence Intervals = [0.04, 0.07], t(df = 35) = 6.5, and p < 0.05) to 2.37 m s−1 by 2017 with an anomaly of 0.26 m s−1. In addition to wind speed, we also found a consistent increase in temperature at a warming rate of 0.03 °C yr−1 (95% Confidence Intervals = [0.02, 0.04], t(df = 36) = 6.4, and p < 0.05) and insignificant long-term trends (slope = 0.04 % yr−1, 95% Confidence Intervals = [−0.15, 0.23], t(df = 36) = 0.41, and p = 0.69) in precipitation anomalies (in percentage) across maize-growing counties from 1980 to 2017 (Fig. S4).

Response of maize yield to growing-season wind speed

During the study period, maize yields exhibited significant increasing trends (slope = 85.8 kg ha−1 yr−1, 95%Confidence Intervals = [79.8, 91.7], t(df = 36) = 29.3, and p < 0.05) across most regions of China (Fig. S5). Figure 2a shows the response curve of maize yield to changes in wind speed anomaly over different maize-growing regions in China, after controlling for temperature anomalies and precipitation anomalies in percentage (Fig. S6). We found a consistent negative relationship between maize yield and growing-season wind speed anomaly across all agro-climatic regions, indicating an average of 9.4% decrease in the maize yield with every 1 m s−1 increase in wind speed. This value ranges from 4.9% (Southeast China) to 17.3% (North China). The wind-yield response curves are monotonic in contrast with convex precipitation-yield curves, which have optimal precipitation levels for maximum yields (Fig. S6).

We performed three tests to assess the robustness of this wind-yield relationship (Fig. 2b–d). First, we examined the scale effect by building the statistical model at the different scales of country, agro-climatic region, and province (Fig. 2b). The average effects from these models ranges from 8.2 to 9.4% yield decrease with 1 m s−1 increase of wind speed, demonstrating that our results are robust across different spatial scales. For simplicity, we reported the results from the agro-climatic region scale hereafter. Second, we examined the uncertainty induced by perturbing variable terms of the statistical model, as well as an extended model incorporating solar radiation, vapor pressure deficit (VPD), and CO2 concentration (Fig. 2c). After testing all potential combinations of the model terms and the extended model, we found the yield response to wind was moderately stable, ranging from 8.9 to 15.0% yield decrease with 1 m s−1 increase of wind speed. The main results are from the baseline model (Eq. 4 in Methods). Finally, we conducted placebo checks to determine if the yield response could have occurred by chance. We generated the distribution of model coefficients from the county-and-year mismatched model inputs (Fig. 2d), which did not overlap with the ones obtained from the original data, indicating a low probability (<0.01%) that the results have occurred by chance.

Cumulative impacts of wind stilling and reversal on production

Using the statistical model built above, we estimated the impact of climate conditions on maize production in China since 1980. We attributed this impact to the contributions from wind, temperature, and precipitation changes (Figs. 3–5). This attribution allowed for a quantitative comparison of the understudied impacts of changing winds with the well-known impacts of rising temperatures on maize production. Overall, the production-enhancing effect of decreasing wind speeds can compensate for the production-reducing effect of rising temperature (Fig. 3). Throughout the entire study period (1980−2017), more than 47% of maize-growing areas exhibited a compensation ratio (Wcum/Tcum) below −1, indicating that the positive effects of wind stilling fully offset the negative effects of rising temperatures (Fig. 3b). In about 91% of maize-growing areas, the compensation ratio was below zero, suggesting that the wind effect partially compensated for the adverse effects of warming (Fig. 3b).

From 1980 to 2010, the historical trend of wind stilling was associated with a cumulative increase in maize production amounting to 101.2 Mt. This production gain from wind stilling was 115% of the production loss due to rising temperatures (–87.9 Mt), thereby effectively offsetting the negative impact of warming (Fig. 3a). Beginning in 2011, as the trend of wind stilling reversed, the compensatory effect of wind on maize production diminished (Fig. 3a, c), so that the wind trend offset only 80% of the production decrease caused by warming. When considering the interaction between wind and temperature, the production loss induced by the combined effects of temperature and wind has increased from 1.5 to 35.1 Mt (Fig. 3a). In contrast, if the wind trend observed from 1980 to 2010 had continued until 2017, cumulative maize production would have been 36 Mt higher (Fig. 3c).

The compensatory effects between wind and temperature also varied across regions and over time (Fig. 4). From 1980 to 2010, all agro-climatic regions except Northeast China saw full compensations for the increases in maize production attributed to wind stilling (Wcum/Tcum < −1), which offset the losses incurred from warming (Fig. 4b–f). However, beginning in 2011, the compensatory effect between wind and temperature diminished in all five regions. The three largest maize-producing regions, namely Northeast, North, and Southeast China, experienced notable declines in the compensatory effects of wind (Fig. 4b, c and f). Additionally, regional differences were noted in the impact of the wind-temperature interaction; while most regions experienced a negative interactive effect on production, North China and Southeast China exhibited a positive impact (Fig. 4c, f).

Although wind and temperature showed compensatory effects on maize production at the national and regional scales, there are cases at the county level in which both wind and temperature contributed to production loss. Between 1980 and 2010, only 4.3% of maize production originated from counties experiencing increases in both wind speeds and temperature (Fig. 5c), resulting in combined reductions in production attributable to changes in wind and temperature (Fig. 5b). From 2011 to 2017, the production coverage of this case increased to 55.3%. Meanwhile, the share of production from counties that experienced warming alongside wind stilling decreased dramatically from 94.3% to 18.5% (Fig. 5c), indicating a reduction of the compensatory effect (Fig. 5a). By 2017, wind speeds had nearly returned to levels observed in the 1980s. Increased wind speed, driven by climate change /variation and anthropogenic activities, may pose a new threat to maize production in the future, potentially comparable to the warming effects.

Cumulative wind impacts estimated by crop models

We used the GSWP3-W5E5 reanalysis climate dataset and simulated maize yield from the Global Gridded Crop Model Intercomparison (GGCMI phase 3), which provides a compilation of crop model simulations, to assess the impact of wind trends43. Based on these data, the estimated impacts of wind trends on cumulative maize production were positive (26.8 ± 20.7 Mt), indicating that increases in yield and production were associated with a slight decrease in wind speed. These findings are generally consistent with estimates derived from statistical modeling of observational data, albeit with substantially smaller magnitudes (Fig. 6). The highest estimate was produced by the PEPIC model under a no-irrigation scenario, while the lowest estimate was provided by the SIMPLACE-LINTUL5 model in a full-irrigation scenario. The choice of irrigation scenarios significantly affects the estimation outcomes (mean difference = 33 Mt, 95% Confidence Intervals = [20, 46], t(df = 7) = 6.0, and p < 0.05), with the no-irrigation scenario consistently projecting a higher wind trend effect compared to the full-irrigation scenario.

The main differences between crop models and statistical models are due to the input meteorological data used. When using the climate-forcing reanalysis dataset GSWP3-W5E5 and observed yield data, we observed a notable underestimation relative to previous estimates derived from observational data and statistical models used in this study, indicating substantial quality limitations of reanalysis wind data for evaluating wind-yield effects. When using GSWP3-W5E5 and simulated yield from different crop models, we saw a slight underestimation compared to estimates derived from W5E5 and observed data, suggesting the presence of additional mechanism gaps. However, due to the huge data gaps in wind input, these estimations may not accurately reflect the relative importance of the different mechanisms employed or the various pathways through which wind impacts yield, as simulated by different crop models. Therefore, it is essential to address both data gaps and mechanism gaps when using crop models to evaluate the effects of wind.

Discussion

Several mechanisms may explain why slower wind could lead to higher yield (Fig. 7); among them, evapotranspiration could be a main pathway. The worldwide slowdown of winds contributed greatly to the decrease in pan or reference evaporation31,44. According to the Food and Agriculture Organization (FAO), the evapotranspiration of a crop is the product of the reference evapotranspiration (ET0) and the crop coefficient (Kc), both of which are influenced by wind speed and can be delicately parameterized in mathematical equations45. Meanwhile, other climatic and growth-related factors could also influence ET0 and Kc, such as temperature on ET0 and crop growth stage on Kc46. Warming does not necessarily cause an increase in ET0, as reductions in wind speed can counterbalance the warming-induced increases47,48. This may explain why reducing wind speed can enhance plant growth in arid environments, where water availability is the primary constraint on growth49. In hot and dry regions, wind regulation on evapotranspiration could be particularly critical for rainfed crops50.

In addition to co-influence on evapotranspiration, wind could also interact with temperature by modifying the microclimate of crop fields. In cold climates, where low temperatures limit yields, slower winds can lead to higher crop temperatures and longer growth periods18. In high latitudes, where short growing seasons limit plant growth, slower winds could delay leaf senescence and mitigate frost damage12. These mechanisms are also applicable to cooler maize-growing regions and at the end of the growing season. In the case where strong wind is a hazard factor for crop growth, reducing wind speed can enhance crop yield39. Additionally, decreased wind speeds can lead to diminished soil erosion, reduced nitrogen loss, and less lodging, as well as other forms of physical damage to plants11,51–55. These various mechanisms could work together in maize production, and explain why statistical models can capture a larger cumulative impact of wind trends compared to crop models. Existing crop models, integrated with a few wind-related mechanisms (Table S1), are limited in their ability to simulate all wind-yield mechanisms effectively.

In crop models, the most widely employed mechanism is the simulation of reference evapotranspiration based on the FAO Penman–Monteith equation41,56,57. The different estimations produced by crop models for irrigation and rainfed regimes well illustrate the wind-to-evapotranspiration impacts. Evapotranspiration is influenced by meteorological factors (e.g., wind), management practices (e.g., irrigation), and other variables. We hypothesize that crop yield exhibits larger sensitivity to wind-mediated evapotranspiration reduction under water-limited conditions (e.g., non-irrigation conditions), while remaining less affected when water supply is sufficient (e.g., full irrigation conditions). This highlights wind’s particular relevance for rainfed crops, especially in water-scarce environments. Another critical wind-related factor influencing the crop’s evapotranspiration, the crop coefficient Kc, is only parameterized in a few crop models (e.g., DSSAT and SIMPLACE-LINTUL5), and is not adjustable in response to changes in wind speed58,59. Some crop models, such as EPIC (e.g., PEPIC and EPIC-IIASA), incorporate consideration of wind-induced soil erosion and outperformed the other ones in capturing the wind effect (Fig. 6). Extreme wind speed may lead to physical damage to crops, such as lodging, which may be included in the statistical models but not in crop models. Overall, it is essential to recognize that there may be additional key mechanisms beyond evapotranspiration, soil erosion, and nitrogen loss that warrant further exploration and development in future crop models.

The robustness of both crop models and statistical models is highly dependent on data quality. While direct observations are generally reliable, they are often unavailable at high spatial-temporal resolution. Reanalysis data with high spatiotemporal resolution are widely used; however, they may not adequately represent all scenarios, particularly as reanalysis wind data often fail to capture long-term wind trends due to missing key processes29,30,60, thereby creating data gaps for crop models when assessing the impacts of wind trends on crop production (Fig. S7). Furthermore, a lack of large-scale gridded evapotranspiration data from cropland also constrains further exploration into the mechanisms of wind effect59,61,62.

The significance of evaluating the impact of wind trends lies in their association and interaction with the effects of the warming trend. Our analysis reveals that, whether at a national or regional level, warming serves as the predominant factor causing climate-related reductions in crop production. The national warming-induced reduction accounted for ~3.2% of the annual production, consistent with the estimates from other studies5,63,64. We found that the substantial potential loss can be offset by the effects of wind trends, although the extent of this offset varies by region and changes over time. Wind trend had the largest impact in the main maize-growing regions, namely Northeast China and North China, which in total accounted for 72% of China’s maize production. In these two regions, most of the counties have experienced significant warming, with nearly half reporting a decline in wind speed. While numerous studies have addressed the risks associated with warming and drought in these regions, the risks related to wind were rarely mentioned38,65,66. The recently observed wind speed increase may compound with warming, drought, and other unfavorable climatic conditions, potentially exacerbating risks to future crop production.

In the past, with the wind trend changing over time, its compensation ratio to the warming impact changed. Specifically, the large-scale reversal of wind trends since 2011 has notably reduced the compensation effect at the national level and, in certain regions, has led to concurrent production losses due to the combined effects of wind speed up and warming. In the future, the anticipated changes in growing-season wind will be a key issue when referring to the impacts of climate change on crop yields. The trends observed in our study, which indicate a shift from decreasing to reversing seasonal wind speeds, are consistent with findings from prior research on annual mean wind trends67,68. Based on CMIP6 projections, future wind trends have high uncertainties60. Some scenarios in CMIP6 project that future wind speeds in some regions of China could increase at a rate exceeding 0.2 m s⁻¹ per decade, indicating potential risks to maize production in those areas60,69. Furthermore, this negative wind impact on maize could extend to other spring and summer crops. Consequently, it is essential to understand and remain cautious regarding the potential yield-reducing effects of winds on crops, as well as their long-term trends.

Even if large-scale wind risk remains unclear, we should still be prepared for potential losses in agricultural production driven by future changes in wind patterns. For the scientific community, more efforts should be made to address potential wind risks for food security by generating specialized wind and evapotranspiration datasets, enhancing the reliability of future wind projections, investigating wind effects across diverse cropping systems and regions, and exploring potential risks associated with extreme wind events and wind-compounded disasters. For farmer practices, strategies such as establishing windbreaks18,39, restoring vegetation35,70, implementing irrigation systems35,71, and adapting crop seasons72, could be potential solutions to mitigate wind-related production risks.

Methods

Data sources and data processing

We used mainly four parts of data, including annual maize yield, planting area, phenology, and the weather conditions during the maize growing season in China from 1980 to 2017. We collected county-level yield records from census bureaus and agricultural yearbooks. The original dataset includes 2269 counties and 71,005 yield records in total. For data cleansing, yield outliers (by 3σ-rule) and the counties with more than 20 years of missing records, in total 230 counties, were excluded. We collected the planting area from the National Bureau of Statistics of China73. We obtained maize phenological records from the China Meteorological Administration (CMA), which provides the most comprehensive phenological data recorded by well-trained agricultural technicians from 313 agro-meteorological stations over China. The Chinese Agricultural Meteorological Monitoring System has implemented a range of standardization and quality control measures on the dataset. These measures include standardizing crop names, growth stage names, data units, and so on. Additionally, integrity checks, duplicate value checks, cross-year value checks, internal consistency checks, and similar procedures have been conducted. These rigorous procedures have guaranteed the reliability and integrity of the dataset74. We then used long-term averaged phenological data for the calculation of weather variables during the growing seasons.

We obtained long-term weather data (CN05.1) from the Chinese Academy of Sciences75. This dataset provides gridded reanalysis meteorological data at a 0.25° × 0.25° resolution, which is created based on observations from 2416 meteorological stations over China76. From this dataset, we extracted the daily temperature, precipitation, 10-m wind speed, relative humidity, and sunshine duration over 1980–2017. We calculated VPD based on relative humidity and temperature, as saturation vapor pressure is a function of temperature45. We calculated solar radiation based on the Ångström-Prescott equation and sunshine duration45,77. The CN05.1 dataset can well capture the long-term climate trend and has been widely used for global and regional climate model validations60,78–80. We harmonized two gridded CO2 concentration datasets with different temporal coverages to construct a continuous record spanning the entire study period43,81,82.

We aggregated the daily weather data into annual growing season means and then converted it into anomalies by subtracting the mean value of the study period (1980−2017). We further converted precipitation values into anomaly percentages83. Then we converted the gridded weather to the county level by grid area weighting83. The crop and the weather data used here are the best available data of high quality and comprehensiveness, which would provide a solid foundation for the analysis and conclusions of this study.

The quadratic time effect and county-fixed effect are used to address potential confounding effects. In specific, the year-related variables (Year and Year2) could capture the influence of unobserved factors that vary over time but are constant across counties, for example, the country-level technological advancements, crop variety change, and management practices improvements. The county-fixed effect (ci) could account for unobserved factors that vary across counties but remain constant over time (e.g., soil type). This approach is consistent with methods established in a number of yield-weather studies5,7,89–94. Robustness analysis of the relation between wind speed and maize production

We carried out a series of robustness checks to verify the response of maize yields to wind speed anomaly. First, we ran the baseline model (Eq. 4) at provincial, regional, and country levels to test any potential scale effect—whether the weather-yield relation is dependent on spatial scale. As the result shows relative consistency in the average marginal effect of wind speed anomaly (Fig. 2b), we then chose the agro-climatic region as the main spatial scale for our study.

Second, we tested all combinations of model terms and compared the corresponding average marginal effects of wind speed anomaly. All tested combinations include wind speed anomaly(W) as a basic term, and the other non-interactive terms rotate over all combinations with the rule that each of the temperature-, precipitation-, and year-based terms should be selected in the equation at least once. For example, the temperature-related term includes T and T2, and their individual (T or T2) and simultaneous (T + T2) inclusion in the equation could be three cases. All potential combinations would be in total 27 variations (3 × 3 × 3 = 27). In addition to the non-interactive terms, we also tested over the equation variations with weather interactive terms including W·T, W·T2, W·P, W·P2, T·P, T·P2, and T2·P. The total variations would be 127 (C71+C72+C73+C74+C75+C76+C77=127). Moreover, we tested an extended model with additional variables including VPD, solar radiation, and CO2 concentration. Table S2 shows the comparison of the coefficients from the baseline model and the extended model. The range of the average marginal effects of wind speed from these tests could reflect how robust the wind effect is to model term variations.

Third, we conducted a placebo check, which was widely used in studies on climate and agriculture89,96,97, on the regional scale. We reshuffled the yield and weather data’s county and year, and then estimated the coefficients within the reshuffled datasets. The distinct difference between the estimated coefficients from the original dataset and those from the reshuffled dataset would suggest that the weather-yield relation exists not by chance. The robustness checks conducted in this study affirm that the statistical model yields a reliable estimation of the aggregate effects of long-term, large-scale wind speed change on maize yield. However, the present analytical framework remains limited in its capacity to investigate more detailed yet mechanistically important questions, such as diurnal variations in wind effects (day versus night), seasonal dependencies across growth stages, or region-specific responses. Future research should develop advanced modeling tools to explore these critical mechanisms.

Estimate the wind trend effect with crop model simulation data

We repeated the modeling process of wind effect estimation with the data from the Global Gridded Crop Model Intercomparison (GGCMI, phase 3) to evaluate whether the regression model’s finding still holds true with other data and models. We need to use GGCMI’s simulated maize yield, phenology, and weather data. Considering the data availability, we selected the direct human forcing scenario “2015soc”, the observation-based climate-related forcing scenario “obsclim”, and the climate-forcing reanalysis dataset GSWP3-W5E5 in the time span from 1980 to 2016 for our estimation. The GGCMI dataset provides simulated maize yield from eight crop models (PEPIC, EPIC-IIASA, SIMPLACE-LINTUL5, LDNDC, ISAM, PROMET, pDSSAT, and LPJmL) at 0.5° × 0.5° spatial resolution. We filtered the weather variables within the maize growing season and aggregated the weather and yield to yearly county means. We also repeated the analysis with GGCMI’s weather data (GSWP3-W5E5) and this study’s observation-based yield data. The GGCMI’s wind data did not show a significant trend during our study period (Fig. S7), but we still stick with the year 2011 as the turning point to be comparable with the previous estimations.
