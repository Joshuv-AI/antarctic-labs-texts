Anti-NMDA receptor encephalitis outcomes in a population-based cohort
Authors: Viridiana Hernandez-Lopez, Jessica B. Smith, Samir Alsalek, Jason N. Scott, Kathryn B. Schwarzmann, Sakar Budhathoki, Annette Langer-Gould
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Background and objectivePrior studies of anti–N-methyl-D-aspartate receptor encephalitis (anti-NMDARE) may have overestimated poor long-term outcomes and relapse rates and underestimated mortality because they were conducted in referral centers. Our objective was to evaluate these outcomes in a population-based cohort.MethodsWe performed a retrospective study of patients with new-onset anti-NMDARE between 2009 and 2022 from the population-based membership of Kaiser Permanente Southern California. All covariates and outcomes were obtained from electronic health records through December 31, 2024.ResultsSeventy-two patients with NMDARE (n = 49, 68% 2014–2022) were identified; of whom 65.3% were female, the median age was 24.5 years, and only 8.3% identified as white, non-Hispanic. First-line immunotherapy was initiated early (median 19 days from symptom onset) and 46 (63.8%) received second-line therapy with rituximab alone (n = 32) or in combination with cyclophosphamide (n = 13). After a median follow-up of 4.7 years (IQR 2.9–9.0), favorable outcomes (modified Rankin Scale ≤ 2) improved from 22% at hospital discharge to 84.5% at the most recent follow-up. Relapses were uncommon (4.1%). Overall mortality declined from 17.4% in 2009–2013 to 4.1% in 2014–2022. Initiation of rituximab within 2 months of symptom onset, increased from 63.6% in 2009–2013 to 82.9% in 2014–2022.DiscussionIn this population-based cohort of new-onset NMDARE patients, the risk of relapses was lower and long-term functional outcomes were better than previously reported. Mortality was high before 2014 and declined significantly thereafter, paralleling the increase in rapid escalation to rituximab in patients with inadequate responses to first-line treatments.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1007/s00415-026-13990-z.

Introduction

Anti–N-methyl-D-aspartate receptor encephalitis (anti-NMDARE) is a rare autoimmune disorder characterized by severe neuropsychiatric symptoms and disability during the acute phase, often requiring prolonged hospitalization, followed by gradual recovery over months to years [1–3]. Prior multicenter studies have demonstrated that although many patients ultimately achieve favorable long-term outcomes, a subset experience persistent deficits and/or relapse [2–9].

More recent studies have advocated for earlier and concurrent use of first and second-line therapies, particularly rituximab, which has been associated with improved outcomes and reduced relapse rates [4, 5]. However, the existing literature is derived from tertiary referral centers, which are enriched for patients with severe disease and provide advanced specialty care, potentially limiting generalizability [2–10]. As a result, the frequency of relapses, long-term recovery, and mortality among newly onset patients in the general population remain uncertain. Additionally, most outcome-focused studies were derived from large multicenter cohorts conducted in Europe and Asia [2–8, 10, 11]. No anti-NMDARE prognostic studies have been conducted in population-based samples, and none in racially and ethnically diverse US populations [12]. We therefore sought to characterize long-term outcomes, relapse rates, and treatment patterns in our population-based incidence cohort of pediatric and adult patients with anti-NMDARE [13].

Methods

This retrospective cohort study was approved by the Institutional Review Board at Kaiser Permanente Southern California (KPSC) with a waiver of informed consent due to the absence of direct patient contact.

KPSC is an integrated healthcare delivery system providing comprehensive care to more than 4.8 million racially, ethnically, and socioeconomically diverse members broadly representative of the Southern California population [13].

Figure 1 depicts the cohort assembly. Patients diagnosed with anti-NMDARE between January 1, 2009, and December 31, 2022, were identified using an electronic health record search strategy previously described [13]. Briefly, cases were identified by searching notes for the text-strings NMDA and encephalitis within the same encounter. This was followed by manual chart review to confirm patients met diagnostic criteria for definite anti-NMDARE (n = 65, 64 based on positive cerebrospinal fluid antibodies and,1 serum positive confirmed on tissue immunohistochemistry) or probable anti-NMDARE (n = 7, all with unavailable CSF results, 5 of whom had positive serum antibodies) [6, 14]. All patients diagnosed with anti-NMDARE who were current or former KPSC members during the study period were included. Clinical data were obtained through manual review of the electronic health record through December 31, 2024. Extracted variables included demographic characteristics, clinical presentation, immunotherapy exposure, relapse events, disability and mortality. Relapse was defined as the new onset or worsening of neurologic symptoms following a period of at least two months of clinical improvement or stability.

Treatment during the initial hospitalization was categorized as first-line therapy alone, stepwise escalation, or conjunctive escalation. Stepwise escalation was defined as the initiation of second-line therapy after a trial of first-line therapy due to inadequate response or clinical worsening. Conjunctive therapy was defined as initiation of first-line and second-line concurrently or in close temporal proximity at the start of treatment. First-line therapies were defined as corticosteroids, intravenous immunoglobulin (IVIG), and/or plasmapheresis (PLEX). Second-line therapies were defined as rituximab, cyclophosphamide, and mycophenolate mofetil.

Functional outcomes were assessed using the modified Rankin Scale (mRS) and the Clinical Assessment Scale in Autoimmune Encephalitis (CASE) based on clinical documentation at hospital discharge and at the most recent follow-up. A favorable outcome was defined as mRS ≤ 2 and CASE ≤ 9 [15]. The secondary outcomes included return to work or school status at the most recent available follow-up which were categorized as follows: 1) return to full capacity or resumption without accommodation or assistance; 2) return with reduced responsibilities or need for accommodations; or 3) inability to return, which included both temporary and permanent limitations.

Descriptive statistics were used to summarize demographic characteristics, treatment patterns, and clinical outcomes. In accordance with mRS and CASE scoring, patients who died were classified as mRS score = 6 and CASE score = 22 at hospital discharge and last follow-up. One patient who was lost to follow-up after hospital discharge was excluded from long-term outcome analyses. Continuous variables were reported as medians with interquartile ranges and compared using the Mann–Whitney U test. Categorical variables were compared using χ2 or Fisher’s exact tests as appropriate. All analyses were performed using SAS software version 9.4 (SAS Institute Inc., Cary, NC). The small number of unfavorable outcomes precluded multivariable analyses.

Results

The median age at diagnosis of the 20 pediatric patients (< 18 years) was 9.3 years (IQR 6.6–13.2), and 28.8 years (IQR 22.7–35.5) among the 52 adults. Serum antibody testing was positive in 29 of 36 patients (80.6%) tested and CSF antibody testing was positive in 100% of the 64 patients tested. Treatment patterns

All but one patient (n = 71) received one or more first-line therapies. Forty-two (58.3%) patients had an inadequate response to first-line treatments, and were escalated to rituximab with or without cyclophosphamide, and 4 (5.6%) received rituximab and first-line therapies concomitantly. Among the 46 (63.8%) rituximab-treated patients, the median time to rituximab initiation was 37 days (IQR 27.5–43). Early rituximab initiation, within 2 months of symptom onset, increased from 63.6% in 2009–2013 to 82.9% in 2014–2022. Compared with patients treated with first-line therapy only, rituximab-treated patients were more likely to receive IVIG (86.9% vs 50%, p = 0.001) and PLEX (45.7% vs 27%, p = 0.14). Rituximab-treated patients also received first-line therapy earlier (median days 17 vs 27 days), although this difference did not reach statistical significance (p = 0.06).

Outcomes

Outcomes at hospital discharge (n = 72) and at most recent follow-up (n = 71), stratified by long-term functional status, are shown in Table 2. After a median hospital stay of 22.5 days, during which 20.8% required feeding tubes and 4.7% were intubated, favorable outcomes were observed in 16 (22%) patients by mRS (≤ 2), and in 45 patients (62.5%) by CASE score (≤ 9). Rituximab-treated patients had a longer hospital length of stay (median = 34 days, IQR 22–61) compared with those treated with first-line therapies only (median = 18 days, IQR 6–30; p = 0.001). However, functional outcomes at time of discharge were similar between rituximab-treated and those treated with first-line therapies only, with median mRS scores of 4.0 in both groups (p = 0.55) and median CASE scores of 7 and 7.5, respectively (p = 0.83). At most recent follow-up, after a median duration of 4.7 years (IQR 2.9–9.0) from symptom onset, favorable outcomes were observed in 84.5% (n = 60) by mRS ≤ 2 and in 91.5% (n = 65) by CASE ≤ 9. Favorable mRS outcomes (≤ 2) were more common among patients with disease onset in 2014 or later (42/47, 89.4%) compared to those with onset in 2013 or earlier (17/23, 73.9%, p = 0.11). Secondary measures of recovery were available for 62 patients. Among these, 74% returned to work or school at full capacity, 8.1% returned with reduced responsibilities or required accommodation, and 14.5% were unable to return, either temporarily or permanently.

Mortality, permanent disability, and relapses

Table 3 provides detailed descriptions of patients with death, persistent NMDARE-related deficits, or relapse. This included 6 (8.3%) patients who died, 5 (6.9%) survivors with persistent NMDARE-related deficits, including 1 of whom relapsed, and 2 additional patients who relapsed but achieved full functional recoveries. Deaths occurred at a median of 11.7 months after symptom onset. Although the immediate causes of death varied, all were attributable to long-term complications of NMDARE, including chronic respiratory failure, prolonged ventilator dependence, pneumonia, pulmonary embolism, and airway complications related to chronic tracheostomy. Notably, mortality declined from 17.4% in 2009–2013 to 4.1% in 2014–2022, and four of the six deaths occurred in patients with disease onset in 2013 or earlier. Among earlier cases, one patient died after hospital discharge from tracheostomy-related complications despite signs of clinical improvement, while another was discharged with an mRS of 5 after receiving only corticosteroids. Of the two deaths in patients with more recent disease onset, one involved a substantial diagnostic delay where misdiagnoses resulted in more than 60 days before initiation of first-line therapy and approximately six months before initiation of second-line therapy. Among patients with persistent deficits, the most common were memory impairment, psychiatric symptoms, and/or epilepsy. Relapses were uncommon, occurring in 3 patients (4.1%), with a median time to relapse of 10.2 months from symptom onset, and all were initially diagnosed in 2020–2022. During the NMDARE onset hospitalization, all patients received first-line immunotherapy followed by escalation to second-line therapy. Treatment timing during the initial hospitalization varied, with initiation of first-line therapy ranging from 4 to 137 days and second-line therapy from 5 to 187 days after symptom onset. At most recent follow-up, all three patients had improved functional status; however, persistent cognitive and psychiatric symptoms remained in two patients (mRS ≥ 2).

Factors associated with poor long-term outcomes

Older age, abnormal brain MRIs, elevated CSF protein, and lack of tumor removal (when present) were associated with poor long-term outcomes (Table 1). Among the 23 tumors identified, 21 were ovarian teratomas, one was a pancreatic dermoid cyst, and one was a pulmonary mass. The pulmonary mass was not resected because the patient declined further intervention and passed away shortly thereafter (Table 3). Higher mRS and CASE scores at discharge, as well as the need for feeding tube support at discharge, were also associated with poor outcomes (Table 2).

Race and ethnicity, sex, clinical symptoms at onset and EEG abnormalities were not associated with worse long-term outcomes defined by either mRS (Table 1) or CASE score (Table 4). Crude comparisons of rituximab treatment or treatment strategy with long-term outcomes showed no statistically significant association. No factors were associated with relapses, likely due to the small number of events. Discussion

In this population-based cohort of racially and ethnically diverse pediatric and adult patients with anti-NMDARE, we found that relapse was uncommon (4.1%), and most patients (84.5%) achieved favorable long-term recovery, despite substantial disability during the acute phase. Compared to prior reports from tertiary referral centers, these findings demonstrate lower relapse rates and more favorable long-term outcomes, particularly among patients with disease onset after 2013 [2–10]. This shift paralleled the marked increase in the frequency and earlier initiation rituximab in our system after 2013. Although Black and Asian races have been previously associated with higher disease incidence, we did not find an association with long-term prognosis [13]. These findings suggest that outcomes reported by tertiary centers may over-represent severe disease, and anti-NMDARE outcomes may be more favorable than previously reported.

Relapse was infrequent (4%) in our cohort and lower than the 9–13% reported in prior studies [5, 16–18]. Several factors likely account for this difference. First, immunotherapy was initiated relatively early in our study, with a median time from symptom onset to first-line therapy of 19 days, compared to reported ranges of 20–75 days in the literature [16, 17, 19, 20]. Early immunotherapy initiation, particularly within 30 days, has been consistently associated with reduced relapse risk [16]. Second, rituximab was utilized in 63% of cases, exceeding the 20–37% reported in other studies at both time points [1, 5, 16, 17]. This upturn reflects practice patterns within our system, favoring earlier rituximab initiation, even in milder disease. Rituximab use has been associate with fewer relapses although the relapse rates varied with some reporting rates as high as 16% with comparable rituximab use [4] and others with similar treatment timing finding lower rates at 7%. Higher rituximab use among patients who did not relapse compared to those who did (72% vs 40%) has also been reported [18]. These inconsistencies are probably due, at least in part, to confounding by indication (i.e. that sicker patients with inadequate responses to first-line therapies were treated with rituximab) which no studies, including ours, have adequately accounted for. Yet the overall pattern of findings reinforce the association between earlier immunotherapy and reduced relapse risk [21]. Third, differences in cohort composition and study design may have influenced rates. Tertiary referral centers may preferentially capture patients with relapses [16, 17]. In addition, the increased awareness of the disease since it was first described in 2007, along with the broader availability of cell-based antibody assays over time, may have led to the detection of milder cases that would have gone unrecognized in earlier years. Insufficient follow-up is not a likely contributor to the low relapse rate we observed as the median follow-up was 4 years and most relapses occur within the first 2 years.

The recovery trajectory in this cohort is consistent with the well-described pattern in NMDARE- potentially severe disability initially followed by slow meaningful improvement that occurs over months [5]. Short-term outcomes at discharge in our cohort were worse with only 22% achieving a favorable mRS compared to 56% in the literature [19]. Yet 83% achieving favorable long-term outcomes consistent with previous reports ranging from 70 to 90%, particularly in cohorts treated early and with second-line agents [3, 5, 16]. Favorable outcomes were more pronounced after 2014, a trend also observed in other studies [4, 16, 17]. Patients who received rituximab generally had greater disease severity and required longer hospitalizations, yet their long-term functional outcomes were comparable to those with less severe presentations who were not treated with rituximab or other second-line agents. Thus, our findings provide additional data that escalation of immunotherapy may help mitigate the effects of disease severity and support meaningful long-term recovery.

Mortality in our cohort was high at 17.4% in 2009–2013 and dropped substantially to 4.1% in 2014–2022 more aligned with prior tertiary center studies reporting 2–11%.[5, 19, 22–24]. We think this is most likely due to increased recognition of the protracted recovery period in NMDARE leading to a lower likelihood of premature withdrawal of intensive supportive care and the importance of early escalation to second-line immunotherapies in our system. Among the earlier deaths, one patient died from inadequate supportive care after hospital discharge despite signs of clinical improvement, and another was discharged with an mRS of 5 after receiving only corticosteroids. Both scenarios would be unlikely today.

We had hypothesized that CASE score would be more sensitive at detecting clinically meaningful impairment than mRS based on the results of prior studies [25–27]. However, in our cohort, CASE classified fewer patients as having unfavorable outcomes at follow-up compared to mRS, despite using a low threshold of (> 10) for unfavorable outcomes. This discrepancy may be due to the small number of patients with persistent disability, limiting the ability of CASE to discriminate between outcome groups. Additionally, assessing CASE scores from electronic health records presents inherent challenges, as many components of the assessment are documented primarily when specific concerns arise rather than as part of the standard assessment. Although validation studies have also been retrospective, our experience suggests that CASE may have limited performance in retrospective studies and is better suited for prospective studies [25–27].

Consistent with prior studies, we found that older age and lack of tumor removal are also associated with worse outcomes [5, 16, 28]. While we previously reported that the risk of NMDARE is highest in Black followed by Asian people [13], we found no association with NMDARE outcomes, a novel finding. This study did not specifically examine factors previously associated with worse outcomes including decreased consciousness at presentation, delayed treatment initiation, older age at onset, higher CASE scores at admission (> 22.5), or poor early clinical response, all of which are limitations [3, 28]. We also did not evaluate outcomes at specific time-dependent intervals (e.g. annually) or assess time to maximum recovery.

Additional limitations should be considered. NMDARE is rare, and poor long-term outcomes were uncommon; thus, we were underpowered to evaluate independent prognostic factors using multivariable analyses. Additionally, clinical data were obtained through retrospective review of the electronic health record. Assessments were recorded by multiple physicians across different specialties throughout the course of care, which may introduce variability in how and which clinical findings were documented and interpreted. Despite these limitations, the low number of poor outcomes also reflects an important strength of this cohort, as most patients ultimately achieved favorable long-term recovery in the context of frequent escalation to rituximab.

Other strengths include that, to-date, this is the only population-based description of anti-NMDARE outcomes from a racially and ethnically diverse population. Patients were identified within a large integrated healthcare system, allowing characterization of outcomes across a broader clinical spectrum. The long duration of follow-up also allowed for more accurate characterization of long-term recovery, relapses, and mortality, which may be underestimated in studies with shorter follow-up or those limited to neurology clinic records.

In conclusion, findings from this population-based study are reassuring for newly diagnosed NMDARE patients and their families. NMDARE patients experienced better long-term outcomes, lower risk of relapses, and, in contemporary patients, lower mortality rates than previously reported by specialty referral centers or compared with our 2009–2013 mortality rates. Future studies to clarify whether intensive immunotherapies can further decrease mortality or lead to faster recoveries are needed.
