A bibliometric analysis of research trends and future directions in early detection of pancreatic cancer
Authors: Beibei Wu, Rui Li, Mengmeng Wang, Xuejie Wang, Chen Qiao, Ding Luo, Jian Liu
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundResearch on the early detection of pancreatic cancer has grown rapidly in recent years; however, existing bibliometric studies in this field have focused on broad research landscapes or treatment modalities, with no systematic analysis specifically mapping the knowledge structure and emerging frontiers of early detection.MethodsLiterature published between January 1, 1986, and December 31, 2025, was retrieved from the Web of Science Core Collection database. Co-occurrence analysis, cluster analysis, and burst analysis were carried out using bibliometric tools such as VOSviewer, CiteSpace, and R-bibliometrix to evaluate publication trends, main contributors, and the dynamic evolution of the research topic.ResultsThis study included original papers and reviews (n = 7,353). The analysis reveals that the volume of publications has shown a strong upward trend since 2004. The United States dominates global output; Johns Hopkins University emerged as the institution with the largest number of publications and the highest number of citations; Pancreas is the most productive journal in this field, while the American Journal of Surgical Pathology received the highest total citations. Six keyword clusters were identified: molecular biology and biomarkers, imaging and endoscopic techniques, pathological classification of mucinous neoplasms, precancerous lesion characteristics, clinical management strategies, and epidemiology/risk factors/artificial intelligence. Current research hotspots focus on the surveillance of high-risk populations (new-onset diabetes, genetic susceptibility syndromes, and precancerous lesions), as well as innovative screening models, including multi-omics liquid biopsies, artificial intelligence, and cyst fluid molecular classifiers. In addition, the study identified barriers to clinical translation in this field, such as insufficient research on cost-effectiveness and psychological outcomes, alongside inadequate funding.ConclusionThe field of early detection of pancreatic cancer is shifting from passive, single-modality imaging to active, risk-assessment-based multimodal surveillance. In the future, large-scale prospective population trials are still necessary, and cost-benefit assessments alongside patient-reported psychological outcomes remain critical to accelerate the clinical application of multi-omics marker platforms and AI models.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at https://doi.org/10.1007/s12672-026-05362-w.

Introduction

Pancreatic carcinoma (PC), although relatively uncommon, has a notably poor prognosis. According to GLOBOCAN 2022, an estimated 511,000 new cases and 467,000 deaths occurred worldwide, ranking 12th in incidence but 6th in cancer-related mortality [1]. In the United States, the overall 5-year survival rate remains approximately 12%–13% [2], and PC is projected to become the second leading cause of cancer-related death by 2030 [3].

Survival rates for PC are closely linked to disease stage [4], and recent advances in neoadjuvant therapy and early surgical intervention have improved overall survival [5, 6]. However, because early-stage PC is usually asymptomatic, most patients are diagnosed at an advanced stage [4, 7].

The predominant histological subtype, pancreatic ductal adenocarcinoma (PDAC), accounts for approximately 90% of malignant pancreatic tumors [8]. Early detection of pancreatic cancer is associated with three precursor lesions: pancreatic intraepithelial neoplasias, intraductal papillary mucinous neoplasms, and mucinous cystic neoplasms (MCN) [9]. Given the relatively low incidence of PDAC, broad-based screening programs targeting the general population are not feasible. Current screening strategies primarily focus on periodic clinical evaluation of high-risk individuals, using imaging modalities [10] such as endoscopic ultrasound (EUS), magnetic resonance imaging (MRI), magnetic resonance cholangiopancreatography (MRCP), and biomarker assays such as CA19-9 [11]. Identified high-risk factors include hereditary syndromes, family history, new-onset diabetes (NOD), pre-existing diabetes, advanced age, smoking, obesity, and a history of chronic pancreatitis [12].

Recent evidence indicates that surveillance of high-risk individuals can shift diagnosis to earlier stages and improve long-term survival in some patients [13–15]. Studies also report an increased proportion of patients diagnosed with stage IA PDAC and improved overall survival between 2004 and 2016, largely due to advances in early detection [4].

Despite these advances, early detection of PC still faces several challenges, including the need to further assess screening efficacy, improve biomarker diagnostic accuracy, and clarify genetic susceptibility mutations associated with PC. Addressing these challenges requires sustained research efforts to improve current early detection strategies.

Bibliometrics uses quantitative methods to analyze the volume of articles, citation patterns, and research trends in various scientific fields [16]. Although bibliometric studies have been conducted on colorectal cancer screening biomarkers [17], gastric cancer endoscopic diagnosis [18], and the broader pancreatic cancer research landscape [19], no study has specifically mapped the knowledge structure of early detection of pancreatic cancer. It is worth noting that the analysis of about 60,000 pancreatic cancer publications found that innovative early diagnosis techniques only accounted for 1%-2% of the total literature, and this critical subset has never been systematically analyzed [19]. This research aims to fill this gap by using a variety of bibliometric methods to describe publication trends, keyword evolution, citation networks, and collaboration patterns in the early detection of pancreatic cancer. Through macro-level analysis, we identified core research topics, milestone studies, current hotspots, and future directions, providing useful references for researchers and policymakers in this field [20–22].

Methods

Data sources and collection

To minimize potential bias caused by database updates, all raw data for this study were extracted from the Web of Science Core Collection database on a single day (March 22, 2026). Recognizing that “topic” searches may inadvertently capture irrelevant literature, the search strategy was restricted to title, author keywords, and keywords plus, informed by previous bibliometric studies [23] and the MeSH database. The detailed search strategy is presented in Table 1. The data collection period spanned from January 1, 1986, to December 31, 2025. Only “articles” and “reviews” published in English were included. The export format was restricted to “plain text”, with the record content specified as “full record and cited references”. To facilitate granular analyses by country and year, bibliometric datasets for the top-ranking nations (by publication volume) and publications from the most recent two years (2024–2025) were exported on the same day. Additionally, funding-related data were retrieved via the Web of Science Core Collection online analysis platform.

Bibliometric analysis and visualization

Microsoft Excel 2019 was used to generate line graphs, pie charts, and tables to identify annual publication trends, pivotal authors, top journals, and funding sources.

CiteSpace (version 6.1.R6) was employed for citation burst analysis and journal overlay mapping. The time slice was set to 1, with all other parameters maintained at their default settings.

VOSviewer (version 1.6.18) was utilized to construct and visualize co-occurrence networks of countries, institutions, journals, authors, and keywords. It was also used to apply network layer overlays to highlight prevailing research themes across significant phases and countries. All parameters and values were set to the software’s default configuration.

The bibliometrix R-package (version 4.3.0) and RStudio were employed to analyze international collaborations, core journals, and prolific authors. Furthermore, this tool was used to generate trend topics and annual publication dynamics relevant to countries, institutions, and journals. Metrics such as the H-index and M-index were computed for journals and authors. The corresponding code is provided in Supplementary Material 1.

Scimago Graphica (version 1.0.35) was utilized to generate a global map and institutional network, illustrating the aggregate publications from different countries and organizations. For the country co-occurrence map, the layout was set to “map”, with label selections set to “document” and “citation”, label placement configured as “Radial”, and the color palette designated by “citation”. For the institutional co-occurrence map, the layout preference was set to “circular”.

Results

Annual publishing trend

We compiled a dataset of articles and reviews (n = 7,353) published between 1986 and 2025. The clear upward trend in publications reflects growing interest in this research field. Overall, annual publication volume can be divided into three developmental stages. In the initial phase (1986–2003), output remained limited, increasing modestly from 1 to 43 publications. In the subsequent period (2004–2019), the output increased significantly from 66 to 404 publications. From 2020 to 2025, the rate of increase moderated, with annual output stabilizing between 483 and 530 publications (Fig. 1A). To identify current research hotspots, we analyzed the proportional contributions of publications (n = 1,042) from the most recent two years (2024–2025) (Fig. 1B). The results show that research during this period was concentrated in Cluster 1, Cluster 3, and Cluster 6 (detailed cluster information is provided in Sect. 3.3, Keyword Analysis). Prominent keywords are primarily focused on cutting-edge diagnostic technologies (e.g., artificial intelligence, deep learning, liquid biopsy, circulating tumor DNA, exosomes, and pancreatoscopy), risk factors (including NOD and obesity), and clinical management strategies (such as risk stratification and the Fukuoka guidelines).

Countries

A total of 110 nations have actively contributed to this research field. The USA leads in publications (n = 2,717; citations = 137,413; total link strength = 1,347), well ahead of all other countries (Fig. 2A). International collaboration is relatively limited, with a modest international co-authorship rate of 16.48% (Fig. 2B). Notably, collaboration between the United States and Italy stands out as a prominent partnership (n = 140).

Analysis of annual publication volumes across various nations reveals distinct timelines regarding their respective contributions (Fig. 2C). The USA has maintained a consistently high output throughout the entire study period (Fig. 2D). Japan began to intensify its research efforts around 2005; however, its publication volume has slightly declined over the past three years. In comparison, Japan’s research emphasis is primarily directed toward Clusters 2, 3, 4, and 5 (Fig. 2E), whereas Chinese scholars focus predominantly on Cluster 1 (Fig. 2F).

Institutions

A total of 5,857 institutions were included in this analysis. The top 10 most productive institutions are predominantly located in the United States. Johns Hopkins University ranked first in both publication output and total citations. Notably, the University of Amsterdam, the Technical University of Munich, Wayne State University, and the University of Kiel published relatively few works but ranked among the top 20 for total citations (Table 2). In contrast, Ohio State University and Sungkyunkwan University showed high output but relatively low citation rates (Fig. 3A).

Figure 3B illustrates the collaborative network, revealing five major regional clusters. The red cluster is dominated by U.S. institutions (e.g., Johns Hopkins University, Massachusetts General Hospital, and MD Anderson Cancer Center); this cluster features the largest nodes and the densest linkages, positioning it as the central hub of the global collaboration network. The green cluster comprises top Japanese institutions (e.g., Tohoku University, Kyushu University, the University of Tokyo, and the National Cancer Center, Japan). The light blue cluster centers on major South Korean medical institutions (e.g., Seoul National University, University of Ulsan, and Yonsei University), while the dark blue cluster is represented by prominent European medical centers (e.g., University of Verona and Technical University of Munich). The yellow cluster consists of leading Chinese research universities (e.g., Zhejiang University, Fudan University, and the Chinese Academy of Medical Sciences).

Journals

The research spans 1,117 journals. In terms of publication output, Pancreas ranked first (n = 288). However, the journal with the highest total citation count was the American Journal of Surgical Pathology (TC = 10,972), followed by Gastroenterology (TC = 10,555) and Pancreatology (TC = 10,408). Among the top 10 most productive journals, the impact factor consistently remains below 10. Conversely, prestigious journals such as The Lancet, Cancer Cell, Nature Reviews Disease Primers, Nature, and Genes & Development have contributed fewer than 10 publications each, yet they ranked among the top 20 for total citations. Although journals such as Cancers, Diagnostics, Frontiers in Oncology, Cureus Journal of Medical Science, Abdominal Radiology, and the International Journal of Surgery Case Reports have published a substantial volume of literature in this field, their total citation counts remain relatively low. Notably, Cancers and Diagnostics rank first and second in the m-index, with their relevant publications starting in 2018, positioning them as emerging sources in this field (Table 3). According to Bradford’s Law, 31 core journals were identified (Fig. 4A). Figure 4B illustrates the early and sustained substantial contributions of Pancreas and Pancreatology in this domain, whereas Cancers and Diagnostics began publishing in this field only in 2018. Co-citation analysis identifies 12,879 co-cited journals, with the Annals of Surgery in the lead (co-citations = 11,025) (Fig. 4C). The majority of the most productive and highly cited journals are classified within the Q1 and Q2 quartiles (Table 4). Finally, journal overlay maps indicate that publications centered on molecular biology and genetics are commonly cited by medicine, medical, clinical, and molecular biology/immunology journals; similarly, journals emphasizing health, nursing, and medicine receive frequent citations from these same medicine, medical, clinical, and molecular biology/immunology journals (Fig. 4D).

Keyword analysis

Keywords are central to summarizing research findings. Among 1,904 keywords occurring five or more times, the main focus of early screening for pancreatic cancer is on PDAC, IPMN, cystic neoplasms, and PanIN. This screening relies on EUS, CT, MRI, liquid biopsy, endoscopic retrograde cholangiopancreatography, “fine-needle-aspiration”, positron emission tomography, and tumor markers; additionally, “artificial intelligence” and “machine learning” can assist in the early identification of lesions. The research primarily targets individuals at high risk, with risk factors primarily encompassing hereditary pancreatitis, chronic pancreatitis, family history, diabetes mellitus, germline mutations (such as BRCA2 and KRAS mutations), cigarette smoking, body mass index, and Peutz-Jeghers syndrome. The “management” and “surveillance” of high-risk populations are essential (Fig. 7A and B). Conversely, keywords related to cost-effectiveness or psychological impact—such as “quality-of-life” (occurrences = 69), “cost-effectiveness” (occurrences = 19), “risk perception” (occurrences = 6), “quality” (occurrences = 6), and “health economics” (occurrences = 5)—appeared less frequently, with only 92 publications involving these keywords. Recent high-frequency and burst keywords mainly involve detection methods, including “liquid biopsy” (“extracellular vesicles”, “circulating tumor DNA”), “biosensors”, “tumor microbiome”, “artificial intelligence”, “machine learning”, and “deep learning” (Fig. 7C). Furthermore, summarizing and categorizing keywords related to molecular pathways (occurrences ≥ 5; classification provided in the Supplementary Material 1) revealed that the RAS-BRAF-MAPK pathway ranked first (occurrences = 631), followed by TP53 (occurrences = 148), both of which have continuously appeared since 1994. DNA damage repair pathways (BRCA1/2, PALB2) have steadily accumulated since 2006, reflecting the continuous deepening of research on genetic susceptibility; the frequency of the GNAS pathway has climbed significantly since 2013; and the Wnt/β-catenin, PI3K/AKT/mTOR, and NF-κB pathways have gradually become active since 2010, suggesting expanding research interest in tumor progression and microenvironment regulation. The total frequency of related keywords has experienced an overall jump since 2013, indicating the progressive deepening of molecular mechanism research in this field.

Keyword clustering grouped the high-frequency terms into six knowledge domains relevant to the early detection of pancreatic cancer: Cluster 1 (red cluster): molecular biology and biomarkers of pancreatic cancer. Cluster 2 (green cluster): imaging and endoscopic techniques. Cluster 3 (blue cluster): pathological classification and differential diagnosis of mucinous neoplasms. Cluster 4 (yellow/secondary cluster): pathological characteristics of precancerous lesions. Cluster 5: clinical management strategies and guidelines. Cluster 6 (cyan cluster): epidemiology, risk factors, and artificial intelligence. As illustrated in Supplementary Material 1, differences exist in keyword clustering between PDAC and its precursor lesions, including intraductal papillary mucinous neoplasm (IPMN), MCN, and pancreatic intraepithelial neoplasia (PanIN). Keywords associated with PDAC (accounting for 25% of the literature) are more concentrated in Cluster 1 and Cluster 6. Keywords for IPMN (accounting for 33% of the literature) are mainly distributed across Cluster 2, Cluster 4, and Cluster 5. MCN (accounting for 69% of the literature) is primarily distributed (over 70%) across Clusters 2, 3, 4, and 5, with considerable distribution in Clusters 1 and 6 as well. Keywords for PanIN (accounting for approximately 8% of the literature) are almost entirely concentrated in Cluster 1, with a low proportion in the remaining clusters.

Moreover, trend topic and burst analyses illustrate how core keywords in this field have evolved (Fig. 7D), from initial focuses such as “cystadenoma”, “codon-12 mutations”, “KRAS mutations”, and “p53” (which represent macroscopic pathological morphologies and basic molecular genetics), to well-defined precancerous lesions such as “intraductal papillary-mucinous neoplasm” (IPMN) and “pancreatic intraepithelial neoplasia” (PanIN), along with their “surgical resection” and “differential diagnosis”, and subsequently to a focus on the follow-up management of high-risk populations, including “international consensus guidelines”, “Fukuoka guidelines”, “surveillance”, and “management”. Initially, high-risk groups were primarily centered on familial pancreatic cancer and chronic pancreatitis populations; however, there has been a growing emphasis on NOD populations in recent years (Fig. 7E). At the technological level, the focus transitioned from initial diagnostic techniques such as “helical CT”, “MR cholangiopancreatography”, “fine-needle aspiration”, and “ERCP”, to non-invasive methods like “liquid biopsy”, “extracellular vesicles”, and “circulating tumor DNA”, further evolving into recent advancements like “artificial intelligence”, “machine learning”, and “deep learning” (Fig. 7F).

Seminal literature and landmark research

The identification of seminal publications in early pancreatic cancer detection is based on local citations within the medical field (Table 7). Highly cited works are mainly clinical management guidelines, led by the 2012 Fukuoka guidelines, followed by the 2006 Sendai consensus guidelines and the 2017 revised Fukuoka guidelines. In addition to these guidelines, highly cited publications also focus on the pathological classification systems of relevant precancerous lesions. In 2001, Hruban et al. formally established the nomenclature and grading system for PanIN [26], which was further integrated with IPMN into an illustrated consensus classification in 2004 [27]. Also in 2004, Adsay et al. systematically described the pathological and biological characteristics of different epithelial types in IPMN, proposing the concept of an “intestinal pathway of carcinogenesis” [28]; subsequently, in 2005, based on an international expert consensus meeting, Furukawa et al. formally established the four subtype classifications of IPMN (gastric, intestinal, pancreatobiliary, and oncocytic types) [29]. The 2015 Baltimore consensus then simplified the grading of PanIN and IPMN into a two-tiered system comprising low-grade and high-grade dysplasia [30]. Between 2001 and 2008, multiple large-sample surgical cohorts systematically described the clinicopathological features, predictors of malignancy (mural nodules, main pancreatic duct dilation, and cytologic atypia), and long-term postoperative prognosis of IPMN, providing evidence-based support for the formulation of surgical indications in the guidelines [31–36]. In 2011, the discovery by Wu et al. regarding the high-frequency occurrence of GNAS mutations in IPMN pioneered a new pathway for the molecular diagnosis of cyst fluid [37]. In 2015, Springer et al. further constructed a cyst classification model combining multiple genes with clinical features, significantly reducing unnecessary surgical interventions [38]. Overall, these milestone publications trace the progression from initial disease recognition and nomenclature standardization, to systematic description of clinicopathological features, and ultimately to the establishment of risk stratification and surveillance strategies, thereby substantially advancing the field of early pancreatic cancer detection.

References analysis

Co-citation refers to the joint citation of two or more scholarly articles by other academic works, reflecting their interconnectedness within the research field [39]. Our methodology uses CiteSpace for conducting co-citation analysis. The top 10 most frequently co-cited articles are all guidelines, consensuses, or recommendations. The most prominently co-cited work is the International Consensus Guidelines for IPMN (the Fukuoka guidelines). Other highly co-cited works build the knowledge base of this field across multiple dimensions, including management recommendations for high-risk populations, risk of malignant transformation and its influencing factors, and molecular diagnosis. A surge in citations is a useful indicator of growing interest or promising research directions. A substantial number of references (n = 1,166) experienced notable citation bursts, indicating that the research focus in this field has shifted from the standardization of pancreatic cystic lesion nomenclature and IPMN pathological classification toward malignant risk prediction and multiple iterative revisions of international management guidelines. In recent years, there has been an increased emphasis on novel detection methods, such as NGS molecular testing of cyst fluid (Fig. 8A). Our analysis of recent trends identified 137 references with the strongest citation surges from 2018 to 2025. These primarily concern the optimization of dynamic surveillance strategies for BD-IPMN, the clinical translation of molecular biomarkers in cyst fluid and blood, and early pancreatic cancer screening systems centered on high-risk populations. Furthermore, AI-assisted image recognition and risk prediction models based on electronic health records are emerging rapidly, indicating the frontier directions of this field (Fig. 8B).

Discussion

General information

Bibliometric analysis helps to clarify the complexity, current state, and future trends of a research field. The literature shows that early detection of pancreatic cancer is receiving increasing attention. A survey of contributions from 110 countries and regions reveals a diverse but unbalanced research pattern. The United States maintained consistently high output throughout the study period and held an important position in all clusters. Japan’s output has increased significantly since 2005, which coincided with the expansion of its EUS-based monitoring projects, but has tended to stabilize in recent years, which may be related to the relative maturity of image-based monitoring technology and the rapid rise of liquid biopsy and artificial intelligence research. China has risen rapidly in the past decade, and its contributions have focused on laboratory research fields such as liquid biopsy and biomarker discovery. However, its average citation impact is still low, indicating the need for clinical translation from basic discovery to clinical validation, and strengthening international cooperation can effectively bridge this translational gap.

Most of the top 10 institutions in terms of publication volume are located in the United States. Johns Hopkins University stands out with the highest output and the most citations, highlighting its central role in promoting early screening research. Notably, well-known institutions including Harvard University, the University of Texas System, the University of Verona, and the Mayo Clinic have also produced high-quality research. However, most inter-agency partnerships are still limited to domestic or regional collaborations. Outstanding scholars mainly come from leading American institutions such as Johns Hopkins University and Harvard University. However, this concentration may partly reflect the citation density of these institutions rather than individual academic influence alone. It is also worth noting that the main monitoring alliances (CAPS, PRECEDE, EUROPAC, etc.) mainly recruit European and North American cohorts, and the representation of low-income and middle-income countries is extremely low, which leads to the relatively insufficient representation of other racial and ethnic groups in screening evidence. Such gaps in cohort representation limit the global applicability of current screening guidelines.

Within the field, Pancreas leads in publication output, while the American Journal of Surgical Pathology and Gastroenterology lead in total citations. In addition, Cancers, Diagnostics, Frontiers in Oncology, Cureus Journal of Medical Science, Abdominal Radiology, and the International Journal of Surgery Case Reports have been recognized for their substantial contributions to the field. Cancers and Diagnostics are emerging journals in the field, and Bradford’s Law identifies 31 core journals; for researchers in this field, it is a wise choice to submit manuscripts to these publications.

The trend of funding support is roughly parallel to the development trajectory of this field. However, research funding for pancreatic cancer remains disproportionate to its substantial disease burden. Despite being the third leading cause of cancer-related mortality in the United States, fiscal year 2025 data from the National Institutes of Health (NIH) indicate an allocation of approximately $8,945 per pancreatic cancer death, compared to $69,800 per breast cancer death [40]. Insufficient funding is strongly related to the decrease in the number of clinical trials, which creates obstacles to the clinical translation of biomarker discoveries and AI models into clinically approved screening tools.

Analysis of keywords – knowledge domains

Keyword analysis quantifies the proportion of relevant molecular pathways in the field of early screening of pancreatic cancer. Consistent with the broader PDAC literature, in the early detection subset, the RAS-BRAF-MAPK and TP53 pathways ranked first and second, respectively. However, several differences also exist: as research on intraductal papillary mucinous neoplasms has deepened, GNAS has increased markedly since 2013 [41]; DNA damage repair pathways (BRCA1/2, PALB2) account for a relatively high proportion because they have a direct role in determining eligibility for genetic screening. SMAD4 is a common late-stage PDAC variant [42], so its contribution to early detection research is relatively small. These molecular insights are increasingly shaping the detection strategy. KRAS mutations in circulating tumor DNA (ctDNA) can be used as diagnostic biomarkers [43], while KRAS and GNAS mutations in cyst fluid reach 94% sensitivity and 91% specificity in identifying IPMN [44]. However, due to the minimal tumor shedding of small lesions, the sensitivity of ctDNA detection for localized PDAC is still only 48–67% [45]. This translational barrier may need to be addressed by shifting from single-analyte detection to comprehensive multi-omics liquid biopsy combinations.

In addition, keyword analysis also shows that research on the cost-effectiveness of pancreatic cancer screening and on the integration of psychological outcomes is insufficient. The existing cost-effectiveness data show a risk-dependent gradient. Screening is cost-effective for high-risk groups. The incremental cost-effectiveness ratio (ICER) reported by the CDKN2A-p16-Leiden cohort (20-year follow-up, n = 347) is only 14,000 euros/quality-adjusted life year (QALY) [46], while microsimulation modeling confirmed that under the threshold of $100,000/QALY, it was only cost-effective for STK11 (relative risk [RR] ≥ 28) and CDKN2A (RR ≥ 12) mutation carriers. For medium-risk mutations (BRCA2, ATM), the ICER exceeded $160,000/QALY; for BRCA1 (RR 2.58) and PALB2 (RR 2.33), the ICER reached $878,000 and $1,187,000/QALY, respectively [47]. For patients with NOD, Wang et al. found that risk-customized screening is cost-effective under the expected 3-year PDAC risk threshold of more than 2% (ICER under willingness to pay $100,000 [WTP] is $63,045/QALY) [48]; while a 2025 analysis of a sequential biomarker strategy for 200,000 patients with NOD shows that the specificity of biomarkers is a key determinant of cost-effectiveness [49]. The psychological literature reveals the same complex situation: a short-term study reported that after being included in the screening, participants’ concerns about cancer decreased and their anxiety levels stabilized [50, 51]; however, the latest 3-year longitudinal data showed that 68% of participants perceived increased stress, accompanied by moderate to severe distress [52]. The key issue is that few studies have incorporated this psychological impact on quality of life into cost-benefit models, and a review by the U.S. Preventive Services Task Force (USPSTF) found that only two of all the evaluated screening studies included a psychological outcome assessment [53]. Future research must fill this gap by embedding validated patient-reported outcome measures into prospective screening trials and incorporating psychological utility into economic evaluations.

The cluster analysis of PDAC and its precancerous lesions reveals differential bibliometric characteristics. PDAC research focuses on molecular characterization and liquid biopsy development for early detection; IPMN/MCN research focuses on imaging surveillance optimization and surgical decision-making; while PanIN research is still largely limited to basic science. This divergence is consistent with the observation that about 85–90% of PDAC originates from PanIN, yet PanIN cannot be detected by current imaging modalities (< 5 mm); in contrast, IPMN accounts for only 10–15% of PDAC cases [54]. Therefore, an effective early detection framework for pancreatic cancer cannot rely on a single screening strategy; it should incorporate guideline-based imaging surveillance for individuals with detectable precancerous lesions, while concurrently conducting blood-based molecular screening for high-risk groups lacking imaging characteristics.

Hotspots and frontiers

Through a comprehensive analysis encompassing keywords and core publications relevant to the discourse on early pancreatic cancer detection, along with co-cited references, we identified surveillance of high-risk cohorts (patients with precancerous lesions, familial pancreatic cancer, NOD, or cancer predisposition genes) and innovative screening modalities (including liquid biopsy, multi-omics biomarkers, and deep learning) as the main focal points of this field.

Screening pattern and high-risk cohorts

Keyword cluster 5 and co-citation analysis indicate that surveillance of high-risk individuals has remained a consistent focus throughout the development of this field [10, 55]; “surveillance” and “management” continue to appear among the high-frequency keywords through 2025. The identified high-risk factors for pancreatic cancer extend beyond hereditary genetic predispositions (germline pathogenic variants in BRCA1, BRCA2, PALB2, ATM, CDKN2A/p16, STK11, and mismatch repair genes associated with Lynch syndrome), hereditary pancreatitis (PRSS1 mutations), and familial pancreatic cancer (defined as having at least two first-degree relatives with a documented genetic link). They also include macroscopic pancreatic cystic precursor lesions, chronic and heavy alcohol consumption, smoking, obesity, and chronic pancreatitis [55]. Current investigations into populations at elevated risk predominantly focus on individuals with genetic predispositions, pancreatic cystic lesions, and NOD [56]. For the monitoring of high-risk groups, the guidelines from the CAPS Consortium (2020), ASGE (2022), AGA (2020), and NCCN (2025) generally agree that for individuals with a lifetime risk of more than 5% of PDAC, MRI/MRCP and/or EUS should be performed once a year, and the starting age of screening is 35–50 years old, depending on the specific genetic syndrome [10, 55, 57, 58]. The multicenter CAPS study provides robust evidence supporting this approach; surveillance-detected PDAC was diagnosed at stage I in 57.9% of cases (versus < 5% in unscreened SEER controls), with an impressive 5-year survival of 73.3% [15]. In addition, the CDKN2A-p16-Leiden cohort confirmed the cost-effectiveness of monitoring (ICER is 14,000 euros/QALY), which establishes a clear basis for monitoring when the lifetime risk of developing PDAC is more than 10% [46].

For branch-duct intraductal papillary mucinous neoplasms (BD-IPMNs), the clinical approach has shifted from preventive radical resection to refined dynamic surveillance. This shift is consistent with our keyword clustering, in which IPMN-related terms are predominantly distributed across Cluster 2 (imaging and endoscopic techniques), Cluster 4 (pathological characteristics of precancerous lesions), and Cluster 5 (clinical management strategies and guidelines). Recent real-world evidence and the 2024 Kyoto International Consensus Guidelines support the safe suspension of monitoring after 5 years of morphological stability in elderly patients (for example, > 75 years old and cysts < 30 mm, or > 65 years old and cysts ≤ 15 mm) [59, 60], which effectively balances cancer prevention with reducing medical costs and surgical morbidity. However, this strategy requires careful screening of patients. Japan’s long-term data (n = 732, BD-IPMN < 15 mm) shows that the cumulative incidences of cancer in the 5th, 10th, and 15th years are 2.2%, 4.6%, and 7.4%, respectively [61]. Therefore, stopping monitoring should comprehensively consider the patient’s overall risk of pancreatic cancer, age, and other relevant factors, not just the stability of the cyst.

Within the high-risk screening framework, attention to high-penetrance mutations has increased, consistent with the frequency of DNA damage repair pathway keywords (e.g., BRCA1/2, PALB2) observed in our bibliometric analysis; however, discrepancies remain among major guidelines. CAPS and AGA require BRCA1/2 carriers to have at least one first-degree relative with the disease [10, 55], while ASGE recommends screening all BRCA1/2 carriers regardless of family history [57]. NCCN recommends screening for ATM, BRCA2, STK11, and CDKN2A carriers regardless of family history, but considers the data insufficient for other gene carriers, including BRCA1, without a close family history [58]. A survey of 54 international monitoring sites by the PRECEDE Consortium in 2025 further clarified this heterogeneity: in the absence of a family history, only 44.4% and 35.2% of the monitoring sites respectively provide pancreatic cancer monitoring to BRCA2 and BRCA1 carriers, while only 13% of the sites provide monitoring for carriers of Lynch syndrome [62]. This inconsistency highlights the urgent need to establish standardized screening criteria through prospective research. Notably, a study of more than 411 asymptomatic high-risk individuals showed that the benefits of screening were greater for mutation carriers than for relatives of patients with familial pancreatic cancer [63]. Experts recommend genetic testing and counseling for relatives of patients with familial pancreatic cancer who meet specific surveillance criteria [55].

The association between newly diagnosed diabetes and pancreatic cancer has attracted increasing attention. Recent studies show that diabetes may be both a consequence of PDAC and a precursor to it [64]. The 2024 Kyoto International Consensus Guidelines include NOD and a cyst growth rate of ≥ 2.5 mm/year as worrisome features [59]. A validation study of 3,336 patients confirmed that the revised criteria effectively stratified short-term and long-term risks: compared with patients without risk factors, the subdistribution hazard ratio of patients with 3–4 worrisome features is 10.1 [65]. In addition, researchers developed the ENDPAC score, which utilizes age of onset, weight change, and blood glucose trajectories to stratify the risk of patients with NOD over the age of 50. Its area under the curve (AUC) reached 0.87 [66]. Cost-effectiveness analysis further supports this strategy: one-time CT screening for ENDPAC-positive patients with NOD was cost-effective, with an ICER of $65,076/QALY—far below the standard willingness-to-pay threshold [67].

Notably, a review of 16 global pancreatic cancer surveillance programs identified cases of interval pancreatic malignancies developing before the next scheduled annual evaluation, underscoring the need to improve diagnostic accuracy and adopt more diversified management strategies [68].

Screening methods

Contemporary screening methods for PC primarily rely on radiological examinations, including EUS, MRI, and MRCP [10]. However, the high cost, invasiveness, and limited sensitivity and specificity of conventional imaging modalities have redirected research attention toward the clinical validation of various non-invasive biomarkers [69], a transition reflected in our trend topic analysis as a temporal shift from imaging-centric keywords to non-invasive detection terms. These include circulating tumor DNA, exosomes, microRNAs, and cell-free DNA [70], with liquid biopsy emerging as a principal detection method [71]. Nevertheless, single biomarkers have inherent limitations [72–74], which has driven the field toward multi-analyte approaches that combine complementary biomarker categories.

In recent years, blood-based broad-spectrum multi-cancer early detection (MCED) platforms—such as Galleri (cfDNA methylation) [75] and CancerSEEK/Cancerguard (ctDNA plus protein biomarkers) [76]—have achieved excellent specificity (> 99%). However, as of early 2026, no MCED test has been approved by the FDA for pancreatic cancer screening, although Galleri has submitted a premarket approval (PMA) application [77], and the Nancy Gardner Sewell Medicare MCED Screening Coverage Act has created a reimbursement channel for it [78]. Furthermore, the sensitivity of these platforms for stage I–II pancreatic cancer is only about 60–72% [76, 79]; this is not due to technical flaws, but rather the biological limitation that early PDAC tumors release minimal cell-free DNA [71, 83]. In contrast, PDAC-specific multi-analyte combinations—such as methylated cfDNA markers combined with CA19-9—achieved 80% and 82% sensitivity for stage I and II disease, respectively, at a preset specificity of 97.5% [80], though the stage I sensitivity is constrained by a very small cohort (n = 5) and a broad confidence interval [80]. Other promising combinations include the PAC-MANN protease activity assay combined with CA19-9 [81], and a four-protein panel (ANPEP, PIGR, CA19-9, THBS2) [82]. This comprehensive multi-analyte strategy is expected to provide more detailed information to guide the early diagnosis of pancreatic cancer [71]. However, the THBS2/CA19-9 combination reached 87% sensitivity in retrospective Phase 2 studies; this performance was not reproduced in prospective pre-diagnostic cohorts [83], indicating that case-control studies cannot reliably predict real-world screening effectiveness.

Co-citation and citation burst analyses indicate that cyst fluid molecular analysis has matured into a clinically actionable diagnostic tool. The PancreaSeq Genomic Classifier (DNA/RNA-based, 74-gene panel) achieved 94.6% sensitivity and 96.4% specificity for mucinous cyst identification and 86.6% sensitivity with 97.9% specificity for advanced neoplasia detection in multi-institutional validation (n = 241) [84]. Meanwhile, cyst fluid glucose (< 50 mg/dL) has emerged as a practical alternative to CEA for mucinous cyst differentiation, achieving 93% sensitivity at dramatically lower cost [85]. The Kyoto 2024 guidelines reference both markers [59]. These molecular tools could refine surgical decision-making.

Additionally, epigenetics-based DNA methylation detection in early tumor screening is considered a promising breakthrough [86], as evidenced in early pancreatic cancer screening, exemplified by the close correlation between the methylation status of ADAMTS1 and BNC1 genes in cfDNA and early pancreatic cancer [87, 88]. A pancreatic juice-derived three-marker panel (comprising FER1L4, C13orf18, and BMP3), when integrated with plasma CA19-9, demonstrated high diagnostic performance in a prospective multicenter validation study. For stage I–II disease, this combined approach achieved an AUROC of 0.95, with a sensitivity of 83% and a specificity of 88% [89]. Levy et al., leveraging the differential hydroxymethylation status between patients with PDAC and non-cancer individuals, formulated a PDAC predictive model [88]. Other validated biomarkers include Glypican-1 circulating exosomes [90] and circulating long noncoding RNAs (ABHD11-AS1) [91], among others, with additional biomarkers still being discovered and validated. Despite these advances, carbohydrate antigen 19 − 9 (CA19-9) remains the only blood marker in routine clinical use and the only serum biomarker recognized by the FDA [11].

These bibliometric findings clearly reveal that artificial intelligence is also changing opportunistic detection in parallel. The PANDA model, recognized as an FDA breakthrough medical device, achieved 92.9% sensitivity and 99.9% specificity for lesion detection in a real-world multi-scenario cohort. For PDAC identification specifically, it demonstrated 92.6% sensitivity and 97.3% specificity, maintaining a sensitivity of 92.2% for tumors smaller than 2 cm within the external validation cohort. Its ability to detect lesions on non-contrast CT enables it to carry out opportunistic screening from routine scans, and its real-world deployment in China has been validated [92]. Electronic health record (EHR)-predictive models represent a parallel methodology. These models serve as pre-screening triage tools, identifying candidates for imaging-based surveillance from routine clinical data. The MIT PRISM model, trained on over 1.5 million electronic health records, identified 35.9% of PDAC cases at a 5.1-fold relative risk threshold (SIR = 5.10) 6–18 months before clinical diagnosis [93]. The CancerRiskNet model achieved an AUROC of 0.88 in the Danish national cohort but showed reduced performance (AUROC = 0.78) when validated on the US Veterans Affairs cohort [94]. Other noteworthy models include the pancreatic cancer risk prediction model for patients with newly diagnosed diabetes [66, 95] and the EUS image-assisted diagnostic system combined with artificial intelligence [96] [100]. By the beginning of 2026, no AI-based pancreatic cancer screening tool has been approved for clinical use, which shows that prospective verification in a diverse population is still essential before clinical integration.

In addition, the use of nanoscale components in biosensor manufacturing has substantially improved detection sensitivity, while advances in manufacturing have markedly reduced production costs. Prasad et al. have designed a paper-based low-cost electrochemical immunosensor based on nanomaterials for high-sensitivity early detection of pancreatic cancer [97], which represents another strategic direction [98, 99].

Clinical significance, translational barriers and future directions

This study reveals a paradigm shift in the early detection of pancreatic cancer: the field is transitioning from passive, single-modality imaging to active, risk-assessment-based multimodal surveillance. Research increasingly focuses on high-risk groups (patients with NOD, germline mutation carriers, and patients with precancerous lesions), prompting researchers and clinicians to prioritize targeted screening. Furthermore, the heterogeneity of existing guidelines regarding genetic mutation screening standards (such as for BRCA1/2), coupled with the almost complete absence of cohorts from low- and middle-income countries in major international screening alliances, suggest the urgent need to establish a standardized surveillance pathway.

Moreover, this analysis highlights key obstacles hindering the translation of research findings into clinical practice. Beyond the biological challenge of minimal tumor shedding in early PDAC [71], current evidence also relies heavily on retrospective case-control and single-center cohort studies, which tend to overestimate the diagnostic performance of biomarkers [83]. Additionally, the limited generalizability of AI models across diverse populations [94], alongside a general lack of cost-effectiveness and psychological outcome assessments, significantly reduces the feasibility and compliance of real-world screening. Importantly, long-term funding disparities continue to restrict the implementation of large-scale, prospective clinical trials.

Therefore, the future framework should move toward a multi-stage, multimodal integrated approach. Large-scale, prospective, population-based trials should advance multi-omics liquid biopsy platforms that combine complementary analytes, with research focused on predefined clinical utility endpoints rather than diagnostic accuracy alone. Within the clinical screening pathway, integrating AI-driven opportunistic screening models (such as PANDA) with electronic health record (EHR)-based risk prediction tools is strongly recommended to enable low-cost opportunistic detection during routine care; however, external validation in diverse populations is a prerequisite for widespread clinical deployment. Finally, future screening trials must expand the recruitment of globally diverse cohorts, establish universal screening criteria, and embed validated patient-reported outcomes into health economic evaluation models. Only then can current laboratory hotspots be truly transformed into clinically approved tools that balance cost-effectiveness with patient-centered care.

Strengths and limitations

This is the first bibliometric study to map the historical knowledge landscape of early detection of pancreatic cancer, providing a visual overview of research distribution, focal points, and trends. The combined use of CiteSpace, VOSviewer, and R-bibliometrix supports reproducibility and enables validation with future data. However, several limitations should be acknowledged: (1) sole reliance on WoSCC data may introduce selection bias; (2) limiting inclusion to English-language publications may underrepresent research from non-English-speaking countries; (3) citation-based metrics inherently favor older publications, potentially underestimating the influence of recent work—a constraint partially mitigated in the present study by citing recent literature from high-impact journals when discussing hotspots and frontiers; and (4) bibliometric methods relying on natural language processing may entail errors.

Conclusion

This study uses bibliometric analysis to trace the evolution of early pancreatic cancer detection from 1986 to 2025. Contemporary research hotspots in this field focus on the development of stratified risk surveillance, multi-analyte liquid biopsies, and artificial intelligence prediction models for high-risk individuals. These advancements are expected to reshape the existing pancreatic cancer screening framework in the near future, potentially increasing the rate of early-stage diagnosis and ultimately improving curative resection rates and overall patient survival. By systematically mapping the intellectual structure, principal contributors, and emerging frontiers of the field, the present analysis offers an evidence-based reference to inform future research prioritization and policy planning in early pancreatic cancer detection.
