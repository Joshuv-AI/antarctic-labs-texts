Can Peripheral Venous Oxygen Saturation Predict Clinical Decompensation in Pediatric Dilated Cardiomyopathy?
Authors: Yasemin Özdemir Şahan, Emine Gülşah Torun, İbrahim Ece, İbrahim İlker Çetin
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
Objective:Children diagnosed with dilated cardiomyopathy (DCM) frequently require hospitalization due to episodes of decompensated heart failure. Impaired oxygen utilization is a hallmark of decompensated heart failure, and peripheral venous oxygen saturation (SpvO2) may serve as a useful indirect marker of the mismatch between oxygen delivery and tissue demand. This study aims to evaluate the predictive value of SpvO₂ in identifying clinical decompensation and mortality in pediatric DCM patients.Materials and Methods:A retrospective review was conducted on 55 pediatric patients diagnosed with DCM from January 2019 to September 2023. Blood gas parameters (pH, SpvO2, lactate, and bicarbonate) obtained from peripheral venous samples, along with laboratory parameters such as N-terminal pro-B-type natriuretic peptide and echocardiographic measurements including left ventricular ejection fraction (EF) and fractional shortening, were recorded. Patients were categorized into stable and unstable (decompensated) groups based on their clinical status, admission type, modified Ross score (ages 1-5), and New York Heart Association classification (ages >5).Results:Left ventricular EF and SpvO2 were significantly lower, while N-terminal pro-B-type natriuretic peptide was significantly higher in children with decompensated heart failure. A SpvO2 cut-off value of 60.7% demonstrated 90% sensitivity and 85% specificity, demonstrating its strong predictive value for decompensation in pediatric DCM. Patients with SpvO2 ≤ 60.7% had a significantly higher mortality rate during the 12-month follow-up period (P = .031), primarily driven by increased mortality within the first 3 months (P = .035). However, no significant association between SpO2 levels and mortality was observed beyond 3 months (P = .846)Conclusion:Peripheral venous oxygen saturation is a valuable parameter for predicting both decompensated heart failure and mortality in children with DCM, complementing clinical and laboratory findings.

Introduction

Dilated cardiomyopathy (DCM) in children is characterized by the presence of a dilated left ventricle with systolic dysfunction, excluding any hemodynamic cause that could account for the existing dilation and dysfunction.1 The DCM leads to some of the most challenging outcomes in pediatric cardiology. The incidence of pediatric cardiomyopathy is 1-1.5 per 100 000 children and approximately 40% of children presenting with symptomatic cardiomyopathy either undergo heart transplantation or face mortality within the first 2 years after diagnosis.1-3

Children diagnosed with DCM often necessitate frequent pediatric emergency admissions and hospitalizations with decompensated heart failure.4 Early prediction of decompensated heart failure can prevent unnecessary treatments and loss of workforce, and early intervention may reduce mortality. Additionally, predicting the needs and risks of these children at an early stage is of the utmost importance, particularly regarding eligibility for heart transplantation and the provision of bridge therapy.5 In the outpatient follow-up of children with DCM and during hospitalization with decompensation, laboratory parameters should be monitored in addition to clinical findings, and echocardiography is important in terms of determining morbidity and mortality.6 While certain risk factors for decompensation have been identified, there is a notable gap in evidence regarding the assessment of tissue oxygenation and the significance of peripheral venous oxygen levels in risk assessment among children with DCM.4,7-9

The objective of this study is to determine whether peripheral venous oxygen saturation (SpvO2), a readily accessible marker of tissue oxygenation, can provide additional predictive value for identifying decompensated heart failure in pediatric patients with DCM, alongside conventional clinical, laboratory, and echocardiographic parameters.

Materials and Methods

Ethical Approval

The study adhered to the Declaration of Helsinki principles and was approved by the Turkish Ministry of Health and Human Ethics Committee of Health Sciences University, Ankara Bilkent City Hospital (decision number E2-23-5048, dated September 27, 2023).Verbal and written informed consent were obtained from the patients’ parents/guardians who agreed to their children’s participation in the study.

Study Population and Design

Children aged 1-18 years who were diagnosed with DCM and either presented to pediatric cardiology clinics or were admitted with decompensated heart failure between January 2019 and September 2023 were retrospectively evaluated. Data were obtained from the hospital’s electronic medical records.

Definition of Dilated Cardiomyopathy

Dilated cardiomyopathy was defined according to guidelines by the European Society of Cardiology and American Heart Association, which was defined as left ventricular dilation with diminished left ventricular systolic function.1,3 Left ventricle dilation was defined as a left ventricular end-diastolic dimension z score >2 value corrected for body size, sex, and/or age, and left ventricular global systolic dysfunction is defined by left ventricular ejection fraction (EF) <50%.4,10

Inclusion and Exclusion Criteria

Children were included if they were aged between 1 and 18 years, had a confirmed diagnosis of DCM, and had undergone peripheral venous blood gas sampling at the time of hospital admission. Exclusion criteria were (1) secondary DCM due to conditions such as myocarditis, metabolic or neuromuscular diseases, or congenital heart disease; (2) post-heart transplantation status; (3) presence of ventricular assist devices; (4) patients receiving oxygen therapy at the time of venous blood sampling; and (5) those with lower respiratory tract infections or sepsis that could lead to decompensation.

Clinical Classification of Patients

Patients were classified into 2 clinical groups based on their presentation and disease severity. Group A consisted of stable patients, while group B included unstable (uncompensated) patients. Classification was made using the setting of admission (outpatient clinic, pediatric emergency department, or intensive care unit), the modified Ross score for children aged 1-5 years, and the New York Heart Association (NYHA) functional classification for children above 5 years of age. Data Collection

This study includes both patients who were admitted at the time of initial diagnosis and those who presented later with new or worsening signs of heart failure. The values of pH, SpvO2, lactate, and bicarbonate from peripheral venous blood gas samples, along with laboratory parameters such as N-terminal pro-B-type natriuretic peptide (NT-proBNP) and echocardiographic measurements including left ventricular EF and fractional shortening (FS), were recorded simultaneously.

Statistical Analyses

The categorical variables were expressed as frequency and percentages. Normality analysis was carried out using the Shapiro–Wilk test. Normality was assessed separately for each group. Variables with a normal distribution are expressed as mean ± standard deviation, whereas non-normally distributed variables are presented as median (minimum–maximum). Categorical variables were compared with the chi-square test. Numerical variables with and without normal distribution were compared using the independent samples t-test or Mann–Whitney U. Spearman correlation analysis was performed for possible correlations between echocardiographic intervals and clinical variables. Correlation coefficients were interpreted as follows: 0.00-0.19 very weak, 0.20-0.39 weak, 0.40-0.59 moderate, 0.60-0.79 strong, and 0.80-1.00 very strong. The data of the study were analyzed by The Statistical Package for the Social Sciences version 26.0 (IBM SPSS Corp.; Armonk, NY, USA). Additionally, a receiver operating characteristics (ROC) analysis was performed to determine the area under the curve and establish a specific cut-off value for SpvO2, which would predict decompensation in patients with DCM; the cut-off value was determined by selecting the point that maximizes Youden’s index (sensitivity + specificity − 1). Kaplan–Meier analysis was used to estimate the time-related probability of death, and the log-rank test was used to compare survival among specific subgroups. P < .05 was set as the statistical significance value.

A total of 50 pediatric patients diagnosed with DCM were included. The demographic and clinical characteristics of the study population are presented in Tables 1 and 2. Correlation Analyses

Correlation analysis of SpvO2 and NT-proBNP showed a negative moderate correlation (Rho = −0.494; P = .001). No significant correlation was found between SpvO2 and age, other blood gas parameters (pH, lactate, bicarbonate), left ventricular EF and FS.

With an optimal SpvO2 cut-off of 60.7, the area under the curve was 0.895 (95% CI: 0.802-0.988), with sensitivity of 90%, specificity of 85%, positive predictive value of 83%, and negative predictive value of 92%. These results suggest good discrimination overall, but the higher negative predictive value indicates greater utility in ruling out decompensation. One-Year Mortality and Kaplan–Meier Survival Analysis

During the 12-month follow-up, 11 of 22 unstable patients (50%) and 5 of 27 stable patients (18.5%) died. Patients were categorized into 2 groups based on the SpvO2 cut-off value (≤60.7% vs. >60.7%) for survival analysis. According to Kaplan–Meier analysis (Figure 2), the 12-month mortality rate was significantly higher in patients with SpvO2 ≤ 60.7% compared to those with higher values (P = .031).

This significant difference was mainly driven by mortality within the first 3 months (P = .035). In the later period (3-12 months), mortality rates were similar between the groups (P = .846), suggesting that low SpvO2 levels are associated with early mortality but do not predict long-term survival.

No significant differences were found between the non-survivor and survivor groups in terms of age, gender, lactate, bicarbonate, partial pressure of carbon dioxide, NT-proBNP, and left ventricular EF and FS.

Half of the unstable patients (n: 11/22), and 18.5% of stable patients (n: 5/27) did not survive during the 1-year follow-up period. The patients were divided into 2 groups based on the cutoff values determined by the ROC analysis (SpvO2 60.7). Survival analysis of patients with DCM across different time intervals was performed using the Kaplan–Meier method (Figure 2). According to the Kaplan–Meier analysis, the mortality rate within the 12-month follow-up period was significantly higher in patients with SpvO2 ≤ 60.7 compared to those with SpvO2 > 60.7 (P = .031). However, this increased mortality rate was primarily due to the higher mortality observed in the first 3 months (P = .035). After the 3-month period (3-12 months), mortality rates were similar between the 2 groups, and no significant relationship was observed between SpO2 levels and mortality (P = .846). This suggests that low SpO2 levels have a negative impact on early prognosis but cannot be associated with long-term mortality. No significant differences were found between the non-survivor and survivor groups in terms of age, gender, lactate, bicarbonate, partial pressure of carbon dioxide, NT-proBNP, and left ventricular EF and FS.

Discussion

Sustaining adequate tissue oxygenation is imperative for preserving vital organ function.12 As is well known, oxygen delivery depends on the hemoglobin concentration, hemoglobin oxygen affinity, arterial partial pressure of oxygen, and cardiac output.13,14 Venous oxygen saturation serves as a valuable and easily accessible metric in the management of critically ill patients, particularly when assessing the adequacy of tissue oxygenation.15 In this context, venous oxygen saturation surpasses cardiac output measurements and can indeed be employed to estimate cardiac output through the application of the Fick equation.16 Previous studies have elucidated the correlation between reduced mixed or central venous oxygen saturation and increased mortality and morbidity rates in patients undergoing cardiac surgery or those experiencing septic shock.17-19

However, in critically ill pediatric patients, particularly in cases of low cardiac output states or emergency conditions, assessment of venous oxygen saturation from blood obtained via peripheral venous access may be less invasive and provide more rapid information due to the difficulty in accessing the central venous route, especially in children. Indeed, studies have reported a good correlation between central and SpvO2.12,19 Furthermore, the clinical evidence to support the use of a pulmonary artery catheter in critically ill children is not strong. Significant complications associated with the use of a pulmonary artery catheter, including pulmonary artery thrombosis, pulmonary infarction, right atrial thrombosis, atrial and ventricular arrhythmias, and right heart valvular damage, have also have been reported.20

In a study conducted by Hollander et al. involving children with DCM, mixed venous saturation was found to be significantly lower in children who experienced death or required transplantation, identifying it as an important risk factor.8 Based on this idea, the study found that SpvO2 values in peripheral blood gas, together with left ventricular EF, left ventricular FS, and NT-proBNP values, are a significant factor in indicating clinical decompensation. Particularly in children with DCM who have similar left ventricular EF on echocardiography, SpvO2 appears to be a reliable marker for risk stratification.

In studies involving children with DCM, lower mixed venous saturation, less reduction in NT-proBNP levels with heart failure therapies, persistent left ventricular end-diastolic dilation, persistently high levels of NT-proBNP, severe stunting of growth, and an increase in the heart failure score have identified specific high-risk factors for adverse outcomes in children with DCM.5,6,8 Despite extensive medical interventions and comprehensive care efforts, half of the unstable patients did not survive during the follow-up period. Patients with SpvO2 ≤ 60.7 had a significantly higher mortality rate during the 12-month follow-up period (P = .031), with this difference primarily driven by increased mortality within the first 3 months (P = .035). The absence of a significant difference between survivors and non-survivors in traditional markers such as lactate, NT-proBNP, left ventricular EF, and FS suggests that SpvO2 may provide additional prognostic information beyond these conventional indicators.

However, between 3 and 12 months, no significant association was observed between SpvO2 levels and mortality (P = .846), suggesting that the long-term impact of low SpvO2 on mortality may be limited. Furthermore, since Cox regression analysis could not be performed in the study, the interaction of SpvO2 with other prognostic variables and its independent prognostic value could not be assessed. Therefore, further studies with larger datasets are needed to better understand the impact of SpvO2 on early and late mortality.

Low SpvO2 levels were found to be significantly associated with higher mortality, particularly within the first 3 months, highlighting the impact of oxygenation impairment and cardiac perfusion deficiency on early clinical outcomes. However, the loss of the most critically ill patients during this period (survivor bias) and the mitigating effects of aggressive treatments on mortality may have contributed to the diminished prognostic value of SpvO2 in the long term. The lack of a significant association between SpvO2 and mortality from 3 to 12 months may be explained by several factors. First, patients who survive the early critical period may benefit from optimized medical therapy and clinical stabilization, reducing the prognostic impact of initial SpvO2 values. Second, survivor bias may play a role, as patients with the most severe hemodynamic compromise (and lowest SpvO2) may have died early, thereby limiting variation in SpvO2 among those who remain at risk later. These findings emphasize that while low SpvO2 is a crucial marker for identifying high-risk patients in the early phase, further evaluation of additional prognostic factors is necessary to better understand long-term mortality.

These findings underscore the potential value of integrating SpvO2 into clinical practice as a practical, less invasive prognostic marker for pediatric DCM. Its routine use in outpatient clinics and pediatric emergency departments could aid in early risk stratification and guide treatment planning. Further research is warranted to assess its role in optimizing therapeutic strategies and improving clinical outcomes. Additionally, the significant association between SpvO2 and early mortality suggests that it may facilitate the early identification of decompensated patients, enabling timely and targeted interventions to enhance clinical management and patient outcomes.

Limitations

The study has notable limitations that may affect the generalizability of its findings. The small sample size and single-center design limit broader applicability, while the retrospective nature introduces potential biases, such as incomplete data and reliance on medical records. Values obtained during intensive care unit follow-ups were excluded due to the confounding effects of oxygen support, which could significantly alter SpvO2 and other related parameters. In the Kaplan–Meier analysis, significant differences were observed between SpvO2-based subgroups within the first 3 months; however, Cox regression could not be performed due to insufficient events, censored data, multicollinearity, and proportional hazards assumption violations. Additionally, hemoglobin and hematocrit levels—which may influence oxygen delivery and SpvO2—were not included in the analysis.

Another important limitation is the absence of detailed etiological data for all patients. Since etiology—particularly genetic and familial background—is a major determinant of both mortality and the risk of clinical decompensation in pediatric DCM, the inability to stratify or adjust survival analyses based on etiology limits the interpretability of SpvO2 as an independent prognostic marker. This aspect should be addressed in future prospective studies with comprehensive etiological work-up and classification.

In conclusion, SpvO2 can be used as a significant parameter for predicting decompensated heart failure in children with DCM, in addition to other clinical and laboratory findings including echocardiography and NT-proBNP. Specifically, a SpvO2 value below 60.7% serves as an early indicator of decompensated heart failure. Its measurement via peripheral venous access makes it easily and quickly accessible. The integration of SpvO2 monitoring into clinical practice is likely to enhance the management and prognosis of pediatric patients with DCM and other cardiac conditions. However, further large-scale, prospective, randomized, multicenter studies are needed in this area.

Take homemessage: Childhood DCM is marked by recurrent episodes of decompensation. Peripheral venous oxygen saturation, together with imaging and laboratory parameters, may provide additional value in the assessment of hemodynamic stability.
