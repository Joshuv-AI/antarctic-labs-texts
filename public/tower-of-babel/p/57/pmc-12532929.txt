Functional genomics and phenotypic characterization of PGP and biocontrol traits of rhizospheric Streptomyces spp. from Mexican milpa agroecosystem
Authors: Lizbeth Vazquez-Hernandez, Esau De la Vega-Camarillo, Juan Alfredo Hernández-García, Lourdes Villa-Tanaca, César Hernández-Rodríguez
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
The traditional Mesoamerican milpa is an ancient agroecosystem with significant cultural and economic importance, commonly practiced by indigenous and rural communities for subsistence agriculture. Recognized for their diversity and sustainability, milpas utilize local resources and help preserve biodiversity. This ecosystem includes various plants of American origin, which establish numerous beneficial ecological relationships and promote interactions between plants and microbes. In this study, a collection of Streptomyces strains was isolated from the rhizosphere of native Cacahuacintle maize, squash, fava bean, and huauzontle. These strains exhibited various in vitro plant growth-promoting (PGP) activities, including ammonium production, phosphate solubilization, and siderophore production. Notably, Streptomyces sp. FR-108, isolated from the fava bean rhizosphere, and Streptomyces griseus MR-45, isolated from maize, increased maize biomass in a short-term greenhouse assay. Among the other strains, Streptomyces anulatus SR-10 from squash and Streptomyces albidoflavus MR-37 from maize demonstrated in vitro extracellular enzyme secretion and antagonistic activities against phytopathogenic fungi. Comparative and functional genomic analyses of these strains revealed their typical size, topology, and G + C content characteristic of Streptomyces genomes. Additionally, a substantial repertoire of genes involved in plant growth promotion was identified, contributing to nutrient acquisition, tolerance to osmotic, drought, chemical, and temperature stresses, as well as mutualistic interactions with plants and biocontrol of fungi, bacteria, insects, and protists. While no studies have previously focused on Streptomyces as plant growth-promoting bacteria (PGPB) in milpa agroecosystems, this genus shows potential as a bioinoculant for sustainable or extensive agriculture. This underscores the significance of traditional agroecosystems as sources of beneficial microorganisms.

Background

The milpa is an ancient agroecosystem from Mesoamerica, primarily practiced in Mexico. It is characterized by sustainable principles that promote biodiversity conservation, soil fertility maintenance, and efficient use of natural resources, making it an exemplary model of conservation agriculture [1]. This traditional polyculture system holds significant cultural and economic importance for subsistence farming, relying on strategic plant interactions to achieve optimal productivity.

The milpa typically includes three main crops: maize (Zea mays L.), common bean (Phaseolus vulgaris), and creeping squash (Cucurbita spp.). Maize, which requires a substantial amount of nitrogen, provides structural support for the climbing common bean, which hosts nitrogen-fixing Rhizobium bacteria. The creeping squash offers a canopy that suppresses weed growth and retains soil moisture [2, 3].

Although polyculture systems like the milpa often have lower overall productivity compared to intensive monocultures, the plant diversity within milpa systems reduces the need for external agricultural inputs. This biodiversity also fosters a more diverse bacterial community in the soil and rhizosphere, enhancing both productivity and ecological health [4, 5]. This approach stands in stark contrast to modern intensive agriculture, which heavily relies on chemical inputs and poses risks to human health and the environment, ultimately undermining long-term sustainability [6–9].

Despite the ecological importance of traditional agroecosystems, there is limited research on the microbial ecology of plant-bacteria interactions in these systems, including the milpa. Plants can recruit various communities of microorganisms in their rhizosphere, phylosphere, and endosphere [10–12]. In milpa agroecosystems, studies on bacterial diversity have shown that the Verrucomicrobia and Actinobacteria phyla contain key genera that influence rhizospheric communities of maize [4]. Research has highlighted the biocontrol potential of Amycolatopsis sp. BX17, an actinobacterium isolated from milpa soils, exhibits antagonistic effects against the phytopathogenic fungus Fusarium graminearum. A proteomic analysis indicated that the absence of glucose in the culture medium enhances the inhibitory activity of this bacterium, leading to increased production of peptide antifungals [13]. Furthermore, native maize seeds host a higher diversity of endophytic bacteria compared to hybrid maize, with fungi-antagonistic strains of Burkholderia spp. being the predominant genus in native maize seeds [14].

The potential of Proteobacteria to promote plant growth (PGP) is well established in different agricultural systems [4, 15], but Streptomyces have emerged as particularly promising partners in conservation agriculture. These bacteria offer an effective strategy for developing sustainable agricultural practices due to their eco-friendliness, low production costs, and ability to reduce the reliance on chemical fertilizers and pesticides [16, 17]. Streptomyces are often isolated from both bulk soil and the rhizosphere, as well as plant tissues, where they can thrive in large populations [18].

Streptomyces possess diverse ecological functions, including the degradation of macromolecules, which contributes to recycling plant, animal, and fungal biomass. They facilitate nutrient solubilization, drive biogeochemical cycling of elements, and enhance mineral bioavailability in both pristine and contaminated soils and rhizospheres [19]. Streptomyces bacteria contain multiple active and cryptic secondary metabolite pathways that encode numerous biologically active molecules. These metabolites serve as natural producers of substances that can antagonize bacteria, fungi, and viruses [20, 21].

The antifungal properties of Streptomyces have been widely recognized in agriculture, with their metabolites successfully reducing yield losses and mycotoxin contamination from Aspergillus spp., Rhizoctonia solani, Fusarium spp., and Pythium spp. in various cereal crops through greenhouse, in vitro, and field studies [22, 23].

In the past decade, the plant growth-promoting activities of Streptomyces and other actinobacterial strains have gained recognition. These plant growth-promoting (PGP) capabilities include the production of phytohormones, induction of systemic resistance in plants against pathogens, and the release of volatile organic compounds (VOCs) that stimulate plant growth both directly and indirectly. Additionally, these strains protect plants from abiotic stresses and enhance the uptake of nitrogen, phosphorus, and iron [24–27].

Streptomyces and other actinobacteria show significant potential in improving plant health, growth, and productivity. They can modulate root exudate production, increase disease resistance, and enhance grain quality within the rhizosphere of Arabidopsis thaliana and economically important legume plants [17, 28–30]. However, to our knowledge, this study is the first to recognize the PGP potential of Streptomyces species from the milpa agroecosystem.

This research explored the genotypic and phenotypic PGP and biocontrol potential of indigenous Streptomyces strains derived from milpa plants. By integrating traditional knowledge of the milpa with molecular and genomic tools, this approach represents a crucial strategy for identifying sustainable agricultural alternatives. It involves isolating and characterizing strains that exhibit a wide range of PGP activities, biocontrol capabilities, and the potential to neutralize abiotic stresses.

Methods

Collection of biological material

Plant and soil samples were collected from a traditional milpa agroecosystem in San Andrés Totoltepec, Tlalpan, Mexico City, Mexico (19°13’33.8"N, 99°09’45.6"W). This site was selected due to its long history of traditional milpa cultivation and minimal use of chemical inputs. Milpa plants, including maize (Zea mays L.), fava beans (Vicia faba L.), huauzontle (Chenopodium nuttalliae Saff), and squash (Cucurbita sp.), were carefully uprooted to preserve the root system and collect the associated rhizosphere soils. The roots and associated rhizosphere were individually placed in sterile plastic bags and transported to the laboratory in coolers maintained at 4 °C to preserve microbial communities and isolate plant growth-promoting bacteria (PGPB) [31].

Isolation of Streptomyces spp.

Plant roots and attached rhizospheric soil were suspended in a 90 mL sterile water solution, and bacteria were isolated by the decimal serial dilution method [32]. Aliquots (100 µL) of each dilution were spread on five different media. All culture media used in this work have been previously recommended for the isolation of actinomycetes in general and Streptomyces in particular. The formulation of the media includes different nitrogen and carbon sources to increase the possibility of collecting a greater diversity of culturable Streptomyces species. The culture media was actinomycete isolation agar [33], arginine glycerol salts agar with cycloheximide [34], Czapek agar with cycloheximide [35], soil extract agar using milpa soil [36], and ISP-2 agar [37]. The plates were incubated at 28 °C for 96 h. Streptomyces-like colonies were purified by repeated streaking, and their spores and mycelium were preserved at − 70 °C in 25% glycerol [38].

PGP phenotypic characterization and antagonism to phytopathogens of Streptomyces strains

Streptomyces spp. spores were adjusted for all experiments as follows: Biomass from 7 days of growth on Arginine-Glycerol medium plates was collected in a 0.1% Tween 20 suspension to reduce surface tension and obtain a homogeneous suspension of the hydrophobic mycelium and spores. To separate the mycelium, the suspension was filtered through a wad of polyester fiber assembled on top of a conical centrifuge tube, and the filtered spores were adjusted to a turbidity of 0.5 McFarland (Kit Densichek Plus Standards, bioMérieux®). A viable count was performed to obtain the total CFU/mL.

Nitrogen oligotrophy

Nitrogen oligotrophy ability was assessed using an assimilable nitrogen-free LGI medium [39]. Strains were streak-inoculated onto LGI agar plates and incubated at 28 °C for 7 days. Colonies showing growth were transferred to 5 mL LGI broth and incubated at 28 °C, 120 rpm for 4 days. This process was repeated twice more. Finally, 100 µL of the culture was inoculated into 5 mL of semi-solid LGI medium (0.175% agarose) and incubated for 21 days at 28°C. Growth was evaluated visually.

Ammonium production

Strains were grown in 3 mL peptone water (1% w/v) at 28 °C, 120 rpm for 11 days. Ammonium production was detected in supernatants by adding Nessler’s reagent (HACH) diluted 1:3 in a volume of culture supernatant. The development of a yellow-to-brown color indicated positive ammonium production, and the absorbance was measured at 405 nm [40]. The ammonia production was quantified by comparison with a standard curve of ammonium (2–25 mg/mL).

Phosphate solubilization

The qualitative assessment utilized the National Botanical Research Institute Phosphate (NBRIP) agar, which contained 5 g/L Ca₃(PO₄)₂ as the sole phosphorus source [41]. Plates were spot-inoculated with 10 µL of bacterial suspension (approximately 10⁶ CFU/mL) and incubated at 28 °C for 21 days.

The phosphate solubilization index (PSI) was calculated as the ratio between the total diameter (colony + solubilization halo) and the colony diameter.

The modified molybdenum blue method was used to quantitatively analyze the best solubilizer strains [42, 43]. In brief, strains were grown in liquid NBRIP medium for 7 days at 28 °C, 150 rpm. Supernatants were collected by centrifugation (10,000 × g, 10 min), and soluble phosphate was measured spectrophotometrically at 880 nm using a standard curve of KH₂PO₄ (0–10 mg/L).

Siderophore production

Siderophore production was detected using the chrome azurol S (CAS) agar plate assay [44]. CAS agar plates were spot-inoculated with 5 µL of bacterial suspension (approximately 10⁶ CFU/mL) and incubated at 28 °C for 7 days. The siderophore production index was calculated as the ratio of the orange halo diameter to the colony diameter.

Antifungal activity

Antagonistic effects against Fusarium oxysporum ENCB-FP-03, Helminthosporium maydis ENCB-FP-07, and Colletotrichum gloeosporoides ENCB-FP-09 were evaluated using the dual culture technique on potato dextrose agar (PDA) [49]. A 5-mm mycelial disc of each fungus was placed at the edge of a PDA plate, and a streak of the bacterial strain was spread at a distance of 3 cm. Plates were incubated at 28 °C for 7 days. Plant growth promotion assay

Native maize seeds were provided at the familiar milpa, where rhizosphere sampling was conducted. “Cacahuacintle” maize seeds were surface-sterilized by immersing them in 2% sodium hypochlorite for 5 min, followed by a 1-minute immersion in 70% ethanol, and then rinsed five times with sterile distilled water. Biomass from 7 days of growth on Arginine-Glycerol medium plates was collected in a 0.1% Tween 20 suspension to reduce surface tension and obtain a homogeneous suspension of the hydrophobic mycelium and spores and adjusted as previously described. Seeds were inoculated by soaking in bacterial suspensions (108 CFU/mL) for 2 h. Control seeds were soaked in sterile water, 0.01% Tween 20 suspension, and LB culture media to validate the PGP activity of Streptomyces spp. Seeds were germinated on sterile filter paper for 4 days at 28°C.

Germinated seeds were transferred to sterile vermiculite pots (one seedling per pot, 10 replicates per treatment). Plants were grown under greenhouse conditions (the average daytime temperature reached a maximum of 29 °C ± 1 °C, and the average nighttime temperature reached a minimum of 13 °C ± 3 °C, with 12.5/11.5 h light/dark cycle) and irrigated every other day with 50 mL of Hoagland solution rotated with distilled water [51]. After 30 days, plants were harvested, and the following parameters were measured: germination rate, shoot and root lengths, and dry biomass (after drying at 70 °C for 48 h).

Molecular and genomic identification of Streptomyces spp. strains

A total of 17 representative strains were selected after plant growth-promoting in vitro analysis from groups A, B, and C (see Fig. 5). The selection was based on their origin to represent the rhizosphere of the four milpa plants (maize, squash, huauzontle, and fava bean). These strains were identified at the genus level through phylogenetic analysis and the similarity of their partial 16S rRNA gene sequences. Plant growth promotion tests were performed on a subset of group C strains with the most relevant PGP and fungal antagonism traits. Finally, four strains that exhibited the best in vitro PGP, fungal antagonism, and plant growth promotion traits in the greenhouse were selected for genome sequencing, species identification, or recognition of new species by a phylogenomic approach.

Molecular identification of Streptomyces strains by 16S rRNA partial sequence analysis

Primary identification of the strains was by recognition of the typical actinobacterial colony morphology, and molecular identification was performed by sequencing of the 16S rRNA gene. The amplification of partial sequence belonging to the 16S rRNA gene was performed PCR using the universal primers 27 F (5′-AGAGTTTGATCMTGGCTCAG-3′) and 1492R (5′-TACGGYTACCTTGTTACGACTT-3′) [52]. The master mix for a 25 µL PCR reaction contained 10X buffer, 25 mM MgCl2, 10 mM each dNTP, 10 pM each primer, and 0.5 units of Taq polymerase. Amplification was detected by 1% (w/v) agarose gel electrophoresis using ultraviolet (UV) fluorescence with ethidium bromide, followed by purification of the reaction product using the Zymo Research DNA Clean & ConcentratorTM−5 Kit (Irvine, CA, USA). After purification, the amplified samples were sequenced using the Sanger method in the laboratories of Molecular Biology and Genomic Sequencing of Biodiversity and Health at the Instituto de Biología of the Universidad Autónoma de México (UNAM).

Phylogenomic approaches of Streptomyces spp. strains with prominent PGP and fungal antagonism

For bacterial whole-genome sequencing, DNA was extracted using the Wizard Genomic DNA Purification Kit (Promega) according to the manufacturer’s instructions. DNA quality was assessed using a NanoDrop spectrophotometer and Qubit fluorometer. The samples were sent to Novogene Corporation, Inc. for genomic library preparation using the Nextera XT DNA Library Preparation Kit (Illumina), with an average insert size of 350 bp. Sequencing was performed on an Illumina NovaSeq PE150 platform, generating 150 bp paired-end reads.

The quality metrics from raw reads were analyzed using FastQC v. 0.11.8 website (https://www.bioinformatics.babraham.ac.uk/projects/fastqc/). Raw reads were quality-trimmed using Trimmomatic v0.39 [53] with the following parameters: LEADING:3 TRAILING:3 SLIDINGWINDOW:4:15 MINLEN:36. De novo assembly was performed with SPAdes v3.13.0 [54] using the careful mode and k-mer sizes of 21, 33, 55, and 77. Assembly quality was assessed with QUAST v5.0.2 [55].

Phylogenomic reconstruction was carried out using a set of orthologous proteins deduced from Streptomyces genomes obtained from this study and the NCBI Genome database. This proteome-based approach, utilizing the Clusters of Orthologous Groups of Proteins (COGs), was identified with OrthoFinder v4.0 [56] from the complete deduced proteomes of the selected organisms. A maximum likelihood (ML) phylogenomic tree was then constructed using the concatenated alignment of shared COGs, applying the LG + F + G4 amino acid substitution model with the IQ-TREE software [57]. The robustness of the resulting tree was assessed using 1000 ultrafast bootstrap replicates [58].

Determination of average nucleotide identity (ANI), average amino acid identity (AAI), and in silico genome–genome hybridization (GGH)

ANI, AAI, and GGH were computed by the Type (Strain) Genome Server (TYGS) [59]. ANI and AAI were determined using the ANI/AAI Calculator with default parameters on the website http://enve-omics.ce.gatech.edu/aai/. The threshold for equal or different species was established based on species allocation criteria [60].

Comparative and functional genomic analysis

Genomes were annotated using Prokka 1.12 [61] and RAST 2.0 [62] with default parameters and the NCBI Prokaryotic Genome Annotation Pipeline [63]. The Plant-Microbe Interactions, Lipopeptides, and Biocontrol Database (PLABase) were used for a more comprehensive functional annotation, primarily focused on plant-microbe interactions [64]. PLABase provides a layered functional annotation, distinguishing between direct and indirect mechanisms. Direct mechanisms encompass bio-fertilization, bio-remediation, and plant signal production. Meanwhile, indirect mechanisms include plant system colonization, competitive exclusion (CE), stimulation of plant immune responses, and stress control/biocontrol. Finally, the prediction of metabolites with biocontrol activity putative in Biosynthetic gene clusters (BGCs) was performed with bacterial antiSMASH version v. 8.0 [65].

Phylogenomically close Streptomyces species to the strains of this study were used for comparison and functional genomic analysis, as shown in Table 1. Also recognized was that PGP Streptomyces species were used in these comparisons. Bacillus subtilis NCBI3610 was included as a far PGPB phylogenetic parent.

The comparative and functional genomic analysis results were visualized using TBtools-ll v1.108 [72].

Statistical analysis

Unless otherwise stated, all PGPB phenotypic characterization and antagonism to phytopathogens were performed in triplicate experiments. For the house green assay, statistical analyses were performed using Python 3.12.2 with SciPy statistical package. All data were tested for normality, and non-parametric tests were employed due to the nature of biological measurements and sample sizes. For morphological parameters (root and plant length), twelve replicates per treatment were analyzed, while biomass measurements (fresh and dry weight) were evaluated using three replicates per treatment. Overall treatment effects were assessed using the Kruskal-Wallis H test, followed by pairwise Mann-Whitney U tests comparing each treatment against the control group. P-values from multiple comparisons were adjusted using the Benjamini-Hochberg procedure to control the false discovery rate at α = 0.05. Statistical significance was set at P < 0.05, with significance levels categorized as: P < 0.001 (highly significant), 0.001 ≤ P < 0.01 (very significant), 0.01 ≤ P < 0.05 (significant), and P ≥ 0.05 (not significant). Data are presented as box plots showing median, quartiles, and individual data points, with significance levels indicated by letter notation above each treatment group.

For data normalization of the semiquantitative and quantitative tests in the heat maps of plant growth-promoting features and comparative and functional genomics, the strain with the highest activity was used as a reference with a value of 100%. The results of the other strains were expressed as relative percentages of the reference strain.

Phylogenetic analysis based on the functional direct and indirect mechanisms clustered the strains into three main clades, with B. subtilis NCBI3610 serving as the outgroup. Clade I comprised S. kroppenstedtii DSM40484, S. ortus A15ISP2-DRY2, and Streptomyces sp. FR-108. Clade II included S. anulatus VUW1, S. anulatus SR-10, S. griseus JCM4516, S. griseus MR-45, and S. fimicarius NRRL ISP5322. Clade III consisted of S. albidoflavus UYFA156, S. albidoflavus MR-37, Streptomyces sp. N1, and Streptomyces sp. L2.

In Clade I, Streptomyces sp. FR-108, isolated from the rhizosphere of the fava bean plant, harbored in its genome the highest relative abundance of genes related to direct and indirect mechanisms of the plant-bacteria relationships detected by PLABase. S. kroppenstedtii DSM40484 from soil and S. ortus A15ISP2-DRY2 from a deep-sea sponge are the closest phylogenetic relatives of Streptomyces sp. FR-108. The genome of these species exhibits lower potential for stimulating plant immune responses, bio-fertilization of sulfur and iron, and bioremediation capabilities for xenobiotic degradation compared to Streptomyces sp. FR-108.

Clade II strains included S. anulatus SR-10 from squash rhizosphere and S. griseus MR-45 from maize rhizosphere, and reference strains S. anulatus VUW1, S. griseus JCM4516, and S. fimicarius NRRL ISP5322. Among Clade II genomes are broadly distributed the direct mechanisms of plant-bacteria association, the potential for bio-fertilization by potassium and phosphate solubilization, and the production of plant signal phytohormones. Additionally, genomes encode indirect mechanisms, such as colonizing plant systems through root colonization, competitive exclusion due to bacterial fitness, quorum sensing responses, and biofilm formation. Finally, the neutralization of abiotic and biotic stress, as well as biocontrol mechanisms is broadly distributed across the genomes of Clade II. However, clade II species genomes show low potential for biofertilization through nitrogen acquisition, colonization of the plant system by adapting to the plant immune system, degradation of the plant cell wall or membrane, utilization of plant-derived substrates, and stimulation of plant immune responses through triggered immunity.

Clade III strains included S. albidoflavus from maize and reference strains S. albidoflavus SM254 from bulk soil, as well as Streptomyces sp. L1 and N1 from the A. thaliana rhizoplane, previously reported as PGPB. Clade III grouped genomes with a lower potential for encoding direct and indirect mechanisms of plant-bacteria association than the species in clades I and II. The unique feature of clade III strains was biofertilization, achieved through the solubilization of potassium and phosphate. However, they maintained some direct mechanisms, such as the potential for bio-fertilization by iron and sulfur, bioremediation potential, and plant signal production.

Although the Streptomyces strains of Clade I harbored the most important and broad features, the Clade II species also contained several relevant genotypic traits in plant-bacteria relationships. Clade III possessed important genetic features that enabled it to colonize and survive in the soil environment, but exhibited the lowest potential to maintain a relationship with plants and neutralize biotic stress by producing antimicrobial or insecticidal metabolites.

Discussion

The plant rhizosphere is a typical habitat of Actinomycete species [18]. This paper demonstrates that the milpa agroecosystem follows this trend. While the ecological role of Streptomyces species as fungal antagonists is recognized in agricultural systems [22, 23], this actinomycete genus has recently gained attention as plant growth-promoting bacteria (PGPB) [17, 27–29]. To our knowledge, there have been no prior studies of PGP Streptomyces associated with milpa plants.

This study examines the diversity of a group of actinomycete bacteria found in the rhizosphere of plants within a traditional agroecosystem, which is believed to be an important reservoir of potentially beneficial bacteria for agriculture [4, 13, 14].

Based on colonial morphology, we isolated 44 Streptomyces-like strains from the rhizospheres of plants growing in a milpa agroecosystem. The population densities in these habitats ranged from 3 × 104 to 1 × 109 CFU/g per gram in squash and maize rhizospheres, respectively, which aligns with commonly reported values for this group of mycelial and spore-forming microorganisms [18].

Molecular identification using partial sequences of the 16S rRNA gene classified the isolates as belonging to the genus Streptomyces, but it was not possible to identify them at the species level using this molecular marker. With current whole-genome sequencing methods and bioinformatic analyses of phylogenomic reconstructions, we can derive valuable insights into taxonomy and phylogeny [60, 73]. Through phylogenetic analysis, ANI, AAI, and GGH of the genome, three species were recognized (S. anulatus SR-10, S. griseus MR-45 and S. albidoflavus MR-37). Additionaly, a possible new species was detected (Streptomyces sp. FR-108), which will require further studies for formal taxonomic classification; however, formal identification is not the primary objective of this study. Although the genus has been known since 1943 and has been widely studied for its extensive secondary metabolism, the discovery of new species continues [74].

Phenotypic features associated with PGP traits suggest that soil and rhizospheric Streptomyces contribute to plant colonization, growth, adaptation, and proliferation [29]. In this study, several Streptomyces strains isolated from the milpa environment thrived in vitro under nitrogen-limited conditions, produced ammonium from peptone water, solubilized phosphate, and used chitin as the sole carbon and nitrogen source. The Streptomyces genomes isolated from milpa plants have the potential to utilize allantoin as a sole nitrogen source and for urea production and harbor 13 to 19 copies of genes related to chitinolytic activity. These bacteria also contain genes associated with the biosynthesis of up to 21 different organic acids for phosphate solubilization, such as valeric, glycolic, and lactic acid. These phenotypic and genomic traits suggest adaptations to a phosphate and nitrogen-limited soil environment, such as a milpa agroecosystem, that typically operates with minimal external inputs [3]. In these conditions, Streptomyces strains and genomic potential offer a selective advantage over other bacteria.

The proteolytic and chitinolytic activities of certain strains play a vital role in mobilizing organic nitrogen, which in turn enhances soil fertility and promotes fungal biocontrol [75]. Among these strains, the extracellular chitinase activity of Streptomyces derived from the rhizosphere of milpa plants is notably widespread. Chitin, which is an aminopolysaccharide found in fungal cell walls and arthropod exoskeletons, serves as a critical substrate and significant component of soil. Its estimated annual production and steady-state amount reach between 1010 and 1011 tons [76]. Consequently, chitin represents an essential reserve of assimilable nitrogen once it undergoes enzymatic hydrolysis. Chitinolytic mycelial Streptomyces are crucial for degrading solid substrates, as chitin contributes to sustainable agricultural practices and decreases reliance on synthetic fertilizers [77–79].

Siderophores produced by soil microbiota aid in plant iron acquisition, induce systemic resistance, and enhance the competition for iron between plants and phytopathogens. Iron is a vital nutrient, yet it is often present in unavailable forms or in limited concentrations within the soil [80–82]. This dual function highlights the complex ecological roles that Streptomyces strains can have in the rhizosphere of milpa plants, as they improve nutrient bioavailability and protect plants against pathogens [83, 84]. However, while there is a correlation between the production of siderophores and the antifungal properties of the siderophore-producing Streptomyces strains examined in this study, a direct cause-and-effect relationship cannot be established.

The analysis of biosynthetic gene clusters (BGCs) revealed that each strain contained 1 to 4 clusters associated with siderophore biosynthesis. Additionally, genomic analysis in PLABase indicated the potential production of up to 17 metallophore molecules, including polyoxopeptin, griseobactin, bacillibactin, pyochelin, and desferrioxamine B. The ability to generate siderophores with potential antifungal activity is particularly significant given the increasing interest in biological alternatives to chemical fungicides, especially in light of concerns about the environmental impact of chemical pesticides and the growing resistance to fungicides [85].

Approximately half of the Streptomyces strains were found to inhibit the growth of three phytopathogenic fungi (F. oxysporum, H. maydis, and C. gloeosporoides) and they proved to be good producers of chitinases and proteases in vitro. These strains also demonstrated good in vitro production of chitinases and proteases. The genomes of the sequenced strains contained three biosynthetic gene clusters (BGCs) encoding non-ribosomal peptide synthetases (NRPS), NRPS-like enzymes, lanthipeptide-class II compounds, and azole-containing ribosomally synthesized and post-translationally modified peptides (RiPPs), all of which have potential antifungal activity [86, 87]. Genomic analysis using PLABase indicated the potential for the biosynthesis of polyenes, including candicidin, amphotericin B, and nystatin, as well as the production of enzymes such as glucosidases, laccases, and cellobiosidases, which function as lytic enzymes. While these phenotypic and genotypic traits are crucial for controlling phytopathogenic fungi, it is important to consider that these strains may also inhibit beneficial rhizospheric or mycorrhizal fungi, as previously reported [88]. Antifungal activity is widely distributed among Streptomyces species, providing them a significant selective advantage over other microorganisms. This advantage is evident in their competition for resources and efficient colonization of their ecological niches. Additionally, their antagonistic activity has been linked to the production of extracellular lytic enzymes and iron-mediated fungal starvation, enabling Streptomyces to displace or inhibit various fungi [89–91].

In greenhouse plant growth promotion assays with maize, the Streptomyces milpa isolates demonstrated their potential for agricultural applications. S. griseus MR-45 and Streptomyces sp. FR-108 exhibited plant growth-promoting (PGP) traits, resulting in increased biomass in maize plants. Notably, Streptomyces sp. FR-108, which was isolated from the fava bean rhizosphere, displayed the highest potential for promoting the growth of native maize. This PGP activity highlights the structural complexity and biological diversity of plants that interact closely within fertile soil and underscores the importance of preserving ancestral crops in sustainable agroecosystems, such as milpa polyculture, as reservoirs of plant growth-promoting bacteria (PGPB) [1, 2, 14, 92, 93].

Comparative genomic analysis of four Streptomyces species from milpa has provided insights into the genetic basis for their observed phenotypic traits and highlighted the genomic potential of these strains in their relationship with plants. Notably, Streptomyces sp. FR-108 and S. griseus MR-45, which were isolated from the rhizosphere of fava bean and maize plants, respectively, appear to have adapted extensively to the milpa plant rhizosphere, similar to observations made in the squash root microbiome [94]. Additionally, S. anulatus SR-10, found in the squash rhizosphere, showed a higher abundance of genes associated with plant promotion and protection. However, this strain did not significantly enhance the growth of maize plants in a greenhouse assay. Conversely, other strains, Streptomyces sp. N1 and L2, isolated from the root endosphere of Arabidopsis thaliana, exhibited a low content of both direct and indirect mechanisms related to the plant-bacteria relationship. Despite this, a plant growth-promoting (PGP) assay conducted with A. thaliana indicated that these strains could promote plant growth [24]. Overall, the functional genomics of Streptomyces strains associated with the rhizosphere of milpa plants suggest moderate to high potential for PGP activities and biocontrol. However, further experiments are necessary to fully understand the relationship between plants and Streptomyces strains that exhibit genotypic PGP potential but do not show relevant PGP phenotypes in vitro, in greenhouse settings, or in field assays. Biofertilization has direct effects such as nitrogen oligotrophy, nitrogen acquisition through the production of allantoin and urea, iron acquisition via siderophores, and the solubilization of phosphate and potassium through organic acid metabolism and phosphatase activity. Plant signal production includes the synthesis of phytohormones such as auxins, cytokinins, gibberellins, and xanthine, as well as the biosynthesis of gamma-aminobutyric acid, terpenoids, carotenoids, and volatile compounds. Additionally, the genomic features related to bioremediation encompass the detoxification of heavy metals, including arsenic, cadmium, chromium, cobalt, nickel, mercury, and other xenobiotics, along with the biodegradation of hydrocarbons and polycyclic aromatic compounds. Recent studies on plant growth-promoting (PGP) and bioremediation traits of Streptomyces have seen significant growth as strategies for bio-reclamation of affected soils for agriculture [95].

The indirect effects revealed with genomic data of plant system colonization involve adaptation to the plant immune system, membrane degradation, root colonization, and the utilization of plant-derived substrates. Competitive exclusion involves bacterial fitness, the production of exopolysaccharides, and biofilm formation. Stimulation of plant immune responses includes the induction of systemic resistance and triggered immunity.

Stress control contains genes that help neutralize abiotic stress, including tolerance to extreme temperatures, osmotic pressures, salinity, and oxidative stress. Biocontrol mechanisms encompass antiprotozoal, antibiotic, fungicidal, and insecticidal activities. The various abilities of Streptomyces genomes from milpa plants indicate potential applications in developing bioinoculants. Notably, Streptomyces sp. FR-108 demonstrates a unique genomic potential to establish relationships in the rhizosphere without eliciting a strong plant defense response. This characteristic is highly valued in microorganisms used as bioinoculants, as previously indicated in other studies of plant-bacteria interactions [96–98].

This research lays the groundwork for understanding the PGP and biocontrol phenotypic and genomic potential of Streptomyces strains from the milpa rhizosphere. Additionally, employing omics approaches could help identify novel species and bioactive compounds with applications in agriculture or pharmaceuticals [99, 100]. It is also essential to study the ecological impact of introducing these strains into different soil ecosystems to ensure safety and evaluate potential long-term effects on native microbial communities [101]. Efforts should focus on developing bioinoculants to enhance plant growth, control phytopathogens, and create field application strategies that improve the survival and efficacy of these strains in agricultural settings [102].

Beyond the cultural and economic significance of Mexican milpa, the diversity and presence of new bacterial species highlight the importance of preserving ancestral crops and traditional agricultural practices. These practices serve as reservoirs of PGP and biocontrol microorganisms with significant potential to enhance sustainable and environmentally friendly agricultural production.

Conclusions

Streptomyces species found in milpa ecosystems possess a range of phenotypic and genomic traits that promote plant growth, induce defense mechanisms, and help manage stress in plants. While it is widely known that secondary metabolites produced by Streptomyces spp. have significant clinical applications as antimicrobials, their role as important plant growth-promoting (PGP) bacteria is just beginning to gain recognition.

In addition to understanding the ecological role of Streptomyces in traditional agricultural soils, the milpa rhizosphere serves as a valuable reservoir of diverse PGP and biocontrol bacteria, as well as potential new species that can be used in bioinoculants. This contribution is vital for enhancing food security while minimizing environmental impacts.

As we confront global challenges in agriculture—such as climate change and the need for sustainable intensification—the potential of these microbial resources from traditional agricultural polycultures is increasingly significant. They offer promising solutions for the future of agriculture.
