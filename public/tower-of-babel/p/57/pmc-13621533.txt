Antimicrobial resistance and genomic characteristics of bovine Escherichia coli from five northern provinces of China
Authors: Xiang-Yu Wang, Xiao-Xuan Zhang, Tong Ye, Li Guo, Hong-Bo Ni, Quan Zhao, Jing Liu, Jian-Gang Ma
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundEscherichia coli (E. coli) from food-producing animals serves as an important reservoir of antibiotic resistance genes (ARGs). To investigate the baseline genomic and antimicrobial resistance (AMR) profiles of bovine-associated E. coli from northern China, 143 fecal samples were collected across five provinces for analysis.ResultsA total of 112 E. coli isolates were recovered from 143 bovine fecal samples, with isolation rates ranging from 60 to 100%. Disk diffusion testing revealed high resistance to ampicillin (81.25%), cefotaxime (75.89%), and tetracycline (75.89%), and 68.75% of isolates were multidrug-resistant. Whole-genome sequencing of 112 isolates identified 47 multi-locus sequence types (notably ST69, ST1431, ST10, and ST58) and 63 serotypes. Closely related isolates across distant provinces suggested the presence of genetically related bovine-associated lineages across regions. A total of 74 ARG subtypes were detected, predominantly belonging to the aminoglycoside and beta-lactam resistance classes. Notably, the ARGs arr-3, floR, mdf(A), qnrS1, and tet(A) exhibited differences in distribution among provinces (p < 0.05), indicating variation in resistance gene profiles across sampling locations. Overall genotype–phenotype concordance was observed, although agreement varied among ARG classes. Virulence profiling revealed a predominance of adherence-related genes, especially fimH and entA-D, while only three isolates were classified as classical diarrheagenic E. coli based on virulence markers. Forty-one plasmid replicon types were identified, with IncFIB and IncHI2 being the most frequent and broadly distributed. The number of detected plasmid replicon types was positively associated with the number of ARGs and VFGs.ConclusionsThis study provides foundational genomic insights into bovine-associated E. coli from northern China and highlights the potential contribution of plasmids to ARG carriage and resistance profiles. These findings support the need for sustained genomic surveillance within a One Health framework.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at https://doi.org/10.1186/s12864-026-13144-5.Keywords: AMR, ARGs, WGS, MLST, Escherichia coli, Phylogroups

Introduction

The extensive global use of antibiotics has resulted in a critical and escalating problem of bacterial antimicrobial resistance (AMR). Previous studies have revealed that AMR, as one of the leading public health threats of the twenty-first century, could result in up to 10 million deaths annually by 2050 [1]. However, AMR in animals warrants greater attention, as they represent a major source of dietary protein, and food products have been shown to significantly influence the abundance of antimicrobial resistance genes (ARGs) [2–5].

Escherichia coli (E. coli) commonly colonizes the intestinal tract of humans and animals as a commensal bacterium [6]. However, pathogenic variants harboring virulence factors can cause disease in otherwise healthy hosts, including diarrhea, urinary tract infections, and septicemia [6]. E. coli serves as an important reservoir of ARGs and has severely limited the effectiveness of clinical antimicrobial therapies [7]. Antimicrobial-resistant E. coli has been reported across various animal species, posing a growing threat to global livestock production [8–12]. In recent years, E. coli strains resistant to multiple classes of antibiotics, especially several critical last-resort antibiotics such as carbapenems, tigecycline, and colistin, have been increasingly reported and have raised significant public health concerns [13, 14]. In China, several antimicrobial classes, including tetracyclines, β-lactams, aminoglycosides, fluoroquinolones, and polymyxins, have been used in veterinary medicine for therapeutic or prophylactic purposes, which may contribute to the selection and persistence of resistant E. coli in food-producing animals [15]. In summary, the escalating threat of antimicrobial resistance in E. coli is a serious global concern. Given its widespread presence in food-producing animals, the resistance patterns observed in bovine-associated E. coli also warrant close attention and continuous monitoring.

Due to the decreasing cost of DNA sequencing, metagenome sequencing and whole-genome sequencing (WGS) are now increasingly used to characterize microbial composition, function analysis, and bacterial isolates from both clinical and environmental sources, providing comprehensive insights into their ARGs, virulence factor genes (VFGs), serotypes, phylogroups, and multi-locus sequence types (MLST) [16, 17]. Multiple studies have employed WGS to uncover the diversity of resistance genes of β-lactam, carbapenems, and aminoglycosides and identify virulence gene profiles in zoonotic strains in recent years. In parallel with genomic insights, plasmids play a crucial role in mediating the horizontal transfer of diverse ARGs, thus significantly contributing to the spread of resistance among bacterial populations [13, 18]. E. coli could be classified into several phylogenetic groups (phylogroups), such as A, B1, B2, D, E, and F, which differ in ecological niches, virulence potentials, and host preferences; MLST is widely used to further distinguish these strains with higher resolution for epidemiological and evolutionary analyses [19, 20]. Ultimately, phylogenetic insights serve as a powerful tool to trace the origin, evolutionary relationships, and geographic dissemination of resistant E. coli strains. By reconstructing evolutionary trees based on WGS, researchers have identified transmission clusters, tracked the emergence and global spread of special strains, and detected region-specific lineages associated with multidrug resistance [21–23]. These findings are essential for informing evidence-based surveillance programs, designing targeted interventions, and shaping antimicrobial stewardship strategies both locally and globally.

Although several studies have reported antimicrobial resistance and genomic characteristics of bovine-associated E. coli in individual regions of China, integrated WGS-based comparative analyses across multiple northern provinces of China remain limited [24, 25]. Therefore, in this study, we conducted a comprehensive genomic and phenotypic characterization of bovine-associated E. coli isolates from five provinces in northern China. WGS was performed to investigate ARGs, VFGs, plasmid replicon profiles, and phylogenetic relationships among the isolates. Correlation analyses were subsequently performed between resistance phenotypes and ARGs, and between plasmid replicon profiles, ARGs, and VFGs to characterize their respective associations. Our findings contribute to a better understanding of the genomic epidemiology of bovine-associated E. coli in northern China and provide reference information for AMR surveillance in livestock systems.

Materials and methods

Sample collection

Fresh fecal samples were randomly collected from diarrheic cattle raised on multiple commercial dairy cattle farms across five provinces in northern China: Heilongjiang (HLJ), Jilin (JL), Inner Mongolia (NM), Gansu (GS), and Xinjiang (XJ). To avoid repeated sampling from the same animal, the sampler waited outside the cattle pens and directly observed defecation events. Once a diarrheic cow defecated, the fresh fecal sample was collected immediately, and the same individual was not sampled again during that farm visit. A total of 143 samples were obtained, including 55 from HLJ, 15 from JL, 20 from NM, 23 from XJ, and 34 from GS. Specifically, samples from HLJ were collected from five independent dairy farms (approximately 10–12 samples per farm), samples from GS were obtained from three dairy farms (approximately 11–12 samples per farm), samples from XJ were collected from three dairy farms at different time points (approximately 6–9 samples per farm), samples from JL were collected from two dairy farms located in two cities (approximately 7–8 samples per farm), and samples from NM were obtained from a single dairy farm (20 samples).

Isolation and identification of bacteria

All samples were pre-enriched in 5 mL Buffered Peptone Water at 37 °C for 4 h and subsequently plated onto MacConkey agar for E. coli isolation. Presumptive colonies were selected from MacConkey agar and subcultured onto Eosin Methylene Blue (EMB) agar for further screening. Individual colonies showing typical morphology were then streaked onto LB agar for two consecutive rounds of purification. After purification, the resulting isolates (strains) were preserved in 25% (v/v) glycerol and stored at − 80 °C for long-term preservation prior to subsequent experiments. Bacterial species identification was performed using matrix-assisted laser desorption ionization time-of-flight mass spectrometry (MALDI-TOF MS; Bruker Microflex LT/SH, Bruker Daltonics, Germany).

Phenotypic antibiotic susceptibility testing

The strains were incubated overnight on LB agar prior to the experiment. Antimicrobial susceptibility testing (AST) was conducted on all isolates using the Kirby-Bauer (K-B) disk diffusion method against ten antibiotics, covering five major classes: aminoglycosides (neomycin sulfate, kanamycin, gentamicin and amikacin), β-lactams (ampicillin, cefotaxime and ceftazidime), quinolones (ciprofloxacin), tetracyclines (tetracycline), and polypeptides (polymyxin B). The corresponding abbreviations-NST, KAN, GEN, AMK, AMP, CTX, CAZ, CIP, TET, and POLB- are used throughout the text. Antimicrobial susceptibility testing was performed by disk diffusion following CLSI M100 guidelines, and results were interpreted according to CLSI M100 criteria [26]. Isolates resistant to at least three antimicrobial classes were classified as multidrug-resistant (MDR). E. coli of American Type Culture Collection (ATCC) 25,922 was used as the quality control strain.

DNA extraction and WGS

Genomic DNA was extracted from the isolates using the TIANamp Bacteria DNA Kit (Tiangen, Beijing, China) according to the manufacturer’s protocol. DNA quantity and quality were assessed using a NanoDrop™ One (Thermo Fisher Scientific, USA). Libraries were constructed using the MGIEasy DNA Library Prep Kit (MGI Tech Co., Ltd., Shenzhen, China) following the manufacturer’s instructions, and sequenced with 2 × 150 bp paired-end reads on the DNBSEQ-T7 platform at BGI Genomics Co., Ltd. (Shenzhen, China).

Genomic data analysis

Adapters and low-quality bases were removed from raw sequencing reads using fastp v0.23.4 [27], and draft genomes were assembled using SPAdes v3.15.5 [28]. The assembly quality was evaluated by QUAST v5.0 [29]. Afterward, Average Nucleotide Identity (ANI) analysis was performed using FastANI v1.34 to confirm species identity against the reference genome GCF_000005845.2 from the NCBI RefSeq database [30]. Genomes were annotated using Prokka v1.14.6 [31] with the parameters “–addgenes –kingdom Bacteria –gffver 3 –norrna –notrna”. The resulting GFF files were cleaned and used as input for Roary v3.13.0 [32]. Core genes were identified using Roary with the parameters “-e -n -p 16”, and a concatenated core-gene alignment file (core_gene_alignment.aln) was generated. Subsequently, a core genome phylogenetic tree was constructed using FastTree v2.1 with the “-nt -gtr” option (https://morgannprice.github.io/fasttree/). FastTree-generated local support values were retained and displayed for major internal branches in Fig. 3. The phylogenetic tree was visualized and refined using the interactive Tree of Life (iTOL) tool for improved presentation. Abricate v1.0.1 was utilized to identify the antibiotic resistance genes, virulence genes, and plasmids (https://github.com/tseemann/abricate) using the ResFinder, VFDB, and PlasmidFinder databases [33–35]. Based on the identified virulence genes, diarrheagenic E. coli pathotypes were assigned using established molecular criteria [36]. Sequence types (STs) were determined using mlst v2.23.0 (https://github.com/tseemann/mlst), and phylogroups were assigned using ClermontTyping [20]. Subsequently, the serotypes were annotated using the ECTyper v1.0.0 [37]. Results were visualized using various R packages, including hchinamap (https://CRAN.R-project.org/package=hchinamap), tidyverse [38], tidyplots v0.1.2 [39], ggpubr v0.6.0 (http://www.sthda.com/english/rpkgs/ggpubr), UpsetR [40], ggplot2 v3.5.1 [41] and the extension GGally (https://github.com/ggobi/ggally) within the R v4.4.0 environment.

Statistical analysis

Alpha diversity of ARG profiles was evaluated using the Shannon and Simpson indices. Overall differences among provinces were assessed using the Kruskal–Wallis test, followed by Dunn’s multiple-comparison test with Benjamini–Hochberg correction for pairwise comparisons where appropriate. Beta diversity was assessed based on Bray–Curtis dissimilarity and visualized by principal coordinates analysis (PCoA). Differences in ARG profile composition among provinces were evaluated using PERMANOVA with 999 permutations. Differences in the numbers of detected ARG classes among provinces were also evaluated using the Kruskal–Wallis test. Spearman’s rank correlation coefficient was used to assess the relationships between antimicrobial resistance phenotypes and corresponding resistance genes, as well as associations between plasmid replicon types and both ARGs and VFGs. Genotype–phenotype agreement was calculated for each antimicrobial as the proportion of isolates showing concordance between phenotypic resistance and the presence of at least one corresponding resistance gene according to the formula (TP + TN)/N.

Results

Isolation of E. coli and antimicrobial susceptibility profiles

All isolates were successfully identified by MALDI-TOF and reconfirmed by ANI through data of WGS. A total of 112 E. coli isolates were recovered from 143 samples in five northern provinces of China, including 33 of 55 (60%) in HLJ, 15 of 15 in JL (100%), 15 of 20 (75%) in NM, 21 of 23 (91.3%) in XJ, and 28 of 34 (82.4%) in GS (Fig. 1). Specifically, in HLJ, the 33 isolates were recovered from five dairy farms (Farm HLJ-1, n = 6; Farm HLJ-2, n = 11; Farm HLJ-3, n = 7; Farm HLJ-4, n = 5; Farm HLJ-5, n = 4). In JL, all 15 samples yielded E. coli isolates, which were obtained from two dairy farms (Farm JL-1, n = 8; Farm JL-2, n = 7). In NM, 15 isolates were recovered from a single dairy farm. In XJ, 21 isolates were obtained from three dairy farms, including repeated sampling from one farm at different time points (Farm XJ-1, n = 4; Farm XJ-2, n = 3; Farm XJ-3, n = 14;). In GS, 28 isolates were recovered from three dairy farms (Farm GS-1, n = 12; Farm GS-2, n = 7; Farm GS-3, n = 9).

AST profiles revealed high resistance to AMP (81.25%, 91/112), CTX (75.89%, 85/112), and TET (75.89%, 85/112) (Fig. 2A). The highest resistance rate was observed for AMP in 4 different provinces except for XJ (Additional file 1G). Notably, the resistance profiles NST-KAN-GEN-AMP-CTX-CAZ-CIP-TET and NST-KAN-GEN-AMP-CTX-CIP-TET were the most common, highlighting the widespread occurrence of multidrug resistance (Fig. 2B). The overall antimicrobial resistance patterns of E. coli isolates indicated a high resistance burden across all regions, characterized by high resistance rates to multiple antimicrobial classes and a high prevalence of multidrug-resistant isolates (Additional file 1A-E).

The proportion of multidrug-resistant (MDR) isolates was 68.75% (77/112). Among them, counts of MDR were found most in the province of GS (25/34) followed by HLJ (21/55), JL (11/15), XJ (11/23) and NM (9/20) (Additional file 1 F). Among the tested strains, 29 (25.89%) were resistant to three classes of antibiotics, 43 (38.39%) to four classes, and 2 (1.79%) were resistant to all five classes used in this study (Additional file 1H).

Analysis of phylogroups, MLST, and serotype

Six kinds of phylogroups were detected in 112 isolates, including B1 (n = 49), A (n = 38), D (n = 14), C (n = 7), E (n = 1), B2 (n = 1), and Unknown (n = 2). Type B1 was the most prevalent phylogroup of bovine E. coli in the provinces of GS, HLJ, and XJ, but D in JL and A in NM (Additional file 4).

MLST results revealed 47 known ST types of E. coli in our study; ST69 (n = 13) was dominant, followed by ST1431 (n = 7), ST10 (n = 6), ST58 (n = 5), and 7 unknown ST types of strains. Therein, types of ST1431, ST69, ST540, and ST10 were the dominant types in the provinces of GS, JL, NM, and XJ, except for unclassified isolates in HLJ (Additional file 4).

A total of 63 distinct serotypes were identified among the 112 isolates, with O77:H18 (n = 10) being the most dominant, followed by O88:H10 and O9:H25 (n = 6). Other serotypes are listed in Additional file 4. There were 26, 17, 16, 9, and 6 kinds of serotype distribution in the provinces of HLJ, NM, XJ, GS, and JL. Therein, the most prevalent serotype is different in 5 provinces. (Additional file 4).

Core genome and phylogenetic analysis

Core genome analysis of the 112 strains identified a total of 34,356 genes, predominantly classified as cloud genes (82.57%, 28,368 genes; existence in 1–15% of strains) and core genes (7.85%, 2,696 genes; existence in ≥ 99% of strains) (Additional file 4).

To determine the evolutionary relationships between 112 isolates, we constructed a phylogenetic tree based on core genes. Results showed that isolates from different provinces were distributed across multiple phylogroups (Fig. 3). Among the classified isolates, phylogroup B1 was the most prevalent, followed by phylogroups A and D. ST69 was assigned to phylogroup D, while ST10, ST617, and ST744 were assigned to phylogroup A. Within phylogroup B1, ST1431 and ST58 were the most frequently detected sequence types. Several serotypes were repeatedly observed across the classified phylogroups, including O77:H18, O9:H10, O89:H10, O-:H45, O8:H10, and O9:H5. Collectively, isolates from multiple provinces encompassed diverse combinations of phylogroups, sequence types, and serotypes (Fig. 3).

Detection and analysis of ARGs, VFGs and plasmids

Across the 112 isolates, a total of 1,207 ARG detection events were identified, corresponding to 74 distinct ARG subtypes (allelic variants) as defined by the ResFinder database. Among these, aminoglycoside resistance genes were most frequent (362/1,207, 29.99%), followed by beta-lactam (172/1,207, 14.25%) and multidrug resistance genes (112/1,207, 9.28%). A similar composition pattern was observed in each province (Fig. 4A). To highlight the predominant resistance determinants, the top 12 ARGs were selected based on detection frequency. Among the top-ranked genes, aph(6)-Id (n = 71), aph(3'')-Ib (n = 37), and aph(3'')-Ia (n = 34) were the main aminoglycoside-related genes, whereas mdf(A) (n = 112) was the most frequently detected macrolide resistance gene (Fig. 4B). Subsequently, we employed the alpha diversity and PCoA analyses based on ARG counts per sample to assess resistome variation. Resistance gene profiles showed partial geographic clustering according to PCoA (R2 = 0.093, p < 0.001; Fig. 2C), with partial overlap with GS strains and closer clustering between some provinces (e.g., HLJ-XJ, NM-XJ). Alpha diversity analysis of ARG profiles suggested variation across the sampled provinces in both Shannon and Simpson indices (Kruskal–Wallis test, p < 0.05; Fig. 2D). Post hoc comparisons indicated that isolates from XJ generally exhibited lower ARG diversity than those from GS. The relative composition of ARG classes was broadly similar among the sampled provinces, although exploratory comparisons suggested variation in several dominant ARG classes (Fig. 4A–B). In particular, the numbers of detected sulfonamide- and phenicol-resistance genes showed variation across sampling locations (Fig. 4C–D). Further comparison of the top detected ARGs showed that several resistance determinants, including arr-3, floR, mdf(A), qnrS1, and tet(A), varied across sampling locations (Additional file 2 C).

Analysis of VFGs across all isolates demonstrated that adherence genes constituted the predominant virulence system, followed sequentially by effector delivery systems and nutritional/metabolic adaptation factors (Fig. 5A). Type 1 fimbriae (n = 869) were the most virulence factors, followed by TTSS secreted effectors (n = 721), ECP (n = 620), and other factors (Additional file 2D). Results revealed the presence of csgF, entA, entB, entC, entD, fepB, and fes in all isolates. Additionally, csgB/G (n = 111), entF/S (n = 111) and spX5 (n = 111) were commonly observed. Notably, genes associated with heme utilization, including members of the chu operon, were rarely detected in our collection. The overall distribution pattern of virulence factors was similar in each province (Fig. 5B).

In addition, strict genome-based classification of diarrheagenic E. coli was performed based on established molecular criteria using canonical virulence marker genes. Only three isolates could be definitively assigned to classical pathotypes, including one EPEC (eaeA +, stx −), one STEC (eaeA +, stx +), and one ETEC (estIa +). No isolates carrying marker genes for EAEC (aggR), DAEC (daaD), or EIEC (ipaH) were detected (Additional file 4).

A total of 41 plasmid types were identified among the 112 E. coli isolates. The most prevalent plasmid was IncFIB (n = 71), followed by Col(pHAD28) (n = 44), IncHI2 (n = 41), IncHI2A (n = 41), and others (Fig. 5C). Additionally, the overall distribution pattern of plasmid types across the provinces was largely consistent (Fig. 5D).

Correlation analysis

Integrating antimicrobial resistance profiles with genomic characterization of ARGs, VFGs, and plasmid-borne elements, we systematically investigated their interrelationships through correlative analytics. Spearman’s correlation analysis revealed a positive association between antimicrobial resistance phenotypes and ARGs (r = 0.677, p < 0.001) (Fig. 5E), with significant correlations also observed for aminoglycosides, beta-lactams, and tetracyclines (Additional file 3F-I). Plasmids showed significant positive correlations with both ARGs (r = 0.510, p < 0.001) and VFGs (r = 0.377, p < 0.001) (Fig. 5 F). Regionally, plasmid-ARG correlations were significant in GS, HLJ, and XJ, while plasmid-VFG and ARG-VFG associations were observed in GS and JL (Additional file 3A-E). Notably, XJ exhibited negative correlations between VFGs and both plasmids and ARGs (Additional file 3E).

To further evaluate antimicrobial-specific genotype–phenotype relationships, concordance rates between resistance phenotypes and the presence of corresponding resistance genes were calculated for each antimicrobial class (Additional file 4). High concordance was observed for beta-lactams (91.07%, 102/112) and aminoglycosides (80.36%, 90/112), followed by tetracycline (78.57%, 88/112), whereas quinolones showed a comparatively lower concordance rate (53.57%, 60/112).

Discussion

A total of 112 E. coli isolates were recovered from 143 bovine fecal samples across five provinces in China, with an overall isolation rate of 78.32% in our study. This prevalence was higher than that reported in bovine feces from West Bengal [42] and comparable to the 80% isolation rate observed in Tongliao, Inner Mongolia [43]. High resistance to AMP (81.25%), CTX (75.89%), and TET (75.89%) was observed in the present study, which is in line with previous reports of elevated resistance to these antimicrobials among bovine E. coli isolates from other regions [42, 44]. Over half of the isolates were resistant to NST, GEN, and CAZ, whereas resistance to KAN, AMK, CIP and POLB was lower. Notably, POLB exhibited the lowest resistance rate in the present study (7.14%, 8/112), which was lower than that reported for cattle-derived E. coli in 2019 (26.92%) [45] and is consistent with the documented reduction in colistin use following China’s 2016 ban on colistin as a growth promoter [46]. Overall, 92.86% of isolates exhibited resistance to at least one antibiotic, suggesting a substantial AMR burden among the collected bovine-associated E. coli isolates compared to reports from other countries [47, 48]. Multidrug resistance (MDR) was identified in 68.75% (77/112) of isolates, with GS samples exhibiting the highest number of MDR strains. Given the non-stratified sampling design, these observations reflect the characteristics of the collected isolates rather than definitive province-level trends.

The alpha and beta diversity analyses revealed variation in resistome profiles among the sampled isolates. Factors such as antibiotic usage and farm management practices may contribute to such variation [2, 10, 49]. In this study, aminoglycoside resistance genes, including aph(3'')-Ib, aph(3'')-Ia, and aph(6)-Id, were predominant, while other widely distributed resistance genes such as blaTEM, sul2, floR, and tet(A) were also frequently detected across provinces, indicating a broadly shared resistance gene repertoire with previous studies of bovine E. coli studies despite differences in the dominant resistance class [50]. Additionally, the widespread detection of mdf(A), encoding a proton motive force-dependent multidrug efflux pump (MdfA) of the major facilitator superfamily, suggests that multidrug efflux mechanisms may contribute to the observed resistance profiles in bovine-associated E. coli [51].In terms of virulence factors, VFGs were widespread among the isolates, with adherence-associated elements such as Type 1 fimbriae, ECP, and TTSS effectors being predominant, which agrees with previous findings showing adhesin genes as dominant virulence determinants in bovine fecal E. coli isolates [52]. Interestingly, enterobactin-related genes (entA-F, entS, fepB and fes) were widespread, whereas heme utilization genes such as chuA were rarely detected, consistent with previous studies showing that siderophore-mediated iron acquisition predominates in E. coli while heme uptake systems are comparatively uncommon [53].

Furthermore, plasmid profiling revealed that IncFIB was the most prevalent replicon in bovine E. coli, followed by IncHI2, and IncHI2A, consistent with previous reports [22, 54, 55]. Phenotypic resistance generally correlated with ARG presence (r = 0.677, p < 0.001), with high concordance for beta-lactams and aminoglycosides but lower concordance for quinolones. The quinolone discordance likely reflects the importance of chromosomal mutations (gyrA, parC) over acquired resistance genes, underscoring the need for combined phenotypic and genotypic surveillance approaches [56, 57]. Moreover, plasmids are recognized not only as vehicles for horizontal gene transfer but also as dynamic evolutionary elements that can enhance bacterial adaptation under antibiotic pressure [49]. In line with this concept, our correlation analyses showed positive associations between detected plasmid replicon types and the numbers of ARGs and VFGs, consistent with previous evidence that resistance plasmids belonging to the IncF and IncH families frequently co-carry multiple antimicrobial resistance and virulence determinants, thereby facilitating their persistence and dissemination in bacterial populations [22, 58]. This finding underscores the need for comprehensive genomic monitoring strategies, in which genomic surveillance of dominant plasmid types and resistance gene combinations, when interpreted alongside routine antimicrobial susceptibility testing, can provide an evidence base to support targeted antimicrobial stewardship.

In this study, substantial genetic diversity was observed among bovine-associated E. coli isolates across provinces, as reflected by the distribution of phylogroups, sequence types (STs), and serotypes. Six phylogroups were identified, with B1 predominating in GS, HLJ, and XJ, A in NM, and D in JL, as observed in previous studies where phylogroups A and B1 were commonly found in E. coli from both humans and animals [12, 59]. MLST analysis identified 47 distinct STs, with ST69, ST1431, ST10, and ST58 being the most common. Notably, ST10 is a high-risk multidrug-resistant clone, while ST69 and ST58 are emerging ExPEC lineages, and ST1431 is increasingly found in livestock, suggesting ongoing adaptation and zoonotic risk [11, 60–62]. Serotyping identified 63 distinct serotypes, with O77:H18, O9:H10, and O8:H10 being the most prevalent, which have also been reported in diverse ecological and host contexts in previous studies [63]. However, under a strict genome-based definition using canonical virulence markers, only three isolates in this study could be definitively assigned to classical diarrheagenic E. coli pathotypes (one EPEC, one STEC, and one ETEC), indicating that most isolates represented commensal or opportunistic lineages rather than typical diarrheagenic strains.

Core genome phylogenetic analysis revealed fine-scale genetic relationships among bovine-associated E. coli isolates. Although most strains clustered according to their major phylogroups, core genome-based analysis further distinguished closely related isolates within the same phylogroup and sequence type, including isolates recovered from different provinces, suggesting the presence of closely related bovine-associated lineages across regions. The observed relationships among STs, phylogroups, and serotypes were broadly consistent with known E. coli population structures [12]. In addition, core genome analysis provided higher resolution than MLST or serotyping alone and identified distinct sublineages within major STs that could not be resolved using conventional typing approaches. Similarly, serotypes such as O77:H18, O9:H10, and O8:H10 were distributed across multiple phylogroups, whereas core genome analysis differentiated genetically distinct isolates sharing identical serotypes. These findings indicate that core genome analysis provides higher resolution for characterizing the genetic diversity and population structure of bovine-associated E. coli than conventional typing approaches. Together with phenotypic antimicrobial susceptibility testing, genomic surveillance may provide complementary information for ongoing AMR monitoring and antimicrobial stewardship in livestock systems.

This study has several limitations. First, the sampling strategy was not designed to represent all bovine E. coli populations or to support representative interregional comparisons, and the number of farms and isolates varied among provinces. Therefore, province-level differences should be interpreted cautiously. Second, although short-read WGS provided contig-level evidence of ARG–plasmid associations, it could not fully resolve complete plasmid structures, complex multidrug-resistance regions, or transmission relationships among closely related isolates. Future studies integrating long-read sequencing and higher-resolution genomic analyses with epidemiological metadata will help clarify the genetic contexts and dissemination dynamics of resistance determinants.

Conclusions

In summary, this study revealed a high AMR burden and substantial genomic diversity among bovine-associated E. coli isolates collected from cattle farms in northern China. Genotype–phenotype concordance varied by antimicrobial class, supporting the value of WGS-based detection as a complementary tool for AMR surveillance while highlighting the need to consider additional resistance mechanisms. Plasmid replicon types such as IncFIB and IncHI2 were associated with ARG carriage, suggesting their potential role in shaping resistance profiles, although complete plasmid structures require further validation. Overall, our findings provide baseline genomic data for bovine-associated E. coli in China and support ongoing One Health–oriented surveillance and antimicrobial stewardship efforts.
