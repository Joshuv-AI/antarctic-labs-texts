Targeting ROR1 with humanized antibody drug conjugates and cytokine fusion proteins for cancer therapy
Authors: Beixue Li, Rui Jin, Ziping Qi, Fengming Zou, Shuang Qi, Aoli Wang, Li Wang, Qingsong Liu, Chen Hu, Wenchao Wang, Jing Liu
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
SummaryROR1 is a promising cancer target due to its tumor-specific expression. Here, we developed two high-affinity humanized anti-ROR1 antibodies (Hu001-2 and Hu005-46) targeting distinct epitopes, with Hu001-2 demonstrating blockade of Wnt5a-ROR1 signaling and rapid internalization. Based on Hu001-2, we generated an antibody-drug conjugate (Hu001-2-vcMMAE) that displayed potent ROR1-dependent anti-proliferative activity in vitro and significantly inhibited tumor growth across multiple ROR1-expressing in vivo models. Furthermore, we designed an antibody-cytokine fusion protein (Hu001-2-IL15Rα-IL15) that demonstrated potential anti-tumor activity in immunocompetent models. This fusion protein successfully activated the immune system within the tumor microenvironment, upregulating the expression of TNF-α and IFN-γ while exhibiting minimal systemic toxicity. Collectively, these novel Hu001-2-based therapeutics validated the feasibility of using the ROR1 fusion protein to trigger the activation of the immune system in the tumor microenvironment.

Introduction

Receptor tyrosine kinase-like orphan receptor 1 (ROR1), an evolutionarily conserved pseudokinase receptor, has emerged as a pivotal regulator of embryogenesis and oncogenic transformation.1 Its tumor-specific expression pattern—highly expressed in malignancies while nearly undetectable in most adult tissues—positions it as an ideal therapeutic target. Accumulating evidence implicates ROR1 in the pathogenesis of diverse malignancies including chronic lymphocytic leukemia (CLL), mantle cell lymphoma (MCL), triple-negative breast cancer (TNBC), lung adenocarcinoma and pancreatic carcinoma.2 Mechanistically, ROR1 orchestrates tumor progression by engagement with non-canonical Wnt signaling and constitutive activation of downstream oncogenic pathways, driving cellular survival, metastatic dissemination, and therapeutic resistance.3

ROR1 is a typical type I tyrosine kinase receptor, which contains the extracellular domain (ECD), transmembrane domain, and intracellular domain. The ECD comprises an Ig-like domain, Frizzled domain, and Kringle domain, which participate in Wnt5a-mediated signal transduction.4 Due to amino acid substitutions in conserved motifs of its intracellular kinase domain, ROR1 is generally classified as a pseudokinase, which lacks ATP-binding ability and catalytic activity. However, the kinase activity of ROR1 remains controversial according to recent research.5,6 ROR1-mediated oncogenesis primarily occurs through the integration of Wnt5a signaling with multiple tumor-promoting pathways, including PI3K/AKT/mTOR, NF-κB, Ras/MAPK, and Hippo-YAP/TAZ pathways.7,8 Wnt5a binds to the Frizzled domain of ROR1, triggering NF-κB activation via phosphorylation and nuclear translocation of p65. This signaling axis promotes the transcription of pro-inflammatory cytokines and survival factors that drive tumor progression. Moreover, ROR1 coordinates crosstalk between non-canonical Wnt pathways and other critical oncogenic cascades. In multiple malignancies, including non-small cell lung cancer and pancreatic adenocarcinoma, ROR1 activation engages the PI3K/AKT/mTOR axis through AKT phosphorylation, enhancing tumor cell proliferation and therapeutic resistance.9

The tumor-restricted expression and multifaceted oncogenic functions of ROR1 have spurred rapid development of ROR1-targeted therapies. Current clinical strategies exploit its extracellular accessibility through monoclonal antibodies (mAbs), antibody-drug conjugates (ADCs), and CAR-T cells, with the limited exploration of alternative antibody-based therapeutic formats.10,11 Meanwhile, small molecule inhibitors targeting intracellular domains of ROR1 are under investigation.12 Zilovertamab is in phase III evaluation (NCT03833180) in combination with ibrutinib for hematologic malignancies. To enhance the therapeutic potency, the ADCs integrate cytotoxic payloads with tumor-targeting specificity. VLS-101 is a novel ADC comprising zilovertamab conjugated to monomethyl auristatin E (MMAE) via a protease-cleavable valine-citrulline (vc) linker. Preclinical studies in murine models of Richter transformation and MCL demonstrated significant antitumor efficacy with tumor growth inhibition rates exceeding 70%, paving the way for its current phase II/III trials in hematologic malignancies and solid tumors.13,14 Despite its therapeutic promise, current ROR1-targeted strategies face challenges, including the lack of systematically investigated antibodies targeting different ROR1 epitopes, on-target/off-tumor toxicity (due to ROR1 expression in adipose and gastrointestinal tissues), adaptive resistance mediated by alternative splicing or immune microenvironment barriers, and limited CAR-T cell efficacy. These limitations underscore the necessity for multidimensional therapeutic approaches combining precision targeting with microenvironment modulation.

Here, we developed a humanized ROR1 antibody that showed high affinity and specificity by hybridoma technology and humanization design. The ADC drug based on this antibody showed potent anti-tumor efficacy in vitro and in ROR1-positive tumor cell xenograft mouse models. Moreover, we generated a novel immune-activating fusion protein comprising the ROR1 antibody conjugated to IL-15, which activates NK cells and CD8+ T cells in the ROR1-positive tumor microenvironment, demonstrating the potential of exploring new therapeutic formats for ROR1-targeted agents.

After chimeric modification by human IgG1 Fc subtype with a kappa (κ) light chain, the human-mouse chimeric antibodies retained similar binding activity to the original murine mAbs. ELISA results showed that the chimeric antibodies exhibited high affinity for purified human ROR1 ECD protein (EC50 ≤ 0.02 nM) (Figure 1D). Flow cytometry analysis on MDA-MB-231 cells revealed that R-001c exhibited superior binding affinity compared to the control antibody UC-961, while R-003c showed relatively weaker binding activity toward ROR1 (Figures 1E and 1F), and the same binding affinity was validated on NCI-H226 with high ROR1 expression (Figure S1).

Epitope mapping and functional validation of anti-ROR1 antibody domains

To analyze the functional epitope of anti-ROR1 antibodies, we overexpressed truncated human ROR1 proteins in CHO cells and measured the antibody-ROR1 binding ability using flow cytometry, which showed that the deletion of Ig-like domain attenuated the binding of UC-961, R-003c, and R-005c to ROR1, indicating that R-003c/R-005c have the same binding mode as UC-961, which binds to the ROR1 Ig-like domain (Figure 2A). Meanwhile, the deletion of the Frizzled domain or the Kringle domain did not disrupt the binding of R-001c ∼ R-009c to ROR1, indicating that the epitopes of all these antibodies are not located in the Frizzled or Kringle regions, or do not bind exclusively to these two domains. We then validated the epitopes with purified ROR1 truncated proteins by ELISA, and the result correlated with the FACS result, showing R-003c/R-005c/UC-961 bind to the Ig-like domain (Figure 2B). And the other antibodies showed weak binding affinity with a single Kringle or Frizzled domain, further indicating their epitopes were located in the spatial linker region between Frizzled and Kringle domains. To explore the function of antibodies targeting different epitopes, we first evaluated the antibody-mediated ROR1 internalization and degradation in cellular models. All the chimeric antibodies effectively induced ROR1 degradation in MDA-MB-231 cells (Figure 2C). ROR1 internalization was monitored by immunofluorescence staining and confocal microscopy using R-001c and R-005c, which represent the two different epitope classes. The result showed that both R-001c and R-005c efficiently promoted the rapid internalization of ROR1 from the cell surface into lysosomes (Figure 2D). This result demonstrated that antibodies targeting any of the three ECDs of ROR1 can mediate rapid internalization and degradation of ROR1.

Then we analyzed the inhibitory effects of antibodies on the Wnt5a-induced Rac1 activation in Jeko-1 cells. The results demonstrated that although the epitopes of R-001c/R-002c/R-004c/R-005c or UC-961 were in the same domain, the antibodies showed different effects on ROR1-mediated activation of GTP-Rac1, as R-001c/R-004c/UC-961 suppressed the formation of GTP-Rac1, whereas R-002c and R-005c failed to inhibit its downstream signaling pathways (Figure 2E). These findings indicated that the inhibitory activity of antibodies against ROR1 signaling pathways may differ depending on their binding modes, even within the same domain, and some epitopes are not functional to block the Wnt5a-ROR1 signaling pathway.

Directed engineering of humanized anti-ROR1 antibodies with optimized affinity

Then, we systematically engineered humanized variants based on parental antibodies R-001c and R-005c. Functional screening by ELISA and flow cytometry identified Hu001-2 and Hu005-46 with superior antigen-binding activity (patent pending, Chinese patent application No. 202310832175.8), with EC50 ≤ 0.04 nM against purified ROR1-ECD protein (Figure 3A) and EC50 ≤ 0.3 nM against ROR1 endogenously expressed on cancer cells (Figure 3B; Figure S2). Dynamic internalization assays further demonstrated that these two antibodies were rapidly recognized and efficiently internalized by ROR1-positive cells, especially Hu001-2, which showed more rapid internalization than Hu005-46 and UC-961 (Figures 3C and 3D; Figure S3A). Surface plasmon resonance (SPR) kinetic analysis revealed high-affinity binding of Hu001-2 and Hu005-46 to ROR1 protein, with equilibrium dissociation constants KD of 6.753 × 10−10 M and 7.269 × 10−10 M, respectively (Figure 3E; Figure S3B). Given that ROR1 shares high structural homology with ROR2, ROR2 represents the most significant potential for off-target binding. To address this, we evaluated the binding specificity of Hu001-2 against recombinant ROR2 ECD via ELISA. The results showed that Hu001-2 exhibited high-affinity binding to ROR1 but no detectable interaction with ROR2 (Figure S4). These data indicate that Hu001-2 possesses high selectivity, distinguishing ROR1 even from its closest family member, ROR2. Anti-tumor efficacy of humanized anti-ROR1 antibody-based ADCs

To enhance the anti-tumor efficacy, we engineered an ADC by site-specifically conjugating the humanized mAb Hu001-2 to MMAE via a protease-cleavable vc linker (Figure 4A). Quantitative LC-MS analysis confirmed a drug-to-antibody ratio (DAR) of 4 (Figure S5), ensuring optimal payload delivery. The ADC drug exhibited similar binding affinity to ROR1 as the parental mono-antibody (Figure 4B). To evaluate the anti-proliferation activity of ADC, we first analyzed the mRNA level of multiple cancer types by the CCLE database, which showed higher expression in lung cancer, triple negative breast cancer, colon cancer, liver cancer and gastric cancer, and basal level expression in multiple normal tissues (Figure S6). The anti-proliferation activity and ROR1 expression were determined in several cancer cell lines by CellCounting‑Lite cell viability assay and flow cytometry. The results showed potent ROR1-dependent cytotoxicity in ROR1-high tumor cell lines; meanwhile, in ROR1 low-expressed cells SK-BR-3 and MCF7, the anti-proliferation activity was significantly weakened (Pearson’s r = −0.7449 between ROR1 surface density and cytotoxicity, p < 0.01) (Figure 4C). Functional characterization of Hu001-2-based immune-activating fusion protein

The Wnt5a-ROR1 signaling pathway contributes to the establishment of an immunosuppressive tumor microenvironment. Through Gene Set Cancer Analysis (GSCA) of immune infiltration associations, we observed that in multiple cancer types, ROR1 expression level exhibits negative correlations with NK cells and CD8+ T cells, and positive association with regulatory T cell (Treg) cells (Figure S8), suggesting suppressed NK cell-mediated immune function in ROR1-positive cancers.15,16 To enhance tumor immune responses using ROR1-targeting mAb-based therapeutics, we selected interleukin-15 (IL-15), a pleiotropic cytokine that plays a pivotal role in immune cell development, survival, and activation, particularly in promoting NK cell activation and CD8+ T cell proliferation. We designed an innovative therapeutic approach by engineering a fusion protein that combines IL-15 with IL-15Rα and conjugates it to an ROR1-targeting antibody Hu001-2. This bispecific construct is designed to selectively deliver IL-15 to the tumor microenvironment by trans-presenting the cytokine on ROR1-positive cells, thereby activating IL-15Rβ-expressing NK cells and CD8+ T cells in situ.

To validate the feasibility of this strategy, we first constructed the expression vectors by tethering hIL15Rα-IL15 to the C terminus of the heavy chain (HC) of the ROR1 antibody Hu001-2 with a flexible linker (Hu001-2-IL15Rα-IL15) (Figure 6A). Fc-fused IL15Rα-IL15 (Fc-IL15Rα-IL15) was similarly constructed as an isotype control (Figure S9). Subsequently, the purified fusion protein was obtained and used to characterize the binding affinity to ROR1 and IL-15Rβ. ELISA results confirmed the fusion protein’s dual high binding capacity for both ROR1 and IL-15Rβ (EC50 for ROR1: 0.0167 nM; IL15Rβ: 0.0937 nM) (Figure 6B). Functional characterization revealed that the fusion protein Hu001-2-IL15Rα-IL15 promoted the proliferation of a human NK-derived cell line NK92 with similar potential as recombinant human IL15 (rhIL15) (Figure S11), confirming that the fusion did not disrupt the immune activation function of IL-15. Moreover, after a 6-day culture of PBMCs, Hu001-2-IL15Rα-IL15 induced the formation of large cell aggregates, indicating the stimulation of the PBMC expansion and activation. FACS analysis revealed an increased proportion of CD3/CD8+ T cells and CD56+ NK cells (Figure 6C), which was associated with the enhanced secretion of cytotoxic cytokines IFN-γ and TNF-α in an Hu001-2-IL15Rα-IL15 dose-dependent manner (Figure 6D). Meanwhile, the Hu001-2-IL15Rα-IL15 primed PBMC cells were co-cultured with MDA-MB-231-Fluc cells for 24 h at different E:T ratios. The luminescence analysis revealed that the cytotoxic activity of PBMC was enhanced by Hu001-2-IL15Rα-IL15 at the ratio of 10:1 and 20:1. And the fusion with antibody did not disrupt the IL-15 function, as compared with the Fc-IL15Rα-IL15 (Figure 6E). The immune activation and cytotoxic efficacy of Hu001-2-IL15Rα-IL15 were then evaluated in an immunocompetent mouse model. Mouse 4T-1 cells stably overexpressing human ROR1 were established via lentiviral transduction and inoculated into BALB/c mice. Following tumor establishment, the mice were treated with IgG1 isotype control, Fc-IL15Rα-IL15, naked ROR1 antibody, or Hu001-2-IL15Rα-IL15. With 20 μg/kg dose (0.685 μM/kg), the Fc-IL15Rα-IL15 treatment elicited systemic toxicity, resulting in mouse mortality following the second injection (Figure 7A). In contrast, equimolar doses of Hu001-2-IL15Rα-IL15 did not induce lethality and demonstrated potential anti-tumor activity with TGI = 71.6% (Figures 7B and 7C). At the end, the TNF-α and IFN-γ expression in tumor tissues was measured by real-time RT-PCR. The result showed that compared to mouse IgG control, the fusion protein significantly enhanced the expression of cytokines in tumor tissue (Figure 7D), which indicated the localized immune activation by Hu001-2-IL15Rα-IL15 in tumors. While the widespread circulation of Fc-IL15Rα-IL15 induced a cytokine storm, fusing IL15Rα-IL15 with an anti-ROR1 antibody facilitated its specific targeting to ROR1-high tumors. Discussion

ROR1 has been established as a critical oncogenic driver, particularly through its interaction with Wnt5a, which activates the non-canonical Wnt signaling pathway to promote tumor cell proliferation, survival, and metastasis.17 ROR1 has emerged as an attractive target for CAR-T and ADC therapies for cancer treatment.17 The clinical landscape of ROR1-targeted therapies has expanded significantly in recent years. Zilovertamab, a humanized mAb inhibiting Wnt5a signaling, has shown promising safety and efficacy in combination with ibrutinib for MCL and CLL.18 Beyond naked antibodies, the ADC Zilovertamab vedotin has demonstrated potent antitumor activity in preclinical models and is currently under clinical evaluation for various malignancies.13,14 Furthermore, T cell redirection strategies, including ROR1-specific CAR-T cells and bispecific T cell engagers (BiTEs) such as NVG-111 (ROR1 × CD3) have exhibited robust cytotoxicity.19,20

While previous studies, such as those involving the clinical-stage antibody, have highlighted the importance of targeting the extracellular Kringle or Frizzled domains to inhibit Wnt5a binding,21 our study provides more information on epitope-function relationships. We identified that antibodies targeting the Ig-like domain and specific conformational epitopes spanning the Frizzled-Kringle domains exert distinct inhibitory effects on Wnt5a-Rac1 signaling. Intriguingly, we observed differential signaling blockade even among antibodies binding the same domain. This suggests that mere domain binding is insufficient for functional inhibition; rather, specific steric hindrance or conformational locking of ROR1 may be required to prevent Wnt5a-Rac1 signaling activation. This finding underscores the potential for dual-epitope targeting strategies to achieve superior blockade of ROR1 signaling, potentially overcoming the limited efficacy observed with mono-epitope blockade.

The efficacy of ADCs is governed not only by target specificity but critically by internalization kinetics and intracellular trafficking.22 For ROR1, its specific functions and internalization efficiency in different tumor microenvironments have not yet been fully elucidated. Our humanized antibodies Hu001-2 and Hu005-46 demonstrated rapid internalization kinetics, a prerequisite for the efficient lysosomal delivery of the payload. The engineered ADC, Hu001-2-MMAE, exhibited potent cytotoxicity that correlated with ROR1 expression. Meanwhile, significant anti-tumor efficacy was maintained even in the ROR1-low-expressing N87 tumor model. This is likely attributable to the membrane-permeable nature of the MMAE payload released via the cleavable linker, which facilitates a bystander effect.23 This mechanism allows the Hu001-2-MMAE to kill neighboring tumor cells that possess low ROR1 density, addressing the challenge of intratumoral heterogeneity often seen in solid tumors. MK-2140(UC-961-MMAE), an ADC comprising the anti-ROR1 antibody UC-961 conjugated to MMAE, is currently undergoing phase III clinical trials for frontline diffuse large B-cell lymphoma (DLBCL). In our comparative studies, Hu001-2 demonstrated faster internalization kinetics and higher binding affinity for ROR1 compared to UC-961 in tumor cell models. Furthermore, in in vivo xenograft models, Hu001-2-MMAE exhibited potent anti-tumor activity comparable to that of the UC-961-MMAE. These data demonstrated Hu001-2-MMAE as a potential clinical candidate with rapid internalization and significant anti-tumor efficiency.

Moreover, based on our novel ROR1 antibody, we developed an Hu001-2-IL15Rα-IL15 fusion protein and evaluated its therapeutic potential using in vitro and in vivo models. The ROR1 expression is frequently associated with an immunosuppressive tumor microenvironment. While IL-15 is a potent activator of NK and CD8+ T cells and lacks the Treg stimulation associated with IL-2, its clinical utility has been hampered by short half-life and systemic toxicity.24 Our data demonstrate that fusing the IL-15/IL-15Rα complex to the anti-ROR1 antibody achieves effective tumor-targeted delivery. In our in vivo models, the non-targeted Fc-IL15Rα-IL15 control induced mortality at therapeutic doses, whereas the ROR1-targeted fusion remained well-tolerated while eliciting potent anti-tumor immunity. This confirms the high-affinity retention of the IL15Rα-IL15 with ROR1 antibody to the ROR1-positive TME reduces systemic exposure and associated adverse events, such as cytokine release syndrome.25 Furthermore, by anchoring IL-15 to the tumor cell surface, the fusion protein mimics the physiological trans-presentation of IL-15, effectively bridging the innate and adaptive immune systems to attack the tumor. This dual mechanism by direct ROR1 targeting combined with local immune reactivation offers a synergistic approach to overcoming the immunosuppressive tumor microenvironment often associated with ROR1-positive malignancies. Although the Hu001-2-IL15Rα-IL15 fusion protein demonstrated a widened therapeutic window in murine models, further investigation is still needed in several key areas. First, we will comprehensively characterize the immunomodulatory effects of the fusion protein on the tumor microenvironment. Second, given the high potency of IL-15 and the low-level basal expression of ROR1 in certain normal tissues, rigorous safety evaluations are required to assess potential on-target/off-tumor toxicities. Moreover, since constitutive cytokine stimulation risks inducing T cell exhaustion, optimizing dosing schedules will be critical to sustain durable immune responses.

In summary, this study characterizes a novel panel of ROR1-targeting antibodies with distinct mechanisms of action. We demonstrated that epitope specificity is a determinant of signaling blockade, and based on the antibody, we developed a potential clinical ROR1-ADC candidate with rapid internalization and potential anti-tumor efficacy. Moreover, our novel cytokine fusion protein Hu001-2-IL15Rα-IL15 provides a proof-of-concept for widening the therapeutic window of cytokine therapies through tumor-targeted delivery, offering a promising strategy to remodel the immunosuppressive microenvironment of ROR1-expressing cancers.

Limitations of the study

While our in vitro and in vivo results confirm the high-affinity binding and potent anti-tumor efficacy of the novel ADC in ROR1-expressing models, the precise structural basis of the antibody-antigen interaction remains to be fully elucidated. Although our current data establish antibody specificity against the closest homolog ROR2, comprehensive tissue cross-reactivity assessments of ROR1 antibody Hu001-2 are required for the preclinical development phase. Furthermore, although the Hu001-2-IL15Rα-IL15 fusion protein demonstrated a widened therapeutic window in murine models, further characterization within the tumor microenvironment—such as dynamic changes in immune cell subsets, the specific contribution of key effector populations, and the spatiotemporal features of local immune remodeling—remains to be fully elucidated. In follow-up studies, we plan to employ multiparameter flow cytometry, single-cell RNA sequencing, and spatial transcriptomics to systematically profile how the fusion protein modulates key immune populations (e.g., T cells, NK cells, and myeloid cells) in the tumor microenvironment, and to clarify the cellular and molecular mechanisms underlying enhanced immune infiltration and reversal of immunosuppression. These investigations will provide a more substantiated mechanistic foundation for the differentiated mode of action of this therapeutic approach. And given the high potency of IL-15 and the low-level basal expression of ROR1 in certain normal tissues, rigorous safety evaluations are required to assess potential on-target/off-tumor toxicities. Moreover, since constitutive cytokine stimulation risks inducing T cell exhaustion, optimizing dosing schedules will be critical to sustain durable immune responses.

Experimental model and study participant details

In vivo subcutaneous tumor model

All animal studies were conducted using female BALB/c nude mice. Five-week-old mice were obtained from Gem Pharmatech (Nanjing, China). For the therapeutic efficacy studies, female BALB/c nude mice aged 4–6 weeks were used. The animals were housed in a specific pathogen-free (SPF) facility with a controlled light/dark cycle and provided with sterile food and water. All procedures were performed in accordance with the animal care regulations of the Hefei Institutes of Physical Science, Chinese Academy of Sciences. The experimental protocols were reviewed and approved by the Hefei Institutes of Physical Science, Chinese Academy of Sciences (approval numbers: IACUC-2023-PD-110, IACUC-2022-PD-133). To generate the tumor model, mice were subcutaneously implanted with tumor cells. When the tumors reached a volume of 100–200 mm3, the mice were randomized into treatment groups. The indicated ADCs were administered via intravenous injection once weekly. Following the start of treatment, body weight and tumor growth were monitored every three days. Tumor volume was calculated using the formula: Tumor volume (mm3) = (W2 × L)/2, where width (W) is defined as the smaller of the two measurements and length (L) is defined as the larger of the two measurements.

4T1-hROR1 orthotopic tumor model

Eight-week-old female BALB/c mice were obtained from Gem Pharmatech (Nanjing, China). All animals were housed in a specific pathogen-free (SPF) facility. All experimental procedures were conducted in accordance with the animal care regulations of the Hefei Institutes of Physical Science, Chinese Academy of Sciences, and the study protocol was approved under the approval number DWLL-2025-75. For tumor inoculation, 4T1-hROR1 cells were harvested during the exponential growth phase, and 1 × 105 cells suspended in PBS were orthotopically inoculated into the fourth right mammary gland of each mouse. When the mean tumor volume reached approximately 50 mm3, the animals were randomized into treatment groups consisting of five mice per group for efficacy studies. The recombinant protein was administered intravenously twice per week at a dose of 0.685 μM in saline. An isotype immunoglobulin G (IgG) was injected as a control using the same schedule. Tumor growth and body weight were monitored every two days throughout the treatment period. Tumor volume was calculated using the formula: tumor volume (mm3) = (W2 × L)/2, where width (W) is defined as the smaller of the two measurements and length (L) is defined as the larger of the two measurements. At the end of the experiment, mice were humanely euthanized, and tumor tissues were collected for gene expression analysis. Total RNA was isolated from tumor tissues using the SPARKeasy Tissue/Cell RNA Kit (SparkJade). The mRNA expression levels of tumor necrosis factor-alpha (TNF-α) and interferon-gamma (IFN-γ) were subsequently quantified by real-time RT-PCR.

Cell culture

The human cancer cell lines HT29, SKCO-1, COLO-678, HCC1187, NCI-H446, HCC827, SK-BR-3, MCF7, NCI-H226, MDA-MB-231, NCI-N87, and PA-1 were obtained from Cobioer Biosciences Co. Ltd. (Nanjing, China). Cells were cultured in the appropriate media as recommended by the manufacturer and maintained at 37°C under 5% CO2. Fresh peripheral blood mononuclear cells (PBMCs) were isolated from healthy volunteer donors. Isolated PBMCs were pre-cultured in RPMI-1640 medium supplemented with 10% fetal bovine serum (FBS), 1% non-essential amino acids (NEAA), and 1% sodium pyruvate, and maintained at 37°C under 5% CO2.

Method details

Generation of ROR1 antibody and ADC

Purified extracellular domain of human ROR1 protein (ROR1-ECD, Sino Biological) was used as immunogen. BALB/c mice were immunized subcutaneously with 50 μg ROR1-ECD emulsified in complete Freund’s adjuvant, followed by two booster immunizations with 25 μg ROR1-ECD in incomplete Freund’s adjuvant. Splenocytes were isolated from immunized mice and fused with SP2/0 myeloma cells using polyethylene glycol (PEG) according to standard hybridoma technology. Fused cells were cultured in HAT selection medium in 96-well plates. Hybridoma supernatants were screened by enzyme-linked immunosorbent assay (ELISA) and flow cytometry. Stable positive hybridoma clones were subcloned by limiting dilution. Total RNA was extracted from selected hybridoma clones, variable region genes (VL and VH) were amplified using the SMARTer RACE 5'/3′ Kit (Clontech). Chimeric antibodies were constructed by cloning the mouse VL and VH regions into expression vectors containing human kappa and human IgG1 constant regions, respectively. Chimeric antibodies were expressed in expi-293 cells by transient transfection and purified by Protein G chromatography. Humanization was performed by CDR grafting. The mouse CDR regions were grafted onto selected human acceptor frameworks with highest homology to mouse framework regions. Two humanized variants, Hu001 and Hu005, were designed, synthesized, and cloned into human kappa and human IgG1 expression vectors, expressed and purified by Protein G affinity chromatography.

The ROR1 ADCs were generated by bioconjugation of monomethyl auristatin E (MMAE) to the cysteine residues on Hu001 or hIgG1 via a maleimide linker. The drug-to-antibody ratio (DAR) was determined by hydrophobic interaction chromatography (HIC) or liquid chromatography-mass spectrometry (LC-MS), confirming an average DAR of approximately 4.

Enzyme-linked immunosorbent assay

A 96-well plate was coated with 100 μL per well of human ROR1 protein diluted to 1 μg/mL in coating buffer (PBS, pH 7.4). The plate was sealed and incubated overnight at 4°C. The coating solution was removed, and the plate was washed three times with 300 μL per well of wash buffer (PBS containing 0.05% Tween 20, PBST) for 3 min each wash. Subsequently, 200 μL per well of blocking buffer (PBS containing 1% BSA) was added, and the plate was incubated at 25°C for 2 h to block nonspecific binding. The plate was washed three times with PBST. Then, 100 μL per well of primary antibody diluted to the appropriate concentration was added, and the plate was incubated at 25°C for 2 h. The plate was washed five times with PBST. Next, 100 μL per well of HRP-conjugated secondary antibody (Cell Signaling Technology) diluted 1:4000 was added, and the plate was incubated at 25°C for 1 h. The plate was washed five times with PBST. Next, 100 μL per well of TMB solution was added, and the plate was incubated at 37°C. When the OD620 reached approximately 1.0, the reaction was stopped by adding 50 μL per well of 2 M H2SO4. The absorbance at 450 nm (OD450) then measured.26

Flow cytometry analysis

A 5-fold serial dilution series of the primary antibody was prepared in serum-free RPMI-1640 containing 1%BSA, with concentrations ranging from 60 μg/mL to 0.000768 μg/mL. For each sample, 100 μL of the cell suspension (1 × 105 cells) was mixed gently with 100 μL of the diluted primary antibody solution, achieving final antibody concentrations ranging from 30 μg/mL to 0.000384 μg/mL. The mixtures were incubated at 4°C for 1 h. Cells were washed twice with PBS. The secondary antibody, PE-goat anti-human IgG-Fc (Abcam), was diluted 1:200 in serum-free RPMI-1640 containing 1%BSA. Cells were resuspended in 200 μL of the diluted secondary antibody solution and incubated at 4°C for 30 min in the dark. Cells were washed twice with PBS and resuspended in 200 μL of PBS. Fluorescence was measured by FACS (Beckman, CytoFLEX). Data were collected and analyzed using FlowJo software.

Immunofluorescence colony staining

MDA-MB-231 cells were seeded in confocal chamber slides and allowed to adhere overnight. After 16 h, cells were washed twice with PBS, and incubated with the ROR1 antibody (diluted in RPMI-1640 containing 10% FBS to a final concentration of 50 nM) at 37°C for 4 h or 4°C for 1 h. After incubation, cells were washed three times with PBS and fixed with 4% formaldehyde (in PBS) for 30 min at room temperature. Then, cells were washed three times with PBS and permeabilized with 0.3% Triton X-100 in PBS for 10 min at room temperature. Cells were washed twice with PBS. For detection of lysosomes and internalized ROR1 antibody, cells were incubated with rabbit anti-LAMP-2 antibody (Abcam) diluted 1:500 in RPMI-1640 containing 1% BSA for 1 h at 37°C. After three washes with PBS, cells were simultaneously incubated with goat F(ab')2 anti-human IgG-Fc (PE) (Abcam; 1:200) and Alexa Fluor® 488 donkey anti-rabbit IgG (H + L) (Jackson Immuno Research; 1:400) for 30 min at 37°C in the dark. Cells were washed three times with PBS, stained with DAPI for 10 min. Images were acquired using the confocal microscope (Olympus, 60 ×).

Cytotoxicity assay

MDA-MB-231 cells were seeded in 6-well plates at 3 × 105 cells per well and incubated overnight. The next day, medium was replaced with fresh medium containing 8 μg/mL polybrene and lentiviral particles overexpressing firefly luciferase and incubated for additional 24 h. Viral supernatant was then replaced with complete medium containing 1 μg/mL puromycin for 48 h to select transduced cells. Surviving cells (MDA-MB-231-Fluc) were expanded and cryopreserved. PBMCs were plated in 48-well plates at 1 × 106 cells per well in 100 μL medium. Cells were treated with 10 nM of either Fc-IL15Rα-IL15 fusion protein or Hu001-2-IL15Rα-IL15 fusion protein. Control wells received an equivalent volume of medium. PBMCs were cultured for 5 days at 37°C in 5% CO2 to allow activation and expansion. On day 4, MDA-MB-231-Fluc target cells were plated in white opaque 96-well plates at 1 × 104 cells per well in 100 μL medium and allowed to adhere overnight. On day 5, activated PBMCs were harvested, washed once with PBS, counted, and resuspended in fresh complete RPMI for co-culture experiments. PBMCs (effector cells) were added to target cells at effector-to-target (E:T) ratios of 10:1 and 20:1 in a final volume of 200 μL per well. All conditions were set up in triplicate. After 24 h of co-culture at 37°C, plates were equilibrated to room temperature for 10 min and added Bright-Glo Luciferase Assay reagent at 100 μL per well. Plates were incubated on an orbital shaker to ensure complete lysis. Luminescence was measured using the microplate reader.

Quantification and statistical analysis

All numerical data were processed using Microsoft Excel and statistical analyses were performed with GraphPad Prism 8 software (GraphPad Software). Data are presented as mean ± standard deviation (SD). The sample size (n) for each experimental group is indicated in the figure legends and represents the number of independent biological replicates or animals per group, as specified. Statistical comparisons between two groups were conducted using two-tailed unpaired Student’s t test. For comparisons involving multiple groups, one-way analysis of variance (ANOVA) was performed, followed by Tukey’s multiple comparison test (for comparisons between all groups) or Dunnett’s multiple comparison test (for comparisons against a single control group), as appropriate. A p-value of less than 0.05 was considered statistically significant. Significance levels are denoted in the figures as follows: ∗p < 0.05, ∗∗p < 0.01, ∗∗∗p < 0.001, and ∗∗∗∗p < 0.0001; ns indicates not statistically significant (p ≥ 0.05).
