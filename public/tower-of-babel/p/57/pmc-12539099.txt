Factors influencing the choice of radiology as a specialty among clinical-year medical students in Nigeria: a multi-center cross-sectional study
Authors: Ntishor Gabriel Udam, Prince Adakole Obaje, Adaobi Chiazor Emegoakor, Edache Las Edache, Elvis Oche Obaje, Abdulrasaq Olalekan Oladosu, Aliyu AbdulKadir, Daniel Toochukwu Nneji, Olamide Daniel Odusola, Athanasius Christopher Ofem, Abubakar Farate, Favour Satkyes Bachi, Munachi Onyebuchi Nwabunike, Alice Edward, Kingsley Chidiebere Nwaogu
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundRadiology is a cornerstone of modern healthcare delivery, yet it remains underrepresented among medical students’ specialty choices in Nigeria. Understanding the factors that influence specialty preference is essential to addressing workforce shortages and improving healthcare services. We aimed to assess the level of interest in Radiology and identify factors influencing specialty choice among clinical-year medical students across Nigeria.MethodologyA multi-center, cross-sectional study was conducted among clinical-year medical students from six Nigerian universities representing all geopolitical zones. Data were collected using a structured online questionnaire and analysed with SPSS Version 20. Associations between the duration of radiology rotation and the choice of radiology specialty, as well as influencing factors, were assessed using chi-square tests, with statistical significance set at p < 0.05.ResultsThe response rate was 97.4% (411/422). Of the 411 respondents, 111 (27.0%) expressed an interest in Radiology. Positive influencing factors included work-life balance (346; 84.2%),job flexibility (189; 46.0%), use of emerging technology (187; 45.5%), intellectual stimulation (245; 59.6%), and community relevance (213; 51.8%). Barriers included concerns about radiation exposure (248; 60.3%), overwhelming breadth of clinical knowledge (285; 69.3%), limited patient interaction (167; 40.6%), and fears about artificial intelligence (139; 33.8%). Longer duration of Radiology rotations was significantly associated with greater interest in the specialty (p = 0.044).ConclusionMultiple factors, including but not limited to clinical exposure, influence students’ interest in Radiology. Enhancing Radiology training experiences, strengthening mentorship, addressing misconceptions, and improving systemic working conditions are crucial strategies to encourage more medical students to pursue Radiology and support Nigeria’s healthcare needs.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12909-025-08067-w.Keywords: Clinical rotation, Medical students, Nigeria, Radiology, Specialty choice

Background

Radiology is an indispensable part of modern clinical practice, proffering both diagnosis and treatment to a plethora of illnesses across various clinical specialties [1–3]. Despite its integral role in patient care, radiologists make up a minimal number of the hospital workforce globally [1, 2]. The role of a radiologist has also evolved from mainly hospital practice to embrace roles in policy making, public health service, and cutting-edge technology that will contribute substantially to healthcare delivery [3]. The recent technological advancement in Radiology and its significance to healthcare delivery have also necessitated an increased demand for radiology services, which has been met with an unceasing surge in radiologists’ shortage globally. Still, Radiology remains among the least preferred specialties among medical students in Nigeria [4].

Nigeria is already experiencing a low doctor-to-population ratio, compounded by inadequate healthcare funding; as such, the need for equitable distribution of medical professionals among specialties is highly significant [5]. Radiology has continually been reported as one of the least preferred specialty, which has also led to the prevalence of non-radiologists performing imaging procedures and interpreting medically acquired images. While thishelps to reduce diagnostic time and hastenpatient care, there are higher chances of errors compared to radiologists who are intensively trained in diagnostic radiology [6, 7]. The recent estimate of radiologists in Nigeria is fewer than 700, representing approximately one radiologist per 660,000 people, compared to about one per 6,000 in high-income countries such as the United States of America (USA). This highlights a significant disparity between the number of specialists and the population they serve. Similar disproportions are observed in other low- and middle-income countries, with one radiologist per 833,000 people in Uganda and one per 270,000 individuals in Kenya [2]. This lack of expertise further diminishes the diagnosis and treatment of diseases, which results in negative patient outcomes and unethical practices [8, 9].

Various factors affect the choice of specialties among medical students and fresh medical graduates. These choices can have a vital influence on medical practitioners’ distribution and maldistribution among specialties, thus affecting the overall quality of the healthcare system [5, 8, 10]. Some medical students have certain specialty preferences even before matriculating and starting their medical training [7]. However, these preferences may be adjusted based on their gratifying exposure during different clinical rotations, which leads to attraction towards a specialty and forms the essence of their career choice down the line [7, 10]. The clinical rotations in Radiology and other least preferred specialties like Ear, Nose and Throat (ENT), and Anaesthesia, are for very short duration of four weeks or less which give students limited exposure and knowledge to the field of radiology and give students a misperception of the specialty which negatively impacts the supply of future radiologist [8, 11]. This limited exposure leads to poor understanding of the specialty and worries, which negatively influence radiology as a specialty of choice [1, 6, 12]. Many other factors influence these decisions, and understanding the determinants of specialty of choice is important to develop interventions aimed at influencing career choices.

This radiologists’ shortage has significant implications, including delays in patient care, burnout, reduced productivity among the few available radiologists, and an increased risk of misdiagnosis or mismanagement when radiographs are interpreted by non-radiologists [13]. In Nigeria, there are instances where radiographers interpret and report radiographs informally in some hospitals. Though research has proposed that, to address the impact of this shortage, radiographers could be formally trained in image interpretation and reporting, given their foundational knowledge of human anatomy and associated pathologies acquired during undergraduate and postgraduate training [14], there is still a need to assess the interest of medical students in radiology and the factors influencing it. This will help inform measures to improve interest and ultimately increase the number of medical students choosing to specialize in radiology in the future.

To the best of our knowledge, no prior Nigerian study has explored factors that encourage or dissuade medical students and early career doctors from the field of Radiology, though studies in diaspora have provided significant findings to understand factors influencing medical students’ inclination towards or away from the radiology specialty [1, 3, 6, 11, 12, 15–17]. The socioeconomic disparity, varied educational background, dissimilar medical training, clinical rotation, and career prospects provided the rationale for the research exploration in Nigeria [1]. Assessing the factors affecting medical students’ preference towards radiology as a specialty choice will allow for lecturers, faculty members and seasoned consultants identify deficiencies that need improvement, improve radiology as a potential career interest and produce a good supply of competent radiologists to meet the growing needs and improve healthcare delivery especially in a developing country like Nigeria [5, 9, 10, 15].We aimed to determine medical students’ specialties of interest with radiology reference and identify factors influencing medical students towards Radiology as a specialty.

Methodology

Study site and population

A two-stage cluster probability sampling technique was employed to select the study sites, which were medical schools across the six geopolitical zones of the Federal Republic of Nigeria responsible for undergraduate medical education. The selected study sites were the College of Medical Sciences, University of Maiduguri; College of Medical Sciences, University of Calabar; College of Health Sciences, Ahmadu Bello University, Zaria; College of Health Sciences, University of Ilorin, Ilorin; Faculty of Medicine, Nnamdi Azikiwe University, Nnewi; and College of Medicine, University of Ibadan. These institutions were carefully chosen to ensure national representation across the six zones, taking into account the regional diversity in medical training environments, institutional size and reflection of potential variations in students’ exposure to and perceptions of Radiology.At the time of data collection, the six institutions represented approximately 44 accredited medical colleges in Nigeria, which collectively graduate about 3,530 medical doctors per year [18]. However, because only six institutions were sampled, the number of clinical-year students across these schools could not be precisely determined.

Study design and participants

This research adopted a cross-sectional study design aimed at exploring the factors influencing medical students’ choice of Radiology as a specialty of interest. The study participants comprised medical students in their clinical years that had completed their Radiology clinical rotations and were available and willing to participate during the data collection period. Clinical-year medical students who had completed a Radiology clinical rotation but declined consent were excluded from the study.

Sampling technique and sample size determination

Within each selected institution, a simple random sample of eligible clinical-year students was drawn from class lists provided by the class representatives. Random numbers were assigned to the full list and participants were selected using a random number generator. The survey invitation was delivered primarily via direct messages by the class representatives to the randomly selected students. For logistics and to improve reach, the questionnaire link was also posted in the official class WhatsApp groups as a general reminder; however, recruitment and analysis were based on the randomly selected invitees from the pre-generated lists.

A total minimum sample size of 422 was determined using the standard formula for cross-sectional studies:

n = Z2×p×(1 − p)d2n = \frac{{Z^2 \times p \times (1-p)}}{d^2}.

Where Z represents the standard normal deviate set at 1.96 for a 95% confidence level, p represents the estimated proportion of students interested in Radiology (assumed to be 0.50 to maximize variance in the absence of a previous nationwide estimate), and d is the margin of error set at 0.05. To accommodate possible non-responses, a 10% adjustment was made to the calculated sample size.

Sample weighting was applied to proportionally allocate the total sample size across the different universities, using the formula: (institutional estimated population ÷ total target population) × total sample size, to ensure representation reflective of the actual distribution of clinical-year students.Thus, institutions with larger clinical-year cohorts were assigned proportionally higher sample sizes to ensure balanced representation of the broader clinical medical student population.

Study instrument and Pre-testing

Data collection was conducted using a structured, closed-ended online questionnaire hosted on Google Forms. The questionnaire was adapted from previously validated studies [1, 5, 11, 19, 20]. Prior to full deployment, the questionnaire was pretested among Radiography students at the University of Maiduguri, as they had comparable exposure to imaging sciences and could provide informed feedback on clarity and feasibility of the questionnaire, prior to administering it to medical students.

The questionnaire was organised into six sections. Section A captured the sociodemographic characteristics of respondents. Demographic variables such as religion were included to explore potential cultural influences on specialty choice.Section B explored students’ top three specialty choices, while section C assessed the reasons for choice of specialty.Non-clinical options such as Business/Entrepreneurship were included to capture alternative career interests outside conventional medical practice. Section D assessed the duration of Radiology clinical rotations. Section E examined factors attracting students to Radiology, while Section F focused on factors dissuading students from choosing Radiology.

Procedure for data collection

Data collection spanned from January 12, 2024, to November 2, 2024. Class representatives from each institution were recruited as research assistants to facilitate questionnaire dissemination, while the principal investigators coordinated data collection. Primary invitations were sent via direct messages to the randomly selected students. To minimize participation from ineligible students, the questionnaire link was shared directly with eligible students via private messages to ensure that selected invitees received the link. Additionally, the questionnaire’s first section contained screening questions to confirm clinical-year status and completion of radiology rotations before access to subsequent sections.

Ethical considerations

Ethical approval for this study was obtained from the Health Research Ethics Committee of Nnamdi Azikiwe University Teaching Hospital (Ref. No: NAUTH/CS/66/VER.3/34/2024/02). Informed consent was obtained electronically from all participants prior to data collection. Participants were assured of the confidentiality of their responses, the voluntary nature of their participation, and their right to withdraw from the study at any stage without penalty. The study was conducted in accordance with the ethical principles outlined in the Declaration of Helsinki.

Variables and outcome measures

The primary outcome variable was “interest in Radiology,” which was explored through a set questions including selecting Radiology as a preferred specialty from a list of medical specialties, and highlighting multiple factors within the “Factors Influencing Specialty Choice” section of the questionnaire. These covered different aspects of the specialty, including enjoyment of the Radiology posting, the appeal of its physics and technology components, intellectual stimulation, relevance to the community, financial remuneration, work-life balance, and the influence of mentorship. Each item was rated on a three-point scale (“Yes,” “No,” or “Uncertain”). For analysis, students who answered “Yes” to the core question, “Are you interested in the field of Radiology?” were considered to have an expressed interest, while the additional items helped to provide context and a deeper understanding of the reasons behind their responses.

Secondary variables included demographic characteristics, duration of Radiology posting, and responses to listed influencing and deterring factors.

Data analysis

The collected data was exported into Microsoft Excel for cleaning to eliminate incomplete, inconsistent, or inaccurate responses. Cleaned datasets were then analysed using IBM Statistical Package for Social Sciences (SPSS) Version 20. Descriptive statistics, including frequencies, percentages means and standard deviations, were used to summarise categorical variables. Inferential analysis was performed using the Pearson’s chi-square test (χ²) to determine associations between the duration of Radiology clinical rotations and students’ interest in Radiology, as well as between students’ interest in Radiology and the factors influencing their specialty choices. All χ² and p-values for significant associations are reported alongside the corresponding results in the Results section. A p-value of less than 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Results

Sociodemographic characteristics

A total of 411 medical students participated in the study, with most respondents being males (229; 55.7%) and females (182; 44.3%). The mean age of respondents was 25.03 ± 2.52 years, with the majority (273; 66.4%) aged between 21 and 25 years, followed by those aged 26–30 years (126; 30.7%), 31–35 years (10; 2.4%), and ≤ 20 years (2; 0.5%). Most respondents were in their final year of study (303; 73.7%), while 94 (22.9%) were in 500 level and 14 (3.4%) in 400 level.

Participants were drawn from six medical schools across Nigeria, with the highest representation from the University of Maiduguri (80; 19.5%) and the University of Calabar (76; 18.5%), followed by Nnamdi Azikiwe University (77; 18.7%), University of Ibadan (71; 17.3%), Ahmadu Bello University (61; 14.8%), and University of Ilorin (46; 11.2%). Most respondents identified as Christian (278; 67.6%), while 129 (31.4%) were Muslim and 4 (1.0%) belonged to other religions (Table 1).

Specialty preferences

Internal Medicine was the most preferred specialty (234; 56.9%), followed by Surgery (200; 48.7%) and Obstetrics & Gynaecology (118; 28.7%). Radiology ranked fourth, with 111 (27.0%) of respondents indicating it as their preferred choice. No respondents selected General Practice, Community Health, or Business/Entrepreneurship as their primary career path (Table 2).

Exposure to radiology and specialty choice

Among participants, 299 students (72.7%) had four weeks or fewer of radiology postings, 80 (19.5%) had postings of 5–8 weeks, and 32 (7.8%) had postings exceeding eight weeks. The duration of radiology exposure was significantly associated with the choice of radiology as a specialty (χ² = 6.240; df = 2; p = 0.044). A higher proportion of students who had longer postings were more likely to consider Radiology as a career (> 8 weeks: 11/32; 34.4% interested) (Table 3).

Factors dissuading the choice of radiology

Students who did not consider Radiology as a career cited several barriers. The most significant deterrents included the perceived overwhelming breadth of required clinical knowledge (285; 69.3%), limited patient interaction (167; 40.6%), lack of a mentor in the specialty (232; 56.4%), and concerns about artificial intelligence posing a threat to the field (139; 33.8%). Additionally, 248 (60.3%) expressed concerns about radiation exposure. Other deterrents were a lack of interest in radiology research (149; 36.3%), no interest in Radiology overall (172; 41.8%), and negative perceptions of the role of radiologists (71; 17.3%) (Table 6).

Perception of radiology

Students’ perception of Radiology was mixed. While 294 (71.5%) acknowledged the role of radiologists in clinical decision-making, 167 (40.6%) found the limited patient interaction unappealing (Table 6). A majority (346; 84.2%) believed the specialty provided a good work-life balance, and 317 (77.1%) agreed that it allowed raising a family (Table 5).

Discussion

We found radiology to be the fourth most preferred specialty, with Medicine being the medical specialty of most interest. Excellent work-life balance was the major reason participants were attracted to radiology, while fear of radiation was the most significant factor dissuading students from considering Radiology as their first specialty choice.

Besides Radiology, the departments of Surgery, Medicine, Obstetrics and Gynaecology (O&G), and Paediatrics were the most considered specialties. Similar studies conducted among medical students across Africa have shown similar results [7–10, 22]. This finding is due to the increased length of exposure of students to these faculties, as they have medical and surgical rotations in their fourth year, early aspects of the fifth year, and most of theirfinal year, while O&G and Paediatrics classes and rotations are done in the fifth year, spanning approximately several months altogether [22]. Previous studies also buttressed that longer exposure to an activity or specialty culminates in greater enthusiasm and an interest in these fields among early-career professionals and students [7, 23, 24]. Notably, institutions with a longer length of radiology exposure in our study had higher rates of students interested in it, and this relationship was significant. Conversely, those with relatively shorter Radiology rotationscompared to other clinical specialties had similar findings with previous studies, highlighting that limited or no radiology exposure negatively impacts students’ interest in the specialty, as they have a poor understanding of the radiologist’s responsibilities and the department’s workflow [1, 3, 11, 17, 24].

Participants who preferred radiology revealed that the major influencing factors were job flexibility with fewer working hours, work-life balance, and use of emerging technology. Other positive drivers included the specialties’ intellectual stimulation, the community relevance of Radiology, and the financial remuneration; findings similar to other local and international studies [1, 3, 5, 8, 10, 11, 17, 19, 21]. Additionally, our study discovered that students who enjoyed their Radiology posting reported an interest in pursuing the specialty. However, a 2020 report in USA found that 45% of radiologists were unsatisfied and stressed with their jobs, which they reported as a reduced work and family life balance [25]. Medical students need to be aware of the work-life realities in various Radiology settings to avoid misconceptions and burnout.

Radiology as a specialty requires an in-depth understanding of anatomy, pathology, physics, and advanced imaging techniques to solve medical dilemmas and arrive at a diagnosis. This makes the training quite stressful but attractive for those seeking intellectual stimulation [12, 24]. Radiologists are also in high demand due to their important role in diagnostic and therapeutic medicine [1, 3, 6]. Radiology also offers a variety of practice settings and work-life balance options, including telemedicine, radio-oncology, and interventional radiology, along with their various hospital managerial roles [26]. Collectively, these factors create a compelling case that sets Radiology as an attractive specialty of choice [3, 16].

Another significant influencing factor towards Radiology was the benefit of having enough time to raise a family. This finding is in contrast to USA and Saudi Arabian studies, where allowance to raise a family in the future was one of the least influential factors influencing the choice of radiology [11, 16]. The Radiologist’s relevance to other clinicians was also another significant encouraging factor, a finding similar to a study in Saudi Arabia [27]. However, a different inference was seen in another study in the same location [11], where less than 30% of respondents were attracted due to the radiologist’s indispensability. This could be due to the low exposure to the specialty in those settings, which culminates in poor knowledge about radiology, thereby making them unaware of the radiologist’s crucial role in patients’ care. Most participants in our study also enjoyed their Radiology rotation as they were likely to engage more actively with the radiologists, develop a deeper understanding of the specialty, and appreciate the radiologist’s impact on healthcare while witnessing firsthand how they inform clinical decisions across other medical specialties.

In the same manner, various factors dissuaded other respondents from Radiology. Students expressed concerns about the wide range of clinical knowledge needed to specialize in radiology, the inadequate patient interaction, and concerns about artificial intelligence (AI) threatening Radiologists’ job security. These concerns may result from poor knowledge of AI’s evolving role in radiology. Rather than replacing radiologists, AI serves to augment the Radiologist’s skill, thereby enhancing diagnostic accuracy, improving workflow efficiency, and allowing radiologists to focus on more complex clinical decisions [28]. Similar misconceptions were noted in a recent report from the Middle East, suggesting the need for broader education to address the evolution of the specialty [27]. Another notable dissuading factor in our study was the risk of radiation exposure, which aligns with other studies [11, 27]. This finding portrays the lack of knowledge about the existence of radiation protection laws and safety guidelines provided by the Nuclear Safety and Radiation Protection Act of 1995 (No.19)which was enforced in compliance with Nigeria Basic Ionizing Radiation Regulations (NiBIRR), relevant regulations established by theNigerian Nuclear Regulatory Authority(NNRA) and internationally recognized standards. The Act established the NNRA whose functions included controlling and regulating of the use of radioactive substances, materials, equipment, emitting and generating ionising radiation. The guidelines ensure the protection of workers, public and the environment from harmful effects of ionizing radiation, without limiting the beneficial uses of ionizing radiation, while enabling Nigeria meet its national and international Radiation Safety Standards, and their implementation in the day-to-day running of radiology departments. These policies, which are already in place in Nigeria, guide the application of ionizing radiation in the country and globally. Furthermore, other studies have shown the unawareness about radiation protocols, with only a handful of participants being properly aware of these measures [29, 30].

In our study, 56.4% of respondents reported not having a mentor in Radiology, which may contribute to reduced interest in the specialty. Similarly, 56.3% indicated no interest in Radiology or Radiology research, suggesting that the absence of mentorship may be associated with disengagement from the field. Various studies have also highlighted similar findings and buttressed the importance of mentorship on specialty choice [5, 10, 15, 31]. The time allocation for Radiology postings, compounded by the lack of career consulting, coaching, and firsthand encouragement, narrows medical students’ awareness of other appealing specialties and makes them select cliché roles [23].Aside from improving the time allotted for these postings, the rotations should serve as opportunities for Radiologists to engage more with the students, identify those undecided about their medical paths or those who piqued interest in medical imaging, and strengthen their resolve towards future specialization in the faculty.

To address the factors limiting radiology’s appeal as a choice among students and early career professionals, several interventions should be implemented. First, increasing exposure to Radiology in medical school curricula through longer medical rotations and structured learning experiences will allow students to develop a deeper understanding of the specialty’s scope, appreciate its relevance, and explore career opportunities. Radiologists should also make these postings more enjoyable and interactive for the students. Radiology departments in medical institutions should create formal mentorship programs led by experienced radiologists.School clubs and interest groups anchored by senior trainees will also provide students with a better understanding of the field, career guidance, address misconceptions, and highlight the long-term prospects of radiology. These groups should be incorporated into the school’s curriculum and should coordinate regular seminars with hands-on workshops, case studies, imaging interpretation, presentation of research projects, and networking opportunities with practicing radiologists, to stimulate students intellectually. The sessions should also serve to reinforce the role of radiology in multidisciplinary patient care by teaching its application across various specialties. Additionally, addressing students’ concerns about radiation exposure is essential, as many students are unaware of existing radiation safety protocols [11, 27], contributing to fears about long-term health risks. These could be incorporated as topics in the curriculum, group seminars, and other informal sessions, while showcasing non-ionizing imaging advancements, to alleviate these concerns and improve confidence in the specialty.

Another factor influencing specialty choice is the growing trend of medical graduates seeking postgraduate training opportunities abroad. The brain drain in the country, combined with the competitive nature of getting into specialty training in diaspora, makes these early career professionals seek specialties with easier entry to specialize in and reduces their interest in radiology. The government should address systemic challenges contributing to brain drain by improving residency training structures, offering competitive remuneration, and enhancing working conditions. This will not only retain more doctors in Nigeria but also make postgraduate Radiology training more appealing as a career choice locally.

Beyond its relevance to medical education, our study also highlights opportunities to tackle the uneven distribution of radiologists in rural and urban areas in Nigeria. By fostering interest in Radiology among medical students, policymakers could develop targeted incentives, strengthen mentorship, and expand rural training pathways to encourage a more balanced workforce and improve access to diagnostic imaging services across the country.

Limitations

This study has several limitations that should be considered when interpreting its findings. Firstly, the sample size was relatively smalland drawn from only one medical school per geopolitical zone in Nigeria. This limited representation may affect the generalizability of the findings to a broader population of medical students across the country. The final sample size of 411 fell slightly short of the calculated 422, which may have marginally reduced the statistical power of the study. Secondly, data collection was conducted using online Google Forms, with student representatives facilitating participation. While this approach allowed data gathering from multiple locations, it may have introduced selection bias, as only students with internet access and interest in the study were more likely to participate. The use of a purely quantitative questionnaire also limited the opportunity to capture the diverse opinions of participants regarding career choices; a qualitative study would have been more appropriate for a deeper understanding of students’ perceptions of Radiology and other specialties. Additionally, reliance on self-reported responses raises the possibility of recall and social desirability biases. Thirdly, regional limitations, including differences in internet connectivity, institutional policies, and academic schedules, may have contributed to the reduced response rate. Moreover, some students may have been hesitant to participate due to concerns about how the study might influence their already existing specialty preferences, potentially leading to response bias. Furthermore, this study did not assess participants’ in-depth knowledge of Radiology as a field or their understanding of the role of a Radiologist.

The cross-sectional nature of this study limits its ability to establish causality between the factors analysed and students’ specialty choices. Future studies with larger, more diverse samples and longitudinal designs would help provide a more comprehensive understanding of these trends. Finally, the study did not follow up to determine whether students who expressed an interest in Radiology eventually enrolled in Radiology residency training programs, to check whether implemented interventions had good results. Despite these limitations, the study provides valuable insights into the factors influencing medical students’ specialty choices in Nigeria, highlighting areas for further research and policy interventions.

Conclusion

This national study explored the factors influencing the choice of Radiology among clinical-year medical students across Nigeria. We found that Radiology was among the moderately preferred specialties, with longer clinical rotations significantly associated with greater interest. However, career preferences were shaped by multiple factors beyond exposure alone. Work-life balance, flexible working conditions, use of emerging technology, and intellectual stimulation were major attractions to Radiology, while fears about radiation exposure, limited patient interaction, and misconceptions about the impact of artificial intelligence served as significant deterrents.

Addressing these barriers requires a multi-pronged approach, including enhancing early and prolonged Radiology exposure during medical training, strengthening mentorship and career guidance, correcting misconceptions about radiation safety and AI, and showcasing the evolving and diverse roles of Radiologists in healthcare. Furthermore, systemic reforms to improve working conditions and residency training locally are essential to retain talent. By implementing these measures, Nigeria can increase the visibility of Radiology as a viable career path and ensure a sustainable workforce to meet the country’s growing healthcare needs.
