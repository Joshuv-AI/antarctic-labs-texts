Economic Burden of Recurrence in Early-Stage Hepatocellular Carcinoma Treated with Surgical Resection or Local Ablation in the United States: A Retrospective Analysis of Surveillance, Epidemiology and End Results–Medicare Data
Authors: Neil Mehta, Liya Wang, Shital Kamble, Yan Song, Emily Gao, Abby Siegel, Angie Lax, Sanjana Sundaresan, Adina Zhang, Vicky Wei, Usha Malhotra
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
IntroductionPatients with early-stage hepatocellular carcinoma (HCC) who undergo curative-intent therapy remain at risk of disease recurrence. This study aimed to quantify the economic burden associated with disease recurrence in these patients.MethodsPatients ≥ 66 years old with early-stage HCC who have achieved complete radiological response via surgical resection or local ablation were selected from the SEER–Medicare database (2007–2017). Patients with evidence of recurrence following complete radiological response with curative-intent therapy were assigned to the recurrence cohort and followed from the date of recurrence (index date). Patients without evidence of recurrence were included in the recurrence-free cohort and followed from an assigned index date based on the distribution of time between curative-intent therapy and recurrence in the recurrence cohort. Healthcare resource utilization (HRU) and costs (2024 USD) were compared between cohorts, adjusting for demographic and clinical characteristics.ResultsA total of 309 patients with recurrence and 302 patients without recurrence were included. In the recurrence and recurrence-free cohorts, respectively, mean age was 75.0 and 74.5 years (p = 0.21), with male patients representing 66.3% and 60.3% (p = 0.12). Mean follow-up was 20.8 and 22.0 months, respectively. Per patient per month (PPPM) mean all-cause total healthcare costs were significantly higher in the recurrence cohort compared with the recurrence-free cohort ($14,697 vs $6223; adjusted cost difference [ACD] $9205; p < 0.01). Similarly, PPPM HCC-related total costs were $11,202 vs $3313 (ACD $10,021; p < 0.01). Inpatient visit costs were the major driver for the higher all-cause and HCC-related total costs in the recurrence cohort versus recurrence-free cohort.ConclusionsFindings suggest a significant HRU burden and high total healthcare costs for patients with early-stage HCC who experience disease recurrence after curative-intent therapy compared with patients who remain recurrence free. This underscores a need for future novel therapies that offer recurrence-free survival benefits and have the potential to reduce the economic burden of managing early-stage HCC.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1007/s12325-026-03562-y.

Introduction

Liver cancer was the third leading cause of cancer-related mortality in 2022 and ranks among the most prevalent cancers worldwide [1]. Hepatocellular carcinoma (HCC) is the most common primary liver cancer, representing 75–85% of all cases globally [1, 2]. The risk of HCC increases with advancing age, with a median age of 60–69 years at diagnosis in the USA [3–5].

Among patients with early-stage HCC where tumors are contained to the liver, curative-intent therapies are generally used [6]. These approaches include surgical resection, liver transplantation, or percutaneous local ablation, with treatment choices depending on factors such as tumor burden, tumor location, and patient characteristics including comorbidities and frailty among older adults [6, 7]. Surgical resection and local ablation are widely used and effective first-line therapies for patients with early-stage HCC [6], although recurrence following these treatments is common. Multiple prospective trials have evaluated recurrence following curative-intent treatment for early-stage hepatocellular carcinoma, reporting 3-year recurrence rates of approximately 50% to 70% [8–10].

Given the high recurrence rates associated with early-stage HCC curative-intent therapies, evaluating the economic burden of recurrence remains an essential component of understanding the potential value of interventions designed to prevent or delay disease recurrence in this population. Prior studies have reported a substantial economic burden associated with HCC in general, particularly among older adults. Specifically, a 2012 study using the Surveillance, Epidemiology, and End Results (SEER)–Medicare linked data estimated that the incremental monthly all-cause direct healthcare cost of treating an older patient (≥ 66 years old) with HCC was $6621 higher than those without HCC [11]. Despite the high overall economic burden of HCC, research on the economic impact of early-stage HCC remains limited [12–15], with a paucity of studies quantifying the financial burden of disease recurrence after curative-intent therapies in the USA. To bridge this gap in knowledge, this retrospective study used SEER–Medicare data to assess the healthcare resource utilization (HRU) and economic burden associated with recurrence in patients with early-stage HCC treated with curative-intent therapies in the USA.

Methods

Data Source

SEER–Medicare data (2007–2017) were used to address the study objectives. SEER–Medicare data reflect the linkage of two large population-based data sources that provide detailed information about Medicare beneficiaries with cancer. The SEER cancer registries contain data on clinical characteristics, demographic characteristics, and cause of death, while the Medicare claims include covered healthcare services from the time of a patient’s Medicare eligibility until death. For this study, the data included all Medicare-eligible patients appearing in the SEER data who were diagnosed with cancer until 2017 and had Medicare claims until 2019. Medicare Part D was available from 2007 onward.

Compliance with Ethics Guidelines

All data in this study were limited and fully adhered to The Health Insurance Portability and Accountability Act of 1996 and was performed in accordance with the Helsinki Declaration of 1964 and its later amendments.

Study Design and Patient Selection

The study included patients ≥ 66 years old with a diagnosis of HCC (International Classification of Disease for Oncology, Third Edition: C220 with HCC histology: “8170” to “8175” and in situ or malignant behavior: “2” or “3”) between 2007 and 2017 who underwent surgical resection or local ablation with curative intent that resulted in complete radiological response. The algorithm used to define complete radiological response, including a 90-day “disease-free” period, was described in a prior publication and summarized in the Supplementary Materials [16]. To be included in the study, patients were required to be continuously enrolled in Medicare Parts A, B, and D for ≥ 6 months before and ≥ 3 months after the date of complete radiological response and have no evidence of stage IV cancer at the initial HCC diagnosis, no diagnosis for any other malignancy prior to the date of complete radiological response, no evidence of participation in a clinical trial during the baseline period (i.e., the 6 months preceding the date of complete radiological response) or the study period, and no radiotherapy or systemic treatment use prior to the date of complete radiological response. The study population was restricted to patients aged ≥ 66 years to ensure that all individuals had a complete baseline period (i.e., ≥ 12 months) of continuous Medicare enrollment while being age-eligible for Medicare coverage.

To classify patients into recurrence and recurrence-free cohorts, recurrence was identified from the Medicare claims (between 2007 and 2019) and defined as the earliest of (1) new treatment initiation for HCC (i.e., surgery, radiation therapy, chemoradiation, or systemic therapy), (2) liver transplantation, or (3) a diagnosis of metastatic malignancy. Patients who experienced HCC recurrence following complete radiological response were included in the recurrence cohort and their index date was defined as the first date of recurrence. Patients who did not experience recurrence following complete radiological response were included in the recurrence-free cohort and their index date was assigned at a date approximating the time point at which recurrence “would have” been observed, in an effort to follow patients across cohorts from similar time points during their disease trajectory. To achieve this, the index date for each patient in the recurrence-free cohort was selected from the distribution of time (beta distribution) between complete radiological response and recurrence that was observed in the recurrence cohort. In this way, the index dates assigned for the recurrence-free cohort matched the overall distribution of duration from complete radiological response to index date in the recurrence cohort. A sensitivity analysis was conducted using an alternate distribution (i.e., normal distribution) to select the index date for the recurrence-free cohort. This distribution was chosen to ensure it was distinct from the beta distribution used in the primary analysis, allowing for an assessment of the robustness of the findings.

Study Measures and Outcomes

The baseline demographic and clinical characteristics were described for both cohorts. All-cause and HCC-related HRU and costs included inpatient admissions, inpatient length of stay (LOS), outpatient visits, emergency department (ED), and skilled nursing facility (SNF). HCC-related HRU and costs were defined based on claims with a diagnosis for HCC. Specifically, a claim was considered HCC-related for an outpatient visit if there was a primary diagnosis of HCC, as secondary diagnoses in outpatient settings may often reflect chronic or a history of HCC rather than the primary reason for the visit [17]. For other types of visits, a primary or secondary diagnosis of HCC was used because hospitalization, ED, and SNF encounters may involve multiple active conditions (e.g., treatment-related adverse event), and HCC may be documented in either the primary or secondary diagnosis position. HCC-related Part D pharmacy costs were defined as costs associated with Part D claims for a National Comprehensive Cancer Network® (NCCN) guideline-recommended HCC therapy [18]. The incidence rates of HRU and healthcare costs were reported as events or costs per patient per month (PPPM) to account for different lengths of follow-up between patients. All costs were adjusted for inflation and expressed in 2024 US dollars using the medical service component of the Personal Consumption Expenditures (PCE) [19].

Statistical Analysis

Continuous variables were described using means and standard deviations, and categorical variables were reported as frequencies and percentages. Statistical differences in the baseline characteristics between the recurrence and recurrence-free cohorts were assessed using chi-square tests for categorical variables and two-sample t tests for continuous variables, and the corresponding p values were reported. HRU was compared between the cohorts using generalized linear models (GLMs) with a Poisson distribution, with an offset to account for varying follow-up durations between patients. Unadjusted and adjusted incidence rate ratios (IRRs) with their respective 95% confidence intervals (CIs) and p values were reported. For unadjusted comparisons, costs were compared between the cohorts using Wilcoxon rank-sum tests, and unadjusted cost differences were reported as the difference in mean costs between the recurrence and recurrence-free cohorts. Adjusted cost differences and inpatient LOS along with corresponding p values were estimated using GLMs with a Tweedie distribution. The baseline covariates considered in adjusted models were selected based on the balance between cohorts and the variable’s clinical significance. Known pathobiological risk factors for recurrence after surgical resection or local ablation—such as tumor stage, viral hepatitis status, comorbidities related chronic liver disease [20, 21]—were assessed at baseline and compared between the recurrence and recurrence-free cohorts to inform variable selection. The final adjusted models included age at index, sex, race, tumor–node–metastasis stage at diagnosis, baseline Quan–Charlson Comorbidity Index (CCI) score, baseline portal hypertension, baseline chronic hepatitis B, type of curative-intent therapy received (i.e., surgical resection or local ablation), and risk of recurrence (i.e., low to intermediate or high to very high).

Results

Patient Characteristics

In the overall population, a total of 611 patients met all selection criteria. Of these patients, 309 (50.6%) patients experienced recurrence after complete radiological response with curative-intent therapies and were included in the recurrence cohort and 302 (49.4%) patients did not experience recurrence and were included in the recurrence-free cohort. Compared with the recurrence-free cohort, the recurrence cohort had a lower proportion of patients with tumor–node–metastasis (TNM) stage I disease (41.7% vs 53.3%; p = 0.004) and a higher proportion of patients with Grade II tumor (60.5% vs 50.7%; p = 0.042) at diagnosis (Table 1). In the recurrence and recurrence-free cohorts, respectively, surgical resection was performed in 82.5% and 83.8% of patients (p = 0.680), and 23.9% and 20.9% were classified as having high to very high risk of recurrence (p = 0.360). The mean (SD) CCI was comparable between patients with and without recurrence (3.3 [1.8] vs 3.0 [1.9]; p = 0.080) although compared with the recurrence-free cohort, a lower proportion of patients in the recurrence cohort had chronic hepatitis B (11.3% vs 17.2%; p = 0.037) and a higher proportion of patients had diabetes without chronic complications (33.7% vs 24.8%; p = 0.017) and portal hypertension (19.1% vs 11.6%; p = 0.010) during the baseline period.

Healthcare Resource Utilization

The mean follow-up duration after index date was 20.8 (median 12.9) and 22.0 (median 15.2) months for patients in the recurrence and recurrence-free cohorts, respectively. During the follow-up period, all-cause inpatient visits were reported in 79.3% of patients in the recurrence cohort and 48.3% of patients in the recurrence-free cohort. Nearly all patients in both cohorts had an all-cause outpatient visit during the follow-up period (94.8% of patients in the recurrence cohort and 97.4% of patients in the recurrence-free cohort). SNF stays were similar between the two cohorts (18.8% in the recurrence cohort and 14.9% in the recurrence-free cohort), while ED visits occurred in 52.4% of patients in the recurrence cohort and 43.7% of patients in the recurrence-free cohort (Table 2). The proportion of patients who had an HCC-related inpatient admission during the follow-up period was 71.2% and 24.2% in the recurrence and recurrence-free cohorts, respectively. HCC-related outpatient visits remained high in both cohorts, 90.3% of the recurrence cohort and 86.3% of the recurrence-free cohort. HCC-related SNF stays were low in both cohorts (7.1% in the recurrence cohort and 4.6% in the recurrence-free cohort). Sensitivity Analysis

The sensitivity analysis, which used an alternative distribution to impute the index date for the recurrence-free cohort, produced consistent differences in HRU and costs between the recurrence and recurrence-free cohorts, reinforcing the robustness of the primary analysis.

Discussion

This retrospective study provides real-world evidence quantifying the substantial economic burden associated with disease recurrence in older patients with early-stage HCC treated with curative-intent therapies. The results show that patients who experienced HCC recurrence generally had higher rates of all-cause HRU, including a 2.1-fold increase in the rate of inpatient admissions and a 1.2-fold increase in the rate of outpatient visits. Consequently, patients who experienced recurrence had significantly higher healthcare costs than those without recurrence, incurring an excess of $9205 PPPM in total medical expenses primarily driven by higher inpatient admission costs.

Notably, while patients without recurrence had fewer inpatient admissions and lower associated costs, their hospital stays were slightly longer when admitted. This may reflect differences in hospitalization drivers, as patients with recurrence were more often admitted for HCC-related complications rather than age-related or comorbid conditions. It is plausible that HCC recurrence is associated with more intensive monitoring, resulting in a pattern of more frequent but shorter inpatient stays. Further research is warranted to better understand the clinical drivers behind these utilization patterns.

Although high HRU and costs among patients with early-stage HCC have been previously reported [11–15], this real-world study is the first to assess the incremental HRU and costs associated with recurrence in older patients with early-stage HCC who had previously achieved complete radiologic response after undergoing curative-intent therapies in the USA. Using SEER–Medicare data from 1991 to 2007, White et al. reported that among patients with primary localized and regional HCC, the average total monthly healthcare costs from HCC diagnosis to the end of follow-up were $7265 and $8072, respectively [11], which is in line with the costs observed among patients in the recurrence-free cohort in this study ($6223). Similarly, the costs reported by White et al. were primarily driven by the cost of inpatient admissions. Importantly, the current study shows that patients with recurrence experienced nearly double monthly healthcare costs ($14,697). While not evaluated in the current study, future research quantifying the incremental changes in HRU and costs across disease etiologies may help identify patient subgroups with a higher economic burden and risk of recurrence.

This study extends the limited body of literature quantifying the economic burden of recurrence among older patients with early-stage HCC in the USA. One preliminary study by Ektare et al. evaluated the indirect costs associated with disease recurrence among patients with early-stage HCC in the USA and showed significant morbidity and premature mortality following recurrence, resulting in an estimated $4.1 billion in total earnings losses (2019 USD) [13]. More broadly, prior economic evaluations of HCC in the USA have focused on overall healthcare costs, costs of specific interventions, or costs within specific etiologic subgroups, rather than costs directly attributable to recurrence, as highlighted in a systematic review by Aly et al. [22] Notably, studies quantifying this burden outside of the USA are scarce. In Spain, Varela et al. quantified the cost of recurrence in patients with early-stage HCC who underwent curative-intent therapy using decision-tree model analysis and showed that the average cost in the first year following recurrence was €26,253 (~ $28,459 in 2024 USD) [12]. While this annual cost is lower than the amount reported in the recurrence cohort in this study ($14,697 PPPM, extrapolated to $176,364 per year), the discrepancy likely reflects differences in care practices, healthcare systems, and payment structures between countries. In addition, the study by Valera et al. was a decision-tree model analysis, evaluating theoretical scenarios and modeling costs based on published literature and expert opinion, rather than real-world patient data, limiting direct comparisons to the current study.

The findings from this study describe the substantial incremental medical costs associated with recurrence from a payer perspective. However, they do not account for broader economic consequences such as patient out-of-pocket spending and indirect costs including productivity losses. As the prior study by Ektare et al. suggests [13], these indirect costs may be considerable, and future research quantifying a patient-centered perspective would provide a more complete understanding of the total burden of recurrence.

Management of early-stage HCC has evolved over the past decade, which affects the generalizability of our findings (2007–2019) to current practice. Several agents evaluated during the study period for recurrence prevention, including vitamin K analogues, retinoids, and sorafenib, did not demonstrate meaningful clinical benefit and therefore did not materially alter recurrence patterns or healthcare costs. More recently, the use of immunotherapies has expanded, particularly as first- and second-line treatments for advanced HCC. Nivolumab received accelerated approval in 2017 as the first immune checkpoint inhibitor for HCC, followed by pembrolizumab in 2018 and subsequent approvals of additional agents [23].

Recurrence following curative-intent treatment in early-stage HCC remains a major clinical challenge, as it is closely tied to poor long-term outcomes and limits the effectiveness of repeated interventions [24]. This study fills an important gap in the HCC literature by quantifying the real-world economic burden of recurrence and establishing the magnitude by which healthcare costs increase for patients who recur. These estimates provide a benchmark for healthcare system decision makers, supporting more informed economic modeling and budget-impact assessments when weighing the trade-offs of therapies aimed at preventing or delaying recurrence among the older adults in the USA.

Limitations

The results of this study should be interpreted in light of certain limitations. Administrative claims data are collected primarily for billing purposes and do not capture detailed clinical information such as imaging or radiographic findings which are required in clinical practice to confirm complete radiological response or detect recurrence. Consequently, this study relied on an algorithm developed using various procedure codes, diagnosis codes, drug codes, and assumptions to approximate complete radiological response using a 90-day disease-free window and identify HCC recurrence. As a result, coding inaccuracies may have led to a misclassification bias. In particular, the proxy used to identify recurrence is susceptible to exposure misclassification, such as when residual disease is mislabeled as recurrence because additional imaging or treatment is deferred beyond 90 days. Given the potential challenges in accurately identifying complete radiological response, data investigations were conducted to assess the suitability of using a 90-day period to define the “disease-free” period by examining alternative durations of 60 and 120 days during which patients were required to have no evidence of recurrence or subsequent treatment for HCC. These investigations confirmed that there was not a considerable difference in the patient populations between the various definitions of a “disease-free” period. Notably, similar proxies have previously been used in studies conducted in adjuvant settings based on SEER–Medicare data [25, 26].

Furthermore, standard staging information for HCC is not consistently available in SEER. As a result, we applied a proxy definition to identify patients with early-stage HCC in order to minimize exclusion due to missing or incomplete staging data. This approach was intended to capture patients who were treated as having early-stage disease in real-world clinical practice. In our study, only a small proportion of patients were classified as AJCC Stage III (7.0%), suggesting a small proportion of patients with more advanced disease are treated as early-stage patients in real-world practice. Similar proxy-based staging approaches have been used in prior real-world studies of HCC [27, 28].

Another key limitation of the study is that the index dates for patients in the non-recurrence cohort were not based on actual event dates but were instead imputed using the distribution of index dates observed in the recurrence cohort. However, by assigning patients in the recurrence-free cohort a time point at which recurrence would have occurred based on the distribution of dates observed in the recurrence cohort, this approach enabled a more comparable evaluation across cohorts by aligning time points in the patients’ HCC disease course. This method is consistent with approaches described by Karim et al. for indexing on time-dependent events and has been applied in prior SEER–Medicare studies [29–32]. In the primary analysis, a beta distribution was selected based on the empirically observed distribution of time to recurrence among patients with recurrence. To assess the robustness of this approach, a sensitivity analysis was conducted using an alternative distribution to evaluate HRU and costs. Results from this sensitivity analysis corroborated findings from the main analysis, increasing confidence in the robustness of findings.

In addition, identification of HCC-related Part D pharmacy costs was limited to NCCN-recommended treatments for HCC. Many supportive medications, such as those for pain control, corticosteroids, and antiemetics, are frequently prescribed for other comorbid conditions, in addition to supportive care for HCC. These medications were therefore excluded from the definition of HCC-related costs.

While adjusted analyses comparing HRU and healthcare costs between the recurrence and recurrence-free cohorts included key patient characteristics, unmeasured covariates may have confounded the results, limiting causal inferences. Retrospective claims studies are subject to selection bias arising from design elements such as continuous enrollment requirements and utilization of claims-based definitions to proxy clinical information that may not be visible in the dataset. In our study, the requirement for at least 6 months of continuous enrollment before complete radiologic response to establish a baseline and the requirement for an additional 3-month disease-free period to proxy complete response may contribute to such bias, specifically potentially overrepresenting patients who had stable health insurance enrollment and survived through the required observation period. The resulting HRU and cost estimates may not fully reflect the broader early-stage HCC population; however, these criteria were applied uniformly to both recurrence and recurrence-free cohorts, helping maintain comparability and reducing differential bias when estimating the incremental difference in healthcare costs.

Finally, as the linked SEER–Medicare database includes patients insured through Medicare who are ≥ 65 years old, the results of this study may not be generalized to a younger patient population.

Conclusions

This retrospective, USA-based study found significant healthcare resource burden and high total healthcare costs among patients with early-stage HCC who experience recurrence after curative therapy compared to those who remain recurrence-free. These findings indicate a need for future novel therapies with recurrence-free survival benefit in the management of patients with early-stage HCC.
