Empirical analysis of the carbon-economy nexus in industrial parks using an integrated decoupling-decomposition framework from a major industrial region
Authors: Qinyuan Zhu, Hanyi Rui, Yousef Alhaj Hamoud, Hiba Shaghaleh, Weina Shen, Qiuke Su, Jingnan Chen
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
Industrial parks as critical carriers of China’s industrial development, they face significant pressure for low-carbon transformation. Current research on carbon emissions primarily focuses on national or sectoral levels, leaving a gap in understanding the driving factors and the carbon emissions–economic development mechanism at the industrial park level, which hinders precise emission reduction strategies. This study examines 44 industrial parks in the Yangtze River Delta, analyzes the temporal evolution of carbon emissions and assesses the economic development levels of the industrial parks based on the TOPSIS model. The Tapio decoupling model is adopted to analyze the decoupling status between carbon emissions and economic development, while the LMDI decomposition method is used to explore the driving factors. Empirical findings reveal that carbon emissions in these parks exhibit fluctuating growth, the decoupling state between carbon emissions and economic development is predominantly weak, transitioning to strong decoupling by 2023, key factors driving carbon emissions include per capita industrial development levels and industrial land scale effects. The combined application of the Tapio decoupling model and LMDI decomposition demonstrates universality in analyzing carbon emission drivers in industrial parks, providing theoretical and practical insights for carbon reduction in industrial parks.

Introduction

Carbon emissions have become a pressing issue with rapid global economic growth and escalating energy demands. Low-carbon development has emerged as a worldwide consensus1 As the largest developing nation, China recognizes the challenges of these goals and integrates carbon peaking and neutrality into its strategic framework, balancing socio-economic development with climate objectives2,3.

The industrial sector, a cornerstone of China’s economic growth, accounts for approximately 60% of national carbon emissions4. As spatially concentrated hubs integrating production factors, industrial parks serve as vital platforms for industrial development5,6. While enhancing industrial efficiency, these parks also concentrate resource consumption and pollutant emissions, contributing 31% of China’s total CO2 emissions7.

Studies on low-carbon development and carbon emission drivers in industrial parks include include Van8 quantifying emissions in Australian parks, Lombardi9 exploring EU policy-driven transition pathways, and Hashimoto10 analyzing cement industry reductions in Kawasaki, Japan. Seok Jung11 conducted decomposition analyses in South Korean industrial regions. Liu12 established a low-carbon evaluation system for a Chinese industrial park using the TOPSIS model. Liu13 constructed a Kaya extended model and used scenario analysis to predict the total amount and peak state of industrial carbon emissions. Yu14 and Pang15 evaluated the decoupling status between carbon emissions and industrial added value using 171 Chinese industrial parks and industrial parks in the western region as examples. Lin and Long16 decomposed carbon emissions in China’s chemical industry, highlighting industrial scale, energy intensity, energy structure, and labor productivity as key factors.

In summary, although some research has accomplished notable results, some shortcomings remain. Firstly, as a “spatial carrier”, the carbon emission driving mechanism of industrial parks differs from that of national and industry levels in terms of exclusive features such as “land use and spatial agglomeration”, but existing research has not fully incorporated these factors. Secondly, the decoupling analysis of “carbon emissions economic development” in industrial parks often relies on a single economic indicator, which fails to reflect the comprehensive economic characteristics of the park as a “multifunctional industrial agglomeration zone”, resulting in a deviation in the mechanism analysis of the two. Furthermore, existing studies often use TOPSIS, Tapio, or LMDI models in isolation, and the limitations of a single method make it difficult for existing research to form a closed-loop cognition of “evaluation decoupling driving”, which cannot provide a complete scientific basis from diagnosis to countermeasures for carbon emissions in industrial parks. This study focuses on 44 industrial parks in the Yangtze River Delta and is not limited to a single method application. The TOPSIS, Tapio, and LMDI models are combined. TOPSIS is suitable for the comprehensive quantification of multi-dimensional economic indicators in the park, Tapio can capture the dynamic elastic relationship between the park’s’ economy carbon emissions’, and LMDI’s residual free characteristics are suitable for decomposing complex driving factors in the park, constructing a complete logical chain of ‘TOPSIS Quantitative Economic Development Indicators Tapio Analysis Decoupling Status LMDI Disassembling Driving Factors’. This study fills the gap of isolated application of methods and improves the accuracy of mechanism analysis by adapting to the characteristics of industrial parks, achieving a closed-loop analysis of’ current situation evaluation relationship characterization reason analysis’. The findings aim to provide actionable insights for industrial park decarbonization, supporting China’s broader carbon neutrality agenda.

Research design

Study area

The Yangtze River Delta situated in the eastern coastal region of China, represents one of the nation’s most economically developed and socioeconomically advanced areas16. Similar to other rapidly growing regions, the Yangtze River Delta faces challenges such as environmental pollution and ecological degradation alongside its high-speed development17. This region hosts numerous industrial parks characterized by rapid economic growth and diversified industries, serving as pivotal platforms for industrial development and economic activities in the Yangtze River Delta18. However, these parks also exhibit high pollutant emissions and intensive resource consumption, prompting them to enter a transition phase of economic restructuring19. Investigating the driving factors of carbon emissions in these industrial parks can provide a scientific foundation for targeted emission reduction strategies.

The Yangtze River Delta encompasses three provinces (Jiangsu, Zhejiang, Anhui) and one municipality (Shanghai). By the end of 2023, the region had a permanent population of 238 million and a regional GDP of approximately 30.5 trillion CNY, accounting for 24.2% of China’s total GDP. It is China’s most economically dynamic, open, and innovative region. Industrial parks in the Yangtze River Delta also lead the country in quantity, development quality, and environmental governance.

This research collects the relevant data from 2014 to 2023 from the time series perspective. Economic development data and carbon emission data are collected from the annual statistical reports of each industrial park.

Data description

It should be noted that the data studied in this article, including energy consumption, industrial added value, industrial land area, population, etc., are derived from some urban statistical yearbooks and government reports. The carbon emission data mainly comes from the annual carbon emission reports of industrial parks.

Method

With the comprehensive industrial parks in the Yangtze River Delta region of China as the research object, data on economic development and carbon emissions from 44 industrial parks from 2014 to 2023 were collected. Using the TOPSIS model to quantify the levels of economic growth in industrial parks, based on data such as carbon emission and economic development, Tapio decoupling and LMDI model are used to thoroughly analyze the driving factors of carbon emission in comprehensive industrial parks and provide suggestions for their low-carbon development. The research framework is shown in Fig. 1. The detailed steps are as follows:

Step 1: Take the industrial parks in the Yangtze River Delta region of China as the research object, collecting relevant data from 2014 to 2023 and constructing an index system of economic development index, using the TOPSIS model to calculate the economic development levels of industrial parks.

Step 2: Collect carbon emission data from industrial parks from 2014 to 2023 and analyze the temporal evolution characteristics of carbon emissions.

Step 3: Conduct a study on decoupling carbon emissions and economic development levels in industrial parks under the Tapio decoupling model.

Step 4: Based on the industrial development characteristics of industrial parks, the LMDI model is used to decompose the factors affecting carbon emissions from energy structure, energy efficiency, per capital industrial development, population density, and industrial land.

LMDI factor

The decomposition of driving factors for CO2 emissions has been a prominent research topic, with scholars aiming to analyze the impact of different factors on emission variations quantitatively. The Logarithmic Mean Divisia Index (LMDI) decomposition method is widely recognized as a robust tool for studying energy-related and carbon emission issues in energy economics. Ang (2004)28 compared various index decomposition methods and concluded that LMDI offers theoretical rigor, universality, simplicity, and interpretability, establishing it as the optimal decomposition model. Ang further provided detailed implementation guidelines for LMDI in 200529. Subsequently, Ang and Liu (2007)30,31 proposed solutions for handling zero and negative values in LMDI decomposition, resolving practical challenges, and solidifying its application in energy economics.

To align with the industrial characteristics of industrial parks, the LMDI decomposition framework is adapted by incorporating factors such as economic development, energy consumption, and population dynamics. Results and analysis

Overall Temporal evolution characteristics

From 2014 to 2023, carbon emissions from industrial parks in the Yangtze River Delta exhibited a fluctuating growth trajectory (Fig. 2), rising from 113.79 million tons to 136.19 million tons, with an average annual growth rate of 2.1%. This rate was notably lower than the national industrial emission growth during the same period, reflecting preliminary progress in the region’s low-carbon transition. However, the evolution displayed marked temporal heterogeneity: emissions surged between 2014 and 2016 (3.8% annual growth) due to industrial expansion and energy-intensive sector dominance, followed by a sharp deceleration to 1.5% annually from 2017 to 2019 under energy efficiency policies and industrial upgrading. The 2020 global pandemic triggered a 2.81% decline, yet emissions rebounded by 6.83% in 2021 alongside economic recovery. Notably, a 1.25% reduction emerged in 2023, directly linked to the region’s accelerated carbon neutrality policy implementation. This trajectory underscores the dual influence of economic cycles and policy interventions, revealing transitional achievements and persistent challenges in reconciling industrial growth with decarbonization goals.

Economic development levels of industrial park

Based on the EWM-TOPSIS method, a comprehensive evaluation of the economic development of the Yangtze River Delta industrial parks from 2014 to 2023 was conducted (Fig. 3). The results showed that the economic development levels of industrial parks in the Yangtze River Delta increased from 0.212 to 0.767, indicating a sustained economic development momentum. The period from 2017 to 2023 was a period of accelerated economic growth. In 2021, the economic development index jumped from 0.39 to 0.54, thanks to the increase in the high-tech output proportion and the service industry’s recovery. By 2023, the region achieved the highest recorded index value due to the explosive growth of strategic emerging industries, which marked a new stage of economic development.

Analysis of decoupling between carbon emissions and economic development

Based on the Tapio decoupling model and economic development index data, we explore the decoupling status between carbon emissions (C) and economic development levels (E). From 2014 to 2023, there were several states of decoupling between carbon emissions and economic development levels of industrial parks in the Yangtze River Delta region: weak decoupling, expansion negative decoupling, decoupling state, weak negative decoupling, strong decoupling (Table 4; Fig. 4). From 2014 to 2018, the decoupling status was mainly weak decoupling, with carbon emissions and overall economic development showing an upward trend. The growth rate of carbon emissions was lower than that of the economy; the economy and environment developed harmoniously. This was closely linked to vigorously implementing energy conservation policies and upgrading the region’s industrial structure. In 2019, there was an expansion connection where carbon emissions were consistent with the speed of economic development. This may have been caused by economic structural adjustments or external factors, reflecting structural contradictions in economic transformation. The weak negative decoupling achieved in 2020 indicated that the decrease in carbon emissions was slower than the rate of economic development, which may be related to production constraints caused by global events such as epidemics. From 2021 to 2022, carbon emissions and economic development returned to a weak decoupling state. In 2023, carbon emissions decreased and showed a strong decoupling state. Industrial parks actively controlled carbon emissions while restoring economic growth, indicating that the low-carbon concept had been implemented in developing industrial parks.

Analysis of carbon emission driving factors

To explore the driving mechanism of carbon emissions of industrial parks in the Yangtze River Delta region. This study uses the LMDI method to decompose carbon emission data from 2014 to 2023. It identifies the impact effects of five major factors: energy structure (CE), energy efficiency (EY), per capita industrial development level (YP), population density effect (PL), and industrial land scale effect (L).

From the perspective of cumulative contributions (Fig. 5), YP and L were the dominant factors driving carbon emissions, accounting for over 70% of the total, highlighting the region’s “land for development” model. Although EY effectively suppressed emissions, its absolute contribution remained lower than the positive driving factors, indicating that emission reduction pressure has not been fundamentally alleviated. CE exhibited short-term volatility, suggesting that policy interventions must be more coherent to avoid disconnecting between short-term goals and long-term strategies.

The contribution amounts and rates of different factors varied (Fig. 6). The contribution of CE to carbon emissions showed a pattern of initial positivity, followed by negativity and fluctuations. From 2014 to 2016, all contributions were positive, indicating that the high proportion of fossil fuels remained the primary driver of carbon emissions growth. After 2017, the contribution declined significantly and repeatedly turned negative, reflecting the gradual optimization of the regional energy structure, manifested by increased renewable energy adoption and reduced coal consumption. However, the sudden increase in contribution in 2021 might be linked to short-term economic rebound and surging energy demand.

EY consistently demonstrated an adverse inhibitory effect, confirming that energy efficiency improvements are the core driver of emission reduction. Except for 2016, the average negative values from 2014 to 2021 confirmed the long-term benefits of technological upgrades on energy intensity. However, the positive contributions in 2022 and 2023 suggested that the marginal benefits of energy efficiency may weaken, warning against potential offsets from technological bottlenecks or capacity expansion.

YP had the highest cumulative contribution, emphasizing industrialization’s significant role in driving emissions. In 2017, 2019, and 2021, its contribution exceeded 300 tons, aligning with manufacturing investment expansion cycles in the region. The peak contribution in 2021 corresponded to rapid post-pandemic industrial capacity recovery.

PL showed low contributions with minimal fluctuations, implying that urbanization has a limited direct impact on emissions.

The cumulative contribution of L ranked second only to YP. From 2014 to 2018, its contribution rose steadily, directly tied to industrial expansion. The carbon emission contribution of L was significantly higher from 2014 to 2018 than from 2019 to 2023, indicating that its driving effect has weakened with the promotion of land intensification policies. In 2022, the contribution increased again, reflecting a surge in industrial land demand.

Discussion

Main discussion

To explore the driving factors of carbon emissions in industrial parks, this study takes 44 industrial parks in the Yangtze River Delta region of China as empirical cases. Based on carbon emissions and socio-economic data from 2014 to 2023, the TOPSIS model, Tapio decoupling model, and LMDI factor decomposition method are comprehensively used to explore the temporal evolution characteristics of carbon emissions systematically, the decoupling relationship with economic development, and key driving factors.

In the past decade, the carbon emissions of industrial parks in the Yangtze River Delta have shown an overall “fluctuating growth”, while at the regional level (such as Jiangxi Province32 and Jiangsu Province33, they have shown a sustained growth trend. The commonality between the two lies in that growth is driven by economic activities, while the difference lies in the sustainability of regional development, with industrial parks being strongly regulated by policies. At the same time, the average annual growth rate of carbon emissions in the Yangtze River Delta industrial park (2.1%) is lower than the national industrial carbon emission growth rate, and is basically the same as the average annual growth rate of the Yangtze River Delta region (2.2%)34. This data intuitively reflects that as the core carrier of regional industrial economy, the low-carbon transformation process of the Yangtze River Delta industrial park has been deeply integrated into the regional development rhythm. In terms of horizontal comparison, the transformation effect of the Yangtze River Delta Industrial Park is relatively outstanding, with a growth rate much lower than that of Nanchang High tech Zone (5.99%)35, typical industrial parks in the western region (6.5%)15, and ecological industrial parks leading in the national green, low-carbon and circular demonstration creation36. As Gao37 pointed out, although the Yangtze River Delta industrial park is characterized by a large number and high industrial added value, it exhibits a benign development resilience of “high growth low emissions”. However, it should be acknowledged that compared to advanced cases in the region and future “dual carbon” target requirements, the emission reduction potential of the Yangtze River Delta industrial park has not been fully released, and the overall emission reduction effect of industrial parks across the country7 highlights the necessity of further exploration and breakthroughs as a benchmark region.

By analyzing the decoupling status between carbon emissions and economic development in industrial parks in the Yangtze River Delta region over the past decade, it can be seen that the general trend is transitioning from weak decoupling to strong decoupling. According to the Environmental Kuznets Curve38, environmental pressure usually transitions from coupled growth to weak decoupling with economic development and ultimately evolves into strong decoupling. However, fluctuations in the decoupling state and long-term stable decoupling have not yet been achieved, Xu39 revealed that the decoupling index between economic growth and environmental pressure in typical industrial parks had fluctuated significantly over the past decade, with absolute decoupling remaining unachievable.We found that the evolution of the decoupling state between carbon emissions and economic development in the Yangtze River Delta industrial park is closely related to the phased adjustment of policy orientation. From 2014 to 2018, weak decoupling was the main trend, and the difference between carbon emissions and economic growth was directly related to the policies promoting energy conservation, emission reduction, and industrial upgrading during the same period. During this period, plans such as “Made in China 2025” and “Action Plan for Energy Conservation, Emission Reduction, and Low Carbon Development” were introduced, proposing to increase industrial energy consumption and optimize industrial structure in industrial parks. Under the promotion of multiple policies, the driving effect of economic growth on carbon emissions gradually weakened. The emergence of growth linkages in 2019 reflects structural contradictions in economic transformation, which is related to the short-term pains brought about by environmental inspections that year. The adjustment of some high energy consuming industries led to a temporary convergence of economic and carbon emission growth rates. The weak negative decoupling in 2020 was significantly impacted by the epidemic, and the introduction of the “Construction Plan for the Yangtze River Delta Ecological Green Integration Development Demonstration Zone” laid the institutional foundation for subsequent emission reduction. In 2021–2022, we will return to weak decoupling, thanks to the policy efforts after the implementation of the “dual carbon” target, and the successive promulgation of policies such as the “Action Plan for Carbon Peak before 2030” and the “Special Action Plan for Carbon Peak and Carbon Neutrality in the Industrial Sector”, which will further improve the energy efficiency of the park. The realization of strong decoupling in 2023 is directly related to the implementation of the “National Carbon Peak Pilot Construction Plan”. Policies will deepen from “restricting emissions” to “coordinated growth and emission reduction”, ultimately achieving a virtuous cycle of coordinated economic and environmental development.

The dominant factors affecting carbon emissions in industrial parks in the Yangtze River Delta are the per capita level of industrial development and the scale effect of industrial land use, which are directly related to the expansion of industrial scale. This conclusion not only logically echoes existing research, but also presents uniqueness due to differences in research scale and objects. Compared with national level research, Liu40 believes that per capita income is a contributing factor to China’s carbon emissions, while Jia41points out that economic output and population size are the main driving factors. There are some differences between the research conducted by the two individuals and the results of the author’s research, due to the fact that regional studies cover multiple fields and incorporate policy factors, without separating the impact of industrial land exclusive to the park. Compared with the research on the Yangtze River Delta region, Song et al42,43. believe that energy intensity is the largest carbon emission reduction effect in the Yangtze River Delta region The population, economy, and other factors mentioned by Zhang et al.44 did not involve the scale of industrial land, which is precisely the core difference of parks as “industrial exclusive spaces”. Compared with other parks in China, Chen45 took Zhengzhou Development Zone as an example and found that the dominant effect on decoupling carbon emissions is the economic scale effect. Chen46 pointed out that the industrial added value per unit industrial land area and per capita industrial added value are the main factors affecting the green and low-carbon development of industrial parks, and the level of economic development plays a leading role in the green and low-carbon development of parks. This confirms that per capita industrial development and land use efficiency are common laws driven by carbon emissions in industrial parks. However, due to policy regulation, the synergy and dynamic changes of the Yangtze River Delta Park are more distinctive.

Regarding decomposing carbon emission factors, current research focuses on traditional factors such as energy structure, energy efficiency, and economic development levels. Future consideration can be given to production processes, waste recycling, and other dimensions to construct a more comprehensive index system. In addition, the research period (2014–2023) includes sudden shocks such as epidemics, which may cause short-term fluctuations to interfere with the conclusions and make it challenging to reveal long-term stable patterns.

Policy recommendations

As an industrial park with high industrial agglomeration and prominent economic and environmental contradictions, the low-carbon transformation of the Yangtze River Delta Industrial Park needs to closely follow the core path of “land intensification, industrial upgrading, and policy coordination”, combined with carbon emission driving mechanisms and decoupling evolution characteristics. The following policy recommendations are proposed for industrial parks.

(1) Based on the LMDI decomposition, the scale of industrial land has a higher contribution rate to carbon emissions, reflecting the negative impact of the development model of industrial parks based on land use. Industrial parks need to establish a carbon efficiency oriented land management system, such as setting rigid constraints on carbon emissions per mu, establishing a linkage mechanism between land replacement and carbon reduction, and dynamically monitoring the carbon footprint of park land.

(2) The per capita industrial output has the highest contribution rate to carbon emissions, and it is necessary to optimize the industrial structure from the source, promote the high-end and low-carbon transformation of the industrial structure. By introducing low-carbon and high value-added industries, building a circular economy system in the park, and promoting the utilization of waste resources and the coordinated reduction of carbon emissions in the industrial chain. Fundamentally weaken the dependence of economic growth on high carbon pathways and achieve deep synergy between industrial structure and low-carbon development.

(3) In 2023, carbon emissions and economic development will achieve a strong decoupling for the first time, which reflects the effectiveness of low-carbon policies and technological interventions. However, we need to be vigilant about the potential impact of short-term economic fluctuations on the decoupling status. We need to continue to strengthen structural emission reduction measures, promote the transformation of policy implementation from fragmented to systematic, and strengthen long-term emission reduction mechanisms.

Conclusions

This study uses 44 industrial parks in the Yangtze River Delta region of China as empirical cases to systematically explore the temporal evolution characteristics of carbon emissions in industrial parks, their decoupling relationship with economic development, and key driving factors. The results indicate that from 2014 to 2023, carbon emissions of industrial parks in the Yangtze River Delta region exhibited an overall fluctuating growth trend, with phased variations in growth rates. The average annual growth rate remained lower than the national industrial carbon emissions growth rate during the same period, suggesting partial success in the region’s low-carbon transition, though emission reduction pressures persist. Applying the Tapio decoupling model reveals that the relationship between carbon emissions and economic development in the Yangtze Delta industrial parks was predominantly characterized by weak decoupling during the study period, reflecting relative coordination between economic and environmental systems. By 2023, a strong decoupling was achieved for the first time, marking the initial decoupling stage between economic growth and carbon emissions. LMDI decomposition shows that YP and L were the primary drivers of carbon emissions growth in the Yangtze Delta industrial parks, cumulatively contributing over 70%. This highlights the persistent negative impact of the “land for development” model. Although EY had long suppressed emissions, its marginal benefits weakened over time. The fluctuation of CE reflects the gradual optimization of the regional energy structure. The study demonstrates that combining the Tapio decoupling model and LMDI decomposition provides a universal framework for exploring carbon emission drivers in industrial parks. These findings offer both theoretical and practical insights for industrial park decarbonization strategies.
