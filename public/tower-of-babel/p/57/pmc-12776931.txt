Background

The literature on sedation in Pediatric Dentistry commonly includes study subjects in whom “basic behavior modification tools have been unsuccessful”.1 There are no well-defined inclusion criteria or characteristics qualifying the children for the study. This leads to a clinical situation where the sedation outcomes could be unpredictable, and the conclusions could be biased. We hypothesized that accurately classifying the behavior of children using any known behavior rating scales2,3 could help the clinician rationally choose appropriate cases for nonpharmacological guidance and pharmacological guidance, respectively. This “Clinical Technique” article will primarily discuss decision-making on when to use nonpharmacological guidance and when to use moderate sedation. It will further elaborate on when to use deep sedation and general anesthesia. It is important to understand, under this context, that nonpharmacological management and moderate sedation go hand in hand. In order to understand this concept, we need to first understand moderate sedation. Moderate sedation (old terminology, “conscious sedation” or “sedation/ analgesia”) is a drug-induced depression of consciousness during which patients respond purposefully to verbal commands or after light tactile stimulation. No interventions are required to maintain a patent airway, and spontaneous ventilation is adequate. Cardiovascular function is usually maintained. The caveat that loss of consciousness should be unlikely is a particularly important aspect of the definition of moderate sedation; drugs and techniques used should carry a margin of safety wide enough to render unintended loss of consciousness unlikely.4 This definition clearly implies that in moderate sedation, the drugs that are used will not, on their own, be able to overcome the child's resistance. So, essentially, that means moderate sedation should not be used to overcome resistance in a child displaying combative behavior. It is most effective in anxious children who are willing to cooperate. Since, by definition, the children are conscious, moderate sedation has to be done in conjunction with sound techniques of non-pharmacological behavior guidance.

Now, based on the Frankl classification3 and the intended length of the procedure, a behavior guidance template can be formulated.

Frankl's Definitely Positive Behavior

The communicative behavior guidance techniques, including tell-show-do, enhancing control, positive verbal reinforcement, and euphemisms, work very well with the child.

The clinician, though, should not take the child's behavior for granted. She/he should be aware of the rendering treatment. This is especially for challenging or complex procedures under local anesthesia. An unpleasant episode can negatively change the child's behavior on the Frankl scale. Inhalation sedation should be used without fail for complex procedures or lengthy dental procedures.5

Frankl's Positive Behavior

For children displaying Frankl's positive behavior, inhalation sedation should be used for both short and long procedures right from the first appointment. These children cooperate with reservation and barring any unpleasant experiences, treatment can progress as planned. The clinician, however, must be very alert to the child's reservations and proceed cautiously. Communicative techniques, such as tell-show-do, help the acceptance of the nasal hood. Nasal hood acceptance and the subsequent use of inhalation sedation are positive steps in the treatment of anxious children.5 The first appointment should be a desensitizing appointment, completing a simple procedure. The lengthy procedures involving local anesthetic injections should be gradually accomplished in the subsequent appointments under inhalation sedation.

An important clinical tip here is that the clinician should recognize the anxious behavior and proactively initiate treatment under moderate/conscious sedation. The decision to sedate should not be made after the child has experienced an unpleasant experience. This will lead to unpredictable sedation outcomes. In case inhalation sedation is not available, then oral midazolam at 0.5 mg/kg body weight will also do the job, though it comes with some of the inherent disadvantages of oral sedation.

Frankl's Negative Behavior

Children whose behavior is classified as Frank negative wear the nasal hood. But the continuous cry or sudden movements preclude nasal hood stability. They also do not follow instructions to breathe through the nose, thus negating the effect of inhalation sedation.

For long procedures, in children displaying Frankl's negative behavior, oral midazolam at 0.5-0.75 mg/kg body weight prior to inhalation sedation is a good way forward.7 Midazolam, through its anxiolytic properties, will calm the child and facilitate communication. The anxiolysis will further encourage the child to breathe through the nose and aid the nasal hood stability.

For short procedures, oral midazolam alone can be used at 0.5-0.75 mg/kg body weight. 0.75 mg/kg body weight is an effective oral midazolam dosage in uncooperative children.7 Clinically, crying may still be seen. But oral midazolam induces anterograde amnesia.8 The sedative effect also lessens the resistance of the child, enabling administration of the local anesthetic injection.

Frankl's Definitely Negative Behavior

The distinctive characteristic of identifying Frankl's definitely negative behavior is that the children will actively resist diagnostic evaluation and the subsequent attempt at treatment. They will not accept the nasal hood.

The background to Frankl's definitely negative behavior has to be explored first through a parent and child interview. Depending on the background of the behavior, based on, whether it is due to subjective or objective fear, the management can vary.

In the case of a definite history of subjective fear, nonpharmacological behavior guidance should be used to change the behavior rating to positive or negative. The clinician should then proceed with the strategies described for potentially cooperative Frankl positive and negative children.

Voice control is an effective nonpharmacological strategy in this group of children, because their behavior stems from subjective and not objective fear. Voice control aims to primarily gain the patient's attention9 and establish two-way communication. Parents may find voice control objectionable,10 and their consent should be obtained before its use.

In case of a history of true objective fear from a previous traumatic dental or medical episode, nonpharmacological methods will not be effective. The intensity of fear or the severity of the debilitating condition in this category of children will preclude any communication of basic behavior guidance techniques. Discussion

Sedation is a continuum of minimal, moderate, deep sedation to general anesthesia. The clinician should be very clear about the depth of sedation required for the planned dental procedure. In this context, there are no guidelines to help the clinician decide on the depth and route of sedation. Classifying child behavior is the first step leading to effective behavior management strategies.2 The Frankl's behavior rating scale, introduced in 1962 by Frankl et al.,3 is often considered the standard among the behavior scales. The simplicity of the Frankl scale is its strength. It further allows the clinician to numerically quantify the behavior between 1 and 4. This article discusses a template based on Frankl's behavior rating and the length of the procedure to aid the clinician in using the proper depth and route, thereby making it predictably successful.

Rating 4: Definitely Positive Behavior

The behavior of this child is ideal. The child responds positively to two-way communication. This child enjoys the dental treatment experience. But complex, long, and challenging procedures would still require inhalation sedation.

Rating 3: Positive Behavior

This potentially cooperative child responds cautiously to the clinician. The child is anxious, or as described by Lampshire in 1970, is “tense cooperative.”2 The child's eyes will follow the clinician and the assistants.

“Timid” children can also display Frankl's positive behavior. This behavior was explained by Wright.2 Clinically, this behavior is manifested by shy or introverted children. The background to the behavior is usually an overprotective parenting style with little external exposure. Commonly, a 3-year-old child who is immediately past the “precooperative”11 stage depicts this behavior. Two-way communication may require repeated attempts. The child will respond to the dentist, but with a reservation.

The dentist has to be alert to the above two scenarios because an unpleasant episode may result in sudden panic and crying. The behavior rating could fast downgrade to Frankl's definitely negative behavior.

So, to give the child a positive dental experience, for both short and long procedures, inhalation sedation or oral midazolam should be used. Now, a valid question is, since Frankl positive children are not uncooperative, why shouldn't we manage them only with nonpharmacological behavior guidance? Well, these children may not be uncooperative, but they are tense and cooperative, and clubbing nonpharmacological behavior guidance with sedation will help the children off to an ideal start.

Rating 2: Negative Behavior

This is the behavior category most difficult to identify and treat. These are children who passively resist treatment. They do not actively resist treatment. They accept the treatment but keep crying and whining. The treatment happens, but not of the highest quality. There is no communication established, and hence, treatment instructions are not followed. Consequently, the nasal hood is not stable in these children. So, for short procedures, instead of inhalation sedation, oral midazolam can be used at 0.5-0.75 mg/kg body weight, depending on the presenting anxiety.

For long procedures, because they don't sportingly accept the nasal hood, it is best to initially dose up the children with oral midazolam 0.5 mg/kg and then follow it up with inhalation sedation. This combination has been shown to be more effective compared to oral midazolam alone.6

Rating 1: Definitely Negative Behavior

The distinctive characteristic of identifying Frankl's definitely negative behavior is that the children will actively resist diagnostic evaluation and the subsequent attempt at treatment.12 But the background to the presenting behavior could be varied.

In one presentation, they could potentially cooperate with children displaying typical features of frightened behavior, including loud crying/screaming and refusing to enter the operatory.13 They will actively resist diagnostic evaluation. They have to be differentiated from Frankl negative children, who may cry but do not actively resist examination. There is no two-way communication with the child not responding to the operator's questions. There is no history of objective fear. The background usually reveals a subjective fear stemming from parent/peer/sibling/media experiences. The child could also be overwhelmed by the new dental environment owing to an overprotective home environment. The fear could also stem from an unpleasant, but not traumatic, previous dental episode, such as a gag reflex to the airotor.

Voice control could be an effective strategy for these children because the underlying fear does not stem from true fear. It can be used to establish communication and change the behavior rating of the child to Frankl negative and then manage as described above.

In another presentation of Frankl's definitely negative behavior, potentially cooperative children display uncontrollable or hysterical behavior. The behavior is typically combative. History could reveal a severe traumatic dental or medical episode. The clinician has to recognize this real fear and avoid aversive conditioning techniques such as voice control. Deep sedation or general anesthesia, along with an anesthesiologist, is the best way forward for children to display this behavior.

The limitation of this article is that the behavior guidance techniques cannot be generalized in every clinical situation. Behavior guidance decisions suggested in this article, based on Frankl's behavior rating, are not intended to trivialize child management in dentistry. These suggestions can be useful guides and should not be considered blanket solutions. Limited clinical presentations of child behavior were explained under each Frankl category. There may also be an overlap between Frankl categories. The intelligent clinician must understand this fact and suitably use the templates based on the clinical scenario.12

Also, accurately classifying the child's behavior under the Frankl behavior rating involves a learning curve for the clinician, and interpretations of Frankl categories could be subjective. The clinicians also should not indulge in sedative combinations till they gain sufficient experience with individual sedative drugs. Finally, these clinical techniques need to be validated by original studies to test their consistent practicality.

Conclusion

Accordingly, in conclusion, the decision to use nonpharmacological, pharmacological (the mode and depth), or a combination of techniques should be based on scientific evidence. Also, for moderate sedation to be successful, a strong foundation of nonpharmacological behavior management is imperative and indispensable. The article gives a practical template to help clinical decision-making for behavior guidance.
