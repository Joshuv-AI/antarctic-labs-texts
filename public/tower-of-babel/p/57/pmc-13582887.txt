Temporal control of Ninj1 activation determines cell-to-cell heterogeneity in IL-33 release
Authors: Takumi Kanokogi, Sachiko Komazawa-Sakon, Shin Murai, Kenta Moriwaki, Yoshitaka Shirasaki, Mai Yamagishi, Kenta Sumiyama, Kazuma Kishi, Hiroyasu Nakano
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Interleukin-33 (IL-33), an IL-1 family cytokine, is released during cell damage. However, the mechanism underlying IL-33 release is not fully understood. Here, we generate IL-33–mCherry-expressing mouse cell lines to monitor IL-33 release at single cell resolution. During necroptosis, SYTOX uptake that represents loss of membrane integrity, and IL-33–mCherry release occur simultaneously, indicating that Mlkl-mediated damage causes immediate membrane rupture. In contrast, some apoptotic and pyroptotic cells exhibit delayed IL-33–mCherry release relative to SYTOX uptake, whereas in other cells, both events occur simultaneously. IL-33–mCherry release is only modestly affected by loss of Gsdmd or Gsdme but is markedly reduced in Ninjurin-1 (Ninj1) knockout cells. These findings indicate that Gasdermins act upstream, whereas Ninj1-mediated membrane rupture represents the principal mechanism of IL-33 release. The delay in IL-33–mCherry release in some cells implies that the activation of Ninj1 occurs with variable timing, thus contributing to cell-to-cell heterogeneity.

Introduction

Interleukin-33 (IL-33) is a member of the IL-1 cytokine family; it resides in the nucleus without apparent function under resting conditions1,2. However, upon tissue injury, IL-33 is released from damaged cells and binds to its cognate receptor, ST2, thereby eliciting a variety of biological functions, including the promotion of T helper (TH)2-type immune responses and tumor development1,2. IL-33 contains an N-terminal nuclear localization domain, an intermediate domain with protease cleavage sites, and a C-terminal IL-1 domain. Owing to its nuclear localization and lack of a signal peptide, IL-33 is thought to be released primarily during necrosis, which are characterized by membrane rupture. Two studies revealed that IL-33 is released from pores by Gasdermin D (GSDMD) or pore-forming proteins derived from fungi, indicating that pore formation is indispensable for subsequent IL-33 release3,4. Additionally, several studies have reported that IL-33 is released from live cells under certain conditions. For example, mechanical stresses induce the release of IL-33 from living fibroblasts5. Additionally, a recent study revealed that papain, a cysteine protease that elicits a strong type II allergic reaction, triggers IL-33 release via the noncanonical cleavage of a pore-forming protein, GSDMD, without inducing cell death6. With respect to the regulation of its biological activity, the cleavage of IL-33 between its N-terminal and intermediate domains by various proteases can occur either intracellularly or extracellularly and significantly increase its biological activity. In contrast, activated Caspase-1, -3, or -7 cleaves IL-33 within the C-terminal IL-1 domain, leading to the inactivation of the biological functions of IL-337,8.

Necrosis has long been regarded as an accidental form of cell death characterized by early membrane rupture. However, recent studies have revealed that regulated cell death, including necroptosis, pyroptosis, and ferroptosis, involves membrane rupture9,10. Necroptosis is defined as RIPK3- and MLKL-dependent cell death triggered by death ligands, viral infection, LPS, and poly(I:C)11–13. Upon stimulation, RIPK3 is activated through oligomerization with RIPK1,TRIF, or ZBP1 and then phosphorylates MLKL. Phosphorylated MLKL undergoes conformational changes and translocates to the plasma membrane, resulting in membrane rupture. During pyroptosis, members of the GSDM family, which are activated by Caspases, form small membrane pores that eventually lead to Ninjurin-1 (Ninj1)-mediated membrane rupture14,15. Specifically, GSDMD is cleaved and activated by Caspase-1 upon inflammasome activation or by Caspase-4, -5 and -11 in response to lipopolysaccharide (LPS), thus enabling the release of mature interleukin-1β (IL-1β) and IL-1816,17. Additionally, Caspase-3–mediated cleavage of GSDME promotes secondary necrosis under certain conditions18,19.

The live-cell imaging of secretion activity (LCI-S) system enables the visualization of cytokine release at single-cell resolution20,21. LCI-S is a modified version of the enzyme-linked immunosorbent assay (ELISA) that provides information on cytokines released through antibodies precoated on dishes, followed by detection with fluorescent dye-conjugated antibodies. The fluorescent signals are visualized using a total internal reflection fluorescence (TIRF) microscopy-based fluorescence immunoassay. Previously, we used the LCI-S system to demonstrate that IL-1β is exclusively released from pyroptotic but not live macrophages22. In addition, we reported that Hmgb1 release from necroptotic cells occurs in two modes: burst and sustained23, further supporting the utility of the LCI-S system as a powerful tool for analyzing the release of danger-associated molecular patterns (DAMPs) beyond Hmgb124. Using LCI-S analysis, we demonstrated that IL-33 release during apoptosis and pyroptosis is governed primarily by Ninj1-dependent membrane rupture rather than direct passage through Gasdermin pores, whereas necroptotic release is mediated by Mlkl-dependent membrane disruption, exhibiting distinct kinetics across cell types and death modalities.

Results

IL-33 is released from MEFs following apoptotic or necroptotic stimulation

We analyzed the release of IL-33 at single-cell resolution using the LCI-S system to overcome the limitations of a bulk analysis of IL-33 release using Western blotting or ELISA20,21. Since the expression of IL-33 was very low in many cells, on the basis of our findings, we stably expressed IL-33 fused to mCherry at the C-terminus in immortalized murine embryonic fibroblasts (iMEFs) (Fig. 1a, b). For simplicity, we refer to IL-33–mCherry-expressing iMEFs as IL-33–mCherry iMEFs hereafter. To induce cell death, IL-33–mCherry iMEFs were treated with TNF and BV6, an IAP inhibitor. Apoptosis was induced by TNF in the presence of the RIPK3 inhibitor GSK’872, whereas necroptosis was induced by TNF in the presence of the pan-caspase inhibitor zVAD. We found that IL-33–mCherry iMEFs efficiently died under apoptosis-inducing or necroptosis-inducing conditions (Fig. 1c). A previous study revealed that Caspase-3 cleaves mouse IL-33 at Asp175 (Fig. 1a), thereby suppressing its biological activity8. In sharp contrast, the released IL-33–mCherry was not cleaved in iMEFs undergoing apoptosis (Fig. 1a, d), suggesting that, under these conditions, apoptotic cells may release biologically active IL-33. Taken together, these results indicate that IL-33 is released from iMEFs during apoptosis and necroptosis.

IL-33–mCherry release in apoptotic cells is driven primarily by Ninj1-dependent membrane rupture

While Gsdme activation is involved in membrane rupture in certain cells in response to apoptotic stimuli18,19, findings from other groups have been inconsistent25,26. We generated control, Gsdme-knockout (KO), and Ninj1-KO IL-33–mCherry iMEFs to determine whether Gsdme and Ninj1 are required for the release of IL-33 from apoptotic cells. LDH release induced by TNF/BV6/GSK'872 was partially attenuated in Gsdme-KO IL-33–mCherry iMEFs but strongly suppressed in Ninj1-KO IL-33–mCherry iMEFs (Fig. 2a). Gsdme was expressed and cleaved to produce an active fragment in control and Ninj1-KO IL-33–mCherry iMEFs upon apoptotic stimulation (Fig. 2b, c). To exclude the possibility that a C-terminal mCherry tag interferes with IL-33 release through Gsdme pores in apoptotic cells, we generated an expression vector encoding IL-33 linked to mCherry via a P2A peptide27, allowing independent expression of untagged IL-33 and mCherry (Fig. 2d). We subsequently generated control, Gsdme-KO, and Ninj1-KO iMEFs expressing IL-33–P2A–mCherry (Fig. 2e). Under these conditions, LDH release was modestly reduced in Gsdme-KO iMEFs but markedly attenuated in Ninj1-KO iMEFs (Fig. 2f). Consistent with the results obtained using IL-33–mCherry, the release of IL-33 and Hmgb1 was strongly suppressed in Ninj1-KO IL-33–P2A–mCherry iMEFs, whereas Gsdme deletion had only a minor effect (Fig. 2g). These findings were further confirmed by ELISA (Fig. 2h). Notably, the apparent molecular weights of released IL-33 and Hmgb1 were approximately 8 kDa greater than their predicted sizes. These results suggest that posttranslational modifications, such as acetylation, SUMOylation, or monoubiquitylation, were preferentially enriched in released IL-33 and Hmgb128. The functional significance of these posttranslational modifications was not further investigated, as they are beyond the scope of the present study.

Together, these results indicate that Ninj1 plays a central role in IL-33 release regardless of the presence or absence of a C-terminal mCherry tag and suggest that Gsdme-independent and Gsdme-dependent pathways may cooperate to activate Ninj1, ultimately leading to membrane rupture.

Distinct membrane disruption dynamics of IL-33–mCherry release from apoptotic and necroptotic cells

We next analyzed the release of IL-33–mCherry from iMEFs using the LCI-S system. A precoated anti-mCherry antibody captured the IL-33–mCherry released from the cells, which was visualized by TIRF microscopy. We determined the timing of the entry of SYTOX Blue, a membrane-impermeable dye that passes through small pores possibly created by the GSDM family29, by stimulating cells in the presence of SYTOX and analyzed the release of IL-33–mCherry by TIRF microscopy. In necroptotic iMEFs, IL-33–mCherry was released extracellularly at the exact time that SYTOX signals appeared, indicating that Mlkl-dependent membrane rupture occurred without the formation of small membrane pores (Fig. 3a, b; Supplementary Movie 1). In one-third of apoptotic cells, SYTOX entry and IL-33–mCherry release occurred simultaneously (Supplementary Movie 2), whereas in the remaining two-thirds of apoptotic cells, the release of IL-33–mCherry was substantially delayed following SYTOX entry (Supplementary Movie 3). On the basis of the observation that necroptotic cells showed IL-33–mCherry release within 2 time frames (4 min) after SYTOX entry, we tentatively classified apoptotic cells into two types: the simultaneous type (S type), in which IL-33–mCherry release occurred within 4 min (Fig. 3c, d), and the delayed type (D type), in which release occurred more than 4 min after SYTOX entry (Fig. 3e, f; Table 1). To further verify the contribution of Ninj1 to IL-33–mCherry release, we analyzed IL-33–mCherry dynamics by LCI-S in control, Gsdme-KO, and Ninj1-KO iMEFs. Compared with that in control or Gsdme-KO iMEFs, IL-33–mCherry release in Ninj1-KO iMEFs was substantially delayed relative to SYTOX uptake (Fig. 3h–k), which is consistent with the results obtained by Western blot analyses (Fig. 2b, c, g). In addition, the duration of IL-33–mCherry release was significantly longer in Ninj1-KO iMEFs than in control or Gsdme-KO iMEFs (Fig. 3l).

In conclusion, membrane disruption in apoptotic cells appears to proceed through two sequential steps. The initial step involves the formation of small membrane pores that permit SYTOX entry, potentially mediated by Gsdme and other factors, whereas the subsequent step is Ninj1-dependent membrane rupture that enables the release of IL-33–mCherry. The time interval between these two steps varied among cells. In contrast, necroptotic cells underwent Mlkl-mediated membrane rupture in a single, rapid step that coincided with the onset of SYTOX permeability.

Papain does not induce Gsdmd cleavage or IL-33–mCherry release in viable MLE-12 cells

IL-33 is also released from damaged lung epithelial cells in response to allergens and plays a crucial role in the development of allergic reactions by activating type II innate lymphoid cells1,2,30. A recent study revealed that papain, an allergen-derived cysteine protease, can release IL-33 via noncanonical cleavage of Gsdmd without inducing pyroptosis in MLE-12 lung epithelial cells6. We analyzed IL-33 release from MLE-12 cells stably expressing IL-33–mCherry (IL-33–mCherry MLE-12 cells) and stimulated them with papain (Supplementary Fig. 2a). We stimulated IL-33–mCherry MLE-12 cells with two different concentrations of papain (1 or 10 μg/mL) for the indicated times and examined the levels of cleaved Gsdmd in the lysates and IL-33–mCherry release in the supernatants by Western blotting. Treatment with either concentration of papain only marginally released LDH from IL-33–mCherry MLE-12 cells (Supplementary Fig. 2b), suggesting that papain did not induce membrane rupture under the conditions. Notably, low-dose papain did not induce Gsdmd cleavage or IL-33–mCherry release (Supplementary Fig. 2c). Unexpectedly, IL-33–mCherry and Gsdmd were rapidly degraded in IL-33–mCherry MLE-12 cells following high-dose papain exposure (Supplementary Fig. 2c), which may be attributed to direct proteolytic cleavage of the substrate proteins by papain in the cell lysates rather than in live cells. Therefore, papain is unlikely to induce IL-33 release from live cells, at least under our experimental conditions.

IL-33–mCherry is released from MLE-12 cells following apoptotic stimulation

The inability of papain to induce IL-33–mCherry release led us to investigate whether similar to MEFs, MLE-12 cells release IL-33–mCherry in a manner associated with cell death. LDH release was lower in TNF/BV6-treated MLE-12 cells than in TNF/CHX-treated cells (Supplementary Fig. 3a). Therefore, we used TNF/CHX to induce apoptosis in subsequent experiments. Notably, zVAD completely blocked TNF/CHX-induced MLE-12 cell death (Supplementary Fig. 3b), suggesting that MLE-12 cells did not die by necroptosis. TNF/CHX led to the generation of cleaved fragments of Caspase 3 and Gsdme (Supplementary Fig. 3c). We detected IL-33–mCherry in the culture supernatants of IL-33–mCherry MLE-12 cells treated with TNF/CHX via Western blotting and ELISA (Supplementary Fig. 3c, d).

We generated control, Gsdme-KO, and Ninj1-KO IL-33–mCherry MLE-12 cells to further determine the contributions of Gsdme and Ninj1 to the release of IL-33–mCherry from cells in response to apoptotic stimuli. LDH release assays revealed that TNF/CHX-induced LDH release was partially and strongly attenuated in Gsdme-KO and Ninj1-KO IL-33–mCherry MLE-12 cells, respectively (Supplementary Fig. 3e). In line with these findings, the release of IL-33–mCherry and Hmgb1 from apoptotic cells was substantially reduced by Ninj1 KO but not by Gsdme KO (Supplementary Fig. 3f, g).

Heterogeneous kinetics of IL-33–mCherry release following membrane pore formation from apoptotic MLE-12 cells

We investigated the kinetics of SYTOX entry and IL-33–mCherry release from IL-33–mCherry MLE-12 cells using the LCI-S system. Consistent with observations in IL-33–mCherry iMEFs, the time interval between SYTOX uptake and IL-33–mCherry release varied among individual cells. Since MLE-12 cells do not undergo necroptosis, we applied the same classification criteria used for iMEFs to distinguish the two types of IL-33–mCherry MLE-12 cells. In a small subset of cells, IL-33–mCherry was released extracellularly at approximately the same time (within 4 min) as SYTOX positivity appeared following TNF/CHX stimulation (Supplementary Fig. 4a, b; Supplementary Movie 4). In sharp contrast, the majority of cells (93.0%) exhibited a substantial delay (more than 4 min) between SYTOX uptake and IL-33–mCherry release (Supplementary Fig. 4c, d; Supplementary Movie 5; Table 1). The duration of IL-33–mCherry release was significantly longer in D-type apoptotic cells than in S-type apoptotic cells, as observed in iMEFs (Supplementary Fig. 4e).

IL-33–mCherry release occurs in iBMDMs following necroptotic, pyroptotic, and apoptotic stimulation

We immortalized bone marrow-derived macrophages (iBMDMs) from wild-type mice and transfected them with IL-33–mCherry to compare IL-33–mCherry release during apoptosis, necroptosis, and pyroptosis within the same cell type (Fig. 4a). Necroptosis was induced by BV6/zVAD, pyroptosis by LPS/nigericin, and apoptosis by venetoclax, a Bcl-2 inhibitor31. (Fig. 4b–e). Western blot analysis confirmed the activation of hallmark molecules for each cell death pathway: phosphorylated Ripk3 and Mlkl for necroptosis; cleaved Caspase-1, cleaved Gsdmd, and cleaved IL-1β for pyroptosis; and cleaved Caspase-3 and Gsdme for apoptosis. To further assess the contributions of Gsdmd and Ninj1 to IL-33–mCherry release, we analyzed IL-33–mCherry dynamics using LCI-S in control, Gsdmd-KO, and Ninj1-KO iBMDMs. Delayed release of IL-33–mCherry relative to SYTOX uptake was observed in a substantial proportion of pyroptotic Ninj1-KO iBMDMs, whereas such delayed release events were observed much less frequently in control and Gsdmd-KO iBMDMs (Fig. 6l–n). Notably, Ninj1-KO cells treated with apoptosis-inducing agents—including iMEFs, MLE-12 cells, and iBMDMs—exhibited canonical apoptotic features, such as phosphatidylserine (PS) exposure and membrane blebbing, followed by cytoplasmic ballooning and subsequent plasma membrane rupture (Supplementary Fig. 6a, c, e). These observations indicate that plasma membrane rupture occurs as a secondary necrotic event following classical apoptosis. We observed a significant delay between Annexin V and SYTOX positivity in Ninj1-KO cells compared to control cells, particularly in iMEFs and MLE-12 cells (Supplementary Fig. 6b, d, f). These findings suggest that NINJ1 may, at least in part, contribute to SYTOX entry. While further investigation of the underlying mechanisms would be of interest, such analyses are beyond the scope of the present study.

Discussion

Using LCI-S, we demonstrate that IL-33 release during apoptosis and pyroptosis is primarily governed by Ninj1-dependent membrane rupture rather than direct passage through gasdermin pores, whereas necroptotic release is mediated by Mlkl-dependent membrane rupture. Across multiple cell types, IL-33 release consistently occurred at or after the loss of plasma membrane integrity. In necroptotic cells, IL-33 release coincided with SYTOX entry, whereas apoptotic and pyroptotic stimuli frequently produced a measurable delay between initial membrane permeabilization and IL-33 release. These findings indicate that small pore formation is insufficient for efficient IL-33 release and that Ninj1-dependent rupture represents a critical terminal step.

In contrast to a previous report8, IL-33–mCherry was not cleaved during apoptosis in iMEFs, despite robust Caspase-3 activation and efficient cleavage of its canonical substrate Gsdme. Cleavage of IL-33–mCherry was readily detected in apoptotic MLE-12 cells and in iBMDMs under both apoptotic and pyroptotic conditions, indicating that Caspase-dependent IL-33 cleavage is cell type– and stimulus-dependent rather than universal. The absence of IL-33 cleavage in iMEFs treated with TNF/BV6/GSK’872 was therefore not attributable to insufficient Caspase-3 activation. Moreover, untagged IL-33 expressed via the P2A system also remained uncleaved in apoptotic iMEFs, excluding interference by the fluorescent tag.

It is conceivable that the fusion of mCherry (~27 kDa) to the C-terminus of IL-33 (~30 kDa) may interfere with its passage through Gasdermin pores, potentially allowing the release of smaller endogenous IL-33 but not IL-33–mCherry. To address this concern, we expressed untagged IL-33 using the P2A system27, enabling direct assessment of IL-33 release independent of fluorescence tagging. Using this system, we confirmed that the overall behavior of untagged IL-33 release closely mirrored that observed with IL-33–mCherry, validating the use of the tagged construct. Moreover, LCI-S analyses revealed that the release kinetics of IL-33–mCherry were unchanged in Gsdme-KO iMEFs, whereas Ninj1 KO markedly delayed the release of IL-33–mCherry after apoptosis induction. Similarly, in iBMDMs treated with venetoclax, untagged IL-33 release was almost completely blocked in Ninj1-KO cells and partially suppressed in Gsdme-KO cells, indicating that the contribution of Gasdermins to IL-33 release is cell type dependent, whereas Ninj1-mediated membrane rupture represents a shared and dominant mechanism. Because Gasdermin pore formation can occur upstream of Ninj1-dependent rupture14, reduced IL-33 release in Gasdermin-KO cells does not, by itself, demonstrate direct passage through Gasdermin pores. Instead, our data indicate that Ninj1-mediated membrane rupture is indispensable for efficient IL-33 release in the tested systems, with Gasdermins contributing in a context-dependent and supportive manner.

Although the C-terminal tag did not appear to interfere with Caspase-3-mediated cleavage of IL-33, we cannot entirely rule out the possibility that it may influence IL-33 function or its release. Notably, we observed that released untagged IL-33 displayed a slight increase in molecular weight, indicative of potential post-translational modification—a shift not seen in IL-33-mCherry. This raises the possibility that the C-terminal tag may affect IL-33 processing, modification status, or release kinetics. Consequently, while our findings provide valuable insights, they should be interpreted with caution. Further studies, particularly those examining endogenous IL-33 in the context of regulated cell death, will be necessary to address these issues rigorously.

Previous studies have reported that IL-33 is released from even living cells in response to various stimuli, including mechanical stress and the allergy-inducing protease papain5,6. Under our experimental conditions, Western blot analyses revealed that papain treatment did not either induce cell death or the extracellular release of IL-33–mCherry from MLE-12 cells. Whether discrepancies reflect differences in experimental systems remains unclear. IL-1β and IL-33 have also been proposed to undergo unconventional secretion via a TMED10-dependent pathway involving the ER–Golgi intermediate compartment33,34. Future studies using LCI-S may clarify the relative contribution of this pathway to IL-33 release.

In iMEFs and iBMDMs undergoing necroptosis, IL-33–mCherry release occurred without a detectable time lag relative to SYTOX entry, indicating that Mlkl-dependent membrane rupture can proceed without prior formation of small SYTOX-permeable pores. In contrast, apoptotic and pyroptotic stimuli frequently induced a pronounced delay between SYTOX uptake and IL-33–mCherry release. This delay suggests that small pore formation mediated by GSDM family–dependent or—independent mechanisms precedes the Ninj1-dependent membrane rupture required for IL-33 release. Notably, the duration of this delay varied substantially among individual cells and cell types, particularly under apoptotic conditions, whereas pyroptotic stimulation with LPS plus nigericin induced more uniform kinetics. These observations indicate that the timing of Ninj1-dependent membrane rupture is more heterogeneous during apoptosis than during canonical pyroptosis and is influenced by cell type-specific factors.

Finally, the delayed release pattern observed for IL-33 was not recapitulated for Hmgb1. Given the strong chromatin-binding affinity of IL-33 and its limited nuclear mobility30, differences in chromatin association may underlie the prolonged interval between membrane permeabilization and extracellular release. In contrast, the more dynamic chromatin interaction of Hmgb1 may permit rapid diffusion once membrane integrity is lost35, suggesting that chromatin-binding properties critically shape the temporal dynamics of DAMP release.

Stable expression of untagged IL-33 in iBMDMs, combined with CRISPR/Cas9-mediated genome editing and clonal selection, resulted in inefficient induction of canonical pyroptosis and preferential activation of the Caspase-3–Gsdme pathway18,19. As a result, we could not directly evaluate Gsdmd-dependent IL-33 release during canonical pyroptosis in this system. Although primary BMDMs from Ninj1-deficient mice constitute an ideal model, endogenous IL-33 expression in BMDMs is extremely low, necessitating ectopic expression for experimental analysis2. We also attempted to generate IL-33–mCherry-transgenic mice; but, these attempts have thus far been unsuccessful, possibly due to toxicity associated with sustained IL-33 expression.

Mice

C57BL/6J mice (Sankyo Lab Service) were housed in a specific pathogen-free facility and received a routine chow diet and water ad libitum. Eight-week-old C57BL/6J male and female mice were used for timed mating to obtain embryos at embryonic day 14.5 (E14.5) for the preparation of MEFs. Pregnant mice were anesthetized with isoflurane and euthanized by cervical dislocation prior to embryo collection. All animal experiments were performed in accordance with the guidelines approved by the Institutional Animal Experiments Committee of the Faculty of Medicine, Toho University (approval number: 23-530). We have complied with all relevant ethical regulations for animal use.

Cell lines, cell culture, and transfection

MLE-12 cells were obtained from the ATCC (CRL-2110) and maintained in Dulbecco’s modified Eagle’s medium/nutrient mixture F-12 (DMEM/F-12) supplemented with 10% fetal bovine serum (FBS). MEFs were prepared from 8-week-old female C57BL/6 mice at E13.5–E14.5 after coitus using a standard method36. After the yolk sac and placenta were removed from the embryo, the embryo was decapitated, and the limbs, tail, and internal organs were removed. The remaining body was minced with scissors and then incubated with trypsin/EDTA (0.25%)-PBS at 37 °C for 30 min. After neutralization of trypsin with DMEM containing 10% FBS, the cell suspensions were passed through a nylon mesh. The resulting cell suspensions were plated on a 10 cm dish and expanded until the cells became confluent. MEFs were immortalized by transfection with a pEF321-T vector encoding SV40 large T antigen (provided by Sumio Sugano) and are hereafter referred to as iMEFs37. iMEFs were maintained in DMEM supplemented with 10% FBS.

Immortalized wild-type BMDMs (iBMDMs) were obtained from Gabriel Nunez and generated as follows. Bone marrow cells were isolated from the femurs of 8-week-old C57BL/6 mice. Briefly, bones were harvested, cleaned, and flushed with sterile PBS to extract the bone marrow. The cell suspension was filtered through a 70-μm strainer, centrifuged at 300 × g for 5 min, and treated with RBC lysis buffer. After being washed, the cells were resuspended in DMEM supplemented with 10% FBS and 10 ng/mL recombinant mouse M-CSF and seeded at 1–2 × 10⁶ cells/mL in Petri dishes. The cells were cultured at 37 °C with 5% CO₂, and the medium was changed every 2–3 days. After 7 days, adherent cells were harvested as differentiated BMDMs. Differentiated BMDMs were infected with culture supernatants of the AMJ2-C11 (ATCC) cell line, which constitutively produces the J2-CRE retrovirus (provided by N. Inohara and H. Hara), as described previously38. After 2 days of culture, the virus-containing medium was replaced with complete growth medium (Iscove’s modified Dulbecco’s medium (IMDM) supplemented with 10% FBS). The culture was then continued until the virus-infected BMDMs spontaneously expanded into colonies, generating iBMDMs.

We amplified murine IL-33 from mouse lung tissue mRNA by reverse transcriptase (RT)-PCR using the primers 5′-AATTCTGCAGTCGACCATGAGACCTAGAATGAAGT-3′ and 5′- GACCGGTGGATCCCGGATTTTCGAGAGCTTAAACA-3′ and inserted them into a pmCherryN1 vector (Clontech) to generate stable transfectants of murine IL-33 fused to mCherry (pmIL-33–mCherry). Afterward, the cDNA encoding IL-33–mCherry was cloned and inserted into a transposon vector, pT2K-CAGGSv3 (provided by Koichi Kawakami)39. To generate the pT2K-CAGGSv3-IL-33–P2A–mCherry vector, the cDNA encoding murine IL-33 fused at its C-terminus to a P2A sequence was amplified by PCR using pmIL-33–mCherry as a template and the following primers: 5′-CAAAGAATTGTCTAGGTCGACCATGAGACCTAGAATGAAG-3′ and 5′-GGCGACCGGTGGATCCCCAGGTCCAGGGTTCTCCTCCACGTCTCCAGCCTGCTTCAGCAGGCTGAAGTTAGTAGCTCCGCTTCCCCGGATTTTCGAGAGCTTAAA-3′. The resulting PCR product was subsequently cloned and inserted into the SalI/BamHI-digested pT2K-CAGGSv3-IL-33–mCherry vector, generating pT2K-CAGGSv3-IL-33–P2A–mCherry. We then transfected MLE-12 cells, iMEFs, and iBMDMs with pT2K-CAGGSv3–IL-33–mCherry or pT2K-CAGGSv3–IL-33–P2A–mCherry together with pCAGGS-T2TP, which encodes the transposase, using Lipofectamine 2000 (11668019, Invitrogen) or Turbofect (R0531, Thermo Fisher Scientific). We also generated iBMDMs expressing Hmgb1–mCherry using the pT2K-CAGGSv3-Hmgb1–mCherry vector23, as described above. After transfection, mCherry-positive cells were sorted with a BD FACSAriaTM III (BD Biosciences).

Cell death induction

IL-33–mCherry or IL-33–P2A–mCherry iMEFs were stimulated with TNF (10 ng/mL), BV6 (1 µM), and GSK’872 (5 µM) to induce apoptosis or with TNF (10 ng/mL), BV6 (1 µM), and zVAD (20 µM) to induce necroptosis. IL-33–mCherry MLE12 cells were treated with TNF (10 ng/mL) plus either BV6 (1 µM) or CHX (5 µg/mL) to induce apoptosis. IL-33–mCherry, IL-33–P2A–mCherry, or Hmgb1-mCherry iBMDMs were stimulated with venetoclax (50 or 100 µM) to induce apoptosis or pretreated with LPS (100 ng/mL or 1 μg/mL) for 16 h followed by nigericin (10 µM) to induce pyroptosis. Necroptosis in IL-33–mCherry iBMDMs was induced by stimulation with BV6 (1 µM) and zVAD (50 µM).

Generation of knockout cell lines

We integrated small guide RNAs corresponding to Gsdmd, Gsdme, and Ninj1 into the BsmBI site of the lentiCRISPRv2 puro to generate knockout cells. lentiCRISPRv2 puro was a gift from Brett Stringer (Addgene plasmid # 98290; RRID:Addgene_98290)40. The sequences of the oligonucleotides used are as follows: Gsdmd-S, 5′-CACCggagaagggaaaatttctgg-3′; Gsdmd-AS, 5′-AAACccagaaattttcccttctcC-3′ Gsdme-S, 5′-CACCGaaaagaagagatactggtgc-3′, Gsdme-AS, 5′-AAACgcaccagtatctcttcttttC-3′ : Ninj1-S, 5′-CACCGactgaggagtatgagctcaa-3′; Ninj1-AS, 5′-AAACttgagctcatactcctcagtC-3′. HEK293 cells were transfected with lentiCRISPRv2 puro with or without corresponding small guide RNAs (sgRNAs) along with packaging plasmids, including pMD2.G and psPAX2, using PEI MAX (24765, Cosmo Bio) to generate lentiviruses. pMD2.G and psPAX2 were gifts from Didier Trono (Addgene plasmids #12259 and #12260; RRID:Addgene_12259 and RRID:Addgene_12260). IL-33–mCherry or IL-33–P2A–mCherry iMEFs, IL-33–mCherry MLE-12 cells, and IL-33–mCherry or IL-33–P2A–mCherry iBMDMs were infected with lentivirus containing the corresponding sgRNAs and cultured in the presence of puromycin (2.5 μg/mL for iMEFs; 2.5 μg/mL for MLE-12 cells; 7.5 μg/mL for iBMDMs) for several days. After antibiotic-resistant cells were obtained, the cells were subjected to limiting dilution. The expression of the indicated proteins was examined by Western blotting, and cells lacking the proteins were used for subsequent experiments. Cells infected with lentivirus derived from lentiCRISPRv2 puro without the indicated sgRNAs were used as control cells.

Western blotting

Cells expressing IL-33–mCherry, IL-33–P2A–mCherry, or Hmgb1–mCherry were seeded in 6-well plates (MLE-12 cells were seeded at 750,000 cells/well, iMEFs at 300,000 cells/well, and iBMDMs at 750,000 cells/well). After stimulation with the indicated agents for the indicated times, the supernatants were collected, and the cells were lysed with RIPA buffer (150 mM NaCl, 50 mM Tris-HCl [pH 8], 1% Nonidet P-40, 0.5% sodium deoxycholate, 0.1% sodium dodecyl sulfate, 25 mM β-glycerophosphate, 1 mM PMSF, 1 μg/ml aprotinin, 1 μg/ml leupeptin, and 1 μg/ml pepstatin) at 4 °C for 30 min. The cell lysates and supernatants were subjected to SDS‒PAGE and then transferred onto polyvinylidene difluoride membranes (IPVH 00010, Millipore). The membranes were analyzed by immunoblotting with the indicated antibodies and developed with Immobilon Western Chemiluminescent HRP Substrate (WBKLS0500, Merck). The signals were analyzed with an Amersham Imager 600 (GE Healthcare Life Sciences). Uncropped images of the results of the Western blots are included in the Supplementary Figures.

Coomassie blue staining

Culture supernatants were separated by SDS–PAGE. Gels were stained with CBB Stain One (04543, Nacalai Tesque) solution for 1 h with gentle shaking and then destained by rinsing with deionized water.

Enzyme-linked immunosorbent assay

Cells expressing IL-33–mCherry or IL-33–P2A–mCherry were seeded in 96-well or 12-well plates and stimulated with the indicated agents for 16 h. Time lapse imaging

Images were acquired using a DeltaVision microscope system (GE Healthcare) built on an Olympus IX-71 inverted microscope, equipped with a CoolSNAP HQ2 CCD camera (Photometrics) and a 60×/1.42 NA PlanApo N oil-immersion objective (Olympus), as previously described23. Cells were seeded on a CELLview Cell Culture Dish (Greiner Bio-One) and cultured in FluoroBrite DMEM supplemented with Alexa Fluor 647 Annexin V (1ː 500) and SYTOX Green (50 μM) in a 37 °C heat chamber with 5% CO2 gas. For time-lapse imaging, differential interference contrast (DIC) and fluorescence images for SYTOX Green and Alexa Fluor 647 Annexin V were captured at 10-min intervals after stimulation with the indicated agents. For SYTOX Green, a filter set (Ex 475/28 nm, Em 525/48 nm) was used. Alexa Fluor 647–conjugated Annexin V was detected using a filter set (Ex 632/23 nm, Em 679/34 nm) and a polychroic mirror suitable for far-red imaging. Fluorescence intensities of Alexa Fluor 647 Annexin V and SYTOX Green were quantified in individual cells using ImageJ software (version 1.54f; National Institutes of Health). For each cell, the time point at which the Alexa Fluor 647 Annexin V signal exceeded 20% of its maximum value was defined as the Annexin V onset time. Similarly, the time point at which the SYTOX Green signal exceeded 20% of its maximum value was defined as the SYTOX onset time. The interval between Annexin V onset and SYTOX onset was calculated for each cell. Distributions of these intervals were then visualized and compared between groups.

Extracellular release of IL-33–mCherry and Hmgb1–mCherry assessed via LCI-S

Live-cell imaging of IL-33–mCherry and Hmgb1–mCherry release was performed using the LCI-S system as previously described41. Custom-made waveguide chips (Live Cell Diagnosis Ltd.) were used to monitor secretion activity during cell culture. Briefly, time-resolved imaging was conducted using a fully automated inverted microscope (ECLIPSE Ti2-E; Nikon) equipped with a CFI Plan Apochromat Lambda D 10× objective lens (Nikon), a stage-top incubator (STXG-TIZWX-SET; Tokai Hit Co.), and a scientific CMOS camera (ORCA-Flash 4.0 V3; Hamamatsu Photonics). Illumination was provided by a multi-LED light source (D-LEDI; Nikon) and a multiline laser diode illuminator (LDI-7; 89 North). For IL-33–mCherry and Hmgb1–mCherry detection, a quad-band excitation filter (ZET405/470/555/640x; Chroma Technology) and its corresponding emission filter (ZT405/470/555/640rpc-UF1; Chroma Technology) were used. For SYTOX Blue detection, a widefield fluorescence filter set (DAPI-U C-FLL-C; Nikon) was employed. Cells were seeded onto LCI-S chips precoated with anti-RFP antibody and cultured in FluoroBrite DMEM supplemented with 1% BSA and 1 μM SYTOX Blue under standard conditions. Cells were either left untreated or stimulated as described in the “Cell death induction” section. Stimulation was initiated simultaneously across all conditions. Live-cell imaging was performed using differential interference contrast (DIC) for transmitted light images, TIRF for mCherry fluorescence images, and epifluorescence for SYTOX blue images. Time-lapse imaging was carried out at 2-min intervals for one field of view per condition using NIS-Elements version 5.4 (Nikon). At each time point, DIC, TIRF (for released IL-33–mCherry or Hmgb1–mCherry), and epifluorescence (for SYTOX Blue) images were acquired. Relative fluorescence intensities of IL-33–mCherry or Hmgb1–mCherry and SYTOX Blue were quantified using ImageJ software (version 1.54f; National Institutes of Health). For each cell, the time point at which the fluorescence intensity of SYTOX Blue exceeded 20% of its maximum value was defined as the death onset time. The increase in the TIRF signal was subsequently fitted using a five-parameter logistic function implemented in Python (version 3.11.5, Python Software Foundation), defined as follows:\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$I\left(t\right)={I}_{max }+\frac{{I}_{b}-{I}_{max }}{{\left({1+\left(\frac{t}{\tau }\right)}^{k}\right)}^{\gamma }}$$\end{document}It=Imax+Ib−Imax1+tτkγwhere I(t) represents the fluorescence intensity at time t relative to death onset. Parameter Ib denotes the baseline intensity before release, and Imax represents the maximum intensity after saturation. Parameter τ is the inflection point at which the release rate is maximal, k corresponds to the Hill coefficient, indicating the steepness of the release, and γ adjusts the asymmetry of the curve. From the fitted curve, two indices were calculated. First, the curve was normalized by setting its minimum and maximum values to 0% and 100%, respectively, and the time required for the signal to rise from 5% to 95% was defined as the duration (T) of release. Second, the time from death onset (when the fluorescence intensity of SYTOX Blue exceeded 20% of its maximum value) to the point when the TIRF signal reached 5% was defined as the lag time. On the basis of the calculated lag time, the cells were classified according to their IL-33 release phenotype. In necroptotic cells, IL-33 is released almost simultaneously with the onset of cell death and is completed within 4 min (two time frames). This immediate release pattern was categorized as the simultaneous type. For pyroptotic and apoptotic cells, those exhibiting similarly short lag times were also classified as the simultaneous type, whereas cells in which IL-33 release occurred more than 4 min (two time frames) after the onset of cell death were classified as the delayed type. Finally, for each mode of cell death, individual fitted curves were aligned at the death onset time (set as 0) and overlaid to generate composite plots. Additionally, the distribution of the release durations (T) was visualized to assess the differences in IL-33 release dynamics across distinct cell death pathways.

Statistic and reproducibility

Statistical analyses were performed using GraphPad Prism (version 11.0.1; GraphPad Software). Data are presented as mean ± SD unless otherwise indicated. Statistical significance was determined using unpaired two-tailed Student’s t test, Mann‒Whitney U test, one-way ANOVA with Dunnett’s or Tukey’s multiple comparisons test, two-way ANOVA with Tukey’s multiple comparisons test, or Kruskal‒Wallis test with Dunn’s multiple comparisons test, as indicated in the figure legends. All experiments were independently repeated at least two or three times with similar results. Biological replicates represent independent experiments, whereas technical replicates refer to repeated measurements within the same experiment. Sample sizes (n), the number of independent experiments, and the statistical tests used for each dataset are provided in the corresponding figure legends. No statistical methods were used to predetermine sample size. The experiments were not randomized, and investigators were not blinded to allocation and outcome assessment.
