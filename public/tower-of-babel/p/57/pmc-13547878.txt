Impact of socioeconomic status on disability in a longitudinal rheumatoid arthritis cohort
Authors: Oscar Russell, Susan Lester, Jessica Stanhope, Rachel J Black, Marissa Lassere, Claire Barrett, Lyn March, Tom Lynch, Rachelle Buchbinder, Catherine L Hill
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractThe impact of socioeconomic status (SES) on health outcomes in Australians living with rheumatoid arthritis (RA) is not known. We aimed to determine whether differences in disability trajectory were present according to individuals' SES using longitudinal data from the Australian Rheumatology Association Database. We found that lower area‐level SES was associated with significantly increased Health Assessment Questionnaire – Disability Index scores (indicating greater disability). These results may indicate the presence of a healthcare inequity for those living with RA and lower SES.Keywords: health service, ambulatory care, rheumatoid arthritis

The social determinants of health (SDoH), including socioeconomic status (SES), education, income and race, among other factors, are increasingly recognised as central factors in determining health outcomes for individuals living with chronic diseases. 1 Despite recent significant advances in disease‐modifying treatments for rheumatoid arthritis (RA), disadvantageous SDoH are persistently associated with poorer health outcomes, including greater disability, in patients with RA from international studies. 2 , 3

Disability is a central health outcome in RA research – it describes the inability to participate in valued life activities and work. 4 , 5 It is typically assessed using patient‐reported outcome measures such as the Health Assessment Questionnaire – Disability Index (HAQ), which is a measure of disability due to arthritis with demonstrated prognostic value in RA patients. 6 , 7 In a dedicated Australian early arthritis clinic cohort, we recently showed that individuals with lower SES had greater composite disease activity and disability during follow‐up. No contemporary studies, however, have examined the relationship between SES and disability in more generalisable ‘real‐world’ Australian RA cohorts. 2 This is significant as Australia's universal healthcare system allows relatively easy access to biological and targeted‐synthetic disease‐modifying anti‐rheumatic drug (b/tsDMARD) treatments, compared to countries without universal healthcare. 8

The primary aim of this study was, therefore, to determine the impact of SES on self‐reported disability (measured using HAQ) in Australian patients. Since prior studies compared the use of area‐level (e.g. that based on neighbourhood or census tract ranking) and person‐level SES (e.g. educational achievement) to predict RA outcomes with conflicting results, 9 the secondary aim was to compare different approaches to determining SES.

We utilised data from the Australian Rheumatology Association Database (ARAD), which is a longitudinal cohort study following individuals with rheumatologist‐diagnosed inflammatory arthritis (RA, psoriatic arthritis, ankylosing spondylitis and juvenile idiopathic arthritis). ARAD participants complete regular surveys with measurement of patient‐reported outcomes including the HAQ. Further details of this cohort have been described elsewhere. 10

We included adults with rheumatologist‐confirmed RA diagnosed after 2003, when bDMARDs first became subsidised under the Australian Pharmaceutical Benefits Scheme. The outcome was HAQ, determined at regular intervals using survey data. The exposure was SES determined using quintiles of the Index of Relative Social Advantage and Disadvantage. This is an area‐based measure based on 2016 Australian census data developed by the Australian Bureau of Statistics and normalised to the Australian population, using individuals’ residential address, measured at the Statistical Area 1 level (census tracts with on average approximately 400 individuals).

Multilevel generalised linear models were fitted to predict HAQ using a scaled fractional polynomial (FP) power variable for disease duration with random effects considered at the participant ID level. Additional covariates in final multivariable models included sex, comorbidity and education. Comorbidity was determined at baseline based on the Rheumatic Diseases Comorbidity Index. 11 Lower educational achievement was determined by less than or equal to secondary school education at baseline. This was used in sensitivity analyses as a comparator definition of SES. Results are presented as coefficients and population‐averaged marginal estimates.

StataNow/MP 18.5 (College Station, TX, USA) was used for all analyses. This study was approved by the Central Adelaide Local Health Network Human Research Ethics Committee (approval number 19434).

There were 1140 individuals who met inclusion criteria for this substudy. This sample had typical demographics for a prevalent RA cohort (at cohort baseline, the mean age was 53 years (standard deviation 13); 75% were female, and median disease duration was 2 years (interquartile range 1–4)) (Table S1).

Using FPs, disease duration was power transformed into two exponentiated variables of disease duration (0.5, 0.5) (chosen based on review of 44 alternative models using the fp command, noting deviance difference and considering model parsimony, see Data S1). The unadjusted model intercept coefficient (reflecting HAQ at cohort baseline) was 2.03 (95% confidence interval (CI) 1.83–2.23) (Table S2). A plot of predicted HAQ by disease duration from this model from onset to 15 years is shown in Figure 1A. After initial improvement, HAQ appears to increase with ongoing disease duration.

In a univariate model, lower SES was associated with higher HAQ (more disability), reflecting a significant ‘total effect’ of SES on HAQ (Quintile 1 (lowest) vs. Quintile 5 (Q5)): b = 0.42 (95% CI 0.30–0.54; P < 0.001) (Table S2). Predictions from this model are shown in Figure 1B.

In a sensitivity analysis using an alternative definition of SES, participants with lower education had higher HAQ than those with greater educational achievement in univariate analyses (b = 0.12 (0.06, 0.18); P < 0.001; Figure 1C, Table S2).

In final multivariable models, including sex, education and comorbidity as covariates, lower SES was persistently associated with higher HAQ, indicating a significant ‘direct effect’ of SES (Q1 vs Q5: b = 0.40 (95% CI 0.28–0.51; P < 0.001)) (Table S2). This effect size was greater than the minimally clinically important difference for HAQ 12 (Fig. 2). Lower education (0.09 (95% CI 0.03–0.15; P = 0.002)), greater comorbidity (0.05 (95% CI 0.04–0.06; P < 0.001)) and female sex (0.29 (95% CI 0.21–0.37; P < 0.001)) were also independently associated with significantly greater coefficients for HAQ (Table S2).

Discussion

We found that in Australian patients with RA, lower SES was associated with significantly greater disability as measured by HAQ, independent of the effect of comorbidity, sex or educational achievement. These results demonstrate that SDoH impact health outcomes even in jurisdictions with universal healthcare access, such as Australia. The factors driving these results remain unknown, requiring further investigation. One hypothesis is that delays in treatment or differences in medication use may contribute to these differences. In Australia, there is significant geographic and socioeconomic variation in accessibility to medical services such as primary care providers (general practitioners (GPs)) who provide fully subsidised services and the availability of specialist rheumatology services. 13 Our prior work using ARAD data demonstrated that Australians with inflammatory arthritis living in more disadvantaged areas had more GP visits but fewer specialist physician consultations, including with rheumatologists. 14

A previous study systematically reviewed different statistical approaches used in modelling HAQ 15 and demonstrated the use of latent class growth models, finding four different trajectory groups and analysing the predictor variables of group membership in two early arthritis cohorts. It found that age, female sex, lower SES, disease activity scores and symptom duration were associated with more severe trajectory group membership. Previous studies also investigated use of FP models to describe the non‐linear responses of HAQ. 16 A FP approach was chosen over latent class growth models for our study as the primary aim was to examine the effect of individual exposures (e.g. area‐level SES), rather than exploring the effect of multiple predictors of HAQ. Additionally, as participants entered ARAD at different stages of disease, data from disease onset were not available for all participants, limiting the feasibility of latent class growth models for this aim. The modelled HAQ trajectory shown in Figure 1 indicates that after an initial improvement in disability scores, there may be an increase in disability with ongoing disease duration. However, this trajectory does not exactly replicate the previously described ‘J‐shaped curve’ HAQ trajectory, 15 , 17 which may reflect more recent advances in therapeutics for RA and broad access to these medications in Australia, as opposed to treatments available for participants of studies conducted around the turn of the century.

Comorbidity is an important consideration in modelling trajectories of patient‐reported outcome measures in individuals with chronic diseases. We found comorbidity was a predictor of HAQ, independent of the effect of SES. The importance of comorbidity and multimorbidity on influencing outcomes in RA has recently been reviewed. 18 Socioeconomic disadvantage is known to be associated with higher comorbidity. 19 Our results highlight that both SES and comorbidity appear important in determining disability outcomes in RA patients.

There are some limitations of our study. The ARAD cohort is composed of individuals who have had contact with a specialist rheumatologist and demonstrated interest in participating in an observational study. These findings may therefore have a conservative bias; in other words, they are likely to represent the ‘best case scenario’, 20 with the implication being that the true impact of differences in SES, at a population level and outside of a cohort study context, may be greater. Most participants (94%) reported Caucasian background, and therefore our capacity to consider the impact of race and/or ethnicity on disability outcomes was limited.

It is notable that when included in the same model, both SES and education were associated with RA disability. This highlights the potential role of area‐level factors in contributing to differences in health outcomes for individuals with prevalent RA.

Despite the presence of a universal healthcare system, Australians living with RA appear to have different disability trajectories relating to SES, with lower SES associated with greater disability. There are many potential explanations for these findings but few definitive answers. Considering the impact of both upstream and downstream SDoH will be important in addressing this unmet research need, as results may have important, practical policy implications at the community and local clinic level respectively.
