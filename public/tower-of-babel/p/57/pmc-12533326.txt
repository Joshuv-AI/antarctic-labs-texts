Generalized probabilistic canonical correlation analysis for multi-modal data integration with full or partial observations
Authors: Tianjian Yang, Wei Vivian Li
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
The integration and analysis of multi-modal data are increasingly essential across various domains including bioinformatics. As the volume and complexity of such data grow, there is a pressing need for computational models that not only integrate diverse modalities but also leverage their complementary information to improve clustering accuracy and insights, especially when dealing with partial observations with missing data. We propose Generalized Probabilistic Canonical Correlation Analysis (GPCCA), an unsupervised method for the integration and joint dimensionality reduction of multi-modal data. GPCCA addresses key challenges in multi-modal data analysis by handling missing values within the model, enabling the integration of more than two modalities, and identifying informative features while accounting for correlations within individual modalities. GPCCA demonstrates robustness to various missing data patterns and provides low-dimensional embeddings that facilitate downstream clustering and analysis. In a range of simulation settings, GPCCA outperforms existing methods in capturing essential patterns across modalities. Additionally, we demonstrate its applicability to multi-omics data from TCGA cancer datasets and a multi-view image dataset. GPCCA offers a useful framework for multi-modal data integration, effectively handling missing data and providing informative low-dimensional embeddings. Its performance across cancer genomics and multi-view image data highlights its robustness and potential for broad application. To make the method accessible to the wider research community, we have released an R package, GPCCA, which is available at https://github.com/Kaversoniano/GPCCA.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12859-025-06227-9.

Introduction

Many real-world datasets can be described from multiple perspectives, where each perspective, typically represented as a matrix, corresponds to a data modality. A dataset that is consisted of multiple modalities collected from the same set of individuals is termed a multi-modal dataset [1]. Examples include medical imaging data combining computed tomography (CT) and magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) scans [2] and multi-omics datasets from The Cancer Genome Atlas (TCGA) [3], which include DNA methylation, messenger RNA (mRNA) expression, and microRNA (miRNA) expression. Technological advances have made the collection of multi-modal data increasingly prevalent, enabling integrative analyses that leverage information across modalities. Analyzing a single modality in isolation is often suboptimal, as each modality may provide distinct yet complementary signals, which, when integrated, can enhance learning performance [4].

A key challenge in multi-modal learning is to capture shared structures across modalities while leveraging modality-specific signals to improve inference and prediction. In the context of unlabeled multi-modal data, an important analytical task is unsupervised clustering, which aims to integrate all available modalities to uncover meaningful data patterns and provide insights. Various computational methods have been proposed to combine multiple modalities, extract shared and complementary information, and enhance clustering accuracy. For example, Rappoport and Shamir [5] provided a comprehensive review of multi-modal clustering methods, covering both omics-specific approaches and generic machine learning techniques. More recently, Leng et al. [6] benchmarked deep-learning-based multi-modal integration methods, comparing their performance in clustering and classification tasks using multi-omics data. These studies highlight the importance of integrating multiple modalities for robust and meaningful clustering results.

Integrative methods for multi-modal data are typically categorized into three main approaches: early, middle, and late integration [7]. Early integration concatenates all modalities into a single matrix, followed by a single-modality clustering method. A common strategy is to first apply principal component analysis (PCA) [8] or its variants for dimensionality reduction before performing clustering. Late integration first clusters each modality separately and then aggregates the clustering results. For example, the PINS method [9] constructs a similarity matrix by integrating connectivity information from each modality before refining the final clustering results.

In contrast, middle integration represents a broad class of methods that jointly model all modalities within a unified framework. These approaches fall into two major categories: similarity-based integration and joint dimensionality reduction. Example similarity-based methods include SNF [10], which constructs sample-sample networks for each modality and iteratively fuses them using message passing, followed by spectral clustering. The NEMO method [11] simplifies earlier similarity-based approaches by avoiding iterative optimization, and it enables application to datasets with missing modalities without requiring prior imputation.

Among joint dimensionality reduction methods, various extensions of nonnegative matrix factorization (NMF) [12, 13] are widely used for extracting latent factors across multi-modal datasets. Canonical Correlation Analysis (CCA) [14] and its variants are another prominent class of joint dimensionality reduction techniques [1]. CCA works by linearly projecting two modalities into a lower-dimensional space such that the correlation between them is maximized. Over time, multiple extensions of CCA have been developed to address different challenges. For example, sparse CCA incorporates a sparsity constraint to identify the most informative features [15]. Sparse weighted CCA (SWCCA) [16] enhances CCA by incorporating sample-specific weights to uncover relevant feature pairs and influential subjects in two-modality data, which is particularly useful for identifying associations in heterogeneous data. The extension to sparse MCCA generalizes CCA to handle more than two modalities, improving its ability to integrate multi-source data [15]. Tilted-CCA decomposes paired multi-modal data into lower-dimensional embeddings, which can separately quantify shared and modality-specific information [17]. With the advancement of neural network models, new methods have been developed to learn nonlinear embeddings of multi-modal data. For example, DGCCA [18] employs deep neural networks to learn nonlinear transformations for each modality, aiming to maximize the correlation among the resulting latent representations across modalities.

Another challenge in multi-modal data integration is the prevalence of missing values in real-world datasets, arising due to system limitations, data corruption, subject dropout, or budget constraints [7, 19]. Through early integration (i.e., concatenating all features into a single input space before modeling), probabilistic PCA (PPCA) provides a flexible framework for handling missing values using the Expectation-Maximization (EM) algorithm [20–22]. In contrast, in CCA-based middle integration, an iterative imputation method was proposed to impute missing values without constructing a probabilistic model [23]. In the specific problem of multi-omics data integration, where each omics type corresponds to one modality, the MOFA method [24] adopts a variational Bayesian framework to simultaneously learn low-dimensional projections and impute missing values. However, its assumption of feature-wise independence may limit its ability to capture inter-feature correlations.

To address the challenge of multi-modal data integration with full or partial observations, we propose Generalized Probabilistic Canonical Correlation Analysis (GPCCA), an extension of probabilistic CCA [25] that handles missing data across two or more modalities. GPCCA makes three key contributions: (1) it learns integrated low-dimensional embeddings in a unified probabilistic framework, (2) it inherently imputes missing values within its parameter estimation process, and (3) it identifies informative features while accounting for correlations between features within the same modality. To enhance numerical stability and model generalizability, we incorporate ridge regularization into the GPCCA model. An EM algorithm is developed to estimate model parameters. As detailed in the Results section, we evaluate GPCCA’s performance on three datasets: a three-modality simulated dataset under diverse settings, a four-modality handwritten numeral image dataset, and a three-modality omics dataset spanning diverse cancer types.

Ridge regularization in the GPCCA model

As the dimensionality of features increases, estimating the error covariance matrix \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\varvec{\Psi }$$\end{document} becomes increasingly unstable due to the high variance and sensitivity of standard covariance estimators in high-dimensional settings. This instability arises because the number of parameters to estimate in \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\varvec{\Psi }$$\end{document} grows quadratically with the number of features, leading to overfitting and unreliable estimates, especially when the sample size is relatively small compared to the feature dimensionality. In many real-world applications, it is reasonable to assume that each feature is directly associated with only a limited subset of other features within each modality, implying a sparse or block-diagonal-like structure in the covariance matrix. Therefore, to address these challenges, we introduce ridge regularization on the error covariance matrix \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\varvec{\Psi }$$\end{document}. The ridge regularization we consider involves adding a penalty term to effectively shrink the off-diagonal entries and stabilize the estimation of of the error correlation matrix [28]. By incorporating this regularization, we achieve a more robust and stable estimation of the error covariance matrix, improving the reliability of the model in both high-dimensional and limited-sample settings.

Suppose we use \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\textbf{R}$$\end{document} to denote the error correlation matrix, then the error covariance matrix \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\varvec{\Psi }$$\end{document} can be factorized as \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\varvec{\Psi } = \varvec{\Psi }_d^{\frac{1}{2}}\textbf{R}\varvec{\Psi }_d^{\frac{1}{2}}$$\end{document}, where \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\varvec{\Psi }_d$$\end{document} is a diagonal matrix with positive values. As derived in Supplementary Methods 1.1, the expected complete log-likelihood can be represented in the form below:9\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\begin{aligned} {{\,\mathrm{\mathbb {E}}\,}}[\ell _{\text {c}}(\varvec{\Theta })] \propto {\mathcal {Q}}(\varvec{\Theta }) = -\dfrac{n}{2}\ln|\varvec{\Psi }| - \dfrac{1}{2}\sum \limits _{k=1}^n\text {tr}(\varvec{\Psi }^{-1}\textbf{G}_k) - \dfrac{1}{2}\sum \limits _{k=1}^n\text {tr}({{\,\mathrm{\mathbb {E}}\,}}(\textbf{z}_k\textbf{z}_k^{\text {T}})). \end{aligned}$$\end{document}The ridge estimator of the error correlation matrix \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\textbf{R}$$\end{document} is the maximum penalized likelihood estimator (MPLE) with a penalty term proportional to \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$-\text {tr}(\textbf{R}^{-1})$$\end{document} [28]. Therefore, to obtain the ridge estimator, we modify \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$${\mathcal {Q}}(\varvec{\Theta })$$\end{document} by adding a penalty term as follows:10\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\begin{aligned} {\mathcal {Q}}_{c}(\varvec{R})&= {\mathcal {Q}}(\varvec{\Theta }) - \dfrac{c}{2}\text {tr}(\textbf{R}^{-1}) \propto -\dfrac{n}{2}\ln|\varvec{\Psi }| - \dfrac{1}{2}\sum \limits _{k=1}^n\text {tr}(\varvec{\Psi }^{-1}\textbf{G}_k) - \dfrac{c}{2}\text {tr}(\textbf{R}^{-1}), \end{aligned}$$\end{document}where \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$c\in (0, n)$$\end{document} and terms not dependent on \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\varvec{R}$$\end{document} are ignored. Then, based on the derivations in Supplementary Methods 1.2, we can show that the MPLE of the error correlation matrix is11\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\begin{aligned} \hat{\textbf{R}}_{\text {ridge}} = \lambda \hat{\textbf{R}} + (1-\lambda )\textbf{I}, \end{aligned}$$\end{document}where \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\lambda = 1-\dfrac{c}{n}\in (0,1)$$\end{document}. Here, \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\hat{\textbf{R}} = \hat{\varvec{\Psi }}_d^{-\frac{1}{2}}\hat{\varvec{\Psi }}\hat{\varvec{\Psi }}_d^{-\frac{1}{2}}$$\end{document} denotes the original estimator of error correlation matrix without introducing the regularization term, and \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\hat{\varvec{\Psi }} = \dfrac{1}{n}\sum \limits _{k=1}^n \text {Bdiag}_{\varvec{\Psi }}[\textbf{G}_k^{\text {T}}]$$\end{document} is the original MLE of \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\varvec{\Psi }$$\end{document}. The corresponding ridge estimator of the error covariance matrix is12\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\begin{aligned} \hat{\varvec{\Psi }}_{\text {ridge}} = \hat{\varvec{\Psi }} + (\dfrac{1}{\lambda } - 1)\hat{\varvec{\Psi }}_d. \end{aligned}$$\end{document}Because of the above relationships between the ridge estimators and the original estimators, this ridge regularization can be easily incorporated into the EM algorithm by updating the M-step in formula (8).

The ridge estimator, \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\hat{\textbf{R}}_{\text {ridge}}$$\end{document}, effectively balances the bias-variance trade-off inherent in correlation matrix estimation. It acts as a weighted average of the sample correlation \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\hat{\textbf{R}}$$\end{document} and the identity \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\textbf{I}$$\end{document}. In the shrinkage term, the choice of \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\lambda$$\end{document} (the regularization parameter) plays an important role in determining this balance. The optimal value of the ridge regularization parameter can vary across datasets due to differences in dimensionality, correlation structures, and noise levels. When applying GPCCA to a new multi-modal dataset, multiple trials with varying \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\lambda$$\end{document} values can be performed in exploratory analysis to identify the most suitable parameter. Based on comprehensive simulation studies conducted on synthetic datasets, we observed that \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\lambda = \frac{1}{2}$$\end{document} and \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\lambda =\frac{2}{3}$$\end{document} consistently achieve good performance across diverse scenarios, suggesting them as robust default choices for the ridge regularization parameter.

Selection of latent factor number

We introduce the method below to guide the selection of \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$d$$\end{document}, the number of latent factors. Consider a collection of candidate values \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\{d_1, d_2, \dots, d_K\}$$\end{document}, ordered such that \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$d_i < d_j$$\end{document} for all \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$1 \le i < j \le K$$\end{document}. For each candidate value \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$d_k$$\end{document}, the EM algorithm for GPCCA is initialized with \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$B$$\end{document} different starting points, and consequently, \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$B$$\end{document} sets of results are estimated for GPCCA given \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$d_k\ (k=1,\dots,K)$$\end{document}. To evaluate the robustness of the model fitting with each candidate dimension, we perform Louvain clustering [29] of subjects on the learned low-dimensional embeddings. The clustering results are aggregated into a matrix \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\textbf{L}_k\in \mathbb {R}^{n \times B}$$\end{document}, where each column corresponds to clustering results from one initialization. Then, we compute a consensus matrix \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\textbf{C}_k\in \mathbb {R}^{n\times n}$$\end{document}, where the \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$(i, j)$$\end{document}-th entry reflects the proportion of clustering results in which subjects \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$i$$\end{document} and \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$j$$\end{document} are assigned to the same cluster. To assess the consistency of the \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$B$$\end{document} sets of clusters resulting from the same latent dimension, we introduce the consensus score below, which provides a quantitative measure of the agreement between the multiple clustering results, offering insights into the reliability of the learned low-dimensional embeddings. The consensus score is formally defined as follows:13\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\begin{aligned} {\mathcal {H}}_k = \sum \limits _{i<j}\textbf{C}_{k,ij}\log _2(\textbf{C}_{k,ij}), \end{aligned}$$\end{document}which has an upper bound of 0 when the \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$B$$\end{document} sets of clustering results are exactly the same. The final number of latent factors \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$d_{k^*}$$\end{document} is selected as the candidate value which corresponds to the largest consensus score. Then, the last step is to find the best initialization \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$b\in \{1,2,\dots,B\}$$\end{document}. For each initialization, we can compute the binary connectivity matrix \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\textbf{C}_{k^*}^{(b)}$$\end{document}, where the \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$(i,j)$$\end{document}-th entry is 1 if subjects \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$i$$\end{document} and \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$j$$\end{document} belong to the same cluster. We compare this connectivity matrix with the consensus matrix based on the root mean squared error (RMSE):14\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\begin{aligned} \text {RMSE}_b = \sqrt{\frac{2}{n(n-1)}\sum \limits _{i<j}\left( \textbf{C}^{(b)}_{k^*,ij} - \textbf{C}_{k^*,ij}\right) ^2}. \end{aligned}$$\end{document}Finally, we select the initialization that leads to the smallest RMSE.

Case B: heavy-tailed data (MCAR). In Case B, we consider heavy-tailed distribution where the assumption of normal distribution in the GPCCA model is violated. The simulation procedure is similar to that of Case A, except that the normal distribution is replaced with the \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$t$$\end{document} distribution. Case C: normal data (MNAR). In Case C, we consider a scenario where the missingness is not at random (MNAR). First, we generate complete data for the three modalities as described in Case A. The hidden variable \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$H_k$$\end{document} determines whether the entire set of features within a modality is missing for a given sample \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$k\ (k=1,\dots,n)$$\end{document}. This design mimics real-world biological or biomedical scenarios where the availability of experimental results for a modality depends on latent factors, such as logistical constraints, sample preparation quality, or inherent biological characteristics of the subject. Specifically, for sample \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$k$$\end{document}, we sample \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$H_k$$\end{document} from a standard normal distribution. For example, Supplementary Figs. Alternative methods and comparative analysis

In the simulation study and real data applications, we consider GPCCA alongside five alternative methods for comparison, including PPCA [30], MOFA [24], SNF [10], NEMO [11] and DGCCA [18]. The above methods are directly applied to the complete data. For incomplete data with missing values, SNF, NEMO and DGCCA cannot be directly applied, and missing values are first imputed using the k-nearest neighbors (kNN) imputation with \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$5$$\end{document} nearest neighbors. We have included a more comprehensive analysis in Supplementary Methods 1.3, considering alternative imputation methods and hyperparameter settings, as summarized in Supplementary Table S1. The results further support the superior performance of GPCCA compared to the other methods regardless of the imputation strategy applied. GPCCA, PPCA, and MOFA can handle the missing values without the need of prior imputation.

To obtain clustering results, for methods capable of learning low-dimensional projections, including GPCCA, PPCA, MOFA and DGCCA, we apply the Louvain clustering algorithm [29] to the projected data with a default resolution parameter of \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$0.8$$\end{document}. GPCCA, MOFA and DGCCA are specifically designed to account for the multi-modal structure of the data, whereas PPCA is implemented either on individual modalities or on a single dataset formed by concatenating multiple modalities. In contrast, similarity-based methods, including SNF and NEMO, compute similarity matrices for each modality, integrate these matrices, and then apply spectral clustering [31] to the final similarity matrix to identify clusters. When ground-truth cluster labels are available, clustering performance is evaluated using the Adjusted Rand Index (ARI) [32] and the Normalized Mutual Information (NMI) [33]. ARI quantifies the agreement between the inferred cluster assignment and the true cluster assignment in the data. It ranges between \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$-1$$\end{document} and \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$1$$\end{document}, with \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$1$$\end{document} indicating a complete agreement between the inferred clusters and the ground truth. NMI measures the similarity between two clustering results or partitions of data samples. It ranges between \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$0$$\end{document} and \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$1$$\end{document}, with \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$0$$\end{document} meaning no similarity and 1 representing perfect agreement between the two given partitions.

In the simulation study, the number of latent factors in GPCCA is selected from \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\{2, 3, 4, 6, 8, 10\}$$\end{document} using the approach introduced in the “Selection of latent factor number” section. PPCA is implemented with the number of principal components (PCs) set to \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$5$$\end{document} for both single-modal and multi-modal data. MOFA is applied with its default parameter settings. The number of clusters in spectral clustering is set to the true cluster number (\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$6$$\end{document}) for SNF as an automatic selection method is not available. In contrast, NEMO determines the number of clusters automatically as part of its algorithmic procedure. For DGCCA, we construct a fully connected neural network for each data modality, consisting of three hidden layers with \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$512$$\end{document}, \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$256$$\end{document}, and \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$128$$\end{document} nodes, respectively. The output dimensionality of each network is fixed at 64. Additional implementation details of DGCCA are summarized in Supplementary Methods 1.4.

In real data applications, the ridge regularization parameter is set to \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\lambda = \frac{1}{2}$$\end{document}, and the number of latent factors in GPCCA is selected from \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\{5, 10, 15, 20, 25, 30\}$$\end{document}. For PPCA, the number of PCs is selected based on cross-validation (using the pcaMethods package [34]) in the multi-view image data application. For the TCGA and DLPFC applications, the elbow method is used to select the number of PCs (using the findPC package [35]) as the cross-validation approach is too time-consuming. MOFA is applied with its default parameter settings. The number of clusters for SNF is set to the ground truth in the multi-view image data application and DLPFC data application. The network architecture used for DGCCA in the real data applications is identical to that employed in the simulation studies.

Real datasets

Multi-view data of handwritten numerals. The dataset consists of four feature sets of handwritten numerals (0 to 9) extracted from a collection of Dutch utility maps [36]. There are \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$2000$$\end{document} observations in total, with \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$200$$\end{document} observations in each class. Each feature set is obtained using a different feature extraction method for images, and we treat each of them as one modality. We use the following four modalities in our analysis: Modality 1 (\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$m_1 = 76$$\end{document}) contains Fourier coefficients of the character shapes; Modality 2 (\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$m_2 = 216$$\end{document}) contains profile correlations; Modality 3 (\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$m_3 = 64$$\end{document}) contains Karhunen-Loève coefficients; Modality 4 (\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$m_4 = 47$$\end{document}) contains Zernike moments. A more detailed introduction to features in these modalities is provided in Supplementary Methods 1.5. In addition, there are no missing values in the original data. To assess method performance in the presence of missing data, we generate four sets of additional data with missing values using the following procedure.

First, we introduce missing values completely at random at rates of \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$20\%$$\end{document} and \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$40\%$$\end{document}, resulting in two datasets: MCAR 20% and MCAR 40%. Next, we introduce modality-specific missingness using a sequence of hidden variables, \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\{H_k\}_{1 \le k \le n} \overset{\text {i.i.d.}}{\sim } {\mathcal {N}}(0, 1)$$\end{document}, which determines whether an entire modality is missing for each sample \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$k$$\end{document} (\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$k = 1, \dots, n$$\end{document}). Specifically, if \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$H_k \ge 0$$\end{document}, the sample has a probability \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$p$$\end{document} of missing an entire modality. Otherwise, if \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$H_k < 0$$\end{document}, the probability increases to \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$2p$$\end{document}. We then introduce missing values into the complete dataset according to these probabilities. By setting \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$p = 0.25$$\end{document} and \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$p = 0.50$$\end{document}, we generate two additional datasets: MNAR 25% and MNAR 50%.

Multi-omics data from the TCGA database. From The Cancer Genome Atlas (TCGA) [3] database, we select \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$10$$\end{document} cancer types and downloaded multi-omics data of three modalities: gene expression levels (Illumina HiSeq), DNA methylation levels (Methylation450k), and miRNA expression levels (Illumina Hiseq). These cancer types were selected based on the following criteria. First, all three data modalities should be available for the cancer type. Second, the phenotype data (survival time) should be available for at least 100 tumor samples. Third, each data modality should contain at least \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$50$$\end{document} tumor samples. As a result, the 10 selected cancer types include lung adenocarcinoma (LUAD), colon cancer (COAD), lung squamous cell carcinoma (LUSC), sarcoma (SARC), bladder cancer (BLCA), breast cancer (BRCA), head and neck cancer (HNSC), kidney clear cell carcinoma (KIRC), lower grade glioma (LGG), and liver cancer (LIHC). After data preprocessing (see Supplementary Methods 1.6), the sample sizes of each cancer type are summarized in Supplementary Table S2.

Spatial transcriptomics data of human dorsolateral prefrontal cortex (DLPFC). The DLPFC dataset was sequenced using the 10x Genomics Visium platform [37]. We focus on one subject with four slices (slice IDs: 151673, 151674, 151675 and 151676). For each slice, the library-size-normalized gene expression levels serve as one modality and the corresponding histology image serves as the other modality. For gene filtering, we only retain genes expressed in at least \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$10$$\end{document} spots. In addition, we use SPARK-X [38] to identify spatially variable genes (SVGs) and select the top \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$1000$$\end{document} SVGs with the smallest P-values. For the histology images, we extract an image patch centered on each spatial spot. Each patch is a square of \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$31 \times 31$$\end{document} pixels with three color channels. Then, we use a pre-trained convolutional neural network model, ResNet50 [39], to extract image features from these patches. To match the default input size required by ResNet50, the original \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$31 \times 31 \times 3$$\end{document} image patches are resized to \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$224 \times 224 \times 3$$\end{document} before feature extraction.

Results

Simulation

To assess the performance of GPCCA in a controlled yet realistic setting with known ground-truth information, we generate four cases of synthetic multi-modal data characterized by distinct distributions and missing mechanisms (see Methods). The four cases are defined as follows: Case A involves normally distributed data with missing completely at random (MCAR); Case B considers heavy-tailed data with MCAR; Case C explores normally distributed data with missing not at random (MNAR); and Case D examines scenarios with correlated modalities under MCAR. Importantly, Cases B-D represent situations where the underlying assumptions of GPCCA are violated in various ways. These cases are designed to evaluate the robustness of GPCCA and the quality of its inferred data embeddings under such challenging conditions. In each case, we generate datasets comprising three modalities and evaluate the performance of GPCCA (regularizatrion parameter \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\lambda = 2/3\ \text {or}\ 1/2$$\end{document}) alongside five alternative methods: PPCA, MOFA, SNF, NEMO, and DGCCA (see Methods for implementation). For dimensionality reduction approaches (GPCCA, PPCA, MOFA, and DGCCA), in order to evaluate the clustering performance, Louvain clustering is employed on their low-dimensional projections. To ensure a comprehensive assessment of method performance, we consider three levels of missingness (\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$0, 20\%, 40\%$$\end{document}) and five levels of feature correlation controlled by the parameter \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\rho \in \{0.3, 0.5, 0.7, 0.8, 0.9\}$$\end{document}.

In Case A, we analyze normal data with an MCAR mechanism. The clustering performance, as assessed by ARI and NMI scores, is summarized in Fig. 2a and Supplementary Fig. S4a respectively. To facilitate a direct comparison between single-modality and multi-modality analyses, PPCA is applied both to data from individual modalities and to concatenated multi-modality data. Since each modality provides only partial information about cluster membership, PPCA applied to the concatenated data consistently outperforms its application to individual modalities. For example, when missing rate is \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$20\%$$\end{document} and \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\rho =0.7$$\end{document}, PPCA over concatenated modalities consistently provides better separation of identified clusters and higher alignment with the true group labels, than PPCA over every single modality (Figs. 3-4). Overall, multi-modal methods outperform single-modality methods in clustering tasks, with GPCCA (\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\lambda = 2/3$$\end{document}) achieving the best performance, followed by GPCCA (\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\lambda = 1/2$$\end{document}), MOFA, and PPCA-123 (PPCA applied to concatenated data). For most methods, an increase in missing data leads to greater challenges in clustering. However, GPCCA demonstrates relatively stable performance across both complete and partial data. Additionally, as within-modality correlations (represented by \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\rho$$\end{document}) increase, clustering becomes more difficult for all methods. For low correlation (\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\rho = 0.3$$\end{document}), MOFA yields the best performance, as it does not account for correlations in the error covariance matrix. In contrast, for intermediate to high correlations (\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\rho> 0.5$$\end{document}), GPCCA with ridge regularization exhibits superior performance. In the example with \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$20\%$$\end{document} missing rate and \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\rho =0.7$$\end{document} (Fig. 4), GPCCA (\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\lambda = 2/3$$\end{document}) achieves an ARI of \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$0.829$$\end{document} and a \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$0.838$$\end{document}, identifying clusters that align well with the true clusters. In contrast, MOFA achieves an ARI of \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$0.664$$\end{document} and a NMI of 0.711, unable to distinguish between clusters C2 and C5, or C1 and C6. In Case C, we analyze data with MNAR patterns, specifically focusing on modality-wise missingness. In this scenario, certain subjects lack data for an entire modality, and the probability of missingness depends on an unobserved hidden variable. This setup reflects real-world challenges where the availability of data for a modality is influenced by certain latent factors, such as logistical constraints or inherent biological characteristics. For instance, in biomedical research, the presence of specific omics data may depend on the subject’s condition or resource availability. In contrast, MOFA (ARI \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$= 0.613$$\end{document}, NMI \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$= 0.654$$\end{document}) and PPCA (ARI \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$= 0.504$$\end{document}, NMI \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$= 0.522$$\end{document}) identify only four clusters. This case highlights the robustness of GPCCA in clustering analysis under the MNAR condition, where its model assumption is violated.

In Case D, we analyze data exhibiting across-modality correlations (i.e., correlations between features in different modalities), which violates the assumption of a block diagonal covariance matrix in GPCCA. In this scenario, GPCCA (\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\lambda = 2/3$$\end{document}) achieves the best performance, followed by GPCCA (\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\lambda = 1/2$$\end{document}), MOFA, and PPCA-123 (Fig. 2d and and Supplementary Fig. S4d). Compared to Case A, both GPCCA and MOFA demonstrate stable clustering performance even in the presence of across-modality correlations. In summary, the simulation study highlights GPCCA’s accuracy and robustness in clustering observations from multi-modality data. Although the model is developed under the assumptions of normal data and an MAR mechanism, it exhibits strong potential for extension to more complex scenarios involving heavy-tailed data or an MNAR mechanism.

To assess the contributions of intra-modality correlation modeling and ridge regularization in the GPCCA framework, we conduct an ablation study. For each simulated dataset, we apply two ablated versions of the GPCCA model: one where the error covariance matrix is constrained to be strictly diagonal (denoted as GPCCA-0), and another where the ridge regularization term is removed from the objective function (denoted as GPCCA-1). We compare these two variants with the full GPCCA model (with regularization parameter \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\lambda$$\end{document} = 2/3) using both ARI (Supplementary Fig. S9) and NMI (Supplementary Fig. S10) as evaluation metrics. The results indicate that the full GPCCA model, which includes both intra-modality correlation modeling and ridge regularization, consistently achieves the best overall performance across various data settings. In particular, omitting ridge regularization substantially degrades model performance in both complete and MNAR scenarios. Meanwhile, modeling intra-modality correlation further enhances performance under diverse MCAR conditions.

Application to multi-omics data

In this section, we analyze multi-omics data from The Cancer Genome Atlas (TCGA) database. We select \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$10$$\end{document} cancer types, each with three modalities: gene expression, DNA methylation, and miRNA expression (see Methods). For dimensionality reduction, we apply PPCA to individual modalities and PPCA, GPCCA, MOFA, and DGCCA to multi-modality data. For each method and cancer type, we perform Louvain clustering on the learned low-dimensional embeddings. Since no ground truth subject groups are available, we use distinction between survival time from phenotype data as a surrogate measure of cluster quality. SNF and NEMO are excluded from this study due to their lack of low-dimensional embeddings, which prevents downstream evaluations as detailed below.

First, we perform two tests to evaluate whether there is significant difference in survival time between the identified subject clusters. The log-rank test is performed to directly evaluate survival differences between clusters, and likelihood-ratio test (LRT) based on the Cox regression model is used to evaluate survival differences after adjusting for two covariates: subject gender and age (at initial diagnosis). The P-values computed for all tested methods are adjusted by the Benjamini-Hochberg method. Using the log-rank test, GPCCA identifies the most significant survival differences, reporting \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$9$$\end{document} out of \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$10$$\end{document} cancer types with P-values below the significance threshold of \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$0.05$$\end{document} (Fig. 7a). PPCA applied to gene expression data (PPCA-1) leads to the second largest number of significant survival differences (\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$8$$\end{document} out of \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$10$$\end{document}). To further investigate the latent factors identified by GPCCA, we focus on those that are statistically significant in the Cox models. Our goal is to compare gene features with varying loadings on these factors. For each cancer type, we identify the top \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$100$$\end{document} genes from each significant factor based on the absolute values of their coefficients in the loading matrix. The union of these genes forms the “informative gene set”. Conversely, we construct the “background gene set” by taking the complement of the top \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$100$$\end{document} genes within each factor and computing their intersection across all factors. To compare these gene sets, we examine their overlap with genes in the OncoSearch database [41], which catalogs gene-cancer relationships by querying Medline abstracts. We find that the informative gene set generally exhibits greater overlap with OncoSearch-recorded genes than the background gene set (Table 3), both in absolute numbers and relative proportions. This result supports the hypothesis that genes with higher absolute loadings in GPCCA’s latent factors are more closely linked to cancer progression. In addition to GPCCA, we applied the same downstream analyses to MOFA and PPCA, the two other methods capable of learning feature loadings on latent factors (Supplementary Tables S4–S5). MOFA’s results show a similar trend to GPCCA: informative gene sets tend to have greater overlap with genes recorded in OncoSearch than background gene sets. However, this trend is not evident in the results from PPCA. At the same time, its background gene sets report the fewest (\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$10.0$$\end{document}) and the lowest proportion (\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$21.95\%$$\end{document}) of overlapping genes, indicating stronger specificity in feature selection. These results highlight the biological interpretability and relevance of the latent factors identified by GPCCA. Additionally, we compare GPCCA’s gene sets with those in the OncoDB database [42], which catalogs differentially expressed genes between normal and tumor samples. Across all cancer types, the informative gene set consistently exhibits smaller P-values than the background gene set (Supplementary Fig. S16), which further confirms that the latent factors identified by GPCCA capture biologically relevant gene markers for different cancer types.

Discussion

In this article, we introduce the GPCCA model for joint dimensionality reduction and multi-modal data integration. GPCCA learns low-dimensional embeddings of observations while preserving both shared and complementary information across modalities, leveraging cross-modal relationships to enhance downstream clustering analysis. It extends the classical CCA method in several ways. First, it formulates data integration within a probabilistic framework. Second, it generalizes to more than two modalities, enabling seamless integration of diverse data types. Third, it treats missing values as latent variables within the model, eliminating the need for separate imputation as a preprocessing step. We evaluate GPCCA through comprehensive simulation studies and two real-world applications. In the analysis of multi-view image data of handwritten digits, evaluations based on ground truth labels demonstrate GPCCA’s superior performance compared to alternative methods. In the application to multi-omics TCGA data, although ground truth subject subgroups are not available, our analysis shows that GPCCA effectively identifies patient groups with differential survival rates across cancer types. Furthermore, the learned latent factors reveal informative gene features closely associated with cancer, highlighting the model’s potential for biomedical discovery.

The development of GPCCA is motivated in part by the prevalence of missing values in real-world multi-modal data. Estimation of GPCCA is achieved through the proposed EM algorithm, which assumes data are missing at random (MAR). However, recognizing that real data often exhibit missing not at random (MNAR) patterns, we assess GPCCA’s robustness when the MAR assumption is violated. In both simulations (Case C) and real data applications, we specifically examine modality-wise missingness, a common scenario in biological and biomedical studies where the presence of a modality depends on latent, unobserved factors. Across multiple datasets with MNAR, GPCCA consistently demonstrates strong performance in identifying meaningful clusters based on the learned latent factors, suggesting its ability to handle complex missing patterns.

To further assess the generalizability of GPCCA in more challenging scenarios such as the analysis of spatial transcriptomics data, we perform an additional comparison based on a human dorsolateral prefrontal cortex (DLPFC) dataset (see Methods). For each slice in the dataset, we treat the normalized gene expression levels as one modality and the image features extracted from the histology image as the other modality. We apply GPCCA and alternative methods (PPCA, MOFA, SNF, NEMO, and DGCCA) to this multi-modal dataset to infer clusters, which we then compare with the six annotated spatial layers. The clustering performance of these methods measured in ARI and NMI scores are summarized in Supplementary Tables S6–S7, respectively. While GPCCA achieves top or near-top performance in most cases, we observe that all methods exhibit relatively modest clustering accuracy across the four slices. This is likely due to the fact that spatial coordinates, which have been shown to be highly informative in spatial transcriptomics analyses, are not utilized during data integration. Developing such extensions of probabilistic multi-modal models represents a promising direction for future research in spatial transcriptomics data integration.

Despite the advantages discussed, we acknowledge several additional limitations of the current work that point to potential directions for future improvements and extensions. First, the computational cost of the EM algorithm increases with data dimensions, primarily due to the matrix inversion required for the error covariance matrix. While we employ block-wise inversion and other numerical techniques to mitigate this issue, runtime remains a challenge on high-dimensional data with missing values. For example, Supplementary Fig. S17 summarizes the computational efficiency of GPCCA compared with alternative methods in representative studies. To address this, we suggest that users consider a filtering step for modalities with too many features (e.g., the DNA methylation modality in the TCGA data) by selecting the most variable features for integration. If the sample correlations between features are low within modalities, we can relax the block-diagonal assumption to a strict-diagonal structure, which is also implemented in our R package. To further enhance computational efficiency, we could explore low-rank approximations of the covariance matrix, adopt a generalized EM algorithm where the error covariance matrix is updated less frequently once it stabilizes, or move toward a variational inference framework that replaces explicit matrix inversion with more efficient operations such as Cholesky factorization or conjugate gradient methods. Second, in our current applications of GPCCA to real data, we set the ridge regularization parameter \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\lambda$$\end{document} to \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$0.5$$\end{document}, which has demonstrated good performance across a variety of simulation settings. However, in some applications, it may be beneficial to adaptively select \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\lambda$$\end{document} using grid search or other methods. Identifying a robust measure for this selection via cross-validation or other model selection techniques remains an important avenue for future work. Third, GPCCA assumes that data follows a multivariate normal distribution. Although many datasets can be well-approximated by normal distributions after appropriate transformations, and our simulations have demonstrated GPCCA’s robustness to heavy-tailed data (Case B), this assumption may limit its applicability to data modalities that significantly deviate from normality. A promising direction for future work is to extend GPCCA’s probabilistic framework to accommodate non-Gaussian distributions, such as binary or count data, to enhance its versatility. Fourth, recent methodological advances have extended CCA to accommodate multi-modal data across multiple biological conditions. For instance, TSCCA [43] generalizes sparse CCA to identify both shared and cancer-specific miRNA-gene associations across multiple cancer types. Similarly, sTPLS [44] integrates multiple pairs of datasets collected under different conditions to capture both common and condition-specific associations between features. While the current GPCCA framework is designed for data from a single condition, its probabilistic formulation provides a natural foundation for future extensions to more complex multi-condition settings. In summary, while GPCCA demonstrates strong potential, these limitations also present exciting opportunities for further refinement, which could broaden its applicability and enhance its performance across a wide range of data types and use cases.
