Major genes and phenotyping of indigenous chickens of the western highlands of Cameroon
Authors: Blaise Arnaud Hako Touko, Janvier Dorian Pouani, Gwladys Laure Medontem, Vanessa Audrey Tagny Nebo, Shey Dogmis Yoniwo, Nadine Jeutsop Voutsa, Diana Muluh, Ngintendem Tangomo Armel, Yacouba Manjeli
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
The Western highlands of Cameroon (WHC) are known for their diverse indigenous chicken phenotypes (ICW), making it difficult to report their performance and adaptability attributes accurately. A study was conducted on 828 adult ICWs sampled from the Noun and Menoua administrative divisions, representing 61% of the surface area of the WHC. The study developed a standardised method for identifying and recording the chickens' traits, including body weight, feather and appendage colour, and associated genes. Based on this data, the chickens were grouped into various "morphotypes" based on their gene combinations. One hundred twenty-five morphotypes were identified and recorded with informative Identifying ID codes. The study found that the significant genes affecting morphotype assignment included two multiallelic genes for plumage colour (10 alleles), plumage colour modifiers (7 genes), plumage colour dilution and enhancers (6 genes), plumage type and distribution (10 genes), comb type (4 genes), comb colour (1 gene), skin, tissue, and shank colour (4 genes). Additionally, ICW body weight varied from light to heavy and was affected by sex and localities (P ≤ 0.05). ICW are primarily medium size including 15% of cocks and 19% of hens in Menoua versus 20% cocks and 47% of hens in the Noun. Heavier chickens are more frequent in Menoua (23% and 7.4% for cocks and hens) than in Noun (2.7% and 11%), whereas Medium and light weight are more frequent in Noun. The study also revealed that the MC1R locus was the most frequent plumage colour determinant (92%), with the autosomal red plumage (E*WH) being the most commonly observed (17.8%) in the Noun division. The black plumage (E) was the most represented (13.10%) in the Menoua division. Overall, this study provided a standardised identification system for indigenous chickens in the WHC and an original methodology for their knowledge and record system.

Introduction

Local chicken populations are traditionally reared in diverse agro-ecological systems across the globe, displaying a remarkable variety of traits. These chickens are predominantly characterised by their smaller body size and significant phenotypic diversity, particularly evident in the wide range of plumage colours that serve as their primary distinguishing features (Temoa et al., 2025). In many developing countries, indigenous chickens hold substantial significance for smallholder families, providing crucial sources of income, meat, eggs, and contributing to various social and ritual practices (Tribudi et al., 2025). In Cameroon, the country's diverse geographical landscape is classified into five distinct agro-ecological zones, which transition from the dense humid forests in the southern regions to the semi-arid Sahel area in the northern part of the country. This ecological diversity plays a vital role in shaping the local chicken populations, influencing their characteristics and the systems in which they are reared (Keambou et al., 2014).

Cameroon's poultry sector has experienced significant growth in production over the years. The national poultry herd and annual meat production increased from 70,180,000 heads and 97,100 tonnes of meat in 2010 to 94 million and 137,920 tonnes in 2016 (INS, 2019). The increase in yearly egg production from 58,000 to 80,000 metric tons between 2010 and 2016 demonstrates the importance of poultry to Cameroon's food security and revenue generation. However, a closer look at the poultry-egg value chain reveals that the industry has relied heavily on imported commercial strains of chicken. According to Food and Agriculture Organisation of the United States report (2022), Cameroon imported chicken meat worth USD 2.82 million while exports were a mere USD 1.286 million in 2020. The disparity continued with fertile egg imports totalling USD 5.5 million, starkly contrasted by just USD 3.96 thousand from table egg exports. Furthermore, the country faced significant live bird imports valued at thousands, with no exports to offset this dependency. This imbalance led to a daunting negative trade balance of USD-7 million (approximately XAF 4.3 billion) during a time when the COVID-19 pandemic heightened food security concerns. This data indicates that local poultry genetic resources failed to meet the high national demand for poultry meat and eggs.

One of the reasons for the failure of the local poultry sector in Cameroon is the poor production performance of indigenous chicken populations and the inadequate management of breeding stocks in low input/output systems vulnerable to severe climatic conditions, disease outbreaks and predators (Fosa et al., 2007; Haoua et al., 2015a). The shift of market and consumption trends towards imported breeds and commercial trends has further exposed the national poultry sector to global inflation and economic crises due to the exclusive dependence on imports of improved breeding stocks and fertile eggs for day-old chick production (FAO, 2022). The recent COVID-19 pandemic and the ongoing Russo-Ukrainian and the Israelo-Palestinian war (MINFI, 2023) have also tested the stability of livestock food systems in developing countries, including Cameroon, exposing the fragility of the extraverted livestock sector. This has highlighted the need to create sustainable strategies towards enhancing the sovereignty of the Indigenous poultry sector, which is a source of cultural identity and legacy for local communities.

Therefore, improving the productivity of the poultry sector could increase the national meat consumption, which currently stands below 13 kg per capita per year (FAO, 2022). To promote the self-determination of Cameroon's economy, the government has adopted the import-export substitution policy, including in the livestock production sector (MINFI, 2023) and unlocking the genetic potential of Indigenous chicken of Cameroon (ICC) is considered a coherent alternative to enhance the national import substitution policy. Previous research studies have been carried out on ICs during the past two decades, including phenotypic and production systems characterization (Keambou et al., 2007) genetic diversity characterization (Fotsa et al., 2011), productive and adaptability traits evaluation on stations (Hako et al., 2009), and low/high susceptibility to Newcastle disease (Hako et al., 2015 and 2021). Complementarily, these researches revealed the tremendous genetic diversity and potential for genetic improvement programmes. However, communicating the phenotypic diversity information of the local chicken ecotypes was quite challenging given the high variability of phenotypic traits, as evidenced by various plumage colours and their combinations, as well as other significant genes and their interactions influencing plumage structure and distribution. This research aims to develop a standardized protocol for the description and recording of genetic resources of ICs for genetic research as well as improvement and conservation perspectives.

Material and methods

Characteristics of the study areas

This research was conducted in the Menoua and Noun divisions, located in the western highlands of Cameroon (Fig. 1). The Menoua division has a population density of 270 inhabitants per km2, covers an area of 1380 km2, and is made up of six subdivisions, namely Dschang, Fongo-Tongo, Nkong-ni, Fokoué, Santchou, and Penka-Michel. The Menoua region shares its boundaries with the Bamboutos region (West region) to the north, the Lebialem region to the west (Southwest region), the Haut-Nkam (Western region) and Moungo (Littoral region) regions to the south, and both the Mifi and Haut-Plateaux regions (West region) to the east. On the other hand, the Noun region covers approximately 52% of the West region with a surface area of 7687 km2 and a density of 57 inhabitants per km2. It comprises nine subdivisions: Bangourain, Foumban, Foumbot, Kouoptamo, Koutaba, Magba, Malentouen, Massangam, and Njimom. The Noun region is bordered to the north by both Mayo-Banyo (Adamawa region) and Donga-Mantung (Northwest region) regions, to the west by Bui and Ngoketunja regions (Northwest region), Bamboutos, Mifi and Khoung khi regions (West region), to the east by Mbam et Kim region (Central Region), and finally, to the south by the Nde region (West region).Fig. 1Map of the study area.Fig 1:

Sampling and experimental procedure

A total of 830 adult cocks and hens of the ICW were sampled based on their morphotype from 257 households, including 422 ICW in Menoua and 408 ICs in the Noun. In the same household, all the morphotypes present were sampled, and only one hen or cock for the same morphotype was considered. Each bird was snapped using a smartphone with a GPS coordinate application, and the corresponding picture code was assigned for the metadata of the same bird, including the live weight and the phenotypic description of the chicken.

A particular combination of major genes of a chicken was considered a morphotype. Data analysis

Qualitative and quantitative data were keyed into Microsoft Excel software and later imported into and analysed using SPSS software (Statistical Package for Social Sciences) version 21.0. Descriptive statistics were used to present the frequency and distribution of the ICW samples according to the locality and plumage colour. The Chi-square test was used to test the association between locality and plumage colour. In contrast, a student test for independent samples was used to compare the live body weight mean according to sex. All the statistical tests were done at 5% significance level.

The body weight of the local chickens of the Western Highlands of Cameroon is similar to that reported in South Africa in Potchefstroom Koekoek (Tadelle and Fasil, 2016), Naked neck, Venda chicken (Manyelo et al., 2020) and Boschveld chicken (Okoro et al., 2017). Similar body weights were also reported in Indonesia in White, Lurik, Wareng, and Ranupane chickens (Suyatno et al., 2023), Kedu and Balanese (Rofii et al., 2018), Kolaka (Nafui et al., 2020), and Gorontalo (Sophian et al., 2021). Lower body weights were observed by Keambou (2007) and Fotsa et al. (2011) in Cameroon, Yapi-Gnaoré et al. (2010) in Côte d’Ivoire, Bembide et al. (2013) in the Central African Republic, Ovambo in South Africa (Manyelo et al., 2020), in Indonesia in Nunukan, Merawang, Kalimantan, and Java chickens (Tribudi et al., 2025), and Kedu Hitam, Gaga, and Pelung chickens (Maharani et al., 2021).

It is well known that lower live weight is typical of local chickens, also known worldwide as indigenous or traditional chickens. Their smaller size is crucial for their environmental fitness, survival and socioeconomical functions. Unlike exotic breeds, which have been selectively bred for rapid growth and high egg production, local chickens are unselected outbred populations. They are more resilient and better suited to their environments (Bembide et al., 2024; Manyelo et al., 2020). Their smaller size and later maturity rates mean they require fewer resources, making them ideal for low-input farming systems, especially in rural areas where access to feed and veterinary care may be limited. Moreover, the disease resistance of these chickens is an invaluable trait, as it reduces dependency on medication and lowers production costs. Local chickens are often more adaptable to local climatic conditions and can thrive on a varied diet of foraged foods, which contributes to their sustainability (Hako and Yoniwo, 2023a and b).

An increased of live weight is observed in this study and in recent ones (Djitie et al., 2023 and 2021) as compared to live weights from earlier studies. These could be the result of improved management conditions as well as traditional selective breeding efforts by IC keepers. Besides, crossbreeding of ICWs and improved exotic ones is a determining factor of body Weight increase (Djitie et al., 2021).

Ten significant genes, including six previously described genes and three described for the first time, were identified within ICW (Fig. 2). The seven genes previously described are typical recessive "Normal" feathering (N) and recessive vulture hook (VH), and the incomplete dominants Naked neck (NA), feathered shank (PTI1, PTI2), frizzle (F), Crested (CR), and Muffs and beard (MB); (Fotsa et al., 2007 and 2011). The three significant genes described for the first time in Cameroon native chickens are the recessive frizzle modifier (FM) (Fig. 2-G, K, and L), the short tail feather (GT) (Fig. 2-H) and the dominant henny feathering (HF) in Fig. 2-G. The genetic characteristics of these significant genes have been clarified in recent studies. Locus GDF7 determines the Naked neck gene on Gallus gallus chromosome 3 (Fernandes et al., 2023). KRT75 determines the frizzle gene (Ng et al., 2012) on chromosome 34, whereas another gene, the frizzle modifier gene, inhibits or attenuates frizzle's expression at the KRT75 locus, which was earlier reported (Somes, 1990a). The crested gene is also located on chromosome 34 but at HOXC10 Locus (Li et al., 2021). Two allele series are involved in the feathered shank expression. PTI1 on chromosome 15 at the TBX5 locus (Bortoluzzi et al., 2020) and PTI2 on chromosome 13 at the PITX1 locus (Li et al., 2020). Variation in regional feather arrangement is under cellular and molecular control. According to Headon (2019), the HOX gene encodes a set of related transcription factors that regulate gene expression in a manner that defines a cell’s position along the head-to-tail axis of the animal. Each transcription factor is a protein that binds to specific DNA sequences to control nearby genes' expression by increasing or decreasing their synthesis rate. So is the case for Muff-and bear “MB” with HOXB7 and HOXB8 at the HOXB cluster (Guo et al., 2016), Crested “CR” with the HOXC cluster and associated with the expression of HOXC8 gene (Wang et al., 2012). The mutation of HOX genes leads to homeotic transformation in which a body part is found in the incorrect location (Headon, 2019). This could be another possible molecular pathway for the feathered shank phenotype. Concordantly, Domyan et al. (2016) reported that the change in gene expression, either in isolation or in combination, causes the skin cells of the leg to develop as wing cells, thus developing feathers instead of scales in chicken and pigeon embryos.

Feathering colour

The plumage colour and patterns proved to be the most diversified phenotypic characteristics of the ICW. Primary colour-based morphotypes are the most abundant numerically, with 52 morphotypes at the MC1R locus, also called E-locus (6 alleles), and one morphotype at the TYR locus (two alleles). The MC1R alleles described (Fig. 3) are extended black (*E), black restriction (E*R), dominant wheaten (E*WH), recessive wheaten (E*Y), brown (E*B), and wild (E*N). The TYR alleles are recessive white (C*N) and the regular dominant (*C). The typical wild plumage colour (E*N) was the most abundant (29.6%), followed by dominant wheaten (E*WH, 23.2¨%), then light brown (E*B) with 17.6%, restricted black (8%) and Extended black (7.2%). The mode of action of gene modifiers was clarified (Olori, 2019). I and I*D alleles inhibit the expression of black colour by blocking the production of melanin pigment in the feathers. The I*S allele allows little melanin production giving the plumage a smoky appearance. A 9bp insertion of three amino acids in exon 10 of PMEL17 protein is associated with the dominant white allele “I”. the dun “I*D” is associated with five amino acids deletion in the trans membrane region while the smoky “I*S” in addition to the 9bp insertion observed in dominant white “I”, is the consequence of the loss of four amino acids from the PMEL17 protein due to a 12bp deletion in exon 6 (Kerje et al., 2004).

Comb type

The most common comb type observed among the ICWs morphotypes was the typical "normal" single comb, comprising 87.3% of the study subjects (Fig. 8, A-E). The variations of the dominant alleles for these loci, along with their interactions, are responsible for the Pea and Rose comb types (Headon, 2015). The Rose comb is represented by the codominant allele R (0.8%, Fig. 8 E, F), while the Pea comb is represented by allele P (0.8%, Fig. 8 G). Their interaction results in the Walnut comb (RP, 0.8%, Fig. 8 I, J), along with other types such as the V-shape (1.6%, Fig. 9 K) and Buttercup (0.8%, Fig. 8 L, M). These comb types illustrate a variable expression level of significant mutant genes, which account for 4% of the comb types described in this study (Fig. 8 N, O, P, Q, R). Previous research into chicken factorial genetics has shown that the single comb is linked to recessive alleles R*N and P*N at the MNR2 locus (Wright, 2009) and the SOX5 locus (Imsland et al., 2012). The three primary variants—single, rose, and duplex combs—are the result of extensive structural mutations that affect the expression of a transcription factor in comb skin. Meanwhile, the V-shape and Buttercup comb types arise from tandem duplications at the CMC1 and EOMES loci (Dorshorst et al., 2015).

Shanks, skin and tissue colours

The shank, skin and tissue colours of the ICW are presented on Fig. 9. The shank's skin presented the highest colour variety, including the white (50.40%), the yellow (29.60%), followed by black (11.20%), then green and pink (3.20% each) and finally grey (2.40%). These results conform with findings from Hutt (1930), Keambou et al. (2007), Fotsa et al. (2007), Bembide et al. (2013), and Haoua et al. (2015a), who showed that the most common tarsus colourations in Galliformes populations are white, yellow and black.

The CL8X coding system for chickens

A total of 125 morphotypes were identified among the 829 ICW, resulting in a frequency distribution of 15.08%. The Menoua region accounted for 65.6% of these 125 morphotypes. Notably, several major genes were newly reported in Cameroonian chicken populations, particularly in Noun and Menoua divisions with their morphotype list and code established (Supplementary material). These include the frizzle modifier gene (Morphotypes 85, 86, 87, and 88), the henny feathering gene (Morphotype 92), the breda gene or combless trait (Morphotypes 35 and 97), and a new muted variant comb (Morphotype 116). The presence of major morphological mutations, as evidenced by Tixier-Boichard (2020), along with their combinations in indigenous chicken populations, helps to explain the diversity of morphotypes observed. Somes and Hutt described the frizzle modifier gene in their work (Somes, 1990; Hutt, 1930 and 1936), which enhances the aesthetic appearance of frizzle feathering. Unmodified homozygous frizzles are characterized by curled feathers along both the rachis and the barbs, leading to a loss of the standard flat vane. This results in single spike feathers that often break off or are molted. Modified frizzling can sometimes exhibit such minimal frizzing that it closely resembles normal feathering. As a result, local poultry farmers tend to systematically reject homozygous frizzles, making the frizzle modifier gene a promising candidate for further study.

In the context of this study, it is essential to acknowledge the reliance on visible phenotypes to infer genotypes, a practice that, while practical in field settings, carries inherent risks of error. The absence of molecular genotyping raises concerns about the accuracy of morphotype classification, potentially leading to misclassification of phenotypes. Additionally, this approach may limit our ability to detect polygenic traits or modifier effects that influence the observed morphotypes. Therefore, we recommend future validation of our findings using molecular tools, which would enhance the robustness of our genetic assessments and lead to more accurate interpretations of phenotypic diversity in these chicken populations.

Conclusions

Factorial genetics and phenotyping play a crucial role in understanding the genetic basis of traits in indigenous chickens. The genetic diversity found in these chickens provides valuable opportunities for selective breeding. This study offers a protocol that effectively communicates information about the diverse phenotypes related to the variations in morphological mutant genes and their allele series in indigenous chickens. Such efforts are essential for preserving genetic resources for improvement and conservation purposes. Future research could shed light on the fizzle modifier gene and other potential mutants, which may help address cultural taboos by enhancing the physical appearance of carriers while preserving their economically essential traits for breeding in challenging climate conditions
