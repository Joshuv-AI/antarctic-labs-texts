Metabolite profiling to evaluate metabolic changes in drought-tolerant transformant (Agb0103) under salt stress
Authors: Gyeong-Min Lee, Sung-Dug Oh, Ye-Jin Jang, Jong-Chan Park, Hyun-Min Park, Seon-Woo Oh, Sang-Gu Lee, Nam-Jin Chung, Soo-Yun Park, An-Cheol Chang
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTHigh soil salinity is one of the most damaging abiotic stresses affecting crop productivity by generating osmotic stress and ion toxicity. In this study, we investigated the metabolic responses of two transgenic rice (Agb0103) lines overexpressing CaMsrB2, L-8 (single-copy insertion) and L-23 (double-copy insertion) in comparison with the non-transgenic parental Ilmi cultivar under conditions involving salt-mediated stress (0, 75, 150, and 225 mM NaCl). Metabolite profiling was conducted using gas chromatography – time-of-flight mass spectrometry, targeting 63 hydrophilic and lipophilic compounds. Comparative analysis after treatment with 0 and 225 mM NaCl for 7 days revealed that the metabolites that showed significant differences included sugars, 10 amino acids, and 6 organic acids. The transgenic lines exhibited fewer changes in proline and γ-aminobutyric acid levels, which are known indicators of salt-induced leaf damage. Additionally, methionine content, which is likely influenced by CaMsrB2 expression, was altered under salt stress. Transgenic lines also accumulated higher levels of sugars, which contribute to reactive oxygen species scavenging, than Ilmi. Conversely, amino acids, such as asparagine, aspartic acid, glutamine, and glutamic acid, showed contrasting trends in the transgenic lines and Ilmi. These differences may be attributed to the roles of glutamic acid and glutamine in nitrogen metabolism and the involvement of glutamine synthetase in osmotic stress tolerance. Overall, this study provides foundational data for understanding the metabolic mechanisms underlying abiotic stress resistance in CaMsrB2-expressing rice and highlights the potential role of this gene in enhancing salt stress tolerance through metabolic regulation.

Introduction

The major abiotic stresses in plants include drought, cold, salinity, and heat, and saline soil conditions pose a significant threat to global food production.1,2 Soil salinity can lead to changes in osmotic pressure and ion imbalance, resulting in secondary stresses, such as metabolic damage and reduced early growth.3,4

For plants to tolerate salt stress, they must be able to mitigate the damage caused by salinity, restore homeostasis in stressful environments, and regulate growth delays induced by stress.5

Approximately 776 million tons of rice (Oryza sativa L.) are produced globally, with approximately 90% of production recorded in Asia.6 Rice is rich in nutrients, such as vitamins, essential amino acids, minerals, and dietary fiber, and plays a crucial role as a major food resource.7 However, rice is the most salt-sensitive crop compared to other major crops, such as wheat and barley.8 Currently, approximately 900 million hectares of land worldwide are affected by salinity,9 which poses a severe threat to food production. In this context, the development of salt-tolerant rice varieties is critical for ensuring food security.

Plants experience oxidative stress because of an imbalance between the production and removal of Reactive Oxygen Species (ROS). Methionine is one of the most oxidation-sensitive amino acids, and it can be oxidized to methionine sulfoxide (MetSO) but can be reduced by methionine sulfoxide reductase (Msr).10 Additionally, methionine plays a role in alleviating growth inhibition and oxidative damage caused by drought, rendering it an important amino acid for protecting plants from water scarcity.11 The inhibition or overexpression of the CaMsrB2 gene derived from pepper (Capsicum annuum) can control the accumulation and reduction of reactive oxygen species (ROS), respectively, thereby regulating pathogen defense responses and oxidative stress. MsrB2 is also involved in this regulation.12 Transgenic rice plants overexpressing the Capsicum annuum methionine sulfoxide reductase B2 (CaMsrB2) gene showed lower oxidative stress symptoms under abiotic stress, such as drought, than the control cultivar, Ilmi. This finding was attributed to the maintenance of chloroplast function, which resulted in increased tolerance. These plants exhibit strong drought resistance during both the seedling and maturation stages.13 Agb0103, a genetically modified rice strain harboring the CaMsrB2 gene and exhibiting drought tolerance, was developed by the Rural Development Administration (RDA) of Korea.1 In drought treatment experiments, T-DNA insertion lines with a single copy (L-8) and double copy (L-23) showed stress tolerance in the order of Ilmi (wild type), L-8, and L-23, both physiologically and photochemically.14 Furthermore, transgenic rice cultivars overexpressing CaMsrB2 exhibit enhanced salt tolerance at both physiological and photochemical levels. Specifically, these plants exhibited enhanced photosynthetic efficiency, reduced ROS accumulation, and higher growth rates under saline conditions compared to wild-type controls. These results provide phenotypic and functional validation that CaMsrB2 expression confers salt stress tolerance in rice.1

Omics technologies, such as metabolomics, transcriptomics, and genomics, aim to comprehensively characterize biological systems. Metabolomics combines analytical chemistry and bioinformatics to comprehensively analyze and characterize low-molecular-weight metabolites. Metabolomics analysis is typically performed by separating compounds using Liquid Chromatography (LC) or Gas Chromatography (GC) coupled with Mass Spectrometry (MS).15 Metabolomics investigates the correlations between the concentrations of various metabolites and provides insights into their interrelationships. Plant metabolic products are essential for understanding the biosynthetic pathways related to growth and defense, and they respond differently to environmental stresses, such as drought.16 Multivariate statistical analyses such as Principal Component Analysis (PCA) and partial least squares discriminant analysis (PLS-DA) are commonly used for classification based on various biological states, qualities, and geographical factors, and to extract and interpret information from multivariate biological systems.17,18 Metabolite changes serve as indicators of cellular regulation processes. Under salt stress, osmotic regulation in rice is crucial, with metabolites such as proline, glycine, GABA, and sugars playing significant roles in salt-sensitive and salt-tolerant varieties.3,19

Salt affects rice growth throughout its growth stages by reducing shoot and root growth, with the seedling stage being particularly sensitive.20,21 In this study, we aimed to further elucidate the metabolic mechanisms underlying salt tolerance in transgenic rice expressing CaMsrB2. Comprehensive metabolomic analyses were performed under various salinity conditions to identify key hydrophilic and lipophilic metabolites involved in stress adaptation. This approach complements existing physiological studies and provides new insights into the metabolic network underlying CaMsrB2-mediated salt tolerance.

Materials and Methods

Plant Materials

The CaMsrB2 transgenic rice variety used in this study was generated by introducing the CaMsrB2 gene derived from pepper (Capsicum annuum) into the pSB11-RbMSR vector using Agrobacterium-mediated transformation of wild-type Ilmi rice. The Bar gene encoding phosphinothricin acetyltransferase was used as a selection marker.13 Two CaMsrB2 transgenic lines, L-8 (single copy) and L-23 (double copy), were selected based on T-DNA insertion and used in the experiment.22 The seeds used in the experiment were derived from wild-type Ilmi rice and the L-8 and L-23 lines, which were provided by the National Institute of Agricultural Sciences.

Salt Treatment Experiment

The seeds were sown in Petri dishes (125 mm × 125 mm × 20 mm) and germinated at 28°C for 7 days. After washing the potting soil three times, the seeds were transplanted into 50-cell pots and grown in the National Agricultural Research Center GMO greenhouse (25°C) for 2 weeks before being used for the experiment. The gradual-increase method with NaCl concentrations of 0, 75, 150, and 225 mM was employed.1,23 On days 0 and 4, 5 L of the NaCl solution was used to irrigate rectangular plastic boxes (550 mm × 380 mm × 205 mm). Leaves and stems were collected on days 3, 5, and 7 of treatment. Sampling was performed in triplicate for each experimental group, and the samples were rapidly frozen in liquid nitrogen and freeze-dried at −80°C for 5 days using a freeze dryer (FD8518, Iishinbiobase Co., Gyeonggi-do, Korea). The samples were then processed into powder form using a planetary ball mill (Pulverisette 6, Fritsch, Idar-Oberstein, Germany) and stored in an ultra-low freezer at −80°C (DF8524, Iishinbiobase Co., Gyeonggi-do, Korea).

Extraction of Hydrophilic Metabolites

The hydrophilic metabolites were extracted, as described by Jung et al.24 A total of 10 mg of rice leaf powder stored at −80°C was placed in a 2-mL tube. To this sample, 1 mL of a methanol:water:chloroform (2.5:1:1, v/v/v) solution and 60 µL of the internal standard (IS) adonitol (200 ppm) were added. The mixture was incubated at 37°C with shaking at 1,200 rpm (161 × g) for 30 min using a Thermomixer (model 5355, Eppendorf AG, Hamburg, Germany). The sample was then centrifuged at 4°C, 13,290 rpm (16,000 × g) for 3 min using a centrifuge (MX-301, Tomy, Tokyo), and the supernatant (800 µL) was separated and transferred to a new tube. To the separated supernatant, 400 µL of H₂O was added, followed by centrifugation at 4°C, 13,290 rpm (16,000 × g) for 3 min. The resulting supernatant (900 µL) was transferred into a new tube. The supernatant was then concentrated by centrifugation for 3 h, followed by freeze-drying for more than 15 h. For derivatization of the extract, 80 µL of Methoxyamine hydrochloride (MOX, 20 mg/mL) was added to the freeze-dried sample and reacted at 30°C, 1,200 rpm (161 × g) for 90 min. Subsequently, 80 µL of N-methyl-N-(trimethylsilyl) trifluoroacetamide (MSTFA) was added, and the reaction was carried out at 37°C, 1,200 rpm (161 × g) for 30 min. The derivatized samples were analyzed for hydrophilic metabolites using GC (Agilent 7890A, Agilent, USA) coupled with a Pegasus HT TOF mass spectrometer (LECO, USA). A 30 m × 0.25 mm silica column coated with 0.25 µm CP-SIL 8 CB (Varian Inc., USA) was used, with a split ratio of 1:25. The injection port temperature was set at 230°C, and the helium gas (He) flow rate was 1.0 mL/min. The oven temperature was initially set at 80°C for 2 min, then increased by 15°C per min until it reached 320°C, where it was maintained for 10 min. Hydrophilic metabolite identification was performed using the ChromaTOF software version 5.50 (Leco, USA) with an in-house library. Metabolite quantification was based on the peak area ratio relative to that of the IS determined using selected ions (Table S1). 24

Extraction of Lipophilic Metabolites

Extraction of lipophilic metabolites (policosanol, tocotrienols, and tocopherols) was performed according to the method described by Kim et al.17 A total of 20 mg of rice leaf powder stored at −80°C was placed in a 15-mL tube. After adding 3 mL of ethanol solution (0.1% ascorbic acid) and 0.05 mL of the internal standard (IS) 5α-cholestane (10 µg/mL), the mixture was reacted at 85°C in a water bath for 5 min. Then, 0.12 mL of KOH (80%, w/v) was added, and the reaction was continued at 85°C in the water bath for 10 min. The reaction tube was placed on ice for 5 min to stop the reaction, and then 1.5 mL of H₂O and 1.5 mL of hexane were added. After mixing for approximately 20 s, the mixture was centrifuged at 1,200 rpm (161 × g) for 5 min at 4°C. The upper hexane layer was transferred to a new tube. Another 1.5 mL of hexane was added, and the mixture was centrifuged using the same method. Approximately 3 mL of the hexane layer collected in the tube was evaporated using nitrogen gas and concentrated using a centrifuge. For derivatization of the extract, 30 µL of N-methyl-N-(trimethylsilyl) trifluoroacetamide (MSTFA) and 30 µL of pyridine were added, followed by incubation with shaking at 60°C and 1,200 rpm (161 × g) for 30 min. The derivatized samples were analyzed for metabolites by gas chromatography (GC; Agilent 7890A, Agilent, USA) coupled with a Pegasus HT TOF mass spectrometer (LECO, USA). A 30-m × 0.25-mm silica column coated with 0.25 µm CP-SIL 8 CB (Varian Inc., USA) was used, with a split ratio of 1:15. The injection port temperature was set to 230°C, and the helium gas flow rate was 1.0 mL/min. The oven temperature was initially set to 200°C for 2 min, then increased by 10°C per min to 310°C, where it was maintained for 10 min. Metabolite identification was performed using the ChromaTOF software version 5.50 (Leco, USA) with an in-house library (Table S2). The identified metabolites are reported based on the peak area ratio relative to the internal standard (IS).24 Qualitative and quantitative analyses were performed using standard calibration curves for each substance.

Statistical Analysis

All experiments were performed in triplicate, and a correlation analysis of the metabolites was conducted after normalizing the data using unit variance scaling. Multivariate statistical analysis was performed using the Soft Independent Modeling of Class Analogy (SIMCA) 12.0 software (Umetrics, Umea, Sweden), and PCA and PLS-DA were conducted. While PCA identifies the directions of overall variance, PLS-DA provides better discrimination between and within groups.25 The goodness of fit was represented by R2 Y, and its predictive ability was indicated by Q.2 An R2 Y value close to 1 suggests a good model fit, whereas a Q2 value above 0.5 indicates strong predictive power. Subsequently, the clustering patterns between the dependent variables (hydrophilic and lipophilic metabolites) and the independent variables were examined. The profiled metabolites were converted to Kyoto Encyclopedia of Genes and Genomes (KEGG) IDs and visualized using a KEGG pathway scatter plot in MetaboAnalyst 6.0 (http://metaboanalyst.ca). Pathway analysis was performed using log2-transformed fold-change (FC) values (log2 FC). The fold-change value of each variety reflected the differences in metabolites between the control group (0 mM NaCl) and the salt treatment group (225 mM NaCl). A t-test was performed using IBM SPSS Statistics 27.0 to test the significance of the differences between the means at a p-value of < 0.05.

Results and Discussion

Multivariate Analysis of Metabolites without Salt Treatment for Ilmi, l-8, and l-23 Leaves

Plants contain low-molecular-weight compounds, such as amino acids, carbohydrates, organic acids, and polyphenols, and their levels change in response to various biotic and abiotic environmental stimuli. By analyzing these metabolic responses, it is possible to infer the metabolic reactions and pathways triggered by specific factors.26,27

Multivariate analyses, such as principal component analysis (PCA) and partial least squares discriminant analysis (PLS-DA), are commonly used to reduce the dimensionality of complex datasets and visualize correlations between variables based on biological networks. In particular, PCA is effective in assessing differences in metabolite profiles among samples.28

The PCA results for the leaf samples on day 0 (prior to salt treatment) showed that the first two principal components (PC1 and PC2) explained 75.4% of the total variance (PC1:40.2%, PC2:35.2%) (Fig. 1a). The metabolite profiles clustered into three distinct groups. Notably, the L-8 and L-23 lines, which contained the bar gene as a selection marker, exhibited metabolic differences compared to the parental Ilmi line. According to Benevenuto et al.,29 the insertion of the bar gene in Arabidopsis leaves induces changes in the metabolic pathways related to carbohydrate, energy, and amino acid metabolism. Therefore, it is likely that the clustering patterns observed before the salt treatment were influenced by the presence of the bar, suggesting that its insertion caused discernible metabolic alterations relative to the parental line. Of the 63 identified metabolites, 48 were successfully mapped to KEGG IDs for pathway prediction. In the pathway enrichment analysis, larger red-colored circles represent pathways with higher significance (lower p-values). Among the pathways that differed between Ilmi and L-8, the most pronounced changes were observed in glycine, serine, and threonine metabolism, which are closely associated with plant abiotic stress tolerance. Glycine and serine play central roles in photorespiration and one-carbon metabolism, thereby contributing to redox homeostasis and stress-induced metabolic reprogramming.30,31 Threonine metabolism is interconnected with methionine and branched-chain amino acid biosynthesis, and the stress-induced accumulation of these amino acids has been reported to support osmotic adjustment and provide alternative energy substrates.32 Additional metabolic changes were also detected in the glyoxylate and dicarboxylate pathways, phenylalanine metabolism, cysteine and methionine metabolism, and alanine, aspartate, and glutamate metabolism, which have been implicated in oxidative stress regulation, osmoprotection, and nitrogen remobilization under abiotic stress.33,34 These alterations were likely influenced by the presence of the CaMsrB2 and bar genes, which may have contributed to the observed metabolic reprogramming under salt stress conditions (Figure 1(b)).

Similarly, when comparing the Ilmi and L-23 levels, glycine, serine, and threonine metabolism showed the most significant alterations. Changes were also observed in alanine, aspartate, and glutamate metabolism, glyoxylate and dicarboxylate pathways, cysteine, methionine, and phenylalanine metabolism (Figure 1(c)). These shifts were closely associated with amino acid, carbohydrate, and energy metabolism, consistent with the findings of Benevenuto et al.29

Multivariate Analysis of Metabolites on Salt Treatment for Ilmi, l-8, And l-23 Leaves

The PCA results for Ilmi, L-8, and L-23 at 0, 75, 150, and 225 mM NaCl on day 3 of the salt treatment explained 55.8% of the total variance (PC1, 35.5%; PC2, 20.3%) (Figure 2(a)). The score plot represents the distribution of the principal component scores for each sample, allowing grouping and differences between the data to be visualized. Based on PC1, L-23, L-8, and Ilmi showed clustering patterns. However, L-8 treated with 225 mM NaCl was distributed in the lower part of the graph based on PC2. The loading plot shows the contribution of each variable (component) to the principal components, allowing the analysis of the variables that had a significant impact on the data (Figure 2(b)). Considering the score and loading plots together, L-8 treated with 225 mM NaCl can be inferred as a metabolite that contributes to the clustering of amino acids such as glutamic acid, valine, proline, and asparagine. Glutamic acid is an active amino acid that plays a crucial role in plant metabolism and signaling, and can regulate growth and responses.35 Therefore, it is important to focus on the responses of glutamic acid, proline, and other metabolites that increase under salt stress. The overall PCA results for Ilmi, L-8, and L-23 at 0, 75, 150, and 225 mM NaCl on day 5 of the salt treatment explained 54.7% of the total variance (PC1, 34.2%; PC2, 20.5%) (Figure 2(c)). On day 3 of salt treatment, L-23 was distinctly clustered, whereas Ilmi and L-8 tended to cluster together. However, on day 5, Ilmi, L-8, and L-23 formed separate clusters. In the loading plot, L-23 was identified as having a high contribution to clustering owing to sugars such as mannose, ribose, and xylose (Figure 2(d)). Some sugars exhibited high levels even without NaCl treatment. Sugars protect cell membranes by removing excessive ROS generated by various stresses, act as osmoprotectants, and regulate plant growth and development.36 Drought- and salt-tolerant wheat varieties have been reported to accumulate more reduced forms of sugars, such as glucose and fructose, than salt-sensitive varieties, which function as osmotic protectors under abiotic stresses such as salt and drought.37 Therefore, the high sugar content of strain L-23 may contribute to its salt tolerance. Furthermore, according to Siddiqui et al,1 on day five of treatment, high concentrations of Ilmi resulted in a decrease in the chlorophyll content index and impaired photochemical efficiency.

The PCA results on day 7 of salt treatment showed that the two principal components explained 58.3% of the total variance (PC1, 36.1%; PC2, 22.2%) (Figure 2(e)). Based on the PC2, Ilmi was separated in the lower direction, whereas L-8 and L-23 were distributed in the upper direction. Notably, for Ilmi, the salt-treated groups were separated and distributed based on PC1. L-8, with a single copy of the T-DNA insertion, showed a distribution pattern similar to that of Ilmi in terms of grouping by salt treatment, with data points closer to those of L-23. This suggests that the transgenic lines L-8 and L-23, which express CaMsrB2 under salt stress, exhibited relatively higher photochemical efficiency and physiological performance than Ilmi. Moreover, this finding supports the conclusion that L-23 exhibits better salt tolerance.1 Upon examining the overlap of the loading plot with the metabolic compounds, we found that in Ilmi, the 150- and 225-mM NaCl treatment groups were associated with clustering attributed to metabolites, such as proline and threonine, whereas the 0- and 75-mM treatment groups showed that polycosanols, such as C20-ol and C22-ol, which are lipophilic metabolites, contribute to clustering (Figure 2(f)). These metabolites are involved in the salt stress-induced growth inhibition of Ilmi.

Multivariate Statistical Analysis of Metabolite Changes on Day 7 in Response to Salt Stress

PLS-DA was performed to investigate metabolite variations under different salt concentrations on day 7 after treatment. In the PLS-DA results for Ilmi under varying salt concentrations on day 7, the model showed an R2 Y of 0.627 and Q2 of 0.465 (Figure 3(a)). Based on the PLS1 axis, the high-salinity group formed a distinct cluster, which was primarily attributed to amino acids such as valine, proline, and isoleucine, as well as sugars including glucose, fructose, and galactose (Figure 3(b)). The levels of these metabolites are likely to increase because of elevated ROS levels under salt stress. Variable Importance in Projection (VIP) analysis identified key metabolites with a VIP > 1 that contributed to clustering: amino acids, such as asparagine, proline, alanine, and threonine, and organic acids, including citric acid, pyroglutamic acid, and glycolic acid (Figure 3(c)). Comparison and Analysis of the Metabolic Differences with Respect to Salt Concentrations on Day Seven of Salt Treatment

Heat maps are commonly used in plant metabolomics to visualize metabolite abundance across various samples and conditions. They enable rapid comparisons of metabolic profiles, highlight condition-specific alterations, and reveal clustering patterns between metabolites and samples. Using color-coded expression levels and hierarchical clustering, heat maps can facilitate the identification of metabolic trends and potential functional associations, particularly in studies related to plant stress responses, genetic variation, and development.38 Positive correlations are represented in red, and negative correlations are shown in blue. Consistent with the PCA and PLS-DA results, metabolites in the low-salinity (0 and 75 mM) and high-salinity (150 and 225 mM) groups were clearly clustered according to the salt treatment concentration. L-8 exhibited an intermediate metabolic profile, with a tendency to cluster between L-23 and Ilmi (Fig. 6). Similar to the PCA and PLS-DA results, metabolites from the low-salt (0 and 75 mM) and high-salt (150 and 225 mM) groups were clearly clustered according to the salt treatment concentration. L-8 showed an intermediate metabolic profile and tended to cluster between L-23 and Ilmi (Fig. 6). As observed in the PCA and PLS-DA results, L-8 and L-23 were positively correlated with sugars. To investigate salt tolerance-associated metabolic changes, the overall metabolic shifts were analyzed according to treatment concentration and duration. Based on this, a comparative analysis was conducted between the high- and low-concentration treatment groups on day 7.

Log₂ fold-change values between the 0 mM (control) and 225 mM (salt-treated) groups were calculated for each cultivar. Metabolites with Log 2 fold-change values were visualized using bar graphs. Higher levels of alanine, proline, isoleucine, threonine, 4-aminobutyric acid (GABA), lysine, and methionine were observed in the high-salt-treated groups of Ilmi and L-8 compared to L-23 (Fig. 7a). Proline and GABA are osmoprotectants involved in the regulation of cellular water stress.34 Proline exhibits multifunctional roles in osmotic adjustment, ROS scavenging, and stabilization of proteins and membranes,39 whereas GABA, synthesized through the GABA shunt pathway, contributes to stress signaling, redox balance, and association with the TCA cycle.40,41 Salt stress in rice leaves induces the accumulation of these metabolites, and while their exact roles remain unclear, their levels correlate with the extent of salt-induced damage. In the present study, proline and GABA levels were significantly elevated in the 225 mM treatment group in Ilmi and L-8, whereas L-23 showed minimal accumulation. Methionine, an amino acid susceptible to oxidation by reactive oxygen species (ROS), also increased under high-salinity conditions in L-8 and L-23 lines harboring the CaMsrB2 gene, which reduces methionine sulfoxide and thereby suppresses oxidative damage. Elevated methionine levels are associated with enhanced tolerance to abiotic stress.19 The synthesis of valine and isoleucine, which are branched-chain amino acids, can be induced via the regulation of the aspartic acid pathway alongside methionine. These branched-chain amino acids serve as osmoprotectants and alternative respiratory substrates under abiotic stress.33,42

In Ilmi, sugars such as fructose, mannose, glucose, and galactose showed higher accumulation in the 225 mM NaCl treatment group than in the 0 mM control (Fig. 7b). These sugars act as osmoprotectants, scavenge ROS, stabilize membranes, and support plant growth and development under stress.36 In contrast, the salt-tolerant lines L-8 and L-23 showed high sugar content even in the absence of salt stress, indicating that constitutive metabolic traits contribute to stress resilience.

Organic acids, such as oxalic acid, malic acid, glycolic acid, and phosphoric acid, showed decreased levels in the high-salinity treatment groups across all cultivars, with relatively smaller variations in L-8 and L-23 (Fig. 7c). Oxalic and malic acids are intermediates in the TCA cycle and play central roles in metabolic regulation, ATP synthesis, and abiotic stress responses.43 Although oxalic acid is not a direct precursor of aspartic acid, fluctuations in malate and oxaloacetate levels can influence the synthesis of aspartic acid and its derivative, asparagine. This may explain why under high-salinity conditions, aspartic acid and asparagine levels decreased in Ilmi but increased in L-8 and L-23.

Glutamine and glutamic acid levels also varied significantly in Ilmi but remained relatively stable in L-8 and L-23 (Figure 7(d)). Asparagine and glutamine serve as nitrogen storage and transport compounds, respectively, in plants. Glutamine, a central amino acid, modulates nitrate reductase activity and interacts with signaling pathways linked to nitrogen assimilation and stress responses.44 It is synthesized from glutamic acid and ammonium by glutamine synthetase (Figure 8). Nitrogen is essential for plant development, and glutamine synthetase plays a critical role in osmotic stress tolerance.45 The relatively low glutamine levels observed in Ilmi under salt stress suggest impaired nitrogen metabolism, which may contribute to its stress-sensitive phenotype. Overall, asparagine, glutamine, and glutamine synthetase are integral to nitrogen metabolism, including its storage and transport. Further investigation is warranted to explore the nitrogen metabolic pathways in CaMsrB2-transgenic lines under salt stress. Conclusion

This study demonstrated that transgenic rice lines L-8 and L-23 harboring the CaMsrB2 gene exhibited enhanced salt stress tolerance, as evidenced by their distinct metabolic profiles under high salinity conditions. Increased levels of metabolites such as GABA, proline, methionine, sugars, glutamic acid, and glutamine are associated with ROS suppression, osmotic adjustment, and activation of nitrogen metabolism. These responses were notably different from those observed in the non-transgenic control Ilmi plants. These findings provide foundational insights into the metabolic responses induced by genetic transformations and highlight the potential of specific gene expression in enhancing tolerance to abiotic stresses.
