Identification of risk factors and construction of a nomogram for predicting postoperative stone recurrence in patients with infective upper urinary tract stones
Authors: Geng-Geng Wei, Qing-Shan Yang, Hai-Rui Chen, Yu Yang, Jun-Feng Tang, Kristine J S Kwan, Zhen-Quan Lu, Lin Xiong, Xiang Xu
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundInfection-related upper urinary tract stones (struvite and/or carbonate apatite) are prone to early postoperative recurrence. This study aimed to identify predictors of 6-month recurrence and develop a pragmatic risk-prediction nomogram to support individualized follow-up.MethodsA retrospective single-center study was conducted in 148 patients with infection-related stones confirmed by infrared spectroscopy who underwent surgical removal between January 2019 and December 2023. Patients were randomly allocated to a modeling cohort (n = 104) and a validation cohort (n = 44) using a 7:3 non-stratified split (internal split-sample validation only). Candidate predictors and outcomes were complete for all patients, and no imputation was required. Recurrence within 6 months was defined as new stone formation or a > 2-fold increase in residual fragment size on ultrasound or plain computed tomography (CT). Multivariable logistic regression was used to identify independent predictors and to construct a nomogram. Model performance was assessed using discrimination, calibration, and decision curve analysis.ResultsIn the modeling group, the stone recurrence rate was 44.2% (n = 46/104), while in the validation group it was 70.5% (n = 31/44). Compared to non-recurrence cases, patients in the recurrence group had significantly higher rates of residual stones (72% vs. 22%, P < 0.001), renal atrophy (33% vs. 9%, P = 0.002), and positive urine cultures at six-month follow-up (39% vs. 3%, P = 0.008), as well as higher median CT values [821 HU (IQR 592–1003) vs. 511 HU (IQR 416–864), P = 0.004]. Multivariate logistic regression identified residual stones (OR = 6.19, 95% CI: 2.12–18.08, P = 0.001), positive urine culture during follow-up (OR = 9.71, 95% CI: 1.73–54.41, P = 0.010), and CT value (OR = 1.02, 95% CI: 1.00–1.04, P = 0.026) as independent predictors of recurrence. A nomogram constructed using these three factors showed strong predictive performance, with an area under curve of 0.86 (95% CI: 0.77–0.93) in the modeling group and 0.75 (95% CI: 0.59–0.91) in the validation group. Calibration curves demonstrated good agreement between predicted and observed recurrence risk, and decision curve analysis indicated favorable clinical utility of the model.ConclusionsResidual stones, positive follow-up urine culture, and higher CT attenuation values were identified as independent predictors of early recurrence after surgery for infection-related upper urinary tract stones. The nomogram showed acceptable internal performance but requires external validation.Keywords: Complication, Struvite stone, Urinary calculi, Urological procedure

Introduction

Infective upper urinary tract stones represent a distinct subset of urolithiasis, accounting for approximately 10% – 15% of cases [1]. These stones, primarily composed of struvite (magnesium ammonium phosphate hexahydrate) and carbonate apatite, are closely associated with urinary tract infections caused by urease-producing bacteria (e.g. Proteus mirabilis) [2]. Struvite stones are characterized by rapid postoperative recurrence, often within 4 to 6 weeks, even after complete stone removal [3]. In certain cases, recurrence can lead to staghorn calculi, contributing to increased morbidity, healthcare costs, and severe complications such as obstructive urosepsis [4].

Established risk factors for stone formation and recurrence include urinary tract obstruction, neurogenic bladder, indwelling catheters or stents, and comorbidities such as diabetes and recurrent urinary tract infections [5]. While these associations are recognized, reliable models for predicting early postoperative recurrence in infection-stone cohorts remain limited. Most existing recurrence prediction tools, such as the ROKS nomogram [6], were developed to predict symptomatic stone episodes over multi-year horizons (e.g. 2, 5, and 10 years) in general stone-former populations rather than postoperative early recurrence in infection-stone cohorts. Other published nomograms [7] typically combine heterogeneous stone types and incorporate broader metabolic or biochemical variables, with endpoints and follow-up windows that differ from infection-stone-specific early recurrence outcomes.

This study aimed to identify independent predictors of early (6-month) recurrence after surgical management of infection-related upper urinary tract stones and to develop an internally validated nomogram using readily available clinical, microbiological, and imaging markers. Particular emphasis was placed on predictors relevant to infection-stone biology, including CT attenuation characteristics and follow-up urine culture status.

Methods & materials

Study design and patient population

This single-center retrospective study was conducted at The University of Hong Kong–Shenzhen Hospital. Patients diagnosed with upper urinary tract stones who underwent minimally invasive surgical treatment between January 2019 and December 2023 were screened. Stone composition was confirmed via infrared spectroscopy. A total of 170 patients with infection-related stone components were identified and included. The study was approved by the ethics committee of the University of Hong Kong–Shenzhen Hospital (Ethics Approval No. [2023]178). The study was conducted in accordance with relevant guidelines, including the Strengthening the Reporting of Observational Studies in Epidemiology (STROBE) guideline [8] and regulations. Due to the retrospective nature of this study, the requirement for informed consent was waived by the ethics committee in accordance with institutional guidelines and national regulations.

Eligibility criteria

Patients were eligible if they had undergone surgical removal of upper urinary tract stones and had stone composition confirmed as magnesium ammonium phosphate hexahydrate (struvite) and/or carbonate apatite. Inclusion required complete clinical data and postoperative follow-up records.

Exclusion criteria included non-infectious stone composition, loss to follow-up, presence of autoimmune diseases, or severe cardiopulmonary dysfunction. Patients with neurogenic bladder, long-term use of immunosuppressants, chronic indwelling urinary catheters or ureteral stents, or those managed non-surgically were also excluded.

Date collection

Collected clinical variables included demographic information (age, gender), body mass index (BMI), urinalysis results (white blood cell positivity, nitrite positivity, urine pH), preoperative and follow-up urine culture results, and imaging findings. Imaging data included CT attenuation values (Hounsfield Units [HU]), presence of residual stones, degree of hydronephrosis (none, mild, moderate, or severe), renal atrophy, and anatomical abnormalities. All candidate predictors and the recurrence outcome were complete for all included patients in this study. Therefore, no imputation procedures were required, and all analyses were performed on complete-case data.

Stone composition was determined using the LIIR-20 infrared spectrometer (BlueMod Scientific Instruments, Tianjin, China). After cleaning and drying, specimens were ground, mixed with potassium bromide at a 1:200 ratio, and compressed into translucent discs for scanning. Spectra were recorded over a range of 4000–400 cm− 1, with a resolution of 1.5 cm− 1 and 32 scans. Composition was determined by comparing the sample spectra to standard reference spectra.

Follow-up outcomes and definitions

The primary outcome was stone recurrence within six months postoperatively, defined as the development of new stones or a > 2-fold increase in residual fragment size on follow-up imaging (ultrasound or plain computed tomography [CT]) [9]. Stone-free status was defined as complete clearance or residual fragments < 4 mm [10, 11]. All patients were followed for six months after discharge through outpatient visits and telephone follow-up. Postoperative imaging was interpreted in routine clinical care; clinicians and radiologists were not formally blinded to baseline clinical information.

Statistical analysis

Continuous variables were assessed for distribution and summarized as mean ± standard deviation when approximately normally distributed, or as median with interquartile range when non-normally distributed. Categorical variables were summarized as number (%). Between-group comparisons were performed using the Student’s t test or Mann-Whitney U test for continuous variables, and the χ2 test or Fisher’s exact test for categorical variables.

Because all candidate predictors and the recurrence outcome were complete for all included patients, no imputation was performed and analyses were conducted as complete-case analyses.

Patients were randomly allocated to a modeling cohort (n = 104) and a validation cohort (n = 44) using a computerized random number generator at a 7:3 ratio. The split was not stratified by outcome. In the modeling cohort, univariate logistic regression was used to screen candidate predictors; variables with P < 0.10 were entered into a multivariable logistic regression model. Backward stepwise selection was applied to derive the final model. Effect estimates are reported as odds ratios (ORs) with 95% confidence intervals (CIs).

A nomogram was constructed based on the final multivariable model coefficients. Model discrimination was quantified using the area under the receiver operating characteristic curve (AUC) with 95% confidence intervals. Calibration was assessed using calibration plots and the Hosmer–Lemeshow goodness-of-fit test. Clinical utility was evaluated using decision curve analysis. Internal validation was performed using split-sample validation: the final model derived in the modeling cohort was applied to the validation cohort using unchanged coefficients. All tests were two-sided, and P < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

All statistical analyses were performed using SPSS software (Version 26.0; IBM Corporation, Armonk, NY) and R version 4.4.1 (packages: rms, pROC, rmda, ggplot2).

The overall 6-month recurrence rate was 52.0% (n = 77/148). In the modeling cohort, 44.2% (n = 46/104) experienced recurrence, whereas the validation cohort demonstrated a higher recurrence rate of 70.5% (n = 31/44).

Comparison between early recurrence and non-recurrence groups

Clinical and imaging characteristics were compared between patients with and without recurrence at 6-month follow-up (Table 2). Patients who developed recurrence had significantly higher rates of residual stones (72% vs. 22%, P < 0.001) and renal atrophy (33% vs. 9%, P = 0.002). Recurrence was also associated with a higher rate of positive follow-up urine culture (39% vs. 3%, P < 0.001). In addition, CT attenuation values were significantly higher in the recurrence group [median 821 HU (IQR 592–1003) vs. 511 HU (IQR 416–864), P = 0.004]. No statistically significant differences were observed between recurrence and non-recurrence groups with respect to age, sex, BMI, preoperative urinalysis parameters, preoperative urine culture, degree of hydronephrosis, anatomic anomalies, or urine pH (Table 2).

Nomogram model construction and performance

A nomogram was constructed based on the final multivariable logistic regression model incorporating residual stones, follow-up urine culture status, and CT attenuation value (Fig. 1). The nomogram demonstrated strong discriminative performance in the modeling cohort with an AUC of 0.856 (95% CI: 0.786–0.925) and acceptable discrimination in the validation cohort with an AUC of 0.747 (95% CI: 0.586–0.907) (Fig. 2). Calibration curves showed acceptable agreement between predicted and observed recurrence probabilities in both cohorts, with Hosmer–Lemeshow test results of P = 0.072 in the modeling cohort and P = 0.174 in the validation cohort (Fig. 3). Decision curve analysis indicated that the nomogram provided greater net benefit than “treat all” or “treat none” strategies across a clinically relevant range of threshold probabilities in both the modeling and validation cohorts (Fig. 4).

Discussion

This retrospective, single-center study identified three independent predictors of early (6-month) postoperative recurrence in patients with infection-related upper urinary tract stones: residual stones, positive urine culture at follow-up, and higher CT attenuation values. A nomogram incorporating these routinely available markers demonstrated strong discrimination in the derivation cohort (AUC 0.86) and acceptable performance in split-sample internal validation (AUC 0.75), with generally good calibration and favorable decision-curve net benefit across clinically plausible threshold probabilities.

Residual stones were the strongest predictor of recurrence, consistent with the established concept that residual fragments can serve as a nidus for persistent bacterial colonization, biofilm formation, and subsequent crystal deposition [12, 13]. Even clinically-insignificant small fragments may harbor urease-producing organisms protected from host immunity and antibiotics, perpetuating the infection-stone cycle and accelerating early regrowth [14, 15]. The independent association between positive follow-up urine culture and recurrence further supports the importance of infection control after surgery; persistent or recurrent bacteriuria likely reflects incomplete eradication of urease-producing bacteria, reinfection, or ongoing colonization of residual fragments. Clinically, these findings reinforce two practical priorities in infection-stone management: maximizing stone clearance and ensuring postoperative antimicrobial strategies are guided by culture results and clinical context [16]. Higher CT attenuation was also associated with recurrence risk, suggesting that denser stones, potentially reflecting greater carbonate apatite content, different microstructure, or greater mineral density, may be linked to more aggressive early regrowth dynamics, although the mechanistic basis requires further study [17].

The overall 6-month recurrence rate in our cohort was high (52%), and recurrence was particularly elevated in the validation subset (70.5%). This is biologically plausible in infection-stone disease given the propensity for rapid regrowth when fragments or bacteriuria persist, but it also highlights statistical and design considerations [1, 15]. Because the cohort was divided using a single non-stratified 7:3 random split and the validation sample was modest (n = 44), substantial differences in outcome proportions can occur by chance alone. Moreover, as a tertiary referral center, our institution may care for patients with more complex disease (e.g. higher burden, anatomical complexity, recurrent infection), which could elevate baseline recurrence risk compared with community practice [18]. For these reasons, the observed performance metrics, particularly those from the smaller validation subset, should be interpreted as preliminary estimates rather than definitive measures of transportability.

Several limitations are important and directly inform interpretation. First, the retrospective single-center design introduces selection bias (including referral bias) and limits generalizability. Our exclusion of certain high-risk populations (e.g. neurogenic bladder, chronic indwelling catheters/stents, immunosuppression) improves cohort homogeneity but may reduce applicability to those clinically important groups. Second, although we performed internal validation, it was split-sample validation only using a single random division. This approach can yield unstable estimates in modest datasets and does not fully address overfitting risk [19]. More robust internal validation (e.g. bootstrap resampling or repeated cross-validation) would provide optimism-corrected performance estimates and more stable calibration assessment; such analyses, along with sensitivity analyses using penalized regression or Firth correction when appropriate, are important next steps to quantify model stability and small-sample bias. Third, our endpoint definition was pragmatic. Recurrence was defined as new stone formation or a > 2-fold increase in residual fragment size, and follow-up imaging could be ultrasound or plain CT depending on routine care. This imaging heterogeneity and the “doubling” threshold were chosen to reduce misclassification from modality- and operator-related measurement variability, but they are not universally standardized definitions of postoperative recurrence in infection stones [10, 11]. Future prospective work should implement standardized imaging modality, timing, and measurement protocols (and, ideally, blinded adjudication) to refine and validate recurrence definitions.

Fourth, outcome assessment was based on routine clinical imaging interpretation without formal blinding to baseline information, which introduces potential assessment bias even though criteria were largely objective. Fifth, microbiological and treatment granularity was limited: we did not systematically capture bacterial species, urease status, resistance patterns, antibiotic selection/duration, suppressive strategies, or adherence, factors that plausibly influence both urine culture positivity and recurrence risk [20]. This limitation is particularly relevant for infection stones, where persistent bacteriuria may reflect reinfection versus incomplete eradication, and where antibiotic strategies may modify recurrence trajectories [16]. Sixth, we did not include comprehensive metabolic evaluation or urinary biochemical parameters. While infection stones are primarily driven by infection and urinary alkalinization, metabolic abnormalities can coexist and may contribute to recurrence [21]; incorporating such measures could improve risk stratification in future cohorts. Finally, the present model has not undergone external validation. Independent testing in prospective, multicenter cohorts with standardized imaging and microbiology protocols is essential to determine generalizability, assess calibration across settings, and establish whether nomogram-guided surveillance meaningfully improves outcomes.

Despite these limitations, this study addresses a practical clinical gap by focusing specifically on infection-related stones and early postoperative recurrence, using predictors readily available in routine care. If externally validated, the nomogram may support individualized follow-up intensity and targeted infection-control strategies, particularly in patients with residual fragments, persistent bacteriuria, or higher CT attenuation values.

Conclusion

This retrospective, single-center study identified residual stones, positive follow-up urine culture, and elevated CT attenuation values as independent predictors of early (6-month) postoperative recurrence in patients with infection-related upper urinary tract stones. A nomogram incorporating these three markers demonstrated strong discrimination and good calibration in split-sample internal validation. However, the model is preliminary and requires prospective, multicenter external validation with standardized imaging protocols, blinded outcome assessment, and more comprehensive clinical and microbiological data before routine clinical implementation.
