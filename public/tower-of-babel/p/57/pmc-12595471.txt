An all-sky light pollution model for global-scale applications that embraces a full range of cloud distributions
Authors: Miroslav Kocifaj, Fabio Falchi, František Kundracik
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
SignificanceClouds significantly influence the propagation of both natural and artificial light at night (ALAN), altering nocturnal light conditions with potential ecological consequences, among other factors. To accurately predict light pollution, we developed a model that computes artificial sky brightness under various atmospheric and cloudiness conditions, from clear skies to complete overcast. This model enhances our understanding of ALAN exposure and its effects on living organisms, aiding ecological assessments. Additionally, it provides a valuable tool for interpreting historical and contemporary light pollution data collected by monitoring networks. By integrating cloud dynamics, our approach offers a realistic representation of nighttime illumination crucial for advancing environmental research and policy.

Light pollution — the anthropogenic alteration of nighttime lighting levels (1, 2) — has mostly been studied in relation to light propagation under clear-sky conditions, reflecting the specific interests of astronomers (e.g., refs. 3–8). The presence of clouds substantially alters the propagation of both natural light and artificial light at night (ALAN), diminishing or increasing the presence of light in the night environment. At sites with low levels of light pollution, for example, the presence of clouds diminishes the light arriving from the natural night sky (9) (i.e., clouds appear dark against the celestial background of stars, Milky Way, and night airglow). Conversely, at moderately to heavily light-polluted sites, clouds usually amplify the light in the nighttime environment (10–12). Until now, models of night sky brightness (NSB) were limited to idealized sky conditions, either completely clear or completely overcast (e.g., refs. 13–16). Moreover, no Radiative Transfer Equation (RTE) existed for spatially heterogeneous skies, like those with isolated clouds or broken clouds coverage, which are most usual. Considering that the average cloud cover over land is about 55% (17), with great seasonal and geographic variability, the absence of a general approach and universal solution represented a great gap in light pollution modeling for environmental applications. Radiative transfer models for daytime conditions [e.g., MYSTIC (18)] have addressed such complexities in the presence of only a single light source, the Sun. NSB models necessarily deal with multiple light sources, even up to millions. Yet to date, no model incorporates realistic, stochastic cloud fields with variable opacities and spatial distributions. The present model addresses this gap by introducing a radiative transfer framework capable of simulating artificial night sky brightness under arbitrary cloud configurations, including fragmented and nonuniform cloud fields that regularly occur in nature. A better qualitative and quantitative understanding of ALAN under different cloud conditions is of paramount importance because of the rising interest of the scientific community in the phenomenon’s ecological consequences, in particular (2, 19).

To address the variations in night sky radiance caused by ALAN in the presence of statistically random, heterogeneous cloud fields, we developed an advanced model. This model, thoroughly detailed in the “Materials and Methods,” accurately accounts for light propagation in the presence of a specific cloud fraction (CF) and cloud morphology. With this model, we simulated different cloudiness situations with a stochastic presence of clouds for a single city and an observer at different distances, from its center to 18.5 km away, and for a group of cities and villages with an observer located outside the urbanized, light-emitting areas. Section Results summarizes our findings, and Section Discussion elaborates our interpretation and the applications and limitations of the model.

Results

To demonstrate the potential of the model, we computed all-sky maps of the radiance produced by ALAN at several sites within and near the city of Žilina, Slovakia, with an approximate urbanized radius R = 2.5 to 3.5 km. Starting from the city center (R=0), the distance was increased to 1.5 km (0.43 to 0.6 R), 4.5 km (i.e., 1 km away from the urbanized area; 1.3 to 1.8 R), 6.0 km (1.7 to 2.4 R), 10.5 km (3 to 4.2 R), and 18.5 km (5.3 to 7.4 R). Expressing distances in terms of the city’s radius facilitates the generalization of our results to other cities of comparable size.

The city of Žilina (population ~80,000) was selected for several reasons. First, the city was systematically investigated in previous studies (e.g., refs. 20, 21). Second, it provides a favorable setting for assessing the isolated impact of urban lighting because of the absence of other dominant or disruptive light sources, particularly toward the northwest and northeast. Žilina is surrounded by natural zones, including Malá Fatra National Park, Kysuce Protected Landscape Area, and the Strážovské Hills, making it well-suited for studying the propagation of light pollution into natural environments from an ecological perspective.

The calculation was performed for Aerosol Optical Depth (AOD) values of 0.1 and 0.3, corresponding respectively to low-turbidity conditions (horizontal visibility of 34 km) (22) and a fairly hazy atmosphere (11-km visibility). The aerosol asymmetry parameter and single scattering albedo were set to 0.65 and 0.90, respectively, which are typical values for continental, urban, and rural aerosols. The Ångström exponent characterizing the spectral dependence of AOD was 1.3, implying that AOD decreases with wavelength proportionally to λ−1.3. For each distance to the city center, the computation was repeated for cloud fractions 0 (clear sky), 0.1, 0.3, 0.5, 0.7, and 0.9 (almost completely overcast). This refers to the nadir-view CF as seen from a satellite (23). The CF is the ratio of the area of the vertical projection of the clouds to the total area of a selected zone. Note that the CF definition used here is different from the cloud cover perceived by an observer on the ground looking upward—see, e.g., Fig. 6 in (24)—and that a CF of 0.9 is almost indistinguishable from a completely overcast sky, except when, by chance, the 0.1 clear fraction is near the observer’s zenith. For each of the selected CF s, except obviously for a cloudless sky, we randomly changed the clouds’ position, shape, and type. After preliminary calculations, we determined that we could obtain reliable statistics by computing seven all-sky maps. All this was made for a cloud base altitude of 2 km and two different bands in the visible spectrum: 400 to 500 nm and 500 to 600 nm (with the corresponding computational datasets provided as part of the SI Appendix).

Fig. 1 shows a sample of the maps for the 500 to 600 nm band and for each combination of CF and distance. Comparing the different sky maps, it is apparent that a partially cloudy sky is not a simple “average” of the cases for clear and overcast skies. In fact, NSB is significantly altered by clouds, often in nontrivial ways. For example, during daytime, when the atmosphere is illuminated by the Sun, a clear-sky window in an otherwise cloudy sky shares many similarities with a completely clear sky. In contrast, during the night, when artificial light illuminates the atmosphere from below, clear-sky patches behind broken clouds can be darker than in a completely clear sky because of the light-screening effect of the clouds. The cloud array might act as a barrier and prevent artificial light from reaching higher atmospheric layers, thus reducing the number of photons scattered downward that enter the observer’s eyes from the unclouded parts of the sky. This is visible in the maps for CF = 0.3 in the case for which the observer is either at the city center or 1.5 km away from it.

Conversely, clouds can amplify night sky radiance both within the city and its close surroundings, scattering artificial light downward more efficiently than under a clear sky. This is apparent in the maps representing the sky inside the borders of the city or just outside of it (0 km, 1.5 km, and 4.5 km columns), where clouds amplify the radiance wherever they appear in the sky vault. Farther away from city center (6 km and 10.5 km), the clouds appear dimmer, while still brighter than in clear-sky conditions. Very far from the city, a cloudy sky is now dimmer than the clear sky. Moreover, the direction of observation is determinative; see, for example, the maps for the 6-km distance from the city center, in which clouds over the city are very bright, acting as an amplifier of the city’s lights and making the whole city appear much brighter. In particular, the sky appears much brighter in the direction of the city than it does under clear-sky conditions (see the maps for CF = 0.3 to 0.9). This situation may potentially lead to phototaxis phenomena in places where, under clear skies, none would otherwise occur. This increase can be more evident if the glow generated by the city under clear conditions is screened by obstacles (e.g., hills or trees near the observer) provided that they mask the first few degrees above the horizon toward the city, where in clear-sky conditions the brightest part of the skyglow is found. On the other hand, the presence of clouds may cause a bright glow above the obstacles’ level, as shown, for example in Fig. 2.

All-sky maps with part of the horizon screened. This figure shows the all-sky maps presented in Fig. 1 for a distance of 6 km and CF = 0.0 (Left panel) or 0.5 (Right panel), but with a screening of the horizon close to the observer that may be caused, for example, by nearby hills or trees. When the glow under clear skies is almost entirely blocked by the screen, it is only partially obstructed in cloudy conditions, which amplifies the contrast between cloudy and clear-sky scenarios.

Contrary to the zenith radiance, where the maximum cloud-induced amplification occurs near the city center, the maximum amplification in horizontal irradiance is found just outside the city limits. Such a shift can be explained by the complex interplay between light emission geometry, atmospheric scattering, and cloud effects. Direct light emissions toward the zenith are often suppressed by luminaire shielding, while emissions near the horizon are reduced or even blocked due to urban structures. In contrast, the amount of ground-reflected light decreases progressively from the zenith toward the horizon. These two components together shape an angular light output pattern that typically peaks at intermediate zenith angles.

Both zenith radiance and horizontal irradiance result from light scattering in the atmosphere. Zenith radiance typically peaks near the city center or its immediate surroundings. Horizontal irradiance, however, integrates radiance from all directions across the upper hemisphere, including low-angle scattered light. Therefore, the scattered signal detected at ground level may be stronger at some distance from the source, particularly in directions where forward scattering dominates. As a result, the shift of the irradiance peak outside the city is more pronounced than that of zenith radiance. With increasing distance from the source, the flux density of beam radiation naturally decreases. The location of the maximum amplification of horizontal irradiance therefore emerges at a finite distance from the city, depending on atmospheric properties and the angular emission pattern of the light sources.

Clouds introduce additional complexity. In the vicinity of the light source, clouds can enhance NSB by reflecting and scattering light downward. At greater distances, however, clouds may reduce NSB by blocking light from higher atmospheric layers. Photons escaping the cloud layer typically exhibit no preferred direction, which can lead to a reduction in sky brightness in angular directions where forward scattering would otherwise dominate. Broken cloud fields further complicate the radiative transfer, necessitating numerical modeling to quantify average zenith radiance and horizontal irradiance under varying conditions. To account for the temporal and spatial variability of cloud configurations, our simulations were designed to produce statistically representative results at any location within the modeled domain. Accordingly, the CF refers to the entire modeled domain, not just the measurement point, and applies uniformly to all locations regardless of azimuthal direction or distance from the city.

Looking at how cloud cover affects zenith sky radiance, Fig. 5 indicates that when the observer is within the city limits and there is a cloud at the zenith, the sky appears brighter, up to a factor of ≈9 in the city center and ≈8 at 1.5 km off-center (0.43 to 0.6 R). The minimum brightness at zenith remains equal to that of a clear sky (see the lower dashed line in the graphs of the first row of Fig. 5) up to CF = 0.6. These situations occur when the zenith is free of clouds, even with consistent cloud presence over the rest of the sky. Outside of the city, the maximum cloud-induced amplification factor (indicated by the higher dashed lines) decreases with R. Moreover, outside of the city, clouds can reduce the zenith sky radiance, as shown by the lower dashed line now below the value of 1, up to CF = 0.6 for distances of 4.5 and 6.0 km. Farther from the city, the minimum radiance is lowered to about 0.6 at 10.5 km (3 to 4.2 R). At a distance of 18.5 km (5.3 to 7.4 R), the range of variation in zenith radiance, compared to the clear-sky conditions, is much narrower and always below 1. It decreases as cloud fraction increases down to a value of ≈0.6 for CF = 0.6 and higher.

The results of calculations presented in Figs. 3–6 pertain to the spectral range of 400 to 500 nm and consider an AOD of 0.1. Since blue light is strongly scattered in the vicinity of light sources even under cloudless conditions, the resulting cloud-induced amplification of zenith brightness at any specific location is lower for blue wavelengths than at the red end of the spectrum. Therefore, measurement devices calibrated in photopic units are expected to report higher values than those restricted to wavelengths below 500 nm. To address this, we analyzed the potential range of sky brightness amplification by clouds in both urban centers and peripheral areas, now considering various cloud heights and two aerosol scenarios: low (AOD = 0.1) and high (AOD = 0.5) aerosol content in the free atmosphere (outside of the cloud layers). The subcloud layer is often influenced by local emissions (e.g., traffic, industry) and intense atmospheric mixing, leading to heterogeneous aerosol composition through space. Accordingly, we implemented a sufficiently broad range of AOD values from 0.1 to 0.5 in our numerical simulations.

Although most of our analysis is based on radiometric quantities such as radiance and irradiance, we include photometric quantities like luminance here to enable comparison with published observational data, which are typically reported in photometric units. In our modeling tool (SkyGlow Simulator) (25), luminance is computed directly by integrating spectral radiance weighted by the photopic luminous efficiency function over the visible wavelengths.

The results show that the ratio of zenith luminance between overcast and clear skies depends on cloud layer height and reaches its highest values over city centers and under low AOD conditions (Table 1). These results represent an ideally controlled experiment, where all environmental, atmospheric, and location-specific variables are identical for both overcast and clear-sky conditions. In a real-world experiment, ensuring that all other parameters remain unchanged would be extremely difficult, if not impossible, and greater variability in measured values compared to theoretical predictions is therefore to be expected.

Optimal conditions for identifying the true effect of clouds on night sky brightness can, of course, only be achieved through controlled numerical simulations. Under low-cloud conditions, the contribution of light from distant, artificially lit surfaces is significantly reduced; thus, local light sources nearest to the observer dominate. Consequently, over a sprawling urban area, the cloud amplification factor may vary substantially depending on the specific location within the city. Jechow et al. (26) reported a 25-fold increase in zenith luminance and a ≈12-fold increase in illuminance in central parts of Berlin. As illustrated in SI Appendix, Fig. S1, the New World Atlas of Artificial Night Sky Brightness (27) predicts a relatively broad area of elevated zenith luminance over Berlin under clear-sky conditions (see the leftmost map). However, intense light emissions are concentrated in central Berlin and can be an order of magnitude higher than emissions from surrounding areas (see the Visible Infrared Imaging Radiometer Suite Day Night Band, VIIRS DNB layer, in the rightmost map). This central area is comparable in size to the city of Žilina, for which the zenith luminance and horizontal illuminance amplification values in Table 1 were calculated, and which closely align with the values reported by Jechow et al. (26).

Discussion

The results presented in the previous section demonstrate the model’s potential in evaluating the effects of various cloud types and coverage inside and outside cities. They depend on the particular configuration(s) used for the light sources (assumed here to represent a small city and some surrounding villages). Hence, the numbers provided (e.g., amplification or reduction of radiance or irradiance caused by clouds at a certain distance) should not be generalized to widely different situations. Nonetheless, the results offer insight into the complexity and variability of artificial night sky radiance and irradiance as a function of variations in atmospheric conditions.

The NSB under a low cloud deck typically reflects the characteristics of light emissions in the vicinity of the measurement site. Cloud-induced amplification of zenith brightness in a city with dimensions significantly larger than the cloud base altitude can therefore vary widely, depending on local light sources surrounding the measurement site. In such a case, the urban models must not only clearly define the geographic distribution of light-emitting surfaces but also account for the heterogeneity of light emissions. In practice, this requires segmenting a digitized model of the city into zones that have approximately equal surface densities of light emissions. A homogenized model treating a large city as a single zone of uniform brightness could lead to significant distortion of the cloud amplification factor in areas exposed to excessive light emissions, such as city centers.

To further quantify the effects of clouds at any desired site, the particular configuration of light sources affecting that site should be studied in detail. For this purpose, the software developed for this model is freely available for scientific, noncommercial uses.

The model can use cloud observation data from meteorological stations as input, allowing for more precise predictions of artificial sky brightness and irradiance from the light-polluted sky at specific sites.

Until now, no model for a realistic, partially cloudy sky has been available; consequently, it was not entirely clear which artificial sky radiance values should be expected for the many possible configurations of light sources and atmospheric conditions. The only qualitative knowledge was that above cities, cloudiness increases the radiance of the night sky compared to cloud-free situations (28), whereas far away from the city clouds tend to screen the artificial light contribution and lower the night sky radiance (9, 29). The critical question is what happens in between these two extremes?

The present results indicate that there is a “transition zone” where radiance strongly depends on the particular cloud distribution. This zone separates the close vicinity of the city (where clouds act as radiance amplifiers) from distant sites (where clouds darken the sky). When photometers are located in this transition zone, it is not easy to discern how artificial night sky radiance changes as stochastic cloud formations pass through the detector’s field of view. It was thus difficult to interpret such data until now, because any increase or decrease in measured radiance could be attributed either to the passage of fine haze or clouds over the detector, or to the background signal from a clear atmosphere—screened or not by other clouds.

Specifically, the present model’s uniqueness is to determine how the artificial radiance changes within cloud windows, particularly when the observation direction passes through the atmosphere with a sparse population of cloud droplets (typically observed when transitioning from a cloud through its edge to a clear atmosphere in the gaps between clouds). In this case, artificial night sky radiance is a combination of partial (weak) reflection from the thin cloud layers and from the clear atmosphere around them. This model is capable of explaining the observed variability in artificial night sky radiance under real cloud coverage conditions.

Useful and practical applications of the model are to aid the interpretation of historical data collected by night sky radiance monitoring networks (30–33) and to disentangle the contribution of changes in the installed light sources’ radiant flux to that of the changes due to the variability of cloud conditions. The model can also predict night sky radiance trends based on the available historical information on prevailing cloud types and cloud fractions over various regions, which is routinely collected by meteorological networks (34, 35).

Materials and Methods

Modeling Stochastic Cloud Coverage.

The descriptive parameters of cloud fields that occur in nature are obviously highly heterogeneous. Under real-life conditions, a wide range of cloud configurations and shapes with complex relationships can be observed between their vertical and horizontal dimensions. Various optical properties, such as cloud opacity (expressed as a dimensionless value between 0 and 1) or the spectral flux of diffuse radiation escaping a cloud layer, depend on local density enhancements and on the microphysical properties of the water droplets or ice crystals forming the cloud. Statistical modeling of cloud fields is highly complex. It involves stochastic geometry, density fluctuations within individual cloud formations, and gaps between clouds. These factors together pose a fundamental challenge to developing a realistic model of the radiation field in nighttime atmospheres, especially when illuminated from below by artificially lit surfaces of finite size.

Through systematic numerical modeling and comparison of results with cloud formations observed in real conditions, we have created a model that is scalable and integrable into current numerical solvers for 3D radiation transfer. The solution concept described below is the product of extensive parameter tuning, resulting in a model that best approximates cloud fields observed at least over Central Europe.

For each cloud, a finite volume box (i.e., a randomized cuboid with predefined lower and upper boundaries of the cloud layer) is generated. Within this volume, cloud elements form, with their dimensions no longer depending on the cloud size or location (i.e., larger and denser clouds are composed of more elements). Some elements may occur below the lower boundary, but only to a limited extent. These individual elements are fairly transparent, gaining real density only when accumulated, which rarely happens at the cloud edges, thus making these structures very subtle.

If the element sizes decrease with the cloud size, or if the cloud volume decreases, the number of generated elements increases significantly, impacting computation time. In our numerical experiments, the number of clouds increased from several hundreds to hundreds of thousands, leading to longer times required for model generation and ray tracing within the cloud field. Longer computer times directly affect the calculation of cloud coverage maps and their rendering. For example, to obtain the cloud coverage of the sky as seen from the observation site (Fig. 7A), a statistically large modeling area of 1,000 km × 1,000 km was required (Fig. 7B). This domain size ensures that clouds are present even at low elevation angles near the horizon, which may correspond to distances exceeding 150 km depending on cloud base height. Additionally, SkyGlow Simulator allows the observer’s position on the map to be freely adjusted, so generating the cloud field over a large territory enables simulations for different cities or locations within the same domain. To avoid regenerating the cloud field for each new observer position and to maintain consistent atmospheric conditions, the cloud field is generated once over a sufficiently large area. This approach also reduces computational overhead, especially for large cloud fractions where cloud generation is time-intensive.

Night Sky Brightness Model Incorporating Broken Cloud Arrays.

Modeling the NSB caused by light emissions from finite-dimensional, artificially lit ground areas is an intricate 3D radiative transfer problem further complicated by the presence of cloud fields. Moreover, clouds cannot be considered well-defined “geometrical” objects, but rather as collections of accumulated condensations (small water or ice particles) characterized by fuzzy rather than discrete edges, as they are not isolated from the surrounding air. Because such stochastic structures present significant challenges for solving the radiative transfer equation (RTE), simplified but realistic cloud models are inevitably necessary to closely approximate the NSB conditions observed in nature. The cloud model described above has been integrated into an advanced RTE solution, with the general framework of NSB modeling schematically described in Fig. 9. This solution, called SkyGlow Simulator, was first introduced in 2007 (14) and 2008 (36), and later improved in 2017 (20). In 2018 (37), the radiative transfer model was used as a generating function for the calculation of higher-scattering-order radiance components.

General framework for modeling night sky radiance incorporating broken cloud arrays. The total radiance is the sum of three components as described in the main text. The radiance components produced by scattering in the subcloud atmosphere (L1), by scattering in the atmosphere above the cloud level (L2), and by cloud-reflected light (La), collectively determine the total night sky brightness. I0z0 represents the intensity of light emitted from a ground-based light source upward to zenith angle z0. Additionally, z and A denote the observational zenith and azimuth angles, respectively. H is the cloud height, and θh is the scattering angle.

The SkyGlow Simulator solution is now formulated as a superposition of radiance components originating from scattering in the turbid atmosphere below the cloud level (L1), reflection by the 3D cloud field (Lα), and scattering in the free atmosphere above the cloud top (L2). Statistically significant changes in the amount of artificial light transmitting through the cloud field into the upper atmospheric layers are related to CF. The light flux above the cloud layer can be expressed as a function of (1-CF), where the number of photons varies depending on the cloud opacity along the beam path. In the limiting case of unit opacity, the cloud acts as a barrier preventing artificial light from escaping the Earth’s atmosphere. The cloud-reflected radiation is backscattered to the ground with intensity reduced by a factor α (the cloud albedo, see Fig. 9).

In general, the intensity of electromagnetic radiation on its path from the source to the detector decays because of interaction with air molecules and aerosols; the rate of decrease is determined by the so-called transmission function T. Thus, the intensity of radiation emitted into the upper hemisphere at a zenith angle z0 decreases from its original value I0z0 to T×I0z0.
