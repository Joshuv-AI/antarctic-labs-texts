ABSTRACT
Key Points•Genetic markers suitable for ctDNA-based MRD tracking were detectable in 98% of patients with PMBL.•Combined PET negativity and undetectable MRD after 4 cycles of immunochemotherapy identifies patients with excellent prognosis.

Introduction

Primary mediastinal large B-cell lymphoma (PMBL) is a rare, highly curable, aggressive subtype of large B-cell lymphoma (LBCL). Five-year survival rates exceed 80% with anthracycline- and rituximab-based immunochemotherapy,1, 2, 3 with improved progression-free survival (PFS) for dose-intense approaches.4

PMBL predominantly affects young adults, with a higher incidence in women, making long-term treatment-related toxicity a major concern.5,6 Survivors have reduced life expectancy compared with the general population,7 largely due to late treatment-related effects, whereas disease-related mortality mainly occurs early as a result of primary chemotherapy refractoriness. The emergence of effective salvage therapies, such as chimeric antigen receptor T-cell therapy,8,9 highlights the need for treatment personalization through deescalation to reduce long-term toxicity or early escalation in refractory disease.

Positron emission tomography (PET) using the Deauville score (DS) remains the standard for staging and response assessment10 but is frequently confounded by persistent, moderately hypermetabolic residual mediastinal masses at interim and end-of-treatment (EoT) evaluations, making it difficult to confirm a complete metabolic response,11 particularly in patients with bulky or fibrotic disease at presentation.12 False-positive PET findings occur in up to 25% to 45% of cases,13,14 potentially leading to unnecessary invasive procedures, repeated imaging, consolidation radiotherapy (CRT), or unwarranted treatment intensification.

Accumulating evidence supports EoT circulating tumor DNA (ctDNA)-based minimal residual disease (MRD) detection as a prognostic marker in LBCL contributing to evolving management strategies. The National Comprehensive Cancer Network guidelines recommend MRD assessment in patients with LBCL who exhibit partial metabolic response on EoT PET. These patients with EoT MRD-negative test results may be managed as complete metabolic response with clinical follow-up alone. However, MRD clinical relevance in PMBL has not yet been systematically investigated15 and is not currently recommended.16

PMBL typically presents as a large, isolated anterior mediastinal mass.5,6 Because the mediastinum is difficult to biopsy, core biopsies are routinely used but are frequently exhausted during standard diagnostic procedures, leaving no tissue for genetic analyses and MRD marker identification.17 An alternative strategy is to derive MRD markers from pretreatment plasma samples; however, it is still unclear whether plasma-derived alterations accurately represent the tumor’s genetic landscape in PMBL and whether their number and type are sufficient for high-depth error-corrected sequencing approaches.

In this study, we sought to determine whether ctDNA provides a reliable source of tumor-specific genetic markers for MRD monitoring that accurately reflects the mutational landscape of PMBL, whether ctDNA-based MRD assessment has prognostic value, and whether it adds predictive information beyond standard PET-based response evaluation.

Patients and methods

Patients

Adult patients with newly diagnosed PMBL were prospectively enrolled in the observational, multicenter CAMIL study (ClinicalTrials.gov identifier: NCT04824950). Patients were treated with curative-intent immunochemotherapy across 30 French LYSA (Lymphoma Study Association) centers. PMBL diagnosis was established locally based on typical clinical presentation and histopathologic criteria according to the 2016 and 2022 World Health Organization classifications,5,18 with systematic support of the French Lymphopath network.17 Detailed inclusion and exclusion criteria are described in the supplemental Methods. All treatments, routine blood testing, and PET examinations were performed as part of standard clinical care. The study was conducted in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki and approved by the institutional review board/ethics committee (ID-RCB: 2020-A03138-31). All patients provided written informed consent for biospecimen, clinical data collection, and biomarker analyses. The data cutoff was 1 May 2024.

Treatments and assessment of response

No investigational regimen was used. Investigators were blinded to ctDNA results, which did not influence clinical decisions. The induction phase consisted of 4 cycles of immunochemotherapy with either R-CHOP14 (rituximab, cyclophosphamide, doxorubicin, vincristine, and prednisone, administered every 14 days) or R-ACVBP (rituximab, doxorubicin, cyclophosphamide, vindesine, bleomycin, and prednisone) administered every 14 days, at the discretion of the treating physician. Responses during induction were assessed by PET after 2 and 4 cycles (PET2/PET4) with change in maximum standardized uptake value (ΔSUVmax) (cutoffs of 66% and 70% at PET2 and PET4, respectively)19,20 and/or DS according to Lugano 2014 criteria.10 Treatment consolidation strategies, supportive care measures, and response assessments were performed according to local practice and PET-guided decision-making at the discretion of the treating physicians, as detailed in the supplemental Methods.

Blood specimens and processing

Blood samples were collected at baseline before antilymphoma treatment and after 2 and 4 cycles for plasma cfDNA and germ line DNA analyses. See the supplemental Methods for detailed information on collection tubes, processing timelines, and storage conditions.

CAPP-seq assay

A cancer personalized profiling by deep sequencing (CAPP-seq) protocol (162 genes, including 30 noncoding regions targeted by aberrant somatic hypermutation [aSHM] was used for the study; supplemental Table 1), with an analytical sensitivity and limit of detection of ∼10−3, corresponding to a variant allele fraction (VAF) of 0.1%, as previously described.21, 22, 23, 24 Nontarget reads were used for the detection of somatic copy number abnormalities (SCNA).25 ctDNA concentration was expressed as haploid genomic equivalents per milliliter of plasma (hGE/mL).26 At follow-up time points, undetectable MRD was defined as the complete absence of detectable ctDNA at the analytical sensitivity of the assay (VAF of <10−3). This corresponds to ctDNA levels below the limit of detection of the CAPP-seq workflow (supplemental Methods).

Pathologic review and tumor molecular landscape assessment

Tumor formalin-fixed paraffin-embedded (FFPE) samples underwent centralized review by expert hematopathologists using standardized morphologic, immunophenotypic, and molecular criteria.5,16 Tumor DNA extracted from FFPE samples was analyzed using the same CAPP-seq methodology as applied to ctDNA (supplemental Methods).

Central PET review

PET scans were retrospectively reviewed in a blinded manner by senior nuclear medicine physicians, with adjudication by a third reader in cases of discordance. Baseline total metabolic tumor volume (TMTV), maximum standardized uptake value (SUVmax), and total lesion glycolysis (TLG) were assessed using standardized methodology (supplemental Methods). PET negativity was defined according to predefined ΔSUVmax thresholds or DS criteria when applicable, based on central or local review as described in the supplemental Methods.

Statistical analysis

Patient characteristics were summarized using descriptive statistics appropriate to variable type. The diagnostic performance of PET4 and minimal residual disease after 4 cycles of therapy (MRD4) for predicting disease progression was evaluated using sensitivity, specificity, positive and negative predictive values (PPV, NPV, respectively), and accuracy, with paired comparisons performed using the McNemar test.

Overall survival (OS) and PFS were defined from the start of first-line immunochemotherapy and estimated using Kaplan-Meier methods, with comparisons performed using the log-rank test. Cox proportional hazards regression models were used to assess associations with OS and PFS. Variables with P value <.05 in univariate analyses were considered for multivariable models, avoiding collinearity. Statistical significance was defined as P value <.05. Additional methodological details are provided in the supplemental Methods. All analyses were performed using R software version 4.2.1.

Induction immunochemotherapy consisted of R-CHOP14 in 51 patients (60.7%) and R-ACVBP in 33 (39.3%), with treatment allocation varying by center (supplemental Table 5). Among 81 patients with available data, consolidation consisted of continued R-CHOP14 in 60.5% or post-ACVBP sequential chemotherapy in 33.3%. Among 68 patients with available central nervous system prophylaxis data, 10.3% received intrathecal methotrexate during induction, 27.9% received high-dose methotrexate during consolidation, 17.6% received both, and 44.1% received none (supplemental Table 6). After a median follow-up of 21.4 months (IQR, 16.1-28), 1-year PFS and OS were 90.5% (95% confidence interval [CI], 84.4-97) and 98.8% (95% CI, 96.5-100; 1 nonlymphoma-related death, supplemental Figure 1).

Baseline ctDNA results and description of the molecular features

Baseline ctDNA was detected in 82 of 84 (98%) patients with a median level of 755 hGE/mL (IQR, 256-1635), a median baseline mean VAF of 9.3% (IQR, 3.92%-15.02%), and median number of 101 (IQR, 62-162) unique somatic variants per sample. Baseline ctDNA concentration was higher in patients who subsequently progressed: median, 1697.7 hGE/mL (IQR, 1340-2971.3; supplemental Table 7) vs 621.8 hGE/mL (IQR, 234.2-1320.1) in nonprogressors (data not shown). Higher (above the median) baseline ctDNA level was also associated with higher LDH (P < .001) and stage I to II disease (P = .005). No association was observed with TMTV (supplemental Table 8).

The genes with the highest number of variants were IGLL5 (n = 3625), BCL6 (n = 2081), SOCS1 (n = 870), CD83 (n = 348), and ITPKB (n = 187), all known targets of activation induced cytidine deaminase (AID)-mediated aSHM (supplemental Figure 2). Analysis of aSHM load revealed a high proportion of AID-related mutational signatures (SBS37, SBS39, SBS84, SBS85),28 with a median contribution of 58.3%. FishHook analysis showed that 26.7% of PMBL cases harbored mutations within AID-hypermutated superenhancer hot spots previously identified in LBCL.29 Recurrent alterations affected the superenhancer regions of BCL6, SOCS1, and CD83, detected in 95%, 86%, and 71% of patients, respectively (supplemental Figure 3).

SCNAs were detected in 53 of 83 assessable cases (median, 3 per case; range, 0-20), with 1 case failing quality control. The most frequent events were gains of 9p (40%), 9q (24%), 9p24.1 (22%), 2p16.1 (22%), and 12q (16%; supplemental Figure 4). Within the genomic regions covered by the selector, breakpoint-level analysis identified genomic translocations in 5 of 84 patients (6%), involving SOCS1, IGVL, IGVH, IL21R, and IL4R, and a single IgLJ1/AICDA event. None of these translocations were recurrent across multiple patients (supplemental Figure 5).

Concordance between ctDNA and tumor biopsy sequencing

To assess assay performance, somatic mutation profiles from plasma ctDNA were compared with matched FFPE tumor DNA when available. Of 55 baseline FFPE samples, 52 (94.5%) passed quality control, whereas 3 failed due to poor DNA integrity. All analyzable FFPE samples harbored at least 1 somatic mutation. Tumor mutational burden (defined as the number of somatic mutations per megabase) was comparable between ctDNA and FFPE samples (mean ± standard deviation [SD], 312.2 ± 206.2 vs 322.8 ± 199.8 mutations per megabase, respectively; P = .77). ctDNA sequencing detected 80.2% of mutations identified in matched tumor biopsies, and FFPE sequencing detected 83.3% of mutations found in plasma ctDNA, indicating substantial concordance between sample types (supplemental Table 9). Median VAF distributions according to the variant detection compartment are summarized in supplemental Table 10. Shared variants showed a moderate correlation in median VAFs between ctDNA and FFPE compartments, with median VAFs of 21% in FFPE and 9.6% in ctDNA (Spearman ρ = 0.34 [0.08-0.57]). However, because ctDNA VAFs are strongly influenced by overall ctDNA abundance, this association should be interpreted with caution. After adjusting for baseline ctDNA concentration as a proxy for tumor burden, the correlation between median ctDNA and FFPE VAFs was no longer observed (Spearman ρ = 0.02 [−0.25 to 0.29]), and FFPE median VAF was not independently associated with ctDNA median VAF in an adjusted multivariable linear regression model (data not shown).

Variants detected in FFPE but not in ctDNA had lower allelic fractions in tumor tissue (median VAF, 13.6% vs 21% for shared variants). Likewise, ctDNA-only variants showed low allelic fractions in plasma (median VAF, 3% vs 9.6% for shared variants). Overall, discordant variants were predominantly low-VAF mutations, suggesting that low tumor allele frequency and limited plasma shedding may explain their absence across compartments.

Exploratory comparisons of baseline genomic features between progressors and nonprogressors were performed among those with available data. Among progressors, baseline plasma was available for ctDNA mutational profiling in 9 cases (7 with assessable SCNA data), and matched FFPE samples were available in 7. No significant differences in AID-related mutational signatures, somatic mutation patterns, or SCNA were observed between progressors and nonprogressors (data not shown). However, in an exploratory analysis, baseline ctDNA–derived tumor mutational burden was higher in progressors than in nonprogressors (median, 484 vs 266; P = .029; supplemental Table 11; supplemental Figure 6).

Prognostic value of PET

At PET2, no significant difference in PFS was observed, whether assessed by ΔSUVmax (P = .22) or Deauville Score (DS) (P = .095; supplemental Figure 7A-B). At PET4, DS4 was frequent among nonprogressors (23/63 with available DS data), highlighting the limited specificity of DS4 in PMBL; in contrast, ΔSUVmax positivity (≤70% reduction) was observed in only 4 of 62 nonprogressors with available ΔSUVmax data (Figure 3). One-year PFS was 93.2% (87.7-99.1) in PET4-negative patients by ΔSUVmax, compared with 62.5% (36.5-100) in PET4-positive patients (P < 10−4; supplemental Figure 8A). Using DS, 1-year PFS was 91.7% (83.1-100) for DS1 to DS3; 95.5% (87.1-100) for DS4, and decreased to 70% (46.7-100) for DS5 (P = .079; supplemental Figure 8B). Prognostic value of MRD

Serial measurements showed a marked decline in ctDNA load during treatment (Figure 4). Among 73 patients assessed at both time points, undetectable MRD was reached in 58 of 73 (79.5%) after 2 cycles (MRD2; supplemental Figure 9) and in 64 of 73 (87.7%) after 4 cycles (MRD4; Figures 3 and 4; supplemental Figure 10) of immunochemotherapy. To further characterize MRD persistence after cycle 4, we examined plasma-detectable mutations among MRD4-positive patients (n = 9). As shown in supplemental Figure 11, post–cycle 4 variants largely overlapped with the baseline mutational landscape and involved recurrently altered PMBL genes, with no shared or specific mutational pattern. These findings suggest that MRD persistence reflects incomplete tumor clearance rather than retention of distinct genetic lesions.

Accordingly, MRD4 status was strongly prognostic for PFS, with a 1-year PFS of 98.4% (95.4%-100%) in patients with undetectable MRD4 compared with 33.3% (13.2%-84%) in those with detectable MRD4 (P < 10−4; supplemental Figure 12).

Prognostic value of MRD in comparison and in combination with PET

MRD4 outperformed PET4 in predicting progressions, with higher sensitivity (100% vs 44%; P = .001) and PPV (89% vs 50%), whereas both methods demonstrated similarly high specificity (99% vs 94%; P = .17) and NPV (100% vs 93%; Table 2). All progressors were MRD4+, whereas 98.5% of nonprogressors were MRD4− (P < .001). PET4 was also significantly associated with progression: 44.4% of progressors were PET-positive, compared with only 5.5% of nonprogressors (P = .004). The combination of ctDNA and PET at cycle 4 was discriminative: all progressors had a MRD4+ result, and 62.5% were PET4− but MRD4+, highlighting the added sensitivity of ctDNA (P < .001; supplemental Table 15). Univariate and multivariate analysis of prognostic factors associated with PFS

In univariate analysis, elevated C-reactive protein, LDH, and DS5 at PET4 were all associated with shorter PFS (Table 3). Baseline ctDNA concentration and mean VAF also emerged as strong prognostic markers. PET4+ and MRD4+ were also significantly associated with shorter PFS (Table 3). A double-negative PET4/MRD4 status was associated with a significantly better outcome (hazard ratio, 0.02; 95% CI,0-0.16) when tested against all other combinations: PET4+/MRD4+, PET4−/MRD4+, or PET4+/MRD4−. Univariate and multivariate analysis of the prognostic factors associated with PFS

Variables for the multivariate Cox regression were selected based on their clinical relevance, their association with PFS in univariate analysis, and the need to preserve model stability given the limited number of events. Although several factors were associated with PFS in univariate analysis, a parsimonious subset was retained for multivariate modeling to avoid overfitting. The “bulky disease” variable could not be included due to nonconvergence issues. Although TMTV could be considered as a proxy, it did not reach statistical significance in univariate analysis and, in addition, its inclusion would have led to the exclusion of 10 patients (due to missing data), thereby reducing statistical power without providing consistent independent prognostic information. LDH was not retained either, because it showed strong collinearity with both baseline ctDNA concentration and mean VAF, preventing its integration into the final model. Overall, the final model was built on 61 patients with complete data for all selected covariates, ensuring stability and interpretability. Its goodness of fit was supported by information criteria values (BIC = 49; AIC = 38), reflecting an adequate balance between model complexity and explanatory performance.

aaIPI, age-adjusted International Prognostic Index; AIC, Akaike information criterion; BIC, Bayesian information criterion; CMR, complete metabolic response; CNS-IPI, central nervous system–International Prognostic Index; CRP, C-reactive protein; HCD, high clonal dominance; IPI, International Prognostic Index; NE, not estimable; PDL1/PDL2, programmed death-ligand 1/2; RT-MLPA, reverse transcription multiplex ligation-dependent probe amplification; ULN, upper limit of normal.

The mean VAF represents the average allele frequencies of the variants detected in baseline ctDNA.

For multivariate analysis, we limited the model to variables with independent prognostic value to avoid collinearity and overfitting. The bulky disease variable could not be included due to nonconvergence of the Cox model, and although TMTV could be considered as a proxy, it did not reach statistical significance in univariate analysis and, in addition, its inclusion would have led to the exclusion of 10 additional patients due to missing data, thereby reducing statistical power without providing consistent independent prognostic information. LDH was not retained either, because it was highly correlated with both baseline ctDNA concentration and mean VAF. The final multivariate model therefore included C-reactive protein, baseline ctDNA concentration, mean VAF, and the combined PET4/MRD4 status. Among these, only the double-negative PET4/MRD4 profile remained significantly associated with longer PFS (adjusted hazard ratio, 0.07; 95% CI, 0.01-0.90; Table 3).

Discussion

In this study, we show that ctDNA analysis using CAPP-seq provides highly informative genetic profiling in patients with PMBL. At baseline, genetic markers suitable for MRD tracking were detectable in 98% of patients, with a strong concordance between mutations identified in plasma and those found in matched tumor biopsies. These findings are consistent with previous studies in classical Hodgkin lymphoma (cHL) and different types of aggressive and indolent BCLs.21,22,31, 32, 33, 34 Given that the sensitivity of MRD detection is directly proportional to the number of available genetic markers, these results collectively suggest that PMBL represents an ideal lymphoma subtype for MRD assessment.

Notwithstanding the high cure rate, 2 major clinical challenges exist in PMBL. First, the early identification of patients with primary chemotherapy-refractory disease as the chance of their cure with second line are broadening, including chimeric antigen receptor T-cell therapies, and combinations of brentuximab with anti–programmed cell death protein 1.8,35, 36, 37 Second, the early identification of patients with highly chemotherapy-sensitive disease, who might benefit from treatment deescalation, as for patients with cHL and limited stage LBCL. We show that MRD after 4 cycles of first-line immunochemotherapy is strongly prognostic in PMBL, independently of clinical scores or PET findings, and provides a nearly 100% accuracy in the discrimination of outcome. In multivariate analysis, the combination of PET4 negativity and MRD4 clearance identified a subgroup of patients with excellent outcomes, suggesting that these dual markers could serve as a composite early end point in future response-adapted strategies. Consistent with previous studies in PMBL, the DS4 has limited prognostic utility.12,13,38 In this subtype, DS4 frequently reflects posttreatment inflammation or fibrosis rather than residual viable lymphoma, and patients with DS4 generally experience excellent outcomes.2,39,40 This pattern was also observed in our cohort, in which most patients with DS4 were MRD-negative and did not experience disease relapse, highlighting the limited specificity of DS4 as a standalone prognostic marker. MRD may help discriminate true responders from false positives, potentially avoiding overtreatment and unnecessary invasive procedures. Persistent MRD4, despite subsequent immunochemotherapy, appears to be a robust indicator of chemotherapy resistance. This observation supports the rationale for developing dedicated risk-adapted strategies for MRD-positive patients, as explored in emerging ctDNA-stratified trials in LBCL (ClinicalTrials.gov identifier: NCT04980222).

Although MRD4 was the strongest single prognostic marker, it was not perfectly specific in our cohort (PPV of 89%). Of note, the only patient with detectable MRD4 who did not experience disease relapse had a negative PET4 result. This discordant case illustrates that PET4 provides complementary, nonredundant information by helping to contextualize potential false-positive MRD signals. Conversely, PET4-positive/MRD4-negative cases, frequent in PMBL due to posttreatment inflammation or fibrosis, did not experience progression, emphasizing the value of MRD for interpreting PET4 abnormalities. Thus, the 2 modalities capture distinct biological dimensions (metabolic vs molecular), and their combination, rather than either test alone, most accurately identifies patients at very low or high risk of relapse.

Interestingly, baseline ctDNA levels were higher in stage I/II than in stage III/IV disease and did not correlate with bulky status or TMTV. Although counterintuitive, this pattern is biologically plausible in PMBL. Localized mediastinal tumors may exhibit marked inflammation, necrosis, and metabolic activation, which might promote disproportionate ctDNA shedding independent of total tumor volume. Disseminated PMBL may conversely represent a biologically distinct phenotype, composed of multiple smaller lesions with lower per-lesion shedding. Another possibility is that differences in metabolic heterogeneity may contribute to variability in ctDNA release. High intratumoral heterogeneity of fluorodeoxyglucose uptake has been shown to predict chemotherapy resistance and inferior PFS in PMBL.41 Although radiomic analyses were not performed in our study, integrating ctDNA with baseline metabolic heterogeneity may represent an interesting avenue for future work.42

Unlike PET4 and MRD4, early assessments after 2 cycles of therapy did not show a strong correlation with PFS. This suggests that, in contrast to cHL or LBCL, early metabolic or molecular responses may not reliably predict long-term outcomes in patients with PMBL. This discrepancy likely reflects the unique biology of PMBL, which may require a greater number of treatment cycles to achieve maximal therapeutic effect. Additionally, the frequent presence of bulky disease and inflammatory or fibrotic components in PMBL can confound the interpretation of PET2 results,20 whereas early MRD measurements may not capture the full dynamics of tumor clearance, limiting their prognostic utility at this early time point. This observation is important for clinical trial design, because it argues against overinterpretation of early PET or MRD results and supports evaluation at later treatment time points (eg, after 4 cycles or EoT) as more informative for risk stratification and treatment adaptation.

The sample size and number of progression events were limited, reflecting the favorable prognosis of PMBL with dose-dense regimens, which may have reduced the precision of some estimates. PET scans were interpreted locally and later retrospectively reviewed centrally, which could introduce variability. ctDNA was analyzed retrospectively, which may limit the generalizability of findings to real-world practice. We acknowledge that the median follow-up of 21 months is short compared with the 5-year benchmark typically used to assess long-term outcomes in PMBL. Given that late relapses, although infrequent, can occur, longer longitudinal follow-up will be essential to confirm the durability of responses, particularly in the PET4−/MRD4− group. Importantly, MRD was assessed only after 4 cycles, and plasma was not collected at EoT. Therefore, MRD4 represents an interim assessment. This distinction is critical, because EoT MRD is the standard prognostic landmark in LBCL studies,43,44 and our results cannot be extrapolated to those frameworks. The absence of EoT sampling limits our ability to determine how many MRD4+ patients might have subsequently cleared ctDNA after consolidation, or whether undetectable MRD4 fully reflects molecular remission. In the absence of routinely available hypersensitive ctDNA assays, repeat PET after EoT for patients with a PET-positive EoT result but no other evidence of progression remains an important approach to distinguish inflammatory uptake from residual active disease; broader implementation of highly sensitive ctDNA-based MRD testing could ultimately reduce the need for repeat imaging.

The analytical sensitivity of the CAPP-seq assay has a detection limit of ∼10−3, lower than that of more recent phased-variants approaches,33,43,45 achieving sensitivities down to 10−6. However, such ultrasensitive detection may not be necessary in PMBL. In our cohort, baseline ctDNA levels were high (median, 755 hGE/mL), 2 to 3 times greater than typically reported in LBCL.22,33,46 At this ctDNA burden, defining MRD negativity as undetectable ctDNA at the CAPP-seq detection limit seems appropriate and likely sufficient to capture a meaningful molecular response associated with excellent prognosis.

Although the study was prospective, treatment decisions were not informed by ctDNA results. Future interventional trials should evaluate ctDNA-guided strategies based on the integration of MRD4 monitoring into future risk-adapted trials in PMBL, with the aim of improving therapeutic precision while minimizing treatment-related toxicity.
