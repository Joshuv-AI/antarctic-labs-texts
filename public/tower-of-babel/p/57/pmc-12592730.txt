Evolutionary dynamics of obligate endosymbiosis in the psyllid genus Cacopsylla
Authors: Erika Corretto, Liliya Štarhová Serbina, Jessica Dittmer, Anna Michalik, Hannes Schuler
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
Phloem-sucking hemipterans maintain obligate, heritable symbiotic bacteria to overcome nutritional deficiencies caused by their unbalanced diet. While some groups retain ancient primary symbionts, others supplement or replace them with additional symbionts conferring adaptive benefits. Here, we explore the diversity and evolutionary dynamics of multiple endosymbionts in psyllids of the genus Cacopsylla. While the primary symbiont Carsonella is found in all analysed species, the ancient co-primary symbiont Psyllophila is absent in Cacopsylla pyrisuga, which instead harbours a Sodalis symbiont with a larger genome, located in the syncytium of the bacteriome. Phylogenomic analyses demonstrated co-divergence of Carsonella and Psyllophila with their hosts, whereas the occurrence of closely related Sodalis across different psyllid lineages indicates several independent acquisitions. The presence of additional facultative Sodalis and Serratia symbionts further highlights the dynamic host-symbiont relationships in psyllids and their essential roles in insect niche adaptation and evolution.

Introduction

Symbiotic associations between eukaryotic and prokaryotic lineages have promoted the diversification of multicellular organisms, contributing to a great proportion of the planet’s biodiversity1,2. In particular, the symbiotic relationships in phytophagous insects played a central role in shaping their ecology by enabling these organisms to occupy new ecological niches and to feed on a nutritionally unbalanced diet3,4. Several groups of phytophagous insects harbour primary bacterial symbionts, which are obligate endosymbionts necessary for the survival of the host. These are usually confined in specialised organs, bacteriomes, and are vertically transmitted to the offspring5. In most cases, these symbionts underwent genome reduction due to their host-associated lifestyle and lost genes involved in cellular regulation, DNA repair, secondary metabolism and autonomous replication. Nevertheless, they retained the genes for the synthesis of nutrients such as essential amino acids (EAAs) and vitamins, that are usually lacking in the insect’s plant sap diet6. Over time, any host-symbiont association is doomed to drag the symbiont into the evolutionary “rabbit hole”, where the host’s efforts to compensate for the essential functions lost by the symbiont paradoxically encourage the symbiont to further streamline its metabolic functions and drastically reduce its genome7,8. When the essential nutritional pathways are compromised in the primary symbiont as well, a dual or multiple symbiosis with another bacterium or fungus can be established. These co-primary symbionts complement the missing genes to rescue the symbiosis and are therefore often located in close proximity with the primary symbiont6,9,10.

Another escape route consists in the replacement of the highly degraded symbiont with a newly acquired one11,12. These are usually sourced from secondary or facultative symbionts, which at first are not essential for the host survival but can provide additional benefits such as protection against natural enemies or detoxification of allelochemicals5,13,14. In general, the replacement of primary symbionts is relatively rare. Such events have been observed in sap feeding hemipterans such as adelgids15, aphids16,17, different phloem-feeding Auchenorrhyncha18 as well as in some weevils19. Despite their surprisingly degraded and reduced genomes, long-term primary associates can present striking genome stability with characteristic low rates of molecular evolution, which may help them to avoid destruction by preventing the fixation of deleterious mutations20. In contrast, co-primary symbionts are often characterised by unstable genomes which are prone to increased fixation of deleterious mutations and elevated rates of nucleotide substitutions, leading to occasional symbiont replacement events20,21. Several studies on phytophagous insects revealed that symbionts were acquired and replaced prior to the diversification of their hosts. Indeed, the phylogenies of some insect lineages and their primary symbionts are congruent, implying that these symbionts formed stable associations and co-diverged with their hosts21,22.

Like other sap-feeding insects, psyllids (Hemiptera: Psylloidea) maintain obligate and heritable relationships with symbiotic bacteria. The primary symbiont ‘Candidatus Carsonella ruddii’ (hereafter Carsonella) has a highly reduced genome (157–175 kb) and is ubiquitous in psyllids, displaying a stable association with its hosts9,21,23,24. While only a few species were described to be associated only with Carsonella9,25, most psyllids harbour an additional symbiont that varies depending on the psyllid species and may have undergone sequential loss and replacement events associated with the colonisation of new niches12,21,25,26. As found in other sap-feeding insects like aphids10,27 and planthoppers28,29, psyllids are often associated with co-primary symbionts that have bigger genomes than the primary symbiont Carsonella. As such, the Sodalis symbionts identified so far in Heteropsylla cubana (Psyllidae), Bactericera trigonica (Triozidae), and Ctenarytaina eucalypti (Aphalaridae)9,30,31 have much bigger genomes (>1 Mb) than the ancient Carsonella, suggesting a more recent acquisition. In Diaphorina spp. (Psyllidae), ‘Candidatus Profftella armatura’ (hereafter Profftella) has a more reduced genome of 460 kb and also harbours a 5.5 kb plasmid13. This bacterium is considered a nutritional and defensive symbiont due to its ability to produce the toxins diaphorin and hemolysin as well as carotenoids, riboflavin and biotin13,32. There are, however, several examples of co-primary symbionts having highly reduced genomes. Among these, Psyllophila from Cacopsylla spp. (Psyllidae) has the smallest genome (221–231 kb) but is still able to complement the genes for the synthesis of tryptophan, carotenoids, riboflavin and biotin that are missing in Carsonella24. Recent studies revealed the presence of closely related symbionts: ‘Secondary_AM’ in Anomoneura mori (Psyllidae)33 and ‘Candidatus Triozidicola socius’ in Trioza apicalis (Triozidae)34 have similar genome sizes, GC content and metabolic complementation patterns as Psyllophila, providing the biosynthetic genes missing in the corresponding Carsonella33,34. While Psyllophila and Profftella are located in the syncytial region of the bacteriome13,24, Sodalis of B. trigonica was found not only in the bacteriocytes but also in the nuclei of the midgut cells30.

Metabarcoding studies targeting the 16S rRNA gene showed that Carsonella and a Psyllophila-like Enterobacteriaceae bacterium are the most abundant taxa in most of the investigated Cacopsylla species, sometimes in combination with others such as Arsenophonus, Rickettsia, Serratia, Sodalis, Spiroplasma and Wolbachia31,35–37. In contrast, C. burckhardti and C. pyrisuga harbour Sodalis instead of an Enterobacteriaceae bacterium, suggesting the replacement of the ancient Psyllophila in these species31,36. The psyllid genus Cacopsylla is therefore an excellent study system for the investigation of the dynamics of a dual symbiosis24,33. In this study, we investigated the dynamic evolutionary relationships between these psyllids and their associated symbionts by sequencing the metagenomes of additional Cacopsylla species to further expand the dataset presented in our previous work24. The newly analysed species include C. mali, C. pruni and C. pyrisuga, which are known to harbour different symbionts based on 16S rRNA gene metabarcoding35,36. Considering that the Carsonella-Psyllophila symbiosis is likely becoming unstable due to the lack of functional redundancy between the two symbionts and the ongoing genome degradation of Psyllophila24, it is possible that co-occurring facultative symbionts could not only provide the same functions as Psyllophila but also additional ones due to their more complete genomes. Our extended metagenomic dataset allowed us to assemble the genome of the Sodalis co-primary symbiont present in C. pyrisuga, confirming the replacement of Psyllophila in this species. The new genome was used to determine its phylogenetic relationships with other Sodalis found in the psyllids H. cubana and Ct. eucalypti as well as in other insect groups. Analysis of its metabolic potential as well as its localisation within the bacteriome revealed a close collaboration of Sodalis and Carsonella in the provision of nutrients for the psyllid host. Furthermore, our metagenomic approach enabled us to reconstruct the genomes of symbionts that might represent additional partners aiding the co-primary symbionts in supporting the host or conferring adaptive benefits such as resistance to environmental stressors or pathogens. Our results highlight key evolutionary events like symbiont gain, loss and replacements in phloem sucking hemipterans, including vectors of agricultural pests.

The newly assembled complete circular genomes of the primary symbiont Carsonella display features similar to the ones found in related Cacopsylla spp. The size of the newly produced genomes ranges from 167,918 bp to 173,941 bp with a GC content of 15.2% to 15.5%. They have 193 to 217 coding sequences (CDSs), a single rRNA operon and 27 tRNAs.

The complete circular genomes of the co-primary symbiont Psyllophila of C. mali and C. pruni are also similar to the already published ones (Table 1). Psyllophila of C. mali is 222,308 bp long with a GC content of 17.9% and has 201 CDSs. It has a higher number of pseudogenes compared to the other Psyllophila genomes, and some of these pseudogenes are involved in RNA processing and binding (smpB, rplP, rsmH), in the synthesis of proteins (glyQ, ileS, leuS, valS), vitamins (bioA) and carotenoids (crtY). Compared to the other genomes, Psyllophila of C. pruni is slightly longer with 238,558 bp, a GC content of 18.7% and 215 CDSs (Table 1).

Cacopsylla mali is additionally associated with a Serratia sp

From the metagenomic data of C. mali, we could also assemble the complete circular genome of Serratia sp. SERmali. This genome has a total length of 5,283,208 bp, a GC content of 59.3% and consists of one circular chromosome and two plasmids (Table 1 and Fig. 1A). Overall, it encodes 4833 CDS, 91 tRNAs and seven copies of the rRNA operon plus a single 5S rRNA gene. The different copies of the 16S rRNA gene showed 93–97% identity with several Serratia marcescens strains and 91–93% identity with Serratia symbiotica strains found in aphids. Cacopsylla pyrisuga harbours a more recently acquired Sodalis instead of Psyllophila

No Psyllophila reads were detected in the metagenomic data from C. pyrisuga. Instead, we assembled the genomes of two Sodalis strains (Table 1 and Fig. 2). The first genome of a Sodalis sp. (hereafter SODpyris1) was assembled using the data from two adult females of C. pyrisuga sequenced on a MinION (Oxford Nanopore Technologies). The assembly resulted in four scaffolds with a total length of 1,807,139 bp, an N50 value of 1,744,785 bp and a GC content of 43.7% (Table 1 and Fig. 2A). It encodes 1141 CDSs, one rRNA operon and 37 tRNAs (Table 1). BLAST analysis of the 16S rRNA gene showed 97% identity with S. praecaptivus HS1 isolated from a human wound (CP006569.1), 95% identity with the endosymbiont of Ct. eucalypti isolate Ceuc_S (CP003546.1) and 93% identity with the endosymbiont of H. cubana isolate Hcub_S (CP003547.1). Additionally, phylogenetic analysis showed that SODpyris1 clustered together with Sodalis ASVs previously identified in C. pyrisuga collected in the same region36 (Fig. S2). While the two longest scaffolds have a similar GC content (43.8%, 44.0%) and showed similarity with the chromosomes of other Sodalis strains, the two shorter scaffolds had lower GC content (37.3%, 42.5%) and showed similarity with the plasmid pSG1 of Sodalis glossinidius (NC_007713.1). Additionally, the two shorter scaffolds have a higher coverage (63x, 83x) compared to the longer scaffolds (41x, 43x), suggesting that they might represent plasmids or repetitive sequences. However, we could not integrate these into the bigger scaffolds nor circularise them to verify that they either represent repetitive sequences within the main chromosome or plasmids. COG category analysis of SODpyris1 showed that the two chromosomal scaffolds harbour genes covering all functional classes (Fig. 2B). In contrast, the two shorter scaffolds harboured only genes for cellular processing and signalling (O, V), information storage and processing (J, K, L), phage-derived proteins (X) and genes with poorly characterised functions (R, S), further suggesting that they might represent plasmids or repetitive regions. A second genome of a Sodalis sp. (hereafter SODpyris2) was assembled using only the Illumina data from a single female. The draft genome has a total length of 4,665,964 bp and is divided into six scaffolds with a GC content of 57.5% and an N50 of 2,587,673 bp (Table 1 and Fig. 2C). It encodes 4039 CDSs, one rRNA operon and 63 tRNAs. Its 16S rRNA gene showed 99% identity with S. praecaptivus HS1 (CP006569.1), which also has a comparable genome size (4.7 Mb). However, no scaffold of SODpyris2 had similarity with the plasmid pHS1 (CP006570.1) of S. praecaptivus. Additionally, we could detect only one copy of the rRNA operon, while S. praecaptivus harbours seven copies plus two additional copies of the 5S rRNA gene. All COG categories are represented in the genome of SODpyris2 with the exception of nuclear structure (Y) and chromatin structure and dynamics (B) (Fig. 2D).

In contrast, SODpyris1 clusters together with the endosymbiont of Ct. eucalypti isolate Ceuc_S from the distantly related family Aphalaridae, which has a comparable genome size (1.4 Mb). To determine the frequency of SODpyris1 and SODpyris2 within samples from the same population (n = 16), we performed a PCR screening targeting unique genes from each strain (Table S1). While SODpyris1 was present in all samples, we could not detect SODpyris2 in any of the screened individuals. These results suggest that SODpyris2 might be present in this population at very low frequency. In fact, only the females used for Nanopore and Illumina sequencing harboured this strain. In particular, based on GC content analysis of the sequenced reads we suspect that one or both individuals used for Nanopore sequencing harboured the two Sodalis strains (two clear peaks at 37% and 50% GC content), while the female used for Illumina sequencing had a higher titre of SODpyris2 compared to SODPyris1 (one broad peak around 55% with a tail around 40%) (Figure. Genes involved in host colonisation and synthesis of secondary metabolites in Sodalis and Serratia associated with Cacopsylla species

The large genome size of both Sodalis strains and Serratia compared to the other two major Cacopsylla symbionts, Carsonella and Psyllophila, indicates that the former were acquired more recently. This hypothesis is consistent with a higher number of genes related to host colonisation, virulence and pathogenicity present in SODpyris2 compared to SODpyris1 (Table S2). Within the RAST38 subsystem for virulence, disease and defence, both Sodalis strains possess genes involved in invasion and intracellular resistance (i.e. Mycobacterium virulence operon), but SODpyris2 has more genes for resistance to antibiotics and toxic compounds. The components of the Tol translocation system (TolR, TolA, TolB, TolC, TolQ and Pal) are present in both Sodalis. However, SODpyris2 has additional membrane transport systems, including the type III secretion system, which are used for the secretion of virulence factors (Table S2). Moreover, SODpyris1 seems to harbour a modified outer membrane protein (ompA) sharing only 77% amino acid identity with that of SODpyris2 and S. praecaptivus, 76% with S. glossinidius and 72% with Sodalis of the spittlebug Philenus spumarius. In contrast, ompA of SODpyris2 shares 100% amino acid identity with S. praecaptivus, 99% with the Sodalis endosymbiont of the parasitic wasp Spalangia cameroni, 96% with S. glossinidius and 90% with Sodalis of P. spumarius. Interestingly, ompA could not be detected in the endosymbionts of H. cubana and Ct. eucalypti, while the Sodalis-like symbiont of B. trigonica shares 71% and 89% amino acid identity with SODpyris1 and SODpyris2, respectively. Genes for motility (i.e. biosynthesis of the flagellum and the motor protein) and iron acquisition (i.e. siderophore production, hemin transport system) were found only in SODpyris2 (Table S2). Analysis of mobile genetic elements revealed the presence of two prophage regions in SODpyris2, while SODpyris1 has only one incomplete prophage region. In contrast, the number of insertion sequence (IS) elements is higher in SODpyris1 (n = 44) compared to SODpyris2 (n = 8) (Table S2). These numbers can be explained by a lack of CRISPR-Cas and anti-phage defence systems in SODpyris1, while SODpyris2 harbours 13 CRISPR-Cas and three different types of anti-phage systems (PD-lambda-4, Mokosh, PrrC) (Table S2). In terms of signalling molecules, both possess genes for the synthesis of auxins, but only SODpyris2 harbours an acyl-homoserine-lactone (AHL) synthase for the synthesis of AHLs involved in quorum sensing mechanisms (Table S2). This gene is present in other Sodalis genomes such as S. praecaptivus (99–100% identity at amino acid level), S. glossinidius (94%), S. pierantonius (97%) and S. ligni (78%).

We compared Serratia sp. SERmali with a related pathogenic Serratia marcescens Db11 from the fly Drosophila melanogaster and two strains of Serratia symbiotica from aphids, namely the gut pathogen 24.1 and the vertically transmitted mutualist IS (Table S2). Overall, SERmali presents a wide array of genes related to host colonisation, virulence and pathogenicity. The genomes of SERmali and S. marcescens Db11 encode a higher number of genes related to resistance to antibiotics and toxic compounds and adhesion, which are absent in the two strains of S. symbiotica (Table S2). All analysed Serratia strains harbour several genes for membrane transport, including the Ton/Tol transport system. While the ompA gene of the two S. symbiotica strains IS and 24.1 seems not functional or absent, ompA of SERmali seems to be intact and is similar to other S. marcescens strains, including Db11, and other species like S. ureilytica (99–100% amino acid identity). The production of siderophores and molecules for iron acquisition is widespread within all four strains (Table S2). In particular, SERmali possesses genes showing similarity with the clusters for the synthesis of aerobactin and enterobactin. Genes for motility and chemotaxis were only found in S. marcescens Db11 (Table S2). The number of CRISPR-Cas and anti-phage defence systems of SERmali is comparable with the one found in S. symbiotica IS and 24.1. However, the latter contains a higher number of mobile genetic elements such as prophages and IS elements (Table S2).

Prediction of secondary metabolite gene clusters (BGCs) using antiSMASH resulted in six putative clusters in SODpyris2, whereas no BGC was predicted in SODpyris1 (Table S3). Besides the AHL synthase mentioned above, these included a type III polyketide synthase (PKS). This PKS is present in other Sodalis genomes with an amino acid similarity ranging from 80 to 100%, while it has lower similarity with other PKS of Streptomyces (63%) and Pseudomonas (57%). Unfortunately, no prediction of the produced compound could be made due to the low similarity with known clusters. SERmali had a higher number of predicted clusters (10 BGCs) compared to SODpyris2 (Table S3). The majority of the BGCs belong to the non-ribosomal peptide synthetase (NRPS) category but have no clear similarity with known BGCs. In contrast, an opine-like metallophore BGC is likely to produce the iron chelator yersinopine. Genes for the synthesis of prodigiosin, an insecticidal molecule produced by S. marcescens KH-001 isolated from D. citri infected with Huanglongbing (HLB) disease39, were not found in SERmali.

Metabolic complementarity in Cacopsylla symbioses

Carsonella and Psyllophila in C. mali and C. pruni maintain metabolic complementarity for most pathways involved in the synthesis of EAAs, carotenoids and the vitamins biotin and riboflavin, as previously shown in other Cacopsylla species24, with a few exceptions (Fig. 4A). The aroKB gene of the chorismate pathway and the pheA1 gene involved in the synthesis of phenylalanine and tyrosine are missing in both Carsonella and Psyllophila. In C. pyrisuga, a few genes involved in the synthesis of EAAs are present in both Carsonella and SODpyris1, indicating some redundancy due to the less degraded genome of SODpyris1 (Fig. 4A). This is the case for carA-1 and carB involved in arginine synthesis; lysC, asd, dapABDEF of the lysine and threonine pathways; aroBQEA of the chorismate pathway and trpE, which is involved in the production of tryptophan. In addition, SODpyris1 provides some genes missing in Carsonella, thus rescuing the biosynthetic pathways for chorismate with aroEKB and for tryptophan with trpDFCAB. The argG gene could not be detected in both Carsonella and SODpyris1, but it is present in SODpyris2. Interestingly, SODpyris1 provides the vitamins biotin and riboflavin, with a slightly more complete pathway for the latter compared to Psyllophila, but it lacks the genes required for synthesis of carotenoids (Fig. 4A). Overall, the metabolic potential of SODpyris1 in C. pyrisuga is more similar to the endosymbiont of Ct. eucalypti isolate Ceuc_S than the endosymbiont of H. cubana isolate Hcub_S (Fig. 4A). This finding aligns with the phylogenetic placement of these symbionts in our analyses (Figs. 3 and S3).

When examining SODpyris2, its genome contains not only nearly all the genes required for the synthesis of EAAs, biotin and riboflavin, but also genes for the synthesis of non-essential amino acids, other vitamins, cofactors and genes involved in metabolic processes such as glycolysis, the pentose phosphate pathway and TCA cycle (Fig. 4B). These genes are also present in the closely related Sodalis species, which share a similar genome size (around 4 Mb). In contrast, SODpyris1 and the two other related Sodalis symbionts of psyllids (Ceuc_S, Hcub_S) lost several of these genes. For instance, the TCA cycle and the thiamine synthesis pathway are highly degraded, retaining only one or two genes.

Similarly to SODpyris2, Serratia sp. SERmali found in C. mali has a complete set of genes for the synthesis of EAAs, biotin and riboflavin, as well as non-essential amino acids, other vitamins, cofactors and genes involved in metabolic processes (Fig. 4). Comparison with other insect associated Serratia revealed that SERmali has a similar metabolic potential as the closely related S. marcescens Db11 and the aphid gut pathogen S. symbiotica 24.1, while in the vertically transmitted S. symbiotica IS several genes for the synthesis of EAAs and biotin are missing.

Genome-wide substitution rates in the dual symbioses of Carsonella – Psyllophila and Carsonella – Sodalis

Genome-wide substitution rates were calculated for Carsonella and Psyllophila using pairs of species from the same Cacopsylla clades identified in our phylogenetic analyses presented below (Table S4 and Fig. S5). Rates of synonymous (Ks) and nonsynonymous (Ka) substitutions showed that Psyllophila (Ka 0.081 ± 0.039; Ks 0.976 ± 0.464) had higher average Ks and Ka values compared to Carsonella (Ka 0.042 ± 0.027; Ks 0.676 ± 0.377) (Mann–Whitney U Test Ka and Ks, P < 0.001) (Table S4 and Fig. S5). Ks = 0 was observed only for one gene in the comparison of the Carsonella symbionts of the closely related species C. melanoneura – C. pruni and two genes in the comparison of C. pyri – C. pyricola. Synonymous divergence appeared saturated (Ks > 2) in 2% of genes (20 out of 748) and 21.72% of genes (139 out of 640) in Carsonella and Psyllophila, respectively. The average Ka/Ks value was also higher in Psyllophila (0.122 ± 0.108) compared to Carsonella (0.101 ± 0.127) (Mann–Whitney U Test, P < 0.001) and only three genes were under positive selection with a Ka/Ks > 1. Two of them are short hypothetical proteins from Carsonella of C. pyrisuga and C. picta, while the third one is an aminotransferase class IV (COG class E, amino acid transport and metabolism) harboured by Psyllophila of C. pyri and C. pyricola.

Genome-wide substitution rates were also calculated for Carsonella and Sodalis sp. SODpyris1 of C. pyrisuga in comparison to the same symbionts present in the psyllids Ct. eucalypti and H. cubana (Table S4 and Fig. S5). In C. pyrisuga, SODpyris1 (Ka 0.164 ± 0.120; Ks 1.123 ± 0.375) had lower average Ks and Ka values compared to Carsonella (Ka 0.198 ± 0.168; Ks 1.230 ± 0.411) (Mann–Whitney U Test Ka and Ks, P < 0.001 and P = 0.005, respectively) (Table S4 and Fig. S5). Synonymous divergence appeared saturated (Ks > 2) in 55.26% of genes (126 out of 228) and 28.23% of genes (227 out of 804) in Carsonella and Sodalis, respectively. The average Ka/Ks value was higher in Carsonella (0.212 ± 0.162) compared to Sodalis (0.154 ± 0.103) (Mann–Whitney U Test, P = 0.041) and only two genes (both hypothetical proteins of Carsonella) were under positive selection with a Ka/Ks > 1.

Discussion

Psyllids (Hemiptera: Psylloidea) are phloem-feeding insects divided into seven families with several species associated with pathogens causing severe plant diseases worldwide42. Due to their unbalanced plant sap diet, they harbour bacterial symbionts that supplement EAAs, vitamins and, in some cases, carotenoids9,25. Carsonella has been found in all psyllid species studied so far. Despite its severely reduced genome, Carsonella retains most but not all genes required for the synthesis of EAAs. The missing genes together with pathways for the synthesis of vitamins and carotenoids are usually complemented by a second co-occurring symbiont with variable taxonomic affiliation (i.e. Profftella, Psyllophila and Sodalis)24,42. Previous phylogenetic analyses based on single gene markers showed that Carsonella follows the same evolutionary path as its psyllid hosts9,23. In contrast, the co-symbionts are more dynamic and can undergo replacements, which might be triggered by events such as changes in host diet, ecological niche or dictated by functional coordination with the host genome9,21,25.

In this study, we investigated the dual obligate symbionts as well as some facultative symbionts of Rosaceae-associated Cacopsylla species, which are commonly found in European orchards. Our metagenomic analyses included the data from four species presented in our previous study24 and new data from three additional Cacopsylla species. Our results confirmed the presence of Carsonella in all analysed psyllid species. In contrast, the co-symbiont Psyllophila was detected only in six of the seven species and was not present in C. pyrisuga, which instead harbours a Sodalis symbiont. While Carsonella and Psyllophila have co-diversified with their psyllid hosts, distinct Sodalis-like strains are present across different psyllid lineages, highlighting occasional gains and losses of symbionts in different psyllids9,12,23. Moreover, our metagenomic data revealed the presence of additional facultative symbionts affiliated with Serratia and Sodalis in C. mali and C. pyrisuga, respectively.

The newly sequenced Carsonella genomes of C. mali, C. pruni and C. pyrisuga have a similar length, GC content and gene synteny as the Carsonella genomes of the other four Cacopsylla species described in our previous study24. In C. mali and C. pruni, metabolic complementarity with the co-symbiont Psyllophila is maintained, with few exceptions. Most notably, the tryptophan pathway is absent in both symbionts of C. mali. This pathway is frequently absent in Carsonella from different psyllid families: Cacopsylla spp. and H. cubana (Psyllidae), Ct. eucalypti (Aphalaridae), P. celtidis and P. venusta (Carsidaridae). While in H. cubana, Ct. eucalypti and most Cacopsylla spp. this pathway can be rescued by the presence of Psyllophila or a Sodalis-like bacterium, no additional symbiont has been identified in P. celtidis and P. venusta so far9. Additionally, Carsonella from all analysed Cacopsylla species can only synthesise arginine starting from ornithine, since the genes for the conversion of glutamate to ornithine are lacking also in their co-primary symbionts. Therefore, these species likely adopted alternative strategies to synthesise all essential nutrients. The host itself might acquire the biosynthetic genes through horizontal gene transfers from previous symbiotic partners43–45. This has been observed in different psyllid species, such as Bactericera cockerelli, Diaphorina citri and P. venusta, where genes involved in the synthesis of arginine, phenylalanine and riboflavin are of prokaryotic origin43,45,46. Differences in the expression of these host genes appear to be species-specific, confined to specific tissues (i.e. the bacteriome), and influenced by the microbiome composition, which might contribute to nutrition43. Alternatively, plant sap-feeding insects can establish multipartite symbioses with additional bacterial and fungal symbionts, which can provide the missing genes47–49. Similarly to the symbiosis Carsonella–Psyllophila, the mealybug Pseudococcus viburni harbours three symbionts with reduced genomes (144, 281 and 754 kb) that have complementary metabolic pathways. While ‘Candidatus Tremblaya princeps’ provides most genes for the synthesis of EAAs, the other two ‘Candidatus Sodalis endoviburni’ strains harbour a few genes for the production of arginine, threonine, chorismate and riboflavin and most notably the full pathway for the synthesis of tryptophan. Despite the presence of the three symbionts, the production of arginine, cysteine, valine, lysine, phenylalanine and the vitamins biotin and riboflavin still rely on genes present in the insect host, some of which are of prokaryotic origin47. In some populations of the psyllid B. cockerelli, Carsonella can share the bacteriome with Wolbachia and the plant pathogen ‘Candidatus Liberibacter psyllaurous’. These symbionts can provide genes for the synthesis of EAAs and riboflavin. In particular, L. psyllaurous harbours the full pathway for the production of arginine, thus reducing the arginine production cost shared by Carsonella and B. cockerelli and also increasing the overall host fitness49.

The individuals of C. mali sequenced in this study harboured Serratia in addition to Carsonella and Psyllophila. Serratia symbionts were previously reported in psyllids31,50,51, including Cacopsylla coccinea which harbours a Serratia strain related to S. symbiotica of the aphid Acyrthosiphon pisum31. However, this genus was not found in a previous microbiome study of C. mali collected in the same region as the individuals used in this work35, suggesting that Serratia might only be occasionally associated with C. mali and could have been acquired horizontally from plants as reported in aphids52. Serratia sp. SERmali has a full set of genes for the synthesis of EAAs and can thus theoretically complement the genes for the production of tryptophan and arginine, missing in both Carsonella and Psyllophila. We cannot exclude a potential pathogenic role for this bacterium based on previous reports in the literature. Members of the S. marcescens complex have been reported as pathogens for several insects like flies, honeybees, mosquitoes and weevils53. In particular, S. marcescens KH-001 has a negative impact on the fitness of D. citri by lowering the titre of the symbionts Carsonella, Profftella and Wolbachia using different strategies such as the production of reactive oxygen species and prodigiosin39,51,54. If SERmali establishes a stable infection in the psyllid population, it could potentially replace Psyllophila and undergo the process of genome reduction and colonisation of a specialised organ such as the bacteriome2.

In contrast to most Cacopsylla species, C. pyrisuga harbours a Sodalis-like symbiont (SODpyris1) instead of the ancient co-symbiont Psyllophila. Members of this taxon have been previously found in ants, seed bugs, flies, lice, mealybugs, weevils55 as well as several other psyllid lineages9,12,30,56, including the closely related Asian pear psyllid C. burckhardti31. Our phylogenomic analyses demonstrate that SODpyris1 is more closely related to the endosymbiont of Ct. eucalypti from the distantly related family Aphalaridae, rather than to the endosymbiont of H. cubana, which, like C. pyrisuga, belongs to the family Psyllidae. While host-symbiont co-speciation has been observed for Sodalis of grain weevils57, independent acquisitions of members of this genus are reported in several feather louse species58. SODpyris1 is located in the syncytial part of the bacteriome, similarly to Psyllophila, ‘Secondary_AM’ and Profftella13,24,33. In contrast, the Sodalis-like symbionts of B. trigonica (Triozidae) and Aphalara itadori (Aphalaridae) reside in an intracellular niche within the midgut cells and in large uninucleated bacteriocytes, respectively30,56. SODpyris1 has a relatively large genome (1.8 Mb), that nevertheless underwent some reduction compared to its free-living relatives (4–5 Mb)59. While the complementarity in the Carsonella–Sodalis association is maintained in the pathways for biotin and riboflavin, some genes involved in the synthesis of arginine, lysine, threonine, chorismate and tryptophan are present in both. This redundancy, together with the larger genome size indicate that SODpyris1 has been acquired more recently, and the genome streamlining process is still ongoing.

In addition to SODpyris1, the sequenced individuals of C. pyrisuga harboured an additional Sodalis sp. (SODpyris2). A PCR screening with specific primers of individuals from the same locality confirmed only the presence of SODpyris1. Moreover, all Sodalis ASVs identified in our previous study clustered with SODpyris1, suggesting that SODpyris2 is present at very low frequency in the analysed populations. This might be the reason why we could not localise SODpyris2 within the tissues of C. pyrisuga, likely because the individuals used for FISH did not harbour this strain. These observations, together with its large genome size (4.7 Mb), phylogenetic placement and metabolic potential, indicate that SODpyris2 is a facultative symbiont. It is closely related to S. praecaptivus isolated from a human wound caused by contact with a dead crab apple tree, S. pierantonius from the weevil Sitophilus oryzae60 and Sodalis sp. from the louse Brueelia nebulosa61. SODpyris2 encodes pathways for the synthesis of both essential and non-essential amino acids, vitamins, cofactors and primary metabolism. For instance, as observed in L. psyllaurous of B. cockerelli49 and here in SERmali of C. mali, SODpyris2 harbours the full pathway for arginine synthesis starting from glutamate, thus offering an alternative pathway to the one present in Carsonella that requires the presence of ornithine, likely synthesised by the host. SODpyris2 also encodes genes that are usually associated with virulence in other Sodalis strains (i.e. type III and Tol secretion systems)55. Additionally, SODpyris2 encodes an AHL synthase similar to the one present in S. glossinidius (from the tse-tse fly), which uses a quorum-sensing system to control gene expression during the invasion of an insect host62. Another important difference between SODpyris1 and SODpyris2 lies in the low amino acid identity between ompA protein sequences. Proteins from the OmpA family not only have a structural role but they can also act as ion-permeable channels and, most notably, can be involved in pathogenicity through different mechanisms such as adherence, invasion and host defence evasion63. Indeed, a modification in this membrane protein is often used to bypass the defence system of the host and for the colonisation of inner organs64.

The investigation of symbiont losses, gains and replacements is a challenging task due to the complex interactions and factors that influence these evolutionary processes. The evolutionary transition from horizontal transmission to endosymbiosis and vertical transmission has been recently described in representatives of the genus Arsenophonus and can be roughly divided into two phases65. Early-stage endosymbionts are characterised by large genomes showing the depletion of defence systems (i.e. CRISPR-Cas) and an increase of mobile elements (i.e. plasmids, prophages, IS elements), thereby maximising the gain of new beneficial functions through horizontal gene transfer65. Only after the establishment of the endosymbiosis with vertical transmission, the symbiont initiates the process of genome reduction through an increased mutation rate, leading to the formation of pseudogenes, unstable proteins and metabolic degradation20,65. Our analyses reveal that SODpyris1 seems to be past this early-stage symbiosis. Despite the presence of putative plasmids, several IS elements and a lack of defence systems, other aspects, such as the small genome size, the high number of pseudogenes and degradation of some metabolic pathways of SODpyris1 compared to SODpyris2, indicate that the process of genome reduction has already started. However, the higher substitution rates observed in Psyllophila compared to the ones of Sodalis of C. pyrisuga, Ct. eucalypti and H. cubana, together with the striking difference in genome size, confirm that Sodalis was acquired more recently, and its genome reduction is proceeding at a slower rate than that of Psyllophila.

Our phylogenomic approach showed that Carsonella and Psyllophila co-diverged with their respective psyllid hosts, whereas Sodalis shows no correlation with the phylogeny of the psyllids. This suggests that these symbiotic Sodalis evolved independently in different psyllid lineages and might have originated from facultative symbionts, opportunistic or pathogenic bacteria acquired horizontally from the environment or from other insects. Considering the perfect metabolic complementarity without any redundant genes between Carsonella and Psyllophila and the observed high substitution rates, it is tempting to speculate that this symbiosis had reached a critical point either in C. pyrisuga or in one of its ancestral states. The ancient degraded co-primary symbiont no longer fulfilled its nutritional role, leading to the replacement of the compromised symbiont, possibly with a bacterium related to Sodalis sp. SODpyris1 present in the surrounding environment. Members of the genus Sodalis, together with other Gammaproteobacteria such as Arsenophonus, Erwinia, Fukatsuia, Serratia and Symbiopectobacterium have often been acquired during replacement events in a wide range of insect hosts and can act as co-primary or secondary symbionts10,55,65,66. Some of these genera, namely Arsenophonus, Serratia and Sodalis, have also been detected in metabarcoding studies of other Cacopsylla species31.

The identification of the ecological and evolutionary factors driving such replacements is a challenging task due to the complex interactions between host, symbiont and the surrounding environment. Our phylogenetic analysis based on mitochondrial genes suggests the presence of two distinct Cacopsylla clades. Interestingly, each clade is associated with a different overwintering behaviour. While C. burckhardti, C. melanoneura, C. picta, C. pruni and C. pyrisuga migrate to conifers for overwintering, C. mali, C. pyri and C. pyricola overwinter on their reproductive host plants40,67. Although our sample size is too small to draw a conclusion about the evolutionary history of the overwintering behaviour of psyllids, it provides novel insights into a potential influence of this behaviour on the symbiont community. Dynamic changes in symbiont compositions of sap-feeding insects have been observed upon shifts in the environmental niche and variation in the dietary composition of the host plant’s sap. For instance, the loss of a Morganella-like symbiont in psyllids of the genus Pariaconus coincided with a lifestyle shift from free-living and open galls to closed galls. Similarly, the colonisation of a different island led to the loss of a Dickeya-like symbiont in open and closed gall Pariaconus spp.25. The frequent symbiont replacements observed in adelgids seem to be favoured by the complex life cycle of these insects, which alternate between development in nutrient-rich galls on spruce and migration to the nutrient-poor phloem of different conifers15. However, the fact that the symbiont replacement observed in this study occurred only in C. burckhardti and C. pyrisuga, thus on selected branches within the clade of Cacopsylla overwintering on conifers, suggests that these species might overwinter on different conifers with distinct phloem compositions68 or that factors beyond a diet change might be involved. Variations in symbiont composition can be influenced by other ecological factors like heat stress, presence of toxic functions (delimiting the fine line between pathogen and symbiont) and the gain of protective traits in a symbiont18,32,49,69,70. Another essential factor involved in the establishment of a symbiotic relationship is the host’s genetics71,72. Psyllids can regulate the symbiont’s densities based on their nutritional needs and their developmental stage73,74. Moreover, studies on aphids and mealybugs showed that different host genotypes adopt distinct regulatory strategies based on the symbiont function75,76. While a higher number of Cacopsylla species is needed to draw definitive conclusions, we can hypothesise that specific ecological or genetic characteristics shared by the sister species C. burckhardti and C. pyrisuga and not by other members from the same clade might have played a role in the replacement of a Psyllophila-like symbiont with Sodalis.

In summary, our findings highlight the dynamic nature of co-symbionts in psyllids, indicating occasional symbiont gains and losses. These events can be influenced by several concurrent factors such as dietary changes, decay of the ancient primary symbiont’s genome and host genetics. To better understand the evolutionary processes underlying a complex history of acquisition, replacement and diversification in psyllid symbiosis, a large-scale evolutionary and genomic analysis is required, spanning a broad range of nutritional partners across the entire psyllid tree of life.

Methods

Psyllid sampling and identification

Adult individuals of three Cacopsylla species were sampled using the beating tray method. Cacopsylla mali was collected in an apple orchard in May 2014 in Laces, Italy. Cacopsylla pruni was collected on plum trees in March 2020 in Brno-Komin, Czech Republic, and C. pyrisuga was collected in a pear orchard in April 2020 in Brno-Starý Lískovec, Czech Republic. All specimens were identified with a stereomicroscope using dichotomous keys77 and then stored in absolute ethanol at -20 °C until further analyses.

DNA extraction and metagenomic sequencing

DNA was extracted from single females using a modified protocol of the PureGene Tissue Kit (Qiagen) as described in ref. 24. DNA quality and quantity were measured with a QFX Fluorometer (DeNovix Inc.) and with the Qubit 1X dsDNA High Sensitivity kit (Invitrogen). Prior to sequencing, the mitochondrial haplotype of the single females was determined using the cytochrome oxidase I gene (COI) as described in refs. 78,79. Based on the partial COI gene sequence, the females of C. mali, C. pruni and C. pyrisuga selected for sequencing belong to haplotypes gt01 (KM206161.1), gt03 (KM206192.1) and 165-1 (MK039612.1), respectively.

A hybrid metagenomic sequencing approach combining short (Illumina) and long (Oxford Nanopore Technologies) reads was applied to the three psyllid species. Genomic DNA from single females was used for library preparation with the Illumina TruSeq Nano kit and sequenced as 150 bp paired-end reads on an Illumina NovaSeq 6000 by a commercial provider (Macrogen Europe). Genomic DNA from two (C. pyrisuga) to four (C. mali, C. pruni) females of the same COI haplotype was pooled to reach 1.5 µg of DNA needed for library preparation using the Oxford Nanopore Ligation Sequencing Kit SQK-LSK 110 (Oxford Nanopore Technologies). Each pool was sequenced on an entire MinION R9.4 Flow Cell (Oxford Nanopore Technologies) for 48 - 72 h. High-accuracy basecalling was performed with Guppy v6.5.7 (Oxford Nanopore Technologies).

Genome assembly of the main bacterial symbionts

Nanopore reads with Q < 7 and shorter than 500 bp were discarded. First, the resulting reads were mapped against a draft genome of C. melanoneura (PRJNA131228580) using Minimap2 v2.2481 and SAMtools v1.182 to remove host reads. Afterwards, the genomes of Carsonella and Psyllophila published by Dittmer et al.24 were used as reference to select reads belonging to these two symbionts using Minimap2 v2.2481 and SAMtools v1.182. After trying different assembly approaches, we decided to use different pipelines for the three datasets.

A hybrid assembly approach with SPAdes v3.15.483, combining the filtered Nanopore reads and the unprocessed Illumina reads of C. mali resulted in the assembly of Carsonella in one circular contig and Psyllophila in four contigs. The latter was processed with Redundans v2.0184 for scaffolding and gap-closing, resulting in one circular contig representing the complete Psyllophila genome. Both genomes were polished with Medaka v1.7.2 (https://github.com/nanoporetech/medaka) and POLCA, which is part of the MaSuRCA toolkit v4.1.085. Additionally, the complete genome of a Serratia sp. SERmali was assembled with Flye v2.9-b1774 using the Nanopore reads filtered against a draft genome of C. melanoneura80 as described above. The resulting circular chromosome and two plasmids were polished with Medaka v1.7.2 (https://github.com/nanoporetech/medaka) and POLCA from MaSuRCA toolkit v4.1.085.

The complete genomes of Carsonella and Psyllophila of C. pruni were assembled with Unicycler v0.4.886 using the filtered Nanopore reads and the unprocessed Illumina reads.

The Carsonella genome of C. pyrisuga was assembled following the same pipeline described above for Carsonella of C. mali. Instead of Psyllophila, the sequenced specimens of C. pyrisuga harboured two Sodalis sp. strains, SODpyris1 and SODpyris2. SODpyris1 was assembled with Canu v2.287 and Redundans v2.0184 using only the Nanopore reads filtered against a draft genome of C. melanoneura80 using Minimap2 v2.2481 and SAMtools v1.182 to remove the host reads. The resulting four contigs were polished with Racon v1.4.1088. When assembling the same reads using a hybrid approach in Unicycler v0.4.886, we obtained several additional contigs with different coverage and GC content. While some belonged to SODpyris1, others seemed to represent a different Sodalis strain. Blast and dotplot analyses showed high similarity of these contigs with Sodalis praecaptivus HS1 (GCF_000517425.1). These contigs served as references to map the Illumina reads with bwa v0.7.1789 in order to select only the reads belonging to this second strain. The selected Illumina reads were used for a second assembly with SPAdes v3.15.483, using as reference the genome of S. praecaptivus HS1, which produced seven contigs that were further processed with Redundans v2.0184. The final assembly of SODpyris2 consisted of six contigs.

Coverage of all genomes was calculated with qualimap v2.390 and their completeness was assessed with BUSCO v5.8.291 and CheckM2 v1.1.092 (Table S5). Annotation was performed with PGAP 2023-10-03.build706193.

Mitochondrial genome assembly

We assembled the mitochondrial genomes of the three Cacopsylla species sequenced in this study (C. mali, C. pruni, C. pyrisuga) as well as of the four Cacopsylla species previously studied in ref. 24 (C. melanoneura, C. picta, C. pyri, C. pyricola). All mitochondrial genomes were assembled using only the Illumina reads produced as described above. Moreover, we assembled the mitochondrial genome of H. cubana using the publicly available Illumina data (SRR13494258). Mitochondrial reads were selected using the mitochondrial genome of C. pyri (MG989222.1) as reference for mapping with bwa v0.7.1789 and SAMtools v1.182. The selected reads were assembled using SPAdes v3.15.483, resulting in one circular contig for each Cacopsylla species. Annotation was performed with MITOS2 v2.1.394 and then manually curated.

Functional genome analysis

Genes for EAA and vitamin biosynthesis were identified using eggnog-mapper v2.1.1295 and BlastKOALA v2.296. RAST38 was used to identify genes related to host colonisation, virulence and pathogenicity. CRISPR-Cas and anti-phage defence systems were identified using CRISPRCasFinder 97 and DefenseFinder 98, respectively. Prophage regions were identified with PHASTEST99, while ISEScan v1.7.3100 was used for the detection of insertion sequences. Secondary metabolite gene clusters such as carotenoids, polyketides and acyl-homoserine-lactones were predicted with antiSMASH v7.1.0101 using ‘relaxed’ detection strictness.

Phylogenomic analyses of psyllid hosts and their symbionts

To assess the evolutionary dynamics between Cacopsylla species and their obligate symbionts, we performed phylogenomic analyses using mitochondrial and endosymbiont genome sequences. The list of all genomes used in this study is provided in Tables S6 and S7.

The phylogenetic analysis of the psyllid hosts was conducted using Bayesian Inference (BI) in BEAST v2.7.5102. In addition to the mitochondrial genomes assembled in this study, we selected two members of the Psyllidae family, namely Cacopsylla burckhardti and Diaphorina citri, as data from their mitochondrial genomes and obligate symbionts are publicly available (Table S6). Four species from Liviidae (Euphyllura phillyreae, Livia junci, Paurocephala sauteri and Melanastera paucipunctata), one species from Carsidaridae (Pachypsylla venusta), and one from Aphalaridae (Lanthanaphalara mira) were used as outgroups (Table S6). A total of 10,694 bp of the 13 mitochondrial protein coding genes (PCG) were aligned with MAFFT v7103 using the G-INS-I algorithm103. The optimal substitution model for each partition, corresponding to the 13 analysed PCGs, was determined using the model-averaging approach implemented in the bModelTest package104. The BI analysis of 100 million generations, with trees sampled each 1000 generations, was run on the CIPRES platform105. Random starting trees were assigned to each partition.

For the endosymbiont phylogenies, OrthoFinder v2.5.4106 was used to identify groups of conserved single copy orthologous genes for each endosymbiont (i.e. Carsonella, Psyllophila, Serratia and Sodalis) (Table S7). The amino acid sequences were aligned with Muscle5 v5.1107. The alignments were manually edited with the help of Gblocks 0.91b108 and subsequently concatenated using catsequences v1.5109. The phylogenetic analyses were performed using BI in MrBayes v3.2.7a110 on the CIPRES platform105. Partitions correspond to the number of analysed genes: 122 single copy orthologous genes for Carsonella (32,173 amino acids), 111 for Psyllophila (26,448 amino acids), 735 for Serratia (238,580 amino acids) and 121 for Sodalis (30,937 amino acids). The best substitution model was estimated using jModelTest v2.1.10111 with model comparison based on the Akaike information criterion (AIC) and Bayesian information criterion (BIC). After excluding the models for mitochondria (mrREV) and chloroplasts (cpREV), the Jones-Taylor-Thornton (JTT) model112 was identified as the best-fit model for the majority of partitions for the whole datasets of Carsonella, Psyllophila, Serratia and Sodalis. Analyses were conducted in two independent runs, each with four Metropolis-coupled Markov chains, for 1 million generations, sampling every 1,000th generation. The prior distributions for tree topology and speciation rates were uniform, with the gamma distribution category count set to 4, and branch length priors following an exponential distribution. A Dirichlet prior was applied to substitution rates of the evolutionary model as well as stationary frequencies, while all other parameters retained their default priors. Nodal support was assessed by posterior probabilities (PP) after discarding the first 30% of samples as burn-in. The Maximum Likelihood (ML) phylogenetic trees of the symbionts were calculated in IQ-TREE v1.6.12113 with the optimal amino acid substitution model for each gene partition114,115 and non-parametric 1000 bootstrap replicates (BS). iTOL v6116 was used for the visualisation of all trees.

Substitution rate analysis

Substitution rates were calculated for Carsonella and Psyllophila. All species combinations used for this analysis were chosen based on the topology of the phylogenomic trees: C. pyrisuga – C. picta, C. melanoneura – C. pruni and C. melanoneura – C. picta, which are part of the first Cacopsylla clade; C. mali – C. pyricola and C. pyri – C. pyricola, which are part of the second Cacopsylla clade. Additionally, substitution rates were calculated for Carsonella and Sodalis sp. SODpyris1 of C. pyrisuga and the Sodalis endosymbionts of Ct. eucalypti and H. cubana.

Amino acid sequences were used to define conserved single-copy orthologous genes for each genome pair with OrthoFinder v2.5.4106, which were aligned with Muscle v5.1107. The resulting amino acid alignments were converted into nucleotide alignments using PAL2NAL v14.1117. Synonymous (Ks) and nonsynonymous (Ka) substitution rates between orthologous genes were calculated with KaKs_Calculator 3.0118, implementing the YN model119. Genes for which the Ks value was near saturation (Ks > 2) or for which the ratio Ka/Ks calculation was not possible (e.g. Ks = 0) were excluded from subsequent analyses. Statistical analysis was performed in R v4.4.0120. The dataset was tested for normality and homogeneity of variance using Shapiro–Wilk and Levene tests, respectively. Mann–Whitney U Test was used to analyse the potential differences in the substitution rates between the different symbionts.

Microscopic analyses

Insects preserved in ethanol were rehydrated and subsequently postfixed in 4% paraformaldehyde for two hours at room temperature. The specimens were then dehydrated through incubation in increasing concentrations of ethanol and acetone, embedded in Technovit 8100 resin (Kulzer, 467 Wehrheim, Germany), and sectioned into semithin slices (1 µm). For fluorescence in situ hybridisation (FISH), the sections were incubated overnight at room temperature in a hybridisation buffer containing specific probes complementary to the 16S rRNA gene sequences of symbionts: Carsonella (probe Carso107: Cy3-ATACTAAAAGGCAGATTCTTG), Sodalis (probes SODpyris1: Cy5-GTGTCTCAGTTCCAGTGTGGCT and SODpyris2: FITC-ACCGTGTCTCAGTTCCATGTGG), and Psyllophila (probe Psyllo118: Cy5-TCCATTGAGTAGTTTCCCAG). All probes were used at a final concentration of 100 nM. Following hybridisation, the slides were washed three times in PBS, dried, mounted with ProLong Gold Antifade Reagent (Life Technologies), and observed using a Zeiss LSM 900 Airyscan 2 confocal laser scanning microscope. A hybridisation control using only DAPI and without any probes confirmed the specificity of the signal and the absence of autofluorescence issues.

To investigate the nature of the black spaces lacking signal in the FISH analysis, some sections were stained with 1% methylene blue in 1% borax and examined using a Nikon Eclipse 80i light microscope.

Statistics and reproducibility

All statistical analyses were performed in R v4.4.0120. Specific details for the evaluation of the results of the genome-wide substitution rate analysis are reported above. Samples sizes are reported in Table 1 and in the Supplementary Information. We do not report any issues with reproducibility.
