Background

Acute asymmetric mydriasis and/or anisocoria present considerable diagnostic challenges within the field of ophthalmology. Common etiologies encompass exposure to topical mydriatic agents, Adie’s tonic pupil, pharmacologic substances, plant toxins, ocular trauma, elevated intraocular pressure, severe optic neuropathy, and oculomotor nerve palsy [1]. Prior studies have reported cases of anisocoria or mydriasis associated with glycopyrrolate exposure, including via axillary antiperspirant patches or gels and oral tablets [1–5]. However, bilateral mydriasis and anisocoria induced by facial sweat-control patches have not been previously documented. Herein, we present a unique case of bilateral mydriasis and anisocoria presumed to result from improper application of a facial antiperspirant patch obtained online. Furthermore, we emphasize the critical importance of preventing periocular exposure as a key safety measure during the application of facial antiperspirants.

Case presentation

A young female patient was referred from another hospital to the Neuro-Ophthalmology Consultation Center, Department of Ophthalmology, presenting with acute asymmetric bilateral mydriasis, photophobia, and blurred near vision upon awakening. Ophthalmic examination demonstrated a best-corrected visual acuity (BCVA) of 20/20 bilaterally with pinhole correction. Intraocular pressure (IOP) was within normal limits in both eyes. Orthophoria was noted without ptosis. Extraocular motility was full, with no diplopia observed. Both pupils were round. Slit-lamp examination employing a frosted filter revealed pupillary diameters of 7.5 mm in the right eye and 4.5 mm in the left eye, with anisocoria more pronounced under bright illumination (Fig. 1). Pupillary light reflex testing indicated an absent direct response in the right eye and sluggish constriction in the left eye; hippus was absent. The anterior segment examination was otherwise unremarkable aside from mydriasis, and funduscopic evaluation revealed no abnormalities. The patient denied neurological symptoms or systemic complaints and reported no recent use of ocular drops, oral medications, transdermal motion sickness patches, scopolamine-containing products, or exposure to Datura plants within the preceding week. There was no history of recent ocular surgery or hyperhidrosis; her general medical history was non-contributory.

Further inquiry revealed that the patient had applied an online-purchased SWEATAROL antiperspirant solution to her eyelids and facial skin the previous night to reduce facial sweating for occupational reasons (Fig. 2). She experienced brief ocular discomfort during application near the periorbital area, but bilateral ocular symptoms manifested only upon awakening the following morning. Glycopyrrolate (23.4 mg/patch), an anticholinergic agent, was identified as the active ingredient in the antiperspirant patch. This agent likely induced unilateral or bilateral mydriasis via transdermal absorption facilitated by the thin periorbital skin. Discontinuation of the patch resulted in complete resolution of symptoms. Neuro-ophthalmic evaluation attributed the anisocoria and mydriasis to inappropriate facial application of the antiperspirant. The patient was advised to return for daily follow-up visits over the next three days. However, she declined in-person review, citing work commitments, and reported that his symptoms had resolved completely by the third day with normalized pupil size. Therefore, a video consultation via WeChat was conducted. On examination, the approximate diameter of each pupil was assessed as 3 mm in ambient room light by evaluation of a short video recorded on a smartphone.

Discussion and conclusion

Acute bilateral mydriasis and anisocoria, accompanied by photophobia and blurred near vision, present significant diagnostic challenges for ophthalmologists. Prompt evaluation is imperative to exclude critical differential diagnoses, including third nerve palsy, pharmacologic mydriasis, ocular trauma, and acute angle-closure glaucoma. Furthermore, exposure to mydriatic agents—whether topical or systemic—such as plant-derived alkaloids or industrial chemicals, should be considered as potential etiologies. In this case, acute bilateral anisocoria (7.5 mm versus 4.5 mm) with an absent light reflex in the right pupil strongly indicated a pharmacologic cause. Key exclusion criteria for tonic pupil included the presence of an intact iris collarette circumferentially at the pupil margin, absence of vermiform iris movements, and absence of a light reflex in the right pupil; all these findings were confirmed upon examination. Subsequent detailed history-taking strongly suggested drug-induced bilateral mydriasis.

The patient applied SWEATROL® Pad, a topical agent intended for facial sweat control, to the periocular and facial skin prior to bedtime. The patient provided a screenshot of the online purchase receipt for SWEATROL® Pad, which, according to the product’s official homepage, states that the product should be kept away from the eyes, nose, and perioral area [6]. This topical exposure was identified as the direct cause of anisocoria and mydriasis observed in the patient. Glycopyrrolate, a synthetic anticholinergic compound, primarily exerts its pharmacological effects through muscarinic receptor antagonism by inhibiting parasympathetic cholinergic signaling pathways responsible for activating the iris sphincter smooth muscle cells, thereby inducing pupillary constriction; inhibition of this pathway results in mydriasis when glycopyrrolate is administered either topically or systemically. In pooled Phase 3 trials (ATMOS I/II) evaluating topical glycopyrronium for the treatment of primary axillary hyperhidrosis, unilateral mydriasis occurred in 6.8% (n = 459) of participants studied [7, 8]. Published case reports consistently associate Qbrexza® (glycopyrronium cloth) with transient pupillary changes that resolve spontaneously within days following discontinuation [1–5, 8–10]. In the present case, periorbital contamination with aglycopyrronium-containing antiperspirant led to bilateral mydriasis and anisocoria, underscoring the critical importance of comprehensive patient history-taking to identify potential exposures to anticholinergic agents, including industrial chemicals or botanicals used in treatments targeting axillary hyperhidrosis or facial perspiration control.

Isolated pupillary dilation seldom occurs in oculomotor nerve palsy in the absence of additional neurological deficits. Conversely, intracranial mass lesions that cause pupillary dilation are typically associated with impaired consciousness. Syphilitic involvement is linked to significant visual loss and various other ocular manifestations. Elevated intraocular pressure results in glaucomatous pupillary abnormalities, which consistently exhibit characteristic signs of glaucoma pathology. Asymmetric optic neuropathies generally present with RAPD and notable visual dysfunction, including impaired visual fields or contrast sensitivity. Accurate identification of the underlying etiology enables the prediction of clinical recovery timelines and guides targeted therapeutic interventions. This strategy also helps to avoid unnecessary procedures, such as cranial magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) or computed tomography (CT) scans. The patient was informed that ocular symptoms would resolve spontaneously within a few days, obviating the need for pharmacologic treatment. This approach minimizes patient anxiety and prevents unwarranted therapies, including the use of miotic eye drops.

Although the complete resolution of ocular symptoms within 48 h supported the accuracy of our initial clinical assessment, a 48-hour in-person follow-up was precluded by patient-related circumstances. Instead, the patient was contacted via WeChat, and reported full resolution of bilateral ocular symptoms with return to normal visual function. A short video provided by the patient demonstrated pupils of equal size and brisk light reflexes. However, we fully recognize that assessment of pupillary characteristics via smartphone video is subject to inherent limitations in precision and cannot replace in-person examination. When feasible, direct measurement with a pupillometer or other standardized device during a face-to-face consultation remains the most accurate method for documenting pupillary size and reactivity. Topical glycopyrronium used for axillary hyperhidrosis may induce transient pupillary alterations, including unilateral or bilateral anisocoria. These effects predominantly result from inadvertent ocular exposure due to contaminated hands following the use of wipes or gels, rather than from direct application of over-the-counter formulations to the face. Consequently, the reported incidence of such ocular side effects remains low [7, 8]. Adamkiewicz et al. [1] documented a case of unilateral pupillary dilation accompanied by ipsilateral facial numbness and weakness after oral administration of glycopyrrolate for hyperhidrosis, with symptoms resolving spontaneously within seven days. Proper application of antiperspirants requires strict avoidance of contact with periocular, nasal, and oral regions, as outlined in product instructions. To reduce ocular and mucosal exposure to glycopyrronium, the use of disposable gloves during application is recommended, followed by immediate handwashing and avoidance of eye rubbing. Notably, topical glycopyrronium-containing agents (e.g., SWEATROL), which are classified as non-prescription cosmetics in certain countries, are readily available through online vendors with limited regulatory oversight. Facial sweat-control products pose a higher risk of unintended mydriasis and anisocoria due to direct exposure of facial skin and periocular mucosa, in contrast to axillary-specific antiperspirants that have minimal potential for ocular contact. The marketing of facial antiperspirant products without labeling that complies with local regulatory standards, particularly regarding ocular exposure risks, presents significant health concerns. Ophthalmologists should consider recent exposure to glycopyrronium-containing facial cosmetics or axillary hyperhidrosis treatments when assessing cases of unexplained unilateral mydriasis, especially after neurological causes have been excluded.

To our knowledge, this represents the first documented case of bilateral mydriasis induced by the misuse of SWEATROL facial antiperspirant patches. While the regulatory classification of SWEATROL (drug vs. cosmetic) is unclear, it is sold as an over-the-counter cosmetic on major Chinese e-commerce platforms (e.g., Taobao, JD.com) without a prescription. Its easy accessibility, combined with limited safety warnings, heightens the risk of periocular exposure—particularly among users who do not thoroughly read instructions. This can result in ocular adverse effects, including anisocoria, blurred near vision, and photophobia.

This case report has inherent limitations. As an outpatient, the patient was not admitted, leading to suboptimal photographic documentation. Specifically, we lack external eye images showing both pupils simultaneously, and the precise illumination during pupillary photography was not recorded. Furthermore, since the symptoms resolved spontaneously within 24–48 h, the patient declined further follow-up, preventing us from obtaining crucial post-recovery pupillary images. These are important aspects to improve upon in the management of future cases.

Acute anisocoria accompanied by mydriasis warrants investigation into the inadvertent ocular exposure to topical anticholinergic agents (e.g., medicated cloths used for axillary hyperhidrosis or facial antiperspirant patches). This represents a modifiable cause that is frequently overlooked in the differential diagnosis. Identification of facial products containing antiperspirant agents, such as glycopyrrolate, necessitates patient counseling to prevent inadvertent ocular transfer. Recommended precautions include: (1) applying the product with single-use applicators to reduce hand contact; (2) avoiding touching the face before performing hand hygiene; and (3) washing hands immediately after application to minimize exposure risks.
