The association of resilience with self‐care and quality of life in people with chronic obstructive pulmonary disease: A cross‐sectional study
Authors: Tamara Pouw, Janneke de Man‐van Ginkel, Johannes A Hardeman, Johannes‐Jurgen Mager, Lian Trapman, Tiny Jaarsma, Saskia Weldam
Published: 2023

ABSTRACT
AbstractAimTo investigate the association of resilience with self‐care and quality of life in people with chronic obstructive pulmonary disease.DesignCross‐sectional study.MethodsData were collected between February and May 2021. Self‐care was measured with the self‐care of chronic illness inventory, quality of life was measured with the clinical chronic obstructive pulmonary disease questionnaire and resilience was measured with the resilience evaluation scale. Possible confounders were included (sex, age, smoking, time since diagnosis of chronic obstructive pulmonary disease, educational level, social support and pulmonary function). Multiple regression analysis was performed among the determinants, confounders and both outcomes.ResultsParticipants scored fairly well on resilience (mean 22.5). Self‐care scored reasonably well (mean maintenance 65.9, mean monitoring 70.9, mean management 59.9 and mean confidence 71.5). Quality of life scored mediocre (mean 2.6). The results of the linear multiple regression were resilience, which is associated with self‐care confidence and quality of life when adjusted for possible confounders. This means people with chronic obstructive pulmonary disease with higher resilience have better self‐care confidence and higher quality of life. The outcome contributes to strengthening nursing care and further developing nurses' knowledge. The results can contribute to increasing awareness for healthcare professionals that resilience can potentially increase self‐care and quality of life.Keywords: chronic obstructive pulmonary disease, quality of life, resilience, self‐care

INTRODUCTION

In people with chronic diseases other than chronic obstructive pulmonary diseases, resilience is a good predictor for improving quality of life and self‐care (Temprado et al., 2019). The degree of self‐care and quality of life can be reduced in people with chronic obstructive pulmonary disease due to stressful life events, and stressful life events can be addressed by building resilience (Cannon et al., 2018). The association among resilience, self‐care and quality of life in people with chronic obstructive pulmonary disease has not yet been studied.

BACKGROUND

People with chronic diseases should be taking increasing responsibility for their own health and need to be more actively involved in self‐care (Riegel et al., 2019). Self‐care behaviours of people with chronic diseases change over time, since the disease is progressing with increasing burden (Matarese et al., 2020). The degree of knowledge and social support contribute to changes in self‐care behaviour (Matarese et al., 2020), meaning that people continuously adapt their self‐care behaviour to new circumstances they are confronted with (Matarese et al., 2020). According to the middle‐range theory (Riegel et al., 2012), self‐care encompasses three dimensions: maintenance, monitoring and management (Riegel et al., 2012). Self‐care maintenance is the behaviour that people use to maintain physical and emotional stability (Riegel et al., 2012). Self‐care monitoring is a process of observing changes in symptoms (Riegel et al., 2012). Self‐care management is the people's response when symptoms are present (Riegel et al., 2012). In addition, there is also self‐care confidence, which is someone's ability to engage in self‐care maintenance and management behaviours (Massouh et al., 2020).

People with chronic obstructive pulmonary disease (COPD) need daily self‐care behaviour to prevent, control and manage the physical, emotional and social consequences of the disease (Matarese et al., 2020). COPD is a disease that is characterized by persistent and progressive airflow limitation, hyperinflation, mucus hypersecretion and abnormal gas exchange and is associated with frequent exacerbations and comorbidities (Cannon et al., 2018). An exacerbation of COPD is as follows: ‘An exacerbation is an event characterized by dyspnea and/or cough and sputum that worsen over ≤14 days, which may be accompanied by tachypnea and/or tachycardia and is often associated with increased local and systemic inflammation caused by airway infection, pollution, or other insult to the airways’ (Celli et al., 2021). The proportion of people with COPD is increasing due to risk factors such as smoking and ageing of the population (Matarese et al., 2020). Treatment objectives focus on relieving and reducing the impact of symptoms, improving participation in daily activities, improving quality of life and reducing adverse events in the future, such as exacerbations (Cannon et al., 2018). Due to symptoms such as shortness of breath, coughing, sputum production and fatigue (Matarese et al., 2020), people with COPD often experience a reduced quality of life (Cannon et al., 2018). There are many definitions of quality of life worldwide (Vanleerberghe et al., 2017). A definition is ‘quality of life is defined as those factors that make people happy or satisfied with their current life situation, past life experiences, and hopefulness for satisfaction with future life circumstances’ (Vanleerberghe et al., 2017). These factors consist of social well‐being, sense of belonging, participation in social activities and membership in family or friendship networks, mental and psychological well‐being, mental and emotional health, self‐esteem and life acceptance (Vanleerberghe et al., 2017).

Self‐care and quality of life can be reduced in people with COPD due to stressful life events (Cannon et al., 2018; Riegel et al., 2019) such as losing a job (Riegel et al., 2019), breathing difficulties and a decrease in daily activities (Lu et al., 2012). Stressful life events can be addressed by building resilience (Cannon et al., 2018; Riegel et al., 2019). In people with other chronic diseases (such as rheumatoid arthritis and diabetes mellitus), resilience is a good predictor for improving quality of life and self‐care (Temprado et al., 2019; Temprado Albalat et al., 2020). Resilience is the ability to adapt, recover and come back from setbacks and difficult experiences (Riegel et al., 2019; Rosa et al., 2017). Therefore, resilience is the ability to make a good adjustment when a life event interferes with the possibility of providing self‐care (Riegel et al., 2019). Twenty years ago, resilience was considered something that a person either has or has not, but now resilience is seen as an ability that can be developed (Rosa et al., 2017). Resilience depends on inner strengths and protective factors such as self‐awareness, self‐response, cognitive and social skills, optimism and a sense of humour (Riegel et al., 2019).

Currently, we found that resilience factors are essential for improving the quality of life in people with COPD (Cannon et al., 2018), such as self‐response, optimism and relationships (Cannon et al., 2018; Riegel et al., 2019). There is also knowledge about effective self‐care in people with COPD (Matarese et al., 2020). However, the association among resilience, self‐care and quality of life in people with COPD has not yet been studied. In other chronic diseases, resilience is a good predictor for improving quality of life and self‐care (Temprado et al., 2019; Temprado Albalat et al., 2020). To strengthen nursing care and further develop nurses' knowledge, it is important to gain an understanding of the association among resilience, quality of life and self‐care in people with COPD.

THE STUDY

Aim and objective

The aim of this study was to investigate the association of resilience with self‐care and quality of life in people with COPD treated at a pulmonary medicine outpatient clinic. The hypothesis in this study was that people with COPD and good resilience have a higher degree of self‐care and a better quality of life.

METHODS/METHODOLOGY

Design

The design of this study was cross‐sectional.

Instrument with validity and reliability

Outcomes

Self‐care

Self‐care was measured with the self‐care of chronic illness inventory. The self‐care of chronic illness inventory is a questionnaire with four independent items with eight questions about maintenance, six questions about monitoring, six questions about management and ten questions about confidence (Riegel et al., 2018). Each question was scored using a 5‐point Likert scale, except questions 14 and 20, which were scored on a 6‐point Likert scale (Riegel et al., 2018). The scores of the independent items were scored individually and standardized to 0–100. Higher scores indicate better self‐care (Riegel et al., 2018). The self‐care of chronic illness inventory is a reliable and valid instrument to measure self‐care in people with chronic diseases (Riegel et al., 2018). A validated Dutch version of the self‐care of chronic illness inventory was used (Weldam, 2019).

Quality of life

Quality of life was measured using the clinical chronic obstructive pulmonary disease questionnaire. The clinical chronic obstructive pulmonary disease questionnaire is a self‐administered questionnaire with 10 questions that were measured on a 7‐point Likert scale (van der Molen et al., 2003). Respond options ranged from 0 (‘never/totally not limited’) to 6 (‘always/totally limited’). All the scores were added together and divided by 10 (van der Molen et al., 2003). Lower scores indicate higher quality of life in people with chronic obstructive pulmonary disease (van der Molen et al., 2003). The clinical chronic obstructive pulmonary disease questionnaire is a reliable, valid and responsive instrument for measuring quality of life in people with chronic obstructive pulmonary disease (van der Molen et al., 2003; Zhou et al., 2017). The clinical chronic obstructive pulmonary disease questionnaire is translated and validated in Dutch (Jungen & Borgmans, 2018).

Determinant

Resilience

Resilience was measured with the resilience evaluation scale, which contains nine questions that were measured on a 5‐point Likert scale (0: totally disagree to 4: totally agree) (van der Meer et al., 2018). All the scores were added together (van der Meer et al., 2018). Higher scores indicate a greater degree of resilience (van der Meer et al., 2018). The resilience evaluation scale is a valid and reliable instrument for measuring resilience (van der Meer et al., 2018). A validated Dutch version of the resilience evaluation scale was used (van der Meer et al., 2018).

Demographic data

The collected demographics were age (years), sex (female, male, other), smoking status (current, former, never), time since diagnosis of COPD (years), educational level (low, intermediate, high), pulmonary function mild GOLD 1 (FEV1 ≥ 80%), moderate GOLD 2 (FEV1 50%–80%), severe GOLD 3 (FEV1 30%–50%), very severe GOLD 4 (FEV1 < 30%) and received social support.

Social support

The interaction subscale of the social support list contains four questions per category: everyday support, support with problems and appreciation on a 4‐point Likert scale (1: rarely or never to 4: very often) and measures perceived social support (van Eijk et al., 1994). All the scores were added together (den Daas et al., 2019). The higher the score, the more social support a person experiences (den Daas et al., 2019). The interaction subscale of the social support list has good reliability, validity and internal consistency (van Eijk et al., 1994; van Sonderen, 1993). A validated Dutch version of the interaction subscale of the social support list was used (van Eijk et al., 1994).

Severity of COPD

The severity of COPD was measured using spirometry results extracted from the electronic patient file. During spirometry, the total exhaled air in the first second of exhalation (FEV1) and the total air from the total lung volume that can be exhaled in one breath (FVC) were measured (Agusti & Vogelmeier, 2020; Wells & Loo, 2019). FEV1/FVC must be <0.70 to determine the classifications (Agusti & Vogelmeier, 2020). Mild COPD (GOLD 1) is when the FEV1 is greater than 80%, moderate COPD (GOLD 2) is when the FEV1 is between 50% and 80%, severe COPD (GOLD 3) is when the FEV1 is between 30% and 50% and very severe COPD (GOLD 4) is when the FEV1 is below 30% (Agusti & Vogelmeier, 2020).

Sampling and recruitment

To achieve a lower risk of selection bias, a consecutive sample was used (Polit & Beck, 2017). Data were collected by using questionnaires between February and May 2021.

Sample size and power

There were 296 people with COPD who were approached to reach the minimum sample size of 110 people with COPD needed for the regression analysis. This sample size is based on the rule of thumb, that is, for each parameter, at least 10 participants must be included (Wilson Vanvoorhis & Morgan, 2007). In this study, three determinants (resilience, self‐care and quality of life) and seven predictors (sex, age, smoking, years diagnosed with COPD, educational level, social support and pulmonary function) were included in the analysis.

Population and sample

The study population consisted of adults with COPD treated at a pulmonary medicine outpatient clinic in a Dutch general hospital.

Inclusion and/or exclusion criteria

To be eligible to participate, the participants had to be able to read Dutch and be physically and cognitively able to complete a questionnaire. Based on the information from the overview of the consultations, it was determined whether the person met the inclusion criteria.

Data sources/collection

After permission, the researcher had access to the overviews of the consultation hours of six pulmonologists and looked for eligible people with COPD. Eligible people with COPD were called by the researcher to explain the study. After willingness to participate, the information letter, informed consent form and questionnaire were sent using research electronic data capture (REDCap). People with COPD who indicated that they could not use computers well received the information letter, the informed consent form, the questionnaire and a stamped addressed envelope by post. If the participant did not complete the questionnaire after 2 weeks, they received a reminder by telephone or an automatic reminder by the research electronic data capture.

Data analysis

Descriptive statistics (frequencies, mean, standard deviation and range) were used to represent the demographic data and the main parameters. Missing data were imputed in line with the instructions from the questionnaires (Health Care Professionals, n.d.; Self‐Care Scoring Algorithm, n.d.; van der Meer et al., 2018; van Sonderen, 1993). Data were checked by histograms and quantile–quantile plots to see if the data were normally distributed. Categorical variables were coded into dummy variables for use in the multiple regression (Polit & Beck, 2017).

To determine the association between resilience and self‐care and between resilience and quality of life, hierarchical linear multiple regression was performed. A two‐tailed test with an alpha of 5% was used. Linear regression analysis was performed for each outcome (quality of life and self‐care). For self‐care, four regression analyses were performed because the four components (maintenance, monitoring, management and confidence) could not be added together (Self‐Care Scoring Algorithm, n.d.). Outcomes were adjusted for possible confounders by entering the determinants in three blocks based on theoretical considerations (Polit & Beck, 2017). The first block consisted of resilience and outcome. Sex, age and educational level were added in the second block. In the third block, smoking, pulmonary function, social support and years diagnosed with COPD were added. Regression analysis was performed five times. In the regression analysis, βs will be used to compare the strength of the various independent variables. The Bonferroni correction was used to reduce the chance of coincidentally finding statistically significant results (Polit & Beck, 2017). Therefore, the results were perceived as statistically significant when the p value was below 0.01 (Baldi & Moore, 2018). To analyse the data, IBM SPSS statistics® (IBM Corporation) version 26.0 was used.

Ethical considerations

This research was not subject to the Medical Research Involving Humans Act (WMO), and this was tested by ‘REDACTED’. Additionally, approval was obtained from the participating organization to recruit participants from the organization ‘REDACTED’. All participants were informed about the aim and procedure of this study. Consent was obtained from all participants.

RESULTS

Study sample

In total, 296 people with COPD were approached for this study. Of these 296, 30 indicated that they did not want to participate. In addition, 134 participants completed the questionnaire, and 132 persons indicated that they would like to participate but did not complete the questionnaire.

In the analysis, nine participants were excluded because they did not meet the criteria for COPD (FEV/FVC < 0.7). Another nine participants had missing values, and these missing values could be imputed in the analysis. Three of nine participants with missing values did not disclose their educational level. One participant had too many missing values in the interaction subscale of the social support list. Therefore, these values were not imputed, but the other determinants from these participants were included. Therefore, in total, data from 125 participants were used in the analysis.

The mean age of the participants was 68.3 years (standard deviation 9.0), and 52% were men. The majority of the participants (52.8%) had a low educational level, 25.6% had an intermediate educational level and 19.2% had a high educational level. 99% of the participants had a smoking history, of which 82.4% were former smokers. The mean time since the diagnosis of COPD was 11.9 years (standard deviation 10.5). Most participants had moderate COPD GOLD 2 (48.8%) or severe COPD GOLD 3 (35.2%). DISCUSSION

In our study, we explored the association of resilience with self‐care and quality of life in people with COPD. We discovered that people with COPD and higher resilience had more self‐care confidence and better quality of life. These associations persisted after correction for confounders, such as sex, age, educational level, smoking, pulmonary function, social support and years diagnosed with COPD.

The results of our study relating to the association between resilience and self‐care confidence are in line with the results of a study in people with heart failure (Chang et al., 2017). However, in people with heart failure, the study found an association between resilience and self‐care maintenance, which could not be confirmed in our study in people with COPD (Chang et al., 2017). The difference could be explained by different levels of resilience or self‐care in people with COPD and people with heart failure. Another reason could be that there may be more focus on self‐care during the treatment of people with heart failure. Moreover, Temprado et al. (2019) found a statistically significant positive correlation between resilience and self‐care in people with a drainage enterostomy. A possible explanation for the positive correlation between resilience and self‐care in people with a drainage enterostomy could be the low percentage of complications. Eighty per cent experienced no complications and were autonomous in the care around the drainage enterostomy (Temprado Albalat et al., 2020). People with COPD likely experience more complications, which may affect their resilience and self‐care behaviour. However, there were important differences between our study and the study of people with drainage enterostomy. Self‐care was measured with different components, namely general, developmental and specific care (Temprado et al., 2019). Due to the different components of self‐care, the study results are not fully comparable. The systematic review and meta‐analysis address a statistically significant positive relationship between resilience and self‐care in people with chronic conditions (Jin et al., 2022). The systematic review included 11 chronic diseases but not COPD (Jin et al., 2022). People with COPD may score differently on self‐care and resilience because; in our study, we did not find a statistically significant positive relationship between self‐care (management, monitoring and maintenance) and resilience. In addition, the article does not include self‐care confidence (Jin et al., 2022). The concept of self‐care confidence used to be part of self‐care, but now it is seen as confidence in the ability to perform self‐care (Riegel et al., 2012). In contrast to our results, we found two studies that demonstrated an association between resilience and self‐management in African American men with diabetes and older adults in the United States (Jia et al., 2020; Simpson & Xu, 2020). This finding may be partly explained by the use of other resilience and self‐care measurement tools. In addition, it may be explained by the different diseases and cultures compared to the Netherlands. Moreover, health care in the United States could not be compared to the situation in the Netherlands. This could mean there is a different emphasis on topics in the treatment of persons with COPD.

The results of our study are in line with the results from previous studies concerning the association between resilience and quality of life. Cannon et al. (2018) found a comparable association between resilience and quality of life in people with COPD measured with different questionnaires. As a result, we can cautiously conclude that the association between resilience and quality of life is not dependent on which questionnaires were used in people with COPD. We found other studies with an association between resilience and quality of life in other diseases (gynaecological cancer and people with drainage enterostomy) (Manne et al., 2015; Temprado et al., 2019; Temprado Albalat et al., 2020), which reinforces the results of the current study. This means that resilience is likely to be an important factor in improving the quality of life of people with chronic conditions. In people with a drainage enterostomy, a greater degree of social support leads to greater resilience and thus a higher quality of life (Temprado Albalat et al., 2020). In the current study, social support was not associated with quality of life. People with COPD may have a different interest in social support compared to people with a drainage enterostomy due to different symptoms and treatments associated with the disease.

Strengths and limitations of the work

To appreciate the findings, some strengths and limitations must be considered. A strength of our study is the generalizability of the results to other people with COPD who are treated in an outpatient clinic in a hospital. The sample of the current study is a reasonable representation of the population. There is approximately the same number of men and women in the Netherlands with COPD aged 65–69 (COPD | Numbers & Context, n.d.). In addition, people with lower levels of education have a greater incidence of chronic obstructive pulmonary disease than highly educated people in the Netherlands (COPD | Numbers & Context, n.d.; COPD: Size and Consequences, n.d.). This is reflected in our study sample. The GOLD status in the sample differs slightly from the population because GOLD 1 (mild) and GOLD 2 (moderate) are the most common in the population (Standard of Care COPD, 2013), while in our study, GOLD 2 (moderate) and GOLD 3 (severe) are most common. This could be explained by a different population. People with COPD treated in an outpatient clinic in a hospital have a higher GOLD stage. This is due to agreements made in health care in the Netherlands. People with COPD GOLD 1 (mild) or GOLD 2 (moderate) are treated in primary care and people with COPD GOLD 3 (severe) or GOLD 4 (very severe) are treated in an outpatient clinic in a hospital. In general, the sample is reasonably representative, and therefore, the results can be generalized to other people with COPD who were treated in an outpatient clinic in a hospital. The consecutive sample used strengthens the study because it ensures a lower risk of selection bias (Polit & Beck, 2017). Although 10.1% of the potential participants actively refused and 44.6% passively refused by not completing the questionnaires, the sample is reasonably similar to the population except for a difference in GOLD stadium. Namely, in the population, most people have GOLD 1 (mild) or GOLD 2 (moderate), and in our sample, most people have GOLD 2 (moderate) or GOLD 3 (severe). This could lead to some selection bias. Furthermore, to our knowledge, this is the first study to investigate the association between resilience and self‐care in people with COPD.

A limitation of our study is that we used GOLD stages 1–4 (mild, moderate, severe and very severe) to determine the severity of the disease instead of the more recently used ABCD classification. The ABCD classifications add the burden of symptoms to classify COPD severity (Mirza et al., 2018). However, during our study, the ABCD classification was not added to every person with COPD. In addition, the spirometry data in our study were not always recent data. Therefore, the GOLD status should be interpreted with caution. In the current study, smoking status was not associated with the outcomes. A possible explanation could be that the sample was too homogeneous, namely 82.4% were former smokers. Another limitation of our study is the small, adjusted r square (increase of 0.02) between Model 1 and Model 3 in the multiple regression analysis with outcome self‐care confidence. Although there is an improvement in the explained variance, it is limited. Our study was conducted during the COVID‐19 pandemic, which could be identified as a limitation. The lives of people with COPD were not the same before the pandemic. In the literature, the experiences of people with COPD during the pandemic were investigated, and it appeared that the pandemic had a low impact on people with COPD (Pleguezuelos et al., 2020). The results of that paper indicate that quality of life scores were the same before the pandemic (Pleguezuelos et al., 2020). To our knowledge, no research has been performed on resilience and self‐care scores in people with COPD before and after the COVID‐19 pandemic.

Recommendations for further research

The results of our study contribute to strengthening nursing care and the development of nurses' knowledge. In people with COPD, it is important to know what influences quality of life and self‐care behaviour. For healthcare professionals and people with COPD, it is important to know how to improve quality of life and self‐care behaviour. Our research also contributes to awareness to focus on resilience during the treatment of people with COPD. Resilience is now seen as an ability that can be developed (Rosa et al., 2017). Therefore, it is first necessary to develop an intervention. The results of our study can be taken away to develop an intervention to improve self‐care confidence and quality of life in people with COPD. To develop an intervention, the Medical Research Council framework can be used (Craig et al., 2008). Future research should focus on intervention development and feasibility testing. An intervention is under development to improve patient resilience after experiencing a stroke (Sadler et al., 2017). In addition, there is a systematic review that aims to contribute to the development of programmes to improve resilience in patients with chronic conditions (Kim et al., 2019). They found interventions for increasing resilience in patients with cancer, cardiovascular disease and diabetes such as education and counselling (Kim et al., 2019). It is important to develop and evaluate an intervention in people with COPD. In the future, an intervention focusing on resilience could lead to improvements in quality of life and self‐care confidence in people with COPD. Another next step could be to conduct a longitudinal study to determine causal relationships between resilience and self‐care and between resilience and quality of life. When other studies are performed, other confounders can be investigated, such as diagnosis of anxiety, depression, marital status, comorbidity, cultural origin and severity of the complaints. These confounders were not included in the current study because not enough participants could be included to measure all the possible confounders. In future research, it may be interesting to use the Self‐Care in Chronic Obstructive Pulmonary Disease Inventory, which is a scale to measure self‐care in people with chronic obstructive pulmonary disease (Matarese et al., 2020). With this disease‐specific inventory for people with COPD, the results could be compared to the results of a generic self‐care questionnaire. In our research, a generic questionnaire was chosen to measure self‐care because it is more useful to compare studies that use the same questionnaire in other people with a chronic disease (Riegel et al., 2018). It would also be an addition to conduct a mixed‐method study so more meaning can be given to the results and discover other relationships (Shorten & Smith, 2017). A number of participants preferred to have the option to receive oral explanations about the association among resilience, self‐care and quality of life.

CONCLUSION

In conclusion, we found that resilience is associated with self‐care confidence and quality of life in people with COPD. This means that people with higher resilience have more self‐care confidence and a better quality of life. This knowledge contributes to strengthening nursing care and further developing nurses' knowledge. This article can contribute to increasing awareness for healthcare professionals that resilience can potentially increase self‐care behaviour and quality of life.
