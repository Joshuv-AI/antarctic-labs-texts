Chemotherapy response in primary peritoneal small cell carcinoma
Authors: Kholoud Alqasem, Sakhr Alshwayyat, Salsabeel Aljawabrah, Sundus Yahya Nser, Ala’aldin Eqleem, Mohammad Abu Shattal, Maisoon Hajir, Mohammed Al-mahdi Al-kurdi
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
BackgroundSmall cell carcinoma (SCC) is a neuroendocrine tumor that usually originates in the lungs but can also arise from extrapulmonary sites. Extrapulmonary small cell carcinomas (EPSCCs) are aggressive and rare, with limited data guiding their management. This case report contributes to the literature by presenting the diagnosis and treatment of primary peritoneal SCC.Case presentationThis case describes a 69-year-old man who presented with abdominal distension and pain, raising concerns for metastasis. He had a history of prostate cancer treated with radical prostatectomy and pelvic lymph node dissection. Biopsy and imaging revealed metastatic SCC involving the peritoneum and omentum. A diagnosis of primary peritoneal SCC or SCC of unknown primary origin was made due to the absence of a detectable primary tumor in typical sites. The patient underwent three separate courses of carboplatin/etoposide chemotherapy (6 cycles each), with each course resulting in significant disease regression and symptom relief. Recurrence was managed effectively with repeated chemotherapy cycles, but long-term follow-up showed the need for continued treatment to maintain disease control and quality of life.ConclusionThis case underscores the importance of a multidisciplinary approach and patient-centered care in managing rare cancers like primary peritoneal SCC. Further research is essential to clarify its molecular characteristics and improve therapeutic options.Graphical abstract Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1007/s12672-025-03433-y.

Introduction

SCC is a histological subtype of cancer categorized under neuroendocrine tumors that is defined by its rapid proliferation and poor prognosis [1]. Typically originating in the lungs, it represents about 15% of lung cancers characterized by a high proliferative rate, predilection for early metastasis, and poor prognosis [2]. EPSCCs most commonly arise in the gastrointestinal tract, cervix, and prostate, posing diagnostic and therapeutic challenges due to their aggressive nature. A comprehensive 2019 study emphasized the rarity of EPSCC, accounting for less than 5% of all SCC [3]. These tumors are characterized by an aggressive course, early widespread metastasis, and a low five-year survival rate dependent on tumor site, ranging between 2%−43% [1, 3].

Primary carcinoma of the peritoneum and omentum is an exceptionally rare entity, distinct from more common secondary cases, and is associated with a poor prognosis. Only 3% of the peritoneal cancers originate from the peritoneum [4]. It is more prevalent in women, with 70% of those affected being elderly [5]. The peritoneal malignancies are mainly characterized by ascites, omental nodules, and peritoneal sheets thickening, while the advanced stages are associated with peritoneal metastasis to the mesentery [6]. Recent research into the molecular biology of SCC emphasizes inactivation of TP53 and RB1 as hallmark mutations, leading to uncontrolled proliferation and aggressive behavior [7, 8]. The tumors involving peritoneal cancer have an overall low five-year survival rate [9].

Immunohistochemistry remains the gold standard for diagnosing peritoneal tumors of neuroendocrine gastrointestinal (GI) origin and for predicting their prognosis. The primary markers include synaptophysin, chromogranin A, p53, and retinoblastoma (Rb) protein. Also, the Ki-67 proliferation index assesses the diagnosis and differentiation between well and poorly differentiated neuroendocrine tumors [10].

This study reports a rare case of primary SCC in the peritoneum and omentum in a 69-year-old male, highlighting diagnostic, therapeutic, and prognostic challenges. By documenting the clinical course, diagnostic findings, and treatment response, this study aimed to contribute to the limited literature on EPSCCs, particularly in those with peritoneal and omental involvement.

Case presentation

This study presents a case of a 69-year-old male patient with a known history of prostate cancer. He had previously undergone radical prostatectomy and pelvic lymph node dissection. He has been on regular follow-up, during which recent imaging and biopsy confirmed the presence of metastatic SCC involving the peritoneum and omentum.

The patient is a non-smoker. His family history is notable for malignancy, with his son being diagnosed with early-onset colon cancer at age 37. The patient underwent a radical prostatectomy and pelvic lymph node dissection for prostatic acinar adenocarcinoma. The pathology report indicated a Gleason score of 7(4 + 3) and extracapsular extension. Postoperative prostate-specific antigen (PSA) levels was 0.1 ng/mL shortly after surgery and salvage external-beam radiotherapy to the prostate bed was delivered with a six-month course of androgen deprivation therapy.

Presentation and Diagnosis

Two years after his initial treatment for prostate cancer, the patient complained of progressive abdominal distention and colicky abdominal pain that had been ongoing for three months before the current evaluation. On examination, vital signs were stable (BP 128/75 mmHg, HR 82 bpm, afebrile), the abdomen was tense with a positive fluid wave, and the rest of the systemic examination was unremarkable. Upon further investigation, a CT scan revealed moderate ascites (increased abdominal fluid) and nodular thickening of the peritoneum, indicating the presence of metastasis. His serum PSA level at presentation was 4.4 ng/mL. Additionally, there was an increased omental thickening, with a maximum thickness of 1.6 cm (Fig. 1).

To determine the primary origin of the metastatic carcinoma, an omental biopsy was performed. Histopathological examination of the biopsy confirmed SCC with immunohistochemical staining positive for markers such as Synaptophysin, Chromogranin, TTF-1, and focal CDX2 (Figs. 2 and 3). However, the cells were negative for CK7, CK20, PSA, and GATA-3 (Figs. 4). Based on the immunoprofile, a primary lung origin was favored, while GI or pancreatic origin was considered less likely.

Given the absence of a clear primary site, a broad differential diagnosis, including primary lung, GI, and pancreatic origins, was considered before reaching a conclusion. The diagnostic work-up was coordinated with input from oncology, pathology, and radiology teams.

PET/CT (Supplementary Fig. 1 A) demonstrated diffuse hypermetabolic omental and nodular peritoneal thickening associated with abdominopelvic ascites. These findings were consistent with biopsy-proven occult primary or primary peritoneal SCC. Minimal bilateral pleural effusion was observed, without definitive hypermetabolic lesions in the pancreas, GI system, or elsewhere. Esophagogastroduodenoscopy and colonoscopy revealed no tumor growth. Considering the diagnosis of SCC of an occult primary origin, palliative chemotherapy with carboplatin/etoposide is recommended.

Treatment and response and surgical consultation

The patient began chemotherapy with carboplatin/etoposide. Following significant GI toxicity and febrile neutropenia after the first chemotherapy cycle, subsequent doses were appropriately reduced, which improved tolerability. Supportive care, including antiemetics, growth factors for neutropenia, and nutritional support, was provided to mitigate chemotherapy-related side effects.

After 2–4 cycles, follow-up CT demonstrated marked reduction in nodular omental thickening and resolution of ascites, consistent with partial response to treatment. By the 6th cycle of chemotherapy, imaging showed no definitive metastatic omental deposits, no ascites, enlarged retroperitoneal lymph nodes, or lung metastases (Fig. 5). The patient’s symptoms, including abdominal cramps and fullness, significantly improved.

Since there was no evidence of disease outside of the omentum/peritoneum, the possibility of primary peritoneal SCC increased. The GI multidisciplinary team discussed the case for possible Cytoreductive Surgery + Hyperthermic Intraperitoneal Chemotherapy (CRS + HIPEC). After reviewing the available research and consulting specialized centers overseas, the patient was offered HIPEC as a potential treatment for his condition. However, he refused the treatment due to potential risks and limited evidence supporting its benefits. Follow-up CT initially showed no disease recurrence; however, a subsequent scan revealed progression in the size of a few peritoneal deposits. (Fig. 6).

The same chemotherapy regimen was restarted. The patient responded positively after six cycles. Chemotherapy was stopped as he showed a complete response with no measurable disease. However, six months later, CT showed disease progression with new omental and peritoneal lesions but no metastasis elsewhere. Given his high sensitivity to the carboplatin/etoposide regimen and good tolerance, the same chemotherapy was pursued again. He completed six cycles and follow-up CT scans showed excellent response, including complete resolution of metastatic deposits and ascites, with no new metastasis. Current condition and patient education

The patient consistently demonstrated a thorough understanding of his condition and treatment options and actively participated in shared decision-making. He was informed about the disease course, potential risks and benefits of therapies, and the importance of regular follow-up. Psychosocial support was also provided to address the emotional and logistical challenges associated with his diagnosis and treatment.

As of March 2025, the patient remained clinically stable (ECOG PS = 1) with no abdominal pain, nausea, or vomiting, and reported good appetite and stable weight. Laboratory values were unremarkable (hemoglobin level, 13 g/dL, creatinine 0.9 mg/dL). Discussion

A smaller fraction of EPSCC originates in the peritoneum and omentum. This case illustrates the challenges in diagnosing and treating EPSCC in the peritoneum and omentum, especially when the primary site is unknown, complicating the prognosis and treatment approach [11]. This aligns with our patient’s presentation, where a broad diagnostic workup and multidisciplinary collaboration were required to confirm the primary peritoneal SCC diagnosis. The patient manifested ascites and abdominal discomfort, which are indicative of peritoneal carcinomatosis [12]. Primary peritoneal SCC was diagnosed after initial tests failed to identify a primary tumor in the lungs, GI system, or other common sites. Histopathological results and immunohistochemical labelling, revealing positivity for markers including Synaptophysin and TTF-1, which are frequently linked to neuroendocrine tumors, validated this diagnosis.

Despite the lack of specific guidelines for peritoneal SCC, the conventional SCC treatment regimen of carboplatin/etoposide was chosen due to its efficacy in managing identical instances to this one [13]. In our case, this regimen led to a marked reduction in omental thickening, resolution of ascites, and significant symptomatic improvement after multiple cycles. The molecular alterations driving SCC aggressiveness and therapy resistance are also critical to consider. TP53 and RB1 mutations, frequently observed in SCCs such as small cell lung cancer (SCLC), are implicated in up to 90% of cases, driving tumor progression [14]. MYC amplifications, present in approximately 20% of SCLC cases, are associated with rapid proliferation and may have contributed to the aggressive nature of our patient’s disease [15].

Chemotherapy had a remarkable effect on the patient, significantly reducing tumor burden and alleviating symptoms. The timeline from diagnosis in October 2020 to the last follow-up in April 2023 is unusual for an aggressive tumor-like EPSCC. Typically, such tumors progress rapidly. The prolonged survival period observed in this case could be attributed to the patient’s exceptional sensitivity to the chemotherapy regimen, as demonstrated by multiple complete responses to treatment. The unexpected sensitivity to the carboplatin/etoposide regimen and the prolonged follow-up period could indicate unique characteristics of the patient’s case. Molecular profiling and next-generation sequencing might be needed to understand this exceptional behavior better.

The consideration of CRS + HIPEC reflects an evolving approach to managing peritoneal carcinomatosis. By delivering targeted chemotherapy and lowering tumor load, CRS + HIPEC may enhance patient outcomes [12]. However, its application in SCC of the peritoneum is not well established, and there are concerns about significant morbidity and the patient’s advanced age, so this intervention was decided against [16]. The patient’s overall health status, procedure-related risks, and potential benefits played a significant role in the decision-making process. This case underscores the need for more robust evidence to guide the application of CRS + HIPEC in managing rare cases like primary peritoneal SCC. Cases of peritoneal carcinomatosis from appendiceal carcinoma demonstrate how combining CRS with HIPEC has achieved median survival of 56.4 months and 5-year survival rates of 45% [17]. Similarly, isolated cases of peritoneal metastases from non-small cell lung cancer (NSCLC) treated with surgery report prolonged survival, suggesting potential benefits in select cases [18].

Radiotherapy appears to be a potential complementary treatment modality for EPSCC, including peritoneal cases, despite the lack of site-specific trials. In a murine model of small cell lung cancer, combining radiotherapy with immune checkpoint therapy improved survival by 3–13 days compared to controls and showed high tumor-specific uptake without notable toxicity [19]. In addition, radiotherapy following four cycles of chemoimmunotherapy in advanced esophageal SCC increased the median depth of response from 34.5 to 64% and achieved a disease control rate (DCR) of 86.7%, with a progression-free survival (PFS) of 16.4 months [20].

Another study of 128 patients with locally advanced or metastatic esophageal SCC treated with radiotherapy and anlotinib reported a 52.9% objective response rate (ORR), 88.2% DCR, median OS of 24.0 months, and PFS of 9.2 months in metastatic cases. In locally advanced cases, the median PFS reached 20.1 months, and the 36-month OS was 61.9% [21]. These findings support the potential role of radiotherapy in peritoneal EPSCC.

Maintenance treatments with less intense chemotherapy are often used in such cases to prevent the tumor from growing or recurring and to reduce treatment toxicity. Feng et al. reported a rare case of lesser omentum primary SCC with an aggressive course and widespread dissemination; the total survival time was only 26 months from diagnosis [22]. The patient was successfully treated with surgical excision and adjuvant chemotherapy, but he relapsed after 20 months with cardiac and fundic carcinoma. Later, liver metastasis was confirmed to have the same small cell histopathological subtype [22]. Another case report documented a primary peritoneal SCC with a special characteristic of a large cell variant with hypercalcemic features [23]. The nodules had disseminated in the abdominal and pelvic peritoneum, suggesting a carcinomatosis process. The approved treatment for this case was surgical excisions (total abdominal hysterectomy with bilateral salpingo-oophorectomy (TAH/BSO)), debulking, and omentectomy. The surgical procedures were supported by adjuvant chemotherapy and consolidation therapy with irinotecan. This patient retained her life without recurrence or relapse [23].

Immunotherapy represents another potential treatment avenue. Immune checkpoint inhibitors, such as pembrolizumab and nivolumab, have extended median survival in aggressive cancers like SCLC by up to 2.2 months when combined with chemotherapy [24]. Additionally, targeting inflammatory pathways, such as IL-6 blockade, has demonstrated tumor control improvements in preclinical models of peritoneal malignancies [25]. Localized therapies, such as oncolytic viruses, are promising, with intraperitoneal administration achieving stable disease in 57% of patients during Phase I trials [26]. Future studies could explore their applicability to rare cancers like primary peritoneal SCC.

In our patient’s case, carboplatin/etoposide therapy provided durable responses across multiple treatment cycles, despite disease recurrence. The patient’s carboplatin/etoposide therapy significantly slowed the course of the disease and relieved symptoms. This result emphasizes the effectiveness of the chemotherapy regimen to treat peritoneal SCC, especially considering the patient’s exceptional tolerance and prolonged response. The possibility of recurrence is reflected in the continuous requirement for monitoring, underscoring the significance of individualized, patient-centered care in managing such challenging and rare cases. Given the challenges in diagnosing rare peritoneal tumors, enhanced awareness among healthcare providers, advanced imaging techniques, and histopathological expertise are essential to reduce diagnostic delays [27]. Moreover, incorporating psychosocial support has been shown to improve patients’ quality of life by addressing emotional, psychological, and logistical challenges [28–30].

Conclusion

Diagnosing and managing rare aggressive cancers such as primary peritoneal SCC necessitates a multidisciplinary approach. The patient’s sensitivity to carboplatin/etoposide chemotherapy facilitated durable disease control and symptom relief over multiple cycles. The recurrence pattern necessitates ongoing monitoring and personalized care, especially in palliative settings, where enhancing quality of life is crucial. The rarity of primary peritoneal SCC underscores the need for further research into its molecular and immunological characteristics, including genetic alterations in TP53, RB1, and MYC, and exploring novel therapies, such as immunotherapy and targeted treatments. This case contributes to the limited literature on primary peritoneal SCC and advocates for continued efforts to optimize diagnostic pathways and develop evidence-based treatment guidelines for this challenging disease.
