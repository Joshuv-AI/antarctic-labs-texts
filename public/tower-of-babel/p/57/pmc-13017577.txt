Balloon pericardiotomy for malignant pericardial effusion: the PMAP trial
Authors: Bryan Ping-Yen Yan, Kevin Ka-Ho Kam, Angel Lai, Canice Lok-Hang Ng, Ngai-Yin Chan, Lap-Shing Chan, Chi-Wing Wong, Ping-Wa Yam, Wai-Kin Chi, Stephen Lam Chan, Randolph Hung Leung Wong, Eugene Brian Wu, Joseph Yat-Sun Chan, Alex Pui-Wai Lee, Guang-Ming Tan
Published: 2025

Introduction

Malignant pericardial effusion (MPE) occurs in up to 21% of patients with metastatic malignancy.1 Although pericardiocentesis (PC) can relieve life-threatening cardiac tamponade, recurrence is observed in about 30% of cases.1–3 Extended pericardial drainage after PC may lower recurrence but comes with increased infection risks and longer hospital stay.2,4–6 Percutaneous balloon pericardiotomy (PBP) has emerged as a safe alternative for managing MPE.7–10 This study compares the efficacy of PBP vs PC with extended drainage in reducing the recurrence of pericardial effusion due to metastatic malignancy.

Methods

The pericardiotomy for malignant pericardial (PMAP) effusion trial was a multi-centre, prospective, randomized open-label study conducted in Hong Kong. The study was approved by local ethics committees, registered on ClinicalTrials.gov (NCT04472468), and conducted under CONSORT guidelines. Eligible patients were those >18 years with histologically confirmed active malignancy and a pericardial effusion measuring ≥10 mm on echocardiography. Key exclusions criteria included previous open-heart surgery, prior pericardial interventions, contraindications to endovascular procedures, or inaccessible effusion via the subxiphoid approach. Patients were randomized 1:1 using a permuted block design to either the PBP group or the control PC group. In the PBP group, pericardial access via the subxiphoid route was obtained under fluoroscopy, followed by balloon pericardiotomy using a 20 mm balloon. A pericardial drain was inserted following PBP and maintained until the output was <100 ml/day. The control PC group underwent echocardiographic guided PC with similar pericardial drain management. Patients were followed for up to 6 months with serial echocardiography and FACT-G quality-of-life (QoL) assessments. The primary endpoint was recurrence of ≥10 mm pericardial effusion. Secondary endpoints included incidence of cardiac tamponade, overall survival, QoL measurement, and procedural complications. Statistical analyses were conducted on an intention-to-treat basis using survival models that accounted for death as a competing risk.

Results (Figure 1)

Cumulative Kaplan–Meier estimates of rate of primary outcome and other outcomes according to study groups

Between 1 January 2020 and 28 January 2024, 50 patients (mean age of 62 ± 22 years, 66% females) out of 130 screened were recruited. Recruited fell short of the original planned 148 patients due to impacts from the COVID-19 pandemic. Common exclusions were urgent off-hour PC (n = 45) or patient refusal (n = 22). Among enrolled patients, 25 were randomized to PBP group and 25 to the control PC group. Five patients assigned to PBP received routine PC due to logistical issues, while two in the PC arm underwent PBP per physician discretion. Baseline variables (age, gender, malignancy type, and history of effusion) were similar between groups, with most presenting with large (>20 mm) or very large (>25 mm) effusions, and tamponade features (98%).

All PBP procedures were performed via a subcostal approach, whereas 72% of PC cases used an apical window. Procedural success was 92% for PBP vs 100% for PC. The aspirated pericardial fluid averaged about 664 ml in both groups, with a drain dwell times of roughly 3 days. Cytology confirmed malignant cells in 86% of cases. All microbiological cultures were negative. Two severe adverse events occurred—one in each group—with no deaths directly attributed to the procedure. One patient in the PBP suffered an iatrogenic right ventricular puncture requiring surgical repair and pericardial window creation, and she died 19 days later from malignancy. In the PC group, one patient developed tension pneumothorax needing resuscitation and chest drain insertion. This patient died 12 days later due to underlying malignancy. Additionally, two PBP patients developed new pleural effusions on subsequent follow-up at week 4 and week 26, and they were managed with pleurocentesis.

At 6 months, MPE recurred in 12% of PBP patients compared with 60% in the PC group (hazard ratio (HR) 0.15, 95% CI 0.05–0.51, P = .001). Median time to MPE recurrence was 30 days for PBP vs 14 days for PC. Cardiac tamponade occurred in 4% (PBP) vs 40% (PC) (HR 0.09, P = .006), and all events were managed with emergency PC. The number needed to treat to prevent 1 MPE recurrence was 2, and 2.8 to prevent 1 cardiac tamponade. Subgroup analyses revealed no significant interaction. In the sensitivity analysis, when the primary endpoint was analysed based on the actual treatment patients received, the HR was 0.30 (95% CI 0.11–0.85, P = .023) consistently in favour of PBP group. The Median survival was 88 days for PBP and 180 days for PC (P = .238). Three- and six-month survival was 48% and 44% in the PBP group vs 64% and 52% in the PC, with tamponade-related deaths of 4% and 16%, respectively. Six-month tamponade-free survival was similar (32% vs 36%, P = .777). Only 23 patients completed the FACT-G QoL questionnaires at weeks 2 and 12, demonstrating no significant difference between groups (mean change of −6.8 in the PBP group vs −1.2 in the PC group, P = .305).

Discussion

Although previous registries have highlighted the feasibility of PBP,7–10 this is the first randomized trial demonstrating the superiority of PBP in reducing MPE recurrence over PC. Additionally, PBP was associated with a significantly lower incidence of cardiac tamponade, while the overall survival, procedural complication rates and quality-of-life were similar between the two interventions. The lower recurrence noted with PBP might be attributable to the mandatory extended catheter drainage which might promote the formation of a pericardial–pleural or pericardial–peritoneal fistula, facilitating pericardial fluid drainage. The comparatively higher recurrence rate in the PC group may be explained by the systematic use of frequent echocardiography which identified even asymptomatic recurrences, the predominance of patients presenting with large effusions and clinical tamponade, and a high proportion of cytologically confirmed malignancy involvement of the pericardium.3 Despite the reduction in recurrence and cardiac tamponade with PBP, overall survival and QoL among patients with MPE remained poor, underscoring that the primary goal in managing MPE is to reduce the frequency of interventions rather than to extend survival. Limitations of this study include small sample size, and a study population comprised mostly of Chinese ethnicity, which may affect generalizability.

Conclusions

In patients with metastatic malignancy and large MPE, upfront intervention using PBP with extended drainage significantly reduces MPE recurrence and incidence of cardiac tamponade at 6 months compared with PC with extended drainage.
