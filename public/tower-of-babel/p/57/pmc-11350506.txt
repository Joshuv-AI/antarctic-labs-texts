A broad-spectrum multiepitope vaccine against seasonal influenza A and B viruses in mice
Authors: Lifang Yuan, Shengze Zhang, Rongjun Bi, Xuejie Liu, Zirong Han, Minchao Li, Xinzhong Liao, Ting Xie, Shaohui Bai, Qian Xie, Chuming Luo, Ying Jiang, Jianhui Yuan, Huanle Luo, Huacheng Yan, Caijun Sun, Yuelong Shu

ABSTRACT

Research in context

Evidence before this study

We searched PubMed for English-language studies from database inception to 10 December 2023, using the search terms “Influenza”, “Multiepitope” and “Vaccine”. Several studies were identified concerning the multiepitope vaccine against different antigens in silico. However, there is a lack of information about the efficacy of the multiepitope vaccine against influenza virus in animal models. Moreover, the dosages of both DNA and protein vaccines employed in our study were determined based on a thorough review of literature retrieved from the PubMed database. This comprehensive search utilized the key terms "DNA vaccine", "protein vaccine" and "mice" to ensure relevance and accuracy of our study design.

Added value of this study

This study developed a multiepitope vaccine (MEV) designed to provide broad-spectrum protection against different influenza viruses, and the results demonstrated the efficacy of this broad-spectrum MEV against different influenza viruses in mice.

Implications of all the available evidence

Our study has implications for clinical practice. Our findings demonstrated the efficacy of this broad-spectrum MEV which incorporates 19 B-cell linear epitopes, 7 CD4+ T cells epitopes, and 11 CD8+ T cells epitopes, highlighting the potential of MEV as a promising universal vaccine candidate. Further research is warranted to refine the vaccine.

Introduction

Influenza is an acute respiratory infection caused by influenza viruses, which poses a global threat to human health with an annual 300,000–500,000 deaths and 3–5 million severe cases worldwide.1, 2, 3 The influenza viruses are categorised into four types (A, B, C, and D), with Influenza A virus (IAV), including H1N1 and H3N2, and Influenza B virus (IBV) being responsible for seasonal epidemics in humans.4 Notably, IAV is characterised by its frequent rapid mutations (antigenic drift) and reassortments (antigenic shift). This dynamic process leads to the emergence of new antigenic variants each year through antigenic drift, and the potential for new influenza viruses to trigger flu pandemics through antigenic shift. In contrast, IBV is divided into B/Victoria and the B/Yamagata lineages,5,6 which exhibit relative conservation and do not undergo an antigenic shift.

Vaccination is the most effective and cost-effective strategy for preventing influenza infection and reducing the disease burden, particularly for high-risk populations.7, 8, 9 Current seasonal influenza vaccines are formulated annually to match the anticipated circulating strains for the forthcoming flu season. The widely used inactivated influenza vaccine and live attenuated influenza vaccine rely on the optimal efficacy of the two key antigenic proteins of the influenza virus, hemagglutinin (HA) and neuraminidase (NA), which are the primary antigens targeted by vaccine. However, the ongoing antigenic drift in influenza viruses leads to frequent mismatches between circulating strains and those included in the vaccine formulations, resulting in reduced vaccine effectiveness.10, 11, 12, 13, 14, 15 Therefore, there is an urgent need for a highly effective influenza vaccine that protects against both IAVs and IBVs, which is becoming increasingly imperative and valuable for public health.

Recently, peptide-based vaccines have garnered increased attention as they can trigger both humoural and T-cell immune responses.16, 17, 18, 19 These vaccines typically target specific immunodominant epitopes, including B-cell linear epitopes, CD4+ T cells epitopes, and CD8+ T cells epitopes. B-cell linear epitopes are critical for being recognized by antibodies or B cell receptors, playing a pivotal role in initiating the B-cell immune response.20 On the other hand, T-cell epitopes require presentation by major histocompatibility complex (MHC) molecules to effectively engage T cells. MHC-I molecules are chiefly responsible for presenting endogenous antigens to CD8+ T cells, whereas MHC-II molecules display exogenous antigens to CD4+ T cells, facilitating the activation of the adaptive immune system.21 Understanding the distinct roles of MHC-I and MHC-II in antigen presentation is crucial for peptide/epitope-based vaccine development. The limited overlap between these molecules presents challenges for vaccine design and epitope selection, as their presentation of antigenic peptides significantly influences immune response outcomes. Advancements in bioinformatics have enabled the prediction of immunodominant epitopes, a strategy that has been successfully applied to various pathogens, including the antigen of Mycobacterium tuberculosis,22,23 Enterococcus faecium,24,25 Acinetobacter baumannii,26 Klebsiella pneumoniae,27 Pseudomonas aeruginosa,28 Salmonella,29 Staphylococcus aureus,30 antigens from SARS-CoV-2,31 and the monkeypox virus.32,33 Peptide-based vaccines, which incorporate conserved antigenic epitopes of influenza viruses, have demonstrated the potential to induce protection against various strains in mouse models34, 35, 36, 37, 38, 39, 40 and humans.17,41 This research supports the feasibility of rapidly developing a universal multiepitope vaccine (MEV) that could provide broad protection against influenza viruses across multiple seasons.

The present study aims to develop a broad-spectrum influenza vaccine based on B-cell linear epitopes and T cells epitopes (including CD4+ T cells and CD8+ T cells epitopes). In our preceding study,42 potential immunodominant B-cell linear epitopes, CD4+ T cells, and CD8+ T cells epitopes of HA and NA from seasonal influenza strains were identified from a human cohort, comprising both 2020–2021 Quadrivalent influenza vaccine (QIV)-vaccinees and H3N2-infected patients. In this current study, these epitopes were further screened through enzyme-linked immunospot assay (ELISPOT) in mice infected with influenza viruses. The designed MEV, consisting of 37 immunodominant epitopes, including 19 B-cell linear epitopes, 7 CD4+ T cells, and 11 CD8+ T cells epitopes, was administered to C57BL/6 female mice through a regimen involving two doses of a DNA vaccine followed by one dose of a protein vaccine. Mice in the MEV group showed substantial protection against various seasonal strains and demonstrated partial efficacy against historical strains when subjected to a lethal challenge with the viruses. Notably, the MEV group showed robust T-cell responses and cellular immune responses compared to the control groups, highlighting the comprehensive immune defence elicited by the MEV. There was also an observation of raised hemagglutinin inhibition antibody titres against both seasonal circulating and historical strains. Moreover, mice vaccinated with the MEV exhibited a significantly lower number of inflammatory cells in the lungs compared to the negative control groups.

In conclusion, our results demonstrate the efficacy of this broad-spectrum MEV against influenza viruses in mice. This suggests that the MEV could be an optimistic option for addressing the antigenic drift of both influenza A and B viruses. However, long-term studies are needed to evaluate the longevity of the immune responses induced by the MEV and to explore its potential application in diverse populations, particularly high-risk groups, which will offer valuable insights for the continued advancement of this promising vaccine.

Methods

Ethics statement

This study was conducted strictly under the principles of the Experimental Animal Regulation Ordinances issued by the China National Science and Technology Commission. Ethical approval for all experiments was obtained from the Office of Research Protection's Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee and Institutional Review Board at Sun Yat-sen University (approval number: 2022-B013). Experiments involving live viruses and animals were conducted in a qualified negative pressure biosafety laboratory level-2 plus (BSL2+) animal facilities at the Centre for Disease Control and Prevention of Southern Military Theatre, adhering to the institutional biosafety manual.

Peptide pools, influenza viruses and cells

A total of 37 potential immunodominant epitopes including 19 B-cell linear epitopes, 7 CD4+ T cells, and 11 CD8+ T cells epitopes of different HA and NA were predicted in our previous study.42 In brief, by utilising an antibody-peptide microarray, the prominent B-cell linear epitopes from the HA and NA proteins of different seasonal influenza viruses were identified in a study population comprising both the QIV-vaccinated individuals and those who had been infected with the virus. Concurrently, bioinformatics approaches were employed to predict the CD4+ T cell and CD8+ T cell epitopes from different seasonal influenza viruses that are most likely to elicit a strong immune response. These selected epitopes underwent further analysis using a range of immunoinformatic tools focusing on those with high antigenicity and immunogenicity. The criteria for selection also included the epitopes being non-allergenic and non-toxic, as well as their conservation across different influenza viruses. These immunodominant epitopes including H1N1, H3N2, and IBV subtypes, were synthesised using a solid-phase synthesis method by GUANGZHOU IGE BIOTECHNOLOGY LTD. Briefly, the α-amino group of amino acids was safeguarded using FMOC (9-fluorenylmethyloxycarbonyl). The synthesis of the peptide was conducted employing the TETRAS™ Peptide Synthesiser, and subsequent detachment from the resin was achieved through trifluoroacetic acid (TFA). Following synthesis, the peptide undergoes purification via high-performance liquid chromatography (HPLC) and undergoes analysis with a mass spectrometer (MS) to ensure a purity exceeding 95%. Each peptide was dissolved in water with no precipitation at a concentration of 0.4 mg/mL and peptides specific to T-cells and B-cells from the identical subtype of the influenza virus were pooled separately at a concentration of 0.4 mg/mL. Consequently, four peptide pools based on H1N1, H3N2, BV, and BY strains were prepared for further experiments, each representative of a specific influenza strain: a pool comprising HA and NA peptides from H1N1 (referred to as the H1-peptide pool), another consisting from HA and NA peptides from H3N2 (known as the H3-peptide pool), a third pool containing HA and NA peptides from BV and BY strains (named the B-peptide pool), and finally, an inclusive pool that integrates all the aforementioned peptides (termed the All-peptide pool).

All seasonal influenza viruses, including A/Guangdong-Maonan/SWL1536/2019 (H1N1), A/Hong Kong/2671/2019 (H3N2), B/Washington/02/2019 (BV), B/Phuket/3073/2013 (BY), A/Puerto Rico/8/34 (H1N1-PR8) and A/Aichi/2/1968 (H3N2-X31), were kindly supplied by the National Influenza Centre of the Chinese Centre for Disease Control and Prevention (China CDC). Influenza viruses were cultured in specific pathogen-free (SPF) embryonated eggs, and the chorioallantoic fluid was subsequently preserved at −80 °C until used. Viral titres were assessed and quantified as 50% tissue culture infective dose(s) (TCID50). All experiments involving influenza viruses were carried out in BSL2+ facilities, adhering to the guidelines set forth by the University of Sun Yat-sen Office of Biological Safety.

Human embryonic kidney 293T cells (HEK 293T cells; derived from human embryonic kidney cells, RRID: CVCL_0063) and Madin–Darby canine kidney cells (MDCK; derived from dog kidney cells, RRID: CVCL_0422) were cultured in complete Dulbecco's modified Eagle's medium (DMEM, Gibco, USA) supplemented with 10% fetal bovine serum (FBS; Gibco, USA) and 1% penicillin/streptomycin (PS; Gibco, USA). The cells were cultured at 37 °C in an environment with 5% CO2. The HEK 293T cell was validated by STR profiling and MDCK was validated by species identification. These cells were regularly tested for mycoplasma contamination using the MycoAlert Detection Kit (Lonza Bioscience, Switzerland) and consistently showed negative results.

TCID50 assay

The viral allantoic fluid was initially diluted at a ratio of 1:100, followed by a series of half-log serial dilutions within a 96-well plate. Lung tissues were initially isolated from infected mice and then homogenised and centrifuged to obtain a viral-containing supernatant. The viral supernatant was then subjected to a series of half-log serial dilutions within a 96-well plate. Subsequently, MDCK was then trypsinized to produce a uniform single-cell suspension, which was subsequently diluted to a concentration of 1.5 × 105 cells/mL. After incubating the diluted virus with MDCK cells for 18–20 h, the cells were fixed using a solution of 80% acetone in phosphate-buffered saline (PBS) and subsequently washed. The cells were then incubated with the appropriate influenza virus-specific antibody, either an anti-IAV antibody (1:4000 dilution, Millipore Cat# AB1074, RRID: AB_90475) or an anti-IBV antibody (1:4000 dilution, GeneTex Cat# GTX128538, RRID: AB_2885796). After the primary antibody binding, cells were treated with horseradish peroxidase (HRP)-conjugated rabbit anti-goat IgG secondary antibody (FdBio science Cat# FDG007, China). The colourimetric reaction was developed using 3,3′,5,'-Tetramethylbenzidine (TMB; Millipore, USA) as the substrate. Measure the absorbance at 450 nm to calculate the TCID50 using the Reed-Muench method.43

Immunodominant peptides screened by ELISPOT assay

The ELISPOT assay was employed to identify immunodominant peptides, following the methodology outlined in previous studies.44,45 The experiments were conducted as follows: Four groups of female C57BL/6 mice (n = 3), aged 6–8 weeks and SPF, were intranasally exposed to H1N1, H3N2, BV, and BY viruses at a concentration of 1TCID50/50 μL. Each group consisted of three mice, which were diligently observed for 4 days before being sacrificed. Subsequently, the splenocyte suspension was subjected to centrifugation at 4 °C and 500 g for 5 min, with the supernatant discarded. The resulting pellet was gently resuspended in 2 mL of 1 × Red Blood Cell Lysis Buffer (Biosharp, China) and incubated at room temperature for 5 min. After two washes with PBS, the concentration of splenocytes was adjusted to 2 × 106/mL using Roswell Park Memorial Institute (RPMI; Gibco, USA) 1640 Medium (Gibco, USA).

To identify immunodominant CD4+ T cells and CD8+ T cells peptides, the interferon-γ (IFN-γ)+ T cells were detected by IFN-γ T cell ELISPOT Ab pair (U-Cytech Cat# CT655-10, Netherlands). In detail, 96-well plates (Millipore, USA) were initially coated with anti-mouse IFN-γ monoclonal antibody and left overnight at 4 °C. PBS served as a negative stimulant, while Concanavalin A (ConA, 10 μg/mL, MedChemExpress, USA) was used as a positive stimulant. Then, plates were washed with PBS and followed by blocking with 10% fetal bovine serum for 2 h at 37 °C. Freshly isolated mouse splenocytes were added at a density of 2 × 105 cells per well to the plates, and 5 μL of each peptide (2 μg/peptide) was promptly added. The cells underwent a 24-h incubation at 37 °C, after which the expression of IFN-γ was detected using biotinylated polyclonal anti-mouse IFN-γ and developed with alkaline phosphatase (ALP)-conjugated streptavidin (U-CyTech, Netherlands) for 1 h, respectively.

To identify immunodominant B-cell linear peptides, IgG B cells were detected. In detail, B-cell linear peptides were coated in 96-well plates at 4 °C overnight. PBS served as a negative control. Freshly isolated mouse splenocytes were added at a density of 2 × 105 cells per well to the plates at an 18-h incubation at 37 °C. Subsequently, the splenocytes were extracted, and a biotin-conjugated anti-mouse IgG antibody (Mabtech Cat# 3825-6-250, Sweden) was introduced, followed by incubation with streptavidin-ALP at room temperature for 1 h. After a 20-min BCIP/NBT reagent (Biosharp, China) incubation, the ELISpot Reader System (Mabtech, Sweden) was employed to quantify the number of spots. The immunodominant peptide was defined with a stimulation index (SI) value greater than two.23,46 The SI value was calculated by dividing the Spot Forming Cells (SFC) count of the experimental peptide by the SFC count of the background. Finally, all the immunodominant peptides specific to T-cells and B-cells from the identical subtype of the influenza virus were pooled separately to be H1–B cell-pool (9 peptides), H1-T cell-pool (6 peptides), H3–B cell-pool (6 peptides), H3-T cell-pool (7 peptides), B–B cell-pool (4 peptides), and B-T cell-pool (5 peptides).

Construction of DNA vaccine

The genetic sequence of microtubule-associated protein 1 light chain 3 beta (LC3B, MPSEKTFKQRRSFEQRVEDVRLIREQHPTKIPVIIERYKGEKQLPVLDKTKFLVPDHVNMSELIKIIRRRLQLNANQAFFLLVNGHSMVSVSTPISEVYESERDEDGFLYMVYASQETFGTAMAV) was acquired from the National Centre for Biotechnology Information (NCBI) data library and codon optimisation was conducted for efficient expression in mammalian cells before chemically synthesised by Invitrogen. Since LC3B has been reported to enhance antigen presentation and immune response in our previous studies,47,48 the N-terminus of immunodominant peptides (IP) was fused with the C-terminus of LC3B to create the IP-LC3B fusion gene. Additionally, a 6 × His-tag (HHHHHH) was added to the C-terminal for the identification of the fused IP-LC3B protein expression. Finally, the fused gene IP-LC3B was optimised according to the preferred codon usage of mammalian cells and synthesised by Ruibio Biotechnology (Guangzhou, China). Subsequently, the LC3B and the fused gene IP-LC3B were respectively cloned into the pVAX-1 expression vector (Invitrogen, USA). A total of three DNA vaccines including pVAX-1, pVAX-LC3B, and pVAX-IP-LC3B were constructed and used in this study, among which pVAX-IP-LC3B was named multiepitope DNA vaccine (MEV-DNA).

Protein expression in vitro

Following codon optimisation, the nucleotide sequence of IP-LC3B was synthesised by Ruibio Biotechnology (Guangzhou, China) and inserted between the XhoI and NcoI cleavage sites of pET28a (+) plasmids. The recombinant plasmid was then introduced into Escherichia coli (E. coli) BL21 cells (Enterobacteriaceae, Wuhan Institute of Biotechnology, China) in vitro.49, 50, 51 The plasmid pET28a (+) was purchased from the Wuhan Institute of Biotechnology (Wuhan, China). Recombinant IP-LC3B was expressed as 6 × His-tagged fusion protein in E. coli, and the molecular weight of the protein was predicted using the Expasy Protparam server (https://web.expasy.org/protparam/).52 The recombinant protein was then purified using Ni-NTA affinity resin (Qiagen GmbH, Germany). Briefly, transformants were selected on agar plates containing the appropriate antibiotic, ensuring the survival and propagation of cells harbouring the recombinant plasmid. A small pre-culture was initiated from a single colony to amplify the transformed cells, which served as an inoculum for the main expression culture. Protein expression was induced in the main culture using an inducible promoter, specifically Isopropyl β-d-1-thiogalactopyranoside (IPTG), and optimal conditions for induction were maintained through careful regulation of timing and temperature. After induction, E. coli cells were harvested through centrifugation, and the subsequent cell pellet containing the IP-LC3B protein was subjected to sonication lysis. The IP-LC3B protein was then purified from the cell lysate using affinity chromatography. To validate the success of the expression and purification processes, the identity and purity of the protein were analysed through Sodium Dodecyl Sulfate-Polyacrylamide Gel Electrophoresis (SDS-PAGE). The purified IP-LC3B protein, named multiepitope protein vaccine (MEV-Pro), was subsequently stored at −80 °C for further use. For a negative control in further immunisation, recombinant Human LC3B protein (Abcam Cat# ab103506, UK) was purchased.

Western blotting analysis

293T cells were seeded at a density of 5 × 105 cells in 6-well plates and transfected with the MEV-DNA plasmid using jetPRIME transfection reagent (Polyplus, USA) in OptiMEM serum-free medium for 24 h. Subsequently, cell lysis was performed using RIPA cell lysis buffer (Beyotime, China), and SDS-PAGE was conducted. The expressed protein was then probed with anti-His antibody (Proteintech Cat# 66005-1-Ig, RRID: AB_11232599) and anti-GAPDH antibody (GeneTex Cat# GTX100118, RRID: AB_1080976) overnight at 4 °C. Following the primary antibody incubation, the membrane was exposed to HRP-conjugated anti-mouse IgG antibody (Fdbio science Cat# FDM007, RRID: AB_2934269) or anti-rabbit IgG antibody (Fdbio science Cat# FDR007, RRID: AB_2934270) and subsequently detected using a chemiluminescent HRP substrate (Millipore, USA).

Animal immunisation regimen and virus infection challenge

In this study, we employed an immunisation strategy consisting of two doses of a DNA vaccine followed by a single dose of a protein vaccine, with a two-week interval between each vaccination. A total of 336 SPF female C57BL/6 mice, with an average age of 6–8 weeks, were intramuscularly vaccinated in the gastrocnemius muscle of both hind legs. Mice were divided into four groups, each with 84 mice: the pVAX group (negative control group for the pVAX-1 vector), the pVAX-LC3B group (negative control group for the LC3B), the MEV group (experimental group), and the QIV group (positive control group). Three days before the initial immunisation, all mice received an injection of 100 μL of 0.5% bupivacaine hydrochloride (Sigma, Germany) into the gastrocnemius muscle of each hind leg.47 The vaccination schedule with a two-week interval was as follows: mice in the pVAX group received two doses of 50 μg pVAX-1 plasmid and one dose of 100 μL PBS; mice in the pVAX-LC3B group were vaccinated with two doses of 50 μg pVAX-LC3B plasmid and one dose of 50 μg LC3B protein; mice in the MEV group were vaccinated with two doses of 50 μg MEV-DNA and one dose of 50 μg MEV-Pro; mice in the QIV group received three doses of 100 μL 2020–2021 QIV, which is designed for human use. On week 5 and week 7, six mice from each group were humanely euthanised for sample collection. Serum samples were collected for antibody detection using hemagglutination inhibition (HI) and Microneutralisation (MN) assays. Spleens were also harvested and subjected to ELISPOT assay and flow cytometry. Specifically, the spleen was carefully excised and placed into ice-cold 1640 medium to prevent cell clumping and maintain cell viability. To process the spleen, it was gently mashed through a 70-μm cell strainer into a collection tube also containing ice-cold 1640 medium. Subsequently, the splenocyte suspension was centrifuged at 4 °C and 500 g for 5 min, and the supernatant was then discarded. The resulting pellet was gently resuspended in 2 mL of 1 × Red Blood Cell Lysis Buffer (Biosharp, China) and incubated at room temperature for 5 min. Following two washes with PBS, the concentration of splenocytes was adjusted to 5 × 106/mL using a Cell Staining Buffer (BioLegend, USA).

Furthermore, the rest of the mice in each group (n = 72 per group) were intranasally challenged with 50 μL of PBS containing 10 mouse-lethal doses of 50% (MLD50) of a single influenza virus, specifically including the H1N1, H3N2, BV, BY, H1N1-PR8, and H3N2-X31 strains. Among them, a portion of the mice (n = 6 per group for each virus) were euthanised at 4 days post-infection, and their lungs were collected for TCID50 assay to determine the virus titre. Another portion of the mice (n = 6 per group for each virus) was observed twice daily for 14 days to monitor body weight changes and mortality. A 20% body weight loss was determined to be the humane intervention point; mice reaching this endpoint were humanely euthanised via carbon dioxide inhalation and their deaths were included in the mortality counts.

Hemagglutination inhibition (HI) assay

Serum samples were initially diluted at a ratio of 1:5 and incubated in the receptor-destroying enzyme (RDE; Denka Seiken, Japan) at 37 °C for 16–18 h according to our previous study.53 Subsequently, serum samples were inactivated at 56 °C for 30 min. The HI assay was carried out using 1% chicken or guinea pig erythrocytes, along with 4 hemagglutination (HA) units of viruses, following the standard procedures outlined by the World Health Organisation (WHO).54 The recorded HI titres represent the highest serum dilution that demonstrated complete inhibition of hemagglutination.

Microneutralisation (MN) assay

Serum samples treated with RDE and inactivated were serially diluted and mixed with 100 TCID50 influenza virus at 37 °C in 5% CO2 for 1 h. MDCK cells were diluted to a concentration of 1.5 × 105 cells/mL and treated with the virus-serum mixture, followed by incubation at 37 °C in 5% CO2 for 18–20 h. After fixation with 80% cold acetone, cells underwent incubation with an anti-influenza virus A antibody (1:4000, Millipore Cat# AB1074, RRID: AB_90475) or anti-influenza virus B antibody (1:4000, GeneTex Cat# GTX128538, RRID: AB_2885796). Subsequently, cells were exposed to HRP-conjugated anti-goat IgG antibody (Fdbio science Cat# FDG007, China) and anti-rabbit IgG antibody (Fdbio science Cat# FDR007, RRID: AB_2934270). The microneutralisation titres were determined by measuring the OD450 nm value using the TMB substrate.43

Enzyme-linked immuno sorbent (ELISA) assay

Serum samples were obtained from the mice on day 21 post-final vaccination. High-binding capacity polystyrene ELISA plates (Corning, USA) were coated with a concentration of 1 μg/mL of the All-peptide pool, integrating all immunodominant peptides, in ELISA Coating Buffer (Solarbio, China) and incubated overnight at 4 °C. On the subsequent day, the plates were blocked with 2% bovine serum albumin in PBS containing 0.05% Tween-20 (PBST) for 2 h at 37 °C. Following the blocking step, the plates were incubated with diluted serum samples at a dilution of 1:100 in PBST for 2 h at 37 °C. After the incubation, the plates were incubated with HRP-conjugated anti-mouse IgG antibody (Fdbio science Cat# FDM007, RRID: AB_2934269) for 1 h at 37 °C. The IgG antibody titres were determined by measuring the OD450 nm value using the TMB substrate.

Flow cytometry assay

The cytokine expression was conducted using flow cytometry for both B and T cells in single-cell suspensions obtained from mouse spleens. To reduce non-specific immunofluorescent staining, anti-mouse CD16/32 (BioLegend Cat# 156603, RRID: AB_2783137) was used to block Fc receptors. For cell-surface staining of B cells, splenocytes were stained with anti-mouse CD3e-BB700 (BD Biosciences Cat# 566494, RRID: AB_2744393), anti-mouse B220-BV421 (BioLegend Cat# 103239, RRID: AB_10933424), anti-mouse CD95-PE (BD Biosciences Cat# 554258, RRID: AB_395330), anti-mouse GL7-APC (BioLegend Cat# 144618, RRID: AB_2800675), anti-mouse CD138-PC7 (BioLegend Cat# 142514, RRID: AB_2562198), anti-mouse CD38-APC A750 (BioLegend Cat# 102738, RRID: AB_2876402), and a dead or live cell dye ECD-Zombie Red (Biolegend, USA) for 30 min on ice.

Subsequently, intracellular cytokine staining (ICS) for T cells was processed according to our previous method.47,55 Briefly, splenocytes were stimulated in a medium containing a peptide pool at a final concentration of 2 μg/mL for each peptide at 37 °C for 2 h, PBS served as the negative control, while phorbol 12-myristate-13-acetate (PMA; 40 ng/mL, Sigma–Aldrich, USA) and ionomycin (1000 ng/mL, MedChemExpress, USA) served as positive stimulants. Brefeldin A (BFA; BD Biosciences, USA) was subsequently added for an additional 16-h incubation at 37 °C. The cells were then stained with anti-mouse CD3e-BB700 (BD Biosciences Cat# 566494, RRID: AB_2744393), anti-mouse CD4-BV421 (BD Biosciences Cat# 562891, RRID: AB_2737870), anti-mouse CD8-FITC (BioLegend Cat# 100706, RRID: AB_312744), and a dead or live cell dye ECD-Zombie Red (Biolegend, USA) for 30 min on ice. Following this, the cells were fixed and permeabilised with Cytofix/Cytoperm (BD Biosciences, USA) for 20–40 min at 4 °C. After washing with perm/wash (BD Biosciences, USA), the cells were stained with anti-mouse IFN-γ-PE (BioLegend Cat# 505808, RRID: AB_315401), anti-mouse IL-2-V605 (BioLegend Cat# 503829, RRID: AB_11204084), and anti-mouse TNF-α-APC (BioLegend Cat# 506307, RRID: AB_315428). Finally, the cells were washed with FACS washing buffer (PBS supplement with 2% heat-inactivated FBS) and resuspended in PBS before collection by CytoFLEX S Flow Cytometer (Beckman Coulter, USA), and the data were analysed by FlowJo software (version 10.8.1).

ELISPOT assay

On day 49 after immunisation, mice were sacrificed and mouse spleen cells were harvested and subjected to ELISPOT assay as described in section 2.4. For IFN-γ detection, splenocytes were stimulated with a mixed CD4+ T cells-specific and CD8+ T cells-specific peptides including H1-T cell pool, H3-T cell pool, and B-T cell pool at a final concentration of 2 μg/mL for each peptide. For IgG detection, the mixed immunodominant B-cell peptides including the H1–B cell pool, H3–B cell pool, and B–B cell pool were coated in 96-well plates at 4 °C overnight. PBS was used as a negative control across all groups. For the pVAX, pVAX-LC3B and MEV groups, the MEV-Pro protein was used as a positive control. In contrast, a 1:20 dilution of the QIV was employed as the positive control specifically for the QIV group. The SFCs were quantified following the previously described methods.

Histopathology assay

Lung tissue from each mouse was prepared through a series of steps involving formalin fixation to preserve the tissue structure, followed by dehydration in a graded ethanol series, and clearing with xylene before paraffin embedding which facilitates the penetration of wax for stable sectioning. The embedded tissue was then sectioned into thin sections using a microtome. These prepared thin sections underwent staining by the hematoxylin and eosin (HE) method, which differentially colours the cellular components for enhanced visualisation. Hematoxylin stains the cell nucleus blue, while eosin stains the cytoplasm and extracellular matrix pink. After staining, these sections were dehydrated further through a reverse-graded ethanol series, cleared with xylene, and mounted with a coverslip using a synthetic resin to prevent the section from detaching or deteriorating. Each mouse lung contributed three tissue sections, which were independently assessed by two researchers under a microscope at 200× magnification (Keyence, Japan) to assess morphological changes and identify any pathological features.

Database and sequence alignment

As in our previous study,42 the strain sequences of 2020–2021 QIV were used as references. These strains' full-length amino acid sequences of HA and NA were downloaded from the influenza virus database (GASAID, gisaid.org).56 The accession numbers of these sequences were as follows: H1N1, EPI1716622 (HA) and EPI1716624 (NA); H3N2, EPI1698489 (HA) and EPI1698488 (NA); BV, EPI1422452 (HA) and EPI1422451 (NA); BY, EPI544264 (HA) and EPI544263 (NA); H1N1-PR8, EPI29937 (HA) and EPI29942 (NA); H3N2-X31, EPI381955 (HA) and EPI381957 (NA). Strains of identical subtypes for each HA and NA were aligned with the immunodominant epitopes using SnapGene software (version 6.0.2).

Statistical analysis

All data analyses were performed using GraphPad Prism 9 software (San Diego, USA). Assessments, including lung viral titres, T cell cytokines and B cell responses evaluated via flow cytometry and ELISPOT on mice, and peptides-specific IgG antobody were subjected to either a one-way analysis of variance (ANOVA) or an unpaired t-test analysis, depending on the data's normality and homogeneity of variance. The results were displayed as mean ± standard deviation (SD), with statistical significance set at a P-value <0.05. Corrections for multiple comparisons were made using the method recommended by the GraphPad Prism 9 software.

Validation of the immunodominant peptides in mice

A total of 19 B-cell linear epitopes, 7 CD4+ T cells, and 11 CD8+ T cells epitopes of different HA and NA were predicted in our previous study.42 These peptides were synthesised and then screened by ELISPOT assay. In brief, female C57BL/6 mice, aged 6–8 weeks and SPF, were intranasally exposed to single virus including H1N1, H3N2, BV, and BY strains at a concentration of 1TCID50/50 μL. The splenocytes of mice were harvested on day 4 post–challenge and were stimulated by each peptide for ELISPOT assay. The results showed that the SI values of all peptides were greater than 2 in three mice of each group (Fig. 1). The details of SFCs analysed by the ELISPOT experiment are shown in Figure S1.

Construction of MEV

In our previous studies, we demonstrated that a DNA vaccine fused with LC3B could effectively activate both humoural and cellular immune responses.47,48 Moreover, an immunisation regimen combining a DNA vaccine with a protein vaccine has been shown to induce a robust B cell and antibody immune response.57 Accordingly, the immunisation protocol utilized in this study involved administering two doses of MEV-DNA, followed by a single dose of MEV-Pro, with a two-week interval between each administration. For MEV-DNA construction, the N-terminus of immunodominant peptides (IP) was fused with the C-terminus of LC3B and a 6 × His-tag (HHHHHH) to obtain the IP-LC3B fusion gene. Specifically, LC3B was connected to IP by an EAAAK linker since this linkage can improve the stability.58 The IP contains 19 B-cell linear epitopes, 7 CD4+ T cells, and 11 CD8+ T cells epitopes of different HA and NA from H1N1, H3N2, and influenza B viruses (Table S1). B-cell linear epitopes were linked using KK linkers to stabilise protein structure and retain the independent immune activity of the vaccine epitope.59,60 Considering the advantages of flexibility, recommended length, and the ability to accelerate immune processing and presentation,61, 62, 63 AAY and GPGPG linkers were employed to connect the immunodominant CD8+ T cells and CD4+ T cells epitopes, respectively (Fig. 2a). The final amino acid sequence, consisting of 687 amino acids, is shown in Fig. 2b. As a result, we designed and constructed two recombinant DNA vectors carrying the LC3B gene (negative control) and IP-LC3B fusion gene, respectively. Using an anti-His antibody to recognise the IP-LC3B fusion protein with a predicted molecular weight of 77 kDa, we demonstrated the appropriate protein expression in 293T cells by Western blotting assay (Fig. 2c). For MEV-Pro expression, the nucleotide sequence of the IP-LC3B fusion gene was inserted between the XhoI and NcoI cleavage sites of pET28a (+) plasmids after codon optimisation. Recombinant IP-LC3B was expressed in E. coli and purified. As a result, MEV-Pro expression was confirmed by SDS-PAGE staining (Fig. 2d).

MEV vaccination induced a robust protective effect against different viruses challenges in mice

To assess the protective effect of the MEV vaccination, female C57BL/6 mice aged 6–8 weeks were subjected to a prime-boost vaccination regimen. The regimen consists of two doses of DNA vaccine followed by one dose of protein vaccine administered at a two-week interval (Fig. 3). Mice were randomly divided into four groups, each with 36 mice: mice receiving two doses of pVAX-1 vector and one dose of PBS were defined as the pVAX group, those administered with two doses of pVAX-LC3B and one dose of LC3B were denoted as the pVAX-LC3B group, mice vaccinated with two doses of MEV-DNA and one dose of MEV-Pro were identified as the MEV group, and mice received three doses of 100 μL 2020–2021 QIV were named the QIV group. Three weeks post-final vaccination, all mice were intranasally challenged with 10-mouse-lethal doses of 50% (MLD50) of H1N1, H3N2, BV, BY, H1N1-PR8, and H3N2-X31 strains. Body weight and survival were monitored for 14 days, with mice losing 20% or more of their body weight being humanely euthanized. The MEV group exhibited significantly less weight loss upon challenge with seasonal strains (H1N1, H3N2, BV, and BY) compared to the negative control groups (pVAX and pVAX-LC3B groups), as depicted in Fig. 4a–d. Mice in the control groups succumbed within 8 days (H1N1) or 6 days (H3N2, BV, and BY) post–challenge, while the MEV group showed a 100% survival rate, enduring beyond the 14-day observation period. Mice in the QIV group exhibited a 100% survival rate, as did mice in the MEV group. This suggests that the MEV offer a similar level of benefit as the QIV in protecting against seasonal strains. In the case of historical strains (H1N1-PR8 and H3N2-X31), the MEV group also displayed reduced weight loss and higher survival rates—66.67% and 50%, respectively—compared to the control groups, which experienced rapid decline and mortality within 4–7 days post–challenge (Figure S2a). In contrast, mice in the QIV group showed a 100% death rate after infection.

Furthermore, to assess the protective efficacy of MEV, we quantified influenza virus loads in lung tissues on day 4 post–challenge. The MEV group exhibited significantly lower virus loads for H1N1, H3N2, BV, and BY strains (P < 0.001, one-way ANOVA) compared to the pVAX and pVAX-LC3B groups, as shown in Fig. 4e. Notably, no significant differences were observed between the pVAX and pVAX-LC3B groups. However, against the historical strains H1N1-PR8 and H3N2-X31, the MEV group did not show a significant reduction in virus loads compared to the control groups (Figure S2b). Despite this, MEV vaccination correlated with a significant decrease in virus loads between day 4 and day 14 post–challenge for both H1N1-PR8 (P < 0.01, one-way ANOVA) and H3N2-X31 (P < 0.01, one-way ANOVA), indicating a sustained protective effect (Figure S2c). In conclusion, the MEV vaccination regimen provides substantial protection against lethal challenges from various seasonal influenza strains and demonstrates a degree of efficacy against historical strains, highlighting its potential as a broad-spectrum influenza vaccine.

MEV vaccination reduced lung pathological lesions in mice

To comprehensively evaluate the protection effects of MEV vaccination, we observed the mice's lung lesions under a 200 × microscope on day 4 post–challenge with H1N1, H3N2, BV, and BY strains. Histopathological examinations revealed a significant extent of lung tissue damage in the pVAX and pVAX-LC3B groups presented with thickened alveolar walls, connective tissue hyperplasia, extensive alveolar detachment accompanied by haemorrhage and vascular inflammation, and substantial infiltration of inflammatory cells (Fig. 5). In contrast, the MEV group exhibited significantly smaller lung lesion areas on day 4 after infection with H1N1, H3N2, BV, and BY compared to both pVAX/PBS and pVAX-LC3B groups (Fig. 5). Moreover, minimal infiltration of inflammatory cells in the lungs was observed in the MEV group compared to the pVAX/PBS and pVAX-LC3B groups. Mice in the QIV group also showed minimal infiltration of inflammatory cells in the lungs (Fig. 5). This suggests that the MEV offer a similar level of benefit as the QIV in protecting against seasonal strains. Remarkably, on day 4 after infection with H1N1-PR8 and H3N2-X31 strains, the MEV group demonstrated significantly smaller lung lesion areas and milder haemorrhage and vascular inflammation compared to the pVAX/PBS and pVAX-LC3B groups, while the QIV group showed more severe pathological changes of lung tissue than the MEV group (Figure S2d). These findings indicate substantial protection by MEV vaccination, as evidenced by reduced lung tissue damage and decreased inflammatory cell infiltration.

MEV elicits vigorous T cell immune responses targeting specific peptides

To investigate T cell immune responses following MEV immunisation in mice, the expression levels of IFN-γ, TNF-α, and IL-2 for CD4+ and CD8+ T cells targeting specific peptides were quantified through flow cytometry assay. Four peptide pools were meticulously prepared for subsequent experiments. The gating strategy for flow cytometry is shown in Figure S3, and the gates for MEV group are shown in Figure S4. On day 21 post-final vaccination, our findings indicated a significant upregulation of cytokine expression in the MEV group compared to the pVAX, pVAX-LC3B, and QIV groups. Notably, for CD4+ T cells targeting the H1-peptide, H3-peptide, B-peptide, and All-peptide, there was a marked increase in IFN-γ, TNF-α, and IL-2 expression (all P < 0.001, one-way ANOVA) in the MEV group than that in the QIV group (Fig. 6a). Within these subsets, TNF-α was most prominently expressed, followed by IFN-γ, while IL-2 showed the least expression in the MEV group. Mice in the QIV group showed highest level for TNF-α, followed by IL-2, and lowest for IFN-γ targeting the H1-peptide, H3-peptide, B-peptide, and All-peptide (Fig. 6a). In CD8+ T cells, the expression levels of IFN-γ (P < 0.001, one-way ANOVA), TNF-α (P < 0.001, one-way ANOVA), and IL-2 (P < 0.001, one-way ANOVA) targeting H1-peptide, H3-peptide, B-peptide, and All-peptide were significantly raised in the MEV group compared to both the pVAX, pVAX-LC3B, and QIV groups on day 21 after the final vaccination. However, the expression levels were highest for TNF-α, followed by IL-2, and lowest for IFN-γ in both MEV and QIV groups (Fig. 6b).

Furthermore, the production of T-cell-derived IFN-γ targeting specific peptides was assessed on day 21 post-final vaccination using the ELISpot assay. A broad-spectrum humoural response induced by MEV vaccination

Complementing our assessment of T-cell responses, we delved into the humoural immune response triggered by MEV vaccination in mice. Three weeks post-final vaccination, mice were humanely sacrificed, and serum and spleen samples were harvested for evaluation of HI and MN antibody titers. Mice from the MEV group demonstrated a significant cross-reactive antibody response, with HI titers reaching 1:20 against H1N1 and H3N2 strains, a maximum of 1:40 against the BV strain, and exactly 1:40 against the BY strain (Fig. 7a). However, HI titers for the H1N1-PR8 and H3N2-X31 strains peaked at 1:10 post-MEV vaccination, indicating a more limited cross-reactivity against historical strains. In contrast, the QIV group showed a maximum HI titer of 1:160 against H1N1, BV, and BY strains, and 1:80 against the H3N2 strain, with no detectable HI antibodies against the H1N1-PR8 and H3N2-X31 strains (Fig. 7a). The pVAX/PBS and pVAX-LC3B groups exhibited no detectable antibody titers for any strains (Fig. 7a). MN antibody levels in MEV-vaccinated mice peaked at a maximum titer of 1:20 against H1N1, BV, and BY strains, and 1:10 against the H3N2 strain. No MN antibodies were detected against H1N1-PR8 and H3N2-X31 strains in any group. The QIV group, likewise, displayed no MN antibodies against H1N1-PR8 and H3N2-X31 strains (Fig. 7b). The pVAX/PBS and pVAX-LC3B groups also showed no MN titers for any strains (Fig. 7b). For delineating the MEV-DNA and MEV-Pro effects, the HI and MN antibody titres were also evaluated on day 21 after two doses of MEV-DNA. Mice vaccinated with MEV-DNA exhibited a modest cross-reactive antibody response with HI titers of 1:10 against H1N1, H3N2, BV, BY, H1N1-PR8, and H3N2-X31 strains (Figure S5c). The QIV group showed a maximum HI titer of 1:40 for H1, BV, and BY strains, and 1:20 for the H3 strain, with no detectable HI antibodies against H1N1-PR8 and H3N2-X31 strains post-QIV vaccination (Figure S5c). MN antibodies were undetectable in MEV-DNA vaccinated mice for all strains tested (Figure S5d). The QIV group also showed no detectable MN titers against H1N1-PR8 and H3N2-X31 strains (Figure S5d). Additionally, we investigated peptides-specific IgG antibodies on day 21 post-final vaccination using an ELISA assay. The MEV group displayed a significant increase in peptides-specific IgG antibody titers (P < 0.001, one-way ANOVA) compared to the pVAX, pVAX-LC3B, and QIV groups (Figure S5e).

Comparative sequence analysis of the immunodominant epitopes

Following the observed variations in immune responses to different strains within the same subtype, we analysed to assess the distinctions in immunodominant epitopes. In the H1N1 subtypes, encompassing B cell linear epitopes, CD4+ T cells, and CD8+ T cells epitopes, most immunodominant epitopes exhibited at least one amino acid mutation in H1N1-PR8 compared to the H1N1 strain. Notably, only one B cell linear epitope (NSRFESVAWS) of the NA protein remained conserved between H1N1 and H1N1-PR8 strains. For the H3N2 subtype, a total of 8 immunodominant epitopes, spanning B cell linear epitopes, CD4+ T cells, and CD8+ T cells epitopes, displayed amino acid mutations in H3N2-X31 compared to other H3N2 strains. However, both H3N2 and H3N2-X31 strains shared 5 conserved immunodominant epitopes (AELLVALENQHTIDL, ALNNRFQIKGVELKS, RDNWKGSNRP, TGFAPFSKDNSIRLS, and RFQIKGVEL). In the case of IBV, 7 immunodominant epitopes (FSLPTFDSLN, LSLYVSASLS, KHFALTHYAAQPGGY, HLLALERKL, KLRHLISVK, YSFGFEIKDK, and DVPCIGIEM) were conserved in both BV and BY strains (Table 1). One mutation was observed in two immunodominant epitopes (SGSCPNATSK changed to SGSCPNITNG, GGFPNQTEDG changed to GDFPDQTEDG), as detailed in Table 1.

Discussion

Yearly vaccination, advocated by the World Health Organisation (WHO) as the most efficient and cost-effective measure for preventing influenza infection and reducing disease burden, especially in high-risk populations,7, 8, 9 has shown variable effectiveness.10, 11, 12, 13, 14, 15 This variability underscores the urgent need to develop an influenza vaccine capable of addressing the antigenic drift of seasonal influenza viruses. Peptide-based vaccines, which can stimulate both humoural and cellular immune responses, have emerged as promising candidates in this endeavor.16, 17, 18, 19

In this study, we presented a MEV that includes 19 B-cell linear epitopes, 7 CD4+ T cells epitopes, and 11 CD8+ T cells epitopes derived from the HA and NA antigens of influenza viruses. These epitopes, predicted in our previous study42 and validated in influenza-infected mice, are critical for activating the adaptive immune system. The MHC class I and class II molecules play distinct roles in presenting antigens to CD8+ and CD4+ T cells, respectively, which is essential for an effective immune response.21

The MEV was constructed by linking immunodominant peptides with LC3B, a marker for autophagy known to enhance antigen presentation and the immune response of both CD4+ and CD8+ T cells.47,64,65 Our earlier studies showed that the autophagosome-linked LC3B protein significantly directs antigens to autophagosomes, leading to their presentation in MHC II compartments through autophagy-mediated degradation within autolysosomes/lysosomes, a mechanism that effectively provokes potential CD4+ T cell responses in vitro.47,48 Moreover, it is reported that the T-cell response driven by CD4+ T cells and CD8+ T cells can elicit a robust immune response against infectious agents.66,67

Building on our previous studies demonstrating the effectiveness of a DNA vaccine fused with LC3B in activating both humoural and cellular immune responses,47,48 we employed an immunisation protocol that combines DNA and protein vaccines. This synergistic approach aims to leverage the strengths of both vaccine types to elicit a robust and comprehensive immune response.57 As expected, our findings indicate that MEV vaccination can stimulate robust T-cell and B-cell responses, as well as an antibody response, in mice. However, we observed that the antibody response was less pronounced compared to the QIV group. It has been reported that traditional inactivated vaccine can typically prevent viral replication in animals.68 The immunisation protocol, combining DNA and protein vaccines, leverages the strengths of both approaches to elicit a strong and broad immune response.

The MEV vaccination provided full protection to mice when challenged with the H1N1, H3N2, BV, and BY strains, equivalent to the protection offered by the QIV vaccination. Importantly, a significant reduction in viral load within lung tissues was observed for these strains in the MEV-vaccinated group, contrasting sharply with control groups. Additionally, MEV-vaccinated mice exhibited a substantial decrease in inflammatory cells in the lungs, associated with diminished viral replication, underscoring MEV's potential as a robust defense against influenza challenges. However, for historical strains H1N1-PR8 and H3N2-X31, the survival rates among MEV-vaccinated mice were 66.67% and 50%, respectively, suggesting partial protection. It is reported that the combination of distinct epitopes from various influenza antigens elicits cross-reactive protection against diverse strains of different subtypes and types since highly conserved epitopes are pivotal targets in the quest for creating universal vaccines.17, 18, 19 These observations may be attributed to the presence of selected immunodominant peptides from different strains within the MEV construct, emphasizing the importance of epitope conservation for universal vaccine development.

Our findings underscored the effectiveness of MEV in augmenting both T-cell and humoural immune responses. The study revealed a significant upregulation in the expression levels of IFN-γ, TNF-α+, and IL-2 in CD4+ T cells targeting H1-peptide, H3-peptide, B-peptide, and All-peptide within the MEV group, compared with the control groups. Additionally, there was a noteworthy elevation in the proportion of GC B cells within the B cell population, as well as an increase in the proportions of plasmablast cells and plasma cells within the lymphocyte population in the MEV group. The difference in HI and MN titres among different strains was presumably influenced by the raised plasmablasts and plasma cells in the MEV group. The raised GC B cell reaction was presumably influenced by the raised T cell responses observed in the MEV group. These findings align with expectations, considering the pivotal role of IL-2 in stimulating and sustaining the proliferation of CD4+ T cells, CD8+ T cells, and B cells. Our study affirms the comprehensive and synergistic immunomodulatory effects of MEV, with promising broad immunity against diverse viral strains.

However, we noted some limitations in this study. Firstly, while the study demonstrates a collective immune response to the peptide pool, the individual contributions of each peptide require further investigation. Interestingly, MEV induced much lower levels of IFN-γ and IgG in H3-peptide than other peptides. Our results revealed that the number of IFN-γ+ SFCs per 2 × 105 splenocytes targeting the All-peptide was the highest, followed by that targeting B-peptide and H1-peptide, while the count for H3-peptide was the lowest in the MEV group. Similar trends were observed for IgG SFCs per 2 × 105 splenocytes, where targeting the All-peptide exhibited the highest count, followed by the B-peptide and H1-peptide, while the count for the H3-peptide remained the lowest in the MEV group. These results may be partly attributed to the rapid genetic and antigenic variability in H3N2 subtype viruses, which has led to the emergence of numerous genetically distinct lineages with various mutant strains co-circulating. This observation aligns with conclusions drawn from the CDC's vaccine effectiveness studies on the general population, which indicate that while the vaccine effectively mitigates the severity of influenza A H1N1 and influenza B infections, its efficacy against H3N2 is relatively low.14,69 However, the phenomenon of heterosubtypic immunodominance among influenza virus vaccine strains remains insufficiently explored. Increasing the dosage of the least immunogenic HA subtype could potentially address the limitations observed in multivalent vaccine formulations. For the future development of a universal MEV vaccine, it will be critical to determine the optimal dosage of the H3N2 antigen to enhance the host immune response to both components post-vaccination.

Secondly, our study found that MEV vaccination successfully induced HI antibody titres of up to 1:40 against both seasonal and historical influenza strains, but MN titres were detectable only against seasonal strains. The HI titer of 1:40 is recognized as the minimum threshold that confers a 50% reduction in influenza infection risk, serving as a benchmark for protective immunity. In our study, the observed HI titres were at most 1:40 for all detected strains, indicating a low HI titre level. The MN titres showed a similar pattern, with at most 1:20 for all detected strains, suggesting a potential influence of the vaccination strategy on antibody responses, particularly for seasonal strains. Utilizing a prime-boost regimen, female C57BL/6 mice were administered two doses of DNA vaccines followed by one dose of a protein vaccine. It's worth noting that while the DNA vaccine alone is not immunogenic enough, the DNA prime-protein boost approach was shown to be effective in eliciting high levels and better qualities of antibody responses.57,70, 71, 72, 73, 74 The DNA vaccine's strength lies in its ability to lay a substantial foundation of antigen-specific B cells, which are essential for a robust antibody response.75,76

Thirdly, the necessity for an additional boost stems from the modest antigen expression levels of DNA vaccines in vivo. This third vaccination step is crucial for amplifying the antigen-specific response. Our data indicate that the MEV, within a DNA prime/protein boost framework, has the capacity to enhance both the diversity and potency of T cell responses against influenza virus antigens, correlating with a significant decrease in lung viral load. This reduction is instrumental in limiting influenza virus replication and bolstering the host's immune defense. The observed protection in this study might also be influenced by variations in the vaccine structure, dosage, vaccination method, challenge virus strain, and culture conditions utilised.77, 78, 79 The MEV, administered via intramuscular injection, provoked robust immune responses, highlighting the importance of this route in facilitating antigen delivery to lymph nodes for processing by antigen-presenting cells (APCs), and subsequent activation of B and T cells. The immunisation protocol outlined in this study may benefit from refinements, particularly through the incorporation of adjuvants that stimulate the production of more broadly neutralizing antibodies to influenza virus proteins. It has been proposed that adjuvants are necessary to elicit a strong immune response to peptide-based influenza vaccines.80 This combined vaccine strategy holds promise in advancing the development of a universal vaccine that triggers higher HI and MN titres against influenza virus infections.

In conclusion, our results demonstrated the efficacy of the broad-spectrum MEV against influenza viruses in mice, also paving the way for future research directions and potential improvements. Since the MEV can be synthetically produced, avoiding the propagation of the virus in eggs and therefore can be rapidly and efficiently manufactured.81, 82, 83 By incorporating additional conserved B or T cell epitopes, we can enhance the immune response, thereby facilitating more effective viral clearance. The T cell and B cell responses observed in our study provide a valuable comparison to those induced by other peptide-based vaccines. Notably, the Multimeric-001 vaccine, which has advanced to human trials, has shown modest effects. These results provide valuable insights into the potential efficacy and mechanisms of peptide vaccines when applied in a clinical setting. Further research could focus on optimizing the vaccine formulation, dosage, and administration protocols to bolster its efficacy against a broader spectrum of viruses, including historical strains. Moreover, long-term studies that evaluate the longevity of MEV-induced immune responses and their applicability across various populations would yield invaluable data. The MEV strategy holds promise as a more potent and flexible approach for the development of a universal influenza vaccine, one that could address the evolving challenges of influenza virus diversity and adaptability in the future. This innovative direction in vaccine development may lead to a paradigm shift in how we combat influenza, offering a robust and adaptable solution to a persistent global health challenge.
