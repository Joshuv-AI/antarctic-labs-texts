Characterization of Mineral Dissolution in Fracture-Pore Type Rocks Using the Lattice Boltzmann Method
Authors: Zihan Zhao, Yutian Zhang, Yunmao Jiang, Yuying Wu, Nan Qin, Yongming He
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Mineral dissolution in fractured-pore rocks is a critical process in subsurface applications, including petroleum extraction, geothermal energy development, and carbon dioxide sequestration. Understanding the evolution of geometric topology and permeability is essential for scaling up research and guiding engineering design. In this study, we integrated a linear Boolean model with a self-affine rough surface approach to generate synthetic fracture-pore rock structures with varying degrees of matrix geometrical heterogeneity. The dual-distribution function lattice Boltzmann method was employed to simulate the mineral dissolution and quantify permeability evolution across a broad spectrum of Péclet (Pe) and Damköhler (Da) numbers. Our results indicate that changes in mechanical aperture and reactive surface area are primarily governed by the Da number. Furthermore, under high Pe and Da conditions, increased matrix heterogeneity leads to nonlinear alterations in permeability. Finally, we proposed a predictive diagram for permeability evolution during mineral dissolution under diverse matrix heterogeneity scenarios.

Introduction

The dissolution of minerals in porous media significantly impacts the properties of reservoir rocks. − This process alters the topology of rock space and affects fluid flow characteristics across various scales, from the pore scale to the reservoir scale. Permeability is a fundamental parameter that characterizes the hydraulic property of porous rock. , Understanding the evolution of permeability due to mineral dissolution is essential for a wide range of underground applications, including geological carbon sequestration, , geothermal exploration and development, − and environmental contaminant remediation. ,

The evolution and formation of dissolution patterns in porous media have been extensively investigated. − Experimental and numerical studies reported that dissolution regimes could commonly be classified into face (compact) dissolution and uniform dissolution , depending on Péclet and Damkohler numbers. These are two dominant parameters that control the dissolution process in porous media. , Recent studies have revealed that the spatial heterogeneity of pore structures also can alter the dissolution patterns under identical chemical and flow conditions. ,, Noiriel et al., (2013) through X-ray micro-CT quantified how pore heterogeneity leads to spatial variations in dissolution rates, revealing the control of local Pe/Da number distribution on dissolution patterns (compact dissolution pattern to wormhole dissolution pattern). Zhang et al., (2022) and Menke et al., (2015) explored the effect of spatial heterogeneity of the pore space on the dissolution processes and developed phase diagrams that quantitatively define transitions between different dissolution regimes for pore-type rocks. However, these studies focus on pure pore media and ignore the presence of fractures, which are the main flow channels in subsurface reservoirs. Dissolution dynamics in fractured systems also have been investigated by Deng et al., (2015), revealing wormhole formation patterns governed by fracture network connectivity, and while matrix–fracture interactions during dissolution have been shown to produce complex permeability-altering feedback mechanisms, , a critical knowledge gap remains regarding pore heterogeneity effects in fracture-pore systems. For example, Lei et al., (2020) only considered the geo-mechanical effect of matrix–fracture interaction but did not consider the influence of matrix pore size distribution on dissolution. Current understanding of dissolution patterns derives predominantly from studies of either pure pore-type rocks or fractured media, leaving the coupled behavior in fracture-pore-type rocks, where matrix porosity and fracture networks interact synergistically, largely unexplored.

Fracture-pore type rocks are more prevalent in subsurface formations, where the pore space can be regarded as the matrix, and the fracture could represent the main flow channel in reservoir rocks. Existing studies on fracture-pore systems are limited to two aspects: one is to simplify the matrix as a homogeneous porous medium, and the other is ignoring the dynamic evolution of fracture aperture during dissolution. Due to the complexity of such geometry structure, mechanical aperture and surface area are usually used to characterize the topology evolution during mineral dissolution and to upscale researches. , Because the aperture strongly correlates to the hydraulic conductivity and surface area is important for the upscaling dissolution rate computation. The relationship between permeability and porosity has been widely studied, leading to classical models such as the Kozeny–Carman equation. , The dynamic permeability–porosity relationships within various dissolution regimes are vital for reservoir-scale modeling and subsurface project applications. Therefore, systemically exploring the aperture evolution, surface area alteration, and permeability–porosity relationships is interesting and significant for the fracture-pore rocks affected by the reactive transport process.

In order to quantify the geometry characterization of fracture-pore type rock, we combined the discrete Fourier transform (DFT) approach and the linear Boolean model to synthesize the fracture-pore type rocks in two dimensions. The dual-distribution functions lattice Boltzmann method (LBM) was employed to simulate the mineral dissolution process and compute dynamic permeability. Beyond simulation results, we found that the Da number has a more important influence on the mechanical aperture evolution and surface area evolution than the Pe number. And the sudden increase phenomena were observed as the porosity increases during mineral dissolution process. The competitive mechanism was found to control the permeability evolution under high Pe and high Da conditions.

Aperture Evolution in Fracture Pore Rocks

Accurately quantifying fracture flow capacity is essential for predicting fluid transport in both engineered and natural systems, yet the complex pore geometry of real fractures renders this task highly challenging. The mechanical aperture a m is an important aperture metric that is obtained through direct measurement based on fracture morphology.

In this study, the following algorithm was employed to determine the mechanical aperture:(1)Beginning at the center cell y = min­(y) + H/2­(H = max­(y) – min­(y)), move vertically upward until the first solid cell is encountered. Denote the distance from the center cell to this cell as h 1x . Similarly, move vertically downward to find the nearest solid cell below and record the corresponding distance h 2x . (2) The local mechanical aperture a mx is defined as the sum of h 1x and h 2x . (3) Repeat (1) and (2) for every x-coordinate, compute the average value of all a mx , and designate this average as the global mechanical aperture a m. It is worth noting that, due to the absence of particles in the solid matrix at the totally dissolved inlet region, we set h 1 = H/2, h 2 = H/2 at the inlet to ensure that a m is non-negative.

For the β = 0.1 geometry model, compared to the other two models, the relationship between mechanical aperture and porosity is more linearized. An increase in matrix pore heterogeneity decreases the evolution rate of the mechanical aperture (Figure ). It is induced by the matrix geometry heterogeneity. As the matrix heterogeneity increases, more solute transport could occur at the matrix pore space, which means more dissolution far away from the fracture channel. Hence, the aperture evolution rate is reduced as the matrix heterogeneity increases.

When the Pe number is 10, as the Da number increases, the evolution rate of the normalized permeability increases. At Pe = 10 and Da = 10, the increase magnitude in normalized permeability is the largest, exhibiting a trend of initially slow flattening followed by a rapid growth. However, when the Pe number is low (Pe ≤ 1), the permeability evolution trends show a weak dependence on Da number.

For β = 0.1 (see Figure a), at Pe = 10 and Da = 10, the dissolution pattern is wormholing, which means that the reactive transport process is mainly controlled by advection. And the dissolution proceeds along preferential flow pathways and creates new preferential channels, and initial preferential channels also can be enlarged. Hence, it can accelerate the permeability alteration rate. And in Figure , before the normalized porosity reaches 1.05­(shown with black circles in Figure a), the permeability increases slowly; once it reaches 1.10, the rate of increase accelerates. Which can be explained by the fact that the initial dissolved area mainly occurs near the inlet, so the permeability evolution rate is slow; once the dissolution front is far away from the inlet, the alteration rate is rocket improved.

For β = 0.3 (see Figure b), at Pe = 10 and Da = 10, the normalized permeability growth curve is also initially relatively flat. However, when normalized porosity reaches ∼ 1.14 (shown with black circles in Figure b), the change rate of normalized permeability begins to increase. And then, the growth rate of normalized permeability increases sharply.

For β = 0.5 (see Figure ), when Pe = 10 and Da = 10, there is no significant abrupt change in the overall growth rate of normalized permeability, with only a slight change near a porosity of 0.68 (shown with black circles in Figure ).

In all relationships between normalized permeability and normalized porosity, we found three fracture-pore models. The permeability increases more rapidly for Pe = 10, Da = 10 and Pe = 10, Da = 1 (i.e., wormholing dissolution regime) than for other Pe, Da conditions. Under wormholing dissolution, permeability rises slowly during the early stage. Once a dominant dissolution channel develops, this results in the channel widening, and the permeability evolution rate is accelerated (shown at Figure ).

With the matrix geometry heterogeneity increase, under the conditions of Pe = 10 and Da = 10, when the final normalized porosity reaches 1.3, the normalized permeability magnitude decreases, being ∼15, 16, and 25, respectively. It can be explained that, for high Pe and high Da conditions, dissolution occurs in high flow velocity regions. In a more homogeneous model, because the difference between pore size in the matrix and fracture aperture is high, the dissolution mainly occurred at the fracture regions, and a dominant wormhole channel was developed. However, as the matrix heterogeneity increased, the difference between pore size in the matrix and fracture aperture decreased. More wormhole channels were generated, which increased the lateral dissolution effect (y-direction dissolution). So, as the matrix heterogeneity increased, with both Pe and Da being high numbers, the evolution rate was induced.

All simulation cases demonstrate that the permeability changes’ rapid curves fall into the range of high Pe (Pe ≥ 1) and high Da (Da ≥ 1) conditions. Which is important for upscaling study and engineering design. Hence, we plotted these simulated results for the three geometry models in Figure . The interesting thing is that with the identical Pe and Da conditions, the relationship between permeability alteration rate and matrix heterogeneity is not always linear. For instance, when Pe = 10 and Da = 10, when the normalized porosity is lower than ∼1.22 (shown with black circles in Figure ), The permeability evolution rate with β = 0.5 is higher than β = 0.3, and when the porosity is higher than the critical point, the permeability change rate is reversed. Which suggests that there are two competitive mechanisms that exist in the dissolution process to accelerate the permeability change. On the one hand, in the more homogeneous matrix model, the wormholing dissolution focuses on the main fracture channel, the initial fracture aperture is widened, and the solute transport more easily penetrates the geometry model (see in Figure a), which can dramatically improve the permeability increase rate. On the other hand, at more heterogeneous matrix fracture-pore model, more wormhole channels are easily generated (see Figure c and Figure e), which also can make the permeability change faster. Hence, these two competitive mechanisms together result in the permeability evolutions showing nonlinear phenomena with the matrix heterogeneity increase.

The fitting parameter η for all simulation cases was shown in Figure . In Figure , it is readily found that when both Pe and Da were high, the fitting parameters showed a large divergence. And for other dissolution regimes (i.e., face and uniform dissolution), the divergence is small. The η demonstrates a dissolution regime dependence. It suggests that in relevant subsurface projects, when the injection rate is high (high Pe number) and the host rock reactivity is high (high Da number), engineers should carefully consider the matrix heterogeneity effect.

From an engineering point of view, our findings can guide subsurface engineering operations and predict permeability changes during reactive processes in fracture-pore rocks.

For instance, in petroleum engineering, our findings can optimize the acid stimulation of fractured reservoirs. For the acid solutions to stimulate the reservoirs, if the we hope to strongly widen the aperture, Da number is the main factor. If we hope to apply the surface area-porosity relationships to upscaling modeling, as the matrix heterogeneity increases, the sudden increase phenomena should be considered. And for predicting permeability during the mineral dissolution process, when the solution pH is low (high Da conditions) and the injection rate is high (high Pe conditions), the matrix heterogeneity influence cannot be ignored.

Conclusions

In this study, we integrated a linear Boolean model with a self-affine rough surface approach to synthesize 2D fracture-pore rock models with varying degrees of matrix heterogeneity. A dual-distribution function lattice Boltzmann method (LBM) was systematically applied to simulate the mineral dissolution process on the pore scale and quantify the dynamic evolution of geometric topology and permeability. Based on 64 simulation cases covering a wide range of Péclet (Pe) and Damköhler (Da) numbers, the following four key conclusions are derived:(1): The Da number is identified as the primary parameter governing the evolution of both mechanical aperture and reactive surface area. Increased matrix heterogeneity leads to a decrease in the rate of aperture alteration.(2): A “sudden increase” in the normalized solid–fluid surface area was observed as porosity increased during the dissolution process. This phenomenon is attributed to concave grain shapes, which significantly impact the upscaling of surface area alteration.(3): Under high Pe and high Da conditions (the wormholing regime), permeability evolution is driven by two competitive mechanisms: the widening of the initial fracture aperture and the generation of multiple secondary wormhole channels. These mechanisms result in nonlinear permeability alterations as matrix heterogeneity increases.(4): The classical Kozeny–Carman power–law relationship is found to be highly suitable for characterizing the normalized permeability-porosity evolution in dissolving fracture-pore rocks. The derived fitting parameters serve as essential inputs for upscaling models in subsurface engineering projects, including optimized acid stimulation, geothermal reservoir modeling, and carbon sequestration.
