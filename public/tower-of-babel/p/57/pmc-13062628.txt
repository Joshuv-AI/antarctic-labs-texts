Contemporary overview of liver transplantation for intrahepatic cholangiocarcinoma and combined hepatocellular-cholangiocarcinoma
Authors: Hae Won Lee
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Historically, intrahepatic cholangiocarcinoma (iCCA) and combined hepatocellular-cholangiocarcinoma (cHCC-CCA) were regarded as absolute contraindications for liver transplantation (LT) due to dismal outcomes characterized by high recurrence rates and poor long-term survival in early experiences. Consequently, these malignancies have been systematically excluded from standard transplant criteria for decades. However, the landscape of transplant oncology is undergoing a significant paradigm shift, driven by a deeper understanding of tumor biology and refined patient selection strategies. Recent multicenter retrospective studies have identified a distinct subgroup of patients-specifically those with “very early” iCCA in the setting of cirrhosis-who achieve excellent post-transplant outcomes comparable to those of hepatocellular carcinoma. This evidence has prompted major international societies to update their guidelines, cautiously opening the door for LT in this selected population. Conversely, cHCC-CCA remains a diagnostic and therapeutic challenge. This narrative review critically analyzes the pivotal data driving the current paradigm shift and synthesizes the latest clinical practice guidelines to provide a contemporary roadmap for the management of iCCA and cHCC-CCA in the transplant setting.

INTRODUCTION

Cholangiocarcinoma (CCA) refers to a diverse group of malignancies originating from the biliary epithelium. CCAs are divided into three subtypes based on their anatomical site of origin: intrahepatic CCA (iCCA), perihilar CCA (pCCA) and distal CCA.1 While hepatocellular carcinoma (HCC) represents the vast majority of primary hepatic malignancies, iCCA, is the second most common primary liver cancer after HCC, accounting for approximately 10-15%, with a steadily rising incidence.2 Combined hepatocellular-cholangiocarcinoma (cHCC-CCA) is a rarer entity, exhibiting histopathological features of both hepatocellular and biliary differentiation, with a reported incidence ranging widely from 0.4% to 14.0% depending on the diagnostic criteria applied.3 Due to the lack of uniform terminology in the literature, obtaining reliable data on effective treatment strategies and outcomes has been challenging. Although surgical resection remains the treatment of choice for both iCCA and cHCC-CCA, a significant number of patients present with unresectable disease due to multifocality, unfavorable anatomical location, or underlying poor liver function. For patients with end-stage liver disease and early-stage HCC, liver transplantation (LT) is established as the gold-standard treatment. However, the application of LT for iCCA and cHCC-CCA has historically been fraught with failure.

Traditionally, CCA has been considered an absolute contraindication to transplantation. Although LT has evolved into an effective treatment option for selected patients with unresectable pCCA over the past two decades, with standard model for end-stage liver disease (MELD) exception point now available,4,5 early attempts of LT for iCCA in the 1990s and early 2000s yielded unacceptably high recurrence rates, often exceeding 50-60% within the first 2 years.6,7 Despite this historical dogma, the lack of effective alternative treatments for unresectable iCCA and the limitations of liver resection (LR) in cirrhotic patients have compelled the transplant community to revisit LT as a potential therapeutic option. Recent advances in genomic profiling, improved imaging techniques, and most importantly, the identification of favorable prognostic subgroups, such as “very early” iCCA, have challenged the status quo. Furthermore, the emerging field of transplant oncology is integrating neoadjuvant systemic and locoregional therapies to select patients with favorable tumor biology who may benefit from LT.8

cHCC-CCA also carries a poor prognosis. Tumor recurrence is frequent even after surgical resection, with 5-year survival rates remaining below 30%.9 Data on outcomes following LT for cHCC-CCA are scarce. Most reports are limited by small sample sizes and the inclusion of heterogeneous tumor types due to inconsistent diagnostic criteria.4 Consequently, reported outcomes vary significantly. While some series suggest that LT outcomes for cHCC-CCA are inferior not only to HCC but possibly also to iCCA,10,11 others report satisfactory results comparable to those of HCC.12,13 Thus, the role of LT for cHCC-CCA remains undefined.

This review aims to provide a summary of the latest insights into the evolving role of LT in iCCA and cHCC-CCA. We will discuss the historical data that led to their exclusion, the pivotal studies that have reshaped current perspectives, and the future directions for clinical trials and guideline development.

LIVER TRANSPLANTATION FOR INTRAHEPATIC CHOLANGIOCARCINOMA

The historical rationale for contraindication

In the early era of transplantation, patients with iCCA were transplanted without specific selection criteria, and the initial results reported in the 20th century were dismal, with 5-year overall survival rates of 0-10%.14,15 These outcomes established iCCA as a representative contraindication for LT.

In 2000, Meyer et al.16 published a retrospective international multicenter study of 207 patients who underwent LT with CCA. Even though 43 patients (21%) had incidental CCA discovered at the time of LT for other indications, the 5-year survival rate for entire cohort was only 23%. A Spanish multicenter study by Robles et al.7 in 2004 analyzed outcomes by distinguishing between iCCA and pCCA. While they observed 5- and 10-year post-LT survival rates of 42% and 23%, respectively, for 24 patients with iCCA, thses were significantly lower than the expected survival for benign indications or HCC. More alarmingly, the recurrence rate was profound, and perineural invasion and multifocality were identified as potent predictors of mortality. Similarly, a comparative analysis by Hong et al.17 from University of California, Los Angeles (UCLA) found that while LT provided the widest possible surgical margins, it did not translate into a survival advantage compared to resection. Recurrence-free survival was disappointingly low in the transplant arm, largely attributed to the immunosuppressive environment accelerating the growth of occult micrometastases present at the time of surgery. In 2016, Vilchez et al.11 analyzed the United Network for Organ Sharing (UNOS) database and reported a 5-year survival rate of 47%, an improvement over previous reports. Nonetheless, it was significantly inferior to that of LT for HCC.

These outcomes solidified the view that the biology of iCCA was too aggressive for transplantation, and the scarcity of donor organs could not justify their allocation to patients with such a poor prognosis. Thus, most LT experience for iCCA since 2000 has been derived from incidental cases where iCCA was diagnosed upon pathological examination of the explant.5

The very early iCCA concept: a paradigm shift

The rigid exclusion of iCCA began to be challenged by observations regarding incidental iCCA. Retrospective analyses suggested that these small, incidental tumors had outcomes vastly superior to those diagnosed preoperatively. This observation was formalized in a Spanish multicenter matched cohort study by Sapisochin et al.,12 which compared 42 patients with incidental iCCA or cHCC-CCA to a matched HCC control group. Although the overall study group had significantly worse survival, a subgroup with single tumor ≤2 cm in diameter achieved 1-, 3-, and 5-year survival rates of 92%, 83%, and 62%, respectively. These rates were statistically comparable to the matched HCC group (P=0.4) and significantly superior to patients with advanced disease, whose 5-year survival plummeted to 44% (P<0.001).

Based on these findings, the concept of “very early” iCCA was introduced. In a subsequent report, Sapisochin et al.18 showed no recurrence during a median follow-up of 36.4 months in patients transplanted for “very early” iCCA, although the sample size was small (n=8). To validate these findings globally, an international retrospective study involving 17 major transplant centers was conducted.19 Analyzing 48 patients with iCCA (15 very early, 33 advanced), the study reported 1-, 3-, and 5-year actuarial survival rates of 93%, 84%, and 65% for the “very early” group. In stark contrast, patients with advanced disease had a 5-year survival of only 45% (P=0.02) and a recurrence rate of 61% (P=0.01). Interestingly, outcomes were determined by the size of iCCA nodule even in cases with separates HCC and iCCA nodules.

This study was pivotal for several reasons. First, it confirmed that the excellent outcomes were reproducible across different centers. Second, it clearly distinguished “very early” iCCA as a separate biological entity, likely representing an earlier step in the carcinogenesis with less potential for angioinvasion or micrometastasis. Finally, it provided a clear, measurable criterion for clinical decision-making. However, a critical limitation remains regarding the clinical application of the “very early” iCCA concept. The existing data are derived almost exclusively from retrospective observations of incidental iCCA discovered during the pathological examination of explants. Translating these findings to patients with preoperatively diagnosed iCCA presents a significant challenge, as the clinical dynamics, including diagnostic certainty, lead-time bias, and patient selection, differ substantially. Consequently, the excellent post-transplant outcomes observed in incidental cases may not accurately reflect the prognosis of known, preoperatively diagnosed tumors, making it difficult to simply generalize these retrospective findings into real-world selection criteria. Therefore, rigorous prospective validation is strictly required to determine whether the “very early” criteria can be safely and effectively applied as a standard of care in the preoperative setting.

Advanced iCCA and downstaging in non-cirrhotic patients

While very early iCCA represents a favorable indication for LT, LR is generally preferred for non-cirrhotic patients. However, locally advanced iCCA with vascular invasions or extensive multifocal involvement is often unresectable even in non-cirrhotic populations. Upfront LT remains contraindicated for such advanced cases due to high recurrence rate.

In this context, the concept of biological selection via response to neoadjuvant therapy is gaining traction. The Houston Methodist and MD Anderson Cancer Center group pioneered a prospective protocol for locally advanced, unresectable iCCA.8 Their criteria included biopsy-proven iCCA, absence of extrahepatic disease or vascular invasion, completion of neoadjuvant chemotherapy, and demonstrated disease stability for at least 6 months. Patients who completed this protocol and underwent LT achieved a remarkable 5-year overall survival of 83% and recurrence-free survival of 50%. Notably, neither tumor volume nor multifocality affected recurrence. This suggests that the response to therapy serves as a biological stress test, selecting patients with indolent disease while filtering out those with aggressive biology.20 This aligns with the principles of transplant oncology, shifting focus from morphological criteria to biological behavior. However, as experience is limited to small-scale studies, this approach has not yet been adopted as a general protocol.

Current guidelines and consensus statements

Accumulating evidence has led to significant updates in international guidelines. First, the working group consensus report from the International Liver Transplant Society (ILTS) in 2020 incorporated the “very early” iCCA concept in their recommendations.4 They stated that patients with single iCCA ≤2 cm in a cirrhotic liver may benefit from upfront LT, whereas those with unresectable, advanced iCCA in a non-cirrhotic liver should only be considered for LT if the tumor remains stable after neoadjuvant therapy. They also strongly recommended tumor biopsy for nodules with atypical imaging features to rule out iCCA and emphasized that LT for unresectable iCCA should be restricted to strict clinical protocols and trials.

Subsequent guidelines, including the European Association for the Study of the Liver (EASL)-International Liver Cancer Association (ILCA) clinical practice guidelines and the 2024 ILTS-ILCA consensus recommendations, have largely aligned with the 2020 ILTS report but expanded the eligible tumor size to 3 cm, using the term “early” iCCA.21,22 This new cutoff is based on a 2016 multicenter study analyzing an “intermediate stage” subgroup (single tumor 2.1-3.0 cm, not poorly differentiated), which showed a 5-year survival of 61%, similar to the “very early” group.19 However, given the small number of patients (n=6) in this subgroup, generalizing the 3 cm cutoff remains difficult.

The American Association for the Study of Liver Diseases (AASLD) remains the most conservative. In their 2023 practice guidance, while acknowledging promising data on “very early” iCCA, they emphasize that LT should be conducted only under Institutional Review Board approved research protocols, highlighting the need to demonstrate tumor stability via an observation period.20 Major recommendations of international societies on LT for iCCA were summarized in Table 1.

LIVER TRANSPLANTATION FOR COMBINED HCC-CCA

Confusion in terminology and diagnostic difficulties

Given that hepatic progenitor cells can differentiate into both hepatocytes and cholangiocytes, biphenotypic mixed tumors can develop. Mixed tumors are classified into various types. In the Allen-Lisa classification, type A indicates that HCC and iCCA nodules are separated within the same liver, while type B indicates that the two nodules are adjacent and intermingled.23 Types A and B can be combined into Goodman type I by more simplified classification.24 The true cHCC-CCA refers to individual masses that have characteristics of both HCC and CCA through intermediate differentiation, corresponding to Allen-Lisa type C or Goodman type II.23,24 However, the term “mixed hepatocellular cholangiocellular carcinoma” has been loosely used to encompass diverse entities. This terminological inconsistency contributes to the lack of clear data on the clinical course and outcomes of cHCC-CCA.

cHCC-CCA poses unique diagnostic challenges. Radiologically, these tumors exhibit heterogeneous features. While some display classic HCC patterns (arterial hyperenhancement and washout), a substantial proportion show progressive centripetal enhancement typical of iCCA, or mixed features. Jeon et al.25 found that while 61.4% of cHCC-CCAs were categorized as LR-M on magnetic resonance imaging, 37.1% were categorized as LR-4/5. Notably, LR-M tumors were associated with higher early recurrence. This radiological ambiguity often leads to misdiagnosis, with the true identity revealed only upon explant pathology. Patients diagnosed with HCC who are found to have cHCC-CCA after LT often suffer worse outcomes than expected.26 Therefore, for radiologically atypical HCC, biopsy is warranted to refine the diagnosis and carefully discuss whether LT is an appropriate.4 However, biopsy sensitivity is limited due to tumor heterogeneity and resulting sampling error. Gigante et al.27 reported that even with the combined use of imaging and biopsy, the diagnostic sensitivity for cHCC-CCA remained at just 60% (specificity 82%). Furthermore, even with a diagnosis, pathological examination lacks a reliable prognostic grading system.4

Prognostic heterogeneity

The post-transplant prognosis of cHCC-CCA is debated. One might hypothesize that outcomes would be intermediate between HCC and iCCA, but current data suggest a more complex reality. In 2011, Sapisochin et al.10 reported that the 5-year post-LT recurrence rate of eight cHCC-CCA patients was 78%, which was significantly higher than recurrence rate of not only HCC patients but also iCCA patients (17% and 58%, respectively, P=0.004). Also, in a 2010 report by Panjala et al.,26 the 5-year survival rate of 12 patients who underwent LT for cHCC-CCA was only 12%. However, in a Spanish multicenter study published in 2014, the 5-year post-LT survival rate of cHCC-CCA patients was not significantly different from that of matched HCC patients (78% vs. 86%, P=0.9).12 In addition, Lunsford et al.13 found that the 5-year recurrence-free survival rate in cHCC-CCA patients was similar to that of HCC patients matched on explant pathology (42% vs. 44%, P=0.45) although it trended toward inferior when matched on pretransplant characteristics (28% vs. 61%, P=0.12). They speculated that cHCC-CCA may not be inherently aggressive but rather more likely to be poorly differentiated than HCC with similar pretransplant features.

Despite these findings, many studies continue to report inferior outcomes of cHCC-CCA. A 20-year UNOS database showed the 5-year survival rate for cHCC-CCA was similar to iCCA (40% vs. 47%) but significantly worse compared to HCC (62%, P=0.002).11 A recent Korean multicenter study also observed significantly lower survival and recurrence-free survival for cHCC-CCA compared to matched HCC (63.8% vs. 84.0% and 56.3% vs. 80.6% at post-LT 3-years, P=0.015 and P=0.012, respectively).28

Current opinion

Some studies attribute the inferior outcomes of cHCC-CCA to poorer differentiation at the time of selection.13 However, since most transplants were performed without a definitive preoperative diagnosis, there is no clinical evidence that selective transplantation of pathologically favorable cHCC-CCA yields acceptable results. Moreover, other than pathological evaluation, no clinical tools for selecting favorable tumors have been identified. Therefore, based on retrospective data, it is reasonable to conclude that LT outcomes for cHCC-CCA are generally inferior to those for HCC.

While it is well established that iCCA outcomes are worse than HCC, comparing cHCC-CCA to iCCA is complex. Although many reports suggest cHCC-CCA has a worse prognosis than iCCA,4 others find them similar or better.11,12 This prognostic heterogeneity likely stems from the rarity of the disease and inconsistent diagnostic criteria. Nevertheless, international societies remain more conservative regarding cHCC-CCA. While the door is opening for “very early” iCCA, no society has issued recommendations for cHCC-CCA. The ILTS working group explicitly stated that cHCC-CCA is not an established indication and recommended biopsy for atypical tumors, with transplant eligibility re-evaluated based on the diagnosis.4 Consequently, establishing selection criteria akin to the Milan Criteria is essential to justify LT for cHCC-CCA, but clinical experience remains insufficient to define such criteria.

FUTURE RESEARCH DIRECTIONS

Genomic landscape and biomarkers

Contemporary management must consider the molecular landscape. While little is known about cHCC-CCA, several mutations have been identified in iCCA, including fibroblast growth factor receptor (FGFR) gene 2 fusions, somatic mutations in isocitrate dehydrogenase 1/2 (IDH1/2), and Kirsten rat sarcoma viral oncogene homolog (KRAS) mutations. These represent promising candidates for targeted therapy.29 Additionally, the infiltration of various immune cells (CD8+, CD4+, dendritic cell) in biliary cancer correlates with improved survival or a reduced potential for metastasis, providing a rationale for immunotherapy.30 For patients with unresectable CCA, the standard approach is systemic chemotherapy with gemcitabine and cisplatin.31 However, its effect is actually not dramatic. Although proven targeted or immunotherapies are currently lacking, the development of effective treatments is anticipated, likely in other malignancies. If applied to LT for iCCA, these modalities could improve outcomes and expand indications.

The presence of specific mutations might help stratify patients who are at high risk of recurrence despite favorable morphological features. Liquid biopsy, specifically the detection of circulating tumor DNA, is emerging as a promising tool. Detecting minimal residual disease or molecular recurrence before it becomes radiologically evident could revolutionize post-transplant monitoring and patient selection. Future protocols may incorporate molecular downstaging, where targeted therapies are used to render a patient transplantable, followed by circulating tumor DNA surveillance.

Key questions to be addressed

As mentioned earlier, several promising results regarding LT for “very early” iCCA have recently been reported. However, the level of evidence remains too low to incorporate these findings into clinical guidelines. This is primarily because most supporting data are derived from retrospective studies involving incidental iCCA.5 Since outcomes for incidental versus preoperatively diagnosed cases differ, prospective validation is essential to establish the feasibility of LT for “very early” iCCA. The most anticipated research was the phase 2 study, liver transplantation for early intrahepatic cholangiocarcinoma (LT for iCCA, NCT02878473), initiated in 2018.32 Unfortunately, this study was terminated prematurely due to low accrual.33 While several prospective studies, including the TESLA trial (NCT04556214), are currently underway, they include locally advanced iCCA or patients receiving neoadjuvant therapy.34 Thus, high-level evidence for early iCCA is unlikely to emerge soon.

First and foremost, prospective validation is required for preoperatively diagnosed “very early” iCCA. Beyond this, strategies for iCCA and cHCC-CCA must be refined. Specifically, research should determine if neoadjuvant therapy should be standard for iCCA, analogous to the protocol for pCCA. Neoadjuvant therapy, followed by an observation period, could effectively exclude biologically unfavorable tumors, thereby serving as a crucial selection criterion to enhance LT survival. Furthermore, studies should assess if LT is a justifiable treatment for downstaged advanced iCCA. In the context of HCC, it is established that patients initially exceeding transplant criteria who undergo successful downstaging via locoregional therapy achieve superior survival compared to continued non-transplant therapies; however, prospective evidence supporting this approach has only recently emerged.35 For cHCC-CCA, identifying criteria for favorable tumors is paramount. Without this, it cannot be an established indication. Finally, effective targeted and immunotherapies could significantly expand the scope of LT for these malignancies.

CONCLUSION

The field of LT for iCCA and cHCC-CCA has evolved from a state of absolute prohibition to one of cautious opportunities. The “very early” iCCA concept (single tumor ≤2 cm) has been validated as a safe oncological boundary, offering curative potential for patients who previously had none. For locally advanced iCCA, the focus has shifted to neoadjuvant downstaging and assessing biological response as a gatekeeper for transplantation. However, substantial challenges remain to be addressed. Until then, LT for iCCA and cHCC-CCA should be performed in centers with specialized expertise, ideally within the context of clinical trials or investigational protocols. We are entering a new era where the contraindication is no longer the tumor type itself, but rather unfavorable tumor biology.
