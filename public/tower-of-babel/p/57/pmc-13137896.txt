Collicocephalus saminae n. sp. (Cestoda: Lecanicephalidea), a new tapeworm from Aetomylaeus wafickii (Myliobatiformes: Myliobatidae) in the Persian Gulf, Iran
Authors: Sara Sadeghi, Mohammad Haseli
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractCollicocephalus saminae n. sp. (Cestoda: Lecanicephalidea) is described from the spiral intestine of the eagle ray Aetomylaeus wafickii Jabado, Ebert & Al Dhaheri in the Persian Gulf. The lecanicephalidean genus Collicocephalus Koch, Jensen & Caira, 2012 was previously known only from its type species, Collicocephalus baggioi Koch, Jensen & Caira, 2012, described from Aetomylaeus vespertilio (Bleeker) in the Gulf of Carpentaria, Australia. In contrast to C. baggioi, which exhibits its greatest width at the scolex, the new species reaches its greatest width at the mature proglottid. The two species also differ in cirrus sac size, in the position of the external seminal vesicle, and in microtriches on the distal surface of the acetabula. Among the three species delineated from the former A. nichofii complex, Collicocephalus has been recorded only in A. wafickii. The use of cestodes with high host specificity is further discussed as a tool for validating the delimitation of eagle ray species within this complex.Keywords: Eagle rays, Cestode, Parasite, Taxonomy, Indian Ocean

Introduction

Lecanicephalidea Hyman, 1951 is an order of cestodes whose members predominantly parasitize batoids, with a small proportion reported from sharks (see Jensen et al., 2017). Its type family, Lecanicephalidae Braun, 1900, contains seven genera (Caira et al., 2026), including Collicocephalus Koch, Jensen & Caira, 2012, erected to accommodate Collicocephalus baggioi Koch, Jensen & Caira, 2012 (Koch et al., 2012). This species was described from Aetomylaeus vespertilio (Bleeker) in the Gulf of Carpentaria, Australia. Because Koch et al. (2012) remains the only account of Collicocephalus to date (Caira et al., 2026), its occurrence in other species of Aetomylaeus Garman remains unknown.

The myliobatid genus Aetomylaeus comprises 7 species distributed across different oceanic regions (Last et al., 2016). Aetomylaeus nichofii (Bloch & Schneider), formerly recognized as a species complex (Naylor et al., 2012), was revised taxonomically by White et al. (2016). Based on the genetic framework established by Naylor et al. (2012) for the A. nichofii species complex, Jabado et al. (2022) recognized the northwestern Indian Ocean populations to represent a distinct species and described them as Aetomylaeus wafickii Jabado, Ebert & Al Dhaheri. Consistent with expectations that this host species would also host a species of Collicocephalus, a new species, Collicocephalus saminae n. sp., is described herein from the Persian Gulf.

Materials and methods

As part of a stock assessment project led by the Iranian Fisheries Science Research Institute, sampling was conducted in November 2014 aboard the research vessel Ferdows. Four specimens (three females and one male) of the eagle ray A. wafickii were collected from the Persian Gulf, south of Qeshm Island (26°45′54.8″N 56°10′40.2″E). Spiral intestines were removed, incised longitudinally, fixed in 10% formalin in plastic bags and transferred to the laboratory. Cestodes were separated under a stereomicroscope, and specimens from each ray were stored in individual vials containing 70% ethanol for enduring storage.

Specimens were stained using Mayer-Schuberg's aceto-carmine, immersed for 20 min and subsequently dehydrated through a graded ethanol series (70%, 80%, 90%, and 100%; 15 min each). Specimens were then cleared in methyl salicylate and mounted on slides with Canada balsam.

Drawings were prepared using a drawing tube attached to an Olympus CH-2 microscope (Tokyo, Japan). Measurements were taken using an ocular micrometer, recorded in SPSS 19.0 (IBM SPSS Statistics for Windows, Version 19.0. Armonk, NY: IBM Corp) and converted to micrometers. Measurements are presented in the text as micrometers (μm) unless otherwise stated and are presented as ranges followed in parentheses by the mean, standard deviation, N (number of specimens measured) and n (total number of observations when more than one measurement per worm was taken).

The cleanest-mounted specimens were selected for scanning electron microscopy (SEM). Specimens were freed from Canada balsam using xylene and gradually hydrated through a graded ethanol series (100% to 70%; 15 min each). Destaining was performed using acid alcohol (2 mL of concentrated HCl in 100 mL of 70% ethanol). Specimens were then gradually dehydrated through a graded ethanol series (70% to 100%; 15 min each), followed by final dehydration with hexamethyldisilazane (HMDS). Specimens were oriented on carbon conductive double-sided adhesive tape, coated with gold to a thickness of approximately 5 nm using a K450X carbon coater (Quorum Technologies, Ltd., Laughton, U.K.), and examined with a Vega 2 LM scanning electron microscope (Tescan Orsay Holding, Brno, Czech Republic) at 15 kV. Chervy (2009) was followed for standard terminology of microtriches.

The holotype and paratypes were deposited in the Collection of the Zoological Museum (ZUTC), University of Tehran, Tehran, Iran.

Description

(Based on 17 mature specimens prepared as permanent mounts for light microscopy and 8 specimens examined using SEM) Worms euapolytic, 1.6–3.8 mm (2.5 ± 0.1, N = 7) long, proglottids 35–67 (49 ± 11, N = 8) in number (Fig. 1A). Greatest width at level of mature proglottids (Fig. 1A). Scolex consists of scolex proper, apical modification of scolex proper, and apical organ. Scolex length in specimens with everted apical organ 78–104 (92 ± 12, N = 5); in specimens with retraced apical organ 64–90 (75 ± 10, N = 6). Scolex width 71–128 (96 ± 15, N = 17). Scolex proper with four acetabula, sucker-like in form, 20–26 (21 ± 2, N = 13, n = 18) long, 20–26 (22 ± 2, N = 13, n = 15) wide. Apical organ retractable (Fig. 2B), large, muscular, with glandular surface, in form of transverse pad when everted (Fig. 1, Fig. 2A).

(Based on 17 mature specimens prepared as permanent mounts for light microscopy and 8 specimens examined using SEM) Worms euapolytic, 1.6–3.8 mm (2.5 ± 0.1, N = 7) long, proglottids 35–67 (49 ± 11, N = 8) in number (Fig. 1A). Greatest width at level of mature proglottids (Fig. 1A). Scolex consists of scolex proper, apical modification of scolex proper, and apical organ. Scolex length in specimens with everted apical organ 78–104 (92 ± 12, N = 5); in specimens with retraced apical organ 64–90 (75 ± 10, N = 6). Scolex width 71–128 (96 ± 15, N = 17). Scolex proper with four acetabula, sucker-like in form, 20–26 (21 ± 2, N = 13, n = 18) long, 20–26 (22 ± 2, N = 13, n = 15) wide. Apical organ retractable (Fig. 2B), large, muscular, with glandular surface, in form of transverse pad when everted (Fig. 1, Fig. 2A).

Apical organ covered with capilliform filitriches (Fig. 2E); scolex proper bears acicular filitriches (Fig. 2H); proximal and distal acetabular surface bears trullate spinitriches and papilliform filitriches (Fig. 2D, F, G). Strobila covered with acicular filitriches (Fig. 2K); scutes present only on posterior strobila (Fig. 2I and J).

Cephalic peduncle very short. Proglottids craspedote, laciniate; laciniations more distinct in immature proglottids, inconspicuous in mature proglottids (Fig. 1A). Immature proglottids initially wider than long, become longer with maturity (Fig. 1A); mature proglottids longer than wide (Fig. 1A). Mature proglottids 1–2 (N = 16) in number, 324–714 (515 ± 19, N = 14) long, 102–216 (163 ± 33, N = 14) wide; width:length ratio 1.0:2.4–3.8 (3.1 ± 0.4, N = 13) (Fig. 1C). Genital atrium (Fig. 1C) lateral, slightly pre-equatorial, irregularly alternates; distance from genital atrium to posterior end of mature proglottid 170–406 (292 ± 61, N = 13), equivalent to 51–64% (57 ± 4, N = 13) of proglottid length.

Cirrus sac oval (Fig. 1C), 50–60 (53 ± 4, N = 7) long, 40–58 (48 ± 6, N = 9) wide. Cirrus coiled, unarmed. Internal seminal vesicle absent. Vas deferens enlarged to form external seminal vesicle in terminal mature proglottids in which testes are degenerated (Fig. 1C). External seminal vesicle extensive, occupies much of median field; extends from ovary to anterior margin of cirrus sac, turns anteriorly and connects by duct to cirrus sac (Fig. 1C). Testes round to oval, in single layer, arranged in two lateral columns, preovarian (Fig. 1C); testes 19–26 (21 ± 3, N = 9) in number, 44–70 (55 ± 7, N = 11, n = 19) long, 20–32 (24 ± 3, N = 11, n = 21) wide. Most testes degenerated in terminal mature proglottid (Fig. 1C).

Uterus ventral, extends from near ootype to anterior margin of proglottid (Fig. 1C). Vagina opens posterior to cirrus sac into genital atrium (Fig. 1C). Ovary (Fig. 1C), follicular, H-shaped in dorsoventral view, symmetrical, tetralobed, 110–190 (149 ± 24, N = 13) long, 88–138 (112 ± 18, N = 11) wide. Mehlis’ gland (Fig. 1C) posterior to ovarian isthmus, 22–34 (28 ± 5, N = 7) long, 20–30 (24 ± 4, N = 7) wide. Vitellaria (Fig. 1C) extend from anterior margin of proglottid to ovary, interrupted at genital atrium, arranged in two lateral fields, each comprises one dorsal and one ventral column; vitelline follicles absent posterior to ovary, 12–76 (37 ± 18, N = 15, n = 44) long, 8–20 (13 ± 4, N = 15, n = 33) wide. Osmoregulatory canals in one pair (Fig. 1C). Eggs not observed.

Remarks

Collicocephalus saminae n. sp. is consistent with the generic diagnosis of Collicocephalus by the presence of a large, muscular, retractable apical organ in the form of a thick pad, laciniate proglottids, and a four-lobed ovary, H-shaped in dorsoventral view. Additional diagnostic features include one dorsal and one ventral column of vitelline follicles arranged in two lateral fields, two columns of testes in a single layer, a sac-like uterus, the absence of post-ovarian vitelline follicles, and the presence of a single pair of osmoregulatory canals per proglottid.

In contrast to C. baggioi, in which the greatest width occurs at the scolex, the new species exhibits its greatest width at the mature proglottid. Furthermore, in the new species the external seminal vesicle is median, whereas in C. baggioi it is lateral. This new species is further distinguished from the type species by a shorter (50–60 vs. 87–170) and narrower (40–58 vs. 67–165) cirrus sac. Furthermore, the two species differ in their surface ultrastructure. In C. saminae n. sp., the distal surface of the acetabula is covered with trullate spinitriches, whereas in C. baggioi, it is covered with capilliform filitriches.

The challenge in preparing the SEM images was that the isolated worms came from an intestine filled with food material. In the other three hosts, no worms could be found to use for SEM (1 out of 4 hosts was infected). This caused the worm surfaces to become dirty, making SEM photography challenging. However, the results did provide interpretable photos after two rounds of photography, despite the fact that their quality is below the standards of the journal.

Fixation in formalin precluded the use of molecular data for species differentiation. With respect to the limited number of taxonomic features used to differentiate C. saminae n. sp. and C. baggioi, it is worth noting that morphometric characters are routinely employed to distinguish species across diverse cestode genera, e.g., in the phyllobothriidean Scyphophyllidium Woodland, 1927 (e.g., Cutmore et al., 2017), the onchoproteocephalidean Phoreiobothrium Linton, 1889 (e.g., Caira and Jensen, 2015), and the lecanicephalidean Tetragonocephalum Shipley & Hornell, 1905 (e.g., Jensen and Guyer, 2021). Our diagnostic criteria extend beyond measurements alone and include qualitative characters as well, namely the difference in microtriches on the distal surface of the acetabula between the two species. This difference cannot be attributed to intraspecific variation. Although microthrix form may change over the course of development, no study to date has documented intraspecific variation in microthrix morphology in the adult stage of any cestode species. Moreover, the two congeners were collected from geographically distant localities and from two different host species, both of which are known to harbor highly host-specific tapeworms.

Discussion

Based on molecular phylogeny of the mitochondrial NADH2 gene region, Naylor et al. (2012) demonstrated that Aetomylaeus nichofii represents a species complex composed of three distinct lineages: A. nichofii from Indonesia (type locality), Aetomylaeus cf. nichofii 1 from the Persian Gulf, and A. cf. nichofii 2 from the Arafura Sea (Australia). Subsequently, White et al. (2016) described Aetomylaeus cf. nichofii 2 as Aetomylaeus caeruleofasciatus White, Last & Baje, 2016. Jabado et al. (2022) described A. cf. nichofii 1 from the Persian Gulf as A. wafickii. Given that host specificity is nearly strict among lecanicephalideans and some other cestode orders (see Jensen et al., 2017), the parasite fauna of each valid species formerly included in the A. nichofii complex can be useful for validating the taxonomic delimitations established within this batoid group.

Current knowledge of the parasite fauna of A. wafickii is limited to the present study and that of Meraji Masouleh Moghadam and Haseli (2014), in which two species of Diphyllidea from the Persian Gulf were described: Halysioncum kishiense and Echinobothrium parsadrayaiense. For A. caeruleofasciatus, the knowledge is limited to the study of Ivanov and Caira (2013), who described Halysioncum arafurense Ivanov & Caira, 2013 prior to the formal taxonomic description of the host species. In A. nichofii sensu stricto, the cestode fauna currently reported comprises representatives of “Tetraphyllidea” (see Caira et al., 2017; Koch et al., 2012), the Lecanicephalidea, Elicilacunosus Koch, Jensen & Caira, 2012 (see Koch et al., 2012) and New Genus 13 (see Jensen et al., 2016), and the Onchoproteocephalidea, Acanthobothrium hanumantharaoi Rao, 1977 (see Rao, 1977).

The greatest diversity of Lecanicephalidea is associated with Myliobatiformes (Jensen et al., 2017). Although 23 species of this batoid order, with 19 species as potential hosts for lecanicephalideans (Caira et al., 2026; Jensen et al., 2017), have been reported from the Persian Gulf (Eagderi et al., 2019), lecanicephalidean species have been recorded from only three hosts in this region: C. saminae n. sp. from A. wafickii, Polypocephalus barsami Momeni Roudsari & Haseli, 2021 from Pastinachus sephen (Fabricius), and Eniochobothrium qatarense Al Kawari, Saoud & Wanas, 1994 from Rhinoptera jayakari Boulenger (Al Kawari et al., 1994; Momeni Roudsari and Haseli, 2021; Caira et al., 2026). Given the richness of potential host species in the region, it is probable that additional lecanicephalidean species will be discovered in the Persian Gulf.
