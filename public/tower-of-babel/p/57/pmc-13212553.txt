High performance Raman amplifier: applications in optical communication and biomedical devices
Authors: Fathy M. Mustafa, Ahmed F. Sayed, Moustafa H. Aly, Tarek M. Said
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
High-performance Raman Amplifiers (RAs) have emerged as a powerful solution for enhancing signal strength and transmission quality in advanced optical systems. By utilizing stimulated Raman scattering in optical fibers, these amplifiers provide wide bandwidth, low noise figures, and flexible gain profiles, making them highly suitable for next-generation high-capacity communication networks. In addition to their established role in long-haul and metro optical communication links, RAs are increasingly applied in biomedical devices that rely on precise optical signal delivery. Key medical applications include Optical Coherence Tomography (OCT), laser-based imaging systems, fiber-optic endoscopy, and biomedical sensing platforms, where improved optical gain directly enhances imaging depth, resolution, and diagnostic accuracy. This work highlights the operational principles, performance advantages, and cross-domain applications of high-performance RAs, emphasizing their growing significance in both communication infrastructure and advanced medical diagnostic technologies. Three backward configurations: two, three and four cascaded Ras are investigated. Simulations are performed with pump powers of 200, 400, and 600 mW using three fiber types: single-mode fiber (SMF), TrueWave, and FreeLight, each with a 100 km amplifier length. The four-stage configuration achieves the highest performance, with 63 dB gain and 59.9 dBm output power at 600 mW using TrueWave fiber, demonstrating significant improvement in gain and output power over related work. Based on our results, RA boosts the returned OCT signal before detection, increasing SNR, enables deeper tissue imaging, and improves axial resolution and contrast, especially in low-reflectivity tissues. For MRI and CT, long-distance sensing along its gantry without electronic repeaters, Higher accuracy in detecting weak sensor signals and Support for multi-sensor networks inside the MRI machine.

Introduction

The rapid growth of global data traffic, driven by the increasing demand for high-capacity broadband and emerging applications such as cloud computing, 5G/6G networks, and quantum communication, places significant pressure on optical communication systems to deliver higher transmission reach and bandwidth1,2. Optical amplifiers are a cornerstone of these systems, enabling long-haul transmission by compensating for fiber attenuation without the need for optical-to-electrical conversion3. The ever-increasing demand for high-capacity and long-haul optical transmission systems has placed stringent requirements on optical amplifiers to deliver low-noise, broadband gain while maintaining system scalability. Raman amplification, based on the principle of stimulated Raman scattering (SRS), has emerged as a key solution due to its ability to provide distributed gain along the transmission fiber and its spectral flexibility compared to conventional Erbium-Doped Fiber Amplifiers (EDFAs)4,5. Amplifier technologies are used in biomedical engineering, with a focus on implantable and portable biomedical devices. It discusses design requirements for amplifiers used in neural recording, biomedical signal processing, and wireless medical systems. Key findings highlight the importance of achieving high gain, low noise, and biocompatibility in amplifier designs6.

Unlike discrete amplifiers, Raman amplifiers allow gain tailoring by selecting appropriate pump wavelengths and power levels, thereby enhancing system reach and capacity in Dense Wavelength Division Multiplexing (DWDM) networks7. The efficiency of Raman gain strongly depends on pump configuration and pump power distribution. Typically, three pumping schemes are employed: forward pumping, backward pumping, and bidirectional pumping8. Backward pumping is widely adopted as it reduces nonlinear interactions between pump and signal, minimizes Relative Intensity Noise (RIN) transfer, and provides better noise performance9. Meanwhile, forward pumping offers simpler implementation but is more susceptible to pump–signal crosstalk. Hybrid or bidirectional pumping combines the benefits of both approaches, allowing optimized trade-offs between gain, noise, and nonlinear effects Furthermore, multiple pump sources are often employed to achieve gain flattening over wide bandwidths10,11. The Raman gain directly influences the quality of the received signal, particularly in terms of Optical Signal-to-Noise Ratio (OSNR), Bit Error Rate (BER), and overall system capacity. Higher pump power levels generally increase Raman gain, but excessive power may lead to nonlinear impairments and amplifier-induced noise12. The integration of an Optical Parametric Amplifier (OPA) with Optical Coherence Microscopy (OCM) shows significant improvements in SNR (up to 15 dB) and penetration depth when imaging in turbid biological tissues. The OPA effectively enhances imaging resolution and contrast in highly scattering environments, making it highly suitable for biomedical imaging applications13. In optical networks, RAs are used in priority-based routing and wavelength assignment with traffic grooming and in priority-based dispersion-reduced wavelength assignment14,15.

Fiber type and effective areas also play critical roles in determining the achievable gain and received signal performance16. Truewave and Freelight fibers exhibit different nonlinear thresholds and dispersion properties compared to standard single-mode fiber (SMF), thereby impacting the maximum signal power and achievable system reach17. Optimizing the balance between Raman gain, pump power, and fiber characteristics is therefore essential to improving the quality of the received optical signal18. One major challenge is the requirement for high pump power to achieve significant Raman gain. Since the Raman gain coefficient is relatively small, tens to hundreds of milliwatts of pump power are typically needed, which raises concerns about nonlinear impairments and system stability19. By leveraging optical parametric amplification, the system enhances sensitivity and signal strength compared to conventional OCT. Applications are particularly relevant for deep tissue imaging and improving image clarity in biomedical diagnostics20. Excessive pump power can induce unwanted nonlinear effects such as Four-Wave Mixing (FWM), Stimulated Brillouin Scattering (SBS), and Cross-Phase Modulation (XPM), all of which degrade transmission quality21.

Many recent studies have improved Raman gain using multiple Raman investigated a backward pumped Distributed Raman Amplifier (DRA) architecture aimed at enhancing gain performance in long-haul optical transmission. Their study demonstrated backward pumping not only improves the overall gain profile but also contributes to reduced noise figure and improved signal quality over extended distances. Using simulation and analytical modeling, they validated the efficiency of backward pumping compared to conventional amplification schemes, highlighting its suitability for next-generation high-capacity optical networks22,23. The work in24 conducted a performance study of forward and backward hybrid optical Raman amplifiers, analyzing how different pumping orientations influence system gain, noise figure, and overall transmission performance. Their results indicated that hybrid pumping schemes can effectively combine the benefits of forward and backward configurations, offering improved gain uniformity and noise performance compared to single-direction pumping.

Amplification in cascaded Raman amplifiers varies with wavelength because the Raman gain coefficient is inherently wavelength‑dependent. Each pump wavelength provides maximum gain at a fixed frequency shift of approximately 13 THz (about 100 nm) toward longer wavelengths, and the gain decreases as the signal moves away from this peak. In cascaded configurations, multiple pumps generate multiple shifted gain bands, producing a wider overall spectrum but still with non‑uniform gain across wavelengths. Therefore, the final gain profile depends on pump wavelengths, pump powers, the fiber Raman gain coefficient, and the wavelength‑dependent fiber attenuation.

This study provides valuable insights into pump configuration optimization for Raman amplification in long-haul optical networks. A four-cascaded RA configuration is proposed to mitigate gain attenuation and enhance signal power across a 100 km transmission link, using three optical fiber types: SMF, Freelight, and TrueWave. The system is investigated under different backward pump power levels and compared with three alternative setups: a single RA (basic model), two cascaded RAs (Proposal One), and three cascaded RAs (Proposal Two). MATLAB-based simulations demonstrate that the four-stage configuration achieves superior performance, outperforming related work.

Beyond telecommunication networks, RAs are also emerging as enabling components in advanced biomedical applications. In hospital optical infrastructures, they support the high-speed transfer of large imaging datasets such as MRI and CT scans. In Optical Coherence Tomography (OCT), they improve the detection of weak optical signals, enhancing diagnostic accuracy. Additionally, when integrated with optical biosensors, RAs significantly boost sensitivity for monitoring critical biomedical parameters.

In its origin, RA is an optical amplifier, and MRI/CT machines do not use Raman amplification directly for image formation. However, RA enhances the optical subsystems that support these machines. In MRI systems, RAs are used to boost weak optical signals over long fibers, improve signal-to-noise ratio (SNR), and enable remote placement of signal processing electronics (outside the magnet room). Optical biosensors inside MRI use RAs to enhance very weak back-reflected optical signals from these sensors and distributed sensing (over tens or hundreds of meters). In CT machines, CT scanners also use optical fibers in several subsystems. Optical sensors in CT gantries use RAs to improve sensitivity of these fiber sensors, especially when long fiber lengths are used and weak signals return from distributed sensing points.

Integration of optical amplifiers with biomedical devices

Optical amplifiers are connected to biomedical devices mainly through fiber-optic channels, where they enhance signal power, improve diagnostic accuracy, enable long-distance biomedical data transfer, and support advanced laser-based treatments.

Optical amplifiers play a significant role in modern biomedical systems, particularly in medical imaging, diagnostics, and therapeutic applications. Their integration with biomedical devices enhances signal strength, improves accuracy, and allows for real-time monitoring over longer distances. The main approaches to linking optical amplifiers with biomedical devices are explained as follows.

Fiber-optic communication in biomedical equipment

Many biomedical devices, such as endoscopes, OCT systems, and laser-based surgical tools, rely on fiber-optic links. By connecting an optical amplifier (such as EDFA or RA) to the fiber channel, weak optical signals can be boosted without converting them to electrical signals. This improves image clarity and reduces noise during transmission34,35.

Long-distance signal transmission

In telemedicine and remote patient monitoring, medical data transmitted over optical fibers may degrade over distance. Optical amplifiers can be inserted at suitable points in the communication link between the biomedical device and the monitoring center to ensure reliable, high-quality data transfer36,37.

Imaging and diagnostic systems

Devices like OCT scanners require high-power, stable light sources. Optical amplifiers are connected to the light delivery system of these devices to increase the optical power, thereby enhancing image resolution and penetration depth into biological tissues38,39.

Laser-based therapeutic applications

In photodynamic therapy and laser surgery, optical amplifiers are integrated with laser systems to provide controlled high-power optical output. This ensures that the medical device delivers the required therapeutic dose while maintaining safety standards40,41.

Integration method

The optical amplifier is typically connected in-line with the optical fiber link of the medical device. Input from the biomedical device’s light source or sensor is directed into the amplifier. The amplified output is then delivered to the imaging system, diagnostic unit, or communication channel. Proper wavelength matching, power control, and biocompatibility measures must be ensured to protect both the device and the patient.

Optical amplifiers such as EDFA, RA, and Semiconductor Optical Amplifier (SOA) play an essential role in medical systems. They are used to improve signal strength and transmission quality in both hospital communication networks and optical imaging devices42–44.

Raman gain versus fiber distance

FreeLight fibers exhibit stronger Raman amplification compared to SMF. At 200 mW, the gain reaches 16.64 dB with Proposal Three, exceeding the SMF counterpart. While SMF requires higher pump power and multiple amplifiers to achieve meaningful gains, FreeLight and especially TrueWave fibers demonstrate much higher efficiency. Conclusion

This paper introduces three models for enhancing RA gain using configurations of two, three, and four cascaded RAs. Simulations are conducted with backward pump powers of 200, 400, and 600 mW across SMF, Freelight, and TrueWave fibers over 100 km. The gain–distance relationship is analyzed for both the basic and proposed models. Among them, the four-stage configuration (Proposal Three) demonstrates the best performance, achieving a maximum gain of 63 dB and output signal power of 59.9 dBm with TrueWave fiber at 600 mW. Benchmarking against prior studies confirms its superiority and improved amplification efficiency.

Beyond telecommunications, Raman amplifiers are also promising in the biomedical field. They enable reliable, high-speed transfer of large imaging datasets such as MRI and CT scans in hospital optical networks, enhance weak signals in OCT to improve diagnostic accuracy, and increase biosensor sensitivity for real-time monitoring of vital biomedical parameters. These results highlight the proposed model not only as advancement in optical communication but also as a valuable contributor to next-generation medical optical technologies.
