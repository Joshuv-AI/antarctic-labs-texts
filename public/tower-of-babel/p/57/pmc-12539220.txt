Genome-wide identification of boron transporters (GhBORs) in upland cotton and the role of GhBOR1 in promoting flowering
Authors: Ying Li, Wenmin Yuan, Jialuo Peng, Wenjiao zhang, Qi Ma, Tingting Zhang, Caixiang Wang, Junji Su
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundBoron (B) is a vital trace element necessary for the healthy growth and development of plants and significantly affects agricultural productivity. The efficient absorption and transport of boron in plants is mainly mediated by boron effector transporters encoded by the BOR gene family. However, research on the boron transporter (BOR) in cotton has been limited.ResultsIn this research, we identified 50 BOR proteins across three cotton species that presented conserved structural features and functions. Phylogenetic analysis revealed that the BOR proteins of different cotton species can be divided into four subfamilies, with most of the BOR proteins from upland cotton assigned to subfamily III. Synteny analysis suggested that gene duplication and rearrangement played a role in the evolution of cotton from diploids to tetraploids. qRT-PCR analysis revealed that GhBOR1 expression was upregulated not only in the leaf tissues but also in the leaves of early-maturity cultivars. We further verified the function of this gene using VIGS. Compared with the control plants (TRV:00), the GhBOR1 gene-silenced plants presented delayed flowering and decreased pollen viability, suggesting that GhBOR1 played a crucial role in regulating flowering time and pollen viability in cotton. Furthermore, silencing GhBOR1 also influenced the expression of critical genes involved in flowering regulation, suggesting that GhBOR1 might promote flowering by regulating the activity of these genes.ConclusionA total of 23 GhBORs were identified in upland cotton, among which GhBOR1 played a pivotal role in controlling flowering time and pollen viability, providing genetic resources for breeding for early maturity.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12870-025-07275-2.Keywords: Upland cotton, Early maturity, Flowering time, Pollen viability, Virus-induced gene silencing (VIGS)

Introduction

Boron (B) is the only nonmetallic element in Group IIIA of the periodic table (boron group elements) and is classified as a quasimetallic or semimetallic element, a substance between metals and nonmetals. B is a vital micronutrient for the growth and development of plants [1] and primarily exists as boric acid (H3BO3) in neutral solutions. The presence of B in both the soil and the water used for irrigation plays a critical role in determining agricultural productivity [2]. In plants, the symptoms of B deficiency occur mainly in the growing or enlarged organs of the plant, including inhibited apical dominance, and reduced B accumulation in flower organs causes reduced fertility [3]. In addition, low B also inhibits leaf expansion and indirectly reduces plant photosynthetic capacity, mainly by disrupting microspore formation and pollen tube growth, reducing fertility and leading to malformed fruit [4, 5]. The typical symptoms of B poisoning occur in the marginal areas of mature leaves, with B accumulation at the ends during transpiration stream transport, resulting in chlorosis or necrosis of these parts. Therefore, excessive or insufficient B content reduces not only crop yield but also quality [6]. In summary, B content is critical to plant growth and development, and B transport genes are indispensable for the uptake and efficient use of boron by plants. There are three main mechanisms of boric acid transmembrane transport: passive diffusion through the lipid bilayer, in which aquaporin (AQP) plays an important role; the promoted transport of the Nodulin 26-like Intrinsic Protein (NIP) channel, which is crucial for promoting the absorption of boron required for plant growth; and root development in a low-boron environment. NIP6;1 and NIP5;1 belong to NIP subgroup II and have similar three-dimensional structures and functions in boron transport in Arabidopsis bud tissue development, both of which play a role in regulating intraplant boron transport [7, 8]. In addition, active transport via BOR transporters [2] plays an important role in the transport of organisms.

Arabidopsis BOR1, an efflux transporter, was the first B transporter found in a biological system [9]. AtBOR1, human Anion Exchanger 1 (hAE1), Na+-coupled acid–base transporter (NBCe1) and Saccharomyces mikatae BOR1 are members of the bicarbonate transport protein superfamily solute carrier 4 (SLC4) [10–14]. To date, a total of 20 BOR transporters have been identified in the Brassica napus genome [15], while 14 TaBOR genes have been identified in common wheat (Triticum aestivum L.) [16]. Additionally, the soybean (Glycine max L.) genome contains 13 distinct GmBOR sequences, the poplar (Populus trichocarpa L.) genome harbors 10 unique PtrBOR gene sequences, and four OsBOR1-like genes have been identified in the rice (Oryza sativa L.) genome [17]. Research has demonstrated that alterations in the maize ROTTEN EAR (RTE) gene, a functional counterpart of Arabidopsis’s BOR1 protein responsible for boron transport, can lead to severe developmental defects and sterility [18]. In brassica-type rapeseed, boron transporter downregulation reduces the accumulation of B in flower organs, ultimately leading to severe sterility and loss of seed yield [3]. In wheat, unlike AtBOR1 and OsBOR1, TaBOR1 acts as a boron effector transporter, and its mRNA accumulation is regulated by boron status [19]. The overexpression of VvBOR1 in grape can restore the wild-type phenotype in B-deficient Arabidopsis bor1-3 mutants [20]. These observations indicate that B transporters play crucial roles in the growth and development of plants.

Cotton (Gossypium spp.) serves as the most significant natural fiber source globally and as a crucial raw material for China’s textile manufacturing sector, as well as for oilseeds and vegetable protein, and serves as a cornerstone of China’s agricultural economic development [21]. B deficiency has been reported in at least 132 crops and 80 countries, including cotton in 23 countries in Europe, North and South America, and Africa. B deficiency in upland cotton in the United States was first identified in field trials during the period from 1935 to 1937, and cotton was shown to be the crop with the greatest sensitivity to boron application among dicotyledonous plants [22]. The application of B fertilizer was found to significantly increase cotton productivity and lint characteristics in various tropical soils [23]. Additionally, foliar application of silicon and boron enhances boll retention, increases lint yield, and improves fiber quality characteristics in transgenic cotton [24]. In summary, this phenomenon may be related to the further influence of B on plant growth and development through the action of specific transporters. However, research on BOR as a boron transport factor in upland cotton is limited. Therefore, for the first time, in this study, we identified the number of BOR genes in upland cotton, Asian cotton, and Raimondii cotton and analyzed their physical and chemical attributes along with the evolutionary connections between diploid and tetraploid species. Additionally, we examined the cis-acting regulatory elements and performed differential expression analysis of upland cotton. We identified candidate genes associated with flowering time by transcriptome expression profiling and qRT-PCR and investigated the key role of GhBOR1 in upland cotton flowering and fertility via virus-induced gene silencing (VIGS). This integrated approach offers a significant understanding of the regulatory mechanisms of GhBOR1 in cotton reproduction and contributes to the improvement of cotton breeding for increased yield and quality.

Materials and methods

Identification of GhBOR gene family members

The Arabidopsis BOR (PF00955) protein sequence obtained from the TAIR website (TAIR - Home) served as a reference to detect BOR family members in the cotton reference genome (http://cotton.zju.edu.cn/ (TM-1) ). All family members of BOR in different cotton species were identified using HMMER 3.0 software with the Hidden Markov Model (HMM) files (http://pfam.xfam.org/). The BOR members with higher credibility were screened by 1E-10 . Moreover, the uniqueness and accuracy of the PF numbers of BOR family members were verified through the use of PfamScan < EMBL-EBI . The longest transcript of each gene was retained, and redundant sequences were eliminated. SMART (SMART: Main page (ember.de)) and NCBI Conserved Domain Search were utilized to determine the conserved domain HCO3 (1e-10) included in BOR family members. The physical and chemical properties of BOR were analyzed using the ProtParam tool on the ExPASy (Expasy - SIB Swiss Institute of Bioinformatics) website, including parameters such as amino acid composition, molecular weight, isoelectric point, and instability index. The subcellular localization of the BOR family genes was predicted using the ProtComp tool on the Softberry website, and the accuracy of the prediction results was verified through WoLF PSORT (plant).

Phylogenetic analysis of GhBORs

The BOR Pfam number was used to identify BOR family members in the genomes of upland cotton (G. hirsutum), Asian cotton (G. arboreum), Raimondii cotton (G. raimondii), cacao (T. cacao), grape (V. vinifera), soybean (G. max), rice (O. sativa), wheat (T. aestivum) and Arabidopsis (A. thaliana). The protein sequences were extracted using TBtools and aligned with ClustalW in MEGA11 software. A phylogenetic tree of the nine species was constructed using the neighbor-joining (NJ) method, with evolutionary distances computed under the Jones–Taylor–Thornton (JTT) model. The reliability of the tree was evaluated with 1000 bootstrap replicates.

Interspecific and intraspecific collinearity analysis

The complete-genome sequence and general feature format version 3 (gff3) annotation files of Raimondii cotton, Asian cotton, and upland cotton were downloaded from cottonFGD, and the CDSs were extracted using TBtools. Bedtools was used to convert gff3 files into BED files, the seqkit tool was used to remove duplicate IDs from the BED files to obtain the whole-genome CDS set and BED file of each species, and the JCVI-compara subcommand in the jcvi toolset was used to generate anchor files. Finally, collinear plots were drawn using the jcvi.graphics.karyotype module.

Analysis of the gene structure and cis-acting elements of GhBORs

MEME (https://meme-suite.org/meme/tools/meme) was used to forecast the conservative motifs of the GhBORs, the base number of the motifs was set to 6, the base width ranged from 6 to 50, MEME was obtained by submitting files, and the TBtools map was used to change the genetic structure and conserve motif visualization. The PlanCARE website was used to predict cis-acting elements in the upstream 2000 bp promoter sequence of the GhBORs, and the predicted results were subsequently analyzed and categorized statistically.

Analysis of the differential expression patterns of GhBORs

We downloaded expression information from COTTONOMICS for eight tissues of the TM-1 cultivar (ZJU), including roots, stems, leaves, anthers, pistils, petals, bracts, and sepals, and subsequently normalized and averaged the RNA-seq data. The expression profiles of GhBORs across different tissues of upland cotton were also determined [25, 26]. The transcriptome data of the four extremely maturity cultivars (early-maturing: ZMS50 and YZ2; late-maturing: GXM11 and STS458) at the three-leaf stage were compared in CottonGen (https://www.CottonGen.org/) after gene ID transformation to assess the relative expression profiles of the GhBORs [27]. A heatmap created by TBtools was used to visualize the expression trends of these genes.

qRT-PCR verification

The upland cotton cultivars provided by the Evolutionary and Developmental Biology Team of Gansu Agricultural University, namely, Zhongmian 113 (ZM113), Zhongmiansuo 50 (ZMS50), Guoxinmian11 (GXM11), and STS458, were used as experimental materials at 25 °C with a light period of 16 h/8 h. After being cultivated in a light incubator with a humidity of approximately 60% until the cotyledons were completely flattened, they were transplanted into flowerpots. Samples of stem tips and young leaves at the three-leaf stage were collected for subsequent experiments, and eight tissues, namely, ZM113 roots, stems, leaves, stamens, pistils, bracts, sepals, and petals, were collected. The tissues were swiftly homogenized in liquid nitrogen and subsequently stored at −80 °C for total RNA extraction utilizing a plant RNA purification kit. First-strand cDNA was subsequently synthesized using a FastQuant RT kit. Gene-specific primers for four candidate genes were designed using Primer Premier 5.0 for qRT-PCR validation (Table S1). Real-time PCR amplification was performed using a LightCycler®96 real-time PCR instrument and 2×Universal SYBR Green Fast qPCR Mix (RK21203; ABclonal, China). The transcript levels were normalized to the housekeeping gene GhActin [28]. The relative expression of the candidate genes was calculated by 2−∆∆CT [29, 30].

Agrobacterium-mediated viral induction of GhBOR1 silencing

The GhBOR1 cDNA sequence was obtained from COTTONOMICS (http://cotton.zju.edu.cn/index.htm). The primer-BLAST tool was used to design primers for the VIGS fragment (400–500 bp), and the fragment was subcloned and transformed into the TRV carrier system (pTRV2) to produce the recombinant TRV:GhBOR1 vector. The primer sequences are shown in Table S2. Following the introduction of the vectors TRV:GhBOR1, TRV:00 (negative control), TRV:GhCLA1 (positive control), and pTRV1 into Agrobacterium tumefaciens GV3101, the strains harboring pTRV1 were combined with those containing TRV:00, TRV:GhCLA1, and TRV:GhBOR1 (OD600 = 1.8) at a 1:1 ratio. The mixture was incubated at 28 °C for 3–5 h before being injected into the cotyledon leaves of 7-day-old cotton seedlings. After they were incubated in the dark for 24 h, GhBOR1-silenced (TRV:GhBOR1), negative control (TRV:00) and positive control (TRV:GhCLA1) cotton plants were produced. The VIGS experiment was considered successful if the TRV:GhCLA1 plants presented an albino phenotype after seven days. The silencing efficiency of GhBOR1 was subsequently evaluated via qRT-PCR. The results revealed that 90% of the VIGS-treated plants exhibited significant downregulation of GhBOR1 expression (≥ 90% reduction in expression compared with the controls).

Pollen viability test

Cotton pollen was collected using 0.2 ml flat-cap thin-walled PCR tubes. Next, 20 µL of 0.5% triphenyltetrazolium chloride (TTC) solution was added and gently mixed. The mixture was placed in a gradient PCR (GM-05-1590) temperature controller and reacted at 37 °C for 15 min. After color development, it was observed under a microscope. Pollen grains with uniform color and a deep red color under TTC staining were determined to be vigorous. Pollen grains that were not colored or were very lightly colored (significantly different from standard dark colors) were determined to be inactive. Two to three slides were observed, each with 5 visual fields, and the staining rate of the pollen was calculated. The staining rate represented the percentage of pollen vitality.

Results and analysis

Genome-wide identification of BORs in upland cotton, Asian cotton, and Raimondii cotton

We identified a total of 50 BOR proteins across three cotton species, namely, G. hirsutum (23), G. raimondii (14), and G. arboreum (13), on the basis of the BOR-specific domain HCO3− cotransp (HCO3−). The proteins were subsequently renamed according to their chromosomal locations. Analysis using ExPASy revealed that the BOR proteins belonged to a family of large proteins, with amino acid sequences ranging from 392 to 744 residues, 80% of which were larger than 600 residues. The molecular weights of these proteins ranged from 44,417.82 to 83,033.73 Da, and their isoelectric points ranged from 5.55 to 8.79. In terms of charge, most BOR proteins in G. hirsutum (17 alkaline and 6 acidic), G. raimondii (10 alkaline and 4 acidic), and G. arboreum (11 alkaline and 2 acidic) exhibited alkaline properties. The instability indices of these proteins ranged from 38.03 to 46.64, with the majority of the proteins in all three species showing instability indices between 40 and 50, indicating moderate thermal stability. The GRAVY values ranged from 0.118 to 0.362, indicating a balanced hydrophilic and hydrophobic nature (GRAVY values closer to zero indicate a neutral balance) (Table 1). It All the BOR proteins were predicted to localize to the plasma membrane, suggesting their potential involvement in essential membrane transport processes. These findings offer a deeper understanding of the structural characteristics and potential functional significance of BOR proteins in cotton species.

Analysis of the physicochemical properties of BOR genes

Phylogenetic analysis of BOR protein in different species

To elucidate the species diversity of plant BORs during evolution, we generated a phylogenetic tree using 112 BOR protein sequences from eight distinct species: G. hirsutum (23), G. arboreum (13), G. raimondii (14), T. cacao (6), V. vinifera (6), G. max (26), O. sativa (4), T. aestivum (13), and A. thaliana (7). These 112 proteins could be classified into four subfamilies. Subfamily III contained the largest number of BOR proteins, including 11 GhBORs, 6 GaBORs, and 8 GrBORs. The second most abundant subfamily was subfamily I, which contained the greatest proportion of proteins from cotton species, comprising 12 GhBORs, 7 GaBORs, and 8 GrBORs. Subfamily II consisted of BORs from monocotyledonous plants, mainly TaBORs and OsBORs, whereas subfamily IV contained the fewest proteins, with only VvBOR4 and two AtBOR proteins (Fig. 1). The results also indicated that BORs from upland cotton (G. hirsutum) were closely related to BORs from G. arboreum and G. raimondii, as well as to those from cocoa and soybean, which also suggested that upland cotton BORs were conserved during the evolutionary process and were more closely related to those from dicotyledonous plants than to those from monocotyledonous plants. In addition, we detected fewer BORs in allotetraploid G. hirsutum than in G. arboreum and G. raimondii combined, suggesting that BOR gene loss and rearrangement might have occurred during the transition of upland cotton from a diploid state to a tetraploid state.

Evolution and functional differentiation of the GhBORs

Analysis of the distribution of the 23 GhBORs across the genome revealed that they were unevenly distributed across 17 chromosomes (Fig. 2). To understand how allotetraploid G. hirsutum evolved from diploids G. arboreum and G. raimondii, we studied collinearity among the three cotton species. Additionally, we examined collinearity within the upland cotton genome. The results revealed that both chromosome duplication and rearrangement occurred during the evolution of upland cotton. Most of the chromosomes of G. arboreum and G. raimondii are homologous, and the homologous genes in G. hirsutum might be expressed in the same tissues or cells (Fig. 3A). Twenty homologous gene pairs and one single gene, GhBOR14, were identified across the upland cotton genome, including 19 pairs derived from whole-genome duplication (WGD). Interestingly, GhBOR20/GhBOR21 showed evidence of both tandem duplication and whole-genome duplication on chromosome D10 (Fig. 3B). These findings indicated that gene duplication played a dominant role in the evolution of GhBORs. To explore the functional divergence of the GhBOR gene family and evolutionary selection pressures, we assessed the nonsynonymous substitution rate (Ka), synonymous substitution rate (Ks), and their ratio (Ka/Ks). A Ka/Ks ratio greater than 1 indicates that a gene has experienced significant adaptive pressure during evolution [31]. The GhBOR members presented a Ka/Ks ratio of less than 0.82 (Fig. 3C), suggesting that these genes experienced strong negative selection pressure and were relatively stable in terms of maintained functionality.

Characterization of GhBORs in upland cotton

To elucidate the structure of the GhBOR genes, we identified the full coding sequence (CDS) and noncoding region for each gene, after which we analyzed the gene sequences and conserved domains. We found that the number of exons in the GhBORs ranged from 10 to 13, with most sequences containing 13 exons. The number of introns ranged from 9 to 12 (Fig. 4A). Homologous sequence alignment revealed high conservation of the BOR domain structure in the upland cotton genome, except that GhBOR13 and GhBOR8 were missing motifs 5 and 6 and that GhBOR2 was missing motif 2 (Fig. 4B). The base composition of different motifs was the structural basis of their functions (Fig. 4C). These findings suggested that sequences with high similarity tended to share conserved domains, indicating functional similarity.

Key roles of cis-acting elements in GhBOR promoters

To further understand the function of the GhBORs, we examined the cis-acting elements within the 2000 bp region upstream of the GhBOR promoter. The findings demonstrated that the roles of GhBORs varied, but almost all the genes contained photoresponsive elements. For example, a total of 73 BOX-4 elements were unevenly distributed in the promoters of GhBORs. Similarly, most GhBORs contained multiple hormone response elements. The GhBORs also responded to several growth- and development-related elements, such as circadian elements, the RY element, the O2 site, the cat box, and the GCN4 motif, with CAT accounting for the largest proportion. Other cis-acting elements included TC-rich repeats, LTRs, AREs, and MBSs, indicating their involvement in the responses to stress and low temperature (Fig. 5). These findings suggested that most GhBORs might play roles in plant reproductive development.

Identification of candidate flowering genes through differential expression analysis of GhBORs

To clarify the transcriptional levels of GhBORs, we integrated the expression data of 23 GhBORs in eight tissues of terrestrial cotton, including roots, stems, leaves, stamens, pistils, bracts, sepals, and petals, as well as early- and late-maturing cultivars. The results revealed that these genes could be divided into two categories. One category included genes with high transcriptional levels in reproductive organs, with higher expression levels in late-maturity cultivars than in early-maturity cultivars (Fig. 6A). The other category included genes that were expressed predominantly in vegetative organs and in early-maturity cultivars (Fig. 6C). On the basis of phylogenetic tree analysis and expression profiling, we preliminarily screened four genes that were intimately connected to BORs from Arabidopsis and soybean; these genes were found to be upregulated in leaves and early-maturity cultivars. We subsequently verified the expression levels of these four candidate GhBORs in different tissues and early- and late-maturity cultivars via qRT-PCR. The expression of GhBOR1 gradually decreased across tissues but was highly expressed in roots and leaves. The expression of GhBOR11 and GhBOR19 first decreased but then increased, whereas the expression of GhBOR7 was very low in many organs, including leaves and petals (Fig. 6B). In addition, the expression of GhBOR1 was significantly upregulated in the early-maturing cultivars ZMS50 and ZM113, whereas GhBOR7 and GhBOR11 were highly expressed in ZM113, suggesting that these genes might be involved in early flower development. However, there was no significant difference in the expression of GhBOR19 between early-maturing and late-maturing cultivars (Fig. 6D). Thus, the qRT-PCR results further confirmed the reliability of the transcriptome data. In summary, we identified GhBOR1 as a key candidate gene that was potentially involved in flowering in upland cotton.

Verification of the function of GhBOR1 in upland cotton

To investigate the effect of GhBOR1 on plant flowering time, we utilized VIGS to knockdown the expression of GhBOR1. To determine the effects of suppressing the transcription of target genes on plant traits, we selected gene-silenced plants with transcription levels that were reduced to less than half the level in the TRV:00 plants. The buds of the TRV:00 plants appeared first, whereas the buds of the TRV:GhBOR1 plants emerged after a delay of 4 to 6 days (Fig. 7A). Statistical analysis revealed that the mean budding time of the TRV:GhBOR1 plants was considerably delayed 5.17 days compared with that of the TRV:00 plants (Fig. 7B). Similarly, compared with that of the TRV:00 plants, the flowering time of the TRV:GhBOR1 plants was significantly delayed, averaging 3.33 days later (Fig. 7C). These results indicated that GhBOR1 played a positive role in regulating flowering time in cotton. Additionally, to assess the impact of GhBOR1 expression on plant fertility, we measured pollen viability in both control and silenced plants. TRV:00 plants presented high pollen viability and good pollination ability, with most pollen grains showing red or purple staining in the TCC assays. In contrast, the TRV:GhBOR1 plants presented a reduced pollen quantity, lighter staining, and lower pollen activity (Fig. 7D). Specifically, 76.32% of the pollen grains in the TRV:00 plants were active, whereas only 23.68% of those in the TRV:GhBOR1 plants were active (Fig. 7E). These findings suggested that the loss of GhBOR1 might lead to pollen abortion.

Verification of GhBOR1 function. A GhBOR1 bud and flowering phenotype analysis. B Bud data statistics. C Flowering data statistics. D Pollen viability analysis. E Pollen viability data statistics. F Analysis of flowering-related gene expression

To investigate the effects of silencing GhBOR1 on key flowering genes in cotton, we assessed the expression levels of four pivotal flowering regulatory genes in both TRV:00 and TRV:GhBOR1 plants: GhAP1 (APETALA1), GhFT (FLOWERING LOCUS T), GhSVP (SHORT VEGETATIVE PHASE), and GhLFY (LEAFY). Compared with those in the TRV:00 plants, the expression levels of GhAP1 and GhFT were markedly reduced in the TRV:GhBOR1 plants, while the expression of GhLFY was also diminished. Conversely, the expression of GhSVP notably increased (Fig. 7F). These findings implied that GhBOR1 might facilitate flowering by modulating the expression of critical flowering genes. In conclusion, GhBOR1 likely exerted a positive regulatory effect on flowering.

Discussion

Research progress on Boron transporters (BORs) in different species

Boron, as a trace element, is crucial for the growth of higher plants and is involved in all metabolic and cellular functions, especially in the formation and stabilization of cell walls [32]. RG-II, a highly intricate pectin-based polysaccharide, serves as a vital structural element within the primary cellular matrix of plants. It is composed of a variety of sugar residues that are connected by different glucoside bonds. RG-II exists as a dimer in the cell wall and is connected through borate diester bonds. This boron crosslinking connects two homogalacturonic acid chains to form a three-dimensional pectin network, which is essential for preserving the structural integrity and functional strength of the cell wall. Boron deficiency reduces the cross-linking of pectin, affecting the properties of the cellular matrix. This may lead to various symptoms associated with boron deficiency in plants [32]. In recent years, with our understanding of the molecular mechanisms of plant nutrition deepening and the identification of BOR protein as a key carrier of boron transmembrane transport, its function analysis has achieved important progress in a variety of model plants. For example, Arabidopsis AtBOR1 has been shown to maintain intracellular boron homeostasis through efflux transport [33], whereas rice OsBOR4 regulates normal pollen germination and pollen tube elongation [17]. Citrus CmBOR1, as a xylem boron-carrying transporter, encodes a boric acid/borate transporter related to plant boron deficiency tolerance [34]. These studies indicate that the domain of the BOR protein is closely related to its transport activity and that functional differentiation occurs among different species. However, as an important global cash crop, cotton prematurity may be partly affected by the dynamic remodeling ability of the cell wall, and the transmembrane transport of boron directly affects the formation of RG-II dimers in the cell wall. At present, the conservation and evolutionary relationships of BOR family genes and their intrinsic domains have been analyzed in Arabidopsis and other crop species, but this gene has not been reported in cotton. Therefore, a systematic analysis of the evolutionary characteristics, domain conservation, and boron transport function of the BOR gene family in cotton will help elucidate the molecular mechanisms underlying the response of cotton to boron stress and provide a theoretical basis and candidate gene resources for the exploration of key genes that regulate early maturity traits.

Characterization and conserved evolution of the BOR gene family in cotton

In this study, we comprehensively identified the HCO3− domain-containing BORs within the genomes of cotton species, including a total of 23 GhBORs in allotetraploid upland cotton, 13 in diploid Asian cotton, and 14 in Raimondii cotton. The quantity of BORs found in the tetraploids was nearly double that found in the diploids. The physicochemical parameters and structural characteristics of the members of the BOR family were similar across the three cotton varieties (Table 1). We classified these genes into four classes on the basis of their evolutionary relationships with GhBOR in upland cotton. Subfamilies I and III included both continuous and independent interspecific clades, indicating that BOR evolution was relatively conservative and ancient (Fig. 1), with cotton BOR genes showing a close phylogenetic relationship to those in soybean, cocoa, and Arabidopsis. Subfamily II contained the BOR genes from rice and wheat, which are monocotyledonous plants. Polyploidy represents a major driving force in evolutionary processes, fostering species diversification and increasing genomic variability [35]. Tetraploid upland cotton originated through chromosome doubling following the interspecific hybridization of diploid Asian cotton and Raimondii cotton [36, 37]. Nevertheless, compared with Asian cotton and Raimondii cotton, upland cotton presented fewer BORs, which may have been due to chromosome loss and rearrangement during evolution. We discovered no significant difference in the Ka/Ks ratio between diploid and tetraploid cotton, and the ratio was less than 1 in all species, indicating the stability of these gene sequences across evolution in this family. To further confirm the selection pressure on this gene, a Ka/Ks ratio analysis was conducted (Fig. 3). The results demonstrated that the ratio was less than 1, concentrated at 0.2, and that all the genes underwent negative selection, indicating that the evolutionary pattern was similar and conservative within this family.

GhBOR1 regulates the reproductive development of cotton by coordinating flowering-related gene expression

Given the critical role of boron in plant development, especially during seed formation, the anion exchange activity of the BOR protein is crucial. The basic feature of the BOR protein is its anion exchange activity, and the HCO3− domain plays an essential role in the anion transport process [38–41]. Previous investigations have shown that the nitrate (NO3−) concentration significantly affects the B concentration in leaves and BOR1 gene expression in roots. Under high-NO3− conditions, the growth of the BOR1 mutant is negatively affected by B restriction, whereas this effect is weak under normal NO3− conditions, indicating that BOR1 is involved in the growth regulation of plants under high-NO3− environments [42]. In addition, BOR1 is transported to vacuoles for degradation via K63-linked polyubiquitination to prevent excessive B accumulation [43]. A previous study revealed that BOR1 interacts with the calcium-dependent protein kinase CPK10 in plants, which regulates BOR1 activity in the absence of B [44]. OsBOR1 in rice plays a similar role in B absorption and xylem transport, and its expression varies in a time- and tissue-dependent manner under different B supply conditions [45]. These studies revealed the key role of BOR1 in the regulation of plant B nutrition. Moreover, the protein encoded by the BOR gene is a key factor in plant reproductive growth. It plays a vital role in plant cell wall synthesis, pollen germination, pollen tube elongation, and seed yield [3, 17, 20, 46]. In this study, we identified candidate flowering genes in upland cotton via qRT-PCR and RNA-seq and subsequently validated the gene function by VIGS (Figs. 6 and 7). In grapes, preferential expression of the VvBOR1 gene is observed within floral tissues, and this gene participates in the accumulation of B in berries [20]. Similarly, the rapeseed BnaC4.BOR1;1c gene, a homolog of Atbor-1, is expressed in roots and stem segments and is critical for inflorescence tissue development [3], confirming that GhBOR1 is a key regulatory gene in cotton flowering. Our study revealed that silencing GhBOR1 delayed bud emergence and flowering, as VIGS inhibition of GhBOR1 expression led to boron transport blockage, resulting in boron deficiency that affected flower organ development and the timing of flowering (Fig. 7A). Additionally, pollen viability measurements in control and silenced plants suggested that the loss of GhBOR1 led to pollen abortion (Fig. 7D). These findings are consistent with those from previous studies of the effects of OsBOR4, a boron effector transporter in rice, during pollen germination and tube elongation [17]. Several key flowering genes in G. hirsutum, including GhAP1 and GhFT, which positively regulate flowering, have been reported [47, 48]. In this study, we revealed that silencing the GhBOR1 gene in upland cotton resulted in significantly reduced relative transcriptional levels of the key flowering-related genes GhAP1, GhFT, and GhLFY, thereby regulating the flowering period, which is consistent with the findings of previous studies. Furthermore, the expression level of GhSVP markedly increased in the silenced lines, which is consistent with the findings of prior studies demonstrating that SVP inhibits the expression of flowering-associated integrons such as FT and SOC1 under specific conditions (Fig. 7F). These results suggest that GhBOR1 may regulate flowering by influencing the expression of key genes, possibly through a complex network involving both positive and negative regulation. Additionally, to elucidate the potential mechanism underlying the regulatory function of GhBOR1 in flowering, we constructed a GhBOR protein interaction network and identified 10 putative interacting proteins (Fig. S1). Among them, NPI5-1, which is known to be similar to BOR1, was previously shown to be the main plasma membrane channel involved in boron (or boric acid) transport in plants [7, 43]. Similarly, although NPI6-1 plays a role in different tissues, its expression pattern is similar to that of NPI5-1, suggesting that they may share a common regulatory pathway [49]. We speculate that GhBOR1 and NPI5-1 coregulate boron homeostasis in plants, thereby further modulating plant flowering.

Conclusion

This study elucidates the pivotal role of GhBOR1 in the early maturation and reproductive development of cotton, with a particular emphasis on its regulatory functions in flowering time and pollen viability. Through comprehensive evolutionary and homology analyses, we identified 50 BOR proteins across three cotton species, revealing their conserved structural features and evolutionary trajectories. Using VIGS technology for functional characterization, we demonstrated that GhBOR1 silencing significantly delayed flowering and impaired pollen viability, underscoring its critical role in cotton growth and development. Furthermore, GhBOR1 modulated the expression of key genes involved in flowering regulation, indicating its integration into the regulatory network of flowering in cotton. These findings offer valuable insights into the regulatory mechanisms of GhBOR1 and establish a crucial genetic resource for future breeding initiatives aimed at developing early-maturity cultivars.
