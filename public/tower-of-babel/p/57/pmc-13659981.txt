Life satisfaction and its related factors among older adults: an analysis based on consumption behavior and demographic characteristics of 1,578 older adults in Hubei, China
Authors: Yali Ren, Jing Yang, Junqing Peng, Xiaoyan Xi, Jiahui Dai, Yaxi Zhang, Huimin Xu
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundGlobal population aging is accelerating, with older adults projected to account for as much as 21.6% of the population by 2050. The aging problem in Hubei Province of China is even more serious. We aimed to explore the correlation between the life satisfaction of older adults and their consumption behavior and demographic characteristics in Hubei Province, China.MethodsThis study adopted a cross-sectional design and conducted a questionnaire survey among 1,578 older adults in Hubei Province through multi-stage sampling from May to September 2025. Participants were dichotomized into a satisfied group (including “satisfied” and “basically satisfied”) and a dissatisfied group (including “not very satisfied” and “dissatisfied”) based on their responses to the life satisfaction question. The demographic characteristics and consumption behavior distributions between the two groups were compared and then the independent factors related to life satisfaction were explored.ResultsThe life satisfaction rate among older adults in Hubei Province was 88.21%. Regression analysis revealed that living arrangements, personal monthly disposable income, and monthly clothing/shoes/hats consumption frequency were independently positively correlated with satisfaction (all adjusted odds ratios > 1, all P < 0.05), whereas health condition, monthly consumption frequency of residential category, and monthly transportation/communication consumption frequency were independently negatively correlated with satisfaction (all adjusted odds ratios < 1, all P < 0.05).ConclusionsThe life satisfaction rate of older adults in Hubei Province of China was relatively high, which was associated with both demographic factors and consumption-related factors. Consumption reflecting active social participation was positively associated with life satisfaction, while consumption reflecting obligatory or stressful activities was negatively associated. The study provided preliminary empirical evidence that may inform the development of aging policies and services.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at https://doi.org/10.1186/s12877-026-07988-5.

Background

The global aging population is becoming increasingly severe, and the life satisfaction of older adults is highly valued. However, the aging process in China has been relatively late, and the life satisfaction of older adults has received insufficient attention, with even fewer perspectives on consumption. In 2020, the United Nations General Assembly declared 2021–2030 as the “Decade of Healthy Aging”. According to UN data, the proportion of the global population aged 60 and above was expected to increase from 12.7% in 2020 to 21.6% in 2050 [1, 2]. In 2023, the proportion of older adults in China has reached as high as 15.4% [3], and it was expected to reach 30% by 2035 [4]. Besides, in Hubei Province, this proportion reached 23.6% in 2023 [5]. Against this macro background, it has become not only a critical issue in social welfare but also a key indicator of societal development to enhance the quality of life and subjective well-being of older adults. Meanwhile, life satisfaction has been an important part of the concept of successful aging [6]. According to the 2017–2018 survey report, 65.61% of elderly Indians were satisfied with their lives [7], and according to the 2018 Chinese Longitudinal Healthy Longevity Survey (CLHLS), 72.50% of elderly people in China were satisfied with their lives [8]. And results of the national-level Fifth China Survey of Older Adults’ Living Conditions collected in 2021 showed that satisfaction had risen to 81.4% [9].

The theory of successful aging stated that the improvement of older individuals’ quality of life lied not only in preventing the decline or impairment of physiological and psychological functions, but also in maintaining high levels of physical and mental function as well as active social participation [6, 10, 11]. In addition, activity theory also emphasized that sustained levels of activity and social interaction were crucial for well-being and healthy aging [12]. Therefore, examining the degree of social participation and economic autonomy among older adults from a behavioral perspective, particularly in terms of consumption behavior, served as a crucial approach to understanding the mechanisms underlying their life satisfaction.

However, few studies have examined life satisfaction among Chinese older adults from the perspective of consumption behavior. Most existing studies [7, 8, 13] focused on traditional variables such as health condition, economic income, healthcare and lifestyle habits, with limited attention to consumption behavior, particularly in developing countries. This has resulted in the relative neglect of the micro-level perspective of consumption behavior, which may reflect individual lifestyles and economic autonomy. In fact, consumption activities serve not only as a necessary means to meet basic living needs but also as a vital vehicle for social participation and self-identity construction. Particularly for elderly individuals who were undergoing role transitions, changes in their consumption patterns may be associated with their life satisfaction. Systematic studies that integrate demographic characteristics with consumption behavior within the same framework remain limited. This study aimed to explore the relationship between life satisfaction and consumption behavior, as well as demographic characteristics in Chinese older adults, extending the existing literature, and providing an empirical basis for formulating targeted elderly welfare policies.

Based on the theoretical framework of successful aging theory and activity theory, consumption behavior was conceptualized as a behavioral carrier through which older adults may achieve social participation and economic autonomy at the micro level in this study. Specifically speaking, the “continuous social interaction” emphasized by activity theory [14, 15] may be reflected in consumption categories such as transportation, communication, education, entertainment; whereas consumption behaviors — including clothing, healthcare, and daily necessities and services — may reflect the economic autonomy dimensions of “maintaining physical and psychological functioning” and “sense of environmental control” as outlined in the successful aging theory [11, 16]. Through this theoretical framing, consumption behavior was treated not merely as fragmented empirical indicators but as potentially meaningful elements in understanding older adults’ life satisfaction. We hypothesized that (1) consumption reflecting active social participation was positively associated with life satisfaction, while (2) consumption reflecting obligatory or stressful activities was negatively associated.

Materials and methods

Design and setting

A cross-sectional design was employed and a structured questionnaire was developed using the online questionnaire platform “Wenjuanxing” of Changsha Ranxing Information Technology Co., Ltd. The questionnaire was specifically designed for the research project “Study on the Consumption Needs of Older Adults – A Case Study of Hubei Province,” commissioned by the Department of Civil Affairs of Hubei Province. An English translation of the core items used in this study is available as Supplementary File. To accommodate older participants who might face challenges with smartphone use, two questionnaire collection methods were implemented. First, older adults themselves operated their phones to scan the QR code of the questionnaire and answered it under the guidance of the investigator. Second, the investigator read the questions aloud, older participants provided verbal answers, and the investigator recorded the response via the QR code on behalf of the participants. In both methods, all answers were provided by older participants themselves. The “Wenjuanxing” platform allows users to access and download all questionnaire information at any time. In order to ensure diversity in age, income, and health condition, a multi-stage sampling method was adopted. In the first stage, purposive sampling was used to recruit investigators from provincial universities. Jingmen Vocational College, Wuhan Polytechnic University, Hubei Minzu University, and Enshi Polytechnic were selected to recruit investigators, who came from all cities, autonomous prefectures and Shennongjia Forest Area in Hubei Province. Standardized training was provided to the investigators, and uniform inclusion/exclusion criteria were applied. The second stage was snowball sampling. The recruited investigators conducted a questionnaire survey on elderly people among their relatives and friends. Besides, key places such as local neighborhood committees, township health centers, farmers’ markets, and elderly activity centers were also chosen to conduct the survey. From May 22, 2025 to September 21, 2025, we recruited 1653 older individuals.

The inclusion criteria were: (1) age ≥ 60 years old; and (2) living in Hubei Province for the past six months. The exclusion criterion was questionnaires with inconsistent logic before and after. In the end, 1578 questionnaires were included in the study. The flow-chart of all questionnaires included was illustrated in Fig. 1. The distribution of questionnaire quantities collected across various cities and prefectures in Hubei Province was shown in Fig. 2.

Variables

Dependent variable

The old adults’ life satisfaction was the dependent variable in the study. Considering the cognitive and comprehension abilities of elderly individuals, and the virtually identical results validity [17], the life satisfaction was assessed through responses to the survey question “Are you satisfied with your current life?” It is a single-item measure with a 4-point scale, namely 1 (satisfied), 2 (basically satisfied), 3 (not very satisfied), and 4 (dissatisfied), which had been confirmed to be consistent with those obtained from the Satisfaction with Life Scale (SWLS) [17]. Due to the insufficient sample size of “not very satisfied” (N = 142, 9.00%) and “dissatisfied’ (N = 44, 2.79%), these two levels were combined into “life dissatisfaction”, while “satisfied” (N = 715, 45.31%) and “basically satisfied” (N = 677, 42.90%) were grouped into “life satisfaction”. In this way, the statistical power of the analysis could be enhanced, and this grouping method was also adopted by Zhu, H et al. [18]

Independent variables

Two aspects of information were collected, namely, demographic information, consumption information. The demographic information included age, gender, GDP region of residence, residence type, residence mode, personal monthly disposable income, pre-retirement occupation and health condition. Notably, based on the per capita GDP rankings of 17 cities and prefectures in Hubei Province reported in the Hubei Statistical Yearbook 2024, cities were classified into three categories. (1) High-income region (≥ 100,000 RMB): Yichang, Wuhan, Ezhou, Xiangyang, Qianjiang. (2) Middle-income region (69,000–100,000 RMB): Xiantao, Jingmen, Huangshi, Shiyan, Shennongjia, Xiaogan, Xianning. (3) Low-income region (< 69,000 RMB): Suizhou, Tianmen, Jingzhou, Huanggang, Enshi. Information related to consumption included the expenses spent on medical care in one year, monthly amount spent on food/tobacco/alcohol, monthly consumption frequency of food/tobacco/alcohol, monthly amount spent on clothing/shoes/hats, monthly consumption frequency of clothing/shoes/hats, monthly amount spent on residential category, monthly consumption frequency of residential category, monthly amount spent on daily necessities and services, monthly consumption frequency of daily necessities and services, monthly amount spent on transportation/communication category, monthly consumption frequency of transportation/communication category, monthly amount spent on education/culture/entertainment, monthly consumption frequency of education/culture/entertainment, monthly amount spent on healthcare category, monthly consumption frequency of healthcare category, monthly amount spent on other supplies and services, monthly consumption frequency of other supplies and services, consumption patterns, the acceptance level of new services and products, the primary shopping method in daily life, aspects that are prioritized when choosing products. These consumption variables were selected referring to Classification of Resident Consumption Expenditure released by the National Bureau of Statistics [19] and points of interest.

Although the consumption behavior variables were exploratory within the overall research design, their selection was not solely empirical. It was systematically guided by successful aging theory and activity theory, allowing for a preliminary exploration of potential associations between consumption behaviors and life satisfaction among older adults. Based on these theories, we operationalized consumption behaviors into two theoretically informed categories: socially participatory consumption (i.e. transportation/communication category; education/culture/entertainment; other supplies and services) and economically autonomous consumption (i.e. clothing/shoes/hats, residential category, daily necessities and services and healthcare category). It should be noted that this study aims to explore empirically unexamined relationships between these theory-guided consumption variables and life satisfaction. Thus, the variable structure remained open-ended without presupposing causal pathways. In this way, these independent variables enhanced the theoretical explanatory power of variable selection within this exploratory framework.

Variable processing

The original classification of “Monthly consumption frequency of clothing/shoes/hats” (3–5, 6–10, > 10) were merged. The merging reasons were as follows. Firstly, the sample size for “Monthly consumption frequency of clothing/shoes/hats” (3–5, 6–10, > 10) of “Dissatisfied group” was 15 in total, indicating a sparse category distribution. Secondly, preliminary analysis indicated that the OR value for “Monthly consumption frequency of clothing/shoes/hats” (6–10) was relatively high (37.437) with a wide confidence interval (3.264–429.447), suggesting unstable estimates. Thirdly, in practical terms, a data analysis showed that the proportions of people purchasing clothing, shoes, etc. less than once a month, 1–3 times a month were 54.5% and 28.4% respectively [20]. Thus, “Monthly consumption frequency of clothing/shoes/hats” was classified into “0”, “1–2”, “>2”, which was clinically interpretable.

Statistical analysis

All statistical analyses were performed using SPSS version 26.0 (IBM Corp., Armonk, NY, USA) and R version 4.5.3 (R Foundation for Statistical Computing, Vienna, Austria). All the variables were categorical data, so they were described using rates and percentages. Inter-group comparisons of ordinal categorical variables were conducted using the Wilcoxon rank-sum test, while chi-square test was used for inter-group comparisons of nominal categorical variables. We attempted to fit an ordered logistic regression model as a robustness check; however, the parallel line assumption was violated (χ² = 356.94, p < 0.001), indicating that ordered logistic was not appropriate for our data. Therefore, binary logistic regression analysis (forward: likelihood ratio) was employed for the analysis of relevant factors. Besides, given that our data were collected from 17 geographic units (prefecture-level cities, autonomous prefectures, and forest districts) in Hubei Province, clustered standard errors were estimated at the geographic unit level to make the standard error more robust. And Marginal effects were analyzed in order to interpret the meaningful effect-size. P < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Results

Sample characteristics

In 72.5% (1144/1578) of cases, investigators read questions aloud and recorded the verbal answers provided by older participants themselves. The proportion of older adults who were satisfied with their current life was 88.21% (1392/1578), and the percentage of those who were dissatisfied was 11.79% (186/1578). Demographically, older participants was distributed as follows: 53.80% aged 60–69 years old, 33.97% aged 70–79 years old, and 12.23% aged 80 + years old, which were consistent with Hubei Province’s 56.25%, 31.25%, and 12.50% proportions in the Seventh National Population Census in 2020. Gender-wise, males accounted for 49.81% and females 50.19%, matching the 0.95:1 ratio recorded in Hubei. Besides, urban dwellers comprised 51.33% of older population, aligning with the 52.76% urban residency rate in Hubei. While the age, gender, and urban residence distribution of our sample approximated the provincial census data, the convenience sampling design precludes claims of representativeness. Comparison of demographic characteristics of older adults

As is shown in Table 1, there were statistically significant differences in the distribution of residence type, living arrangements, personal monthly disposable income, pre-retirement occupation, and health condition between the dissatisfied group and the satisfied group of older adults (P < 0.05). Binary logistic regression analysis of factors associated with satisfaction of older adults

In Tables 1 and 2, there are a total of 19 variables with P values < 0.2. These variables were incorporated into the logistic regression model, and binary logistic regression analysis was conducted using the forward stepwise (likelihood ratio) method. Collinearity was assessed using variance inflation factors (VIF). All VIF values were below 3.0 (ranging from 1.22 to 2.55), indicating no serious multicollinearity. Besides, the model fitting evaluation results indicated that the model was statistically significant overall (Omnibus test: χ² = 224.019, P < 0.001). The Hosmer-Lemeshow test indicated good model fit (χ² = 8.737, P = 0.365). This model accounted for 25.7% of the variance in life satisfaction (Nagelkerke R² = 0.257), with an overall predictive accuracy of 88.3%. The results of factors associated with life satisfaction were detailed in Table 3, indicating that living arrangements, personal monthly disposable income, monthly consumption frequency of clothing/shoes/hats were positively associated with the satisfaction independently, and health condition, monthly consumption frequency of residential category, monthly consumption frequency of transportation/communication category were negatively associated with the satisfaction independently. Notably, the correlation between monthly amount spent on healthcare category and life satisfaction was statistically significant (χ² = 13.558, P = 0.019), although none of the specific levels reached statistical significance (P > 0.05). This may indicate that the relationship between healthcare spending and satisfaction was complex and further investigation was needed.

Discussion

To our best knowledge, this study is the first to explore the life satisfaction of older population in the entire Hubei Province of China and its associations with consumption behavior and demographic characteristics. The results showed that the majority of older adults (88.21%) were satisfied with their lives, which was significantly higher than the reported 72.50% in China [8] in 2018, and 81.4% in China [9] in 2021. Cross-sectional comparisons should be interpreted with caution due to differences in time. There were two possible reasons for this discrepancy. One tentative explanation for the descriptive difference was the time gap. The CLHLS were collected in 2018, and the national-level Fifth China Survey of Older Adults’ Living Conditions were collected in 2021, while our data was collected in 2025, a gap of seven and four years, respectively. During this period, China has continuously increased investment in elderly care services and social security, and the macro policy environment has had a positive impact on the life satisfaction of older adults. Rowe and Kahn have emphasized that successful aging can be promoted by society adjusting and optimizing education, work environments, retirement systems, healthcare, housing systems, and community planning to meet the growing needs of an aging population and enhance its adaptability [21]. However, differences in sampling methods and geographic coverage may also explain the observed differences. The other tentative explanation was that we only selected Hubei Province with purposive sampling and snowball sampling methods, not all 34 provincial-level administrative regions in China, which means the data did not reflect the national situation. Notably, a certain proportion of older adults (11.79%) remained dissatisfied, suggesting that while pursuing the improvement of general well-being, it is crucial to pay attention to and identify the needs of specific groups. Multidimensional factors associated with satisfaction were revealed through systematic comparison and regression analysis, providing empirical evidence for the design of precise elderly-friendly policies and market services.

Demographic characteristics and life satisfaction

Our finding indicated that some demographic characteristics were significantly associated with life satisfaction among older adults. Firstly, higher personal monthly disposable income was associated with greater life satisfaction, which is consistent with the view that economic resources form the material foundation for maintaining quality of life and freedom of choice in old age. This finding aligns with a large body of literature [8, 22, 23]. However, the observed inverted U-shaped pattern (where very high income showed slightly lower marginal effects) merits further investigation. This may be explained by Easterlin [24], who had summarized that in less developed countries, the positive relationship between happiness and economic growth is non-linear: it exists only up to a certain point, after which further economic growth is not associated with additional gains in happiness.

Secondly, compared to living alone, living with family or relatives showed a positive but statistically non-significant association. In contrast, living in nursing home was associated with significantly higher life satisfaction, although this finding should be interpreted with caution due to the small sample size (n = 80, including only 3 dissatisfied cases). The non-significant finding for living with family or relatives may be explained by the fact that its effects were largely mediated through economic support and health condition [25], which were already controlled for in the model. Additionally, many older adults living with their spouses were reported feeling lonely [26]. Loneliness is often considered a key indicator in the life satisfaction of older adults rather than living alone. The strong association for institutional living may reflect access to professional care, social activities, and enhanced sense of security, though selection bias (healthier, wealthier older adults being more likely to choose institutional care) cannot be ruled out.

Thirdly, poorer health condition was a negative correlate, highlighting the fundamental constraints of physiological decline on life experiences. It had been illustrated that chronic illness and functional impairments (e.g., vision, hearing, mobility, strength) limit well–being especially in very old age [27]. Meanwhile, health-promoting behaviors had been proven to improve the quality of life in older adults during old age [28, 29], thereby enhancing life satisfaction. Furthermore, the dose-response relationship we observed underscores the graded nature of this association.

These findings collectively supported a core perspective: establishing a tripartite integrated support system encompassing “financial security, social support, and health management” may be more effective than relying solely on single-dimensional intervention to enhance elderly satisfaction.

Consumption information and life satisfaction

Previous research [30–32] has explored the correlations between various consumption behaviors and life satisfaction in other population groups. However, few studies have reported the association between consumption behaviors and life satisfaction specifically among older adults. Our study identified that consumption behaviors across different categories exhibited distinct correlations with life satisfaction in older adults: monthly consumption frequency of clothing/shoes/hats showed a positive correlation, while monthly consumption frequency of residential category and monthly consumption frequency of transportation/communication category demonstrated negative correlations; no significant association was observed for monthly amount spent on healthcare category. This pattern suggested that the impact of consumption behaviors on elderly well-being depends on the social functions they fulfill rather than the amount spent.

First, when discussing the monthly clothing/shoe/hat consumption frequency, buying such category once or more per month showed a significant positive correlation with life satisfaction, exhibiting a dose-response relationship. Drawing on successful aging theory and activity theory, one possible interpretation is that this category of expenditure may transcend basic needs, demonstrating stronger associations with social engagement, personal image management, and self-worth perception. Higher consumption frequency may reflect older adults’ more active social lives and positive self-perception. Simultaneously, it challenges the stereotype of elderly consumers as “frugal”, suggesting that a considerable proportion of older adults maintain a proactive consumption attitude.

Second, regarding the monthly consumption frequency of residential category, higher consumption frequency may reflect higher housing instability. Monthly residential category expenses incurred 1–5 times were negatively correlated with life satisfaction, whereas above 5 times showed no significant effect. This may be explained by the fact that frequent housing-related costs (e.g., rent and maintenance) indicated unstable living conditions or financial pressure, indirectly affecting satisfaction through increased psychological burden. However, interpretations for the higher-frequency group should be approached with caution due to its small sample size.

Third, monthly transportation/communication consumption frequency showed a monotonic negative correlation with life satisfaction: the higher the frequency was, the lower the satisfaction was. This phenomenon likely stemmed not from communication behaviors per se, but rather from the involuntary, high-pressure demands inherent in frequent travel—such as repeated visits to medical facilities or managing children’s affairs. These findings suggested that the underlying drivers (active enjoyment versus passive coping) of consumption behaviors were pivotal in interpreting their relationship with happiness. Meanwhile, this aligned with the study’s finding that health condition was the strongest predictor; individuals with poorer health condition simultaneously experienced higher demands for medical travel and lower satisfaction levels.

Fourth, no significant correlation was observed in the specific monthly amount spent on healthcare category. This may be attributed to three reasons: (1) medical expenditures primarily were essential consumption, accompanied by disease and financial burdens, resulting in a net offsetting effect; (2) the impact on individual direct expenditure was mitigated by basic medical insurance; (3) and the fact that, after controlling for health condition, medical expenditure may function as an “intermediate variable”, lacking independent explanatory power.

Diverse pathways to enhancing elderly well-being

It is worth noting that life satisfaction may be collectively shaped by complex interactions between consumption behaviors and demographic factors. The regression analysis incorporated both demographic and consumption variables, with the final model retaining both variables, indicating their independent explanatory power. This finding aligned with previous research [23, 33] demonstrating that income alone does not fully explain variations in subjective well-being; rather, how individuals use their income matters. For instance, after controlling for income levels, clothing/shoes/hats consumption frequency remained predictive, suggesting that even with similar economic conditions, differences in consumption preferences and lifestyle choices still were associated with subjective well-being. This finding was consistent with the consumer behavior literature [34], which had recognized that consumption serves not only utilitarian functions but also symbolic and social functions. These findings suggested diverse pathways [35, 36] to enhance elderly well-being: both economic measures like increasing pensions and non-economic approaches such as encouraging social participation and developing age-friendly cultural and recreational products/services. Therefore, policy and market interventions may need to move beyond the single-minded focus on economic subsidies to promote comprehensive lifestyles which encourage older adults to adopt active, autonomous, and socially connected living [15, 37].

Limitations

While the methodological design of this study ensured the credibility of the findings, several limitations remained. Specifically speaking, first, the cross-sectional design limited causal inference, making it difficult to determine the direction of causality: whether consumption behavior influenced satisfaction or reverse causality (satisfaction status guided consumption choices). Second, consumption data relied on self-reporting, which may be affected by social expectation effects and recall bias. Third, although the age, gender and urban residence distribution of our sample approximated the Hubei provincial census data, this does not establish representativeness on other key dimensions. Therefore, cautious verification was needed when the conclusions were generalized to the broader older adult population in Hubei Province or other culturally and economically diverse regions. Lastly but not least, this study did not collect data on several important potential confounders, including education, marital status, social participation, personality traits, family resources, social support, and prior health shocks. With cross-sectional data, we cannot rule out the possibility that these unobserved factors influence both the independent and dependent variables. Therefore, all findings should be interpreted as conditional associations only; causal claims are not warranted. To address these limitations, we propose a longitudinal study that combines objective consumption records with subjective reports across multiple provinces and time points, with the goal of identifying key factors for future investigation.

Conclusions

In conclusion, this study found that older adults in Hubei Province of China have a relatively high life satisfaction, which was associated with both demographic factors and consumption-related factors. Consumption reflecting active social participation was positively associated with life satisfaction, while consumption reflecting obligatory or stressful activities was negatively associated. These findings suggested that multidimensional coordinated interventions may be required to enhance elderly life satisfaction. If replicated in future research, our findings tentatively suggest that: at the policy level, improving economic security and healthcare service may be beneficial; at the social level, supporting diverse social connections could be considered; and at the market level, developing age-friendly products and services that promote social participation and self-expression may warrant attention. If replicated in causal studies, integrating resources across these dimensions could help older adults achieve a higher quality of life in their later years.
