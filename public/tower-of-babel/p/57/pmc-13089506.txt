Bridging the Gap: A Needs Assessment of Resources and Support for International Medical Graduates Navigating Visa-related Career Decisions in Infectious Diseases
Authors: Brian Chow, Alfredo J Mena Lora, Vera P Luther, Javier A Neyra, Kartikey Acharya, Majd Alsoubani, Cesar G Berto, Paloma Cariello, David Dobrzynski, Henry Donaghy, Sujan Jamarkattel, Anna Kaltsas, Julia Kostka, Luis Medina-Garcia, Holly Murphy, Christopher Sellers, Magdalena Slosar-Cheah, Ann Stapleton
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractInternational medical graduates (IMGs) in infectious disease encounter distinct career and immigration-related barriers during training. We conducted a needs-assessment survey to better understand challenges IMGs face. Results underscore the importance of developing targeted guidance and structured support for IMGs and program leadership.Keywords: Medical Education, Infectious Diseases Workforce, International Medical Graduates, IMG, Program Director

BACKGROUND

International medical graduates (IMGs) comprised 24.7% of the U.S. physician workforce in 2023 [1]. In infectious diseases (ID) training programs, IMGs comprise 39% of current fellows [2, 3]. IMGs are more likely than U.S. graduates to practice in health professional shortage areas in the United States, likely because of visa-waiver programs [4]. Between 2021 and 2025, the number of applicants matching to ID training programs has been decreasing [3], whereas data from the Infectious Diseases Society of America (IDSA) and Accreditation Council on Graduate Medical Education (ACGME) show an increase in the overall number of ID physicians trained. International medical graduates are 1 source of recruitment, both in the Match and after-Match. Although they are valued members of their fellowships, they face numerous regulatory hurdles to transitioning to independent practice.

Fellows who require visa sponsorship to train in the United States often face sharply limited options after fellowship. The J-1 Exchange Visitor visa, 1 of the most common pathways, allows IMGs who are not U.S. citizens or lawful permanent residents (USC/LPR) to train in the United States, but is subject to a 2-year return requirement to their home country unless they obtain a J-1 waiver, a pathway that remains challenging and competitive to secure. H-1B visas provide an easier transition to independent practice but impose a substantial financial and administrative burden on sponsoring institutions, with proposed 2025 changes adding further uncertainty. Other pathways, such as O-1 visas for individuals with extraordinary ability, spouse-based work authorization, or visas tied to bilateral treaties, are available but far less common. O-1 visas have stringent definitions of extraordinary ability that are difficult to meet and have work restrictions for dependents. Candidates with an Employment Authorization Document based on a spouse's visa may have a time-limited duration tied to their spouse's status, which may not cover the anticipated duration of training, increasing perceived risk that they may not be able to complete fellowship training. Citizens of certain countries may face caps on visas issued and variable timelines limiting these opportunities. Program directors (PDs) may not be fully familiar with the nuances of each option.

IMGs are particularly critical in rural underserved areas, where just 32% of Americans live within 20 km of an ID physician compared to 88% of urban-dwelling Americans [5]. Several states are exploring alternative licensure pathways [6], and the American Board of Internal Medicine pilot allows IMGs with international internal medicine training plus an ACGME-accredited fellowship to become board-eligible, shortening the time to independent practice.

There are few surveys of IMGs in ID training programs or of recent graduates assessing their experiences navigating the visa and medical system in the United States as an IMG. In 1 such survey, pediatric ID trainees reported feeling unsupported in navigating career decisions [7], a concern likely shared by adult ID trainees. To address this, the IDSA Training Program Directors Community of Practice Workforce Workgroup (WW) conducted a needs assessment of ID-trained IMGs to understand what resources can be developed to support IMGs and their program directors.

METHODS

A needs assessment survey was drafted by the WW (Supplementary 1). The survey was deemed to be exempt from review by The Lundquist Institute Institutional Review Board and was approved by the IDSA Board of Directors for dissemination through IDSA community platforms in March and April 2025. Current program directors were asked to share the anonymous internet-based survey link (SurveyMonkey) with any IMGs who were currently in training or who had graduated within the past 3 years. While reforwarding the survey was not prohibited, it was not specifically requested. In a separate survey, PDs were asked to record the number of people to whom they forwarded the survey link. The survey was available for 4 weeks. A reminder email was sent midway through the survey period. Respondents were included if they graduated from a medical school outside the United States or Canada, were currently in fellowship, or graduated within the past 3 years. Branching logic within the survey targeted questions based on training level. Respondents could skip questions. PDs reported the number of individuals they contacted. Data were analyzed using SurveyMonkey and Microsoft Excel.

Data Availability

Data available on request.

Subject Consent

The survey was deemed to be exempt from review by The Lundquist Institute Institutional Review Board. Written consent was deemed not necessary. The voluntary and anonymous nature of the survey was stated in the introduction to the survey (Supplementary 1).

RESULTS

Thirty-four PDs shared the assessment with 101 IMGs. Sixty-nine responses were received; 56 met inclusion criteria. Of these, 27 (48%) were current fellows and 29 (52%) had completed fellowship within 3 years. Six (12%) were USC/LPR (ie, Green Card holders) (Table 1). Most respondents (38/44, 86%) completed an U.S. ACGME-accredited residency, whereas others reported completing an internal medicine residency or practicing independently before fellowship training. The most common visa reported was the J-1 visa.

Respondents perceived variable institutional understanding of visa-related hiring processes: 24% reported their institutions had no or little understanding, 28% moderate understanding, and 48% good or excellent understanding. Only 18/42 (43%) had access to individualized visa guidance.

About half of respondents sought out visa-specific advice when applying to fellowships, mostly from their program director (n = 10), colleagues/friends (n = 9), or attorneys (n = 5). Of these, 69.6% felt they received mostly accurate advice. Only 13% felt they received completely accurate advice.

In contrast, 74% of graduates sought visa advice for their first position after fellowship. Most (70.6%) graduates found the advice they received was mostly accurate. About one third of graduates did not find their program leadership helpful in navigating visa-related career options.

Respondents reported that visa considerations had a great impact on various aspects of their postfellowship career decisions: infectious diseases versus general internal medicine (40%), academic versus nonacademic practice (64.1%), and geographic location (76.9%) (Table 1).

Respondents reported a range of satisfaction with postfellowship career options. When fellowship institutions had individualized resources for counseling or advice on visas, respondents reported greater satisfaction with and understanding of their career options. Similarly, when respondents perceived that their fellowship institution understood the onboarding process with respect to their visa, they reported greater satisfaction with and understanding of their career options.

Regarding policies in which state-level legislation could remove training requirements for medical licensure for certain IMGs, 35% reported that this would greatly impact their career decision. A subset of respondents was asked whether changes to board certification allowing a pathway to board certification without repeating residency (internal medicine or pediatrics) would impact their decision to seek subspecialty training in the United States; all 9 respondents indicated they would be more likely to seek subspecialty training.

Several opportunities for improvement were identified, including streamlining licensing and board certification processes (Table 2). In addition to visa-related advice, mentoring on transitioning to independent practice and assistance with job searches may be helpful to IMGs.

DISCUSSION

IMGs are a key group in driving and sustaining growth in infectious diseases; however, they operate in a complex milieu, simultaneously navigating training in a new specialty while also trying to understand the U.S. immigration system. Our needs assessment shows that IMG fellows and recent graduates report a lack of support in these areas. Our findings are similar to those in other medical specialties. A survey of nephrology chiefs and PDs found that they were only somewhat knowledgeable about J-1 visas and their restrictions [8]. Nephrologists face similar recruitment challenges and have comparable proportions of IMGs in their fellowships [9].

Program directors face challenges in finding reliable information and often turn to internal resources, such as a GME office or international office. However, our needs assessment shows that such resources are not universal, increasing the challenge of counseling ID fellows.

PDs are often the most trusted advisors during critical career and training decisions, and U.S. immigration law is complex and subject to change on short notice. For IMGs navigating restrictive visa pathways, the PD's guidance can significantly influence professional opportunities, job attainment, and long-term career viability. Access to accurate, up-to-date information and practical resources on visa options is critical to providing informed, equitable, and supportive counsel. Ensuring PDs are equipped to offer this guidance is a key component of promoting trainee success and maintaining a just and inclusive training environment. Non-PD mentors, such as through IDSA's idMentorship365, should also be aware the challenges IMGs face. Consequences of poor support are reflected in this needs-assessment: respondents who reported dissatisfaction with career options tended to cite lack of resources and lack of assistance in securing a postfellowship job position.

The generalizability of this needs assessment may be affected by the limited number of respondents. We limited the survey to those currently in training or who had completed fellowship within 3 years to better capture contemporary needs, as immigration and visa regulations change often. IMGs have diverse backgrounds and individual situations; the respondents' experiences may not capture each circumstance in detail. In particular, USC/LPR IMGs may have different challenges that were not captured in this needs assessment. However, findings are consistent with the experience of IMGs in other medical specialties [8]. Trainees entering the ID specialty additionally face low compensation relative to other medical specialties and politicization of the field with related harassment, which further complicates career decisions [10].

Streamlining medical licensure and board certification pathways would likely be well received by IMGs. However, challenges in evaluating qualified candidates should be addressed [11].

The WW identified a need for resources for training program directors and fellows. Follow-up evaluation using this needs-assessment instrument after dissemination of new toolkits for PDs and fellows can assess their effectiveness. The WW calls on professional societies, training institutions, and academic partners to develop support mechanisms and share best practices to ensure success of IMGs and the continued growth of our field.
