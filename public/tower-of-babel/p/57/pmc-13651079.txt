Medical students admitted solely by grade point average show a narrow but highly prosocial personality profile
Authors: Lena Kristin Bache-Mathiesen, Torbjørn Moum, Reidar Tyssen, Jarle Breivik
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundThe distribution of students’ personality traits may indicate whether an admission system selects candidates suited to medical practice. While many countries use multi-step selection procedures, Norwegian medical schools admit students based solely on upper-secondary school grade point averages (GPA). We primarily aimed to characterise the personality profiles of medical students at the University of Oslo, and then compare them with previous studies of the Norwegian general population and medical students from systems in countries using alternative admission models.MethodsWe conducted an observational study of newly admitted medical students, 2022–2026 (mean age = 22.1 years, SD = 4.2; 68% female). Personality was assessed using the Big Five Inventory (BFI; five cohorts, n = 535, response rate (RR) = 89%) and the Myers–Briggs Type Indicator (MBTI; six cohorts, n = 632, RR = 95%).BFI scores were compared with available historical data: general Norwegian workers, German final-year, and French first‑ and third‑year medical students using one‑sample t‑tests. MBTI types were compared with previous studies on U.S. and South Korean medical students and the Norwegian general population using chi-squared tests.ResultsNorwegian medical students scored significantly higher than Norwegian workers on all BFI dimensions (p < 0.001). They also scored higher in Agreeableness than German final-year and French first- and third-year students (4.33 vs 4.17, 3.80 and 3.60, respectively) and lower in Neuroticism (2.50 vs. 2.85, 3.00 and 3.00; all p < 0.001).Norwegian students’ MBTI personality-type distribution differed significantly from the Norwegian general population and U.S. and South Korean medical students (p < 0.001). Most students were Judging (78%) and Extraverted (70%), with ENFJ the most frequent personality type among women (29%) and ENTJ among men (25%).ConclusionsMedical students admitted through the Norwegian GPA-only admission system at UiO demonstrated high Agreeableness and Conscientiousness, with a marked overrepresentation of ENFJ women and ENTJ men. These findings indicate a consistently prosocial and structured, but relatively narrow, personality profile. While this profile includes interpersonal and organisational characteristics that may be relevant to clinical practice, the observed distribution also raises questions about limited diversity in personality type distribution and gender-related patterns in recruitment to medical education.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at https://doi.org/10.1186/s12909-026-09929-7.Keywords: Pre-clinical medical education, Medical student selection, Medical student admission, Personality traits, Personality types

Background

Medical school admission systems determine who gains access to medical education and shape the social and psychological composition of the future medical workforce. Yet we know little about how specific admission models select for particular psychological characteristics in the student population. Personality traits, which are associated with attributes beneficial for medical practice such as empathy [1], stress resilience [2–4], and professional behaviour [5–7], offer a useful perspective for examining how different selection models affect the composition of the student body. Rather than applying personality testing as a selection tool, this study explores the personality profile of a medical student cohort admitted through an established selection system.

In psychological research, personality is often assessed using trait-based models such as the Big Five Inventory (BFI), which measures individuals along five continuous dimensions: agreeableness, conscientiousness, extraversion, openness, and neuroticism [8]. These traits show consistent associations with educational and professional outcomes, with conscientiousness and agreeableness linked to greater success and professionalism [4], and neuroticism associated with higher levels of distress and burnout [9]. Importantly, similar patterns have been reported in studies of medical students, where Big Five traits predict academic performance, well-being, and later professional functioning [2, 3, 10–12].

Typology-based personality models such as the Myers–Briggs Type Indicator (MBTI) build on similar underlying continua but dichotomise them into intuitive personality types. MBTI has been criticised for information loss from this dichotomisation [13], and inconsistent criterion validity with BFI [14, 15], but remains widely used in educational and organisational settings due to its intuitive structure and accessibility as a framework for discussing personality differences [16]. Although not recommended for selection of applicants [17], typological-based personality models may still offer an applicable tool for describing and comparing personality profiles across groups and has been used in several studies to characterise typological patterns among medical students [18, 19].

Against this theoretical background, international guidelines increasingly emphasise that medical school admissions should be evidence-based and use multiple methods to assess both academic and non-academic attributes [20–22]. Both the Association for Medical Education in Europe (AMEE) and the Association of American Medical Colleges (AAMC) recommend multi-step procedures that combine prior academic performance with tools such as admission tests, interviews, situational judgement tests, and multiple mini-interviews to select for suitability [17, 23]. Accordingly, expert reviews have criticised admission systems that rely predominantly on grade point average (GPA) for neglecting non-academic personal traits and lacking mechanisms for excluding applicants not suited for the medical profession [20]. Empirical studies suggest that such systems may admit candidates who perform less favourably when evaluated on broader, non-academic criteria [24].

In contrast to these recommendations, in Norway admission to the six-year medical programme is based almost exclusively on upper-secondary school GPA through the national admissions system [25]. Applicants must meet specific subject requirements and receive bonus points for high-level science and mathematics. They then compete in two quotas: The first-diploma quota comprises at least 50% of student places, is open to applicants up to the year they turn twenty-two, and ranks applicants by their initial GPA. The ordinary quota allows applicants to retake or add subjects to improve their GPA and provides additional points for age and for one year of either higher education, military service, or civilian service [25].

Medicine is consistently among the most competitive study programmes in the country, and the design and consequences of the admissions system have been widely discussed in policy reports [26] and professional debate [27–29]. While transparent and standardised, the system has been criticised for failing to capture non-academic aspects of suitability. It also creates strong incentives for repeated grade improvement, which delays entry into higher education while favouring applicants with greater socioeconomic resources. Moreover, the admission system’s lack of mechanisms to influence the composition of the student body contributes to gender imbalance with women comprising around 70% of recent cohorts [29]. Once admitted, attrition is about 10% over the six-year programme, suggesting that the composition of the student body and thereby the future medical workforce is largely attributable to the admission system.

In this context, Norwegian medical education provides an opportunity to describe the personality profile of students admitted through a highly competitive GPA-only admission system. In general, there is a lack of empirical knowledge about the personality composition of medical students admitted through different systems and how these student populations differ from the general population and from medical students admitted in other contexts. This study therefore aims to characterise the personality profiles of medical students at the University of Oslo (UiO), compare them with relevant benchmark groups, and discuss implications for medical education and the Norwegian health care system.

Methods

Study design

We conducted an observational study of personality characteristics in first‑semester medical students at UiO admitted between fall 2022 and spring 2026. Data collection was embedded in a mandatory teamwork training course held during the second week of the six‑year medical programme. From fall 2024 onward, these data were linked with demographic data in the faculty’s educational quality registry (MonKeyMed). Earlier cohorts (fall 2022–spring 2024) provided personality data anonymously and were therefore analysed without registry linkage.

Of 758 enrolled students, 43 were excluded due to withdrawal, non-registration, non-payment of tuition, or leave of absence, leaving 715 eligible participants. Of these, 636 (89%) completed at least one questionnaire. Response rates across cohorts and questionnaires averaged 91% (Table 1). Since all questions were mandatory, there were otherwise no missing data.

During the teamwork training course, students responded to an online baseline survey. This was administered via the university’s online survey platform with a direct link to the MonKeyMed educational quality registry and was also distributed by email to students who did not attend the teamwork training course. Three background items regarding parent education and migrant background were designed by the authors. These are provided in Additional File 1.

Age at admission, from the registry data, was used as a proxy for admission quota, since applicants older than 21 years can only be admitted through the ordinary quota. From the survey, whether they or at least one parent was born abroad, was used as an indicator of migrant background, and whether the student had recently moved to Oslo, was used to distinguish local from non‑local students.

This combined approach allowed us to examine how different instruments capture the outcomes of the GPA‑based admission system in comparison with external benchmark groups. From spring 2024, personality traits were assessed with the Norwegian short version of the Big Five Inventory (BFI-20S) [30]. Students completed this questionnaire as part of the online baseline survey described previously. Items were rated on a 7-point Likert scale, and dimension scores were calculated as the sum of the corresponding items according to the standard scoring procedure, yielding scores from 4 to 28 per trait.

The correlations between items within BFI dimensions and their Cronbach’s α values varied from low to high (Additional File 2, Table S1, α = 0.58–0.85). Construct validity was examined using exploratory factor analysis (details in supplementary methods, Table S2), which yielded five latent variables with strong correlations to corresponding dimensions (Table S1, all r > 0.9, p < 0.001).

Personality type was assessed during the same course using the MBTI, administered as part of a structured learning activity on how differences in personality may affect teamwork. Students completed the Norwegian version of the MBTI on the publicly available website, which offered 60 items on a 7-point Likert scale [31, 32]. Students then submitted their four‑letter type (e.g. ENFP) via a secure online form with a direct link to the MonKeyMed registry, except in fall 2022, where MBTI types were collected on paper during a student project and subsequently entered into the registry [33].

MBTI was included because it has been widely used in medical education and career coaching [34], and offers sixteen intuitive typologies that can be compared with previous MBTI studies from other medical schools [19]. It has also demonstrated reasonable reliability in college-age students in meta-analyses [35]. Still, we recognize that MBTI also has well‑documented psychometric limitations, particularly its dichotomous scoring [13], and weak criterion validity against established BFI measures [15]. To assess the impact of these limitations, we conducted two psychometric analyses. First, in the spring 2026 cohort we collected continuous scores for all MBTI dimensions to examine the potential bias of dichotomisation. This analysis revealed that the data was somewhat skewed towards Extraversion and exceedingly skewed towards Judging, while the remaining dimensions were approximately normally distributed around the cut-off (Table S3, Figure S1). Second, we assessed the criterion validity of MBTI by examining Pearson’s correlations between MBTI dichotomies and our BFI scores, using BFI-20 as a well-established reference. In line with previous studies [14, 15], all BFI dimensions correlated with corresponding MBTI dichotomies, except Agreeableness, which had only a modest correlation with Feeling (Table 4). These analyses were used to verify that MBTI scores were psychometrically adequate for inclusion in our combined personality profiling of the medical student population.

Comparison samples

To contextualise the personality characteristics of the Norwegian medical students, we used PubMed and Google Scholar to identify available national benchmark data and data from medical schools in other countries. We identified fourteen studies describing personality traits of medical students or Norwegian populations. To reduce the number of hypothesis tests, we compared our study sample with six of these studies. Studies were selected based on the use of validated, construct‑comparable personality instruments (BFI or MBTI), reporting of summary statistics suitable for quantitative comparison (e.g. means and standard deviations or full type distributions), and clearly described study design and recruitment procedures with adequate sample size and response rate. We gave preference to more recently published cross‑sectional studies that met these criteria. Our justifications for including and excluding studies were documented in a version-controlled, publicly available protocol. The final version is available as a supplementary file, Additional File 3. These criteria resulted in the following comparison samples:

For Big Five, we used Norwegian workforce data from Vaag, Sund [36], who applied the same Norwegian BFI-20S as in our study. This served as a national norm to position medical students’ trait levels relative to the overall Norwegian population. In this study, traits were not reported across age and gender. We also used a study from a French medical school that studied BFI in both an unselected first‑year cohort and a competitively selected third‑year cohort, and a German national study of final‑year medical students [37, 38]. Together, these samples provided European medical‑student benchmarks from different admission systems and stages of training.

For MBTI, the general type distributions of the Norwegian population were obtained from the Myers & Briggs Foundation [39]. These served as a national reference. Collected anonymously, age and gender were not available. Two studies, one from a U.S. and one from a South Korean medical school, offered additional medical‑student benchmarks from two different admission systems, cultures, and educational contexts [19, 40]. Statistical analysis

Descriptive statistics were first calculated for demographic variables, BFI dimensions, and MBTI types. We then used inferential statistics to compare BFI and MBTI with the comparison samples and between student subgroups (Table 5). These comparisons assume that the published benchmark values represent either population values or estimates from sufficiently representative samples. Groups with low or unreported response rates should therefore be interpreted with caution. Analyses were tailored to the variable type (continuous BFI dimensions vs. categorical MBTI types) and the reporting format of each benchmark group.

BFI dimension distributions were summarised with means and standard deviations (SD) and visualized in histograms. Differences in gender and admission quota (first diploma vs. ordinary) were assessed by comparing 95% confidence intervals (CI) without calculating p-values, to reduce risk of Type I errors. Using one-sample t-tests, the BFI dimensions were compared with the BFI comparison samples (Table 5). This approach was used as an approximate benchmarking method because comparison studies only reported summary statistics. The sample size of 535 was considered robust to deviation from normality [42]. Reference scores were calculated from other studies, using summed scores for Norwegian workers and mean scores for German and French medical students. To compare with the French medical students, the 7-point scale was transformed to a 5-point scale, assuming that response distributions were similarly shaped in both studies [43]. For details regarding this transformation, see Supplementary Methods.

Percentages of the four MBTI dichotomies and sixteen types were calculated with Wilson 95% CIs [44]. Using chi-square tests, MBTI was compared with the MBTI comparison groups (Table 5). Post-hoc comparisons were not conducted, to avoid multiple testing issues. For the U.S. medical students, the sixteen personalities were estimated from reported dichotomies [19], subject to rounding errors. Chi-square tests also assessed differences in MBTI distribution between genders, and between first diploma and ordinary admissions quota. Bar graphs visualised differences between groups.

Most analyses were conducted in R version 4.3.2 [45], using packages for confidence intervals [46, 47]. For psychometrics – between-item BFI correlations, Cronbach alpha, EFA and correlations between BFI and MBTI – IBM SPSS Statistics 31.0.0.0 was used instead. Significance was determined by α = 0.05.

Ethics

The Monitoring Key Quality Indicators at the Faculty of Medicine (MonKeyMed) student quality registry was established in autumn 2024 to facilitate systematic, longitudinal monitoring of educational outcomes and learning experiences among medical students [48]. The registry was approved by the Norwegian Agency for Shared Services in Education and Research (234,769) and was organised as a pseudo‑anonymous registry in accordance with Norwegian and EU data protection regulations. Collection of student administrative and quality data was conducted under the Norwegian Act relating to Universities and University Colleges ( [49], §2–1). Student participation was voluntary and based on written informed consent. The study was conducted in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki [50].

The analysis protocol was developed and made available prior to the statistical analyses, and the manuscript was prepared in accordance with STROBE recommendations for observational research [51]. De‑identified data, protocol and both R and SPSS analysis code from this study are publicly available [52].

An institutionally regulated ChatGPT platform operated by the University of Oslo was used during manuscript preparation to assist with language and R code editing. The AI system only had access to aggregated, anonymous data, and all AI‑assisted content was critically reviewed and approved by the authors, who are fully responsible for the final manuscript.

Results

Across seven cohorts from fall 2022 to spring 2026, 636 first‑year medical students at UiO were eligible and completed the baseline survey (response rate 89%, Table 1). Of these, 535 reported BFI, and 632 reported MBTI. For cohorts with linked registry data (fall 2024–spring 2026, n = 434), the mean age was 22.1 years (SD = 4.2) and 68% were women, with similar gender distributions across cohorts (Table 6). Students older than 21 (47%) were admitted through the ordinary quota, whereas 53% were primarily of the first diploma quota (Table 6).

Big Five Inventory among first-year Norwegian medical students

Norwegian first-year medical students showed a distinctive Big Five profile compared with Norwegian workers, scoring higher on all Big Five dimensions (Fig. 1, all p < 0.001). On the 4–28 scale, mean scores for Agreeableness and Conscientiousness were particularly elevated (24.0 vs. 22.2 and 22.8 vs. 20.5, respectively), with most students scoring in the upper range of these traits. Extraversion (20.4 vs. 19.0), Openness (18.8 vs. 17.8), and Neuroticism (13.0 vs. 12.0) were more uniformly distributed, yet also modestly higher than in the workforce. However, the gender-adjusted mean in Neuroticism (11.6) appeared lower. See Supplementary Table S4 for details.

Distribution of Big Five Inventory dimensions for 535 medical students who submitted the baseline questionnaire. Agreeableness and Conscientiousness appeared left-skewed. Norwegian students’ means (dashed lines) were significantly different at p < 0.001 from the mean of Norwegian workers (solid lines) for all dimensions

Among the Norwegian medical students, women scored higher than men on Agreeableness (24.4 vs. 23.2), Conscientiousness (23.3 vs. 22.0), and Neuroticism (13.6 vs. 11.6), while Extraversion and Openness were similar for men (19.0) and women (18.7) given overlapping CIs (Table S5). There were only minor, non-significant differences between students admitted through the first-diploma and ordinary quotas (Table S5).

Comparisons on the harmonised 1–5 scale showed that Norwegian medical students were higher in Agreeableness and lower in Neuroticism than the German and French medical student comparison samples (Fig. 2). They scored higher in Agreeableness than German final-year students (4.33 vs. 4.17) and French first-year (3.80) and third-year (3.60) students (all p < 0.001). Conscientiousness in Norwegian students (4.14) was higher than in French first- and third-year students (both 3.40, p < 0.001), but slightly lower than in German final-year students (4.23, p = 0.001). Norwegian students were also higher in Extraversion than French (3.73 vs. 3.10 and 3.20, both p < 0.001) and German students (3.63, p = 0.007), while Openness was comparable across all groups. In contrast, Neuroticism was lower in Norwegian students (2.50) than in German students (2.85) and both groups of French students (3.00, all p < 0.001). At the level of dichotomies (Table 7, all p < 0.001), the vast majority were classified as Judging rather than Prospecting (78% vs. 43%) and Extraverted rather than Introverted (70% vs. 48%), while somewhat fewer were Intuitive (59% vs. 64%) and a similar proportion was Feeling (66% vs. 68%).

Norwegian medical students differed significantly (p < 0.05) on all four dichotomies compared with the international medical student samples (Table 7). The proportion classified as Extraverted was higher than in U.S. (57%) and South Korean (53%) students. Intuitive types also formed a majority among Norwegian students (59%), whereas they were in the minority among U.S. (28%) and South Korean (37%) students. Norwegian students were more often classified as Feeling (66%) than in the U.S. (35%) and South Korean (40%) students, and Judging was again the most distinctive dichotomy, with 78% of Norwegian students classified as Judging compared with 37% of U.S. and 56% of South Korean medical students.

Norwegian medical students also differed from the comparison groups at the level of the sixteen MBTI types (Fig. 3, Table S8, p < 0.001). ENFJ and ESFJ were grossly overrepresented, comprising 22% and 17% of students, respectively, compared with 6% ENFJ and 8% ESFJ in the Norwegian benchmark population (Fig. 3A, Table S8). The proportion ENFJ was also substantially higher than among U.S. (2%) and South Korean (3%) medical students (Fig. 3B).

Type distributions differed significantly between women and men among the Norwegian medical students (p < 0.001; Fig. 4A, Table S9). Among women, ENFJ (29%) and ESFJ (15%) were the most frequent types, whereas among men ENTJ (25%) and INTJ (13%) were the most common. ENFJ and ESFJ were nevertheless also relatively frequent among men (11% and 7%, respectively), with a distribution comparable to South Korean women (7% ENFJ, 8% ESFJ).

The overall distribution of the sixteen types did not differ significantly between students admitted through the first‑diploma and ordinary quotas (p = 0.118; Fig. 4B, Table S10). Similarly, there was no evidence of differences by migrant background (whether the student or at least one parent was born abroad; p = 0.719), recent relocation to Oslo (p = 0.642), or parental education level (p = 0.318).

Discussion

This study examined the personality characteristics of medical students admitted through Norway’s GPA-only admission system. Compared with national reference populations and international samples of medical students, the student in in this single-institution study showed distinct personality characteristics on both BFI and MBTI measures. On the BFI, they exhibited particularly high levels of Conscientiousness and Agreeableness, while the MBTI results showed a predominance of Extraverted and Judging. Within the MBTI typology, nearly 40% of students were classified as ENFJ or ESFJ.

Given the longstanding concerns that GPA-only admissions may prioritize cognitive performance over interpersonal orientation, these findings are somewhat unexpected. The AMEE guide to selection into medical school emphasises that selection should be robust, defensible, fair, and constructively aligned with the attributes considered important for medical education and clinical practice [17]. From this perspective, our findings do not show whether GPA-only admission is suitable or unsuitable, but they suggest that the personality composition of students admitted in this context should be considered when evaluating whether the system achieves the intended goals of medical selection. Rather than indicating a narrow academic focus or limited interpersonal orientation, the personality profile of Norwegian medical students in the current study appears to be characterised by conscientiousness, structure, and social engagement. High levels of Conscientiousness and Extraversion among Norwegian medical students have also been reported earlier [2].

Several factors may help explain these findings. First, achieving the high grades required for admission to medicine in Norway demands a capacity for delayed gratification, traits closely aligned with high Conscientiousness and the Judging types [4]. Second, success in upper secondary school often depends on cooperation, compliance, and maintaining positive relationships with teachers, favouring individuals higher in Agreeableness. Finally, the competitive and demanding nature of the admission process implies a form of self‑selection of applicants with a strong “calling” to medicine. This is reflected in the recent findings of Hur and Yeo [53], where Korean medical students, also passing highly competitive admission processes, were found to be largely agreeable and structured. In the context of the egalitarian and collective culture of the Norwegian healthcare system, these mechanisms align with the observed personality profiles.

Overall, students admitted to medical school through the Norwegian system exhibit personality characteristics that are considered advantageous for success in medical school and many roles in clinical practice [6, 7, 54]. Its key limitation, however, may lie in what the admissions model potentially fails to capture. There is evidence that Conscientiousness, correlated with MBTI Judging, predicts perceived medical school stress [2]. About four-fifths (78%) of the students were categorized as Judging, while Prospecting types such as ESTP, ISTP, and INFP were markedly underrepresented, also relative to the Norwegian general population and international medical student samples. People with these profiles are considered more adaptable and responsive to changing situations, which may be more suitable for managing uncertainty in clinical contexts, a competency increasingly considered important in medical practice [55]. This pattern may have implications for the diversity of problem-solving styles and coping mechanisms among medical students and Norwegian physicians.

The gender imbalance in Norwegian medical school admission, with approximately 70% women, is well documented [56], as is the tendency for women to score higher than men on Neuroticism [2, 57]. Since the gender-adjusted Neuroticism observed among Norwegian medical students was relatively low (mean = 11.6), the unadjusted, higher level (13.0) compared with Norwegian workers (12.0) is likely a reflection of this gender distribution. Concurrently, Norwegian students scored around half a point lower on Neuroticism on a 1–5 scale than French and German medical students despite similar gender compositions. They also appeared lower than in a nation-wide Norwegian student sample, although the latter had a low response rate and should be interpreted with caution [58]. Taken together, these findings suggest that Norwegian medical students are relatively emotionally stable.

ENFJ and ESFJ types were remarkably frequent among women, together accounting for 44% of female students. ENFJ was also common among male students (11%) but was outnumbered by ENTJ and INTJ types, which together comprised 50% of men. These marked gender differences, which also contrast with the patterns observed in the comparison samples, may be the result of several features of the admissions system. First, the clustering of women in ENFJ/ESFJ types and men in ENTJ/INTJ types aligns with patterns in the general population: Women are more often categorised as Feeling (F), with higher levels of Agreeableness, while men are more often Thinking (T), with higher levels of assertiveness [59–61]. Second, GPA-based selection may favour organised, goal-oriented Judging traits [4], which may manifest as Extraverted–Feeling profiles among women and Intuitive–Thinking profiles among men. Finally, male and female physicians tend to choose different medical specialties [61, 62], suggesting that their motivations and self-selection into medicine may also differ. In combination, these mechanisms might impact gender distributions in healthcare institutions, which warrants future investigations into the role of personality, gender and career choices.

Norwegian medical students are of high socioeconomic background status [63], consistent with our finding that 74% had at least one parent with higher education. We found, however, no evidence that parental education, immigrant background, or being new to Oslo was associated with significant differences in personality profiles. The narrow personality spectrum observed among Norwegian medical students therefore appears to be recruited relatively broadly from the population.

The main strength of this study was the inclusion of six cohorts of medical students, with high response rates for the BFI (89%) and MBTI (95%), making the findings largely representative of the UiO medical student population. Because Norwegian medical programmes admit students through the same national GPA‑based system, the results may also reflect broader personality patterns among Norwegian medical students. However, as applicants may rank their preferred medical school, and one school includes a quota for local applicants, there may be systematic differences in student composition, including personality. This is an observational study from a single institution, so the findings may not generalise to other countries or admission contexts, and they do not necessarily reflect causal effects of GPA‑only admission systems.

A further strength of this study is the combined use of complementary trait‑ and type‑based personality instruments, which rendered a more comprehensive assessment of personality characteristics than either approach alone. Both instruments showed acceptable psychometric properties: the BFI had high construct validity and reasonable reliability, and the MBTI showed the expected associations with corresponding BFI dimensions [14, 15]. The main psychometric concern was the relatively low reliability of the Agreeableness scale and its low correlation to the MBTI Thinking-Feeling dichotomy. This pattern appears largely attributable to the strongly skewed distribution towards high Agreeableness, which reduces variation and attenuates correlations. Concurrently, the high level of Agreeableness among Norwegian medical students, also in comparison to French and German medical student, is one of our main findings.

The use of previously published comparison samples, including both Norwegian reference populations and international cohorts of medical students, provided an external frame of reference for interpreting our data. It enabled comparisons with medical schools operating under other admission systems, thereby strengthening the external validity of the study. However, although we aimed to use the most compatible samples available, these comparisons should be interpreted with caution. The benchmark samples were selected from published research, not deliberately collected samples, and differences in educational stage, demographic composition, cultural context, questionnaire versions, and response rates may have influenced the comparisons.

The Norwegian benchmark populations did not allow age or gender-adjusted comparisons. The data from other medical schools represented different stages of training and used different questionnaire versions or scales. In particular, BFI scales were transformed to compare with the French medical students, which assumed that distributions were equal in both studies [43]. The validity of the studies was also unclear, as in several cases response rates were not reported (Table 5) or very low, as in the U.S. study (34%). Accordingly, the responders may not be representative of the full sample, as missing questionnaire data are frequently associated with sampling bias [64].

As a natural extension of this single-institution study, future research should examine whether the observed personality pattern is consistent across all Norwegian medical schools. Future longitudinal studies should investigate whether personality characteristics are associated with student attrition, academic progression, and career outcomes. It is also important to evaluate whether admissions processes achieve constructive alignment between selection methods, the resulting student composition, and the competencies expected in medical education and clinical practice, as recommended by the AMEE guide to selection into medical school [17].

Furthermore, 40–45% of Norwegian doctors are educated abroad, predominantly at Eastern European universities with lower admission reliance on GPA [65]. Studies on these students could clarify how different selection environments shape the personality composition of the Norwegian physician workforce. In general, comparative studies across other disciplines with high GPA requirements, such as law or engineering, could further clarify whether the observed personality profile is specific to medical education or also seen in other highly competitive educational contexts.

Longitudinal studies are needed to examine how these personality profiles relate to outcomes such as academic performance, stress, burnout, professionalism, and specialty choice. In particular, future work should explore whether the underrepresented Prospecting profiles follow distinct trajectories in clinical training, innovation, or leadership roles. Another avenue is to consider the impact of gender differences in personality. A recent review on Norwegian physicians found that women experienced more occupational stress than men up to 20 years of the medical career [66]. In the same vein, another Norwegian study found that neuroticism had a larger impact (interaction) on job stress during internship among female than male doctors [67]. Gender differences in personality may partially explain why the medical career appears to take a heavier toll on female doctors. Research should assess how changes in admission systems or selection tools may influence personality distributions and whether greater diversity in the distribution of personality types can be achieved without compromising academic standards.

In conclusion, medical students admitted through the GPA-only admission system showed high Agreeableness and Conscientiousness, with a predominance of Extraverted–Judging profiles and marked clustering of women in ENFJ/ESFJ and men in ENTJ/INTJ types. This pattern indicates a cohort of socially oriented and responsible students, but also a relatively narrow personality composition, with marked gender patterns and limited representation of more exploratory profiles. While these characteristics may be relevant to many aspects of clinical work, the observed distribution raises questions about whether selection based solely on academic performance captures the broader range of personal attributes relevant to medical education and professional practice. These findings further discussion of Norway’s GPA‑only admissions policy and of how complementary approaches might support greater diversity in personality types without compromising academic standards.
