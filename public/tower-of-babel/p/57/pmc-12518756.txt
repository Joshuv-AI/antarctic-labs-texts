Risk of Stroke in COVID-19 Survivors With Asthma: A Nationwide, Population-Based Cohort Study
Authors: Sungmin Zo, Youlim Kim, Jong Seung Kim, Min Gu Kang, Ji-Yong Moon, Jongkyu Park, Sang-Heon Kim, Kwang Ha Yoo, Ho Joo Yoon, Kyung Hoon Min, Hyun Lee
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
BackgroundThe impact of coronavirus disease 2019 (COVID-19) on stroke risk in adult individuals with asthma post-recovery remains unclear.MethodsUsing a longitudinal retrospective cohort design based on the Korean National Health Insurance Service claims database, we analyzed stroke risk in two parts. Study 1 examined stroke risk in individuals with asthma according to COVID-19 severity, and Study 2 compared stroke risk between individuals with asthma (asthma cohort) and the general population without asthma (general population cohort), considering COVID-19 severity. Both studies used propensity score matching and Cox proportional hazards regression models for risk estimation.ResultsIn Study 1, which evaluated stroke risk in the asthma cohort, the risk of stroke was significantly higher in the asthma cohort with severe COVID-19 compared to the asthma cohort without COVID-19 (adjusted hazard ratio [aHR], 1.88; 95% confidence interval [CI], 1.02–3.46). In Study 2, which evaluated stroke risk across six groups stratified by asthma status (asthma cohort and general population cohort) and COVID-19 severity (no, non-severe, and severe COVID-19), stroke risk was highest in the asthma cohort with severe COVID-19 (aHR, 3.42; 95% CI, 1.72–6.79, referring to the general population group without COVID-19), suggesting that severe COVID-19 has a more substantial effect on stroke risk in individuals with asthma.ConclusionThis study highlights a significant association between severe COVID-19 and stroke occurrence in adults with asthma, emphasizing the need for ongoing stroke monitoring to improve long-term asthma outcomes during the COVID-19 endemic period.

INTRODUCTION

In 2019, the novel virus severe acute respiratory syndrome coronavirus 2 (SARS-CoV-2) emerged and led to the global coronavirus disease 2019 (COVID-19) pandemic.1 This viral infection not only involves the respiratory system but also causes many short-term and long-term complications.23 For instance, the occurrence of cerebrovascular diseases such as stroke, which were more frequent among patients with severe SARS-CoV-2 infection, has resulted in worse disease courses following COVID-19.45

Patients with asthma are known to have an increased risk of stroke, which is associated with poor outcomes within this population.67 One significant factor contributing to the development of stroke in patients with asthma is acute exacerbation, which is commonly triggered by viral infections.8 Accordingly, it might be postulated that COVID-19 could exacerbate asthma and potentially elevate the short-term risk of stroke.9 However, given the lingering effects of COVID-19 and its persistent residual risk, individuals with asthma may face an increased likelihood of stroke even after the acute phase of COVID-19, particularly if they experience severe COVID-19.1011 However, little information is available on this issue.

Therefore, this study aimed to investigate whether COVID-19 has lingering effects of increasing stroke risk in adults with asthma even after they recovered from COVID-19, with a focus on COVID-19 severity, utilizing a nationwide cohort dataset (Study 1). Additionally, we aimed to evaluate whether the risk of stroke is further elevated in individuals with asthma compared to the general population without asthma, according to COVID-19 severity (Study 2).

METHODS

Data source

We performed a longitudinal retrospective cohort study using the Korean National Health Insurance Service (NHIS) claims database, which covers approximately 97% of the Korean population (about 50 million people).12131415 The NHIS serves as a government-managed universal insurance provider in Korea. The NHIS dataset includes disease diagnoses based on the 10th revision of the International Classification of Disease (ICD-10) codes, demographic variables, socioeconomic factors, healthcare utilization, health screening examination results, medical treatment or procedures, and mortality data provided by Statistics Korea, an initiative of the Ministry of Strategy and Finance of Korea.

During the COVID-19 pandemic, the Korean government actively promoted testing for COVID-19 by offering financial support for the diagnosis of COVID-19 in individuals with suspected symptoms. Financial support was also provided for the treatment of those diagnosed with COVID-19. As part of this financial support program, the NHIS collected COVID-19 testing results and merged them into the NHIS database, resulting in the NHIS SARS-CoV-2 database (NHIS-2022-1-623).

The NHIS SARS-CoV-2 database (NHIS-2022-1-623) comprises medical information collected from 8,463,712 individuals who underwent testing. This includes 561,009 individuals diagnosed with COVID-19 at least once between October 8, 2020, and December 31, 2021, and 7,902,703 individuals with negative test results. These data were extracted from the NHIS database using randomized stratified sampling based on age and sex.

Study population

Study 1: Stroke risk in individuals with asthma according to COVID-19 status

Among the 689,395 patients diagnosed with asthma from January 1, 2015, to December 16, 2021, we excluded 491,949 subjects who were younger than 20 years (n = 358,880) and those without health screening examinations (n = 133,069) between January 1, 2019 and December 31, 2020 (Fig. 1). We identified 197,446 adults diagnosed with asthma, comprising 13,169 individuals with a history of COVID-19 (asthma cohort with COVID-19) and 184,277 individuals without COVID-19 (asthma cohort without COVID-19).

Among the asthma cohort with COVID-19, participants who recovered from COVID-19 after December 31, 2021 (due to a lack of available follow-up data, n = 2,036), individuals who died before the index date (n = 189), and those diagnosed with stroke before the index date (n = 799) were excluded. Of the remaining 10,145 subjects, individuals matched to the asthma cohort without COVID-19 (n = 306) were further excluded, resulting in a final 9,839 individuals in the asthma cohort with COVID-19.

We performed 1:1 propensity score (PS) matching between 10,145 individuals in the asthma cohort with COVID-19 and 184,277 individuals in the asthma cohort without COVID-19, resulting in 10,145 individuals. Then individuals diagnosed with asthma after the index date (matched date) (n = 185), those who died before the index date (n = 32), and those diagnosed with stroke before the index date (n = 89) were further excluded. Ultimately, 9,839 participants were included in the asthma cohort without COVID-19.

Study 2: Stroke risk in individuals with asthma vs. the general population without asthma according to COVID-19 status

As shown in Supplementary Fig. 1, to evaluate whether the risk of stroke is further increased in individuals with asthma compared to the general population without asthma, we established a general population cohort without asthma who were age and sex-matched to the asthma cohort. Of 689,395 individuals without asthma, we excluded individuals aged less than 20 years (n = 356,184) and those who did not have health screening examination data (n = 146,379). Of the remaining 186,832 individuals without asthma, 11,535 had COVID-19 and 175,297 did not have COVID-19. After 1:1 PS matching, we identified 9,094 individuals with COVID-19 (general population cohort with COVID-19) and those without COVID-19 (general population cohort without COVID-19). In both studies, asthma diagnosis was established by the presence of the ICD-10 diagnosis codes J45 or J46 before the diagnosis of COVID-19 between January 1, 2015, and December 16, 2021, with medical claims indicating at least three prescriptions for asthma medications. Asthma-related medications included inhaled or oral corticosteroids, bronchodilators, leukotriene receptor antagonists, and xanthine derivatives.1617

The index date was the recovery date from COVID-19 for the COVID-19 cohort and the date of matching for the matched cohort. The index date for the COVID-19 cohort was defined as follows: 1) 14 days after the diagnosis of COVID-19 for individuals who were not admitted, 2) 14 days after the diagnosis of COVID-19 for individuals who required admission but were discharged within 14 days, or 3) the discharge date for individuals who were hospitalized for more than 14 days after the diagnosis of COVID-19. Both cohorts were followed up until the incidence of stroke, death, or December 31, 2021, whichever came first.

Study exposure: COVID-19

The study exposure of both studies was COVID-19, which was defined by a positive result from real-time reverse transcription polymerase chain reaction assay of nasal or pharyngeal swabs from individuals with a history of SARS-CoV-2 (U07.1) defined using the ICD-10 code.17181920

Severe COVID-19 was defined as the documented use of the following treatments during hospitalization due to COVID-19: 1) oxygen therapy, 2) admission to the intensive care unit, 3) mechanical ventilation, or 4) extracorporeal membrane oxygenation.17181920

Study outcome: occurrence of stroke

The study outcome of both studies was the development of stroke, defined by a hospitalization code and either I63 or I64 with claims for brain imaging (computed tomography or magnetic resonance imaging).2122

Covariates

Basic demographic characteristics and socioeconomic variables were provided from the study dataset. Body mass index (BMI), calculated as body weight divided by the square of height (kg/m2), was classified into five groups using Asian categories232426: low (< 18.5 kg/m2), normal (18.5–22.9 kg/m2), overweight (23.0–24.9 kg/m2), obese (25–29.9 kg/m2), and highly obese (≥ 30 kg/m2). Based on a self-reported questionnaire, personal habits were collected, including smoking status (never, ex, or current smoker) and alcohol consumption (none, 1–2 times a week, 3–4 times a week, and almost every day).2728 Regular physical activity was defined as one of the following: 1) ≥ 30 minutes of walking at least five times per week, 2) ≥ 30 minutes of moderate physical activity at least five times per week, or 3) ≥ 20 minutes of vigorous physical activity at least three times per week.29 Economic status was divided into three categories based on income: high for the upper 30%, low for the lowest 30% and medical aid recipients, and middle for the remainder. The residential areas were classified into rural areas, middle- and small-sized cities, and metropolitan cities. Comorbidities were defined as the presence of ICD-10 codes during the previous three years, including a history of hypertension (I10–13, and I15), diabetes mellitus (E10–14), chronic kidney disease (N18), dyslipidemia (E78), chronic obstructive pulmonary disease (J431, J432, J438, J439, or J44),303031323334353637383941 ischemic heart disease (I20–I25), chronic heart failure (I50) and atrial fibrillation/flutter (I48).

Statistical analysis

The results are presented as numbers (%) for categorical variables and as means ± standard deviations, medians (interquartile range [IQR]), or ranges for continuous variables. Two groups were compared using the χ2 test for categorical variables and t-tests for continuous variables.

The incidence rates of stroke were calculated by dividing the number of incident events by the total follow-up period (per 10,000 person-years). A cumulative incidence plot was used to compare the incidence of stroke, and the differences between the two groups were assessed using the log-rank test.

Study 1: Comparison of stroke risk according to COVID-19 status in the asthma cohort

For the analysis, PS matching was performed using a 1:1 greedy nearest-neighbor algorithm to compare the COVID-19 and control cohorts. Matching was based on age, sex, BMI, smoking status, alcohol consumption, regular physical activity, economic status, residential area, previous history of severe asthma exacerbation (defined as emergency room visits or hospitalizations related to asthma, accompanied by the use of oral or intravenous corticosteroids42), and comorbidities (hypertension, diabetes mellitus, chronic kidney disease, dyslipidemia, chronic obstructive pulmonary disease, ischemic heart disease, congestive heart failure, and atrial fibrillation/flutter).

PS was derived from the predicted probabilities of individuals in the asthma COVID-19 cohort. A logistic regression model was utilized with adjustments for the abovementioned covariates. The standardized mean difference (SMD) was used to examine the balance of covariate distribution between the groups, with an SMD > 0.25 considered to indicate imbalance.43

Unadjusted Cox proportional hazards regression analyses were conducted to compare the risk of stroke between the COVID-19 cohort and the PS-matched control cohort, as we comprehensively matched the two groups in terms of all baseline variables (all SMDs were < 0.25) (Table 1). In the subgroup analysis by COVID-19 severity, the SMDs were < 0.25 for all variables, except for age (Supplementary Table 1). Accordingly, we adjusted for age in the comparison of stroke risk. A two-sided P value < 0.05 was considered statistically significant. All statistical analyses were carried out using SAS version 9.4 (SAS Institute Inc., Cary, NC, USA).

Study 2: Comparison of stroke risk in the asthma cohort vs. general population cohort according to COVID-19 status

We performed PS matching using a 1:1 greedy nearest-neighbor algorithm to compare individuals with COVID-19 and those without COVID-19 among the general population without asthma (general population cohort). Matching was based on age, sex, BMI, smoking status, alcohol consumption, regular physical activity, economic status, residential area, and comorbidities (hypertension, diabetes mellitus, chronic kidney disease, dyslipidemia, chronic obstructive pulmonary disease, ischemic heart disease, congestive heart failure, and atrial fibrillation/flutter).

To evaluate the risk of stroke in the asthma cohort vs. the general population cohort according to COVID-19 severity (no, non-severe, and severe COVID-19), we examined the SMDs of all baseline characteristics among the six groups. Since all six groups were well balanced except for age (SMD < 0.25 for all variables except for age) (Supplementary Table 2), age was adjusted for the comparison of stroke risk among the six groups.

Ethics statement

Our study protocol was approved by the Institutional Review Board of Korea University Anam Hospital (No. 2024AN0249). The requirement for informed consent was waived because all patient records were anonymized before use.

RESULTS

Baseline characteristics

Study 1

The baseline characteristics of the asthma cohort are described in Table 1. The asthma cohort with COVID-19 and those without COVID-19 each comprised 9,839 individuals, with a mean age of 57 years and approximately 40% male. Following PS matching, the baseline characteristics of the study participants were adequately balanced (all SMDs < 0.25).

Study 2

The baseline characteristics of the Study 2 population are summarized in Supplementary Table 2, categorized into six groups based on asthma status and COVID-19 severity. The general population cohort included 9,094 individuals without COVID-19, 8,085 with non-severe COVID-19, and 1,009 with severe COVID-19. In the asthma cohort, as in Study 1, there were 9,839 individuals without COVID-19, 8,462 individuals with non-severe COVID-19, and 1,377 individuals with severe COVID-19. After propensity matching, the baseline characteristics of the study participants were adequately balanced (all SMDs < 0.25), except for age.

Outcome

Study 1: Stroke risk according to COVID-19 status in the asthma cohort

During a median follow-up of 76 days (IQR, 20–193 days; range, 1–434 days), 0.40% (40/9,839) of the asthma cohort with COVID-19 developed stroke, compared to 0.36% (35/9,839) of the asthma cohort without COVID-19. The incidence rates of stroke were significantly higher in the asthma cohort with COVID-19 compared to the asthma cohort without COVID-19 (126.9 vs. 110.8 per 10,000 person-years) (Table 2). The cumulative incidence rate analyses between the two cohorts are depicted in Fig. 2 (log-rank P = 0.56).

DISCUSSION

In this study, the relationship between COVID-19 severity and the long-term risk of stroke in patients with asthma was evaluated using a large nationwide dataset. In Study 1, we analyzed stroke risk based on COVID-19 severity within the asthma cohort, while in Study 2, we compared the stroke risk based on asthma and COVID-19 severity. The analyses in Study 1 revealed an increased risk of stroke following severe COVID-19 in individuals with asthma, with about 1.9-fold higher risk compared to those without COVID-19. In study 2, we observed that the risk of stroke was highest when asthma coexisted with severe COVID-19.

The strength of our study lies in being the first study to demonstrate the association between the severity of COVID-19 and the relatively long-term risk of stroke in individuals with asthma using a large cohort. Since individuals with asthma already face heightened risk of stroke, the additional impact of COVID-19 underscores the need for careful monitoring of post-COVID-19 complications in those who experienced severe COVID-19, even after recovery. This finding offers valuable insight into the long-term consequences of COVID-19 and highlights a potential vulnerability for patients with asthma, especially in the endemic period.

The relationship between asthma and stroke,4445 as well as the relationship between COVID-19 and stroke,464648 has been well documented in previous studies. It may be inevitable that experiencing severe COVID-19 could further amplify the risk of stroke in people with asthma. Several potential mechanisms may explain the observed increased risk of stroke within this subgroup. The cornerstone of the shared pathophysiology between asthma and stroke development lies in atherosclerosis,749 each through different pathways. In patients with asthma, chronic airway inflammation spills over into a systemic response, marked by elevated levels of circulating inflammatory cells, cytokines, and acute-phase proteins.50 These inflammatory mediators activate the vascular endothelium, causing endothelial dysfunction and destabilization of plaques, making them vulnerable to rupture. The subsequent formation of blood clots within these ruptured plaques, that is atherothrombosis, can then potentially lead to stroke.51 On the other hand, acute viral infections may also induce a systemic inflammatory response in the host, triggering infection-related platelet activation and aggregation, inflammation-induced thrombosis, and impairment of endothelial function.525254 Similar to other viral infections, SARS-CoV-2 infection triggers a vigorous inflammatory response that is accompanied by coagulopathy with frequent presence of antiphospholipid antibodies. This inflammatory state likely contributes to the high prevalence of thrombosis observed in COVID-19 patients.5255 The elevated D-dimer levels observed in individuals who experienced severe COVID-19 also suggest an increased susceptibility to developing cerebrovascular diseases, including stroke.56 Furthermore, while long-term cardiovascular complications following sepsis are well-documented, severe COVID-19 shares similar pathophysiological pathways with sepsis—such as systemic inflammation, immune dysregulation, and endothelial injury—further exacerbating the risk of stroke associated with COVID-19.575759

In addition, acute asthma exacerbations can trigger transient periods of cerebral hypoxemia, which is a condition that has been demonstrated to be associated with an increased risk of stroke.60 Viral infections are well-established triggers of asthma exacerbations8; COVID-19 in particular can cause acute exacerbations and chronic deterioration of asthma control.61 Furthermore, certain asthma medications, particularly used in uncontrolled conditions (e.g., short-acting beta2-agonists and systemic corticosteroids) may potentially be an additional risk factor for stroke.6263 In other words, inadequate control of asthma due to COVID-19 may act in multiple ways to increase the risk of stroke.

The findings of our study have significant clinical implications, particularly for the management of patients with asthma during and after the COVID-19 pandemic. The increased risk of stroke in patients with asthma who experience severe COVID-19 underscores the necessity for vigilant monitoring and the proactive management of stroke risk factors in this vulnerable population. Clinicians should consider incorporating routine stroke risk assessment and preventive strategies into the long-term care plans for asthmatic patients who are recovering from severe COVID-19. Future research should focus on elucidating the underlying mechanisms that are driving the increased stroke risk in this subgroup, potentially exploring the roles of chronic inflammation, coagulopathy, and endothelial dysfunction. Additionally, longitudinal studies with larger and more diverse populations are needed to validate our findings and assess the generalizability across different ethnic groups and healthcare settings. Investigating the efficacy of targeted interventions, such as anti-inflammatory treatments or anticoagulants, in reducing stroke risk among asthmatic COVID-19 survivors could also provide valuable insights for improving patient outcomes.

Our study also has several limitations. First, our study only included individuals who had undergone health screening examinations, which might have introduced a selection bias, potentially limiting the inclusion of patients with relatively healthier habits. Another limitation concerns the potential for misclassification bias caused by using ICD-10 codes. To address this, we implemented a stricter definition of asthma that required the presence of at least three relevant ICD-10 codes alongside the concurrent use of asthma medications. In addition, as our study includes data spanning from 2019 to 2021 and encompasses various variants, careful interpretation is required, particularly considering the recent emergence of the Omicron variant.64 However, there remain diverse perspectives regarding neurological complications associated with different variants.6566 Given the continuous emergence of new variants, ongoing monitoring, and investigation of neurological outcomes are deemed necessary. Finally, as this study exclusively examined the Korean population, it may not be generalizable to other countries or ethnic groups.

In this nationwide population-based cohort study, a significant association between severe COVID-19 and the occurrence of stroke was noted in adults with asthma. While individuals with asthma already face an elevated risk of stroke, our findings suggest the importance of monitoring for stroke occurrence in patients with asthma to improve long-term outcomes during the post-COVID-19 era, especially for those who have experienced severe COVID-19.
