Multifaceted roles of casein kinase 1 in health and disease: beyond cancer
Authors: Semi Kim, Ji Hoon Oh
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractCasein kinase 1 (CK1) enzymes, a family of serine/threonine-specific protein kinases, are remarkably conserved throughout evolution and exhibit diverse functionalities across eukaryotic species. While initially characterized by their role in casein (a type of milk protein) phosphorylation, subsequent investigations have unveiled their extensive involvement in fundamental biological processes, including cell division, maintenance of DNA integrity, programmed cell death, and the intricate regulation of gene transcription. Furthermore, CK1 significantly influences circadian rhythm mechanisms, highlighting its systemic regulatory importance. In mammals, multiple CK1 isoforms have been identified, each contributing to both physiological functions and various disease states. Dysregulation of CK1 activity is consistently associated with oncogenesis, where it promotes tumor cell proliferation, survival, metastasis, and resistance to therapeutic interventions. Emerging evidence also points to the critical relevance of CK1 in non-malignant conditions, such as neurodegenerative diseases, metabolic syndromes, and immune dysfunctions. In these conditions, CK1 often mediates pathogenic signaling through aberrant phosphorylation and the disruption of temporal gene expression. This review aims to re-examine the CK1 family as a versatile regulator that interacts with various pathological conditions, extending beyond its traditional classification as merely a signaling kinase. We provide an overview of the structural and functional properties of CK1 isoforms, summarize their relevance across a range of diseases, and explore novel possibilities for therapeutic interventions targeting this kinase family. Moreover, by reviewing the current understanding of CK1, we search for a new perspective on its role in maintaining cellular balance and its contribution to disease mechanisms, thereby proposing novel avenues for future research.Keywords: Casein kinase 1, Cancer, Circadian rhythm, Metabolic diseases, Neurodegenerative diseases

INTRODUCTION

The casein kinase 1 (CK1) family, a highly conserved group of serine/threonine kinases indispensable to eukaryotes, plays fundamental roles in a wide array of cellular processes. Its involvement spans basic cellular maintenance to the regulation of complex physiological functions (1). Early research primarily focused on defining the pivotal cellular roles of CK1, highlighting its involvement in critical events, such as cell cycle progression, DNA damage response and repair, gene expression regulation, cell division and differentiation, cell migration, intracellular vesicle trafficking, apoptosis, autophagy, and circadian rhythm maintenance (2-4).

To date, seven CK1 isoforms (CK1α, CK1β, CK1δ, CK1ε, CK1γ1, CK1γ2, and CK1γ3) have been identified that showcase further functional diversity arising from alternative splicing. Each isoform performs specialized or sometimes redundant functions, and any perturbation within this intricate kinase network can precipitate a wide range of disease states, including various forms of neoplasia (5). These isoforms exhibit both specialized and overlapping functions, forming a complex kinase network whose dysregulation can lead to various pathologies, including numerous cancers. Indeed, extensive evidence highlights the pivotal role CK1 isoforms play in tumor initiation and progression (6). Aberrant expression, gene mutations, or disruption of regulatory mechanisms within CK1 pathways are implicated in driving tumor cell proliferation, evasion of apoptosis, enhanced metastatic capacity, and resistance to anticancer therapies across diverse malignancies (3). Collectively, these insights position CK1 as a compelling target for therapeutic intervention (7).

However, accumulating studies on the CK1 family demonstrate that its impact extends beyond cancer (8). CK1 isoforms are now recognized as essential regulators involved in a broad spectrum of human diseases that affect nearly all physiological systems (9). Notably, in neurodegenerative diseases, such as Alzheimer’s disease (AD), Parkinson’s disease (PD), and amyotrophic lateral sclerosis (ALS), CK1 isoforms play critical roles by modulating pathological protein phosphorylation, aggregation, and neuronal apoptosis (10).

Furthermore, CK1 isoforms, particularly CK1δ and CK1ε, are indispensable for maintaining sleep-wake cycles and circadian rhythms that regulate metabolic homeostasis (11). Dysregulation of CK1-mediated circadian control is associated with an increased risk of sleep disorders and metabolic diseases, underscoring the significant pharmacological relevance of CK1 across multiple pathologies (12-14).

Considering these insights, the CK1 family is currently undergoing conceptual reevaluation and is increasingly regarded as a multifaceted regulator at the nexus of diverse pathological processes (15). Beyond their canonical phosphorylation functions, CK1 isoforms influence various diseases largely through their ability to regulate protein stability. CK1 modulates phosphorylation-dependent protein degradation, thereby exerting broad control over key signaling pathways governing cell proliferation, survival, and stress responses (3, 5, 16).

This review comprehensively examines CK1’s structure, functions, and involvement in various diseases. Additionally, it emphasizes that the dysregulation of CK1-mediated control over protein stability constitutes a common mechanistic framework linking cancer, neurodegenerative disorders, immune dysfunction, and metabolic diseases. Collectively, this synthesis positions CK1 not merely as a versatile kinase family but as a central regulator of cellular homeostasis and disease pathogenesis, thereby highlighting new avenues for future research and therapeutic development.

CK1 family and regulatory mechanisms

The diverse physiological functions and pathological implications of the CK1 family arise from a complex regulatory framework governing the distinct biochemical properties and enzymatic activity of each CK1 isoform (Fig. 1A). These isoforms are predominantly characterized by lipid modification motifs, such as palmitoylation at the N-terminus, which facilitate their preferential localization to specific cellular membranes (Fig. 1). Additionally, CK1 isoforms are implicated in signaling complex assembly, such as through interactions with Frizzled receptors and LRP5/6 co-receptors in the canonical Wnt signaling pathway (17) (Fig. 2).

Overview of CK1 isoforms

Despite sharing a conserved catalytic kinase domain encompassing ATP-binding and substrate recognition motifs, CK1 isoforms exhibit significant variability in their N-terminal and C-terminal regions (17). These non-catalytic extensions vary in length and amino acid sequence and are critical for isoform-specific functions, subcellular localization, substrate selectivity, and interactions with regulatory proteins (2).

CK1α possesses relatively short terminal regions and is ubiquitously expressed across cellular compartments, playing essential roles in cellular viability and homeostasis. Functionally, CK1α phosphorylates β-catenin and the adenomatous polyposis coli protein within the Wnt/β-catenin signaling cascade, modulates p53 stability and transcriptional activity (3), contributes to DNA damage response pathways, and regulates autophagy through the phosphorylation of proteins that include the UV radiation resistance-associated gene (UVRAG) protein (16, 18, 19). Notably, immunomodulatory drugs (IMiDs), such as lenalidomide and pomalidomide, facilitate the proteasomal degradation of CK1α via cereblon (CRBN)-binding, a mechanism implicated in del(5q) myelodysplastic syndrome (MDS) pathogenesis (20).

CK1δ, together with CK1ε, acts as a core component of the circadian clock by phosphorylating PERIOD (PER) proteins, thereby modulating their stability and nuclear translocation (8, 13). CK1δ is also involved in key developmental and stress-response pathways, including cell cycle regulation, DNA damage signaling, and vesicle trafficking, as well as Wnt, Hedgehog (Hh), and Hippo signaling pathways, in part through the regulation of Wee1 kinase degradation (16, 21). CK1δ is frequently overexpressed or genomically amplified in numerous cancers, where it promotes tumor proliferation, survival, invasiveness, metastasis, and therapeutic resistance. In neurodegenerative diseases, CK1δ contributes to disease pathogenesis by phosphorylating pathological proteins, such as Tau in AD and TDP-43 in ALS and frontotemporal dementia (FTLD)-TDP (22).

CK1ε shares high sequence similarity with CK1δ and frequently exhibits overlapping or complementary functions; it plays key roles in circadian regulation, Wnt signaling, and embryonic development (17, 23). CK1ε has been implicated in several malignancies and is of particular interest in glioblastoma (GBM), where it facilitates tumor cell survival and the self-renewal of cancer stem cells. In hematologic malignancies, CK1ε promotes oncogenic c-Myc translation through its interaction with the PI3Kδ signaling axis, thereby supporting cellular proliferation. Furthermore, CK1ε has emerged as a genetic determinant of behavioral sensitivity and drug response to psychostimulants, garnering attention in neuropsychiatric and addiction research (24).

CK1γ isoforms (CK1γ1 and CK1γ3) facilitate necroptosis, a regulated form of cell death mediated by tumor necrosis factor-α (TNF-α). These isoforms interact with the necrosome and phosphorylate receptor-interacting protein kinase 3 (RIPK3), thereby initiating the necroptotic cascade (25). Mutations in CK1γ1 are linked to severe developmental delays and autism spectrum disorder (ASD), underscoring its importance in neurodevelopment (26).

In summary, CK1 isoforms function as serine/threonine kinases with wide-ranging substrate targets. Their structural diversity, subcellular distribution, substrate specificity, and distinct regulatory mechanisms contribute to either specialized or overlapping biological activities. A nuanced understanding of these isoform-specific roles is essential for the rational design of selective and efficacious therapeutics targeting the CK1 family in a wide array of diseases (Table 1) (5).

Dysregulation of CK1 isoforms in diverse human diseases

Dysregulation of various CK1 isoforms plays distinct pathological roles. CK1α, δ, ε, and γ1/3 each contribute to disease onset and progression through unique pathological mechanisms in conditions such as cancer, neurodegenerative disorders, metabolic diseases, and immune disorders. Furthermore, some isoforms exhibit context-dependent dual roles, possessing both tumor-suppressive and pro-tumorigenic functions.

Regulatory mechanisms of CK1

CK1’s enzymatic activity and functional diversity are intricately controlled by multiple regulatory mechanisms that reflect isoform-specific biochemical features and dynamic cellular contexts. These include post-translational modifications (PTMs), targeted protein–protein interactions, and precise subcellular localization, and their disruption in cancer cells can perturb CK1-dependent signaling pathways, thereby promoting tumorigenesis and malignant progression (5).

CK1 isoforms are substrates for diverse PTMs that modulate their catalytic activity, stability, substrate selectivity, and interaction profiles. Among these, phosphorylation represents a key regulatory modality (27). CK1 isoforms are subject to phosphorylation by upstream kinases and can undergo autophosphorylation. Phosphorylation within or proximal to the kinase domain can induce conformational changes that directly influence catalytic efficiency and substrate accessibility. A defining feature of CK1 family members is their preferential phosphorylation of serine/threonine residues within the pS/T-X-S/T motif, particularly when preceded by acidic residues (8). Nevertheless, isoform-specific structural elements further refine substrate specificity. For example, autophosphorylation of C-terminal regulatory domains in CK1δ and CK1ε exerts an autoinhibitory effect on kinase activity, whereas dephosphorylation relieves this inhibition and enhances enzymatic function. Activation of upstream oncogenic pathways, such as receptor tyrosine and mitogen-activated protein kinases, can modulate the phosphorylation states of CK1 isoforms in cancer cells, altering their function within key signaling cascades, including the Wnt, Hippo, and p53 pathways (18, 28). For example, TGR-1202 (dual PI3Kδ/CK1ε inhibitor) suppresses CK1ε autophosphorylation, which is a critical step for the phosphorylation of 4E-BP1 and initiation of cap-dependent mRNA translation (29), thereby disrupting translational programs essential for cancer cell proliferation. These findings underscore the importance of phosphorylation-mediated regulation in fine-tuning CK1ε activity and sustaining oncogenic signaling (10).

Subcellular compartmentalization is essential for directing CK1 isoforms to appropriate substrates and signaling hubs. CK1 family members display isoform-specific and dynamic localization across various cellular compartments (30). For example, CK1γ isoforms are membrane-bound through N-terminal lipid modifications, whereas CK1δ and CK1ε shuttle between the nucleus and cytoplasm to regulate distinct substrate pools. In cancer, structural abnormalities or the misexpression of anchoring proteins can alter CK1 localization, leading to aberrant signaling or loss of tumor-suppressive activity (5, 31).

The ubiquitin–proteasome system stringently controls CK1 protein stability. Specific E3 ubiquitin ligases mediate CK1 polyubiquitination, targeting it for degradation by the 26S proteasome (24). IMiDs can bind to CRBN, a substrate receptor of the CRL4CRBN E3 ubiquitin ligase complex. This interaction reprograms substrate specificity, promoting the degradation of CK1α, an essential mechanism underlying the clinical efficacy of IMiDs in hematologic malignancies, such as del(5q) MDS (32). Dysregulation of E3 ligases or the altered activity of deubiquitinating enzymes in cancer may lead to abnormal stabilization or excessive degradation of CK1 isoforms, thereby promoting oncogenic signaling (20).

CK1 isoforms can also undergo SUMOylation, acetylation, and methylation, which may influence their localization, protein interactions, and enzymatic function (27). However, the extent and mechanistic implications of these modifications in cancer remain undefined, highlighting an important avenue for future investigation (33).

CK1 activity is also modulated by interactions with various regulatory proteins and frequently functions within larger multiprotein complexes that coordinate signal transduction (5). In the Wnt pathway, scaffold proteins facilitate the assembly of CK1, GSK3β, and β-catenin, enabling efficient substrate phosphorylation and pathway activation. In cancer, alterations in complex composition or binding dynamics can profoundly affect CK1-dependent signaling strength and duration (34).

Several oncogenes and tumor suppressors are regulated through interactions with CK1, further underscoring its relevance in cancer biology. Notably, CK1ε cooperates with PI3Kδ to regulate c-Myc translation in hematologic malignancies, as demonstrated in studies with TGR-1202. Likewise, the therapeutic effects of IMiDs depend on CRBN-mediated degradation of CK1α, reinforcing the translational potential of targeting CK1 in cancer (29). Together, these multilayered regulatory mechanisms highlight the precision with which CK1 isoform activity is controlled (35). Disrupting this regulatory balance can drive disease pathogenesis, emphasizing the need for continued investigation into CK1-targeted therapeutic strategies (3, 29, 36).

CK1 in cancer

The CK1 family precisely regulates key signaling pathways involved in various stages of cancer progression (1). However, each CK1 isoform often plays a dual role as a tumor promoter or inhibitor depending on the specific cancer type, underlying genetic environment, and cellular microenvironment (37, 38). Indeed, changes in CK1 isoform expression levels are commonly observed in several cancer types, often strongly correlating with patient prognosis (39-41).

Overexpression or gene amplification of certain CK1 isoforms often drives malignant progression (41). For example, CK1δ is frequently amplified in human epithelial ovarian cancer (EOC), thereby promoting the proliferation, cell cycle progression, chemoresistance, and migration of ovarian cancer cells (21). Conversely, pharmacological inhibition or knockdown of CK1δ effectively suppresses tumor growth and enhances sensitivity to chemotherapeutic agents, such as cisplatin. In triple-negative breast cancer (TNBC), CSNK1D (encoding for CK1δ) is frequently overexpressed due to gene amplification at 17q25.3, which is associated with aggressive migration and metastasis (42). CK1ε plays a crucial role in maintaining GBM cell viability and the self-renewal capacity of cancer stem cells (43, 44). In hematologic malignancies, it promotes proliferation via c-Myc translation in cooperation with the PI3Kδ pathway (29). Additionally, the CK1α gene is commonly overexpressed in melanoma cells (45).

Conversely, CK1 isoforms can function as tumor suppressors depending on the cellular context. For instance, CK1α acts as a bona fide tumor suppressor in medulloblastoma, a pediatric brain tumor driven by Sonic hedgehog (SHH) signaling (46). The recently developed CK1α activator, SSTC3, enhances the phosphorylation and subsequent degradation of GLI transcription factors, thereby suppressing SHH pathway activity and the growth of metastatic medulloblastoma, a malignancy often resistant to conventional therapies (45, 47). In hepatocellular carcinoma (HCC), CK1α phosphorylates UVRAG, promoting its ubiquitination by the E3 ubiquitin ligase, SMURF1 (24). This PTM enhances autophagosome maturation, ultimately inhibiting HCC cell proliferation. In acute myeloid leukemia (AML), CK1α deficiency paradoxically activates the p53-mediated DNA damage response while simultaneously promoting pro-leukemic Wnt signaling, reflecting a complex and often contradictory regulatory role. Thus, co-inhibition strategies targeting CK1α and CDK7/9 (transcription-associated kinases) have been explored to effectively induce apoptosis in AML cells (28, 48, 49). Notably, lenalidomide-induced degradation of CK1α serves as a pivotal therapeutic mechanism that targets abnormal hematopoietic stem cells in patients with del(5q) MDS (20, 28, 32, 49, 50). Moreover, CK1α functions as a critical tumor suppressor in colorectal cancer by regulating β-catenin activity. In tumors with constitutive Wnt signaling, CK1α is frequently found in an autophosphorylated, catalytically inactive state, suggesting that Wnt pathway activation may involve the evasion of CK1α-mediated suppression rather than its direct stimulation (38).

In breast cancer, while CK1ε can inhibit Wnt/β-catenin signaling, it also activates the Wnt/Rac1/JNK and NFAT pathways, reducing cell adhesion and enhancing migration (17). These effects suggest that certain CK1ε mutations may facilitate metastasis. Conversely, other studies report that high CK1ε expression correlates with improved disease-free survival in patients with wild type p53 or poorly differentiated tumors. These divergent findings highlight that the functional impact of CK1 isoforms in cancer is highly contingent upon the cellular context, including p53 and estrogen receptor status (37).

As previously discussed, CK1 modulates multiple stages of the Wnt cascade, functioning as either a positive or negative regulator depending on subcellular localization and context (17). In the cytoplasm, CK1α collaborates with GSK3β to phosphorylate β-catenin, triggering its degradation and thereby repressing Wnt signaling. Contrastingly, at the plasma membrane, CK1 phosphorylates the LRP5/6 co-receptors, promoting Wnt pathway activation. It also regulates key branching points in Wnt signaling through Dvl protein phosphorylation (51-53).

Aberrant Wnt activation is a hallmark of many cancers, and CK1’s context-dependent functions reflect its dual roles. For example, in GBM, CK1ε depletion paradoxically activates β-catenin and induces apoptosis, revealing a noncanonical regulatory mechanism. These observations emphasize that CK1 isoforms can exert opposing effects based on tumor-specific cellular environments, moving beyond a simplistic dichotomy of oncogenic versus tumor-suppressive activity (43, 44).

Hh signaling is critical for embryonic development, adult stem cell maintenance, and the progression of several malignancies (34, 54, 55). CK1 isoforms (particularly CK1α, CK1δ, and CK1ε) regulate the activity, stability, and nuclear translocation of GLI transcription factors, the central effectors of the Hh pathway, via site-specific phosphorylation. Typically, CK1-mediated phosphorylation induces partial proteolysis or degradation of GLI, thereby attenuating Hh signaling. Consequently, CK1α activators have emerged as promising therapeutic agents for Hh-dependent tumors (34, 54).

The Hippo signaling pathway functions as a key tumor suppressor by controlling cell proliferation, apoptosis, and organ size (56). Its core effectors, YAP and TAZ, are transcriptional coactivators tightly regulated by phosphorylation. CK1δ and CKε modulate this pathway by directly phosphorylating YAP/TAZ or influencing upstream kinases, such as LATS1/2 (44). Aberrant Hippo signaling is frequently observed in various solid tumors, suggesting that CK1 isoforms contribute to tumorigenesis via its dysregulation (56). Notably, overexpression in TNBC is associated with enhanced malignancy through Hippo pathway modulation (42).

As a master tumor suppressor, p53 orchestrates cellular responses to genotoxic and oncogenic stress by inducing apoptosis, cell cycle arrest, or senescence. The p53 protein regulates its stability and transcriptional activity either through direct phosphorylation at multiple residues or by indirectly modulating MDM2, its principal negative regulator (36). For instance, CK1δ-mediated phosphorylation at Ser15 promotes p53 activation. Given that p53 dysregulation is a hallmark of many cancers, CK1’s regulatory role in this pathway critically influences cancer cell fate (1). In EOC, CK1δ affects tumor proliferation and chemosensitivity via p53 regulation (21). As such, the CK1 family engages in multifaceted roles across various cancer types, and an accurate understanding of the distinct functions and regulatory mechanisms of each isoform is essential for establishing efficacious anti-cancer therapeutic strategies.

CK1 in neurodegenerative diseases

The CK1 family plays a critical role in preserving normal cerebral function (57), with substantial evidence implicating CK1 in the pathogenesis of various neurodegenerative diseases. A common hallmark of these diseases is the abnormal misfolding, aggregation, and intracellular accumulation of specific proteins (10, 22).

Interactions and regulation with key pathological proteins

Hyperphosphorylation of the microtubule-associated protein, Tau, and its aggregation into neurofibrillary tangles (NFTs) constitute central pathological features in tauopathies, including AD, FTLD with Tau pathology, and progressive supranuclear palsy (10, 33, 58). CK1δ directly phosphorylates multiple serine and threonine residues on Tau, promoting its dissociation from microtubules and markedly enhancing aggregation propensity. This aberrant phosphorylation accelerates NFT formation, ultimately leading to synaptic dysfunction and neuronal death. Notably, increased CK1δ expression positively correlates with Tau pathology severity in AD brains (41).

In synucleinopathies, α-synuclein misfolding and aggregation into Lewy bodies and neurites are pathological hallmarks (41). CK1ε and CK1δ phosphorylate α-synuclein at Ser129, a modification that facilitates aggregation and exacerbates neurotoxicity, making phosphorylated α-synuclein a principal Lewy body component (59). Recent studies have shown that this pathological phosphorylation can be modulated by mutations in upstream genes, such as CHCHD2 (9). In familial PD cases associated with CHCHD2 mutations, mutant CHCHD2 mislocalizes to the mitochondrial outer membrane, recruiting CK1ε/δ and phosphorylating α-synuclein and neurofilament light chain, which promotes aggregate formation and dopaminergic neuron degeneration (9, 60).

Hyperphosphorylated, ubiquitinated, and insoluble cytoplasmic inclusions of TDP-43 are found in approximately 97% of ALS and FTLD-TDP cases. CK1δ and CK1ε directly phosphorylate multiple serine residues in the C-terminal region of TDP-43, promoting its aggregation, cytoplasmic mislocalization, and impaired RNA metabolism, thereby contributing to neurotoxicity (10). A strong correlation exists between phosphorylated TDP-43 pathology and CK1ε expression in ALS motor neurons. Moreover, TDP-43 binds CK1ε mRNA, suggesting a potential feedback regulatory loop (31).

Mutations in fused in sarcoma (FUS), an RNA-binding protein akin to TDP-43, cause familial ALS and FTLD-FUS, with phosphorylation implicated in its pathology. Notably, CK1δ/ε-mediated phosphorylation enhances FUS solubility and reduces aggregation, ameliorating neurodegeneration in FUS-mediated models, indicating that isoform functions can vary depending on substrate and cellular contexts (33).

Repressor element-1 silencing transcription factor (REST) plays a critical neuroprotective role by promoting neuronal survival and stress resistance in aging brains. REST expression is markedly decreased in AD brains, correlating with cognitive decline (8). CK1δ phosphorylates REST, targeting it for proteasomal degradation. Increased amyloid-beta (Aβ) burden elevates CK1δ expression, leading to REST depletion and increased neuronal susceptibility to Aβ toxicity and neuroinflammation (57).

Disease-specific role analysis and neuroprotective effects of CK1 inhibitors

CK1δ contributes to AD pathology through multiple mechanisms, including REST degradation, Tau hyperphosphorylation, and facilitation of Aβ production via astrocyte-derived extracellular vesicles (57). Granulovacuolar degeneration (GVD), characterized by cytoplasmic inclusion bodies in AD and related disorders, contains CK1δ as a core component and reliable marker (61, 62). GVD is closely linked to impaired proteostasis and believed to exacerbate Tau pathology. GVD contributes to neurodegeneration through the abnormal aggregation of sortilin, a type I transmembrane receptor involved in intracellular trafficking and neurotrophic factor sorting (61). Pathological conditions cause sortilin to aggregate intracellularly, disrupting vesicular transport and lysosomal function, thereby promoting Tau misfolding and neurotoxicity. Preclinical studies demonstrated that CK1δ inhibitors can restore Aβ-induced REST reduction, attenuate Tau phosphorylation, reduce neuroinflammation, and improve cognitive function (63).

CK1ε/δ-mediated phosphorylation of α-synuclein at Ser129 is a critical step in PD pathogenesis. Consequently, selective CK1ε/δ inhibitors are actively being investigated as therapeutic agents to block α-synuclein aggregation and protect dopaminergic neurons. Given the central role of TDP-43 hyperphosphorylation in ALS and FTLD-TDP, CK1δ/ε inhibitors have shown efficacy in suppressing TDP-43 phosphorylation, thereby reducing aggregation and neurotoxicity. These findings highlight the therapeutic potential of CK1-targeted inhibition for neurodegenerative diseases (10, 63).

Overall, CK1 isoforms play pivotal, context-dependent roles in the pathogenesis of multiple neurodegenerative disorders. Isoform-specific inhibition offers a promising therapeutic strategy; however, precise targeting tailored to disease context and substrate specificity remains critical and warrants further investigation.

CK1 in immunoregulation

The CK1 family serves as a key regulator in innate and adaptive immune responses by modulating inflammatory signaling pathways and the development, activation, differentiation, and programmed cell death of immune cells (64). Dysregulation of CK1 activity can disrupt immune homeostasis, thereby contributing to the initiation and progression of various immune-related diseases (65, 66).

Impact on key immune signaling pathways

Considerable evidence supports the significant involvement of CK1 isoforms in major immune pathways. CK1 phosphorylates key components of the nuclear factor kappa-light-chain-enhancer of activated B cells (NF-κB) pathway, such as the IκB kinase complex and IκBα (negative regulator), thereby facilitating NF-κB activation and nuclear translocation (67) (Fig. 2). In neurodegenerative diseases, the co-localization of CK1δ and phosphorylated p65 (active subunit of NF-κB) within GVD structures further suggests a spatial and functional link between CK1 and inflammatory signaling (25, 61).

Type I interferons (IFNs; IFN-α/β) are critical cytokines that play a central role in host defense against viral infections. CK1 modulates antiviral immune responses by participating in downstream signaling cascades, including the production of IFNs and activation of STAT proteins through IFN receptors (68).

In the TNF-α pathway, CK1α negatively regulates TNF-α-induced apoptosis by phosphorylating serine residues on TNF-α receptor 2, thereby promoting cell survival and limiting excessive inflammation. Conversely, CK1γ1 and CK1γ3 directly associate with the necrosome complex (comprising RIPK1-RIPK3-mixed lineage kinase domain-like protein [MLKL]) and phosphorylate RIPK3, thereby promoting TNF-α-mediated necroptosis. During apoptosis, caspase-8-mediated cleavage of CK1γ1 blocks necroptosis, suggesting that CK1 isoforms function as molecular switches that govern the mode of cell death (25).

Regulatory roles in inflammatory diseases

Aberrant CK1 activity contributes to the pathogenesis of inflammatory diseases by sustaining inappropriate immune activation and chronic inflammatory responses. In autoimmune and infectious conditions, dysregulated CK1 isoforms can alter pathogen recognition, disrupt innate immunity, and impair the transition to adaptive responses, thus exacerbating disease severity and tissue damage (68). In this context, an increase in CK1ε levels has been observed during the progression of chronic liver disease (CLD), which involves liver fibrosis, inflammation, and even cancer. This suggests that CK1ε affects hepatocyte function and the advancement of fibrosis. In fact, CK1ε inhibitors, such as PF-4800567, PF-670462, and IC261, have shown promise in preclinical models by affectively reducing tumor growth and fibrosis, highlighting CK1ε as a promising target for CLD treatment (69) (Table 2).

Potential for developing CK1-targeted immunotherapies

CK1’s role in immune signaling highlights its potential as a pharmacological target in immunological disorders. Notably, IMiDs target CK1α for proteasomal degradation via CRBN-mediated ubiquitination. Loss of CK1α alters T and NK cell function and suppresses proinflammatory cytokine production, offering a therapeutic benefit in hematologic malignancies and autoimmune diseases (32).

Pharmacologic targeting of CK1 is also under active investigation in cancer immunotherapy, where enhancing antitumor immune responses and improving the efficacy of immune checkpoint inhibitors remain critical goals (15).

CK1 in circadian rhythms and metabolic diseases

Disruption of circadian rhythms is increasingly implicated in metabolic diseases (12, 14), as they rigorously regulate metabolic processes, such as glucose homeostasis, lipid metabolism, and insulin sensitivity (23). CK1ε and CK1δ play central roles in maintaining molecular circadian rhythms through which they regulate major metabolic processes (8, 70).

Circadian rhythms are precisely regulated by a transcription-translation feedback loop in mammals wherein transcriptional activators, such as circadian locomotor output cycles kaput (CLOCK) and basic helix-loop-helix ARNT-like protein 1 (BMAL1), drive the expression of PER and cryptochrome (CRY) genes (23, 71). The resulting PER and CRY proteins accumulate in the cytoplasm, translocate into the nucleus, and inhibit CLOCK:BMAL1 activity, thereby repressing their own transcription. This negative feedback loop produces circadian rhythms with a period of approximately 24 h, regulating various physiological functions. CK1δ and CK1ε play pivotal roles in regulating the stability of PER proteins by phosphorylating multiple serine/threonine residues, targeting them for ubiquitin-mediated proteasomal degradation (23). This process is critical for defining both the period and amplitude of circadian rhythms (8). Genetic mutations in CK1δ (e.g., the tau mutation in hamsters) or CK1ε (e.g., specific CK1ε mutations in mice) alter the circadian period, leading to observable changes in sleep patterns and metabolic functions (72). A CK1 inhibitor was found to affect circadian rhythms in Eurydice pulchra (speckled sea louse); it influences EpCLK and EpBMAL1 phosphorylation in E. pulchra and disrupts EpCLK-BMAL1 to mediate transcription in Drosophila S2 cells. These findings suggest a potential link between the two positive circadian regulators and tidal behavior (73).

Abnormal CK1 activity or expression can interfere with circadian metabolic pathways by altering feeding behavior, energy expenditure, and hormone secretion patterns. Genetic variations or dysregulated expression of CK1 isoforms are associated with increased susceptibility to obesity and diabetes in humans, highlighting the therapeutic potential of targeting CK1 in circadian rhythm-related metabolic diseases (14, 69).

Given the underlying role of CK1 in several cellular processes and diseases, the effect it has on aging and longevity is currently under investigation. Circadian rhythm disruptions, significantly influenced by CK1, are widely recognized as a hallmark of aging and closely linked to the progressive decline in metabolic and cognitive function. These findings indicate that the multifaceted role of CK1 is also linked to age-related metabolic dysfunction (70).

Cardiac diseases

In the heart, gap junctions are essential for establishing physical and electrical coupling between adjacent cardiomyocytes (74, 75). Through these channels, action potentials propagate between cells, triggering calcium influx and subsequent calcium release from the sarcoplasmic reticulum within cardiomyocytes, thereby enabling synchronized cardiac contractions (76). Connexin 43 (Cx43), the most abundant gap junction protein in the heart, regulates gap junction function, assembly, and stability in a phosphorylation-dependent manner (77). In vitro studies have shown that CK1 phosphorylates Cx43 at serine 325, 328, and 330—modifications critical for proper gap junction assembly (78). Disruption of this phosphorylation impairs electrical coupling between cardiomyocytes, reducing pumping efficiency. The resulting contractile dysfunction contributes to the pathogenesis of heart failure by destabilizing intracellular calcium homeostasis (77). Although CK1 does not directly modulate calcium channels or transporters, it acts as an upstream regulator of electrical connectivity, thereby indirectly influencing calcium-dependent cardiac contractility.

The importance of CK1-mediated Cx43 phosphorylation and gap junction regulation has been further demonstrated in vivo. Notably, in zebrafish embryos, CK1δ inhibition leads to dose-dependent cardiac dysfunction, characterized by reduced cardiac output, bradycardia, blood pooling, pericardial edema, and loss of ventricular contractility (79). These findings suggest that disrupting CK1δ activity during development may impair electrical conduction and calcium homeostasis, ultimately leading to heart failure (77, 78).

Developmental disorders

CK1 family gene mutations are associated with various developmental disorders. Mutations in CK1γ1 have particularly been causally linked to severe early-onset epilepsy and ASD, with affected patients often exhibiting facial dysmorphism and developmental delays. CK1γ1 regulates neuronal growth and morphology by modulating NMDA receptor phosphorylation and synaptic transmission, and its dysfunction may underlie synaptic neurodevelopmental abnormalities (80).

Additionally, de novo mutations in CK1α disrupt its interaction with β-catenin, leading to abnormal β-catenin accumulation and dysregulated downstream gene expression, resulting in severe epilepsy in patients with infantile spasms syndrome (81). These findings highlight that Wnt/β-catenin pathway dysregulation contributes to both cancer and neurodevelopmental and epileptic disorders. Collectively, these results underscore the multifaceted role of CK1 and its significant therapeutic potential across a broad spectrum of human diseases (Fig. 2).

Challenges and future directions in CK1-targeted therapy

The increased understanding of the CK1 family’s notable involvement in a broad spectrum of human diseases firmly establishes CK1 as an exceptionally promising therapeutic target. Nevertheless, the development of efficacious and safe CK1-targeted therapies is confronted by several formidable challenges that necessitate meticulous attention (6, 82).

Therapeutic selectivity and toxicity

Developing CK1 inhibitors presents a significant challenge due to the difficulty of achieving isoform-specific selectivity. Given the substantial sequence homology within the catalytic domains of CK1 isoforms, designing an inhibitor highly specific to a single isoform demands advanced technical strategies and a deep understanding of CK1 isoforms (39).

This challenge leads to unintended off-target effects on other CK1 isoforms or unrelated kinases, which can cause severe adverse reactions and limit therapeutic applicability. For example, while pan-CK1 inhibitors may show efficacy in certain contexts, they can interfere with essential physiological processes regulated by CK1δ/ε, such as circadian rhythms, potentially resulting in side effects that include sleep disturbances and metabolic dysfunction (23). Additionally, as CK1 isoforms often have contradictory functions, non-selective inhibition could paradoxically activate pathways that promote disease progression or harm normal cells. For instance, while inhibiting CK1δ may have therapeutic effects in some cancers, suppressing CK1α can negate tumor-suppressive effects under certain conditions (65, 82). Therefore, a precise understanding of the distinct roles of each CK1 isoform in specific diseases and patient populations is critical for the development of highly selective inhibitors that minimize off-target effects. Meeting these demands requires the application of advanced drug discovery platforms capable of stringent isoform-specific screening and comprehensive safety profiling.

Another significant issue is systemic toxicity, which can occur when high drug concentrations are required. For brain tumors, a major technical hurdle in drug delivery is penetrating the blood-brain barrier (BBB). To address this, localized drug delivery methods are being considered. For example, in liver cancer, intra-arterial hepatic infusion can deliver high concentrations of therapeutic agents directly to the tumor while reducing systemic exposure (15). Similarly, in brain tumors, intracerebral delivery systems, such as convection-enhanced delivery, can bypass the BBB to achieve targeted drug accumulation. While the local delivery of CK1 inhibitors remains in early developmental stages, these precedents provide a practical framework for future CK1-targeted therapies.

Finally, monotherapy is a primary cause of inevitable drug resistance over time, which compromises therapeutic efficacy and limits long-term success. To overcome these complex challenges, taking a multi-faceted approach that clarifies the distinct roles of each isoform, employing precision medicine for accurate patient stratification, and integrating innovative strategies, such as allosteric inhibitors and combination therapies, are essential.

Future directions

Future research in CK1 biology and therapeutics should focus on innovative approaches that overcome the limitations of conventional drugs (83). Traditional drugs often target the active site of an enzyme, which is similar across CK1 isoforms, leading to selectivity issues.

To address these challenges, several key strategies are emerging. A prominent approach involves developing allosteric inhibitors that bind to unique sites on the enzyme rather than to the highly similar ATP-binding site. A recent study provided an important foundation for developing highly isoform-selective allosteric inhibitors with unique targeting via anion-binding (84). Another cutting-edge strategy involves targeted protein degradation with PROTACs. This technology goes beyond simple inhibition by leveraging the cell’s natural degradation machinery to physically remove the target protein. For example, a recently developed PROTAC, AH078, selectively degrades CK1δ/ε, demonstrating immense potential for this approach (85).

Beyond drug design, innovative therapeutic strategies are crucial (86, 87). Localized drug delivery systems are being explored to maximize therapeutic effects while reducing systemic toxicity. In liver cancer, hepatic arterial infusion can deliver high concentrations of a drug directly to the tumor, whereas in brain tumors, systems that bypass the BBB are used for targeted accumulation. Furthermore, combination therapy holds significant promises for enhancing efficacy and overcoming drug resistance. Notably, the role of CK1δ in overcoming resistance to SMO inhibitors underscores the importance of targeting downstream effectors to circumvent established resistance mechanisms (82). In AML, combining CK1α and CDK7/9 inhibitors has shown promising results (28, 88). In chronic lymphocyte leukemia, a CK1 inhibitor combined with ibrutinib produced synergistic effects (46, 89).

Finally, the emerging role of CSNK1G1-derived circular RNAs (circRNAs) in cancer progression underscores the necessity of evaluating RNA-based therapeutic strategies, such as antisense oligonucleotides, that specifically target these circRNAs (26). These approaches could represent a novel and promising frontier in CK1-related cancer therapy (90).

Ultimately a precision medicine approach is essential for successful clinical implementation (21). Given that CK1 isoforms can act as either oncogenes or tumor suppressors depending on the cancer type, accurate patient stratification is crucial (39). This requires identifying and validating predictive biomarkers (28, 89, 91). For instance, a consistent association between CK1δ gene amplification and dependency in EOC suggests its potential utility as a predictive biomarker for patient selection (7, 17, 28, 89, 91).

In summary, the future of CK1-targeted therapies hinges on a holistic approach that integrates advanced drug design with strategic therapeutic interventions and precise patient selection, rather than relying on a single drug.

CONCLUSION

The intricate regulatory mechanisms governing CK1 activity, including PTMs, subcellular localization, and protein–protein interactions, underscore the complexity of their biological functions and highlight their unifying role in controlling protein stability and turnover. These regulatory mechanisms not only provide multiple avenues for therapeutic intervention but also establish CK1 as a mechanistic hub that links diverse pathological processes. While CK1-targeted therapies hold immense promise, challenges such as achieving isoform-specific selectivity, overcoming drug resistance, ensuring patient stratification through robust biomarkers, and mitigating toxicity to normal cells, must be addressed.

Continued advancements in structural biology, high-throughput screening, and the development of sophisticated preclinical models will be instrumental to designing highly selective and potent CK1 modulators. Furthermore, comprehensive translational research focusing on patient-specific vulnerabilities and combination strategies will pave the way for bringing these innovative therapies from bench to bedside. As a pleiotropic regulator at the nexus of diverse pathological conditions, the CK1 family stands as a compelling frontier for future biomedical research and therapeutic development, offering a mechanistic basis and therapeutic opportunities for intractable human diseases.
