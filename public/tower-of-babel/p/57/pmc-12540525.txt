A novel three-block method for laparoscopic partial splenectomy: improved spleen preservation and reduced complications
Authors: Xingru Wang, Zexiao Liu, Li Cao, Linxia Nie, Yong Cao, Shuguo Zheng, Feng Tian, Jianwei Li
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
PurposeHistorically, laparoscopic partial splenectomy has been associated with high rates of conversion to open surgery due to technical challenges. This emphasizes the need for surgical options that allow benign splenic lesion removal with splenic function preservation. Previous approaches primarily blocked the splenic artery, with inadequate intraoperative bleeding control. We aimed to evaluate postoperative clinical recovery and prognostic outcomes of patients who underwent modified three-block laparoscopic partial splenectomy.MethodsThis retrospective study assessed 18 patients with benign splenic lesions who underwent modified three-block laparoscopic partial splenectomy between April 2019 and February 2024. Patient eligibility was determined using predefined criteria; those with malignant lesions or severe comorbidities were excluded. The same laparoscopic hepatobiliary specialist performed all procedures using both splenic artery and pedicle occlusion techniques to minimize intraoperative blood loss.ResultsThe cohort included 7 men and 11 women, with no conversions to open surgery. The mean operation time was 167.33 ± 59.08 min. The intraoperative blood loss was 50.00 [50.00, 100.00] mL, with no blood transfusion. All patients ambulated within 24 h post-surgery, with a mean hospital stay of 6.5 ± 1.76 days. Only one patient experienced transient pleural effusion classified as a Child-Pugh grade I complication. Follow-up evaluations demonstrated successful tissue preservation, with no overwhelming postsplenectomy infection.ConclusionsModified three-block laparoscopic partial splenectomy is a safe and effective approach for managing benign splenic lesions with preservation of splenic function. This technique not only reduces intraoperative bleeding but also facilitates rapid postoperative recovery, thereby mitigating the risks associated with total splenectomy.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1007/s00423-025-03868-5.

Introduction

The spleen is an important peripheral immune organ that regulates circulating blood volume, participates in blood filtration, produces various immunoglobulins, and modulates the endocrine system. Historically, limited understanding of the splenic vascular system resulted in the frequent use of complete splenectomy for benign splenic lesions, leading to post-surgery complications such as elevated platelet counts, splenic vein thrombosis, thrombosis at other sites, and potentially life-threatening overwhelming postsplenectomy infection (OPSI) [1]. OPSI is the most severe complication following total splenectomy, with an incidence rate of 4.4% and mortality rates as high as 50–80% [2]. Current data indicate a significant reduction in the incidence of OPSI after partial splenectomy. Retaining part of the spleen not only reduces the risk of postoperative infections but also eliminates the need for vaccinations to boost immunity [3]. Consequently, surgeons have been searching for surgical methods that can safely excise pathological lesions while preserving the immune function of the spleen and preventing OPSI.

With the advancement of minimally invasive laparoscopic techniques, Uranus successfully performed the first laparoscopic partial splenectomy [4]. In 1995, Poulin et al. reported that the incidence of complications and length of hospital stay after laparoscopic splenectomy were both lower than those following total splenectomy [5]. With advancements in laparoscopic techniques, the clinical application of laparoscopic partial splenectomy has gradually increased. However, challenges remain, including the technical difficulties associated with splenic dissection and bleeding control during laparoscopic splenectomy, leading to a high rate of conversion to open surgery. Given these issues, many surgeons hesitate to adopt this surgical approach.

On the basis of our experience in laparoscopic liver resection and the anatomical features of the spleen, a novel modified three-block technique for laparoscopic partial splenectomy was proposed and successfully applied to clinical practice with favorable outcomes. The aim of this retrospective study was to analyze postoperative clinical recovery and prognostic outcomes of patients who underwent modified three-block laparoscopic partial splenectomy and provide detailed descriptions of the technique. This manuscript is in accordance with the STROBE checklist [6].

Materials and methods

Demographic data

In this study, we retrospectively analyzed clinical data from a continuous cohort of patients diagnosed with intraparenchymal lesions of the spleen (ILPS; benign splenic lesions), with pathological exclusion of malignant lesions, at a tertiary referral hospital between April 2019 and February 2024. According to the established inclusion and exclusion criteria, 18 patients were included, all of whom underwent surgery performed by the same laparoscopic hepatobiliary specialist. The inclusion criteria were as follows: (1) benign lesions of the spleen, located at the margin or one end of the spleen; (2) symptoms of compression or rupture due to splenic cysts; (3) splenic cysts with a diameter > 5.0 cm; and (4) exclusion of malignant tumors by preoperative evaluations. Patients with (1) severe underlying conditions such as cardiac, pulmonary, or cerebrovascular diseases who could not tolerate laparoscopic surgery; (2) a history of major upper abdominal surgeries; (3) malignant tumors of the spleen; (4) lesions located centrally or adjacent to the splenic hilum; (5) splenic metastases; (6) liver cirrhosis with splenomegaly and hypersplenism; or (7) confirmed blood system disease were excluded (Fig. 1). All patients underwent cardiopulmonary function assessments and blood biochemistry tests to determine their ability to tolerate laparoscopic surgery and general anesthesia. Each patient underwent abdominal enhanced computed tomography (CT) and abdominal angiography. Postoperative data, including blood biochemistry results and drainage records, were documented. One month after surgery, blood indices and abdominal ultrasound results were reviewed in the outpatient department. Regular follow-up was conducted 6 months postoperatively through telephone consultations and outpatient reviews every 3 months.

Surgical procedure

Splenic artery occlusion

The main trunk of the splenic artery (SA) was dissected at the upper margin of the pancreas and suspended. During the transection of the splenic parenchyma, the SA was temporarily occluded with a laparoscopic bulldog clip to control the blood supply to the spleen. Notably, because the short blood vessels in the stomach and the vessels in the splenic ligaments provide collateral blood supply to the spleen, blocking the main SA cannot completely obstruct the blood supply and can only reduce bleeding to a certain extent.

Splenic pedicle occlusion

In most cases, the tertiary splenic pedicle is separated before entry into the spleen parenchyma, providing a safe operative space at the surface of the parenchyma. Before operation, the classification of splenic blood vessels should be clearly defined, and the second and third splenic pedicles should be treated separately. With the advantage of the laparoscopic amplification effect, branches of the splenic pedicle or blood vessels that need to be excised on the surface of the spleen can be precisely dissociated. These vessels can be divided into bundles and ligated on the side near the spleen while avoiding damage to the pancreatic tail tissue to prevent pancreatic fistula.

Usually, the ischemic area becomes visible on the spleen surface within 2 to 3 min after the splenic pedicle is severed. After a certain observation period, blood supply recovery may be observed in some ischemic cases, resulting in deviation of the ischemic line. This is due to the complex connectivity of blood flow channels in the spleen parenchyma. Therefore, it is necessary to allow a sufficient observation duration after ligation of the splenic pedicle and mark the resection range after the ischemic area of the spleen is relatively stable.

Splenic parenchyma occlusion

Based on the texture and size of the spleen, a 16–18G T-tube was reasonably selected and appropriately trimmed, with the length adjusted to exclude the T-arm. One side of the tube was drilled using an ultrasonic scalpel, and the other end was cut into a triangular diagonal. After circumvention of the spleen on the preresected side, the spleen parenchyma was wrapped in a circular motion, approximately 0.5–1 cm from the ischemic line. Once the bevel end of the T-tube passed through the hole on the other side, its elasticity was used to implement the block. After the block was confirmed to be satisfactory, a hemo-lock clamp was applied to prevent the tube from slipping.

Tips

Proper dissociation is beneficial for fine treatment of the splenic pedicle and corresponding adjustment of the splenic parenchymal disjunction plane. When the spleen is mobilized, the peripheral blood supply of the retained portion should be protected from any damage. During excision of the upper pole of the spleen, protection of the splenocolic ligament and the surrounding tissue of the lower pole of the spleen is crucial [7, 8]. However, for some lesions in special locations, such as the posterior upper extremity of the spleen, complete dissociation of the perisplenic ligaments may be necessary to facilitate intraoperative manipulation. This enables substantial overturn of the spleen, providing better exposure to the operative field. At this stage, only the splenic ligaments can be preserved as much as possible to maintain maximum blood supply to the residual spleen tissue. When excising the lower pole of the spleen, surgeons should pay attention to protection of the short blood vessels in the upper part of the splenic and gastric ligaments. If these anatomical structures are accidentally damaged during the operation, the blood supply to the reserved spleen may be compromised, resulting in a reduction in the volume of the reserved spleen and even failure of splenic preservation.

The routine use of an ultrasonic scalpel for splenic parenchymatous dissection is recommended, as it plays a better role in parenchymatous dissection and vascular coagulation of the spleen under a stimulated state. Vessel structures with a larger diameter usually appear near the splenic portal, and those with a diameter > 3 mm should be clamped with tissue clips or titanium clips to avoid postoperative bleeding. Bipolar electrocoagulation can achieve a satisfactory hemostatic effect on a severed wound.

Postoperatively, we administered low molecular-weight heparin sodium at a minimum anticoagulant dose of 2500 units once daily. Our retrospective data demonstrate that low-dose anticoagulation does not increase the risk of postoperative bleeding, thereby achieving a balance between thromboembolic prevention and hemorrhagic safety.

The key to a successful laparoscopic partial splenectomy lies in the fine separation of secondary splenic pedicle vessels during the operation. Meanwhile, the perisplenic band should be avoided during the operation and the blood supply to the residual spleen should be monitored to prevent ischemic infarction of the residual spleen after the operation. In particular, detaching the short gastric vessel before treating the upper pole of the spleen is necessary. When dealing with inferior polar masses of the spleen, attention should be paid to the blood supply from the left gastroepiploic artery and the medial branch of the splenic ligament to prevent injury [9]. In some patients, the SA and its branches are tortuous at the splenic portal [10]. When the splenic lobe vessels corresponding to the lesion site are ligated but no ischemic changes are observed in the corresponding splenic tissue, it means that the vascular blood supply has persisted; thus, dissection should be continued and other vessels should be identified necessary. To minimize intraoperative wound bleeding during splenic parenchyma transection, an incision should be made approximately 5–10 mm inward from the ischemic line to the target area. Blocking the splenic vein is not recommended, as it may cause splenic congestion and exacerbate bleeding [11]. The splenic parenchyma is transversed using an ultrasonic scalpel and a small mouth clamp similar to those for liver transection, and full hemostasis at the wound site can be achieved using energy equipment. To better monitor postoperative bleeding, placement of a drainage tube in the splenic fossa is recommended. The drainage tube can be removed 2 days after the operation if no abnormalities are observed.

Statistical analyses

Statistical analysis was performed using R software version 4.3.2. Normally distributed data are presented as mean ± standard deviation, with the independent sample t-test used for comparing two groups and one-way ANOVA used for comparing multiple groups. Non-normally distributed data are presented as median (quartile), with the Mann–Whitney U test used for comparing two groups and the Kruskal–Wallis (H) test used to compare multiple groups. Categorical data are presented as frequency and percentage, and between-group differences were assessed using the Chi-square test or Fisher’s exact test as appropriate. Significance was determined by a two-tailed test with a P-value of < 0.05.

Results

Laparoscopic partial splenectomy was successfully completed in all 18 patients (7 men and 11 women), without conversion to laparotomy. Postoperative pathological findings of the patients are presented in Table 1. In the upper pole group, seven (63.64%) cases involved cysts. Spleen lesions were found at the upper pole in 11 cases and lower pole in seven cases. The mean operation time was 167.33 ± 59.08 min, with intraoperative blood loss of 50.00 [50.00, 100.00] mL. No intraoperative or postoperative blood transfusion was required. All patients were able to ambulate on the first day following surgery, with a mean postoperative hospital stay of 6.5 ± 1.76 days. After the operation, a small amount of left pleural effusion, which was spontaneously absorbed, was observed in one patient. No complications, such as abdominal infection, pancreatic fistula, and bleeding, occurred in other cases. Portal vein thrombosis was not observed postoperatively. The blocking times for the SA and splenic parenchyma were 25.00 [25.00, 31.50] and 15.00 [10.00, 20.00] min, respectively. Three patients received anticoagulation therapy with enoxaparin sodium during hospitalization, followed by oral aspirin after discharge (Option 1), while 15 patients were treated with enoxaparin sodium alone during hospitalization (Option 2). The maximum diameter of the lesion and the amount of intraoperative blood loss were higher in the upper pole group than in the lower pole group. Discussion

In this study, the elastic hilar occlusion method, commonly used in liver surgery, was adapted for splenic parenchyma occlusion by combining it with the previously standard SA and corresponding splenic pedicle vessel occlusion for better intraoperative bleeding control. To our knowledge, this method has been reported for the first time for benign splenic lesions, and it demonstrated better clinical results than previously reported techniques.

Teperman discovered that 3 h of warm ischemia in the spleen led to necrosis in dogs and recommended a safe occlusion time of 1 h for the SA trunk in humans to accommodate most splenic surgeries [12]. In this study, the longest blocking time was 45 min, with a median of 25 min. While temporary blockade of the SA trunk in our study did not cause splenic infarction, there were concerns about potential vascular damage due to arterial occlusion. Patients were followed up using upper abdominal CT for up to a month after surgery, and no pseudoaneurysms, strictures, atresia, or distal embolic events were observed. On the basis of these findings, we cautiously recommend SA trunk occlusion for a period shorter than 1 h to ensure safety. Further research is needed to determine the optimal duration to complete splenectomy without damaging the vasculature and splenic function.

Laparoscopic triple-block partial splenectomy was selected for benign splenic lesions at our center, resulting in the lack of control cases. Because of the low incidence of benign splenic lesions, the number of cases in this study was small. However, this study introduces a new surgical approach that can maximize bleeding control, facilitate the application of partial splenectomy, and preserve more normal splenic tissue for more patients.

To our knowledge, the described method has been reported for the first time, and it demonstrated better clinical outcomes than did methods described in previously published studies [13–15], especially in terms of lower rates of intraoperative conversion to laparotomy, intraoperative bleeding, and postoperative complications.

The indications for laparoscopic partial splenectomy mainly include benign splenic lesions, such as splenic cysts, splenic vasculoma, splenic abscess, splenic hemangioma, partial splenic trauma, and certain hematological diseases. Partial splenectomy is not suitable for malignant tumors of the spleen. Severe splenomegaly restricts the space for laparoscopic surgery, making smooth operation difficult. Therefore, laparoscopic surgery should be carefully selected under this condition. Bader-Meunier et al. showed that retention of 25% of the spleen with sufficient perfusion was required for maintaining splenic function [16]. Studies have shown that preservation of 25–30% of the splenic parenchyma allows an appropriate immune response to antigen stimulation [17, 18]. Therefore, we recommend that partial splenectomy should preserve at least 25% of the splenic volume with sufficient blood supply. Meanwhile, the principle “life-saving first, spleen preservation second” should be adhered to, and this operation is not recommended for patients with unstable perioperative vital signs. Selective laparoscopic splenectomy is considered safe for splenic trauma. However, in cases of hemodynamic instability or rapid bleeding (clinically estimated at > 500 mL/h), splenectomy or open surgery should be directly selected [19]. Although partial splenectomy seems to achieve a balance between spleen preserving and lesion excision, allowing removal of the lesion while effectively preserving splenic function, it is essential to avoid expanding the indications for partial splenectomy in pursuit of spleen preservation. Ensuring the safety and effectiveness of this procedure is a priority.

Unlike liver resection, splenectomy does not require strict calculation of the residual splenic volume. The spleen exhibits a consistent segmental anatomy and is typically divided into 2–3 major segments. In this study, upper or lower pole resection was performed. Based on anatomical principles, the residual splenic volume after such resections is inherently sufficient to maintain physiological function, obviating the need for preoperative volume assessment.

Preoperative three-dimensional visualization imaging enables surgeons to clearly predict the distribution of splenic lesions and peripheral blood vessels, facilitating the planning of the splenectomy plane. Intraoperative ultrasound provides real-time evaluation of the resected plane, ensuring maximum preservation of the residual spleen after complete removal of the lesion. Because of the fixed segmental architecture of the spleen and the demonstrated adequacy of residual volume following upper/lower pole resection, routine assessment of ischemic areas using indocyanine green (ICG) is unnecessary. Unlike negative staining with ICG in the resected liver [20, 21], intraoperative ICG injection initially causes fluorescent staining of the perisplenic and splenic areas with normal blood supply. Subsequently, the fluorescent stain gradually migrates from the area with normal blood supply to the ischemic area over time, resulting in delayed staining in the ischemic area and persistence of the stain for a relatively longer duration. This may be attributed to the rich blood supply of the spleen and the low uptake of ICG, leading to greater retention of ICG in the ischemic area. Further research is needed to optimize the application of ICG staining to guide partial splenectomy.

Conclusions

Triple-block laparoscopic partial splenectomy for benign lesions occupying the splenic space in the upper and lower poles of the spleen, non-central regions, or areas far from the splenic hilum demonstrates positive prognostic outcomes. This approach not only preserves splenic function but also minimizes intraoperative bleeding, promotes rapid postoperative recovery, reduces surgical trauma, and ensures safety and effectiveness. A larger, possibly multicenter, prospective study is required to validate the benefits of this approach.
