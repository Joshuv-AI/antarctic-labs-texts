Animal digital twins: systems architecture for climate-smart protein production
Authors: Suresh Raja Neethirajan
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractAnimal digital twins couple sensor observations and farm records with process-based, data-driven or hybrid models to maintain continuously updated representations of animal state and management outcomes. Using ruminant methane as the principal illustration, this Perspective presents a nested architecture spanning animal, farm, supply chain and governance layers, and proposes six actions for climate-smart deployment. Credibility depends on ground-truth calibration, uncertainty-aware verification, farmer control and designs matched to local infrastructure.Subject terms: Climate sciences, Ecology, Ecology, Environmental sciences, Environmental social sciences

The livestock–climate protein paradox

The global food system must satisfy rising demand for nutrient-dense food while approaching net-zero greenhouse-gas emissions. Livestock occupies most agricultural land and supplies a substantial share of dietary protein, but its climate footprint is uneven across species, regions and production systems. Ruminants account for much of livestock methane through enteric fermentation. In some dairy and beef systems, methane represents 60–80% of farm-level greenhouse-gas intensity, although the share varies with energy use, production intensity, land-use history and accounting boundaries1–6. Global averages are therefore useful for setting direction, but insufficient for judging individual farms or regions.

The central question is not whether livestock should remain unchanged, but how production can become more measurable, adaptive and accountable. Better forage, grazing management, feed additives and manure treatment can reduce emissions, yet effects are context dependent, and adoption is constrained by cost, knowledge and infrastructure7–11. Meanwhile, animal-source food demand is rising most rapidly in parts of South Asia and Sub-Saharan Africa12,13. The architecture proposed here is applicable across livestock sectors, but ruminants and enteric methane provide the principal illustrations because the mitigation challenge and mechanistic evidence base are comparatively mature. Species-specific variants can extend the same principles to poultry, swine and mixed systems.

Animal digital twins couple sensor observations and farm records with mechanistic, data-driven or hybrid models to maintain an evolving representation of an animal, group or farm14–18. Their advantage is not sensing alone, but the disciplined assimilation of observations into models that can compare management options, expose trade-offs and report uncertainty. A mechanistic twin is most useful where causal biology is sufficiently understood and interpretability matters, such as nutrition or thermal stress. Data-driven twins may suit high-volume detection tasks, including behaviour or anomaly recognition, while hybrid designs can combine biological constraints with flexible learning. Individual-animal twins may fit intensive dairy or breeding applications; flock, herd or batch-level twins may be more appropriate where individual identification is impractical. The architecture should follow the decision, not the availability of fashionable technology. The design problem is therefore epistemic as much as computational. More data do not necessarily improve a twin if measurements are biased, weakly linked to the target process or collected at the wrong scale. Selective parsimony may be more valuable than exhaustive sensing: each stream should justify its cost by reducing decision uncertainty or revealing a previously unmanageable risk.

This distinction matters because a digital twin is more than a dashboard and less than an omniscient replica. It does not eliminate uncertainty or create missing biological knowledge. Its value lies in making assumptions explicit, updating estimates as evidence arrives and testing counterfactual decisions before they are imposed on animals or farms. Existing management software rarely combines high-frequency sensing, causal modelling, uncertainty estimates and scenario analysis across animal, group and farm scales15,17,19.

At the system level, twins could connect farm decisions with supply-chain commitments and climate reporting. Food-system emissions alone could erode the feasibility of ambitious climate pathways without changes in both production and consumption20–22. Yet livestock mitigation remains one component of wider decarbonisation across energy, industry and land use. Digital twins are not substitutes for dietary change, subsidy reform or broader climate policy. Their narrower, but potentially consequential, role is to improve decisions within livestock systems and make the evidence behind those decisions inspectable.

Digital twins as systems architecture for livestock

A livestock digital twin integrates three nested knowledge domains: representations of the animal life cycle, sensor-derived observations and farm management records. The life-cycle domain includes growth, reproduction, production, health and disease; sensors describe behaviour, intake, physiology and local conditions; records add genetic, treatment, economic and managerial context. These layers feed farm-level indicators and, where appropriate, supply-chain and governance functions. Hyper-local weather, pathogen pressure and other disturbances modulate all three domains and should enter the twin as uncertainty drivers rather than unqualified inputs. The nested structure is therefore informational as well as organisational: each outer layer depends on the integrity of the layers within it.

High-frequency data cannot be passed directly into biological models without mediation. An interface layer filters and aggregates second-to-minute streams into features aligned with hourly or daily model steps, such as feeding bouts, intake proxies and heat-load indices. Time synchronisation, calibration metadata, quality flags and missingness indicators are essential because apparently precise streams can still be biologically misleading. State-estimation procedures can bridge short gaps, but imputation should not conceal persistent sensor failure. The twin should report when evidence is insufficient, not manufacture certainty.

Uncertainty has several sources and should not be collapsed into a single confidence score. Measurement uncertainty arises from sensors, structural uncertainty from incomplete biology, and contextual uncertainty from weather, pathogens or management change. Their relative contribution matters because each demands a different remedy: recalibration, model revision or cautious transfer to a new setting.

Because errors propagate, performance is constrained by sensor reliability, model adequacy and data-pipeline integrity. In vivo experiments and targeted on-farm measurements remain indispensable for calibration, independent evaluation and surveillance of sensor or model drift. Evaluation must also extend across breeds, diets, climates and management systems, since a model that performs well in one domain may fail silently in another. Outputs should not support verification when minimum data-quality or calibration requirements are unmet.

Unlike a standalone stochastic simulation, a digital twin maintains a persistent state estimate for a specific animal or group and updates it as evidence arrives; stochastic methods may still represent biological and measurement uncertainty. This persistent link allows farmers and advisers to compare milk yield, methane intensity, longevity, welfare and margin over feed cost within the same decision space23,24. The architecture may extend beyond the farm gate, but only where provenance, version history and uncertainty remain traceable.

Technical feasibility is advancing. Sensor networks, modular software, edge computing and cloud services have been combined in dairy and poultry prototypes, while digital phenotyping uses computer vision and acoustics to characterise flock or herd behaviour14,17,25–27. Edge and fog resources can perform time synchronisation, filtering, feature extraction and lightweight inference locally, reducing bandwidth demand and improving resilience under intermittent connectivity. They also allow sensitive raw data to remain on the farm while selected summaries are shared, although local computation does not by itself solve governance or cybersecurity risks.

Evidence for complete end-to-end twins remains more limited than evidence for individual components. Video methods can classify grazing, ruminating, resting and social behaviour under research conditions and may inform models of intake or welfare, but commercial integration with methane prediction is not yet established28,29. Satellite imagery can complement proximal sensors by estimating pasture biomass and forage quality in extensive systems30. Classification error, sensor bias and environmental domain shift must propagate into downstream uncertainty and be checked against ground truth before outputs are used for benchmarking or verification. A twin becomes credible not when every data stream is included, but when each inclusion measurably improves a defined decision.

Six coordinated actions for climate-smart transformation

Meaningful deployment requires coordinated changes in infrastructure, modelling, governance and incentives.

Action 1: Establish shared data standards and interoperable platforms

Digital twins will falter if data remains trapped in proprietary systems. Open standards should define not only field names and interfaces, but also units, calibration metadata, provenance, quality flags and missingness. Without these descriptors, interoperable data may still be incomparable. Publicly funded projects should share code, model documentation and anonymised calibration data where ethically and legally possible. Reference datasets should represent diverse breeds, production systems and regions rather than reproduce the conditions of a few well-instrumented farms31–35.

Existing initiatives offer a practical foundation. The ICAR Animal Data Exchange Working Group develops standards for exchange between recording devices, databases and farm management systems, while its open ADE repository provides machine-readable specifications36,37. Embedding such standards within twin platforms would reduce vendor lock-in, support independent evaluation and permit farmer-controlled integration of sensor, management and advisory data. Open interfaces should also support model substitution, so that producers are not bound indefinitely to one vendor’s algorithm.

Interoperability is therefore not a technical convenience; it is a condition for contestability, accountability and long-term value. A shared syntax, however, is not enough. Semantic alignment, version control and documented transformations are needed so that a change in software does not silently alter the meaning of a welfare score, methane estimate or financial benchmark.

Action 2: Couple precision feeding with credible methane estimation

Enteric methane depends strongly on diet and intake, yet animals on the same ration can differ because of rumen function, genetics, health and behaviour8,38,39. Digital twins could combine feed composition, intake patterns, body weight and production data to compare ration changes, anticipate nutritional imbalance and identify rising methane intensity before losses accumulate. Their strongest near-term role is decision support, not autonomous control: recommendations should remain interpretable and reversible.

Predictions are only defensible within evaluated domains. Comparative studies show that methane-model performance varies across regions and depends heavily on accurate dry-matter intake15,39. Continuous individual-animal methane sensing also remains costly and unevenly available. Near-term twins will therefore combine periodic direct measurements with diet, intake and performance proxies. Weak links include sensor durability, ration-data quality, model transferability and the labour required to verify recommendations. Uncertainty should widen when a herd, diet or climate departs from the model’s calibration domain.

Dietary interventions can reduce enteric methane, sometimes without compromising productivity, but responses are system-specific and adoption remains limited by cost and usability8,10,40–42. Twins may lower some barriers by tailoring recommendations, identifying non-responders and comparing predicted with observed responses. They cannot compensate for unavailable feed ingredients, weak advisory services or incentives that reward output without regard to emissions.

Priorities are affordable sensing, open methane models evaluated across production systems, links to feed and advisory networks, and transparent estimates of farm-level costs, benefits and uncertainty. Where outputs affect carbon credits or regulation, periodic empirical measurement should remain mandatory.

Action 3: Close manure-to-nutrient loops

Manure is both an emissions source and a nutrient asset. Digital twins can couple nutrient-excretion models with crop, soil, storage and weather models to estimate nitrogen, phosphorus and potassium flows, identify loss risks and match application to crop demand9,43. Because manure composition changes with diet, water intake and production stage, dynamic profiling can improve application timing and quantify avoided synthetic fertiliser use.

When linked to anaerobic digestion, twins may also optimise feedstock mixtures, energy yield and digestate quality. Circularity also requires spatial context. Nutrients may be valuable within a crop-livestock region, yet become pollutants where animal density exceeds surrounding land demand. Twins should therefore represent transport distance, receiving-soil capacity and seasonal constraints, rather than treating manure recovery as an intrinsically circular outcome. Yet optimisation at one point can displace burdens elsewhere. Maximising biogas output, for example, is not necessarily equivalent to minimising whole-system emissions or nutrient loss. Priorities are purpose-specific model evaluation, field pilots and incentives tied to measured environmental outcomes rather than assumed technology performance32,44.

Action 4: Make governance part of the architecture

A recommendation to alter a ration, move animals or flag treatment is not merely a model output; it reallocates labour, risk and liability. Farmers, therefore, need intelligible reasons, confidence estimates and the ability to contest decisions. Primary data should remain under producer control and should not be repurposed for model training or sale without informed consent31,45. Data sovereignty must also include practical portability, because nominal ownership has little value when information cannot be retrieved in a usable form.

Governance must extend to high-value derived assets, including predictions, optimised scenarios and model updates. Contracts should specify access, benefit sharing, retention, audit rights and liability when advice causes economic or environmental harm. Algorithmic audits should examine not only average accuracy, but also failure across farm sizes, breeds, regions and data-rich versus data-poor settings. Model updates should be documented because an apparently identical service may change materially over time46.

The EU AI Act offers one regulatory reference, not a universal agricultural template. Where systems meet high-risk criteria, requirements include risk management, data governance, transparency, human oversight and post-market monitoring47. Comparable safeguards should be adapted to national law, production scale and institutional capacity. The governing principle is straightforward: digitalisation should expand farmer agency, not convert farms into involuntary data suppliers or subjects of opaque surveillance.

Action 5: Build verification that can withstand incentives

Farm-specific accounting could improve on default emission factors and reveal genuine management effects. Predicted methane, feed conversion, manure handling and energy use can contribute to an auditable profile, while privacy-preserving aggregation can support product footprints and supply-chain reporting13,23,38. Traceability may create premiums or more stable contracts, but a modelled estimate is not automatically a verified fact.

When premiums, credits or compliance depend on twin outputs, sensor drift, field-calibration error and uncertainty in proxy signals must be disclosed. Model and parameter versions must remain auditable, and protocols should distinguish quantities requiring empirical measurement from those that can reasonably be modelled. Independent checks are needed to deter selective reporting, strategic sensor placement or manipulation of inputs. Verification rules should be stable enough for investment yet responsive to improved evidence. Auditability must also survive software evolution. A historical emissions claim should be reproducible using the model, parameters and data available when it was issued, even after the platform has been updated. Versioned records are therefore part of measurement integrity, not merely software administration.

Financial benchmarking also creates distributional risk. Sparse, unverified or systematically biased inputs may misclassify farms that operate with limited sensors or service support. Eligibility and pricing should therefore require minimum data-quality criteria, uncertainty disclosure, bias and distributional-impact audits, human review and accessible appeal. Outputs derived from inadequate evidence should not determine access to credit.

The central test is not whether a platform can produce a number, but whether that number remains credible under financial pressure. Verification must establish reproducibility, traceability and fairness, while preserving enough uncertainty to prevent false precision.

Action 6: Coordinate research without centralising control

Individual farmers cannot absorb all capital and learning costs, vendors have weak incentives to build shared standards, and governments need credible routes from farm data to inventories. International coordination could align data standards, sensor evaluation, welfare metrics, emissions accounting and capacity building while preserving regional autonomy8,9,32,34,44.

A lean coordination mechanism could maintain open reference datasets and model repositories, support pilots across representative agroecological zones and convene scientific and farmer advisory groups. It could also define minimum reporting requirements for uncertainty, calibration and model updates, making evidence comparable without prescribing a single software architecture. Regional networks should adapt common standards to local production systems rather than import assumptions from intensive, well-connected farms.

The aim is coordination without technological monoculture: shared rules for interoperability and evidence, combined with plural models of implementation. Success should be judged by reusable infrastructure, independent evaluation and local capability, not by the number of farms enroled in a single platform.

Barriers and enabling conditions

Technology is only one barrier. In South Asia, Sub-Saharan Africa and other settings with unstable power, limited bandwidth or sparse maintenance services, early deployment may require herd-level rather than individual twins, asynchronous mobile uploads, local processing and modular sensors that can be repaired within existing support networks. These are not inferior versions of a universal design; they are context-appropriate architectures. Fully real-time individual twins should be treated as one deployment model, not the default.

Institutional capacity is equally important. Extension services, veterinary networks and advisers need skills in data quality, uncertainty and model interpretation. Farmers need support to act on recommendations, not merely dashboards that transfer interpretive labour to the user. Capacity building should therefore accompany deployment budgets rather than appear as an afterthought.

Market design must align private and public value. Finance, procurement or incentives should reward verified outcomes rather than sensor ownership alone. Strong farmer organisations are needed to negotiate data rights and commercial terms, while public investment may be justified where climate or welfare benefits exceed private returns. Poorly designed subsidies could instead lock farms into proprietary systems or reward measurement without mitigation.

Operational trust depends less on headline accuracy than on failure burden. Health, oestrus and welfare systems should report positive predictive value and specificity alongside sensitivity, because repeated false positives impose verification labour and rapidly erode confidence. Digital twins complement, rather than replace, veterinary judgement and empirical research. Continued ground-truth collection is necessary to recalibrate models, detect drift and define acceptable error bounds.

Pathways to 2050 and conclusions

Deployment should proceed in three conditional phases. A foundation phase should establish open standards, reference datasets, governance rules and multi-region pilots that measure emissions, welfare and economic outcomes. Pilots should test failure modes and distributional effects, not merely technical feasibility. A scaling phase could integrate evaluated twin outputs into advisory services, inventories and climate finance as sensor costs fall and local capability grows. A transformation phase would make product-level traceability and adaptive management more routine, particularly in high-emitting systems32,34,43. Progress should be assessed through decision-relevant outcomes: avoided emissions with uncertainty bounds, welfare effects, net farm value, verification burden and distribution of benefits. Adoption counts alone can conceal systems that are widely installed but rarely trusted, maintained or used.

The mitigation potential shown in Fig. 1 is a scenario, not a forecast. It assumes broad adoption, reliable sensing, transferable models, interoperability, cost reductions and aligned incentives. Under those conditions, combinations of methane mitigation, nutrient recovery and productivity gains could reduce livestock emissions substantially relative to baseline trajectories8,10,38,40–42. Digital twins do not create these reductions by themselves; they make interventions more targeted, measurable and reversible. Failure of the enabling conditions would narrow the gains and could concentrate benefits among already well-resourced producers (Box 1).

Nested systems architecture of animal digital twins for climate-smart protein production.

Concentric rings show how an individual-animal twin is embedded within sensor and local environmental layers, farm decision systems, supply chains and governance. At the core, high-frequency sensor streams, farm records, hyper-local weather and pathogen-related context are assimilated into mechanistic and AI models of the animal life cycle to estimate productivity, welfare and emissions. The farm layer supports monitoring, health and welfare alerts, precision feeding, circular resource use and herd or flock optimisation. The supply-chain layer links calibrated and uncertainty-aware outputs to traceability, carbon accounting, market incentives, verification and auditing. The governance layer connects digital-twin data to climate inventories, reporting frameworks, standards, model evaluation and international coordination. Cross-cutting bands indicate data sovereignty, algorithmic transparency and equity. The outer rim presents a conditional pathway from foundation to scaling and transformation by 2050, with mitigation potential dependent on adoption, interoperability, cost reductions, ground-truth calibration and aligned incentives. Governance frameworks are illustrative and require adaptation to production scale and national context. Under these conditions, wide deployment could contribute to 20–35% lower emissions intensity and approximately 400 to 600 megatonnes CO₂-equivalent annual mitigation relative to baseline trajectories8,10,38,40–42.

The technical foundations are emerging, but the decisive questions are institutional. Can uncertainty remain visible when money is attached to predictions? Can farmers retain rights over both data and derived value? Can systems designed for well-connected farms be reconfigured for settings with intermittent power and limited service capacity? Animal digital twins will become trusted infrastructure only if these questions are treated as design requirements rather than downstream ethics.

Digital twin

A continuously updated, animal- or group-specific model that couples mechanistic, data-driven or hybrid representations with sensor observations and farm records to estimate biological states, quantify uncertainty and compare management scenarios.

Climate-smart protein systems

Livestock systems that seek context-specific reductions in emissions intensity while supporting animal welfare, producer resilience and productivity, recognising that trade-offs are unavoidable and outcomes depend on local biological, economic and infrastructural conditions.

Data sovereignty

Farmer ownership, consent rights, and control over data generated by digital twins, including transparent benefit-sharing mechanisms when data informs commercial product development, research, or policy decisions.

Mechanistic model

A mathematical representation of biological processes, such as rumen microbial fermentation, nutrient metabolism, or thermal stress responses, that simulates physiological outcomes under specific environmental, nutritional, and management conditions.

Enteric methane

Methane is produced by microbial fermentation within ruminant digestive systems. Methane yield varies with animal genetics, diet composition, and ruminal microbial community structure, and has a global warming potential far greater than CO₂ over 20-year timeframes.

Resource circularity

Closed loop nutrient and energy flows in which manure nutrients, biogas, and organic matter are recovered, profiled for composition, and reintegrated into production systems or sold as value-added products instead of being treated as waste streams.
