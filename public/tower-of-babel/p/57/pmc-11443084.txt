Telomere length exhibits inverse association with migraine among Americans aged 20–50 years, without implications beyond age 50: a cross-sectional study
Authors: Dandan Geng, Huanxian Liu, Haoyuan Wang, Hebo Wang
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
Migraine, common in individuals under 50 years, is linked to oxidative stress. The association between telomere length shortening and migraine, along with potential age-related influences, has not been comprehensively studied. This cross-sectional study included data from 6169 participants in the National Health and Nutrition Survey (NHANES) from 1999 to 2002, encompassing information on peripheral blood leukocyte telomere length, severe headache or migraine, and potential confounders. Stratifying by age (20–50 years, > 50 years), we employed multivariable logistic regression, restricted cubic splines and interaction test to investigate age-influenced telomere length in relation to migraine. In participants aged 20–50 years, the odds ratio (OR) for migraine in the shortest telomere length group T1 (0.39–0.89) was 1.35 (95% confidence interval [95% CI] 1.01, 1.79) compared to the longest group T3 (1.10–9.42), whereas in those aged > 50 years, the OR of T1 was 0.93 (95% CI 0.60, 1.43). Additionally, telomere length and age interacted in the development of migraine (p for interaction: 0.010). In individuals aged 20–50, an L-shaped relationship was found between telomere length and migraine, with an inflection point at 1.02T/S ratio. The OR was 9.34 (95% CI 1.56, 55.99) for telomere lengths < 1.02T/S ratio. These findings suggest age influences the association between telomere length and migraine in U.S. adults.

Introduction

Migraine is among the most prevalent primary headache disorders, impacting more than one billion individuals globally1. Additionally, migraine presently stands as the second primary cause of disability, claiming the top spot among the youth demographic. It contributes to 4.9% of all one-year disabilities, thus imposing a substantial socioeconomic burden2–5. Although the exact pathogenesis of migraine remains to be fully elucidated, current researches indicate the possible mechanistic correlation between a notable surge in cerebral oxidative stress and migraine attacks. This association involves multiple pathways that could potentially induce DNA damage or impair DNA repair pathways6–8.

Telomeres constitute nucleotide sequences situated at the chromosomal termini, encompassing the repetitive TAAGGC nucleotide sequences. These sequences play a pivotal role in upholding the structural integrity of chromosomes and overall genomic stability9. Huda et al. conducted a twin study that revealed a significant similarity yet discernible variation in telomere lengths among twins10. This observation underscores the potential influence of environmental factors on telomere length variation. Previous studies have shown that oxidative stress can lead to direct damage to telomeric DNA and telomere abrasion11. Notably, the diminishment of telomere length seemingly has been linked to compromised defense mechanisms against oxidative stress12,13. This process creates a vicious cycle that has a detrimental effect on the organism. In addition, the association of telomere length with aging progress and chronic ailments including cardiovascular disease and Alzheimer’s disease has been corroborated by many studies, demonstrating that telomere length is closely related to heredity14–18.

Oxidative stress is generally regarded as playing a pivotal role in the pathogenesis of migraine and is intricately linked to telomere wear. Nonetheless, the exploration of the association between telomere length and migraine remains limited. In addition, migraine as a chronic neurological disorder, has an age-specific onset19. The peak incidence of migraine is from 30 years old to less than 50 years old2,20. Since then, the incidence rate has decreased with age. Aging is accompanied by an escalation in cellular divisions, which ultimately leads to replicative cellular senescence21. Prior research has demonstrated that this phenomenon correlates with the depletion of telomere repeat sequences within DNA during each cell division13,22. The regular assessment of telomere length in peripheral blood leukocytes serves as a usually adopted marker of cellular aging at present21,23. Accordingly, the critical influence of age on telomere length and migraine cannot be ignored. Despite earlier studies detecting a link between telomere length and migraine among Caucasian women in limited samples24, studies in the US population are lacking. Based on these insights, we hypothesized that persons with shorter telomere lengths may exhibit an elevated predisposition to developing migraine in contrast to those possessing longer telomere lengths. Furthermore, given the impact of age on migraine and telomere length, we assumed that this relationship was stronger in those under 50 years of age.

Materials and methods

Study population

This cross-sectional study focused on the adult population aged 20 years and above, drawing from the National Health and Nutrition Examination Survey (NHANES), which covers data on migraine and leukocyte telomere length for the period 1999–2002. NHANES, overseen by the National Center for Health Statistics, serves to evaluate the health and nutritional status of both adults and children in the United States. Since 1999, the survey has been conducted annually, encompassing approximately 5000 individuals across 15 diverse counties nationwide. Employing a complex multistage probability design, the sample is meticulously chosen to ensure the representativeness of the entire U.S. population25. Comprehensive insights into NHANES procedures and data collection methodologies are accessible on the official website (http://www.cdc.gov/nchs/nhanes.htm). Ethical considerations were paramount in the NHANES study, with the research protocol gaining approval from the Institutional Review Board of the National Center for Health Statistics. And we confirmed that all methods were performed in accordance with the relevant guidelines and regulations. Written informed consent was duly obtained from all participating individuals. In addition, NHANES investigation data are publicly accessible on the Internet, catering to researchers worldwide. Our study included all nonpregnant participants who participated in the migraine questionnaire and had leukocyte telomere length measurements. The exclusion criteria encompassed individuals with incomplete covariate data.

Assessment of migraine

We used the NHANES questionnaire on miscellaneous pain to identify people living with migraine disease—“Have you had a severe headache or migraine in the past 3 months?”, which is widely used in current studies to assess migraine26–28. Although the participants in the questionnaire may not solely represent individuals with migraine disease, it is noteworthy that the American Migraine Prevalence and Prevention Study indicated a significant presence of severe headaches. The study revealed that out of the participants, 17.4% reported experiencing severe headaches. Among these, 11.8% and 4.6% fulfilled the criteria for migraine and probable migraine, respectively, as outlined in the Standard International Classification of Headache Disorders (2nd edition)29. Therefore, it is plausible to conclude that a significant majority of individuals who reported experiencing severe headaches in our study experienced the pain and disability of migraine disease.

Measurement of leukocyte telomere length

Genomic DNA was extracted from peripheral blood samples using the QIAamp 96DNA Blood Kit (Qigen, Valencia, CA) and kept at − 80 °C. The extracted and purified DNA underwent analysis at the laboratory of Dr. Elizabeth Blackburn, situated at the University of California, San Francisco, utilizing a quantitative polymerase chain reaction (PCR) methodology. The assessment of relative telomere length involved the computation of the T/S ratio, which signifies the ratio of telomeric repeats (T) to the number of albumins single-copy genes (S). This was achieved by comparing the T/S ratio of experimental samples against that of a standard reference DNA derived from the human diploid fibroblast cell line IMR90, as meticulously detailed elsewhere30,31. Each sample was measured on replicate wells that were blinded to the investigator, and each sample was measured three times over 3 days, excluding 8+ invalid control wells and 4+ runs with control DNA values outside of 2.5 SD (< 6% of runs). The mean T/S ratio was calculated after the greatest and lowest T/S ratio values within each sample were identified as potential outliers and eliminated. Any value was deemed an outlier if the absolute logarithmic ratio between the recalculated mean (excluding potential outliers) and the potential outlier exceeded 0.4. The inter-batch coefficient of variation for leukocyte telomere length was 4%. Specifics regarding telomere length measurements are provided on the official NHANES website as well as in earlier studies32–35.

Covariates

We identified variables that may confound the association between telomere length and migraine based on clinical experience and available literature evidence, including demographic variables, lifestyle variables, physical measures, laboratory indicators, and related diseases. In detail, demographic variables include age, sex, race/ethnicity, educational level, and family income. The study found that chronic migraine risk is higher in individuals who are older, female, and have lower education levels36. Moreover, telomere length is influenced by socioeconomic status and race37,38. Lifestyle variables include smoking status, alcohol use, and physical activity reported associated with migraine or telomere length39,40. The body mass index (BMI, kg/m2) was calculated from the ratio of standard measurements of height and weight. Laboratory indicators are C-reactive protein (CRP, mg/dL) and homocysteine (Hcy, μmol/L). The levels of CRP and homocysteine were found to be associated with an increased risk of migraine41,42. The medical history included diabetes, metabolic syndrome (MetS), and cardiovascular diseases (CVD).

In addition to considering sex, we incorporated the following categorical variables into our analysis: race/ethnicity was classified into distinct groups, specifically Mexican American, non-Hispanic white, non-Hispanic black, and others. Education level was classified as < 9 years, 9–12 years, and > 12 years. Family income was categorized based on the poverty income ratio (PIR) reported by the US government43 as low (PIR ≤ 1.3), medium (PIR > 1.3 to 3.5), and high (PIR > 3.5). Smoking status was identified by individual questionnaires as never, former, and current smokers27. Likewise, participants were stratified into never, former, or current drinkers according to alcohol drinking status44. Physically active was defined as participants performing moderate or vigorous activities for more than 10 min over the past 30 days; otherwise, they were defined as physically inactive. The presence of diabetes was diagnosed based on various criteria, including doctor’s diagnosis, glycated hemoglobin (HbA1c) levels > 6.5%, fasting glucose ≥ 7 mmol/L, random blood glucose ≥ 11.1 mmol/L, 2-h oral glucose tolerance test with blood glucose ≥ 11.1 mmol/L, or use of diabetes medications or insulin. CVD was identified based on the doctor’s diagnosis or meeting specific criteria such as coronary heart disease, congestive heart failure, heart attack, stroke, or angina. Diabetes and CVD were diagnosed as long as they met one of the listed criteria. MetS was defined by the 2005 adult treatment panel III criteria45. Detailed explanations of the process and measurement methods for obtaining covariates can be found on the official NHANES website.

Statistical analysis

According to the analysis guidelines provided in the NHANES data release document, we applied mobile examination center weights for weighted analysis. Specifically, the 4 year sample weights (WTMEC4YR) set was used for a comprehensive examination of the NHANES 1999–2000 and 2001–2002 datasets. No preliminary statistical power estimations were conducted; thus, the sample size was derived solely from the available NHANES data, guiding the scope of our study.

To show the basic characteristics of the participants, continuous and categorical variables were summarized using means (standard error) and unweighted counts (weighted percentages), respectively. We explored differences between continuous variables using linear regression analyses and described differences between categorical variables employing chi-square tests.

To assess the differences in telomere length between two distinct age groups (20–50 years and over 50 years) among individuals with and without migraine, we conducted subgroup analyses using stratified weighted multivariable logistic regression. The outcomes were expressed through odds ratios (ORs) accompanied by corresponding 95% confidence intervals (CIs). Telomere length was incorporated as both a continuous variable (calculated OR per 1T/S ratio shortened) and a categorical variable (categorized into three equal groups). We applied four distinct models. The initial model, the crude model, was unadjusted. Subsequent models were sequentially adjusted as follows: Model 1 included adjustments for age, sex, race/ethnicity, education level, and family income. Model 2 expanded upon Model 1 by further adjusting for BMI, physical activity, smoking status, and alcohol use. Model 3 went a step further by incorporating adjustments for diabetes, MetS, CVD, CRP, and Hcy. The median of each group within the three telomere length categories was included as a continuous variable in the model to test the linear trend. Besides, sex as a subgroup was investigated for its effect on telomere length and migraine, and the interaction effect was explored.

In addition, a potential nonlinear relationship between telomere length and migraine incidence was examined with restricted cubic splines (RCS) regression in two age groups by adjusting for relevant variables in Model 3. Four knots were located at the 5th, 35th, 65th, and 95th percentiles of telomere length. The median telomere length of each age group served as the cutoff value. Subsequently, a two-piecewise logistic regression model was formulated to delve into the potential association between telomere length and migraine, with adjustment for conceivable confounding factors as outlined in Model 3.

All statistical analyses in our study were performed using R (version 4.2.2, http://www.R-project.org), R survey package version 4.1-1, and Free Statistics software (version 1.8, Beijing Free Clinical Medical Technology Co., LTD). The threshold for statistical significance was set at bilateral p < 0.05.

Participations

Information on 21,004 individuals who completed the NHANES survey in 1999–2002 was used as our data source. Of these, 10,713 people younger than 20 years and 602 women who were pregnant were excluded from the study. Populations missing data for severe headache or migraine (n = 8), as well as leukocyte telomere length (n = 2370), were not included in the study. Participants with missing covariate information (n = 1205) were not considered either. Age affects the association between telomere length and migraine

Age exhibits a noteworthy interaction effect in the context of the relationship between telomere length and migraine risk, even after comprehensive adjustment for all covariates (Table 2). Among adults aged 20–50 years, a compelling 57% rise in migraine risk per unit decrease in the T/S ratio of telomere length was observed (OR: 1.57, 95% CI 1.08, 2.29). This association was still significant (OR: 1.48, 95% CI 1.01, 2.17), independent of other confounding factors. However, it was not significant in people older than 50 years. A further interaction effect of age on the association between telomere length and migraine displayed significance, with the interaction likelihood ratio test indicating a p-value of 0.001. As same when the telomere length was trisected, the interaction effect was significant between telomere length and migraine in participants aged 20–50 years and those older than 50 years, as evidenced by a p-value of 0.010 for the interaction test. To elaborate, compared with the T3 (1.10–9.42) group, the adjusted weighted odds ratio (OR) for migraine in the shortest telomere length category was 1.35 (95% CI 1.01, 1.79), while this relationship did not exhibit statistical significance among adults above 50 years, denoted by an OR (95% CI) of 0.93 (0.60, 1.43) within the T1 (0.39–0.89) group in model 3. Subgroup analysis and interactive effect of sex on telomere length and migraine

In the cohort of 3206 male participants, there were 451 individuals with migraine. After adjusting for potential covariates, each unit of telomere length reduction increased the risk of migraine by 41% in men (OR: 1.41, 95% CI 0.86, 2.29) and 39% in women (OR: 1.39, 95% CI 0.76, 2.57). Briefly, the shortening of telomere length was associated with an increasing trend in the incidence of migraine, but this association was not statistically significant. Similarly, no significant sex-based interaction effects were noted (p for interaction = 0.618). Details can be found in the supplementary information.

Non-linear relationships

With RCS, we explored the non-linear relationship between telomere length and migraine risk. In the 20–50 age group, an L-type curve (p for non-linearity: 0.041) indicated the non-line relationship between telomere length and migraine (Fig. 2A). The threshold effect analysis suggested an inflection point of roughly 1.02T/S ratio (Table 3). After adjusting for all confounding factors, an inverse association emerged between telomere length and migraine when telomere length was < 1.02T/S ratio (OR: 9.34, 95% CI 1.56, 55.99), which suggested that the risk of migraine is increased by 834% with every 1T/S ratio shortening in telomere length. When telomere length exceeded the 1.02T/S ratio, a significant association between telomere length and migraine no longer persisted, as evidenced by an OR (95% CI) of 1.28 (0.83, 1.96). This indicated that migraine risk no longer decreased with shortened telomere length. Details are shown in Table 3. Discussion

In this large cross-sectional study representing US adults, we discovered that telomere length was inversely associated with migraine risk in those aged 20–50 years, while no relationship was observed in those aged > 50 years. The interaction test supported these findings, confirming an interaction between age and telomere length in migraine. Further analysis showed an L-shaped curve between telomere length and risk of migraine in the 20–50 age group, exhibiting an inflection point at 1.02T/S ratio. The relationship remained stable after constant adjustment for relevant confounders.

Only a limited number of investigations have delved into the relationship between telomere length and migraine. Notably, Hua Ren et al. undertook a study involving Caucasian women, including 142 individuals with migraine disease aged 18 to 77 years, alongside 143 well-matched healthy controls spanning the age range of 17 to 77 years24. Applying regression models to control for confounders showed that relative telomere length was on average 0.114 units shorter (95% CI − 0.136, − 0.091) in the migraine group than in the control cohort. It was observed that individuals with migraine had shorter telomere lengths than healthy controls. Our present study expanded the sample size to 6,169 and used a weighted approach to examine the relationship between leukocyte telomere length and migraine in the US. Notably, we extended our analysis beyond the confines of sex, incorporating the influence of age on this complex relationship. Our data underscored the inverse relationship between telomere length and migraine, specifically within the 20 to 50-year age range with telomere lengths shorter than 1.02T/S ratio when correcting for potential confounding factors that might affect the relationship. This finding was highly consistent with the results of Hua Ren’s research. In subgroup analyses, there was no association between telomere length and the incidence of migraine in either male or female participants, which was inconsistent with the research findings of Ren Hua et al. No significant sex-based interaction effects were noted either. One possible reason for the differing results in this study is the exclusion of additional confounding factors. The relationship between telomere length and migraine merits further investigation, particularly among individuals of different genders and various age groups.

Telomere damage stimulates the DNA damage response mechanism, according to previous studies46. Telomere wear then induces the upregulation of P53 expression, which further disrupts mitochondrial structural integrity by inhibiting PGC-1α and β expression, leading to oxygen radical efflux and oxidative stress damage13,47,48. In addition to structural damage, telomere damage promotes mitochondrial dysfunction and weakens the protective effect of telomere against oxidative stress damage13. In brain neurons, oxidative stress prompts excessive production of reactive oxygen species (ROS)7,49. ROS can diffuse from neurons and activate transient receptor potential cation channel subfamily A member 1 (TRPA1)50. Activated TRPA1 conveys neural signals of oxidative stress to the trigeminal nervous system and meningeal receptors associated with injury. This activation subsequently triggers the release of calcitonin gene-related peptide (CGRP) and induces a cascade of effects potentially implicated in pain signaling and neuroinflammation leading to migraine51. The increased ROS in migraine can also trigger DNA damage6. 8-hydroxy2ʹ-deoxyguanosine (8-oxoG) is one of the most prevalent biomarkers of ROS-induced oxidative DNA base damage and was observed to be elevated in the plasma of individuals with migraine by Geyik, S. et al52. Notably, in tissue culture experiments, exposure to ROS appears to accelerate the process of telomere shortening53. In vitro experiments have demonstrated that environmental factors, such as oxidative stress, can directly damage telomeric DNA, thereby influencing telomere length regulation. In addition, findings from epidemiological studies conducted in vivo suggest a potential role for oxidative stress in telomere attrition11,54.

In conclusion, telomere wear accelerates the disruption of mitochondrial structure and function, which leads to the leakage of ROS and impairment of antioxidant stress function. The ensuing elevation of oxidative stress sets the stage for migraine development through a complex interplay of pathways and in turn damage to telomeric DNA. This intricate process seems to operate within a framework of positive feedback regulation, creating a vicious circle. Our findings align with this pattern, as we observe a negative association between telomere length and the risk of migraine onset, particularly within the context of shorter telomere lengths.

Telomere shortening and damage are widely recognized as significant contributors to cellular senescence and the aging process, playing a pivotal role in organismal aging and the onset of various age-related diseases55,56. Research conducted in both animal and human subjects has consistently demonstrated that telomere length naturally shortens with age57–59. Given these established findings, it was initially hypothesized that as individuals age and experience telomere wear, the risk of developing migraine would increase. However, interestingly, our study found that there was no association between telomere length and migraine in people older than 50 years. As we know, the effect of age on migraine has been documented to exhibit a reduction in both incidence and severity, accompanied by a decrease in frequency as age progresses1,2. Maas, H J’s study found that treatment sensitivity increased with age among individuals with migraine compared to the placebo group60. These characteristics may be attributed in part to the brain’s reduced sensitivity to oxidative stress as we age. Migraine perhaps not only be a disease state, but part of an evolutionary adaptive response to protect the body from stress-induced brain energy deficits7. In contrast, damage to telomere from oxidative stress is continuous with aging34. More importantly, as cells continue to divide with aging, telomere length is progressively shortened by genetic factors13,57. Thus, age has an important influence on telomere length and migraine, and the interaction of age stratification supports our hypothesis. The underlying mechanism needs to be further explored.

The strengths of our study are the larger sample size and the use of a weighted approach to provide a comprehensive picture of the relationship between leukocyte telomere length and migraine within the US population under the influence of age. We also take additional confounding factors into account such as lifestyle, blood indicators, and disease status, which all have an impact on migraine and telomere length.

However, some limitations are regretfully still present in our study that need to be communicated to the reader. First, we adjusted our statistical model for most confounding factors, but could not exclude residual bias. Therefore, to elucidate the clinical relevance of measuring telomere length, further studies with a rigorous study design are necessary to correct for all conceivable variables that could affect telomere length and migraine. Second, the inherent nature of our cross-sectional study design precludes us from establishing a causal relationship between telomere length and migraine. Nevertheless, our findings may provide some basis for future research. Third, we acknowledge that not all of the patients who reported severe headache may have experienced the pain and disability of migraine disease. The diagnostic criteria for migraine were not aligned with the international standard ICHD-3 and there was some selection bias. Nonetheless, the proportion of migraine in our subjects was generally consistent with that reported in the US Migraine Prevalence and Prevention Study. Furthermore, despite the utilization of a substantial sample size, our study population was confined to US residents. Hence, appropriate consideration of this limitation is crucial when extrapolating our results to other populations. It is worth noting that, due to database limitations, we were unable to explore the relationships between telomere length and various migraine subtypes and characteristics (age of onset, duration, and frequency of episodes), and the differences between the age when migraine symptoms begin and the timing of telomere sampling. Future work will refine these research questions and collect relevant data to further enhance this study.

Conclusions

In conclusion, this study revealed that age may affect the association between telomere length and migraine. And an L-shaped relationship between telomere length and migraine was observed among Americans aged 20–50 with a rough inflection point at 1.02T/S ratio. Further longitudinal studies and randomized controlled studies are needed in the future to provide deeper insights into the relationship and underlying mechanistic associations between migraine and leukocyte telomere length.
