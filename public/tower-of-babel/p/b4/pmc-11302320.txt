Evaluation study of the urban governance of the COVID-19 crisis in the city of Antwerp
Authors: Margot Bracke, Jil Molenaar, Annelies Colliers, Bruno Marchal, Daan Sanders, Sara Van Belle, Lore Van Praag, Josefien van Olmen
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
BackgroundThe COVID-19 pandemic has demanded crisis management at all governance levels. While most research has focused on responses of national governments, city-level governance had significant potential to develop tailored approaches. This study explored how the local COVID-19 response was organised and adapted to the specific city population and context in the City of Antwerp, Belgium.MethodsA case study using semi-structured interviews was set up with 20 key informants with a central role in the coordination and implementation of the city-level COVID-19 response in Antwerp. Thematic analysis of transcripts was guided by an adapted version of the OECD risk management cycle.ResultsRespondents’ accounts provide a granular understanding of pandemic preparedness, crisis management, and response and adaptation to the COVID-19 crisis in a mid-size European city. Its size was large enough to have a strong capacity within its government bodies, to utilise the expertise of strong partners in the area and to mobilise a critical mass for action, yet small enough to quickly establish connections and trust. In addition to a universal approach, they developed tailored responses to specific neighbourhoods and groups. Well-established community and organisational ties enhanced the effectiveness of grassroots initiatives. The perceived feeling of joint action contributed to a strong collective agency, but respondents noted the need for a system for monitoring and learning.ConclusionsLocal governments are important to develop tailored strategies and organise a locally relevant crisis response in cities. They need expertise and agency, with enough flexibility to experiment. To harness promising practices and avoid repeating errors in future crises, it is crucial to develop a system that better evaluates and monitors local responses, before, during and after crises.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12889-024-19569-5.

Background

In response to the outbreak of the COVID-19 pandemic in 2020, national governments needed to coordinate across different levels to quickly and efficiently implement an adequate response to the crisis [1, 2]. Belgium’s multi-level governance system is particularly complex due to the country’s partially overlapping layers of administration at the federal, regional, and local levels, which is also visible in the organisation of the Belgian health system. The federal government is responsible for the regulation and financing of the compulsory health insurance, creating the normative framework and programmes for the hospitals [3]. In addition, the Flemish, French and German-speaking communities each have their own Ministries of Health [4]. The regional governments (i.e., Brussels-Capital-Region, Flanders and Wallonia) are responsible for maternity and child health services, health promotion, some aspects of elderly care, and hospital accreditation standards [5].

While the COVID responses of national level government have been well analysed, also in Belgium [1, 6–9], responses of cities have not been studied to the same extent. This is unfortunate, as especially in a federal structure, the role of local governments is crucial in implementing higher-level measures and adapting these to the local context, as well as in stimulating and creating synergies with bottom-up initiatives [10]. During the first phase of the pandemic, many local governments in Belgium had to wait for decisions from central level crisis committees [11]. Later, responsibilities were redistributed and local level actors were given more autonomy [1], e.g. to contextualise and adapt lockdown strategies to the local infection rates [12]. Additionally, the impact of COVID-19 was socially patterned, not just in terms of morbidity and mortality rates but also in terms of the consequences of the restrictions and emergency lockdown measures [13]. This resulted from multiple drivers of inequities, as social determinants such as people’s economic status and built environment shape not just their infection risk, but also their clinical vulnerability to severe COVID-19 disease, as well as their access to care [14–16]. The unequal impacts of the pandemic in many Belgian cities required local policy makers to adapt national guidelines to respond to the diverse needs of an urban city population [17].

In urban contexts, a major challenge is to develop a multi-sectoral response. Health actors need to collaborate closely with actors across sectors, engage meaningfully with the multiple urban communities and take into account all levels, from individuals and families to neighbourhoods and the city as a whole [18]. This requires governance arrangements that allow for inclusion and that are geared towards adaptation [19]. Adaptations would mean paradigm shifts at local level policymaking from medical care to public health, from public health to healthy cities, from sectoral to intersectoral planning, or from emergence preparedness to resilience. However, most empirical studies had relatively little attention for governance.

Our study aims at examining how the City of Antwerp organised its responses to the COVID-19 crisis, taking into account the diverse composition of the city population and the local context. Antwerp is a Belgian city with around 545.000 inhabitants, located in Dutch-speaking Flanders [20]. It is a globally networked port city characterised by increasing (super)diversity and inequity. In 2023, only 45% of Antwerp’s population was of Belgian descent, followed by 14% of Moroccan descent, 5% of Dutch and 4% of Turkish descent. Correspondingly, 46% of the primary school children speak another language at home than Dutch [21]. Antwerp has the highest poverty index in Flanders at 15.8%, which is even higher among mothers with an immigrant background (32.4%) [22]. Research studies have documented various aspects of the impact of the COVID-19 pandemic in the city of Antwerp. For example, studies have reported on mental health consequences [23], spatial inequalities [24], impact among specific communities (e.g. Orthodox Jewish communities and migrant communities) [23, 25, 26], demand for police intervention [27], local contact tracing [28], the network structure in the Port of Antwerp [29], and changes in the provided prehospital critical care [30]. However, studies on multiple aspects of urban governance are lacking.

This paper is based on an evaluation study of the urban governance of the city of Antwerp during the COVID-19 crisis, commissioned by the health department of the city of Antwerp and conducted by a multidisciplinary research team consisting of sociologists, public health researchers and general practitioners (GPs). The study focuses specifically on the city-level governance response during the early phase of the pandemic, before vaccines became available (February 2020 - February 2021). The purpose of the evaluation was to draw lessons from the urban COVID-19 response to inform future actions, not just in the context of potential similar health crises, but especially in continued cooperation efforts between the city’s health and welfare actors more generally.

Methods

Study setting: the organisational levels of COVID response in Belgium

The responsibilities in handling the COVID-19 crisis were spread across different levels of governance and were delegated across two main axes: (a) health through the Ministries of Health and (b) crisis management through General Affairs (see Fig. 1). For health-related responsibilities (Axis a), responsibilities are divided between the federal level (financing, organisation and professional regulation) and the regional level (disease prevention, health promotion, organisation of primary care, home care and social care) [31]. Just before the pandemic started, the Agency of Care and Health (FACH) initiated a decentralisation reform in 2019 in the Flemish Region establishing 60 Primary Care Zones (PCZs), where care for a given population can be planned and organised [32]. The administrative entity of a PCZ is a Care Council (CC). The PCZs were given additional COVID-related tasks: local contact tracing, monitoring organisations such as schools, retirement homes or jails, monitoring infections, awareness raising and the organisation of vaccination centres. The coordination and organisation of testing and triage was initially delegated by the federal government to GPs, later in coordination with the Flemish government through test centres. Contact tracing was a responsibility of the FACH.

For crisis management (Axis b), the federal government (Ministry of General Affairs) takes the lead. The Crisis Act created the legal framework for the federal government to – without prior parliamentary approval – take emergency measures for effective crisis response. From the beginning of the COVID-19 pandemic, a crisis unit was established at the federal level, with a consultative committee bringing together national and regional governments to determine national COVID policy. Crisis units were also established at the provincial and municipal levels, which were directly linked to the federal level.

Theoretical framework for analysis: Adapted OECD risk management cycle

To guide the study’s focus on urban governance of the COVID-19 crisis, we adapted the OECD risk management cycle [33], which was developed to evaluate the COVID response in various OECD-countries. Although not developed for an urban-level analysis, this framework allows the analysis of different phases in adapted governance that are relevant at the sub-national level. The OECD states that (urban) governments need to evaluate three main types of policy responses (pandemic preparedness, crisis management and response and adaptation) to better understand whether certain measures worked and how they could be improved [33]. Using the risk management cycle as a tool to evaluate the city of Antwerp’s urban governance during the pre-vaccination phase of the COVID-19 pandemic not only provides insight into how well the city of Antwerp handled the COVID response, but also provides lessons as to how the city can be more resilient in the future [34].

We adapted the framework for use at the city-level, while maintaining the three main response domains (Fig. 2; see Additional file S1 for a comparison of the terms used in the original risk management cycle and the adapted ones). These domains correspond to the three phases in the risk management cycle:

Data collection and analysis

A case study using in-depth semi-structured interviews with 20 key stakeholders in local governance of the city of Antwerp was conducted between July-November 2022. Interviews took place both online and face-to-face, with an average duration of 72 min. Purposeful sampling was used to maximise variation in the sample in terms of professional roles and in terms of gender. Our sample consisted of key informants who played a central role in the coordination and implementation of the city-level COVID-19 response in the city of Antwerp, including both individuals employed by the city and individuals employed by relevant local health and social care organisations (Table 2). The commissioned body (i.e., city of Antwerp) listed relevant actors who were contacted by the first author.

All interviews were carried out in Dutch, transcribed ad verbatim and analysed using NVivo software. We adopted thematic analysis [35], deductively coding the data within the OECD risk management cycle [33]. The key concepts used in the OECD’s risk management cycle formed the basis for our coding tree, as did the activities and measures the city wanted to be evaluated. While coding the interviews, we modified the concepts in the risk management cycle, resulting in our adapted version of the OECD risk management cycle (see Additional file S1 for a comparison). The first author coded all interviews. The first three interviews were double coded by two other researchers (JM & AC). The resulting themes were discussed in sessions with all authors. All interviewees were invited to a feedback meeting where preliminary analyses of all interviews were shared. Most participants attended and shared reflections, which were incorporated into the final analysis.

Ethics

This study was approved by the Ethical Committee of the University Hospital of the University of Antwerp (no. 3599 - BUN B3002022000102). We obtained written informed consent from all participants, and data were collected following the EC GDPR (General Data Protection Regulation) guidelines.

In other words, higher-level policies, guidelines and tools were adapted to local needs. We present the findings following the adapted version of the risk management cycle. Pandemic preparedness

Mandates and legislation in case of pandemic

Several respondents noted there was confusion about government (federal, regional, provincial or local) mandates and responsibilities, causing delays in the response.Some weeks passed before everything was clear and running smoothly. But once that role structure was in place, everything went more smoothly. (City02, city employee communication department).

Some weeks passed before everything was clear and running smoothly. But once that role structure was in place, everything went more smoothly. (City02, city employee communication department).

This was complicated by the development of Primary Care Zones (PCZs) in Flanders, which had become operational in July 2019, just before the outbreak. PCZs were meant to be a platform for coordination and collaboration between primary health care providers, social care actors and local authorities [32], but staff were still grappling with their exact role and position, and their relationship with local government. As a relatively large city in the Belgian landscape, the Antwerp city health department had a strong expertise and existing strategy for prevention and health. The differentiation with the PCZ mandate was not very clear, further complicated by the fact that the geographical area of Antwerp was divided into four PCZs. Respondents indicated that, as a result, the coordination of the interventions and negotiation between key stakeholders was a major challenge.In my experience, the city administration sometimes filled in what a PCZ should do. And sometimes I got the feeling that, because they are also big, they imposed ‘we expect you to take up this task’. Sometimes it was certainly justified and sometimes we had mixed feelings about it. This is also because the mandates were not so clearly divided in the beginning. (City16, PCZ employee).

In my experience, the city administration sometimes filled in what a PCZ should do. And sometimes I got the feeling that, because they are also big, they imposed ‘we expect you to take up this task’. Sometimes it was certainly justified and sometimes we had mixed feelings about it. This is also because the mandates were not so clearly divided in the beginning. (City16, PCZ employee).

For example, the PCZs were given the task to monitor the incidence of COVID-19 in organisations such as schools, retirement homes and jails, while schools were normally also part of the city authorities’ mandate. In the beginning of the pandemic, it was not clear who was responsible for what, leading to unnecessary competition and duplication.

Capacity to anticipate and respond to risks

The capacity of the city authorities to anticipate and respond was, according to the participants, strengthened by an existing network between city authorities and key stakeholders and organisations in the community and knowledge institutions, as well as existing expertise across departments. Pre-existing relationships with key stakeholders in communities of different cultural backgrounds in the city allowed for rapid mobilisation and dialogue between city and stakeholders, because there was sufficient trust from both sides. For example, the initial strategy of the city health department preparing communication materials for specific groups was adapted after key stakeholders told them that:

“It was better not doing that, these key stakeholders preferred to make their own adapted translations” (City02, city employee communication department).

Good relationships with (second and third generation) migrant organisations were equally important, because of their credibility and trust among different communities which allowed them to act as a broker between the city and the communities. Long-standing collaborations with actors and organisations of the social and health sector strengthened and expanded the city authorities’ capabilities to organise a rapid and adjusted response (see example “Collaboration city health department and organisation Atlas” in Table 3).

Material and knowledge readiness in critical sectors

The city had a strong operational capability that meant that materials and knowledge were ready and could be mobilised quickly. Building on experience with the organisation of large, city-wide events, existing skillsets and routines could be applied to the COVID-19 context. For instance, experiences with large-scale events facilitated the design and roll-out of a COVID-19 test village at ‘Spoor Oost’1 that centralised all testing facilities for the entire city, enabling efficient testing on the largest possible scale.You need an operational organisation, for which a local government is very appropriate, precisely because that’s what we do to manage a football match or manage the Meir [a busy shopping street in Antwerp]. So, you could apply that recipe to a health crisis. (City01a, city employee health department).

You need an operational organisation, for which a local government is very appropriate, precisely because that’s what we do to manage a football match or manage the Meir [a busy shopping street in Antwerp]. So, you could apply that recipe to a health crisis. (City01a, city employee health department).

Besides their own operational and logistic capacity, the city authorities could build upon a strong network of partners. Respondents highlighted the importance of research centres, scientists and the Port of Antwerp who could support them with advice that was embedded in the local context:Antwerp has a very large source of scientists, at the Institute of Tropical Medicine, at the University Hospital of the University of Antwerp, who really can give well-founded scientific advice, while still knowing the local situation. (City11, expert public health).

Antwerp has a very large source of scientists, at the Institute of Tropical Medicine, at the University Hospital of the University of Antwerp, who really can give well-founded scientific advice, while still knowing the local situation. (City11, expert public health).

The major barriers to the pandemic preparedness were related to the monitoring capacity and to governance. The capacity to monitor the COVID-19 pandemic at the city level was constrained by the inadequate access to timely health-related data, especially at the beginning of the epidemic. It was not possible to combine and cross-link COVID-19 data available at different organisational levels (general practitioners, city of Antwerp, Flemish region and federal level). Local authorities had very limited access to the federal data system for tracking infections and contacts due to the EU GDPR legislation and the complex web of responsibility distribution between federal, regional and local levels, and the lack of flexibility to deal with these in this situation. This limited identification of clusters and an efficient contact tracing programme.Ideally, you don’t set up a data infrastructure only after the fire breaks out. Ideally, something like that [already] exists: a local data infrastructure. And if there is a new crisis, that it is really just a matter of connecting new data sources to that from which actions can then emerge. (City10a, data manager).

Ideally, you don’t set up a data infrastructure only after the fire breaks out. Ideally, something like that [already] exists: a local data infrastructure. And if there is a new crisis, that it is really just a matter of connecting new data sources to that from which actions can then emerge. (City10a, data manager).

Crisis management

Governance structures

Early in the pandemic, a COVID Advisory Board (Table 3) was established with representatives from the city departments of health, education and general management, of hospitals, PCZs, academic institutions, and data experts. It became an important consultation and steering body at local level, albeit not with a formal mandate/formal governing power. The board promoted inter-organisational and inter-sectoral coordination and discussion and was important for fostering joint sense-making and mutual understanding. One board member explained:

[We were] confronted with different views, which made you much more attentive to each other’s concerns and challenges (City03, emergency plan coordinator).

The representation of people from different sectors and different levels in the local system led to a broad perspective on the problems and needs, beyond the purely clinical dimension. The presence of people with day-to-day terrain experience such as COVID coaches (Table 3) was crucial to make this visible and concrete:For example, for a doctor it is very simple: if you are sick, you go into quarantine and the quarantine lasts until you are no longer sick. But if you have a family with 5–6 children and they have been in quarantine for 3 weeks and the parents who were sick at first are no longer sick, but they still have to remain in quarantine because child 3 is sick. And child 4, 5 and 6 are also going to get sick. That’s a whole other thing. (City06, coordinator COVID Coaches).

Crisis communication and information

Strong internal communication within the city administration and a core supportive network were of great importance to effective crisis communication and dissemination of information in multiple directions. For instance, the possibility to directly communicate with ‘authoritative’ people or leaders in the sector enabled people in the city response teams to transmit context-specific needs to higher policy levels and advocate for action:Our chairman [chairman of a PCZ] was part of the provincial crisis unit, which meant we had a very short line to policy and decisions through him. If I said something like: ‘Oh yes, we have noticed this in the field and it is starting to peak’, he was able to convey this to higher policy levels and actions were soon taken. (City09, PCZ employee).

Our chairman [chairman of a PCZ] was part of the provincial crisis unit, which meant we had a very short line to policy and decisions through him. If I said something like: ‘Oh yes, we have noticed this in the field and it is starting to peak’, he was able to convey this to higher policy levels and actions were soon taken. (City09, PCZ employee).

Clear communication and information available to all groups and people in the city was a key priority of the city of Antwerp and the PCZs. Apart from translation of written and audio-visual material, the city developed a peer-educator initiative under the name Sensi Ambassadors (Table 3). A total of 45 volunteer ambassadors - typically people with an extensive network in their neighbourhood or community - were recruited and trained by the city, particularly in more vulnerable neighbourhoods. The selection criteria were that this person should be considered trustworthy in their community and have similar background characteristics as the group the sensi ambassador represented. They acted as neighbourhood brokers, feeding information into the neighbourhood and countering disinformation. The ambassadors also allowed the health department to be in close touch with the day-to-day reality in different parts of the city. Through the ambassadors, dialogues were established, which promoted trust and facilitated tailored communication to diverse target groups:And especially in that kind of epidemic, it is very important to have a dialogue and not just fire off messages. […] And, above all, to build a relationship of trust. (City01a, city employee health department).

And especially in that kind of epidemic, it is very important to have a dialogue and not just fire off messages. […] And, above all, to build a relationship of trust. (City01a, city employee health department).

This strategy was complemented with the organisation of webinars on testing, COVID measures and initiatives on COVID in the city of Antwerp to give the most recent information to the representatives of local civil society organisations who were then better capable of answering questions in the communities. A respondent stated it as follows:We found that we did not reach everyone with our communication and a lot of questions went to the intermediaries and if they are well informed, then the population is also better informed. (City16, PCZ employee).

The collaboration with people of knowledge institutions was important in the information and communication strategy. Experts took part in the webinars and were available on demand, which provided the city authorities with crucial insights, increased their confidence and credibility in their networks.

Whole-of city approach

The pandemic caused an unprecedented increase in new collaborations and initiatives, as well as changes in existing collaborations. People from different sectors and backgrounds united to strive for a whole-of-city approach that went beyond issues directly related to public health. For example, for the erection of a large-scale ‘test village’, organisations of the events sector were involved, traffic was re-arranged and the allocation of public space was adapted to accommodate optimal functioning. Interviewees emphasised that the pooling of people’s strengths was a powerful instrument to address gaps in service delivery:Initially, the organisation of testing was delegated to GPs (General Practitioners), who are not trained in organising mass events. I thought about how you should approach such testing and then I started thinking out of the box. Because of my experience with organising large running manifestations, I thought: ‘Look, what you need is an infrastructure, a ticketing system, and registration and those people are perfectly equipped to do that.’ (City12, emergency doctor and coordinator Test Village).

Response and adaptation

Economic, financial and human resources/support

The city of Antwerp decided quite early on to redeploy city employees who could not fulfil their regular role due to COVID-19 lockdown measures tailored to urgent local needs. They were redeployed for instance as helpline operators, guides to help people in the test village or helping with the distribution of protective equipment. The city prefinanced the deployment of the ‘test village’ before federal fundings were disbursed.

To be able to monitor the local COVID situation in the absence of an adequate central data infrastructure, the city decided to invest in a local version of a COVID Data Dashboard (Table 3) to monitor the transmission of infections at the sub-municipal level to anticipate local outbreaks. The data were analysed and discussed in the COVID advisory board on a weekly basis and guided discussions on adapting local action.

The city also quickly mobilized medical scientific experts from the University of Antwerp, the University Hospital, and the Institute of Tropical Medicine Antwerp familiar with the local context. Their skillsets and expertise were used to strengthen the Antwerp response team, to support information campaigns and to develop evidence-informed adaptation strategies.

Tailored response

According to respondents, quite some population groups were not reached by the regular communication channels and routine interventions, including people without official residence and transient migrants. This was due to for instance language barriers (see crisis communication and information); the consultation of different information channels such as media from home countries or people with standing in a particular community e.g. religious leaders (see crisis communication and information); the reality that not everyone is equally mobile to get to a particular place or does not have the possibilities to do so. The city authorities decided to complement the general strategies with targeted approaches, based upon principles of solidarity and protection. This was done for the contract tracing strategy and – at a later stage – for vaccination. Contact tracing was largely organised by the Flemish government with regional call centres, but the city of Antwerp used Flemish additional resources for local sensibilisation, prevention, source detection and quarantine guidance [36] to operationalise the COVID coaches project (Table 3). COVID coaches were given access to testing data and called individuals who tested positive. Special attention was given to people in a vulnerable situation (e.g. living in precarious neighbourhoods, being over 75 years old, etc.). Supported by an app with an overview of local support options, the coaches informed these persons about the isolation and quarantine measures to be taken, offered support (for example with grocery shopping), and performed contact tracing.

Another example were the diversified testing strategies. The city opted for large-scale testing in the ‘testing village’ for efficiency reasons, but it recognized that this would limit the access of people in vulnerable situations. Therefore, mobile test teams were set up (Table 3) in places with local outbreaks, whereby familiar and accessible places such as schools were used as a basis. Another example is the test centre set up in the Antwerp train station (Table 3) allowing free and ad hoc walk-in testing which lowered thresholds for many people such as homeless persons. The acceptability of testing appeared when there were improvements in geographical access and when there was a possibility to meet familiar people of one’s own neighbourhood, which contributed to trust in the program.“From the perspective of the profile of Antwerp students it was much easier to mobilise people when testing was organized in the school context.” (City13, city employee education department).

Decision-making

To mitigate the impact of the COVID-19 virus, the city of Antwerp, the Antwerp PCZs and other external partners developed many new interventions in a hitherto unseen and unpredictable situation. Respondents pointed to the importance of experimentation during a crisis and of flexibly adjusting interventions, even though some strategies did not have the intended effect. Respondents expressed the feeling of being uncertain about the effectiveness of the interventions:

“It was just really hard to tangibly see what our actions achieved” (City02, city employee communication department).

Discussion

Main findings

In this study, we explored how the city (health) authorities of Antwerp, starting from the federal and regional COVID policies, developed strategies that better fitted the local context of this diverse city. Our analysis, following an adapted OECD risk management cycle [33] revealed key findings for each of the three phases.

First, concerning pandemic preparedness, our findings indicate that thanks to its relatively large size and location near a large port and knowledge hubs, Antwerp had a significant level of pandemic preparedness. This comprised of in-house expertise, its network with organisations in the community and with well-placed knowledge institutions and a critical mass of motivated people and experts available. Antwerp is large enough to have such resources but still at a scale that key persons know each other or can find each other relatively easily, which created both momentum and the potential to mobilise rather quickly. The available material and human resources within the city administration and the availability of competent and motivated health and social care providers facilitated the organisation of the response.

Second, when it came to crisis management, our findings were that despite initial uncertainty about mandates and roles, actors managed to develop joint purpose and a ‘bottom up’ workable ad hoc coordination structure. This facilitated internal communication and external information in multiple directions, from local organisations, communities, neighbourhoods, to the local government.

Finally, regarding response and adaptation our findings highlight that there was a strong collective agency in Antwerp’s institutions to develop city-specific initiatives, with adequate space for experimentation. However, a need for a system for monitoring and learning remained. The technical capacity that has been developed in monitoring dashboards and targeted approaches will be transferred into routine practices such as for population health management and in newly emerging crises. The city has decided to strengthen its institutional emergency preparedness more broadly, embedding it into their governing structure by assigning a Chief Resilience Officer.

Lessons learned

Our analysis of the COVID-19 response in Antwerp provides important lessons in relation to future shocks in cities with highly diverse populations. Complementary to a universal approach, there is a need for neighbourhood- and group-specific responses, including minorities and people in vulnerable situations such as the homeless, sex workers, etc. Strong and pre-established connections with communities and organisations strengthen the potential of bottom-up initiatives and administration-civil society dialogue. Developing such responses requires adaptive and agile governance arrangements at city level [38]. In Belgium’s multi-level governance system which scatters mandates and resources across the federal, regional, provincial and city-level, developing adaptive responses at city-level also requires astute brokering and coordination skills. Important context conditions were the relatively high level of expertise and resources available to the city (health) authorities of Antwerp, and the strong shared sense of a public health urgency that motivated collaboration across sectors.

The analysis of Antwerp is partly in line with other analyses of urban crisis management during the pandemic. Reflections about multiple case studies point to the importance of the amount of resources, flexibility, collaboration and self-organisation as enhancing the risk management capacity or resilience of cities [39]. An example of a well-performing city is Hanoi [40], in which a strong faith in public institutions and subsequent collaboration of the public led to high implementation of containment measures. While COVID-19 has led to increased social tensions [41], cities who facilitated community-drive initiatives were able to strengthen solidarity during the crisis management [42].

Implications

The insights of this study have implications for the Antwerp context and for other cities of similar set-up. This study points to the role of city health departments in the health system. Midsized and large cities dispose of considerable resources and networks across sectors, which can be leveraged not only during crises, but also to foster a “healthy city” approach which locates health at an overarching multi-sectoral level. Related to this is the question of the role of the meta-governor in urban health governance, which includes not only the coordination of actors and of service delivery but more importantly ensuring accountability to the public [43, 44].

This study points to the need and possibilities to systematically learn from a crisis. Many studies and evaluations have been commissioned (including the study presented here). Beyond evaluating the effectiveness, cost, efficiency and acceptability of interventions, at this stage of the shock, after the absorption and adaptation phase, evaluations should inform the transformation of services and systems where needed [45]. The pandemic exposed structural weaknesses and triggered both bottom-up and top-down innovations. In order to harness promising innovations and not repeat mistakes, the city recognised the need to avoid losing the expertise accumulated during the COVID crisis, for instance by installing a city-level crisis cell or appointing a population health manager in each PCZ. This will also help to steer institutional strengthening for future crisis management, not by preparing endless emergency plans, but by creating the capability for agile management and governance. Such capability can support the city actors to steer the decision-making process if the drive for efficiency (rapid response, reaching scale as quickly as possible) clashes with the imperative of not leaving out vulnerable groups by way of tailored approaches.

Future research

Avenues for future research include how to deal with these tensions in the current societal context; to compare distinct approaches in cities of similar sizes in and beyond Europe; meta-governance in the urban context and how to understand the relationship between local government and other sectors and government levels; and how to generate and maintain relationships with different groups in the city to reduce inequities and promote inclusiveness.

Limitations and strengths

Despite its value, this study has some limitations. We focused on stakeholder perceptions of one city, where people put a lot of effort during times of crisis, often on top of individual struggles to deal with the COVID situation. This may have resulted in an overly positive narrative. Two Belgium-wide evaluations of the COVID response capacity at local level showed that the urgency of crises contributed to a common goal that facilitated collaboration, but also that in many places local governance was stretched and that efficient and democratic functioning of local governance structures needs sufficient resources and support [46, 47]. Our single case study demonstrates local relevance, and allows readers from other contexts to have a granular understanding of crisis response in a mid-size city, providing a template for analysis and inspiration. Nonetheless, the collaboration between researchers and local government allowed for locally relevant questions, a high response rates of respondents, a very productive respondent validation exercise, and a joint ownership of lessons presented in this paper.

Conclusion

This study appraised the local government response to the COVID-19 pandemic in 2020 and 2021 of the city of Antwerp (Belgium). Given the complex multi-level governance system in Belgium, local government demonstrated agility in its mode of governance through continuously adapting national and regional measures to the local context. The findings from this case study highlight the relevance and flexibility of the city response. The city demonstrated its ability to manage the pandemic, due to existing expertise, organisations, and networks available in the city that facilitated fast mobilisation. The strengths of the local response consisted of creating new local governance structures with agile decision-making and acting, good communication with local leaders in a whole-of-city approach.
