Fractionated ethanolic red ginger extract as antibacterial agent against Aggregatibacter actinomycetemcommitans and Porphyromonas gingivalis: In silico and in vitro studies
Authors: Prawati Nuraini, Sindy Cornelia Nelwan, Seno Pradopo, Amirah Saraswati Pronorahardjo, Putri Qomaria Andarini, Yulanda Antonius, Mohammed Ahmed Aljunaid
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundPeriodontitis is a chronic inflammatory disease associated with oral microbiome dysbiosis, where Aggregatibacter actinomycetemcomitans and Porphyromonas gingivalis contribute through adhesion, biofilm formation, and tissue-destructive virulence factors. Although chlorhexidine is widely used as an adjunctive antimicrobial agent, its long-term use is limited by adverse effects, highlighting the need for safer natural alternatives. Red ginger (Zingiber officinale var. rubrum) exhibits antibacterial potential; however, data on fractionated extracts and their interaction with specific virulence proteins remain limited.ObjectiveTo evaluate the antibacterial activity of fractionated ethanolic red ginger extract against A. actinomycetemcomitans and P. gingivalis using integrated in silico and in vitro approaches.MethodsTwenty-two bioactive compounds identified from the fractionated extract were analyzed via molecular docking against cytolethal distending toxin (Cdt) of A. actinomycetemcomitans and fimbrial protein Mfa5 of P. gingivalis, with chlorhexidine as reference. In vitro assays determined minimum inhibitory concentration (MIC), minimum bactericidal concentration (MBC), bacterial adherence, biofilm formation, and viability.ResultsDocking analysis showed that cyclooctacosane and gamma-sitosterol demonstrated binding affinities comparable to chlorhexidine toward both target proteins. The extract exhibited MIC/MBC values of 0.062/0.125 mg/mL for A. actinomycetemcomitans and 0.031/0.062 mg/mL for P. gingivalis. Significant reductions in bacterial adherence, biofilm formation, and viability were observed at concentrations of 0.031–0.125 mg/mL.ConclusionFractionated ethanolic red ginger extract demonstrates promising antibacterial activity against key periodontal pathogens by targeting virulence mechanisms related to adhesion and biofilm formation, supporting its potential as a natural adjunct in periodontal therapy.

Introduction

Periodontitis is a multifactorial chronic inflammatory disease characterized by the progressive destruction of the supporting tissues of the teeth, including the periodontal ligament, cementum, and alveolar bone, ultimately leading to tooth loss. The disease is strongly associated with dysbiosis of the oral microbiome, in which Gram-negative anaerobic bacteria play a central pathogenic role. Among these, Aggregatibacter actinomycetemcomitans (Aa) and Porphyromonas gingivalis (Pg) are considered key periodontal pathogens due to their potent virulence factors and strong association with tissue destruction and disease progression 1, 2, 3, 4, 5, 6, 7.

Aggregatibacter actinomycetemcomitans is particularly implicated in aggressive periodontitis, especially in adolescents and young adults, through the production of virulence factors such as leukotoxin, lipopolysaccharide, and cytolethal distending toxin (Cdt), which disrupt host immune responses and promote alveolar bone resorption.5,6,8,9 Meanwhile, Porphyromonas gingivalis is a keystone pathogen in chronic periodontitis, possessing fimbriae, gingipains, and outer membrane vesicles that facilitate adhesion, biofilm formation, immune evasion, and persistent inflammation10, 11, 12, 13, 14. In particular, fimbrial proteins such as Mfa5 play a crucial role in bacterial attachment and biofilm maturation, making them attractive molecular targets for antimicrobial intervention.11,15

Current periodontal therapy focuses on mechanical plaque control combined with adjunctive chemical agents, among which chlorhexidine remains the most widely used antimicrobial mouthwash. Although chlorhexidine is clinically effective against periodontal pathogens and biofilm formation, its long-term use is associated with several adverse effects, including tooth and mucosal staining, taste alteration, xerostomia, and mucosal irritation3,16, 17, 18. These limitations have prompted increasing interest in safer, natural antimicrobial alternatives that can be used for prolonged periods without compromising patient compliance or oral health.19

Medicinal plants have emerged as promising sources of novel antimicrobial agents due to their structural diversity and bioactive potential. Red ginger (Zingiber officinale var. rubrum) has been traditionally used for its anti-inflammatory, antioxidant, and antimicrobial properties20, 21, 22, 23. Previous studies have demonstrated that crude red ginger extracts exhibit antibacterial activity against various oral pathogens, including Streptococcus mutans, Porphyromonas gingivalis, and Aggregatibacter actinomycetemcomitans.21,24 However, crude extracts contain complex mixtures of volatile oils and non-volatile compounds, which may reduce reproducibility, specificity, and mechanistic clarity.22,23

Fractionation of plant extracts represents an important strategy to isolate bioactive non-volatile compounds while minimizing interference from essential oils. Despite the growing interest in red ginger as an antimicrobial agent, studies investigating fractionated red ginger extracts remain limited, particularly against Gram-negative periodontal pathogens. Previous work has primarily focused on Gram-positive bacteria, and evidence regarding A. actinomycetemcomitans and P. gingivalis remains scarce.25

Molecular docking offers a valuable in silico approach to predict interactions between bioactive compounds and bacterial virulence proteins, thereby providing mechanistic insights and guiding experimental validation. Targeting virulence-associated proteins such as Cdt in A. actinomycetemcomitans and Mfa5 in P. gingivalis may interfere with bacterial survival, adherence, and biofilm development, enhancing the effectiveness of antimicrobial strategies.11,26,27

To our knowledge, studies integrating molecular docking and in vitro antibacterial evaluation of fractionated red ginger extract targeting virulence proteins of A. actinomycetemcomitans and P. gingivalis remain limited. Therefore, the present study aimed to evaluate the antibacterial potential of fractionated ethanolic red ginger extract against Aggregatibacter actinomycetemcomitans and Porphyromonas gingivalis using an integrated in silico and in vitro approach. The primary objective was to assess the interaction between red ginger bioactive compounds and key bacterial virulence proteins through molecular docking analysis. The secondary objectives were to determine the minimum inhibitory concentration (MIC) and minimum bactericidal concentration (MBC), to evaluate bacterial adherence and biofilm formation, and to assess bacterial viability following exposure to fractionated red ginger extract.

Materials and methods

Preparation of red ginger extract

Fresh rhizomes of red ginger (Zingiber officinale var. rubrum) were cleaned, sliced, air-dried at room temperature, and ground into a fine powder. The powdered material was subjected to maceration using 96% ethanol at room temperature for 72 h with intermittent stirring. The extract was filtered and concentrated under reduced pressure using a rotary evaporator to obtain a crude ethanolic extract.28

Fractionation procedure

The crude ethanolic extract was further processed using a liquid–liquid partitioning approach to obtain a fractionated non-volatile ethanolic extract enriched in bioactive compounds. The fractionation step was performed to reduce compositional complexity and minimize the contribution of volatile essential oil components. The resulting fractionated ethanolic extract was evaporated to dryness and stored at 4 °C until further analysis 23,28, 29, 30.

Chemical profiling by GC–MS analysis

GC–MS analysis was performed using a capillary column GC system. The injector temperature was set at 250 °C. The oven temperature was programmed from 60 °C (held for 2 min) to 280 °C at a rate of 10 °C/min. The mass spectrometer was operated in electron impact (EI) mode at 70 eV with a scan range of m/z 40–600. Compound identification was based on mass spectral comparison with reference libraries and published data. The compounds identified through GC–MS analysis were subsequently selected as candidate ligands for molecular docking simulations.23,31

In silico molecular docking analysis

Molecular docking was conducted using PyRx software version 0.8 integrated with AutoDock Vina version 1.1.2. The chemical structures of the 22 red ginger compounds were obtained from the PubChem database in SDF format. Ligands were energy-minimized using Open Babel with MMFF94 force field and converted into PDBQT format 32, 33, 34, 35.

Protein targets included cytolethal distending toxin (Cdt) from Aggregatibacter actinomycetemcomitans (PDB ID: 2F2F) and fimbrial protein Mfa5 from Porphyromonas gingivalis (PDB ID: 6T01). Protein preparation involved removal of crystallographic water molecules and heteroatoms, addition of polar hydrogens, and assignment of partial charges prior to conversion into PDBQT format.

Docking was performed under identical settings for all ligands and the reference compound (chlorhexidine). For each ligand, the best-ranked pose (lowest binding energy) was recorded for analysis.

Docking interpretation and cut-off criteria. Docking results were interpreted using a comparative approach. Binding energies (kcal/mol) of red ginger compounds were ranked and compared with chlorhexidine (reference). Compounds showing binding energies equal to or more favorable than chlorhexidine, and/or within a close range to the reference under identical docking settings, were considered to have potentially relevant binding.

Validation statement. Classical docking validation via redocking RMSD requires a co-crystallized ligand in the binding site. As a suitable co-crystallized ligand for redocking was not available for the selected protein structures in this study, RMSD-based redocking validation was not performed; this limitation is acknowledged. To support reproducibility, all docking parameters including grid box coordinates and sizes are fully reported.

Prediction of biological activity was further supported by PASS (Prediction of Activity Spectra for Substances) analysis, where activities with Pa (probability of activity) > Pi (probability of inactivity) were considered biologically relevant.

Preparation of bacterial inoculum

Reference strains of A. actinomycetemcomitans and P. gingivalis were cultured in Brain Heart Infusion Broth (BHIB) under anaerobic conditions. Bacterial suspensions were adjusted to a 0.5 McFarland standard (approximately 1 × 108 CFU/mL) and subsequently diluted 1:100 in BHIB to obtain a final inoculum concentration of approximately 1 × 106 CFU/mL.5,21,36

Sample size and number of replicates

All experiments were performed in independent biological replicates. MIC and MBC assays were conducted in triplicate. Adherence, biofilm formation, and MTT viability assays were performed using 4 technical replicates per group.37

Description of control groups

The experimental design included the following control groups: (i) negative control consisting of bacterial cultures without treatment, (ii) positive control using chlorhexidine at a concentration of 1.2 mg/mL, and (iii) vehicle control containing the same final concentration of solvent used to dissolve the extract without active compounds.

Preparation of test solutions

The fractionated ethanolic extract was dissolved in sterile distilled water to obtain stock solutions. Serial dilutions were prepared to achieve final concentrations expressed in mg/mL for all in vitro assays, including minimum inhibitory concentration (MIC), minimum bactericidal concentration (MBC), bacterial adherence, biofilm formation, and viability assays.

Determination of MIC and MBC

The antibacterial activity of the fractionated ethanolic red ginger extract was evaluated using the broth microdilution method. Serial two-fold dilutions of the extract were prepared to obtain final concentrations ranging from 0.031 to 0.125 mg/mL.

Chlorhexidine 1.2 mg/mL, served as the positive control, while the negative control consisted of bacterial suspension in BHIB without extract or chlorhexidine. As sterile distilled water was used to reconstitute the extract, no separate solvent control was included.

Bacterial assays

After incubation at 37 °C for 24 h under anaerobic conditions, the minimum inhibitory concentration (MIC) was defined as the lowest concentration showing no visible bacterial growth, while for minimum bactericidal concentration (MBC) determination, aliquots from wells without visible growth were subcultured onto BHIB agar plates, incubated for 24 h, and visible colonies were manually counted; bacterial inocula were standardized to a 0.5 McFarland suspension prior to each experiment, microtiter plate wells were allocated according to a predefined layout to minimize positional bias, and due to the in vitro nature of the experiments, blinding was not applied, although all procedures were conducted under identical experimental conditions.21

Bacterial adherence and biofilm formation, and viability assays

Biofilm formation was assessed using a microtiter plate method. Bacterial adherence and biofilm biomass were quantified following exposure to the fractionated extract at selected concentrations.21 MTT assay was employed to assess metabolic activity and viability of bacteria within biofilms, based on the reduction of tetrazolium salt by metabolically active bacterial cells.38

Statistical analysis

Statistical analyses were performed using SPSS software (version XXI). Data normality was assessed using the Kolmogorov–Smirnov test, and homogeneity of variances was evaluated using Levene's test. Differences between groups were analyzed using one-way analysis of variance (ANOVA), followed by Duncan's post hoc test where appropriate. All data are presented as mean ± standard deviation (SD), and statistical significance was set at p < 0.05.

Results

Molecular docking studies

The molecular docking results demonstrated variable binding affinities of red ginger–derived compounds toward key virulence proteins of periodontal pathogens. γ-Sitosterol and cyclooctacosane showed relatively stronger binding, predominantly mediated by hydrophobic interactions, while shogaol exhibited moderate binding supported by both hydrogen bonding and hydrophobic contacts. Overall, these interaction profiles provide a molecular rationale supporting the observed in vitro antibacterial, antibiofilm, and viability outcomes. Discussion

This study evaluated the antibacterial, antibiofilm, and viability effects of a fractionated ethanolic red ginger extract against Aggregatibacter actinomycetemcomitans and Porphyromonas gingivalis, supported by in silico molecular docking analysis targeting virulence-associated proteins. Overall, the results demonstrate that the fractionated extract exhibited antibacterial activity in a concentration-dependent manner, although the magnitude and pattern of response differed between planktonic growth, adherence, biofilm formation, and metabolic viability assays.21,23,39

A notable strength of this study is the integration of molecular docking analysis with multiple in vitro antibacterial assays, enabling the evaluation of both potential molecular interactions and experimental antibacterial activity.

Molecular docking analysis provided complementary molecular-level insights into potential interactions between selected red ginger compounds and bacterial virulence-associated proteins. Compounds such as γ-sitosterol and cyclooctacosane exhibited relatively stronger binding affinities, predominantly mediated by hydrophobic interactions, while shogaol showed moderate binding supported by both hydrophobic contacts and hydrogen bonding. The reference compound chlorhexidine displayed stronger and more consistent interactions, particularly with the Mfa5 protein. Importantly, these in silico findings were interpreted as supportive evidence rather than direct confirmation of inhibitory mechanisms, as similarities in binding energy do not necessarily translate into equivalent biological efficacy40

The MIC and MBC findings indicate that the fractionated red ginger extract was able to inhibit and reduce bacterial growth at higher concentrations, whereas lower concentrations did not consistently suppress bacterial proliferation. In some assays, increased bacterial growth or biofilm formation was observed at sub-inhibitory concentrations, particularly for A. actinomycetemcomitans. This phenomenon may reflect adaptive bacterial responses to environmental stress or a hormetic effect, in which low concentrations of phytochemicals stimulate metabolic activity or biofilm production as a survival strategy. Additionally, plant-derived extracts may contribute trace nutrients or interact with the growth medium, potentially influencing bacterial behavior at lower concentrations 41, 42, 43.

Bacterial adhesion is a key early event in periodontal pathogenesis, enabling colonization and subsequent biofilm formation by AA and PG; therefore, targeting adhesion is considered an effective preventive strategy against periodontal disease progression.44,45 The dose-dependent reduction observed in AA is consistent with its relatively linear adhesion mechanisms mediated mainly by fimbriae and surface adhesins, which can be progressively disrupted by increasing concentrations of bioactive compounds, explaining the comparable effect to CHX at intermediate concentrations.46 In contrast, PG required a higher effective concentration to achieve maximal inhibition, likely due to its multifactorial adhesion system involving major and minor fimbriae and gingipain-associated functions; sufficient concentrations may be necessary to interfere with these interconnected virulence pathways simultaneously.45,47 The ability of fractionated red ginger extract to match or surpass CHX in inhibiting bacterial adhesion supports its potential as a natural anti-adhesive agent and adjunctive approach for early periodontal intervention, particularly considering limitations associated with long-term CHX use.48

Biofilm formation represents a critical virulence strategy of periodontal pathogens, conferring resistance to host defenses and antimicrobial agents. The significant reduction of biofilm in both AA and PG following CHX treatment confirms its role as a benchmark antibiofilm agent in periodontal research, validating the experimental model used in this study.48 The concentration-dependent inhibition observed in AA is consistent with its biofilm architecture, which relies strongly on surface adhesins and fimbrial structures involved in early attachment. Progressive disruption of these components by increasing concentrations of bioactive compounds may explain the graded reduction in AA biofilm formation observed with fractionated red ginger extract.46,49 In contrast, PG exhibited a higher tolerance at lower extract concentrations, likely reflecting its multifactorial virulence system, including major and minor fimbriae and proteolytic enzymes that contribute to biofilm maturation and community stability. Effective inhibition at higher concentrations suggests that simultaneous interference with multiple virulence pathways is required to suppress PG biofilm formation, supporting the potential role of fractionated red ginger extract as a natural antibiofilm adjunct in periodontal disease control.15,47

MTT-based viability assessment reflects metabolic activity and is widely used to evaluate antimicrobial effects against periodontal pathogens. The pronounced reduction in AA and PG viability following CHX exposure confirms its strong bactericidal activity and supports its use as a reference standard in in-vitro periodontal studies.48 The MTT assay showed reduced bacterial metabolic activity at higher extract concentrations, indicating decreased viability within established biofilms. Because MTT reduction reflects metabolic activity rather than direct cell counts, the results were interpreted as indicators of metabolic viability, which is particularly relevant for metabolically heterogeneous biofilm-associated bacteria. Although originally developed for mammalian cells, tetrazolium-based assays have been widely applied to evaluate bacterial viability and biofilm metabolic activity.50

Taken together, the results suggest that fractionated red ginger extract possesses antibacterial and antibiofilm potential against Gram-negative periodontal pathogens, with effects that are highly dependent on concentration and assay context. The integration of in vitro and in silico approaches provides a coherent framework for understanding these effects while highlighting the need for further mechanistic studies and in vivo validation before clinical relevance can be inferred.

This study has several limitations. The use of a fractionated ethanolic extract, rather than isolated pure compounds, and inherent variability in plant extraction may affect reproducibility, while the in vitro antibacterial and biofilm models may not fully represent clinical conditions. In addition, bacterial viability was assessed using the MTT assay as a metabolic activity indicator, and molecular docking results provide supportive but not confirmatory evidence of direct inhibitory mechanisms, warranting further mechanistic and in vivo investigations.

Another limitation relates to the molecular docking approach used in this study. Molecular docking provides a static prediction of ligand protein interactions and does not fully account for protein flexibility, solvent effects, or the dynamic biological environment. Therefore, the docking results should be interpreted as preliminary mechanistic insights, and further validation using molecular dynamics simulations or experimental protein-binding assays may be required.

Future studies are recommended to include detailed chemical profiling of the active fractions, evaluation of dose–response relationships at sub-inhibitory and inhibitory concentrations, and validation using in vivo periodontal models or clinically relevant systems. Such approaches will help to further elucidate the therapeutic potential and translational relevance of fractionated red ginger extract in periodontal therapy.

Conclusion

This study shows that a fractionated ethanolic red ginger extract exhibits concentration-dependent antibacterial and antibiofilm effects against Aggregatibacter actinomycetemcomitans and Porphyromonas gingivalis. Molecular docking provides supportive evidence of potential interactions with virulence-associated proteins, although further mechanistic and in vivo studies are needed to confirm these findings.
