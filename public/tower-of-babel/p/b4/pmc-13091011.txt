When a hydrocele is more than a hydrocele: Warning symptoms and signs of malignant mesothelioma of the tunica vaginalis testis
Authors: Joel Cornfield, Mary Lee, Roohollah Sharifi
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractMalignant mesothelioma of the tunica vaginalis testis (MMTVT) is a rare tumor with no pathognomonic features. It most often presents as a hydrocele. Preoperative assessment, surgical management and prognosis of MMTVT are different than that of a hydrocele. When MMTVT is not suspected and a transcrotal hydrocelectomy is performed, the latter predisposes to tumor cell seeding, increases the risk of tumor recurrence, and decreases the patient's life survival. Therefore, it is prudent for physicians to identify warning symptoms and signs of MMTVT so that they can identify when a hydrocele is more than a hydrocele.

Introduction

We present a patient with a hydrocele and MMTVT was not suspected. On physical exam, ultrasonography, and color doppler ultrasonography, there was minimal indication of the presence of MMTVT. A hydrocelectomy was performed, and pathologic findings revealed MMTVT with positive margins. We discuss the rationale for a second surgical procedure, follow-up monitoring for MMTVT recurrence, and the patient's outcome more than three years after a right radical orchiectomy, hemiscrotectomy, and inguinal node dissection.

Case

A 37 year old male presented with severe right testicular pain, two days in duration. He denied any history of trauma, urinary tract infection, urolithiasis, or sexually transmitted disease. The pain was not responsive to over the counter analgesics.

The patient had no significant relevant past medical history. The patient smoked socially at parties starting in 2015, increased to 2-3 cigarettes daily in 2020, and stopped in 2021. He may have been exposed to asbestos when he served in the Navy; he was stationed on an old ship in the Middle East for four years.

His vital signs were normal. On physical exam, the testes were normal. On palpation, there was a hydrocele and minimal tenderness of the epididymis on the right; no hydrocele or tenderness was on the left. No induration or erythema of the scrotal skin was present.

A grayscale and color doppler scrotal ultrasound showed two normal testicles, and a large right hydrocele with internal debris. Blood flow to each testicle was unremarkable (Fig. 1). He was diagnosed with right epididymitis with a reactive hydrocele. He was treated empirically with ciprofloxacin 500 mg by mouth twice a day for two weeks.

Grayscale scrotal ultrasound shows a normal right testicle with a large right hydrocele.

Soon after completing ciprofloxacin, the patient reported persistent scrotal swelling, which was managed with a scrotal support and hot/cold packs as needed. We reassured him that resolution of all symptoms may take additional time.

One month after completing ciprofloxacin, the patient reported having mild pain in the right scrotum. However, the patient wanted to have scrotal swelling treated as it interfered with his job as a personal trainer. A physical exam showed bilateral testes without masses and a right 3-4 cm palpable hydrocele. The left scrotal sac was normal.

He underwent a right hydrocelectomy two weeks later. On gross examination, the right hydrocele was tense; the hydrocele sac had a cobblestone appearance. The right testicle appeared normal.

The surgical specimen was sent to the Defense Health Agency, Joint Pathology Center (JPC) for consultation. The final report revealed MMTVT, epithelioid type. Tumor involved the cauterized edge of the tunica vaginalis. Immunostaining was positive only for calretinin and WT-1, which was consistent for a mesothelial origin. There were few if any mitotic figures, moderate nuclear atypia, and absent necrosis, indicating a grade 1 or low grade epithelioid MMTVT. By the time we had prepared this case report for publication, only hematoxylin and eosin stains of the surgical specimen were available and these are provided in Fig. 2. We were unable to recover images of immunostaining slides, which had been sent to JPC more than three years prior.

Hematoxylin and eosin stained slide of pathology of hydrocelectomy specimen, which shows MMTVT, epithelioid type.

Post-operative clinical staging included computed axial tomography of the chest, abdomen, and pelvis which was normal except for a single 2 mm pulmonary nodule in the right lower lung lobe of unclear etiology and no evidence of neoplasm in the abdomen. A whole body bone scan showed no evidence of skeletal metastases. A PET-CT scan from the skull to the thigh was normal except for a small focus of FDG accumulation at the right scrotal region/anteriorly and a smaller focus superolaterally. No focal abnormal FDG accumulation was seen in the right lower lung lobe or in the remainder of the study.

Based on the patient's age, MMTVT involving the cauterized margin which was concerning for ileal inguinal lymph node contamination, and two foci of FDG accumulation in the scrotal region on PET-CT scan, he underwent a hemiscrotectomy, right radical orchiectomy, and right inguinal lymph node dissection. This is considered standard of care for localized MMTVT. The pathology findings included a grade 1/low grade, epithelioid type MMTVT; all margins and all six inguinal lymph nodes were negative for malignancy. The testis and epididymis were histologically unremarkable.

Since no surveillance guidelines for MMTVT were available, the oncologist recommended that the patient's care should follow peritoneal mesothelioma National Comprehensive Cancer Network guidelines that include CT scans every 3-6 months for the first 5 years, then annually until year 10. The patient sought a second opinion about chemotherapy, which confirmed that adjuvant chemotherapy was not warranted in view of the absence of metastases and negative surgical margins after the right radical orchioscrotectomy. The patient was told that recurrences can be localized and that he should conduct regular self-exams.

After the second surgical procedure, the patient has had ultrasounds of the scrotum and left testicle and CT scans of the abdomen, pelvis, and chest every three or six months for three and one half years. Thus far, all tests show no evidence of MMTVT recurrence. The solitary pulmonary nodule has remained stable and unchanged on all subsequent CT scans of the chest.

Discussion

MMTVT is a rare tumor comprising less than 1% of all malignant mesotheliomas. MMTVT may also involve the spermatic cord, epididymis, and testis. MMTVT lacks pathognomonic clinical or radiologic imaging features. MMTVT symptoms most commonly mimic the clinical presentation of a hydrocele,1 a testicular or paratesticular mass, or inguinal hernia.2 Often a patient with MMTVT undergoes a hydrocelectomy, which can lead to tumor seeding and local recurrence in the scrotal skin, or lymphatic spread to the inguinal nodes.3 MMTVT can be aggressive and the median and mean survival after diagnosis ranges from 18 to 23 months and 26-36 months, respectively.4 Lymphatic spread of MMTVT to inguinal, retroperitoneal, pelvic, or other distant lymph nodes occurs in up to 25% of patient cases.5

When MMTVT is suspected, additional diagnostic testing is recommended before any surgical procedure. A scrotal ultrasound and color doppler imaging may identify if the hydrocele is more than a hydrocele. Abnormalities consistent with MMTVT include a thick or nodular tunica vaginalis, a testicular or paratesticular mass, or echogenic hydrocele fluid with vegetations along the wall of the tunica vaginalis.12 Color doppler ultrasonography may reveal increased vascularity, a characteristic of paratesticular masses. In general aspiration cytology of hydrocele fluid is not recommended as it may result in tumor cell seeding along the needle track. Also, cytological analysis infrequently is helpful.13

If hydrocelectomy is performed, additional signs of MMTVT may include hemorrhagic hydrocele fluid; studding or nodules covering the serosal surface and/or thickening of the tunica vaginalis.4,6,8 MMTVT is typically described as a solid mass or nodules, yellow or white in color.8

Once the diagnosis of MMTVT is confirmed, clinical staging should be done. Node positive disease has been treated with surgery, plus chemotherapy and/or radiation therapy, however, no national treatment guideline is currently available.7,14

With localized MMTVT, an enbloc (radical) inguinal orchiectomy and hemiscrotectomy should be performed.7, 8, 14 If the patient has palpable lymph nodes, or if computed axial tomography shows probable lymph node involvement, an inguinal lymphadenectomy should also be done.6 With initial complete resection at the outset, long term follow-up for up to ten years shows that most patients who had surgical resection with negative margins had tumor-free survival.14 Staged surgery, i.e., performing a transcrotal hydrocelectomy first, followed by an enbloc orchiectomy and lymphadenectomy at a later date, is not recommended as hydrocelectomy alone is associated with MMTVT recurrence in up to 50% of patients and 90% of patients within the first 2 and 5 years, respectively.7

MMTVT recurs commonly within the first two years after seemingly effective surgery, however, some recurrences have occurred much later, i.e., 10-15 years after surgery.6 Thus, close follow-up is recommended for the first five years after MMTVT is treated. Based on the patient's age, MMTVT pathology and stage, and treatment response, patients should be monitored at 3 to 6 month intervals for the first 2-5 years with repeat physical exam, scrotal ultrasonography, and CT scan of the chest, abdomen, and pelvis. If there is no MMTVT recurrence, follow-up annual monitoring visits should be continued for the next 5-10 years.6

Conclusion

MMTVT should be suspected when a hydrocele has developed rapidly; the scrotum feels thickened, hard, edematous, studded, or nodular; or the hydrocele is painful. Preoperatively, a scrotal ultrasound, color doppler study, computed tomography or PET scan should be done so that the most appropriate surgical procedure can be performed.
