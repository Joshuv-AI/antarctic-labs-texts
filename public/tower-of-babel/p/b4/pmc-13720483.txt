Altered NCAM1, NRXN1, NLGN4, and CDH2 Levels in Individuals with Autism Spectrum Disorder and Their Unaffected Siblings
Authors: Ayşegül Tuğba Hıra Selen, Necati Uzun, İbrahim Kılınç, Ahmet Osman Kılıç
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractObjectiveAutism spectrum disorder (ASD) has been increasingly associated with alterations in synaptic function and plasticity, in which cell adhesion molecules (CAMs) play a critical role. This study aimed to compare peripheral levels of NCAM1, NRXN1, NLGN4, and CDH2 among individuals with ASD, their unaffected siblings, and healthy controls, and to examine their associations with autism severity and autistic traits in siblings.MethodsThe study included 41 children aged 18−72 months diagnosed with ASD according to DSM-5 criteria, 41 unaffected siblings of children with ASD (control group 1, aged 24−72 months), and 41 typically developing children (control group 2, aged 18−72 months). The severity of ASD symptoms was evaluated using the Childhood Autism Rating Scale and the Autism Behavior Checklist. Parents of the siblings completed the Autism Spectrum Screening Questionnaire to assess subclinical autistic traits. Serum NCAM1, NRXN1, NLGN4, and CDH2 levels were measured in peripheral blood samples using enzyme-linked immunosorbent assay.ResultsNo statistically significant differences were observed among the study groups in serum levels of NCAM1, NRXN1, NLGN4, or CDH2. However, within the ASD group, a significant positive correlation was found between NCAM1 and NRXN1 levels, suggesting a potential interrelation between these synaptic adhesion molecules.ConclusionAlthough peripheral levels of NCAM1, NRXN1, NLGN4, and CDH2 did not differ among groups, the observed molecular correlation in ASD cases may reflect subtle mechanisms influencing synaptic connectivity. Further biochemical and genetic studies are warranted to clarify the roles of these molecules in the etiology and neurobiology of ASD.Keywords: Autism spectrum disorder, Cell adhesion molecules, NCAM1, NRXN1, NLGN4, CDH2

INTRODUCTION

Autism spectrum disorder (ASD) is a neurodevelopmental disorder characterized by deficits in social communication and interaction, restricted interests, and stereotyped behaviors, with symptoms beginning in early childhood [1]. According to the Autism and Developmental Disabilities Monitoring Network reports evaluating the prevalence of ASD in the population, the prevalence in 2022 was 1 in 31 [2]. Although the pathogenesis of ASD has not been fully elucidated, it is known that various genetic factors play a significant role, and numerous neurobiological and environmental factors have been identified as contributing to the development of the disorder [3].

Cell adhesion molecules (CAMs) mediate cell–cell interactions both in the developing and mature nervous system. CAMs play roles in cell migration, synapse formation, and target recognition. Synaptically localized CAMs activate presynaptic and postsynaptic sites to establish the initial trans-synaptic contacts and regulate the maturation of newly formed synapses, thereby playing a crucial role in synaptogenesis [4].

Neural Cell Adhesion Molecule 1 (NCAM1) is a cell surface glycoprotein belonging to the immunoglobulin superfamily of CAMs. Through homophilic and heterophilic interactions, Neural Cell Adhesion Molecule (NCAM) contributes to Ca2+-independent cell–cell and cell–substrate adhesion [5]. NCAM accumulates on the neuronal cell surface and regulates the interactions of developing neurons with the extracellular environment. NCAM is involved in cell adhesion, cell migration, neural differentiation, synapse formation, and synaptic plasticity [6,7]. The relationship between synaptic plasticity abnormalities and ASD has been established [8]. In an experimental study where NCAM1 function was disrupted in rats, the animals displayed increased anxiety, reduced exploratory activity, abnormal stress responses, impairments in spatial learning, and heightened intermale aggression—behavioral characteristics resembling those observed in individuals with ASD [9,10]. Furthermore, plasma NCAM1 levels were significantly lower in individuals with ASD compared to controls, and these levels were correlated with social motivation, social communication, and gross motor skills, with no significant sex differences observed [9].

Neurexins (NRXN) are a large family of presynaptic proteins functioning as neuronal cell-surface receptors. NRXN1 binds to neuroligins (NLGNs) at central nervous system synapses to form the NRXN–NLGN complex, which plays a critical role in neurotransmission and synaptic interactions [11,12]. A homozygous exonic and intragenic NRXN1 deletion was identified in siblings presenting with West syndrome and ASD, suggesting variable phenotypic expression within the same family [13]. Neurons derived from induced pluripotent stem cells of individuals with ASD carrying the NRXN1 alpha isoform exhibited increased excitability, suggesting that NRXN1 deletions may disrupt synaptic functions and lead to neuronal hyperexcitability [14].

Neuroligin 4 (NLGN) is an adhesion molecule located on postsynaptic neurons [15]. The NRXN–NLGN interaction is thought to be involved in synaptic function, formation, and stabilization [16]. A study reported significantly higher plasma NLGN4 levels in children with ASD compared to healthy controls, suggesting that increased NLGN4 may reflect synaptic dysfunction associated with ASD or changes in other synaptogenesis-related components [17].

N-cadherin (CDH2, NCAD), encoded by the human CDH2 gene, is a cadherin family cell–CAM that mediates Ca2+-dependent homophilic interactions and transduces signals through interactions with cytoplasmic proteins [18]. Asymmetric and selective postsynaptic NCAD expression has been shown to lead to impaired synaptic functions and synapse elimination [19]. Upon reviewing the psychiatric literature, mutations in CDH2 have been reported to be associated with attention-deficit/hyperactivity disorder and impaired synaptic functioning [20]. However, to the best of our knowledge, no studies to date have specifically investigated the role of ASD.

NCAM1, NRXN1, NLGN4, and CDH2 are CAMs that play key roles in synapse formation and stabilization. ASD is increasingly conceptualized as a synaptopathy, characterized by alterations in synaptic development, transmission, and plasticity [21-23]. An autopsy study reported decreased levels of NCAM1 protein in the brains of individuals with ASD, particularly a significant reduction in the longest NCAM1 isoform (NCAM-180); however, no corresponding changes were observed in NCAM1 mRNA expression levels, and serum NCAM protein levels did not differ significantly from those of controls [24]. Most experimental studies on biomarkers of neurological disorders have relied on tissue samples or whole blood; however, assessing biomarker concentrations in plasma, which is a biologically complex fluid, can provide indirect information about pathological processes occurring in the central nervous system. Although peripheral measures cannot directly mirror central synaptic alterations, plasma provides a clinically accessible matrix that may capture downstream or systemic correlates of neurodevelopmental processes [25,26].

In addition, a range of shared endophenotypic features has been identified between individuals with autism and their unaffected siblings, spanning social cognition, executive functioning, sensory responsiveness, and structural and functional brain characteristics, albeit with differing levels of severity [27-30]. Consistent with the endophenotype framework, shared genetic alterations in synaptic cell adhesion genes may give rise to distinct clinical phenotypes within the same family. For example, a homozygous NRXN1 deletion was identified in two siblings, one diagnosed with ASD and the other with West syndrome, demonstrating marked intra-familial phenotypic variability [13]. Similarly, mutations in genes encoding NLGN3 and NLGN4 have been reported in both individuals with ASD and their unaffected siblings [16]. These findings support the notion that alterations in synaptic cell adhesion pathways may reflect shared genetic vulnerability rather than diagnosis-specific effects, underscoring the value of sibling comparisons in disentangling disease-related changes from familial genetic background. Accordingly, comparing children with ASD, their unaffected siblings, and healthy controls allows the examination of whether alterations in peripheral CAM levels reflect ASD-specific pathology or shared familial and genetic vulnerability.

The literature on CAM in ASD is mainly based on small postmortem brain studies and peripheral measurements using heterogeneous methods. Many studies do not adequately address clinical phenotype differences, developmental timing, or clinical correlations, and CAMs are often examined at the level of single molecules in cross-sectional designs. Moreover, variations in methods, molecular forms assessed, and age groups limit the comparability of findings. Consequently, the role of CAM alterations in ASD and their associations with clinical subtypes remain unclear.

In this study, based on the synaptopathy framework of ASD and previous findings suggesting alterations in CAMs, we hypothesized that children with ASD would exhibit differences in serum CAM levels compared to healthy controls. We further anticipated that unaffected siblings might show intermediate patterns, reflecting shared familial or genetic vulnerability. Additionally, we explored whether peripheral CAM levels would be associated with clinical severity measures within the ASD group.

METHODS

Participants

According to the Diagnostic and Statistical Manual of Mental Disorders, Fifth Edition (DSM-5) [31] diagnostic criteria, the study included 41 children aged 18−72 months who were diagnosed with ASD and presented to the Child and Adolescent Psychiatry Outpatient Clinic of a university hospital, along with their siblings without ASD aged 24−72 months. Participants were recruited consecutively from children diagnosed with ASD at our clinic during the study period. Children who met the inclusion criteria were enrolled.

Exclusion criteria for the ASD group included the presence of chronic physical, metabolic, genetic, respiratory, or neurological disorders (e.g., diabetes mellitus, hypertension, epilepsy, cerebral palsy, developmental delay), a history of severe head trauma or organic brain injury, and psychiatric medication use within the past six months. The same exclusion criteria were applied to the sibling group, with the additional criterion of absence of an ASD diagnosis.

Additionally, 41 unrelated healthy children aged 18 to 72 months who presented to the Department of Pediatrics of the same university hospital for routine developmental check-ups and had no known health problems were included as a control group. These participants had no diagnosis of ASD, intellectual disability, developmental delay, or other psychiatric disorders, no use of psychiatric medication within the past six months, and no active infection or chronic physical illness. The Autism Spectrum Screening Questionnaire (ASSQ) was administered only to unaffected siblings to assess subclinical autistic traits within the familial context.

The study was approved by the Ethics Committee of Necmettin Erbakan University (Approval No.: 2023/4295) and conducted in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki and relevant local regulations. Written informed consent was obtained from all participants and their parents after a detailed explanation of the study’s purpose and procedures. Additionally, oral assent was obtained from all participants.

Procedures

To confirm the ASD diagnoses, children presenting to the Department of Child and Adolescent Psychiatry at a university hospital were clinically evaluated by an experienced child and adolescent psychiatrist based on DSM-5 diagnostic criteria. Subsequently, the same clinician assessed the severity of autistic symptoms using the Autism Behavior Checklist (ABC) and the Childhood Autism Rating Scale (CARS). For unaffected siblings parents completed the ASSQ.

Psychological Measures/Instrumentation

Sociodemographic data form

A form developed by the researchers was used to collect sociodemographic information, including the child’s age and sex, parents’ educational levels, and the family’s socioeconomic status, as well as clinical characteristics such as the presence of psychiatric or chronic illnesses within the family.

Autism Behavior Checklist

The ABC was originally developed by Krug et al. (1980) to identify the severity and frequency of autistic symptoms in school-aged children. The scale consists of 57 items grouped into five subscales: sensory, relating, body and object use, language skills, and social and self-help skills. The validity and reliability study specific to our population has been conducted [32].

Childhood Autism Rating Scale

CARS was developed by Schopler et al. in 1971 to assess children over the age of two with suspected ASD and to differentiate autistic individuals from those with other developmental disorders. The scale consists of 15 items evaluating areas such as imitation, social interaction, use of objects, adaptation to change, emotional response, body use, and general impressions. Each item is rated on a 7-point scale ranging from 1 to 4, with half-point increments (1, 1.5, 2, 2.5, 3, 3.5, 4). Based on the total score, children scoring 15 to 29.5 are classified as non-autistic, 30 to 36.5 as mildly to moderately autistic, and 37 to 60 as severely autistic. The validity and reliability study specific to our population has been conducted [33,34].

Autism Spectrum Screening Questionnaire

ASSQ is a 27-item questionnaire rated on a 3-point Likert scale: ‘No’ (0 = normal), ‘Somewhat’ (1 = some abnormality/atypicality), and ‘Yes’ (2 = definite abnormality/atypicality), yielding a total score between 0 and 54 [35]. The items were originally designed to assess four factors: social interaction, communication problems, restricted and repetitive behaviors, and other associated features such as motor clumsiness and motor/vocal tics [36]. Later studies by Guo et al. [37] and Hattori et al. [38] reorganized the questionnaire into three domains: restricted and repetitive behaviors (items 2, 3, 9, 10, 18, 20, 21, 22, 23, 24, 27), social interaction (items 1, 12, 14, 15, 16, 17, 19, 25, 26), and communication problems (items 4, 5, 6, 7, 8, 11, 13). The validity and reliability study specific to our population has been conducted [39].

Blood Samples

Venous blood samples were obtained from the antecubital vein of participants in the patient and control groups in the morning of the psychiatric evaluation and collected into serum separator tubes containing clot activator and gel. Blood samples were centrifuged in a Hettich Rotina 46R (Hettich Zentrifugen) machine at 4°C, 1,000 g, and 10 minutes to separate serum samples. Serum samples were stored at −80°C in a New Brunswick U570 (New Brunswick Scientific) refrigerator until the analyses of Neural Cadherin (E-EL-H0195, Elabscience Biotechnology Co.), NCAM (ELK1813, ELK Biotechnology), Neurexin-1 (ELK8784, ELK Biotechnology), and Neuroligin-4 (RE3694H, Reed Biotech Ltd) were performed. The concentrations of the molecules were measured using a double-antibody sandwich ELISA method in accordance with the manufacturers’ instructions. During the washing steps of the ELISA procedures, a BioTek ELX50 microplate washer (BioTek Instruments) was used. Spectrophotometric measurements were performed using a Bio-Rad xMark microplate absorbance reader (Bio-Rad Laboratories). Based on absorbance–concentration calibration curves, the concentrations of Neural Cadherin, NCAM, and Neuroligin-4 were calculated in ng/ml, while Neurexin-1 concentrations were calculated in pg/ml. The laboratory analyses were performed using coded samples, and the personnel conducting the assays were blinded to group status.

Statistical Analysis

All statistical analyses were performed using IBM SPSS Statistics version 25.0 (IBM Corp.). Categorical variables were expressed as percentages (%) and frequencies (N), while quantitative variables were presented as mean ± standard deviation (SD) or median with interquartile range (IQR), depending on their distribution. The normality of quantitative data was assessed using histogram analysis, skewness and kurtosis values, Q–Q plots, and the Kolmogorov-Smirnov test. Homogeneity of variances across groups was evaluated with Levene’s test. Depending on whether parametric assumptions were met, appropriate statistical tests were applied.

For normally distributed variables (NCAM1, NLGN4, NCAD), one-way analysis of variance (ANOVA) was used to assess group differences. For non-normally distributed variables (NRXN1), the Kruskal-Wallis test was applied. To further evaluate the potential effects of age and sex on the results, a logarithmic transformation of NRXN1 was performed, followed by analysis of covariance (ANCOVA). Post hoc analyses were conducted with Bonferroni correction where applicable. Correlation analyses were performed within the ASD group to assess the relationships between serum CAM levels (NCAM1, NRXN1, NCAD, and NLGN4) and clinical scale scores (CARS and ABC). Pearson’s or Spearman’s correlation coefficients were used as appropriate based on data distribution, and partial correlations were calculated controlling for age and sex. A type I error (α) of 0.05 and a pvalue of < 0.05 were considered statistically significant. Post hoc pairwise comparisons were performed using Bonferroni adjustment.

RESULTS

The study sample consisted of three groups: the ASD group, a sibling group including healthy siblings of children with ASD (control group 1), and an unrelated healthy control group without siblings diagnosed with ASD (control group 2). The total sample included 123 participants: 41 in the ASD group, 41 in the sibling group, and 41 in the healthy control group. The mean age of the children and adolescents with ASD was 43.54 ± 16.62 months (range 18−72 months); the ASD study sample consisted of 31 (75.6%) males and 10 (24.4%) females. The mean age of the control group 1 was 52.02 ± 18.07 months (range 18−72 months); the control group 1 consisted of 23 (56.1%) males and 18 (43.9%) females. The mean age of the control group 2 was 43.41 ± 16.51 months (range 18−72 months); the control group 2 consisted of 23 (56.1%) males and 18 (43.9%) females. There is no statistically significant difference between the groups in terms of age and sex distribution (p = 0.076, p = 0.108, respectively) (Table 1). DISCUSSION

The aim of this study was to compare serum NCAM1, NRXN1, NLGN4, and NCAD levels between individuals with ASD, their siblings, and healthy controls, and to examine the associations of these molecular markers with ASD severity and autistic trait features. In this study, no significant differences were observed in serum NCAM1, NRXN1, NLGN4, or NCAD levels across the three groups. Moreover, none of these molecular markers showed a significant relationship with clinical scale scores. These findings suggest that peripheral levels of these synaptic molecules may not reliably reflect ASD-related neurobiological alterations, at least within the parameters of the present cohort.

In our study, NCAM1 levels initially appeared to differ significantly among the ASD, sibling, and control groups (p = 0.04); however, this association became non-significant after adjustment for potential confounders (p = 0.14). No significant associations were detected between NCAM1 concentrations and CARS or ABC scores. Previous research has produced inconsistent findings: one study reported lower plasma NCAM1 levels in ASD and an association with behavioral problems [9] whereas Purcell et al. [24] found no differences in serum NCAM levels among ASD patients, siblings, controls, and individuals with other neurological disorders. Consistent with the latter, our findings do not support a direct association between NCAM1 and ASD. Also, when NCAM1 levels were evaluated across age groups, children with ASD at 4 years of age exhibited lower serum NCAM1 levels compared with children with ASD at 6 years of age and with healthy controls. This pattern may suggest that differences in NCAM1 are more apparent during earlier phases of development, a period marked by dynamic synaptic organization and circuit formation. As neural networks mature and become more stable over time, peripheral NCAM1 levels may show reduced variability, which could limit the sensitivity of serum-based assessments to detect ASD-related neurobiological differences at later developmental stages. Consideration of such developmental timing effects may help to explain the largely null findings reported in studies including broader age ranges [9].

Similarly, no significant associations were observed between NRXN1 levels and ASD, nor with clinical scale scores. Although deletions of the 2p16.3 region containing the NRXN1 gene have been linked to ASD [12], there are no previous studies that have evaluated serum NRXN1 levels in ASD. Thus, our findings provide preliminary data but do not support peripheral NRXN1 as a potential biomarker. One of the notable findings was a positive Spearman correlation between serum NRXN1 and NCAM1 levels in the ASD group (ρ = 0.332, p = 0.034). Given the exploratory nature of the analyses and the absence of significant group differences, this association should be interpreted cautiously. As both molecules are involved in synaptic adhesion and neurodevelopment, their concomitant variation may reflect shared synaptic or neuroinflammatory regulatory processes; rather than supporting a causal or ASD-specific mechanism, this association may represent a peripheral reflection of a broader neurodevelopmental risk network. To our knowledge, the relationship between serum NRXN1 and NCAM1 levels has not previously been examined in ASD, and replication in independent samples is required.

In the present study, NLGN4 levels also did not differ significantly between the ASD, sibling, and control groups, and no relationships were found with CARS or ABC scores. Previous research has identified a single amino acid mutation in the NLGN4 gene of a male patient with ASD, which inhibited glycosylation and impaired synaptic localization, potentially contributing to ASD-related synaptic dysfunction [40]. Additionally, elevated plasma NLGN4 levels have been reported in children with ASD [17]. Although mutations in NLGN4 may disrupt synaptogenesis and cell–cell interactions, our findings indicate that peripheral NLGN4 levels may not reflect such mechanisms in preschool-aged children.

NCAD levels were also comparable across groups, and no significant association was found with clinical scale scores. Genome-wide association studies have implicated various cadherin family genes in psychiatric disorders—including CDH9/10, CDH5, and CDH11 in ASD [41]—and studies of CDH2 expression in postsynaptic regions have demonstrated impaired synaptic functions [19]. Despite these links, our findings do not support NCAD as a peripheral marker of ASD risk or severity.

One of the strengths of this study is its contribution to the limited literature evaluating siblings of children with ASD in comparison with typically developing controls. The inclusion of younger participants, representing early developmental stages, and concurrent evaluation of both ASD patients and their siblings provide informative insights into potential endophenotypic markers. The lack of significant differences in serum CAM levels between unaffected siblings and healthy controls may suggest that these molecules do not constitute robust peripheral endophenotypes of ASD. Alternatively, this finding may reflect the limited sensitivity of serum-based measures to detect subtle neurobiological alterations in individuals at genetic risk. Accordingly, the absence of differences in the sibling group should be interpreted with caution.

However, several limitations must be acknowledged. The relatively small sample size may have reduced statistical power to detect subtle group differences or associations with clinical characteristics. The absence of standardized developmental assessments limited the ability to correlate biomarker levels more precisely with neurodevelopmental profiles. Additionally, given the influence of age and sex on certain molecular levels, subgroup analyses could not be performed, preventing a more detailed exploration of these effects.

In summary, no significant associations were identified between serum NCAM1, NRXN1, NLGN4, or NCAD levels and ASD in preschool-aged children. This study is the first to examine these markers concurrently in children with ASD and their siblings, addressing a notable gap in the literature. Nevertheless, further research with larger, well-characterized samples and multimodal methodologies is needed to clarify the potential roles of CAM-related pathways in ASD.
