Barriers and facilitators to using practice facilitators to implement a remote cancer symptom management intervention: a mixed methods study
Authors: Sarah A. Minteer, Jennifer L. Ridgeway, Deirdre R. Pachman, Jessica D. Austin, Joan M. Griffin, Kathryn J. Ruddy, Andrea L. Cheville
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
BackgroundAs part of a pragmatic cluster-randomized trial for patients with cancer, we recruited representatives from each care setting and trained them to be practice facilitators (“Symptom Sages”). This mixed methods study evaluated barriers and facilitators individuals encountered in this role.MethodsSymptom Sages were invited to complete a brief web-based survey and semi-structured interview, developed using constructs from the Consolidated Framework for Implementation Research (CFIR) 2.0. Survey responses were summarized descriptively. Two researchers coded interview transcripts to CFIR 2.0 constructs, reviewed coded text, and created memos to identify themes. Final themes were agreed on via discussion.ResultsBetween August and November 2023, 12 practice facilitators participated (11 completed surveys and 7 completed interviews). Interviews revealed the following themes: 1) training and knowledge acquisition for the facilitator role were sufficient but time was not, 2) mixed perceptions of the intervention and inability to modify the intervention impacted role motivation, and 3) there were challenges motivating colleagues and getting organizational support. Survey results revealed similar findings.ConclusionTraining alone is insufficient for practice facilitators’ success in the absence of allocated protected time. Additional implementation strategies may be needed to support adoption in oncology settings.Trial registrationThis trial was registered at ClinicalTrials.gov, NCT03892967, on March 25, 2019, https://clinicaltrials.gov/study/NCT03892967. Background

Barriers to implementation of patient reported outcome measures (PROMs) and electronic patient reported outcome measures (ePROMs) in oncology have been documented [1–3]. Some studies have considered champions a key solution when retrospectively evaluating challenges to PROMs implementation [4] or prospectively exploring potential challenges to ePROMs implementation [5]. A champion is “a local visionary who uses expert knowledge to persuade others to adopt an innovation.” [6] Moreover, their importance has been noted for implementation of PROMS in patients with cancer, specifically [5, 7].

Use of clinical champions is one of the many evidence-based implementation strategies listed in the Expert Recommendations for Implementing Change (ERIC) [8]. However, a literature review exploring the effectiveness of clinical champions revealed mixed findings on whether their use increased intervention uptake by providers, and details about what the clinical champion role entailed were sparse [9]. The literature also offers little guidance on how to best identify champions. In one systematic review, more studies had champions who were recruited as opposed to champions who emerged [10].

Another ERIC implementation strategy is facilitation, which involves active dynamic problem-solving to create needed change [8]. A clinical facilitator is “an individual who provides continuous (primarily) local assistance to others through a formal implementation process using peer support (e.g., formal/informal education, shared learning and being a resource person).” [6] Clinical champions and practice facilitators support implementation in different ways [6]. The champion role is one of enthusiastic support, while the facilitator role supports formal implementation efforts. Facilitators may also champion the innovation or recruit champions to help them.

Facilitators may be tasked with discovering change barriers and providing resources to address them, educating and supporting their peers, tracking changes, and acting as a resource [6]. They must be agile and skilled to address challenges to implementation as they pertain to the innovation being implemented, those receiving the innovation, and the context [11]. Research has explored facilitators’ experiences related to activities performed in the facilitator role [12], training received [13], characteristics of facilitators [14], barriers and/or facilitators [14–17], and facilitation strategies [16, 18]. However, little work has explored the role of practice facilitators in oncology clinics, which may have unique challenges and opportunities. A publicly available compilation of articles published between 1996 and 2021 on implementation facilitation contained 510 articles, but only 11 had any relation to cancer care [19]. Moreover, of these 11 articles, most explored facilitation in the context of increasing cancer screening in primary care settings [19] and only one studied facilitation in oncology clinics [20]. The latter study piloted PROMS implementation in two oncology practices and evaluated barriers and facilitators to implementation from the perspective of a novice and expert facilitator [20].

The present study reports the perspectives of members of oncology care teams who were clinical champions trained to be implementation facilitators (termed “Symptom Sages”) in the Enhanced, electronic health record (EHR)-facilitated cancer symptom control (E2C2) cohort cluster randomized, pragmatic Type II hybrid effectiveness-implementation trial of an EHR-facilitated collaborative care (ECC) intervention. The ECC intervention used a collaborative care model (CCM)-based approach to address Sleep interference, Pain, impaired Physical functioning, Pain, Anxiety, Depression, and Energy deficit/fatigue (SPPADE) symptoms [21] reported via ePROMS completed by patients living with and beyond cancer. Patients with moderate or worse symptoms were offered symptom-specific self-management materials. Patients with severe symptoms were encouraged to work with a symptom care manager (SCM) to develop an individualized management plan. Both effectiveness and implementation outcomes of the E2C2 trial were assessed and will be reported elsewhere. This manuscript addresses a gap in the literature related to how key individuals within care teams support the complex implementation of ePROMs for symptom management and CCM interventions. Using survey and interview methods, we sought to understand barriers and facilitators to fulfilling the Symptom Sage role and supporting implementation from their perspectives. This manuscript was prepared following the COnsolidated criteria for REporting Qualitative research (COREQ) checklist [22].

Methods

Setting

The E2C2 trial was conducted in the medical oncology clinics of a multi-site health system with a shared EHR. Oncology clinics were based in a quaternary medical center in Rochester and four regions (Southwest Wisconsin; Northwest Wisconsin; Southeast, Minnesota; and Southwest, Minnesota) of a dispersed network of community hospitals and free-standing clinics. Oncology clinics were assigned to one of 15 clusters. Clusters were randomized to transition from the enhanced usual care to the intervention condition at one of five sequential timepoints between October 2019 and June 2022, as part of the stepped-wedge trial design. (See Finney Rutten and colleagues [23] for more details on the trial design.)

Recruiting practice members to the symptom Sage role

In the weeks before each oncology clinic was scheduled to begin delivering the ECC intervention, we approached any provider(s) in a target cluster, who had expressed interest in the trial or worked previously in symptom management, to assess their interest in serving as a Symptom Sage. If this was not fruitful, practice leaders and members were asked to identify a nurse, advanced practice provider, or physician who was enthusiastic about symptom management and might be willing to champion the intervention, assist colleagues in engaging with the clinical decision support (CDS) tools and SCMs, and serve as a primary liaison between the oncology care team and the research staff. Prospective Symptom Sages met with members of the study team to discuss the trial scope and aims and specifications of the Symptom Sage role (see Fig. 1). Symptom Sages were offered funding to protect 5% of their time for training in the three months around their cluster’s “go-live” transition from usual care to ECC intervention delivery.

Symptom Sage training and ongoing support

Symptom Sages were expected to inform colleagues about E2C2, support integration of the ECC intervention into their practice, and promote the intervention and encourage their colleagues to engage with it. To support Symptom Sages in fulfilling their role, a member(s) of the study’s implementation team led three ~ 1.5-2-hour long training sessions, before intervention “go-live.” Training sessions were held in person for practices that went live prior to the COVID-19 pandemic, and virtually for practices that went live during the pandemic. The first two sessions were informational. The third session provided Symptom Sages an opportunity to practice presenting the trial in preparation for presenting the trial to their clinical practice team at a scheduled meeting prior to “go-live.” Training was consolidated into two sessions for the last group of practices to “go live.” Sages were encouraged to use this time to review materials, ask E2C2 team members questions, and identify the types of support and resources that would help to make them successful. This training, totaling six hours, was intended to help Symptom Sages feel prepared to act as practice facilitators and was designed to cover the needed information, but was shorter than another two-day implementation facilitation training program described in the literature [24].

Each Symptom Sage received a binder of documents describing the trial, intervention, processes and workflows, patient-facing resources, symptom management resources, and information about implementation science and implementation strategies. The binder also included a frequently asked questions document. The study team, including a palliative care physician specializing in symptom management, met with Symptom Sages each month to provide them with study updates and an opportunity to connect with other Symptom Sages. These meetings also allowed time for feedback from the Symptom Sages and the opportunity to develop strategies to address implementation challenges.

Additional evidence-based implementation strategies

Additional evidence-based implementation strategies deployed included EHR-enabled CDS tools and audit and feedback [8]. Symptom Sages engaged with these strategies by promoting them and supporting clinician engagement with them.

CDS

EHR-enabled CDS for clinicians allowed them to manage and track patient symptoms, facilitated referral to an SCM, and reminded them about E2C2 resources for symptom management. Patients were assigned ePROMs around the time of their clinic visits and could complete them via patient portal, tablet in clinic, or by telephone using interactive voice recognition (IVR) software. Patient reporting of any severe SPPADE symptoms triggered an alert that appeared in the left-hand navigation pane of users’ EHR interface. Once opened, the alert offered providers a means to view longitudinal ePROM reports and to place orders for evidence-based symptom-specific management. Additionally, a Smart Phrase enabled providers to auto-populate a patient’s recent symptom scores in their clinical notes. Patients’ symptom scores were also viewable from a dashboard within the EHR. These CDS were designed to support clinicians in viewing patients’ symptoms and to facilitate patient referrals to available resources. However, clinicians could choose whether and how to interact with these CDS. Irrespective of whether a clinician chose to view the symptom reports or use the CDS, patient reporting of at least moderate symptoms automatically triggered self-management symptoms to be sent to patients via their patient portal. Those reporting severe symptoms were automatically sent an offer to speak with an SCM. The extent to which ePROM reporting had already been integrated into clinical practice varied amongst practices. Symptom Sages were intended to be a resource to help clinicians who had questions about the CDS or their role in supporting the intervention. They were also meant to provide at-the-elbow support (e.g., how to find a tool in the EHR) and act as a liaison for sharing CDS feedback with the study team.

Audit and feedback

The audit and feedback strategy was deployed in the form of newsletters distributed to clinicians and relevant clinical stakeholders monthly by email. Newsletters were tailored to each practice and included the proportion of the practice’s patients reporting severe SPPADE symptoms for each of the last three months. This allowed providers to view changes in the proportion of patients reporting severe symptoms over time. Earlier versions of the newsletter included patient reported moderate or severe symptoms and numbers of patients contacted by the SCMs. Later versions included the number of patients with severe symptoms who requested to speak with an SCM. This corresponded with the switch from an opt-out to an opt-in approach for patients to be contacted by an SCM if they reported severe symptoms. Our audit and feedback approach used newsletters with aggregated data for all patients within a practice rather than reports of individual provider performance based on feedback about the acceptability of provider-level reports in the oncology practice, especially in the context of Covid-19 practice pressures. Newsletters also included contact information for each practice’s Symptom Sages, instructions on how to refer patients to an SCM, and tips on how clinicians could locate a dashboard in the EHR showing their patients’ symptom scores relative to their colleagues.

Survey and interview recruitment and data collection

Individuals who served in the Symptom Sage role during the course of the study and who completed at least one prior research activity and had at least minimal engagement in the role (n = 17 of 20 Symptom Sages) were eligible to participate in surveys and interviews about their experience. We attempted to recruit 17 Symptom Sages (out of 20 total). Recruitment took place from August - October 2023. (See Fig. 2.) We emailed Symptom Sages, inviting them to participate in a brief, web-based survey and a 30-minute interview. The email described the purpose of the survey and interview, contained an embedded link to a web-based survey unique to each respondent, and instructed those interested in participating in an interview to email or call the research coordinator. The email also described the voluntary nature of participation, research purpose, what participation would consist of, the minimal risk nature of participation, and remuneration offered. In lieu of written informed consent, we received Institutional Review Board approval to consider completing the survey and contacting the research coordinator to schedule an interview assent for participation in the survey and interview, respectively. The second recruitment attempt was made via email. For the third and final recruitment attempt, the study principal investigator called non-responders to invite them to participate and the study coordinator emailed the survey link. Interviews were scheduled for 30-minutes via Microsoft Teams with an experienced qualitative researcher (SAM or JLR). SAM and JLR are both female with PhDs and extensive field experience in qualitative data collection. SAM was a Research Associate and JLR was an Associate Professor. Both have conducted qualitative research for numerous projects and attended ongoing trainings on qualitative research. Some participants may have been familiar with JLR from her role on the study’s implementation team, but SAM had a more minimal role in supporting study implementation. SAM and JLR clearly stated the goal of the interviews at the start. Interviews were semi-structured and recorded, but only the audio was uploaded and transcribed for analysis verbatim.

The survey and semi-structured interview guide were both developed specifically for this study and informed by the Consolidated Framework for Implementation Research (CFIR) 2.0 [25]. The CFIR 2.0 is a frequently used framework in health care research to identify barriers and facilitators to implementation of an innovation. The survey was intended to gather data about Symptom Sages perceptions of the E2C2 intervention and the Symptom Sage role, while interviews were intended to provide an opportunity to discuss Symptom Sages’ experiences in their role in more detail, including their perceptions of the implementation process and other implementation strategies that they were engaged to support (e.g., CDS adoption). Study team members reviewed drafts of the survey and interview guide, suggested revisions, and approved of the final versions. The survey contained 8 items asking about Symptom Sages’ experiences with the E2C2 intervention and 9 items asking about their experiences in the Symptom Sage role rated on 5-point Likert-Type scale (1 = strongly disagree, 5 = strongly agree). (See Supplement 1). The semi-structured interview guide contained 10 questions asking about familiarity with the intervention, understanding of the Symptom Sage role, support from the study’s implementation team, experiences engaging colleagues, relative importance of the intervention, impact of the pandemic, feedback from the study team and from colleagues, and future consideration for Symptom Sage recruitment. (See Supplement 2). The survey was estimated to take under 10 min to complete. Participants received $40 remuneration for survey completion and $40 remuneration for interview completion. All methods were approved by the Mayo Clinic Institutional Review Board, #18-007779.

Data analysis

Survey data were downloaded from Qualtrics and uploaded to Stata 18 to calculate the mean and standard deviation, median, and mode for each of the survey questions. All interview transcripts were independently coded to CFIR 2.0 constructs by JLR and SAM, who met to review the coded transcripts. Coding discrepancies were resolved via discussion and the final agreed upon coded transcripts were uploaded to NVivo, qualitative research software [26]. Text coded to each CFIR 2.0 construct was queried and independently reviewed by JLR and SAM, who produced thematic memos. JLR and SAM met to arrive at consensus around key themes via discussion and shared findings with the larger multidisciplinary study team for additional discussion and interpretation.

Results

Twelve Symptom Sages (70.59% response rate) participated between August and November 2023. Eleven completed surveys (64.71% response rate) and seven completed interviews (41.18% response rate) (See Table 1). On the survey items asking about experiences with the E2C2 intervention, six items received a median rating of 4 (agree): the intervention was well designed, improved upon existing symptom management processes, their practice saw the need for the intervention, their practice received sufficient information and training, their practice had the resources needed for intervention delivery, and their department leadership saw the value of the intervention. Two statements about the compatibility of the intervention with practice workflow and making slight modifications to deliver the intervention received a median rating of 3 (neither agree nor disagree). For survey items asking about the Symptom Sage role, the following six items received a median rating of 4 (agree): leadership was supportive of their role, they received adequate training for the role, they encouraged colleagues to use E2C2, their colleagues shared their thoughts with them about E2C2, and they knew how to communicate care team feedback to the E2C2 team. Two items (colleagues looked to them for guidance on using E2C2, their work environment made it easy to interact with colleagues about E2C2) received a median rating of 3 (neither agree nor disagree). One item about having adequate time for the Symptom Sage role received a median rating of 2 (disagree). The mean, median, and mode for all survey items is presented in Table 2. Symptom Sage training and knowledge acquisition was sufficient, but time to conduct the role’s responsibilities was not. Moreover, the process of identifying symptom sages did not always consider their motivation for filling the role. [Access to knowledge and information, implementation facilitators- capability]

The first key theme was related to the way that Symptom Sages were identified to be the facilitator and trained to take on the role. Interviews found that three of the Symptom Sages elected to take on the role. Of those three, two were nursing leaders who said their motivation was wanting to have an understanding of the intervention in order to support others in their practice. The third was an oncology physician who said he was interested because he was enthusiastic about symptom management. The other four Symptom Sages we interviewed said they were appointed into the practice facilitator role by their practice leadership. Some described being told they would be serving in the role without knowing much about the intervention. One Symptom Sage shared, “…before I got... dubbed a Symptom Sage... I had no clue what it was. But then... meeting with the team... and getting rolled out, I had a pretty good understanding of what it was and what its purpose was.” 016 While most Symptom Sages said the training was helpful, some Symptom Sages found the binder of materials to be too exhaustive and less helpful for understanding the intervention and their role. Some Symptom Sages described better learning what to do once the intervention was underway. One Symptom Sage stated, “…a lot of times even though you get training, you don’t become the expert until you experience it… when you’re actually doing it—it’s almost like hands-on learning. It’s just a lot— easier than information overload.” 009.

Despite being offered protected time in the first three months, Symptom Sages said it was challenging to fulfill the role, including attending ongoing meetings with the study team and with other Symptom Sages. Symptom Sages who were nursing leaders (compared with floor nurses and clinician providers) reported more time to attend meetings than others. Symptom Sages suggested fewer or shorter meetings, relying more on email to share information, and one-on-one instead of group meetings. One Symptom Sage who was a nursing leader and had other Symptom Sages within her practice shared, “…all the proactive information should be in email… but anything that is newly implemented… or... is a big deal, then those meetings might be once or twice a year … then we can... work as a clinic to say, okay, who can cover? … ‘cause we know it’s of value. These ongoing— ones, I don’t know if I would find coverage or ask them to step away.” 009 Symptom Sages who were advanced practice providers felt particularly challenged by the time pressure of filling in the role in the face of competing demands. One shared:“… I think... the training in the beginning was good…I just think it could have been rolled out more to the entire care team. I don’t think it had to be just one specific person... because, again, people aren’t gonna just listen to me, and I don’t always have the time to do one-on-one training, so I think if everyone were to get the training and the information upfront, that would be helpful... just to let everyone know, get everyone on the same page instead of putting it all on one person. Because again, we’re all busy.” 016.

Teams randomized to start delivering the intervention earlier in the trial noted some trial-and-error when implementing the intervention, which impacted time in their role. Teams that went later learned about the intervention from teams that went earlier.

Mixed attitudes about the intervention and their inability to modify the intervention impacted motivation in fulfilling their role [relative advantage, tension for change, innovation recipients- need]

The second theme was related to how Symptom Sages viewed the intervention and their practice’s need for it. Some Symptom Sages thought the intervention could help with symptom management and ease nursing burden by providing additional resources. These Symptom Sages chose to prioritize the intervention for patients’ benefit. One Symptom Sage stated, “… we would always try to bring you guys in— because it helped the load from us …if the patient was really experiencing a lot of fatigue, and they’re calling all the time, it would be helpful ’cause we don’t have that big kit that you guys have…” 003 However, other Symptom Sages deprioritized the intervention due to perceived redundancy of the intervention with existing practices. One Symptom Sage shared, “… I think that my clinic tended to be a little bit more hesitant or... had questions about like... why does this program exist? What is it adding that we can’t do... via palliative care or via nurse visits, et cetera… I think there remained a little bit of that hesitancy throughout the program.” 014 Less favorable impressions of the intervention design or features were described as limiting motivation to champion the intervention or encourage peers to use it. Symptom Sages generally thought there were good avenues for sharing feedback and concerns back with the study team (although some said group meetings for facilitators were not ideal). However, they perceived little opportunity to modify or adapt the intervention, meaning their concerns were not always addressed.

Perceptions varied according to the care team patient populations. For instance, one Symptom Sage thought the intervention was helpful in addressing symptoms in their patients who typically received oral chemotherapies and had less contact with their care team. Another team thought the intervention was less advantageous because their patients may have specific disease-related symptoms that their team is expert in and SCMs may be less familiar with. One Symptom Sage stated, “…for example if a patient has fatigue... one of the drugs we give can actually cause adrenal insufficiency so... a symptom sage might not know that and provide resources for the fatigue. And then.... we don’t always hear about that fatigue right away from the symptom sages…And it would have been probably better if they’d spoken to… our team nurses who are more familiar with our drugs and the possible side effects…” 014.

Some Symptom Sages also found that the remote nature of the CCM-based support that SCMs provided to patients was incompatible with their team-based approach to patient care. Epic communication from SCMs created additional clinical burden when messages sent by SCMs to providers about patients were long and generic. Some care teams would have preferred SCMs to be physically present to allow for informal conversations. One Symptom Sage shared, “… it would be helpful to have more direct contact with... Symptom Care Manager…being able to... actually talk with them and explain to them the things that our patients need— you know, like, what we hear all the time in the clinic …”017.

There were challenges motivating colleagues and gaining organizational support [high-level leaders- motivation, innovation deliverers- motivation]

The third theme related to the Symptom Sage role of motivating colleagues and championing intervention use. Support among their colleagues varied by role. Symptom sages said their high-level leaders were supportive of the intervention in theory but were somewhat removed from its implementation in the practice. Providers on their care teams, in contrast, would forget about the intervention if they were not reminded of it, in large part because of the decentralized nature of the model of care, where the SCM component was delivered remotely and was “behind-the-scenes” for care teams. One Symptom Sage shared “…despite my I think good effort… I still think that it never made everybody feel that it is a active, ongoing, available made thing... and a resource for everybody... mainly because there was not too much face-to-face and the fact that everything—was happening as a workflow behind…” 008 Symptom Sages also said it was hard to get some providers on their care teams excited about the intervention because they perceived the intervention would add time. This was described as especially challenging when they lacked time to motivate and encourage their colleagues. A couple of Symptom Sages tried to help providers with the EHR-enabled CDS and experienced mixed receptivity, care teams that saw the benefit to patients or providers having the greatest buy-in. One Symptom Sage stated, “…the... symptom manager, I offered to show them how to put that in their Epic... but no one ever took me up on that either…” 014.

The impact of the COVID-19 pandemic on intervention delivery varied based on whether practices went “live” before or after the worst of COVID. Some thought the COVID-related shift from in person to virtual Symptom Sage training may have lessened engagement and motivation to fulfill the Symptom Sage role. Symptom Sages who said their day-to-day operations were greatly impacted by the COVID-19 pandemic noted needing to figure out how to administer back-up strategies for completing the intervention patient questionnaires because of concerns about handing out tablets prior to visits, shifting priorities during COVID-19 peaks, and contending with provider burnout related to the pandemic and staffing impacts.

Symptom Sages liked seeing the monthly audit and feedback report data on the ePROM completion rate and patients’ symptoms in their cluster. Reports were emailed to them by the E2C2 research team (with the intention of motivating clinician adoption), but they did not report using it in practice. Symptom Sages noted the aggregate nature of this data limited its utility in motivating colleagues to increase E2C2 adoption. One Symptom Sage stated, “…unless you’re tracking specific patients… some of these are new. Some of the patients have passed… yes, these trends are changing, but are they of the same patients? And that makes it less... the data kind of less reliable for me.” 014.

Discussion

Through a mixed-methods study, we captured the experiences of individuals from oncology clinics who were expected to act as both clinical champions and practice facilitators (i.e., Symptom Sages) during a hybrid trial of a CCM-based, remote symptom management intervention to address symptoms experienced by patients with cancer. The main facilitator identified for the Symptom Sage role was that training to be a practice facilitator was perceived to be adequate, which was reflected in interview and survey responses. Additionally, during interviews some Symptom Sages shared that the E2C2 intervention was an extra resource for symptom management that could ease nursing burden, particularly if they did not routinely see patients in clinic or had exhausted all their resources for symptom management. Similarly, survey responses indicated that Symptom Sages thought their practice saw the need for the E2C2 intervention and that it improved upon existing processes for managing patients’ symptoms. However, in the interviews, some Symptom Sages expressed doubts about the helpfulness or need for the intervention and concerns about SCMs addressing patients’ symptoms.

A key barrier identified in both the survey and interviews was that Symptom Sages lacked adequate time for the Symptom Sage role. Additional barriers identified via the interviews included perceptions that print educational materials provided to Symptom Sages were too exhaustive (many were provided solely for reference) and perceptions that the study team was not able to modify the intervention in response to Symptom Sage feedback, which reduced role motivation. The survey also revealed that Symptom Sages did not strongly perceive their practices to have made slight modifications to intervention delivery. SCMs attended clinics and presented at case conferences in response to feedback desiring better integration of SCMs within oncology care teams. However, some Symptom Sages voiced their preference for even more embedding of SCMs within the clinic (rather than being a completely remotely-delivered intervention component).

Some Symptom Sages lacked the enthusiasm and motivation to promote adoption of the intervention typical of clinical champions, in part due to their perceptions that the intervention lacked compatibility with their clinic workflow. This may have been related to the fact that about half the Symptom Sages interviewed did not volunteer to be a Symptom Sage. Facilitators are sometimes chosen based on logistical convenience [15]. However, challenges identifying the right facilitators from the start, may later manifest in lack of engagement [27]. Our study team encouraged clinics to choose whoever they thought would be best for the role, yet required Symptom Sages to agree to fulfill the role after being briefed and encouraged to ask questions. However, it was difficult to ascertain prospective Symptom Sages’ motivation and enthusiasm some had limited understanding of the intervention. Engaging Symptom Sages prior to the start of the trial may have helped identify enthusiastic individuals and increase their investment, but may not have been feasible due to Symptom Sages’ time constraints.

Support from a more experienced mentor [28] and peers [15, 29] may be helpful to practice facilitators, and monthly meetings between supervisors and facilitators may help with role clarification and process-related discussions [30]. Further, having a group that practice facilitators are expected to provide progress updates to can help with accountability [29]. However, Symptom Sages did not always have time to join monthly touchpoints. E2C2 offered Symptom Sages 5% time coverage for training and early implementation efforts, but Symptom Sages found the paperwork required to receive this funding burdensome and chose not utilize these funds. Similarly, past research found facilitators struggled to preserve time for facilitation despite funding [15, 27]. This underscores the importance of working with practice leadership to ensure practice facilitators have time allotted to the facilitator role that is not infringed upon by competing clinical demands. If doing so is not feasible, using external facilitators who are not part of the practice and can protect their time better may be more feasible.

Symptom Sages had difficulty motivating provider colleagues to interact with the intervention (e.g., use the CDS, refer patients to SCMs), especially in the context of pandemic-related challenges. This was also reflected in Symptom Sages’ survey responses in which they neither agreed nor disagreed that their colleagues looked to them for guidance using E2C2. Challenges getting buy-in from clinician peers is consistent with findings from past research [14]. Moreover, lack of buy-in and motivation hindered care team adoption of the CDS designed to facilitate clinician implementation [31]. Many of the CDS elements were designed to help physicians and advanced practice practitioners (APPs) adopt the intervention, but only one Symptom Sage was an oncology physician. Research suggests clinical champions should possess influence in the form of formal or informal authority or the savvy to navigate organizational culture and context, giving them the ability to work across silos [32]. It is possible that oncology physicians would have been more influential as Symptom Sages due to their level of influence within the professional role hierarchy within medicine.

Other research has similarly noted pandemic-related challenges faced by practice coaches, acting as practice facilitators, in trying to recruit primary clinics to participate in a trial during this time [33]. In our study, oncology practices had already been randomized to start the intervention at one of five timepoints, before the pandemic occurred. However, competing demands that practices faced because of the pandemic complicated some parts of intervention delivery and dampened providers’ and Symptom Sages’ enthusiasm for implementing something new in clinic given other COVID-related changes. Pandemic related changes to how care was provided (e.g., more telehealth appointments) may also explain why Symptom Sages neither agreed nor disagreed with the statement on the survey that their work environment made it easy for them to interact with their colleagues about E2C2.

Our implementation team modified our planned audit and feedback strategy to minimize provider stress by providing practices with data on the proportion of patients within the practice reporting severe symptoms. A similar study of ePROMs implementation in cancer clinics noted that they took a similar approach to providing audit and feedback data at the aggregate level based on sites’ preferences [7]. However, Symptom Sages were unable to track changes in patients’ symptoms over time. Providing clinician level data may have offered Symptom Sages the opportunity to follow up with individual physicians who had a large proportion of patients reporting severe symptoms.

Strengths and limitations

Our evaluation adds to the literature by highlighting the challenges that practice facilitators faced supporting implementation of a remote symptom monitoring intervention for patients with cancer using ePROMs. Our findings can help others implementing similar ePROM- and CCM-based interventions to determine whether practice facilitators may be a useful implementation strategy and anticipate potential barriers. Soliciting direct feedback from practice facilitators via interview and survey methods helped us understand how we could have better supported their role. While the number of participants in this study was moderate for this type of study design, data were sufficient for the scope of this targeted inquiry [34] and in line with studies suggesting similar sample sizes for focused interview studies [35]. Furthermore, the combination of surveys and interviews provides a more comprehensive assessment (i.e., data triangulation) [36].

We administered the survey via an identifiable web link to allow us to track participation, which may have biased practice facilitators’ responses due to the lack of anonymity. However, surveys were administered after the study was over and responses were reported in aggregate, increasing the likelihood of honest reporting. Further, facilitators who participated in interviews appeared comfortable critiquing the intervention and expressing challenges pertaining to their role, increasing our confidence they were comfortable expressing negative opinions via their survey responses.

Triangulation of the survey and interview data provided a richer perspective on the Symptom Sage role. Survey responses were generally positive (4 out of 5 on a Likert scale from 1 = Strongly disagree to 5 = Strongly agree), while interviews appeared more skewed towards challenges. It is possible that when given the opportunity for more in-depth discussions about their role, they took the time to expand on barriers more so than strengths. Moreover, the barriers discussed during the interviews are consistent with items receiving lower ratings on the survey, suggesting this may have indeed been the case.

Only half of the survey respondents participated in an interview. While the sample is small for comparison, there were only two survey items that showed potentially significant differences between survey respondents who did versus did not participate in an interview. For those who participated in an interview, the median rating for receiving adequate training for the Symptom Sage role was 4 and the median rating for colleagues looking to them for guidance on how to use E2C2 was 3, compared to a median for 3 and 2, respectively, for non-interviewees.

Another limitation of our study is that we administered the survey and conducted interviews with Symptom Sages approximately 6–9 months after the study had ended, potentially impacting participant recall. Further, because our study took place during the COVID-19 pandemic, it is unclear to what extent our findings generalize to other oncology clinics in a post-pandemic environment. Additionally, only three of the Symptom Sages we interviewed took on the Sympton Sage role because of their desire or enthusiasm to fulfill the role. The other four we interviewed were volunteered into the role and many of them lacked the time or motivation to fulfill it. It is unclear to what extent the challenges we observed were due to the Symptom Sage selection process and lack of protected, funded time versus challenges using practice facilitators as a strategy for ePROMS implementation in oncology practices more broadly. Providing Symptom Sages adequate time to fulfill the role may have made this strategy more effective and is likely a necessary component for this strategy to be successful. Lastly, we did not include data from notes taken during Symptom Sage trainings or monthly touchpoints, which may have helped identify barriers to using this implementation strategy.

Conclusion

To implement a CCM-based remote symptom management intervention for patients with cancer using ePROMS, we relied heavily on individuals from medical oncology care teams, known as Symptom Sages, who elected or were identified by their leadership to support implementation in the clinical practice and with peers. Interviews and surveys conducted with Symptom Sages following the trial revealed that despite robust upfront education, Symptom Sages encountered challenges including lack of time; mixed perceptions of the intervention, which contributed to lack of motivation to fulfill the Symptom Sage role; and difficulty motivating colleagues and gaining organizational support for the intervention. Future efforts should provide funding and verify that practices are able to allocate individuals time to engage in practice facilitation activities, although different mechanisms may be needed for practice-based efforts without extramural funding. If practices are unable to allocate time to members of the care team to engage in facilitation activities, external facilitators who do not have competing clinical demands may be more appropriate to hold the practice facilitation role. In this case, members of the practice may be better suited to focus on the role of clinical champion by voicing support and encouragement to colleagues without having the increased time commitment of engaging in facilitation activities. This study adds to the scarce literature on how to implement ePROMS as part of a CCM-based intervention in oncology clinics.
