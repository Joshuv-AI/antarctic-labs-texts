Authors' response to the commentary on “do certain blood groups increase COVID-19 severity and mortality?”
Authors: Tegene Atamenta Kitaw, Ribka Nigatu Haile
Published: 2026

Dear Editor,

We thank Kokiwar et al. [1] for their interest in our article and for the opportunity to respond. While we respect the commentators' perspective, we believe their concerns do not undermine the validity or conclusions of our study, and we wish to address each point directly.

However, before addressing the specific methodological points, we must first correct a fundamental mischaracterization of our conclusions. The commentators frame our findings as concluding that “ABO is irrelevant to COVID-19 outcomes.” This does not reflect what we concluded. Our paper explicitly states that “no statistically significant association was found between ABO blood group and COVID-19 severity and mortality” in this cohort, and we explicitly called for further well-designed controlled studies. These are epistemologically distinct statements. In epidemiology, the absence of a statistically significant association in a single study does not establish biological irrelevance a point well established in the methodological literature. Absence of evidence is not evidence of absence. Furthermore, association and causation are conceptually separate; claiming no association was detected in this cohort is fundamentally different from claiming ABO blood group is irrelevant to COVID-19 outcomes at a biological or population level. We respectfully urge the commentators to engage with what was actually concluded rather than with a stronger claim that was never made.

On the question of blood group distribution and selection bias, the commentators draw a comparison with Amhara regional blood donor data to suggest our cohort may be atypical. We respectfully note that this comparison is not appropriate. Eka Kotebe General Hospital was the designated national COVID-19 treatment center during the study period, receiving patients from across Ethiopia not exclusively from the Amhara region. ABO blood group distribution varies considerably across Ethiopian ethnic and regional populations, and our cohort's distribution reflects this national diversity. Furthermore, blood donors are a self-selected, healthy population and are not representative of the general or hospitalized population. The suggestion that the observed distribution implies upstream ABO-related selection into hospitalization remains speculative and is not supported by data presented in the commentary.

Regarding statistical power, we acknowledge that subgroup cell counts for blood groups AB and O were relatively small, as is common in single-center retrospective studies. However, we would note that our multivariable analysis alongside with Kaplan-Meier survival curves, log-rank tests, and both multinomial and binary logistic regression with appropriate goodness-of-fit testing consistently showed no statistically significant association across all blood groups and all outcomes. The confidence intervals for all ABO groups overlapped substantially with the null, and no consistent directional trend was observed that would suggest a clinically meaningful effect being missed. The commentators' power calculation is based on assumed baseline risks and effect sizes that are not established in our population. Underpowered studies can fail to detect true effects, but they cannot manufacture consistent null findings across multiple independent statistical tests. Our call for larger studies in the conclusion already reflects appropriate scientific humility on this point.

On residual confounding, the commentators suggest that sex, obesity, vaccination status and calendar period should have been included. We wish to clarify that our study was conducted during the early phase of the COVID-19 pandemic in Ethiopia, prior to widespread vaccine availability. Vaccination status was therefore not a relevant confounder in this cohort. Regarding obesity and other socioeconomic variables, these were not recorded in clinical charts a well-recognized constraint of retrospective studies in low-resource settings. We are confident that the covariates included in our multivariable models represent the primary clinical determinants of COVID-19 severity and mortality available in this setting.

Finally, we wish to emphasize that our findings are consistent with multiple published studies from diverse settings including the United States and Turkey [2], [3], and that the broader literature on this question remains genuinely inconclusive. Our study contributes data from an African low-resource context that is significantly underrepresented in the global evidence base. The absence of an association in our cohort is a legitimate and reproducible scientific finding, not merely a consequence of methodological limitations.

We stand by our conclusion that ABO blood group was not significantly associated with COVID-19 severity or mortality in this cohort, and we maintain that well-designed larger studies are needed to resolve this question definitively across diverse populations.
