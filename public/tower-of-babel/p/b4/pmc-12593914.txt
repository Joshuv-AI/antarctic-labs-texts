Impact of muscle mass on mortality in patients with rheumatoid arthritis: insights from NHANES 1999–2018
Authors: Zhi-Ming Ouyang, Jie Pan, Hui-Qin Zou, Pei-Wen Jia, Ying Yang, Kui-Min Yang, Jian-Zi Lin, Yao-Wei Zou
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundMuscle loss is linked to multiple adverse outcomes, but its impact on rheumatoid arthritis (RA) prognosis is unclear. This study aimed to examine the association between muscle mass and mortality in RA patients.MethodsRA patients from the NHANES database were followed for survival until December 31, 2021. Muscle mass was measured using dual X-ray absorptiometry, low muscle mass was defined as appendicular skeletal muscle mass index (ASMI) < 7.0 kg/m² in men or < 5.5 kg/m² in women. The relationship between ASMI and mortality was analyzed using weighted Cox regression.ResultsThe study included 892 participants (weighted mean [SE] age 52.22 [0.59] years, 57.36% female). During a median (SE) follow-up of 11.44 (0.33) years, 291 deaths (32.62%) were recorded, of which 197 (28.23%) were attributed to cardiovascular disease. In fully adjusted models, a 1 kg/m²increase in ASMI was associated with decreased all-cause and cardiovascular mortality risk by 34% (HR = 0.66, 95% CI 0.50–0.86) and 40% (HR = 0.60, 95% CI 0.38–0.93), respectively. When ASMI stratified, RA with low muscle mass had a 1.42-fold higher risk of all-cause mortality (HR = 1.42, 95% CI 1.01-2.00) and a 2.58-fold higher risk of cardiovascular mortality (HR = 2.58, 95% CI 1.18–5.62) than those with normal muscle mass. Restricted cubic spline analysis showed a nonlinear association between ASMI and cardiovascular (Pnonlinear = 0.04) but not for all-cause mortality (Pnonlinear = 0.25).ConclusionsMuscle loss in RA patients is linked to higher mortality risk, underscoring the need to recognize its harmful effects.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12937-025-01220-7.Keywords: Rheumatoid arthritis, Mortality, Cardiovascular, NHANES, Appendicular skeletal muscle mass index

Background

Rheumatoid arthritis (RA) is a common chronic inflammatory disease causing symmetrical joint inflammation, pain, swelling, and impaired function [1]. RA is a major global public health concern, with a worldwide prevalence of 0.5–1.0%, and its burden will keep rising, reaching 31.7 million affected individuals globally by 2050 [2]. Aside from the deterioration in the joints, RA has an impact on daily activities, work capability, and social participation, lowering overall quality of life and life expectancy [1]. Studies show that RA patients have a 50–60% higher mortality rate than healthy individuals, with cardiovascular disease (CVD) being the main cause of premature death [3]. Studies showed that RA patients have a 1.5-fold higher risk of developing CVD than the general population [4, 5]. Understanding factors affecting mortality, especially cardiovascular mortality, in RA is crucial for developing effective management strategies.

Previous researches on the related factors of CVD mainly focused on traditional risk factors, joint inflammation, and therapeutic drugs for RA [6, 7]. However, in a large international RA cohort, traditional risk factors and RA characteristics explained 70% of CVD risk, with 30% remaining unaccounted for [8]. Thus, further investigations into specific risk factors associated with CVD in RA are required, to prevent premature death and improve long-term health benefits. Muscle loss, commonly with aging, is linked to adverse outcomes like falls, metabolic disorders, frailty, disability, and mortality among the general population [9–12]. Recently, mounting evidence shows that muscle loss is also a notable comorbidity of RA, which affects approximately 31% of individuals with RA and was associated with poor prognosis, such as joint damage and functional impairment [13–16]. However, the understanding of how muscle mass is related to mortality in RA remains limited. Thus, we aim to examine the link between muscle mass and mortality in RA adults using National Health and Nutrition Examination Survey (NHANES) data.

Methods

Study design and population

This cohort study used data from the NHANES, a biennial program that assesses the health and nutrition status of the US civilian population through a complex, multistage probability sampling method [17]. The protocols of the NHANES study were approved by the Institutional Review Board of the National Center of Health Statistics. All participants provided informed written consent at enrollment.

We used data from US adults aged ≥ 18 years who participated in 8 NHANES cycles (1999–2006 and 2011–2018). RA diagnosis was based on self-report survey responses. Participants answered two questions: “Has a doctor or other health professional ever told you that you have arthritis?” and “Which type of arthritis was it?”. Those who answered “yes” to the first and identified “RA” for the second were assigned to the RA group.

Assessments of muscle mass

In NHANES, whole-body dual energy X-ray absorptiometry (DEXA) was performed using Hologic QDR 4500 A (1999–2006 cycles) and Discovery model A (2011–2018 cycles) densitometers. Appendicular skeletal mass was calculated as the total lean mass of arms and legs, excluding bone mineral content. The appendicular skeletal mass index (ASMI) was defined as this mass divided by height squared (kg/m²). Low muscle mass was defined as ASMI < 7.0 kg/m² in men or < 5.5 kg/m² in women, based on EWGSOP2 criteria [18].

Ascertainment of mortality

Follow-up and mortality data were obtained by linking NHANES records to the National Death Index. All-cause mortality includes death from any cause. Cardiovascular mortality was defined using ICD-10 codes for heart disease (I00-I09, I11, I13, I20-I51) and cerebrovascular disease (I60-I69).

Assessment of covariates

Demographic and lifestyle factors were collected via standardized questionnaires during household interviews. These included age, gender, race/ethnicity (Mexican American, non-Hispanic Black, non-Hispanic White, and other races), marital status (never married, married/living with a partner, widowed/divorced/separated), education level (less than high school, high school graduate, some college or above), family poverty income ratio (PIR, ≤ 1.0, 1.0–3.0, ≥ 3.0), smoking status [never smoker (defined as < 100 cigarettes in a lifetime), current smoker (defined as 100 cigarettes in a lifetime), former smoker (defined as 100 cigarettes and had quit smoking)], and drinking status [never drinker (consumed < 12 drinks in a lifetime), current drinker (consumed ≥ 12 drinks in one year), former drinker (consumed ≥ 12 drinks in one year but not in the last year, or did not drink in the last year but consumed ≥ 12 drinks in a lifetime)]. Body weight and height were measured during physical exams, and body mass index (BMI) was calculated as weight divided by height squared. The estimated glomerular filtration rate (eGFR) was calculated using the Chronic Kidney Disease Epidemiology Collaboration formula (2009) based on creatinine. Comorbidity conditions [chronic kidney disease (CKD), hypertension, diabetes, hyperlipidemia, CVD, malignancy] were defined based on self-reported diagnoses or prescribed medications.

Statistical analysis

All analyses used sample weights, stratification, and clustering to ensure nationally representative estimates following NHANES guidelines. Data are shown as unweighted frequencies (weighted percentages) for categorical variables and weighted means with standard errors (SE) for continuous variables. Group differences were compared using weighted t-tests for continuous variables and weighted χ² tests with Rao-Scott correction for categorical variables.

Kaplan-Meier curves assessed muscle loss (low vs. normal muscle mass)-mortality associations, with log-rank tests determining significance. Weighted Cox regression models estimated mortality hazard ratio (HR) and 95% confidence interval (CI). Follow-up was from interview date to death or 31 December 2021, whichever came first. Multivariate analyses adjusted for age, gender, race/ethnicity, marital status, education, family PIR, smoking, drinking, CRP, BMI, eGFR, hypertension, diabetes, hyperlipidemia, CVD, and malignancy. The HR-mortality relationship was graphically assessed using restricted cubic spline (RCS) plots with four knots (at the 5th, 35th, 65th, and 95th percentiles).

We further stratified analyses by age (< 60, or ≥ 60 years), gender (male, or female), race/ethnicity (non-Hispanic White, or others), education status (less than college, or some college or above), marital status (married, or others), family PIR (< 3.0, or ≥ 3.0), smoking (never, or now or former), drinking (never, or now or former), CKD (yes, or no), hypertension (yes, or no), diabetes (yes, or no), hyperlipidemia (yes, or no), CVD (yes, or no), and malignancy (yes, or no). Multiplicative interactions between ASMI and these variables were tested using likelihood ratio tests. All analyses were conducted in R (version 4.2.3), with P < 0.05 indicating statistical significance.

Results

Demographic and clinical characteristics of the participants

Data from NHANES 1999 to 2006 and 2011 to 2018 incorporated 80,630 participants in total and the initial sample included 2290 adults with self-reported RA diagnosed by a clinician. After excluding the population with missing data and loss to follow-up, a total of 892 participants were included in final analysis (Fig. 1). The mean (SE) age of the participants was 52.22 (0.59) years, with female comprising 57.36%. There were 179 (7.06%) of Mexican American, 216 (13.39%) non-Hispanic Black, 386 (66.75%) non-Hispanic White, and 111 (12.80%) participants were categorized as other races. Besides, 32.84% participants were current smoking and 64.39% were current drinking. There were 16.14% participants with a history of CKD, 50.16% with hypertension, 15.57% with diabetes, 77.71% with hyperlipidemia, 17.55% with CVD 12.95% with malignancy and at baseline (Table 1).

Clinical characteristics of RA patients according to muscle mass

Among the study population, 134 (14.37%) of patients with RA were categorized in low muscle mass. Compared with those with normal muscle mass, participants with low muscle mass were older (56.96 [1.52] years vs. 51.43 [0.63] years), higher percentages of non-Hispanic White (77.53% vs. 64.94%) and more current smoker (50.85% vs. 29.82%), but had lower levels of BMI (21.72 [0.28] kg/m2 vs. 29.88 [0.33] kg/m2), lower percentages of hyperlipidemia (67.89% vs. 79.36%, all P < 0.05, Table 1). In addition, no significant differences were found in gender, marital status, education level, family income, CRP and eGFR, as well as percentages of drinker, history of CKD, hypertension, diabetes, CVD and malignancy between the 2 group (all P > 0.05).

The mortality rates in RA patients during follow-up

During a median (SE) follow-up of 11.44 (0.33) years, 291 (32.62%) deaths were recorded, of which 90 (30.93%) cardiovascular death (including heart diseases [27.49%] and cerebrovascular diseases [3.44%]), 63 (21.65%) were attributed to malignancy, 20 (6.87%) were attributed to chronic lower respiratory diseases, 12 (4.12%) were attributed to diabetes, 9 (3.09%) were attributed to Alzheimer’s disease, 8 (2.76%) were attributed to nephropathy, 6 (2.06%) were attributed to influenza and pneumonia and 83 (28.52%) were attributed to others (Supplemental Figure S1).

Deceased subjects were significantly older and predominantly non-Hispanic White, widowed/divorced/separated, drinker, higher level of CRP, and higher proportion of history of CKD, hypertension, diabetes, hyperlipidemia, CVD and malignancy, but had lower level of education levels, family income and eGFR compared with survivors. Importantly, deceased subjects had significantly lower level of BMI (27.43 [0.37] kg/m2 vs. 29.12 [0.40] kg/m2) and ASMI (6.89 [0.09] kg/m2 vs. 7.64 [0.09] kg/m2), as well as higher percentages of low muscle mass (24.77% vs. 10.06%, all P < 0.001, Supplemental Table S1).

Association of muscle mass and mortality risk in RA patients

Kaplan-Meier curves showed RA with low muscle mass had lower rates of all-cause and cardiovascular survival (both log-rank tests: P < 0.05, Fig. 2). In fully adjusted models, a 1 kg/m² increase in ASMI reduced all-cause mortality risk by 34% (HR = 0.66, 95% CI 0.50–0.86) and cardiovascular mortality risk by 40% (HR = 0.60, 95% CI 0.38–0.93, both P < 0.05, Table 2). Similar results were observed when individuals were categorized by ASMI levels (both P < 0.05, Table 2). After adjusting for covariates, RA patients with low muscle mass had a 1.42-fold higher risk of all-cause mortality (HR = 1.42, 95% CI 1.01-2.00) and a 2.58-fold higher risk of cardiovascular mortality (HR = 2.58, 95% CI 1.18–5.62) compared to those with normal muscle mass. The dose-response associations between ASMI and mortality are shown in Fig. 3. A nonlinear relationship was observed between ASMI and cardiovascular mortality (P = 0.04 for nonlinear), but not for all-cause mortality (P = 0.25 for nonlinear) in restricted cubic spline analyses (both P < 0.01 for overall).

Discussion

In this study, we firstly explored the relationship between low muscle mass and the risk of mortality in patients with RA through a nationwide population-based cohort. It was demonstrated that decreased ASMI was associated with elevated risk of all-cause and cardiovascular mortality, and RA patients with low muscle mass had a 1.45-fold and 2.48-fold higher risk of all-cause and cardiovascular mortality than those with normal muscle mass. These findings offered the understanding of muscle mass and mortality relationship, and could be considered in the clinical management of prognosis in RA patients.

RA, being a most prevalent chronic inflammatory diseases, has garnered significant attention, particularly concerning survival. It is noteworthy that a prior meta-analysis, which examined 11 longitudinal observational studies of early RA cohorts from 1955 to 1995 and included 51,819 RA patients, revealed that RA patients experience a 1.5-fold increase in mortality compared to the general population [19]. This increased mortality risk is attributed to the chronic inflammatory nature of the disease, which affects multiple organ systems and leads to a variety of complications. Modern therapeutic approaches, including the use of disease-modifying anti-rheumatic drugs (DMARDs) and biologic agents, have significantly improved disease control and reduced inflammation [20]. However, the residual risk of mortality persists, for example, recent population-based studies from the UK, Norway, and Sweden suggest that, despite decreasing mortality rates, RA remains linked to higher mortality [21–23], particularly after 10 years of disease duration [23]. In the present study, we found that 32.62% of RA patients died during a median follow-up of 11.4 years, which was higher than that in US general adults (10.5%) [24], and CVD is the leading cause of premature mortality in RA patients, accounts for 30.93%, follow by malignancy (21.65%), which was like previous study [25]. In brief, these findings indicate that RA patients still experience a heavier burden of mortality and merely controlling inflammation is insufficient to enhance the prognosis of RA, which highlighting the need for more comprehensive management strategies.

Previous studies raised that older, male, long-standing disease duration, chronic disease, seropositivity and higher disease activity are important factors associated with mortality in RA patients [23, 26, 27]. Moreover, recent studies have identified several other new risk factors that contribute to the high mortality rate in RA, such as depression [28], malnutrition [29], elevated blood cadmium [30] or uric acid [31], et al. In this study, we firstly reported that muscle loss was associated with higher risk of mortality in RA patients, and 1 kg/m2 increase in ASMI, the risk of all-cause and cardiovascular mortality decreased by 37% and 44%, respectively. Muscle loss is a common disorder caused by aging, and is linked to faster CVD progression, higher mortality and fall risk, and lower quality of life, especially in older adults [9]. RA patients are susceptible to muscle loss, which may be related to the following reasons, such as disuse atrophy, chronic inflammation, hyper-metabolic state, and malnutrition, etc [14]. A meta-regression analysis, encompassing 17 studies with a total of 3,140 RA subjects, revealed that nearly 31% of RA patients experienced muscle loss, which was significantly higher than that observed in the general population [13]. Furthermore, our previous study found that RA patients with muscle loss were associated with about 1.5-folds increasing risk of active disease, 2.5-folds risk of physical dysfunction and 2.7-folds risk of radiographic joint damage [15, 16]. These data in a median 11.44-year longitudinal prospective cohort first explored the link between muscle loss and mortality risk in RA, providing robust evidence that muscle mass is positively associated with mortality in RA patients. Though patients with low muscle mass had significantly lower BMI, the result of multivariate Cox analyses showed that ASMI remained significantly negatively correlated with the risk of mortality in RA patients after adjusted for BMI, which supports muscle mass as an independent predictor. In addition, our previous study showed that RA patients with normal BMI also had higher prevalence of low muscle mass than controls (43.3% vs. 22.1%) [32], which support the importance of early monitoring of muscle mass to mitigate adverse mortality outcomes.

Though the exact mechanisms linking muscle loss to mortality are unclear, several potential pathways exist. First, skeletal muscle acts as an endocrine organ, releasing myokines that reduce inflammation and insulin resistance [33, 34]. Reduced muscle mass may thus worsen inflammation and drive CVD development. Second, skeletal muscle is crucial for glucose homeostasis and carbohydrate metabolism [35]. Muscle loss can disrupt glucose balance, alter lipid metabolism, and cause endothelial dysfunction [36]. Additionally, muscle loss lowers basal metabolic rate and physical activity, increasing fat mass [37]. All this play key roles in the development of CVD and then increasing risk of mortality. Clinically, our research highlighted the important effect of muscle loss on mortality risk in RA, which imply the significance of muscle improvement for the prevention of mortality in RA patients. Interventions such as resistance training, which enhances muscle mass and suppresses inflammation via myokines secretion, should be prioritized. Combining resistance exercises with anti-inflammatory therapies (e.g., IL-6 inhibitors) may yield synergistic benefits. Additionally, nutritional strategies-such as leucine supplementation, shown to stimulate muscle protein synthesis-could complement pharmacological treatments [38–40]. Future trials should evaluate whether such multimodal approaches reduce mortality risk in RA.

Our study has important clinical implications by establishing a longitudinal link between muscle mass and mortality risk in RA patients, potentially improving mortality risk identification. However, there were still several limitations. First, the observational nature of the study, which cannot conclusively determine causality. Second, RA diagnosis based on self-reported questions without classification criteria. Third, there were lack of data on disease severity of RA, such as RA disease activity, functional limitation, and radiographic joint damage, which may introduce confounding bias. However, we found that there were no significantly different in CRP, an inflammatory marker, between normal or low muscle mass group. In addition, we have adjusted for CRP in multivariate Cox analyses, and the result showed that ASMI remained significantly negatively correlated with the risk of mortality in RA patients. Forth, a substantial proportion of RA patients (n = 1396) were excluded due to missing DEXA measurements, which may introduce selection bias, as participants with severe disability or comorbidities might be less likely to undergo DEXA scanning. Lastly, the study’s focus on US patients limits the generalizability of the findings to RA patients from other populations. Future large-scale, multi-center prospective studies are needed to address this limitation.

Conclusions

In conclusion, this study found that low muscle mass is associated with increased mortality risk in RA patients, emphasizing the need to recognize the harmful effects of muscle loss. Our findings support early monitoring of muscle mass to mitigate adverse mortality outcomes.
