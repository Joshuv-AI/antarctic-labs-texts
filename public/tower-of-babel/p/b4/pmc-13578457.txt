New orientation technique of aligned D–Q reference frame for controlling doubly-fed induction generators
Authors: Mahmoud M. Elgamasy, Mohamed S. Zaky, Tamer A. Kawady, Abdel-Maksoud I. Taalab, Nagy I. Elkalashy
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Doubly-Fed Induction Generators (DFIG) control requires orienting the d–q reference frame in a specific position. The d-axis is aligned with the grid voltage vector in grid voltage orientation. Conventionally, a Phase-Locked Loop (PLL) scheme is used to determine the position of the voltage vector. However, if there is a disturbance in the rotational speed of the voltage vector—due to frequency changes or phase angle disturbances—the PLL will produce an unstable output, disrupting the control performance particularly if the PLL gains are not accurately tuned. This paper proposes a new orientation method to demonstrate its effectiveness and reliability under frequency and phase angle disturbances. The proposed control is independent of the PLL scheme. To validate the proposed orientation scheme, a complete experimental test bench for a small-scale DFIG system was built, utilizing two Texas Instruments dSPACE boards DS1104 and DS1102 to control the Rotor-Side Converter (RSC) and Grid-Side Converter (GSC), respectively. The proposed orientation method and the comprehensive control of the DFIG are evaluated. The system’s performance under different operating conditions is examined to guarantee independent active and reactive power control. The results prove that operating without a PLL, the proposed orientation technique ensures stable and effective performance under frequency and phase angle disturbances. A performance comparison of the proposed orientation technique and PLL technique is also provided to validate the efficacy of the proposed orientation approach.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at https://doi.org/10.1038/s41598-026-62037-w.

Introduction

The doubly-fed induction generator (DFIG) based wind turbine, operating in a variable-speed regime, is one of the most prominent topologies due to its excellent grid support capability. It offers independent control of active and reactive power, enhancing system flexibility. Additionally, the use of partial-scale converters provides a more cost-effective and efficient solution, with converters rated at 30% of the machine’s total capacity1–4.

Fig. 1DFIG configuration.

DFIG configuration.

The DFIG configuration, shown in Fig. 1, consists of a wound-rotor asynchronous machine. The stator is connected to the grid, while the rotor is excited through back-to-back converters. The Rotor-Side Converter (RSC) and Grid-Side Converter (GSC) require coordinated control to manage the power flow in DFIG systems. To ensure stable operation and smooth integration of the DFIG with the grid, precise control of the converters is essential. If the doubly-fed induction machine operates as a motor, both the stator and rotor are typically supplied by converters fed from DC sources, eliminating the need for synchronization with the grid5. However, when the machine operates as a generator, the stator is directly connected to the grid. Therefore, in DFIG systems, orientation with respect to the grid voltage vector is commonly employed to ensure proper synchronization and accurate power control. The Phase-Locked Loop (PLL) plays a crucial role in DFIG control as it facilitates orienting the d–q reference frame with the grid voltage6,7.

The main challenge arises from the dependence of PLL-based orientation techniques on precise tuning of its parameters. Inappropriate tuning can lead to oscillations, degraded dynamic response, or even instability under grid disturbances. To address these limitations, the proposed method introduces a PLL-free orientation scheme that eliminates the need for PLL parameters tuning while ensuring accurate alignment of the reference frame with the grid voltage and stable control performance.

Numerous studies in the literature have attempted to enhance the stability of DFIG systems while still employing a PLL. Table 1 summarizes some articles that discuss different aspects related to the inclusion of the PLL. The common challenges in these studies include the need for careful tuning of PLL parameters, limited stability due to PLL dynamics, and high sensitivity to parameter variations.

The dynamics of DFIG systems with integrated PLL are examined from various perspectives. In8, the PLL monitors frequency changes to support the virtual inertia controller, with an additional stabilizer designed to improve performance and limit damping. In9, the PLL helps estimate the angle, ensuring a smooth transition of the DFIG system with the grid. The behavior of the PLL in DFIG systems is discussed in10, highlighting potential oscillation problems that can lead to instability. When sub-synchronous oscillations occur, the performance of the PLL-based controller is affected, requiring improvements to effectively suppress these oscillations11. The stability of DFIG systems under asymmetric fault conditions is analyzed in12, focusing on PLL adjustments to enhance dynamic stability. Moreover, the ability of DFIG wind turbines to provide inertial support to the power system, assisting with frequency stabilization during disturbances, is explored through various PLL settings in13. Overall, the stability of the system in different configurations is shown to depend significantly on the dynamics of the PLL14–16. Moreover, advanced control philosophies such as model predictive control (MPC) still rely on PLL-based voltage angle estimation17,18. This reveals an important research gap, namely the continued dependence on closed-loop synchronization methods using PLL that require parameter adjustment and whose performance can vary under different disturbance conditions.

In contrast to these studies, the present work proposes a PLL-free orientation technique. The grid voltage phase could be estimated with very high accuracy without the need to tune any parameters with achieving robust alignment and stable control performance. The proposed method demonstrates stable control, even under grid frequency variations or phase angle changes. Additionally, an experimental prototype is tested with the inclusion of the proposed strategy in its control system. Various conditions, including DFIG start-up and control of active and reactive power, are tested, and the results show strong performance across all scenarios.

The paper is organized as follows: “Modelling and control of DFIG” covers the modeling and control of DFIG, while the proposed technique is presented in “Proposed orientation technique”. The proposed method for determining the grid voltage angle is validated numerically in “Numerical validation of the proposed voltage angle estimation under grid frequency disturbances”. The DFIG test system used is explained in “DFIG test system”, and the validation results are discussed in “Validation results”, followed by a comparison study in “Performance comparison of the proposed orientation technique and PLL technique”, and the conclusion in “Conclusions”.

Rotor-side converter control

The RSC control is implemented based on the grid voltage orientation, aligning the grid voltage vector with the direct axis of the reference frame, as shown in Fig. 2. To achieve this orientation, the position of the voltage vector needs to be determined. This is performed conventionally using a phase-locked loop (PLL)12,13, however, this paper presents another orientation scheme. Aligning the grid voltage in this way ensures that the voltage quadrature component is null. Assuming the stator resistance is negligible, the stator flux linkage vector will align with the negative quadrature-axis, resulting in a zero direct component. Mathematically, this can be described as:

The obtained distinctive equations in (12) clarify the decoupling feature between the active and reactive power which represents the main notion in control design.

As depicted in Fig. 3, the RSC control comprises two loops. The first loop compares the set points of power levels with the measured values, utilizing PI controllers for error processing to determine the reference values of rotor current components. The second inner loop compares the reference values with the actual current components. Upon regulating the current errors, compensating terms are added to obtain the direct and quadrature components of the converter output voltages. These components are then transformed into their corresponding three-phase values for pulse width modulation, generating the pulses necessary to drive the converter.

These calculations are executed at every time step and are independent of grid-frequency measurements, without any need for parameter tuning. Consequently, during frequency disturbances, the reference-frame orientation remains stable, and the control system continues to operate properly. This makes the proposed method inherently robust to grid-frequency variations, which is particularly beneficial in weak grids or systems with high inverter-based generation, where frequency stability is a key concern.

Performance during start-up of DFIG

The proposed orientation method is tested during the start-up of the DFIG system. Traditionally, starting the DFIG involves control modifications to synchronize the stator with the grid before switching21–23. In contrast, the adopted start-up procedure for the DFIG system does not require any control modifications. Initially, the prime mover is operated at a speed close to the synchronous speed. Next, the stator is connected to the grid by closing circuit breaker CB1, followed by connecting the rotor to the RSC through CB2. The GSC is then connected to the grid by closing CB3. During this process, the control pulses to both the RSC and GSC are disabled. Subsequently, the GSC control is activated to regulate the DC link voltage to 270 V. Finally, the start-up process is completed by enabling the RSC control pulses.

The results obtained are presented in Fig. 13, where the DFIG machine operates at a rotational speed approximately 5% lower than the synchronous speed, as shown in Fig. 13a. Selection of PLL gains

The proposed study in this paper presents an alternative method to the PLL. The PLL is limited by the need to tune its gains to obtain the desired response. If the gains are not accurately tuned, the response of the PLL is significantly affected. Furthermore, there are always trade-offs in tuning the PLL, depending on whether it is optimized for frequency changes, phase changes, or other operating disturbances; that is, there is no single set of optimum gains suitable for all cases24.

For a fair comparison between the PLL and the proposed method, two different sets of optimized PLL gains are utilized. Both sets are considered optimal as they allow the DFIG system to operate properly without any instability or performance issues. The first set is based on the gains adopted in the benchmark DFIG model available through the MATLAB Help Center. The results in “Validation results” are with adopting this set of gains. Conclusions

The results from both the experimental and theoretical studies confirm that the proposed orientation scheme of the d–q reference frame provides superior control performance under various operating conditions, including DFIG start-up, controlled active and reactive power, frequency disturbance, and phase angle change. When compared to the PLL method, the PLL-based control exhibits instability issues, whereas the proposed method demonstrates its robustness and reliability. Furthermore, the proposed method is featured by its open loop calculations without the need to tune parameters. These findings validate the potential of the proposed orientation technique for use in systems subjected to frequency disturbances. This supports stable and continuous operation without the adverse effects commonly observed in conventional PLL-based methods. Future work will extend the analysis to investigate the control behavior when integrated with model predictive control, as well as under subsynchronous resonance and fault conditions, particularly for DFIG systems connected through series-compensated transmission networks.
