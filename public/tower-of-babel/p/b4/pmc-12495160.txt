Growth performance and body composition of Eimeria-infected broiler fed berry pomaces and a Limosilactobacillus reuteri isolate
Authors: Philip HW Mak, Muhammad Attiq Rehman, Xianhua Yin, Carl Julien, Yan Martel-Kennes, Kelly Ross, Elijah G Kiarie, Moussa Sory Diarra
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractIn the present study, a Limosilactobacillus reuteri strain isolated from chicken ceca alone or in combination with wild blueberry and American cranberry pomaces were evaluated for their effects on growth performance and body composition in Eimeria-infected broiler. A total of 480 day-old Cobb500 male broilers were placed into 60 cages allocated to 8 treatments: T1: non-infected (NI); T2: Eimeria-infected (EI); T3: EI + anticoccidials (125 mg/kg zoalene for D0-D28, 60 mg/kg salinomycin for D28-D35); T4: EI + 1 % blueberry pomace (BP); T5: EI + 1 % cranberry pomace (CP); T6: EI + 108 CFU Limosilactobacillus reuteri (L. reuteri); T7: EI + 1 % BP + L. reuteri; T8: EI + 1 % CP + L. reuteri. The EI was done by gavaging 2 × 105 oocysts of mixed Eimeria species (E. acervulina, E. maxima and E. tenella). No significant differences were observed between treatments for body weight (BW) and average daily gain, however, berry pomaces reduced average daily feed intake (P < 0.05) and the combination of L. reuteri with berry pomaces reduced feed conversion ratio in broilers when compared to control EI birds (P < 0.05). Eimeria infection reduced both bone mineral content and density as well as fat content at D21 (P < 0.05). At D21, the lowest total fat masses (% of BW) were observed in T4, T5 (pomaces) and T3 (P < 0.05). In conclusion, these findings indicate that berry pomace alone or in combination with the studied L. reuteri isolate may be beneficial for growth performance and body composition in the broiler grower and finisher phases.Keywords: Broiler chicken, Berry pomace, Limosilactobacillus reuteri, Growth performance body composition

Introduction

Coccidiosis is an infectious disease induced by various Eimeria species such as E. acervulina, E. maxima, and E. tenella which damage tissue at different intestinal sites from the duodenum to ceca leading to inefficient nutrient absorption, increased mortality, and predisposition to secondary infections such as necrotic enteritis (NE) caused by Clostridium perfringens (Wade and Keyburn, 2015; López-Osorio et al., 2020). Coccidiosis is responsible for significant socio-economic issues in the poultry industry worldwide, resulting in profit losses of approximately $12.9 billion USD annually (Blake et al., 2020). Various methods to prevent, inhibit or kill Eimeria spp., including vaccination and the use of coccidiostats, are used in the poultry industry to fight against coccidiosis, (Martins et al., 2022). The use of coccidiostats may result in reduced effectiveness due to resistance (Martins et al., 2022). Thus, there is an urgent need for novel, cost-effective natural alternative strategies to improve coccidiosis prevention in poultry production.

To replicate the significant health contribution and disease prevention benefits of antibiotics in animal production, alternative products such as probiotics, prebiotics, feed enzymes, phytogenics, organic acids, and essential oils have been receiving more attention for their health potentials (Mak et al., 2022). Probiotics are beneficial bacteria used to improve the gut microbiota by eliminating or inhibiting pathogenic bacteria by competitive exclusion. In poultry, the most common probiotic bacteria include the Limosilactobacillus (L. bulgaricus, L. plantarum, L. acidophilus, L. helveticus, L. lactis, L. salivarius, L. casei, and L. reuteri), Enterococcus (E. faecium and E. faecalis), Streptococcus (S. thermophilus), and Bacillus (B. subtilis) genera (Khan and Naz, 2013). Probiotics have been investigated for their combinatory effects with other feed additives to further enhance their benefits, such as improved immune responses and growth performance in animals (Krysiak et al., 2021). Their use in poultry feed has been associated with improving health and growth promotion in birds. Probiotics have demonstrated strong potential for the improvement of poultry meat quality by reducing the total cholesterol and increasing lipid oxidative stability of poultry meat (Popova, 2017). Moreover, probiotics demonstrated immunomodulatory and antioxidant effects against poultry coccidiosis alone and in combination with another alternative to antibiotic products such as vaccine administration (Mohsin et al., 2021; Stringfellow et al., 2011; Ritzi et al., 2016). Despite this, there are few studies investigating the effect of probiotics in combination with phenolic-based feed additives such as berry pomaces.

The use of fruit pomaces by-products of the juice industry in poultry feed has received significant attention for their carbohydrates, vitamins, minerals, proteins, and polyphenols. (Bhushan et al., 2008; Gassara et al., 2011; Kasapidou et al., 2015). North American cranberry (Vaccinium macrocarpon) and wild/lowbush blueberry (V. angustifolium) pomaces are a significant source of flavonoids, phenolic acids, anthocyanins, and proanthocyanidins which exhibit antibacterial, anti-inflammatory, anti-carcinogenic, and antioxidant activities (Reque et al., 2014; Ross et al., 2017). Berry pomaces as feed additives have been reported to modulate the gut microbiota and improve body weight (BW) in broilers (Salaheen et al., 2017; Islam et al., 2019; Kithama et al., 2023).

Dietary cranberry and blueberry pomaces have been found to increased the abundance of Limosilactobacillus in broiler ceca (Das et al., 2020 and 2021). Both cranberry and blueberry pomace were reported to decrease the incidences of intestinal coccidiosis (Eimeria spp.) and Clostridium perfringens induced necrotic enteritis (NE) lesions and in broiler chickens (Das et al., 2020). Plant antispastic activities, depending on the bioavailability of their active compounds, have been reported (Ranasinghe et al., 2023). Thus, this study evaluated the synbiotic activities of a combination of L. reuteri (probiotic) and berry pomaces (prebiotics) to enhance the growth of beneficial bacteria which would degrade pomaces to improve availability of their bioactives to protect against Eimeria spp. Berry pomace alone or in combination with a L. reuteri isolate were evaluated on growth performance, bone mineral, and body fat contents in broilers infected with Eimeria spp.

Materials and methods

Animal ethics

The chicken trial was performed at the Centre de Recherche en Sciences animales de Deschambault (CRSAD, Deschambault, QC, Canada). All experimental procedures performed in this study were approved (protocol # 1920-AV-388) by the Animal Care Ethics Committee of CRSAD (CPA-CRSAD) according to guidelines described by the Canadian Council on Animal Care (CCAC, 2009).

Berry products

Frozen wet organic American cranberry (V. macrocarpon) and lowbush/wild-blueberry (V. angustifolium) fruit pomaces were acquired from Fruit D’Or (Villeroy, QC, Canada). The processing of berry pomaces and chemical compositions have been previously described (Ross et al., 2017; Kithama et al., 2023). Briefly, the berry pomaces were freeze-dried, and ground using a cutting mill and sifted through a 2-mm mesh screen, followed by storage at −20°C until use (Kithama et al., 2023). The feed inclusion rate of cranberry and blueberry pomace was 1 % as previously described (Das et al., 2020).

Birds and housing

Four hundred and eighty (480) day-old male broilers (Cobb500) were distributed into cages (8 birds/cage, 8 cages/treatment, single tier wired cages, 0.46 m2/cage) at the CRSAD (Deschambault, QC, Canada) and raised for 35 days. Before placement, all chicks were visually examined for health and chicks that were in extreme body weight ranges were not included in the trial. Feed and water were offered ad libitum through nipple drinkers and feed troughs, respectively. The chicks were exposed to light for 24 hours on the 1st day, 23 hours on the 2nd day, 20 hours on the 3rd to 10th day, 18 hours on the 11th to 25th day and 20 hours on the 26th to 35th day. The cage temperature was first set at 33°C and then gradually reduced by 1 to 2°C every week until reaching 20.5°C for the remainder of the trial. Birds were managed according to the Cobb recommendations; starter (crude protein 21.0 %, metabolizable energy 2.9 MCal/kg); grower (crude protein 19.1 %, ME 3.0 MCal/kg) and finisher (crude protein 18.2 %, ME 3.1 MCal/kg). The composition of the starter (days 0–14), grower (days 14–28), and finisher (days 28–35) diets included corn as the principal cereal and soybean products as protein concentrates (Table 1) to meet the nutrient requirements for broiler Cobb500 (Cobb-Vantress, 2022).

Study design

The 60 cages were randomly assigned to one of eight treatments using a complete randomized block design. The eight treatments (T) consisted of T1, blank control (non-Eimeria-infected or non-EI, non-medicated), T2, negative control (EI, non-medicated), T3, positive control (EI and medicated), and five EI experimental groups receiving T4 (1 % blueberry pomace or 1 % BP), T5 (1 % cranberry pomace or 1 % CP), T6 (108 CFU L. reuteri), T7 (1 % BP + 108 CFU L. reuteri), and T8 (1 % CP + 108 CFU L. reuteri). The T3 group was medicated with 125 ppm of zoalene (3,5-dinitro-o-toluamide) (Zoamix, Zoetis Inc, New Jersey, USA) from hatch to 20 days of age, and 60 ppm salinomycin (Coxistac, Phibro, Teaneck, NJ, USA) from 20 to 35 days of age. Treatments with blueberry and cranberry pomaces were mixed in feed and were applied from hatch to 35 days of age. The three growth-phase feed was manufactured as crumble-pellet in one batch per feeding phase at a commercial milling (Agri-Marché, Saint-Isidore, QC, Canada) and remixed with pomace premixes at the CRSAD laboratory to ensure homogeneity. Premixes were made by mixing the feed with a final concentration of 0.48 % vegetable oil for 5 min to coat the pellet/crumble with oil, followed by mixing pomaces for 5 min. The control diet also received the same vegetable oil addition. Limosilactobacillus reuteri isolated from broiler ceca was administered by gavage at a rate of 1 ml/bird daily from seven to 21 days of age. Birds were inspected at least twice a day to record general health and mortality.

Coccidiosis challenge

All animals except for blank control (T1) received an inoculum of mixed field Eimeria isolates by oral gavage at 2 × 105 oocysts per bird on day 14. This oocyst number has been reported to be sufficient in reducing weight gain by approximately 15 % in addition to causing coccidiosis lesion scores of about 2 and fecal oocyst excretion (+7 log10) 6-days post infection (Decaux et al., 2020). Oocysts of mixed species of Eimeria (E. acervulina, E. maxima and E. tenella) were isolated from birds’ droppings at the CRSAD. Oocysts were propagated in healthy chickens, sporulated at 30°C in 2 % potassium dichromate for 48 hours and washed 3 times with water before being inoculated into the birds. For each bird, 1 ml of oocyst suspension at 2 × 105 oocysts/ml was administered by oral gavage.

Oocyst counts

At 17, 20, 23 and 27 days of age, fecal samples were collected under each cage using a sterile spatula for Eimeria oocysts counting as described by Das et al. (2021). Eimeria oocyst counts were determined by microscopy using McMaster slides, and quantitative polymerase chain reaction (qPCR) analysis was performed from bird droppings at 20 days (peak of oocyst counts) to quantify E. acervulina, E. maxima and E. tenella. For values lower than the quantization threshold, half of the quantization threshold was used. The standard curves were constructed by dilutions (10°, 101, 102, 103, 104 and 105) of the amplicons for each species.

Intestinal lesion scoring

At 7, 21 and 28 days of age, two birds per cage were euthanized (120 birds) to score intestinal coccidiosis by E. acervulina (duodenum), E. maxima (jejunum) and E. tenella (cecum) and necrotic enteritis (NE) by Clostridium perfringens lesions, using methodology described by Das et al. (2020) and (2021). The remaining birds were euthanized at day 35 and lesions were scored. Briefly, the scoring system ranges from a 0 to +4, where 0 indicates no lesions and +4 indicates severe lesions and excessive bleeding. No coccidiosis or NE lesions were found in birds at day 7, prior to infection with Eimeria spp. oocysts on day 14.

Data collection

All chicks were weighed at the beginning of the trial (day 0) and weekly thereafter (7, 14, 21, 28, 35 days). Birds were inspected at least twice daily, and culls/mortalities were removed. Performance parameters, including body weight (BW), average daily feed intake (ADFI), feed conversion ratio (FCR), and average daily gain (ADG), were measured and calculated at each growth phase (starter, grower, and finisher). The total cage BW at each phase was corrected for mortality. Feed conversion ratio (FCR) was corrected to account for mortalities in each treatment and phase, which was adjusted when calculating the ADG. The corrected FCR for a given growth phase was then calculated by dividing the average daily feed intake (ADFI) per cage by the ADG for each growth phase. At day 7, 21, 28, and 35 days of age, 8 to 16 birds/treatment (with average cage weight) were euthanized to analyze bone mineral content (BMC), bone mineral density (BMD), fat tissue, and lean tissue analysis using dual-energy X-ray absorptiometry (DXA, Discovery W, Hologic, Bedford, MA, USA).

Statistical analysis

Statistical analyses for growth performance, bone content, fat and lean mass were conducted using a randomized complete block design using the Linear Model and Standard Least Squares and Restricted Maximum Likelihood (REML) method of the JMP Pro software (version 14.0, SAS Institute, Cary, NY, USA). Treatment and age were used as the sources of variation and the individual cages as experimental units. The normality of the residuals, the homogeneity of the variance, and the absence of outliers (± 3.0 probabilistic residues) were verified for all the variables. Statistical analyses for lesion scores and Eimeria oocyst counts were analyzed using the general linear model (GLM) procedure of the SAS 10 software (version 9.4, SAS Institute Inc, Cary, NC). The least significant difference (LSD) test was used to separate means when the F-values were significant. The association Cochran–Mantel–Haenszel test was used to determine the relationship between treatments and the incidence of intestinal lesions using the FREQ procedures of SAS. The differences between groups were considered significant at P < 0.05.

Intestinal lesion scoring

At day 21, coccidiosis lesions attributable to Eimeria species were observed in all groups exposed to mixed Eimeria species on day 14 (Fig. 2A to C). Small foci of necrosis of 1 to 5 mm in diameter (average score of 1) caused by E. acervulina (duodenum) were observed in day 21 birds in addition to lesion scores of less than 1 due to E. maxima (jejunum) and E. tenella (cecum) (P < 0.05). The lowest lesion scores due to E. maxima were observed in group T7 (1 % BP + 108 CFU L. reuteri) (P = 0.0092 compared to lesion scores in birds from the negative control T2 group). Bodyweight (BW). During the starter phase, no significant differences between treatment groups were observed for BW (P > 0.05). However, during the grower phase, birds in the blank control group (T1) were heavier than those in Eimeria-infected group as expected. No significant differences were observed between the BW of market weight (day 35) birds from the conventionally medicated positive control group (T3) and the experimental treatment groups (T4 to T8). It is noted that the lowest BW for birds in the T3 and T5 to T8 treatment groups approaches the objective for 35-day old Cobb male broilers of 2,694 g/bird.

Average daily gain (ADG). During the starter and the finisher phases, no significant differences between treatments were observed for the ADG, whereas during the grower phase, an EI effect was observed, where the blank control group (T1) exhibited a higher ADG than other treatments (P < 0.0001). Additionally, during the grower phase, the ADGs of birds from the experimental treatment groups (T4 to T8) tended to be lower than those of the control groups (T1 to T3). Although not significant, in the finisher phase, T5 to T8 exhibited a higher ADG compared to T3, with treatments in combination (T7 & T8) showing the highest ADG out of the experimental treatments. Overall, birds in the blank control group (T1) showed the highest ADG, while the lowest ADG was observed in birds fed blueberry pomace (T4). Although the ADG for the T1 group was significantly higher than the other treatments (P < 0.0023), no statistically significant differences were found between T3 and the experimental treatments (T4 to T8) for the cumulative ADG (P > 0.05).

Average daily feed intake (ADFI). During all three growth phases, significant differences between treatments were observed for the ADFI (P < 0.05). In the starter phase, the ADFIs in birds fed 1 % BP (T4, P = 0.015), receiving L. reuteri only (T6, P < 0.0001), and a combination of berry pomaces and L. reuteri (T7, P = 0. 0076; T8, P = 0.006) were significantly higher than the positive control group (T3). However, during the grower phase, birds fed berry pomaces (T4, T5, T7, T8) exhibited lower ADFIs than birds in control groups (T1, T2, T3) (P < 0.0001). Moreover, birds treated with L. reuteri (T6) also exhibited significantly lower ADFI compared to the blank (T1, P = 0.0028) and negative (T2, P = 0.024) control groups but showed statistically similar ADFI to the conventionally treated (T3) positive control group (P = 0.687). In the finisher phase, the experimental groups (T4, T5, T7, T8) had significantly higher ADFI compared to the control groups (P < 0.0001), except for group T6, which exhibited similar ADFI to the EI control groups (T2 and T3) (P > 0.05). Overall, the cumulative ADFI revealed that the non-infected (T1) and negative EI (T2) control groups demonstrated the highest ADFI out of all treatments, with the berry pomace treatments (T4, T5, T7, T8) exhibiting lower ADFI compared to these control groups (P < 0.05). Moreover, the positive control group (T3) had the lowest ADFI out of all the treatments (P < 0.0001) which was comparable to that of group T5.

Feed conversion rate (FCR). Significant differences were observed for the FCR between the treatments at all growth phases (starter, grower, and finisher) (P < 0.05). During the starter and grower phases, the highest FCRs were observed in birds fed berry pomaces (T4, T5, T7, T8) (P < 0.0001). Moreover, the group treated with L. reuteri alone (T6) exhibited similar FCR to the control groups. During the finisher phase, T1, T2, T3 and T6 exhibited the lowest FCR of all treatments (P < 0.0001). Overall (cumulative days 0 to 35), the highest FCRs were found in T2, T4 and T6, while the T1, T3, T5, T7 and T8 showed the lowest comparable FCR (P = 0.0445). It is noted that the FCR values for groups T4, T5, T7 and T8 approached or surpassed the objectives of 1.02, 1.36 and 1.48 for Cobb male broilers for the starter, grower, and finisher phases, respectively.

No significant effects of treatments were observed on the mortality rates (P > 0.05).

Bone mineral content (BMC) and density (BMD)

Results of the DXA measurements, including total BMC (g), proportional BMC to BW (%BMC), and BMD (g/cm2), are summarized in Table 3. Significant treatment effects were observed on the total BMC at the end of the grower (P = 0.0365) and finisher (P = 0.0086) phases; however, when corrected with the BW (%BMC), treatment effects were observed only during the starter phase before the infection, which was done at 14 days of age (P = 0.0246). At 7 days of age, the highest %BMC was noted in birds fed-cranberry pomace (T5), while the lowest %BMC was observed in birds fed the combination of L. reuteri + 1 % CP (T8). No significant treatment effects were observed on the BMD (P > 0.05). The %BMC and BMD of 28 and 35 days-old birds were lower than those of 7 and 21 days-old due to the increase in body weight and a decrease in BMD/body weight ratios. Bone mineral content was lowest in broilers that were infected with Eimeria spp. in all three growth phases. Total BMC in treatment groups fed L. reuteri in combination with berry pomace (T7 and T8) were comparable to that of the conventionally medicated control group (T3). Overall, the treatments had no effect on the %BMC or BMD, except for an increase in the %BMC in 7-day-old birds fed 1 % CP (T5).

Discussion

The potentials of each L. reuteri and dietary polyphenolic-rich products alone have been previously investigated in poultry (Yu et al., 2008; Das et al., 2020, 2021; Kithama et al., 2023). However, the combinatory (synbiotic) effects of these products on broiler health, performance, and protection against disease have yet to be explored. Thus, the effects of a L. reuteri administered alone or in combination with a berry pomace diet were investigated, hypothesizing that L. reuteri in combination with a berry pomace diet will improve the gut health and protect against coccidiosis to maintain/improve growth performance and body composition in broilers infected with Eimeria spp.

Quantification of Eimeria oocysts is a key parameter in coccidiosis research (Adams et al., 2022, Edgar, 1955). In the present study, the positive control medicated with zoalene had similar OPG as those of both berry pomace alone treatments at day 20 in the alignment with a previous study (Das et al., 2021). Flavonoids such as anthocyanins and proanthocyanidins have been associated with improving coccidiosis control (Quiroz-Castañeda and Dantán-González, 2015). The phenolic compound curcumin from Curcuma longa showed potential in reducing the severity of Eimeria-induced lesions (Allen et al., 1998). The addition of L. reuteri in poultry feed containing berry pomaces could improve the functionality of berry pomaces through various synbiotic effects (Yan et al., 2019; Kiššová et al., 2022). Overall, when compared the coccidiostats Zoamix, 1 % blueberry and cranberry pomace inclusion in poultry feed mixed with L. reuteri supplementation shows the highest potential in reducing OPG produced by Eimeria spp.

Lesion scoring of the intestines is an effective diagnostic tool that allows the quantification and statistical analysis of intestinal lesions based on severity (Gibson-Corley et al., 2013). Eimeria maxima is commonly known to cause avian coccidiosis in the jejunum and ileum of the poultry small intestines, where most of the amino acid and fatty acid absorption occur, respectively (Gabriel et al., 2006; Simon et al., 2019; Behnamifar et al., 2019). It is plausible that the fermentation of pomaces by L. reuteri could enhance the antioxidant and anti-parasitic effects of polyphenolic compounds present, reducing E. maxima and thus lesion scores observed in the present study. Moreover, the absorption of fatty acids and amino acids could have further improved the control of E. maxima (Puertollano et al., 2013; Castro et al., 2020).

After 14 days post-EI (day 28), all L. reuteri treated-birds reduced lesion scores caused by NE compared to the controls, which indicate promising efficacy of our tested L. reuteri-berry pomace combination in controlling intestinal lesions. Although the novelty of combining berry pomaces with L. reuteri yields no comparisons to past studies, L. reuteri has been previously observed to significantly reduce NE lesions which was attributed to their particular action against C. perfringens via lactic acid production (Vieco-Saiz et al., 2022). The present study showed that L. reuteri combined to in feed cranberry pomace has the potential in the control of coccidiosis and NE control however, further investigations are warranted.

The decrease of BW shown in the present study aligns with previous studies showing the effects of Eimeria infection in decreasing the BW of broilers (Decaux et al., 2020). The positive control (T3), 1 % CP (T5), L. reuteri (T6), L. reuteri + 1 % BP (T7) and L. reuteri + 1 % CP (T8) reached the BW of 2,694 g/bird at 35-day old which align with the male Cobb500 broilers BW (Cobb-Vantress, 2022). The low ADG in the berry pomace treatments, could be attributed to theirs antinutritional compounds such as tannins, which could result in reduced the nutrients digestibility (Kithama et al., 2023; Ross et al., 2017). Dietary fibers may bind to the nutrients, hindering their intestinal absorption and subsequently impairing growth (Grujic-Vasic et al., 1986; Sosnówka-Czajka et al., 2023). The ADFI and FCR are important performance indicators in broilers, reflecting daily feed consumption and the efficiency of converting feed into body weight gain, respectively. In the present study, treatments T4 to T8 exhibited the lowest overall ADFI compared to the blank (T1) and negative (T2) controls. However, the positive control (T3) exhibited the lowest ADFI among all treatments. In Cobb, the target FCR in the starter, grower, and finisher phase are 1.02, 1.36, and 1.48, respectively (Cobb-Vantress, 2022). The studied berry pomace treatments (T4, T5, T7, T8) were close to achieving these numbers. Probiotic supplementation is well known to improve broiler growth efficiency by improving their FCR (Fesseha et al., 2021). The prebiotic-like components, such as berry pomace extracts, could enhance the growth of beneficial gut bacteria such as Limosilactobacillus spp., further enhancing nutrient absorption and FCR (Tabashsum et al., 2020)., A previous study reported that Limosilactobacilli fermentation of blueberry pomace enhanced the active ingredients in blueberry pomace, improving antioxidant activity and health benefits (Yan et al., 2019). Limosilactobacilli fermentation of blueberry also demonstrated potential to improve the colonic barrier functions via inhibition of the MAPK-NF-κB-MLCK signaling pathways, improving the intestinal production of short-chain fatty acids (SCFA) and SCFA receptors (Cheng et al., 2022). The production of SCFA from fermentation of blueberry pomace fibers could be utilized by L. reuteri to produce a bacteriocin, an antimicrobial compound, called reuterin. This antimicrobial compound inhibits various pathogens and promotes beneficial gut colonization (Stewart et al., 2009; Cheng et al., 2022). Previous research also reported that the administration of phenolic compounds in broilers improved FCR by changing the surface area of the intestine and digestive enzymes activity, improving overall nutrient absorption (Mahfuz et al., 2021). More research needs to be conducted to better understand the underlying mechanisms of L. reuteri interactions with blueberry and cranberry pomaces in the gut.

Monitoring bone mineral content and density are crucial to improving bone health, especially in broilers due to the increasing meat yield and rapid growth causing negative impacts on bone development (Shim et al., 2012). Proper mineralization of bones improves bone strength, allowing the bones of broilers to withstand the fast-growing weight and reduce mortality (Shim et al., 2012). In the present study, total BMC in 21, 28 and 35-day-old broilers infected with Eimeria were significantly lower than non-infected broilers. This finding is supported in previous studies, which can be attributed to the increased immune response in the bone marrow due to the Eimeria challenge and systemic oxidative stress, leading to lower BMC and BMD (Tompkins et al., 2023; Akbari Moghaddam Kakhki et al., 2019). Comparable results can be seen in the BMD of 21-day-old birds, with the non-infected broilers revealing a higher BMD compared to the infected broilers, although not significant. Since Eimeria infection is the only differentiating factor between these two groups, it is plausible that our experimental treatments do not negatively affect BMC or BMD. Interestingly, the experimental data also reports a significantly increased BMC percentage (%BMC) in T5 birds, but a significantly decreased %BMC in T8 birds. At 7 days of age, the only difference in the treatments is the berry pomace in feed. Since T5 and T8 are both 1 % cranberry pomace treatments, the significant difference in the %BMC of 7-day old broilers is difficult to explain. To our knowledge, there is a lack of studies that investigate effects of berry pomace on BMC and BMD in poultry. However, past studies suggest consumption of high flavonoid content produce such as fruits and vegetables can improve BMD and BMC in humans (Prynne et al., 2006). The BMD and BMC are typically used as indicators of bone strength and mineralization, which can be impaired by a fragile cortical bone layer due to porosity (Roschger et al., 2003). Consequently, a poorly mineralized cortical bone can significantly increase the risk of bone fractures and deformities in rapidly growing broilers (Turner, 2002). Moreover, the anisotropic patterns of vascular canals in the cortical bone are influenced by increased growth rate and the functional loading on the bone, which contribute to the cortical bone's structural properties such as strength, stiffness, and fracture resistance (Pratt and Cooper, 2018). Our data also indicates no statistically significant differences in %BMC between treatments post-Eimeria infection, indicating that our experimental treatments do not affect BMC levels. There was no statistically significant difference between %BMC from broilers fed L. reuteri probiotic + 1 % cranberry pomace (T8) and that of the positive control (T3). This could be due to an increased calcium absorption by L. reuteri, increasing serum 25OH vitamin D levels (D’Amelio and Sassi, 2019). Moreover, L. reuteri could ferment the cranberry pomaces, enhance their antioxidant properties and reduce oxidative stress, which causes bone degradation (Yan et al., 2019; Tompkins et al., 2023). More research is required to better understand the impact of cranberry, blueberry, and L. reuteri on bone health.

In broilers, meat quality can be confirmed by measuring lean muscle and fat mass. As higher BW is typically desired in broiler production, it is not always the case that a higher BW correlates with higher meat yield or improved growth performance. A rapid increase in BW could be attributed to excessive fat deposition resulting in poor meat quality and inefficient dietary energy allocation (Fouad and El-Senousey, 2014). Ideally, broilers should have lower %Fat as fat can reduce the overall meat yield and quality and are more susceptible to metabolic diseases and health issues such as Wooden Breast caused by an increased lipid metabolism gene (Papah and Abasht, 2019). In the present study, the total fat mass in post-Eimeria infected birds was observed to be significantly lower in 21, 28 and 35-day old birds. It is well understood that during an infection, the body uses a large amount of energy to heighten the immune response function. When the body requires additional energy, the stored adipose tissue is converted from triglycerides to ATP which can be used as sources of energy for the immune system. Moreover, the total fat mass of birds fed 1 % berry pomaces alone reported the lowest total fat mass. Anthocyanins are flavonoids produced by cranberries and blueberries that have many beneficial lipid-related effects in humans such as lipid peroxidation inhibition, anti-lipid production, and anti-inflammation (Sivamaruthi et al., 2020; Tan et al., 2022). However, in poultry, there is a lack of experimental data that investigate the effect of phytochemicals from berries on adipose tissue. Previously, it has been reported that coccidiosis reduces fat digestibility in poultry, which could be another explanation for the low levels of fat mass in the body (Amerah and Ravindran, 2015). Interestingly, 21-day-old birds fed 1 % cranberry pomace (T5) had a higher fat percentage compared to Eimeria-infected controls (T2 and T3). This finding contrasts with previous studies, which have shown that cranberry extracts typically reduce fat accumulation, lower triglyceride and non-esterified fatty acid levels, and even have anti-obesity effects (Sun et al., 2016; Das et al., 2020; Zhou et al., 2020). In human studies, various trials observed a null effect of cranberry juice on their lipid profiles (Chew et al., 2018). Other treatments have lesser %Fat compared to the blank control but were not as efficient as the positive control. Out of all the experimental treatments, the combinatory treatments T7 and T8 showed the lowest %Fat, which were statistically comparable to the positive control. A plausible explanation for this could be explained by the effect probiotics have on fat metabolism (Wang et al., 2017). Previous studies investigated the effects of Limosilactobacillus johnsonii BS15, which was shown to lower serum triglyceride and low-density lipid cholesterol, in addition to the reduction of the lipoprotein lipase mRNA in adipose tissue (Wang et al., 2017). There is a lack of studies that investigate the impact of blueberry and cranberry pomace on fat metabolism. However, proximate analysis including fat in meat quality of broilers fed 1 % cranberry pomace and 1 % blueberry pomace revealed no effect on the proximate composition (moisture, fat, protein and ash) (Xu et al., 2021). More research must be conducted to better understand fat mobilization in broilers fed berry pomace diets combined with a Limosilactobacillus probiotic. Significant differences in lean muscle mass were observed in broilers on day 21, showing improvements in all treatments despite being infected with Eimeria compared to the blank uninfected control. However, the statistically significant treatments were T4 (1 % BP) and combination treatments (T7 and T8) in 21-day broilers. No noticeable differences in lean muscle mass were observed at market weight (day 35), which is consistent with previous findings (Xu et al., 2021). The above data could indicate that the L. reuteri, in combination with 1 % BP and 1 % CP, could aid in improving broiler health and may improve meat yield and reduce fat mass.

In conclusion, based on our growth performance data, the L. reuteri isolate was effective in improving the health benefits of cranberry and blueberry pomaces in broilers. The beneficial properties of reuterin produced by L. reuteri and phenolic acids derived from berry pomace are well studied. The combination of berry pomaces and the L. reuteri showed the potential to improve broiler growth performance. Continued exploration of the symbiotic effects between berry pomaces and novel or established probiotics could significantly support the poultry industries’ efforts to reduce antimicrobial use and resistance, while simultaneously enhancing poultry health.
