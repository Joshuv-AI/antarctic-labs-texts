The mind, body, sexuality triangle in the postmenopausal period: evaluation of menopausal attitude, depression, and sexual life quality
Authors: Sevil Şahin, Emine Ilkin Aydın, Rukiye Dikmen, Alaettin Ünsal
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundThis study investigates the attitudes of postmenopausal women toward menopause and various related factors and assesses levels of depression and quality of sexual life.MethodsThis cross-sectional study was conducted between March and December 2023 in a public hospital in Turkey with a total of 160 menopausal women. Data were collected through face-to-face interviews using structured questionnaires. Menopause Attitude Assessment Scale (MAAS), Beck Depression Inventory (BDI) and Sexual Quality of Life Questionnaire were used as data collection tools.ResultsThe study group’s ages ranged from 42 to 75, with a mean age of 54.76 ± 5.42 years. Their scores on the MAAS varied from 6 to 48, averaging 26.71 ± 8.14. Women with mild menopausal symptoms tended to hold more positive attitudes toward menopause. No significant correlation was found between the BDI and the MAAS scores (r = -0.129; p = 0.104). However, a positive correlation emerged between positive menopausal attitudes and higher sexual quality of life (r = 0.287; p = 0.001). In multivariable analysis, Beck Depression Inventory scores (β = 0.285, p < 0.001) and quality of sexual life scores (β = − 0.271, p < 0.001) were found to be independently associated with MAAS scores.ConclusionThis study suggests that menopausal attitudes in postmenopausal women may be associated with sexual quality of life and depression levels. However, causal inferences cannot be made due to the cross-sectional design.Keywords: Postmenopausal period, Menopausal attitude, Sexual quality of life, Depression, SDG 3: Good health and wellbeing

Background

Menopause is medically defined as the “permanent cessation of menstruation due to the loss of ovarian follicular activity without identifiable physiological or pathological causes” [1]. Occurring typically between ages 45 and 55, menopause signifies the end of a woman’s reproductive years [2–4]. Hormonal changes during the menopausal transition bring about symptoms affecting over 80% of women [5]. Although menopause itself is a natural physiological process, the accompanying symptoms hot flashes, night sweats, sleep disturbances, fatigue, anxiety, depression, vaginal dryness, dyspareunia, and joint pain can complicate it and turn it into a challenging experience [5, 6].

Attitudes, perceptions, and expectations are essential psychosocial factors shaping the experience of menopause [7]. While the physiological processes of menopause are universal, attitudes toward it vary widely due to social and cultural influences, which can affect both symptom perception and reporting [8, 9]. Research shows that women with negative attitudes toward menopause often report more severe symptoms [4]. Additionally, menopausal attitudes are influenced by societal meanings attached to this life stage [6]. For example, studies have found that women who view menopause as a sign of aging or as marking an “end of sexuality” experience high menopausal-related stress and stigma [10, 11]. Understanding these cultural and social attributions can contribute to assessing menopausal attitudes and anticipating symptom severity more accurately.

Menopausal symptoms experienced during this period can negatively impact body image perception and overall quality of life for many women [12, 13]. Research suggests that postmenopausal women have a higher likelihood of experiencing depressive symptoms and depressive disorders compared to premenopausal women [5, 14]. A systematic review investigating the connection between menopause and increased risk of depression and anxiety found that individuals with prior histories of depression or anxiety [15, 16], postpartum depression [17] or negative attitudes toward menopause were at a greater risk of mental health issues during menopause [12]. Raising awareness about these potential psychological changes in menopause can enable earlier diagnosis and intervention, helping women to manage their symptoms more effectively during this transition.

Sexual quality of life includes a woman’s sexual activity and satisfaction levels [18]. Postmenopausal endocrine changes, including vulvovaginal atrophy, can significantly impact sexual function in women [19]. The decline in estrogen levels contributes to issues such as vaginal dryness, urogenital atrophy, decreased tissue elasticity, and dyspareunia, all of which can negatively affect sexual quality of life [20, 21]. Other studies have shown that postmenopausal women may abstain from sexual activity to avoid discomfort, such as vaginal burning during intercourse [22, 23]. Furthermore, Erbaş and Demirel’s research indicated that menopausal complaints such as sleep disturbances, tension, muscle pain, and constipation were associated with lower sexual quality of life [24].

The relationship between menopausal attitudes, sexual quality of life and depression is both complex and multifaceted as shown in the literature (see Supplementary Fig. 1). The changes that occur during menopause can significantly impact on an individual’s physical, psychological, social, and sexual well-being. From a biopsychosocial perspective, these dimensions interact with one another, suggesting that biological changes during menopause may influence psychological outcomes such as depressive symptoms and attitudes toward menopause, while also affecting interpersonal aspects such as sexual quality of life [25, 26]. Understanding the interactions between these areas is essential for providing better support to women during this stage of life. This study aims to assess menopausal attitudes in postmenopausal women, explore related variables, and evaluate depression and sexual quality of life. Methods

Type of research

This study was designed as a descriptive and correlational cross-sectional study to examine the relationships between menopausal attitudes, depression, and sexual quality of life. The research was conducted between March 2023 and December 2023 with postmenopausal women who attended the Gynecology and Obstetrics Outpatient Clinic of Yozgat Bozok University Research and Application Hospital for any reason. Due to the cross-sectional design of the study, the findings were interpreted to evaluate the relationships among variables rather than to establish causal relationships.

Universe and sample

Convenience sampling, a non-probability sampling method, was used in the study. Eligible participants who attended the outpatient clinic during the study period were included in the research. Postmenopausal women who were 40 years of age or older, who had not menstruated for 12 months or more regardless of natural or surgical process, who could understand and speak Turkish, and who approved the informed consent form were included in the study.

During the data collection period, a total of 220 women were approached. Of these, 20 women declined to participate in the study, and 40 were excluded because they were in the premenopausal period and did not meet the inclusion criteria. As a result, the study was conducted with a total of 160 postmenopausal women who met the inclusion criteria (see Fig. 2).

Participant flow diagram of the study

The sample size was determined using G*Power 3.1.9.4 based on a bivariate correlation test (two-tailed), with the Menopause Attitude Assessment Scale (MAAS) total score considered the primary outcome. Assuming a medium effect size (r = 0.30), a significance level of α = 0.05, and a statistical power of 0.95, the minimum required sample size was calculated as 147 participants. To prevent potential data loss, the sample size was increased and the study was completed with 160 women. The obtained sample size exceeded the minimum required sample size calculated through power analysis and ensured a high level of statistical power, indicating that the sample was sufficient for examining the relationships among the study variables.

Data collection tools

The research data were collected using the “Descriptive Characteristics Form,” the “Menopausal Attitude Assessment Scale,” the “Beck Depression Inventory,” and the “Sexual Quality of Life–Female Questionnaire.” [25–32].

Descriptive characteristics form

This form was developed by the researchers in line with the relevant literature and consists of questions evaluating the participants’ sociodemographic characteristics and menopause-related features. The form includes a total of 20 questions covering variables such as age, educational level, marital status, family income status, employment status, duration of menopause, method of menopause onset, and other related variables.

Menopause is characterized as the permanent cessation of menstruation due to the decline in ovarian function [33]. It may occur either naturally or surgically. Natural menopause is marked by the spontaneous absence of menstruation for at least one year due to follicular depletion, whereas surgical menopause results from medical treatments (e.g., chemotherapy or radiotherapy) or surgical procedures (e.g., hysterectomy or oophorectomy) [34]. In this study, participants’ menopause status was classified as either “natural” or “surgical”.

In the study, the severity of menopausal symptoms experienced by the participants was assessed using the Visual Analog Scale (VAS) (e.g., hot flashes, night sweats, muscle/joint pain, irritability, decreased sexual desire, vaginal dryness, dyspareunia, etc.). The VAS is a valid and reliable measurement tool for assessing subjective symptom severity [35]. Since menopausal symptoms are largely based on individual perception, the use of the VAS has been reported to be appropriate for evaluating these symptoms [36, 37]. Participants were asked to indicate the severity of the symptoms they experienced, and the obtained scores were classified according to cut-off points determined by the researchers as “mild (0–3),” “moderate (4–6),” and “severe (7–10).” In this study, a single VAS score was used to represent the overall severity of menopausal symptoms.

Women who were employed in an income-generating job were classified as “working.” Family income status was determined based on the participants’ self-reports and categorized as “high,” “middle,” or “low.” Participants were also classified according to their personality characteristics. Type A personality was defined as a behavioral pattern characterized by competitiveness, a sense of time urgency, and a high achievement orientation, whereas Type B personality was defined as being more relaxed, patient, and having lower stress reactivity [38].

Menopause Attitude Assessment Scale (MAAS)

The Menopause Attitude Assessment Scale (MAAS) was used to assess women’s attitudes toward menopause. Developed by Koyuncu et al. in 2015, the scale underwent a validity and reliability study. It consists of 13 questions; each rated on a 5-point Likert scale. The responses are scored as follows: “0” for strongly disagree, “1” for disagree, “2” for undecided, “3” for agree, and “4” for strongly agree. The total score on the scale can range from 0 to 52, with higher scores indicating a more positive attitude toward menopause. The Cronbach’s alpha for the scale is 0.74 [28]. In this study, the Cronbach’s alpha coefficient of the scale was found to be 0.78.

Beck Depression Inventory (BDI)

In this study, the Beck Depression Inventory (BDI) was used to assess the depression levels of women. Developed by Beck and colleagues in 1961 [30], the Turkish validity and reliability study of the scale was conducted by Hisli in 1988 [29]. The scale consists of 21 four-point Likert-type questions, where the answers are scored from 0 to 3. The total score can range from 0 to 63, with higher scores indicating a higher level of depression. A score of 17 or above suggests suspected depression. Cronbach’s alpha value of the scale was 0.80 [29]. In this study, the Cronbach’s alpha coefficient of the scale was found to be 0.92.

Sexual quality of life-female questionnaire

The participants’ sexual quality of life was assessed using the Sexual Quality of Life-Female Questionnaire, developed by Symonds et al. in 2005 [31]. The validity and reliability of the scale in Turkey was conducted by Tuğut and Gölbaşı in 2010 [32]. The scale consists of 18 six-point Likert-type items, and participants are asked to reflect on their sexual life over the past four weeks when answering the questions. The items are scored between 1 and 6, with the total score ranging from 18 to 108. Higher scores on the scale indicate better sexual quality of life. For ease of interpretation, the raw scores were transformed to a 0–100 scale, with higher scores indicating better sexual quality of life. The Cronbach's alpha value of the scale was reported as 0.83 [32]. In this study, the Cronbach’s alpha coefficient of the scale was found to be 0.88.

Ethical consideration

Prior to initiating the study, approval was obtained from the Ankara Yıldırım Beyazıt University Health Sciences Ethics Committee (decision number 02—47, dated February 15, 2023), along with institutional permission from Yozgat Bozok University Research and Application Hospital. The study adhered to personal data protection regulations and the principles outlined in the Declaration of Helsinki. The method, possible risks and benefits of the study were explained in a clear and understandable language, and it was emphasized that participation was completely voluntary. Informed consent forms were obtained from all participants.

Data collection

The data were collected using the Descriptive Information Form, Menopause Attitude Rating Scale, Beck Depression Scale and Sexual Quality of Life Scale-Female. Before starting the research, participants who met the inclusion criteria were interviewed face to face and informed about the purpose and duration of the research, and informed consent forms were obtained from those who agreed to participate in the research. The data collection process was carried out in a suitable environment with privacy of the participants. The participants completed the survey forms in face-to-face interviews, which took approximately 15—20 min.

Data analysis

The data were analyzed using IBM SPSS Statistics version 15.0. The dataset was checked for missing values prior to statistical analysis, and no missing data were identified. Therefore, all 160 participants were included in the final analyses. To assess the suitability of the data for normal distribution, the Kolmogorov–Smirnov test was performed. Homogeneity of variance was examined using the Levene test.

When the assumptions of parametric tests were satisfied, the independent samples t-test was used for comparisons between two groups, and one-way analysis of variance (ANOVA) was used for comparisons among three or more groups. When the normal distribution assumption was not met, Spearman correlation analysis was used to evaluate the relationships between variables. Multiple linear regression analysis was applied to determine the factors associated with the Menopause Attitude Assessment Scale (MAAS) total score. The assumptions of linear regression were evaluated before performing the regression analysis. Age, Beck Depression Inventory total score, and Sexual Quality of Life-Female total score were included as independent variables in the regression model. In all analyses, the significance level was accepted as p < 0.05.

Discussion

Menopause represents a significant phase in a woman's life, and perceptions of it as either a natural transition or a medical condition can shape attitudes toward the experience [39]. Previous research has reported scores on the Menopausal Attitude Scale ranging from 26.8 ± 7.0 to 30.91 ± 5.13 [7, 36]. The mean score of 26.71 ± 8.14 found in this study aligns with these prior findings, indicating that the women’s attitudes toward menopause in this sample are generally moderate, consistent with existing literature. This may be due to the individual characteristics of the participants, their cultural structure and psychosocial factors.

Menopause is a universal physiological event for women, yet attitudes toward it can vary significantly due to social and cultural factors [9]. Variables such as age, education level, income, employment status, knowledge about menopause, place of residence, and religious and cultural background can all influence these attitudes [37]. In a study by Uslu Sahan et al. [38, 40], participants aged 56—65 years, university graduates, employed women, and those who perceived their income as moderate were found to have more positive attitudes toward menopause. Older women and those with higher education levels tended to have more positive views about menopause [41]. However, in contrast to these findings, the current study found no significant relationship between age, education level, or employment status and menopausal attitudes. Interestingly, women with better family income status in this study exhibited more negative attitudes toward menopause (Table 1). This situation shows that the effect of socioeconomic level on menopausal attitude is not one-way; it should be evaluated in interaction with cultural values, lifestyle and individual expectations. Therefore, comprehensive and multi-center studies are needed that take into account cultural differences and examine the effects of employment status and income level on menopausal attitude.

There is limited and often contradictory information in the literature regarding the relationship between marital status (married, single, or widowed) and menopausal symptom severity and quality of life. Some studies suggest that married women tend to have more positive attitudes toward menopause and report better overall quality of life compared to divorced or separated women [42]. However, other research indicates that married women in the menopausal stage report higher incidences of vasomotor or urogenital symptoms than their single or widowed counterparts [40, 43]. Furthermore, the perceptions and attitudes of men toward menopause could potentially influence their wives' understanding and experience of the menopausal transition [44, 45]. In contrast to these findings, this study observed that married women had more negative attitudes toward menopause than single women (Table 1). It is thought that one of the most important factors affecting the attitudes of married women in this study towards the menopause process may be their husbands' views on menopause and sexuality. This situation may reveal the effect of the cultural environment and family dynamics on the perception of menopause.

It is widely believed that the symptoms women experience during menopause are closely linked to their attitudes toward this life stage [4, 6]. In this study, women with “mild” menopausal symptoms exhibited more positive attitudes toward menopause (Table 3). A systematic review reported that women with negative attitudes toward menopause tend to experience more severe symptoms, such as hot flashes, night sweats, vaginal dryness, irritability, depression, insomnia, and headaches, which in turn worsened their perceptions of menopause [46–48]. Similarly, a study conducted by Batool et al. [49, 50] in Pakistan found a significant negative correlation between the severity of menopausal symptoms and menopausal attitudes, indicating that women with more intense symptoms tend to have more negative attitudes toward menopause. The findings of this study are consistent with these previous studies. Therefore, to improve women’s attitudes toward menopause, it is essential to effectively evaluate and address menopausal symptoms and plan interventions that focus on reducing the severity of the symptoms [51]. Effective management of menopausal symptoms may not only reduce the burden of physical symptoms but also contribute to more positive attitudes toward menopause and improved quality of life.

In this study, no relationship was found between women's attitudes toward menopause and their depression levels (Fig. 3). However, existing literature indicates a connection between depression and attitudes toward menopause [5, 6]. Factors such as menopausal quality of life, stress, social support, and perceived health status have been identified as influencing depression in middle-aged women [52]. Studies suggest that the risk of depression during the menopausal transition is four times higher than in premenopausal women [53]. In a study to examine menopausal attitudes, body image, and depression levels of menopausal women, women with positive attitudes toward menopause reported lower levels of depressive symptoms compared to those with negative attitudes [8]. Conversely, Marvan et al. [50, 54] observed that while negative attitudes toward menopause increased depressive symptoms, positive attitudes did not reduce these symptoms. The discrepancy between the findings of this study and others could stem from various confounding variables not considered in the analysis, such as the type of menopause (natural or surgical), the severity of menopausal symptoms, hormone therapy (HT) use, and a history of depression [55]. In addition, cultural and social factors can affect menopausal attitudes. The perception of menopause in the context of the culture in which the participants in this study lived may have led to a weaker relationship between depression and menopausal attitudes. For example, while menopause is considered a natural life stage in some cultures, it can become a process that creates psychological pressure on women in other cultures. In addition, the multiple linear regression analysis conducted in this study showed that depression was a significant predictor of menopausal attitudes after controlling for age and sexual quality of life. Higher Beck Depression Inventory scores were associated with higher MAAS scores. These findings suggest that psychological factors may play an important role in shaping women’s perceptions and attitudes toward menopause, highlighting the importance of supporting women’s psychological well-being during the menopausal period. In addition, the regression model explained a modest proportion of the variance in menopausal attitudes, suggesting that other psychosocial and cultural factors may also contribute to women’s perceptions of menopause.

Vasomotor, urogenital, psychosocial, and depressive problems during menopause can significantly impact women's psychological health and sexual life [56, 57]. In this study, the average score on the Sexual Quality of Life Questionnaire was 30.48, which is consistent with findings from Uslu Sahan et al. [38, 40], who reported a moderate quality of sexual life in postmenopausal women with an average score of 43.54. To fully understand the effect of menopause on sexual quality of life, it is important to explore the factors that influence the menopause process and women’s attitudes toward menopause [4, 54]. A systematic review showed that education, age, employment status, and ethnicity (minority status) can affect the severity of menopausal symptoms and, consequently, attitudes toward menopause [6]. In this study, a significant relationship was found between menopausal attitudes and sexual quality of life, with women who had positive attitudes toward menopause reporting better sexual quality of life, albeit at a low level (Fig. 4). These results align with other studies in literature. For example, Gözüyeşil et al. [58, 59] found that two-thirds of their participants had negative attitudes toward menopause, and these negative attitudes were associated with poorer sexual performance. In a study conducted with postmenopausal women in the Turkish Republic of Northern Cyprus, attitude toward menopause was found to be a key factor in determining sexual quality of life, with a positive attitude toward menopause correlating with higher sexual quality of life [38]. Similarly, a study involving premenopausal and postmenopausal women in Mexico found that positive attitudes toward menopause were associated with better sexual function, while negative attitudes were linked to worse sexual function [50]. However, the findings from a study by Alirezaei et al. [50, 60] with postmenopausal women in Iran indicated no relationship between sexual performance and attitude toward menopause. The discrepancy in Alirezaei et al.’s study may be attributed to differences in sample size, demographic characteristics, location, and cultural factors. However, cultural, socioeconomic, and individual differences, as well as the perception of sexuality as a private or taboo topic in many societies, may limit women’s willingness to openly report their experiences. Similarly, the regression analysis indicated that sexual quality of life was independently associated with menopausal attitudes. Women with better sexual quality of life reported more positive attitudes toward menopause.

The findings of this study revealed that the quality of sexual life decreased significantly as the level of depression increased in postmenopausal women (Fig. 5). A negative correlation was found between the score obtained from the Beck Depression Inventory and the quality of sexual life. This finding shows that the psychosocial changes experienced by women during menopause affect sexual life. The decrease in estrogen levels with menopause can affect sexuality by causing vaginal dryness, loss of libido and sexual dysfunction [61, 62]. At the same time, depressive symptoms experienced by women during menopause can negatively affect the pleasure and satisfaction received from sexuality [59]. It is stated in the literature that the effect of depression on sexual function becomes more pronounced during important life transitions such as menopause [20, 21, 63]. In this regard, supporting the psychological well-being of women in menopause is of critical importance not only in terms of their general quality of life but also in terms of maintaining their quality of sexual life.

Limitations

This study has several limitations. First, the cross-sectional design of the study does not allow causal interpretation of the relationships between menopausal attitudes, depression, and sexual quality of life. In addition, the use of self-report questionnaires for data collection may have introduced response bias, particularly for sensitive topics such as depression and sexual quality of life. Conducting the study in a single hospital and using a convenience sampling method may limit the generalizability of the findings. Furthermore, evaluating menopausal symptom severity using a single Visual Analog Scale (VAS) score may not fully capture the multidimensional nature of menopausal symptoms. Cultural characteristics may also have influenced participants’ perceptions of menopause and sexual life; therefore, the findings should be interpreted with caution in different cultural contexts. Future studies with larger samples from multiple centers and longitudinal research designs are recommended to examine these relationships more comprehensively.

Conclusion

This descriptive cross-sectional study aimed to assess the menopausal attitudes of postmenopausal women, explore factors influencing these attitudes, and evaluate depression and sexual quality of life. The findings offer valuable insights, particularly for health professionals, by highlighting the relationship between sexual quality of life and attitudes toward menopause in postmenopausal women. Health professionals should be knowledgeable about the factors affecting women’s sexual health during menopause, as well as their beliefs, attitudes, and value judgments regarding menopause and sexuality. They should offer counseling services based on this understanding. When providing education and counseling, it is essential to consider the individual and sociocultural characteristics of women to support their adaptation to menopause effectively. Although no direct relationship was found between women’s attitudes toward menopause and depression, supporting women’s psychosocial well-being during the menopausal period may contribute to improving their overall well-being. Therefore, psychosocial support and education programs tailored to the needs of postmenopausal women may be beneficial in promoting women’s adaptation to the menopausal transition.
