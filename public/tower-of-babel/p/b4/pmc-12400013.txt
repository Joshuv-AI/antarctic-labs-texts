Performance of hybrid diffuse reflectance spectroscopy (HDRS‐ISO 23698) methodology for assessment of sunscreen protection in the ALT‐SPF Consortium validation study
Authors: Eduardo Ruvolo, Curtis Cole, Mathias Rohr, Jay Silverman, Omid Yousefian, Jan Batzer, Nele Lange, Rita Touti, Florence Pouradier, Luciano Nogueira, Bertrand Colson
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractObjectiveAlternative methods to the traditional in vivo sun protection factor (SPF) test are desirable for many reasons. Hybrid DRS (HDRS) (combining in vitro transmission spectroscopy with in vivo DRS spectroscopy) was used in an extensive ring study to characterize its ability to estimate topical sunscreen protection and compared with the currently recognized ‘gold standard’ human in vivo Sun Protection Factor (SPF‐ISO24444), Ultraviolet‐A Protection Factor (UVA‐PF), and Critical Wavelength (CW) (ISO 24443) Reference Methods sponsored by the Alternative SPF Consortium.MethodsDiffuse reflectance spectroscopy (DRS) of skin provides a rapid, convenient non‐invasive tool to evaluate skin properties as well as the optical properties of materials applied to the skin, such as sunscreens. DRS methodology combines in vivo measurement of sunscreen absorbance on skin in the Ultraviolet A (UVA) region with full spectrum in vitro spectrophotometric absorbance scans to provide a full spectrum measurement of the protection of sunscreen on skin.ResultsResults from testing with both monochromatic and polychromatic HDRS devices provided strong correlation of SPF, UVA‐PF and Critical Wavelength results with their corresponding ‘gold standard’ counterpart methods in Study 1 for bias (or ‘accuracy’), however with more variability within certain product types. Further training of personnel conducting the HDRS studies was conducted prior to a second follow‐up ring study using many of the same blinded samples from Study 1 in addition to Reference Standard sunscreens.ConclusionsThe results showed marked improvement in the variability to a level equivalent to the reference methods while maintaining accuracy of the estimates of the protection values. Virtually all estimates were within the Prediction Intervals (PI) of the reference methods for the second study. HDRS offers the ability to accurately assess sunscreen performance for SPF, UVA‐PF as well as CW without invasive damage to test subjects and offers in the future the opportunity to assess water resistance performance.

BACKGROUND OF HDRS METHODOLOGY

One of the first documented sunburn protection tests was published by Blum in 1945 [1] in which a ‘P’ (Protection) factor was calculated as the ratio between the UV dose required to cause a sunburn on skin protected by sunscreen and the dose without the sunscreen on skin. The first codified sunscreen protection test was published by the US FDA in 1978 [2] with other national and international test standards following subsequently, all relying on the use of in vivo tests using various ultraviolet sources to cause erythema reactions with and without sunscreens to determine a ‘Sun Protection Factor’. The most recent international publication was the ISO 24444:2019 [3] standard, which incorporates numerous changes to reduce interlaboratory variability in the SPF values obtained [4].

Since the publication of the US FDA test method in 1978 [2], attempts have been made by many to develop alternative methods for the prediction of sunscreen SPF values. Spectrophotometric analysis of sunscreen absorbance has been conducted on a variety of substrates to identify an ‘ideal’ one that interacts optically with the sunscreen similarly to how it interacts with human skin to provide an accurate estimate of protection [5]. The ISO 23675:2024 [6] method is the culmination of that search for a purely in vitro spectroscopic method to predict sunscreen SPF that uses two different types of polymethylmethacrylate (PMMA) plates.

The HDRS method was pioneered by Dr. Nik Kollias described in a book chapter in 1992 [7]. Diffuse reflectance spectroscopy (DRS) is a technique that analyzes the re‐emitted light from a sample to determine its optical properties, including absorption and scattering [8]. The method was initially developed only as a UVA‐PF test method, measuring sunscreen absorbance on human skin. A series of subsequent papers confirmed the usefulness of DRS to evaluate sunscreens for UVA protection [9, 10, 11]. The primary limitation to this method results from the strong absorption of UVB radiation by endogenous components in the skin such as melanin, proteins and DNA, thereby limiting the information regarding absorption characteristics of the overlying sunscreen within the UVB region. The method is also currently limited to Fitzpatrick Skin Phototypes I, II and III [11, 12] similar to ISO 24444 and the FDA monograph SPF test methods.

The UVB absorbance limitation was then resolved by combining (hybridizing) the UVA‐PF determination from DRS measurements with the full sunscreen absorbance spectrum derived from traditional in vitro UV spectrophotometer measurement. The technique was described by Ruvolo [12] as a way to use the UVA absorbance spectrum determined on human skin, and then to ‘mend’ the UVB portion of the in vitro absorbance spectrum onto the lower end of the UVA spectrum to provide a full spectral analysis of the sunscreen that is appropriately scaled to predict the in vivo SPF value. HDRS was used to assess the remanence of sunscreen over time and exposure to sweating to highlight its usefulness in studying sunscreen performance in actual use conditions on humans [13]. Rohr et al [14] reported results testing 80 products versus in vivo test results in his laboratory, showing excellent correlation with both SPF and UVA‐PF results measured with ISO24444 and ISO24443 respectively. A subsequent multi‐laboratory ring study [15] testing 25 products yielded further support for the utility of this non‐invasive optical approach to accurately assessing sunscreen protectiveness. All these studies were performed using a monochromatic HDRS device which assessed the absorbance of materials on the skin using a monochromatic filtered emitter and a monochromatic filtered detector wherein the two monochromators are synched to the same wavelength as they scan through the UV wavelengths [12, 13, 14, 15].

A polychromatic HDRS device [16] was more recently envisioned and developed for testing that used a UVA light source identical to the one used for UVA‐PF testing as described in ISO24442:2022 [17], and a detector system with a response spectrum similar to the persistent pigment darkening action spectrum (PPD) described in ISO24443:2021 [18]. This combination allows direct determination of the UVA‐PF from two polychromatic source measurements on the skin (with and without sunscreen), eliminating the need for monochromators and the lengthier scanning of the spectrum. The in vitro absorbance spectrum (UVB + UVA) is simply scaled according to the UVA‐PF observed by the DRS measurement, and the SPF of the sunscreen can be estimated by this technique [16]. This polychromatic HDRS system was also tested in the multi‐laboratory ring study [15] in conjunction with the monochromatic HDRS devices and yielded similar SPF and UVA‐PF results.

The primary benefits of the HDRS approach to sunscreen evaluations lie in its reliance on in vivo human skin assessment, the ultimate substrate of interest for sunscreen protection. Testing can be conducted under laboratory conditions or under actual performance of sunscreens on human skin under stress (sweat, water exposure) conditions [13]. They can also be performed ‘in‐field’ under actual use conditions. The amount of UV exposure has been determined to be <3% of an erythema dose for monochromatic HDRS measurements and <0.02% for the polychromatic DRS device. The use of HDRS measurements for water resistance SPF determinations is envisioned in the near future and will be supported by further data.

HDRS test methodology for sunscreen protection assessment was accepted as a New Work Item by ISO TC217 WG7 at the Warsaw Poland meeting in 2018, as an alternative method to the current ISO 24444:2019 in vivo SPF and ISO 24443 UVA‐PF test methods. A subcommittee of WG7 members, supported by Quodata, a statistical firm, developed a large multi‐laboratory ring study sponsored by the Alt‐SPF Consortium, to characterize the new alternative methods (HDRS and Double Plate) for consideration by WG7. This report provides the acquired results which served as the basis for recommendation of advancement of the HDRS method to an ISO standard for sunscreen protection assessments (SPF, UVA‐PF and Critical Wavelength).

PRINCIPLES OF HDRS MEASUREMENTS

Monochromatic DRS

Monochromatic DRS (mHDRS) system measurements of sunscreen absorbance on the surface of the skin are performed using a xenon arc light source filtered through a ‘emission’ monochromator into a fibre optic bundle placed on the surface of the skin. A baseline (no sunscreen) measurement is first conducted using a fibre optic bundle that has ‘emitter’ and ‘detector’ fibres that are separated in distance from each other such that they cannot ‘see’ each other. The detector fibres can only ‘see’ photons that have left the emitter fibres, transversed into the skin, scattered randomly within the surface of the skin and been ‘re‐emitted’ to be collected by the detector fibres, carried to a second monochromator and measured with a photomultiplier detector (PMT). By scanning the two monochromators in synchrony across the spectrum 310–400nm, the spectral ‘baseline’ is recorded. This is repeated a minimum of three times for the baseline measurement within a test site.

A series of at least 5 measurements is then conducted after sunscreen has been applied to the skin and has dried for at least 15 min. The light from the emitter monochromator must now traverse the sunscreen barrier before entering the skin, scatter within the skin and then traverse the sunscreen barrier a second time before being picked up by the detector fibres, passed through the detection monochromator and then into the PMT for measurement. By taking the square root of the ratio of the signals (with and without sunscreen application) at each wavelength, an absorbance curve of the sunscreen is constructed. The square root function is required as the photons are required to pass through the sunscreen barrier twice, being reduced in intensity with each passage.

Polychromatic HDRS

Polychromatic HDRS (pHDRS) systems use the same principle but measure the total intensity of polychromatic PPD weighted UVA energy remitted from the skin without and with the sunscreen applied, and the square root of the ratio of these two measurements is the UVA‐PF that is then used to scale the initial (before UV exposure) in vitro absorbance spectrum of the sunscreen. Nine subsite measurements are made for unprotected skin and sunscreen‐protected skin at a given test site, yielding nine UVA‐PF values that are averaged for the test site.

Photostability and UVB hybridization

To account for sunscreen changes in magnitude and shape due to spectral photodegradation under UV exposure conditions, the in vitro absorbance scans include both an initial (no UV exposure) and a post‐UV exposure measurement. The UV exposure dose is 1.2× the initial HDRS determined UVA‐PF value in J/cm2 of UVA from a clinical (SPF) spectrum solar simulator. The ratio of these two absorbance measurements provides the Spectral Ratio of PhotoDegradation (SRPD) spectrum that is used to mathematically adjust the DRS absorbance measurements proportionally. For monochromatic DRS devices, the UVB portion of the post‐exposure in vitro absorbance spectrum is then mathematically attached [19] to the lower end of the DRS measured UVA absorbance spectrum to create the complete HDRS absorbance spectrum 290–400 nm from which SPF, UVA‐PF and Critical Wavelength can be calculated by conventional methods. For the polychromatic system, after scaling the initial in vitro absorption spectrum to the UVA‐PF from skin, the SRPD is applied to the scaled absorption spectrum and the final SPF, UVA‐PF and Critical Wavelength can be calculated by conventional methods.

ALT‐SPF CONSORTIUM RING STUDIES

Design study 1: SPF

The design of the Alt‐SPF Consortium ring studies is provided in detail in other publications [20]. Briefly, the study employed a rigorous experimental and statistical framework based on ISO 5725‐3:2023 [21], using a balanced, partially nested factorial design to ensure both efficiency and robustness despite a limited number of laboratories (four to five per method). This approach allowed decomposition of sources of variability while maintaining statistical power typically associated with larger‐scale interlaboratory studies.

The product selection process was designed to reflect the diversity of sunscreens available on the global market. Thirty‐two sunscreen formulations were selected and distributed across eight distinct product groups. These products were systematically categorized along three key dimensions: SPF level (SPF 6, SPF 15, SPF 30 and SPF 50+), formulation type (including emulsions, ethanol‐based one‐phase sprays and all‐mineral formulations) and viscosity (classified as low, medium or high based on shear rate measurements at 100 s−1). This design enabled assessment of the method's performance across a range of physical properties and application behaviours. All test samples were blinded and coded to ensure impartiality, with no compositional details or SPF label claims disclosed to the alternative method laboratories. Each product was tested twice using different blinded identifier codes in order to assess the intralaboratory variability of the test methods (Reference SPF as well as the Alternative Methods).

To determine whether an alternative method such as HDRS could be considered equivalent to the ISO 24444 reference method, five quantitative acceptance criteria were established prior to the study by an expert working group within ISO TC 217 WG7. These criteria were designed to evaluate both trueness and precision. Criterion 1 required that the reproducibility standard deviation of the alternative method not exceed that of the reference method. Criterion 2 set an upper limit of 0.3 ln SPF for persistent laboratory bias. Criterion 3 ensured that the combination of bias variability across individual products and the method's reproducibility remained within the reproducibility variance of the reference method. Criterion 4 established a decision limit (DL) for the overall method bias within each product group, beyond which the bias would be deemed unacceptable. Finally, Criterion 5 required that the variation in method bias across product groups be within a specified consistency limit (CL), thereby ensuring the stability of the method across different product categories. These five criteria were intended to work as an integrated system to support decisions on method equivalence and potential standardization.

Each laboratory participating in the HDRS evaluation tested a complete set of samples under eight specific experimental settings, representing all possible combinations of three operational factors: panel of volunteers, product applicator and grader (in the case of ISO 24444 comparisons). For HDRS, the design focused on operational variables relevant to optical instrumentation, such as instrument calibration, operator handling of the probe and environmental conditions, which were treated as nested random effects within each laboratory.

Six laboratories volunteered to participate in the ring study with both monochromatic and polychromatic HDRS devices; four laboratories were commercial testing facilities (CROs) and 2 manufacturers laboratories conducted the study internally. Only three of the laboratories that participated had previous experience conducting HDRS measurements (monochromatic devices) and none of the participating laboratories had previous experience with polychromatic HDRS measurements. Testing was done using the same site (one application) divided in half for mHDRS and pHDRS measurements on the same subject. Because of the number of samples to be tested at each laboratory (64) and the time constraints for study completion, it was decided a priori to only conduct testing on a panel of 10 subjects for each tested material, without adherence to the 17% CI/mean statistical criteria recommended in the draft ISO proposal. Subjects used in the test were enrolled according to the enrollment criteria of ISO 24444 [3] including the Individual Typology Angle‐ITA [22]. Individual Typology Angle (ITA) is an instrumental measure of the darkness of the skin—primarily driven by melanin content in the skin. The instrument is known as a ‘colorimeter or chromameter’. The ISO 24444 requires a minimum ITA° of 28, and a mean test panel ITA° between 41 and 55 for skin phototypes. When possible, subjects with ITA°s in each of the three ITA° bands, 28° to 40°, 41° to 55° and >56° must be included.

As part of the factorial design for the statistical analysis, the HDRS methods used two different factors considered to be key elements contributing to variability in the test results, which were the panel (2 panels of 10 subjects each) and the technician (2) conducting the in vitro measurements used in the hybridization procedure.

Together, this design and its statistical rigour provided a comprehensive framework for evaluating HDRS as a reliable, non‐invasive and ethically advantageous alternative to the current gold‐standard in vivo SPF testing.

Design study 1: UVA‐PF and CW

Additional analyses for UVA‐PF and Critical Wavelength values were performed against the in vitro Reference Method ISO 24443:2021. For this analysis, additional laboratories to perform the Reference Method measurements were recruited and screened by WG7 ISO 24443 experts to assure competence of the laboratories to participate in this study. Eight laboratories were selected to participate based on prior experience and an audit of past studies and observation of laboratory equipment (UV light source, spectrophotometer, analytical scales), log of instruments calibration, laboratory procedure and technician training log for ISO 24443 compliance. The same test samples utilized for the Alt‐SPF Consortium study were blind coded and distributed to the test laboratories such that results from 4 laboratories could be combined for a given test sample. ISO 24443 requires input of the in vivo SPF value to scale the absorbance curves to determine the UVA‐PF values. Each sample was given two different input SPF values, one randomly assigned corresponding to the SPF value determined by a laboratory in the Alt‐SPF Consortium ring study, and a second value that was the average SPF of all 4 laboratories testing that particular sample. Only the randomly assigned SPF value from an individual laboratory was used for the Reference method (ISO24443:2021) UVA‐PF determinations for comparison with the alternative HDRS UVA method to represent the true variability inherent in the ISO 24443 method. The statistical analysis criteria were identical to those used for the SPF values. Critical Wavelengths were also collected for both the Reference Method (ISO 24443) and the HDRS method and correlation comparisons and limits of agreement by Blandt‐Altman [23] were performed.

Study 2: Approaches to improving performance

A second ring study was planned with the purpose of determining if interlaboratory variability could be improved and to confirm the calibration scalar/functions of the results. A number of changes were made to address these sources of variability: Given that the HDRS test method and equipment was new to many of the test laboratories at the beginning of Study 1, additional training on how to perform the ISO 23698 method was conducted at each participating laboratory with a round of test products (4 products from different product groups) with known SPF values.One of the largest sources of variability was known to be sunscreen application homogeneity on the skin by the technicians. An HDRS‐based training module was created to evaluate sunscreen application uniformity on the skin, using the HDRS device itself as the ‘detector’ of uniformity and to map protection variability within the test site. Technicians tested their ability to spread products with a variety of galenic properties to improve their spreading technique. Design study 2

Study 2 was designed to replicate a portion of Study 1 using some of the same samples to be able to show direct improvement (particularly in lowering variability between and within laboratories), as well as Reference Sunscreen Standards from ISO 24442, ISO24443 and 24444. All four products in Product Group 1 were utilized and repeated in blinded fashion to replicate the factorial design of Study 1). Other samples from Product Groups 2, 3, 4 and 8 were chosen to provide a full range of SPF values in addition to the ISO Reference Standard sunscreens P5, P6, P8 and S2. The statistical analysis was the same for Product Group 1 samples, and the other samples were evaluated against statistical criteria for the in vivo SPF values and prediction intervals determined during validation of the Reference Sunscreen SPF and UVA‐PF tests. For these Reference Standard sunscreens, there were between 12 and 19 laboratories involved in determining the in vivo SPF and UVA‐PF values. Notably, there was no in vivo SPF data available for S2, and no UVA‐PF data available for P6 for comparisons. Five test laboratories volunteered to participate in HDRS Study 2, four that had previously participated in Study 1, as well as one new laboratory.

DISCUSSION

This set of studies represents the culmination of many years of incremental development and improvement in DRS equipment and methodologies. The technique yields data derived from optical measurement of sunscreen protection as measured directly on human skin under controlled use conditions. It also provides the capability to study sunscreen protection provided under actual in vivo consumer use conditions as well, which can then be used for further research and product improvements. The development of the methodology has been limited primarily by availability of appropriate measurement devices, and the early belief that its utility was limited to measurement of UVA protection. With the conception of a hybridized in vitro–DRS approach to provide a full spectrum analysis of sunscreen protection [12] the development of the method for SPF determination was accelerated, and evaluations against in vivo SPF results were pursued.

Another factor that has long hampered development of alternative SPF test methods lies in the fact that the ‘gold standard’ in vivo SPF test has such high inter‐laboratory variability, making correlation and calibration against the ‘gold standard’ a fuzzy and ever‐moving target. The variability in the in vivo SPF results seen both during validation of the Reference Standard sunscreens and in this Alt‐SPF Consortium ring study [20] highlights this fact, indicating the variability still inherent in the ISO 24444:2019 test method that remains to be addressed. The laboratories participating in this Consortium ring study were chosen by and fully audited by ISO WG7 experts prior to initiation of this ring study.

One difference between the ring study and a typical sunscreen test was the lack of a ‘target’ SPF value for the ring study. For a typical sunscreen test at any given laboratory a ‘target’ SPF value is provided by the sponsor that is their best estimate of the SPF (based on findings on other similar formulations, UV filter concentrations and preliminary in vivo or in vitro test results). This is done to initiate testing within a given range of UV exposures to eliminate unnecessary testing in a range that is not expected to give determined SPF results, with either over‐exposures of test subjects or no responses at all. This sets up expectations that the responses should result within the range of the target SPF and within the middle of the SPF range, thus biasing the grader of the responses. Because of this potential bias, no ‘target’ SPF range was given for the Reference Method Alt‐SPF Consortium study in vivo laboratories. Thus, the laboratories had to initiate testing on subjects with a low SPF value ‘target’ and based on the results, increase the exposures until erythema responses were observed before completing the study with a full panel of subjects. While this may have contributed to some of the variability observed in the SPF values in this test, it also reflects the reality that different laboratories have different interpretations and perceptions of what represents qualifying erythema responses. It should also be noted that the quality of the erythema responses (colour, intensity, completeness) of the unprotected exposed site is often quite different from the sunscreen‐protected exposed site, depending on the type and amount of UV filters used in the particular formulation, thus compounding the interpretation of qualifying responses resulting in different SPF values in different laboratories.

It should also be noted that all the products were not tested by all the same in vivo ISO24444 SPF laboratories, but different sets of four laboratories that were randomly assigned to the different Product Groups, so that laboratory‐specific biases are randomly affecting some, but not all, of the specific Product Groups. This potentially skews some of the bias correlations across the product groups, making for non‐linear correlations across the product groups.

One additional factor should be considered: Both HDRS (ISO 23698) and in vitro Double Plate methods (ISO 23675) rely purely on measurement of optical protection of the sunscreens, while the in vivo SPF test method may also be influenced by ingredients in the sunscreen that may function biologically as well. The ingredients in the formulations tested in this ring study were unknown, some purchased with ingredients listed, but others were donated with no ingredient listings. It is possible that some contained ingredients that provided antioxidant protection (for example) that would not be detected optically by either HDRS or Double Plate methods. This might explain why some products within a specific Product Group yielded quite different SPF values compared to the other products in that same group for all the Alternative Methods (PG 4 for example).

Study 1 results provided the evidence that the HDRS method provides overall accuracy in the SPF, UVA and CW test results. Study 2 provided significant improvement in the variability seen in Study 1 that was addressed with the application of calibration functions/factors, additional training of laboratory personnel, application of statistical limits and device software and hardware improvements. There is no reason to assume the observed improvement in performance (PG1, PG3, PG4 and PG8 samples) will not also hold for the other product groups (PG2, PG6, PG7 and PG8) for the monochromatic and polychromatic systems tested in the studies. With the publication of the ISO 23698:2024 method, there will be the deployment of commercially available equipment specifically designed for sunscreen testing that will rapidly increase the number and quality of the laboratories performing this test method.

CONCLUSIONS

Overall, the search for alternative methods to invasive in vivo sunscreen test methods has been long and difficult for all the reasons considered here in this paper. The results of this test have demonstrated that the HDRS Method (ISO 23698) has finally reached a level of accuracy, repeatability and reliability equivalent to the ‘gold standard’ test method for sunscreen testing. In addition, the UV radiation used in the traditional SPF in vivo test is known to increase the likelihood of skin cancers, and the effects of UV exposures are cumulative. Given the success of alternative methods to reproduce the testing results equivalent to the ‘gold standards’ we can proceed with these new, unbiased, modern and non‐invasive test methods.
