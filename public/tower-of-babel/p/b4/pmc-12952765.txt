Rapid loss of viability in acute myeloid leukemia cells upon telomerase inactivation
Authors: Elisa Aquilanti, Lauren Kageler, Vincent Bozinov, Matthew Meyerson
Published: 2026

TO THE EDITOR:

Acute myeloid leukemia (AML) is a myeloid neoplasm characterized by a clonal expansion of malignant hematopoietic cells coupled with differentiation arrest.1,2 AML prognosis remains poor, with 5-year survival rates of <30%1 for all patients and <10% for older adult patients,1,3 indicating an unmet need to develop novel therapeutics for treating AML. Telomerase-based therapeutics have emerged as a promising strategy to treat AML.4, 5, 6 Telomerase provides cancers and stem cells with cellular immortality by maintaining telomere length.7, 8, 9, 10 The telomerase inhibitor imetelstat11,12 is being evaluated as a therapeutic agent in AML.5,13,14 Notably, imetelstat does not induce changes in telomere length in treated AML cells compared with phosphate-buffered saline-treated controls,5 raising the possibility that the mode of action of imetelstat may not be through on-target inhibition of telomerase.15

Since prior studies of telomerase inactivation in AML primarily used pharmacological tools4,5 or transgenic animal models,4 we aimed to use genetic tools to systematically assess responses to telomerase inactivation in preclinical models of AML. Results from the Cancer Dependency Map16 indicate that cancers of myeloid lineages exhibit the strongest dependency on TERT (Figure 1A). Although we did not detect a correlation between TERT mRNA expression and TERT dependency (supplemental Figure 1), we detected a modest but significant inverse relationship between telomere content17 and TERT dependency (Figure 1B). Using the established telomere restriction fragment assay,18 we measured telomere length in a panel of leukemia cell lines and patient-derived models (PDXs)19,20 (Figure 1C-D; supplemental Table 1). Cell lines MV-4-11, MOLM-13, MONO-MAC6, and SEM (Figure 1C) and PDXs CBAM-68552 and DFAM-16835 (Figure 1D) have short telomeres, with average telomere lengths ≤3 kb. To determine the effects of telomerase loss in the leukemia cell lines described earlier, we used CRISPR interference (Figure 1F; supplemental Table 2)22, 23, 24, 25 to inactivate TERT in MV-4-11, MOLM-13, MONO-MAC-6, and SEM (Figure 1G). TERT knockdown caused a rapid reduction in viability for cell lines MV-4-11 and MOLM-13, whereas it did not significantly affect the viability of MONO-MAC-6 and SEM over a period of 15 to 20 days (Figure 1G; supplemental Figure 2). To determine whether these results translate to in vivo models,20 we used doxycycline-inducible CRISPRi targeting TERT (sgTERT1) (Figure 1I),25 which suppressed TERT mRNA levels in the AML in vivo by >80% upon doxycycline treatment (Figure 1J). Animals in the doxycycline-treated group had significantly prolonged survival compared with the control group, with median survival of 37 days in the control group and 61 days in the doxycycline-treated group (Figure 1K).

To determine whether the rapid TERT dependency in AML cells is because of loss of telomerase catalytic activity, we generated catalytically inactive TERT mutant harboring active-site mutations D868A and D712A (TERT catalytically dead or TERT CD) (Figure 2A-B). Overexpression of wild-type TERT and TERT CD in MV-4-11 cells significantly increased TERT mRNA levels (supplemental Figure 3A). Immunoblotting revealed a faint TERT protein band only in wild-type TERT–transduced MV-4-11 cells, whereas no band was seen for green fluorescent protein or TERT CD (supplemental Figure 3B). However, in 293T cells, both TERT and TERT CD produced detectable protein bands, suggesting cell-line specific differences in TERT protein regulation or tolerance (supplemental Figure 3C). Although wild-type TERT overexpression significantly lengthened telomeres in MV-4-11 cells, TERT CD did not (Figure 2C). TERT CD ectopic expression also caused a significant reduction in viability, in a similar timeframe to anti-TERT single guide RNAs (Figure 2D). These results are in line with prior studies assessing the effects of catalytically inactive telomerase in cells,26,27 and indicate that telomerase catalytic activity is essential for the survival of TERT-dependent leukemia cells. Next, we performed rescue experiments and demonstrated that TERT overexpression, but not green fluorescent protein ectopic expression, fully rescues the loss of viability caused by sgTERT1 (Figure 2E-F). Lastly, to determine whether telomere elongation in the absence of TERT overexpression rescues the TERT dependency in MV-4-11 cells, we elongated telomeres in MV-4-11 cells by treating them with 100μM thymidine28 for 80 days (supplemental Figure 4A). Using an Annexin V and propidium iodide–based assay, we observed strong induction of apoptosis upon TERT knockdown in TERT-dependent cells (Figure 2G-H; supplemental Figure 5). We also detected increased levels of p53 protein expression in TERT-knockdown MV-4-11 cells (Figure 2I) and phosphorylated p53 at the serine 15 residue (S15) in TERT-knockdown MV-4-11 and MOLM-13 cells (Figure 2I-J).30,31 We detected telomere shortening 7 days after TERT knockdown in MV-4-11 but not MOLM-13 cells (Figure 2K). These results suggest that telomerase inactivation induces a cytotoxic response in a subset of AML cells, that this response is associated with p53 pathway activation, and that the presence of an intact p53 pathway distinguishes the rapid response seen in MV-4-11 and MOLM-13 cell lines compared with MONO-MAC-6 and SEM.

Cellular immortality through telomere maintenance is one of the hallmarks of cancer,32,33 yet only 1 telomerase-targeting therapy is approved to date.34 A concern about the ability to translate telomerase inhibitors to the clinic is the time lag between the onset of telomerase inhibition and tumor cell death.35 To improve clinical translation, identifying cancers that respond rapidly to telomerase loss is critical. We show that a subset of AMLs with short telomeres is among this rapidly responding group of cancers. We also demonstrate that this dependency relies on telomerase catalytic activity. Although telomerase overexpression rescued the phenotype, long-term thymidine treatment to elongate telomeres did not, suggesting that the dependency may not be entirely related to telomere length and further work is required to elucidate the underlying mechanism. This result could also be explained by insufficient telomere elongation in thymidine-treated cells, or less likely by noncanonical function of telomerase.

Mechanistically, telomerase-dependent AML cells undergo p53-dependent apoptosis, suggesting that intact p53 serve as a biomarker. Additional validation in p53-intact and p53-null PDXs of AML, as well as isogenic models, is required to definitively demonstrate this. The relationship between intact p53 and an apoptotic response to telomerase inactivation in AML has been previously demonstrated using pharmacological tools36 or transgenic animal models.4 To build on this work, we used CRISPRi, a genetic tool with minimal off-target effects, to demonstrate that AML cells with short telomeres can exhibit a rapid induction of an apoptotic response upon telomerase loss. These findings contrast with reports of imetelstat inducing ferroptosis,5 highlighting the complementary value of genetic and pharmacological tools. A limitation of this study is the small sample size of telomerase-dependent AML cells. The leukemia cell lines used in this study capture key features of aggressive disease, such as FLT3 mutations, complex karyotypes and MLL rearrangements (supplemental Table 2),37,38 and telomere lengths fall within those reported in primary AML specimens.21 Given the marked heterogeneity of AML,38 future studies would benefit from incorporating additional cell-lines that capture this broader mutational spectrum to enhance generalizability. Overall, our findings advance the understanding of telomerase dependency in AML and support additional preclinical studies to support the development of telomerase inhibitors and their further evaluation in experimental AML model systems.
