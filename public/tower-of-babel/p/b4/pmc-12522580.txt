Impact of a peer-to-peer coaching program on emergency medicine residents: a pre-post intervention study
Authors: Raiyan Y. Almaini, Nawaf M. Alkharashi, Mohammed Z. Alshalhoub, Atheer F. Almutairi, Abeer B. Alfadhliah, Zeyad A. Almatroudi
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
BackgroundThe inherent stress of change, increased responsibilities, and lack of guidance associated with the transition to residency often result in disillusionment, emotional exhaustion, and depersonalization ultimately affecting patient care. A possible solution that has been proposed is to implement coaching programs for residents, especially since such programs have been shown to improve physician well-being, communication, and goal setting. This study aimed to evaluate the effect of peer-to-peer coaching on first-year emergency medicine residents.MethodsA baseline pre-intervention survey was administered to 25 first-year emergency medicine residents, assessing emotional exhaustion, depersonalization, resilience, goal setting, perceived impact of coaching, perceived level of support from their peers and residency program, and self-confidence. The coaches were fourth-year emergency medicine residents, conducting the coaching sessions using the GROW (Goal, Reality, Options, Way forward) model. At the end of the six-month coaching period, the survey was re-administered, and pre- and post-intervention results were compared.ResultsThe participants reported more emotional exhaustion (p = 0.062) and depersonalization (p = 0.203) in the post-intervention survey, indicating that coaching possibly did not benefit these domains. No statistically significant change in resilience was measured (p = 0.932). Attitudes toward goal setting may be improved by coaching, though the finding was statistically insignificant (p = 0.089). Responses in the follow-up survey also indicate that perceptions toward the residency program and coaching improved significantly (p = 0.039).ConclusionPeer-to-peer coaching may positively impact residents’ professional fulfilment, personal development, peer-to-peer communication, patient communication and more. Developing peer-to-peer coaching programs and continually improving them may contribute to healthier, more effective residents, and potentially improve patient care and outcomes.

Background

The transition from medical school into residency is one of the most significant milestones in a physician’s career, marked by increased responsibilities, a pivotal shift to self-directed learning, and the inherent stress of change. These factors often manifest in burnout [1–3], poor professional fulfillment, and most significantly in emergency medicine, decreased resilience. These manifestations are often compounded by the lack of guidance or a coaching figure, and thus cumulatively affect the residents’ work-life balance, mental health, and subsequently result in degradation in quality of patient care [4, 5].

Repercussions of these issues are bound to be present status quo without intervention, especially given that shortages in healthcare personnel are projected to be an issue for the foreseeable future [6]. Some residency programs and researchers have introduced coaching programs in an effort to address these issues, with their reports demonstrating positive impacts in the form of improved resilience, professional fulfillment, and self-reliance, particularly in residents experiencing burnout [7]. Coaching has even been reported to improve physician-patient communication [8].

Coaching has also been demonstrated to lead to a significant reduction in emotional exhaustion and improved well-being among physicians [9, 10], as well as improved adaptability and receptivity to feedback [11]. Consequently, coaching programs can enhance professional competence, while also providing a path to psychosocial and personal development of physicians within their most formative years. The positive effects of coaching may therefore culminate in higher quality, more efficient patient care.

Despite the growing body of evidence indicating the value of a coaching program to the professional and psychosocial development of residents, the data on coaching programs specific to emergency medicine residents remains limited. Burnout in emergency medicine residents may be exacerbated by frequent exposure to acute undifferentiated critical illness and the absence of patient-physician relationship, compounded by circadian disruption due to shift-based work hours. We therefore aimed to evaluate the impact of a semi-structured peer-to-peer coaching program on the professional development and well-being of first-year emergency medicine residents (PGY-1s).

Methods

Study design

This was quasi-experimental study, with a single-group pre-post intervention design aimed to evaluate the self-reported (via online survey) impact of peer-to-peer coaching. The study surveyed domains related to residents’ emotional wellbeing, professional fulfillment and development, career satisfaction, perceptions on the residency program’s receptiveness to the residents’ needs regarding work-life balance and career development, and many others. The primary outcome researched in this paper was emotional exhaustion.

Setting and participants

The eligible cohort comprised all incoming PGY-1 emergency medicine residents at the institution in the 2024–2025 academic year (n = 25). The incoming PGY-1s were approached during the residency program’s orientation day and contacted via follow-up email by program leadership. Participation was voluntary and uncompensated. The PGY-1 were provided with an information sheet and had to provide written informed consent prior to enrollment. All eligible residents enrolled and completed the baseline and post-intervention surveys (no missing data). There were no additional inclusion or exclusion criteria applied.

Coach training

Peer coaches (PGY-4 EM residents; n = 16) completed a two-session, faculty-led training. The first session was didactic, lasting approximately 45 min, introducing the coaches to their new role, expectations, boundaries, and the GROW coaching model (Goal, current Reality, exploring Options, determining the Way forward). The coaches were then assigned self-directed learning on the GROW model after it was briefly demonstrated. The second session, lasting approximately two hours, was an interactive discussion and small-group practice to refine the coach’s utilization of the GROW model, where the common goals, issues, and barriers faced by PGY-1s were also discussed.

Intervention

PGY-1s were not coached for approximately the first month of their residency, after which the composite survey was administered to determine a pre-intervention baseline. The PGY-1s were then matched to PGY-4 coaches randomly (although all of them preferred for their coaches to be of the same gender), coached for six months, followed again by the administration of the survey.

All PGY-1 residents completed two scheduled peer-to-peer coaching sessions. Additional sessions were PGY-1-initiated and encouraged according to individual need. Sessions were semi-structured, consistent in format, and capped at 60 min. Coaches opened with neutral, open-ended check-in questions to elicit reflections on recent clinical and academic experiences and priorities; conversations then followed the GROW framework to set a specific, near-term goal, examine current barriers and resources, generate options, and agree on an action plan.

Data collection

The composite survey compiled by Winkel et al. [7] was used as a template for the survey used in our study, though some of the scales were omitted to avoid repetition fatigue and inattentive responses from the respondent’s perspective. Despite this, the same domains were assessed in our adapted survey. The following constructs were surveyed: burnout, using single-item surrogates for emotional exhaustion and depersonalization [12]; resilience to stress, via the Brief Resilience Coping Scale (BRCS) [13]; attitudes towards setting goals, using the survey items developed by Winkel et al. [7] derived from the publication on goal setting by Li et al. [14]; perceived support from the coaching program in career advancement and work-life balance, with the help of the C-Change Resident Survey [15]; the perceived impact of coaching, using the survey developed by Winkel et al. [7]; and finally self-reported workplace confidence, by adapting a survey developed by Gold et al. [8]. Note that only two items from the C-Change Resident Survey [15] were used.

The attitudes toward goal setting [7, 14], the BRCS [13], C-Change Resident Survey [15], and confidence in communication [8] surveys all used a five-point agreement scale (Likert scale); with one being ‘Strongly Agree’ and five being ‘Strongly Disagree.’ The single-item surrogates for emotional exhaustion and depersonalization used a zero to six (0–6) scale with zero being ‘Never’ and six being ‘Everyday’. Finally, the perceived impact of coaching [7] survey used a zero to four (0–4) scale with zero being ‘My coach has been a negative influence’ and four being ‘My coach has had a huge positive influence’. Note that the BRCS anchors were modified to be an agreement scale to reduce risk of respondent confusion. The scales, items, anchors, and scoring keys can be found in our Open Science Framework (OSF) repository [16].

Internal consistency reliability (Cronbach’s α) was computed for the modified scales used in this paper; results are summarized in Table 1. Internal consistency was acceptable; however, the perceived impact of coaching [7] scale had a Cronbach’s alpha of ≥ 0.97 indicating item redundancy and potential for respondent burden. Full reliability outputs (including item-total correlations) are available in our repository [16].

At the end of the post-intervention survey, participants were asked an open-ended question: “What would you change for next year’s peer coaching program?” Responses were collected in their free-text format.

Statistical analysis

Since some of the surveys were up to 15 items long, it was decided that for ease of reading, the group-level mean (sometimes referred to as the mean of means) of the answers to each survey would be presented in the descriptive statistics, as opposed to all individuals’ answers to each survey item within a scale (except for BRCS). The group-level mean was calculated by averaging each individual’s answers to all items within a scale, then summing the averages of all individuals, and dividing them by the number of individuals.

As for the inferential statistics, the mean (and sum score for BRCS) of each individual’s responses to the items within a scale was calculated pre-coaching and post-coaching and compared using paired t-test, Wilcoxon test (for robustness check), and Holm–Šídák method as a correction method for secondary outcomes, via GraphPad Prism version 10.5.0. Cohen’s d for the outcomes and the internal consistency reliability of scales were computed using JASP version 0.95.1.0. A p-value of less than 0.05 (p < 0.05) was considered statistically significant.

Participants reported improved support after peer-to-peer coaching compared to prior to the coaching program (p = 0.072; 95% CI −0.67, 0.03); while the difference is statistically insignificant, there is a trend towards better support post-coaching (Table 3).

Post-coaching, the PGY-1s also reported being more confident in peer-to-peer communication, patient communication, and their role as residents, though the difference was statistically insignificant (p = 0.158; 95% CI −0.41, 0.071) (Table 3).

Post-coaching responses regarding the impact of peer-to-peer coaching indicated that it had a significant positive impact on the initiative towards self-directed learning, professional fulfillment, personal development, teamwork, and work-life balance (p = 0.039; 95% CI −0.71, −0.02) (Table 3).

Though statistically insignificant, there was a trend towards coaching positively affecting the proactivity of the PGY-1s, in that their ability to set goals was augmented (p = 0.089; 95% CI −0.45, 0.03) (Table 3).

The open-ended remarks section was very enlightening on ideas for improvement of the coaching program. The most recurring suggestions was to include at least one mandatory in-person session at the beginning of the program for rapport-building, followed by more scheduled encounters conducted via the coachee and coach’s preferred means of communication (e.g., telephone, in-person). Many also suggested that shift coordinators should group the coach and coachee together on a regular basis, not only to improve rapport but also to give the coach an insight into gaps for improvement. Some also suggested that sessions should be more structured.

Discussion

In a cohort study, Winkel et al. [7] reported that coached residents were less likely to experience burnout, which contrasts with our findings. This discrepancy likely reflects the limitations of our single-group, quasi-experimental design. Nevertheless, our results align with Winkel et al. [7] in demonstrating positive effects of coaching on self-directed learning and professional development. Similarly, Gold et al. [8] and Rassbach et al. [17] observed improvements in confidence and communication skills among coached residents, consistent with the enhanced self-confidence and communication reported in our program. In addition, our participants described improvements in goal setting, work–life balance, career advancement, wellness, and professional fulfillment, paralleling outcomes reported in other recent coaching interventions [7, 9]. In emergency medicine specifically, a mixed methods pilot study by Myers et al. [18] found that coaching did not significantly reduce burnout, a finding consistent with our results.

Collectively, this suggests that peer-to-peer coaching in emergency medicine can provide benefits comparable to those observed in other specialties. The results also indicate that peer-to-peer coaching may be equivalent in effect to other described coaching or mentoring programs, which usually involve senior program faculty. While faculty-led coaching programs are valid, peer-to-peer coaching offers a more feasible option, as the number of senior residents typically matches the number of junior residents, unlike the ratio of senior program faculty to residents.

This study’s single-group pre-post design limits causal inference. The observed increase in reported emotional exhaustion and depersonalization post-coaching may reflect the natural PGY-1 trajectory, rather than the effect of the coaching itself. To disentangle these influences, future work on the subject should include a control group. The modest sample (n = 25) also limits the yield of the inferences and generalizability. In the absence of a control group, the results regarding workplace confidence and goal setting are also susceptible to time-related confounding due to increased clinical exposure in the participants. Finally, the number of coaching sessions per resident was not recorded, precluding dose-response analyses, which would be an impactful addition to future research on the subject.

Conclusion

Peer-to-peer coaching may positively impact residents’ professional fulfilment, personal development, peer-to-peer communication, patient communication and more. Coaching programs, specifically peer-to-peer programs, may benefit new residents and perhaps provide a further sense of professional and personal fulfilment to the senior residents. We recommend that residency programs study and implement such programs where possible.
