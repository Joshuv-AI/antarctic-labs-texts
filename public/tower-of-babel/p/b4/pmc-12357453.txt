Association between basal metabolic rate and cognitive function in Chinese older adults: the mediating effect of sarcopenia
Authors: Hong Ding, Lei Zhang, Chao Ma, Zhizi Shao, Xiaojiang Zhao, Changqing Li
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundCognitive aging poses a major worldwide health concern. The association between basal metabolic rate (BMR) and cognition isn’t well understood. This research examines sarcopenia’s potential role as a mediator: linking BMR to cognitive function.MethodsAnalysis used China Health and Retirement Longitudinal Study (CHARLS) data from adults aged ≥ 60 years (2015, 2018 waves). Mental integrity and episodic memory recall assessed cognitive function. Linear multivariate analyses were conducted to examine the associations between BMR, sarcopenia, and cognitive function, adjusting for potential confounders. Mediation analyses explored sarcopenia’s role in the BMR-cognitive function relationship. Restricted cubic splines assessed nonlinear relationships, and subgroup analyses evaluated group heterogeneity.ResultsLongitudinal linear multivariate modeling, after covariate adjustment, demonstrated a significant positive association linking BMR to cognitive function (β = 1.23, 95% CI: 0.25–2.21, P = 0.014). Sarcopenia showed an inverse relationship with cognitive function (β = − 0.75, 95% CI: −1.11– −0.40, P < 0.001). Critically, sarcopenia mediated 26.98% of BMR’s total effect on cognitive function, with an indirect effect of 5.27 × 10⁻⁴ (95% CI: 8.61 × 10⁻⁴– 1.54 × 10⁻³). Subgroup analysis indicated that smoking status and place of residence had significant effects on the relationship between basal metabolic rate and cognitive function.ConclusionsThe study found that BMR is positively associated with cognitive function, whereas sarcopenia is inversely associated with cognitive function and mediates the BMR- cognitive function association.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12877-025-06296-8.Keywords: Sarcopenia, Basal metabolic rate, Cognitive function, Older adults

Introduction

The global aging demographic presents an unprecedented challenge, as projections indicate that the population aged 60 and over will reach 2.1 billion by 2050. Cognitive function encompasses information reception and transformation processes, including memory, fluid reasoning, visual processing, and auditory processing [1]. Numerous studies have consistently demonstrated that various factors, including hypertension [2], psychological stress [3], diabetes [4], obesity [5], dyslipidemia [6], fatigue [7], smoking [8], and physical inactivity [9], are implicated as potential risk factors influencing cognitive function. Previous research estimated that in 2010, there were 15.07 million dementia cases and 38.77 million mild cognitive impairment cases among Chinese adults aged ≥ 60 years [10]. Globally, dementia currently affects more than 50 million people, with projections indicating this number could rise to 152 million by 2050 [11]. Age-related cognitive deterioration poses a critical public health challenge, causing adverse individual outcomes and financial strain on families/society [12]. Identifying modifiable factors to slow cognitive deterioration in China is therefore imperative.

Basal metabolic rate (BMR)—the minimal resting energy expenditure for vital physiological function—represents a key metabolic component linked to health outcomes [13]. As a high-energy-consumption organ, the brain requires adequate energy to sustain neuronal activities and cognition. Elevated BMR potentially signals enhanced metabolic efficiency, facilitating optimal cognitive performance through sufficient cerebral energy supply. Empirical findings confirm positive correlations between increased BMR and cognitive test scores, suggesting metabolic advantages may support enhanced cognition [14]. Additionally, BMR may affect cognitive function by affecting neuroprotective processes. Metabolic rate is associated with the regulation of oxidative stress and inflammation, which are key contributors to cognitive decline [15, 16]. Higher BMR helps to enhance antioxidant defenses and reduce inflammatory responses, thereby protecting the brain from damage and protecting cognitive function. The protective role of BMR has been further emphasized by studies showing reverse causality between BMR and the risk of Alzheimer’s disease and Parkinson’s dementia, suggesting that higher BMR may be a protective factor against these diseases [14].

Recent studies highlight BMR as a key physiological factor linked to sarcopenia in older individuals. Sarcopenia, characterized by the gradual loss of muscle mass and function, increases with age [17]. New findings propose that a lower BMR might act as an indicator for identifying sarcopenia, particularly among older males. A study involving 305 men found that those with a BMR under 1612 kcal/day had a notably higher incidence of sarcopenia compared to individuals with higher metabolic rates [18]. Sarcopenia contributes to decreased energy expenditure, a BMR component. Maintaining adequate BMR may thus help mitigate sarcopenia risk [19]. Additionally, Physical activity (PA) and dietary habits influence the BMR-sarcopenia link. Evidence shows that regular exercise, especially when paired with sufficient caloric intake, can boost BMR and decrease the likelihood of sarcopenia, particularly in aging populations [20].

Cognitive decline is linked to sarcopenia [21]. Research in biology and population-based studies indicate that impaired muscle function correlates with diminished cognitive abilities in older adults [22]. Hu et al. (2022) found individuals with probable sarcopenia face elevated risks of incident mild cognitive impairment [23], a result corroborated by international analyses [24]. Nevertheless, existing evidence concerning the sarcopenia-cognitive function relationship predominantly relies on cross-sectional investigations [25–27], necessitating large-scale prospective studies for further confirmation. Shared pathological mechanisms likely underlie both sarcopenia and cognitive impairment. Common mediators like inflammation, oxidative stress, and hormonal fluctuations can influence both conditions. For instance, elevated inflammatory markers such as interleukin-6 (IL-6) correlate with both muscle degeneration and cognitive deterioration, implying systemic inflammation might connect these pathologies [28]. Moreover, alterations in hormones known to impact muscular and neurological health, particularly testosterone and insulin-like growth factor-1 (IGF-1), may help explain the association between sarcopenia and cognitive decline [28].

Consequently, the present investigation employed the 2015 third wave of the China Health and Retirement Longitudinal Study (CHARLS) as its baseline assessment, with follow-up conducted using the fourth wave (2018). This dataset features a 3-year gap between collection points, permitting longitudinal analysis of BMR and cognitive function in China’s older population. Furthermore, we examined sarcopenia’s potential mediating effect within this association. Our work extends previous findings in two significant ways: (1) It offers initial longitudinal evidence connecting BMR to cognitive function within an older Chinese demographic; (2) it establishes sarcopenia as a mediating factor in this relationship. Consequently, these results will aid in detecting older individuals at elevated risk and guide the formulation of tailored preventive approaches and interventions to reduce cognitive deterioration.

Methods

Study design and population

CHARLS is a national longitudinal survey representing Chinese adults aged ≥ 45 and their spouses. It gathers high-quality microdata reflecting China’s midlife/older population using multi-stage proportional sampling. Face-to-face interviews occurred across 450 communities within 150 counties spanning 28 provinces. Extensive demographic, lifestyle, and health data were gathered. Survey methodology details are published elsewhere [29]. Biennial follow-ups combined questionnaires and physical measurements. Data access: http://charls.pku.edu.cn. Peking University’s Biomedical Ethics Committee approved this work (IRB00001052-11014/11015) with participant informed consent.

The 2015 baseline survey encompassed 21,095 individuals. Progressive exclusion criteria ensured methodological consistency: age < 60 years (n = 11,251); clinical dementia/Parkinson’s diagnosis (n = 252); incomplete cognitive assessments (n = 2,516); missing BMR data (n = 2,845); absent sarcopenia measurements (n = 15); and 2015–2018 attrition (n = 1,511). This study’s analytical group included 2,705 qualified participants. Figure 1 outlines selection and exclusion criteria.

Assessments

Cognitive function

Cognitive function was assessed during the 2018 follow-up using episodic memory and mental intactness measures. Participants attempted to recall up to ten words immediately after auditory presentation. Following a 10-minute interval, they recalled the same words again (delayed recall). The episodic memory score (0–10 points) represented the mean of immediate and delayed recall performances. Mental intactness evaluation involved copying intersecting pentagons, reporting date/season/weekday, and completing five serial-7 subtractions from 100. Correct answers scored one point (max = 11) [30, 31]. Participants’ overall cognitive status was quantified by summing all components (range: 0–21), with higher scores indicating superior function. Per established protocols, cognitive impairment was operationalized as performance at or below 1.0 standard deviation (SD) under the cognitive mean [32, 33].

Basal metabolic rate

BMR estimation applied the Mifflin-St Jeor equations [34]:

Males: BMR = 10 × weight (kg) + 6.25 × height (cm) − 5 × age (years) + 5.

Females: BMR = 10 × weight (kg) + 6.25 × height (cm) − 5 × age (years) – 161.

Sarcopenia

2019 Asian Working Group for Sarcopenia (AWGS2019) criteria [35] exclude sarcopenia diagnosis in those with intact chair stand ability and preserved strength. Probable sarcopenia applies to borderline cases showing strength or functional impairment. Definitive diagnosis requires low muscle mass plus either strength deficiency or functional limitation.

Handgrip strength (HGS) evaluation employed a calibrated TM WL-1000 dynamometer (kg units). Skeletal muscle strength was assessed by averaging peak measurements across both trials. Per diagnostic thresholds [35], reduced strength was defined as HGS < 28 kg (men) or < 18 kg (women).

Functional impairment was evaluated using the Short Physical Performance Battery (SPPB), chair stand test, and gait speed assessment. Impairment criteria were defined as meeting any of the following thresholds: gait velocity below 1.0 m/s, > 12 s for five-repetition chair stand, or SPPB score ≤ 9 [35].

Appendicular skeletal muscle mass (ASM)—a principal indicator of muscle quantity—was calculated using validated prediction Eqs. [23, 36]. Studies demonstrate high concordance between ASM values derived from these equations and dual-energy X-ray absorptiometry (DXA), the reference method for body composition analysis. This measurement reliability is further verified in Chinese demographic samples [37].

ASM = 0.193 × weight (kg) + 0.107 × height (cm) − 4.157 × sex (1 = male, 2 = female) − 0.037 × age (years) − 2.631.

Standardized instruments measured anthropometrics: Seca TM213 stadiometer (height) and HN-286 scale (weight). The anthropometric index ASM/Ht² was derived by dividing ASM by height squared (m²). Cohort-specific low muscle mass thresholds utilized sex-stratified 20th percentile cutoffs: males: ≤6.79 kg/m², females: ≤4.90 kg/m² [23, 36].

Control variables

Covariates included age, sex, marital status (married/unmarried), residence (urban/rural), chronic disease count (0/1/≥2), smoking status (current/never), alcohol consumption (yes/no), and education (≥ high school, middle school, ≤primary school/illiterate). Chronic diseases included hypertension, diabetes, and other specified conditions, quantified by the number present: 0, 1, or 2 or more. Body mass index (BMI) was calculated from height/weight data and categorized: underweight (< 18.5 kg/m²),normal weight (18.5–24.9 kg/m²),overweight (25–29.9 kg/m²),obese (≥ 30 kg/m²).

Statistical analysis

Statistical analyses employed R 4.3.3 and Free Statistics platform 2.0. Sample characteristics are summarized as mean ± standard deviation for continuous measures and frequency (percentage) for categorical variables. Continuous variable normality was evaluated using the Shapiro-Wilk test; BMR underwent natural logarithmic transformation. Relationships between primary variables were assessed via Spearman’s rank correlation. Linear multivariate regression quantified associations between BMR, sarcopenia, and cognitive function using standardized β coefficients. Four progressively adjusted models were specified: (1) Model 0: Unadjusted. (2) Model 1: Age, sex, education, marital status, residence. (3) Model 2: Model 1 + smoking, alcohol consumption. (4) Model 3: Model 2 + BMI, 14 chronic diseases. Restricted cubic spline (RCS) regression assessed possible nonlinear associations with Model 3 covariate adjustment. Predefined demographic, lifestyle, and health-related subgroups were analyzed to assess effect heterogeneity. Sensitivity analyses using multiply imputed complete samples evaluated missing data impact. To mitigate reverse causation risk, baseline cognitively impaired participants were excluded. Multicollinearity was assessed via variance inflation factors (VIF), with values > 5 indicating critical collinearity. Mediation analysis employed the Baron-Kenny framework to evaluate sarcopenia’s role in the BMR-cognition relationship [38]. Specifically, the mediation proportion was computed, with significance determined by 1000 bootstrap iterations. Statistical significance threshold was p < 0.05.

Result

Baseline features of the individuals included in the study

The cohort (number = 2,705; mean age 66.6 ± 5.3 years) was stratified by sex, with characteristics detailed in Table 1. Participants comprised 1,144 females (66.2 ± 5.1 years) and 1,561 males (66.9 ± 5.5 years). Most resided rurally (56.0%) and were married (86.4%). Significant sex differences (p < 0.001) emerged across sociodemographic factors (age, residence, marital status, education), lifestyle behaviors (smoking, alcohol consumption), and clinical measures (BMI, chronic disease prevalence). Notable disparities included BMR (females 1027.8 ± 140.2 vs. males 1322.5 ± 145.2 kcal/day), sarcopenia prevalence (32.6% [n = 371] vs. 12.9% [n = 201]), and cognitive scores in both 2015 (11.0 ± 3.2 vs. 11.7 ± 2.6) and 2018 (10.2 ± 3.9 vs. 11.1 ± 3.3), all statistically significant (p < 0.001). Furthermore, sensitivity analysis revealed no significant multicollinearity (the maximum VIF = 3.74 < 5), supporting the robustness of the multivariate estimates (Supplementary Table 1).

The relationships between important variables

Table 2 shows associations among baseline BMR, sarcopenia, and 2018 cognitive function. Preliminary evaluation of 2,705 individuals demonstrated marked unadjusted BMR-cognition associations (β = 3.75, 95% CI:3.06–4.43, P < 0.001). This relationship remained significant after progressive covariate adjustment: Model 1 (demographic factors: β = 1.38, 95% CI: 0.52–2.25, P = 0.002), Model 2 (Model 1 + lifestyle: β = 1.38, 95% CI: 0.52–2.24, P = 0.002), and Model 3 (Model 2 + clinical variables: β = 1.23, 95% CI: 0.25–2.21, P = 0.014). After correcting for these confounders, cognitive scores may increase by 1.23 points for each unit increase in BMR. The minimal attenuation of effect sizes across increasingly stringent models indicates BMR’s robust independent association with cognitive performance, suggesting its potential protective role in cognitive function.

Furthermore, baseline sarcopenia demonstrated a negative association with 2018 cognitive function (β = -1.57, 95% CI: -1.89 to -1.24, P < 0.001). Subsequent stepwise adjustments revealed a diminished yet consistently significant association: Model 1, adjusted for demographic variables, yielded β = -0.73 (95% CI: -1.04 to -0.43; P < 0.001); Model 2, with additional adjustments for lifestyle factors, indicated β = -0.72 (95% CI: -1.02 to -0.41); and Model 3, fully adjusted for clinical covariates, remained significant with β = -0.75 (95% CI: -1.11 to -0.40; P < 0.001). After correcting for these confounders, each unit increase in sarcopenia may result in a 0.75-point decrease in cognitive scores. This enduring negative relationship underscores a robust and independent association between sarcopenia and cognitive function, even after controlling for multiple covariates, suggesting that sarcopenia may constitute a risk factor for cognitive decline.

RCS analysis identified no significant nonlinear association between BMR and cognitive function (P = 0.598; Supplementary Fig. 1).

Supplementary Table 2 displays the results of subgroup analyses for the association between BMR and cognitive function, highlighting that smoking (P = 0.02 for interaction) and place of residence (P = 0.01 for interaction) significantly influenced this relationship. Furthermore, Supplementary Table 3 presents the subgroup analysis results regarding the association between sarcopenia and cognitive function, demonstrating that marital status (P = 0.003 for interaction) significantly affected this association.

Sarcopenia mediated the relationship between baseline basal metabolic rate and follow-up cognitive function

Table 3 illustrates the interrelationships among baseline BMR, sarcopenia, and 2018 cognitive function. BMR was positively associated with cognitive function (r = 0.22, P < 0.001). Additionally, BMR was negatively associated with sarcopenia (r = -0.52, P < 0.001), while sarcopenia was negatively associated with cognitive function (r = -0.18, P < 0.001). These association patterns suggest that sarcopenia may partially mediate the relationship between BMR and cognitive function.

β0 was the total effect of basal metabolic rate on cognitive function; β1 represents the effect of basal metabolic rate on sarcopenia; β2 represents the effect of sarcopenia on cognitive function. The mediation effect was computed as the product of “β1” and “β2“(β1 × β2), and the mediation proportion was calculated as the ratio of the mediation effect product to total effects [(β1 × β2)/β0]. Discussion

This cohort investigation of older Chinese adults revealed a positive association between BMR and cognitive function, contrasting with sarcopenia’s negative relationship with cognitive function. Individuals with elevated BMR demonstrated lower probability of cognitive deterioration during the 3-year follow-up, whereas sarcopenia correlated with increased susceptibility to cognitive decline. Significantly, this pioneering research establishes sarcopenia’s mediating effect within the BMR-cognition relationship. Consistent with our hypothesis, sarcopenia partially mediated the connection between BMR and cognitive function. Subgroup analyses indicated smoking status and geographic residence significantly modified the BMR-cognition association, while marital status substantially influenced the sarcopenia-cognition relationship.

A previous cross-sectional study using data from the National Health and Nutrition Examination Survey demonstrated that BMR was positively associated with cognitive scores [14]. However, due to its cross-sectional design, this study does not allow for causal inference. Additionally, the study population in the United States is limited to a specific demographic group, necessitating further prospective studies with more diverse samples to validate these findings and investigate potential biological mechanisms.

Longitudinal findings revealed a positive association between BMR and cognitive function. Quantitatively, each unit elevation in BMR corresponded to a 1.23-point cognitive function enhancement (β = 1.23, 95% CI: 0.25–2.21, P = 0.014). Elevated BMR potentially indicates superior energy metabolism efficiency, crucial for sustaining neuronal viability and cognition. Cerebral energy requirements are substantial, accounting for approximately 20% of the body’s total energy consumption. Inadequate energy provision directly compromises neurotransmitter synthesis and release—critical processes for neural signaling [39]. Furthermore, deficient metabolic activity may initiate intracellular stress responses, subsequently damaging cerebral structure and cognitive capacity [40]. Prior research indicates chronic metabolic insufficiency potentially induces neurodegenerative pathology, including aberrant tau protein phosphorylation and β-amyloid accumulation in Alzheimer’s disease (AD) [41]. Enhanced BMR signifies greater systemic metabolic efficiency, improves cerebrovascular circulation, and augments oxygen/nutrient delivery to neurons [42]. This optimized perfusion and nutritional support maintains normal neurophysiological operations and facilitates neuronal repair [43]. BMR’s potential neuroprotective effects may also derive from its role in preserving neuronal homeostasis. Neurodegenerative conditions frequently correlate with diminished cerebral metabolic rates. Increased BMR provides sufficient energy to sustain neuronal integrity, potentially delaying neurodegenerative progression. Existing studies suggest elevated BMR confers protection through enhanced mitochondrial function and reduced oxidative stress [44]. Mitochondria function not merely as cellular powerplants but also as central regulators of oxidative stress pathways [45]. In neurodegeneration, impaired mitochondrial activity may cause energy deficits and accelerate neuronal degeneration. Additionally, heightened BMR could improve cerebral glucose utilization efficiency, thereby offering neuroprotection [46]. Given glucose’s primacy as the brain’s energy substrate, disruptions affecting its metabolism impair neuronal survival and function [47]. In AD and Parkinson’s dementia, glucose metabolic abnormalities represent early markers of pathological progression.

Nutritional intake critically influences BMR and thereby modulates sarcopenia risk. Sufficient consumption of macronutrients—especially proteins and carbohydrates—proves essential for preserving muscular integrity. Evidence indicates that elevated BMR and BMR normalized to body surface area (BSA) function protectively against sarcopenic obesity and sarcopenia. This benefit partly arises from nutrients like carbohydrates; moderate intake positively affects skeletal muscle index (SMI) through BMR regulation and optimized hydration status. Balanced carbohydrate consumption may specifically enhance muscular health by elevating BMR and improving fluid distribution, both crucial for muscle performance [48]. PA elevates energy expenditure, promoting favorable body composition through muscle preservation and adipose reduction. This alignment between energy intake and muscular maintenance demands reduces sarcopenia susceptibility [49]. Resistance training particularly demonstrates an inverse association with sarcopenia, showing significant protective effects among males aged < 50 years. Regular PA participation modifies the BMR-sarcopenia relationship by enhancing metabolic efficiency and muscle retention. Resultant increases in lean mass and metabolic rate foster advantageous energy equilibrium, diminishing sarcopenia probability [49].

Mediation testing revealed sarcopenia’s partial mediation of the BMR-cognitive function pathway. After comprehensive adjustment of covariates, for every 1 unit increase in sarcopenia, cognitive function scores decreased 0.75 points (β = -0.75, 95% CI: -1.11–0.40; P < 0.001). These findings position sarcopenia as a plausible risk factor for cognitive deterioration. HGS—a key sarcopenia diagnostic component—may reflect neurological integrity [50]. Existing evidence connects neural pathways to executive functioning [51]. Cortical and subcortical regions governing manual dexterity concurrently regulate cognitive processes [52]. The motor cortex, which coordinates voluntary movements, potentially influences cognition through connections to prefrontal and hippocampal regions essential for executive control, memory encoding, and attentional processes [53]. Subcortical structures including the basal ganglia (involved in procedural memory) and thalamus (sensory-motor relay center) support both motor coordination and cognitive operations. Impairments within these networks correlate with diminished HGS and cognitive deficits across memory, linguistic, and attentional domains [53]. Shared pathological mechanisms like oxidative stress and chronic inflammation may further underlie the sarcopenia-cognition relationship [54–56]. Oxidative damage from free radical-detoxification imbalances particularly harms muscular and neural tissues. In sarcopenia, this process exacerbates muscle atrophy and functional decline, while cerebral oxidative injury accelerates neurodegeneration and cognitive impairment [57]. Age-related chronic inflammation (“inflammaging”) features elevated Interleukin-1β (IL-1β), IL-6, and Tumor Necrosis Factor-α (TNF-α) cytokines that disrupt muscle protein equilibrium and promote neural inflammation, thereby advancing both sarcopenia and cognitive decline [58–60]. IGF-1, critical for muscular maintenance and neuroplasticity, declines with aging. Reduced IGF-1 availability impairs muscle protein synthesis—modulated by IGFBP-1 binding proteins where elevated Insulin-like Growth Factor Binding Protein-1 (IGFBP-1) predicts muscle loss—while simultaneously hindering adult neurogenesis. Testosterone may further modulate muscle-brain crosstalk via androgen receptor interactions influencing metabolic and cognitive pathways [61].

Based on the findings of this study, public health strategies should emphasize integrated interventions targeting both metabolic and muscular health. Community-based initiatives that promote protein-rich diets and resistance training can help sustain BMR while mitigating sarcopenia, particularly among high-risk subgroups, such as rural residents and smokers, where the associations are most pronounced. Health systems should integrate BMR and sarcopenia screening into geriatric care protocols to enable early detection and intervention. Such cost-effective measures, which align with the World Health Organization’s objectives for healthy ageing, can address both energy metabolism and musculoskeletal health, thereby substantially reducing the burden of cognitive decline in rapidly ageing populations, including China.

Our study has several benefits. Firstly, the multi-stage sampling of CHARLS across 28 provinces in China improves the generalizations to Chinese older adults. Secondly, this is the first longitudinal evidence that sarcopenia may mediate the relationship between BMR-cognitive function (the mediation rate was 26.98%). Finally, BMR and sarcopenia emerged as potentially modifiable factors that could be targeted to prevent cognitive decline. However, several limitations warrant consideration. Firstly, the study concentrated on an older population within the specific sociocultural and demographic context of China, restricting the applicability of the results to younger age groups or diverse ethnic and cultural populations. Secondly, we estimated BMR by means of the formula (Mifflin-St Jeor) rather than indirect calorimetry. Thirdly, unmeasured confounding variables – including specific PA, diet, or chronic inflammatory disorders–could have impacted observed relationships and modified mediation magnitude. Fourthly, excluding incomplete participants may cause selection bias, limiting findings’ generalizability. Fifthly, sarcopenia uses anthropometric measures (rather than the DXA gold standard) and adjusting muscle mass to the square of height can be misleading - particularly in overweight or obese individuals - and can mask or underestimate low muscle mass in such individuals [62]. Finally, this longitudinal study did not adjust for baseline cognitive function as a covariate, so temporal relationships and causal inferences may be limited, and reverse causality cannot be ruled out.

Future research should prioritize several directions to validate and deepen insights into the BMR-sarcopenia-cognition nexus. Firstly, employ objective measures: indirect calorimetry for BMR and DXA for muscle mass to address methodological limitations. Secondly, integrate biomarker analyses (e.g., inflammatory cytokines, IGF-1, oxidative stress markers) to elucidate underlying biological pathways. Thirdly, conduct intervention trials testing combined metabolic-muscle targets (e.g., resistance training + nutritional support) to establish causality. Fourthly, extend longitudinal designs with baseline cognitive adjustments and longer follow-ups across diverse populations (younger cohorts, multi-ethnic groups) to enhance generalizability and explore life-course effects. Finally, leverage advanced neuroimaging to assess brain-metabolism interactions.

Conclusion

Our findings suggest that BMR is positively associated with cognitive function, while sarcopenia is negatively associated with cognitive function. In addition, sarcopenia mediates the relationship between BMR and cognitive function. A higher BMR might serve as a protective element for cognitive function, while sarcopenia could act as a risk factor contributing to cognitive decline. These results emphasize the significance of preserving metabolic well-being and mitigating sarcopenia to enhance cognitive abilities in the older population.
