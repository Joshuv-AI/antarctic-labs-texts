A practical approach to ABO-incompatible heart transplantation from the Pediatric Heart Transplant Society (PHTS)
Authors: Neha Bansal, Simon Urschel, Melanie Everitt, Lakshmi R Gokanapudy Hahn, Benjamin S Mantell, Estela Azeka, Madeleine Townsend, Shahnawaz Amdani, Hugo Martinez, Joseph A Spinner, Lori J West
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractABO-incompatible (ABOi) heart transplantation, first performed in infants by West and colleagues in 1996, transformed pediatric heart allocation by challenging longstanding immunologic constraints. Early success of this approach leveraged the developmental immaturity of the neonatal immune system and has since shown comparable short- and long-term outcomes to ABO-compatible (ABOc) transplantation. Over three decades, increasing clinical experience and policy evolution, including broader ABOi eligibility criteria and relaxed titer thresholds, have expanded access to donor hearts and reduced waitlist mortality, particularly among blood group ABO-O candidates. Despite these advances, marked variability persists among centers in the measurement, interpretation, and reporting of ABO antibody titers, directly influencing candidate selection, perioperative management, and post-transplant surveillance. Most assays rely on manual hemagglutination techniques that are subject to significant inter-laboratory and intra-laboratory variability. Emerging single-antigen bead–based methods utilizing the Luminex™ platform may provide an opportunity for standardized, semi-quantitative assessment of graft-relevant anti-A and -B antibody levels.Perioperative strategies are individualized according to ABO antibody titer, with intraoperative plasma exchange or immunoadsorption typically employed when titers exceed a center-specific level. Standard immunosuppression regimens are generally sufficient, with anti-B cell therapies (e.g., rituximab) reserved for elevated or rebound titers. Long-term outcomes remain excellent, with most patients not producing donor-specific ABO antibodies after ABOi transplant. Most centers perform surveillance biopsies only when rising titers, an uncommon occurrence, are accompanied by graft dysfunction or biopsy-proven antibody-mediated rejection, managed with combinations of plasmapheresis, IVIG, and anti-B cell therapies as indicated. Future multicenter collaborations incorporating standardized reporting and longitudinal follow-up will be critical to optimize candidate selection, refine management algorithms, and further improve utilization and outcomes of ABOi heart transplantation.Keywords: ABO incompatible, Heart transplant, Pediatrics

History of ABOi heart transplantation

Intentional transplantation of ABO-incompatible (ABOi) donor hearts in children was first undertaken by a team led by West et al. three decades ago with the first transplant on February 14, 1996.1 This approach was based on the understanding that during the first months of life there is minimal ABO antibody production and an immature immune system that could preclude hyperacute rejection, a longstanding concern amongst transplant professionals.2 The first clinical cohort not only demonstrated the short-term safety of ABOi heart transplantation (HT) in early life but also highlighted that mid-term outcomes with ABOi organs were comparable to ABO-compatible (ABOc) transplants. Subsequent studies from the Pediatric Heart Transplant Society (PHTS) and the U.S. national transplant registry (UNOS) evaluating this approach in patients less than two years of age have demonstrated similar results3, 4, 5: (a) post-HT survival and freedom from coronary allograft vasculopathy (CAV) for ABOi HT recipients are similar to those receiving ABOc HT; (b) post-transplant infection rates appear to be lower in those receiving ABOi HT; (c) offering ABOi HT leads to earlier transplantation, especially for ABO-O patients; and (d) in the US, consideration of ABOi HT has often been reserved for children who are sicker than those receiving ABOc HT.

While ABOi listings have been allowed under the US allocation system since 2002, it was only in 2016 that substantial revisions were implemented to improve utilization of ABOi hearts.6 Additionally, ABO antibody titer thresholds for eligibility were increased from 1:4 to 1:16.7 With the implementation of this policy, ABOi HT in the US increased by 18%.8 Waitlist times for candidates listed as ABOi-eligible were noted to be 63% shorter than those listed as ABOc only. More recently, the OPTN removed the 24-month age cap, making all pediatric recipients with titers of 1:16 or less eligible, and to include regular risk (Status 2) and simultaneous heart/lung candidates.9

ABO antibody levels

Eligibility for ABOi HT is usually determined by anti-A and/or anti-B ABO antibody titers. The only currently available method for clinical measurement of anti-A and anti-B antibodies in patient serum or plasma is based on hemagglutination as first described by Landsteiner in 1901. All clinical ABO antibody titer methods use serial dilution of patient serum or plasma to agglutinate reagent erythrocytes, typically with a subjective reading of the final dilution at which red cell agglutination is visualized. However, many method variations are used, resulting in substantial inter-hospital as well as inter- and intra-individual variability in titer results10, 11, 12, 13 (Table 1). In a national survey conducted by the Japanese ABO Transplantation Committee, titers among 29 institutions analyzing the same serum sample differed by six titer steps (32-fold) in IgM and nine titer steps (256-fold) in IgG measurements between the maximum and minimum value reached.13 Implementation of education and a standardized protocol reduced the variability below four titer steps (8-fold); nonetheless, this remaining variability represents a marked difference (i.e., a titer of 1:8 could have been read as 1:2 to 1:32).

Similarly, there is no standardization of methods, nor even consensus on best methods among the transfusion medicine laboratories at pediatric HT centers performing these tests. Indeed, the methods are not typically reported to the clinical team. This is relevant because defining eligibility for ABOi HT and establishing titer thresholds for biopsy or treatment to address antibodies are center-specific. Yet these activities, and comparing ABOi outcomes between centers and reported in the literature, are based on ABO antibody titers obtained by non-comparable assay methods. When Pediatric Heart Transplant Society (PHTS) centers in the working group were surveyed, each center reported a different method for measuring titers. Among five pediatric HT centers, four used manual tube dilution with red cells and one center reported an automated testing method using red cell adherence technology. There were also differences in whether centers used ABO-A1 vs non-A1 (A2) subgroup reagent red cells for testing anti-A titers (non-A1 erythrocytes are seldom used by transfusion laboratories for titer testing). A number of different methods were used for IgG and IgM isotype assessment. Some centers tested for IgM only, others for both IgM and IgG, whereas most did not differentiate. Incubation times varied from five minutes up to one hour. Among centers that reported testing for “IgG only”, one center treated the sample with dithiothreitol (DTT) to disrupt IgM pentamers, another reported “washing” with saline three times after the initial incubation, and another added anti-IgG (anti-human globulin) to enhance detection of IgG versus IgM. (It is notable that if a test method does not use a denaturing reagent to disrupt IgM, the resultant titer value reflects total IgM and IgG, irrespective of temperature or length of incubation, but is often reported as “IgM”).

These responses highlight the heterogeneity of titer testing and the critical need for method standardization to study multi-center outcomes and to set reasonable and consistent titer guidelines. Harmonization is essential for future comparative studies and clinical trials. Clinicians need to be aware of the limitations of ABO antibody titer testing, the subjective nature of titer interpretation, the lack of standardization across labs, and the impact this has on generalizability of titer results in the current ABOi transplant literature. Importantly, a titer value alone is meaningless without accompanying information on the hemagglutination method used, whether for comparison among centers for protocols for ABOi transplant eligibility or for comparison of outcomes.

A newly created single-antigen bead-based assay using technology employed for assessment of HLA antibodies holds great promise for clinical use, with reporting of antibody levels as mean fluorescence intensities (MFIs).14, 15, 16, 17 Luminex™ technology is highly standardized and widely available in histocompatibility labs worldwide as a semi-quantitative tool for antibody detection. Accurate and reproducible assessment of IgM and IgG anti-A and anti-B levels of all subtype-specificities will be helpful in overcoming the limitations of the hemagglutination assay.

Pre-transplant management

Anti-A and/or anti-B titers should be followed at least monthly during the candidate waiting period, especially after blood product transfusions or IVIG administration, which can transfer ABO antibodies passively and alter their circulating levels in serum. Though no published data yet report an impact on ABOi transplant candidacy or outcomes, passive transfer of ABO antibodies can be minimized by limiting the need for transfusion (i.e., non-critical lab draws, use of pediatric containers to minimize blood volume drawn for required labs, maximizing iron stores, use of erythropoietin, and potentially reinfusion of blood pulled back during line draws (if the re-infection risk is considered minimal), as well as exploring collaboration with the blood banks to utilize blood from a single donor). In addition, data on strategies to reduce passive antibody transmission from blood products, such as red blood cell washing or the use of volume-reduced platelet transfusions, are limited. These practices are largely center-dependent, and blood product preparation methods vary considerably across institutions and countries.18

ABO antibody titers in infants on mechanical circulatory support such as ECMO, who have required blood product administration, can reflect passive antibodies transferred in transfusions, and do not necessarily indicate either de novo ABO natural antibody development or "sensitization" in the patient. Passively-acquired ABO antibodies (i.e., administered with blood products) can be removed by whole blood exchange during cardiopulmonary bypass at time of transplantation (i.e., removing blood and reinfusing ABO-O red blood cells and ABO-AB plasma/platelets). Alternatively, immunoadsorption can be used for antibody removal; incorporation of A/B-antigen columns into the cardiopulmonary bypass circuit provides a novel approach that minimizes blood use.19 For a patient requiring ECMO and expected to progress to transplantation, it may be prudent to use ABO-AB plasma products and recipient-group or ABO-O PRBCs during ECMO support to minimize passively acquired ABO antibodies, though such a strategy is not evidence-supported and might be operationally challenging to implement. ABOi HT outcomes remain excellent across centers with varying approaches to transfusion practices.

Peri-operative management

Strategies for immunosuppression, blood products, and plasma exchange/immunoadsorption depend on the presence (and level) or absence of anti-A and anti-B antibodies, and vary considerably by organ. The upper limit of age or ABO antibody titer for safe ABOi transplantation remains unclear. The latest British Transplantation Society guidelines consider a pre-treatment titer of 1:256 to be a risk factor for antibody-mediated rejection of ABOi kidney grafts, but individual centers may rule out ABOi transplant with a titer of 1:64 or even 1:32.20 Eurotransplant still requires centers to perform ABOi transplants under a research protocol with ethics approval. Although institutional practices vary and titer methods are not usually reported or discussed, plasma exchange is commonly performed when ABO antibody titers are >1:8. Some centers perform intraoperative plasma exchange regardless of titers. It is critical to remember that any titer threshold or comparison of titers requires detailed knowledge of the ABO antibody assay methods used by each center’s transfusion medicine laboratory. Intraoperative immunoadsorption and plasmapheresis have also been used to remove ABO antibodies.19, 21 Antigen-specific immunoadsorption columns are very effective and in Europe are used routinely for antibody removal for ABOi kidney transplantation. Whole blood should never be administered to a child after ABOi HT. Red blood cells can be matched to the recipient ABO group; plasma products (and platelets) should not contain antibodies to either the donor or recipient, thus can be matched to the donor ABO group if the recipient is ABO-O but should be ABO-AB if the recipient is non-O (Table 4, Supplemental tables). It is recommended to communicate these specifications with the entire medical team with documentation in patients’ medical records, direct communication with the institution’s blood bank, and educating families about which group blood products their child can receive. It is recommended and important to notify parents that ABO-O red blood cells and ABO-AB plasma products can be administered in any donor/recipient blood group combination of ABOi HT. It is important to note that blood product processing varies across institutions and internationally, and therefore center-specific tables should be adapted to local blood bank practices (some examples are listed in supplemental tables).

Standard immunosuppression induction practices have been used in the setting of ABOi transplantation.22, 23 Induction therapy with basiliximab and steroids have been reported to safe in the setting of ABOi HT, however, the choice of induction is center-specific in relation to ABO antibody titers, where higher titers may prompt use of more aggressive agents including anti-thymocyte globulin (ATG).24

A recent study reported 14 patients who underwent ABOi HT between ages 2–9 years, concluding that ABOi HT is a safe and effective option for carefully selected older children up to nine years of age.25 However, since ABO antibody production varies greatly across the age spectrum, patient age should not be the criterion by which a decision is made to undertake ABOi HT. Beyond infancy, clinical experience with ABOi HT in teenagers and adults is limited. Tyden et al reported two successful adult cases of ABOi HT performed in 2010 using their center’s clinical protocol devised for ABOi kidney transplants; both these patients are alive and well 15 years post-transplant.26 Further, Bergenfeldt et al reported data from the ISHLT registry showing that 30 ABOi adult HT undertaken since 2005 had short- and long-term outcomes comparable to ABOc transplants.27 This was likely due to careful pre-operative planning for removal of preformed ABO antibodies and use of blood products of the appropriate ABO groups to avoid passive administration of antibodies against the donor and recipient blood groups.

When considering older patients, anti-B cell therapies (e.g., rituximab) may be considered as part of the induction immunosupression (if rituximab was not administered previously for B-cell depletion as part of HLA desensitization). Rituximab can also be considered preoperatively, together with intra-operative antibody removal, if isohemagglutinin titers are higher than center-specific thresholds, or if the titers increase in the immediate postoperative period. It should be noted that preoperative administration of rituximab must be performed well ahead (minimum six hours) of any intraoperative antibody removal, and absence of CD19-expressing B cells confirmed in peripheral blood. Rejection surveillance can be similar to ABOc HT recipients unless clinical issues raise concerns.

Desensitization strategies

Although desensitization protocols are not established for ABOi HT in the pediatric population, adult transplant centers have reported utilization of anti-B cell therapies (e.g., rituximab) in desensitization for ABOi liver/kidney transplantation in the setting of elevated ABO antibody titers.28, 29 Other therapeutic options that have been used in combination with rituximab include plasmapheresis, intravenous immunoglobulin (IVIG) infusions and even splenectomy.30, 31 A small cohort of ABOi liver transplant patients showed no survival difference between patients receiving pre-transplant rituximab plus plasmapheresis vs. rituximab alone, and a rebound rise in ABO antibody titers did not occur in either group.32 In kidney transplantation, the initial strategies for ABO antibody reduction were plasmapheresis and splenectomy. These methods have been replaced by ABO antigen-specific immunoadsorption or plasma exchange and rituximab.33 Although rebound of ABO antibodies before and after kidney transplantation has been observed if desensitization is interrupted, this phenomenon does not seem to hamper a successful transplant.34, 35 Indeed, a post-transplant rise in serum antibody titers to the donor ABO group without concomitant graft damage has been commonly observed in adult ABOi kidney transplantation; this phenomenon is referred to as “graft accommodation”, the precise mechanisms of which remain unclear.

Long-term post-transplant management

Data reported by West and colleagues post-transplantation demonstrated that ABOi HT in early childhood induced B-cell tolerance to donor ABO antigens rather than sensitization.1, 36, 37 In the first reported cohort it was observed that ABO antibody titers specifically against the donor blood group remained absent or substantially lower than in ABOc recipients (≤1:8); in ABO-O recipients antibodies against the third-party (not transplanted) blood group developed normally with age.1, 24 Absence of normal range ABO antibody titers towards the donor blood group has been documented to persist many years post-transplantation and with re-transplantation.24, 38

Given the uncertainty around the clinical significance of isolated titer elevations, most expert recommendations support combining serial ABO antibody titers with clinical assessment and noninvasive surveillance (echocardiography and biomarkers) and reserving endomyocardial biopsy when titers are rising in association with graft dysfunction or when histologic confirmation of antibody-mediated rejection (AMR) is needed to guide therapy. In practice, some centers will perform a biopsy if titers exceed selected thresholds or if there is graft dysfunction. It is critical that AMR related to HLA donor-specific antibodies (DSA) be ruled out in this situation. If the biopsy shows evidence of AMR in the absence of HLA DSA, the expert consensus/recommendations from this author group are to consider use of plasmapheresis as first-line for antibody removal and anti-B cell therapies such as rituximab to diminish further antibody production (± IVIG monthly x six doses as immunoglobulin replacement for infectious risks). Diffuse intragraft capillary C4d deposition is common in ABOi organ transplantation, possibly related to accommodation and non-pathologic carbohydrate antibody binding. Distinguishing features between expected C4d positivity and true AMR includes histologic evidence of endothelial cell activation and swelling, intravascular macrophages, microvascular inflammation, and correlation with clinical graft dysfunction. Long-term or randomized data on this topic are lacking, given the rarity of ABO-related AMR in the pediatric HT setting. Both rituximab and repeated IVIG carry important adverse-event profiles that must be weighed against potential benefit. Rituximab can lead to prolonged hypogammaglobulinemia, late-onset neutropenia and increased risk of infections and may require immunoglobulin replacement in some patients.

ABO-A2 subgroup donor hearts and anti-A1 titers

Within the ABO blood group system, subgroup distinction in ABO-A (and ABO-AB) organ donors is clinically relevant. Approximately 80% of ABO-A individuals are of the A1 subgroup while about 20% are non-A1, often referred to collectively as A2. ABO-A2 individuals express lower density A-antigen epitopes on cells and tissues than A1, and a more limited array of A-antigen sugars. Thus, organs from A2 donors are less immunogenic; accordingly, the immune reaction of an ABO-O or -B recipient to an ABO-A2 donor organ is attenuated. The plant lectin derived from Dolichos biflorus seeds selectively agglutinates A1 erythrocytes and can be used to discriminate ABO-A1 subgroup phenotype individuals; however, ABO genotyping has been reported to have greatly superior accuracy than A1-lectin phenotyping, and some groups advocate for mandatory ABO genotyping to avoid errors in A-subgrouping.39, 40 Some transplant centers (mostly renal and liver) have exploited the lower immunogenicity of the A2 subgroup to expand the available donor pool for ABO-O and -B candidates. In that context, adult patients with low anti-A titers can successfully receive A2 donor kidneys without pre-transplant antibody removal, with outcomes similar to ABOc transplants;41 US allocation policy has encouraged this practice. The literature addressing this issue in heart transplantation is very limited. One report described a 41-year-old ABO-O recipient who unintentionally received an ABO-A2 donor heart.41 The patient was hemodynamically stable until retransplant four days post-transplant for non-immunologic reasons; the explanted heart showed no evidence of antibody deposition or early rejection. This report provides proof-of-concept that the donor A2 subgroup may attenuate the ABO barrier in HT in concordance with the abundant success reported with ABO-A2-incompatible kidney transplantation.42, 43 Several pediatric ABOi HT patients who had received a non-A1 donor heart were anecdotally identified retrospectively (West, unpublished data) after developing high (i.e., normal) anti-A1 titers with no immunological impact on the graft (absence of rejection); this finding suggests that A-antigens in A2 hearts are insufficiently dense to induce ABO tolerance as observed in recipients of ABO-A1 (and ABO-B) donor hearts. Regarding a possible impact of ABO-A subgroup mismatching between donor and recipient, a recent study of ABO-A individuals (both A1 and non-A1) who received A-subgroup-mismatched hearts, one-year outcomes, including survival and rejection, were not significantly different compared to A-subgroup-matched recipients.44 Donor ABO-A subgrouping by genotyping can be determined using DNA from donor lymphocytes and is increasingly performed by HLA laboratories at the time of HLA genotyping.39 Given the immunologic nuance of A1/A2 mismatch, centers could test donors for A1/non-A1 subtype and recipients for anti-A2 titers if the donor is known A2 in replacement to the standard anti-A1 titers or for both subtypes if genetic confirmation of the donor blood group subtype is not possible. The measurement of anti-A titers is typically performed using A1 reagent erythrocytes and not A2 erythrocytes. Regarding more nuanced measurement of anti-A2 titers using ABO-A2 reagent erythrocytes, recent unpublished data (West et al) suggest that expression of A antigens on A2 erythrocytes is too low to allow effective agglutination by anti-A antibodies in patient serum. Although it would be helpful for clinical management to know the donor A-subgroup prior to acceptance of an ABO-A heart for an ABOi HT, ABO-A sub-grouping by transfusion medicine laboratories is not routinely performed. However, the A2-incompatible transplant concept aligns with the broader strategy of diminishing the ABO barrier in pediatric HT.

Re-transplantation

Absent or consistently low serum antibody titers toward the donor blood group after infant ABOi HT typically represents induced B cell tolerance against donor blood group antigen(s), as described above.1, 36, 37 Rarely, however, some patients have been reported to produce "donor-specific isohemagglutinins" after ABOi transplantation in childhood,45, 46 with older age at transplant, pretransplant anti-B titer greater than 1:8 and HLA sensitization suggested as risk factors.46 An alternative explanation put forward was that the titers included antibodies with specificities for ABO antigens expressed on reagent erythrocytes used for the hemagglutination assay but not in the heart graft itself, thus were not truly donor-specific (and were irrelevant to the transplant).47 Indeed, the general absence of donor-specific ABO antibodies in the years after infant ABOi HT allows consideration of the possibility of a second ABOi HT should re-transplantation be required.47 A case has been reported of an infant who underwent ABOi heart transplantation (recipient ABO-O and donor ABO-A), with no evidence of donor-specific ABO-antibody even four years after ABOc re-transplantation.38 The evaluation of ABO antibody titers after ABOi transplantation showed a normal pattern of antibodies toward the non-donor blood group, with maximum anti-B titers of 1:512. In contrast, only minimal anti-A titers were measurable (maximum anti-A1 1:2, anti-A2 1:1). After ABOc retransplantation anti-A titers remained low (maximum anti-A1 and anti-A2 1:4), with a normal anti-B titer (1:128). Thus, re-transplantation in patients who previously underwent ABOi HT is feasible but requires careful immunologic evaluation.

Conclusion

Three decades of experience have firmly established ABOi HT as a safe and life-saving option for infants, and with limited data also for older pediatric candidates. The immunologic immaturity of early life, combined with advances in perioperative antibody removal and immunosuppression, have rendered ABOi HT outcomes comparable to ABOc transplants. Nonetheless, variability in ABO antibody testing methods and the absence of standardized titer interpretation, together with other inherent limitations in current ABO antibody assays such as lack of antigen-specificity, hinder consistent clinical decision-making and multicenter comparisons. As allocation policies and clinical practices continue to evolve, harmonizing laboratory practices and evidence-based immunologic management will be key to ensuring safe expansion of ABOi HT to larger pediatric populations.
