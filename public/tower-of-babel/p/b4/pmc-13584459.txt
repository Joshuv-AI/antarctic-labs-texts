Novel HD-Zip transcription factor PCD8 integrates developmental gene networks to shape rice panicle morphogenesis
Authors: Shuying Ling, Hao Wu, Yihao Xing, Juan Huang, Zhigan Xie, Yitao Liu, Zhanying Zhang, Qijin Lou, Zhong Wang, Dongjin Qing, Renmin Liang, Jingcheng Li, Jiada Huang, Lei Chen
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundPanicle architecture is a key agronomic trait determining rice yield. Identifying and characterizing the regulatory genes controlling panicle morphogenesis is essential for breeding improved rice varieties.ResultsTo clarify the regulatory pathways underlying panicle architecture and identify novel candidate genes, we performed RNA sequencing (RNA-seq) of 1 cm and 15 cm young panicles from Zhonghua 11 (ZH11). Numerous differentially expressed genes were identified, primarily enriched in metabolic and cellular processes. Among the differentially expressed transcription factors, we identified a previously uncharacterized Homeodomain-Leucine Zipper (HD-Zip) family gene, PaniCle Development 8 (PCD8; LOC_Os08g37580), which was highly expressed in young panicles. CRISPR/Cas9-mediated knockout of PCD8 (PCD8-KO) significantly reduced grain number per panicle, seed setting rate, and grain width while increasing grain length, grain length-to-width ratio, and chalkiness, indicating that PCD8 regulates panicle morphogenesis. Analysis of the 3,000 Rice Genomes (3 K-RG) population identified four major PCD8 haplotypes. Haplotype4 (Hap4), a temperate japonica-specific haplotype associated with long grains, carries a G/A substitution at − 2464 bp in the promoter. Haplotype3 (Hap3) and Hap4 likely evolved from Haplotype1 (Hap1), and all four haplotypes are closely related. PCD8 showed lower nucleotide diversity in temperate japonica and indica than in wild rice. Neutrality tests indicated balancing selection in wild rice and positive selection in indica, whereas reduced diversity in temperate japonica mainly reflects domestication bottlenecks rather than strong locus-specific selection. DNA affinity purification sequencing (DAP‑seq) identified candidate downstream targets of PCD8, and electrophoretic mobility shift assays (EMSA) confirmed direct binding to selected targets.ConclusionsMulti-omics analyses demonstrate that PCD8 is a novel transcription factor regulating panicle morphogenesis through downstream gene networks, providing a promising candidate for high-yield rice breeding.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at https://doi.org/10.1186/s12870-026-09399-5.Keywords: Rice, PCD8, Panicle development, RNA-seq, DAP-seq

Background

Rice is the staple food for more than half of the global population [1], and its stable, increasing production is closely linked to food security and social stability. Rice yield is primarily determined by three core components: effective panicle number, grain number per panicle, and 1000-grain weight [2]. Among these, grain shape is a major determinant of 1000-grain weight [3], while grain number per panicle defines sink capacity and sets the upper limit of yield potential per unit area [2]. Therefore, dissecting the genetic basis and molecular regulatory network of rice panicle morphogenesis is of strong theoretical importance for breeding high-yield varieties through molecular design.

Panicle morphogenesis is a complex agronomic trait regulated by a series of coordinated developmental processes, including inflorescence meristem initiation, transition of shoot apical meristems to inflorescence meristems, formation of secondary branches, and spikelet differentiation and determinacy, which ultimately form grains [4]. These processes are precisely controlled by both endogenous and exogenous signals, with genetic factors playing a central role. Advances in functional genomics have enabled the cloning and characterization of key genes regulating grain number per panicle and grain shape. For example, GNP6 encodes a nuclear-localized GRAS family protein that regulates axillary meristem formation during vegetative and reproductive growth, thereby influencing panicle development [5–6]. GNP2 encodes a conserved GSK3-like kinase that phosphorylates and stabilizes Gnp4/LAX2, with the phosphorylated Gnp4/LAX2T175,262D promoting yield through modulation of transcription factors involved in panicle development [2]. Spatial activation of BRD3 increases grain number without the typical reduction in grain size caused by BR deficiency [7]. In addition, brassinosteroid signaling mediated by the OsIAA7–OsGSK2–OsBZR1 module regulates seed size in rice [8]. Together, these studies highlight the central roles of hormone regulation and meristem maintenance in determining grain number per panicle and provide valuable genetic resources for high-yield breeding.

In the regulatory network of panicle morphogenesis, transcription factors function as key molecular switches that bind cis-acting elements of downstream genes to activate or repress transcription, thereby controlling developmental progression. Multiple transcription factor families are known to participate in this process. A cis-element variation in the SGW5 promoter alters its binding affinity for WRKY53, resulting in differential SGW5 expression and consequent grain size variation [9]. In the OsMOB1A–OsSTK38–OsCycC pathway, the kinase OsSTK38 phosphorylates OsCycC, disrupting its interaction with the transcription factor OsKNAT7 and thereby promoting grain size and weight through coordinated cell proliferation and expansion in the lemma and palea [10]. Similarly, the wild rice gene GL12W regulates grain shape through a G/T promoter variation that influences its interaction with WRKY53 and the coactivator GIF1. This modulation affects GL12W expression in young panicles and controls cell elongation via downstream genes (e.g., GS2), ultimately increasing grain length [11]. Together, these findings highlight the central role of transcription factors in regulating grain number per panicle.

Despite these advances, the regulatory landscape of transcription factors in rice panicle morphogenesis remains incomplete. First, most studies focused on individual or few transcription factors, lacking systematic analysis of transcription factor families during key developmental stages. Second, identifying transcription factors with novel functions from global transcriptomic dynamics, particularly during primary and secondary branch differentiation, remains challenging. Moreover, many transcription factors showing significant expression changes have unclear in vivo roles and unknown regulatory interactions, limiting the construction of comprehensive regulatory networks.

To address these gaps, we analyzed gene expression during rice panicle development and identified PCD8, a transcription factor involved in panicle morphogenesis. RNA-seq of ZH11 young panicles revealed PCD8 as a highly expressed HD-Zip transcription factor. CRISPR/Cas9-mediated knockout of PCD8 altered panicle and grain traits, confirming its important role in panicle morphogenesis. Analysis of 3 K-RG data identified four PCD8 haplotypes, including Hap4, a temperate japonica favorable haplotype carrying an associated promoter G/A substitution and showing distinct selection patterns among rice groups. DAP-seq and EMSA analysis further revealed its downstream candidate targets. This multi-omics analysis establishes PCD8 as a novel regulator and a potential target for high-yield rice breeding.

Results

Dynamic gene expression in rice panicle and grain formation

To investigate dynamic gene expression changes during panicle grain number formation, we performed RNA-seq to analyze gene expression in ZH11 at the young panicle differentiation stage (1 cm panicle length) and the booting stage (15 cm panicle length). Clean reads were aligned to the Oryza sativa reference genome (osa1_r7.asm), with alignment rates ranging from 98.28% to 98.7%, Q30 percentages exceeding 95.37% (Table S1). A total of 44.99% of transcripts were > 1800 bp (Fig. 1a). Exon distribution closely corresponded with transcript distribution (Fig. 1b). Inter-sample correlation analysis showed low correlation among samples (R2 < 0.8) (Fig. 1c). Differential expression analysis was conducted using DESeq2 with thresholds of |log2FC| ≥1 and Padjust < 0.05. In total, 37,700 expressed genes were identified, including 36,116 known genes and 1,584 novel genes. Additionally, 63,123 expressed transcripts were detected, comprising 44,350 known transcripts and 18,773 novel transcripts. In the comparison between 1 cm and 15 cm ZH11 panicles, 4,729 genes were upregulated, and 2,121 genes were downregulated (Fig. 1d and e). These results indicate substantial transcriptomic differences between the two developmental stages, reflecting distinct gene expression profiles. We further analyzed alternative splicing events during panicle development. Intron retention (RI) was the most frequent type (34.63%), followed by exon skipping (SE, 25.84%), alternative 3’ splice site (A3SS, 23.3%), alternative 5’ splice site (A5SS, 14.25%), and mutually exclusive exons (MXE, 1.98%). Thus, RI was the predominant alternative splicing mode (Fig. 1f). We also examined the distribution of single nucleotide polymorphisms (SNPs) across genomic regions. SNPs were mainly located in upstream (24.98%) and downstream (29.19%) regions, suggesting potential regulatory roles in gene expression. They were also present in intronic (14.72%) and intergenic regions (9.36%). In coding regions, missense (7.75%) and synonymous (6.55%) variants were relatively common, whereas disruptive variants such as frameshift and nonsense mutations were rare (each < 0.2%). Variants in untranslated regions (UTRs) and splice sites accounted for approximately 7% and may affect mRNA stability or translation. Overall, these findings demonstrate extensive differential gene expression during rice panicle development and indicate that alternative splicing and SNP variation may increase proteomic diversity to support panicle grain number and grain shape development.

Differentially expressed genes (DEGs) are enriched in biological process and metabolism modules

To elucidate the core biological processes during panicle development, this study performed enrichment analysis of DEGs using Gene Ontology (GO), Kyoto Encyclopedia of Genes and Genomes (KEGG), evolutionary genealogy of genes: Non-supervised Orthologous Groups (EggNOG), Non-Redundant Protein Sequence Database (NR), Swiss-Prot, and Pfam. GO classification was grouped into three categories: biological process, cellular component, and molecular function. In the molecular function category, binding- and catalytic activity-related genes constituted the majority of DEGs. In the cellular component category, cellular anatomical entities were dominant. In the biological process category, metabolic and cellular processes represented the main DEGs (Fig. 2a). Major enriched GO terms included cellular component, polysaccharide metabolic process, cellular component organization, oxidoreductase activity, mitotic cell cycle process, carbohydrate metabolic process, cell cycle process, and cellular component organization or biogenesis (Fig. 2b). KEGG functional annotation classified enriched DEGs into five major categories: Metabolism, Genetic Information Processing, Environmental Information Processing, Cellular Processes, and Organismal Systems. Within these, Carbohydrate metabolism was the predominant category in Metabolism; Translation dominated Genetic Information Processing; Signal Transduction was the main category in Environmental Information Processing; and Environmental Adaptation was the primary category in Organismal Systems (Fig. 2c). Key enriched KEGG pathways included DNA Replication, Photosynthesis–Antenna Proteins, Ribosome, Base Excision Repair, Cutin, Suberin and Wax Biosynthesis, and Photosynthesis (Fig. 2d). iPath metabolic pathway analysis indicated significant alterations in multiple pathways, particularly those related to carbohydrate metabolism, energy metabolism, and secondary metabolite biosynthesis (Fig. S1). The significant enrichment of genes associated with cellular and metabolic processes suggests that inter-sample differences mainly reflect developmental stage transitions. The 1 cm young panicles likely rely more on pathways related to cell division and growth, whereas the 15 cm mature panicles prioritize energy metabolism, substance transport, and environmental adaptation. These functional characteristics collectively reflect the transition of rice panicles from a rapid growth stage to a mature and stable stage.

PCD8 regulates rice panicle morphogenesis with pleiotropic effects

Transcription factors regulate downstream functional genes during panicle development and thereby influence panicle morphogenesis. In this study, 3,324 transcription factors from 49 categories were identified (Table S2). MYB, bHLH, NAC, MYB_related, B3, ERF, bZIP, WRKY, C2H2, and HB-other each contained more than 10 differentially expressed transcription factors (Fig. S2a). Among them, 1,443 transcription factors were expressed at both the 1 cm and 15 cm panicle stages (Fig. S2b). Of these, 8.2% belonged to the bHLH family and 2.6% to the HD-ZIP family. The expressed transcription factors were classified into two groups: those with decreasing expression and those with increasing during panicle development. To validate the RNA-seq data and further examine the expression dynamics of key regulatory genes during panicle development, we performed quantitative real-time polymerase chain reaction (qRT-PCR) on a set of panicle development-related genes, including ENL1, GS5, GNP6, GS2, OsARF14, OsNPH1b, OsSUS2, ROSES1, OsPsbR3, OsNRAMP5, OsValRS3, and NOG1. The qRT-PCR results were consistent with the transcriptome data (Fig. 3a − l).

To further investigate the role of HD-Zip transcription factors in panicle development, we selected LOC_Os08g37580, designated PaniCle Development 8 (PCD8), for further analysis (Fig. S3). Phylogenetic tree analysis showed that PCD8 belongs to the HD-Zip I subfamily, whose members play important roles in plant stress responses, growth and development (Fig. S4). Analysis of the Rice Expression Database (https://ngdc.cncb.ac.cn/red) revealed that PCD8 was expressed in all examined tissues, with the highest expression in panicles (Fig. S5a). This expression pattern was confirmed by qRT-PCR, supporting a potential role for PCD8 in panicle development (Fig. 4a, b). To investigate this potential role, we generated two CRISPR/Cas9 knockout lines, PCD8-KO1 and PCD8-KO2, in the japonica rice variety ZH11 (wild type, WT) (Fig. 4c). Genotyping showed that PCD8-KO1 carries a 2-bp (GC) deletion and three single-base substitutions in the coding region, whereas PCD8-KO2 carries a 2-bp deletion (Fig. 4c; Fig. S5b, c). Both mutations are predicted to cause frameshifts and subsequent loss of function. To assess the impact of PCD8 loss‑of‑function on yield‑related traits, we analyzed key agronomic traits in the two mutant lines (Fig. 4d, e). Compared with WT ZH11, the PCD8-KO mutants showed significant reductions in grain number per panicle, filled grain number per panicle, and seed setting rate, but no significant differences in plant height, panicle length, effective panicle number per plant, or primary branch number per panicle (Fig. 4f − l). Notably, stem diameter was significantly greater in the PCD8-KO lines than in WT (Fig. 4m). We further examined the effect of PCD8 on grain quality traits, including grain length, grain width, length-to-width ratio, and chalkiness. Compared with WT, the mutants showed increased grain length, length-to-width ratio, and chalkiness, whereas grain width was significantly reduced (Fig. 4n–u). Given that glume cell size and endosperm structure jointly determine grain appearance, we examined spikelet hull cell size and endosperm structure. In the mutants, glume cells were longer and narrower, and the endosperm structure was looser than in WT (Fig. 4v–y). Together, these results indicate that PCD8 regulates panicle and grain development. Haplotype analyses of PCD8

To investigate natural variation at gene PCD8, we analyzed its polymorphisms in the 3 K-RG panel [12]. Haplotype analysis identified four major haplotypes at this locus. Hap1 was the most frequent and predominated in indica, temperate japonica and tropical japonica accessions (Fig. 5a), whereas Haplotype2 (Hap2) was mainly found in tropical japonica and Aus. Grain-length comparisons showed that, within temperate japonica, Hap4 was associated with significantly longer grains, representing the favorable haplotype (Fig. 5b). In tropical japonica, Hap3 was linked to significantly shorter grains than Hap2 (Fig. 5c). In indica and Aus, no significant grain-length differences were observed between Hap1 and Hap2 (Fig. 5d, e). Across the 3 K panel, Hap4 was identified as a haplotype prevalent in temperate japonica that is correlated with the long-grain trait. A G-to-A substitution at − 2464 bp in the promoter represents one of the polymorphic sites linked to this haplotype (Fig. 5a). Haplotype network analysis further showed that the four cultivated rice haplotypes are closely related, with Hap3 and Hap4 likely derived from Hap1 (Fig. 5f). Nucleotide diversity (π) analysis revealed substantially lower diversity at PCD8 in temperate japonica and indica than in wild rice (Fig. 5g). Neutrality tests indicated distinct evolutionary patterns among groups. In wild rice, Tajima’s D for PCD8 was 3.68062 (> 0, P < 0.001), suggesting balancing selection at this locus. In indica, Tajima’s D was − 2.29329 (< 0, P < 0.05), consistent with positive selection. In contrast, Tajima’s D in temperate japonica was − 0.28834 and not significantly different from zero, indicating that the reduced nucleotide diversity in this group likely reflects demographic effects such as the domestication bottleneck rather than strong locus-specific selection.

DAP-seq analysis of candidate target genes and downstream pathways of PCD8

To investigate the downstream regulatory network of PCD8, we performed DAP-seq to identify its candidate target genes. Raw reads were processed by unique mapping and duplicate removal. In total, 76,214,162 reads were obtained, of which 75,544,750 (99.12%) mapped to the reference genome, 63,168,146 (82.88%) were uniquely mapped, and 54,607,678 (71.65%) remained after duplicate removal. Peak analysis showed that most peaks ranged from 250 to 500 bp (Fig. 6a). Overlapping peak region analysis of peaks and motifs from two biological replicates identified 68,582 overlapping peaks (Fig. 6b). We then analyzed peak distribution across genomic elements and found that most peaks were located in intergenic (40.08%) and promoter (29.56%) regions (Fig. 6c). We further quantified read distribution within a 2 kb window upstream and downstream of the transcription start site (TSS), showing that most reads were enriched upstream of the TSS (Fig. 6d). Subsequently, motif analysis of PCD8-binding sites identified 41 distinct motif types. Among them, the LMI1 (HB) motif accounted for 34.45% of target sites, the G/A-biased SeqBias motif for 27.93%, and the polyA-repeat motif for 21.11% (Table S3). GO functional enrichment analysis of the differentially enriched genes classified them into three main ontologies: Molecular Function, Cellular Component, and Biological Process. The dominant categories included obsolete intracellular part, organelle, intracellular organelle, and membrane-bounded organelle (Fig. 6e). KEGG pathway analysis showed enrichment in the mRNA surveillance pathway, glycerophospholipid metabolism, ribosome biogenesis in eukaryotes, and peroxisome (Fig. 6f). These pathways were mainly grouped into four categories: Cellular Processes, Environmental Information Processing, Genetic Information Processing, and Metabolism (Fig. 6g). We then integrated the identified downstream candidate target genes with RNA-seq data from the ZH11 panicle transcriptome. Transcripts were merged, and expressed proteins and hypothetical proteins were excluded. Genes not expressed in 1 cm and 15 cm panicles of ZH11 were also removed. Combined analysis identified 2,313 significant DEGs. Among them, 747 genes showed expression patterns consistent with PCD8 and were downregulated in 1 cm vs. 15 cm panicles of ZH11, whereas 1,566 genes showed the opposite trend and were upregulated in the same comparison (Fig. S6). ENL1, GS5, GNP6, GS2, and OsARF14, which regulate panicle grain number and grain development, decreased during panicle development. In contrast, OsNPH1b, OsSUS2, ROSES1, OsPsbR3, OsNRAMP5, and OsValRS3, which are involved in panicle morphogenesis [13], increased during this process. qRT-PCR analysis confirmed these expression patterns (Fig. 6h–r). We further performed EMSA experiments to determine whether the CAATAATT motif in the promoters of 11 downstream candidate genes mediates PCD8 binding. A distinct shifted band was detected when the biotin-labeled probe was incubated with the MBP-PCD8 fusion protein (lane 3), but not with MBP alone (lane 2) (Fig. S7). The shifted band gradually disappeared with increasing concentrations of unlabeled competitor probe (lanes 4–6), and no shifted band was observed when the core motif was mutated (lane 7), demonstrating the specific binding of PCD8 the CAATAATT sequence (Fig. S7). Collectively, these results indicate that PCD8 regulates multiple downstream candidate target genes and thereby mediates panicle morphogenesis during development.

Discussion

Panicle architecture is a key determinant of rice yield, yet the roles of HD-Zip transcription factors in panicle morphogenesis, and the regulatory network underlying the grain number–grain shape trade-off, remain largely unclear [14, 15]. In this study, we first characterized the genome-wide transcriptomic landscape of rice young panicles at the critical branch differentiation stage (1 cm) and booting (15 cm) stages, and identified numerous DEGs enriched in cell proliferation, carbohydrate metabolism, and transcriptional regulation. Among the dynamically expressed transcription factors, we identified PCD8, a previously uncharacterized HD-Zip family member predominantly expressed in young panicles, as a regulator with pleiotropic effects on panicle morphogenesis. Integrated population genetic analysis and DAP-seq further revealed its natural variation, evolutionary features, and downstream regulatory network. These findings expand the transcriptional regulatory framework underlying rice panicle development and provide a promising target for molecular breeding of high-yield rice.

PCD8 acts as a novel HD-Zip transcription factor with pleiotropic effects on rice panicle morphogenesis via targeting key grain development regulators

HD-Zip proteins constitute a plant-specific transcription factor family that plays critical roles in meristem maintenance, organ polarity establishment, and hormone signaling transduction in Arabidopsis and rice [16–20]. However, their functions in regulating rice panicle branching and grain morphogenesis remain poorly understood. Current knowledge of rice panicle development is largely centered on GRAS, WRKY, and MADS transcription factors, as well as phytohormone signaling pathways [6, 9, 14, 21, 22]. For example, GNP6 regulates panicle branching by controlling axillary meristem formation [6], the OsIAA7–OsGSK2–OsBZR1 module mediates brassinosteroid signaling to regulate seed size [8], and WRKY53 influences grain shape through transcriptional regulation of downstream genes [9, 20, 21]. Although these studies have established a core regulatory network for rice panicle development, the contribution of HD-Zip transcription factors remains largely unexplored.

In this study, CRISPR/Cas9-mediated knockout of PCD8 significantly reduced grain number per panicle, grain width, and seed setting rate while producing longer, more slender grains with increased grain length, length-to-width ratio, and chalkiness. Cytological analysis showed that loss of PCD8 function resulted in longer, narrower glume cells and a looser endosperm structure, explaining the altered grain phenotype of the mutants. These findings demonstrate that PCD8 co-regulates panicle grain number and grain shape in rice. Integrated DAP-seq, RNA-seq, and EMSA analyses indicated that PCD8 directly binds the promoter of key grain development regulators, including GS5 and GNP6. GS5 promotes grain width and weight by enhancing cell proliferation in the lemma and palea, whereas GNP6 regulates panicle branching and grain number by controlling axillary meristem formation [6, 13]. PCD8 expression gradually declined during panicle development from branch differentiation to spikelet maturation (Fig. 4b), potentially altering its transcriptional regulation of these downstream targets, thereby influencing panicle and grain development. Together, these findings identify PCD8 as an upstream transcriptional regulator that coordinates the grain number-grain shape trade-off, revealing a previously unrecognized HD-Zip-mediated pathway controlling rice panicle morphogenesis.

Natural variation of PCD8 drives grain shape divergence

Natural variation in key regulatory genes underlies agronomic trait divergence and rice domestication [12]. Previous studies showed that grain shape genes, including GS3, GW2, and GW5, underwent strong artificial selection during domestication, resulting in markedly lower nucleotide diversity in cultivated than in wild rice [23–26]. However, most elite long-grain haplotypes are shared between indica and japonica, whereas subpopulation-specific elite haplotypes for grain shape remain rare, limiting targeted breeding strategies for different rice subpopulations [12, 27].

Based on the 3 K-RG panel, we identified four major PCD8 haplotypes with subpopulation-specific distributions. Hap1 predominated in indica, temperate japonica, and tropical japonica, whereas Hap4 was specific to temperate japonica and was significantly associated with long grains. A G-to-A substitution at − 2464 bp in the promoter was identified as a polymorphic site tightly linked to Hap4. Population genetic analyses indicated that natural variation in PCD8 contributes substantially to grain shape differentiation in temperate japonica, with the subpopulation-specific Hap4 representing a promising resource for grain shape improvement. However, although these haplotypes are defined by promoter SNPs, whether they differ in PCD8 expression remains unknown. Future studies should systematically compare PCD8 expression among accessions representing all four haplotypes and combine these analyses with promoter-reporter assays to determine the functional effects of the promoter variants. Nucleotide diversity analysis showed that PCD8 diversity was significantly lower in temperate japonica and indica than in wild rice, with distinct evolutionary patterns among subpopulations. Neutrality tests suggested that balancing selection maintained PCD8 variation in wild rice, potentially preserving adaptive genetic diversity. In contrast, indica showed signatures of positive selection consistent with domestication and improvement, whereas the reduced diversity in temperate japonica was primarily attributable to domestication bottlenecks rather than locus-specific positive selection at PCD8 [23, 25].

Breeding application potential of PCD8

Optimizing the trade-off between grain number and grain size remains a major challenge in rice breeding because many high-yield genes have antagonistic effects on these traits [28–32]. Although PCD8 knockout mutants produced the market-preferred slender grain phenotype [33, 34], the accompanying reductions in grain number and seed-setting rate, together with increased chalkiness, limit the value of simple loss-of-function strategies. Instead, breeding efforts should focus on fine-tuning PCD8 expression or exploiting favorable haplotypes. Although the G/A promoter variant may provide a useful resource for marker-assisted selection, its regulatory function requires experimental validation. Because PCD8 regulates multiple genes involved in panicle and grain development, pyramiding favorable PCD8 alleles with beneficial variants of downstream target genes may help establish a balanced regulatory network. Overall, PCD8 represents a promising target for rice improvement, but its effective use will require optimized haplotype selection, network-based allele pyramiding, and field validation.

Conclusions

In this study, we identified the HD-Zip transcription factor PCD8 as a regulator of rice panicle morphogenesis with pleiotropic effects on grain number and grain shape. CRISPR/Cas9-mediated knockout of PCD8 significantly reduced grain number per panicle, seed setting rate, and grain width, while increasing grain length, the grain length-to-width ratio and chalkiness, demonstrating its dual role in regulating panicle architecture and grain quality. Population genetic analysis of the 3 K-RG panel identified four major PCD8 haplotypes with subpopulation-specific distributions. Among them, Hap4, a temperate japonica-specific haplotype carrying a candidate G/A promoter variant, was significantly associated with the long-grain phenotype. Furthermore, integrated DAP-seq, RNA-seq, and EMSA analyses identified the putative downstream regulatory network of PCD8, including several well-characterized grain development regulators. Together, these findings expand the transcriptional regulatory framework underlying rice panicle development and identify PCD8 as a promising target for molecular breeding to optimize the trade-off between yield components and develop high-yielding, high-quality rice varieties.

Methods

Haplotype, selection and evolutionary analysis

Based on gene annotations, variants located in exonic regions and the 2-kb upstream promoter region of PCD8 were extracted from the high-quality SNP dataset for haplotype construction. Grain length phenotypes from the 3 K-RG were integrated to compare trait values among haplotypes. Haplotype-based phenotypic summaries, statistical analyses, and plotting were performed in R (v4.5.1). For selection analysis, sequences spanning PCD8 and its 20-kb flanking regions were obtained from deep sequencing data of the 3 K-RG and publicly available wild rice (Oryza rufipogon) genomes. Nucleotide diversity (π) was calculated for different subpopulations using custom Perl scripts, and Tajima’s D was calculated with DnaSP (v5.10). For phylogenetic and haplotype network analyses, SNPs and Indels from wild and cultivated rice were integrated by comparing publicly available wild rice genome sequences. For haplotype network construction, sequence data were processed in DnaSP to generate NEX, HAP, and ARP files. The NEX files were imported into PopART (v1.7) to construct a minimum spanning haplotype network [36].

DAP-seq analysis

The full-length coding sequence of PCD8 was cloned into a modified pET28a vector containing an N-terminal 6×His-MBP tag and transformed into Escherichia coli BL21 (DE3) cells. Protein expression was induced with 0.5 mM IPTG at 18 °C for 16 h. Cells were harvested, lysed, and the His-MBP-PCD8 fusion protein was purified using Ni-NTA affinity chromatography. The purified protein was dialyzed into storage buffer (20 mM HEPES-KOH pH 7.5, 100 mM KCl, 1 mM DTT, 20% glycerol) and quantified. High-quality genomic DNA (gDNA) was extracted from 1-cm young panicles of ZH11 (Oryza sativa ssp. japonica) using the CTAB method and fragmented to 200–300 bp using a Covaris S220 sonicator. Fragmented DNA was end-repaired, A-tailed, and ligated to Illumina-compatible adapters using the KAPA HyperPrep Kit. The adapter-ligated library was amplified by limited-cycle PCR, purified, and quantified. For DAP-seq, 500 ng of the genomic adapter-ligated library was incubated with 2 µg of purified His-MBP-PCD8 protein in binding buffer (10 mM Tris-HCl pH 7.5, 50 mM NaCl, 1 mM EDTA, 5 mM DTT, 0.1% Triton X-100, 10% glycerol) for 1 h at 4 °C. A parallel reaction using the His-MBP tag alone served as a negative control. Protein–DNA complexes were captured with pre-washed MagHis Ni-NTA magnetic beads and subjected to stringent washing. Bound DNA was eluted, purified, and used to construct indexed sequencing libraries. Paired-end (150 bp) sequencing was performed on an Illumina NovaSeq 6000 platform. Raw reads were quality-trimmed and aligned to the Nipponbare reference genome (v7.0) using BWA-MEM. Peaks were identified using MACS2 (v2.2.7.1) by comparing the PCD8 sample with the MBP-only control (FDR < 0.05). High-confidence peaks were annotated to the nearest transcription start site (TSS) using ChIPseeker. The de novo motif discovery was conducted using HOMER (v4.11). GO and KEGG pathway enrichment analyses were performed using the clusterProfiler R package (v4.2.2).

EMSA

The PCD8 CDS was cloned into the pMAL-c5x vector. MBP-tagged PCD8 and the empty MBP control were expressed in Escherichia coli Rosetta and purified by amylose affinity chromatography. Biotin-labeled WT and mutant probes were used for binding assays. For competition assays, unlabeled WT probes at 10×, 20×, or 50× molar excess were pre-incubated with MBP-PCD8 before addition of the biotin-labeled probe. Binding reactions were performed at 25 °C for 30 min, separated on 6% non-denaturing polyacrylamide gels, transferred to nylon membranes, and detected by chemiluminescence. The assay was performed by Wuhan Biorun Biotechnology Co., Ltd. (Wuhan, China). Probe sequences are listed in Table S5.

Plant material and Phenotyping

All transgenic and WT rice plants were grown in a greenhouse at the Rice Research Institute, Guangxi Academy of Agricultural Sciences (GXAAS), Nanning, China. The transgenic lines included the mutant lines PCD8-KO1 and PCD8-KO2, generated by knocking out PCD8 in ZH11 (WT) using CRISPR/Cas9. ZH11, a japonica rice cultivar widely used as a model for rice functional genomics because of its high genetic transformation efficiency, suitable growth duration, and high physical and chemical mutagenesis efficiency, has been maintained in the GXAAS germplasm bank for many years [35]. CRISPR/Cas9-mediated gene editing was performed by Biogle Gene Technology Co., Ltd. (Changzhou, Jiangsu, China). All plants were grown under standard greenhouse conditions. At full maturity, panicles were manually harvested and air-dried under well-ventilated shade for three months to equilibrate grain moisture. The filled grains were then collected for quality assessment. Grain appearance traits (grain length, grain width, length-to-width ratio, and chalkiness) were measured using an automated seed analyzer (SC-G and SC-E, Hangzhou Wanshen Test Technology Co., Ltd., China). Agronomic traits, including effective panicles per plant, grains per panicle, primary branches per panicle, seed setting rate, plant height, panicle length, and stem diameter, were measured from at least 10 plants. Fully filled grains were used to determine the 1,000-grain weight. Spikelet hull and endosperm structures were examined using scanning electron microscopy, and cell size was calculated using ImageJ software.

RNA-seq analysis

Total RNA was extracted from panicles of ZH11 plants grown under normal conditions for RNA-seq analysis. Young panicles measuring 1 cm and 15 cm in length were collected, with three biological replicates per stage. Samples were immediately frozen in liquid nitrogen and stored at − 80 °C until RNA extraction. Libraries were prepared using the NEBNext Ultra Directional RNA Library Prep Kit for Illumina (NEB, Ipswich, MA, USA) according to the manufacturer’s instructions and sequenced as 100-bp single-end reads on an Illumina HiSeq-2000 platform. cDNA sequencing was performed by Genedenovo Biotechnology Co., Ltd. (Guangzhou, China). mRNA was enriched using capture beads. After index construction, clean reads were aligned to the reference genome using HISAT2 v2.1.0 with default parameters. Gene abundance was quantified by assembling mapped reads with StringTie v1.3.1 using a reference-based approach. Gene expression levels were calculated as fragments per kilobase of transcript per million mapped reads (FPKM) using RSEM. Principal component analysis (PCA) was performed using the R package gmodels http://www.rproject.org/. DEGs were identified using DESeq2 and edgeR with thresholds of false discovery rate (FDR) < 0.05 and |log2FC| ≥ 1. GO and KEGG enrichment analyses were performed to identify significantly enriched GO terms and metabolic pathways among DEGs using a Bonferroni-corrected P-value < 0.05. GO and KEGG enrichment analyses were conducted using GOATOOLS and Python SciPy, respectively.

RNA extraction and qRT-PCR

For qRT-PCR validation of the RNA-seq data, ZH11 panicles were collected at 1 cm and 15 cm in length. To compare gene expression between WT and PCD8-KO mutants, 3-cm panicles at the young panicle differentiation stage were collected from both ZH11 and PCD8-KO plants. Collected tissues were rapidly frozen in liquid nitrogen and stored in pre-cooled tubes at − 80 °C until use. Total RNA was extracted using an RNA Extraction Kit (Biomed, RA106-01). First-strand cDNA was synthesized from 2 µg of total RNA using a reverse transcription kit (Biomed, MT423-01) following the manufacturer’s instructions. Gene-specific primers are listed in Table S4. Real-time PCR was performed on a CFX384 Optical Reaction Module (Bio-Rad) using SYBR qPCR Mix (Biomed, MT503-02) according to the manufacturer’s instructions. OsUBIQUITIN2 (UBQ) served as internal control. Each analysis was performed in triplicate with consistent results. Gene expression levels were calculated using the 2−ΔΔCt method.

Statistical analysis

Differences between groups were assessed using a two-sided Student’s t-test. Multiple comparisons were analyzed using one-way ANOVA followed by Tukey’s post hoc test. Statistical analyses were performed using GraphPad Prism (v8.0).
