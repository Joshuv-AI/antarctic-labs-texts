Interpretable machine learning based decision tree model for predicting obstructive airway disease in a large non-smoking health screening population
Authors: Chih-Yueh Chang, Hsiang-Shi Shen, Yen-Liang Kuo, Li-Na Lee, Kuo-Ching Kao, Tzu-Chi Liu, Chi-Jie Lu
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Obstructive airway disease is defined by reduced FEV₁ and an FEV₁/FVC ratio below 70%. While pulmonary function testing is essential, few studies have used demographic, biochemical, and lifestyle data to predict disease risk in non-smoker group. This study aimed to develop interpretable machine learning (ML) models for early risk prediction and clinical screening. We analyzed data from 81,055 non-smoking individuals drawn from a health screening cohort of 549,825 participants. Six ML algorithms including CART, RF, XGBoost, LightGBM, CatBoost, and Lasso were applied to develop predictive models. All models demonstrated strong predictive performance, and an ensemble feature aggregation approach was used to identify key predictors. A CART was built with the identified features to generate a visualized decision tree, generating decision rules to support clinical screening. The key predictors included age, waist-to-hip ratio, blood pressure, and biochemical markers. This is the first large-scale ML study predicting obstructive airway disease in non-smoker group using health exam data. Interpretable models may assist early detection and clinical risk stratification.

Introduction

In recent years, the prevalence of obstructive airway diseases has risen significantly, with asthma and chronic obstructive pulmonary disease (COPD) being the two most common conditions. Asthma is a widespread chronic respiratory disorder affecting between 1% and 29% of the population globally1,2. In 2019 alone, approximately 262 million people were living with asthma, resulting in an estimated 455,000 deaths3. Similarly, COPD has become one of the leading causes of death worldwide, accounting for more than 3 million deaths in 2012—approximately 6% of all global mortality—with 90% of these deaths occurring in low- and middle-income countries4,5. Notably, the incidence of COPD peaks around the age of 456, while adult-onset asthma becomes the predominant phenotype in men by age 507.

Both diseases significantly affect individuals and their families, posing major challenges to healthcare systems. A common and critical pathophysiological hallmark of both asthma and COPD is the progressive decline in pulmonary function. Pulmonary function testing (PFT) plays a vital role in assessing respiratory health. It is widely used for evaluating lung function in healthy individuals, monitoring disease progression, and determining treatment efficacy. The hallmark of obstructive airway disease is a reduction in the forced expiratory volume in one second (FEV₁) and a decreased FEV₁/FVC (forced vital capacity) ratio, typically below 70%.

However, there was still a lack of research constructing an effective prediction model for investigating obstructive airway disease through the demographic, biochemistry and lifestyle data of health exam participants. Our study aimed to construct an explainable predictive model to predict obstructive airway disease among health screening non-smoker populations. This predictive model was developed for clinical practice, based on ML decision tree methods, in order to screen clinical factors and decision rules for clinical practice.

ML methods such as decision tree techniques can provide interpretable insights that closely resembles a clinical diagnosis process, providing extra useful information to support clinicians. However, to the best of our knowledge, there is no study that utilized decision tree techniques to build models for obstructive airway disease between health exam people. To address the research gap, this study aims to develop a two-staged ensemble risk factor identification (TERFI) scheme for predicting pulmonary function of patients with obstructive airway disease from non-smoking population. Importantly, rather than proposing a novel machine learning (ML) algorithm, this study adopts an application-driven design that integrates multiple well-established ML models to derive interpretable decision rules. The primary contribution lies in translating complex model outputs into clinically meaningful insights with direct translational relevance for population-based health screening, particularly among non-smoking individuals in whom obstructive airway impairment may be overlooked.

The proposed TERFI scheme first ranks the feature importance with six well-known ML models, and aggregates them to identify the most important features. Then, a decision tree will be constructed with the identified features to generated interpretable decision rules for supporting clinicians. The six used ML models are classification and regression tree (CART), random forest (RF), extreme gradient boosting machine (XGB), light gradient boosting machine (LightGBM), categorical boosting machine (Catboost) and least absolute shrinkage selective operator (Lasso), as these models have consistently shown promising performance in the field of clinical studies8–12.

Methods

Data material

Data for this study were obtained from the Taiwan MJ Cohort, an ongoing prospective health examination program conducted by MJ Health Screening Centers in Taiwan13. All participants were individuals undergoing routine health examinations as part of a population-based screening program, rather than patients recruited from clinical care settings. The program includes over 100 clinical and biochemical measurements, as well as standardized self-administered questionnaires covering medical history, lifestyle, physical activity, sleep, and dietary habits14.

Participant data used in this study were sourced from the MJ Health Clinics and managed by the MJ Interpretation Foundation. At the time when the participants underwent the health checkup, a general informed consent form for future anonymous study was obtained from the participants. Data access was granted by the MJ Health Research Foundation and MJ Interpretation Foundation (Authorization Code: MJHRF2023007A). The authors are solely responsible for the analyses and interpretations presented, which do not necessarily reflect the views of the data providers.

As a secondary analysis of de-identified data, this study did not involve new sample collection and thus did not require participant re-consent. Details of the data collection procedures are available in the annual technical report14. A brief ethical review of this study was conducted and approved by the Institutional Review Board of Fu Jen Catholic University (IRB No.: FJUH112321), and all procedures were conducted in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki.

Figure 1 visualized the process of subject identification, which 549,825 non-smokers were enrolled in the beginning. Never-smoking status was determined based on standardized self-administered questionnaires used in the MJ Health screening program. Participants who reported no history of cigarette smoking were classified as never-smokers. Biochemical verification of smoking status was not available in this population-based cohort.

Our study included many variables (24 items) and this was a general health screening database. Due to the structure of the MJ Health screening program, participants may undergo different examination packages, and not all laboratory and questionnaire variables are uniformly collected across visits. Consequently, a substantial proportion of individuals did not have complete data for all selected predictors. To ensure methodological consistency across all six machine learning algorithms, a complete-case analysis approach was adopted, resulting in the exclusion of participants with missing values in any included variable. The majority of missingness was therefore attributable to incomplete examination panels rather than data entry errors. So, the missing data we excluded was 458,520 subjects. Then, we excluded 10,350 subjects age beneath 18-year-old or above 90-year-old. The remained eligible subjects were 81,055. In this study, obstructive airway disease was operationally defined using a fixed FEV₁/FVC ratio ≤ 70%, which is a widely used screening criterion for airflow obstruction in population-based settings15,16. It should be noted that this definition reflects functional airflow limitation identified during routine health examinations rather than a definitive clinical diagnosis of asthma or chronic obstructive pulmonary disease confirmed by physician assessment or longitudinal follow-up.

The fixed ratio approach was adopted to ensure consistency, simplicity, and interpretability in a large-scale health screening cohort. In such contexts, standardized thresholds facilitate reproducible risk stratification and support the development of transparent ML decision rules. Given that the primary objective of this study was early risk identification and clinical screening rather than disease diagnosis, the use of a fixed FEV₁/FVC threshold was considered appropriate.

Analysis methods

As mentioned that ML algorithms are known to effectively capture the complex feature interactions hidden within the data, six effective ML algorithms are used in this study, namely, CART, RF, XGB, LightGBM, Catboost and Lasso. CART has a tree based structure that is generated by recursively splitting the dataset based thresholds that best separate the data into distinct classes. This approach aims to maximize class purity in the resulting subsets for making effective classification. Moreover, the constructed tree can further generate decision rules supporting diagnosis and decision making17. RF is an ensemble based extension from decision tree structure. It builds multiple decision trees using randomly sampled subsets of data and features, then aggregates their outcome, enhancing the model’s robustness and generalization to unseen data18. XGB is an ensemble learning algorithm based on gradient boosting of decision tree. XGB builds models sequentially, where each new tree is trained to correct the residual errors made by the previous ensemble tree. It incorporates unique mechanisms such as regularization and weighted quantile sketching to enhance model performance19.

LightGBM is a gradient boosting framework that builds decision trees using a leaf-wise growth strategy with depth constraints, which allows for faster convergence and improved accuracy. Histogram based algorithms is utilized by LightGBM to discretize continuous features, enhancing model efficiency and overall performance20. CatBoost follows a similar gradient boosting framework as XGBoost and LightGBM but introduces a unique concept called ordered boosting. This technique reduces prediction shift and overfitting by carefully managing the use of target values during training, particularly enhancing the model’s ability to handle categorical features effectively21. Lasso employs a penalty term that encourages sparsity in the model coefficients by shrinking weights of less influential feature on target outcome to zero, providing a subset of relevant features that reduce the complexity of the features and improve the model performance22. All of the six ML models can capture diverse feature interactions due to their own unique mechanism which aggregates them can provide extra useful information on pulmonary function prediction.

Proposed scheme

To identify the risk factors influencing pulmonary function and provide information support decision making, a two-staged ensemble risk factor identification (TERFI) scheme is proposed and presented in Fig. 2. The proposed TERFI scheme is designed with an emphasis on interpretability and clinical applicability rather than algorithmic novelty. The scheme consists steps of data preprocessing, model development with cross-validation, performance evaluation, aggregation of feature importance rankings across multiple ML models, and generation of decision rules to support health screening. By aggregating feature importance rankings across multiple established ML models and subsequently constructing a decision tree based on the consensus predictors, the framework enables the translation of ensemble model behavior into transparent and clinically accessible decision rules suitable for large-scale health screening contexts.

As shown in Fig. 2, data preprocessing is deployed on the collected MJ health exam data for subject identification and data cleaning as explained in Fig. 1 of the data material section. After the data is prepared, each ML model is initially built. The dataset is divided into 80% for training and 20% for testing. Since different ML algorithms have their own unique hyper parameters required to be tuned, 3-fold cross-validation (3f-CV) is employed for hyper parameter tuning during training phase. 3f-CV further divides the 80% training dataset into three equal-sized subsets first, then trains the model three times while using two folds for training and the remaining one for validation in each time. The average performance across the three runs is used to evaluate each hyper parameter set. The best performing hyper parameter set is then used to build the final model for testing. A round of complete model constructing process includes 3f-CV and testing. Each of the six ML models follows the same construction process and is repeated for 10 rounds to ensure robust and stable performance. In addition, since the dataset exhibits class imbalance, a bootstrap-based undersampling strategy is applied exclusively to the training set to achieve a 1:1 class ratio during model training; a total of 10 bootstrap resampling iterations are performed within each fold from 3f-CV. This ensures that positive and negative classes were equally represented during the training process, preventing the model from being biased toward the majority class and facilitating balanced learning of class-specific patterns. The testing set is left unchanged to provide an unbiased evaluation of model performance.

Next, after all ML models are constructed, their performance is evaluated on the testing data, and models demonstrating reasonable and reliable performance are selected. The importance rankings of each risk factor are then obtained from the selected models. The most important risk factor is given the ranking of 1 whereas the second important one is given the ranking of 2 and so on. Furthermore, any risk factor dropped from the model is assigned the lowest possible ranking, reflecting the total number of candidate features evaluated. Specifically, the lowest possible ranking was 25, as a total of 25 factors were considered in this study.

The aggregated risk factor set is obtained by calculating the average of corresponding rankings of each factor. Results

Table 2 presents the performance of six ML models evaluated over 10 repeated runs. As shown, all models exhibit comparable performance in terms of balanced accuracy, sensitivity, specificity, and AUC (approximately 0.7), indicating their ability to capture both positive and negative cases reasonably well. Although precision values are relatively low, reflecting a trade-off that favors sensitivity for early risk identification, the reasonably high sensitivity and specificity suggest that the models can effectively identify both classes, albeit with a tendency toward false-positive predictions. Moreover, since the target population represents a large-scale screening cohort with a low prevalence of obstructive airway disease in a less explored group, lower precision is expected from a clinical perspective27. In such screening settings, prioritizing sensitivity is generally preferred to minimize missed high-risk individuals, while false-positive cases can be further evaluated through subsequent confirmatory examinations. Therefore, the observed performance is considered clinically acceptable for the intended purpose of early risk screening, provided that the results are interpreted with appropriate clinical judgment and caution. Given the screening-oriented nature of this study, this limitation is considered acceptable. Precision is reported for completeness but should not be interpreted as a primary performance indicator in this low-prevalence screening population. Moreover, all six models demonstrated stable performance, as evidenced by small standard deviations. Hence, the importance rankings of each risk factors are aggregated from all six ML models as all of the models perform similarly.

Across the six machine learning models evaluated, predictive performance was broadly comparable, with balanced accuracy ranging from 0.70 to 0.72 and AUC values consistently exceeding 0.74 (Table 2). Ensemble-based methods, including random forest, XGBoost, LightGBM, and CatBoost, demonstrated slightly superior discrimination compared with CART, as reflected by marginally higher AUC values, while maintaining similar sensitivity and specificity. This pattern suggests that ensemble learning benefited from capturing nonlinear interactions among predictors, whereas CART provided a more transparent structure with only modest trade-offs in discrimination. Aggregation of feature importance rankings across all six models identified a coherent and clinically interpretable set of dominant predictors (Table 3), with age consistently emerging as the most influential factor, followed by biochemical markers such as lactate dehydrogenase (LDH) and white blood cell count, as well as anthropometric and socioeconomic variables including height, weight, systolic blood pressure, and education level. Although LDH is a non-specific biomarker, its importance persisted despite the concurrent inclusion of liver enzymes, inflammatory markers, and metabolic variables, indicating that it captures a broader dimension of physiological burden relevant to pulmonary function. These ensemble-derived findings were subsequently translated into interpretable decision rules using CART models constructed with varying feature cutoff percentages, demonstrating that the top 30% feature set achieved near-optimal discrimination comparable to the full-feature model (Fig. 3) and enabled the generation of concise, clinically accessible decision pathways (Fig. 4). Furthermore, the stepwise feature cutoff analysis provides an additional layer of robustness assessment. The preservation of discrimination performance when restricting the model to the Top30% feature subset indicates that the TERFI framework is stable and not overly dependent on any single predictor.

As shown, model performance increases from the Top10% and Top20% feature sets and reaches its peak at the Top30% feature cutoff, which achieves the highest AUC (0.7473). Notably, this performance is very close to that of the full-feature model (AUC = 0.7477). Beyond Top30%, the AUC slightly declines when more features are considered (Top40% and Top50%). This result indicates that the Top30% feature set provides an optimal balance between model performance and feature parsimony, achieving performance comparable to the model with full feature while using substantially concise features. Importantly, this progressive feature inclusion process also serves as a structured robustness evaluation. By systematically restricting the model to increasingly larger subsets of the highest-ranked predictors, we were able to assess how performance changed when only partial feature information was retained. The minimal difference between the Top30% model and the full-feature model suggests that predictive discrimination is not driven by a single dominant variable but rather reflects the collective contribution of the consensus-ranked predictors. This structured cutoff analysis therefore functions as an implicit ablation framework supporting model stability.

In the figure, the node located at the top is the root node which is the beginning point of the decision rules when predicting pulmonary function. When a subject and its risk factor values being imported into the CART decision tree, starting at the root node, the splitting condition of a risk factor at a node decide the path of the subject’s classification should belong to, which this process repeats until reaching the node that does not have any further connection to other node. This node is the leaf node, which provides the prediction outcome of the imputed subject. A path from the root node to a leaf node denotes a decision rule. For example, Age that exceeds 58 will be classified as pulmonary function positive. The significance of these decision rules in clinical term is discussed in the discussion section.

In addition to overall performance metrics, inter-model consistency in feature importance rankings was examined. Age consistently ranked first or near-first across five of six models, demonstrating strong consensus despite methodological heterogeneity. Biochemical markers such as LDH and WBC also demonstrated stable mid-to-high ranking positions, suggesting biological relevance beyond model-specific artifacts. The relatively small standard deviations observed across repeated runs further indicate performance stability and reproducibility. Collectively, these analyses reinforce the internal consistency and reliability of the proposed TERFI scheme.

Discussion

The novelty of this study does not reside in the introduction of new ML algorithms, but rather in the application-driven integration of established methods to generate interpretable and clinically actionable decision rules. By focusing on a non-smoking health screening population, this work highlights a group in which obstructive airway impairment may be under-recognized and demonstrates how ML can be leveraged to support early risk identification and translational clinical decision-making.

This is the first study to use ML decision tree method for predicting obstructive airway disease (FEV1/FVC ≦ 70%) between health screening population. We use the six ML method and generate the best and most promising classification results and provide an output of eight clinical features that are critical for determining the risk of obstructive airway disease. Figure 3 shows the AUCs of CART built with different important feature cutoff percentages whereas Fig. 4 summaries decision rules of combinations of clinical factors from Fig. 3. The first-level decision tree was obtained from the determining factor: Age. The accuracy (ACC) obtained was 73.1%. The second-level decision tree was generated by the following factors: height, LDH, education and weight. Therefore, the decision tree can be divided into positive finding (obstructive airway disease) or negative finding (no obstructive airway disease) situations. The accuracy ranged from 55.9% to 84.2%.

There are two predominant diseases causing obstructive airway dysfunction, such as asthma and COPD. According to the Global Initiative for Chronic Obstructive Pulmonary Disease (GOLD), approximately 50% of smokers will eventually develop COPD28,29. However, it is estimated that 25% to 45% of individuals with COPD have never smoked30. The diagnosis of COPD typically occurs around the age of 60, making it a condition that predominantly affects older adults, with significant consequences for their health and quality of life31. Epidemiological data show a COPD prevalence of 9.2% among individuals aged 40 to 59 years, which rises markedly to 22.6% in those aged 60 to 79 years32. In 2004, the prevalence of asthma among individuals aged 65 years and older in the United States was 7%, with approximately 1,088,000 older adults reporting at least one asthma attack within the preceding 12 months33. In our study, age (> 58) showed positive finding with the accuracy of 73.1% among the health screening populations. This further underscores the importance of aging as a simple yet critical predictor of obstructive airway disease.

The decision rule identifying age greater than 58 years as a predictor of obstructive airway impairment should be interpreted with caution. Although age consistently emerged as the most influential factor, reflecting normal age-related declines in pulmonary function, the use of a fixed FEV₁/FVC threshold of 0.70 in screening settings may increase the likelihood of classifying older individuals as having airflow limitation. Therefore, Rule 3 (Age > 58) should be considered an indicator of increased screening risk rather than definitive evidence of pathological obstruction. Additional clinical evaluation, including confirmatory spirometry with age-adjusted reference values and clinical correlation, is recommended to ensure appropriate interpretation in older adults.

Our result showed the age between 58 and 45 years old with high LDH level (> 330 IU/L) composing positive finding with the accuracy: 57.8%. Roth et al.34 reported that elevated lactate dehydrogenase (LDH) activity in the airways may arise from multiple sources, including necrosis or disruption of airway and/or alveolar epithelial cells, as well as damage to alveolar macrophages. LDH is a ubiquitous intracellular enzyme present in nearly all major organs, and its release into the extracellular environment serves as a well-established marker of cellular injury or death. Given its widespread distribution, abnormal LDH levels can be observed in a variety of pathological conditions.

It should be emphasized that lactate dehydrogenase is a non-specific biomarker and may be elevated in a variety of systemic conditions, including liver dysfunction, inflammation, hemolysis, malignancy, and general tissue injury. Therefore, the association observed between LDH and obstructive airway impairment in this health screening population should not be interpreted as evidence of lung-specific pathology.

To partially address potential confounding, liver enzymes (AST, ALT, GGT), inflammatory markers (CRP, WBC), and metabolic variables were included in the modeling process, allowing LDH to compete with related systemic indicators during feature selection. Nonetheless, residual confounding cannot be excluded in this cross-sectional setting. The observed association may reflect overall health status rather than a direct causal relationship with pulmonary dysfunction, and this limitation should be acknowledged when interpreting the findings.

Consequently, LDH has gained recognition as a reliable biomarker for pulmonary function. In a cross-sectional study involving 3,453 participants35, LDH levels demonstrated a significant negative linear correlation with pulmonary function indices. These findings highlight the potential of LDH as a serological indicator for monitoring respiratory diseases and assessing subclinical impairments in lung function.

The relationship between body weight (BW) and the risk of pulmonary dysfunction remains insufficiently investigated in the general population. However, a cross-sectional study conducted in a Korean cohort36 found that underweight status was associated with reduced pulmonary function. One proposed mechanism is the lack of adequate muscle mass, particularly in respiratory muscles such as the diaphragm, which may impair ventilatory capacity in underweight individuals. Conversely, a growing body of evidence indicates that excess body weight, obesity, and adult weight gain are consistently linked to deteriorations in pulmonary function37. According to our results, the populations aged between 58 and 45 years old with LDH level ( ≦ 330 IU/L) and body weight > 52 kg revealed negative finding and ≦ 52 kg unveiled positive finding with accuracy: 55.9% and 63.4% respectively.

Almaasfeh et al.38 reported a significant negative correlation between age and both forced vital capacity (FVC; r = 0.48) and forced expiratory volume in one second (FEV₁; r = 0.60). In contrast, a significant positive correlation was observed between height and FVC (r = 0.62) as well as FEV₁ (r = 0.69). Notably, a very strong correlation was found between FEV₁ and FVC, suggesting that their relationship is largely independent of height and age. Age-related declines in lung function and respiratory muscle strength contribute to symptom progression and reduce the ability to clear airway secretions, thereby increasing the risk of respiratory infections such as pneumonia and exacerbations of chronic respiratory diseases, including COPD and asthma. According to Pushpa et al.31, individuals with greater height or older age exhibited a higher prevalence of obstructive airway disease. Our study demonstrated that Age ≦ 45 + height > 176 + LDH > 335 contained positive finding with accuracy: 84.2% and Age ≦ 45 + height > 176 + LDH ≦ 335 contained negative finding with accuracy: 63%. The results of our study aligned with and support existing literature.

In a cross-sectional study conducted in the Netherlands involving 2,679 males aged 26 to 66 years39, educational attainment was categorized into three levels: low (intermediate secondary education or less), intermediate (intermediate vocational or higher secondary education), and high (higher vocational or university education). Individuals with lower educational levels exhibited significantly reduced baseline FEV1 compared to those with higher education. Similarly, a population-based study from Norway reported a fourfold increase in the prevalence of obstructive lung disease among individuals over 40 years of age with only primary education, compared to those with college-level education or higher, even after adjusting for smoking and occupational exposures40. Hetlevik et al.41 also found that the prevalence of both COPD and asthma was higher among individuals with basic education compared to those with higher educational attainment. In our study, education level was classified to 6 levels. The decision tree conducted by our research demonstrated that younger age (age < = 45) and shorter populations (height < = 176) with lower education level (education < = 2) showed positive finding (accuracy: 60.7%).

The findings of this study should be interpreted in the context of a routine health screening population, which differs from clinic-based or disease-enriched cohorts. This study has several important clinical implications. First, the prevalence of obstructive airway disease may be underestimated among non-smokers, highlighting the need for comprehensive evaluation, including detailed medical history, laboratory findings, and, most critically, spirometry. Second, the study identified associations between pulmonary function and various demographic, biochemical, and lifestyle factors among individuals undergoing health examinations. Third, the ML model developed in this study demonstrated potential for predicting the likelihood of obstructive airway disease within this population. The model can be integrated with additional clinical parameters, such as liver function tests, blood pressure, complete blood count, and lipid profile to enhance its predictive utility. This study recommends the model as a preliminary screening tool to aid in the early identification of obstructive airway disease during routine health evaluations.

This study had several limitations. First, the data were cross-sectional, which limits the ability to infer causal relationships; future longitudinal studies are needed to validate these findings. Second, the study population consisted exclusively of individuals of Asian descent, and therefore, generalizing the results to other ethnic groups should be approached with caution. Third, all participants were non-smokers, and thus, the findings may not be applicable to smoking populations. Further research including smokers is warranted to explore potential differences and using other external large-scale database for further validation. Fourth, the relatively low precision observed across models reflects an increased rate of false-positive predictions, which is inherent to screening-oriented approaches in low-prevalence populations. As the models are intended for early risk screening rather than definitive diagnosis, clinicians should interpret the results with appropriate caution and consider follow-up confirmatory testing. Fifth, although valuable insights have been obtained from the large cohort in this study, the data is derived from a single health examination system. Validation through large scale data from other clinical facilities could further strengthen the findings. Therefore, future studies are suggested to focus on longitudinal validation, broader population inclusion, and external validation to enhance clinical applicability and generalizability.

Overall, from a clinical perspective, the proposed decision rules are intended to serve as a preliminary screening aid during routine health examinations. Individuals identified as high risk based on readily available demographic, anthropometric, and laboratory features may be prioritized for confirmatory spirometry or closer respiratory evaluation. Importantly, the decision tree is not designed to replace pulmonary function testing, but rather to assist clinicians in identifying individuals who may otherwise remain unrecognized in asymptomatic screening populations.

Conclusion

This study applied an interpretable machine learning framework to predict airflow limitation (FEV₁/FVC ≤ 70%) in a large non-smoking health screening population. By integrating ensemble feature aggregation with decision tree modeling, the proposed TERFI scheme translated complex multivariable relationships into clinically interpretable decision rules. The identified predictors—including age, biochemical markers, and anthropometric factors—demonstrated stable performance across multiple algorithms.

From a translational perspective, the proposed model may serve as a preliminary screening tool to identify individuals who warrant confirmatory spirometry or closer respiratory evaluation. Importantly, the framework emphasizes interpretability and reproducibility, aligning with real-world health screening needs.

Future research should focus on longitudinal validation to assess predictive value over time, external validation across diverse ethnic populations, incorporation of age-adjusted spirometric thresholds, and prospective implementation studies to evaluate clinical utility and cost-effectiveness. Additionally, integration with other physiological and imaging biomarkers may further enhance predictive performance and clinical applicability. Overall, the findings support the feasibility of interpretable ML-driven risk stratification for early detection of obstructive airway impairment in non-smoking populations.
