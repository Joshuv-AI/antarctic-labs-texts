Congenital ventral urethrocutaneous fistula: A case report☆☆☆
Authors: Amy Cherry, Gregory Loftis, Israel Franco
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
Congenital urethrocutaneous fistulas are rare, with only around 63 reports in the medical literature. They differ from hypospadias in that the urethral meatus is in its correct position, but an additional opening is present along the urethra. The etiology is poorly understood, but surgical management is typically curative. This case report discusses a two-year-old boy with a congenital ventral urethrocutaneous fistula who presented with an abnormal urinary stream and post-void dribbling. The patient was treated with surgical repair, which was successful.

Introduction

Congenital urethrocutaneous fistulas are exceedingly rare, with approximately 63 cases documented in the literature.1 These are often mistaken for hypospadias, a well-known diagnosis. Hypospadias results from the failure of the urethral folds to fuse, leading to an ectopic meatus along the ventral penis or, in more severe cases, along the scrotal raphe or the perineum. This differs from a urethrocutaneous fistula, where the urethral meatus is in its orthotopic position, but an additional abnormal opening is identified along the course of the urethra. Urethrocutaneous fistulas can occur on either the ventral or dorsal surface. A congenital dorsal urethrocutaneous fistula is often associated with a duplicated or accessory urethra.2 The etiology of congenital fistula development is not well understood. In this case report, we discuss a pediatric patient with a congenital ventral urethrocutaneous fistula and its subsequent management.

Case presentation

A 2-year-old boy was referred to the pediatric urology clinic after his mother noticed an abnormal urinary stream while potty training. The patient's urine stream deviated downward and laterally, with persistent post-void dribbling. His past medical history was significant for a patent ductus arteriosus, which was surgically closed at six months of age. He had no history of urinary tract infections or other urinary abnormalities.

A physical examination revealed an uncircumcised phallus with an orthotopic meatus and no defect in the prepuce. However, an additional opening was identified on the ventral surface of the penis, along the midshaft (Fig. 1). Management

The patient underwent surgical repair under general anesthesia. The penis was degloved and a mild ventral chordee was corrected using the Nesbit procedure. The urethral fistula was excised. A two-layer urethral closure was performed over an 8 French Zaontz stent. To reinforce the repair, a vascularized tunica vaginalis flap was mobilized from the left hemiscrotum and secured over the reconstructed urethra. The penile skin was adjusted and re-approximated to ensure adequate coverage. There were no complications and the patient was discharged home on the same day with an 8 Fr Zaontz urethral stent in place. On postoperative day six, the stent was removed in the office and the patient was able to void appropriately. He had an additional follow-up at six weeks post op, which showed a well-healed incision with no evidence of fistula recurrence.

Discussion

Embryologic development of the male urethra begins at 8 weeks and is completed by 15 weeks of gestation. The urethral plate originates as an outgrowth of the walls of the cloaca and urogenital sinus.3 The urethral groove forms from posterior to anterior. The urethral folds consist of inner endoderm and outer ectoderm layers; mesenchymal mesodermal proliferation guides fusion of the endodermal layer leading to the tubularization of the urethra and the subsequent merging of the ectodermal edges generates the median raphe.3, 4, 5, 6 The glans urethra, of ectodermal origin, was initially proposed to develop separately as an invagination of surface epithelium towards the urethral plate3; however, signaling interactions between epithelium and surrounding mesenchymal tissue may induce differentiation of stratified squamous epithelium in the glans urethra, known as endodermal differentiation theory.

While hypospadias results from the failure of the urethral groove to close, the mechanism behind urethrocutaneous fistula formation is unknown.3,6 One proposed mechanism involves arrest of the distal migration of the urethral plate or a focal loss of urethral plate preventing fusion of the urethral folds.7 Another suggests fistula formation is secondary to the misalignment of glanular and penile urethra, given separate initial development.3 One theory involves disruptions to epithelial-mesenchymal signaling with subsequent abnormal differentiation of the urethral plate into stratified squamous epithelium.8 Yet another potential cause is anomalous infolding of the urethral groove with resultant defect of either corpus spongiosum or incomplete proliferation of spongiosum with subsequent erosion of the overlying skin.5,9 Fistula development has also been suspected to be due to embryonal urethral “blowouts” secondary to distal congenital obstruction.10 A transient decrease in testosterone during fetal development could lead to a deficiency in the closure of the urethral groove.11 Environmental exposures such as ionizing radiation have been described to lead to genitourinary anomalies.12

Several genes associated with genitourinary development have been implicated in congenital urethrocutaneous fistula formation. Eph 2 disruption leads to impaired ephrin b2 and associated receptor signaling, with resultant reduced cell adhesion during urethral tubularization.13 Several genes including Sonic hedgehog (SHH), fibroblast growth factor 8 &10 (FGF8 & FGF10), and homeobox A13 and D13 (HOXA13 and HOXD13) are involved with outgrowth and patterning of the genital tubercle and have been implicated in urethral developmental anomalies like hypospadias.14 Activation transcription factor 3 is proposed to induce arrest of urethral development as it is highly upregulated in patients with hypospadias.14,15 While no direct causal link has been established, dysregulation of transcription of any of these genes may predispose one to congenital fistula formation.

Anterior urethrocutaneous fistulas have been described with and without concomitant hypospadias and have also been reported as as cryptospadias.4 While posterior congenital urethrocutaneous fistulas are known to be associated with anorectal malformations, isolated anterior urethrocutaneous fistulas are rare with less than 60 reports identified in the literature.4,16 One recent systematic literature review examined 34 reports of urethrocutaneous fistulas; of a total of 63 reported cases, 29 were subcoronal, 24 were midshaft, four were proximal penile to subcoronal, and one was penoscrotal.1 Reports vary regarding associated structural abnormalities. While some patients have been identified with distal shaft, urethra, and corporal spongiosum intact, others have experienced concomitant chordee, and fistulas have been reported regardless of circumcision status.5,9,16, 17, 18 However, amongst published cases, no distal urethral stricture or meatal stenosis has been described.16

Given their rarity, there is no standardized approach to management, though surgical correction is typically recommended to restore normal urinary function and prevent complications. In this case, surgical intervention involved closing the fistula and reconstructing the urethra. Various surgical techniques have been described for repair of urethrocutaneous fistulas. Methods of primary closure vary based on location and severity of anatomic defect.1,5,17 For simple presentations, repair can include a modified Thiersch-Duplay technique with or without the Snodgrass tubularized incised plate5,17,19 if distal penile fistula is present. A modified Denis Browne urethroplasty has also been described as a means of surgical repair regardless of location of penile urethrocutaneous fistula.18 In cases with significant tissue loss or possible fistula occurrence after primary hypospadias repair, buccal mucosa graft has been utilized to allow for adequate closure of the ventral defect.20 Small, simple defects have been closed primarily with success.5,18 Reinforcement approach for the primary repair relies on island pedicle flaps2,5,11 or rotational preputial skin flaps.4,6,8,12,16,17 In this case of a mid-penile urethrocutaneous fistula, we performed a primary repair with use of a tunica vaginalis flap, as described by Biswas et al. Given the rarity of case presentation, no robust data exists regarding optimal surgical approach. However, similar to hypospadias repairs, postoperative outcomes are favorable, with minimal recurrence when appropriate surgical techniques are employed.19

Conclusion

Congenital urethrocutaneous fistulas, though rare, should be considered in the differential diagnosis of abnormal urinary stream patterns in pediatric patients. Careful clinical examination and imaging studies are essential for accurate diagnosis. Surgical repair remains the mainstay of treatment, with good long-term outcomes. Further research is needed to better understand the embryological mechanisms leading to this condition and to establish optimal surgical management strategies.
