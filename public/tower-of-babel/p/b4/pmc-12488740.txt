A case of hypertension due to iatrogenic glossopharyngeal neuropathy after aesthetic procedure
Authors: Erdinç TUNÇ, Tansel Turan, İrem Hengirmen Acu, Hatice Aygun
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractIn our case, we report a previously unreported complication of a thread lift procedure in the jowl region. This complication was caused by damage to the glossopharyngeal nerve through compression. The case also illustrates the diagnostic and therapeutic aspects of iatrogenic injury to the glossopharyngeal nerve. The widespread use of thread lift in aesthetic medicine has increased the variety and frequency of complications. The thread lift procedure is an blind intervention in which critical anatomical structures are not directly visualised. Therefore, the risk of damage to various structures is also high. Damage to the nerves in the application area is manifested by specific symptoms such as sensory deficits, pain, swallowing impairment, and autonomic dysfunction. We describe the diagnosis and treatment of a glossopharyngeal nerve injury caused by compression of the nerve trunk following a facial thread lift procedure.Keywords: Thread lift procedure, Jowl region, Aesthetic medicine, Glossopharyngeal

Case

A 52-year-old woman patient presented with complaints following a thread lift procedure performed at a private aesthetic clinic. The patient had sought treatment for facial contour correction and sagging of the submental region. The procedure was initiated from the right side without complications, with the thread anchored to the mastoid process in the posterior auricular region. However, during the procedure on the left side, the patient experienced sudden, sharp pain radiating from the throat and tongue root toward the left palatine tonsil. Following the completion of the procedure, the patient developed hypotension (80/45 mmHg) and bradycardia (50 bpm), along with a sensation of impending fainting.

Despite resting at the clinic, her complaints persisted. Over the next week, the patient repeatedly visited the clinic, complaining of dysphagia, pain radiating toward the left ear, persistent pain localized to the left tonsillar region, dizziness, and episodes of near-fainting. The clinic provided only symptomatic treatments, and did not conduct further investigations.

During the ongoing complaints, intermittent blood pressure measurements revealed fluctuations from an initial 120/85 mmHg to 140/95 mmHg and later 150/100 mmHg. The patient was referred to an internal medicine specialist with a preliminary diagnosis of hypertension. No organic pathology was detected, but due to intermittent palpitations and elevated blood pressure, treatment with metoprolol tartrate 25 mg was initiated for presumed essential hypertension.

Concerned that her symptoms coincided with the thread lift procedure, the patient sought a second opinion from an the author regarding the patient’s complaints. Based on the clinical presentation, damage to the glossopharyngeal and vagus nerves was suspected, and referral to neurology was recommended.

Neurological examination identified sensory deficits in the left oropharynx, tongue root, palate, and tonsil. Additional findings included edema in the floor of the mouth and periauricular region, ageusia in the posterior third of the tongue, and xerostomia. These findings confirmed a glossopharyngeal nerve injury. Brain MRI revealed edema localized in the posterior-inferior aspect of the left parotid gland. After otolaryngology consultation, surgical exploration and removal of the threads were performed. The patient underwent surgical revision, and her symptoms gradually resolved within 20 days postoperatively. Full sensory recovery was documented at the end of four months.

Discussion

The glossopharyngeal nerve (cranial nerve IX) serves both motor, sensory, and parasympathetic functions. It exits the posterior cranial fossa via the jugular foramen with cranial nerves X and XI. The nerve courses superficially alongside the internal carotid artery and jugular vein, passing under the palatine tonsil toward the tongue base. Within the parapharyngeal space, it supplies motor fibers to the stylopharyngeus muscle, parasympathetic fibers to the parotid gland, and sensory fibers to the pharynx, tonsils, and posterior one-third of the tongue. Taste fibers from this region terminate in the nucleus tractus solitarius (NTS), and general sensory fibers from the tongue and middle ear terminate in the spinal trigeminal nucleus [3, 8].

Of particular importance in this case is the glossopharyngeal nerve’s role in the baroreflex mechanism. Baroreceptors in the carotid sinus detect changes in blood pressure and transmit these signals via the glossopharyngeal nerve to the brainstem. Here, the signals synapse in the NTS to coordinate appropriate autonomic responses. Increased baroreceptor firing in response to elevated blood pressure leads to enhanced parasympathetic output via the vagus nerve, slowing heart rate and reducing cardiac output, alongside decreased sympathetic activity that results in vasodilation and lowered blood pressure. In our patient, the sequence of blood pressure changes provides mechanistic insight. The initial hypotension with bradycardia can be attributed to excessive afferent firing of the glossopharyngeal nerve due to acute compression, resembling the mechanism described in glossopharyngeal neuralgia with vascular compression. Enhanced carotid baroreceptor input to the nucleus tractus solitarius (NTS) transiently increased parasympathetic outflow via the vagus nerve while suppressing sympathetic tone, leading to hypotension and bradycardia. As the compression-induced neuropathy progressed, afferent conduction was reduced, leading to loss of baroreflex buffering. This allowed unopposed sympathetic drive from the rostral ventrolateral medulla (RVLM), resulting in sustained hypertension. This dual-phase response highlights the paradoxical cardiovascular manifestations of glossopharyngeal nerve dysfunction (Fig. 1) [4, 5, 9–12, 14].

Thread lift is a minimally invasive aesthetic procedure for correcting facial and submental sagging. The technique involves the placement of dissolvable or long-lasting threads under the skin using thin cannulas, fixed at strategic points to achieve lifting effects. Although the threads are intended to pass superficially, above the superficial musculoaponeurotic system (SMAS), placement depth and direction deviations can occur. Excessive tension or incorrect anatomical placement can lead to complications, including nerve injury, as described in previous case series [1, 6, 13, 16].

In clinical literature, glossopharyngeal nerve damage has been described in association with tonsillectomy [15], carotid endarterectomy [2], and endotracheal intubation [7]. In the presented case, excessive traction or improper placement of the thread near the mandible likely compressed the glossopharyngeal nerve. The patient’s specific symptoms pain radiating to the ear, taste disturbances, and localized tonsillar pain are characteristic of glossopharyngeal neuralgia and nerve dysfunction.

Notably, hypertension has rarely been reported as a complication of bilateral glossopharyngeal nerve damage, mainly in the context of tonsillectomy [15]. To the best of our knowledge, this case represents the first documented instance of glossopharyngeal nerve injury and resultant hypertension following an aesthetic thread lift procedure.

This case highlights the importance of anatomical awareness and careful planning during thread lift procedures. The superficial nature of thread passage does not eliminate the risk of injury to deeper structures, especially in the submental and parapharyngeal regions. The patient’s specific symptoms should be carefully evaluated, and consultation with neurology and otolaryngology specialists is strongly advised. Early recognition and intervention may prevent permanent nerve damage and systemic complications.

Conclusion

Thread lift procedures, though minimally invasive, carry risks of serious complications if anatomical planes are not respected. Glossopharyngeal nerve damage may present with oropharyngeal pain, sensory deficits, and blood pressure dysregulation. In such a case, surgical revision and removal of the threads should be considered for recovery. This case emphasizes the importance of clinician awareness regarding rare but significant complications of aesthetic procedures and the need for multidisciplinary evaluation in the presence of neurological symptoms.

Beyond its relevance for aesthetic surgeons, this case underlines the crucial physiological role of the glossopharyngeal nerve in cardiovascular regulation. Injury to this nerve may paradoxically present with either hypotension or hypertension, depending on whether afferent firing is excessive or absent. Such cases should raise awareness among neurosurgeons and neurologists regarding the autonomic consequences of glossopharyngeal neuropathy.
