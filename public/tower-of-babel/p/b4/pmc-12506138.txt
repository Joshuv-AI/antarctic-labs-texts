Support-t, an online training and peer support platform to accompany youth living with type 1 diabetes transitioning to adult healthcare: protocol of an effectiveness-implementation trial
Authors: Amélie Roy-Fleming, Meranda Nakhla, Elise Mok, Andréanne Vanasse, Liana Cianci, Jessica Kichler, Judith Simoneau-Roy, Yohann Couture, Julie Gagné, Mariève Dupont, Anne-Sophie Brazeau
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractIntroductionType 1 diabetes (T1D) demands self-management skills, knowledge and confidence to prevent medical complications. Adolescents living with T1D have distinct developmental challenges resulting in a worsening in glycaemic stability, irregular care and an increased risk for complications all while transitioning to adult healthcare. Age-specific online platforms could facilitate transition by fostering self-management education and support. The Support online self-guided training platform has been shown to increase the confidence of adults with T1D in managing their glycaemia. We aim to test the effectiveness of Support-t (ie, adapted for youth), compared with usual care, in improving haemoglobin A1c (HbA1c) and to understand the context of its implementation.MethodsWe will conduct a multisite, assessor-blinded, randomised controlled, parallel group, two-arm, superiority trial, evaluating effectiveness and implementation of Support-t versus usual care in 200 adolescents (14–16 years old) living with T1D. The active arm will have an 18-month access to Support-t, and their healthcare team will be trained on the platform’s content. The control arm will receive usual care. The primary outcome is HbA1c at 18 months. Secondary outcomes include self-efficacy for diabetes self-management, transition readiness, diabetes-specific quality of life, diabetes distress, continuous glucose monitoring metrics, number of severe hypoglycaemic events, diabetic ketoacidosis, T1D-related emergency department visits and hospitalisations as well as engagement and satisfaction. A subgroup of participants in the active arm and of healthcare providers will be interviewed assessing barriers, facilitators, engagement and fidelity of the intervention. Primary analysis will be by intention-to-treat. The difference in mean HbA1c at 18 months (with a 95% CI) will be calculated between both arms. A cost-effectiveness analysis is also planned.Ethics and disseminationDecember 8, 2024 version of the protocol was approved by the McGill University Health Centre Research Ethics Board (MP-37-2024-9734). Results will be disseminated through peer-reviewed publications and patient-partners’ network.Trial registration numberClinicalTrials.gov (NCT05910840).

Introduction

Type 1 diabetes (T1D) requires daily insulin administration paired with diabetes knowledge, self-management skills and self-efficacy to balance glucose levels in order to prevent acute and chronic diabetes-related complications.1 As their parents start to step back, adolescents living with T1D must increase their knowledge and confidence to self-manage their condition and take on more responsibilities. Meanwhile, they also confront concurrent social, physical and emotional changes,2 coupled with the burden of diabetes-related stigma.3 Adolescence and young adulthood often correspond to a worsening of blood glucose management, increased risk of diabetes-related complications (eg, diabetic ketoacidosis)4 and inconsistent medical follow-up.5

Like most youth living with a chronic medical condition, those with T1D will ultimately transition to adult medical care, with transfer occurring at approximately 18 years of age in Quebec, Canada. Transition care is defined as the purposeful, planned movement of adolescents with chronic medical conditions from child-centred to adult-oriented healthcare systems.6 Transition care aims to deliver healthcare that is well-coordinated, continuous and developmentally appropriate, in order to foster the acquisition of decision-making and self-care skills.6 However, over 50% of adolescents with chronic health conditions indicate a lack of sufficient support and services during the transition to adult care.7 While the transfer to adult care is a specific event, transition care is an ongoing process that should start in adolescence6 especially in T1D due to complex treatment and the significant level of responsibility it requires by the person living with T1D.8 Although transition programmes have been developed, involving transition coordinators, educational material and/or young adult clinics, few have been tested with comparison groups.5 Those programmes often encounter significant barriers to implementation due to cost, resource allocation and scalability limitations.9 Diabetes self-management education (DSME) should be provided at critical times when people with T1D are most vulnerable to poor outcomes such as at time of diagnosis and during transitions of care.10 11 Some educational interventions aiming to bridge these gaps exist but are often time-consuming and costly (eg, multiple sessions, group-based approaches).10 Furthermore, the American Academy of Pediatrics identified the lack of patient knowledge and engagement as one of the five main barriers to successful transition.12

In a qualitative study, caregivers, transitioning youth with T1D and healthcare professionals (HCPs) have identified the development of an age-appropriate T1D app/website as ‘very important’.13 Online resources make it possible to look up specific topics when needed compared with generalised DSME, which youth feel is mostly for newly diagnosed individuals.14 Youth have also expressed the need for online peer support as they prefer to learn from peers.14 Therefore, web-based DSME and support platforms could potentially contribute to improving transition care for adolescents and young adults with T1D by making DSME accessible geographically and in a timely manner. The first apps designed for this population were developed to collect information to be discussed during medical consultations15 and to provide feedback on blood glucose readings16 17 without offering complete DSME and support. In adults with type 2 diabetes, online training platforms showed promising results in improving glucose levels,18 fostering patient empowerment and providing ongoing support19 all while remaining cost-effective.20

To address T1D specifically, the BETTER project (BEhaviors, Therapies, TEchnologies and hypoglycemic Risk in T1D) developed Support (www.type1support.ca), an evidence-based self-guided online T1D DSME and support platform.21 In a feasibility trial, we demonstrated an increase in confidence level in preventing hypoglycaemia and managing blood glucose levels after 6 months of access (n=207 adults with T1D; ages 24–79 years; 34% men).22 Diabetes knowledge increased and overall level of satisfaction was high. HCPs (n=66) also tested Support and after having access to the platform for 3 months, participants (dietitians, nurses, pharmacists and residents in medicine and endocrinology) reported an increased level of confidence for treating individuals with T1D.23

We adapted the Support platform for youth, now called Support-t (t for ‘transition’), through a co-design process with 14- to 24-year olds living with T1D and HCPs working with this age group figure 1. Individual interviews with participants revealed youth value online information that is reliable, applicable and novel.24 During focus group discussions, both youth and HCPs identified the potential of addressing sensitive or time-consuming topics through an online platform.25 Support-t is designed to empower youth with T1D to participate in their own care. On top of educational material, through its discussion forum, it fosters peer-to-peer support which has been demonstrated to improve adherence to diabetes self-care and reduce diabetes-related stress in youth with T1D.26 27 Various behaviour change techniques28 are nested within the platform, such as feedback, social support, progression and information about health consequences, which can potentially increase the facilitators and decrease the barriers of diabetes self-management among youth.

Overview of Support-t.

Objective

The overall objective of our study is to assess, among 14- to 16-year-old youth with T1D preparing to transition to adult healthcare, the effectiveness of Support-t, an online platform to help with glucose management and self-efficacy and the context of implementation in paediatric diabetes clinics serving different populations (geodiversity, various sociodemographic status and ethnicity). Our primary outcome is haemoglobin A1c (HbA1c) at 18 months (effectiveness outcome). Secondary outcomes include change in self-efficacy for diabetes management, transition readiness, diabetes-related quality of life and diabetes distress. Implementation endpoints include process evaluation, cost effectiveness of the intervention, usage and satisfaction of the platform over the 18 months, barriers and facilitators of integrating Support-t it in clinic.

Methods

Study design

This is a hybrid effectiveness-implementation design which allows us to determine the potential effectiveness of the intervention while simultaneously exploring how Support-t can be implemented in clinical care.29 It is a multisite, assessor-blinded, randomised controlled, parallel group, two-arm, 1:1 allocation ratio, superiority trial, evaluating the effectiveness of Support-t when integrated into routine diabetes care (active arm) compared with usual care alone (control arm) in 14- to 16-year-old adolescents with T1D. Assessments in adolescents will be performed at baseline, 6, 12 and 18 months (T0, T6, T12, T18). In parallel, a series of qualitative interviews with adolescents and HCPs from the paediatric clinics will be conducted as part of the process evaluation aspect of the study.

Inclusion/Exclusion criteria

For youth with T1D, eligible participants have a clinical diagnosis of T1D, are 14–16 years of age, receive care in one of the four participating clinics in Quebec, Canada (Laval University Hospital Center, McGill University Health Center, Sherbrooke University Hospital Center and Clinique pédiatrique Le Copain, Gatineau), understand English or French, have access to internet and have an active email address. They are excluded if they have severe neurocognitive disabilities, conditions associated with shortened erythrocyte survival, such as haemolytic anaemia or other conditions associated with inaccurate HbA1c.

For HCP, any physician, nurse, dietitian, psychologist, social worker, pharmacist or other HCP actively providing care to youth with T1D in one of the four participating clinics is eligible.

Intervention

Adolescents randomised to usual care (ie, control arm) will attend their usual 3-month interval diabetes clinic visits and any other service included in their regular care (eg, phone follow-up, group classes). HCPs will not discuss the Support-t platform with them. Participants of the control arm will be granted access to use the Support-t platform after the 18-month assessment if they wish.

Participants in the active arm will have access to the Support-t platform for 18 months in addition to their usual care. HCPs will also have access to the platform for the duration of their patients’ participation in the study. They will be encouraged to use Support-t when they feel appropriate for those in the active arm, but not for those in the control arm. Table 1 shows a summary of the Support-t components.

Not accessible to HCPs.

Several implementation strategies will be put in place (figure 2). At baseline, all HCPs will receive a 1 hour in-person group training about the platform (content, features, case studies). If needed additional individual virtual training will be offered. The in-person training will be followed by a group discussion about intended use of the platform, implementation strategies and foreseen barriers and facilitators to implementation. The discussion will be based on a pre-established interview guide, recorded and transcribed for further analysis. HCPs will be given a printed ‘cheat sheet’ (online supplemental material 2) which summarises the topics covered on the platform, has a list of downloadable teaching tools and has the QR code of the platform for quick referral. They will have their own access to Support-t and will receive a monthly newsletter specific to HCPs. One or two ‘champion’” HCPs in each clinic will be identified (eg, physician, nurse or dietitian) on a joint agreement between the clinic’s HCPs and the research team. Champions will act as super users for the local team and will receive a monthly email from the platform coordinator to ask about additional needs for training within the clinic and invite them to contribute ideas for blog articles that align with the patients’ needs and interests.

Recruitment and informed consent

Onsite research assistants will be responsible for recruitment and obtaining informed consent of youth (see model consent form in online supplemental material 3). They will contact potential participants through phone or email. Posters will also be in clinic waiting areas. Participants will undergo informed consent in person with a parent or legal guardian. Youth will then be randomised to either the active or control arm.

All HCPs, at each designated clinic, will be invited to participate through an email invitation. Local research assistants will obtain their informed consent and organise the logistics for the group training.

A subgroup of randomly selected adolescents in the active arm will receive an email invitation for an individual interview at 18 months. All participating HCPs will be invited for an individual interview at 12 months.

Randomisation

The allocation sequence will be computer generated by an independent statistician and uploaded to the central database in the Research Electronic Data Capture (REDCap) software. To reduce the possibility of study personnel predicting the allocation sequence (allocation concealment), participants will be randomised with a 1:1 allocation ratio using blocks of random sizes. Because of the nature of the intervention, participants, HCPs and investigators cannot be blinded. The outcome assessors and data analysts will be blinded.

Data collection

For youth, all outcome measures will be assessed at 0 month (T0; baseline), 6, 12 and 18 months (T6, T12, T18) unless specified (see table 2). At each time point, onsite research assistants will extract glucose management data from medical charts and participants will be contacted through email to complete online questionnaires in REDCap. Email and phone reminders are planned to make sure adolescents complete the questionnaires. Financial compensation will be sent to adolescents for each data collection time point they complete to keep them engaged over time.

Outcome measures

Primary outcome

The primary outcome is HbA1c at 18 months.4 HbA1c is extracted from the medical chart at each time point. If the value is more than 2 months ago, participants will be sent a HbA1c Test Kit, which is a non-fasting, finger prick, whole blood mail-in test (Bio-Rad Laboratories Canada Ltd).31 The blood sample size is 0.001 mL. The test kit is one of the most accurate and comprehensive HbA1c tests available. Samples are stable for 45 days at room temperature.

Secondary outcomes in youth

Continuous glucose monitoring (CGM) metrics will be retrieved from medical charts at each time point when available (if participant uses a CGM) and reported as suggested in a recent international consensus statement for the use of CGMs in clinical trials32: percentages of time in range (3.9–10.0 mmol/L), time above range, time below range, the SD and the coefficient of variation of the past 14 days. Number of severe hypoglycaemic events, diabetes ketoacidosis, emergency department visits and T1D-related hospitalisations from the past 6 months will be taken from medical chart and assessed through online questionnaires at each time-point.

Self-efficacy for diabetes management will be assessed through the Self-Efficacy Diabetes Self-Management Measure (SEDM),33 a 10-item self-administered online questionnaire. Earlier studies involving adolescents indicated that self-efficacy is correlated with HbA1c levels within the target range and adherence to diabetes care.3335 Diabetes distress will be assessed with the Problem Areas in Diabetes: Teen questionnaire (PAID-T)36 which has 14 items and is self-administered online. Diabetes distress refers to the negative emotions that arise from living with and managing diabetes,37 and is commonly described as experiencing depression-like symptoms, but they are specific to the burden of managing diabetes. Diabetes-specific quality of life will be assessed with the type 1 diabetes and life (T1DAL) which is designed for 12–17 year olds’ living with T1D and has 23 items.38 Readiness to transition to adult healthcare will be assessed with the Readiness Assessment of Emerging Adults With Type 1 Diabetes Diagnosed in Youth (READDY) questionnaire, a 46-item tool designed to assess readiness of adolescents and emerging adults living with T1D when transitioning from paediatric care to adult care and their confidence levels on T1D related knowledge and skills.39

Platform satisfaction and use of platform in clinic follow-up will be collected through an online questionnaire. Participants will rate their satisfaction with Support-t on a scale from 1 to 10 and to share their experience with the platform (open-ended, written question about barriers, facilitators and motivation when using the platform) and if they discussed Support-t with their HCP. Engagement with the platform will be evaluated with a range of metrics extracted using Google analytics (eg, time spent on the platform, participation in the discussion forum, specific content accessed, total pages viewed, downloaded documents, use of calculators) as it has been suggested in the literature.40 Feedback from a subgroup of participants will be gathered through a 60-min virtual semistructured individual interview to understand the experience in the context of implementation of the Support-t platform to guide the realist evaluation which is to understand what works, for whom and under what circumstances.41

Secondary outcomes in HCPs

Clinical practice descriptive data will be taken from paper questionnaires to assess profession, years of practice, usual web resources they use for patients. Evaluation of the 1-hour training will also be a paper questionnaire. Projected use of Support-t will be assessed in a short focus group discussion with HCPs to get a sense of their intended use of the platform as well as barriers and facilitators they foresee and evaluation of the implementation strategies. Engagement with the platform and feedback will be collected (same methods and metrics as above).

Sample size

The sample size of adolescents with T1D was calculated to detect a 0.6%-unit difference in HbA1c between the active and control arm at 18 months. Assuming a SD of 1.4%, based on an on-going trial42 (n=153; 16–17 years old), for a two-sided alpha=0.05 and 80% power to detect such a difference using t-test, a minimum of 85 participants per group will be required. Allowing a loss to follow-up of up to 15%, the required sample size is 100 participants per arm (n=200). For the end-of-study individual interviews, 4–5 participants from the active arm in each clinic (50% female) for a total of 16–20 participants will be recruited as well as a minimum of three HCPs in each participating paediatric clinic (12–16 HCPs in total).

Patient and public involvement

Individuals living with T1D (including A. Vanasse, a nurse) were involved since the beginning of the co-creation of the Support-t platform and in the funding application for this trial for which a patient-partner was a co-principal applicant. Their input has shaped the content and design of the platform (eg, topics addressed, mobile-first design, type of media), the research question and the choice of outcomes and questionnaires to be used in the trial. They were consulted through focus groups, individual interviews, informal discussions, email and could provide their feedback directly on shared documents. Patient-partners were also involved in the development of testimonial videos and are asked to contribute to the discussion forum throughout the study. Participants in the active arm will also shape the intervention through their involvement in the discussion forum. Topics addressed will guide the writing of news blogs by the research team.

Patient-partners will participate in the data interpretation and dissemination of the results, which will be facilitated by their involvement since the earlier stages of the research and since they have taken ownership of the project.

Data analysis

Statistical analysis

Initial analysis will be descriptive and baseline data will be summarised. Baseline continuous data will be reported as means with standard deviations (median and IQR if data are skewed). Categorical data will be reported as proportions. Our primary outcome is the difference in mean HbA1c (continuous variable) measured at 18 months between the two arms. To account for the centre effect, we will use linear mixed effects models where centre is entered as a random effect to compare the primary outcome between groups. Secondary outcomes are the between-arm differences at 6, 12 and 18 months in severe hypoglycaemic events (requiring help for treatment) (Y/N; rates/P-Y), T1D-related hospitalisations and ED visits (rates/P-Y), self-efficacy, diabetes readiness to transfer, diabetes distress and quality of life (QOL) . Between-arm differences in means will be calculated using student t-tests or Poisson distribution as appropriate. To account for repeated measures, secondary analysis will also be conducted to calculate within-arm differences, using paired t-test for continuous data and McNemar test for proportions in the above outcomes between T0–T6, T6–T12 and T12–T18 to calculate changes during the trial. Missing data will be handled with simple imputation using last observation carried forward. The primary analysis will be by intention to treat, which will include data from all participants and every participant will be analysed according to their randomised group assignment, regardless of how often they use the platform. If regular use of the Support-t platform is an issue for some, the intention to treat approach may underestimate the magnitude of the effect of the intervention but provide unbiased comparisons between groups. For this reason, a secondary ‘as treated’ analysis that will include only participants who remained active on the platform over the 18-month period will be conducted. A participant will be considered as being active if they have logged into the platform at least once every 3 months. Although the trial is not powered for subgroup analysis, we plan to conduct exploratory analysis by sex at birth and self-reported gender, by medical care setting (academic vs non-academic) and by baseline glycaemic control (suboptimal defined as HbA1c >9,0%).

Cost-effectiveness

We will conduct a health economic evaluation, measuring total direct costs and incremental cost effectiveness of the intervention from the perspective of the health system to inform policy and future scale-up of the intervention. All costs related to the interventions will be collected prospectively from costs related to Support-t and costs incurred by the healthcare system, respectively: (a) costs of intervention activities (eg, newsletter, HCP to moderate forum); (b) costs incurred by the healthcare system (eg, ED visits, hospitalisations). Incremental cost-effectiveness ratios, described as the ratio of the difference in costs to the difference in outcomes between the Support-t platform (intervention) and usual care (control), will be calculated for the outcomes (HbA1c, diabetes distress, QOL and self-efficacy); 95% CI will be calculated using bootstrap method. A 0.5% difference in HbA1c is considered clinically meaningful and has been used as a standard threshold in diabetes trials. A change of ≥0.5 SD on the SEDM and T1DAL will be considered a meaningful improvement in self-efficacy and QOL. A change of ≥5 on the PAID-T will be considered a meaningful improvement regarding diabetes distress.

Qualitative analysis

For both group (projected use of Support-t by HCPs at T0) and individual (HCPs at T12 and T1D participants in the active arm at T18) interviews, the discussions will be transcribed. In the case certain parts of the recording are dysfunctional or difficult to hear, the interviewer may use his or her recollection to fill in the gaps or use the field notes. French meeting transcriptions will then be translated to English using a forward-backward translation method. To improve the reliability of the findings, two researchers will independently review and transcribe material. Two researchers will perform the coding independently. Although it is not expected that they will generate the exact same codes, memos and note-taking during the coding process will ensure consistency. Additionally, we will use bracketing to allow researchers to acknowledge their own assumptions and experiences in the research topic area (ie, how they are situated with the study data) to better understand the participant’s unique perspectives. Bracketing will be done by the interviewer and all researchers involved in the analysis process by writing analytical memos. Analytical memos are ‘notes to self’ to record a train of thought, and to keep a record of reflections. Memos are also used to record any biases or contradicting information that may impact the data interpretation process. Interviews will be analysed based on an iterative inductive approach. Codes having similar topics will be merged into categories and further into themes, using concept mapping.43

Confidentiality

Participants will use their personal email to access the platform and manage their password. However, emails will not be displayed publicly. T1D participants are encouraged to engage in forum discussions using a pseudonym. Posts revealing personal information will be removed by the facilitator, who monitors the forum daily and ensures all identifiers are promptly deleted.

Ethics and dissemination

This research project was approved by the McGill University Health Center’s ethics board (MP-37-2024-9734) and registered at ClinicalTrials.gov (NCT0591084). A multicentric process is underway to get approval to recruit from the other three clinics. Preliminary findings will be presented at national and international conferences as abstracts and posters. We will submit a manuscript in a peer-reviewed journal. The BETTER project’s (www.type1better.com) network will also participate in dissemination of results (eg, website, newsletter, social networks).
