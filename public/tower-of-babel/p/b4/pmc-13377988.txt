In silico study, synthesis, radiolabeling, and preliminary biological evaluation of 99mTc-DTPA-benzothiazole for breast cancer imaging
Authors: Teguh Hafiz Ambar Wibawa, Hendris Wongso, Tita Puspitasari, Alfian Mahardika Forentin, Tursino, Muhayatun Santoso, Daryono Hadi Tjahjono
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Benzothiazole derivatives have attracted attention for radiopharmaceutical development due to their potential interactions with biological targets associated with breast cancer. In the present study, a novel 99mTc-DTPA-benzothiazole-17 (TDB-17) compound was synthesized, radiolabeled, and preliminarily evaluated for breast cancer imaging research. In silico studies were performed to predict the interaction of the benzothiazole derivative with breast cancer-related biological targets. TDB-17 was synthesized by conjugating 2-aminobenzothiazole with CaNa3-DTPA and subsequently radiolabeled with 99mTc using SnCl2 as a reducing agent. The synthesized compound and radiocomplex were characterized using radio-high-performance liquid chromatography (radio-HPLC), high-resolution mass spectrometry (HRMS), and nuclear magnetic resonance (NMR). The radiolabeling process produced a radiochemical purity of 96.2 ± 1.2%. A preliminary biological evaluation showed that TDB-17 exhibited hydrophilic properties (log P = −1.5 ± 0.2) and time-dependent cellular uptake in MCF-7 cells, reaching 78.4% at 120 min. Biodistribution and gamma-camera imaging studies in normal mice showed predominant accumulation of TDB-17 in the liver, lungs, and spleen, providing initial information on the in vivo distribution of the radiocomplex. Overall, TDB-17 demonstrated favorable radiochemical characteristics and a preliminary biological profile, supporting further investigation of this 99mTc-labeled benzothiazole derivative for breast cancer imaging applications.

Introduction

Cancer remains a major global and national health challenge. In 2020, there were approximately 19.3 million new cases and nearly 10 million deaths globally, making cancer the second-leading cause of death.1 In Indonesia, the incidence of cancer reached 141.1, and mortality was 85.1 per 100 000 population. Limited early detection and inadequate diagnostic facilities contribute to high mortality rates.2

Breast cancer is a major contributor to the cancer burden because it is the most common cancer in women (±18%). It is heterogeneous, with different molecular subtypes that affect therapy response. The disease has a high risk of metastasis, especially to the brain, and a low response to therapy. This results in a decrease in 5 year survival from 99% in local cases to 26% in metastatic cases, emphasizing the importance of early detection using sensitive nuclear techniques.3,4 Nuclear imaging using single-photon emission computed tomography (SPECT) or positron emission tomography (PET), especially in hybrid systems (PET/CT, SPECT/CT, PET/MRI), is an effective method for early cancer detection. These techniques can visualize anatomical structures and biological processes. Target receptors, such as HER2, EGFR, IGF-IR, and estrogen receptors, are used in the development of radiopharmaceuticals for breast cancer.5

Several 99mTc-labeled tracers targeting estrogen receptors, and benzothiazole-based compounds have previously been reported for breast cancer imaging. However, limitations related to synthesis complexity, radiochemical performance, and biological evaluation remain. Therefore, developing new benzothiazole-based ligands with favorable radiochemical properties and preliminary biological characteristics remains of interest.

Benzothiazole derivatives show biological activity and potential as radioisotope-carrier ligands. Combined with the favorable imaging properties of Technetium-99m (99mTc) for SPECT imaging, these compounds remain promising candidates for radiopharmaceutical development.6 In this study, we investigated a novel benzothiazole derivative targeting estrogen receptors overexpressed in breast cancer cells through in silico interaction studies, synthesis, radiolabeling, and preliminary biological evaluation, including cellular uptake in breast cancer cells as well as biodistribution and gamma camera imaging in normal animal models. These findings provide preliminary insights for further investigation of benzothiazole-based radiopharmaceutical candidates for breast cancer imaging.

Experimental

Materials

This research used computing facilities and software, including a PC (Intel® Core™ i7 processor, NVIDIA® GeForce® RTX 1070 GPU (8 GB), and 32 GB DDR4 RAM), with Windows 10 and Ubuntu 2024 operating systems, and High-Performance Computing (HPC) Mahameru BRIN. AutoDock4 was used for molecular docking studies, MGL Tools 1.5.6 for preparing input files and analyzing docking results, BIOVIA Discovery Studio 2021 for visualizing molecular interactions, GaussView for preparing quantum calculations, UCSF Chimera 1.17.3 for 3D visualization and analysis, and GraphPad Prism 8.4.3 for statistical analysis. The experimental analysis equipment included analytical RP-HPLC (Agilent 1260; ZORBAX Eclipse XDB C18 column, 4.6 × 150 mm), 1H and 13C NMR spectrometers (Bruker Ascend 700 MHz), HRMS, UV cabinet, rotary evaporator (Heidolph), biosafety cabinet (Thermo Scientific 1300 Series A2), gamma counter (Capintec), and glassware for thin-layer chromatography. Materials used included benzothiazole derivatives, CaNa3-DTPA, coupling reagents, organic solvents, sodium pertechnetate (Na99mTcO4), SnCl2, MCF-7 breast cancer cells, Hanks' balanced salt solution (HBSS), and fetal bovine serum (FBS).

Methods

This study focuses on the design, synthesis, and labeling of 99mTc-labeled benzothiazole derivatives and on their biological evaluation as candidate radiopharmaceuticals for breast cancer diagnostics, examined using computational approaches and laboratory experiments.

In silico study

In silico studies were conducted as an initial step to select candidate benzothiazole derivatives with the potential to be developed as radiopharmaceuticals. A total of 18 benzothiazole derivative compounds and one reference compound, 2-(4-aminophenyl)benzothiazole (TDAB), were designed and evaluated computationally, including ligand design, protein and ligand preparation, molecular docking validation, molecular dynamics (MD), and binding energy analysis. Based on the in silico evaluation results, TDB-17 was the best-performing compound. Therefore, in this study, the TDB-17 compound was selected for further discussion and synthesis, radiolabeling, and biological evaluation.

The structures of TDB-17 and the reference compound TDAB were drawn using ChemDraw and GaussView software. The next steps included preparing target protein and ligand structures, validating the molecular docking method, performing molecular docking and molecular dynamics simulations, and analyzing the simulation results to evaluate complex stability and ligand–protein interaction affinity.

Receptor preparation

The crystal structure of the ER (PDB ID: 3ERT) was downloaded from the Protein Data Bank and processed in BIOVIA Discovery Studio 2021 by removing ligands, water, and non-essential residues, adding polar hydrogens, and assigning partial charges with AutoDock4 parameters. The final structure was saved as .pdb for molecular docking.7

Ligand preparation

The TDB-17 and reference compounds were prepared by drawing 2D structures in ChemDraw, converting them to 3D structures in GaussView, and optimizing the geometry in Gaussian to obtain minimum energy conformations. The structures and atomic charges were verified before saving as .pdb files for molecular docking.8

In silico study of TDB-17 compound

Validation of the molecular docking simulation method

Docking validation involved redocking the natural ligand into the active site of the receptor in AutoDock4 using the same parameters as the test ligand. An RMSD below 2 Å confirmed an accurate reproduction of the ligand position.9,10

Molecular docking simulation

Molecular docking simulations were performed using AutoDock4 with the Lamarckian Genetic Algorithm to predict ligand and receptor affinity and binding mode, using a population parameter of 100 individuals, a maximum energy evaluation of 2.5 × 106, and 27 000 generations. The best conformation was determined based on the lowest binding energy and ligand orientation on the active site of the receptor.11

Molecular dynamics simulation

Molecular dynamics (MD) simulations were performed using AMBER 22 to evaluate the stability of the ligand and receptor complex under physiological conditions, with protein preparation using H++ and the application of the ff19SB force field and the OPC water model, the Na+/Cl− ion disolvation and neutralization system, and the parameterization of the 99mTc ion using the Metal Center Parameter Builder (MCPB). The simulation was run at 310 K after the minimization and equilibration stages with a production time of 200 ns, and the MM-PBSA method was used to analyze the binding free energy.12

In silico data analysis

Data analysis was initiated by visualizing the ligand and receptor complex using BIOVIA Discovery Studio 2021 and UCSF Chimera 1.17.3 to identify molecular interaction patterns, including hydrogen bonds, hydrophobic interactions, electrostatics, and metal coordination, as well as ligand orientation and the involvement of key residues in the receptor's active site.

The stability and dynamics of the complex during MD simulations were analyzed through RMSD and RMSF calculations to assess the system stability and flexibility of protein residues, while the binding free energy was calculated using the MM-PBSA method to compare ligand affinities and confirm the results of MD simulations.

Synthesis of TDB-17 compound

Based on the results of in silico studies, the synthesis of the TDB-17 compound was carried out through the conjugation of 2-aminobenzothiazole with CaNa3-DTPA through the formation of an amide bond using HOBt and EDCI as activators and DIPEA as a base, with the reaction taking place at a temperature of 50 °C for 24 h in a nitrogen atmosphere.6

The reaction mixture was further purified through liquid–liquid extraction using ethyl acetate, washing with aquabidest and brine solution, and drying using anhydrous Na2SO4, followed by solvent evaporation to obtain the product. The synthesized product was characterized using C18 reversed-phase HPLC (UV detection 254 nm) for purity evaluation, as well as HRMS and 1H and 13C NMR to confirm the molecular mass and chemical structure of the TDB-17 compound.6 DB-17 was obtained as a pale yellow solid (110 mg, 20.11% yield, purity >96%. 1H NMR (700 MHz, CD3OD) δ 8.57 (s, 1H), 7.60 (d, J = 7 Hz, 1H), 7.40 (d, J = 7 Hz, 1H), 7.26 (t, J = 7 Hz, 1H), 7.08 (t, J = 7 Hz, 1H), 3.49 (d, J = 14 Hz, 1H), 2.65–3.24 (m, 6H), 3.07 (d, J = 14 Hz, 2H), 2.90 (t, J = Hz, 2H), 2.79 (t, J = 10.5 Hz, 2H), 2.66 (d, J = 14 Hz, 2H), 2.43 (d, J = 14 Hz, 2H). 13C NMR (175 MHz, CD3OD) δ 178.96, 178.77, 178.64, 168.33, 151.65, 130.53, 125.45, 121.39, 120.44, 117.42, 63.71, 62.40, 59.64, 56.82, 56.58, 48.13. HRMS (LC-MS/MS) for 218.017053, observed 218.01068 ([M + H + 2Na]3+).

Radiolabeling of the TDB-17 compound with the radioisotope 99mTc

Radioisotope labeling was performed by reacting 0.5 mL of DB-17 solution with Na99mTcO4 (3 mCi/750 µL) in a lead container. The pH was adjusted to 5–7, and SnCl2 (3 µL) was added as a reducing agent. The mixture was incubated at room temperature for 15 minutes (min) to produce the 99mTc-DTPA-Benzothiazole-17 (TDB-17) complex.

The radiochemical purity of the product was evaluated using radio-HPLC with a C18 column, a flow rate of 1 mL min−1, UV detection at 254 nm, and a NaI(Tl) radioactivity detector, and a mobile phase of acetonitrile : water with a gradient of 5–95% acetonitrile, where the purity value was determined based on the proportion of the radioactivity peak of the labeled compound to the total radioactivity detected.13

Lipophilicity test

To evaluate the lipophilicity of the compound, the partition coefficient (log P) of TDB-17 was determined using a two-phase system of n-octanol and sodium chloride solution. Each sample was placed in a microtube containing 1 mL n-octanol and 1 mL NaCl solution. The mixture was then homogenized for several minutes using a vortex mixer and allowed to stand until the two phases were completely separated.

To minimize contamination, the aqueous phase was carefully separated and washed again with NaCl solution. The radioactivity in each phase was then measured using a gamma counter. The log P value was calculated based on the ratio of radioactivity distributed in the n-octanol phase to the NaCl phase, as an indicator of the radiopharmaceutical compound's lipophilic or hydrophilic tendencies.14,15

Cellular uptake test

A cellular uptake assay was performed to evaluate the internalization of TDB-17 in MCF-7 cells. Cells were incubated with TDB-17 at 37 °C in HBSS for 15, 60, and 120 min to characterize time-dependent uptake kinetics. At each incubation time, the non-internalized fraction was pipetted from the medium and washed with cold PBS, while the internalized fraction was obtained by cell harvesting, lysis using 0.2 N NaOH, and lysate collection, followed by radioactivity measurement in both fractions using a gamma counter. The percentage of internalization was calculated based on the radioactivity distribution normalized to 106 cells, with Na99mTcO4 as a non-specific control.16

Biodistribution test

The biodistribution test of the TDB-17 radiopharmaceutical was conducted in normal female Balb/c mice via intravenous administration of 100 µCi/100 µL to evaluate the organ distribution pattern. Organ samples (blood, large intestine, skin, muscle, bone, spleen, pancreas, liver, heart, lungs, kidneys, small intestine, stomach, urinary bladder, thyroid, and tail) were collected at 30, 60, and 180 min and 18 h post-injection, followed by euthanasia with high-dose ketamine and organ harvest, and then radioactivity was measured using a gamma counter. The results were expressed as the percentage of radioactivity per gram of tissue (%ID/g). Sixteen mice were used in the experiments.17,18

All animals received humane care, and the animal experiments were conducted in accordance with the Institutional Animal Use and Care Committee of the National Research and Innovation Agency of Indonesia (BRIN) (approval number: 187/KE.02/SK/09/2025, 10 September 2025).

Imaging with a gamma camera

In vivo imaging studies of the TDB-17 compound were conducted using normal female Balb/c mice to evaluate the distribution, accumulation, and elimination patterns of the radiopharmaceutical in the body. The TDB compound was prepared with an initial activity of 5 mCi in 5 mL of saline and then administered via tail vein injection at a dose of 100 µCi in 100 µL per mouse.

Gamma camera imaging was performed at 30, 60, and 180 min, as well as 18 h post-injection using a Solo Mobile Gamma Camera with a 15 min acquisition time. The obtained images were analyzed qualitatively and semi-quantitatively to assess the relative biodistribution patterns and target organs.19

Results and discussion

In silico study

Validation of the molecular docking simulation method

The in silico study started with the validation of the molecular docking method to assess the accuracy of the simulation parameter's accuracy. Validation was performed through the redocking process of the natural ligand tamoxifen on the estrogen receptor (PDB: 3ERT) by comparing the conformation of the simulation results with the X-ray crystal structure.

The redocking results (Fig. 1) showed a match in the orientation and position of the ligand in the receptor's active site, which was confirmed by the RMSD value of 0.9 Å. This value meets the validity criteria of the docking method (RMSD ≤2.0 Å); thus, the method was deemed valid and suitable for molecular docking simulations of the tested compounds.20,21

Visualization of the conformation of the native ligand (blue) and the redocking result (green) in the estrogen receptor (PDB ID: 3ERT).

Molecular docking simulation

Molecular docking simulations of TDB-17 and TDAB compounds showed that all ligands successfully bound to the ER binding pocket (PDB ID: 3ERT). Visualization of the complex (Fig. 2) showed that TDB-17 occupies the same binding site as the natural ligand, with the benzothiazole core skeleton oriented toward the receptor's hydrophobic pocket. Variations in substituent groups lead to differences in ligand orientation and penetration depth, which contribute to differences in binding energy between compounds. These results indicate that the modification of the benzothiazole structure does not hinder the ability of the ligand to effectively interact with the estrogen receptor.22

3D visualization of molecular docking simulation results.

The binding free energy values of TDB-17 and the reference compound TDAB are −8.9 and −10.7 kcal mol−1, respectively, indicating that TDAB showed a stronger predicted binding interaction based on molecular docking simulations. However, the docking score of TDB-17 still indicates potential interaction with the receptor binding site, with relatively similar involvement of several key residues compared to TDAB. It should be noted that molecular docking provides an initial estimation of ligand binding orientation and interaction under relatively simplified and static conditions. Therefore, the docking results may not fully reflect the dynamic stability of the ligand–receptor complex under physiological environments. Differences in functional groups, steric effects, conformational flexibility, and solvent contributions may also influence the interaction profile observed during subsequent molecular dynamics simulations and MM-PBSA analysis.

Molecular dynamics simulation

Visualization of the MD results

A 200 ns MD simulation was performed to evaluate the dynamic stability of the ligand-ER complex under physiological conditions. The trajectory analysis (Fig. 3) showed that throughout the simulation, both TDB-17 and TDAB remained within the receptor binding pocket during the simulation period. Unlike molecular docking, molecular dynamics simulations incorporate conformational flexibility and time-dependent interactions between the ligand, receptor, and solvent environment, thereby providing additional information regarding the dynamic behavior of the complex. Therefore, MD simulations and subsequent MM-PBSA calculations were used as complementary approaches to further evaluate ligand-receptor interactions beyond the initial docking predictions.

Compounds TDB-17 (orange) and TDAB (dark blue) consistently showed the highest stability with RMSD values <2 Å consistently until the end of the simulation, reflecting a geometric fit and stable noncovalent interactions with key residues in the estrogen receptor active site. Flexibility of residues and proteins

RMSF analysis during MD simulations showed that most estrogen receptor residues exhibited low fluctuations (<2 Å), indicating that the protein backbone structure throughout the simulation, with higher fluctuations confined to the flexible loop and terminal regions (Fig. 5). In the complex with TDB-17 (orange) and TDAB (dark blue) ligands, the residues around the binding pocket exhibit low and stable RMSF values, consistent with the RMSD results that indicate stability after the initial adaptation phase. The agreement between the RMSD and RMSF profiles indicates that both ligands can maintain stable interactions without disrupting the dynamics of key residues in the estrogen receptor's active site, thus forming a structurally and dynamically stable complex.23

RMSF of estrogen receptor (3ERT) residues during the MD simulations.

Bond energy during the simulation

Binding free energy calculations were performed after MD simulations using the MM-PBSA method to evaluate the strength of ligand-receptor interactions. TDB-17 and TDAB showed negative ΔGbinding values, indicating energetically favorable binding, with the main contributions coming from van der Waals and electrostatic interactions, while polar solvation energy was unfavorable due to the desolvation process in the predominantly hydrophobic estrogen receptor binding pocket. TDB-17 showed a calculated ΔGbinding value of −22.7 kcal mol−1, whereas the reference compound TDAB showed a value of −7.4 kcal mol−1, as presented in Table 1. Although molecular docking predicted a stronger docking score for TDAB, the MM-PBSA analysis suggested a more favorable binding energy profile for TDB-17 during dynamic simulation. This discrepancy may arise from methodological differences between molecular docking and MM-PBSA approaches, since docking calculations primarily evaluate ligand binding under relatively simplified and static conditions, while MM-PBSA incorporates conformational flexibility, dynamic interactions, and solvent contributions obtained from molecular dynamics trajectories. Therefore, the MM-PBSA results should be interpreted as complementary computational information for evaluating ligand–receptor interactions rather than as definitive evidence of biological binding affinity.

In silico ADMET analysis

Based on pkCSM predictions (Table 2), TDB-17 has a high molecular weight (525.5 Da) with a log P value of −0.5. The TDB-17 ligand exhibits a similar number of H-bond donors/acceptors as TDAB, as well as low CaCO2 permeability and intestinal absorption, which are not crucial considering the intravenous route of administration. TDB-17 exhibits low blood–brain barrier (BBB) and central nervous system (CNS) permeability, which makes it suitable for use in non-cerebral radiopharmaceuticals. It is predicted not to interact with CYP2D6 and CYP3A4, suggesting a low potential for cytochrome-mediated metabolic interactions and adverse pharmacokinetic effects. Although the TDB-17 ligand is non-mutagenic (AMES negative), its potential for hepatotoxicity requires further experimental verification through comprehensive in vitro and in vivo toxicity evaluations.

DB-17 synthesis and characterization

DB-17 was synthesized by forming an amide bond between the amine group of 2-aminobenzothiazole and the carboxylate group of CaNa3-DTPA using EDCl and HOBt with DIPEA as a base at 50 °C under a nitrogen atmosphere to increase reaction efficiency and prevent side reactions (as shown in Fig. 6). This conjugation combines benzothiazole as a pharmacophore with DTPA as a chelator to achieve a balance between target affinity and radionuclide binding stability. The product was then separated by triplicate liquid–liquid extraction with aquabidest and ethyl acetate, followed by washing with aquabidest and brine to remove residual inorganic reagents, utilizing the amphiphilic nature of DB-17 resulting from the benzothiazole–DTPA conjugation. The organic phase was dried using anhydrous Na2SO4, filtered, and evaporated to obtain DB-17 as a pale yellow solid.24,25

Conjugation reaction of 2-aminobenzothiazole with CaNa3-DTPA.

Further purification of the DB-17 compound was performed using silica gel column chromatography with a dichloromethane : methanol (100–80%) gradient mobile phase, resulting in 14 fractions to accommodate the compound's increased polarity due to conjugation with DTPA. The selected fractions were then characterized using HPLC, HR-MS, 1H and 13C NMR. HPLC analysis (Fig. 7) showed a shift in the retention time from 9.0 min for 2-aminobenzothiazole to 8.2 min for 2-aminobenzothiazole and DB-17, indicating an increase in the polarity of the compound after conjugation.26 HR-MS detected DB-17 at an experimental m/z value of 218.01068, which closely matches the theoretical value of the [M + H + 2Na]3+ species, indicating a typical DTPA-based compound ionization pattern with a doubly charged carboxylic group (Fig. S1).

HPLC chromatogram of 2-aminobenzothiazole and DTPA-benzothiazole-17.

1H NMR characterization confirmed the formation of an amide bond between 2-aminobenzothiazole and DTPA, indicated by the appearance of a singlet amide proton signal at δ 8.57 ppm, which was not present in the starting compound (Fig. S2). 13C NMR spectrum also confirmed formation of DB-17 (Fig. S3).

The aromatic proton signal of benzothiazole was observed in the δ range of 7.08–7.60 ppm with a consistent coupling pattern, indicating that the aromatic system remained stable after conjugation. Meanwhile, the aliphatic proton signal of the DTPA skeleton appeared at δ 2.42–3.50 ppm with characteristic ethylenenene coupling, indicating the flexibility of the DTPA chain. The presence of benzothiazole, DTPA, and new amide proton signals in one spectrum indicated the successful synthesis and structure purification of DB-17.27

Radiolabeling and TDB-17 characterization

Radiolabeling the DB-17 ligand with the radioisotope 99mTc has produced a TDB-17 complex with high radiochemical purity (96.2%) (as shown in Fig. 8). The 99mTc radioisotope was obtained from a non-fissionable 99Mo/99mTc generator based on mesoporous gamma-alumina developed at BRIN-Serpong.28 Radio-HPLC analysis showed a radioactivity peak at a retention time of 1.4 min that correlated precisely with the UV peak at the same time, confirming the specific binding of 99mTc to the DB-17 ligand (Fig. 9). Compared with nonradioactive DB-17, which eluted at 8.2 min, the TDB-17 complex showed a significant shift in retention time toward the earlier, indicating an increase in polarity due to metal–ligand coordination and charge redistribution. These results confirm the adequate chelating ability of DB-17 toward 99mTc. The coordination of 99mTc with the DTPA moiety is proposed to involve both amine nitrogen atoms and carboxylate oxygen atoms, as commonly reported for DTPA-based technetium complexes.

Lipophilicity testing

Lipophilicity is a physicochemical parameter that influences radiopharmaceutical distribution, membrane penetration, nonspecific binding, and elimination. Therefore, measuring the log P value is important for predicting in vivo behavior.29 TDB-17 showed an experimental log P value of −1.5, lower than the in silico prediction (−0.5), indicating increased polarity due to 99mTc complexation and the limitations of computational approaches in representing the effects of radiometal coordination and the solution environment.30 This low log P value reflects the dominant hydrophilic nature of the adipose tissue, which potentially reduces nonspecific binding and increases the target-to-background ratio during imaging. The hydrophilic character is more dominant due to the contribution of polar groups and 99mTc coordination, indicating that ligand structural modification can alter lipophilicity without compromising desirable properties for diagnostic imaging applications.31

In vitro cellular uptake assay

A cellular uptake assay was conducted to evaluate the ability of TDB-17 radiopharmaceutical to interact with and accumulate in breast cancer cells, as cellular internalization is an important preliminary parameter for assessing the cellular interaction and intercellular retention behavior of radioligands, which may provide initial information regarding their biological characteristics.32

The results demonstrated a significant time-dependent increase in TDB-17 uptake, increasing from 26.0%/106 cells at 15 min to 42.3%/106 cells at 60 min and reaching 78.4%/106 cells at 120 min (p < 0.001), while Na99mTcO4 showed very low and relatively constant uptake (approximately 0.3%/106 cells) (Fig. 10). The increasing uptake profile suggests progressive cellular association and retention of TDB-17 in MCF-7 cells under the present experimental conditions. However, additional studies such as competitive binding assays and evaluation using estrogen receptor-negative cell lines are required to further confirm receptor specificity.32,33

Internalization profile of radiopharmaceutical TDB-17 in MCF-7 cells at various times of incubation.

Biodistribution studies

Biodistribution profile of TDB-17 in female Balb/c mice at 30 min and 1, 3, and 18 h after injection showed the pharmacokinetic behavior and elimination pathway of the TDB-17 (Fig. 11). In the early phase (30–60 min), the highest accumulation of radioactivity was observed in the liver, which increased further and reached a peak at 3 h, suggesting predominant hepatobiliary involvement in the clearance process. This pattern is consistent with reports of estrogenic ligand-based 99mTc radiopharmaceuticals that show initial accumulation in the liver before gradual clearance.34

TDB-17 biodistribution profile in normal female Balb/c mice.

Relatively elevated uptake was also observed in the spleen and lungs during the initial phase, which gradually decreased over time. This distribution pattern may reflect early reticuloendothelial system (RES) involvement and/or other nonspecific physiological interactions. In contrast, accumulation in the kidneys and gastrointestinal tract was relatively low to moderate, suggesting that renal and gastrointestinal excretion were not the predominant elimination pathways under the present experimental conditions. In addition, low gastric uptake may indicate limited release of free pertechnetate in vivo.

The gradual decrease in blood radioactivity over time indicates systemic clearance of the radiocomplex from circulation. Since the present biodistribution study was conducted only in normal mice, these findings should be considered as preliminary information regarding the in vivo behavior and pharmacokinetic profile of TDB-17 rather than definitive evidence of tumor-targeting capability.35

In vivo imaging using a gamma camera

In vivo imaging was performed using a mobile solo gamma camera to visualize the distribution and dynamics of the radiocomplex TDB-17 after intravenous administration in female Balb/c mice, to complement the quantitative biodistribution data and provide preliminary information regarding the in vivo distribution behavior of the compound under the present experimental conditions. In the early phase (15 min post-injection), the radioactivity signal was predominantly observed in the hepatic area with contributions from the lungs and spleen, reflecting the initial distribution through the systemic circulation and possible involvement of reticuloendothelial organs.36 At 60 min, the liver signal intensity began to decrease, and distribution became more defined, indicating redistribution and the beginning of elimination, consistent with the decreased uptake observed in several organs in the biodistribution study.37

At later time points (180 min), the signal intensity decreased significantly with a lower background and a more homogeneous distribution, suggesting progressive systemic clearance of the radiotracer from circulation and tissues. At 18 h post-injection, the radioactivity approached background levels, indicating substantial elimination of the radiocomplex over time. However, since the imaging study was conducted only in normal mice, the present findings should be interpreted as preliminary observations regarding the pharmacokinetic and distribution profile of TDB-17 rather than definitive evidence of tumor-targeting capability, in vivo stability, or imaging performance. Further studies, including serum stability evaluation and tumor-bearing animal models, are required to comprehensively assess the biological performance of the radiotracer (Fig. 12).38

Conclusions

Integrated in silico studies, synthesis, radiolabelling, and preliminary biological evaluations showed the feasibility of developing 99mTc-DTPA-benzothiazole as a radiolabeled benzothiazole derivative for breast cancer imaging research. Molecular docking and molecular dynamics (MD) simulations suggested potential interactions between the compound and the estrogen receptor through hydrogen bonding and hydrophobic interactions. The compound was successfully synthesized and radiolabelled with high radiochemical purity and showed cellular uptake in MCF-7 cells. Furthermore, preliminary biodistribution and imaging studies in normal mice provided initial insights into the in vivo behavior and pharmacokinetic profile of the 99mTc-DTPA-benzothiazole. Nevertheless, further studies including receptor specificity assays, serum stability evaluation, and tumor-bearing animal models are necessary to further evaluate its suitability for breast cancer imaging applications.
