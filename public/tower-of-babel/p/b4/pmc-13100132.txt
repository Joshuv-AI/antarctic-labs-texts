CryoEM structure of mGlu6 captures receptor activation prior to G protein coupling
Authors: Seo Young Lee, Chu-Ting Chang, Yaejin Yun, Jeong Seok Ji, Kirill A Martemyanov, Hyung Ho Lee
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractThe metabotropic glutamate receptor 6 (mGlu6) is essential for synaptic communication of rod photoreceptors, and mutations in mGlu6 lead to a blinding disorder. However, its structural organization remains unknown. Here, we present the structure of agonist-bound mGlu6, revealing an asymmetric dimer arrangement in the absence of a G protein. This indicates that agonist binding alone can induce the homodimeric receptor asymmetry in metabotropic glutamate receptors and structurally prime mGlu6 for activation by pre-organizing the transmembrane domain dimer interface for G protein binding. The structure also identifies noncanonical interactions between the cysteine-rich domain and extracellular loop 2, forming a unique interface that likely stabilizes the activation state. Mutational analyses of this interface reveal its role in maintaining rapid Gαo activation and surface targeting. The structure also permits mechanistic investigation of congenital stationary night blindness and reveals diverse effects of pathogenic mutations on surface trafficking, Gαo coupling, and activation dynamics, including unexpected gain-of-function. These results provide critical insight into the intermediate asymmetric structure of mGlu6 and offer a molecular framework for understanding the pathogenesis of inherited retinal disorders.Subject terms: Cryoelectron microscopy, Cryoelectron microscopy, G protein-coupled receptors

Introduction

Vision is an essential sensory function that allows organisms to detect, interpret, and respond to their environment, shaping both perception and behavior. In the mammalian retina, visual processing begins through distinct vertical pathways established at the synapse between photoreceptors and bipolar cells (BCs)1. In the dark, photoreceptors continuously release glutamate from their synaptic terminals. However, exposure to light causes photoreceptors to hyperpolarize, leading to a reduction in glutamate release2. This reduction is sensed by the postsynaptic partners of photoreceptors, the two types of bipolar cells3,4. The ON-bipolar cells (ON-BCs) depolarize when light intensity increases, while OFF-bipolar cells hyperpolarize3. While cone photoreceptors signal to both ON- and OFF-BCs, rods transduce their signals exclusively to ON-BCs. Thus, the synaptic dysfunction in ON-BCs leads to an inability to see in dim light, a genetically diverse group of retinal disorders known as congenital stationary night blindness (CSNB).

A key component of synaptic transduction machinery in ON-BCs is the metabotropic glutamate receptor (mGlu6), a principal neurotransmitter receptor which belongs to class C of G protein-coupled receptors (GPCRs). In darkness, glutamate released by photoreceptors binds to mGlu6, leading it to activate the G protein Gαo. Activated Gαo then inhibits TRPM15–7, an effector channel that mediates depolarizing response of ON-BCs. The rapid inactivation of Gαo requires a GTPase-activating protein, known as a regulator of G protein signaling, which interacts with and is regulated by another class C GPCR, GPR1798–10. However, the cascade is ultimately controlled by changes in mGlu6 signaling upon light-driven fluctuation of synaptic glutamate concentration. The molecular architecture of mGlu6 remains unknown, limiting our understanding of visual signal transduction at the synapse between photoreceptors and ON-BCs.

mGlu6 belongs to the group III metabotropic glutamate receptor (mGluR) family, which also includes mGlu4, mGlu7, and mGlu8, among the eight known mGluRs (mGlu1 to mGlu8). Notably, mGlu6 is the only mGluR known to mediate direct synaptic transmission in the nervous system11. Like other mGluRs, mGlu6 contains a large extracellular domain (ECD) that harbors the orthosteric ligand-binding site12,13. The ECD is predicted to contain a ligand-binding Venus flytrap (VFT) domain and a cysteine-rich domain (CRD). Canonically, in mGluRs, the VFT is linked to the transmembrane domain (TMD) via the CRD, which acts as a hinge, transmitting conformational rearrangements induced by glutamate binding to the VFT, ultimately leading to TMD rearrangement. A key hallmark of class C GPCR activation is the reorientation of TM6 at the dimer interface14–29. However, in the absence of structural information, the precise mechanism by which glutamate binding to the VFT of mGlu6 induces conformational changes in the TMD to facilitate subsequent Gαo activation remains unclear.

Agonist binding to GPCRs triggers structural rearrangements that promote subsequent G protein coupling17,24,30–32. A hallmark of class C GPCR activation is the emergence of asymmetry between the two protomers, typically allowing G protein binding to only one. However, whether this asymmetry arises prior to or only upon G protein engagement remains unresolved. While previous studies have shown that positive allosteric modulators (PAMs) can induce asymmetry in mGluRs before G protein binding—thereby favoring coupling to the PAM-bound protomer33—it is unclear whether such asymmetry can arise intrinsically in the absence of external modulators. This uncertainty is primarily due to the lack of structural snapshots of intermediate states preceding G protein association.

Here, we present the cryo-EM structure of human mGlu6 bound to L-serine O-phosphate (L-SOP), a potent agonist of group III mGluRs. Unexpectedly, the structure reveals distinct features not observed in other homodimeric mGluRs in the absence of G protein binding, including an asymmetric domain arrangement that appears to structurally prime the receptor for G protein coupling, and noncanonical interactions between the CRD and extracellular loop 2 (ECL2). The mGlu6 structure enabled structure-function analysis of CSNB-associated mutations in the receptor providing essential insights into the pathogenic mechanisms of CSNB.

Results

Structure determination of human mGlu6 homodimer bound to L-SOP

To better understand the activation mechanism of mGlu6, we purified the full-length human receptor in the presence of L-SOP, a selective group III mGluR agonist with similar efficacy to glutamate but with higher affinity34,35. Using cryo-EM, we resolved the structure of the human mGlu6 homodimer bound to L-SOP at an overall resolution of 3.2 Å in C1 symmetry (Fig. 1, Supplementary Figs. 1, 2, and Supplementary Table 1). The mGlu6 protomer comprises three key structural domains: the VFT, the CRD, and the TMD (Fig. 1a). Reconstruction of the L-SOP-bound mGlu6 homodimer was performed without imposing symmetry (C1), which revealed substantial protomer bending that gives rise to a clear asymmetry between the two subunits. This observation prompted us to perform further particle classification without alignment using the C1 reconstruction (Supplementary Fig. 1f), which yielded three distinct classes that primarily differed in the degree of protomer bending and were refined to resolutions of 3.39 Å, 3.27 Å, and 3.51 Å, respectively (Supplementary Figs. 1f and 2c–e). Unless otherwise noted, the consensus structure prior to classification is referred to throughout the text.

To improve the local resolution of the TMD, we performed local reconstruction, which yielded an improved TMD map at a resolution of 3.82 Å (Supplementary Figs. 1f and 2b). As a result, the overall densities of the VFT domain, CRD, and TMD, as well as L-SOP, were well-resolved. These domains assemble into a homodimer, with the entire complex measuring approximately 95 Å × 70 Å × 175 Å (Fig. 1a). Notably, we identified potential glycosylated asparagine residues (N296, N451, and N567) with extra densities extending from their side chains (Supplementary Fig. 1g, h).

Cryo-EM structure of L-SOP-bound mGlu6

The mGlu6 dimer adopts a dimerization mode characterized by an active conformation at the TM6 interface (Fig. 1a) and represents key structural features of active mGluRs. First, the VFT domain of mGlu6 adopts an ‘Acc’ (active closed–closed) conformation, a typical conformation of mGluRs upon agonist binding (Fig. 1b)14–16,18,20,32,33,36–39. The inter-bottom lobe 2 (LB2) distances are 11.7 Å when measured from the Cα position of E227 and 21.7 Å from Q235, which is consistent with measurements reported for active mGlu333 and mGlu514 as agonists typically reduce the inter-lobe distance at LB2 of the ligand-binding domain (Supplementary Fig. 3a). Additionally, the Acc conformation is accompanied by a reorientation of the VFT dimer interface, resulting in a reduced angle of B-C helix14,36,38. In the L-SOP-bound mGlu6 structure, this angle is approximately 60°, reduced from the 125° observed in the inactive mGlu7 of group III and consistently decreased relative to other inactive mGluRs (Fig. 1b and Supplementary Fig. 3b)16. Moreover, an electrostatic interaction is observed between D102 (helix B) and R186 (D/E loop) from the opposing chain in the L-SOP-bound mGlu6 structure (Fig. 1b). This interaction is absent in the inactive mGlu7 structure (Fig. 1b), suggesting its potential role in stabilizing the active conformation. A previous single-molecule FRET study of mGlu2 demonstrated that the corresponding interaction between D95 and R177 (structurally equivalent to D102 and R186 in mGlu6) contributes to stabilizing the Acc conformation of the VFT domain40. This finding indicates a conserved role of this electrostatic interaction in supporting receptor activation. To assess the functional significance of the R186–D102 interaction, we introduced an R186A mutation, disrupting the salt bridge, and examined its effect on L-glutamate and L-AP4 potency. The mutation significantly increased the half-maximal effective concentration (EC50) for L-glutamate- or L-AP4-induced G protein activation, indicating reduced potency (Fig. 1c, d and Supplementary Fig. 4c, d). Additionally, a small but significant increase in maximal efficacy (Emax) was observed, while cell surface expression levels remained unchanged (Fig. 1d and Supplementary Fig. 4a, b). These data suggest that the R186–D102 interaction is crucial for both agonist potency and efficacy.

Second, the mGlu6 structure adopts a compact CRD conformation upon activation. The distance measured from the Cα of C555 is 24 Å, aligning with the 20–25 Å range for active mGlu2, mGlu3, and mGlu533. Additionally, the distance for V534 (corresponding to E527 in mGlu5), a known activation marker in mGlu5, is reduced to approximately 10 Å, consistent with features of the active conformation (Fig. 1e)14. Third, in the TMD, the dimer interface is formed by the TM6 helices of the two protomers, with a distance of 5 Å at I802 (Fig. 1e). This distance is a well-established marker of activation in mGluRs14–20,32,38,39 (Supplementary Fig. 3c, d). The TMD dimer interface is predominantly stabilized by hydrophobic interactions (Fig. 1e). Fourth, the ionic locks—a conserved electrostatic interaction between residues K6713.50 and E7816.35 of TM3 and TM6, along with the less well-conserved residues R6743.53 and E777 of TM3 and ICL3—are disrupted in the L-SOP-bound mGlu6 structure due to TMD rearrangements, as observed in other active mGluRs (Supplementary Fig. 3e)14,16,24,41–43.

Asymmetric domain architecture of L-SOP-bound mGlu6

To investigate the structural characteristics arising from mGlu6 homodimer asymmetry, chain A and chain B were superimposed based on their TMD alignment (Fig. 2a). In the LB1 region of the VFT, the distance between helix C and helix C’ (where the prime denotes the corresponding segment from the adjacent protomer) at residue L166 was approximately 12 Å. In the LB2 region, the angle between helix F and helix F’ differed by 9.8°, further highlighting the asymmetry between chain A and chain B (Fig. 2b). A comparison of helix F-F’ angles in other mGluR structures with C1 symmetry shows similar values: mGlu2 (PDB: 7MTR) exhibits a twist of 10.9°, while mGlu5 (PDB: 8TAO) has a twist of 11.6°, aligning closely with the asymmetry observed in mGlu6 (Supplementary Fig. 5a).

For a more detailed comparative analysis of asymmetry, the hinge region between the VFT and CRD was designated as hinge 1 (S516), while the hinge region between the TMD and CRD was designated as hinge 2 (L579) (Fig. 2a). When chains A and B of L-SOP-bound mGlu6 were compared by superimposing either LB2 or the TMD, the relative orientations of the CRD varied significantly (Fig. 2c, d). Based on the LB2 alignment, the CRD orientation around hinge 1 revealed that chain A exhibits a slightly larger angle than chain B (Fig. 2c, e). When aligned by the TMD, the CRD rotation around hinge 2 showed that chain A undergoes a slightly greater rotation than chain B (Fig. 2d, f). These results suggest that mGlu6 displayed asymmetry at these hinges, which may underlie the overall asymmetric organization of its domains.

We speculated that the bending observed in mGlu6 reflects dynamic flexibility rather than a fixed conformation. To further investigate the structural heterogeneity of mGlu6, we performed 3D variability analysis44, which revealed a global bending motion of the mGlu6 structure (Supplementary Movie 1). Consistently, particle classification without alignment using the C1 reconstruction (Supplementary Fig. 1f) resulted in three classes (referred to as classes 1–3), each characterized by differing bending angles—smaller, comparable, or larger relative to the original consensus map prior to classification (Fig. 2g). To quantify this variation, we aligned structures by their TMDs and measured the distance between the Cα atoms of L166 in helix C from each protomer, capturing the extent of inter-subunit bending (Fig. 2h). Class 1 displayed minimal bending and a nearly symmetric conformation, including at the TMD interface (Supplementary Fig. 5b–d). Class 2 showed a 9.5 Å increase in bending compared to class 1, closely matching the configuration of the consensus structure. Class 3 exhibited the most substantial bending, with an additional 5.9 Å increase in L166 separation compared to class 2, although the degree of asymmetry at the TM6–TM6 interface remained similar (Fig. 2h). Together, these results suggest that agonist-bound mGlu6 adopts a range of conformational states, accompanied by varying degrees of inter-protomer asymmetry.

Interestingly, the C1 symmetry structure of L-SOP-bound mGlu6 exhibits an asymmetric TM6–TM6 interface even in the absence of a PAM or G proteins. Chain B adopts a more overall bent conformation compared to chain A, with TM6 shifting higher toward the VFT (Fig. 2i). In the upward-shifted chain B protomer, Y790 points toward the opposing protomer, similar to the conformation observed in the PAM-bound state of mGlu5 (Fig. 2i)38. This structural shift in TM6 and the associated domain orientation resemble conformations seen in other mGluR protomers bound to G proteins (Supplementary Fig. 5e, f). These findings suggest that, despite the symmetrical binding of L-SOP to both subunits, mGlu6 adopts an asymmetric conformation, potentially priming chain B for G protein binding in subsequent steps (Fig. 2j). Because full agonists are known to engage nearly identical activation mechanisms and functional responses on mGluR35,45, we speculate that this observed asymmetry is a general feature of mGlu6 activation rather than a conformation specific to L-SOP, an idea that remains to be determined.

L-SOP binding mode at the glutamate binding site

We next examined the L-SOP binding site, where activation of mGlu6 is initiated upon agonist binding (Fig. 3). L-SOP acts as a potent agonist for group III mGluRs, including mGlu635,46,47, and is known to reduce cyclic AMP levels through mGluR-mediated inhibition of adenylyl cyclase activity48. In our structure, L-SOP was bound to the canonical orthosteric site within the VFT domain (Fig. 3a), consistent with the L-AP4 binding site in mGlu8 (PDB: 6BT5)49 and the L-SOP binding site in mGlu4 (PDB: 8JD6)32,49 (Fig. 3c). However, the specific interactions with surrounding residues were distinct in mGlu6.

In mGlu6, the phosphate group of L-SOP interacts with Q64 and R68 in the helix A region, along with nearby residues K312 and K400 (Fig. 3a). Among these, Q64—previously suggested to play a role in the selectivity of L-SOP for group III mGluRs35—forms a hydrogen bond with the phosphate group of L-SOP (Fig. 3a, b). T177, which is also known to be involved in glutamate binding, interacts with the serine group of L-SOP (Fig. 3a). The T177M mutation has been associated with CSNB50. Structurally, this mutation likely disrupts ligand binding due to alterations in side chain length and charge. Additionally, R192, another CSNB-associated mutation site, does not directly interact with L-SOP but stabilizes the helix C/D loop involved in L-SOP binding through interactions with the loop’s backbone Cα and van der Waals forces (Fig. 3a). Mutations such as R192Q may disrupt these stabilizing interactions, compromising the loop’s structural integrity and hindering ligand binding. The residues corresponding to R68, T177, D307, and K400 are strictly conserved across all mGluR subtypes. Mutational analyses in other subtypes have shown that mutations at these positions markedly reduce or abolish L-AP4- or L-glutamate-induced binding and activation, indicating that they form part of the conserved orthosteric pocket. Overall, our cryo-EM structure of L-SOP-bound mGlu6 identifies key residues involved in L-SOP binding and provides structural insights into how CSNB-associated mutations near the glutamate binding site destabilize the receptor, leading to functional impairment.

Allosteric coupling mediated by noncanonical interactions between the CRD and ECL2

It is well established that structural coupling from the VFT to the CRD is largely influenced by CRD–ECL2 interactions14,16, ultimately driving rearrangements within the TMD. In this context, to better understand how ligand-induced conformational changes in the VFT are communicated to the 7TM domain via the CRD, we examined the lower portion of mGlu6 extending from the L-SOP binding site (Fig. 4). Upon L-SOP binding, significant conformational changes in the VFT bring the two CRDs closer together, forming an interface crucial for mediating structural transitions in mGluRs14,16,18,51. In the active mGlu2 structure (PDB: 7EPB), this CRD interface is stabilized by hydrophobic interactions16.

Notably, in the L-SOP-bound mGlu6 structure, the inter-CRD distance is even shorter than in active mGlu4 (PDB: 8JD6), another group III mGluR. Based on the distance between the Cα atoms of H539 residues located in β-sheet XV, the inter-CRD distance measures 9.2 Å in mGlu6, while it is 12.2 Å in mGlu4 (Supplementary Fig. 6a, b). This unique configuration is maintained by hydrophobic interactions involving V531 and V534, polar interactions between H539 and the backbone of M530 in the opposing protomer, and histidine-histidine contact pairs between H539 residues (Fig. 4a). These distinctive H539-H539’ and H539-M530’ interactions define a CRD interface in mGlu6 that differs from those observed in mGlu2 and mGlu5 (Fig. 4a and Supplementary Fig. 6c, d).

Notably, in the L-SOP-bound mGlu6 structure, we observed noncanonical CRD–ECL2 interactions. Compared to other mGluR groups, group III mGluRs feature a relatively longer ECL2, which establishes an additional interface along the CRD (Fig. 4c, d)16,17. Specifically, E736 in ECL2 forms salt bridges with R563 and R572 in the CRD, contributing to the unique structural configuration of mGlu6 (Fig. 4e). Additionally, R731 in ECL2 forms hydrogen bonds and a cation–π interaction with the backbone of G560 and the side chain of Y546 in the CRD (Fig. 4f). The lower CRD and ECL2 regions are further stabilized by hydrophobic interactions, which are highly conserved in group III mGluRs (Fig. 4f).

To investigate the structural significance of the CRD–ECL2 interaction, we examined the roles of R731 and E736 through functional analyses of mGlu6 mutants (R731A, E736A, and the double mutant R731A/E736A), assessing surface expression, Gαo coupling efficacy, and activation kinetics (Fig. 4g–j). Surface biotinylation studies revealed that the R731A and R731A/E736A mutants exhibited significantly reduced surface expression, while E736A showed levels comparable to wild-type (WT) (Fig. 4g and Supplementary Fig. 4a, b), indicating a key role for R731 in maintaining proper surface expression of mGlu6. We then assessed Gαo activation using a BRET-based kinetic assay, which detects L-glutamate-induced Gβγ dissociation from Gα in real time (Fig. 4h and Supplementary Fig. 4e, f). Despite their reduced surface expression, the R731A and R731A/E736A mutants generated maximal response amplitudes and pEC50 comparable to those of the WT receptor (Fig. 4i and Supplementary Fig. 4e, f). This indicates that the receptor concentrations in our BRET assay were saturating, supporting the conclusion that the observed slowness of activation (Fig. 4h, j) is unlikely to be a consequence of reduced receptor abundance.

Having excluded receptor abundance as a confounding factor, we next compared activation kinetics across mutants. Activation kinetics were notably slower in the R731A and R731A/E736A mutants, as indicated by a reduced activation rate (1/τ) (Fig. 4h, j), whereas the E736A mutant was unchanged. To confirm that this kinetic deficit was not simply a consequence of their reduced surface expression (Fig. 4g), we titrated the expression of WT mGlu6 by transfecting varying amounts of DNA (Supplementary Fig. 4g). This experiment revealed that the activation rate of WT receptors remained rapid and was independent of the surface expression level, even when its expression was reduced to levels comparable to the R731A mutants. This result validates that the R731 residue is essential for rapid Gαo activation. Together, these results demonstrate that R731, part of the unique ECL2 extension in group III mGluRs, is essential for efficient surface expression and rapid Gαo activation, reflecting the functional importance of the CRD–ECL2 interaction in mGlu6 signaling.

Functional characterization of CSNB-associated mGlu6 mutations

mGlu6 is essential for relaying signals from photoreceptors to ON-BCs, and mutations in its gene cause CSNB52–54. To assess the functional impact of CSNB-associated mutations, we analyzed all clinically reported mGlu6 variants identified in CSNB patients. For each mutant we measured total and surface expression, Gαo coupling efficacy, and Gαo activation kinetics (Fig. 5a–c). The majority of these mutants exhibited reduced surface expression—less than 50% of that observed in WT mGlu6. Among them, the W20_A24del mutant, located in the signal peptide region, showed nearly undetectable localization of the surface. In contrast, G150S and D183N mutants displayed only mildly reduced expression, while the V513M mutant maintained expression levels comparable to WT mGlu6 (Supplementary Fig. 7a, b).

We next evaluated Gαo activation using a BRET-based kinetic assay, which measures L-glutamate-induced Gβγ dissociation from Gα in real time (Fig. 5a and Supplementary Fig. 7c). To account for variations in surface expression that could influence response generation, we constructed a correlation curve by titrating WT mGlu6 cDNA. The relative surface expression levels of the CSNB mutants, quantified from surface biotinylation data, were then compared with their corresponding maximum response amplitudes. Mutants G51V, V513M, and E781K fell within or near the 95% confidence interval, suggesting normal G protein coupling efficacy, whereas most other mutants fell below the threshold, indicating significantly impaired signaling (Fig. 5c).

To further evaluate activation kinetics, we calculated the activation rate (1/τ). Interestingly, even among mutants with preserved coupling efficacy, a reduced activation rate was observed (Fig. 5b and Supplementary Fig. 7c, g). Although many of the CSNB mutants exhibited functional impairments, the V513M mutant retained normal surface expression, G protein coupling efficacy, and activation kinetics, suggesting that this mutation does not directly compromise mGlu6 signaling. The pathogenicity of this variant is therefore dubious, as evident from the literature55,56.

Notably, the R352C and I405T mutants displayed complex phenotypes. They exhibited clear gain-of-function behavior in maximal efficacy (Emax), with responses significantly above the WT confidence interval (Fig. 5c). However, this was not reflected in their potency values (pEC50) or activation kinetics which remained comparable to WT (for R352C) or decreased (for I405T) (Supplementary Fig. 7d–g). As R352 and I405 are located on the surface of the VFT domain (Fig. 5d, e), the structural basis for this gain-of-function remains unclear. Overall, these results show that most CSNB-associated mGlu6 mutants exhibit defects in surface expression, G protein coupling efficacy, or activation kinetics. While many act as loss-of-function mutations, as expected for disease-associated variants, the unexpected gain-of-function observed in R352C and I405T provides intriguing insights that warrant further investigation. Despite their enhanced G protein coupling capacity, the reduced surface expression of R352C and I405T likely limits their access to extracellular glutamate (Supplementary Fig. 7a, b). Together with their previously reported loss of ELFN1 binding and synaptic mislocalization at ON-BCs57, these combined defects likely ultimately lead to an overall loss-of-function phenotype from the physiological perspective.

Discussion

The ON-bipolar neurons serve as exclusive synaptic partners for mammalian rod photoreceptors that detect dim light. Furthermore, segregating visual inputs of cones into the parallel ON and OFF pathways enables contrast sensitivity. Being the principal neurotransmitter receptor at the synapses of the ON-bipolar neurons, mGlu6 is indispensable for rod mediated vision52,58. Given its critical role, disruptions in mGlu6 function are closely linked to the pathophysiology of CSNB and other visual impairments52,59. Therefore, elucidating the molecular mechanisms of mGlu6 activation and its signaling dynamics remains a crucial focus for understanding retinal diseases and developing therapeutic interventions. In this study, we unveil the cryo-EM structure of full length mGlu6 bound to L-SOP (Fig. 1 and Supplementary Fig. 9). Our findings reveal distinct structural features not observed in other mGluRs, including an asymmetric domain arrangement (Fig. 2) and noncanonical CRD–ECL2 interactions that further stabilize the active conformation (Fig. 4). Guided by structural insights we conducted a comprehensive structure-function study exploring mechanisms of all pathogenic mutations in mGlu6 reported to date that provided rich insights into CSNB pathology, deepening our understanding of how defects in visual signal transmission cause disease.

One of the notable observations is the asymmetry of the mGlu6 homodimer. The agonist L-SOP binds to the orthosteric site of each protomer in the mGlu6 homodimer, inducing the canonical active conformation observed in other mGluRs. However, despite the symmetric binding of L-SOP, mGlu6 adopts an asymmetric structure, with the two subunits exhibiting distinct conformations. These conformational differences can be characterized based on two key criteria. First, the hinge region connecting the VFT, CRD, and TMD domains shows distinct structural variations. Specifically, chain B adopts a more bent conformation compared to chain A (Fig. 2a, b). This observation is further supported by 3D variability analysis and 3D classification, which reveal a range of bending angles consistent with asymmetric structural dynamics. Second, the TM6 interface reveals that TM6 in chain B is shifted further upward toward the extracellular side relative to TM6 in chain A (Fig. 2i and Supplementary Fig. 5f).

Previous studies have shown that most mGluRs maintain a symmetric conformation when bound to ligands symmetrically, with asymmetry arising only upon asymmetric binding of PAMs or G proteins16,18,32,60. While asymmetry in homodimers has been observed in G protein-bound mGluRs, no asymmetric homodimer structure has been reported for an mGluR in the absence of G protein or PAM binding. This raises the question of whether mGluR asymmetry can be induced solely by agonist binding, or if G protein interaction is required. In this context, our finding that L-SOP-bound mGlu6 adopts an asymmetric dimer suggests that dimer asymmetry can arise independently of G protein binding, potentially priming the receptor for subsequent G protein association (Fig. 2j). This enhanced efficiency is particularly critical for the specific physiological demands of mGlu6, which must continuously signal at the resting state only to be transiently deactivated when photoreceptors detect light and hyperpolarize.

Notably, the structural differences between chain A and chain B in mGlu6 resemble the distinctions observed between the G protein-bound and unbound subunits in mGlu2 and mGlu417. This led us to hypothesize that G protein coupling in mGlu6 preferentially occurs via chain B, which structurally resembles the G protein-binding subunits in mGlu2 and mGlu4. Given that only one trimeric G protein typically binds per homodimer in mGluRs, chain B may serve as the primary G protein binding subunit. In this context, the L-SOP-induced asymmetric domain rearrangement in mGlu6 may represent an intermediate structural state that structurally primes mGlu6 for G protein binding by pre-organizing the TMD dimer interface—a possibility that warrants further investigation. This feature together with the unique CRD–ECL2 contacts present in mGlu6 likely serve to optimize the efficiency of G protein activation beyond the level observed in other members of the mGluR family. This might reflect the evolutionary adaptation of mGlu6 to the pressure of maintaining high volume of tonic G protein activation required to keep the ON-bipolar neurons silent. Reversing this process in a highly concerted fashion upon rapid reduction in synaptic glutamate concentrations during the light response would then be required for the TRPM1 channel disinhibition and generation of the light response.

The analysis of the CSNB mutations performed in this study revealed a multitude of mechanisms by which structural alterations affect the function of mGlu6. The mutations compromise ligand binding capacity or the conformational transitions across multiple regions in mGlu6 without particular prevalent ‘hot-spot’ mechanism or location. In the vast majority of cases this leads to a clearly detectable loss in the ability of mGlu6 to activate its G protein substrate, Gαo. On a cell biological level, this is further compounded by the associated loss in the ability of the mGlu6 to traffic to the cell surface as well as overall reduction in the expression level. This reflects a complex relationship between structural architecture of mGlu6 to serve as an efficient GPCR, while fine tuning its properties to enable correct localization as it is required to be targeted to synapses of ON-bipolar neurons for proper function. Interestingly, when normalized for the deficits in trafficking, we found that two mutations actually increase the functional activity of mGlu6. At the moment, we have no rational explanation for the mechanisms behind this functional augmentation. However, despite this slight elevation in activity, the overall net effect of the mutations is likely the loss of function due to mistargeting deficits that these variants have. Together, these results provide a comprehensive structure-function framework for understanding this pivotal sensory receptor.

Our L-SOP-bound mGlu6 structure provides the direct physical rationale for many of these functional defects. The CSNB-associated mutation sites are distributed across the VFT domain, CRD, and TMD of mGlu6 (Fig. 5d and Supplementary Figs. 7h, i and 8). For instance, G150S resides in the VFT domain within a tightly packed hydrophobic environment composed of L44, V148, A151, V156, and V160. These residues, located in the upper lobe, contribute to maintaining its structural integrity. Substituting glycine with serine at position 150 likely disrupts these hydrophobic interactions, destabilizing the upper lobe and impairing proper VFT activation (Fig. 5f). G464R, also in the VFT domain, is expected to compromise structural stability due to steric clashes with nearby residues, including R446, resulting from the larger arginine side chain (Fig. 5f).

In the upper VFT lobe, C423 forms a disulfide bond with C430, which stabilizes the interaction between helix L and the L/M loop (Fig. 5f). The C423R mutation likely disrupts this disulfide bridge, weakening the domain’s structural cohesion. Similarly, the C522Y mutation in the CRD disrupts a critical disulfide bond with C540, and the bulkier tyrosine side chain introduces steric clashes with adjacent residues such as W538, further compromising domain stability. Both C423R and C522Y have been reported to impair ER-to-surface trafficking of mGlu6, possibly due to misfolding and reduced structural stability57,61.

In the TMD, R653, located in TM3, forms stabilizing interactions with M641 and A643. The R653C mutation likely disrupts these electrostatic interactions, destabilizing the TMD. E781, a residue involved in forming an ionic lock in the inactive conformation, becomes disengaged upon L-SOP binding (Fig. 5f). The E781K mutation likely prevents proper ionic lock formation, hindering normal activation of the receptor. Together, our structural mapping provides crucial insights into how CSNB-associated mutations destabilize key domains, ultimately leading to the observed functional impairments.

Methods

Expression and purification of mGlu6

The human mGlu6 WT (residues 1–877) sequence was cloned into a pFastBac vector with EGFP and Twin-StrepII tags at the C-terminus, both of which were removable by 3 C protease cleavage (mGlu6 forward 5′-CGG ATC TCG GTC CGA AAC CGC CAC CAT GGC TCG GCC TCG GAG-3′, mGlu6 reverse 5′-GTA CCG TGG ACG CTC TAG ATT CGA ATT TGT GGG CCT CGG CAT CTT-3′). mGlu6 was expressed in Spodoptera frugiperda (Sf9) cells (ATCC) using the Bac-to-Bac baculovirus expression system. Sf9 cells were authenticated by the vendor. For expression, Sf9 cells at a density of 1.2 × 106 cells/ml were infected with baculovirus and cultured at 27 °C. Cells were harvested by centrifugation 48 h post-infection and stored at −80 °C.

For purification, thawed cells were resuspended in a buffer containing 20 mM HEPES-NaOH (pH 7.5), 200 mM NaCl, 20% glycerol, and 5 mM L-serine O-phosphate (L-SOP) with protease inhibitors (1 mM PMSF, 4.7 μM leupeptin, 2 μM pepstatin A, and 2 μM aprotinin). To solubilize the membrane, 1% (w/v) lauryl maltose neopentyl glycol (LMNG) and 0.1% (w/v) cholesteryl hemisuccinate (CHS) were added, and the lysate was incubated at 4 °C for 2 hours. Cell debris was removed by ultracentrifugation at 100,000×g for 1 h.

The supernatant was incubated with Strep-Tactin resin (IBA Lifesciences) at 4 °C for 3 hours. The resin was washed with buffer containing 20 mM HEPES-NaOH (pH 7.5), 200 mM NaCl, 10% glycerol, 5 mM L-SOP, 0.01 mg/ml brain lipid, 0.01% (w/v) LMNG, and 0.001% (w/v) CHS. The protein was eluted using buffer containing 100 mM HEPES-NaOH (pH 7.5), 200 mM NaCl, 10% glycerol, 5 mM L-SOP, 0.01 mg/ml brain lipid, 10 mM D-desthiobiotin, 0.01% (w/v) LMNG, and 0.001% (w/v) CHS.

The eluted protein was concentrated and incubated with 3 C protease for 1 h. The sample was then loaded onto a Superose 6 Increase 10/300 column equilibrated with buffer containing 20 mM HEPES-NaOH (pH 7.5), 200 mM NaCl, 1% glycerol, 5 mM L-SOP, 0.01 mg/ml brain lipid, 0.005% (w/v) LMNG, and 0.0005% (w/v) CHS. Peak fractions containing mGlu6 were pooled, concentrated to 1.0–1.5 mg/ml, flash-frozen, and stored at −80 °C for grid preparation.

Cryo-EM grid preparation and data acquisition

For grid preparation, 4 μl of protein sample was applied to glow-discharged Quantifoil 1.2/1.3 300-mesh Cu holey carbon grids (Electron Microscopy Sciences). The grids were blotted for 4.0 s at 4 °C under 100% humidity and plunge-frozen in liquid ethane using a Vitrobot Mark IV (FEI). The prepared grids were imaged at the Institute for Basic Science (IBS) on a 300 kV Krios G4 cryo-EM (Thermo Fisher Scientific, TFS, USA) equipped with a Gatan K3 BioQuantum detector using automated data acquisition. A total of 11,002 movies were collected at a pixel size of 0.848 Å per pixel, with a defocus range of −0.7 to −1.7 μm.

Data processing and 3D reconstruction

All data processing was performed using CryoSPARC v4.4.062. First, motion correction and CTF estimation were carried out using Patch Motion Correction and Patch CTF Estimation, followed by curate exposure to exclude micrographs with a CTF resolution worse than 5 Å.

Particles were initially selected using blob-based particle picking to generate templates for the subsequent template-based particle picking. This resulted in a total of 5,718,287 particles picked from 10,821 movies.

Particles were extracted with a box size of 432 pixels and then binned by 2 for more efficient initial processing. This binned particle stack was subjected to several rounds of reference-free 2D classification, and 433,734 high-quality particles were retained for further processing. An ab initio reconstruction was performed to generate an initial map, followed by heterogeneous refinement. The best class (244,570 particles) was re-extracted at full scale and subjected to homogeneous refinement. Non-uniform (NU) refinement without symmetry constraints resulted in a 3.20 Å resolution map, based on gold-standard Fourier shell correlation (GSFSC).

To assess conformational heterogeneity, 244,484 particles were re-classified in 3D classification without alignment. The initial five classes were regrouped into three classes depending on the degree of protomer bending. The regrouped particles were subjected to NU refinement, yielding three structures with resolutions of 3.39 Å, 3.27 Å, and 3.51 Å, respectively. To improve the TM domain map quality, 433,734 particles from ab initio reconstruction underwent homogeneous refinement, NU refinement, and local refinement without symmetry, producing a map with a resolution of 3.82 Å.

Model building and refinement

The initial model was generated using AlphaFold 3 on the AlphaFold Server (https://alphafoldserver.com/about)63 and fitted into the global density map in UCSF ChimeraX64. The model was manually adjusted using COOT65 and further refined with real-space refinement in Phenix66. To improve the model of the transmembrane domain (TMD), the TMD portion of the model was first refined into the 3.82 Å locally refined map. This improved TMD model was then combined with the extracellular domain model (built into the 3.20 Å consensus map). This final composite model was then subjected to global real-space refinement against the 3.20 Å consensus map to optimize the overall geometry and resolve clashes. All structural figures were generated using ChimeraX.

cDNA Plasmids

The mGlu6 construct (Cat# GRM6000000) was obtained from the Missouri S&T cDNA Resource Center. All mGlu6 mutants were generated using conventional site-directed mutagenesis methods. All constructs were confirmed by Eurofins Nanopore sequencing.

Cell culture and transfection

HEK293T/17 cells were cultured in Dulbecco’s Modified Eagle Medium (DMEM) supplemented with 10% fetal bovine serum (FBS), minimum essential medium non-essential amino acids (Life Technologies), 1 mM sodium pyruvate, penicillin (100 U/mL), and streptomycin (100 µg/mL) at 37 °C in a humidified incubator with 5% CO2. For transfection, cells were seeded in 3.5 cm dishes at a density of 1 × 106 cells/dish. After 4 hours, cells were transfected with the appropriate expression constructs using Metafectene Pro (Biontex Laboratories GmbH) according to the manufacturer’s instructions. Transfected cells were harvested and used for experiments approximately 36 hours post-transfection.

Cell surface biotinylation and Western blotting

mGlu6 surface proteins were labeled using the PierceTM Cell Surface Protein Biotinylation and Isolation Kit (Thermo Fisher Scientific, Cat# A44390) according to the manufacturer’s instructions. Approximately 2 × 107 cells were used per condition. Total protein lysates and biotinylated eluates were mixed with sample buffer containing β-mercaptoethanol and incubated at 42 °C for 10 min. SDS-PAGE and Western blotting were performed following blocking with 5% non-fat milk. Blots were incubated with anti-mGlu6 antibody (Invitrogen, Cat# PA5-28770) and anti-GAPDH (Millipore Sigma, Cat# MAB374) at 1:1000 dilution in 5% milk. HRP-conjugated secondary antibodies (Kindle Biosciences) were used at 1:2000 dilution in 5% milk. Images were acquired using a KwikQuant Imager and KwikQuant ECL Solutions (Kindle Biosciences). Band intensities were quantified using Fiji software.

BRET assays for G protein activation

Cells were transfected in 3.5 cm dishes with 0.1 µg of mGlu6, 0.25 µg each of GαoA, Venus 156–239-Gβ1, Venus 1–155-Gγ2, and masGRK3ct-Nluc-HA, using Metafectene Pro (3 µL/dish). At the time of transfection, 0.1% Matrigel (Corning, Cat# CB-40230A) was added to the culture medium. The total amount of cDNA was adjusted to 2 µg/dish with the empty vector pcDNA3.1(+). At 2 days post-transfection, cells were harvested, washed three times with PBS, and seeded into 96-well white flat-bottom microplates (Greiner Bio-One) in Tyrode’s solution (Thermo Fisher, Cat# AAJ67593AP) at a density of 5–10 × 104 cells/well. The NanoLuc substrate (Nano-Glo® Luciferase Assay Reagent; Promega) was diluted 1:250 in Tyrode’s solution and added to the wells. Glutamate (300 µM in Tyrode’s solution) was injected after obtaining a baseline value over 10 seconds, with BRET signal acquisition every 100 ms using a PHERAstar FSX microplate reader (BMG Labtech). All measurements were performed at room temperature. BRET was calculated as the ratio of light emitted at 535 nm (Venus-Gβ1γ2) to that at 475 nm (masGRK3ct-Nluc-HA). G protein activation was determined as the change in BRET ratio upon L-glutamate addition. The rate of activation (1/τ) was calculated using GraphPad Prism.

For dose–response measurements of L-glutamate and L-AP4, the baseline value was recorded for 5 seconds after NanoLuc addition. Ligand was then applied, and signals were collected for an additional 5 seconds. The response amplitude was determined as the mean signal during the 5-second period after ligand addition minus the baseline value.

Statistics

Data are presented as mean ± SEM. Comparisons were made using two-tailed, Student’s t-tests. Dose–response curves were fitted to a non-linear regression curve giving pEC50. Each statistical analysis included a minimum of three biological replicates. All statistical analyses were performed using GraphPad Prism version 10.4. Significant difference was expressed as *P < 0.05, **P < 0.01, ***P < 0.001, and ****P < 0.0001. Not significant (ns) was indicated where appropriate.
