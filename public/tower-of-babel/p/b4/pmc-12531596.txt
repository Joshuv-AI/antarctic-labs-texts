Innovative Se‐Flutamide Derivatives: Enhanced Activity Toward Androgen Receptor (AR)‐Dependent and ‐Independent Prostate Cancer Cells
Authors: Cristina Morán‐Serradilla, Carmen Sanmartín, Asif Raza, Ignacio Encío, Daniel Plano, Arun K. Sharma
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTSixteen novel selenoderivatives of flutamide, which is used for treating androgen receptor (AR)‐dependent prostate cancer, were developed. All the Se derivatives displayed promising activity against the NCI‐60 human cancer cell line panel, which also includes two AR‐independent prostate cancer cell lines, DU‐145 and PC‐3. Conversely to flutamide, compounds a2, a5, and b4 exhibited a potent antiproliferative effect toward AR‐dependent LNCaP cells. Several cell death inhibitors were used to determine the underlying regulated cell death processes of a5. Regarding its mechanism of action in these cells, this compound promoted apoptosis by activating both intrinsic and extrinsic apoptotic pathways, without generating reactive oxygen species (ROS). Besides, it induced the G0/G1 cell cycle arrest. Altogether, it could be concluded that this Se‐flutamide analog could be a feasible and promising candidate for further development for the treatment of both AR‐dependent and ‐independent prostate cancers.

Introduction

In recent years, prostate cancer has been ranked as one of the most diagnosed tumors in men. Androgens, testosterone, and 5α‐dihydrotestosterone (DHT) play a pivotal role in the growth and maintenance of the prostatic cells. Many prostate cancers depend on the interaction between the androgen and its receptor (AR). In view of the former, androgen deprivation therapy (ADT) with steroidal or nonsteroidal antiandrogens has been the mainstay therapeutic option for the treatment of localized prostate cancer with a response rate higher than 80%. It has been reported that the nonsteroidal agents (such as flutamide and bicalutamide) are preferred over the steroidal antiandrogens (i.e., cyproterone acetate and mifepristone) as the latter induce adverse side effects owing to their interaction with the progestin and glucocorticoid receptors. In recent years, selective androgen receptor modulators (SARMs) have been considered as the feasible future of androgen therapy as they can selectively target the AR in a tissue compared to the traditional antiandrogens. However, in many patients, it eventually progresses to castration‐resistant prostate cancer (CRPC), probably due to the emergence of mutations on the AR, with a worse prognosis, and has become a major clinical ongoing issue. Furthermore, the therapeutic potential of the AR antagonists currently marketed or undergoing clinical trials is hampered due to efficacy‐limiting side effects and drawbacks [1, 2, 3]. Thus, researchers are eager to find novel potent and safe therapeutic agents for both hormone‐independent and ‐dependent prostate cancer cells. Currently, one of the practical approaches to these challenges is the direct manipulation of existing drugs to improve their therapeutic properties and efficacy. In this line, several modifications of the nonsteroidal antiandrogens (i.e., flutamide and bicalutamide) have been made [4, 5, 6]. Insertion of selenium (Se) functionalized groups in a vast array of active molecules is a current trend in medicinal chemistry, applied to improve the biological effects of the parent compounds [7]. In addition, the role of a great number of organic and inorganic selenocompounds in prostate cancer cell death and growth inhibition has gained interest in the past few years [8, 9, 10, 11].

Here, we report the synthesis of 16 Se‐derivatives of flutamide, divided into three different libraries. All the compounds were submitted to the National Cancer Institute's (NCI) Developmental Therapeutic Program (DTP) for cytotoxic screening. The prostate subpanel includes PC‐3 and DU‐145 prostate cancer cell lines, both of which are AR‐independent cells. Amidst them, a2, a5, b4, and c3 were selected for further assessment of their potential use as anticancer candidates. The evaluation of their cytotoxic activities on hormone‐dependent (LNCaP) prostate cancer cells has been reported. Although flutamide was devoid of activity toward these cells, our compounds had an interesting antiproliferative effect against them. This difference may be attributed to the reported mutation in the steroid‐binding domain of the AR in the LNCaP cells [12], which can convert this drug into an AR agonist rather than an antagonist, thereby failing to inhibit tumor growth. Thus, the antiproliferative activity observed for our compounds suggests that they either effectively antagonize AR, even in its mutated form, or act through additional underlying mechanisms independent of AR signaling. Compound a5 stood out amidst the evaluated selenoderivatives and was selected for further biological studies. Cell death inhibitors (ferrostatin‐1, wortmannin, z‐VAD‐FMK, and Y‐27632) were used to gain a first insight into the underlying regulated cell death processes of this selenocompound. The capacity of a5 to induce apoptosis, produce intracellular reactive oxygen species (ROS), and modulate the cell cycle was also studied in vitro at different time points. Additionally, in silico studies were carried out to predict the theoretical absorption, distribution, metabolism, excretion, and toxicity (ADMET) profile of compound a5.

Results and Discussion

Structural Design

Herein, we have designed and synthesized a series of 16 novel analogs of flutamide (Figure 1). The obtained compounds were classified into three series: a, NSAIDs analogs; b, monocyclic substituents; and c, fused cycles derivatives. The structures of all the compounds present a trifluoromethyl substituted anilide as it appears to be critical for the biological activity observed for several nonsteroidal antiandrogen compounds. We decided to include a Se functionality in the design, given that this incorporation has demonstrated to increase the biological activity of the parent compounds in most of the cases. Thus, among all the possible Se functionalities, the selenoester functional group was chosen since it can be easily cleaved, hence releasing some selenol or selenide intermediate with potential biological activity.

Design of the novel selenoesters. Compounds were arranged in 3 series (a, b, and c).

Chemistry and Characterization

Synthetic Chemistry

The target selenoester analogs of flutamide were synthesized following a previously reported procedure [13]. Briefly, sodium hydrogen selenide (NaHSe) was formed by the reduction of elemental Se with sodium borohydride (NaBH4) in water. As reported in numerous articles, two Se species can be formed in aqueous or ethanolic solutions in this reaction, depending on the ratio of NaBH4. The fastest and simplest way to recognize both intermediates is their color: NaHSe is colorless and Na2Se is dark red [14]. Thereby, we added the corresponding acid chloride when the aqueous solution was colorless to ensure that only NaHSe was the sole intermediate, and the reaction mixture was stirred for 30–60 min at room temperature (r.t.). The yield of this step depended on the aqueous solubility of the corresponding acyl chloride. THF was added to achieve good yields in cases where the organic reagents presented poor aqueous solubilities. Notwithstanding several other Se‐compounds can be formed in the reaction mixture, including diacyldiselenides and selenoanhydrides, especially when THF is used, the main Se‐compound formed is selenoester, which can be easily purified from the other by‐products containing Se by chromatographic column. The acid chlorides that were not commercially available were synthesized as depicted in Figure 2 and were then used without further purification. Finally, the desired derivatives were obtained through a nucleophilic substitution over the bromide intermediate. The latter was obtained by treating a solution of 3‐(trifluoromethyl)aniline in methylene chloride (DCM) with bromoacetyl chloride in the presence of triethylamine (TEA) and was used without further purification.

Synthesis of the target Se‐derivatives. Reagents and conditions: (i) H2O, 10–15 min, room temperature (r.t.); (ii) THF, r.t., 30–60 min; (iii) r.t. for 12 h; (iv): SOCl2, reflux, 3–6 h, or ClCOCOCl, DCM, N,N‐DMF (drops), r.t., 6–12 h; (v) DCM, TEA, r.t. for 2 h.

The compounds were purified by silica gel column chromatography with a gradient of hexane and ethyl acetate as eluent. Some derivatives of the series were difficult to purify, which explains the differences among yields. The structural characterization of all the compounds was determined by 1H, 13C, and 77Se nuclear magnetic resonance (NMR) and 2D‐NMR experiments as detailed in the Experimental Section. Regarding the 1H NMR spectra, the presence of two microsatellites reflecting the coupling of 1H with 77Se could be observed in the signal belonging to the –CH2– attached to the Se atom. Besides, the peak most shifted upfield corresponds to the –NH in all the flutamide analogs at 9–11 ppm. Concerning the 13C NMR spectra, a characteristic quartet signal related to the –CF3 group was found at 124–135 ppm (J C‐F = 271–273 Hz) along with other quartets with smaller coupling constants due to the splitting of the farther carbons by the same fluorine atoms. Of note, the 13C and 19F coupling constants were in accordance with the literature reports [15]. Additionally, the most downfield‐shifted peak corresponded to the carbonyl group of the selenoester bond in all the compounds, with an overall chemical shift between 180 and 209 ppm. The carbonyl group of the amide functionality appeared almost in the same position as a sharp peak at 167–169 ppm for all the compounds except compound c2, as it was found more downfield (178 ppm). The signal belonging to the –CH2– was found at 28–44 ppm, and in some cases, it was under the solvent signal. The most downfield‐shifted peaks corresponded to the ibuprofen (44.2 ppm) and indomethacin (41.5 ppm) derivatives. In the 77Se NMR, the chemical shifts of the Se peak among the reported derivatives appeared in the range of 537–579 ppm with no significant differences between them. All the derivatives herein reported were stable at room temperature (r.t.) for at least 6 months.

Biological Evaluation

Antiproliferative Activities of the Se‐Flutamide Analogs Evaluated in the One‐Dose Assays of the NCI‐60 Human Cancer Cell Line Screening Program

As a first approach to determine the possible antiproliferative properties of the newly synthesized selenoesters, their effect on cell viability was determined in vitro through the screening platform of the National Cancer Institute (NCI)‐60 human cancer cell lines. The results are expressed as the ratio of the growth inhibition of the treated cells (10 µM concentration, 48 h treatment) to the growth percentage (GP) of the untreated controls. Of note, GP values in the interval of 0% and 50% stand for antiproliferative properties, around 0% means a cytostatic effect, and between 0% and −100% means that the compound is cytotoxic. For the sake of clarity, Figure 5 provides a summary of the results obtained in this initial screening, while Figure 6 displays the mean growth inhibition values obtained in the NCI‐60 cancer cell line panel. All the compounds, except derivatives a6, b1, b2, and b3, exhibited outstanding cytotoxic activities against almost all the cell lines. Nevertheless, these active compounds displayed different behaviors (cytotoxic and antiproliferative) depending on the cell lines tested within the different cancer subpanels. The mean growth inhibition values for the colon subpanel were the highest for all the compounds except for a6. Interestingly, among all the compounds, only a5 and b4 displayed a remarkable antiproliferative activity toward the K‐562 cell line from the leukemia subpanel with a GP value of ‐81.86% and ‐81.77%, respectively. Compound a6 demonstrated potent and selective cytotoxic activity in the leukemia HL‐60(TB) cell line.

It should be noted that a wide range of resistant cell lines from the NCI‐60 [16] were sensitive to a great number of the reported selenoesters. All the compounds exhibited cytotoxic behavior in the SNB‐19, SK‐MEL‐28, EKVX, A498, and DU‐145 cell lines. Remarkably, at least one resistant breast cancer cell line (BT‐549, HS 578 T, and MDA‐MB‐231) was susceptible to all the compounds tested. Furthermore, almost all of them demonstrated cytotoxic activity toward TK‐10, SK‐OV‐3, OVCAR‐5, SK‐MEL‐2, SF‐268, and NCI‐H226. The most resistant colon cancer cell line, HCC‐2998, was susceptible to compounds a2, a3, a4, a7, b4, and c5.

Five‐Dose Assay

Further investigation was carried out for all the compounds since they met the NCI one‐dose screening parameters for threshold inhibition. The derivatives were tested at five concentrations (0.01, 0.1, 1, 10, and 100 μM) in the NCI‐60 cell panel to determine their dose–response curves along with three response parameters [50% growth inhibitory concentration (GI50), the total growth inhibition concentration (TGI), and the 50% lethal concentration (LC50)] for each cell line. The GI50 value was defined as the concentration of the derivative that resulted in a 50% decrease in net cell growth, the TGI value (cytostatic activity) corresponded to the concentration that completely inhibited the cell growth, and the LC50 value (cytotoxic activity) was the concentration that led to the 50% loss of initial cells upon completion of the incubation period. The outcomes of the five‐dose analysis of all the compounds are given in the Supporting Material. The mean values of these parameters (in μM) for each subpanel are presented in Figures 7, 8, 9. Of note, all the compounds exhibited potent anticancer activity against the whole panel with mean GI50 values (1.55–5.40 µM) lower than 5‐fluorouracil (NSC 19893) (18 µM) in the same panel of cell lines [17]. Likewise, all the reported selenoderivatives, except b1 and b3, also exhibited mean GI50 values lower than gefitinib (NSC 715055) (3.2 µM) and oxaliplatin (NSC 266046) (2.8 µM), which are other cytotoxic drugs used in clinic [17]. As a general consideration from the data reported in Figure 7, compounds from series b presented GI50 values lower than their corresponding analogs from series c. Notably, the only organoselenium compound bearing an aliphatic substituent (b1) was less effective than the rest of the derivatives reported herein. Another interesting point from the data is that five of the reported selenoesters (a1, c1, c2, c3, and c4) showed GI50 values in the low micromolar range against several resistant cell lines from the NCI‐60 [16]. Compounds c1, c2, c3, and c4 showed GI50 values of 0.42, 0.60, 0.46, and 0.43 µM against the most resistant colon cancer cell line, HCC‐2998. Likewise, a1 exhibited a GI50 value of 0.99 µM toward the UACC‐2557 melanoma cell line. Derivatives c3 and c4 demonstrated great growth inhibitory activity against OVCAR‐4 ovarian cancer cell line with GI50 values of 0.50 and 0.70 µM, respectively. Inspection of the TGI data revealed that compounds from libraries a and c, except the aspirin (ASA) derivative (a6), presented an overall mean TGI value ranging from 3 to 5 μM (Figure 8). On the other hand, b3 was comparable with gefitinib as they presented mean TGI values of 17.4 and 19 μM, respectively [17]. Regarding the LC50 values (Figure 9), it can be observed that a wide array of cancer cells from different subpanels were more resistant to a6 and b3.

Based on the promising findings from both assays from the NCI's DTP, a2, a5, b4, and c3 were selected as representative compounds to gain deeper insight into their potential use as anticancer agents against prostate cancer.

Antiproliferative Activity Toward Androgen‐Dependent LNCaP Cell Line

As evident from the NCI‐60 cell line panel screening described above, many cancer cell lines responded effectively to the reported organoselenium compounds. Although a deeper investigation into the different sensitive cancer cell lines is required to understand the underlying mechanisms, we focused on prostate cancer herein. The primary reason for this selection was that the NCI‐60 panel only contains androgen receptor (AR)‐independent DU‐145 and PC‐3 cell lines against which our compounds exhibited significant anticancer activity, and we thought it would be interesting to evaluate their antiproliferative activities in the androgen‐dependent LNCaP cell line as well. In addition, flutamide, based on which these organoselenium compounds are designed, has been used in AR‐dependent prostate cancers. It has been widely reported that the AR gene in these cells contains a point mutation in the ligand‐binding domain. As a consequence, the AR can be activated not only in response to several antiandrogens (e.g., flutamide) but also by estrogens and progestagens [4, 12].

LNCaP and nonmalignant HaCaT cells were treated with different concentrations (1, 2.5, 5, 10, 25, 50, and 100 µM) of a2, a5, b4, and c3 using the [3‐(4,5‐dimethylthiazol‐2‐yl)‐5‐(3‐carboxymethoxyphenyl)‐2‐(4‐sulfophenyl)‐2H‐tetrazolium] (MTS) bioassay to determine their respective antiproliferative activities and selectivity indexes (SI) (Table 1). The SI for each compound was calculated using the formula: SI = (IC50 for nonmalignant line HaCaT)/(IC50 for LNCaP cell line).

In view of the former, a5 stood out amidst the evaluated selenoderivatives as it displayed significant effectiveness in this cell line (IC50 values below 10 µM) and acceptable cytotoxicity toward HaCaT cells with an IC50 of 28.71 µM, proving to be the most selective compound (SI of 3.23). Inhibitors of Regulated Cell Death

To gain a first insight into the type of regulated cell death induced by a5, we conducted a cytotoxicity assay by combining our selenocompound with different cell death inhibitors and measuring the change in its cytotoxic activity in LNCaP cells. Thus, we used: ferrostatin‐1, a lipid peroxidation and ferroptosis inhibitor; wortmannin, an autophagy inhibitor; Z‐VAD‐FMK, a pan‐caspase inhibitor; and Y‐27632, a selective Rho kinase‐associated coil‐containing protein kinase (ROCK) inhibitor. The concentrations and incubation times of the different inhibitors were determined based on previously reported literature [18, 19, 20]. LNCaP cells were pretreated with the corresponding inhibitors (10 μM) and incubated at 37°C for 1 h. After this time, the cells were treated with the IC50 value of a5 for 48 h. The cells, untreated and treated with either only the inhibitors or a5, were used as controls. The data represented in Figure 11 indicate that treatment with ferrostatin‐1, Z‐VAD‐FMK, and Y‐27632 protects LNCaP cells from death caused by a5; thus, this selenocompound could exert its antiproliferative activity through several types of regulated cell deaths. The increased cell viability observed following ferrostatin‐1 pretreatment and subsequent exposure to the IC50 concentration of a5 suggests that ferroptosis may contribute to cell death. On the other hand, it is a well‐known fact that Z‐VAD‐FMK is a widely used inhibitor of the activity of caspases, which play a pivotal role in the apoptotic process. Thus, caspase‐dependent apoptosis could also be a feasible mechanism of action associated with a5. Additionally, the antiproliferative activity of the studied compound diminished when combined with Y‐27632, a selective ROCK inhibitor. Several reports have indicated that ROCK is essential for various aspects of intrinsic and extrinsic apoptotic processes in various cell types [21]. Therefore, to further corroborate and understand the molecular mechanisms underlying the antiproliferative effect of a5 in this prostate cancer cell line, more studies are needed.

Compound a5 Induced Apoptosis in Androgen‑Dependent LNCaP Prostate Cancer Cells

The previously described cell viability assays demonstrated that a5 exhibits strong antiproliferative activity. The Annexin V & Dead Cell and Caspase 3/7 assays were carried out to further investigate if this compound could induce cell death through apoptosis. It is a well‐known fact that Annexin V, a phospholipid‐binding protein, is widely used to detect apoptotic cell death as it has a high affinity for the phosphatidylserine (PS) residues exposed on the outer layer of the membrane of the apoptotic cells [22]. Additionally, 7‐aminoactinomycin D (7‐AAD), a fluorescent DNA‐binding dye able to bind to double‐stranded DNA, is used to exclude necrotic and late apoptotic cells as it stains their compromised membrane integrity. Double staining with Annexin V and 7‐AAD indicates which cells are already dead or in the late stage of apoptosis [23]. On the other hand, caspases are proteolytic enzymes that have a pivotal role in regulating cell death. Caspases involved in the apoptotic process have been subclassified as initiator or executioner caspases depending on their mechanism of action. Caspase‐3 and caspase‐7 belong to the latter, and their activation results in the cleaving of numerous sets of substrates that lead to the characteristic hallmarks of apoptosis [24].

Based on the IC50 value previously determined for a5, LNCaP cells were treated with 10 µM of this compound for 24 and 48 h. The experiments were performed following the manufacturer's protocol, and the results are compiled in Figure 12A,B. Flow cytometric analysis distinguished four different populations of cells: necrotic cells [both Annexin V and Caspase 3/7 negative and 7‐AAD positive (upper left quadrant]; late apoptotic or dead cells [Annexin V, Caspase 3/7 and 7‐AAD positive (upper right quadrant)]; healthy cells [Annexin V, Caspase 3/7 and 7‐AAD negative (located in the lower left quadrant)]; and early apoptotic cells [Annexin V and Caspase 3/7 positive and 7‐AAD negative (located in the lower right quadrant]. Moreover, a quantitative analysis of the difference in the cell population between the two treatment periods is shown in Figure 12C,D. Cells treated with the vehicle (DMSO) were located mainly in the lower left quadrant in both assays and were used as negative controls. In the Annexin V & Dead Cell assay (Figure 12A,C), the treatment with a5 induced a shift from healthy cells toward an apoptotic state. Results revealed that the ratio of apoptotic cells increased significantly at the end of the 48 h treatment, indicating that a5‐apoptosis was time‐dependent. The percentage of late apoptotic/dead cells rose from 0.75% in the control to 28.90% following 48 h of treatment with the Se‐compound. A significant increase in the early apoptotic population was also observed after 48 h of treatment. Notably, only a small percentage of necrotic cells (0.80%–4.05%) was detected at the end of the treatments. Regarding the Caspase 3/7 assay, 35.71% and 43.05% of the cells were in the late apoptotic/dead stage when treated with a5 for 24 and 48 h, respectively. Taken together, the results obtained in both assays, along with the antiproliferative effect displayed by a5 in LNCaP cells, strongly suggest that apoptosis might be the underlying mechanism by which the tested compound induces cell death in this cell line, with a clear involvement of caspase 3/7.

To gain a deeper insight into the molecular mechanism of apoptosis induction in LNCaP cells by the hit compound, western blot analysis was carried out. Hence, we assessed the effect of compound a5 (10 µM) on the expression levels of pro‐caspase 3, pro‐caspase 9, caspase 3, caspase 9, caspase 8, and BAX. As shown in Figure 12E,F, treatment with the tested compound for 48 h led to decreased levels of the inactive forms of caspase‐3 and caspase‐9. Conversely, we observed a remarkable increase in the BAX levels. Besides, results revealed an increase in the caspase‐3, caspase‐8, and caspase‐9 expression upon treatment with a5 by 3.66, 2.36, and 1.22 folds, respectively, compared to the control cells. These results suggest that both intrinsic and extrinsic pathways are activated, with a more prominent effect on the first one as the caspase‐9 levels were higher and the proapoptotic BAX protein is upregulated.

Compound a5 Marginally Induces ROS Production During 24 h of Treatment

Next, we aimed to determine whether a5 induces apoptosis through intracellular ROS production. As previously described [25], we measured the production of ROS in LNCaP cells after the treatment with DMSO (negative control), H2O2 (positive control), or a5 (10 µM) for 12 and 24 h using a Muse™ Oxidative Stress Kit. The histogram (Figure 13A) shows two distinct cell populations: ROS‐negative (ROS−; M1 peak/blue) and ROS‐positive (ROS+; M2 peak/red) cells. As illustrated in Figure 13, the treatment with a5 did not lead to a significant change in the total ROS levels between the two time points, reaching about 13% and 15% at 12 and 24 h, respectively, of ROS (+) cells. These results suggest that apoptosis induction by a5 may be ROS‐independent.

Compound a5 Inhibited LNCaP Cell Growth by Inducing Cell Cycle Arrest

To further elucidate the underlying mechanism for the reduced cell viability of LNCaP prostate cancer cells induced by a5, the cell cycle was analyzed by flow cytometry. Before the treatment, the cells were synchronized in G0/G1 phase through serum starvation for 48 h. Subsequently, cells were treated with the IC50 concentration of a5 for 48 h. Control cells were supplied with fresh supplemented media. Flow cytometry revealed that a5 inhibits the cell cycle progression by arresting the cells in the G0/G1 phase (Figure 14). The percentage of cells in this phase increased from 41.6% (control group) to 69.8% for the cells upon treatment with a5. The percentage of cells in the G2/M phase of the cell cycle in the control was 15.8%, whereas in the cells treated with the selenocompound, it was 3.3%. Likewise, the percentage of cells in the S phase was reduced.

In Silico Studies: Drug‐Likeness and ADMET Predictions

The theoretical ADMET profile of compound a5 was predicted using the SwissADME and ADMETlab 3.0 algorithm [26, 27]. According to Lipinski's rule of five [28], an orally active drug can have no more than one violation of the following claims: molecular weight (MW) ≤ 500, partition coefficient ≤ 5, ≤ 5 hydrogen donors (nHD), and ≤ 10 hydrogen acceptors (nHA). Compound a5 meets four of these criteria as it has a MW of 547.02 g/mol. Besides, a5 showed no alerts for Pan Assay Interfering substances (PAINS) and was predicted to act as a p‐glycoprotein (Pgp) substrate. It was also predicted to be free of hepatotoxicity or parameters related to cardiotoxicity, eye irritation, and corrosion, as well as drug‐induced neurotoxicity. This Se derivative displayed a favorable synthetic accessibility score (SA score), which is a metric used to assess the ease of synthesizing drug‐like molecules. Although Caco‐2 cell permeability results suggested that a5 did not have optimal membrane permeability, the computed values for human intestinal absorption (HIA) indicated that it possesses a high likelihood of being effectively absorbed through the intestinal membrane. The distribution of this selenoderivative within plasma protein binding (PPB) was 96.05%, with normal volume distribution (VD) and low blood‐brain barrier (BBB) penetration. The excretion parameters revealed a moderate average clearance and a short half‐life. Furthermore, the AMES toxicity profile showed that a5 did not present the potential to be toxic. The full reports are shown in Figure S145 and Table S9 in the Supporting Material.

Conclusions

In summary, the incorporation of Se into the scaffold of flutamide rendered potent analogs with promising anticancer activity. Contrary to flutamide, compounds a2, a5, and b4 inhibited the proliferation of LNCaP cells as confirmed by the MTS and trypan blue dye exclusion assays. This finding suggests that our Se‐analogs might either antagonize the mutated AR effectively or act through AR‐independent mechanisms. Furthermore, according to the NCI data, they displayed an antiproliferative effect toward the AR‐independent DU‐145 and PC‐3 cell lines, reinforcing the hypothesis that their observed anticancer activity is not solely reliant on AR inhibition. As a5 was the most potent and selective derivative toward the LNCaP cells, a cytotoxic assay involving several cell death inhibitors was carried out to gain an initial insight into the feasible type of regulated cell death induced by this compound in this cell line. Furthermore, a5 induced cell death in LNCaP cells through an apoptotic mechanism evinced by caspase 3/7 activity and the presence of PS on the outer membrane of these cancer cells. This Se‐flutamide analog also inhibited the growth of LNCaP cells by arresting the cells in the G0/G1 phase. Overall, our results show that incorporation of Se into the flutamide scaffold (i) substantially enhances the potency toward several cancer cell lines while increasing the SI, (ii) renders activity against both AR‐dependent and ‐independent prostate cancer cell lines, unlike flutamide, which is used for AR‐dependent tumors, and (iii) likely minimizes the concerns of hepatotoxicity associated with flutamide. Although more studies are required, these findings underscore the potential of a5 as a feasible candidate for further preclinical evaluation and development as a novel therapeutic agent for prostate and potentially other cancers.

General Information

All the reagents were obtained from commercial suppliers and used without further purification unless otherwise specified. The progress of each reaction was monitored by thin‐layer chromatography (TLC) plates and the spots were visualized using an ultraviolet (UV) lamp (λ = 254 nm). The chemical shifts (δ) were expressed in parts per million (ppm) downfield from internal tetramethylsilane (TMS) at 0.00 ppm. Coupling constants (J) were reported in hertz (Hz). Melting points (mp) were obtained on a Metler FP82+FP80 apparatus. Elemental analyses for carbon (C), hydrogen (H), and nitrogen (N) were carried out on a Thermo Fisher FlashSmart™ Elemental Analyzer. The purity of the compounds was assessed by high‐resolution mass spectrometry (HRMS) using a triple quadrupole mass spectrometer (6410 Agilent Technologies) with an electrospray ionization source. Data acquisition parameters (i.e., monitoring ion pairs, fragmentor voltage, and collision energy) were optimized for each compound. The InChI codes of the investigated compounds, together with some biological activity data, are provided as Supporting Information.

General Synthesis of the Selenoesters

A solution of sodium borohydride (9.4 mmol) in 12.5 mL of distilled water was added to a stirred suspension of grey Se (4.7 mmol) in 12.5 mL of distilled water at r.t. The reaction mixture was stirred until an almost colorless solution of NaHSe was formed. Then, the corresponding acyl chlorides (4.7 mmol) were added and the reaction mixture was magnetically stirred for 30–60 min at r.t. If the acyl chloride was not commercially available, it was previously synthesized from its corresponding carboxylic acid and used without further purification. Afterward, the corresponding halide (4.7 mmol) was added to the reaction, and the mixture was stirred overnight at r.t. This intermediate was prepared as previously described and used without further purification [13]. The final crude compounds were isolated, by extraction with DCM (3 × 20 mL). The combined organic phases were washed with water (2 × 30 mL) followed by drying over anhydrous sodium sulfate. The solvent was removed under vacuum by rotatory evaporation. The derivatives were purified by silica gel column chromatography with a gradient of hexane and ethyl acetate as eluent.

Biology

NCI‐60 Analysis

The newly synthesized compounds were submitted to the NCI's Development Therapeutics Program (DTP). Their antitumoral activity was assessed in the NCI‐60 human cancer cell line used to screen and identify test compounds that display an effect on cell proliferation following the methodology available [32, 33].

Cell Culture Conditions

The cell lines were obtained from the American Type Culture Collection (ATCC). LNCaP and HaCaT cells were cultured in DMEM (Thermo Fisher Scientific, USA), supplemented with 10% FBS (Gibco, USA) and 1% antibiotics (10.00 units/mL penicillin and 10.00 mg/mL streptomycin; Gibco, USA). Cells were preserved in tissue culture flasks at 37°C under an atmosphere containing 5% CO2. The culture medium was replaced every 3 days.

Cell Viability Assay

The novel selenoderivatives were dissolved in DMSO at a concentration of 10−2 M, and serial dilutions were prepared. The cytotoxic and antiproliferative activities of the hit compounds were evaluated in LNCaP and HaCaT cells at seven concentrations ranging from 1 to 100 μM using the MTS assay, as previously described [34]. Briefly, the cells were seeded in 96‐well plates at a density of 1 × 104 cells per well. The following day, cells were treated with either DMSO or increasing concentrations of the respective compound for 48 h in triplicates. At the end of the treatment duration, the media were removed and fresh media were added containing 20 μL of MTS (Promega, USA) per well. Absorbances of the solubilized formazan crystals were measured at 490 nm after 1 h incubation. The IC50 values were calculated using OriginPro 8.5.1. software by nonlinear curve fitting, and the SI were calculated as the ratio of the IC50 values determined for the nonmalignant and the corresponding prostate tumoral cells. Graphpad Prism 7.0 Software was used to represent the corresponding graphs. Data were obtained from at least three independent experiments performed in triplicates.

Trypan Blue Staining Assay

The trypan blue staining assay is a well‐established method for assessing cell viability [35]. Briefly, LNCaP cells were seeded in 12‐well plates at a density of 6 × 104 cells per well and allowed to adhere for 24 h. Cells were then treated with three different concentrations (5, 10, and 25 μM) of a2, a5, b4, and c3 for 48 h. At the end of the treatments, the cells were harvested and mixed with a 1:1 volume of 0.4% Trypan Blue dye (Invitrogen, USA). Cells were loaded over the hemocytometer and counted separately using a bright‐field microscope. Live and viable cells were unstained and presented a clear cytoplasm, whereas dead or membrane‐compromised cells were stained with the dye and appeared blue. Data were obtained from at least three independent experiments performed in triplicates. Graphpad Prism 7.0 Software was used to obtain the corresponding graphs.

Apoptosis Assays

The proapoptotic activity of a5 was assessed using Caspase 3/7 and Annexin V & Dead Cell assay kits (EMD Millipore, Germany). LNCaP cells were seeded in 6‐well plates at a density of 6 × 105 cells per well and incubated for 24 h. Cells were then treated either with DMSO (control) or with the IC50 concentration of a5 for 24 and 48 h. At the end of the treatments, cells were collected and treated with the respective dyes according to the manufacturer's protocol. For the Caspase 3/7 assay, they were stained with 5 µL of Muse™ Caspase 3/7 working solution and incubated at 37°C for 30 min. After incubation, 150 mL of Muse™ Caspase 7‐AAD working solution was added and the samples were incubated for another 5 min in the dark at room temperature before analysis. For the Annexin V assay, cells were stained with 100 µL of Muse™ Annexin V & Dead Cell Reagent and incubated for 20 min at room temperature in the dark. Samples for both assays were analyzed on a Muse™ Cell Analyzer (Merck Millipore, Germany) using Muse™ version 1.4. software. Data were obtained from at least two independent experiments. Graphpad Prism 7.0 Software was used to represent the corresponding graphs.

ROS Assay

Measurement of total ROS levels in LNCaP cells was performed by using the Muse Oxidative Stress Kit (EMD Millipore, Billerica, MA) following the manufacturer's protocol. The kit utilizes Muse Oxidative Stress Reagent for the detection of intracellular ROS. The kit distinguishes between two different cell populations: ROS (−) cells represented by the M1 peak and the ROS (+) population depicted by the M2 peak in the histogram. The LNCaP cells were plated in six‐well plates at a density of 6 × 105 cells/well for 24 h before starting treatment for either 12 or 24 h. Cells were treated with 250 μM of H2O2 for a positive control to induce ROS. The cells treated with DMSO were used as a negative control. After treatment, the cells were detached using 0.25% trypsin/EDTA and centrifuged for 5 min at 300 g. The cells were then diluted with 200 μL of the working solution and incubated for 30–60 min at 37°C before analysis. The proportions of ROS‐negative and ROS‐positive cell populations were quantified using a Muse cell analyzer. The experiments were repeated at least two times independently. Graphpad Prism 7.0 Software was used to obtain the corresponding graphs.

Cell Cycle

The cell cycle analysis was determined by using the Muse Cell Cycle Kit (EMD Millipore, Billerica, MA) following the manufacturer's protocol with slight modifications. LNCaP cells were seeded in 6‐well plates at a density of 6 × 105 cells per well. For synchronized cell‐cycle analysis, the cells were serum‐starved for 72 h and then treated with a5 in media with 10% FBS for 72 h. The cells were collected after the period time and centrifuged at 300g for 5 min. Subsequently, the cells were fixed in ice‐cold 70% ethanol (70%) and the tubes were kept in a freezer at −20°C overnight before staining. After fixation, the ethanol was removed by centrifugation (300g for 5 min at room temperature), washed with pre‐cooled PBS, and the cells were suspended in 200 µL of Muse® Cell Cycle Reagent, which includes propidium iodide (PI) and RNAse A, at r.t. for 30 min in the dark. The distribution of the cell cycle phase was detected using a Muse cell analyzer. Data were obtained from at least two independent experiments. Graphpad Prism 7.0 Software was used to obtain the corresponding graphs.

Preparation of Total Cell Lysates and Protein Quantification

Cell lysates were prepared from LNCaP cells treated with a5 and untreated (control) to assess the expression of the target proteins. Cells were initially washed twice gently with ice‐cold 1× PBS and harvested using 0.25% trypsin. Subsequently, 200 μL of modified RIPA buffer (Thermo Scientific, USA) was added per sample. This modified RIPA buffer typically consists of 50 mM Tris‐HCl (pH 7.4), 150 mM NaCl, 1 mM EDTA, 1% NP‐40, and a protease inhibitor cocktail (1 μL per 100 μL of lysis buffer; Thermo Scientific, USA). The samples were kept on ice for 30 min and centrifugated at 10,600 rpm for 10 min. The resultant supernatants were carefully collected, transferred into new tubes, and Laemmli SDS sample buffer (200 μL/sample) was used for protein sample preparation. The tubes were kept in ice for 20 min and then heated at 95°C for 3 min to break the 3D structure of the proteins. The Bradford method (Thermo Scientific, USA) was used to estimate the total protein concentration as previously described.

Western Blot

Western blot chemiluminescent analysis of PARP, caspase 3, and caspase 9 in LNCaP cells treated with 10 μM of a5 for 24 h was conducted. Equal amounts of cell lyases (30 mg) were separated using sodium dodecyl sulfate‐polyacrylamide gel electrophoresis (SDS‐PAGE) (100 V for 1 h) and electrotransferred to a polyvinylidene difluoride (PVDF) membrane. For protein transfer, the cassette was assembled as follows: foam pad, filter paper, gel, PVDF membrane, another layer of filter paper, and a final foam pad. All the components were fully wet with the transfer buffer and no air bubbles were present. The transfer cassette was loaded into the transfer tank and it was run at 80 V for 2 h. Ponceau solution was applied for 30 s to stain the membranes and assess protein transfer efficiency. Afterward, they were washed and blocked using a 5% nonfat dry milk solution in 1× PBST (phosphate‐buffered saline with 0.02% of Tween‐20) for 1 h. The membranes were then incubated overnight at room temperature on a rocker with different primary antibodies (Cell Signaling Technology): PARP (46D11, Rabbit mAb, 1:1000), glyceraldehyde‐3‐phosphate dehydrogenase (GAPDH) (14C10, Rabbit mAb, 1:1000), cleaved‐caspase 3 (Asp175, Rabbit antibody, 1:1000), and cleaved‐caspase 9 (Asp31, Rabbit antibody, 1:1000). After incubation with the primary antibody, membranes were washed three times for 5–10 min with PBST and then incubated with the appropriate secondary antibody (1:1000, Anti‐rabbit; Cell Signaling Technology) for 2 h. To visualize the protein bands, enhanced chemiluminescence (Thermo Scientific™ Pierce ECL Western blot analysis Substrate) was applied, and the image was captured using the Bio‐Rad ChemiDoc™ XRS+ imaging system.

Statistical Analysis

Data are presented as mean ± standard deviation (SD), and all experiments were conducted in triplicate at least three times unless otherwise stated. Nonlinear curve regression analysis calculated by OriginPro version 8.5.1. software was used to obtain the IC50 values for the novel organoselenium compounds. For western blot analysis data, band intensities were measured using ImageJ and normalized with GAPDH. The unpaired t test was used to calculate the statistical significance of differences comparing the Se‐derivatives and control. Data were analyzed using GraphPad Prism version 8.0.1. and the statistically significant values (p‐value) for unpaired t test analysis were taken as ****p < 0.0001, ***p < 0. 001, **p < 0.01, and *p < 0.05 when comparing control and a5.
