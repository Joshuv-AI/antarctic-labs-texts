Multimodality Imaging Evaluation of Hepatic Epithelioid Angiomyolipoma
Authors: Yi-Tzu Chen, Ying-Ju Kuo, Suk Yee Lau, Chui-Mei Tiu, Yi-Hong Chou
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Hepatic epithelioid angiomyolipoma (HEAML) is a rare benign tumor in the liver. It is crucial to differentiate HEAML from hepatocellular carcinoma. We herein report the case of a 42-year-old female who was diagnosed with HEAML by multiple imaging studies. The histopathological examination also confirmed the diagnosis. Imaging studies include magnetic resonance imaging, digital subtraction angiography (DSA), and contrast-enhanced ultrasound (US). Typical image findings of HEAML by US include early drainage of hepatic veins, higher peak intensity, shorter time to peak on time-intensity curve, and a larger wash-in area under the curve after ultrasonic contrast injection. Early hepatic drainage vein(s) detected at CEUS are a crucial diagnosis of HEAML. DSA can also provide valuable vascular details. This case highlights the importance of recognizing the imaging features of HEAML to avoid misdiagnosis and unnecessary surgical interventions. We present various imaging techniques and their findings and emphasize the application of CEUS in the diagnosis of HEAML.

INTRODUCTION

Hepatic epithelioid angiomyolipoma (HEAML) is a rare benign hepatic tumor. Most of the hepatic angiomyolipoma (AML) shows fatty components just such as renal AML. However, HEAML is a subtype of hepatic AML, mainly composed of epithelioid cells and scanty fat tissue, with potential malignancy.[12] The HEAML shares many similar characteristics with hepatocellular carcinoma (HCC) on image studies and is often misdiagnosed as HCC.[3] Thus, it is important to differentiate HEAML from HCC to avoid unnecessary treatments. We herein present various imaging techniques and imaging findings of HEAML.

MATERIALS AND METHODS

Patient

A 41-year-old female had an incidental hepatic nodule, which was found at a health check-up with medical imaging, including ultrasound (US). Since the initial US study could not distinguish a benign mass from malignancies, the patient was referred to the Department of Imaging Medicine for further evaluation using other imaging modalities.

Imaging techniques

The imaging modalities available in our department include all kinds of state-of-the-art devices. For example, among the eight magnetic resonance (MR) machines (1.5- to 3-Tesla), Artist 1.5 Tesla and Excite HDxt 1.5 Tesla (GE Healthcare, Milwaukee, WI, USA) are commonly used for liver imaging. Computed tomography (CT) machines, such as TSX-306A and TSX-303A (Canon Healthcare, Otawara, Tochigi, Japan), are tightly scheduled for body imaging. LogiqE10 and E9 (GE Healthcare, Milwaukee, WI, USA) are commonly used for contrast-enhanced US (CEUS) of the liver. Azurin 7B20 (Philips Healthcare, Cambridge, MA, USA) and Artis Q (Siemens Healthineers, Bayern, Germany) are used for abdominal endovascular interventions.

Computed tomography and magnetic resonance imaging for focal liver lesions

A standard CT imaging protocol for focal liver lesion (FLL) involves a triphasic contrast-enhanced study, including arterial, portal venous, and delayed phases, to assess lesion vascularity and characteristics. An iodinated contrast agent is injected intravenously at a rate of 3.0 mL/s or higher. The bolus tracking method is used to ensure optimal timing for subsequent phases. Images acquired are reconstructed in axial, coronal, and sagittal planes.[4] Both dynamic CT and magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) protocols involve the rapid imaging of a specific region of the liver or areas containing FLLs, following the administration of an intravenous contrast agent, to evaluate perfusion and blood flow. Parameters such as scan delay, contrast administration rate, and image acquisition frequency, as well as MRI sequences including pre-contrast T1 mapping for dynamic contrast-enhanced MRI, should be appropriately selected to provide essential information on a lesion’s characteristics. Specific slice thicknesses for CT and MRI, temporal resolution considerations, and the use of various analysis models and subtraction images for quantitative assessment are also the important factors for a valuable imaging diagnosis.[5] A comprehensive liver MRI protocol for FLLs includes fat-suppressed and nonfat-suppressed T2-weighted images, in- and opposed-phase T1-weighted sequences, and dynamic pre- and post-contrast fat-suppressed T1-weighted images. In addition, diffusion-weighted imaging (DWI) and apparent diffusion coefficient (ADC) values are used for lesion detection and characterization.[6]

Precise timing of intravenous contrast agents (iodine for CT and gadolinium for MRI) is crucial for both contrast injection and image acquisition to capture the different phases of contrast enhancement (e.g., arterial, portal venous, and delayed phases).[7]

Contrast-enhanced ultrasound

CEUS is a commonly used technique in our department for evaluating an FLL. The contrast agent we used is Sonazoid©(perflubutane microbubbles, GE Healthcare, Milwaukee, WI, USA). A single intravenous dose of Sonazoid (0.12 μL/kg) is injected intravenously in a bolus method. Sonazoid was reconstituted in accordance with the manufacturer’s instructions.[8] The injection of Sonazoid was followed by a flush with 5–10 mL of 0.9% sodium chloride.

RESULTS

After a thorough discussion at a multidisciplinary meeting, MRI was suggested as a potentially diagnostic imaging. The US-detected 2.5-cm nodule was then demonstrated on an MRI study [Figure 1]. The lesion showed hypo-intensity on T2-weighted images with fat saturation, hyperintensity on DWI, mild hypo-intensity on ADC, no signal drop between in-phase and out-of-phase images, and early contrast washout on delay phase. Digital subtraction angiography (DSA) [Figure 2] was arranged to rule out HCC. However, the nodule showed an early arterial stain and a draining hepatic vein on the portovenous phase.

Magnetic resonance imaging of the hepatic nodule (arrows) shows no signal drop between in-phase (a) and out-of-phase (b) image, hypointensity on T2-weighted image with fat-saturation (c), hyperintensity on diffusion-weighted imaging (d), mild hypointensity on apparent diffusion coefficient imaging (e), and presence of early contrast washout on delay phase (f)

Digital subtraction angiography of the common hepatic artery shows the early arterial stain of the tumor (arrow) on the arterial phase (a) and a drainage hepatic vein (arrowhead) on portovenous phase (b)

Meanwhile, laboratory tests disclosed WBC, hemoglobin (Hb), platelet, albumin, total bilirubin, glutamic oxaloacetic transaminase, glutamic pyruvic transaminase, gamma-glutamyl transferase, prothrombin time, activated partial thromboplastin time, alpha-fetoprotein, carcinoembryonic antigen, and CA199 were all within the normal limits. Her hepatitis markers showed negative hepatitis B surface antigen, negative anti-Hbs Ab, and negative Anti-hepatitis C virus Ab. The patient also denied a medical history of hepatitis B.

In the grayscale US study [Figure 3], the nodule appeared mainly hypoechoic, especially in the peripheral portion, and a small focal area of iso-echogenicity in the central portion. The tumor showed increased blood flow with low resistance on color and spectral Doppler studies, probably due to shunting to the relatively low-pressurehepatic vein. Focal nodular hyperplasia was considered first. However, since the possibility of HCC cannot be completely excluded, she underwent US-guided biopsy for further investigation.

Gray-scale ultrasound (US) of the hepatic nodule on the transverse scan. The nodule (arrow) appears mainly hypoechogenicity, especially in the peripheral portion and focal area of isoechogenicity in the small central portion (a). The tumor shows increased blood flow in color Doppler US (b) and spectral Doppler US shows arterial flow in the feeding arteries with low resistance (RI = 0.45) (c), probably related to shunting to the low-pressure hepatic vein

On histopathological study of the representative specimens, the resected tissue fragments [Figure 4] measured up to 1 cm × 0.3 cm × 0.1 cm, with microscopic findings of liver tissue stripes with the presence of AML, which is composed predominantly of epithelioid myoid cells. Immunohistochemically, these cells are positive for human melanoma black-45, Melan-A, and smooth muscle actin.

Pathologic finding of the hepatic nodule. (a) At high power magnification, the tumor exhibits sheets of epithelioid myoid cells. (b) The tumor cells are positive for human melanoma black 45. (c and d) The tumor cells are positive for Melan-A (c) and Actin (d). HMB45: Human melanoma black 45

With the benign histopathological results, she was advised to have regular clinical and radiological follow-ups.

One year later, a follow-up US was done. At the additional CEUS using low mechanical-index (MI) technique (MI = 0.20), the lesion showed not only early parenchymal enhancement (18 s) but also a vividly enhanced rim (40 s), prominent drainage hepatic vein within 44 s after contrast injection, and early washout in venous phase and late vascular phase (92 s after contrast injection) [Figure 5]. The vividly enhanced rim indicates a benign process or metastasis, but not HCC.

Contrast-enhanced ultrasound shows a well-circumscribed lesion (arrowheads) with early enhancement on arterial phase (18 s after contrast injection) ([a] arrow), and prominent drainage hepatic veins ([b] arrowheads) within 24 s after contrast injection. A contrast-enhanced rim surrounding the hepatic nodule is noted at 40 s ([c] arrowheads). Early washout is noted in the late vascular phase (60 s after contrast injection). The drainage hepatic vein is demonstrated ([d] arrowheads)

The microbubbles were broken 23 min after contrast injection using a high-MI technique (MI = 1.2). The lesion was evident with relatively lower echogenicity on high MI CEUS imaging. At the same time, the rest of the normal liver parenchyma was significantly brightened due to the stimulated acoustic emission (SAE) effect during the high MI imaging process. The high MI CEUS pattern is indicative of a benign hepatic tumor.

She has had regular US follow-ups for her hepatic nodule at our hospital for at least 9 years. The lesion showed a slightly increased size [Figure 6] without malignant signs. She has been doing very well so far.

Gray-scale ultrasound (US) of the hepatic nodule in transverse view 7 years later. The tumor (arrows) appeared slightly increased in size (a). Increased blood flow on color Doppler US (b), and high peak flow on spectral Doppler US with low resistance (RI = 0.44) (c) are still noted

DISCUSSION

HEAML and HCC share some common imaging findings but have specific features for differential diagnosis. In DSA, both tumors have hypervascularity and tumor stains.[131415161718] However, HCCs show early drainage of contrast into the portal vein; in contrast, a HEAML shows a normal portal vein and scattered small pooling of contrast medium in the whole liver, which was suspected as dilatation of the peripheral hepatic arteries or small hemangiomas.[13]. In addition, HEAMLs with early drainage veins into both the hepatic and portal veins[14] or even arterioportal shunting[15] have been mentioned.

However, the vascularity of HEAMLs may not be demonstrated on contrast-enhanced CT (CECT) or contrast-enhanced MRI (CEMRI).

The grayscale US study of an epithelioid HEAML usually shows a nonspecific hypoechoic focal lesion in the liver.[131415]

The CEUS findings suggesting a HEAML include heterogeneous echotexture, increased vascularity in the arterial phase, parenchymal staining in the late vascular phase, and the presence of feeding and efferent vessels.[1314]

In HEAML, there is specific early drainage into the hepatic veins, which can be demonstrated using CEUS.[141516] The histological intratumoral arteriovenous connections[17] are compatible with the rich arterial flow and rapid drainage into the hepatic vein.

Furthermore, in the Kupffer phase, there is hyperenhancement on the peripheral area of HEAML, higher peak intensity, shorter time to peak on the time-intensity curve (TIC), and a larger wash-in area under the curve (AUC).[1516] A more prominent and dilated drainage vein may also contribute to a more confirmatory differential diagnosis of HEAML.[1819] Under high MI (MI = 1.2 in this instance), the microbubbles are broken immediately. Since there are still some functioning Kupffer cells remaining in HEAML, the lesion can show certain SAE effect and may appear relatively lower echogenicity on high-MI CEUS imaging, while the normal liver parenchyma shows significantly stronger echogenicity secondary to the SAE effect during the high-MI mode imaging. Thehigh-MI CEUS pattern is suggestive of a benign hepatic tumor because of the remaining Kupffer cells.[20]

We emphasize the usefulness of Sonazoid CEUS in the diagnosis of HEAML. Sonazoid is a microbubble US contrast agent that consists of microspheres containing perfluorobutane gas within a hydrogenated egg phosphatidyl-serine membrane. The Sonazoid microbubble has a median diameter of approximately 2.6 μm (range 2–5 μm) and exhibits good stability after reconstitution, with no significant changes in properties for at least 2 h.[21] Sonazoid CEUS is generally considered safe and well-tolerated for liver tumor evaluation. Sonazoid provides safe, nonionizing radiation, real-time imaging without nephrotoxic agents. Most adverse events are mild and self-limiting, such as abdominal pain and heart rate irregularity.[22]

A literature review shows the presence of early drainage of hepatic veins on CECT, CEMRI, or CEUS, a higher peak intensity, larger wash-in AUC, and shorter time to peak on TIC at CEUS are more in favor of HEAML rather than HCC. Although variants of the drainage vein have been reported, CEUS and DSA can help identify the vascular detail for demonstration of the feeding arteries and the drainage hepatic veins and thus help distinguish rare HEAML from the more common HCC.[18]

CONCLUSION

To the best of our knowledge, this is the first technical report of HEAML diagnosed using multimodality imaging techniques, including CEMRI, DSA, and CEUS studies. Both low- and high-MI methods were used at CEUS. The patient has been regularly followed for the hepatic nodule within the past nine years till now, which confirms the benign process of the lesion.
