Can Resting Segmental Strain Identify Obstructive Coronary Artery Disease in Chronic Coronary Syndrome Patients Referred to Coronary Artery Bypass Grafting?
Authors: Assami Rösner, Didrik Kjønås, Marie Leiros Sandberg, Håvard Dalen, Henrik Schirmer
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractIntroductionNoninvasive identification of patients with chronic coronary syndrome (CCS) with obstructive coronary artery disease (CAD) remains a clinical challenge. Strain imaging enables detection of subtle myocardial dysfunction at rest and may serve as a sensitive marker of multivessel or left main CAD. This study aimed to assess whether segmental systolic strain at rest can identify patients with obstructive CAD referred for coronary artery bypass grafting (CABG) and to compare diagnostic performance of tissue Doppler imaging strain with two-dimensional speckle-tracking echocardiography in a retrospective analysis of a selected CABG-referred cohort.MethodsThis retrospective study included 61 patients with CCS and obstructive CAD referred for CABG and 61 age- and sex-matched healthy controls from the population-based Nord-Trøndelag Health Study. All participants underwent echocardiography with both tissue Doppler and speckle-tracking strain analysis. The number of hypokinetic segments within one heart was counted, determined at different segmental peak longitudinal strain thresholds of −8% to −16%. The area under curve (AUC) using receiver operating characteristic analysis was used to evaluate diagnostic performance. Late gadolinium enhancement (LGE) cardiac magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) was used to assess myocardial scarring.ResultsLGE MRI confirmed that 78% of patients had no visible myocardial scar and that overall scar burden was low (2.8%). At a hypokinetic strain threshold of −8%, tissue Doppler strain demonstrated excellent diagnostic accuracy for identifying myocardial dysfunction in CABG-referred patients (AUC 0.95, 95% confidence interval [CI]: 0.91–1.00), performing at least as well as speckle-tracking strain (AUC: 0.79, 95% CI: 0.70–0.87). Tissue Doppler strain identified obstructive CAD with 95% sensitivity and 86% specificity. Comparable AUCs were found in a subgroup of 20 patients without coronary occlusion or myocardial scarring, indicating that regional hypokinesia was equally detectable in patients with normal EF and no LGE-defined scars.ConclusionSegmental strain imaging at rest – by both tissue Doppler and speckle tracking – can detect subclinical regional dysfunction in CABG-referred patients with obstructive CAD, even in patients with preserved global left ventricular function and no evidence of myocardial scarring. Tissue Doppler strain showed at least as high diagnostic accuracy as speckle-tracking strain. These findings underscore the clinical potential of resting segmental strain analysis as a sensitive, noninvasive tool for identifying obstructive CAD in this selected CABG-referred population.

Introduction

Noninvasive identification of obstructive coronary artery disease (CAD) in patients with chronic coronary syndrome (CCS) remains a clinical challenge. Current guidelines [1] recommend resting echocardiography for assessment of left ventricular (LV) function in patients with suspected CAD; it may provide diagnostic information beyond global LV performance by detecting subtle myocardial dysfunction that reflects obstructive CAD. A substantial proportion of patients referred to invasive coronary angiography are found to have no or non-obstructive CAD. To address this gap, segmental strain analysis has gained attention as it may provide more detailed insights into regional myocardial dysfunction, which could be particularly relevant in patients with obstructive CAD who exhibit subtle functional impairments not detectable by global strain measures.

Two-dimensional speckle-tracking echocardiography (2D STE) for strain analysis is now widely available on modern ultrasound systems. Numerous studies have shown that resting global/peak longitudinal strain (GLS/PLS) by 2D STE can identify patients with obstructive CAD [2–10]. An earlier meta-analysis reported a pooled diagnostic accuracy with an area under the curve (AUC) of approximately 0.8 [7], while subsequent work by our group indicated that high diagnostic accuracy was dependent on the disease severity [8].

Importantly, studies that stratified patients according to disease severity found that the diagnostic accuracy of resting strain imaging decreases in patients with low SYNTAX scores or single vessel disease, whereas patients with multivessel or left main CAD referred for coronary artery bypass grafting (CABG) often show more readily identifiable global or regional hypokinesia. [5, 6, 8, 11]. Some studies suggest that intermittent ischemia may lead to subtle myocardial dysfunction detectable by global and regional strain at rest [11–13].

Studies using global, regional, or combined strain parameters have achieved excellent diagnostic accuracies for multivessel or left main CAD (AUC: 0.90–0.95) [6, 8, 11, 14], with both global and regional longitudinal strain demonstrating strong discriminatory power. This gradient likely reflects the greater cumulative ischemic burden and myocardial stunning in advanced CAD. However, many studies included only limited numbers of patients with multivessel or left main CAD, and these findings therefore require confirmation in larger surgically referred cohorts.

Various analytical approaches have been explored to refine diagnostic performance [10, 12, 15], including segment-focused analyses, but evidence remains heterogeneous and method-dependent [8, 14]. Tissue Doppler imaging (TDI) was the first available technique for quantifying myocardial strain. Despite its known limitations – angle dependency, noise, and time-consuming analysis – no study has conclusively shown that TDI strain is inferior to 2D STE strain. Nevertheless, publications on TDI strain have become scarce following the widespread adoption of 2D STE strain. Evidence on resting TDI strain in CCS with obstructive CAD, and especially direct comparisons between TDI and 2D STE strain, remains limited [16].

The present study therefore aimed to evaluate segmental myocardial deformation by both TDI and 2D strain in a selected CABG cohort and to compare myocardial function with the distribution of scar tissue by magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) and with that of age- and sex-matched control groups. Thus, the novelty of the present work is a direct, segment-level comparison of resting TDI and 2D STE strain in a CABG-referred cohort obstructive CAD, including evaluation against late gadolinium enhancement (LGE)-defined scar distribution and assessment of modality-specific dropout/artifact effects.

Methods

Patients and Control Subjects

This retrospective study is based on the PRODOS study (PRedictive value Of DObutamine stress-echocardiography with Strain rate imaging in patients with CAD, University Hospital of North Norway, Tromsø) which included patients between 2005 and 2008. The PRODOS cohort included consecutive CCS patients with obstructive CAD who underwent dobutamine stress echocardiography and LGE cardiac MRI prior to CABG [17]. Echocardiographic examinations were performed 1–7 days before the planned surgery.

Exclusion criteria were chronic obstructive pulmonary disease, asthma, atrial fibrillation, unstable angina, significant valvular heart disease, myocardial infarction within 3 months prior to CABG, bundle branch block, and contraindications to MRI such as pacemaker implantation, impaired renal function, or claustrophobia. Moderately reduced ejection fraction or a previous myocardial infarction were not considered exclusion criteria.

Results were compared with a PRODOS reference cohort consisting of 15 healthy individuals who underwent the same resting echocardiography protocol. In addition, a second reference cohort was created using age- and sex-matched strain echocardiography data from the Nord-Trøndelag Health Study (HUNT) [18]. From this dataset, 61 age- and sex-matched healthy controls were randomly selected from a total of 1,266 participants examined between 2006 and 2008. All selected individuals were free from hypertension, diabetes, known cardiovascular disease, valvular or myocardial pathology, and arrhythmias [18].

Echocardiographic Data Acquisition

Echocardiographic acquisition protocols for PRODOS and HUNT have been described previously [17, 18]. In brief, resting echocardiography included standard apical views with both TDI and gray-scale recordings suitable for segmental strain analysis. Because the cohorts were acquired in different studies, ultrasound systems, acquisition settings, and frame rates differed between PRODOS and HUNT (details provided in online suppl. Methods; for all online suppl. material, see https://doi.org/10.1159/000553322).

MRI

Patients in the PRODOS study underwent late gadolinium enhancement MRI to quantify myocardial scarring as a sign of prior myocardial infarction. All images were analyzed using the standard 16-segment model applied in echocardiography, as previously described [17].

Myocardial scar burden was assessed visually based on the segmental transmurality of the scarring: no scar (LGE = 0%); LGE 1–25%; LGE 26–50%; LGE 51–75%; and LGE 76–100%, averaged across the numbers of scarred segments per heart. Echocardiographic and MRI analyses were performed blinded to each other’s results.

Conventional Echocardiographic Measures

Conventional echocardiographic parameters were analyzed by one experienced observer in each dataset. End-systolic and end-diastolic LV volumes and ejection fraction were calculated using the biplane Simpson method. Stroke volume and cardiac output were derived from LV volumes and heart rate.

TDI Analysis (PRODOS and HUNT)

TDI strain analyses were performed separately in PRODOS and HUNT by experienced observers using the original study-specific software platforms and analysis workflows previously described [17, 18]. Because different software and readers were used in the two cohorts, this was considered in interpretation of between-cohort comparisons (see Limitations and online suppl. Methods).

Speckle-Tracking Strain in PRODOS and HUNT

A single experienced observer analyzed 2D STE for both the PRODOS and HUNT studies using commercially available software (eSie Velocity Vector Imaging; Siemens Medical Solutions, Mountain View, CA, USA). 2D STE analysis was performed using the same software and observer across cohorts. Tracking quality was visually verified, and segments with unreliable tracking were excluded. Detailed tracking procedures are provided in the online supplementary Methods.

Definition and Extraction of Peak Strain Values

Peak strain values from the HUNT and PRODOS studies were extracted from all 2D STE and TDI strain curves using identical methodological definitions. A 16-segment model was used for final analysis.

The beginning of the cardiac cycle was defined at the R-wave peak. To exclude post-systolic deformation, strain during LV ejection time was analyzed (defined by aortic valve opening and closure on continuous-wave Doppler recordings). The highest absolute (positive or negative) strain value occurring between aortic valve opening and closure was defined as the PLS during systole.

Statistical Analysis

The study dataset comprised 61 patients, 61 age- and sex-matched healthy control subjects, and 15 healthy volunteers, all examined with both TDI strain and 2D STE strain analysis. All statistical analyses were performed using IBM SPSS Statistics, version 24.0 (IBM Corp., Armonk, NY, USA).

Comparisons of continuous variables between two groups were performed using unpaired two-tailed Student’s t tests. Categorical variables were compared using the chi-square test. In all analyses, p values <0.05 were considered statistically significant.

Segmental hypokinesia was defined according to varying thresholds of PLS at −8%, −10%, −12%, −14%, and −16%. For each threshold, the number of hypokinetic segments per LV was counted and expressed as a percentage of total segments (number of hypokinetic segments/16).

Receiver operating characteristic (ROC) curve analysis was performed using the percentage of LV hypokinetic segments at each hypokinesia threshold. Data are presented as AUC of the ROC curve with corresponding 95% confidence intervals and cutoff values representing the percentage of hypokinetic segments. Cutoff values were selected from ROC analysis based on the highest combined sensitivity and specificity (with the highest sensitivity chosen in case of ties). Patients without myocardial scarring were chosen for a subgroup analysis in order to investigate the relation of scar tissue and detectability of myocardial dysfunction.

Reproducibility

Reproducibility data for TDI strain were based on previously published assessments in the PRODOS and HUNT studies [17, 18]. Reproducibility of 2D strain analyses was reassessed for the present study in randomly selected participants from both cohorts using the previously established re-test protocol. Detailed reproducibility procedures and metrics (ICC and level of agreement) are provided in the online supplementary Methods.

Results

Patient Characteristics

Of the 63 recruited patients, 61 were ultimately included in the analysis. One patient was excluded because of atrial fibrillation and another due to logistical constraints. In 4 patients, MRI could not be performed – two because of claustrophobia and two due to logistic reasons.

The final cohort consisted of 58 men (95%) and 3 women (5%). Female patients referred for CABG were generally older and more likely to meet exclusion criteria, primarily due to a higher prevalence of atrial fibrillation. In the HUNT study, 61 patients were available for 2D strain analysis, whereas only 41 had prior TDI data.

Similarly, the right panel of Figure 3 and Table 3 and online supplementary Table S2 show the ROC analysis results for 2D-derived PLS. Subendocardial 2D strain was also evaluated, yielding nonsignificantly lower AUCs compared with total myocardial 2D strain. AUCs differed significantly between TDI and 2D STE at the −10% (p = 0.0023) and −12% (p = 0.0288) hypokinesia thresholds.

A subgroup of 20 patients without coronary occlusions or prior myocardial scars was analyzed separately using the same cutoff values (−8%, −10%, −12%, −14%, and −16%), producing AUCs of 0.934, 0.929, 0.919, 0.880, and 0.732 for TDI strain, and 0.769, 0.812, 0.846, 0.836, and 0.826 for myocardial 2D strain, respectively. These results were broadly consistent with those obtained in the overall patient cohort.

When excluding the same segments that were omitted from the TDI analysis, AUCs for myocardial 2D STE at −8%, −10%, −12%, −14%, and −16% cutoff values were 0.785, 0.809, 0.843, 0.826, and 0.823, respectively. Comparable findings were obtained in the healthy control group of 15 participants from the PRODOS study.

Discussion

The present study suggests that regionally reduced strain, within several hypokinetic segments, shows high diagnostic discrimination for identifying obstructive CAD in patients referred to CABG. Both TDI and 2D STE strains displayed strong diagnostic performance, with AUC values of 0.96 and 0.86, respectively. Importantly, regional dysfunction at rest could be detected in the majority of patients with obstructive CAD, even in the absence of scar tissue or coronary occlusions in this selected cohort.

Hypokinesia in CABG Candidates

Detection rates from segmental strain analyses depend on [1] the accuracy of the test method [2], the presence of regional functional impairment within the cohort, and [3] the reliability of the reference standard for obstructive CAD. The diagnostic standard for obstructive CAD has improved considerably with the introduction of fractional flow reserve assessment. Recent studies, including the present one, reporting AUC values between 0.86 and 0.98, have shown high diagnostic discrimination in selected study populations [10, 21]. It can therefore be concluded that regional hypokinesia is present in the majority of patients referred to CABG and represents a useful marker of obstructive CAD for this patient group.

Myocardial stunning, first described by Heyndrickx et al. in 1975 [22], refers to persistent myocardial dysfunction after brief, reversible coronary occlusion, despite the absence of structural or metabolic injury. Such stunned myocardium can only be detected by functional imaging. Hypokinetic regions in patients with obstructive but nonocclusive CAD likely reflect repetitive ischemic episodes downstream of significant coronary stenoses. Traditional parameters such as ejection fraction or wall motion scoring are not sensitive enough to detect these subtle changes [23].

The present and previous studies suggest that strain imaging provides a sensitive marker of both global [2, 6, 7, 9, 11, 24] and regional hypokinesia [13, 14, 25–27]. In our cohort, 59% of patients were free from detectable myocardial scars and had no history of myocardial infarction, and 54% had no coronary occlusions. Nevertheless, 96% demonstrated either angina or electrocardiographic abnormalities during stress testing [23]. Reversible regional hypokinesia at rest, despite the absence of infarction or scarring, is therefore consistent with myocardial stunning.

In a subgroup of 20 patients without scar tissue, infarction history, or coronary occlusion, results were nearly identical to the 41 patients with small amounts of scar tissue, which is compatible with repetitive myocardial stunning. This observation aligns with prior studies in patients with CCS showing recovery of regional function three to 6 months after percutaneous coronary intervention but not immediately post-revascularization [28]. Additionally, micro-scarring undetectable by late gadolinium enhancement MRI may contribute, as suggested by earlier findings in the PRODOS cohort showing only limited improvement in resting strain following CABG [17]. Whether due to stunning or microscopic scarring, regional hypokinesia at rest remains a frequent finding associated with multivessel or left main obstructive CAD.

Strain as a Marker of Obstructive CAD

Several previous studies have used reduced PLS to detect obstructive CAD [2, 6, 9, 11, 24, 29]. A meta-analysis reported an overall AUC above 0.8, though differences according to disease severity were not considered [7]. The largest included study, by Biering-Sørensen et al., analyzed 296 chest pain patients and defined obstructive CAD as ≥70% area stenosis; resting PLS added significant diagnostic value (AUC 0.84) [2]. More recent investigations that considered CAD severity have consistently shown that high diagnostic accuracies are mainly achieved in multivessel/left main cohorts [8, 10, 11, 14]. Most studies remain underpowered to detect negative results and therefore do not distinguish between disease severities. However, recent data have demonstrated a correlation between PLS and SYNTAX scores and low diagnostic accuracies for 1 vessel disease or PCI-treated patients [10, 11].

In our study, hypokinetic regions were observed independently of coronary occlusion, suggesting that regional dysfunction may occur beyond the vascular territory of the most severe stenosis. In multivessel disease with collateralization, areas of reduced strain may not correspond directly to the occluded vessel, consistent with previous findings [30].

Differences between TDI and 2D Strain

TDI strain quantifies deformation from tissue velocities and segmental length, whereas 2D STE strain derives deformation from speckle displacement across the cardiac cycle. Only a few studies have directly compared these techniques in patients with CAD [16]. Interestingly, test accuracies for TDI and 2D strain were broadly comparable across several analytic conditions.

In the present study, the HUNT cohort (TDI) and the PRODOS cohort (2D) were analyzed with different systems and investigators, yet comparable results were obtained. Furthermore, when 15 healthy subjects from the PRODOS study were analyzed using identical ultrasound systems, software and observers, ROC results remained consistent, supporting directional consistency of the findings, although residual methodological confounding cannot be excluded.

Strain imaging – particularly segmental analysis – is susceptible to artifacts. The handling of noise and the criteria for discarding poor-quality segments strongly influences the diagnostic accuracy. TDI artifacts (e.g., reverberations or angle deviations) often produce visually apparent distortions and are therefore easily identified and excluded. In contrast, 2D STE artifacts may generate subtler curves that mimic pathology.

One possible explanation is that artifact-related abnormalities in 2D STE were more difficult to distinguish from true pathology despite visual quality control. Assuming that the frequency of noise and missing segments should be similar across techniques, the discrepancy between 3% (2D STE) and 8% (TDI) discarded segments is notable. When identical segments were removed from both datasets, 2D strain results did not improve, suggesting that artifact characteristics differ fundamentally between methods.

Moreover, 2D strain incorporates spatial smoothing, which may underestimate peak strain values in healthy segments and overestimate strain in dysfunctional segments [31]. TDI strain, calculated from independent ROIs, may be more sensitive to subtle regional dysfunction. Differences in vendor-specific algorithms further affect test performance. It should also be noted that older 2D datasets – such as those in this study – were noisier, particularly in apical regions. The higher diagnostic accuracies in recent reports likely reflect improved image quality, software-based noise filtering, and refined tracking algorithms.

In the HUNT study, up to 30% of TDI segments were discarded to ensure reliable measurements of normal segmental strain, whereas only 8% were excluded in the PRODOS dataset. This discrepancy may reflect differences in acquisition protocols – standard apical (two-wall) views in HUNT vs. wall-by-wall imaging in PRODOS – and in manual versus automated ROI placement. Despite the high exclusion rate in healthy subjects, test quality remained unaffected, as few strain values were hypokinetic. In contrast, excessive segment rejection in patient cohorts would likely reduce test accuracy and clinical usability. Segments with inadequate image quality were excluded and no imputation was performed. Obvious artifacts were more readily identified and excluded in TDI than in 2D STE, where artifact-related abnormalities may be subtler, differential segment dropout may have affected the apparent balance between sensitivity and specificity of the two methods. As diagnostic performance depends on the ratio of normal to pathological segments per patient, the number of analyzable segments is important for high accuracy of the test.

Taken together, our results highlight the methodological challenges associated with both 2D STE and TDI strain analysis. While TDI remains less user-friendly and is rarely used in clinical practice, its diagnostic performance in this dataset was at least comparable to that of 2D STE strain for assessment of segmental dysfunction related to obstructive CAD. Modern STE platforms with refined tracking algorithms may reduce these modality differences, though TDI could maintain advantages in stress echocardiography where 2D tracking quality declines with higher heart rates. Moreover, spatial smoothing may impair 2D STE’s to identify segmental hypokinesia relative to TDI.

Global longitudinal strain was not assessed here due to methodological limitations of the TDI data, but prior work suggests it achieves similar discrimination to segmental approaches in broader cohorts.

Limitations of the Study

This retrospective, two-center study has inherent limitations. The age- and sex-matched HUNT cohort was analyzed using a different TDI system and by separate observers. Restricting inclusion to CABG candidates versus healthy controls limits generalizability to the broader chest pain population. The study population was predominantly male (only 3 female patients), which limits generalizability and precludes sex-specific analyses. In the recently published RECCAD study, evaluating in an unselected chest pain cohort; the number of CABG patients was too small to provide a comparator group of similar size to the present study. Although invasive coronary angiography was not performed in the control group, the likelihood of obstructive CAD in asymptomatic individuals without risk factors is estimated to be below 5%. Obstructive CAD was defined angiographically, without systematic physiological assessment (FFR/iFR), which should be considered when interpreting strain as a marker of lesion-related functional significance. However, the consistently reduced myocardial function in the patient group, compared with both reference groups, suggests that obstructive CAD in the study cohort was unlikely to have been substantially overestimated.

Myocardial scar burden was assessed visually by segmental transmurality, rather than by contemporary quantitative late gadolinium enhancement (LGE) methods (e.g., % LV mass, 5 standard deviation thresholding, or full width at half maximum), due to historical and technical constraints; scar burden may therefore have been underestimated, and comparability with contemporary CMR studies is limited. The absence of parametric mapping techniques (e.g., native T1/ECV) in this study limits our ability to assess diffuse myocardial remodeling, which may contribute to reduced strain in segments without visible LGE.

Residual confounding due to inter-cohort differences in ultrasound systems, acquisition settings, software, and observers cannot be excluded, particularly for TDI strain. However, for 2D STE just one reader used the same software for all analyses. Given the modest sample size and study design, this investigation is primarily descriptive, intended to enhance understanding of myocardial stunning in obstructive CAD. Nevertheless, it provides valid intra-sample comparisons of TDI and 2D STE as diagnostic modalities.

Clinical Implication

The present findings align closely with prior publications demonstrating the high predictive value of resting strain imaging for detecting multivessel or left main obstructive CAD [8, 10, 11]. The present study extends these observations in a larger CABG-referred cohort. In the present cohort, reduced myocardial function was in the vast majority of CABG-referred patients. By contrast, we previously showed, that obstructive CAD in patients without previously known CAD could not be identified, even by refining imaging and data analysis methods [8]. Therefore, these findings cannot be extrapolated to diagnostic triage in unselected CAD populations.

Consistent with previous studies, our results suggest that regional hypokinesia at rest is present in nearly all patients with CABG-referred obstructive CAD, even in the absence of scar tissue. Therefore, regionally reduced strain may be a sensitive marker of myocardial dysfunction associated with angiographically defined obstructive CAD in CABG-referred patients; its ability to identify physiologically significant lesions requires further studies with systematic FFR/iFR assessment [8, 13, 26, 27]. Future studies should prospectively evaluate resting segmental strain in broader, less selected chest pain populations and integrate it with contemporary coronary physiology and CT-based approaches to clarify its incremental clinical value.

Conclusion

This study suggests that regional hypokinesia detected by both TDI and 2D STE strain provides high diagnostic discrimination for identifying obstructive CAD in patients referred to CABG with healthy reference groups as comparator. Importantly, these findings were equally observed in patients with normal ejection fraction and no evidence of myocardial scarring, indicating that strain imaging can reveal subclinical regional dysfunction not detectable by conventional measures. Thus, resting strain – particularly segmental analysis – may be a useful noninvasive marker of myocardial dysfunction associated with angiographically defined obstructive CAD in this selected cohort.
