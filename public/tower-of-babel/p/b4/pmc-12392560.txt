The prevalence and associated factors of alcohol consumption among pregnant women in Africa: a systematic review and meta-analysis
Authors: Fantahun Andualem, Mamaru Melkam, Girum Nakie, Techilo Tinsae, Setegn Fentahun, Gidey Rtbey, Girmaw Medfu Takelle, Getasew Kibralew, Berihun Agegn Mengistie, Getachew Muluye Gedef, Amlaku Nigusie Yirsaw, Gebresilassie Tadesse
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundAlcohol consumption is a major global risk factor for poor health, contributing to a wide range of physical, mental, and social problems. Among vulnerable populations, alcohol use during pregnancy is a particularly critical concern due to its harmful effects on both maternal and fetal health. This issue is especially pressing in low- and middle-income countries (LMICs), where alcohol consumption is increasing and healthcare systems often lack the capacity to address related consequences. In Africa, characterized by diverse cultural norms and socioeconomic disparities, the burden of alcohol use during pregnancy remains inadequately understood. While several studies have explored this issue across various African settings, their findings have been inconsistent. Therefore, this systematic review and meta-analysis aimed to estimate the pooled prevalence of alcohol consumption during pregnancy and identify associated factors among pregnant women in Africa.MethodsThis study followed the PRISMA 2020 guidelines and was registered in PROSPERO (CRD42024557559). A comprehensive search was conducted across PubMed, MEDLINE, EMBASE, Scopus, and the Cochrane Library. Eligible cross-sectional studies reporting alcohol consumption among pregnant women in Africa were included. The methodological quality of each study was assessed using the Joanna Briggs Institute (JBI) critical appraisal checklist. Only studies scoring five or higher were included. Data extraction was done using a structured Excel sheet. Publication bias was assessed using funnel plots and Egger’s test.ResultsA total of 21 studies from various African countries involving 11,726 pregnant women were included. The pooled prevalence of alcohol consumption during pregnancy was 28.33% (95% CI: 22.68–33.99%). Subgroup analysis showed prevalence estimates of 34.09% in Ghana, 29.19% in Ethiopia, and 19.80% in Uganda. Significant associated factors included rural residence, lack of formal education, poor social support, family history of mental illness, history of abortion, lack of awareness, pre-pregnancy alcohol use, partner alcohol use, and unplanned pregnancy.ConclusionThis systematic review and meta-analysis found that the pooled prevalence of alcohol consumption among pregnant women was high. This study provides valuable insights that can inform future public health initiatives and research on this critical topic.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12888-025-07260-x.Keywords: Prevalence, Alcohol use, Alcohol consumption, Associated factors, Pregnant women, Africa

Introduction

Alcohol consumption is one of the leading risk factors for poor health globally, contributing to a wide range of physical, mental, and social health problems [1, 2]. According to the World Health Organization (WHO), alcohol consumption is a major contributor to the global burden of disease, with over 3 million deaths attributed to alcohol-related causes each year [2]. Alcohol consumption has raised significant health concerns, especially when it comes to vulnerable populations such as pregnant women. Alcohol has a profound effect on both the mother and the fetus, and its use during pregnancy is associated with various health complications [1, 3]. Alcohol consumption among pregnant women is a pressing public health issue, particularly in low and middle income countries (LMICs), where the prevalence of alcohol consumption is rising [2, 4]. This issue is of particular importance in Africa, a continent with significant diversity in terms of cultural norms, socioeconomic status, and access to healthcare.

The effects of alcohol are particularly concerning during pregnancy, as it has the potential to cause fetal alcohol spectrum disorders (FASD), which are characterized by birth defects, neurodevelopmental issues, and physical and cognitive impairments in children [1, 3]. It also increases the likelihood of preterm birth, miscarriage, stillbirth, and other complications [1–3, 5]. Furthermore, pregnancy-related alcohol consumption can result in behavioral and mental health problems for the child both during early years and later in life [1, 3, 6]. Pregnant women may drink alcohol without fully understanding the extent of its harmful effects [7]. Alcohol can cross the placenta, and the blood alcohol content of the fetus may be the same as, or even higher than, that of the mother, which can adversely affect fetal development [8–10]. The first trimester of pregnancy is especially crucial, as alcohol consumption at this stage can seriously affect the health and neurodevelopment of the unborn child [11, 12]. As per the World Health Organization (WHO) and the Centers for Disease Control and Prevention (CDC), there is no safe level, type, or timing of alcohol consumption during pregnancy [2, 13]. Despite this, alcohol is often perceived as less harmful compared to other substances [14].

Alcohol consumption among pregnant women found in systematic reviews and meta-analyses carried out in East Africa [15], and Sub-Saharan Africa [16] using different study designs such as cross-sectional, case-control, and cohort study designs. A qualitative study conducted in Uganda revealed that women hold diverse opinions about pregnant mothers who drink, and some are drawn to alcohol consumption due to social influences, such as alcohol production for income or participating in customary celebrations centered around alcohol [17]. A prospective cohort study conducted in Ethiopia, the incidence rates of low birth weight, preterm birth, and stillbirth were found to be 12.63%, 6.05%, and 4.27%, respectively, in pregnant women who consumed alcohol [9]. In the general population, the estimated prevalence of alcohol intake during pregnancy ranged from 2.2% [2]. The prevalence of alcohol consumption during pregnancy in African countries has been reported range from 8.1% [18] to as high as 58% [19].

Socio-demographic characteristics are strongly correlated with alcohol consumption [13]. As women’s alcohol consumption rises alongside changing gender roles and economic development, the health burden related to alcohol consumption is becoming a significant public health concern [20, 21]. Public health initiatives, such as labeling alcoholic products with warnings about the risks of alcohol consumption during pregnancy, could help reduce alcohol-related harm [22].

Although individual studies on alcohol consumption during pregnancy have been conducted in Africa, their findings have been highly inconsistent. Furthermore, there is a lack of comprehensive data on the pooled prevalence of alcohol consumption disorder among pregnant women in Africa. Therefore, this systematic review and meta-analysis aims to determine the pooled prevalence of alcohol consumption and its associated factors among pregnant women in Africa.

Materials and methods

Study approval and procedures

This systematic review and meta-analysis adhered to rigorous ethical and methodological standards, ensuring transparency and the proper handling of data. As this study involved aggregating previously published data from individual studies, no direct interaction with participants was required. Nevertheless, all studies included in this review had undergone ethical review by their respective Institutional Review Boards (IRBs) or Ethical Review Committees (ERCs) in compliance with local regulations. These studies obtained informed consent from participants where applicable and followed ethical guidelines for research involving human subjects. Since this is a secondary data analysis, ethical approval for this specific review was not required. However, we ensured that all the primary studies included had obtained proper ethical clearances.

Study registration

This systematic review and meta-analysis was registered with the International Prospective Register of Systematic Reviews (PROSPERO) to ensure transparency and avoid duplication of efforts. The registration number is CRD42024557559.

Adherence to guidelines

The systematic review and meta-analysis followed the Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Reviews and Meta-Analyses (PRISMA 2020) guidelines [23] (Supplementary File 1) for conducting systematic reviews and meta-analyses. This ensures that all aspects of the review were conducted with rigorous, transparent, and reproducible methods. These guidelines include a structured approach for identifying relevant studies, data extraction, quality assessment, and synthesis of results.

Data selection and inclusion criteria

The study selection process was conducted systematically and transparently. A comprehensive search of multiple electronic databases, including PubMed, MEDLINE, EMBASE, Cochrane Library, and Scopus, was conducted on July 30, 2024 using predefined keywords (Supplementary File 2). This search was limited to peer-reviewed articles in the English language, published from January 2000 to July 2024, reporting on alcohol consumption among pregnant women in Africa. Duplicates were removed using EndNote X20, and titles and abstracts were screened to identify studies that met the inclusion criteria. Full-text articles of potentially eligible studies were reviewed to confirm their eligibility. The final selection of studies was made through consensus between the reviewers, with any disagreements resolved through discussion.

Study selection process

The searching and screening of papers was done independently by two authors Fantahun Andualem (FA) and Gebresilassie Tadesse (GT) using a standardized data extraction form. This form captured key study characteristics such as author(s), publication year, study setting, study design, and country of origin, as well as prevalence data on alcohol consumption among pregnant women and associated factors. The quality of the included studies was assessed using the Joanna Briggs Institute (JBI).

Study quality assessment

Girum Nakie (GN) and Getachew Muluye Gedef (GMG), the two authors, evaluated the original research’s quality independently using the Joanna Briggs Institute (JBI) [24]. The research quality of the cross-sectional studies included in this review was assessed methodologically using JBI critical appraisal checklist. This checklist evaluates the methodological rigor of studies based on nine key criteria. The first criterion examines whether the target population was clearly defined, ensuring that the study specified the characteristics and selection process for participants. The second criterion looks at whether the sampling method was clearly described, assessing if the participants were chosen through an appropriate and transparent process. The third criterion evaluates whether the study subjects included in the analysis were properly accounted for, ensuring that no eligible participants were excluded without justification. The fourth criterion focuses on whether the outcome of interest, such as alcohol consumption, was measured using valid and reliable methods. The fifth criterion assesses the appropriateness of the statistical analyses employed to evaluate the study’s outcomes, ensuring that the methods used were suitable for the data. The sixth criterion examines whether the sample size was sufficiently justified to ensure the results were statistically meaningful. The seventh criterion evaluates whether the study subjects were representative of the broader population of pregnant women in Africa, ensuring that the findings can be generalized. The eighth criterion looks at whether there was a description of non-respondents or dropouts, considering any potential biases introduced by participant loss. Finally, the ninth criterion assesses whether ethical approval was obtained for the study and whether proper informed consent procedures were followed to protect participant rights and privacy. Studies that scored 5 or higher out of 9 on these criteria were included in the final analysis, ensuring that only studies with a reasonable degree of methodological rigor were considered in this systematic review and meta-analysis.

Data extraction

After screening the studies that satisfied the qualifying requirements and had quality of assessment scores of five or higher, the data was retrieved using a preformatted Microsoft Excel file. Using a consistent data extraction format, the two authors, Mamaru Melkam (MM) and Setegn Fentahun (SF), separately extracted all the required data from the papers. The following elements were present in the data extraction format: The data extraction format contained the following information: the name of the first author, the year the study was published, the nation where the study was carried out, the study setting, the study design, the alcohol use assessment tool, the sample size of the participants, the prevalence of alcohol use, and associated factors.

Data synthesis and analysis

For the meta-analysis, a random-effects model was used to calculate pooled prevalence rates of alcohol consumption among pregnant women. Statistical heterogeneity was assessed using the I² statistic, which was interpreted as low (≤ 25%), moderate (25–50%), substantial (50–75%), or high (> 75%) heterogeneity [25, 26]. Sensitivity analyses were conducted to evaluate the robustness of the findings. Publication bias was assessed using a funnel plot [27] and Egger’s regression test [28], with a p-value < 0.05 indicating the presence of bias. If bias was detected, a trim and fill analysis was conducted to adjust for this bias. Subgroup analyses were performed based on study country, setting (community-based vs. institutional-based), and the assessment tool used for measuring alcohol use, in order to explore sources of heterogeneity in the data.

Results

Description of included studies

A total of 1,235 papers were identified through database searches. After removing 146 duplicates, 1,040 papers were excluded based on title and abstract screening. An additional 21 papers were excluded after full-text review due to ineligibility. Furthermore, seven papers were removed: four due to missing required data [29–32] and three because they were duplicates [33–35]. Ultimately, 21 studies met the inclusion criteria and were included in the final systematic review and meta-analysis (Fig. 1).

PRISMA flowchart of review search on the prevalence of alcohol consumption among pregnant women in Africa

Study quality assessment

To assess the quality of the selected articles, the Joanna Briggs Institute (JBI) quality evaluation standards were applied. Each study included in this analysis achieved a JBI score of 5 or higher, indicating that all selected papers meets the criteria for good quality. Further details on the quality assessment process and individual scores for each article can be found in Supplementary File 3.

Characteristics of included studies

This review incorporated 21 cross-sectional studies conducted across African countries, with a total of 11,726 participants. More than half of the studies were conducted in Ethiopia [18, 19, 36–45], followed by two studies each in Uganda [17, 46] and Ghana [47, 48], One study was conducted in each of the following countries: São Tomé and Príncipe [49], Nigeria [50], Zambia [51], Tanzania [52], and South Africa [53]. In terms of study setting, 13 studies were conducted in institutional health services, seven in community settings, and one in a population-based area. Various assessment tools were used to measure alcohol consumption among pregnant women. Five studies utilized the Alcohol Use Disorder Identification Test (AUDIT) [17, 38, 41, 42, 52], while four studies used the Alcohol Use Disorder Identification Test Consumption (AUDIT-C) [18, 37, 43, 45]. Three studies employed standardized questionnaires [46, 49, 50], while two studies each used T-ACE (T-tolerance, A-annoyed, C-cut down, E-eye-opener) [39, 51] and the Alcohol, Smoking, and Substance Involvement Test (ASSIST) [44, 53]. Other tools included CAGE (C-cut down, A-annoyed, G-guilty feeling, E-eye opener) [36], a structured questionnaire [47] and self-reported (yes/no) questionnaire [40]. Two studies did not report the assessment tool used [19, 48] (Table 1).

The pooled prevalence of alcohol consumption among pregnant women

Numerous studies have examined alcohol consumption during pregnancy, with reported prevalence rates ranging from 8.1 to 58%. In this meta-analysis, the pooled prevalence of alcohol consumption among pregnant women in Africa was found to be 28.33% (95% CI: 22.68–33.99%) (Fig. 2). The meta-analysis was conducted using a random-effects model due to the substantial heterogeneity among the included studies (I² = 98.2%, p < 0.001).

Forest plot of the pooled prevalence of alcohol consumption among pregnant women in Africa

Subgroup analysis

Given the significant heterogeneity in the pooled prevalence of alcohol consumption (I² = 98.2%, p < 0.001), a subgroup analysis was performed based on study country, study setting, and assessment tool. The highest pooled prevalence of alcohol consumption among pregnant women was observed in Ghana at 34.09% (I² = 98.2%, p < 0.001), followed by Ethiopia at 29.19% (I² = 98.9%, p < 0.001) and Uganda at 19.80% (I² = 88%, p < 0.004). Regarding study setting, the pooled prevalence of alcohol consumption was higher in community-based studies (40.85%, I² = 96.3%, p < 0.001) compared to institutional-based studies (22.02%, I² = 97.2%, p < 0.001). An analysis based on the assessment tool used in the studies showed that the highest pooled prevalence was reported in studies using the Alcohol Use Disorder Identification Test (AUDIT) at 32.02% (I² = 97.4%, p < 0.001), followed by those using the Alcohol Use Disorder Identification Test Consumption (AUDIT-C) at 23.93% (I² = 98.9%, p < 0.001) (Figs. 3, 4 and 5).

A leave-out-one sensitivity analysis

A sensitivity analysis was conducted to assess the contribution of each individual study to the pooled prevalence of alcohol consumption among pregnant women. This involved systematically removing one study at a time and re-evaluating the overall prevalence to examine its impact on heterogeneity.

The results (Table 3; Fig. 8) indicate that the removal of any single study did not significantly alter the pooled prevalence, which remained within a range of 26.83–29.35%. This suggests that the findings of this systematic review and meta-analysis are robust and not unduly influenced by any single study.

Associated factors analysis

Using an adjusted odds ratio (AOR) in logistic regression analysis, we identified significant socio-demographic and other characteristics linked to alcohol consumption in pregnant women (Table 1). When a factor was reported in two or more studies, a pooled analysis was conducted to determine the overall effect. The individual studies identified several factors associated with alcohol consumption in pregnant women, including poor social support [36, 38, 42], a family history of mental illness [36, 43], a history of abortion [18, 36, 41, 43], living in rural areas [19, 44], lack of awareness of the harmful effect of alcohol consumption [37, 41, 45, 50], pre-pregnancy alcohol consumption [18, 37, 38, 42, 45, 50, 52], a partner uses alcohol [37, 39, 45, 51], unplanned pregnancy [18, 39, 41, 42], and not having formal education [38, 41].

In terms of demographic characteristics, pregnant women living in rural areas were significantly more likely to develop alcohol consumption (AOR = 6.95; 95% CI: 1.16, 41.77), as were those without formal education (AOR = 12.13; 95% CI: 2.57, 57.16). Regarding behavioral and clinical factors, poor social support (AOR = 6.12; 95% CI: 4.21, 8.90), a family history of mental illness (AOR = 7.83; 95% CI: 3.64, 16.84), a history of abortion (AOR = 5.67; 95% CI: 4.00, 8.03), lack of awareness of the harmful effects of alcohol consumption (AOR = 6.21; 95% CI: 4.80, 8.03), pre-pregnancy alcohol consumption (AOR = 5.82; 95% CI: 4.15, 8.17), having a partner who uses alcohol (AOR = 3.88; 95% CI: 2.91, 5.19), and experiencing an unplanned pregnancy (AOR = 3.84; 95% CI: 2.80, 5.27) were all significantly associated with alcohol consumption among pregnant women (Fig. 9).

Discussion

The prevalence of alcohol consumption among pregnant women varies significantly depending on geographic location, socio-demographic factors, cultural norms, and access to healthcare. In many high-income countries, the prevalence of alcohol consumption during pregnancy has been well-documented. However, in lower-income countries, particularly in Africa, alcohol consumption during pregnancy is often underreported or not well documented due to cultural stigmas, lack of awareness, and limited access to healthcare resources. In this study, the pooled prevalence of alcohol consumption among pregnant women was 28.33% (95% CI: 22.68–33.99%). This result aligns with prior research on alcohol consumption among pregnant women in Sub-Saharan Africa, using demographic and health survey data [54]. However, it is higher than findings from systematic reviews conducted in East Africa [15] and Sub-Saharan Africa [16]. This discrepancy may be attributed to differences in the number of included studies, countries covered, sample sizes, and study designs (cross-sectional vs. case-control/cohort studies). While this study analyzed data from 11,726 pregnant women in 21 countries, previous reviews used 40,474 participants from four countries [15], and 20,840 participants from 11 countries [16], incorporating different methodologies.

In terms of subgroup analysis, the pooled prevalence of alcohol consumption among pregnant women was highest in Ghana (34.09%), followed by Ethiopia (29.19%) and Uganda (19.80%). The higher prevalence in Ghana could be due to the smaller sample size (647 participants from two studies), compared to Ethiopia (8,181 participants from 12 studies) and Uganda (925 participants from two studies). Sociocultural differences, research settings, and assessment tools may also contribute to these variations. Additionally, the pooled prevalence was 40.85% in community-based studies compared to 22.02% in institution-based studies, suggesting that higher-risk individuals who may not seek institutional care were more represented in community-based research.

Regarding the associated factors, this study found that pregnant women living in rural areas had a significantly higher association of alcohol consumption (AOR = 6.95 (1.16, 41.77)) compared to those in urban areas. This contrasts with previous findings where urban areas had higher risk [55], indicating the need for further research. Pregnant women without formal education were more likely to get al.cohol consumption than those with formal education. Compared to women with formal education, pregnant women without formal education may have a higher chance of developing alcohol consumption because the former are more likely to have been informed about the risks of alcohol use during pregnancy, including its effects on fetal development and the potential for fetal alcohol spectrum disorders (FASD). Based on behavioral and clinical variables, pregnant women with low social support compared to those with high social support were linked to alcohol consumption. Pregnant women who don’t have strong support systems may feel more stressed, anxious, or depressed. Some women may use alcohol as a coping strategy to control their emotions as a result of these mental health issues [1, 56], which was supported by the another study [22]. Pregnant women with a family history of mental illness were more likely to experience alcohol consumption than women without such a history. It could be because families with a history of mental illness tend to be more stressed, unstable, or conflicted, which can make family members feel more depressed or anxious. Pregnant women may therefore use alcohol as a coping mechanism in these kinds of situations. Compared to pregnant women who had never had an abortion before, those who had a history of abortion during a prior pregnancy were more susceptible. It could be caused by a number of things. For instance, women who experience an abortion, whether planned or unplanned, may experience severe emotions of loss, grief, and depression. These feelings can carry over into ongoing pregnancies, adding to the stress and emotional vulnerability that might cause some women to turn to alcohol as a coping method. One risk factor that was substantially linked to alcohol consumption in pregnant mothers was not awareness about the adverse effects of alcohol intake on the fetus and the mother, as compared to knowledge of these effects. There could be a number of reasons for this, such as the fact that pregnant women who are not aware of the risks associated with alcohol use may not be aware of how seriously alcohol affects fetal development. Additionally, women who are unaware may not have had access to public health campaigns, educational materials, or prenatal care. This outcome was consistent with the previous studies [16, 22]. Compared to women without such a background, pregnant women who have a history of alcohol drinking before becoming pregnant are more likely to experience alcohol consumption throughout their pregnancy. There could be a number of reasons for it. For example, women who drank heavily prior to becoming pregnant might have developed difficult-to-break drinking habits. Maintaining these behaviors throughout pregnancy may raise the chance of getting alcohol consumption, particularly if the person is unaware of or does not completely comprehend the hazards. It was corroborated by an additional earlier study [57]. Social pressure (peer pressure) is one of the risk factors of alcohol use disorders [1]. In this study, which evaluated the partners of pregnant women who used alcohol, there was a risk of alcohol consumption when compared to the pregnant women whose partners did not use alcohol. Our finding was the same as the previous studies [16, 55, 58]. An unplanned pregnancy can be an overwhelming experience that causes immediate stress, anxiety, and emotional turmoil. As a result, it was found to be a significant risk factor for alcohol consumption in pregnant women when compared to mothers who had planned pregnancies. Some women may use alcohol as a coping strategy to deal with the unplanned change in their lives as a result of these feelings, which was compared to the earlier research [16, 22].

Due to the high rates of alcohol consumption and binge drinking during pregnancy in various African nations, screening programs, education campaigns, and tailored interventions are necessary for women who are or may become pregnant. According to our study, regulations and interventions that can aid in lowering alcohol use during pregnancy should be implemented. Raising awareness of the detrimental effects of alcohol on the health of pregnant women and their children should be the main focus in health education. Regular alcohol screening at prenatal care visits establishes a suitable clinical care referral system and gives healthcare providers a chance to discuss potential risks of alcohol consumption during pregnancy. Antenatal care providers are particularly important in ensuring that women receive appropriate guidance regarding alcohol use and support to manage the issues that arise, particularly for expectant mothers who live in rural areas, lack formal education, have low social support, have a family history of mental illness, have had an abortion in the past, are unaware of the adverse effects of alcohol consumption, drink before becoming pregnant, have a partner who drinks, or become pregnant unplanned.

Strengths and Limitations of the study

Selection and reporting bias were minimized by carefully applying rigorous inclusion criteria, focusing on peer-reviewed and relevant studies, and conducting sensitivity analyses to assess the impact of study characteristics on the results. Subgroup analysis further helped contextualize variations across countries, study settings, and assessment tools. However, the study has some limitations. All included studies employed a cross-sectional design, which prevents causal inference as it captures data at a single point in time and cannot establish temporal relationships between variables and alcohol consumption during pregnancy. Only research published in English was included, which may have introduced language bias. Additionally, the limited number of studies from some African countries reduced geographic representation. Publication bias was assessed using funnel plots and Egger’s regression test. While some confounding factors, such as socioeconomic status and mental health, may not have been fully controlled for in individual studies, efforts were made to account for their potential influence. Furthermore, variations in assessment tools across studies made standardization challenging.

Conclusion

This systematic review and meta-analysis found that the pooled prevalence of alcohol consumption among pregnant women was high. Key associated factors included sociodemographic factors (rural residence, lack of formal education) and behavioral and clinical factors (poor social support, family history of mental illness, history of abortion, lack of awareness, pre-pregnancy alcohol consumption, partner alcohol use, and unplanned pregnancy). These findings highlight the need for early screening, policy interventions, and targeted health education programs. Policymakers, healthcare providers, and public health officials should implement evidence-based strategies to reduce alcohol consumption during pregnancy. Furthermore, this study provides valuable insights that can inform future public health initiatives and research on this critical topic.
