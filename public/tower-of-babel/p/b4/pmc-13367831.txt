Liquid–liquid phase separation enables chromatography-free purification and high-performance spidroin-amyloid hybrid silk fibers
Authors: Karin Tufvesson, Viktoria Langwallner, Tomas Bohn Pessatti, Gabriele Greco, Elin Karlsson, Sarah Stadlmayr, Axel Leppert, Michael Landreh, Anna Rising, Benjamin Schmuck
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
SignificanceThe commercial success of artificial spider silk has been limited by inefficient protein production and costly purification methods. Here, we present a strategy addressing both challenges by combining protein engineering with a purification method based on liquid–liquid phase separation. The engineered spider silk proteins are efficiently produced in microorganisms, while purification costs, processing time, and water consumption are reduced by up to 99%, 80%, and 92%, respectively, compared with conventional chromatography, without requiring tag removal, heat, or denaturing agents. The purified proteins can be directly wet-spun into tough artificial silk fibers using an all-aqueous process. These advances demonstrate the potential of liquid–liquid phase separation for sustainable protein production and could facilitate industrial manufacturing of artificial spider silk.

Major ampullate silk, the most well-characterized silk type spun by spiders, is a protein-based material renowned for its exceptional combination of high tensile strength and extensibility (1–4). This unique combination of mechanical properties has inspired diverse applications, ranging from medical applications (5–8), to components in lithium-ion batteries (9), high-performance fibers for aerospace (10, 11), to different types of sensors (12–14). Spider silk has also found exploratory uses in cosmetic products (15) and is increasingly viewed as a sustainable alternative to conventional textile fibers (16–18).

Despite its potential, large-scale production of natural spider silk remains infeasible due to the territorial and cannibalistic behavior of spiders (19). To overcome this, significant effort has focused on producing artificial spider silk using recombinant spider silk proteins (spidroins) and wet-spinning techniques (20–28). However, the heterologous production and purification of spidroins pose considerable technical challenges, stemming from their size and repetitive nature. Spidroins are, in general, large, with major ampullate spidroins (MaSp) ranging in molecular weight (Mw) from 100 to >545 kDa (29–31). They possess a highly conserved N-terminal domain (NT) (32, 33) and C-terminal domain (CT) (34) that flank an extensive repetitive region (Rep). The terminal domains contribute to the stability and solubility of the spidroins and respond with conformational changes when exposed to lowered pH and shear, triggering polymerization (34–38). Within the Rep region, poly-Ala blocks alternate with Gly-rich regions (39, 40). In the mature fiber, the poly-Ala blocks form β-sheet crystals in which individual β-sheets are held together by dry steric zippers, whereas the Gly-rich repeats form a flexible matrix embedding these crystalline domains (41–49).

Recombinant expression of native-sized spidroins is notoriously difficult (50–52). Protein yield and solubility decrease sharply with increasing molecular weight (>40 to 60 kDa) (53), and the abundance of Gly and Ala residues imposes high demand on glycyl- and alanyl-tRNA pools, which are not naturally abundant in Escherichia coli (54). Early work by Xia and colleagues demonstrated that supplementing glycyl-tRNA improved expression of native-sized MaSp1 repetitive regions, though the produced spidroins formed insoluble aggregates (27). Later, Bowen et al. combined tRNA upregulation with codon optimization to minimize mRNA secondary structure formation, thereby enabling the expression of a 290 kDa MaSp1 construct (25). Although such advances have boosted yields to as high as 13.5 g L−1 in bioreactors (25, 27, 55–57), the largest constructs are still expressed at only a fraction of this level and remain insoluble, necessitating denaturing purification and organic solvents like hexafluoroisopropanol for spinning dope preparation, and methanol for coagulation. This approach can yield fibers with strengths approaching 1 GPa (25, 58), but the reliance on harsh chemicals renders the process expensive and environmentally unsustainable.

To simplify production, smaller “mini-spidroins” (~30 to 50 kDa) that feature NT, CT, and a truncated Rep have been engineered (59–63). These constructs are water-soluble, correctly folded, and yields can reach 20 g L−1 (23, 64), which is a substantial improvement compared to previous recombinant systems. The mini-spidroins can be purified under native conditions and spun into fibers using a fully aqueous spinning method (28, 59–62, 65, 66), yielding fibers with tensile strengths between 100 and 500 MPa (28, 59, 66–68). However, downstream purification typically relies on affinity chromatography (64, 66, 68), an expensive and environmentally problematic step. Metal ion-coupled resins and imidazole, essential for immobilized metal affinity chromatography (IMAC), account for up to 65% of material costs (69), and pose substantial environmental and health risks. Consequently, the high cost of purification remains a key barrier to the commercial viability of recombinant spider silk (22, 70).

In a recent paper, we reported that mini-spidroins containing more than three poly-Ala blocks in the Rep region could not be expressed in E. coli (62), possibly due to excessive demand for alanyl-tRNA (55, 58). This prompted us to explore whether substituting poly-Ala motifs with amyloidogenic peptides, which are short sequences known to form highly ordered, β-sheet-rich fibrils (55, 57, 58, 71), could relieve the translational burden while preserving the fiber-forming capability. Amyloid fibrils are characterized by their stiffness, insolubility, and β-sheet stacking, which is stabilized by steric zippers, and thus closely resembles the crystalline regions in native spider silk (72, 73). Consequently, we hypothesized that replacing poly-Ala motifs with amyloidogenic sequences could enable expression of larger, soluble mini-spidroins while maintaining structural integrity.

Here, we report the design of water-soluble mini-spidroins in which the poly-Ala segments are replaced with the nonnatural and amyloidogenic β16 peptide (74, 75). Constructs containing two to seven β16 repeats were successfully expressed in E. coli at high yields and remained soluble in aqueous buffer. Remarkably, addition of NaCl to the bacterial lysates causes the β16-containing spidroins to undergo liquid–liquid phase separation (LLPS), which allowed us to develop a simple, chromatography-free purification method. This process enables the direct recovery of highly concentrated, soluble proteins that can be readily spun into fibers using an all-aqueous biomimetic spinning method. The resulting fibers exhibited excellent mechanical performance, demonstrating that amyloid-based design and LLPS-driven purification together provide a powerful strategy for sustainable production of recombinant spider silk.

Results and Discussion

Design of the β16 Constructs and Development of a Chromatography-Free Purification Method.

Previous attempts to express spidroin-amyloid hybrids by Li and coworkers (55, 58), and by Lin and coworkers (56, 57), resulted in high yields after expression (up to 13.5 g L−1 in bioreactors) and fibers with GPa strength. However, these constructs still require purification under denaturing conditions and the use of organic solvents for fiber spinning. To generate water-soluble spidroins, we designed a series of mini-spidroins based on the NT2RepCT scaffold (63), featuring NT from Euprosthenops australis MaSp1, CT from Araneus ventricosus MiSp, and a MaSp1-based Rep also from E. australis. In our design, the poly-Ala motifs in the Rep region were replaced by the amyloidogenic β16 peptide (ALEAKLAALEAKLA; SI Appendix, Table S1) (74). The β16 peptide, originally developed by Takahashi et al. (74, 75), comprises 14 residues, including Lys (2×) and Glu (2×) residues that confer charge, and predominantly hydrophobic Ala and Leu residues that promote self-assembly. β16 has been shown to form fibrils near neutral pH in Tris-HCl buffer at 25 to 50 °C (74, 75). Compared with other amyloidogenic peptides previously used in spidroin hybrids (57, 71) β16 is more soluble due to its charged nature. Additionally, its 14-residue length closely matches the 14 to 15 residues typically found in the poly-Ala motifs of MaSp1 from E. australis, making it a suitable structural analog. Using this sequence, we constructed a series of β16-containing variants comprising two, three, five, or seven repeats (SI Appendix, Table S1). These are referred to as β16_xRep, where x denotes the number of β16 motifs. The Gly-rich spacer regions separating the β16 blocks were derived from the native MaSp1 sequence.

All the spidroin-amyloid hybrid variants were successfully expressed in E. coli BL21 shake flask cultivations (SI Appendix, Fig. S1). Notably, the larger variants, β16_5Rep and β16_7Rep (42.7 and 48.9 kDa, respectively), displayed higher expression yields, compared to what we have observed before with equivalent MaSp1 constructs containing five or seven poly-Ala blocks, which did not express at all (62). To evaluate the solubility of the β16_xRep variants, the wet cell pellet was resuspended in 20 mM Tris-HCl buffer at a dilution ratio of 1:8, followed by cell lysis and centrifugation. Under these conditions, we observed that only β16_2Rep and β16_3Rep were soluble, whereas the majority of β16_5Rep and β16_7Rep were found in the insoluble fraction (SI Appendix, Fig. S2A). The solubility of the longer constructs improved substantially when the resuspension ratio was increased to 1:20 for β16_5Rep and 1:40 for β16_7Rep, yielding more than >90% soluble protein after lysis and centrifugation (Table 1 and SI Appendix, Fig. S2B).

Attempts to purify the β16_xRep constructs using IMAC were unsuccessful for the larger constructs. The majority of β16_5Rep and β16_7Rep failed to bind the column and were recovered in the flow-through or wash fractions (SI Appendix, Fig. S3 and Table S2). We noticed that the inclusion of 0.15 M NaCl in the lysis buffer caused the hybrid constructs to sediment together with cell debris in the insoluble fraction. To probe this effect, NaCl was added directly to the clarified lysate prepared in Tris-HCl alone. Immediate turbidity developed, suggesting aggregation; however, careful inspection using light microscopy revealed droplet-like assemblies, suggesting LLPS rather than simple protein aggregation (Fig. 1A). The NaCl concentrations required for immediate induction of turbidity for the different β16_xRep lysates were 40 mM, for β16_2Rep; 60 mM, for β16_3Rep; 30 mM, for β16_5Rep; and 20 mM, for β16_7Rep.

To determine whether the addition of NaCl to the respective lysates induced LLPS or aggregation, we assessed reversibility and retained fluidity using turbidity measurements, light microscopy, and Sodium Dodecyl Sulfate Polyacrylamide Gel Electrophoresis (SDS–PAGE). This was done by first inducing turbidity in the respective lysates by adding NaCl. Then, turbid lysates were dialyzed to remove the salt. In parallel, we separated the putative spidroin condensates by centrifugation, yielding a HDP from the remaining lysate, which was subsequently reconstituted in Tris-HCl buffer. Turbidity measurements from both experimental approaches demonstrated that the process is fully reversible (Fig. 1B), as the turbidity of the lysates after dialysis, and of the resuspended HDP, returns to zero. Full reversibility was further confirmed by the absence of any structures or assemblies under bright-field microscopy (SI Appendix, Fig. S4). As an additional evaluation, following reconstitution, the HDP was subjected to centrifugation (22,000 × g), yet no visible pellet formed. The supernatant of this sample was carefully removed, after which any remaining pellet that could not be seen with the naked eye was solubilized in 8 M urea. The following SDS–PAGE analysis of all samples confirmed that the HDP consisted almost exclusively of the β16_xRep variants, while other bacterial proteins remained in the supernatant (i.e., the low-density phase Fig. 1C). Moreover, more than 99% of all β16_xRep that was sedimented into the HDP was successfully reconstituted into Tris-HCl buffer, with only a negligible fraction remaining insoluble.

Further attesting to the fluidity of the droplets that appear in the lysates after adding NaCl, droplet fusion events were observed (SI Appendix, Fig. S5). Finally, the HDP was assessed using DroProbe (76), a noncovalent probe that fluoresces in viscous environments, allowing us to distinguish buffer from protein condensates. The results show that the HDP is a viscous fluid phase with buffer inclusions that rapidly fuse, change shape, and move (buffer inclusions appear as dark areas in the bright viscous phase; SI Appendix, Fig. S6 and Movies S1–S8). Taken together, these results corroborate the reversibility of the NaCl-induced assembling behavior as well as the liquid nature of the resulting droplets and the HDP.

These findings inspired us to develop a simple chromatography-free purification method for the β16-spidroins based solely on NaCl-induced LLPS (Fig. 1D and SI Appendix, Fig. S7). In this process, NaCl was added to the clarified lysate to induce target protein self-assembly. Then, the coacervates were isolated into an HDP by centrifugation, and the supernatant was decanted. The HDP displayed a viscous liquid-like character and was easily reconstituted by adding Tris-HCl buffer before dialysis to remove NaCl. After dialysis, the protein concentration was ~100 mg mL−1, which enabled rapid concentration to ~300 mg mL−1, the level required for spinning-dope preparations (28, 62). All expression and purification metrics of the different β16 variants are presented in Table 1. The method described in this work represents a demonstration of direct spidroin purification from bacterial lysates via LLPS, without the need to use chromatography (65, 77).

Next, we investigated whether purification via NaCl-induced LLPS could be extended to other mini-spidroins. As a test case, we selected NT2RepCT (63), a well-characterized mini-spidroin known to undergo phase separation in the presence of kosmotropic salts or under crowded conditions (78, 79). Its LLPS propensity is mainly attributed to the CT-domain and the glycine-rich segments that contain the stickers Try and Arg (79, 80). However, even in the presence of 1 M NaCl, bacterial lysates containing NT2RepCT did not exhibit observable phase separation (SI Appendix, Fig. S3B), and NT2RepCT could therefore only be purified by affinity chromatography.

In general, LLPS of purified recombinant spidroins is induced by kosmotropic salts, with potency following the Hofmeister series (79, 81), including phosphate (65, 77, 79, 81–84), citrate (77, 79), acetate (81, 82), bicarbonate (79), sulfates (81), or by pH changes (85). Indeed, all β16_xRep variants readily underwent phase separation when kosmotropic salts were added to the lysates (Fig. 2A and SI Appendix, Fig. S8).

However, the use of NaCl as an LLPS inducer is rather unusual, as NaCl is not strongly kosmotropic. Interestingly, recent studies have observed NaCl-induced LLPS of mini-spidroins derived from aciniform and minor ampullate silks (86, 87). In those systems, hydrophobic linkers containing Tyr and Phe residues, both recognized as “stickers,” were proposed to mediate the low-affinity intermolecular interactions required for phase separation (79). Since the Gly-rich regions between the β16-peptide segments in our engineered mini-spidroins are derived for native spidroin sequences and contain Tyr, the phase separation is likely mediated by a similar mechanism in our system. Compared to the poly-Ala blocks in NT2RepCT, the β16 peptides contain two Lys which are known to act as stickers (80), which could explain the increased LLPS propensity of the β16_xRep.

To assess the robustness of β16_xRep phase separation in the lysates, we constructed a phase diagram by varying the initial protein concentration (~8 mg mL−1 to 0.5 mg mL−1), while adjusting NaCl concentrations between 5 and 160 mM (Fig. 2 B and C). Because protein levels varied across lysates due to different buffer-to-pellet ratios, lysates containing β16_5Rep and β16_7Rep were concentrated to similar mini-spidroin starting concentrations as the β16_2Rep and β16_3Rep lysates. For each condition, the formation of coacervates was evaluated by bright-field microscopy (SI Appendix, Figs. S9–S12) and turbidity measurements. The variants showed distinct salt-dependent LLPS behavior: β16_2Rep required relatively high protein and NaCl concentrations (~80 mM at 8 mg mL−1) to phase separate (SI Appendix, Fig. S9), whereas β16_3–7Rep formed droplets already at 5 mM (SI Appendix, Figs. S10–S12). In comparison, other spidroin constructs required up to 100 mM NaCl at even higher protein concentrations (~50 mg mL−1) before phase separation was observed (86, 87). The appearance of the coacervates also varied, with β16_3Rep forming the largest droplets and β16_7Rep the smallest (Fig. 1A). Interestingly, the overall droplet morphology does not appear to correlate with efficiency in recruiting β16_xRep into the HDP, as ~40% of the protein is lost for β16_3Rep but only 6% for β16_7Rep (Table 1).

To verify the scalability of the process, we expressed β16_7Rep in a bioreactor and achieved an expression level of ~30 g L−1 (SI Appendix, Fig. S15), which is higher than what has been achieved before (56, 57, 64, 88). Importantly, the β16_7Rep expressed in this way could be purified in the same manner as the protein expressed from shake flask cultures, yielding 25 g per L of culture after purification. From a process perspective, further optimizations could include: increasing lysate protein concentration to minimize working volume, replacing the centrifugation for clarifying cell lysates by industrial-standard tangential flow-filtration (89), and to reduce the NaCl concentration to lower costs and facilitate downstream dialysis.

Characterization of the Spinning Dope Obtained from the β16_xRep Constructs.

Following purification and dialysis, all β16x_Rep constructs were concentrated to ~300 mg mL−1 to prepare the spinning dopes (Fig. 1D). Initial observations indicated that dopes prepared from the different proteins had distinct flow properties. To systematically investigate these differences, we characterized the samples using rheology, circular dichroism (CD) spectroscopy, turbidity measurements, size-exclusion chromatography (SEC), and mass photometry (MP).

Rheology was used to directly quantify the viscosity of the spinning dope. In line with previous reports on NT2RepCT (90) and other mini-spidroins (61) with comparable size and under similar conditions, in this work, NT2RepCT (33 kDa) at 300 mg mL−1 showed a low viscosity of 1 Pa s at a concentration of 300 mg mL−1 (22 °C), at a shear rate of 1 s−1 (Fig. 3A). For the β16 constructs, the viscosity increased with increased size of Rep (at a concentration of 300 mg mL−1). The changes span two orders of magnitude, from 0.5 Pa s (β16_2Rep) to 50 Pa s for β16_7Rep. Additionally, the shear thinning behavior appears to increase for the larger constructs, indicating higher intermolecular entanglement at the resting state.

CD spectroscopy confirmed that all β16_xRep variants retained the α-helical secondary structure characteristic of natively folded spidroins in diluted spinning dopes (Fig. 3B) (23), further corroborating LLPS as a suitable method to purify these proteins while retaining their native state. β16_2Rep exhibited reduced signal intensity while preserving the α-helical signature, suggesting either a modest reduction in helical content or slight inaccuracy in protein quantification (SI Appendix, Table S3).

When preparing the spinning dopes, a slight increase in turbidity was observed in particular for the β16_7Rep variant, which indicated aggregation or formation of larger protein assemblies. NT2RepCT has been shown to form larger protein assemblies, termed protein clusters, at a concentration of 20 mg mL-1 in 20 mM Tris-HCl pH 8.0 (85). To assess the propensity of the different constructs to form protein clusters in the dope at 300 mg mL-1, turbidity was measured by recording absorbance at 600 nm (91). Spinning dopes prepared from constructs with fewer repetitive units (β16_2Rep and β16_3Rep) had turbidity levels comparable to NT2RepCT (Fig. 3C). In contrast, constructs with more extensive repetitive regions (β16_5Rep and β16_7Rep) showed markedly increased turbidity, consistent with enhanced clustering and formation of higher-order assemblies.

To further investigate the degree of clustering of the proteins, SEC analyses were conducted (Fig. 3D). Samples were prepared from diluted spinning dopes (from 300 mg mL−1 to 20 mg mL−1), and in Tris-HCl buffer without NaCl to reflect the conditions in the dope preparation. For the NT2RepCT, three main peaks were detected: a high-molecular-weight fraction eluting in the void volume (>400 kDa), a smaller peak at 58 mL (∼265 kDa), and a third peak at 64 mL (~158 kDa), which likely corresponds to the dimeric form previously observed by Andersson et al. (63). The shift toward higher apparent molecular weights relative to standards (run with NaCl-containing buffer) could reflect the expanded hydrodynamic radius of the spidroins in salt-free solutions (92).

Even though the majority of NT2RepCT sample eluted as dimers, the presence of NT2RepCT in the void volume aligns with results from previous studies using ultracentrifugation (85) and cryoelectron microscopy (63), which identified large clusters under neutral pH in the absence of NaCl. In contrast, under saline conditions, using a sample that has not been concentrated, NT2RepCT elutes mainly as a dimer (63). This suggests that cluster formation is enhanced at higher concentrations and may depend on buffer conditions.

Quantitative integration of the SEC profiles revealed that 82% of the β16_2Rep eluted in the void fraction and 18% as dimers. For β16_3Rep, the corresponding numbers were 53% in the void fraction and 47% as dimers or small oligomers. For β16_5Rep and β16_7Rep, the void fraction comprised 87% and 96% of the total protein, respectively. In comparison, the β16_xRep mini-spidroins were more prone to cluster than the NT2RepCT, for which 36% was found in the void fraction, with the remaining 64% corresponding to dimers and smaller oligomers. These findings indicate that increasing the number of β16 repeats in mini-spidroins enhances self-assembly and clustering, a conclusion supported by the turbidity measurements, which displayed a similar trend (Fig. 3 C and D).

Next, we wondered whether β16_xRep clusters could be dissociated in super-dilute solutions using mass photometry (MP), a single-particle technique that quantifies molecules at nanomolar concentrations based on interferometric detection of scattered light. We dissolved concentrated spinning dopes (~300 mg mL−1) to nanomolar concentrations and detected predominantly dimers and a small fraction of tetramers, but importantly, no large clusters, demonstrating the reversibility of these assemblies (Fig. 3E).

Additionally, since MP is a single-particle tracking method, it allowed us to determine the apparent sample purity in the 30 kDa to a few MDa range by comparing their mass counts to those of all detected particles. β16_xRep assemblies accounted for the majority of detected particles, with apparent purities in the range of 85 to 94%, which is comparable to NT2RepCT purified by conventional IMAC (SI Appendix, Table S4). LLPS-purified proteins may contain trace amounts of host cell components, such as nucleic acids or endotoxins, which may require additional downstream polishing depending on the intended application, particularly for high-purity or biomedical uses. However, such impurity carryover is not unique to LLPS-based purification. Conventional IMAC can likewise lead to copurification of host cell proteins, endotoxins, and suffer from residual contaminants such as nickel and imidazole.

The Mechanical Properties of Artificial Spider Silk Fibers.

The concentrated spinning dopes (~300 mg mL−1) were extruded into a spinning bath containing acetate buffer at pH 5 using a glass capillary (SI Appendix, Fig. S16) (28, 62, 63, 93). All constructs, except the β16_2Rep, showed excellent spinnability, with smooth extrusion, continuous fiber collection, and no evidence of clogging. Despite substantial differences in viscosity and clustering between NT2RepCT and the different β16_xRep variants, these variations did not significantly influence spinnability. Optical microscopy of the resulting fibers revealed characteristic longitudinal grooves, a common morphological feature observed in wet-spun fibers, including artificial silk (94) (Fig. 4A). The fiber diameters ranged between 6 to 10 µm (SI Appendix, Table S5), consistent with previous reports (11, 28, 67, 93).

In terms of mechanical performance (i.e., tensile testing) and structural features, as assessed by attenuated total reflectance Fourier-transform infrared spectroscopy (ATR-FTIR) and birefringence analysis, the fibers from all constructs, including NT2RepCT, showed comparable properties, which is notable given the large differences in molecular weight and dope rheology. Tensile strengths ranged from 100 to 120 MPa, with strains at break of 0.7 to 0.9 mm mm−1. The Young’s moduli ranged between 2 to 3 GPa, and the toughness moduli were around 75 MJ m−3 (Fig. 4B). These values align closely with literature data for fibers spun from recombinant spidroins under similar conditions (28, 62, 64, 82), and their toughness moduli are comparable to those of major ampullate silk (3, 95) and Bombyx mori silk (95), which makes them promising candidates for a wide range of silk-inspired applications.

Interestingly, no significant correlation was observed between protein molecular weight and fiber mechanics, a result that contrasts with prior reports linking higher spidroin molecular weights to improved tensile strength (24, 25, 27, 58, 96). Moreover, all fibers show overlapping absorbance spectra (ATR-FTIR) in the amide I region, but they also have comparable birefringence indices, indicating no major differences in β-sheet content and overall similar molecular alignment (SI Appendix, Fig. S17). The lack of this relationship here likely reflects the modest molecular weights of the β16 constructs (<100 kDa), which fall below the threshold generally required to elicit major enhancements of the fiber mechanical properties (62, 97). It should also be noted that the spinning conditions used in this study were intended as an initial assessment of spinnability and were not specifically optimized for the β16 variants but for NT2RepCT (28). Thus, improved mechanical performance should be expected for the fibers spun from β16_xRep variants upon careful optimization of the spinning parameters, which will be addressed in future work.

Cost and Time Comparison of the Purification Methods.

Since the first commercialization efforts of artificial spider silk almost three decades ago, progress has been relatively slow, with limited availability of artificial spider silk-based materials and market penetration (70). A major reason for the slow progress is likely the high production cost of the material, which is two orders of magnitude higher than that of competing synthetic fibers (22, 70). Current artificial silk production typically involves a multistep recombinant expression and purification process, with chromatography and IPTG being the primary cost drivers (22, 69). The use of imidazole and nickel in protein production of soluble spidroins introduces environmental and safety concerns, further impeding sustainable scale-up.

For proteins in general, a common nonchromatographic purification method is salting out, which implies addition of salts to proteins in solution. The ionic compounds act as water scavengers, disrupting the protein’s hydration shell, exposing hydrophobic patches, and triggering precipitation (liquid to solid phase seperation). Salting out is typically used as a prepurification step (98, 99) or as an additional polishing step (100), and in some cases, organic solvents are required to redissolve the salted-out proteins (101). Another chromatography-free method is based on the precipitation properties of elastin-like polypeptides tags (102–105), which, however, could require their removal using proteases (106).

For spidroins, chromatography-free protocols have been developed based on salting out with ammonium sulfate, washing with isopropyl alcohol, followed by lyophilization (107). Another very common method is heat purification, in which E. coli proteins are first removed by heating the lysate to 80 °C, after which the spidroins are salted out with ammonium sulfate, before lyophilization (100, 108, 109). The LLPS phenomenon was also already exploited for the purification of spidroins, but only after conventional chromatography steps, to separate spidroins from proteases after His6-tag cleavage, or to speed-up the concentration process (65, 77)

The finding that NaCl-induced LLPS can serve as an efficient, nonchromatographic purification strategy for natively folded β16 mini-spidroins represents a major advancement toward cost-effective production. To evaluate its economic impact, we compared the material cost per gram of purified protein obtained via traditional IMAC vs. NaCl-induced LLPS at the laboratory scale (Fig. 5 and SI Appendix, Tables S6–S11). At the laboratory scale, chromatography-based purification was over 96 times more expensive than LLPS-based purification for β16_3Rep (Fig. 5A). For comparison, the purification of the mini-spidroin NT2RepCT via IMAC costs 8.72 USD g−1, whereas purification of β16_3Rep via LLPS costs only 0.30 USD g−1, representing a 29-fold reduction. A detailed cost breakdown revealed that IMAC resin accounted for 50% of total expenses, with Tris-HCl buffer contributing another 38%, primarily due to the large buffer volumes required during dialysis to remove imidazole when using chromatography. Yet another benefit of using the LLPS approach is that it yields much more concentrated protein (10 to 30 mL, compared to 181 mL for IMAC, from 6 L of bacterial culture), thereby greatly reducing dialysis time and costs.

In addition to material savings, the LLPS purification process was significantly faster. The complete workflow from cell lysis to concentrated dope required only ~24 h, compared to over 6 d for IMAC-purified protein (Fig. 5B and SI Appendix, Fig. S18 A and B). These time savings primarily arose from the reduced sample volumes required during dialysis and concentration. This also translated into a markedly lower water consumption. For example, purification of β16_3Rep via LLPS required approximately 12-fold less water compared to IMAC purification of NT2RepCT (SI Appendix, Fig. S18C).

Notably, the cost estimates above exclude equipment (e.g., FPLC systems), laboratory space, and personnel costs. Accounting for these factors would further amplify the economic advantage of LLPS purification, which requires minimal instrumentation, is rapid, and avoids the need for toxic chemicals. Looking ahead, several key experiments will be important to further advance this platform. First, a quantitative characterization of impurity partitioning during LLPS is needed to better assess selectivity and downstream purity. Second, scale-up studies should evaluate the robustness and reproducibility of LLPS behavior under industrially relevant conditions. Third, optimization of spinning conditions for β16-based variants will be necessary if improved mechanical properties are desired.

Conclusion

Through rational protein design, we present a series of mini-spidroin constructs in which the poly-Ala blocks were replaced with the amyloidogenic β16 peptide. These spidroin-amyloid hybrids are highly expressed in E. coli as water-soluble and natively folded proteins. This is in contrast to previously described spidroin-amyloid hybrid designs which are water-insoluble and require denaturing conditions for purification and spinning (55–58). Moreover, the β16_xRep constructs can be purified directly from bacterial cell lysates via a NaCl-induced LLPS-based method, thereby completely bypassing chromatography. This is particularly relevant given that, according to a recent techno-economic analysis, chromatographic resins alone can account for up to 33% of the annual operating costs in facilities producing ~9,000 tons of spidroins per year (110). The fact that phase separation can be induced by NaCl is particularly attractive due to the low cost, low toxicity, and widespread industrial availability of this chemical. In addition, compared to other chromatography-free purification methods, NaCl-induced LLPS-based purification does not require tag removal with proteases, heat, or denaturing agents in any part of the process. The simplicity of the NaCl-induced separation method makes it potentially scalable, enabling the high-yield recovery of pure mini-spiroins in a cost-effective and environmentally sustainable manner. Crucially, the proteins can be spun into tough fibers using native conditions and aqueous buffers, thereby offering a sustainable alternative for future material applications. Hence, the production and purification method presented herein is potentially transformative in the realization of sustainable recombinant spider silk manufacturing.

Materials and Methods

Protein Expression.

Details for sequence design and cloning are described in SI Appendix. To express β16_xRep, overnight cultures (100 mL LB medium; 70 µg mL−1 kanamycin) were prepared by inoculation with glycerol stocks and incubated at 30 °C with shaking at 110 rpm. This culture was then used to inoculate one or more flasks, each containing one L of fresh LB medium (70 µg mL−1 kanamycin). Then, the cultures were grown at 30 °C and 110 rpm until the optical density at 600 nm (OD600) reached 0.6, at which point the incubation temperature was lowered to 20 °C. When OD600 reached 0.8 to 0.9, protein expression was induced by adding isopropyl β-D-1-thiogalactopyranoside (IPTG) to a final concentration of 0.3 mM. Cells were harvested after a 20 h incubation period by centrifugation (7,000 × g for 15 min), and the resulting pellets were resuspended in Tris-HCl-buffer (20 mM Tris-HCl, pH 8), using a wet cell pellet to buffer ratio of 1:1. The cell pellets were stored at −20 °C until further processing.

NT2RepCT and β16_7Rep were expressed in a fed-batch culture, as described previously using a bioreactor (64, 111) (SI Appendix).

Purification Via Ni-IMAC and Spinning Dope Preparation of NT2RepCT.

Cell pellets were resuspended in Tris-HCl-buffer using the following final ratio of wet cell pellet to buffer: NT2RepCT 1:10; β16_2Rep 1:8; β16_3Rep 1:8; β16_5Rep 1:20; β16_7Rep 1:40. Cell lysis was performed with a high-pressure cell disruptor (CF1 Cell Disruptor from Constant Systems) at 30 kpsi and 5 °C. To remove the cell debris, the lysate was centrifuged at 24,000 × g for 20 min, and the supernatant was filtered through Filtropur S (Sarstedt) 0.2 μm syringe filters. Depending on the scale, the clarified lysate was loaded onto Capto Chelating resin (Cytiva) packed in either a HiScale 16/60 or HiScale 50/60 column (Cytiva), using an ÄKTA Avant (Cytiva) at 4 °C, and a flow rate of 10 mL min−1 (HiScale 16/60) or 80 mL min−1 (HiScale 50/60). Then, the column was washed with 3 to 5 column volumes (CV) of 20 mM Tris-HCl, pH 8, followed by a wash of 2 mM imidazole in 20 mM Tris-HCl, pH 8, until the absorbance dropped below 150 mAU. Elution was performed with 2 CV of 20 mM Tris-HCl, pH 8, containing 200 mM imidazole. To remove imidazole, the eluted proteins were dialyzed against 20 mM Tris-HCl, pH 8, using a Spectra/Por 1 Dialysis membrane (Spectrum Labs) with a molecular weight cut-off (MWCO) of 6 to 8 kDa.

NT2RepCT was concentrated to ~300 mg mL−1 using either a 500 mL stirred cell device (Merck Millipore), at 6 °C, with a N2 pressure of four bar, and with a 10 kDa cutoff regenerated cellulose membrane, or an Amicon Ultra-15 centrifugal filter unit, 10 kDa MWCO (Merck Millipore), at 4,000 × g, and 4 °C. Before determining the protein concentration, the concentrated mini-spidroin solution was mixed well, by several rounds of gentle stirring, and brief centrifugation. Then, the absorbance was determined spectrophotometrically, by measuring the absorbance at 280 nm at least in triplicates, after transferring 3 µL of the concentrated spinning dope with a Multipipette E3x (Eppendorf) into 997 µL Tris-HCl-buffer. To calculate the protein concentration in the spinning dope, we accounted for dilution factors and the protein-specific extinction coefficient, obtained with the ProtParam tool on the ExPASy server (112). The concentrated NT2RepCT solution was then transferred into a 1 mL syringe with a Luer lock (BD) and stored at −20 °C until further use.

Spidroin-Amyloid Hybrid Purification Via LLPS Formation.

Resuspension of the wet cell pellets with Tris-HCl-buffer was implemented according to the following final ratios: β16_2Rep 1:8, β16_3Rep 1:8, β16_5Rep 1:20, β16_7Rep 1:40. Cell lysis of resuspended cell pellet from up to 6 L shake flask cultures was performed with a high-pressure cell disruptor (CF1 Cell Disruptor from Constant Systems) at 30 kpsi and 5 °C. The cell lysates were centrifuged at up to 50,000 × g for 40 min, and 4 °C. Then, the supernatant was filtered through Filtropur S (Sarstedt) 0.45 μm syringe filters, followed by filtration using Filtropur S (Sarstedt) 0.2 μm syringe filters. 5 M NaCl was added to the clarified lysates at increments, mixed, until the solution turned irreversibly turbid. The final molar concentration of NaCl in the clarified lysate for inducing LLPS were β16_2Rep - 150 mM; β16_3Rep - 75 mM; β16_5Rep - 30 mM; β16_7Rep - 20 mM. For NT2RepCT, final NaCl concentrations of 10, 100, 500, and 1,000 mM were tested. To visualize the appearance of phase-separated droplets, 15 μL of clarified lysate was collected before and after NaCl addition, and the samples were viewed under an light microscope (Revolve, Echo). Then, the clarified lysates containing NaCl were incubated on ice for 30 min, followed by centrifugation at up to 18,000 × g for up to 30 min. The supernatant was discarded, and the spidroin condensates were reconstituted in 20 mM Tris-HCl, pH 8, aiming at keeping the volumes as low as possible (max 30 mL). The reconstituted spidroin condensates were dialyzed against 20 mM Tris-HCl pH 8 at 12 °C using a Spectra/Por 1 Dialysis membrane (Spectrum Labs) with a MWCO of 6 to 8 kDa. The buffer was changed three times within 18 h. After dialysis, the protein solution was filtered through a Filtropur S (Sarstedt) 0.2 μm syringe filter, and the β16_xRep variants were concentrated using centrifugal concentrators (Amicon Ultra 15 Centrifugal Filter, 10 kDa MWCO, Merck Millipore) to ~300 mg mL−1 within a few hours. The protein concentration was determined by measuring absorbance at 280 nm with a spectrophotometer and using the protein-specific extinction coefficient as described above. The spinning dopes made from β16_xRep were used directly for biophysical characterizations and spinning experiments, both performed on the same day.

Phase Diagram of Bacterial Cell Lysate.

The lysates were clarified as described in “Spidroin-amyloid hybrid purification via LLPS formation.” The initial protein concentration in the different lysates was estimated by SDS–PAGE (Table 1). Since a higher wet cell pellet to buffer ratio for β16_5Rep (1:20) and β16_7Rep (1:40) was used to recruit the target proteins into the soluble fraction, they had to be concentrated to also reach ~8 ± 1 mg mL−1. The lysates were concentrated by a factor of 4.5× (β16_7Rep) and 2.3× using a stirred cell (Merck) with a 10 kDa membrane, on ice at four bar (air).

A twofold dilution series of all lysates (~8 to 0.5 mg mL−1) was prepared using Tris–HCl buffer, while the NaCl concentration was varied between 5 and 160 mM for each concentration (Fig. 2 B and C). After NaCl addition, samples were incubated on ice for >15 min prior to turbidity measurements at 600 nm using a NanoDrop Ultra (Thermo Scientific), performed in triplicate. In addition, each condition was inspected using bright-field light microscopy (SI Appendix).

SDS–PAGE.

SDS–PAGE was performed to analyze expression and purification. The samples were mixed with an equal amount of 2× Laemmli + β-mercaptoethanol buffer, heated to 95 °C for 5 min, and 5 μL were loaded onto each well of a Mini-PROTEAN TGX Stain-Free Precast Gel 4 to 20%, with 15 wells (Bio-Rad). The gels were imaged with a ChemiDoc Touch (Bio-Rad), and to estimate band intensities, the Image Lab software (v6.1.0, Bio-Rad) was used.

Rheological Measurements.

For rheological measurements, an MCR302e (Anton Paar) equipped with a 50 mm conical plate (CP50) was used. 800 µL of the spinning dope with a protein concentration of 300 mg mL−1 was carefully injected into the center of the stage using a syringe, which was then equilibrated at 22 °C. The measuring gap was 0.1 mm. The flow curve was recorded using 21 measurement points according to a logarithmic ramp for shear rates between 0.1 to 100 s−1. The measurement time for each point was dependent upon reaching a steady state but was never longer than 30 s. If enough spinning dope was available (all except β16_2Rep), the flow curve was recorded in triplicates, with a freshly applied sample for each experiment.

CD Spectroscopy.

The concentrated dope samples were diluted to around 20 mg mL−1 and filtered using a 0.2 µm Filtropur S (Sarstedt). The protein samples were further diluted to 1 mg mL−1 using 20 mM Tris-HCl pH 8. The spectra were recorded on a combined J-1500 CD/Fluorimeter (Jasco) between wavelengths 185 to 260 nm using 1 nm bandwidth, 0.1 nm data pitch, a scanning speed of 50 nm min−1, and a data integration time of 0.5 s. The cuvette path length was 0.02 cm. Each spectrum represents an average of three individual accumulations. The measurements were performed in 20 mM Tris-HCl pH 8, at a temperature of 22 °C.

SEC.

SEC was performed on an ÄKTA Avant (Cytiva) with a HiLoad 16/600 Superdex 200 pg column (Cytiva). Concentrated spinning dope samples were diluted to 20 mg mL−1, filtered using a Filtropur S (Sarstedt) 0.2 µm syringe filter and applied on the capillary loop (0.5 mL) prior to injection. The loop was overfilled by a factor of 5. The sample was separated using a flow rate of 1 mL min−1 in 20 mM Tris-HCl pH 8, at 4 to 6 °C. The apparent size of the eluted fractions was estimated based on the expected elution volumes of protein standards provided by the manufacturer.

Turbidity Assay of Spinning Dope.

Turbidity measurements were performed at 600 nm and 22 °C using a Tecan M1000. 100 μL of concentrated dope was placed with a syringe into a 96-well optical plate together with a triplicate of 100 μL Tris-HCl 20 mM pH 8, serving as a blank.

Mass Photometry.

Mass photometry measurements were performed using a TwoMP instrument (Refeyn) operated in standard measurement mode with a regular field of view. Protein samples were diluted from concentrated stocks (300 to 320 mg mL−1) to a final concentration of 3.5 nM in 20 mM Tris-HCl (pH 8.0). The MassFerence™ P1 Calibrant (Refeyn) was used for mass calibration at a 1:500 dilution in 20 mM Tris-HCl (pH 8.0). Data were acquired for 60 s (3,000 frames) using AcquireMP software (version 2024 R1.1). Data analysis was performed in DiscoverMP (Refeyn), and mass distributions were obtained by fitting histograms with Gaussian functions. Sample purities were estimated by dividing the number of particles from spidroin assemblies by the total number of detected particles.

Biomimetic Fiber Spinning.

Artificial spider silk fiber spinning was performed as previously described using an all-aqueous spinning setup (28, 62). Briefly, glass capillaries with an outer diameter of 1 mm were pulled with a Micro Electrode Puller, and the tip was cut to obtain an inner diameter of 50 ± 10 µm. The capillary was connected to a syringe filled with mini-spidroins stored in 20 mM Tris-HCl, pH 8, at 300 mg/mL through a series of silicone tubing. The mini-spidroin was extruded at 17 µL min−1 into an 80 cm-long bath containing 0.75 M acetate buffer at pH 5. Once extruded, the soluble protein formed a solid fiber almost instantaneously, which could be pulled through the coagulation bath. At the end of the bath, the fiber was placed on a rotating wheel, with the fiber collected at 59 cm s−1. After spinning, the fibers were stored in a drying chamber at 22 °C and a relative humidity below 20%. Mechanical testing of the spun fibers was performed as described previously (28, 95), but is in detail described in SI Appendix.

ATR-FTIR.

ATR-FTIR spectra were recorded on fiber bundles using an Invenio S spectrometer (Bruker) equipped with an A225/Q Platinum-ATR accessory and an LN-MCT detector. The fibers were dried in a drying chamber before conducting the measurements. The sample compartment and optical bench were purged with dry and clean N2 prior to and during the experiments. For each fiber variant, the signal from six fibers was measured and averaged. The fibers were positioned onto the ATR crystal either parallel (n = 3) or perpendicular (n = 3) to the incoming light beam and pressed toward the crystal with minimal force. The aperture size was 1 mm and the scanner speed was set to 60 kHz. The spectra were recorded between 4,000 to 750 cm−1 at 2 cm−1 resolution and 300 accumulations were performed per sample. The spectra were background subtracted with air-only recordings, and atmospheric compensation was used during the measurements. The spectra were cut to include wavenumbers 1,720 to 1,580 cm−1 prior to normalization using min-max normalization with the Bruker OPUS software version 9.0. Smoothing was not applied.

Measurement of Fiber Diameter and Birefringence Using Polarized Light Microscopy.

To measure fiber diameter and birefringence index, we used a microscope (Axioskope 5/7 KMAT, Zeiss) equipped with a 10× objective. To measure the birefringence index, we employed a Compensator Tilting B 0 to 5 Lambda, 6 × 20. Under white light, this compensator was used to measure the Phase difference (also known as retardation), which was then divided by the fiber diameter to obtain the birefringence index.
