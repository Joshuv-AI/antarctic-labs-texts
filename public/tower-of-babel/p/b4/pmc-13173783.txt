A nomogram model for predicting acute kidney injury in critically ill patients with upper gastrointestinal bleeding: a study based on the MIMIC-IV database and external validation
Authors: Shuohui Li, Xiaoli Xie, Weiwei Niu, Jiajing Xing, Kun Lei, Feng Gao, Fanhao Lu, Hongjing Zhao, Shuxian Cheng, Na Wang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundAcute kidney injury (AKI) represents a prevalent and critical complication of upper gastrointestinal bleeding (UGIB). Predicting AKI is essential for timely intervention.MethodsThis retrospective research leveraged data from the MIMIC-IV 3.1 database and data from patients hospitalized at the Second Hospital of Hebei Medical University (HMUSH). The Boruta algorithm was employed in the training set to determine independent predictors of AKI. The study developed a nomogram based on logistic regression results to forecast AKI in UGIB. Its performance was evaluated using ROC curves, calibration curves, and decision curve analysis (DCA) in training, validation, and external validation sets.ResultsThis study incorporated 2,020 patients suffering from UGIB and 421 participants admitted to HMUSH. The Boruta algorithm identified 9 key predictors of AKI: age, creatinine-to-albumin ratio (CAR), prothrombin time (PT), alanine transaminase (AST) to aspartate aminotransferase (ALT) ratio (DeRitis), blood urea nitrogen (BUN), total bilirubin (TBIL), platelet count (PLT), pneumonia, and ascites. A nomogram was plotted utilizing these independent predictors. The nomogram demonstrated robust accuracy with area under the curve values of 0.776, 0.744, and 0.785 for the training set, validation set, and external validation set, respectively. Calibration curves disclosed good agreement between predicted and observed incidence rates of AKI. Additionally, the DCA disclosed net clinical benefit of the nomogram.ConclusionThis study presented a tool developed to forecast the likelihood of developing AKI in individuals with UGIB. The tool is potentially useful for guiding timely intervention and improving clinical results.Clinical trial numberNot applicable.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12876-026-04786-6.Keywords: Acute kidney injury, Upper gastrointestinal bleeding, Nomogram

Introduction

Upper gastrointestinal bleeding (UGIB) represents a prevalent emergency presenting as hematemesis and tarry stool, occasionally accompanied by dizziness or palpitations [1]. Its mortality rate ranges from 2% to 10% [2, 3]. In UGIB patients, rapid decrease of circulating blood volume, loss of hemoglobin, and reduced oxygen-carrying capacity place the body in a state of ischemia and hypoxia. The kidneys are highly sensitive to ischemia and hypoxia. Hence, acute kidney injury (AKI) frequently afflicts individuals with UGIB [4]. The incidence of AKI in UGIB varies from 30% to 40%. AKI is closely linked to unfavorable outcomes of UGIB [5, 6]. Individuals with concurrent UGIB and AKI exhibit markedly elevated mortality rates (41%–47%), prolonged hospital stays, and substantially increased medical costs [4, 7].

In current clinical practice, potential AKI following acute UGIB is primarily prevented and treated by timely supportive interventions, such as adequate fluid resuscitation, avoidance of nephrotoxic medications, and early use of renal protective medications [8]. However, these interventions are often implemented based on clinical manifestations of AKI that have already occurred, rather than as preventive strategies. This is particularly problematic because the widely adopted Kidney Disease: Improving Global Outcomes (KDIGO) criteria for diagnosing AKI, utilize serum creatinine, a marker that lags behind kidney injury. Consequently, by the time a patient is diagnosed with AKI, the prognosis is often unfavorable. Moreover, electronic AKI alert systems have not consistently improved patient survival, further highlighting the need for early detection of AKI [9].

This delay in diagnosis underscores the importance of identifying high-risk patients before they develop overt AKI. Early application of AKI care bundles in high-risk patients has been shown to improve composite kidney outcomes [10]. Early intervention strategies, such as biomarker-directed fluid management, have demonstrated significant benefits in critically ill patients [11]. Therefore, it is imperative to develop a predictive model that can identify patients at risk for AKI early in their clinical course, thereby facilitating timely intervention and improving outcomes.

Existing scoring systems (e.g., Rockall, Glasgow-Blatchford) primarily focus on bleeding severity or rebleeding risk, failing to integrate early warning indicators of renal function impairment [12, 13]. Therefore, it is imperative to develop a tool that can forecast AKI early in further research.

In recent years, nomograms have been extensively applied in prognostic models for critically ill patients owing to their advantages in visualizing and individualizing predictions [14]. Although studies have refined predictive models for AKI in individuals with acute variceal bleeding and those with acute non-variceal bleeding [5, 15], comprehensive research on UGIB remains insufficient. This study innovatively utilized data from MIMIC-IV, incorporated key variables (e.g., vital signs, laboratory parameters, and complications), and established a nomogram model for predicting AKI in critically ill individuals suffering from UGIB. To further validate the generalizability of the model, an independent external dataset was utilized to validate its clinical utility and robustness.

Materials and methods

Data sources

Data from MIMIC-IV 3.1 [16, 17] were leveraged in this study. This database incorporates more than 190,000 patient records and 450,000 hospitalization records from 2008 to 2022. It covers demographic data, laboratory parameters, drug therapies, vital signs, surgeries, diagnoses, medication management, and survival follow-ups. The authors received the US National Institutes of Health (NIH) training on protecting human research participants and passed the Collaborative Institutional Training Initiative (CITI) exam (Record ID 68429492) to obtain access to the data. All patients were anonymized in this database. Hence, no informed consent was required.

Data were also collected from patients receiving intensive care for UGIB at The Second Hospital of Hebei Medical University (HMUSH) between May 2023 and April 2025. Personal information and laboratory test results, imaging findings, treatments, and diagnoses during hospitalization were stored in a non-public electronic medical record (EMR) system. The HMUSH Medical Ethics Committee approved this study (Approval No.: 2025-R517). All medical information in this study was de-identified. This study did not interfere with clinical care plans, thus requiring no informed consent.

Study design and population

The following patients were incorporated: (1) Individuals admitted to the intensive care unit (ICU) due to UGIB, with diagnosis codes based on the International Classification of Diseases (ICD-9/10) as follows: K250, K252, K254, K256, K260, K262, K264, K266, K270, K272, K274, K276, K2901, K2921, K2931, K2941, K2591, K2961, K2971, K2981, K2991, K3182, K31811, K920, K921, I8501, I8511, 4560, 45,620, 5780, 53,082, 53,021, 53,100, 53,101, 53,120, 53,121, 53,140, 53,141, 53,160, 53,161, 53,200, 53,201, 53,220, 53,221, 53,240, 53,241, 53,260, 53,261, 53,300, 53,301, 53,320, 53,321, 53,501, 53,511, 53,521, 53,531, 53,541, 53,571, 53,551, 53,561, 53,783, and 53,784 (Supplementary Table S1); (2) Individuals aged ≥ 18 years; (3) Individuals admitted to the ICU for the first time; (4) Individuals staying in the ICU for over 24 h. Individuals with missing or abnormal values for albumin, creatinine, alanine aminotransferase (ALT), aspartate aminotransferase (AST), or AKI outcomes within 24 h of admission were excluded. Patients from MIMIC-IV were assigned to the training and validation sets randomly in a 7:3 ratio, while patients from HMUSH were leveraged for external validation (Fig. 1).

Data extraction

Patient information from MIMIC-IV was extracted utilizing SQL code in PostgreSQL 17.2. Information about patients admitted to HMUSH was collected from the EMR system. The collected patient information included: (1) Demographic characteristics: age, gender; (2) Baseline vital signs upon admission to ICU: heart rate (HR), systolic blood pressure (SBP), diastolic blood pressure (DBP), respiratory rate (RR); (3) Comorbidities: diabetes, hypertension, pneumonia, liver cirrhosis, ascites, coronary atherosclerotic heart disease, identified using ICD-9/10 codes (Supplementary Table S1); (4) Baseline laboratory parameters within 24 h of admission to ICU: albumin, creatinine, white blood cell (WBC) count, hemoglobin (HB), platelets (PLT), alkaline phosphatase (ALP), alanine aminotransferase (ALT), aspartate aminotransferase (AST), total bilirubin (TBIL), blood urea nitrogen (BUN), sodium ion (Na), potassium ion (K), chloride ion (Cl), magnesium ion (Mg), international normalized ratio (INR), and prothrombin time (PT); (5) Outcome: complicated by AKI or not.

Following the KDIGO guidelines [18], AKI was diagnosed when any of the criteria below was met: (1) Increase of serum creatinine (SCr) by ≥ 0.3 mg/dl (≥ 26.5 µmol/l) within 48 h; (2) Increase of SCr to ≥ 1.5 times baseline level within 7 days; (3) Urine output < 0.5 mL/kg/hour for ≥ 6 consecutive hours. Baseline SCr was defined as the most recent value recorded within 7 days prior to ICU admission, or the value measured at the time of ICU admission if no prior value was available.

CAR = Serum creatinine (µmol/L) / Albumin (g/L) [19].

DeRitis = AST (IU/L) / ALT (IU/L) [20].

Statistical methods

Quantitative data with > 20% missing values were excluded. Multiple imputation based on the random forest approach was applied to quantitative data with < 20% missing values in the training set. The normality of quantitative data was tested. The Mann-Whitney U test was employed to test the intergroup comparisons of non-normally distributed quantitative data, which were reported as median (interquartile range). The Pearson’s chi-square test or Fisher’s exact test was applied to test the intergroup comparisons of count data, which were reported as frequencies and percentages. To identify key variables linked to AKI, all variables were screened in the training set. Given the critical impact of the selection of variables on model performance, the Boruta algorithm was applied. This algorithm is a random forest-based method designed to select variables most relevant to the target variable from a large pool of candidate variables. The multicollinearity among variables was ascertained by computing the variance inflation factors (VIFs). A nomogram model for predicting AKI in UGIB was established utilizing the selected variables. Due to the high prevalence of AKI, a class weighting strategy was employed to mitigate the bias introduced by class imbalance in the model’s predictions. Specifically, higher weights were assigned to the non-AKI group to penalize the model for misclassifying the minority class. The nomogram model was further validated for performance by utilizing the validation set. The predictive capability of the nomogram model was examined by plotting and analyzing receiver operating characteristic (ROC) curves. The accuracy of the model was appraised by utilizing calibration curves. The clinical utility of the model was validated by decision curve analysis (DCA). Data from HMUSH were utilized for external validation. All statistical analyses were executed in R 4.5.1, with P < 0.05 signaling statistical significance.

Results

Following the inclusion and exclusion criteria, 2,020 patients were incorporated, comprising 1,599 individuals from MIMIC-IV and 421 individuals from HMUSH.

Baseline characteristics

Patients from MIMIC-IV were assigned in a 7:3 ratio to the training and validation sets, with 1,120 individuals in the training set and 479 individuals in the validation set. Table 1 and Supplementary Tables S2-S5 present the incidence of AKI in UGIB across the training and validation sets, along with demographic characteristics and clinical information. The incidence rates of AKI were 84.91%, 84.96%, and 32.78% in the training set, validation set, and external validation set, respectively. In this study, the incidence of AKI in patients from MIMIC-IV was markedly elevated compared to previous investigations, while the incidence of AKI in individuals with HMUSH was essentially consistent with previous studies. Furthermore, the training set and validation set exhibited no significant differences in baseline clinical data (P value > 0.05) except for BUN, CAR, and DeRitis.

UGIB patients with AKI exhibited elevated HR and RR, lower SBP, elevated levels of WBC, TBIL, ALP, BUN, K, Mg, anion gap (AG), INR, PT, CAR, and DeRitis at admission, and decreased Na and Cl levels relative to those without AKI. Besides, individuals with AKI often experienced comorbidities such as ascites, coronary heart disease, liver cirrhosis, and pneumonia.

Variable selection

The training set was imputed and then the Boruta algorithm was applied. A total of 10 variables were selected as meaningful predictors for AKI in UGIB, including age, CAR, INR, PT, DeRitis, BUN, TBIL, PLT, pneumonia, and ascites (Fig. 2). Multicollinearity analysis of these 10 variables revealed collinearity between INR and PT. After excluding INR, the remaining 9 variables all exhibited a VIF value below 2, indicating no multicollinearity (Supplementary Tables S6-S7).

Development and validation of the nomogram model

A nomogram was drawn as per the 9 predictors identified via the Boruta algorithm to forecast the risk of AKI in UGIB (Fig. 3). The scores assigned to each variable were summed, and the total score of each patient represented the predicted probability of AKI in UGIB.

The nomogram for predicting acute kidney injury in patients with upper gastrointestinal bleeding. The nomogram presented 9 variables, including ascites, admission_age, BUN, platelet, pneumonia, PT, TBIL, CAR and DeRitis. To calculate the scores, a vertical line was drawn from each variable to the “Points” line. The values were summed as the final score. Eventually, to ascertain the risk of AKI in UGIB, a vertical line was plotted downward from the “Total Points”

ROC curves, calibration curves, and DCA were utilized to corroborate the accuracy of this nomogram model. As revealed by the ROC curve analysis, the training set and validation set exhibited an area under the curve (AUC) of 0.776 and 0.744, respectively (Fig. 4), implying robust predictive utility of the model. Calibration curves accurately reflected the alignment between predictions derived from the nomogram and the actual outcomes of each patient. Results (Fig. 5) showed that the calibration curves of both data sets fitted the diagonal line (the ideal reference line). This indicated that the model was well fitted, with high consistency between predicted outcomes and actual outcomes. DCA results (Fig. 6) revealed that patients in both data sets benefited more from interventions guided by this nomogram model. When the threshold probability for clinical decision-making ranged from 58% to 98%, compared to either the “treat all” or “treat none” strategy, the nomogram designed to forecast AKI offers greater net clinical benefit (NCB).

Besides the training set and internal validation set from MIMIC-IV, data from 421 patients with UGIB admitted to HMUSH were gathered as an external validation set to independently validate this novel predictive model. Discussion

This investigation built and validated an accessible and useful nomogram to forecast the likelihood of AKI among patients receiving intensive care for UGIB. Our nomogram incorporated nine predictors: age, CAR, PT, DeRitis, BUN, TBIL, PLT, pneumonia, and ascites. This model may be used to forecast AKI in individuals and thereby support the decision-making for individualized treatment and monitoring strategies. Data from different sources were leveraged for internal and external validation to support the robustness and generalizability of our model. The fit of the model was examined via internal validation, while the generalizability of the model was evaluated through external validation. Both validations were based on real clinical practice, which enhanced the reliability of the model.

Research has suggested age as a significant influencing factor of AKI [21]. With advancing age, renal functional reserve declines markedly, which is characterized by reduced nephron number, glomerulosclerosis, and decreased renal blood flow. Consequently, older patients with UGIB become more susceptible to renal injury during acute hemorrhage and hypoperfusion [22]. Besides, older UGIB patients often have underlying comorbidities such as hypertension, diabetes, and cardiovascular diseases, along with a high rate of polypharmacy (such as non-steroidal anti-inflammatory drugs [NSAIDs], angiotensin-converting enzyme inhibitors [ACEIs], diuretics), further elevating the risk of renal injury [23].

By integrating SCr and albumin levels, CAR reflects renal injury, inflammation, and malnutrition triggered by UGIB. It has recently been studied as a predictor of poor outcomes in various diseases (including infections and cardiovascular diseases). Particularly, it reflects systemic inflammation and nutritional status [24, 25]. Elevated creatinine is fundamental for diagnosing AKI. The creatinine level at admission demonstrates predictive value for AKI related to sepsis, AKI after cardiac surgery, and AKI in liver cirrhosis [24–26]. Albumin is the primary protein maintaining the colloid osmotic pressure of plasma. Hypoalbuminemia results in the transfer of intravascular fluid into interstitial spaces, exacerbating hypovolemia [27]. Severe UGIB (especially when complicated by infection or shock) can trigger intense inflammatory responses. Inflammatory mediators can induce endothelial injury, microcirculatory disturbance, and damage to renal tubular epithelial cells, directly promoting AKI development. A low albumin level serves as a negative acute-phase marker for this inflammatory state [28, 29].

Recent studies have indicated that DeRitis and TBIL may be used to predict AKI [30–34], though the precise mechanisms remain unclear. DeRitis and TBIL are closely associated with liver function impairment [20, 35]. Hepatic insufficiency disrupts the balance between vasodilatory and vasoconstrictive factors, resulting in visceral and systemic vasodilation alongside renal vasoconstriction, consequently causing renal function impairment [36]. Additionally, elevated DeRitis is strongly linked to inflammation [37]. As previously noted, inflammation is strongly linked to AKI. Moreover, ALT is predominantly present in the liver, while AST is ubiquitous throughout the body, including the heart and kidneys [38]. Thus, elevated DeRitis may indicate significant impairment of cardiac and renal function potentially linked to AKI.

BUN has been demonstrated as a predictor of AKI [39–41]. BUN is produced from the hepatic hydrolysis of amino acids and eliminated by the kidneys, with 40% of BUN reabsorbed in the proximal tubules under the regulation of the renin-angiotensin-aldosterone system (RAAS) [42, 43]. During UGIB (particularly gastric and duodenal bleeding), blood enters the intestinal tract. The protein-rich blood in the intestinal tract is broken down by intestinal bacteria, producing large amounts of ammonia (NH3). NH3 is absorbed into the liver via the portal venous system and converted into urea via the urea cycle. Increased urea synthesis in the liver leads to elevated BUN levels [44, 45]. Thus, elevated BUN serves as a strong signal of the risk of renal hypoperfusion and an indirect indicator of bleeding severity and duration.

Prolonged PT and thrombocytopenia are markedly linked to higher risks of AKI in UGIB. Prolonged PT indicates dysfunction of the extrinsic coagulation pathway (deficiency of coagulation factors II, V, VII, and X), while thrombocytopenia suggests that massive platelets aggregate and are depleted at the bleeding site [46]. Both factors make UGIB difficult to control. Massive blood loss sharply decreases effective circulating blood volume, which subsequently reduces the renal perfusion pressure and glomerular filtration rate (GFR), directly inducing prerenal AKI [4]. During UGIB, inflammatory responses usually occur. The inflammatory process may injure the vascular endothelium, activate platelets, and lead to coagulation dysfunction [47]. Subsequently, activated platelets aggregate and their granules release cytokines and chemokines. This exacerbates inflammation and excessively activates the coagulation system, both of which play crucial roles in the subsequent formation of microthrombi leading to renal injury [48]. The resulting fibrin deposition in glomerular capillaries further intensifies ischemic renal injury [49].

Pneumonias are closely linked to AKI. AKI frequently occurs in individuals hospitalized for community-acquired pneumonia (CAP) and increases mortality. About one-fifth of patients with severe pneumonia develop AKI [50, 51]. Besides microthrombus formation in the kidneys triggered by inflammatory responses as described above, inflammatory mediators directly damage renal tubular epithelial cells, inducing acute tubular necrosis (ATN) and ultimately leading to AKI [52]. Another study has indicated that mechanical ventilation is associated with AKI. Pneumonias cause hypoxemia, renal tissue hypoxia, and mitochondrial dysfunction, triggering apoptosis or necrosis of renal tubular cells [53]. Additionally, drugs used to treat pneumonia, such as glycopeptide antibiotics, are nephrotoxic and can damage renal tubular epithelial cells [54].

Ascites, particularly cirrhosis-related ascites, substantially elevates the risk of AKI in UGIB patients through complex and interrelated mechanisms. First, ascites (especially massive ascites) elevates intra-abdominal pressure, compressing the inferior vena cava and renal vessels and thereby impeding venous return and renal blood flow [55]. Second, ascites predominantly occurs in patients with liver cirrhosis, who exhibit marked visceral vasodilation. This causes blood stasis in the splanchnic circulation and relative depletion of effective arterial blood volume. This state activates the RAAS and sympathetic nervous system, inducing renal vasoconstriction and thereby exacerbating renal injury [56].

This study is the first to apply a nomogram model to forecast AKI in patients suffering from UGIB. Compared with traditional single biomarkers or clinical scoring systems, nomograms provide a more intuitive, easily understandable, and user-friendly risk assessment tool by integrating multiple clinical variables. Our nomogram model was based on readily accessible clinical data, demonstrating high clinical utility. Compared to complex predictive models, nomograms are simpler to use. Early identification of high-risk patients not only improves outcomes but also reduces the incidence of AKI. This lowers the treatment costs and the risk of complications during hospitalization. Early intervention strategies can substantially enhance the efficacy of clinical treatment.

However, while the biological plausibility of individual predictors is important, the more immediate priority is to explore how our nomogram performs compared to current clinical practice and how it could be implemented in real-world settings. Unlike traditional scoring systems such as the Sequential Organ Failure Assessment (SOFA) or the Acute Physiology and Chronic Health Evaluation II (APACHE II), our model is based on easily accessible clinical data and is adoptable for routine practice, especially in settings where complex scoring systems are not routinely applied. However, the absence of a standardized scoring system in our self-built database precludes a direct head-to-head comparison with established ICU scoring systems, such as SOFA or APACHE II, which could have demonstrated the incremental clinical value of our model in comparison to current practice. Consequently, there is a critical need for further prospective validation studies to evaluate how our nomogram could be integrated into clinical workflows and improve patient outcomes in real-world settings.

This study also shows certain limitations. First, the self-built database used in this study does not include a standardized scoring system, which somewhat limits the comparability of data and the interpretation of results. Second, while this retrospective study has identified potential clinical variables and models, how to apply these findings to individualized patient assessment and treatment strategies remains to be further validated. Finally, patients from MIMIC-IV exhibit a markedly higher risk of AKI than patients admitted to HMUSH, while the AUC value for external validation is higher than that for internal validation. This may be attributed to differences in populations and data quality. Patients in the MIMIC-IV dataset and patients admitted to HMUSH may exhibit significant disparities in demographic characteristics (e.g., gender, racial distribution) and variations in disease severity, ratio of comorbidities, and treatment methods or quality. In terms of data quality, data from MIMIC-IV spanned from 2008 to 2022, covering a long timeframe, whereas data from patients admitted to HMUSH spanned from 2023 to 2025, covering a short period. Besides, different hospitals may have diverse specifications and procedures of data entry and employ diverse equipment and technologies. Moreover, due to substantial missing values, several potential influencing factors, such as relevant variables including the use of inotropes or vasopressors upon admission, need for intubation as a marker of severe bleeding, neutrophil-to-lymphocyte ratio, and volume of blood transfusion, are not included in the analysis. Therefore, it is impossible to exclude the potential confounding effects of these factors on the risk of AKI. Although known influencing factors are controlled as much as possible during data analysis, the retrospective design makes it difficult to eliminate potential confounders. Therefore, the findings need to be prospectively validated to enhance the reliability of the conclusion.

Conclusion

This investigation built and validated an accessible and potentially useful nomogram model. The model incorporates age, CAR, PT, DeRitis score, BUN, TBIL, PLT, pneumonia, and ascites as meaningful predictors of AKI in UGIB. This nomogram model may be used to forecast the likelihood of AKI in individuals with UGIB and help clinicians identify high-risk individuals. This may facilitate the development of individualized treatment plans and improve the outcomes of UGIB patients.
