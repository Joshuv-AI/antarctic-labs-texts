Applying machine learning to predict quality ANC determinants in Bangladesh: a BDHS-2022 cross-sectional study
Authors: Tanzila Tamanna, Shohel Mahmud
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
Quality antenatal care (ANC) is critical for maternal and neonatal health. Despite improvements in healthcare, disparities in ANC access and quality persist, particularly in underserved areas of Bangladesh. This study aimed to identify the key determinants of quality ANC in Bangladesh and provide evidence to support data-driven maternal health strategies aligned with Sustainable Development Goal (SDG) 3. This study analyzed data from 3,549 women aged 15–49 years from the BDHS 2022. Machine learning models, including Random Forest, XGBM, Neural Networks, and Logistic Regression, were applied to predict quality ANC. Feature importance was assessed using SHapley Additive exPlanations (SHAP) and Gini-based rankings to identify the most influential predictors. Only 21.9% of women received quality ANC. Wealth index, maternal and partner education, maternal age, media exposure, and urban residence emerged as the strongest determinants. Random Forest demonstrated the highest predictive performance (accuracy: 74.1%, precision: 79.8%, F1-score: 0.83). SHAP and feature importance analyses confirmed that wealth index was the most influential predictor. Significant inequalities in ANC quality and coverage exist in Bangladesh. Targeted interventions addressing socioeconomic and educational disparities, along with improved media outreach and urban-rural healthcare access, are essential to enhance maternal healthcare. These findings provide actionable insights for policy and programs aiming to achieve SDG 3 and reduce maternal mortality.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1038/s41598-025-21411-w.

Introduction

Pregnancy is a crucial phase in a woman’s reproductive life, necessitating comprehensive healthcare to ensure favorable maternal and neonatal health outcomes. Antenatal care (ANC) plays an essential role in monitoring and managing maternal health during this period, aiming to detect potential complications early and improve pregnancy outcomes. ANC comprises a structured set of health interventions, including medical screenings, vaccinations, therapeutic measures, and educational support, all provided by professional healthcare providers1. High-quality ANC facilitates early identification and management of pre-existing maternal health conditions, reducing risks associated with pregnancy and childbirth. Maternal and infant mortality rates can be significantly reduced with the implementation of quality ANC services. Research has demonstrated that effective ANC is vital in preventing neonatal deaths, stillbirths, and various obstetric complications2.

Quality ANC is characterized by a comprehensive framework of interventions, ensuring optimal maternal health throughout pregnancy. This includes physical examinations, laboratory testing such as urine analysis, blood group and Rh factor determination, and stool sample assessment for parasite infestations. Furthermore, essential components of quality ANC include iron and folic acid supplementation for at least 90 days, the administration of at least two tetanus toxoid vaccinations, and counseling on postnatal care (PNC), nutrition, rest, birth preparedness, and complication readiness3,4. Evidence suggests that ensuring access to quality ANC services could prevent approximately 28% of maternal deaths worldwide5. Given its critical role in maternal healthcare, ANC has been recognized as a fundamental aspect of reproductive health strategies globally.

Reducing maternal mortality remains a priority in global health agendas, as highlighted by the third Sustainable Development Goal (SDG)6,7. Despite efforts to enhance healthcare accessibility, maternal mortality continues to present a significant challenge, with over 800 women dying daily due to pregnancy-related complications8. In 2020, an estimated 287,000 women lost their lives during pregnancy and childbirth, with approximately 95% of maternal deaths occurring in low- and middle-income countries (LMICs). A significant decline in maternal mortality has been observed in South Asia, with the maternal mortality ratio (MMR) dropping by 67%—from 408 to 134 per 100,000 live births9. However, despite notable progress, deficiencies in the quality of maternal healthcare services continue to impede further improvements in maternal and neonatal health outcomes10.

The Sustainable Development Goals (SDGs) aim to reduce the global maternal mortality ratio (MMR) to 70 per 100,000 live births and neonatal mortality to 12 per 1,000 live births by 203011. Bangladesh faces considerable challenges in achieving these targets. According to the 2022 Bangladesh Sample Vital Statistics report, the MMR in Bangladesh remains 156 per 100,000 live births, and the neonatal mortality rate (NMR) is 20 per 1,000 live births12. Data from BDHS 2022 further highlight persistent gaps in ANC coverage and quality: while 41% of pregnant women attended at least four ANC visits, only 21% received quality ANC. Despite a slight improvement from 18% in 2017–18, 73% of women still cannot access four or more ANC visits with skilled health professionals due to barriers such as healthcare accessibility and resource limitations13,14. The World Health Organization (WHO) recommends a minimum of eight ANC visits for optimal maternal and neonatal health outcomes, yet a substantial gap remains in Bangladesh’s adherence to these recommendations.

Although previous research has analyzed ANC coverage in Bangladesh, studies specifically focusing on quality ANC are limited14–16. Most prior studies have used data up to BDHS 2017–18 and relied on traditional regression or association-based methods14–16, which assume linear relationships and may fail to capture complex, non-linear interactions among multiple demographic, clinical, and contextual factors17,18. Consequently, there is a knowledge gap regarding the current determinants of quality ANC and the complex patterns influencing maternal healthcare utilization in Bangladesh.

Machine learning offers several advantages over traditional methods. It can uncover hidden patterns, handle high-dimensional data, model complex non-linear relationships, and improve predictive accuracy18,19. These features make machine learning particularly suitable for identifying key determinants of quality ANC and providing actionable insights for policy and program design. Though maternal mortality can be significantly reduced through quality ANC utilization, an in-depth assessment of variations in ANC service quality and its evolution over time is essential20–23, to date, no study has applied machine learning to analyze quality ANC determinants in Bangladesh, representing both a methodological and substantive gap in maternal health research.

To address these gaps, this study uses BDHS 2022 data to analyze quality ANC in Bangladesh. The objectives are to identify the key determinants of quality ANC and leverage machine learning techniques to capture complex relationships among predictors, providing updated, evidence-based insights to inform targeted interventions, improve ANC accessibility and service quality, and contribute to reducing maternal and neonatal mortality.

Methods and materials

Figure 1 illustrates our meticulously designed study plan, which guided every step of the process, from initial data collection and analysis to the presentation and interpretation of the results. In overview, this study used nationally representative secondary data from Bangladesh Demographic and Health Survey (BDHS) 2022 dataset24. Women aged from 15 to 49 considered as target participants. Since data with missing values should be handle carefully; reviewing some other articles, predictors with less than 40% missing values were considered in this study, and predictors with more than 40% missing values were removed from the data set25–27. Then predictors with less than 40% missing values were imputed using random forest method for imputing missing values28–30. In this study, the descriptive statistics were employed to show the basic information about the respondents. Boruta algorithm was then used to select the important features and the confirmed and tentative variables were used to perform the analysis. After that, the data was randomly split into training and test sets in 75:25 ratios. To address the class imbalance in quality ANC data, a smote sampling strategy was implemented during model training. This approach involved generating synthetic samples of the minority class by interpolating between existing instances, thereby equalizing the class distribution and reducing the model’s bias toward the majority class. The smote sampling technique was seamlessly integrated into the training workflow by specifying “sampling = smote” in the cross-validation process of the caret package31. By ensuring a more balanced representation of both classes, this method enhanced the fairness and reliability of model evaluation, leading to more robust predictions. Important features were extracted using the Boruta method. Furthermore, K-Nearest Neighbours (KNN), Neural Networks (NN), Classification and Regression Tree (CART), Light Gradient Boosting (LGBM), Support Vector Machine (SVM), Random Forest (RF), Extreme Gradient Boosting (XGBM), and Logistic Regression (LR) were used, along with other machine learning techniques. Each method was validated using ten-fold cross-validation. The performance of each model was assessed and compared using the following metrics: accuracy, Cohen’s kappa, sensitivity, specificity, F1 score and precision. Ultimately, the best feature selection method and the key characteristics of quality ANC were found.

Data source

This study utilized nationally representative secondary data from the 2022 Bangladesh Demographic and Health Survey (BDHS), conducted under the auspices of the National Institute for Population Research and Training (NIPORT) in collaboration with Mitra and Associates. Technical and financial support was provided by the United States Agency for International Development (USAID) and ICF International (Rockville, MD, USA). The BDHS employed a stratified two-stage sampling design. In the first stage, 675 enumeration areas (EAs) were selected with probability proportional to size, comprising 237 urban and 438 rural EAs. In the second stage, an average of 45 households per EA was systematically selected, yielding a total sample of 30,375 residential households. The survey captured a wide array of data, including socio-demographic characteristics, educational attainment, regional distribution, household decision-making, and health-seeking behaviors of Bangladeshi women. Additional details and access to the dataset are available at https://dhsprogram.com/data/.

Study variables and measurements

Dependent variables

Based on BDHS definitions and incorporating core indicators from BDHS, a quality ANC index was created. High-quality ANC was defined as having at least four visits, at least one of which was conducted by a medically trained provider, and the provision of essential services, such as blood and urine testing, weight and blood pressure measurement, and counseling on pregnancy complications.

This study uses quality ANC as the dependent variable. To identify contributing factors, each quality ANC index was binary coded (0 for not getting the quality ANC, 1 for getting the quality ANC). Potential risk factors for getting quality ANC in Bangladesh were also assessed.

Independent variables

The study considered a range of categorical independent variables potentially associated with the outcome of interest. These variables reflect socio-demographic, reproductive, and household characteristics relevant to maternal healthcare utilization. A detailed description of all independent variables is provided in supplemental table A1.

Feature selection method

Boruta algorithm

Feature selection was performed using the Boruta algorithm to enhance model interpretability while preserving key information. Boruta identifies relevant features by introducing shuffled “shadow features” and comparing importance scores, such as Mean Decrease Accuracy or Mean Decrease Impurity. A feature is significant if its Z-score consistently surpasses the highest shadow feature Z-score. While robust in detecting predictors, Boruta is computationally intensive due to repeated model training, requiring careful consideration of accuracy-efficiency trade-offs, especially in high-dimensional datasets32.

Traditional predictive model

Logistic regression

Logistic regression, a binomial GLM, models binary outcomes using maximum likelihood estimation and a logit link between response and predictors. Common in population health research, it supports inference and prediction without hyper-parameter tuning, making it well-suited for binary data analysis32,33.

Machine learning methods

K-nearest neighbors

K-Nearest Neighbors (KNN) is a non-parametric algorithm for classification and regression, assigning labels based on a majority vote among k nearest neighbors. It uses distance metrics like Euclidean and is influenced by training data distribution34,35. While simple and adaptable, KNN requires feature normalization and is sensitive to outliers, especially in high-dimensional datasets. Weighted variants enhance accuracy by prioritizing closer neighbors36,37.

Neural networks

Neural networks, inspired by biological neurons, learn complex patterns through iterative training. Their architecture includes input, hidden, and output layers, where activation functions like sigmoid, ReLU, or tanh introduce nonlinearity. Known for high predictive accuracy, they effectively handle correlated inputs and model intricate relationships in high-dimensional data38.

Extreme gradient boosting model (XGBM)

XGBoost is a high-performance ensemble algorithm based on gradient boosting, optimizing accuracy and efficiency across regression, classification, and ranking tasks. Its L1 and L2 regularization reduces overfitting, making it ideal for structured tabular data. Known for speed and robustness, XGBoost consistently excels in machine learning competitions and predictive modeling39–41.

Light gradient boosting model (LGBM)

To optimize ANC visit prediction, LightGBM with Information Gain (IG) selects key features for classifying valid and fraudulent visits42, ensuring efficiency and precision. Bayesian hyperparameter tuning refines num_leaves, max_depth, and learning_rate. LightGBM’s scalability is enhanced by feature bundling and gradient-based one-side sampling (GOSS), which reduces complexity and prioritizes high-gradient samples for accurate estimation43.

Classification and regression tree (CART)

CART is an interpretable machine learning algorithm for classification and regression, recursively partitioning data into a hierarchical tree structure. As a non-parametric method, it handles high-dimensional datasets without distributional assumptions. Splitting data into training and validation sets allows optimal pruning, improving prediction and reducing overfitting. Its simplicity makes it valuable in health research where explainability matters44,45.

Support vector machine (SVM)

SVM is a supervised learning model for classification, leveraging support vectors to define decision boundaries while ignoring irrelevant samples46. Though computationally intensive, it excels in binary classification. Using the kernel trick, SVM performs non-linear classification by mapping data to higher-dimensional spaces47,48. This study applied a linear SVM classifier for data modeling.

Random forest

Random Forest (RF) is an ensemble learning method that builds multiple decision trees using bootstrap samples to enhance stability and reduce variance49. It randomly selects features for each tree, lowering correlation and improving accuracy. Predictions are based on majority voting, mitigating overfitting, especially with more trees50.

Statistical analysis

Descriptive statistics summarized participant demographics, while the Chi-square test assessed associations (p-value < 0.05) with quality ANC. Boruta, leveraging random forests, enhanced feature selection by capturing complex interactions, improving accuracy in categorical and high-dimensional datasets32.

Seven widely used machine learning algorithms were considered for model development: K-Nearest Neighbors (KNN), Neural Networks (NN), Classification and Regression Tree (CART), Light Gradient Boosting (LGBM), Support Vector Machine (SVM), Random Forest (RF), Extreme Gradient Boosting (XGBM), and Logistic Regression (LR). Model performance was evaluated using multiple metrics, including Cohen’s Kappa, accuracy, 95% confidence intervals, sensitivity, specificity, F1-score, and precision. Cohen’s Kappa, a statistical measure, assesses the agreement between two raters or classifiers while accounting for chance agreement, making it particularly useful in classification tasks51. While accuracy provides an overall assessment of model performance, Cohen’s Kappa was used to adjust for chance agreement, improving reliability in imbalanced datasets. Sensitivity (recall) was prioritized to ensure the accurate identification of malnourished children, with specificity employed to reduce false positives. Precision was utilized to confirm the accuracy of positive predictions, particularly when false positives could have significant consequences. The F1-score, which balances precision and recall, was selected for its ability to provide a more comprehensive evaluation of model performance in imbalanced datasets52–54.

To enhance the robustness and generalizability of the models, 10-fold cross-validation was applied to reduce overfitting. The best-performing model was selected to identify key factors contributing to quality ANC, and their significance was further evaluated using a Feature Importance plot (based on Gini importance) and Shapley Additive Explanations (SHAP) values. All statistical analyses were conducted using R (Version 4.1.1) and IBM SPSS (Version 25.0) for Windows.

Results

Supplemental Table A2 summarizes the characteristics of the 3,549 respondents included in this study. Most women were aged 20–29 years, with the largest proportions residing in Chattogram (17%), Dhaka (15%), and Mymensingh (13%). The majority lived in rural areas (67%), had access to electricity (85%), and belonged to male-headed households (89%). Muslim women accounted for 92% of the sample. Among contraceptive users, 63% reported using modern methods, while an additional 20% indicated intentions for future use.

Regarding child characteristics, 98% of children were alive, with an almost equal distribution between boys and girls. The majority of infants (76%) were reported to be of normal size at birth. Access to healthcare remained a challenge for 34% of women, and 47% reported some level of media exposure. Substantial wealth inequality was observed, with 41% of respondents falling in the poorest quintile and 39% in the richest. Educational attainment was diverse, with 19% having completed higher education. Employment among women was relatively low, with 77% not engaged in formal work, and nearly all women (99%) were married.

Supplemental Table A3 indicates that only 21.9% of women received quality ANC. Uptake was significantly higher among women aged 25–29 years (p = 0.003) and those living in urban households with electricity (p < 0.001). Strong associations (p-value < 0.001) were also observed with household wealth, media exposure, and delayed age at first birth, whereas access to medical help showed only a marginal effect (p = 0.042). In contrast, no significant associations were found with women’s occupation, marital status, child sex, or sanitation access.

Discussion

This study provides significant insight into the healthcare, social, and demographic variables affecting the quality of ANC in Bangladesh. By evaluating the predictive performance of multiple machine learning models, the study identified key determinants of quality ANC among women aged 15–49. The Boruta algorithm effectively selected relevant features, and the integration of machine learning techniques allowed a nuanced understanding of the complex relationships influencing ANC quality. Among the models tested, Random Forest (RF) consistently demonstrated superior overall predictive performance, capturing non-linear interactions and handling high-dimensional data effectively. Although logistic regression (LR) showed a high area under the curve (AUC), RF and XGBoost (XGB) provided robust classification capabilities, emphasizing their utility in identifying at-risk subgroups.

The most influential predictors identified through both Gini index feature importance and SHAP analysis were wealth index, residence, media exposure, respondent education, partner education, and maternal age. Wealth status emerged as a primary determinant of ANC quality; 59% of women from higher-income families received quality ANC. Consistent with previous Bangladeshi studies, women from poorer households were significantly less likely to receive high-quality ANC15,55. This aligns with findings from broader low- and middle-income country analyses, emphasizing that economic disparities remain a barrier to equitable healthcare access56,57.

Residence also had a strong influence on ANC quality. Urban women were more likely to receive high-quality care than rural women, likely due to better access to healthcare facilities, trained personnel, and diagnostic services55,58,59. Rural residence, therefore, continues to be a critical factor for policy-targeted interventions. Media exposure further enhanced ANC uptake, as women with access to television, radio, or other information channels were more aware of pregnancy-related health needs and services15,55,61,62.

Maternal and partner education were key predictors of quality ANC. Women with higher education were more likely to utilize comprehensive ANC services, consistent with previous Bangladeshi studies15,55,59,64–67. Higher educational attainment empowers women to make informed health decisions and influences household health-related practices. Similarly, partners’ education positively correlated with ANC utilization, highlighting the importance of household-level educational empowerment68–70.

Maternal age also affected ANC quality, with older women more likely to receive comprehensive care than younger mothers15,66,71,72. This may be due to greater awareness, prior pregnancy experience, and understanding of healthcare benefits.

Regarding model behavior, RF’s superior performance demonstrates the value of ensemble methods in capturing complex, non-linear relationships that traditional regression-based approaches might miss18,20–22. Models like KNN and CART exhibited lower sensitivity, indicating that certain women at risk of receiving poor-quality ANC could be overlooked. This emphasizes the importance of careful interpretation when applying predictive models to policy and program design.

In summary, this study highlights socio-economic and educational disparities in ANC utilization in Bangladesh and provides evidence for targeted interventions. Policies aiming to improve maternal health should prioritize economically disadvantaged and rural populations, enhance educational opportunities for women and their partners, and expand media-based health promotion campaigns. Machine learning models offer actionable insights for identifying high-risk subgroups, which can guide more effective, data-driven strategies to improve ANC quality and coverage.

Conclusions

The sociodemographic and economic factors affecting the quality of prenatal care (ANC) in Bangladesh are examined in this study, which uses a machine learning methodology to identify trends that traditional methods of statistical analysis could miss. The Boruta algorithm was successful in identifying important predictors, and the Random Forest model demonstrated the highest prediction accuracy among the different algorithms. Structural disparities in ANC access and quality are highlighted by the continuous identification of mother and partner education, wealth index, residence, media exposure, and maternal age as significant variables.

According to the results, women who live in cities, have higher incomes, are more exposed to the media, and have more education are far more likely to obtain high-quality ANC. These discrepancies reflect historical injustices in access to healthcare. The significance of household-level educational empowerment is further shown by the influence of partners’ education on mothers’ health-seeking behavior. While younger and rural women continue to be underserved, older women were found to obtain better ANC care, maybe as a result of their increased awareness and experience.

This work directly supports SDG 3: Good Health and Well-Being, especially Target 3.1, which seeks to increase access to qualified ANC treatments in order to lower maternal mortality worldwide. It also supports SDG 5: Gender Equality by highlighting the importance of partners’ and women’s empowerment and education in gaining access to healthcare. Our research also contributes to SDG 10: Reduced Inequalities by exposing systematic disparities in the distribution of ANC services, emphasizing the necessity of focused interventions for underprivileged and rural communities.

The advancement of national and international maternal health goals depends on evidence-based policies and practices that support fair access to maternal healthcare, as well as media and educational outreach initiatives. In conclusion, enhancing ANC quality in Bangladesh requires addressing the discrepancies found in this study, particularly those pertaining to wealth, parental education, residency, mothers age and media exposure.

Strengths, limitations and future work

The use of sophisticated machine learning techniques, especially ensemble approaches and model interpretability aids like SHAP values and feature priority rankings, is one of this study’s main strengths. Our conclusions are strengthened by these tools, which also made it possible to evaluate intricate inter-variable connections in a nuanced manner.

There are, however, several limitations to this study. Because of its cross-sectional nature, which limits the ability to draw conclusions about causality, longitudinal research is necessary to fully comprehend the connections between maternal health, socioeconomic determinants, and high-quality ANC. The possibility of residual confounding cannot be ruled out, as certain contextual or health system variables not captured in the BDHS dataset (e.g., provider quality, community-level cultural practices) may also influence ANC quality. Furthermore, recall and reporting bias are potential concerns, since many indicators of ANC services were self-reported by mothers and may be affected by memory limitations or social desirability, which could influence the accuracy of reported care quality.

The use of only one feature selection technique (Boruta) might have caused bias; investigating other techniques could improve the robustness and performance of the model even more. Accuracy may also have been impacted by issues with data quality, such as missing values and possible inconsistencies, which could be addressed using data imputation or more thorough pretreatment techniques. Even though Random Forest lessens overfitting, there are still dangers associated with high-dimensional data, and using regularization and cross-validation techniques would increase the generalizability of the model. Additionally, because the results are specific to Bangladesh, they might not be as broadly applicable to other areas with distinct socioeconomic and healthcare contexts. While Bayesian approaches may help overcome dataset imbalances by adding prior information, hyperparameter adjustment may further optimize model accuracy. Future research can create a more reliable framework for anticipating and treating quality ANC by integrating these methods.

Policy and program implications

From a policy perspective, these findings underscore the need for multi-pronged strategies. Improving maternal and partner education should be prioritized through health literacy programs and community-based awareness initiatives. Financial protection mechanisms targeting low-income households could reduce wealth-related inequalities in ANC access. Investments in rural health infrastructure and outreach services are necessary to bridge the urban–rural gap. Furthermore, mass media and digital health campaigns can be leveraged to enhance awareness of ANC services, especially among younger and less-educated women. By directly linking the identified determinants to actionable interventions, these findings provide program managers with evidence-based entry points to improve ANC quality in Bangladesh.
