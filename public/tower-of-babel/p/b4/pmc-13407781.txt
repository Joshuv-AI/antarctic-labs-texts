Up-regulation of SLC26A3 enhances oxaliplatin sensitivity in colorectal cancer
Authors: Junyang Wang, Shaodong Tang, Min Zhang, Songhe Li, Dacheng Wen
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
The role of SLC26A3 in the sensitivity to oxaliplatin, a widely used chemotherapy drug for colorectal cancer (CRC), remains unclear. This study aimed to explore the association between SLC26A3 expression and oxaliplatin sensitivity. Total 240 differentially expressed mRNAs were identified, with 124 downregulated and 116 upregulated in oxaliplatin-resistant CRC. WGCNA identified a module significantly associated with resistance. GO and KEGG analyses revealed enrichment in fatty acid metabolic processes and organic anion transmembrane transporter activity. PCA and feature gene selection identified a 15-gene signature, with SLC26A3 highlighted by machine learning models as a key discriminator of chemosensitivity. Immunohistochemical staining and survival analysis indicated that higher SLC26A3 expression correlated with better survival outcomes. Additionally, a significant negative correlation between SLC26A3 and AKT1 was observed, suggesting that while SLC26A3 may enhance oxaliplatin sensitivity, its effect may be overshadowed by other factors in resistant cells. SLC26A3 may play a crucial role in oxaliplatin sensitivity in CRC. The identified gene signature could potentially serve as a biomarker for predicting chemosensitivity to oxaliplatin.

Introduction

Colorectal cancer is a common malignant malignancy. Newly diagnosed cases of colorectal cancer in China rank second among all malignancies, and the incidence rate is higher in males than in females [1]. According to the National Cancer Center, approximately 517,100 new cases were reported in 2022. About 35% of diagnosed colorectal cancer patients are in the late stages. Currently, Chemotherapy remains the cornerstone of therapy for advanced colorectal cancer [2]. Traditional chemotherapeutic agents represented by fluorouracil, irinotecan, capecitabine, and platinum agents, as well as newer chemotherapeutic agents such as trifluridine and tipiracil hydrochloride tablets, expand therapeutic options for the treatment of advanced colorectal cancer [3]. These drugs have substantially prolonged the survival time of patients with advanced colorectal cancer.

Oxaliplatin (oxPt), as a third-generation platinum-based antitumor drug, was developed after cisplatin and carboplatin [4]. It binds to DNA and prevents DNA double-strand deconvolution, thus inhibiting DNA replication and exerting anti-tumor effects [5]. Compared with the previous two generations of platinum drugs, oxaliplatin has better water solubility and anti-tumor properties, which causes relatively less toxicity related to the gastrointestinal tract, liver, kidneys and bone marrow [6]. However, long-term use of oxaliplatin leads to genetic mutations in cancer cells, which in turn lead to drug resistance [7]. Approximately 40–50% of patients manifest primary resistance to oxaliplatin during first-line therapy [8]. Resistance accumulates rapidly with successive treatment lines, exceeding 85% by the third line or beyond [9]. Virtually all patients with metastatic colorectal cancer ultimately develop oxaliplatin resistance, only a small minority of MSI-high/TMB-high subtypes remain an exception [9]. Resistance to oxaliplatin seriously affects its therapeutic efficacy, which is one of the urgent problems to be solved in clinical practice. Therefore, understanding the molecular mechanisms of resistance to oxaliplatin has important clinical implications for the treatment of colorectal cancer.

There are few predictive biomarkers that are beneficial for chemotherapy in colorectal cancer. Retrospective cohort studies indicates that the 3435T allele is associated with elevated P-glycoprotein expression and oxaliplatin resistance. The C3435T variant operates via the NF-κB/STAT3 axis to up-regulate P-glycoprotein (P-gp) and cooperates with ERCC1 and GSTP1, thereby establishing a dual “efflux + DNA repair” resistance pathway. P-gp overexpression enhances oxaliplatin efflux and reduces intracellular drug concentrations by 30–50%, resulting in a 3- to 5-fold increase in the in vitro IC₅₀ [10]. The T allele is associated with hypomethylation of the ABCB1 promoter, augmenting transcriptional activity; demethylation with 5-azacytidine reverses the resistant phenotype [11]. miR-1271-5p has been shown to directly bind the PLCB4 3′ UTR at nucleotides 1338–1344, thereby repressing PLCB4 translation. This down-regulation attenuates the IP₃/DAG–Ca²⁺–PKC signaling cascade, leading to diminished MAPK/ERK and VEGF pathway activity. Consequently, apoptotic evasion and reduced angiogenesis ensue, which collectively drive oxaliplatin resistance [12]. Finding ways to overcome therapeutic resistance paves the way for precision oncology treatments, allowing for more personalized patient care. In this study, we compared oxaliplatin-resistant and non-resistant colorectal cancer patients by bioinformatics analyses, screened for oxaliplatin sensitivity related genes, and validated them by cell and in vivo experiments. This study provides new targets that are expected to overcome drug resistance, improve treatment efficacy, and prolong patient survival.

Materials and methods

TCGA data acquisition and processing

The Cancer Genome Atlas (TCGA) colon adenocarcinoma (COAD) and rectum adenocarcinoma (READ) RNA-seq data (HTSeq-TPM) and corresponding clinical information were downloaded from the UCSC Xena Browser (https://xenabrowser.net/datapages/). The TPM (Transcripts Per Million) values were log2-transformed (log2(TPM + 1)) for downstream analysis. After removing samples with missing clinical data, a total of 621 colorectal cancer (CRC) patients were included in the final analysis, comprising 457 COAD and 164 READ cases.

SLC26A3 expression stratification

Based on the median expression level of SLC26A3 (median = 5.375 log2(TPM + 1)), patients were divided into two groups: SLC26A3-high (n = 311) and SLC26A3-low (n = 310). The optimal cutoff value was determined using the surv_cutpoint function from the “survminer” R package.

Data source and differently expressed mRNAs screening

Raw Data of mRNA were obtained from the GEO database (http://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/geo/) and the Xena TCGA database (https://xenabrowser.net/datapages/?dataset=TCGA-COAD.star_tpm.tsv&host=https%3 A%2 F%2Fgdc.xenahubs.net&removeHub=https%3 A%2 F%2Fxena.treehouse.gi.ucsc.edu%3A443). Data inclusion criteria were as follows. Samples were colorectal cancer samples or cell lines, controls were included in the data, and data were mRNA expression profiles. A total of 2 datasets, GSE83129 (https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/geo/query/acc.cgi? acc=GSE83129) and GSE42387 (https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/geo/query/acc.cgi? acc=GSE42387) were screened according to the criterion. GSE83129 data contains 26 oxPt-unresistance colorectal cancer cells and 9 oxPt-resistance colorectal cancer cells. The GSE42387 data contains 9 control colorectal cancer cell lines and 9 cisplatin-treated colorectal cancer cell lines. The gene chip platform was the Agilent-014850.

Grouping information and expression data of GSE83129 data were obtained using the GEOquery package of R language, and analyzed using the limma package. Compared with oxPt-resistance groups, differently expressed mRNAs were screened with the criteria of p.adj < 0.05 and \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:\left|\mathrm{l}\mathrm{o}\mathrm{g}2\mathrm{F}\mathrm{C}\right|$$\end{document} >1.

Following differential-expression analysis, a sample dendrogram integrated with a trait heatmap was generated to corroborate sample-classification robustness and to visualize inter-sample heterogeneity in relation to drug-resistance phenotypes. The limma-adjusted mRNA expression matrices from GSE83129 and GSE42387 were concatenated after restricting the feature space to genes common to both Agilent platforms. The merged matrix was subjected to missing-value imputation, log₂ transformation and quantile normalization. Pairwise Jensen–Shannon divergence between samples was computed and hierarchically clustered with Ward’s minimum-variance method (Ward.D2) implemented in hclust. Cluster stability was evaluated by 1,000 multiscale bootstrap resamples using pvclust; branches with approximately unbiased (AU) probability ≥ 0.85 were retained. Phenotypic variables (oxPt-sensitive, oxPt-resistant, cisplatin-treated, control) were encoded as binary indicators and concatenated with experimental batch as a covariate matrix. The resulting dendrogram and trait matrix were co-visualized with ComplexHeatmap: the tree was truncated at a height corresponding to 1-AU = 0.15 to define major clades, while the adjacent heatmap displayed color-coded resistance status and batch origin for each sample, permitting an intuitive assessment of concordance between transcriptional clustering and phenotypic annotations.

Weighted gene co-expression network analysis

Weighted gene co-expression network analysis (WGCNA) is a systems biology method used to characterize gene association between different samples. It can be used to identify highly synergistically variable sets of genes and screen candidate biomarker genes. In this study, samples were analyzed by WGCNA using R language package (1.72). Using the normalized expression matrix, the top 5000 genes with the largest mad values were selected to construct a dendrogram of clustered samples, and the soft threshold value of 10 was confirmed after removing inappropriate samples. A one-step method was used to construct the co-expression matrix, which was analyzed combining with the clinical data.

Gene ontology and KEGG analysis

The intersection of genes in the significant module with the screened differential genes was taken. Differential mRNA gene names were converted to enterzid names using the org.Hs.eg.db package (version 3.11.4) for R. The cluster Profiler package (version 3.16.1) was used for GO enrichment analysis of differential expressed mRNAs. Differentially expressed genes (adj. P < 0.05 and |log₂FC| > 1) were mapped against the KEGG database (release 2023-03) and subjected to over-representation analysis with the clusterProfiler R package using the hypergeometric test.

Principal component analysis (PCA) and feature-gene selection

The differential-expression matrix (adj. P < 0.05 and |log₂FC| > 1) was mean-centered and scaled to unit variance prior to PCA performed with the prcomp function in R. A scree plot was used to inspect the variance explained by each component; the criterion of > 50% variance explained by PC1 was applied to designate the informative axis. PC1 loadings were extracted, ranked by absolute value, and the top 15 genes were retained as the PC1-signature gene set. Sample scores were recalculated with this gene set, plotted in the PC1–PC2 plane, and color-coded by oxaliplatin-resistance status to visually assess discriminatory performance.

Machine-learning signature derivation and cross-validation

To derive a minimal mRNA signature that distinguishes chemosensitive from chemo-resistant tumours, we first reduced dimensionality by PCA (prcomp, R v4.3.1) applied to the 15-gene panel retained after differential expression analysis (|log2FC| > 1, FDR < 0.05) in the discovery set. A Random Forest (RF) classifier was then built with the caret package (v6.0-94) using the six top-ranking PC-loading genes. Owing to the limited sample size, model performance was evaluated by stratified 5-fold cross-validation; each test fold therefore contained only 6–7 samples. Hyper-parameters (mtry = 3, ntree = 2000, nodesize = 1) were kept constant to minimise over-tuning. In parallel, feature significance was assessed with the Boruta wrapper (Boruta v8.0.0, 2000 permutations, confidence level = 0.99). Models generated in each fold were archived in./Boruta_plots/.

External validation

The four genes jointly ranked among the top five by both RF (mean Gini decrease) and Boruta (confirmed) were extracted. Their expression values were retrieved from the independent GEO data set GSE83129 and visualized with ggplot2 boxplots. Gene expression analysis of consensus genes

To investigate the impact of oxaliplatin treatment on gene expression in colorectal cancer cells and normal primary cells, we selected two datasets from the GEO database: GSE42387 (colorectal cancer cells, divided into oxaliplatin-treated and untreated groups) and GSE83129 (normal primary cells, similarly divided into oxaliplatin-treated and untreated groups). We performed RMA normalization on the raw CEL files using the affy package in R and batch effect correction with the ComBat method from the sva package. Subsequently, we statistically analyzed the gene expression differences between the treated and untreated groups using the limma-voom method, adjusting P-values with the Benjamini-Hochberg method, considering FDR < 0.05 as statistically significant. Finally, we used the ggplot2 package to generate boxplots to display the expression levels of each gene in the treated and untreated groups.

Immunohistochemical analysis

Immunohistochemical (IHC) images of SLC26A3 can be obtained from the Human Protein Atlas (HPA) database (https://www.proteinatlas.org/ENSG00000108108-SLC26A3/tissue/colon) by downloading the original IHC images (https://images.proteinatlas.org/36055/70821_A_8_3.jpg).

Expression and survival analysis

The expression levels of SLC26A3 were analyzed using colorectal adenocarcinoma (COAD) data from The Cancer Genome Atlas (TCGA) dataset (https://xenabrowser.net/datapages/?cohort=GDC%20TCGA%20Colon%20Cancer%20(COAD)&removeHub=https%3 A%2 F%2Fxena.treehouse.gi.ucsc.edu%3A443). The Wilcoxon rank-sum test was applied to compare the expression levels between normal and tumor tissues. Kaplan-Meier survival analysis was conducted to assess the correlation between SLC26A3 expression and patient survival, with the log-rank test used to determine statistical significance.

Gene correlation analysis

To explore the relationship between SLC26A3 and other genes potentially involved in oxaliplatin resistance, we analyzed gene expression data from the GSE42387 and GSE83129 datasets. Pearson correlation coefficients were calculated to evaluate the linear relationship between SLC26A3 and genes known to be associated with oxaliplatin resistance, including AKT1. A heatmap was generated to visualize the correlation matrix between the two datasets.

General information of patients

Total 36 colorectal cancer patients who were hospitalized in the Second Hospital of Jilin University between August 2022 and August 2023 were selected as study subjects, including 19 males and 17 females, aged 41–89 years, with a mean of 65.16 ± 11.82 years. In clinical research, patients with colorectal cancer (CRC) treated with oxaliplatin-based regimens (e.g., FOLFOX) are routinely classified into “oxaliplatin-sensitive” and “oxaliplatin-resistence” according to the Response Evaluation Criteria in Solid Tumors version 1.1 (RECIST 1.1). The adjudication algorithm is as follows. All patients must complete at least four cycles (≈ 8 weeks) of standard FOLFOX chemotherapy. Within 4 weeks after cycle 4, contrast-enhanced CT of chest, abdomen, and pelvis (supplemented by MRI if indicated) is performed. Target lesions are re-measured and the sum of the longest diameters (∑LD) is compared with baseline. The study was approved by the Ethics Committee of The Second Hospital of Jilin University (SB2021143), and the patients or their families signed the informed consent. The study was conducted according to the guidelines of the Declaration of Helsinki. Surgically resected cancer tissues were collected and total RNA was extracted by lysed tissue extraction with TRIzol reagent.

Cell culture and treatment

Human colorectal cancer cell line (SW620) was purchased from the cell bank of the Chinese Academy of Sciences and cultured in DMEM with 10% fetal bovine serum. The cells were cultured in a constant temperature incubator at 37℃ with 5% CO2, and were grown in monolayer. When the cells were in the logarithmic phase, increasing concentrations of oxaliplatin were treat to induce drug resistance in SW620 cells. The initial concentration of 2 µmol was gradually increased to 60 µmol/L.

SW620-Oxa cells in logarithmic growth phase were inoculated in 6-well plates at 5 × 105/well and transfection was started 24 h later. Cells were cultured in serum-free medium for 4 h prior to transfection and transfected with si-SLC26A3 and siNC according to the instructions of Lipofectamine™ 2000. After transfection for 48 h, subsequent experiments were performed. Si-SLC26A3#1, si-SLC26A3#2, si-SLC26A3#3 and si-NC were designed and synthesized by Daweike co., ltd (Shanghai, China). The sequences of the siRNAs used were shown as follows: Si-SLC26A3#1, (upstream) 5′-GGAUUCAAGGACUUCGUTT-3′, (downstream) 5′-ACAAGAAGUCCUUGAAUCC-3′; Si-SLC26A3#2, (upstream) 5′-CAGUACAAUGCCUAUCAATT-3′, (downstream) 5′-UUUGAUAAGGCAUUGUACUG-3′; Si-SLC26A3#3, (upstream) 5′-UUCGAGAAGCUGACUGUATT-3′, (downstream) 5′-UAACAGUCAGCUCUUCGATT-3′.

Cell viability

Assay Cell viability was assessed using the MTT assay. SW620 sensitive and resistant cells were treated with varying concentrations of oxaliplatin for 48 h. The absorbance was measured at 570 nm using a microplate reader.

Immunohistochemical (IHC) staining assay

For the expression analysis of SLC26A3 in sensitive and resistant tissues, fresh-frozen colorectal cancer tissue samples were categorized based on their response to oxaliplatin treatment. IHC staining was performed on 5 μm tissue sections after fixation, rehydration, and antigen retrieval. Sections were then blocked for peroxidase and non-specific binding, followed by incubation with a primary SLC26A3-specific antibody overnight at 4 °C. After washing, sections were treated with an HRP-conjugated secondary antibody for 1 h at room temperature. The signal was developed with DAB chromogen, and sections were counterstained with hematoxylin, dehydrated, and mounted. Two pathologists independently scored the staining intensity and the percentage of positively stained cells, and a composite score was calculated. Tissues were classified as high or low expressers based on the median composite score.

Q-PCR assay

For tissues and cells of each group, total RNA was extracted using Trizol regent. Reverse transcription was performed using the PrimeScript RT kit, SLC26A3 and AKT1 was amplified by PCR. The reaction conditions included pre-denaturation 95 °C for 5 min, 95 °C for 20 s, 60 °C for 20 s, 72 °C for 30 s, repeated for 40 cycles. At the end of the reaction, the results were analysed using GAPDH as an internal reference, and the ΔCt value was automatically derived by GraphPad Prism software. Three replicate wells were used in each group and the experiment was repeated three times.

Western blot assay

Tumor tissue or cells were taken, cell lysate was added, ground and centrifuged. The total protein concentration was determined by Caulophyllotoxin method. Total protein 40 µg was separated by electrophoresis. After membrane transfer, proteins were enclosed by 5% skimmed milk powder, and primary antibody (SLC26A3, CSB-PA005693, 1:1000, CUSABIO CO. LTD, China; AKT1, EP2109Y, 1:1000, Abcam) and secondary antibody were added for incubation. The proteins were chromatographed by BCIP/NBT, and the grey value of SLC26A3 was analyzed by an automatic electrophoresis gel imaging analyzer. The ratio of the target band to the internal reference band represented the expression level.

Colony formation assay

SW620-Oxa cells were cultured in DMEM medium supplemented with 10% FBS at 37 °C in a 5% CO2 incubator. Cells were transfected with si-NC (non-targeting control) and si-SLC26A3 using Lipofectamine 2000 according to the manufacturer’s protocol. 24 h post-transfection, cells were seeded at a density of 500 cells per well in a 6-well plate. After 10–14 days of incubation, colonies were fixed with methanol for 10 min, stained with 0.5% crystal violet for 10 min, and washed with PBS to remove excess dye. Colonies consisting of more than 50 cells were counted.

In vivo xenograft model and drug treatment

All animal experiments were approved by the Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee (IACUC) and conducted in accordance with the NIH Guide for the Care and Use of Laboratory Animals. BALB/c nude mice (6–8 weeks old, male) were purchased from the Laboratory Animal Center and housed under specific pathogen-free (SPF) conditions. SW620 cells (5 × 10⁶) stably transfected with si-SLC26A3 or si-NC were resuspended in 100 µL PBS mixed with Matrigel (1:1, v/v) and subcutaneously injected into the right flank of nude mice (n = 6 per group). Three days after tumor cell inoculation, when palpable tumors were detected, all mice received intraperitoneal injections of oxaliplatin (OXA, 5 mg/kg) dissolved in 5% glucose solution twice a week for three consecutive weeks. Tumor volumes were measured every 7 days using digital calipers and calculated according to the formula: V = (length × width²)/2. At the end of the experiment (day 21), mice were euthanized, and tumors were excised, photographed, and weighed. Tumor tissues were snap-frozen in liquid nitrogen for subsequent molecular analyses or fixed in 4% paraformaldehyde for histological examination.

Statistical analysis

Baseline clinical and pathological characteristics were compared between SLC26A3-high and SLC26A3-low groups. Continuous variables (age, OS.time, SLC26A3 expression) were presented as mean ± standard deviation (SD) and compared using unpaired Student’s t-test or Mann-Whitney U test as appropriate. Categorical variables (gender, cancer type, TNM stage, survival status) were presented as frequencies (percentages) and compared using Pearson’s chi-squared test or Fisher’s exact test. All statistical analyses were performed using R software (version 4.3.1) with the following packages: “survival”, “survminer”, “ggplot2”, and “tableone”. A two-sided P-value < 0.05 was considered statistically significant. Patient survival data were stratified according to SLC26A3 expression levels, and Kaplan-Meier survival curves were generated to compare the survival outcomes between high and low expressers. Statistical analyses were performed using SPSS 20.0 software. Mean ± SD was used to express the measurement data that conformed to normal distribution. One-way analysis of variance (ANOVA) was used to compare the data of multiple groups, and Mann–Whitney method was used to compare the data of two groups. All experiments were repeated for 3 times. P < 0.05 indicated that the difference was statistically significant.

Results

Association between SLC26A3 expression and clinicopathological characteristics in TCGA-CRC cohort

To investigate the clinical relevance of SLC26A3 in colorectal cancer, we analyzed the relationship between SLC26A3 expression levels and clinicopathological features using the TCGA-COAD/READ combined cohort. Patients were stratified into SLC26A3-high (n = 311) and SLC26A3-low (n = 310) groups based on the median SLC26A3 expression level (5.375 log2(TPM + 1)).

As shown in Table 1, SLC26A3 expression was significantly higher in the SLC26A3-high group compared to the SLC26A3-low group (7.24 ± 1.27 vs. 3.51 ± 1.84, P < 0.001). No significant differences were observed between the two groups regarding cancer type (COAD vs. READ, P = 0.059), gender (P = 0.809), age (65.89 ± 12.31 vs. 66.97 ± 13.03 years, P = 0.290), T stage (P = 0.297), N stage (P = 0.769), or M stage (P = 0.661). Similarly, there were no significant differences in AJCC TNM stage distribution between the two groups (Stage I/II/III/IV, P = 0.703). Notably, survival status analysis revealed that the SLC26A3-low group had a significantly higher proportion of deaths compared to the SLC26A3-high group (24.6% vs. 17.2%, P = 0.029), suggesting an association between low SLC26A3 expression and worse prognosis. Although overall survival time did not reach statistical significance between the two groups (25.26 ± 21.35 vs. 28.68 ± 26.57 months, P = 0.079), the trend toward shorter survival in the SLC26A3-low group warrants further investigation in larger cohorts. These findings indicate that SLC26A3 expression is not significantly associated with conventional TNM staging parameters but may serve as an independent prognostic indicator for survival outcomes in colorectal cancer patients.

GO enrichment and KEGG analysis of intersecting mRNAs

A total of 99 intersecting genes were obtained by taking the intersection of mRNAs related to the Blue module and differently expressed mRNAs (Fig. 2A). These mRNAs were mainly enriched in fatty acid metabolic process, apical part of cell and organic anion transmembrane transporter activity. Moreover, the resulting KEGG pathway enrichment profile revealed the top five significant pathways as “Coronavirus disease–COVID-19”, “Ribosome”, “Bile secretion”, “Pancreatic secretion”, and “Proximal tubule bicarbonate reclamation”. Notably, genes associated with oxaliplatin resistance were markedly enriched in the biological themes of protein translation and bile acid metabolism, implying that ribosome biogenesis and the bile acid micro-environment may contribute to the response of colorectal cancer cells to platinum-based drugs (Fig. 2B and C).

Machine-learning signature derivation and external validation

Figure 4A and B shows the five-fold cross-validation output of the Random Forest (RF) classifier. In every fold the model achieved perfect class separation: confusion matrices contained no off-diagonal elements, driving both sensitivity and specificity to 100% and yielding an AUC of 1.00 (Fig. 4A). The mean Gini-importance ranking identified CA2, PLCE1, ZG16, SLC26A3 and CEACAM7 as the five most discriminatory features (Fig. 4B). Figure 4C and D displays the corresponding Boruta analysis. All 15 input genes were submitted to 2 000 permutation tests; the identical 5 genes (PLCE1, SLC26A3, CA2, MIF and ZG16) were confirmed as “hits” in every fold (Fig. 4C). The temporal profile of Z-scores (Fig. 4D) illustrates that these attributes rapidly exceeded the significance threshold and remained consistently above the shadow-distribution ceiling, corroborating the RF-importance ranking. Intersecting the top-five lists from RF and Boruta yielded four consensus genes: PLCE1, SLC26A3, CA2 and ZG16. Their expression values were extracted from the independent validation set GSE83129. Box-plot analysis revealed significantly higher transcript abundance in chemo-resistant tumors for all four genes (P < 0.001, Wilcoxon rank-sum; Fig. 4E–H), validating the biological relevance of the small-sample-derived signature.

Knockdown of SLC26A3 attenuates the therapeutic efficacy of oxaliplatin in metastatic colorectal cancer xenografts

To investigate whether SLC26A3 expression modulates the chemosensitivity of metastatic colorectal cancer to oxaliplatin, we employed the SW620 cell line, a well-established model derived from lymph node metastasis of colorectal adenocarcinoma. SW620 cells with stable SLC26A3 knockdown (si-SLC26A3) or negative control (si-NC) were subcutaneously implanted into nude mice, and all animals received standard oxaliplatin treatment (5 mg/kg, twice weekly) to evaluate the therapeutic response under SLC26A3-deficient conditions.

As shown in Fig. 9A, tumor growth was significantly accelerated in the si-SLC26A3 group compared to the si-NC group following oxaliplatin treatment. At day 21, the average tumor volume in the si-SLC26A3 group reached 391.5 ± 20.4 mm³, whereas the si-NC group exhibited markedly suppressed tumor growth with a volume of 215.2 ± 15.3 mm³ (P < 0.001). Consistent with the growth curve data, excised tumor specimens from the si-SLC26A3 group were substantially larger than those from the si-NC group (Fig. 9B). Quantitative analysis of tumor weights further confirmed these findings. The si-SLC26A3 group displayed a significantly higher tumor burden compared to the si-NC group, representing an increase in tumor mass (P < 0.001, Fig. 9C). These results demonstrate that silencing SLC26A3 significantly compromises the anti-tumor efficacy of oxaliplatin in metastatic colorectal cancer cells, indicating that SLC26A3 is essential for maintaining chemosensitivity even in advanced-stage disease.

Discussion

Oxaliplatin is one of the commonly used chemotherapy drugs for the treatment of colorectal cancer [13]. However, long-term use of the drug may induce resistance to Oxaliplatin, which limits its therapeutic efficacy. The development of resistance is a complex process involving multiple mechanisms, including increased drug metabolism, impaired apoptotic pathways, activation of drug efflux pumps, enhanced DNA repair mechanisms, autophagy, and changes of microenvironment [14]. Thereby, the molecular mechanism of Oxaliplatin resistance in colorectal cancer was researched in this study, and SLC26A3 was screened as a critical gene for Oxaliplatin resistance.

SLC26A3, a chloride/bicarbonate exchanger, exerts tumor-suppressive effects by maintaining pH homeostasis and intestinal barrier integrity. Its expression is frequently downregulated in colorectal cancer, with higher levels correlating with improved prognosis. The core mechanism involves a negative regulatory axis with AKT1: activated AKT1 phosphorylates FOXO transcription factors, inducing their nuclear exclusion and transcriptional repression of SLC26A3. Concurrently, AKT1-driven Warburg effect generates lactate accumulation, which induces SLC26A3 lactylation modification under acidic tumor microenvironment, thereby altering its functional stability. Downregulation or functional impairment of SLC26A3 disrupts ion transport and pH homeostasis, promoting tumor proliferation and metastasis, ultimately leading to oxaliplatin resistance. This intricate interplay underscores the strategic activation of pro-survival AKT1 signaling coupled with SLC26A3 suppression as a mechanism for therapeutic evasion in colorectal cancer.

Bioinformatics analyses provide valuable information to understand the molecular mechanisms of oxaliplatin resistance in colorectal cancer and guide future therapeutic strategies [15]. Through these analyses, researchers identify potential biomarkers and therapeutic targets, providing a theoretical basis for the development of new therapeutic agents and personalized treatment options. Through bioinformatics analysis, several genes associated with colorectal cancer prognosis were identified, such as AQP8, CXCL8 and ZG16 [16–18]. The expression levels of these genes are significantly associated with the prognosis of colorectal cancer, which may serve as potential diagnostic and therapeutic targets. Giacomini et al. [19] proposed that solute carrier (SLC) family proteins affect chemosensitivity by regulating drug uptake/efflux. For example, the efflux of platinum-based drugs mediated by ABCG2 is a key mechanism of drug resistance. Khan et al. [20] reviewed the metabolic regulatory role of SLC proteins in the tumor microenvironment in Cell, pointing out that SLC26A3 maintains intracellular pH homeostasis by mediating Cl⁻/HCO₃⁻ exchange, which may affect the efficiency of DNA damage repair. Birsoy et al. [21] found through metabolic screening that the inhibition of SLC family members can enhance the sensitivity of cancer cells to metabolic inhibitors. This study screened SLC26A3 as an oxaliplatin sensitivity gene of colorectal cancer.

SLC26A3 is a chloride/bicarbonate exchanger that plays a role in a variety of physiological and pathological processes [22]. According to the 2025 AACR meeting abstract, under acidic microenvironmental conditions SLC26A3 undergoes lactylation modification, and its expression level directly modulates colorectal cancer cell sensitivity to oxaliplatin as well as their invasive and migratory capacities [23]. Several studies have suggested that the expression of SLC26A3 in colorectal cancer cells may be related to the biological behavior of tumors [24, 25]. In previous studies, the expression of SLC26A3 in colorectal cancer is usually lower than in normal tissues, but its high expression may be associated with improved long-term survival of patients [24]. This suggests that SLC26A3 may play an inhibitory role in the development of colorectal cancer. In addition, the PI3K/AKT signaling pathway may have a bidirectional regulatory effect on SLC26A3 expression [26]. In Caco-2 cells, moderate activation of the PI3K/AKT signaling pathway may promote SLC26A3 expression, whereas sustained over-activation may inhibit its expression. By analyzing the pattern of immune cell infiltration in colorectal cancer and its association with clinical characteristics and overall survival, it was confirmed that there are different types of immune cell infiltration patterns in colorectal cancer, which are closely associated with tumor progression and overall survival [27]. These immune cells include T cells, dendritic cells, macrophages, neutrophils, mast cells and others. Their type, function, density and infiltration location in the tumor microenvironment reflects host defense and tumor progression. The correlation between SLC26A3 expression and long-term survival of patients was investigated and verified in this study.

The negative correlation between AKT1 and SLC26A3 in colorectal cancer arises from a multi-layered regulatory network. At the transcriptional level, activated AKT1 phosphorylates FOXO family transcription factors (including FOXO1 and FOXO3a), promoting their binding to 14-3-3 proteins and subsequent nuclear export to the cytoplasm, thereby abolishing FOXO transcriptional activity and downregulating expression of its target gene SLC26A3. This establishes a core regulatory axis of “AKT1 activation → FOXO nuclear exclusion → SLC26A3 transcriptional repression” [28]. At the metabolic-epigenetic level, AKT1-driven Warburg effect enhances tumor glycolysis, leading to massive lactate accumulation in the tumor microenvironment, which subsequently induces SLC26A3 protein lactylation and alters its function and stability [29]. Furthermore, as a tumor suppressor, SLC26A3 downregulation disrupts intestinal epithelial ion transport and pH homeostasis, and promotes tumor proliferation and metastasis through the SLC26A3/NHERF2-IκB/NFκB/p65 feedback loop [30]. Tumor cells coordinately activate the pro-survival AKT1 pathway while suppressing SLC26A3 as a consistent malignant transformation strategy. This regulatory network provides a theoretical basis for therapeutic strategies targeting the PI3K/AKT pathway or restoring SLC26A3 function.

The function of SLC26A3 may be involved in maintaining the integrity of the intestinal epithelial barrier, which is essential for preventing inflammation and tumor development. In patients with ulcerative colitis (UC) and colorectal cancer, the expression of DRA (a protein encoded by the SLC26A3 gene) is reduced, which may be associated with a decrease in mucosal HCO3- secretion [31]. The absence of DRA has been confirmed to be associated with damage to the mucus layer, which may make animals more susceptible to colitis [32]. In addition, SLC26A3 plays a role in physiological processes such as maintenance of intestinal fluid absorption and cellular acid-base balance. In colorectal cancer, the expression and function of SLC26A3 may be affected by a variety of factors, including the activation status of the PI3K/AKT signaling pathway [26].

There are fewer studies on SLC26A3 and oxaliplatin resistance. However, SLC26A3 acts as a key factor in maintaining the integrity of the intestinal epithelial barrier and the tumor microenvironment, which may be related to oxaliplatin resistance. According to recent studies, oxaliplatin-resistant colorectal cancer cells may develop resistance by activating drug efflux pumps such as ABCG2 to reduce intracellular drug concentrations [33]. In addition, oxaliplatin-resistant colorectal cancer cells may enhance sensitivity to TRAIL by increasing death receptor 4 (DR4) expression [34]. This evidence suggests that by modulating the molecules and pathways associated with resistance, it could help overcome or reverse oxaliplatin resistance. Besides, previous studies have shown that SLC26A3 expression in colorectal cancer cells may correlate with tumor invasiveness and metastatic potential. Loss of SLC26A3 correlates with poor prognosis in colorectal cancer, and its reduced expression in colorectal cancer cells may be associated with enhanced proliferation and metastatic ability of tumors. For example, it was demonstrated that the positive expression rate of SLC26A3 in colorectal cancer tissues was significantly lower than that in paracancerous tissues, and that low expression of SLC26A3 was closely associated with choroidal infiltration and Ki-67 index. This suggests that SLC26A3 may play an inhibitory role in the proliferation and metastasis of colorectal cancer. Interestingly, our results also confirmed that knockdown of SLC26A3 significantly improved the proliferate of oxaliplatin treated colorectal cancer cells. In addition, our results indicate that SLC26A3 knockdown in SW620 cells not only promotes tumor growth but also confers resistance to oxaliplatin, as evidenced by the substantial increase in tumor volume and weight in the si-SLC26A3 group compared to controls under identical drug treatment conditions.

Our findings have important translational implications for metastatic CRC. First, SLC26A3 expression levels may serve as a predictive biomarker for oxaliplatin sensitivity in mCRC, enabling patient stratification and personalized treatment selection. Patients with low SLC26A3 expression, particularly those with metastatic disease, might benefit from alternative therapeutic strategies or combination regimens targeting SLC26A3-related pathways. Second, restoration of SLC26A3 expression or pharmacological modulation of its interacting partners (such as HuR/CUGBP1 or NF-κB) could represent promising approaches to overcome chemoresistance in the metastatic setting. Preclinical studies have shown that normalization of the acidic tumor microenvironment using sodium bicarbonate or MCT1 inhibitors can upregulate SLC26A3 expression and enhance oxaliplatin efficacy, providing a rationale for combining microenvironment-modulating agents with standard chemotherapy in mCRC. However, there are some limitations in this study. The role of this gene in colon cancer cells has only been confirmed in the SW620 cell line and remains to be further validated in additional cell lines. Moreover, the mechanism of drug resistance in colon cancer is complex and related to multiple genes and pathways. The related pathway of SLC26A3 will be the focus of our research in the future. Furthermore, more regulatory mechanisms (transcriptional for example) in perturb seq data in the context of different cancer models will be researched.

In conclusion, SLC26A3 is a critical gene for oxaliplatin sensitivity in colorectal cancer. Up-regulated of SLC26A3 in drug-resistant patients is expected to provide more hope for colon cancer patients.
