Combined indwelling pleural catheter with thoracoscopic talc poudrage for malignant pleural effusion management: a randomized non-inferiority trial
Authors: Jitanong Sootlek, Nophol Leelayuwatanakul, Virissorn Wongsrichanalai, Thitiwat Sriprasart, Vorawut Thanthitaweewat
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundMalignant pleural effusion (MPE) causes debilitating dyspnea, severely impacting quality of life (QoL) among patients with advanced stage cancer. While talc pleurodesis effectively prevents recurrence, it requires lengthy hospital stays. Indwelling pleural catheters (IPC) offer outpatient management but are associated with slightly lower pleurodesis success rates and prolonged catheter presence. Given hospital capacity constraints, we evaluated the efficacy of combined thoracoscopic talc poudrage (TTP) and IPC versus TTP alone for symptomatic MPE.MethodsThis randomized, non-inferiority-controlled trial, conducted at King Chulalongkorn Memorial Hospital, randomly assigned 38 patients to receive either combined TTP + IPC or TTP alone. The primary outcome was 12-week pleurodesis success rate with a pre-specified non-inferiority margin of − 20%. Secondary outcomes included hospital length of stay (LOS), symptoms control, and QoL. The trial was registered with the Thai Clinical Trials Registry under the registration number TCTR20230312001 (retrospectively).ResultsThirty-eight patients (19 per group) completed the study. Baseline characteristics were comparable, except for significantly worse dyspnea, chest tightness, and QoL scores in the TTP + IPC group. Successful 12-week pleurodesis was 89.5% for TTP + IPC versus 68.4% for TTP alone (difference 21.1%; 95% CI, − 4.0% to 46.1%), establishing non-inferiority as the lower bound exceeded the − 20% margin. The TTP + IPC group demonstrated a significantly greater reduction in dyspnea scores (− 6.39 vs. − 3.67, p < 0.001) and shorter median hospital LOS (1 vs. 4 days, p < 0.001). After adjusting for baseline imbalances (ANCOVA), dyspnea improvement at 6 weeks and EQ-5D-5 L utility scores at 12 weeks remained significant, whereas the VAS QoL difference was attenuated. Complication rates did not differ significantly between groups.ConclusionTTP + IPC demonstrated non-inferiority to TTP alone regarding 12-week pleurodesis success, thereby preserving pleurodesis efficacy. Furthermore, the combined approach was associated with shorter hospitalizations and improvements in symptom burden; baseline-adjusted analyses confirmed robustness of some but not all patient-reported benefits.Trial RegistrationTCTR20230312001.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at https://doi.org/10.1186/s12890-026-04463-0.Keywords: Malignant Pleural Effusion, Thoracoscopic talc poudrage, Indwelling pleural catheter, Pleurodesis, Quality of life, Dyspnea

Introduction

Malignant pleural effusion (MPE) is a common complication of advanced cancer, though it can also be the initial presentation of malignant pleural mesothelioma, affecting an estimated 5 million people worldwide annually [1]. Most patients experience significant breathlessness, which impairs their quality of life (QoL) and performance status [2–4]. As MPE signifies incurable disease with a median survival of 3 to 12 months, management aims to relieve symptoms and prevent fluid recurrence, thereby reducing hospitalizations [3, 5, 6].

Talc pleurodesis and indwelling pleural catheter (IPC) drainage are two established approaches for symptomatic MPE. Talc pleurodesis has a success rate of 70–90% [7, 8] but requires a median hospital stay of 4–6 days [7, 9, 10]. Conversely, IPCs can be inserted in an outpatient setting, improve dyspnea in up to 96% of patients [9, 11, 12], but are associated with lower spontaneous pleurodesis rates (24–50%) [9, 13, 14], a 5-12.6% infection risk [9, 15], and the burden of home drainage [16].

Increasing MPE patient volume and limited hospital bed capacity, exacerbated by the COVID-19 pandemic, challenged our institution’s ability to provide timely talc pleurodesis. This led us to investigate a pragmatic combination of thoracoscopic talc poudrage (TTP) with IPC insertion in a single procedure, aiming to harness the benefits of both techniques while mitigating their drawbacks.

Previous non-randomized studies on this combined approach showed promising results but lacked randomized control comparisons [17–20]. Although the recent TACTIC trial [21] evaluated this combination, its primary focus was on hospital stay and short-term breathlessness within a UK cohort largely comprising mesothelioma patients. Building on these existing insights, the present study aims to complement the current body of literature through three specific incremental contributions: (1) evaluating 12-week pleurodesis success as the primary outcome to further explore whether the combined approach preserves pleurodesis efficacy compared with TTP alone; (2) investigating an intensive, twice-daily self-administered IPC drainage regimen via a one-way valve system; and (3) providing randomized data from a Southeast Asian setting with a predominantly lung and breast cancer MPE population, thereby potentially enhancing the generalizability of evidence for this strategy.

Methods

Study design and oversight

This was a single-center, randomized, open-label, non-inferiority trial conducted at King Chulalongkorn Memorial Hospital, a university-affiliated hospital, Bangkok, Thailand, from February 2022 to May 2023. This study was conducted in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki. The protocol was approved by the Institutional Review Board of the Faculty of Medicine, Chulalongkorn University (IRB No. 971/64), and was registered with the Thai Clinical Trial Registry (TCTR20230312001). Informed consent to participate was obtained from all participants involved in the study. Financial support for this study was provided by the Ratchadapisek Research Funds from the Faculty of Medicine, Chulalongkorn University, Bangkok, Thailand.

Participants

Eligible patients were adults (≥ 18 years) with symptomatic MPE requiring definitive intervention. The diagnosis of MPE was confirmed either by positive fluid cytology or pleural biopsy. In the absence of a definitive diagnosis, MPE was presumed in patients with a known primary malignancy who presented with an exudative pleural effusion responsive to drainage. All participants had an Eastern Cooperative Oncology Group (ECOG) performance status of 0–3 and an estimated survival of over 3 months. Exclusion criteria included unexpandable lung (pleural elastance > 14.5 cmH₂O/L or radiological evidence), significant pleural loculation, contraindications to thoracoscopy, or pregnancy.

Randomization

Patients were randomized 1:1 to either the TTP + IPC or TTP alone group. The randomization sequence was computer-generated using permuted blocks of four, with stratification by malignancy type (lung vs. non-lung) and age (> 60 vs. ≤60 years). Allocation concealment was ensured by securing the sequence in sequentially numbered, sealed, opaque envelopes, which were opened by an independent coordinator only after baseline assessments were completed and eligibility was confirmed. Due to the nature of the interventions, blinding of patients, treating clinicians, and outcome assessors for subjective endpoints was not feasible, and the study was conducted as an open-label trial. To mitigate performance and detection biases, standardized post-procedure protocols and identical follow-up schedules were enforced across both arms. Crucially, the radiographic component of the primary outcome (chest radiograph assessment for pleurodesis success) was evaluated by independent reviewers who were strictly blinded to the treatment allocation.

Procedures

The procedures were performed within 72 h of randomization. All patients were typically admitted at least one day for pre-procedure evaluation. Ultrasound-guided medical thoracoscopy was performed by one of four interventional pulmonologists. The procedure was conducted via a single 10 mm port under local anesthesia with moderate sedation (intravenous midazolam and fentanyl). After fluid drainage and any necessary adhesiolysis via medical thoracoscopy, 4–8 g of sterile, graded talc (Steritalc®; Novatech) was insufflated to ensure complete pleural coverage under direct visualization.

TTP Group: A 20-French chest tube was inserted through the thoracoscopic port, then was connected to low-pressure suction (≤ -20 cmH₂O), and removed when drainage was ≤ 150 mL/day for two consecutive days with lung expansion confirmed by chest radiograph. Patients were discharged after tube removal.

TTP Group: A 20-French chest tube was inserted through the thoracoscopic port, then was connected to low-pressure suction (≤ -20 cmH₂O), and removed when drainage was ≤ 150 mL/day for two consecutive days with lung expansion confirmed by chest radiograph. Patients were discharged after tube removal.

TTP + IPC Group: A 16-French IPC (Rocket® IPC-PCTM, Rocket Medical, Hingham, MA, USA) was tunneled subcutaneously and inserted through the thoracoscope point of entry, initially connected to low-pressure suction (≤ -20 cmH₂O). The day after the procedure, once a chest radiograph confirmed resolution of the pneumothorax, suction was discontinued. The IPC was then connected to a one-way valve drainage bag (Rocket® Ambulatory Chest Drain Bag), allowing for repeated, self-administered drainage. Patients were trained in self-management, which included performing twice-daily effusion drainage and managing overall IPC care.They were then discharged if clinically stable. The IPC was removed in the outpatient clinic under local lidocaine anesthesia once pleurodesis was achieved. Pleurodesis success was defined as drainage of ≤ 50 mL/day for two consecutive days and confirmed via both chest X-ray and thoracic ultrasound.

Outcomes and follow-up

Patients were followed for 12 weeks or until death, with visits at 1–2, 6, and 12 weeks. Patients with a still-indwelling IPC were seen every two weeks. At these visits, the catheter was evaluated for removal upon radiographic confirmation of pleurodesis.

The primary outcome was pleurodesis success at 12 weeks, defined as the composite of: (1) patient-reported absence of pleural effusion-related dyspnea, (2) no significant pleural fluid re-accumulation on chest radiograph (≤ 50% of baseline by blinded reviewers), and (3) no requirement for further pleural interventions on the ipsilateral side. Failure was defined as not meeting these criteria or declining intervention [22].

Secondary outcomes included: post-procedure length of hospital stay (LOS); pleurodesis success at 6 weeks; patient-reported outcomes including dyspnea, chest tightness, and pain via a Visual Analog Scale (VAS; 0–10); and health-related quality of life at 12 weeks using the EuroQoL 5-Dimension 5-Level (EQ-5D-5 L) utility score and a QoL VAS (0-100). Adverse events were also recorded.

Statistical analysis

To establish non-inferiority of TTP + IPC versus TTP for 12-week pleurodesis success, we calculated that 38 patients (19 per group) would be needed. This sample size was determined based on assumed success rates of 92% for TTP + IPC and 82% for TTP [19], a non-inferiority margin of -20%, 80% power, a two-sided α of 0.05, and an anticipated 20% dropout rate.

The non-inferiority margin of − 20% was determined via a clinical trade-off framework. Given baseline success rates for standard TTP (70%–90%) [7, 8] and reported success for the combined approach (~ 92%) [17–20], this margin represents the maximum acceptable efficacy deficit. This threshold ensures that, even at the lower bound of non-inferiority, the intervention maintains a therapeutic effect that outweighs the burden of standard inpatient care, particularly by enabling reduced hospitalization and greater ambulatory autonomy.

The primary outcome was analyzed in both intention-to-treat (ITT) and per-protocol (PP) analysis. In the conservative worst-case ITT analysis, the deceased patient in the TTP + IPC group was counted as a pleurodesis failure, and the deceased patient in the TTP group was counted as a pleurodesis success. For the per-protocol analysis, both deceased patients were excluded. Secondary outcomes were analyzed using available data (complete case analysis). Continuous variables were compared using independent t-tests or Mann-Whitney U tests, and categorical variables with Chi-square or Fisher’s exact tests. A p-value < 0.05 was considered statistically significant for secondary outcomes. Analyses were performed with SPSS version 29. To account for baseline imbalances, we performed ANCOVA for all secondary symptom and quality-of-life endpoints, adjusting for baseline values. Detailed results are reported in Supplementary Table S1.

Results

Patient population

Between February 2022 to May 2023, 54 patients were screened; 16 were excluded (Fig. 1). Thirty-eight eligible patients were randomized 1:1 (19 to TTP + IPC, 19 to TTP). Two patients (one in each group) died from cancer progression before the 6-week follow-up.

The study flow

The mean age was 59.2 years, 71.1% were female, and the most common malignancies were lung (63.2%) and breast (21.1%) cancer. Baseline characteristics were largely similar between groups (Table 1). However, the TTP + IPC group had significantly worse baseline VAS scores for dyspnea (8.11 ± 1.37 vs. 6.58 ± 1.77; p = 0.005), chest tightness (7.26 ± 2.10 vs. 5.53 ± 2.12; p = 0.015), and EQ-5D-5 L QoL VAS (40.53 ± 8.48 vs. 47.89 ± 10.18; p = 0.021). The procedure time was not different (53.74 ± 8.72 vs. 51.16 ± 9.28, p = 0.383). Pleural fluid analysis was similar between the groups, except for pH which was significant lower in the TTP group compared to the TTP + IPC group (7.42 ± 0.07 vs. 7.48 ± 0.06, p = 0.03).

Primary outcome: pleurodesis success at 12 weeks

In the ITT analysis, pleurodesis success was achieved in 89.5% (17/19) of patients in the TTP + IPC group versus 68.4% (13/19) in the TTP group (Fig. 2). The absolute difference in success rates was 21.1% (95% CI, -4.0% to 46.1%). As the lower bound of the 95% CI (-4.0%) was well above the pre-specified non-inferiority margin of -20%, non-inferiority of the combined approach was established.

Primary outcome: pleurodesis success rate at 12 weeks. TTP + IPC group: 17 of 19 patients (89.5%; 95% CI: 68.1–97.1%) vs. TTP group: 13 of 19 patients (68.4%; 95% CI: 45.9–84.4%). Intention-to-treat analysis, p-value = 0.111.

The per-protocol analysis showed a success rate of 94.4% (17/18) for TTP + IPC versus 66.7% (12/18) for TTP, with an absolute difference of 27.8% (95% CI, 3.6% to 52.0%).

Secondary outcomes

Pleurodesis success at 6 weeks

Analysis showed success rates of 94.7% (18/19) for TTP + IPC and 84.2% (16/19) for TTP (difference 10.5%, 95% CI -8.7% to 29.8%). PP analysis yielded consistent results, with 100% (18/18) success for TTP + IPC versus 83.3% (15/18) for TTP (difference of 16.7%, 95% CI, -0.5% to 33.9%).

Hospital length of stay

Median post-procedure hospital stay was significantly shorter for the TTP + IPC group (1 day; IQR, 1–2) compared to the TTP group (4 days; IQR, 3–5) (p < 0.001). No patients in either group required re-hospitalization for pleural-related issues during 12-week follow-up.

Dyspnea and chest tightness

The TTP + IPC group experienced a significantly greater reduction in mean VAS dyspnea from baseline at both 6 weeks (− 6.17 ± 1.54 vs. −3.78 ± 1.56; p < 0.001) and 12 weeks (− 6.39 ± 1.88 vs. −3.67 ± 2.20; p < 0.001). Similarly, the reduction in chest tightness was significantly greater in the TTP + IPC group at 6 weeks (-5.72 ± 1.81 vs. -2.94 ± 2.10; p < 0.001) and 12 weeks (-5.67 ± 2.43 vs. -3.28 ± 2.35; p = 0.005) (Fig. 3).

Pain score

Post-procedural pain scores before discharge were significantly lower in the TTP + IPC (median 2; IQR, 2-2.25) vs. TTP (median 3; IQR, 2–4) (p = 0.011). No significant difference was observed during follow-up.

Quality of Life

At 12 weeks, the TTP + IPC group reported a significantly higher EQ-5D-5 L utility score (0.916 ± 0.079 vs. 0.830 ± 0.107; p = 0.010) and demonstrated a greater improvement from baseline (0.337 ± 0.173 vs. 0.204 ± 0.203; p = 0.021). The improvement in VAS QoL score was also significantly larger in the TTP + IPC group (30.833 ± 16.912 vs. 19.444 ± 14.742; p = 0.038) (Fig. 4).

Mortality

All-cause mortality at 12 weeks was identical between groups (1 patient per group, 5.3%; p = 1.000), with no procedure-related deaths.

Median duration of IPC placement was 10 days [IQR, 8–11 days]. No IPC-related infections, subcutaneous emphysema or fluid leakage events were observed. No further complications related to the IPC were noted following device removal at 12 weeks. However, one patient in the TTP + IPC group accidentally removed her IPC, though no further pleural intervention was required. Additional details are provided in Table 2.

After adjusting for baseline values, ANCOVA demonstrated that between-group differences remained statistically significant at 6 weeks for both dyspnea (adjusted p = 0.008) and chest tightness (adjusted p = 0.006). However, by week 12, these improvements were attenuated. The between-group difference for dyspnea decreased to marginal significance (adjusted p = 0.052), while chest tightness (adjusted p = 0.273) and the EQ-VAS QoL score (adjusted p = 0.413) did not reach statistical significance. Conversely, the change in EQ-5D-5 L utility scores remained robust and statistically significant (adjusted p = 0.007). Complete cross-sectional ANCOVA results are detailed in Supplementary Table S1.

To evaluate the overall treatment trajectory across the study period, a repeated measures analysis of covariance (RM-ANCOVA) was performed. Incorporating both post-baseline timepoints, the model confirmed a significant global between-group main effect favoring the TTP + IPC group for dyspnea (F[1,33] = 6.879, p = 0.013), chest tightness (F[1,33] = 4.659, p = 0.038), and pain (F[1,33] = 5.742, p = 0.022) after adjusting for baseline covariates. Notably, no significant Time × Group interactions were observed, indicating that the therapeutic benefits were statistically consistent across the 12-week follow-up.

Discussion

Current guidelines recommend talc pleurodesis or IPC insertion as definitive management of symptomatic MPE [1]. We investigated whether combining both modalities in a single procedure could preserve pleurodesis efficacy while offering the ambulatory advantages of an IPC.

In this randomized trial, a combined strategy of TTP with IPC insertion appeared to be non-inferior to TTP alone for achieving pleurodesis at 12 weeks in patients with MPE. Furthermore, this combined approach was associated with favorable secondary outcomes, including a shorter hospital stay, greater relief from dyspnea and chest tightness, reduced post-procedural pain, and improved quality of life; these findings suggest the potential utility of this approach in the management of patients with MPE.

Our primary outcome demonstrates that combining an IPC with TTP does not compromise the efficacy of talc pleurodesis. The 12-week pleurodesis success rate was 89.5% in the TTP + IPC group and 68.4% in the TTP-alone group, successfully meeting the pre-specified threshold for non-inferiority. Although a higher success rate was numerically observed in the combined arm, this descriptive variance should be interpreted with caution given the trial’s non-inferiority design and sample size limitations; nevertheless, the direction of this effect remains clinically encouraging. The 68.4% success rate in our TTP-alone arm is lower than the historical rates reported in trials like TAPPS [23], likely reflecting our predominantly high-symptom cohort (ECOG performance status 2–3) and our stringent composite definition of pleurodesis success. Conversely, the 89.5% success rate in the TTP + IPC arm aligns consistently with previous observational studies evaluating similar combined strategies [17–20, 24].

Importantly, our success rate exceeds the 53% reported for the combined arm of the TACTIC trial [21]. This variance likely stems from fundamental differences in trial design and drainage intensity rather than a disparity in technique. While the TACTIC trial utilized a pragmatic, real-world design specifying drainage at least five times per week using a drainage bottle—reflecting standard healthcare delivery models and outpatient resource allocations—our protocol evaluated an intensive, twice-daily self-controlled regimen via a one-way valve. More intensive drainage has been associated with accelerated pleurodesis [13] and facilitates the early clearance of fluid reaccumulation. This mechanism may also explain why the success rate in our TTP-alone group declined from 6 to 12 weeks (84.2% to 68.4%), while remaining stable in the TTP + IPC group. Additionally, the TACTIC trial enrolled a substantially higher proportion of patients with pleural mesothelioma (46%), a malignancy associated with lower pleurodesis success rates, whereas our cohort was dominated by lung (63.2%) and breast (21.1%) cancer. Our protocol also differed from some earlier combined-procedure studies [17, 20] by not using a supplementary large-bore chest tube alongside the IPC — an approach similar to Lovato et al. [24], who reported comparable success rates (85% at two months) with a median IPC indwelling time of 25 days. Our notably shorter median indwelling time (10 days), compared to 25 days in Lovato et al. and 42 days in TACTIC [21], likely reflects the more intensive twice-daily drainage regimen. The absence of IPC blockage from talc particles in our cohort confirms that the IPC alone is sufficient for post-poudrage drainage.

The procedure time did not increase in the TTP + IPC group (53.74 ± 8.72 vs. 51.16 ± 9.28 min; p = 0.383), suggesting that the combined technique is technically straightforward. No IPC-related infections, subcutaneous emphysema, or fluid leakage events were observed — even with a port larger than the standard IPC dilator — underscoring the procedural integrity of this approach. These safety findings are concordant with the TACTIC trial, which reported comparable adverse event rates between the combined and standard arms [21].

The reduction in median post-procedure LOS from 4 days (TTP alone) to 1 day (TTP + IPC) directly addresses a major challenge for healthcare systems managing growing volumes of MPE patients. These findings align with the TACTIC trial [21], which similarly demonstrated a significant 2-day reduction in LOS within its per-protocol analysis. Our results independently corroborate this directional benefit, strengthening the cumulative evidence that the combined procedure meaningfully minimizes hospitalization.

Although a formal cost-effectiveness analysis was not performed, several cost and resource considerations merit discussion. The TTP + IPC approach incurs additional direct costs, including the IPC insertion kit, one-way valve drainage bags, and an outpatient IPC removal visit; notably, the overall additional cost is approximately 12,000–15,000 THB (400–430 USD). Remarkably, this upfront expense is balanced by the 3-day reduction in hospital admission, which otherwise costs between 15,000 and 20,000 THB (430–570 USD). Nevertheless, a formal cost-effectiveness analysis within this specific healthcare context remains an important next step.

The TTP + IPC group experienced significantly greater relief from dyspnea and chest tightness despite reporting worse symptoms at baseline — an imbalance that can occur by chance in small randomized trials. The improved quality of life, measured by both EQ-5D-5 L utility score and VAS, underscores the clinical meaningfulness of this approach. While the TACTIC trial found no significant between-group differences in dyspnea or quality-of-life measures [21], both studies consistently demonstrated clinically meaningful breathlessness improvement in both treatment arms. ANCOVA adjusting for baseline values provided a more nuanced picture: dyspnea improvement at 6 weeks, chest tightness improvement at 6 weeks, and EQ-5D-5 L utility score improvement remained robust after adjustment, whereas the differences in chest tightness at 12 weeks and VAS QoL were attenuated and no longer significant. These findings suggest that the observed treatment effects on some symptom endpoints are genuine and not solely attributable to regression to the mean, while the VAS QoL finding should be interpreted with greater caution.

Unlike the AMPLE trial, which found comparable QoL between IPC and pleurodesis alone [25], our combined approach was associated with greater QoL improvement than pleurodesis alone in this cohort, suggesting that the combination may offer symptomatic advantages beyond what either modality achieves independently. Post-procedural pain scores were also lower in the TTP + IPC group, likely reflecting the smaller and more flexible caliber of the IPC compared to the 20-French large-bore chest tube — consistent with TACTIC, where higher day-1 pain scores were similarly observed in the standard-care arm [21]. The short median IPC indwelling time (10 days) positions the combined approach in a therapeutic ‘sweet spot’: achieving rapid pleurodesis without the lengthy hospitalization of TTP alone or the patient apprehension associated with long-term IPC drainage — a concern that also posed a significant recruitment challenge in our study, as similarly noted by the TACTIC investigators [21].

Nevertheless, whether routine IPC insertion at thoracoscopy is justified for all patients warrants consideration. Home IPC management represents a non-trivial burden, and a twice-daily drainage regimen may not be feasible for patients with limited dexterity, cognitive impairment, or inadequate social support. Whether less intensive regimens (e.g., once daily) can achieve similar outcomes outside our center remains an important question for future investigation. Therefore, rather than advocating routine use, the combined approach should be tailored to specific clinical scenarios: patients at high risk of pleurodesis failure (e.g., partial lung entrapment or extensive pleural disease), settings with severe hospital bed constraints, or patients with a strong preference to avoid readmission who possess the necessary support to manage home drainage.

This study has several limitations. It was a single-center, open-label trial with a small sample size (n = 38), which limits statistical power and precludes robust subgroup analyses. However, the primary outcome incorporated blinded radiographic assessment, and the consistency of benefit across multiple endpoints strengthens confidence in the findings. The baseline imbalance in symptom severity warrants cautious interpretation of the patient-reported secondary outcomes, though the direction of this imbalance likely biases toward underestimation of the treatment effect. Nevertheless, regression to the mean cannot be excluded as a contributing factor, particularly for outcomes that were attenuated after baseline-adjusted analyses. Additionally, our cohort was drawn from a single Southeast Asian tertiary center and entirely lacked mesothelioma patients, contrasting with the high-mesothelioma demographic of the TACTIC trial; as noted previously, this baseline diagnostic variance may partly explain our higher overall success rates. The observed TTP-alone success rate (68.4%) was lower than the 82% assumed in the sample size calculation [19], likely reflecting our predominantly high-symptom cohort and stringent composite success criteria. While the non-inferiority criterion was met based on the 95% confidence interval, this discrepancy may have reduced post-hoc statistical power, and the absolute between-group difference should be interpreted with appropriate caution. These findings require validation in larger, multicentre randomized trials.

Conclusion

Our findings suggest that the TTP + IPC strategy is non-inferior to TTP alone for achieving 12-week pleurodesis success, potentially maintaining clinical efficacy. Notably, this combined approach was associated with shorter hospitalizations and improvements in symptom burden; however, baseline-adjusted analyses confirmed that some symptom benefits (dyspnea and chest tightness at 6 weeks, EQ-5D-5 L utility) were robust to adjustment, while others (VAS QoL, chest tightness at 12 weeks) were attenuated.
