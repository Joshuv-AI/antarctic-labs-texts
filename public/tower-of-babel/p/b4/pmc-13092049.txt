Capturing the late complications of venous thromboembolism
Authors: Anette Arbjerg Højen, Elin Baddeley, Stine Foged Lindegaard, Stian Ingemann-Molden, Frederikus A Klok
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractA state-of-the-art lecture titled “Capturing the late complications of venous thromboembolism: the role of patient-reported outcomes” was presented at the International Society on Thrombosis and Haemostasis (ISTH) Congress 2025 in Washington, DC. Venous thromboembolism (VTE) is increasingly recognized as a chronic condition with substantial long-term complications that extend beyond recurrence, bleeding, and survival. Many patients with VTE experience persistent pain, dyspnea, fatigue, anxiety, and reduced quality of life. Frequently used terms for clinical sequalae include postthrombotic syndrome and postpulmonary embolism syndrome, the latter of which includes chronic thromboembolic pulmonary hypertension. Despite the impact of these long-term complications, they are rarely addressed systematically in routine daily practice. Patient-reported outcome measures provide a structured framework for capturing these late complications, complementing clinical outcomes and offering unique insights into the patient perspective. This review outlines why late complications should be systematically captured, describes the patient-reported outcome measures commonly used to assess them, and discusses key opportunities and challenges for their integration into clinical practice. Finally, we summarize relevant new data on this topic presented during the 2025 ISTH Congress.Keywords: long-term management, patient-centered care, patient-reported outcome measures, pulmonary embolism, venous thromboembolism

Introduction

Venous thromboembolism (VTE), including deep vein thrombosis (DVT) and pulmonary embolism (PE), is the third most common cardiovascular disease globally, with an annual incidence of 1 to 2 per 1000 persons [1,2]. Beyond the acute event, VTE leads to long-term sequalae in a substantial proportion of patients. Postthrombotic syndrome (PTS) occurs in approximately one-third of patients after DVT, and nearly half of PE survivors experience postpulmonary embolism syndrome (PPES), with 2% to 3% ultimately progressing to chronic thromboembolic pulmonary hypertension, a rare but debilitating and life-threatening complication [[1], [2], [3], [4], [5], [6]].

The long-term complications of VTE extend beyond the vascular system. Persistent dyspnea, fatigue, and leg symptoms restrict physical activity and contribute to progressive deconditioning [1,2,5,7]. Psychological health is likewise affected, with many patients experiencing anxiety, depression, hypervigilance, and loss of trust in bodily signals [[8], [9], [10]]. Social participation, work capacity, and family life are often disrupted, and many survivors report changes in self-image and identity. Together, these multidimensional sequelae demonstrate that VTE is not merely an acute thrombotic event but also a chronic condition with lasting physical, functional, and psychosocial complications.

Despite the frequency of these outcomes, they are rarely systematically assessed in routine follow-up. Clinical care has traditionally focused on VTE recurrence, bleeding, and survival, while patient-reported symptoms most relevant to daily life often go undetected and unaddressed. This leaves patients with unmet needs and clinicians without the full picture required to tailor care. In this State of the Art Review, we outline why late complications must be systematically captured, describe PROMs available to assess them, and highlight opportunities and challenges for their integration into clinical practice.

From Symptoms to Strategy—Why we Must Capture Late VTE Complications

Late complications of VTE are common but frequently underrecognized. In most settings, patients do not receive structured follow-up after VTE, and when provided, the focus is often on anticoagulant management and cardiopulmonary complications. While these are essential aspects of VTE follow-up, they do not adequately capture the broader impact of VTE on patients’ daily lives [11]. Consequently, patients report unmet needs, dissatisfaction with follow-up, and lack of psychological support [8,[12], [13], [14], [15]].

Diagnosis of late VTE complications can be challenging. PTS and PPES lack standardized diagnostic criteria, and their symptoms overlap with other conditions [13,16,17]. As a result, diagnosis is often delayed, as seen in chronic thromboembolic pulmonary hypertension, which has an average delay of up to 14 months [[16], [17], [18]]. At the same time, patients report feeling unprepared and insufficiently informed about which late complications to expect or how to respond to new symptoms [15,19]. Thus, unless clinicians actively inquire and become attentive to these concerns, they often remain invisible, leading to underdiagnosis, missed opportunities for intervention, and avoidable deterioration in physical, psychological, and social well-being [16,[20], [21], [22]]. To prevent this, symptoms must be captured in a structured way and translated into actionable strategies (Figure 1).This requires systematic approaches as improving long-term outcomes depends on consistent detection, monitoring, and appropriate treatment of late complications.

Patient-reported outcome measures (PROMs) provide such a framework, enabling clinicians to systematically capture the patient perspective and translate it into tailored interventions. PROMs are standardized instruments, most often questionnaires, which capture patients’ perceptions of health status, symptoms, functioning, and quality of life. In VTE care, they extend the focus beyond recurrence, bleeding, and survival by systematically documenting fatigue, dyspnea, anxiety, and other long-term complications that are rarely addressed in routine follow-up. PROMs do not replace clinical assessments but complement them, offering a more comprehensive picture of recovery and ensuring that care remains aligned with what matters most to patients. Increasingly, they are recognized as essential tools for capturing the patient perspective in VTE, providing insights into how survivors experience illness and recovery, and serving as a valuable complement to traditional clinical outcomes [23,24]. In clinical follow-up, PROM data may support targeted responses, for example, by prompting referral to structured exercise or rehabilitation or referral to counseling for those with psychological distress. PROMs may also enhance clinician awareness and support more focused communication and shared decision making during follow-up [25].

PROMs Used in VTE

A wide range of PROMs have been used in VTE research to quantify changes in health status after diagnosis, characterize the trajectory of recovery, identify drivers of persistent impairment such as PTS and PPES, and evaluate the impact of novel treatment modalities on quality of life [22,23]. However, the large number of PROMs available can make selection challenging, particularly since only a limited number have undergone formal validation in a VTE setting. To provide guidance, the International Consortium for Health Outcome Measurement (ICHOM) has proposed a core set of outcome measures to cover important aspects affecting patients after a VTE event [24]. Efficient use of PROMs requires that administration is aligned with the presentation of late complications and that instruments are sufficiently responsive to track changes over time and inform treatment decisions. Generic PROMs

Several generic PROMs have been applied in VTE populations [22,23]. The most commonly used are the European Quality of Life 5 dimensions 5-levels (EQ-5D-5L) and the Short Form (SF)-36 questionnaires, both of which evaluate health status [22]. In addition, the ICHOM group has recommended the Patient Health Questionnaire 9, Generalized Anxiety Disorder 7, and selected instruments from the Patient-Reported Outcomes Measurement Information System (PROMIS), including the short form global health, pain intensity, and dyspnea severity scales [24].

Validation evidence supports both the EQ-5D-5L and the SF-36 as reliable measures of health-related quality of life in VTE populations. The EQ-5D-5L can detect clinically meaningful improvements or deterioration over time, and its scores are comparable with those captured by disease-specific instruments [26]. The SF-36 has shown moderate correlations with VTE-specific PROMs, supporting its validity for assessing overall health-related quality of life [[27], [28], [29], [30], [31]]. However, both instruments are less sensitive to subtle or VTE-specific changes, such as fluctuating dyspnea, fatigue, or fear of recurrence, which means they may underestimate the true burden of disease compared with disease-specific VTE tools.

The Patient Health Questionnaire 9 and Generalized Anxiety Disorder 7 are widely used and well-validated screening tools for depression and anxiety in many chronic disease populations [32,33]. They are straightforward to administer, provide benchmark scores that can be compared with general population norms, and are useful for detecting the presence and severity of mood symptoms. However, in VTE populations, their ability to track changes over time has not been well studied [[34], [35], [36]]. This potentially limits their value for monitoring recovery or assessing treatment response. In clinical practice, they are best interpreted as indicators of symptom burden that may warrant further evaluation, rather than as diagnostic instruments or longitudinal outcome measures.

The PROMIS instruments align closely with domains that VTE patients identify as important, which supports their relevance in this setting [22]. The PROMIS Global Health Short Form is brief and easy to administer, giving a rapid overview of physical, emotional, and functional well-being [37]. However, its broad scope means it cannot provide detailed information about specific functional limitations. The PROMIS Dyspnea Severity Scale has shown validity in chronic obstructive pulmonary disease [38] but limited responsiveness in that population [39], and VTE-specific studies are needed to determine whether it can detect meaningful changes after an acute event. The PROMIS pain intensity scale captures average and worst pain over the past week [40,41]; yet, in VTE, patients often emphasize the interference of pain with daily activities rather than intensity alone, suggesting this tool may need to be supplemented by disease-specific instruments [42,43]. Overall, PROMIS measures are relevant and feasible, but further validation, including establishment of minimal clinically important differences (MCIDs), is essential before they can be used reliably to monitor recovery in VTE. Taken together, generic PROMs are valuable for broad assessment and crosscondition comparison, but limited responsiveness and insufficient validation in VTE reduce their clinical utility as stand-alone tools.

Disease-specific PROMs

Multiple disease-specific PROMs have been developed for VTE [22,23]. Those recommended by ICHOM include the Pulmonary Embolism Quality of Life (PEmb-QoL) questionnaire, the Venous Insufficiency Epidemiological and Economic Study Quality of Life (VEINES-QOL) questionnaire, the Post-VTE Functional Status (PVFS) scale, and the Anti-Clot Treatment Scale (ACTS).

The PEmb-QoL is the most widely used PE-specific instrument [22]. It has been validated across multiple languages and can reliably measure changes over time [27,29,30,[44], [45], [46], [47]]. It is sensitive to changes in overall health status, including symptom burden, daily functioning, and emotional well-being, and an established MCID of 15 points allows interpretation of clinically meaningful improvement or deterioration [30,48,49]. The instrument correlates well with generic measures such as the EQ-5D-5L and SF-36, supporting its ability to measure quality of life as a construct. However, its ability to discriminate between subgroups at baseline has been inconsistent, likely reflecting heterogeneity in study populations rather than a limitation of the tool itself.

The VEINES-QOL, originally developed for chronic venous disorders of the leg, has subsequently been applied and validated in patients with DVT across several languages and shows stable test–retest properties [47,[50], [51], [52]]. It has proven sensitive to both mild and severe symptoms, with scores worsening as disease severity increases. Importantly, it is responsive to changes over time, making it useful for detecting clinical improvement or progression to PTS. A lack of change in VEINES-QOL scores may serve as an early signal of patients at risk of developing PTS [53].

The PVFS is a single-item scale designed to capture overall functional limitation after VTE. It can reliably measure changes over short- to mid-term follow-up (3-16 weeks) and correlates with other health-related quality of life measures, supporting its ability to measure functional limitations [[54], [55], [56]]. The PVFS is responsive to changes in functional status during recovery, making it suitable for tracking improvement or identifying patients with persistent limitations. Its simplicity and ease of use support feasibility in routine clinical care, although the broad scope of a single-item measure means that more detailed aspects of physical and psychosocial functioning may be missed.

The ACTS has been validated in multiple languages and is a reliable tool to assess changes across follow-up intervals ranging from 15 days to 12 months [57]. It captures both the perceived benefit and burden of anticoagulation, including the impact of bleeding on daily activities. These properties make it a practical tool for evaluating treatment experience over time. However, the ACTS was developed in the vitamin K antagonist era, and some items (eg, dietary interactions) may be less relevant for patients treated with direct oral anticoagulants, highlighting a potential need for adaptation. Therefore, ACTS is best used in combination with other instruments [58].

Do Existing PROMs Capture What Matters Most to Patients With VTE?

While PROMs are a key tool for capturing late complications of VTE, it is equally important to consider how well they reflect what matters most to patients. Instruments recommended by ICHOM cover many of the domains patients identify as important, including physical symptoms, functional limitations, and emotional well-being [22]. However, not all aspects of the patient experience are fully represented. A recent mixed-methods review found that existing PROMs used in patients with PE did not capture outcomes such as hypervigilance about bodily signals, worrisome thoughts, and illness-related uncertainty (Figure 2) [22]. These outcomes are frequently reported by patients with VTE and are often linked to insufficient information or guidance on how to interpret new or ongoing symptoms [8,19,59]. Gaps in information provision are widely reported, with patients describing lack of emotional support and inadequate information about long-term symptoms, recovery, and treatment [8,12,14,15,[58], [59], [60], [61]]. Importantly, these challenges often originate at the time of diagnosis and can have lasting negative consequences well beyond the acute event.

These gaps highlight that, although PROMs provide a solid foundation for assessing recovery, additional strategies are needed to capture the full range of patient concerns and to ensure follow-up care addresses both physical and psychosocial needs [62,63]. Common recommendations based on patients’ experiences include improved information provision, greater access to psychological support, and structured follow-up care [11,12,21,58,64]. The combination of timely, informative diagnosis and systematic follow-up, complemented by the use of PROMs, may go a long way toward capturing and addressing all patient concerns [65].

Challenges of Implementing PROMs in Clinical Practice

While PROMs have clear potential to improve VTE follow-up care, their implementation into routine clinical practice faces several challenges. A central limitation lies in the origin and design of most instruments. Many PROMs used in VTE populations were developed for research purposes, particularly clinical trials, rather than day-to-day clinical use [66,67]. As a result, they often lack contextual validation, clinical interpretability, and clear cutoff values for guiding decisions. Even when psychometrically robust, PROMs may not be feasible or meaningful in a busy clinical setting [66]. Without standardized thresholds, clinicians report uncertainty about how to translate PROM scores into actionable strategies, which risks reducing their use to passive data collection rather than a tool for shaping care [23,[66], [67], [68], [69], [70], [71]].

Integration into clinical workflows presents another challenge. PROMs are most useful when embedded into existing electronic health record (EHR) systems, where they can be automatically scored, tracked over time, and made available in real time during consultations [23,25,69,72]. However, such integration requires investment and careful design to ensure that data are displayed in an interpretable, clinically relevant way [23]. Two recent initiatives illustrate both the potential and the challenges of implementing PROMs in routine VTE care. In Denmark, the Attend-PE model (A structured integrated post Pulmonary Embolism model) integrated PROMs into the EHR and generated automatic summaries before follow-up visits [73,74]. Clinicians reported greater time efficiency, more patient-centered consultations, and the ability to uncover otherwise hidden concerns such as psychosocial distress in the feasibility study of the model [23,[69], [70], [71]]. Similarly, a mixed-method study from the Netherlands found that both patients and professionals viewed PROMs positively for structuring consultations and preparing follow-up [62]. Not only both initiatives demonstrate that PROMs can enhance follow-up care, but they also highlight the need for further evaluation and refinement. Clinical expertise and culture also play a decisive role. Interpreting PROM data, particularly for complex symptoms such as fatigue, dyspnea, or psychological distress, requires adequate training and support. Without this, PROMs risk being perceived as an administrative burden, rather than a tool to improve care [23,71]. In parallel, existing PROMs may benefit from simplification by reducing overlapping or low-value items and focusing on the most clinically meaningful content. Such streamlining may improve feasibility in routine care and support more consistent and efficient use of PROM data in clinical practice.

International Society on Thrombosis and Haemostasis 2025 Congress Report

At the International Society on Thrombosis and Haemostasis (ISTH) 2025 Congress in Washington, DC, several presentations highlighted advances in or understanding of the late complications of VTE and related outcomes. A Delphi consensus study on post-PE follow-up recommended PROMs as central to capturing long-term sequelae, while a qualitative study of patients described persistent uncertainty, frustration with gaps in follow-up, and the value of shared decision making and specialized services, the full findings of which have recently been fully published [75]. Outcome standardization was also discussed in a Scientific and Standardization Committee session, emphasizing that PROMs should be standardized and anchored to a patient-meaningful change with precise estimates of the minimal important difference to ensure clinical relevance.

Several studies expanded understanding of late complications. Registry data from RIETE demonstrated that geographic and health care disparities, rather than race alone, drive differences in recurrence and mortality [76]. A nationwide Dutch cohort found pulmonary hypertension after PE to be more common than previously recognized, concentrated within the first year, and strongly associated with poor survival [77]. Two studies on PTS reported complementary findings: a meta-analysis estimated ∼5 million prevalent cases in Europe, while long-term CELEST trial follow-up confirmed a persistent risk of burdensome PTS, mitigated by compliance with compression therapy [78,79]. Together, these presentations highlight the value of PROMs and provide important new data on the burden of long-term VTE complications.

Conclusion and Future Directions

The integration of PROMs into VTE care is still at an early stage, but several priorities are clear. First, existing instruments require further validation across diverse VTE populations and clinical contexts. Key properties such as responsiveness, MCIDs, and contextual relevance must be established to move PROMs from research into everyday decision making.

Second, reducing burden is essential. Many current instruments are lengthy and overlapping, contributing to low completion rates. Streamlined, harmonized tools ideally codesigned with patients are needed to ensure feasibility and to capture outcomes that matter most to patients.

Third, digital integration will be critical for success. Embedding PROMs within EHR systems—with automated scoring, longitudinal tracking, and intuitive dashboards—can make results immediately actionable. Finally, large-scale prospective studies are required to demonstrate whether PROM-informed care improves outcomes for VTE survivors, not only in terms of symptom control but also regarding physical, psychological, and functional well-being.

In summary, late complications of VTE are common, multifaceted, and often underrecognized in routine care. PROMs provide a structured means of making these complications visible, in a timely manner, while complementing traditional clinical outcomes and supporting a more holistic, patient-centered approach. Realizing their full potential will depend on rigorous validation, careful integration into clinical workflows and alignment with both patient priorities and clinical decision making. Addressing these impacts remains challenging, as gaps and limitations persist in available strategies for managing long-term sequelae, including structured rehabilitation and psychological support. At the same time, PROMs can support clinical practice by helping clinicians identify patients with persistent functional limitation, dyspnea, or psychological distress; structure follow-up consultations; and initiate referral to relevant services where available.
