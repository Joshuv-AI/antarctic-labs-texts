Durable Responses and Cystectomy Avoidance With Interleukin-15 Receptor Agonist NAI Plus Bacillus Calmette-Guérin in Bacillus Calmette-Guérin–Unresponsive Nonmuscle-Invasive Bladder Cancer With Carcinoma In Situ ± Papillary Disease

Bladder cancer is the 10th most diagnosed cancer, and approximately 75% of new diagnoses are nonmuscle-invasive bladder cancer (NMIBC).1 First-line treatment for intermediate-risk or high-risk NMIBC is transurethral resection of the bladder tumor (TURBT) with subsequent intravesical instillation of bacillus Calmette-Guérin (BCG).2 For patients with bladder carcinoma in situ (CIS) disease, a subtype of NMIBC, BCG is effective in approximately 70% to 80% of cases.3-5 Nevertheless, BCG is ineffective in some cases (refractory), and in others, NMIBC will recur after an initial response (relapsed).6,7 While cystectomy is the definitive treatment for BCG-unresponsive NMIBC, alternative treatments are needed for those patients who are unwilling or unable to undergo cystectomy.8,9 The ongoing global shortage of intravesical BCG has accelerated the development of alternative therapies.

The approved combination of NAI plus BCG thus provides a highly tolerable option for therapy in patients with BCG-unresponsive NMIBC CIS with a high likelihood of durable efficacy that is associated with bladder preservation, but without increased risk for poor outcomes or fatalities associated with other approved treatments including chemotherapy.

Currently, US Food and Drug Administration (FDA)–approved therapies for BCG-unresponsive NMIBC include anti-PD1 immunotherapy pembrolizumab, interferon alpha-2b–based therapy nadofaragene firadenovec, and gemcitabine intravesical system TAR-200, with reported response rates of 41%, 51%, and 82%, respectively.10-12 Less than ∼50% of responders to these therapies maintain responses at 12 months. The latest approved agent of gemcitabine intravesical system has reported low lymphocytes in 24% of recipients, which could have a negative impact on duration of response and a 1.2% fatality rate.12 Valrubicin is also approved for this indication but is not commonly used because of a relatively low response rate.

In 2024, the FDA approved the use of intravesical nogapendekin alfa inbakicept-pmln (NAI; Anktiva; also known as N-803) in combination with BCG for BCG-unresponsive NMIBC CIS ± Ta/T1 disease. This approval was based, in part, on interim findings reported by Chamie et al (2022) from the phase 2/3 study QUILT-3.032 of the combination in that indication, which elicited a response rate of 71% and median duration of response of 26.6 months.13-15

NAI is an interleukin-15 (IL-15) receptor super agonist fusion protein of a human N72D IL-15 variant bound to a dimeric human IL-15Rα sushi domain and human IgG1 Fc. NAI trans-presents IL-15 through the IL-15Rα sushi domain to the IL-2/IL-15 common βγc receptor on NK and CD8+ T cells. Results in the proliferation and activation of those lymphocytes without proliferation of immuno-suppressive regulatory T cells.16 NAI increases release of interferon-γ and has the ability to restore MHC I expression.17,18

Both preclinical and early clinical studies supported the testing of NAI in combination with BCG in BCG-unresponsive NMIBC and in BCG-naïve NMIBC.19-23 It is hypothesized that NAI, acting as a lymphocyte-stimulating agent, synergizes with BCG by reactivating the innate immune responses that are necessary to elicit BCG efficacy and that may otherwise be inadequate in patients with BCG-unresponsive disease.24-27

The ongoing trial QUILT-3.032 is fully enrolled, and here we report updated findings including 36-month follow-up of duration of responses, cystectomy, and disease-specific survival for cohort A participants with BCG-unresponsive NMIBC CIS ±Ta/T1 disease.

MATERIALS AND METHODS

QUILT-3.032 (NCT03022825) is a registrational, open-label, single-arm multicenter trial (32 clinical trial sites) of intravesical NAI (Anktiva; ImmunityBio, Inc) and BCG in participants with BCG-unresponsive NMIBC. The study protocol is provided in a prior report.13

Participants

Cohort A (N = 100) participants had histologically confirmed BCG-unresponsive NMIBC CIS ± papillary (Ta and/or T1) disease. Those with high-grade Ta and/or T1 disease had a TURBT (resectable papillary disease had to be absent; CIS disease was not subject to maximal resection or fulguration) before study treatment. Inclusion criteria included age 18 years or older, male or female, ECOG (Eastern Cooperative Oncology Group) status of 0 to 2, and ability to provide informed consent. Exclusion criteria included inadequate organ function, severe cardiac dysfunction, history or evidence of muscle-invasive, locally advanced, metastatic and/or extravesical bladder (or other) cancer, within the past 5 years, and/or life expectancy of less than 2 years. Definitions and additional details can be found in the Supplementary Methods (https://www.jurology.com).

Also included were a cohort B (N = 80) of participants with BCG-unresponsive papillary-only NMIBC receiving NAI in combination with BCG and a cohort C (N = 10) of participants with BCG-unresponsive NMIBC CIS ± papillary disease receiving NAI alone. The findings for cohorts B and C are reported in Chamie et al,14 and follow-up findings for the fully enrolled cohort B are presented in a separate manuscript.28

Treatment and Assessment

Cohort A participants received 400 mcg NAI in combination with 50 mg BCG (TICE) intravesically (urinary catheter to the bladder), weekly for 6 consecutive weeks during induction. The first response assessment including biopsy, cystoscopy, and urine collection (voided or collected during cystoscopy) for urine cytology occurred at month 3. Participants who achieved a complete response (CR) at month 3 went on to receive maintenance treatment which began at month 3 and continued until a participant was no longer eligible for additional therapy or through month 36, whichever is shorter. Reinduction, an additional 6 weeks of treatment, was offered to participants who did not achieve a CR at month 3. For those who underwent reinduction, a response assessment was performed at month 6 as described for the month 3 assessment. Ongoing responses were assessed every 3 months through month 24 and every 6 months from month 24 to month 60. Cystoscopy was performed at each response assessment, and urine was collected for cytology.

Cohort A is an independent study cohort and was evaluated separately from other cohorts for efficacy. The efficacy population included participants who had reached the month 3 response assessment, the month 6 assessment for those who received a second induction, or discontinued treatment before that assessment (July 15, 2024, data cutoff).

End Points

The primary end point for cohort A was the incidence of CR at any time, defined by investigator assessment of biopsy pathology (local), urine cytology, and cystoscopy.

Secondary end points were duration of CR response (DOR), time to cystectomy, progression-free survival (PFS), disease-specific survival (DSS), and overall survival (OS). Definitions are in the Supplement (https://www.jurology.com).

Safety

The safety population consisted of all participants who received at least one instillation of the study drug. Safety was assessed by the incidence and severity of treatment-related adverse events (TRAEs), treatment-emergent serious adverse events (SAEs) leading to hospitalization, adverse events of special interest, clinical laboratory assessments, vital signs, treatment exposure, concomitant medications, immunogenicity, and physical examinations. All TRAEs were graded using Common Terminology Criteria for Adverse Events version 4.03 and coded using the Medical Dictionary for Regulatory Activities.

Statistical Analysis Methods

For cohort A to meet the primary end point, the lower limit of the 95% CI of the CR rate at any time had to exceed 20%. The study design was based on achieving a 30% CR rate at month 3 or 6 in this population. This led to a sample size of at least 76 participants using the CI sample size estimation method with a 2-sided CI width of 0.216 when the CR rate of this population is 30%. The primary end point of CR rate at any time was analyzed using a 2-sided exact 95% CI calculated using the Clopper-Pearson method.

Duration of subjects' follow-up was defined as time from the initial study drug administration to the date of last follow-up visit. Duration of follow-up was summarized by descriptive statistics.

All time-to-event end points (DOR, PFS, DSS, OS, and time-to-cystectomy) were analyzed using Kaplan-Meier (KM) methods. The detailed definitions and censoring rules for these time-to-event end points were described above in the Endpoints section. For the, end points DOR, PFS, and OS, death was not a competing event since death was considered as an event in the KM analyses. However, for DSS and time-to-cystectomy, death from other causes (for DSS) and all types of death (for time-to-cystectomy) represent a competing event that precludes the occurrence of these 2 end points. Therefore, in addition to the protocol-planned KM analysis, a sensitivity analysis was performed to adjust for death (deaths from other causes for DSS; all deaths for time-to-cystectomy) as a competing risk. Cumulative incidence function (CIF) and its corresponding 95% CIs were estimated for both end points to account for the competing event by using PROC LIFETEST with options of OUTCIF and PLOTS = CIF(TEST) in SAS software v9.4. The probabilities of DSS and time-to-cystectomy at months 12, 24, and 36 were then calculated as 1-CIF using the estimated CIF at these time points.

Descriptive statistics were used to summarize the demographic, baseline, and safety data.

Trial Oversight

QUILT-3.032 was designed by the sponsor, ImmunityBio, Inc. The study was conducted in accordance with the International Conference on Harmonization for Good Clinical Practice guidelines and with the principles of the Declaration of Helsinki. Ethics bodies and Institutional Review Boards at each participating institution approved the protocol and all amendments. All participants provided written informed consent before enrollment. Data gathered by study investigators were analyzed by Biostatistics at ImmunityBio, Inc, DCL Pathology (Greenwood, Indiana) performed central pathology review.

RESULTS

Participant Demographics

Between July 2017 and July 2024, a total of 190 participants were enrolled in the United States. Cohort A was fully enrolled, N = 100. Disposition is shown in Supplementary Figure S1 (https://www.jurology.com).

The median age of participants in cohort A was 73 years (range 50-91; Supplementary Table S1, https://www.jurology.com). Most participants were male (87%) and White (90%).

Most participants (83%) had a baseline ECOG performance status score of 0 (patient was fully active). The median number of prior TURBT procedures was 4 (range 1-15). Forty-four percent of participants were refractory, and 56% were relapsed.

Sensitivity Analyses for DSS and Time-to-Cystectomy

The competing risk analyses adjusted for death as a competing event showed that the probability of DSS at 12, 24, and 36 months was 100% (95% CI 100.0-100.0) and 98.3% (95% CI 92.0-99.9; at both 24 and 36 months), respectively, and the probability of cystectomy-free rates at 12, 24, and 36 months was 95.7% (95% CI 88.9-98.9), 90.8% (95% CI 82.2-96.3), and 85.2% (95% CI 73.6-93.5), respectively.

Safety and Pharmacokinetics

As reported in Chang et al., the most frequently reported TRAEs (incidence ≥3%) for participants who received NAI in combination with BCG (cohorts A and B, n=180) were those expected for intravesical instillation of BCG.28 TRAEs are listed in Supplementary Table S3 (https://www.jurology.com). In Cohort A (N=100), the overall incidence of grade 1-2 TRAEs was 59%, incidence of grade 3 was 4%, with no grade 4 or 5 TRAEs. Grade 3 TRAEs occurred at an incidence of 1% for hematuria, urinary tract infection, pain in extremity, and arthralgia, and 2% for myalgia. There was no evidence of systemic NAI in any patient. One participant (1%) in Cohort A experienced grade 3 SAE hematuria resulting in study drug interruption. The SAE resolved, and the participant completed all remaining study treatments.

DISCUSSION

For cohort A, participants with BCG-unresponsive NMIBC CIS treated with NAI in combination with BCG; the CR rate of 71% exceeds rates reported for other several approved options for this indication, specifically 41% for pembrolizumab and 51% for nadofaragene firadenovec, and is comparable with rates reported for the recently approved TAR-200.10-12 This exceeds the CR rate of at least 30% at 12 months and 25% at 18 months suggested as a target for clinical efficacy by Kamat et al,29 US FDA experts, the AUA panel, and the International Bladder Cancer Group consensus.

The duration of CRs is also notable, with a median duration of > 45 months and range up to 53 months (FDA-approved population). Both the CR rate and duration are of importance to patients with BCG-unresponsive bladder CIS because this greatly increases the likelihood of cystectomy avoidance, shown by the KM estimated cystectomy avoidance rate of over 90% in cohort A responders at 24 months. This high rate of bladder preservation was not associated with inferior oncologic outcomes, and responders in cohort A had an estimated 3-year DSS rate of 98.2%.

CR rates and durations of response for subgroups were comparable with those of the primary efficacy population, including in the subgroup with the highest clinical risk (ie, CIS/T1). Participants with BCG-refractory or relapsed disease had response rates of 66% and 75%, respectively, which points to the contribution of NAI to efficacy, even in participants who had never responded to BCG alone. Of interest, 10 of 18 participants in cohort A who had previously been re-treated with BCG after a diagnosis of BCG-unresponsive (unlikely to be effective)8 responded to NAI in combination with BCG. Responses in these participants point to the contribution of NAI to efficacy.

Reinduction, the standard of care for BCG treatment, was an option for participants who did not achieve a CR at month 3 and proved to be merited, with 50% of cohort A participants achieving a CR after reinduction. This is perhaps expected, as within the trained immunity paradigm for BCG efficacy and NAI’s contribution to it, restimulation is believed to amplify immune responses.30

A key limitation of this study is its single-arm design, reflecting infeasibility of a BCG comparator cohort given the current BCG shortage and the impracticality of administering BCG monotherapy to participants with demonstrated BCG-unresponsive disease. Relatively homogenous participant demographics are also a limitation. Compared with other approved treatments in this disease state, the demographic profile of these participants was similar to other published trials (mostly male and White). NAI + BCG showed reasonable safety, with the most common TRAEs related to bladder instillation (eg, dysuria, pollakiuria, and hematuria). Most TRAEs were grade 1 to 2, consistent with pharmacokinetic findings showing no evidence of systemic NAI after intravesical administration. These data demonstrate that NAI in combination with BCG, approved for use in BCG-unresponsive NMIBC CIS ± Ta/T1 papillary disease, elicits a meaningful response rate along with prolonged duration of responses resulting in high rates of bladder preservation without compromise of oncologic outcomes.

CONCLUSIONS

The CR rate and durability of responses > 53 months reveal the efficacy of NAI in combination with BCG treating BCG-unresponsive NMIBC with CIS ± Ta/T1 disease. The cystectomy-free rate of 84% and DSS of 98% at 36 months suggest that NAI plus BCG is a safe and efficacious option for NMIBC with CIS ± Ta/T1 disease.
