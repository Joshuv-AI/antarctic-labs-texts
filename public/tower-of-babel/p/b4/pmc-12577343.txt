Mid-term outcomes of paclitaxel-coated balloon for de novo non-small coronary lesions: a pooled analysis
Authors: Xingqiang He, Yi Liu, Zhiyong Yin, Fei Li, Robert-Jan van Geuns, Scot Garg, Yoshinobu Onuma, Patrick W. Serruys, Chao Gao, Ling Tao
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
BackgroundThe efficacy of drug-coated balloons (DCB) for the treatment of de novo, non-small coronary lesions remains uncertain. This study aimed to evaluate the real-world performance of DCB-only angioplasty in this setting.MethodsPatients with de novo, non-small coronary lesions (≥ 2.5 mm) treated exclusively with either drug-eluting stents (DES) or DCB were included from the DCB registry and two DES registries. The primary endpoint was the device-oriented composite endpoint (DoCE), including cardiovascular death, non-fatal target vessel myocardial infarction (TV-MI), and target lesion revascularization (TLR). Differences between cohorts were compared and adjusted by inverse probability of treatment weighting (IPTW).ResultsThe study included 4621 patients, with 3780 in the DES group and 841 in the DCB group. At 3 years, the primary endpoint occurred in 51 (6.1%) patients receiving DCB and 143 (3.8%) patients with DES (HRIPTW: 1.87, 95% CI: 1.04–3.36, P = 0.039). Cardiovascular death occurred in 12 (1.4%) patients in the DCB group and 88 (2.3%) patients in the DES group (HRIPTW: 1.24, 95% CI: 0.34–4.48), non-fatal TV-MI occurred in 5 (0.6%) patients in the DCB group and 18 (0.5%) patients in the DES group (HRIPTW: 1.21, 95% CI: 0.33–4.38), and TLR occurred in 34 (4.0%) patients in the DCB group and 45 (1.2%) in the DES group (HRIPTW: 2.94, 95% CI: 1.72–5.01).ConclusionsDCB-only angioplasty is associated with a higher rate of DoCE and TLR compared with DES group in patients with de novo coronary lesions which were ≥ 2.5 mm in diameter.Trial registrationRegistered on clinicaltrials.gov (NCT02929030, NCT03916432, and NCT05133921).Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12916-025-04429-9.

Background

Drug-eluting stents (DES) have long been recommended as the default device for patients undergoing percutaneous coronary intervention (PCI) [1]. Despite the remarkable progress in stent designing and crafting, 2% to 3% of the DES-treated patients still require repeat interventions annually, while the risk of life-threatening late and very late stent thrombosis persists [2].

Drug-coated balloons (DCB) have emerged as an alternative therapeutic approach for obstructive atherosclerotic lesions by rapidly transferring antiproliferative drugs into the arterial wall through a lipophilic matrix during balloon inflation without needing the permanent implant [3]. Consequently, they may potentially limit the adverse effects associated with polymer-based DES, especially in coronary bifurcation lesions, in-stent restenosis (ISR), small coronary lesions, and patients with high bleeding risk [4]. In the setting of de novo small vessel disease, the randomized BASKET-SMALL 2 trial demonstrated similar clinical efficacy between DCB and DES [5]. However, findings from our recent REC-CAGEFREE I trial, a randomized study involving 2272 patients with de novo, non-complex coronary artery disease regardless of vessel diameter, revealed that DCB with rescue stenting was associated with a higher incidence of the device-oriented composite endpoint (DoCE) at 2 years compared to intended stenting [6]. Although our randomized trial provided robust evidence for the treatment of de novo lesions, the inherent limitations of randomized trials, including their stringent design, often restrict the generalizability of findings to real-world clinical practice [7]. As such, the findings from such rigorously conducted trials may not fully reflect the complexities of routine clinical settings [7].

In this study, conducted in a real-world setting, we compared patients treated with paclitaxel-DCB to those in two contemporary second-generation DES registries [8, 9] to evaluate the mid-term outcomes of DCB versus DES in patients with de novo, non-small coronary lesions.

Methods

Study design and patient population

Patients treated with paclitaxel-coated balloon (SeQuent Please, B. Braun, Germany) were consecutively enrolled in the REC-CAGEFREE registry (NCT05133921), which was a single-center, prospective study conducted in Xijing Hospital, China, between December 1, 2015, and December 1, 2019, aimed to document the real-world performance of the DCB in the non-small vessel (≥ 2.5 mm). The baseline of all participants (N = 2473) of the REC-CAGEFREE registry is provided in Additional file 1: Table S1.

Patients who received second-generation DES in the NANO [8] and HELIOS [9] registries were used as the comparator. The REC-CAGEFREE, NANO, and HELIOS registries were all conducted by the same group of investigators and used the same baseline and endpoint definitions. The NANO registry (NCT02929030) was a prospective, multicenter clinical registry conducted in 26 centers in China between August 1, 2016, and January 1, 2017, which enrolled 2481 consecutive patients treated exclusively with the Nano plus (Lepu Medical, China) sirolimus-eluting stent. The HELIOS registry (NCT03916432) was a prospective, multicenter study conducted in 38 centers across China between November 1, 2018, and December 1, 2019, which enrolled 3060 consecutive patients treated exclusively with the HELIOS (Kinhely Medical, China) sirolimus-eluting stent. The results of these two registries have been published previously [8, 9].

To compare DES to DCB, patients with de novo lesion, DES/DCB ≥ 2.5 mm in diameter, and had DES-only or DCB-only treatment were included; those who had STEMI, ISR lesions, DES bailout after DCB treatment, or a combined strategy of DES and DCB were excluded (Fig. 1). The definition of a small vessel is inconsistent across trials; however, to standardize the design of clinical studies and clinical decision-making, an expert consensus proposed using a reference vessel diameter of < 2.5 mm as the definition of small vessel coronary artery disease (CAD) [10] and was applied in the current study.Fig. 1Study flow chart. The study was performed in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki and was approved by the ethics committees of the participant centers of each study. All the patients signed the written informed consent prior to participation.

Intervention procedure

PCI with DES or DCB was performed in accordance with international revascularization guidelines [4, 11] with the details of the NANO and HELIOS registries described previously [8, 9]. In brief, in the REC-CAGEFREE registry (DCB cohort), pre-dilatation of the target lesion was recommended using a non-compliant balloon, scoring balloon, or cutting balloon in a balloon-to-vessel ratio of 0.8–1.0. Commonly, a DCB treatment would only be performed after successful lesion preparation, defined as residual stenosis after pre-dilatation ≤ 30% by visual assessment based on at least two perpendicular angiographic views, a target vessel TIMI (Thrombolysis In Myocardial Infarction) flow grade 3, and the absence of a significant flow-limiting dissection which was no greater than type C [4]. The choice of diameter and length of DCB was left to the operator’s discretion. Recommended inflation time was at least 45 s; however, the maximum inflation pressure and total inflation time were ultimately determined by the operator according to the patient’s tolerance and the reaction of the treated lesions. As set out in the protocol of the REC-CAGEFREE registry, bailout stenting using a DES was strongly recommended if the target lesions/vessels post-DCB treatment failed to have a TIMI flow grade 3, dissection type less than type C, and residual diameter stenosis ≤ 30% by visual assessment.

Definition of the DCB with optimal balloon preparation, DCB with optimal post-procedural computational pressure-flow dynamics derived fractional flow reserve (caFFR), and optimal post-DCB subpopulations

The optimal balloon preparation subgroup was defined as exhibiting an optimal angiographic result after lesion preparation, including no dissection or type A, B, TIMI III blood flow, and residual stenosis (QCA) < 30% [12]. The optimal post-procedural caFFR was defined as caFFR value ≥ 0.89 after DCB angioplasty [13]. The optimal post-DCB subpopulation was defined as exhibiting an optimal angiographic result after DCB angioplasty, including no dissection or type A, B, TIMI 3 blood flow, and residual stenosis (QCA) < 30% [12].

Outcomes and follow-up

The primary endpoint was the device-oriented composite endpoint (DoCE) defined as a composite of cardiovascular death, non-fatal target vessel myocardial infarction (TV-MI), and clinically driven target lesion revascularization (TLR). Other secondary endpoints of interest were the patient-oriented composite endpoint (PoCE) and the individual components of the DoCE and PoCE. PoCE was defined as a composite endpoint including all-cause death, any non-fatal MI, any stroke, and any repeat revascularization. The endpoints of all three studies were defined according to the Academic Research Consortium-2 recommendations [14, 15] and adjudicated by the independent clinical event committees of the same members. In the REC-CAGEFREE registry, angiograms pre- and post-DCB were analyzed using quantitative coronary angiography (QCA, Medis, Leiden, The Netherlands) [16] and caFFR (Rainmed Ltd, Suzhou, China) [17], with details provided in Additional file 1: Methods.

In both the NANO and HELIOS registries, patients were contacted at 1, 6, and 12 months by scheduled outpatient clinic visits. After 12 months, they were contacted by phone annually to assess the clinical status and adverse events. In the REC-CAGEFREE registry, patients were contacted at 1 month and 12 months by scheduled outpatient clinic visits and by phone contact annually thereafter. The median follow-up of REC-CAGEFREE, NANO, and HELIOS registries were 2.4 (1.8–3.2), 3.1 (3.0–3.1), and 2.0 (2.0–2.1) years, respectively. A total of 290 of patients (7.6%) in the DES arm (NANO and HELIOS registries) and 42 (5.0%) in the DCB arm (REC-CAGEFREE registry) were lost to follow-up.

Statistical analysis

Continuous data were expressed as mean ± SD and categorical data as absolute numbers and proportions. Wilcoxon rank-sum test or the Pearson chi-square test was used to compare patients’ demographic, clinical, and procedural characteristics. The percentage of missing values are presented in Additional file 1: Table S2 in the Supplementary information. Cox proportional hazard regression models were used to estimate hazard ratios (HRs) for each outcome at the time point of interest, and the proportional hazard assumptions were tested and met in all models. To reduce the confounding bias of baseline characteristics, two statistical approaches, including inverse probability of treatment weighting (IPTW) and 1:1 propensity score matching (PSM), were performed [18]. The variables included in these adjustments are listed in Additional file 1: Methods. Kaplan–Meier was used to illustrate the outcomes between groups. We also used restricted cubic splines to flexibly fit the association of device diameters (DCB and DES) and clinical outcomes. Analyses were performed using R-project version 4.3 (R Foundation, Vienna, Austria). A two-sided P value less than 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Results

Of the 8014 patients enrolled in the three studies, a total of 4621 patients fulfilled the inclusion and exclusion criteria and were included, of whom 3780 (81.8%) underwent DES-only treatment and 841 (18.2%) DCB-only treatment (Fig. 1). Of note, 3.5% of patients in the NANO registry had a visually assessed reference vessel diameter < 2.5 mm; however, all were treated with DES ≥ 2.5 mm in diameter. The propensity score matching (PSM) dataset included 661 matched pairs of patients, accounting for 17.5% of the DES cohort and 78.6% of the DCB cohort. Patients were on average 63 and 57 years old respectively in the DES and DCB cohorts, mostly male, and had high rates of cardiovascular risk factors, including diabetes, hypertension, dyslipidemia, and smoking. Compared to the DCB group, the DES group exhibits a higher proportion of chronic total occlusion and severe calcification, while showing a lower proportion of bifurcation lesions. In the DCB cohort, 787/841 (93.6%) patients achieved an inflation time of at least 45 s, with a mean inflation duration of 59.0 ± 7.9 s. The mean diameter of devices in the DES and DCB cohorts was 3.03 mm and 2.88 mm, respectively. The distribution of device diameter is presented in Additional file 1: Fig. S1. Detailed lesion characteristics of the DCB cohort, including the method of lesion preparation, dissection type, QCA (pre-procedure, post-balloon angioplasty, and post-DCB dilatation), and caFFR (pre- and post-procedure), are shown in Additional file 1: Fig. S2 and Table S3. In the DCB arm, a cutting balloon was used in 60.0% of lesions and a scoring balloon in 20.0% (mutually exclusive); the mean post-DCB diameter stenosis was 29.77 ± 11.49%, while the mean post-procedural caFFR was 0.90 ± 0.05.

Clinical outcomes

At 3-year follow-up, the primary endpoint, DoCE occurred in 51 (6.1%) patients receiving DCB and 143 (3.8%) patients treated with DES. The risk of DoCE was significantly higher in the DCB group than the DES group in the crude analysis (HRcrude: 1.59, 95% CI: 1.15–2.18, P = 0.005) and adjusted analyses (HRIPTW: 1.87, 95% CI: 1.04–3.36, P = 0.039; HRPSM: 1.67, 95% CI: 1.01–2.74, P = 0.045; Table 3 and Fig. 2A). Because the Kaplan–Meier curves of the two groups crossed at 270 days, a landmark analysis was performed. Patients in the DCB and DES groups showed numerically similar rates of DoCE during 0 to 270 days (HRIPTW: 0.98, 95% CI: 0.47–2.08, P = 0.965), whereas the rate of DoCE was higher in DCB as compared to DES at 270 days to 3 years (HRIPTW: 2.20, 95% CI: 1.03–4.67, P = 0.043; Fig. 2B). The event of PoCE occurred in 103 (12.2%) patients in the DCB cohort and 411 (10.9%) in the DES cohort (HRIPTW: 1.35, 95% CI: 0.97–1.88, P = 0.075; Table 3 and Additional file 1: Fig. S3A), and the landmark analysis of PoCE was provided in Additional file 1: Fig. S3B. Discussion

In the current study, we found that in the real-world cohort of patients with de novo non-small coronary lesions, DCB-only angioplasty was associated with significantly higher rates of DoCE and TLR compared to DES implantation at 3 years, supporting the preferential use of DES in this specific patient population.

De novo CAD represents a promising but currently infrequent indication for DCB. The theoretical advantages of DCB involve the reduction in neointimal proliferation through evenly distributed drug elution together with there being no further reduction in the lumen by the absence of metallic struts [5, 19], thus avoiding the adverse effects associated with polymer-based permanent prostheses and allowing the vessel to remodel to its natural configuration [20].

Studies have confirmed the safety and efficacy of using DCB in de novo, small vessel CAD [5]. In the plain old balloon angioplasty (POBA) era, Foley et al. [21] showed that after successful percutaneous transluminal coronary angioplasty, larger vessel size independently predicted smaller late luminal loss (LLL) and larger minimal luminal diameter at follow-up angiography. With this notion, PCI operators have tried using DCB-only angioplasty in non-small, de novo lesions with optimal pre-dilatation and stents reserved for bailout [22]. The DEBUT study showed that in a randomized cohort, treating simple coronary lesions with DCB was superior to bare-metal stents in patients at high bleeding risk [23]. However, clinical data regarding the performance of DCB to DES in the de novo non-small lesions remains scarce. Our recent randomized REC-CAGEFREE I trial [6], which enrolled 2272 patients with all vessel diameters, demonstrated that the use of DCB with the option for rescue stenting was inferior to the routine use of DES in terms of the composite clinical endpoint at 2 years. This result was primarily driven by a higher rate of clinically and physiologically indicated target lesion revascularization, while the rates of cardiovascular death and target vessel myocardial infarction were numerically similar between the two strategies [6]. To verify this finding in the real-world setting, in the current study, we included 4621 patients with de novo CAD who were treated with implanted devices ≥ 2.5 mm in diameter from three all-comers registries. In line with the REC-CAGEFREE I results, we found that the DCB-only strategy also led to a higher risk of DoCE, TLR, and any revascularization compared with DES at 3-year follow-up. Although the underlying pathomechanisms of this finding remain to be elucidated, our results showed that a DCB-only strategy remains challenging for non-small de novo coronary lesions due to a potentially higher rate of repeat revascularization.

In the present study, we observed a late divergence of the Kaplan–Meier curves for DoCE beginning around 270 days, consistent with findings from the REC-CAGEFREE I trial [6]. A similar trend was reported in the OPUS-1 study, where the revascularization curves began to diverge at approximately 100 days in favor of bare-metal stents [24]. Potential explanations include late lumen restenosis due to neointimal hyperplasia or vessel recoil in the DCB group, despite the frequent use of cutting or scoring balloons in both the REC-CAGEFREE registry and REC-CAGEFREE I trial. However, the lack of scheduled imaging follow-up limits mechanistic interpretation and highlights the need for further dedicated investigations.

Among patients with de novo lesions, the mortality at 3 years was higher in the DES cohort than in the DCB cohort, with cardiovascular mortality also numerically higher in the DES group. These findings are consistent with the decade-long follow-up of the ISAR-DESIRE 3 trial, which reported lower mortality with DCB compared to DES in patients with in-stent restenosis (ISR) [25]. Conversely, in our REC-CAGEFREE I trial, both all-cause and cardiovascular mortality rates were numerically elevated in the DCB group compared to the DES group [6]. The main difference between the ISAR-DESIRE 3 study, our real-world analysis, and the REC-CAGEFREE I study is found in the characteristics of the populations studied. In REC-CAGEFREE I, we specifically targeted patients with non-complex de novo lesions, in whom the efficacy of contemporary DES may already be quite favorable. This indicates that DCB might not represent the best approach for this subset of patients. Nevertheless, in populations that are typically deemed less appropriate for DES, such as those with ISR, diffuse disease, or bifurcation lesions, which were also part of our current real-world cohort, DCB might possibly be a viable option and its role of DCB requires further investigation.

The optimization of DCB angioplasty during PCI is closely related to prognosis. In our study, although the investigators were required to follow the recommendations of current consensus [4, 12], namely performing DCB only if the residual stenosis after pre-dilatation ≤ 30%, a target vessel TIMI flow grade 3, and the absence of dissection which was no greater than type C [4]. However, assessed by Corelab based on QCA, there were only 35.2% of patients fulfilled all of these criteria, suggesting that in real-world practice, it would be rather difficult to fulfill these criteria, especially the criterion of residual stenosis after pre-dilatation ≤ 30%. It has been suggested that intravascular imaging could aid in refining lesion selection and optimizing both procedural and clinical outcomes. In the ULTIMATE III trial, 260 patients with high bleeding risk and de novo coronary lesions were randomized to either IVUS-guided or angiography-guided DCB angioplasty. The results demonstrated that IVUS-guided DCB angioplasty was associated with significantly lower in-segment late lumen loss at 7 months compared to angiography guidance [26], supporting the value of imaging-guided DCB strategy in improving outcomes. This finding highlights the inherent limitations of relying solely on angiographic visual assessment and may possibly suggest the value of intravascular imaging.

The FFR/angio-based FFR measured post-PCI by DES has been shown to independently predict long-term adverse events. However, the cutoff point is still controversial. The proposed value ranged from 0.89 to 0.96 [27]. However, so far, there is no data regarding the cutoff point of post-PCI FFR/angio-based FFR in DCB patients. Using an arbitrary truncation value, we found that in the DCB group, 97.8% of vessels achieved an immediate post-DCB caFFR of 0.75, 95.3% of 0.80 (categorized as “acceptable angiographic result” by the Third Report of the International DCB Consensus Group) [4], 85.6% of 0.85, and 67.3% of 0.89 [13]. Nevertheless, even confining the DCB population by reaching the most strict value of post-DCB FFR ≥ 0.89, these DCB-treated patients were still associated with a 3.65-fold higher risk of TLR compared to DES.

Both DAPT regimens and lipid control are well-established prognostic factors, particularly in patients with coronary artery disease [28]. Inadequate antiplatelet intensity or suboptimal LDL-C management may influence long-term outcomes, including the risk of ischemic events and the need for repeat revascularization [28, 29]. However, due to financial constraints, detailed longitudinal data on DAPT regimen and LDL-C levels were not systematically collected in the current study. Therefore, the inability to adjust for these variables as intercurrent events in the analyses limits the precision with which the observed clinical events can be attributed solely to the device strategy. Future prospective studies incorporating standardized medication tracking and longitudinal laboratory monitoring will be necessary to disentangle the relative contributions of procedural strategy versus medical therapy in determining long-term outcomes of DCB-based interventions.

Aggregating data from different studies that have varied inclusion and exclusion criteria, temporal variations, diverse definition for outcomes, and distinct standards for study management and data collection, would result in considerable heterogeneity in the findings, ultimately leading to less reliable conclusions [30]. However, the three registries utilized in the present study was executed with a similar objective and study design. All three registries were designed in an “all-comers” fashion, aimed to document the contemporary real-world performance of DES or DCB. Consequently, all three studies featured less inclusion and exclusion criteria and endeavored to consecutively enroll all available patients throughout the study enrollment phrase. Furthermore, the three registries were conducted by same research group, adhering the same study management and data collection protocols. The endpoints were all adjudicated by the Academic Research Consortium-2 recommendations. Taken together, we believed that due to the similar population eligibility and study design, identical definitions of outcomes, uniform data collection standards, and quality control measurement, the study results were generated from a scientifically robust dataset.

Limitations

There are limitations of the present study. (1) The rate of non-fatal MI was relatively low and might be underestimated, as CK-MB and cTn levels were not routinely tested in all participants post-PCI. This was attributed to resource constraints and the absence of a protocol requirement, likely leading to an underestimation of peri-procedural MI rates. However, the rates of peri-procedural MI were highly variable depending on the definitions; the inclusion of peri-procedural MI as an endpoint was also under debate [31]. Additionally, patients were followed by phone contact at 3-year follow-up, which would lead to fewer silent MIs being diagnosed. (2) Lesion preparation and treatment decisions were based primarily on angiographic visual assessment, without the routine use of intravascular imaging. This may have limited the ability to accurately assess lesion characteristics and optimize procedural outcome. (3) Imbalances in baseline characteristics exist between DES and DCB groups. To adjust for the confounding factors, statistical approaches such as of IPTW and propensity score matching were performed to estimate the actual treatment effects between groups; however, the inability to eliminate the impact of unmeasurable confounders remains, and this results in bias that cannot be adjusted for. (4) While the NANO and HELIOS studies investigating the two stents were multicenter studies, the REC-CAGEFREE registry was a single-center study. Additionally, the NANO plus and HELIOS stents used in this study were commonly used only in China, which may limit the generalizability of the findings to a broader population. (5) Device thrombosis events were not prespecified for collection in the REC-CAGEFREE registry cohort, limiting the ability to compare device-related thrombosis risk between DCB and DES groups. (6) The median follow-up duration was less than 3 years, potentially limiting the reliability of long-term outcome estimates. Longer-term follow-up is warranted to confirm these findings. (7) Detailed longitudinal data of DAPT regimen and LDL-C profiles were not available in the current study, which limited us to further evaluate their potential impact on clinical outcomes.

Conclusions

At 3 years, we found that DCB was associated with significantly higher rates of DoCE and TLR than DES for treating de novo, non-small lesions. However, given the observational nature of the study, all results should be considered hypothesis-generating.
