Development and psychometric validation of a medication literacy scale for glucose-lowering therapy in Chinese adults with type 2 diabetes mellitus
Authors: Siying Liu, Nan Wan, Zhaoyi Ye, Lingna Yu, Xiaochun Yang, Meizheng Lai, Yutao Lan
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundMedication literacy is essential for the safe and effective use of glucose-lowering therapies in adults with type 2 diabetes mellitus (T2DM). Existing instruments insufficiently capture the multidimensional competencies required for long-term medication management, and no validated tool is available for Chinese adults. This study aimed to develop and psychometrically validate the medication literacy scale for glucose-lowering therapy in adults with T2DM (MLSGT-T2DM) based on the Information–Motivation–Behavioral Skills (IMB) model.MethodsThe study was conducted from December 2024 to November 2025 and divided into three phases. Phase 1: An initial item pool was developed through a comprehensive literature review and semi-structured interviews with 18 patients with T2DM. Phase 2: Two rounds of Delphi expert consultation were conducted with 16 multidisciplinary experts, among whom 10 evaluated the content validity of the scale, and two rounds of cognitive interviews were carried out with 11 patients to assess face validity. Phase 3: The reliability of the scale was tested by internal consistency, split-half reliability, and retest reliability, and the validity of the scale was measured by exploratory factor analysis (EFA = 300) and confirmatory factor analysis (CFA = 276).ResultsEFA supported a six-factor structure nested within three IMB dimensions (information, motivation, behavioral skills). CFA confirmed good model fit (χ²/df = 2.264, CFI = 0.924, TLI = 0.914, RMSEA = 0.068, SRMR = 0.056). Convergent and discriminant validity were satisfactory. Reliability indices demonstrated strong internal consistency (Cronbach’s α = 0.902; McDonald’s ω = 0.904), acceptable split-half reliability (0.766), and high test–retest stability (ICC = 0.898). The final MLSGT-T2DM comprises 24 items.ConclusionThe MLSGT-T2DM is a psychometrically robust and theoretically grounded instrument for assessing glucose-lowering medication literacy among Chinese adults with T2DM. Its multidimensional structure enables precise identification of knowledge, motivational, and behavioral skill gaps, supporting targeted patient education and more effective diabetes self-management interventions.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12889-026-26800-y.

Introduction

Type 2 diabetes mellitus (T2DM) is the most common form of diabetes worldwide and requires lifelong pharmacotherapy to achieve durable glycaemic control and prevent complications [1]. Despite substantial advances in glucose-lowering therapies, preventable medication errors [2] and poor adherence remain widespread among long-term users [3]. In China, where treatment regimens are largely managed independently at home or within community settings, these problems are further compounded by insufficient medication-related knowledge, prevalent misconceptions, and limited self-management skills [4, 5]. Consequently, inadequate understanding and inconsistent adherence undermine treatment effectiveness and increase the risk of diabetes-related complications [6].

Medication literacy has emerged as a critical determinant of these challenges. It refers to an individual’s capacity to obtain, comprehend, evaluate, and apply medication-related information to make informed therapeutic decisions, integrating cognitive understanding, risk–benefit appraisal, and practical behavioral skills [7]. Within the broader framework of chronic illness self-management, self-care behaviors represent observable actions. Importantly, medication literacy reflects the underlying capacity that enables individuals to engage in effective and sustained self-management and serves as an upstream determinant of patients’ daily disease management decisions [8]. In addition, medication literacy is often conceptualized as a context-specific application of health literacy in relation to medication use and includes competencies consistently linked to self-care behaviors across a range of chronic conditions [9], underscoring their foundational role in effective disease management. Evidence consistently demonstrates that higher medication literacy is associated with better medication adherence [10], fewer medication-related adverse events, and improved clinical outcomes [11], whereas limited medication literacy contributes to suboptimal diabetes self-management and poor glycaemic control [12].

Although several instruments have been developed internationally to assess medication literacy, most focus primarily on functional skills, such as interpreting medication labels or dosage instructions [13]. Disease-specific and culturally adapted measures for chronic conditions remain scarce, particularly for Chinese adults with T2DM. This gap limits clinicians’ ability to systematically identify literacy deficits and design targeted interventions to support safe and effective medication use [13, 14].

Conceptual models of medication literacy describe multiple competencies shaped by personal, social, and healthcare system factors [15]. The Information–Motivation–Behavioural Skills (IMB) model further provides a theoretical explanation of how these competencies translate into real-world behaviour: information underpins informed decision-making, motivation sustains adherence, and behavioural skills enable correct implementation of treatment plans [16]. Together, these perspectives suggest that T2DM medication literacy is a multidimensional construct encompassing informational, motivational, and behavioural components essential for long-term pharmacotherapy. The present study draws on the IMB model to develop a diabetes-specific framework that captures these cognitive, motivational, and behavioural determinants within the context of glucose-lowering therapy.

To address the existing measurement gap, this study aimed to develop and psychometrically validate the Medication Literacy Scale for Glucose-Lowering Therapy in Chinese Adults with T2DM (MLSGT-T2DM). By integrating the information, motivation, and behavioural skills dimensions, the MLSGT-T2DM provides a comprehensive and theoretically grounded instrument to support patient-centred diabetes management and optimize medication use outcomes.

Methods

Study design

This methodological study adopted a multi-phase design consisting of qualitative item generation, expert content validation, cognitive testing, and large-sample psychometric evaluation. Data collection was conducted between December 2024 and November 2025.

Phase 1: item generation

Item development was informed by a systematic literature review and semi-structured interviews to ensure comprehensive coverage of medication literacy in glucose-lowering therapy. A review of major Chinese and international databases (up to October 2024) was conducted to identify existing frameworks and themes related to disease understanding, medication safety, motivational determinants, and behavioural skills. Semi-structured interviews with adults diagnosed with T2DM were then performed to further refine these themes. Maximum variation sampling was applied to ensure diversity in age, education, treatment duration, and medication regimens. All interviews were audio-recorded, transcribed verbatim, and analysed using directed content analysis [17]. Candidate items were generated based on the emergent themes and mapped onto the three core components of the IMB model.

Participants were eligible if they: (1) were aged ≥ 18 years; (2) had been diagnosed with T2DM according to the 1999 World Health Organization (WHO) diagnostic criteria [18]; (3) had used glucose-lowering medications for ≥ 3 months; (4) were able to read and communicate in Chinese; and (5) provided written informed consent. Participants with severe cardiac, hepatic, or renal dysfunction, or with cognitive or psychiatric conditions that could affect communication, were excluded.

Phase 2: content and face validity assessment

Content validity

A two-round Delphi consultation was conducted with multidisciplinary experts in endocrinology, nursing, pharmacy, and chronic disease management [19]. Expert inclusion criteria were as follows: (1) engagement in clinical practice or research related to endocrinology, chronic disease management, or medication safety; (2) a minimum of 10 years of professional experience; (3) a bachelor’s degree or higher; and (4) agreement to participate in both Delphi rounds.

Experts independently evaluated each item for importance, and clarity using a five-point Likert scale ranging from 1 (not important at all) to 5 (very important), and also provided qualitative comments. Items were retained, revised, merged, or removed based on predefined criteria (mean score ≥ 3.5, coefficient of variation ≤ 0.25, and full-score rate > 20%), combined with expert feedback [20].

Content validity was assessed by asking the experts to rate each item according to the item’ s relevance on a four-point scale: 1 = not relevant, 2 = slight relevance, 3 = certain relevance, and 4 = very relevant.

Face validity

Face validity was examined through two rounds of cognitive interviewing using a think-aloud technique. Adults with T2DM completed the preliminary scale while verbalizing their understanding of each item. The research team used the Question Appraisal System–99 (QAS-99) [21] to identify problems related to wording, comprehension, ambiguity, or excessive technical terminology. Items were refined to improve clarity, readability, and cultural appropriateness before proceeding to pilot testing.

Phase 3: Psychometric Evaluation

The final phase assessed the psychometric properties of the MLSGT-T2DM through a cross-sectional survey conducted in three tertiary hospitals and two community health service centers in Guangzhou, Foshan, and Shenzhen. Based on the guideline of recruiting 5–10 participants per item and adding 10% for potential invalid responses, a minimum of 215 participants was required for exploratory factor analysis (EFA), and at least 220 participants were required for confirmatory factor analysis (CFA) [22].

Test–retest reliability was evaluated in a convenience sample of 40 patients recruited from the Second People’s Hospital of Guangdong Province. Participants completed the scale twice at a 14-day interval, and items demonstrating poor temporal stability were subsequently removed [23].

Measures and procedures

A general information questionnaire collected demographic and clinical characteristics, including age, sex, duration of diabetes, medication history, type of glucose-lowering therapy, blood glucose indicators, and socioeconomic status. The MLSGT-T2DM (preliminary version) consisted of 39 items across three IMB-based dimensions: (1) Information (12 items): Dichotomous responses (yes/no/not sure) coded as 1 = correct and 0 = incorrect, assessing diabetes-related and medication-related knowledge. (2) Motivation (8 items): A 5-point Likert scale (1 = strongly disagree to 5 = strongly agree) evaluating motivation toward medication use; negatively worded items were reverse-scored. (3) Behavioral skills (19 items): Items assessed abilities in accessing, understanding, communicating, calculating, and appraising medication information. Most items used a 5-point Likert scale (1 = never to 5 = always), while several scenario-based items were scored as single-best-answer questions.

Participants completed the questionnaires in a private setting, and responses were checked immediately for completeness. All data were anonymized and managed according to standard quality-control procedures.

Data analysis

Data were analyzed using SPSS 27.0 and AMOS 29.0. Item screening followed Classical Test Theory (CTT) procedures, including discrimination analysis using the high–low group method [24], item–total correlations, Cronbach’s α [25], and the dispersion trend method. Items failing to meet at least two criteria were considered for deletion. EFA was conducted to explore the latent structure of the MLSGT-T2DM and evaluate its construct validity. Pearson correlation matrices were used for EFA, and principal component analysis was employed as the extraction method with oblique rotation. The number of factors retained was determined by considering multiple criteria, including eigenvalues greater than one, the results of parallel analysis, the magnitude and pattern of factor loadings (primary loadings > 0.40 with minimal cross-loadings), and theoretical interpretability [26]. CFA was subsequently performed to evaluate model fit. Maximum likelihood estimation was used in CFA, and model fit was assessed using multiple indices: Comparative Fit Index (CFI) and Tucker–Lewis Index (TLI) values ≥ 0.90 were considered acceptable, Root Mean Square Error of Approximation (RMSEA) ≤ 0.08, and Standardized Root Mean Square Residual (SRMR) ≤ 0.08 [27].

Internal consistency was evaluated using both Cronbach’s α and McDonald’s ω coefficients. Cronbach’s α was reported to facilitate comparison with previous medication literacy studies, where α remains the most commonly reported reliability index. Given the multidimensional structure of the scale and the mixed response format (binary and Likert-type items), McDonald’s ω was additionally calculated, as it provides a more appropriate estimate of reliability for heterogeneous scales. Split-half reliability was assessed using the Spearman–Brown coefficient [28]. Test–retest reliability was evaluated using the intraclass correlation coefficient (ICC), calculated based on a two-way mixed-effects model with absolute agreement. Convergent validity was examined through standardized factor loadings, composite reliability (CR), and average variance extracted (AVE). Discriminant validity was assessed using the Fornell–Larcker criterion, comparing the square root of each factor’s AVE with its inter-factor correlations [29]. Together, these analyses ensured a comprehensive evaluation of the scale’s psychometric properties.

Floor and ceiling effects

Floor and ceiling effects were assessed for the total MLSGT-T2DM scale and each dimension to evaluate the scale’s sensitivity. A floor or ceiling effect was considered present if more than 15% of participants achieved the minimum or maximum possible scores, respectively [30]. The percentages of participants scoring the minimum and maximum were calculated for each dimension.

Ethical considerations

This study was approved by the Ethics Committee of Guangdong Second Provincial General Hospital [2024-KY-KZ-385-01]. Written informed consent was obtained from all participants. Participation was voluntary, and confidentiality and privacy were maintained throughout the research process.

Results

Item pool development

A total of 46 preliminary items were generated through a combined process of literature review and semi-structured interviews. The literature review included Chinese and international databases and ensured comprehensive coverage of key concepts related to medication literacy in T2DM, including knowledge, motivational beliefs, and behavioural competencies. Insights from 18 patient interviews were used to refine and expand the item pool, ensuring alignment with the three core components of the IMB model—information, motivation, and behavioural skills.

Content validity

A two-round Delphi consultation involving 16 experts in diabetes care, endocrinology, pharmacy, and community health. The expert panel consisted of professionals with substantial clinical and research experience; more than 80% held associate senior or senior professional titles, and all had at least 10 years of practice. This ensured the credibility and representativeness of their evaluations.

The first Delphi round achieved a 100% response rate, with experts providing 20 suggestions regarding item refinement, including deletion, modification, and merging. The second round achieved a 93.75% response rate, during which four additional suggestions were made. After two rounds, expert opinions reached convergence, and the initial version of the MLSGT-T2DM was finalized with 39 items. The authority coefficients for the two rounds were 0.888 and 0.910, indicating high reliability of expert judgement [31]. The Kendall’s coefficients of concordance were 0.305 (P < 0.001) and 0.229 (P < 0.001), demonstrating acceptable agreement among experts across both rounds.

The content validity of the scale was evaluated by 10 experts in relevant fields using the Content Validity Index (CVI). The item-level CVI (I-CVI) ranged from 0.800 to 1.000, and the scale-level CVI (S-CVI) was 0.950, indicating excellent content validity of the MLSGT-T2DM.

Face validity

To assess face validity, two rounds of cognitive interviews were conducted with 11 participants. In the first round of cognitive interviews, six participants were interviewed. Analysis of the interview data identified eight items with comprehension issues. Most problems fell under the “clarification” category, including one item involving a wording issue (3a), three items related to ambiguity (3c), and four items categorized as professional terminology issues (3b) due to the use of technical terms.

For the item categorized under “3a wording,” participants reported that the phrasing was not smooth and required extra effort to understand, which increased their response time. Issues categorized as “3b professional terminology” mainly stemmed from medical or technical expressions that exceeded the language familiarity of typical patients. Terms such as “drug interactions” were described by participants as “hard to understand” or “unclear,” which affected their ability to accurately interpret the item. Items falling under the “3c ambiguity” category were reported to have unclear meanings or vague directions. In the second round, 5 participants were interviewed, all of whom reported clearly understanding all items, with no new issues or revision suggestions raised. These cognitive interviews further refined the scale, ensuring clarity, comprehensibility, and cultural appropriateness prior to large-scale psychometric testing.

Item analysis

To evaluate item quality and guide item retention, four methods were applied: the dispersion trend method, critical ratio method, correlation coefficient method, and Cronbach’s α coefficient method. Items that failed to meet at least two criteria were recommended for deletion.

The dispersion trend method was used to assess the sensitivity and variability of each item. Because the MLSGT-T2DM included items with different scoring formats, the coefficient of variation (CV) was selected to minimize the influence of scale-type differences. CV was calculated as CV = standard deviation / mean, with a deletion threshold of CV < 0.15. All items exceeded this threshold, indicating adequate response variability and sensitivity.

Participants were ranked according to their total scores, with the top 27% and bottom 27% designated as the high-score and low-score groups, respectively. Independent-samples t-tests were performed to compare item scores between groups. Items 4–6, 8, 36, and 38 had t values < 3, and items 5, 6, 8, and 36 showed no statistically significant differences between groups (P > 0.05), suggesting limited discriminative power.

Items total correlations were calculated to assess item representativeness. Correlations > 0.40 were considered strong, values between 0.30 and 0.40 acceptable, and correlations < 0.30 indicated poor performance. In this study, items 1, 3–9, 12, and 35–39 showed correlations < 0.30, and items 4, 5, and 8 were not significantly associated with the total score (P > 0.05), suggesting inadequate representativeness.

The overall Cronbach’s α of the preliminary scale was 0.892, indicating good internal consistency. “α if item deleted” analysis showed that removing items 8 and 36 increased the total α value, suggesting that these items may negatively affect overall reliability.

Based on the combined results of the four evaluation methods, items that failed to meet at least two statistical criteria (items 4–6, 8, 36, and 38) were removed. The revised scale therefore retained 33 items for subsequent factor analysis.

Exploratory factor analysis

EFA was conducted to examine the underlying factor structure of the MLSGT-T2DM and to assess its construct validity. Principal component extraction with oblique rotation was employed, as correlations among factors were theoretically anticipated and the primary aim was to derive an interpretable factor structure for scale development.

In the initial analysis, the Kaiser–Meyer–Olkin (KMO) measure was 0.868, and Bartlett’s test of sphericity was significant (P < 0.001), indicating the adequacy of the data for factor analysis. Parallel analysis was used as an important reference for determining the number of factors and suggested a three-factor solution. However, factor retention decisions were ultimately based on a comprehensive evaluation of multiple criteria, including eigenvalues, factor loading magnitude and pattern, and theoretical interpretability.

Although ten factors with eigenvalues greater than one were initially extracted, item-level examination identified several psychometric concerns. Specifically, items 7, 16, and 21 exhibited factor loadings below 0.40; items 18, 35, 37, and 39 failed to load onto their theoretically hypothesized dimensions; and items 10 and 17 showed substantial cross-loadings. Consequently, items 7, 16, 18, 21, 35, 37, and 39 were removed, whereas items 10 and 17 were provisionally retained for further evaluation.

A second round of EFA was then performed on the remaining 26 items, yielding a KMO value of 0.867. Seven factors with eigenvalues greater than one were extracted. Inspection of the rotated factor matrix indicated that items 13 and 17 were inconsistent with the theoretical framework, and item 1 demonstrated cross-loadings. Given that the loading difference for item 1 exceeded 0.20, only items 13 and 17 were removed prior to the subsequent analysis.

In the final EFA of the 24-item scale, the KMO value remained high (0.864), and Bartlett’s test of sphericity was significant (χ² = 3542.510, P < 0.001), confirming sampling adequacy. A six-factor solution was ultimately retained, as it demonstrated clearer factor loadings, fewer cross-loadings, and superior conceptual interpretability compared with more parsimonious alternatives. These six factors jointly explained 62.899% of the total variance (Table 2). The resulting six-factor solution showed a clear structure and stable item clustering within each dimension.

Confirmatory factor analysis

CFA was subsequently conducted on the second dataset (n = 276) to validate the six-factor structure identified through EFA. The model fit indices indicated an acceptable fit to the data (χ²/df = 2.264, CFI = 0.924, TLI = 0.914, RMSEA = 0.068, SRMR = 0.056). All standardized factor loadings were statistically significant (P < 0.001) and exceeded 0.50 (Fig. 1). No modifications based on modification indices were applied; all model specifications strictly followed the hypothesized IMB-based factor structure to preserve theoretical consistency.

The square roots of AVE for each dimension (Information = 0.726, Motivation = 0.741, and Behavioral Skills = 0.797) were all greater than the corresponding inter-factor correlations. Specifically, inter-factor correlations ranged from 0.440 to 0.614, which were consistently lower than the square roots of AVE for each construct. These findings indicate satisfactory discriminant validity among the three dimensions of the MLSGT-T2DM (Table 4).

Reliability analysis

The internal consistency reliability of the final 24-item scale was assessed using Cronbach’ s α and McDonald’ s ω coefficients. The total scale demonstrated excellent reliability, with Cronbach’ s α = 0.902 and McDonald’s ω = 0.904. The three dimensions also showed acceptable to excellent internal consistency, with Cronbach’s α values of 0.756, 0.739, and 0.906, and McDonald’s ω values of 0.746, 0.742, and 0.900, respectively. The split-half reliability was 0.766 for the total scale and 0.724, 0.736, and 0.769 for the three dimensions, respectively.

Test–retest reliability of the MLSGT-T2DM was assessed in 40 participants over a 14-day interval using the intraclass correlation coefficient (ICC, two-way mixed-effects model). The ICC was 0.898 (95% CI: 0.808, 0.946) for the total scale and 0.757 (95% CI: 0.399, 0.888), 0.801 (95% CI: 0.595, 0.899), and 0.859 (95% CI: 0.734, 0.925) for the information, motivation, and behavioral skills dimensions, respectively (Table 5).

Floor and ceiling effects

A floor or ceiling effect was defined as more than 15% of participants achieving the minimum or maximum possible score, respectively. No floor or ceiling effects were observed for the total scale or for the motivation and behavioral skills dimensions. In contrast, the information dimension showed a ceiling effect, with 29.5% of participants achieving the maximum score, exceeding the predefined threshold. Only 3.3% of participants scored the minimum, indicating no floor effect (Table 6).

Discussion

This study developed and validated the MLSGT-T2DM, a multidimensional instrument designed to assess medication literacy among Chinese adults with T2DM. Grounded in the IMB model, the scale captures essential cognitive, motivational, and behavioral skill components related to medication use. The final 24-item, six-factor structure demonstrated generally sound psychometric performance.

Although the Kendall’s coefficients of concordance were modest, all values reached statistical significance, indicating a meaningful level of overall agreement. The moderate level of concordance may be attributed to the multidisciplinary composition of the expert panel, as experts from nursing, endocrinology, pharmacy, and chronic disease management may prioritize different aspects of medication literacy, such as clinical applicability, patient education, or medication safety. Divergent opinions were addressed through iterative discussion and consensus-building guided by predefined quantitative decision criteria, thereby maintaining both methodological rigor and theoretical coherence.

The reliability analysis indicated that the MLSGT-T2DM exhibits strong internal consistency and satisfactory temporal stability, supporting the robustness of the scale. In addition, the results of both exploratory and confirmatory factor analyses provided empirical evidence for the proposed six-factor structure and its conceptual alignment with the IMB framework. The IMB dimensions were modeled as correlated but independent first-order latent constructs, consistent with the IMB framework’s conceptualization of information, motivation, and behavioral skills as functionally distinct yet interrelated components. This approach provides a theoretically faithful representation of the IMB framework and allows for dimension-specific interpretation, facilitating identification of differential patterns across information, motivation, and behavioral skills that can guide targeted clinical or educational interventions [32].

Beyond its psychometric performance, the MLSGT-T2DM is grounded in a construct that is pertinent to diabetes self-management. Existing literature suggests that medication- and health-related literacy are associated with key self-care behaviors, including medication adherence, dietary management, blood glucose monitoring, and problem-solving skills, as well as glycaemic outcomes such as HbA1c levels and hypoglycaemic events [33]. Taken together, this body of evidence highlights the importance of medication literacy in diabetes care and provides a relevant clinical context for the application of the MLSGT-T2DM. The information dimension exhibited a ceiling effect, likely due to participants’ prior diabetes education and the relatively basic nature of the items, which may limit its ability to differentiate among individuals with high medication knowledge.

Collectively, these findings suggest that the MLSGT-T2DM represents a theoretically informed and psychometrically supported instrument for assessing medication literacy among individuals with T2DM, and may be useful for informing tailored patient education and intervention strategies in both clinical and research settings.

Comparison with existing instruments

From an application perspective, the MLSGT-T2DM was compared with existing medication literacy instruments. Most tools currently used in China and internationally, such as the MedLitRxSE [34] and MLQ [35], are general literacy measures that primarily assess functional skills. Others focus on narrow constructs, such as medication beliefs [36]. Although these instruments have demonstrated utility, they share several limitations: they frequently evaluate only a single dimension (e.g., knowledge, reading, or numeracy), overlook deeper components such as motivation and behavioral skills, and lack disease-specific relevance. The MLSGT-T2DM addresses these gaps by systematically applying the IMB framework to evaluate medication literacy among Chinese adults with T2DM. This approach enables the scale to move beyond traditional assessments of functional literacy and establish an integrated evaluation system encompassing information, motivation, and behavioral skills.

Regarding disease specificity, although several diabetes-related tools have recently been developed in China, such as scales assessing insulin medication literacy [37], these instruments typically target a single therapy. In contrast, the MLSGT-T2DM covers both oral and injectable glucose-lowering medications, making it applicable to a broader range of patients with T2DM.

In terms of cultural adaptability, the item pool of the MLSGT-T2DM was initially derived from a comprehensive literature review and existing instruments, and then refined through qualitative patient interviews, Delphi consultation, and cognitive interviews in mainland China.The language, clinical scenarios, and value orientations embedded in the scale closely align with local cultural contexts. This development strategy avoids the cultural mismatches common in directly translated instruments [38] or scales primarily validated in Taiwan [39], thereby ensuring the scale’s clarity and practical applicability in mainland Chinese clinical settings.

Interpretation of the factor structure

Information dimension

Within the information dimension, two distinct factors emerged: disease knowledge and medication knowledge. The disease knowledge factor captures patients’ macro-level understanding of diabetes, including glycaemic targets, principles of individualized treatment, and broader management goals. It reflects the why of treatment, which is patients’ comprehension of the overall purpose and value of diabetes management. In contrast, the medication knowledge factor reflects the how of daily therapy, encompassing practical information such as managing adverse effects, correct medication storage, drug–drug interactions, and the need for regular follow-up.

The separation of these factors suggests that patients do not view diabetes knowledge as a single construct but as two complementary aspects [40]. A patient may understand glycaemic targets yet lack skills to manage hypoglycaemia or store medications properly, or vice versa. This divergence has direct clinical relevance: limited disease knowledge may lead to misunderstandings of treatment plans and unrealistic expectations [41], whereas insufficient medication knowledge may contribute to medication errors and improper management of adverse effects, increasing safety risks [4].

Motivation dimension

This dimension encompasses patients’ perceived severity of diabetes, beliefs about the necessity of pharmacotherapy, perceptions of non-pharmacological treatments, and their overall evaluation of treatment benefits and risks. Among Chinese adults with T2DM, medication motivation is not driven by a single determinant but reflects a dynamic, holistic psychological decision-making process. Patients’ willingness to initiate or maintain medication use represents an integrated judgment shaped by multiple attitudinal components, including their understanding of disease seriousness, confidence in medication efficacy, perceived capability for self-management, and concerns about potential adverse effects [42, 43]. For instance, patients who strongly believe that lifestyle modification alone is sufficient for glycemic control, or who have heightened fears regarding medication side effects, may exhibit poor long-term motivation and adherence—even when they possess adequate medication knowledge [44].

Behavioral skills dimension

The behavioral skills dimension yielded three factors. The first factor, information seeking and appraisal, reflects patients’ ability to actively manage medication-related information. This includes proactively seeking updates, monitoring treatment progress, participating in health education, and critically evaluating information from various sources. In an era of information overload, this information literacy safeguards against misinformation and ensures evidence-based decisions [45]. The second factor, communication and comprehension, underpins effective patient-provider interaction. It involves understanding medication regimens, including timing, dose adjustments, and combination therapy, and seeking clarification when needed. Deficiencies in this area are linked to medication errors, especially in patients with complex conditions or those on frequent regimen adjustments [46, 47]. The third factor, self-monitoring and calculation, captures operational skills required for safe medication use, such as monitoring adverse effects, addressing dosing errors, and adjusting medications based on glucose readings. These skills are crucial for patients on high-risk medications like insulin, where precise self-management is essential to prevent severe events like hypoglycemia [48]. Importantly, such self-management extends beyond medication administration to everyday contexts, as daily food-related decisions can substantially affect postprandial glycaemic responses [49], This underscores the importance of applying medication-related information in real-life situations rather than relying solely on isolated clinical guidance.

The five scenario-based items initially included were removed during item analysis and exploratory factor analysis. This does not diminish the value of scenario-based assessments, but rather suggests that such items may tap into complex cognitive processes not fully aligned with the IMB model’s conceptualization of behavioral skills [50]. Future studies could explore these tasks as standalone performance assessments rather than part of a self-report scale.

Public health and clinical implications

Medication literacy is particularly critical in T2DM, where pharmacological therapy must be continuously integrated with daily dietary choices that directly influence glycaemic control. Existing evidence suggests that medication literacy is closely linked to key aspects of diabetes management, including self-care behaviors and glycemic outcomes [51]. Patients’ understanding of medication timing, dose adjustment, and potential interactions with meals plays a central role in achieving stable glycaemic outcomes [52]. The MLSGT-T2DM offers a structured and theory-based framework for identifying patients with inadequate literacy related to glucose-lowering medications. In primary care settings, the scale can help clinicians pinpoint specific gaps in information, motivation, or behavioral skills that affect medication use, enabling more targeted education, counseling, and follow-up. For example, low scores in the information domain may indicate the need for structured medication education, whereas deficits in motivation may call for counseling to address treatment beliefs and concerns. Low behavioral skills scores may suggest the need for skills-based training or enhanced follow-up support. By shifting from general diabetes education to individualized, needs-based support, the tool may contribute to improved treatment understanding and safer medication management in routine clinical care.

In addition, its multidimensional behavioral skills framework offers actionable guidance for designing community-based diabetes education programs and pharmacist-led interventions aimed at enhancing safe use of glucose-lowering medications, strengthening patient engagement, and reducing preventable therapy-related problems. The MLSGT-T2DM may therefore support ongoing efforts to improve chronic disease management within community and primary care systems.

Strengths and limitations

Several limitations of this study should be acknowledged. First, participants were recruited primarily from urban hospitals and community health centers in southern China, which may limit the generalizability of the findings to rural settings or populations with different patterns of healthcare access. Second, criterion validity was not examined because no validated gold-standard instrument for assessing diabetes-specific medication literacy is currently available. Third, the MLSGT-T2DM comprises a mixture of dichotomous knowledge items and ordinal Likert-type items. In the present study, Pearson correlations were used in exploratory factor analysis, and maximum likelihood estimation was applied in confirmatory factor analysis, without employing analytic methods specifically designed for mixed or categorical data. As a result, factor loadings and model fit indices may have been somewhat biased. Future research is therefore encouraged to apply polychoric or tetrachoric correlation matrices in EFA and robust estimators suitable for categorical indicators (e.g., WLSMV) in CFA to further strengthen the psychometric evidence.

Conclusion

The MLSGT-T2DM is a valid and reliable instrument for assessing medication literacy among adults with T2DM. Its multidimensional structure reflects the cognitive, motivational, and behavioral competencies required for safe medication use and offers important guidance for individualized patient education and public health interventions. The scale has the potential to improve diabetes management by identifying literacy gaps and informing more effective, targeted strategies to support long-term medication adherence.
