What's in a Name? Psychiatric Concept Creep and the Moral Legibility of Student Suffering within the Canadian University Context
Authors: Adrianna Nicole Wiley
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractThis article explores how students experiencing mental unwellness negotiate psychiatric constructs of mental health to make their suffering morally legible within the North American University context. I argue while the psychiatric construct remains pervasive, students are ambivalent toward it as a metaphor for their distress. Students engage in bottom‐up psychiatrization pragmatically to navigate relational contexts when appropriate for them, while recognizing that the impacts of top‐down psychiatrization can limit institutional moral care obligations, constraining their authority to communicate their subjective experiences authentically outside psychiatric idioms. In essence, psychiatric labeling is constraining, but students often exert agency by refusing a psychiatric master status while still recognizing their need to use psychiatric labels to allow their suffering to be seen and addressed within the current cultural‐historical moment of psychiatrization.

INTRODUCTION

“My friends have been struggling a lot and … their professors are not understanding of how they feel. [Faculty] tend to be very skeptical of a student who claims to be struggling with mental health. And we see that on our course outlines; they always talk about in case you miss an assignment, in case you have to miss a quiz, or anything like that, you need a doctor's note” (Ricky, upper‐year undergraduate without a diagnosis).

Over the past two decades student mental suffering has come to be defined as a “crisis” across North American campuses (Gordon, 2024; Wilson, 2025). This perception arises from interdisciplinary research and activism suggesting higher than ever rates of diagnosable experiences of mental distress in student populations (Council of Ontario Universities, 2017; P. Rosenbaum & Weatherford, 2018). While the university is not traditionally constructed as a medicalized setting, its role as in loco parentis comes with a moral duty of care. As society becomes more sensitive to mental distress as a form of suffering (Haslam, 2016), an inability to maintain student mental health is viewed by stakeholders as a moral failing of the university. Universities tend to fulfil this latest care obligation through psychiatric solutions (e.g. on‐campus counselling, and insurance coverage for psychiatric treatment), allowing them to define student distress as something individual and medical, rather than acknowledging complicities in structural causes of suffering such as ableism, racism, and patriarchy (Aubrecht, 2014; Gordon, 2024; Landry, 2023; Singh & Vora, 2023). However, anthropological research suggests many students reject official psychiatric care options in favor of building more personally meaningful self‐care journeys that incorporate a recognition of the structural factors central to their understandings of distress (Gordon, 2024, 2025b, 2025a; Landry, 2023). Further, when surveyed, students self‐report experiences associated with diagnosable mental disorders at much higher rates than they report professional diagnoses (Gordon, 2024; Linden et al., 2018). Nonetheless, other research suggests mental distress is also a form of “cultural capital” in university spaces, providing proof that one is meeting the expectations of the neoliberalized university regarding hyperproductivity (Armstrong et al., 2023; Wilson, 2025; Wiley, 2023). This suggests a complex relationship between student experience and the expanding use of psychiatric constructs to describe experiences of mental distress.

In the university, a psychiatric diagnosis is typically required to receive accommodations for mental distress (Condra et al., 2015; University of Guelph 2016). Several works have explored how the university leverages psy‐language to consolidate power and exert authority over student mental life (Aubrecht, 2014; P. J. Rosenbaum & Liebert, 2015). I build on previous work on psychiatric distress as “cultural capital” within the neoliberalizing university (Armstrong et al., 2023; Wilson, 2025) to explore the increasing use of psychiatric terminology within this space as an evolution of power relationships wherein psychiatric terms are used by students as a “tool” for soliciting care, rendering suffering “bureaucratically legible” (Armstrong et al., 2023), even as these terms constrain authentic expressions of suffering. I argue psychiatric expression of distress becomes “ambivalent” to students; the use of psychiatric terminology has simultaneous contradictory meanings. While this language serves important functions, it also packages suffering into the neat box of biological disfunction (Rose, 2007) disregarding ultimate structural causes and authentic, subjective needs (Gordon, 2024, 2025b).

PSYCHIATRIZATION

Within neoliberal society psychiatric language has explanatory power (Armstrong et al., 2023; Grinker, 2007; Lester, 2011; Luhrmann and Marrow, 2016). To overcome institutionalized skepticism, students are asked to suffer in a socially sanctioned manner by seeking translation of their suffering by a medical professional into a psychiatric diagnosis. Psychiatrization is the relatively recent phenomenon in which the knowledges, institutions, and practices of psychiatry have increased in importance within the social imagination (Beeker et al., 2021). This process involves redefining disturbances to well‐being as pathological using a psychiatric lens, and the increasing use of psychiatric idioms in everyday language, known as “concept creep” (Beeker et al., 2021; Haslam, 2016; Haslam et al., 2021). Broadly, “concept creep” is the process whereby harm‐related concepts experience semantic inflation such that they refer to an increasingly broad range of phenomena (Haslam, 2016). While concept creep is generally understood to be symptomatic of a society increasingly sensitive to various forms of harm, this creeping of psychiatric terms often neglects the sociopolitical contexts in which unwellness occurs, pathologizing human experiences through biological determinism (Adrianna N. Wiley, 2023; Adrianna Nicole Wiley, 2024; Beeker et al., 2021; Haslam et al., 2021). Thus, there exists a tension between the use of psychiatric terms to translate suffering into something culturally legible, and the use of these terms to obscure subjective experiences, and any ultimate, sociopolitical causes of this suffering (Rose, 2007). Previous anthropological critiques of the psychiatric construct of mental health have outlined how the decontextualization of expressions of distress obscure patterns of structural violence (Scheper‐Hughes & Lock, 1986), and create mismatches between the care/accommodations associated with a given diagnosis and an individual's subjective needs (Grinker, 2007). As prevalence of psychiatric diagnosis increases (Kessler et al., 2009), it becomes increasingly difficult to distinguish between when psychiatrization is beneficial in meeting previously unmet needs and when it has created artificial needs for pharmaceutical profit or control of newly defined forms of “deviance” (Beeker et al., 2021). Further, the skepticism of those in power reinforces the biopower of diagnostic labeling by categorizing un‐psychiatrically labeled suffering as invalid.

Psychiatrization may operate top‐down as psychiatry's reach widens, and professionals/institutions impose diagnostic terms on sufferers. However, psychiatrization may also operate bottom‐up, whereby suffering individuals adopt psychiatric terminology as they seek recognition for subjective suffering in a socially sanctioned manner (Beeker et al., 2021; Haslam et al., 2021). I expand on previous conceptions of psychiatrization, to reveal the tensions between these two directionalities. In the university setting, top‐down psychiatrization of student mental life limits the domains of suffering for which the university is morally responsible. Students engage in bottom‐up psychiatrization of their own experiences in order to validate their suffering in this context, but experience ambivalence with regards to the psychiatric meaning of their suffering, which they recognize as a constraint on personal identity.

As contradictions arise in how people construct their mental unwellness, and they attribute nonclinical meaning to psychiatric terms, understanding these contradictions as meaningful, rather than obstructive, becomes important. Individuals with mental unwellness regularly perturb psychiatric labels assigned to them by mocking and/or reframing them (Martin, 2007). Rejection, subversion, or reclamation of psychiatric terms are all possible responses suffering individuals may have to psychiatrization of society. However, individuals may also use diagnostic terms to communicate their experiences with others while still having alternative or supplemental understandings of these experiences in nonpsychiatric terms (Bindhulakshmi, 2012). The students I spoke to participate in bottom‐up psychiatrization by appearing to accept a diagnosis while negotiating the precise meaning and communication of this label, and being selective in the times and spaces of its use (Callard, 2014).

In this study of Canadian university students’ subjective experiences of mental unwellness, students use psychiatric constructs pragmatically to navigate relational contexts when appropriate for them, while negotiating the limits psychiatric labels place on their authority to communicate their subjective experiences authentically. I use a medical anthropology framework which views subjective mental unwellness as an illness experience distinct from, though potentially interactional with, biological disease processes (Helman, 1981; Kleinman, 1978, 2013; Kleinman et al., 1978). By studying how students negotiate experiences of mental unwellness within a nonmedicalized setting we can understand mental health more fully as a human experience (Adrianna Nicole Wiley, 2024) that does not necessarily connote a patient status. Existing in the university environment, these students’ experiences provide unique insights into the operation of psychiatrization in their daily lives in a traditionally nonmedicalized setting as they choose to engage with, or resist, this process in various situations (Beeker et al., 2021).

ETHNOGRAPHIC CONTEXT

The University1 is a comprehensive university2 known for its school of veterinary medicine, and diverse programs in agriculture and environmental sciences. While students often live, work, and play throughout the small city, the University itself is a clearly delineated campus environment with infrastructure clustered in one part of the city. Demographically, 60% of students are women (University Factbook 2022). In 2021 the University had a student enrollment of 29290; 92.2% coming from Ontario, and 4.7% being international students (University Factbook 2022). While only 49.5% of courses have enrollments over 100, student participants mainly had courses of 250–600 students (University Factbook 2022). Tuition, including fees for academic, health, and wellbeing services, for domestic undergraduates entering the University in 2021 ranged from $6091 per year for Arts & Science programs to $11,286 for Engineering, while tuition for international students ranged from $28,334 per year for Arts & Science programs to $73,721 for Business Administration (tuition in CAD; University Factbook 2022). Participants describe professors as kind and supportive. While some professors, particularly in more competitive programs like veterinary medicine, had negative attitudes toward mental unwellness and were not accommodating, most were described as supporting student wellness through voluntary accommodation when students requested.

Students feel mental health is openly discussed by both students and professors on campus; however, experiences like anxiety and depression were less stigmatized than experiences associated with labels like obsessive compulsive disorder (OCD). Participants feel there is a lack of awareness about available campus resources, but also identify many on‐campus supports including: formal peer‐to‐peer support programs; sexual violence services; group therapy (e.g. men's group, social anxiety group etc.); stress‐busting events (e.g. therapy dogs); gym; student clubs; a wellness line; academic‐stress related workshops; and student accommodation services. Students also identify on‐campus counselling as an available resource, but most view it negatively. They feel there are not enough counselors, visits are too short and infrequent, counselors are not sufficiently knowledgeable, and their concerns are sometimes invalidated. Off‐campus private counselling is also available and is partially covered by student fees. Some students identified this as the best option but noted barriers to access including distance from campus and that coverage ran out quickly as private counselors often charge upwards of $125 CAD per hour of therapy.

Reflective of the general demographics of the university, most of my participants are White (or white‐passing) feminine‐presenting persons, though perspectives from people of color, and diverse masculine‐presenting individuals are included (see Table S1 for a profile of disclosable student identity categories). While students had the opportunity to discuss their identities in reference to their mental health if they felt these factors were important to their experiences, the interview questions themselves did not ask about this directly. I focus here on themes that crosscut identity categories; however, the lack of broad representation presents a limitation and the impact of intersectional identities on ambivalence and perceived usefulness of psychiatric terms are not addressed.

All participants self‐identified as experiencing “mental health struggles,” but only 13 of the 24 participants (54%) had a diagnosis, while 11 did not (47%). Two participants without a diagnosis were in the process of gaining one, and a third had an “unofficial” diagnosis from a counsellor (rather than medical professional). Similarly, two of the participants with a diagnosis were unsure of the officialdom of their diagnosis either because they did not know the qualifications of their diagnostician or because they did not believe a 30‐minute medical appointment was long enough for an appropriate evaluation.

Over summer 2021, I conducted semi‐structured interviews with 24 UofG students who were between 18 and 24 years old via online conferencing software, as the ongoing COVID‐19 pandemic made it unsafe to meet in‐person. Participants provided oral consent which was recorded in interview transcripts, and all data was deidentified using pseudonyms selected by the individual participants. While there are barriers to virtual rapport building, certain aspects of my positionality allow some mitigation. Both my participants and I are digital natives; we grew up with virtual technologies and came of age with social media. Virtual spaces, while still presenting challenges in terms of, for example, interpreting body language, are not unknown to us as places where relationships are crafted. Additionally, I grew up near the university and UofG students have long been my coaches, mentors, and neighbors. Lastly, when I conducted these interviews I was only a year out of my own undergraduate degree at a similar institution also in southwestern Ontario. Like other insider anthropologists who have studied mental health following their own struggles (Martin, 2007; Nakamura, 2013), disclosing my own experiences as a student experiencing mental unwellness helped me build rapport with participants in a short time. This made them feel more comfortable with their own vulnerability and created a trust that I could understand their stories empathetically.

Interview transcripts were analyzed in NVIVO using line‐by‐line coding to generate substantive codes which were then subjected to thematic analysis (Oktay, 2012; see Wiley, 2022 for full coding methodology). A psychiatric construct is here defined as either a narrative account which understands experiences of mental unwellness as arising from biological processes readily addressed with pharmaceutical medication, or accounts which rely on diagnostic language derived from the American Psychiatric Association's Diagnostic and Statistical Manual (DSM). I asked participants about their experiences with mental wellness within the university context to collect data on what constructs they use to describe these experiences. I also asked about the interactions between university‐life, mental health, and student identity to understand how mental unwellness impacts self‐perception. Additional questions asked about student relationships to diagnostic labels, which labels best captured their experiences, and which were most useful to them. The interview concluded by asking about any insights participants have gained from their experiences (for full interview guide see Supplementary Materials).

LEGIBILITY OF STUDENT SUFFERING

Moral obligations of care and deservingness

“It's important that they have that medical diagnosis to help them get what they need, but I also don't like that it puts labels on people, cause ʻit's definitely a spectrum, mental health. So, drawing a line, it's like anybody who's 0.5 mm on this side is worth it, but 0.5 on this side is not … drawing a line and saying okay, well, you're not that bad, kind of diminishes the whole purpose of [destigmatizing] mental health” (Daniella, a fourth‐year undergraduate with a diagnosis).

Psychiatrization in general constrains the “moral circle” of individuals seen as deserving of care, requiring engagement with a psychiatric construct even for those individuals who may not actually view their own experiences through this lens (Beeker et al., 2021). Regardless of diagnostic status, students who struggled with mental health saw gaining and organizing appropriate support as an important outcome and key benefit of psychiatric labeling. James, a fourth‐year undergraduate with a diagnosis, explains that finding a diagnosis allows one “…to deal with your issues and get on the correct medication if necessary and get the correct help. Like when I was diagnosed with anxiety it was like okay, yes, I have anxiety, let's see how to fix this and see how to change my thinking.” Kary, a first‐year master's student with a diagnosis, notes the value of diagnosis for help‐seeking while observing this benefit is situational: “I don't think a diagnosis has any benefit unless you're providing services to help them deal with it. I'm not like completely anti‐diagnosis all the time, but I think that psychologists should be more careful about just throwing out diagnoses immediately to everyone. I think you really need to weigh the pros and cons with each person”.

“I don't think a diagnosis has any benefit unless you're providing services to help them deal with it. I'm not like completely anti‐diagnosis all the time, but I think that psychologists should be more careful about just throwing out diagnoses immediately to everyone. I think you really need to weigh the pros and cons with each person”.

The university policy on “Academic Accommodations for Students with Disabilities” necessitates the use of the psychiatric construct to gain accommodations by requiring students provide documentation of a diagnosis or “assessment of functional limitations” from a medical doctor. For this reason, Participant 12, a fifth‐year undergraduate with a diagnosis, identified the university context as the only place a psychiatric construct felt useful for her: “When I talk about mental health with friends and such I just say how I'm feeling as opposed to “this is my anxiety.” Maybe because it's just easier to say where these feelings are stemming from and coming from over referring to it by its actual diagnosis. And other than with university, in other parts of my life, I don't usually have the need to bring up my anxiety for things like work or activities. So honestly, other than the fact of school, I'm pretty neutral on the stance of where the actual term and diagnosis stands.”

Marie, an undergraduate with a diagnosis, explains she also found a psychiatric construct helpful “… at least for school, because I was able to put myself on the accommodations list, and, I guess, finding a therapist and being able to say ‘yeah, this is kind of what I want to work on’ that's probably, good.” As such, psychiatric labels may demonstrate to institutions and others a need for support/services. Students who are experiencing a negative sense of wellbeing, and thus are in need of care, may actively perpetuate psychiatric concept creep in order to fit into a moral framework where their suffering is recognized by the institution as deserving of care. Here psychiatrization might be seen not as a symptom of a society with an expanded sense of harm, but rather as a symptom of a society with a contracted sense of care obligations (Gordon, 2025b; Haslam, 2016; Haslam et al., 2021).

Using psychiatric terminology can transform experiences of suffering into a “thing” which may be more legible to a neoliberal institution and those who are enmeshed in these value systems. As Bella, a third‐year undergraduate without a diagnosis for her mental unwellness, explains: “I tend to just use the proper diagnoses [to talk about my struggles], even though sometimes I do feel uncomfortable since I have not been ‘properly’ diagnosed. However, I find that putting words to it that are generally known and accepted does help people to understand what I'm going through a lot more quickly than just saying here's a list of symptoms…”

However, I find that putting words to it that are generally known and accepted does help people to understand what I'm going through a lot more quickly than just saying here's a list of symptoms…”

Similarly, Maanaav, a first‐year master's student without a diagnosis, felt that, “For clinicians, having that label's super useful in the treatment, as well as in actually identifying symptoms.” Beyond clinicians, Racheal, an upper‐year undergraduate who does not have a formal diagnosis but works on issues related to anxiety and OCD with her therapist, also felt psychiatric terminology was simply the most effective way to communicate her struggles to people in her life, such as friends and family: “I think the biggest one I would use is just saying anxiety … I think it's sort of the one that people … understand the most. So, I feel like if I say [anxiety] I don't need to quantify or explain it as much … especially as a student, living in these times, I think people can easily understand where, by and large, anxiety comes from.”

“I think the biggest one I would use is just saying anxiety … I think it's sort of the one that people … understand the most. So, I feel like if I say [anxiety] I don't need to quantify or explain it as much … especially as a student, living in these times, I think people can easily understand where, by and large, anxiety comes from.”

However, it is important to note that Racheal specifically chooses “anxiety” as opposed to “OCD,” to communicate her struggles because she feels the way the lay public understands the term “anxiety” more closely approximates her experiences than the way they may understand the term “OCD”. As Callard (2014) argues, diagnoses and an individual's relationships to these labels are contextually dependent and are mediated by both what diagnostic label is used, and the spatial‐temporal space in which it is applied. Thus, while these communications can serve to make suffering more legible to others, they are still nuanced and negotiated with the understanding that not all biomedical terms hold the same level or accuracy of explanatory power.

By making student suffering legible, psychiatric terminology provides external validation of subjective suffering; validation that student experiences of unwellness are real and deserving of compassion and concern. Psychiatric terminology makes suffering legible not only to university policy, but also to the sufferer themselves within their cultural milieu. Daniella, a fourth‐year undergraduate, explains that she likes having a diagnosis: “Definitely just for verification of how you're feeling, right? I feel like everybody looks for verification on how they're feeling. Some super‐evolved humans can verify themselves and have that validation, inside themselves, but I'm one of those people, I need external validation”.

“Definitely just for verification of how you're feeling, right? I feel like everybody looks for verification on how they're feeling. Some super‐evolved humans can verify themselves and have that validation, inside themselves, but I'm one of those people, I need external validation”.

Similarly, Melanie, a first‐year master's student with a diagnosis, felt better understanding her struggles as an “objective disease,” explaining “I think initially [a diagnosis] was validation that there's actually something wrong with me, and I'm not crazy, because it felt that way a little bit initially.”

Sammie, a second‐year undergraduate with a diagnosis, found the validation associated with a diagnosis helped her with self‐compassion and reinforced other benefits of psychiatric naming: “Getting a diagnosis and learning it was so much more than [the stereotypical idea of OCD] really changed my view on it, and also made me a lot nicer to myself. ‘Cause I was like, this isn't necessarily your fault. You're not being overly sensitive or weird about this. This is just how your brain is wired…I think it feels really good to get a diagnosis. I think it's really validating and if you do that then you can get any medication you need, any treatment you need.”

This validation works to make student suffering legible to structures of support within a neoliberal context in part by shifting the blame from student mindset or “willpower” (Rose, 2007), to instead identify these struggles as an “actual health issue” or biological “disease.” Naming one's mental unwellness psychiatrically has the power to situate blame for suffering in the random events of biology, rather than as a failure of individual morality (Bergey, 2017; Bracken & Thomas, 2017; Grinker, 2007; Luhrmann, 2000). Writing about how psychiatrists construct mental illnesses, Luhrmann (2000) discusses how the mind‐body dichotomy prominent in Western medical philosophies lends a moral tint to constructs of mental illness. Whereas the mind is seen as under the control of the individual, the body is innocent—genetics are not considered the fault of the individual themself—and thus if mental illnesses arise from biological causes then they are of the body rather than of the mind and the individual is blameless (Luhrmann, 2000). This conception of mental health allows students, and the university, to absolve themselves from blame for these struggles, and this understanding of psychiatric self‐labeling typically fosters self‐compassion. Bella, explains: “I tend to frame my struggles in my own mindset and the way that I say them, as this is just an actual health issue. This is something that I don't have any control over, I've got asthma as well, I'll just frame it like that…I was born with this. This is something that I deal with. This is something that yeah, I need medication for, yeah, I need some help with. But it's not something that I have chosen to do… viewing it on my own as just a medical issue has really helped me get through it.”

In this way, psychiatric naming of mental unwellness works to make experiences of suffering legible by validating these experiences as something “real.” Killingsworth and colleagues (2010) discuss how Vietnamese–Australian women in their study experienced diagnosis as a process of “becoming‐cared for.” Instead of being fit into narratives of personal weakness these women began to understand themselves as struggling with something “real” which was deserving of care. Validation worked similarly in this study. As students’ engaged in bottom‐up psychiatrization, they changed their perspective on mental unwellness, reframing it biomedically, thus moving the locus of suffering from their masterable minds to their blameless bodies, such that they could view their suffering as a serious issue deserving of care rather than a personal failing resulting from unreasonable “sensitivity” or “dramatics.”

Power and authenticity

Nonetheless, this validation was not necessarily accessible to everyone. For some participants psychiatric terms feel like inauthentic “exaggerations” compared to their lived experiences, and the persistent psychiatrization of suffering within the university leads them to minimize their struggles. Racheal initially felt her experiences were not severe enough to be considered a mental disorder. As she explains “I had this idea of mental health, like anxiety, depression neither of that is me, it's sort of like that's a very serious condition and people that are depressed or have anxiety, it's a big thing.” A psychiatric construct and diagnosis felt too serious to students who just experience mental unwellness as a “normal” part of their daily lives, something they may wish to change or improve, but not as something necessarily pathological. Similarly, while Jeff, an upper‐year undergraduate without a diagnosis, uses psychiatric terminology to communicate his experiences, he says it has never really been great at describing his struggles: “I've always found it difficult to fully express the extent of something I'm going through. A lot of times I feel like I fell back on terms like depression or just mental illness in general…You can kind of narrow it down to simpler terms, but I feel like for me the biggest disconnect would be just not being able to fully express everything and kind of capture the true feeling in what I've experienced.”

“I've always found it difficult to fully express the extent of something I'm going through. A lot of times I feel like I fell back on terms like depression or just mental illness in general…You can kind of narrow it down to simpler terms, but I feel like for me the biggest disconnect would be just not being able to fully express everything and kind of capture the true feeling in what I've experienced.”

Some students suggest the hegemony of psychiatric terms often led them to minimize and invalidate their own struggles. These students avoid concept creep but bear the costs of suffering in a manner not viewed as a moral care obligation by the university. By understanding mental unwellness as the result of a biological pathology which must be removed, the sufferer's subjectivity and the diverse contexts of symptom manifestation are erased (Gordon, 2025b; P. J. Rosenbaum & Liebert, 2015). This creates a situation where students’ diverse individual needs may not be met as their suffering is treated as a generalizable phenomenon with a universal cure, rather than arising from their unique situation.

Furthermore, many students critiqued the power of clinicians to diagnose their mental health struggles, feeling that this top‐down psychiatrization constrained their personal authority to define subjective experiences authentically. Students often felt the process of diagnosis was impersonal and cursory. They felt interpretations of their suffering and what it meant were better left to their best friends or romantic partners than to unfamiliar medical professionals. Mary‐Ann, an undergraduate student with a diagnosis, describes “I did once talk to [my therapist] about seeing for a bipolar label, I don't think that's what I have, even my best friend was saying I don't think that's what you have either.” Similarly, Sunflower, a third‐year master's student, was distressed to have her situation judged by an emergency room psychiatrist, explaining: “And then I was additionally depressed and overwhelmed and talking to this person, you meet them for the first time and you talk to them for 30‐minutes and all of a sudden they're giving you a diagnosis that, yeah, I mean I can recognize that I'm not feeling myself and I'm depressed, but even my friends and my boyfriend were like to say you have Clinical Depression, I don't know, like you're just going through a really hard time right now.”

“And then I was additionally depressed and overwhelmed and talking to this person, you meet them for the first time and you talk to them for 30‐minutes and all of a sudden they're giving you a diagnosis that, yeah, I mean I can recognize that I'm not feeling myself and I'm depressed, but even my friends and my boyfriend were like to say you have Clinical Depression, I don't know, like you're just going through a really hard time right now.”

Marie similarly had doubts about her diagnosis due to the impersonal nature of the diagnostic experience, “I don't know how it typically is, how long, how many sessions the clinician has to see the person to make a diagnosis, but when I saw somebody, it was just one session, which seems like it's probably not enough, I don't know.”

While Luhrmann (2000) has found that psychiatrists are trained to decontextualize mental unwellness, seeing diagnoses as representing something objectively present, discoverable, and masterable in the individual brain, both Sunflower and Marie critique this method, expressing a concern that without knowing their total context a clinician is not qualified to judge their situation and “name it.” Essentially, students felt they should be considered authorities of their own experience, able to express their suffering authentically to receive appropriate support but that the psychiatric model, which limits the power of defining struggles to clinicians, constrains their ability to do so as their suffering is reinterpreted through a psychiatric lens by someone unfamiliar with their total context.

Further, to make these experiences of suffering legible, the psychiatric construct of mental health tends to reify experiences into stagnant “things.” These “things” can be helpful tools facilitating communication, and providing access to resources; however, sometimes they become burdens which students have to “deal with” in addition to their actual unwellness. Students express concern that reification of experiences of mental unwellness as biological entities obscures the social causes of their discomfort and ignores their total context. They demonstrate the ways in which biological reification removes responsibility from the university structure to address issues such as toxic hyperproductivity (Adrianna N. Wiley, 2023). Students feel their suffering cannot be divorced from their total context, like the stress they are dealing with academically. While scholars have raised similar theoretical critiques of the psychiatric construct (Bergey, 2017; Goicoechea, 2013; Good, 1997; Jenkins, 2015; Scheper‐Hughes & Lock, 1986; Tseris, 2017; Young, 2001) and the psychiatrization of society (Beeker et al., 2021; Haslam, 2016; Haslam et al., 2021), these students emphasize authenticity of lived experience in their critiques.

Melanie, who began her diagnostic journey in high school, illustrates this reification process as she relates her experiences with diagnosis to “collecting stamps:” “Someone told me I had bipolar, that was my GP's diagnosis, which is funny now, looking back on it, because I don't at all meet that criteria. I also got borderline at one point…based on one interaction…[though] it didn't fit with my experience of myself. So, when I was told that, all of the sudden it was like, Oh my gosh is this what I am…it isn't consistent, but maybe I'm not seeing that. And then also, there's obsessive compulsive personality disorder, my psychiatrist that I saw for the longest time thought I had that…it was like every couple of months it would be someone throwing something new at the wall and then you'd get used to thinking about yourself that way and you'd be like oh, I guess this is what I have and this is what I'm dealing with, and then all of a sudden you see another doctor and it would be a new label. So that was really confusing…I think with the anxiety and depression, that's been useful, but it's with those other labels it's kind of like collecting stamps…like ooh I have all these things. And that kind of feels messed up.”

So that was really confusing…I think with the anxiety and depression, that's been useful, but it's with those other labels it's kind of like collecting stamps…like ooh I have all these things. And that kind of feels messed up.”

Melanie describes diagnoses as physical “things” which were “thrown at her.” If they hit her and stuck these things came to define her experience and identity psychiatrically as she began to think of herself “in that way.” Melanie felt she collected these things and carried them with her; while some of the “things” were useful tools, like the anxiety and depression labels, others misinterpreted, and thus added to, her suffering, becoming burdens she had to carry about with her.

Another metaphor used to describe this misfit between individual experiences of mental unwellness and reified psychiatric terms was the “box.” Similar to how Melanie describes herself as collecting stamps, Bella understands diagnostic categories as boxes, physical “things,” into which clinicians attempt to fit an individual's experiences: “I see a lot of people get misdiagnosed or go through many diagnoses before they actually get to the correct one, or people who have crossovers like comorbid, depression, anxiety, ADHD, all the different things. And it's tough to see it because on one side we're just taking symptoms and trying to put them into boxes and a lot of the boxes overlap. It's a very hard time trying to put everything correctly in just little boxes you're always going to have side effects leftover…”

“I see a lot of people get misdiagnosed or go through many diagnoses before they actually get to the correct one, or people who have crossovers like comorbid, depression, anxiety, ADHD, all the different things. And it's tough to see it because on one side we're just taking symptoms and trying to put them into boxes and a lot of the boxes overlap. Bella saw misdiagnosis and the medical uncertainty associated with the classification of mental disorders (see also Grinker,, 2007; ch. 5) as reflecting the mismatch between the fluidity of mental unwellness as experiences and the rigid structure of official psychiatric definitions. Turning mental unwellness experiences into “things” which were expected to fit easily into predefined boxes would always leave some aspects of an individual's suffering without validation and support as they could not be neatly explained by a simplified system of psychiatric labeling. Sunflower also uses the box metaphor, explaining: “Oh, I was also depressed, diagnosed by the psychiatrist was like “I think you're going through a major depressive episode, and I think you have clinical depression.” And I was like “That's great. I don't know what you want me to say to that but okay.” So, I don't know if it's depression, I don't know if it's how I really feel, and I don't know if it's hormones…And for me I had a hard time when she said [I had depression] because it's like I guess I don't like labels, I don't like having to be put into a box. ‘Cause it makes me feel like Oh now this is another thing I have to try to get out of and overcome when maybe I just need a little bit of support and extra help right now.”

For Sunflower the “box” of a diagnosis was experienced as something from which she must escape. Beyond simply relieving her suffering or moving through a difficult experience she now felt she also had to find a way to “de‐psychiatrize” (Beeker et al., 2021). She felt her experience was fluid and had many contributing factors such as hormones, and normal emotional reactions to academic stress and interpersonal difficulties. “Depression” was experienced as a new challenge that she was expected to overcome, whereas before diagnosis she was just trying to manage a difficult situation with support from her community and fix a hormonal problem caused by her new birth control.

Sunflower also feels that reification of her struggles through psychiatric diagnosis made them more difficult to remove because these labels manifest chronicity. By talking about herself as though she had a disease instead of as though she was simply going through a hard time which required extra support and self‐compassion, she was making herself more unwell. Jay, an upper‐year International undergraduate student with a diagnosis, also remarked that the issue with diagnosis for him, while generally validating, was that he was constantly reminded of this label throughout the healing process which he found frustrating.

As Luhrmann and Marrow (2016) found in their cross‐cultural examination of Schizophrenia, when psychiatric diagnoses are emphasized, labels felt permanent and constructed students as suffering from a chronic disease which was not always consistent with their subjective experiences. When psychiatric diagnoses are reified and presented as physical “things,” like boxes or stamps, which the individual must carry and which did not fully fit with individuals’ subjective experience, these “objects” came to feel like heavy and permanent burdens rather than “tools” helpful in the navigation of difficult situations. Although agentic participation in bottom‐up psychiatrization was often validating and brought care/support for student experiences of mental unwellness within the university, students who faced top‐down psychiatrization often experienced the opposite effect with negative impacts for their self‐identification.

Many students, including many of the same individuals who also saw psychiatric constructs as beneficial, felt that ideally one should have the authority, independent of a psychiatric label, to define one's own suffering authentically and get appropriate support regardless of whether others/society felt one's struggles were valid. Celine, an undergraduate with a diagnosis, explains: “In general, with labels I think, probably there's too much focus on them. Because, I think, if you're suffering, you know best that you're suffering, right? Why do you need some mental health professional to tell you, yeah, you're this label? If you're clearly not doing well, you should focus on getting better instead of focusing so much on the label.”

“In general, with labels I think, probably there's too much focus on them. Because, I think, if you're suffering, you know best that you're suffering, right? Why do you need some mental health professional to tell you, yeah, you're this label? If you're clearly not doing well, you should focus on getting better instead of focusing so much on the label.”

Emma, a first‐year master's student without a diagnosis, felt similarly: “I think…most labels don't really capture necessarily the person…depression can manifest in one person very differently, than it manifests in another person…if you feel a certain way, you feel a certain way. You don't need to be diagnosed, as someone with depression to feel that you are depressed this day, right?”

Emma and Celine suggest that those who do not feel a biomedical label precisely captures their struggles lack the authority to define their own subjective experiences authentically. Students felt obliged to either use a psychiatric term and risk misunderstanding and inauthenticity, or to not use these terms and risk invalidation. Further, they express a desire to focus on connecting the student with the support they feel best addresses their subjective experience rather than focusing on what to name this experience.

Rosenbaum and Liebert (2015) similarly identify issues of authority as an unintended side‐effect of the biomedical framing of mental health on college campuses in the United States. They argue while psychiatric labels have explanatory power easing conversations between students and clinicians and bringing validity to an individual's suffering in communications with others, such as friends and family, it situates clinicians as the ultimate authorities on mental unwellness and forecloses on individual meaning‐making processes. Similarly, the participants in my study saw the explanatory power of psychiatric names as a benefit, while also identifying constraints on their own authority over naming as a problem. Vanthuyne (2003) suggests the psychiatric construct can be useful for individuals experiencing mental unwellness because it provides narrative structure for their experiences. For these students, psychiatric terminology makes their suffering legible to both clinicians, and lay people. It means they do not have to take a deep dive into explaining personal experiences if they did not wish to. In this way the names act as a “tool” to make their suffering legible in interpersonal communication. Like Rosenbaum and Liebert (2015) however, these students also felt placing naming power entirely in the hands of mental health professionals foreclosed on personal meaning making processes by limiting their authority to express their subjective experiences authentically. Thus, in general, many students found engaging in bottom‐up psychiatrization allowed them to communicate and find validation for their unwellness in a social‐sanctioned manner, while top‐down psychiatrization tended to create issues of identity.

Biomedical ambivalence

Callard (2014) argues, psychiatric diagnosis is a mediated process “often not something that ‘matters’ for a diagnosed person in the same way in all the spaces in which she lives her life” (528). Ambivalent positions vis‐à‐vis mental health labels are vital to a nuanced understanding of how mental health is experienced (Callard, 2014). Similarly, the literature on psychiatrization views outcomes as ambivalent, having both costs and benefits to society (Beeker et al., 2021; Haslam, 2016; Haslam et al., 2021). Here, understanding ambivalent positions allows us to see how students employ labels agentically. While Callard (2014) defines diagnosis as a process leading to ambivalence as meanings shift between contexts, these students view psychiatric labels as stagnant “things,” but as “things” they can use to negotiate the university institution which views unlabelled suffering skeptically. As Melanie points to in an earlier quote, students were generally more concerned with receiving appropriate care than with using a particular label. For students, like Sunflower and Jay, a psychiatric label manifested chronicity such that they felt more unwell when using these terms. Additionally, when a diagnosis is inaccurate to the individual's subjective experience it sometimes sets them on the wrong path, providing supports which do not match their experiences.

Kary, a first‐year master's student, diagnosed herself at 10 years old, illustrates this point on ambivalence: “I kind of have mixed feelings on [diagnosis]. I think it just depends on the person. In my case being diagnosed so young, I don't know if that was really the best way to go about it. I hear from some people when they're diagnosed later in life that it's actually really helpful for them because they're like, oh, I finally have an understanding of what I've been struggling with, and there's a word for it, and now it all makes sense. Whereas for me I feel like it was the opposite, because I wasn't really aware that I had a problem so much, like I was missing school and I felt anxious and stuff, but then suddenly I had a label thrown at me and I'm like I don't know what this is but I have a disorder, like I'm sick … I'm not completely anti‐diagnosis all the time, but I think psychologists should be more careful about just throwing out diagnoses immediately to everyone, I think you really need to weigh the pros and cons with each person.”

Similarly, Leah, a third‐year undergraduate without a diagnosis, feels “like that could really spiral in a little bit of an identity crisis … I feel like if you're looking for it, and you are seeking help, and you would like it, then it could be helpful and you could use it to your advantage. But if you're if you're not looking for it, it can be pretty upsetting to just get that label on you.”

These students identify how legibility and reification articulate in an individual's total context with the directionality of psychiatrization processes to determine whether a diagnosis will “work” for an individual or not. Top‐down psychiatrization creates a power structure which progressively limits the “moral circle” of care such that a negative sense of wellbeing is viewed as a moral failing unless it is a biomedical “deficiency” recognizable to psychiatry. Students are active agents in psychiatrization, engaging with mental health care systems and perpetuating concept creep to gain recognition and care for their suffering in this environment. While they engage in bottom‐up psychiatrization to access the benefits associated with making their suffering morally legible to the neoliberalizing university, they recognize the costs to their identity, authenticity, and subjectivity stemming from top‐down psychiatrization. Thus, a sense of ambivalence arises as psychiatric terminology is used both as a tool against this power structure and as a tool by the power structure to constrain society's care obligations to medically validated suffering.

CONCLUSION

While psychiatric constructs of mental health are widely used by the university students in this study, as in North American society more generally, these students did not apply the construct uncritically, instead understanding it as a “tool” which made their suffering legible, allowing them to navigate relationships and express their experiences of distress in a socially sanctioned manner only when the terminology used adequately expressed the authenticity of their experiences. In that case, students found the psychiatric construct “worked” for them, facilitating communication and access to care, removing individual blame, and providing validation. However, the psychiatric construct also serves to limit the university's moral care obligations such that students’ authority to communicate their subjective experiences authentically was similarly constrained. In these cases they found their experiences reified in a stagnant manner, manifesting a sense of chronicity. Thus, these students’ understandings and use of the psychiatric construct is nuanced and contextually dependent. While they actively participate in bottom‐up psychiatrization to validate their experiences in a social‐sanctioned manner, many students found top‐down psychiatrization had negative impacts. While psychiatric constructs could be beneficial when actively employed by the student themself, they were also often harmful to individuals’ self‐perceptions, and foreclosed on other ways of understanding mental anguish, particularly when they were imposed on students who were not seeking to view their suffering through this lens. Psychiatric naming makes students’ suffering legible to university structures, but simultaneously introduces a mistranslation that can obscure subjective and/or systemic causes of suffering. Students understand this and negotiate labeling agentically within their shifting contexts. Their ambivalent, bottom‐up psychiatrization allows them to navigate structures of power to their benefit while retaining agency over their subjective understanding of their experiences of suffering.
