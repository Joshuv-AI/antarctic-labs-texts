KREPA6 functions in RNA editing catalytic complex structural organization and gRNA utilization in Trypanosoma brucei
Authors: Brittney Davidge, Jason Carnes, Isaac Lewis, Tyler Rodshagen, Maxwell Tracy, Suzanne M McDermott, Kenneth D Stuart
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractFunctional mitochondrial mRNAs in Trypanosoma brucei are generated by the post-transcriptional guide RNA (gRNA) directed insertion and deletion of uridine residues, called RNA editing, that is catalyzed by three closely related multiprotein RNA Editing Catalytic Complexes (RECCs). These RECCs contain a common set of 12 proteins including KREPA6 which is largely comprised of an oligonucleotide binding (OB)-fold domain with a predicted intrinsically disordered region (IDR) at its C-terminus. Here we show that certain single amino acid substitutions throughout KREPA6 or deletion of the IDR inhibit the growth and viability of bloodstream form (BF) parasites. These mutations variously impact RECC structure, many alter but do not eliminate RNA editing, and some result in differential utilization of gRNAs. The results indicate that KREPA6 protein has multiple functions some of which stem from its interactions with multiple RECC proteins and perhaps with substrate RNA in each of the three different RECCs. These functions likely involve dynamic interactions of KREPA6 with key domains of other RECC proteins, other editing proteins, and with messenger RNA/gRNA substrates during the multiple catalytic and noncatalytic steps that occur during the complicated editing process.

Introduction

Trypanosoma brucei, T. cruzi, and Leishmania parasitic protists cause Human African trypanosomiasis (sleeping sickness), Chagas’ disease and various leishmaniases, respectively, resulting in extensive human disease, death and economic costs globally. The life cycles of each of these parasites include transmission by a different insect vector to the human host within which they develop, proliferate and cause disease before transmission back to a vector. Procyclic form (PF) T. brucei parasites in the tsetse fly vector (or in culture) primarily generate energy via mitochondrial oxidative phosphorylation (OXPHOS) whereas bloodstream form (BF) parasites in the animal host are reliant on glycolysis [1, 2]. Two ribosomal RNAs, and 18 messenger RNAs (mRNAs) encoding proteins of the parasite’s OXPHOS complexes, mitoribosomal proteins, and proteins with unknown function, are encoded in the ∼22 kb maxicircle component of the mitochondrial DNA, aka kinetoplast DNA (kDNA). A total of 12 of these mRNAs are edited by U insertion and deletion which results in their translatability [3–6]. The complements of edited mRNAs differ between PFs and BFs, which is regulated by mechanisms that are not understood, and contributes to the adaptation of the parasites to the different vector and host environments [2, 7, 8]. COII mRNA is edited by the insertion of four Us [9] whereas other mRNAs are more extensively edited by both U insertion and to a lesser extent by U deletion. For example, ATPase subunit 6 (A6) mRNA has +447 and -28 Us, mitoribosomal protein S12 (RPS12/uS12m) mRNA has +132 and −28 Us, and cytochrome oxidase subunit III (COIII) mRNA has +547 and −41 Us [2, 9–12]. Editing generally proceeds 3′–5′, and the editing sites (ESs) in each mRNA are specified by numerous gRNAs that are predominantly encoded in hundreds of ∼1 kb kDNA minicircles that are inter-catenated with each other and with the maxicircles [1, 3, 13–16]. Each gRNA typically specifies editing of ∼10 ESs which can include both insertion and deletion ESs. Editing of larger mRNAs therefore requires the use of multiple, often overlapping gRNAs, which are progressively utilized as each gRNA specifies the editing of a block of sequence [17]. Enzymes in multiprotein RNA Editing Catalytic Complexes (RECCs) cleave mRNA at each ES and add or remove Us and ligate the products, and we previously showed that editing of multiple ESs by RECCs is nonprocessive [18]. The editing of multiple ESs and the use of multiple gRNAs also requires the functions of RNA Editing Substrate Complexes (RESCs), accessory factors (AFs) such as KRGG1, and the KREH2 helicase complex REH2C that may transiently interact with the RECCs, RESCs and mRNA/gRNA substrates [19–22]. Recent cryoEM structures of RESCs suggest that they function in gRNA pairing with mRNA to facilitate editing by RECCs, although the precise timing of events remains unclear [21]. RESCs, REH2C, and AFs appear to be primary players that are involved in matching gRNA with mRNA to make substrates for RECC-mediated editing [19, 20].

Trypanosoma brucei has three distinct RECCs that are compositionally and functionally distinguished by the unique presence of the dimeric KREN1/KREPB8 RNase III endonuclease and the KREX1 exo-Uase in RECC1 which catalyzes deletion editing, and the dimeric KREN2/KREPB7 or KREN3/KREPB6 RNase III endonucleases in RECC2 and RECC3, respectively, which catalyze insertion editing [23–25]. The three RECCs also contain a common set of 12 proteins that includes the KRET2 TUTase, KREX2 exoUase, the KREL1 and KREL2 ligases, and the KREPB4 and KREPB5 proteins that have catalytically inactive RNase III motifs. The common proteins include KREPA1-6 that each have a C-terminal OB-fold domain and predicted intrinsically disordered regions (IDRs) [26–28]. KREPA1-3 also contain two C2H2 zinc finger domains [19]. KREPA1/T2/L2 and KREPA2/X2/L1 are associated in trimeric insertion and deletion subcomplexes [24]. Most RECC proteins are essential for BF and PF parasite viability and editing, although KREPA5 is untested [29–31]. Protein stoichiometries in RECCs are uncertain, but current evidence suggests that each RECC contains more than one copy of KREPB4, KREPB5, KREPA3, and KREPA6, and their interactions within RECCs and with other complexes, e.g. RESCs and AFs, may be transient during the dynamic process of editing [19, 26, 30, 32–35].

At 18 kDa, KREPA6 is the smallest RECC protein and largely consists of an OB-fold domain with short N- and C-terminal extensions. The OB-fold has a β-barrel that is comprised of at least five strands, and is capped with an α-helix (C34), as are other OB-folds [30, 36–38]. The encoded N-terminal extension is a mitochondrial targeting sequence which is cleaved off in vivo upon import into the mitochondrion [39–41]. The C-terminal ∼30 residues are predicted to form an IDR [30]. Recombinant KREPA6 can form homo- and hetero-dimers and ternary complexes with itself or with the OB-folds of other KREPA proteins as revealed by X-ray crystallography [26, 30, 36]. The proximity of KREPA6 to other RECC proteins is shown by its protein–protein crosslinking to 13 other RECC proteins, including the OB-folds of KREPA1-5. These proximities support the available structural data and underscore the likely presence of multiple copies of KREPA6 in RECCs, and its importance to overall RECC structure [30, 35, 36]. Furthermore, KREPA6 is essential in PFs and its repression results in loss of KREPA3, L1, A1, and A2 from 20S glycerol gradient fractions, indicating its importance for the structural integrity of RECCs in vivo [42].

We show here that KREPA6 is essential for growth, editing, and the structural integrity of all three RECCs in BF T. brucei and that its function is associated with the selective utilization of gRNAs for editing. The effects on RECC structural integrity of various single amino acid substitutions, which range from minimal RECC alteration to RECC elimination, indicate that KREPA6 interacts with multiple RECC proteins and implies that it may function in RECC assembly in vivo. Single amino acid substitutions which did not eliminate ∼1 MDa RECCs altered and reduced editing, which was insufficient for cell survival. The differences in the amounts and character of edited sequences that result from the substitutions imply that KREPA6 influences the selective use of different gRNAs, as has also been observed for the RNA helicase KREH2 [43, 44]. Additionally, the reduced amount but not character of edited mRNAs sequences following deletion of the C-terminal IDR suggests that the IDR may function in editing efficiency, perhaps via molecular interactions with substrates and/or other proteins. Together, these results show that KREPA6, and by extension RECCs, play an active role in recognizing mRNA/gRNA substrates and influencing whether catalysis should proceed.

Materials and methods

Cloning of pLew100v5-KREPA6 and pHD1344tub(PAC)-KREPA6-C-term3V5

Cloning of the KREPA6 open reading frame (ORF) was facilitated by using the Gateway recombination technique, in which BP or LR clonase enzyme mixes are used to transfer DNA fragments flanked by att recombination sites [45]. The KREPA6 ORF was amplified by polymerase chain reaction (PCR) with primers 11 634 and 11 636 that added attB sites, cloned via BP reactions into pDONR-221, and then transferred via LR reactions to pHD1344tub(PAC)-C-term-3V5 and pLew100V5(BLE)GW to create the V5-tagged exclusive-expression construct [pHD1344tub(PAC)-KREPA6-C-term-3V5] and untagged tet-regulatable KREPA6 construct [pLew100V5(BLE)GW-KREPA6 WT], respectively (Supplementary Table S1) [46].

Generation of the BF KREPA6 conditional null cell line

BF single-marker 427 (SM427) cells were used for construction of the KREPA6 conditional null (CN) cell line [47]. In short, both endogenous KREPA6 alleles were replaced with selectable markers and a tet-regulatable KREPA6 allele inserted into the ribosomal DNA (rDNA) locus. The first KREPA6 allele knockout construct contained a blasticidin resistance gene and was produced via two steps of PCR reactions with Phusion polymerase (NEB) as previously described [48]. All primer sequences for this process are detailed in Supplementary Table S1. First, two homology arm targeting regions in the UTRs near the KREPA6 ORF were designed using primers 11 648 with 11 649 (for 5′ UTR) and 11 650 with 11 652 (for 3′ UTR). Genomic DNA was used as a template for this first set of reactions. Next, the blasticidin drug resistance cassette was added by fusion PCR using the above 5′ and 3′ UTR homology arms with the pyrFEKO-BSD plasmid as a template (Supplementary Table S1) [46, 48]. This PCR fused homology arms targeting the region flanking the KREPA6 locus with the floxed BSD-HSVTK selectable marker [48]. The resulting construct (BSD-HSVTK with homology arms) was transfected into BF SM427 cells using the Amaxa nucleofector as previously described and transfectants were selected with 4 µg/ml blasticidin to isolate the BF KREPA6-SKO cell line [48].

Next, pLew100V5(BLE)GW-KREPA6, which encodes the tet-regulatable, untagged KREPA6 allele (KREPA6reg) was linearized by Not1, transfected into KREPA6-SKO cells, and selected for with 2.5 µg/ml phleomycin to create the BF KREPA6-RKO cell line. Expression of KREPA6reg from the rDNA locus was confirmed by western blot of cells grown in plus and minus tet, which resulted in loss of KREPA6reg protein upon repression of this allele (tet− conditions). Following incorporation of the tet-regulated allele, the blasticidin (BSD)-HSVTK construct was removed using the Cre/lox system. The purpose of this was twofold: (i) to remove the HSVTK cassette which shares homology with the second allele knockout (KO) construct, and (ii) to remove the BSD marker which will render the cells BSD-sensitive thus allowing this selectable marker to be used again for future transfections. A plasmid encoding Cre-recombinase was transiently transfected as previously described and resulting cells were selected using ganciclovir (GCV) in HMI-9 without thymidine, which selects against cells that still contain the HSVTK marker as previously described [46, 49, 50]. The resulting GCV-resistant cell line BF KREPA6-RKO-flox was confirmed to be BSD-sensitive prior to subsequent transfections.

BF KREPA6-RKO-flox cells were then transfected with a second KREPA6 allele KO construct containing an HYG-HSVTK drug cassette was generated in a similar manner as the first, except long primers 11 653 and 11 654 containing different homology arms targeting the 5′ and 3′ UTRs closest to the KREPA6 ORF were used (Supplementary Table S1) [48]. Along with the second allele construct, saCas9 Nuclease NLS Protein (Applied Biological Materials) and three KREPA6-targeting CRISPR gRNAs, were also transfected to improve efficiency. The procedure for preparing the CRISPR/Cas9 constructs has been previously described [51]. KREPA6-specifc gRNA sequences were designed using the ChopChop tool (https://chopchop.cbu.uib.no/) and are shown in Supplementary Table S1 [52]. Following transfection of the second allele KO construct, clones were selected with 5 µg/ml hygromycin (HYG) and one HYG-resistant cell line, BF KREPA6 CN (abbreviated “CN” hereafter), was obtained. This CN cell line exhibits a strong growth defect and loss of KREPA6 protein upon repression of KREPA6reg (minus tet condition) as seen in Figs 1 and 2, consistent with cells that lack endogenous KREPA6 protein. The CN cell line was maintained in HMI-9 with 5 µg/ml hygromycin, 2.5 µg/ml phleomycin, 2.5 µg/ml G418, and tetracycline. The KREPA6-SKO cell line was confirmed by PCR of gDNA with primers 11 648 and 11 652, which resulted in an ∼2.3 kb product consistent with the size of the BDS-HSVTK construct and a second ∼1.5 kb product consistent with the size of the second endogenous KREPA6 allele (Supplementary Table S1; data not shown). The CN cell line was confirmed by the same PCR except no ∼1.5 kb band was observed, indicating loss of the endogenous alleles (data not shown).

The CN cell line was also used to create the cell line expressing L262P Mutant Gamma ATP synthase (MGA) allele, BF KREPA6 CN + MGA (abbreviated “CN + MGA” hereafter), by transfecting the pEnT6 + ATPaseGammaWT-3UTR plasmid as previously described and selecting with 4 µg/ml blasticidin [25, 27, 53, 54]. Expression of the L262P MGA allele uncouples the F1 and FO parts of the ATP synthase complex, with cells using ADP/ATP carrier to maintain mitochondrial membrane potential; as a consequence these cells no longer require RNA editing [53]. MGA cell lines with exclusively expressed KREPA6 alleles were created by transfecting the appropriate version of the pHD1344tub(PAC)-KREPA6-C-term3V5 vector into the CN + MGA cells and selecting with 0.1 µg/ml puromycin.

KREPA6 mutagenesis library preparation and screening

Four alternative KREPA6 mutagenesis libraries using varying amounts of KREPA6 ORF (100, 200, 500, and 1000 ng) from the pHD1344tub(PAC)-KREPA6-C-term3V5 plasmid (wild type sequence) as template were produced by 30 cycles of error-prone PCR-mediated mutagenesis as previously described using the GeneMorphII kit (Agilent). This plasmid contains the WT KREPA6 ORF without the stop codon flanked at the 5′ by an attB1 site and at the 3′ by an attB2 site. Primers 11 550 and 11 512 (Supplementary Table S1) [27, 55, 56] that anneal to the attB sites were used for the error-prone PCR to allow for subsequent Gateway cloning.

The library resulting from 200 ng of template was used for full-scale preparation as 45% of sequences analyzed were WT, 37.5% had one amino acid change, 8.3% had two changes and 8.3% had three or more changes. This library was cloned into pDONR-Express [57] via a BP reaction and screened for full length clones using kanamycin as previously described [55]. KREPA6 ORFs from approximately 10 000 Kanamycin resistant clones were then transferred back into the pHD1344tub(PAC)-C-term-3V5 to produce the pHD1344tub(PAC)-mutant KREPA6-C-term3V5 library as previously described [55]. For quality control, 46 library clones were sequenced using primer 9571 (Supplementary Table S1) and analyzed. A total of 64 mutations were identified, and of these, 58 were distinct. There was an average of ∼1.4 mutations per ORF. Of the 46 sequenced alleles, 14 were wild-type (WT) nucleotide sequences with no mutations, 14 had a single nucleotide change, 8 had two changes, 6 had three changes, and 4 had four changes. One mutated ORF was observed 3 times; all others had distinct nucleotide sequences. The sequenced ORFs were translated in silico to assess the effects of the mutations on the protein sequence. Selection on kanamycin for intact ORFs was effective, as no nonsense or frameshift mutations were identified. Within these 46 sequences, 43% encoded WT KREPA6 protein, 22% had one amino acid substitution, 22% had two amino acid substitutions, and the remaining 13% had three or more amino acid changes. Thus, the mutagenesis library, while not expected to be exhaustive, broadly covers a wide variety of mutant alleles.

The pHD1344tub(PAC)-KREPA6-C-term3V5 library was linearized using Not1 and 50 µg was transfected into 1.5 × 108 BF KREPA6 CN cells and plated on 24 well plates with 5 ng/ml tetracycline, the minimum amount of tet required for growth. Approximately 756 clones were obtained, consolidated into 96 well plates and replica plated into media containing either 5 ng/µl tetracycline or 0 ng/µl tet. Clones were grown for six days in the plus or minus tet conditions and passaged once at day three. Alamar blue screening was then conducted to assess cell growth to identify mutants with growth defects in the absence of tetracycline as previously described [27, 55, 56]. A total of 121 clones were identified with a full (blue) growth defect and 27 clones were identified with a partial (purple) growth defect. These cell lines were isolated from the corresponding plus tet replica plate, genomic DNA was extracted, and the mutant KREPA6 ORFs recovered by PCR as previously described using primers 5355 and 6931 (Supplementary Table S1) [55]. PCR products for 48 full growth defect clones and 15 partial growth defect clones were sequenced using primers 9571/10 622 to identify amino acid changes. Due to the nature of this screen, any dominant negative mutations of KREPA6 would not be identified.

Of the 13 single point mutations analyzed in this study, 10 were found as single amino acid substitutions in the screen. Of the remaining three, H70Y and H32R were found more than once, and Y40S was a site-directed mutation that was made based on information from the previous A3 screen [27]. All substitutions of interest and the ΔIDR were reconstructed via site-directed mutagenesis (QuickChange Lightning, Agilent) with the pHD1344tub(PAC)-KREPA6-C-term3V5 “eWT” plasmid as a template (Supplementary Tables S2 and S3). These new plasmids were then transfected into the CN or CN + MGA cell lines for further analysis.

RNA extraction and profile reverse transcriptase-polymerase chain reaction

All procedures for RNA extraction, quantification, and reverse transcriptase-polymerase chain reaction (RT-PCR) followed previously published approaches [27, 56]. Briefly, 5 × 107–1 × 108 BF cells were collected and stored in Trizol as previously described [27]. Total RNA was extracted, treated with DNase I, and resuspended at a concentration of 1 µg/µl. The concentration and quality of RNA was checked with the BioAnalyzer (Agilent) and NanoDrop One Spectrophotometer (Thermo Fisher) prior to complementary DNA (cDNA) synthesis as previously described [27]. For RT-PCR, cDNA was synthesized using the TaqMan reverse transcription reagents, MultiScribe Reverse Transcriptase, and a gene-specific reverse primer for each RNA transcript analyzed (MURF2, A6, RPS12 or ND4). PCR was performed using primer pairs 3704/3580 (A6), 3619/3620 (RPS12), 6204/4934 (MURF2), and 3706/3707 (ND4) as previously described (Supplementary Table S1) [27]. The RT-PCR products were run on a three percent agarose Tris Borate EDTA (TBE) gel, stained with ethidium bromide, and visualized. RT-PCR products in Fig. 3 represent at least two biological replicates for all cell lines except the ΔIDR, ΔIDR + MGA, D68G + MGA, and H124Y + MGA, which each represent one biological replicate.

MURF2 and A6 library preparation and Illumina sequencing

Reverse transcription was performed using the Taqman MultiScribe reverse transcription reagents and gene specific reverse primers (11 502 for MURF2 and 11 505 for A6). For each 20 µl reaction, 0.5 µl (500 ng) of total RNA was added to 19.5 µl of a mastermix containing 1.4 µl MgCl2, 2 µl of the 10× reaction buffer, 1 µl of the appropriate primer at a 2 µM starting concentration, 4 µl dNTPs, 1 µl of Multiscribe RT, 1 µl of RNase inhibitor, and 9.1 µl of dH2O per reaction. Reactions were then placed in the thermocycler with the following parameters: 25°C for 10 min, 42°C for 30 min, and 95°C for 5 min. Second strand synthesis was completed by adding the entire 20 µl first strand reactions to 20 µl of a master mix containing 1 µl of the appropriate primer (11 552 for MURF2 and 11 554 for A6) and 19 µl of NEB NEXT polymerase 2× master mix. Second strand reactions were then placed in the thermocycler with the following parameters: 95°C for 1:15 min, 45°C for 15 s, 72°C for 6 min, followed by a 4°C hold. Following second strand synthesis, 10 µl of each reaction was treated with Exonuclease I in a 14 µl reaction volume to remove excess primers, then Exonuclease I was heat-inactivated by incubation at 80°C for 20 min. The entire Exonuclease I-treated reactions were then amplified by 20 cycles of PCR with UMI-containing forward and reverse primers (11 508 and 11 509 for all samples, see Supplementary Table S1) in 50 µl reactions using NEB NEXT polymerase. Thermocycling parameters were as follows: 98°C for 1 min, then 20 cycles of 98°C for 10 s, 55°C for 15 s, 72°C for 45 s, and a final extension at 72°C for 1 min. PCR products were gel extracted from a 2% agarose Tris-base Acetic acid EDTA (TAE) gel and 15 ng of each product was indexed using the Illumina UDI indexes set A (September 2023 version). For indexing, KAPA HiFi HotStart ready mix (Roche) was used with thermocycling parameters as follows: 95°C for 3 min, then 12 cycles of 95°C for 30 s, 55°C for 30 s, 72°C for 30 s, and a final extension at 72°C for 5 min. Indexed PCR products were brought up to a volume of 50 µl with dH2O and purified using 100 µl of SPRIselect beads (Beckman Coulter) per PCR reaction. The concentration of each indexed library was measured using a Qubit and then pooled into a 10 nM library with equimolar amounts of each product. The resulting pooled library was sequenced using Illumina MiSeq 600 v3 with a paired end 300 × 300. A total of 20% PhiX was added prior to the sequencing run. One biological replicate of each sample was included.

Deep sequencing data analysis

FASTQ.gz files generated by deep sequencing were analyzed using custom software package PARERS v2.0, which was written using Python 3.12. Reads were paired and merged using BBMerge [58]. Merged reads where then checked for matching forward and reverse primer sequences and any reads with mismatched primers were excluded from the downstream analyses. Next, the UMI barcodes were deduplicated so that only one instance of each UMI pair was analyzed. Non-T (A/C/G) sequences were aligned to the corresponding A/C/G maxicircle sequence and any sequences containing a non-T mismatch were excluded from the analysis [full sequences used for fully edited (FE) and pre-edited (PE) A6 and MURF2 in Supplementary Table S4]. A summary of the numbers of reads filtered and retained at each step in the pipeline is shown in Supplemental Table S5. Sequence alignments were generated using PARERS v2.0. Data output from PARERS v2.0 was used to generate difference bar graphs and bubble plots with R version 4.4.0. Data output from PARERS was used to generate the editing summary bar graph and cumulative editing graphs with Microsoft Excel. Cumulative editing graphs were generated by cumulative summing the number of normalized reads (either edited or not edited) at each ES in the 3′–5′ direction staring with the most 3′ ES.

R1 and R2 files for sequences used in this study have been deposited in the Sequence Read Archive under BioProject ID PRJNA1199013. PARERS software link Zenodo doi: 10.5281/zenodo.17593461 [25].

Glycerol gradient fractionation

A total of 2–3 × 109 BF cells were collected, lysed, and loaded onto 10%–30% glycerol gradients as previously described [27]. Gradients were centrifuged at 38 000 rpm in a Beckman Optima XPN-80 ultracentrifuge for 8 h using an SW40 rotor. Then, 500 µl fractions were collected and 100 µl was added to Tricine sample buffer for SDS–PAGE analysis. The remaining 400 µl was flash frozen and stored at -80°C for future use.

SDS–PAGE, BN-PAGE, and western blotting

All western blots including Blue Native (BN) PAGE gels were prepared and run as previously described [56]. Antibodies used for detection are listed in Supplementary Table S6 [56]. Rabbit polyclonal antibody that recognizes RESC13 was a generous gift from Laurie Read [59]. Cloning and expression of recombinant mAbs against RECC proteins: IgG and IgK variable regions for monoclonal antibodies for KREPA1, KREPA2, KREPA3, and KREL1 were recovered from the respective hybridoma cell lines (P4D8-F6, P1H3-D7, P3C12-B6, and P3C1-G2, respectively), cloned into a pcDNA3.4 vector containing the sequences for the antibody constant regions (mouse IgG1 for heavy chains and mouse IgK for light chains), transiently transfected and expressed as previously described [60–62]. Before use, the recombinant mAbs were validated by SDS–PAGE blotting of 20S mitochondrial fractions from PF 29–13 T. brucei as equivalent to the hybridoma-produced mAbs. Both hybridoma produced and recombinant mAbs were used for this study.

Results

KREPA6 repression, single amino acid substitutions, or IDR deletion affect BF growth

We identified single amino acid substitutions in KREPA6 that impact growth of BFs and mapped them, and the predicted IDR that we deleted, to the KREPA6 gene and its protein crystal structure (see Methods; Fig. 1A and B) [26, 27, 55, 56]. We did so by introducing an exogenous allele with tet-dependent expression (KREPA6reg) into the rDNA locus of BF KREPA6 conditional null (CN) cells in which we had deleted both endogenous KREPA6 alleles. The CN cells undergo loss of growth and viability within 48–72 h following removal of tet but are rescued from this defect by expression from the tubulin locus of a C-terminal 3xV5 tagged WT KREPA6 allele (Fig. 1C). This shows that KREPA6 is essential in BF cells and that exclusive expression of WT KREPA6 (i.e. eWT) restores its function (see Methods and Supplementary Information for details) [47, 48]. To identify substitutions that affect KREPA6 function, the CN cells were transfected with a library containing ∼10 000 mutant KREPA6 ORFs expressed from the tubulin locus, screened for growth defects in −tet versus +tet, and cloned by limiting dilution. This resulted in 756 clonal cell lines of which 121 had substantial growth defects and 27 others had lesser growth defects. DNA sequencing identified the single amino acid substitutions that affected growth, and these mapped to various positions throughout the KREPA6 OB-fold. No inhibitory single substitution was found within the C-terminal IDR or in the N-terminal region that encodes the mitochondrial targeting peptide that is cleaved off upon entry into the mitochondrion [39–41]. We independently reconstructed cell lines with single amino acid substitutions based on growth defects of the cloned cell lines that had single amino acid substitutions or that had a substitution at the same position in multiple clones. We also made the Y40S substitution in KREPA6, as the F307S substitution in the orthologous position of the KREPA3 OB-fold had also resulted in altered RECC integrity and editing, and made a KREPA6 ΔIDR cell line by deleting the C-terminal residues 140–164 (see Methods) [27].

Effects on growth and CCs

Overall, the 13 single amino acid substitutions and C-terminal IDR deletion had varying impacts on growth which ranged from minor (H70Y and E77K), to moderate (S24G, N96Y, N110I, I118T, P122S, H124Y, and ΔIDR), or severe (H32R, F38L, Y40S, D68G, and K111E) which recapitulated their growth phenotypes in the screen (Fig. 1C). Analysis of the conservation of these 13 amino acids among 64 kinetoplastid species showed a range from 48.7 to 100 percent identity, indicating that many, but not all of the identified essential residues are very highly conserved (Supplementary Fig. S1A and Supplementary Table S3). Exclusive expression of the mutant KREPA6 allele was confirmed by anti-KREPA6 western analyses that show represssion of the tetracycline-regulated WT KREPA6 allele in the absence of tetracycline (Supplementary Fig. S1B). Because the growth defects that arise between 48 and 72 h after elimination of KREPA6 expression in CN cells or mutant KREPA6 exclusive expression might result in secondary effects due to the reduced viability, we also constructed continuously viable cell lines by inserting a mutant gamma ATP synthase (MGA) allele (see Methods and Supplementary Fig. S1C). The inserted MGA eliminates the requirement for editing in BFs and enables their continuous cultivation in the absence of KREPA6 [22, 25, 27, 54]. Therefore, the MGA allele aids our ability to analyze the effects of KREPA6 elimination, expression, or mutation on the abundances and integrity of RECC proteins and RECCs, and on RNA editing in growing cells. We used BN-PAGE to analyze whole cell lysates from MGA versus non-MGA CN, eWT and mutant cells, which showed broadly similar results (Supplementary Fig. S2); we therefore focused on the MGA samples (Fig. 1D). Analyses of whole cell lysates from MGA-expressing eWT cells 96 h after tet withdrawal showed that RECCs are predominantly ∼1 MDa in size, and that WT KREPA6 is present in these RECCs whether expressed from the endogenous, rDNA (CN + tet), or tubulin (eWT) locus including with a C-terminal V5 tag (Fig. 1D and Supplementary Fig. S2). In contrast, elimination of KREPA6 expression in CN + MGA cells resulted in the absence of ∼1 MDa RECCs and loss of Western signals for RECC proteins KREPA1, KREPA2, KREL1 and KREPA3. Thus, KREPA6 is essential for the presence of complete RECCs and its proteins in BF cells.

V5 tagged KREPA6 proteins were at similar levels relative to the HSP70 loading control in cells that exclusively expressed eWT or mutant KREPA6-V5 except in I118T and H124Y cells (Fig. 1D and Supplementary Fig. S2). However, the abundances of the KREPA1, KREPA2, KREL1, and KREPA3 proteins that are present in all three RECCs were diminished in some mutants (e.g. S24G, D68G, I118T, and H124Y) and in some cases their relative proportions were altered between the mutants (e.g. KREPA1 versus KREPA2 in S24G versus F38L versus E77K). Importantly, exclusive expression of KREPA6 with various single amino acid substitutions or the ΔIDR had different effects on the abundances of RECCs (Fig. 1D). These ranged from almost complete (F38L) or substantial (D68G, I118T, H124Y) loss of ∼1 MDa RECCs to little effect on the abundance of these complexes (S24G, E77K, P122S and ΔIDR). Exclusive expression of KREPA6 with some substitutions also resulted in incomplete or disrupted RECCs (e.g. H32R, F38L, Y40S, E77K, N110I, K111E, P122S, and ΔIDR) and exclusive expression of N96Y KREPA6 resulted in ∼242 kDa complexes that contain KREPA3 but not KREPA2 (Fig. 2B). The effects of the KREPA6 substitutions on RECC abundance and structural integrity were somewhat parallel to the effects on the individual RECC protein abundances. For example, the near absence or great reduction of RECCs in F38L and D68G exclusive expressors parallels reductions in KREPA2, KREL1, and KREPA3 proteins, with KREPA1 notably reduced in D68G sample (Fig. 1D, Supplementary Fig. S2). That KREPA3 protein was detected in F38L by western blotting following SDS–PAGE, but not following BN-PAGE, could be due either to the exclusion of KREPA3 from RECCs, or to the F38L mutation in KREPA6 shifting the location of the epitope target of the KREPA3 antibody to an inaccessible position within the RECCs. In contrast, none of the substitutions in KREPA6 and even the loss of RECCs in the CN cells had an appreciable impact on the abundances of the RESC1 and RESC13 proteins relative to the HSP70 loading control. Thus, in general, various substitutions in KREPA6 that inhibit editing-dependent growth of BFs differentially affected the abundances of RECC proteins, their integration into RECCs, and they had various consequences on the abundance and structural integrity of the RECCs. The loss or disruption of RECCs that resulted from these various single substitutions underscores the importance of KREPA6 for the overall organization and structural integrity of the RECCs.

The range of effects of the amino acid substitutions on the abundances of RECCs and their proteins and RECC structural integrity are exemplified by analyses of the F38L, Y40S and N96Y cell lines (Fig. 1D). F38L is an extreme example with dramatic loss of ∼1 MDa RECCs, Y40S contains ∼1 MDa and ∼800 kDa complexes, and N96Y contains ∼1 MDa and ∼242 kDa complexes. We therefore decided to look more closely at RECCs in these cells by glycerol gradient sedimentation and immunoprecipitation. The RECCs in cells that exclusively express WT A6-V5 (eWT) primarily sediment in glycerol gradients at ∼20S, consistent with their ∼1 MDa position following BN-PAGE when probed with antibodies for KREPA2 or KREPA3 (Fig. 2A and B). Additionally, immunoprecipitation of eWT with anti-V5 isolates KREPA1, KREPA2, KREPA3, and KREL1, and eWT A6-V5 is also present following immunoprecipitation of KREPA3 (Fig. 2C). Interestingly, we observed both untagged KREPA6 and KREPA6-V5 in anti-V5 immunoprecipitates, indicating that there are multiple copies of KREPA6 present in RECCs. Results from cells exclusively expressing single amino acid substitutions are notably different from eWT A6-V5. For example, F38L cells contain complexes that sediment at ∼10S and migrate as heterogeneous ∼0.5 to ∼1 MDa complexes following BN-PAGE. F38L glycerol gradient fractions contain KREPA1 and KREPA3, but diminished amounts of KREL1, and lack KREPA2. Intriguingly, the presence of KREPA3 in the ∼10S complexes is visible by western blotting following SDS–PAGE but not BN-PAGE (Fig. 1D), again suggesting that the KREPA3 epitope may be masked in native proteins from these cells. Immunoprecipitation via KREPA3 or KREPA6-V5 from F38L cells also show the reduced abundances for KREL1 and KREPA2 compared to eWT (Fig. 2C). Furthermore, immunoprecipitation of the F38L mutant KREPA6-V5 does not directly isolate KREPA3 (Fig. 2C). The Y40S cells also contain complexes that primarily sediment at ∼10S and lower. Gradient fractions from the ∼20S region (fractions 11–13) have KREPA2, KREPA3, and KREPA6-V5-containing ∼1 MDa complexes, whereas fractions 7–9 have KREPA2-containing ∼1 MDa and ∼800 kDa complexes and KREPA3-containing ∼1MDa complexes, as well as heterogeneous KREPA6-V5-containing ∼1 MDa, ∼800 kDa, and <145 kDa complexes (Figs 2A and B). KREPA1, KREPA2, KREPA3, KREL1, and KREPA6 are detected by western blotting following SDS–PAGE of Y40S cell lysates and, except for KREPA1, in KREPA3 or KREPA6-V5 immunoprecipitates (Fig. 2C). The N96Y cells have complexes that primarily sediment at ∼10S and contain KREPA1, KREPA3, and KREPA6-V5, but not KREPA2 or KREL1. The N96Y cells also have complexes that sediment at ∼20S that contain KREPA2, KREPA3, KREPA6-V5, and KREL1, but not KREPA1 (Fig. 2A). Complexes from the ∼10S region migrate near the ∼242 kDa marker as assessed by BN-PAGE and western blotting for KREPA3, whereas those from the ∼20S region are ∼1 MDa when probed for KREPA2, KREPA3, or KREPA6-V5 with some ∼800 kDa subcomplex present upon probing for KREPA6-V5 (Fig. 2B). The sedimentation profiles of RESC13 and HSP70 from the mutants resemble those from eWT and thus were not visibly altered by the KREPA6 mutations. Overall, these results show that different single amino acid substitutions in KREPA6 variously affect the abundances, compositions, and structural integrity of RECCs. Notably, several KREPA6 substitutions (e.g. F38L and N96Y) have differential effects on KREPA1 versus KREPA2 (Fig. 2A), which potentially reflects the presence versus absence of the KREPA1/KRET2/KREL1 and KREPA2/KREX2/KREL2 trimeric subcomplexes [24, 63]. Similarly, six of the mutated residues, S24, Y40, D68, H70, K111, I118, and H124 are located at positions that were shown to contact KREPA3 in KREPA3/KREPA6 crystal structures, and all others are within two amino acids of one or more residues in this interface [36]. In most cases, we show that the abundance of KREPA3, and/or the abundances and integrities of KREPA3-containing complexes was impacted by these mutations, illustrating the importance of the KREPA6-KREPA3 interaction for RECC assembly and/or stability in vivo.

Effects on RNA editing

The overall effects of each substitution and ΔIDR on the editing of MURF2, RPS12, and A6 mRNAs were surveyed by RT-PCR profiling of the PE, FE, and the diverse incompletely edited (IE) mRNAs, using never edited ND4 mRNA as a control (Fig. 3). These analyses compared mRNAs taken at 48 h after tet withdrawal from CN and exclusive expressors of the eWT or mutant KREPA6 alleles i.e. before inhibition of cell growth by ∼96 h, as well as mRNAs taken at 120 h following tet removal from cells that also contained an inserted MGA allele. The MGA had little to no effect on the RT-PCR profiles of these three mRNAs from eWT or from CN tet+ cells which express KREPA6. However, the edited mRNAs were virtually absent from CN cells in which KREPA6 expression was repressed, and only PE mRNAs were present in CN + MGA cells. The RT-PCR profiles of RNAs from the non-MGA F38L and D68G cell lines had more IE mRNAs than the CN cells but only PE mRNAs were detected in the corresponding F38L and D68G MGA cells. This dramatic loss of edited mRNAs parallels the almost complete loss of ∼1 MDa RECCs and some RECC proteins in the F38L and D68G cell lines (Figs 1 and 2). RT-PCR profiles of mRNAs from cell lines containing the other substitutions and ΔIDR revealed the presence of multiple IE mRNAs. In most of these cases, little difference was seen in the MURF2 mRNA editing profiles of non-MGA or MGA cells that were exclusively expressing eWT versus mutant KREPA6. However, the profiles of RPS12 and A6 mRNAs, which have larger regions of editing than MURF2, revealed a combination of abundant similar sized and less abundant diverse sized RT-PCR products that were not observed in cells that were exclusively expressing eWT KREPA6. For example, the RPS12 profiles from H32R and K111E had prominent products that are slightly larger than PE RNAs that are seen in F38L and D68G (Fig. 3). Therefore, most of the substitutions appeared to alter but not eliminate editing. A phenotypic summary of the identified substitutions highlights the magnitude these alterations in the context of observed growth and RECC integrity defects (Fig. 3D).

Effects on editing of ESs and mRNA sequences

Several KREPA6 mutants that did not eliminate ∼1 MDa RECCs (Fig. 1) and altered the RT-PCR profiling patterns of edited mRNAs (Fig. 3) were selected for analysis by deep sequencing (Figs 4–6). The detailed effects on edited mRNAs of exclusive expression of Y40S, E77K, N96Y, N110I, H124Y, and ΔIDR mutated KREPA6 were determined from >300 000 paired-end reads of mRNA RT-PCR amplicons from MGA CN and MGA WT/mutant exclusive expression BF cell lines. The sequenced amplicons spanning the entire edited sequence of MURF2 mRNA and a sizeable portion of the edited 3′ region of A6 mRNA were amplified in the linear range using primers for never edited 3′ and 5′ MURF2 mRNA, and for never edited 3′ and PE 5′ sequences of A6 mRNA (Fig. 3C). Quantification of PE, FE, and IE sequences and ESs were determined using PARERS v2.0 (see Methods) (Supplementary Figs S3–S5, and Supplementary Tables S4, S5, S7, and S8) [25].

To obtain an overview of MURF2 and A6 editing, we classified each read sequence as PE, FE, or IE, and compiled the percentages of each class for the amplicons from both transcripts in the assayed samples (Fig. 4, Supplementary Table S7). These analyses show that KREPA6 repression in the CN + MGA cell line (Fig. 4A, CN-R) broadly reduces editing of both MURF2 and A6, with no FE and only a very small proportion of IE sequences observed. These IE sequences likely reflect either retention of residual mRNAs edited prior to KREPA6 repression, some degree of incomplete KREPA6 repression, or a combination of both. Editing of MURF2 in each exclusive expression mutant cell line differed from eWT to varying degrees, with all showing reduced FE and both H124Y and ΔIDR also showing reduced IE. In contrast, because A6 amplicons are generated with a 5′ PE primer, FE sequences over the entire length of the amplicon are exceedingly rare in any sample, even eWT. However, the frequency of IE sequences is greatest in the eWT sample, with H124Y and ΔIDR mutants again showing the largest reductions in editing. In complementary analyses, we tracked the cumulative amount of editing at each ES from the 3′ end of each mRNA towards the 5′, aggregating the number of reads that have any amount of editing at each ES (Fig. 4B and C). Interestingly, more MURF2 editing is observed in E77K and N96Y mutants than in eWT in this analysis, indicating that these mutants produce a notable amount of editing that does not correlate with FE sequences (Fig. 4B). Analysis of A6 editing shows eWT having noticeably greater cumulative editing than all mutants (Fig. 4B and C). Interestingly, a closer look at just the 3′ region of the A6 amplicon shows that both N96Y and N110I mutants have reduced amounts of editing at the very 3′ end of A6 compared to either eWT or the other mutants, showing that the various mutations affect editing in distinct ways. Together, these data show that the analyzed mutants have altered editing with respect to eWT that reflects both qualitative and quantitative changes in editing rather than a broad elimination like that observed upon KREPA6 repression in the CN MGA cell line.

Most ESs in MURF2 mRNA from eWT cells were FE except for three IE ESs near the 3′ end (Fig. 5A). MURF2 editing can be directed by four identified gRNAs, g1-4 (Supplementary Table S8 and Supplementary Figs S3 and S4), that specify overlapping regions of edited sequences, with the exception that only g-2 can direct editing of several 3′ ESs (Fig. 5) [64]. Furthermore, both g-2 and g-4 would direct editing that does not match the expected FE sequence, and g-4 was previously identified as directing editing of CR4 mRNA in the region of nt 251–300 [16, 64, 65]. Editing of MURF2 mRNA ESs in each mutant differed from that in eWT and from each other, although there were some similarities between mutants. The mRNAs from both E77K and N96Y had increased IE in the three ESs near the 3′ end (see brackets Fig. 5B), and decreased FE with concomitant increased IE at the upstream insertion ESs and the one deletion ES. The mRNAs from both H124Y and ΔIDR cells generally had reduced FE at insertion and deletion ESs but no concomitant increase in IE. Editing of the three IE ESs near the 3′ end was decreased in ΔIDR and somewhat increased in H124Y. Editing of most MURF2 ESs was reduced in Y40S mRNA with no concomitant increase in IE, especially more 5′. Editing of ESs in N110I differed little from WT (Fig. 5B). Overall, each of the substitutions and the ΔIDR resulted in a combination of similarities and unique differences from editing of MURF2 ESs in eWT, prominent among which are those that affected the 3′-most ESs, reciprocal changes in FE and IE ESs, especially in E77K, N96Y and N110I cells, and the overall reduction of editing at all MURF2 ESs in RNA from H124Y or ΔIDR cells. The prominent effects at the 3′ end may be associated with preferential usage of the g-2 gRNA relative to other gRNAs.

We focused our more detailed analyses of A6 only on the 3′-most region due to the rarity of FE sequences over the entire length of the A6 amplicon. We observed A6 editing that matches the FE sequence identified in previous publications that is distinct from the originally reported FE A6 sequence; therefore, we use the sequence shown in Supplementary Table S4 as the FE A6 sequence [16, 66, 67]. Editing of this A6 mRNA 3′ region that we analyzed can be directed by three previously identified gRNAs that specify overlapping sequences, only one of which can direct editing of the six 3′ terminal ESs (g-1) (Fig. 5). Additionally, some editing in the 3′ region that would not lead to FE A6 mRNA can be directed by the A6 g-2 gRNA, which also specifies editing in nucleotides 176–216 of CR4 mRNA (Fig. 5 and Supplementary Table S8) [43]. We identified IE mRNA sequences in eWT cells that could be specified by g-2 (Fig. 5A) that were present at the same frequency as FE sequences. These frequencies are consistent with those observed for WT 427 strain cells in a previous study [43]. As for MURF2 mRNA, editing of the numerous A6 mRNA ESs in the mutants differed from that in eWT and from each other, although as for MURF2, there were similarities between some mutants. The mRNAs from both N96Y and N110I mutant cells had reduced FE, while the Y40S, E77K, H124Y mutants had increased FE relative to eWT at the five 3′ terminal ESs. Editing in the ΔIDR cells resembled that in the eWT cells at these sites. Notably, the editing that could be specified by g-2 was also decreased in the Y40S, E77K, H124Y, and ΔIDR mutants relative to eWT (Supplementary Fig. S5). Thus, as for MURF2, the mutations appear to have affected gRNA selection for editing, especially of g-1 that specifies editing of the first ES and initiates editing of the mRNA, in addition to that of g-2.

Differences from eWT in the most frequent edited MURF2 and A6 sequences for each of the mutants also emphasize the impacts of each of the mutations on the relative abundances of edited MURF2 and A6 mRNAs (Fig. 6). The FE MURF2 mRNA sequences accounted for 20.9% of the reads from eWT which was reduced to 6.1%–11.7% across different mutants, with corresponding changes in PE and IE sequences, including those attributable to MURF2 g-2, (Fig. 6A). The 3′ A6 mRNA IE sequences that were obtained accounted for 75.0% of the reads in eWT and were reduced to 51.6%–66.2% depending on the mutant (Fig. 6B). As noted earlier, several mutants (Y40S, E77K, H124Y, and ΔIDR) had lower proportions of A6 sequences that could be attributed to editing via the A6 g-2 (a CR4 gRNA), which was reduced to between 7.7% and 18.7% in these mutants compared to 30.6% in eWT cells (Fig. 6). In general, the total percent of ESs that were edited per mutant was less than in eWT indicating that there was less overall editing and that the rate of editing was diminished in each mutant. Additionally, the nature of editing differed between eWT and each of the mutants, including editing attributable to gRNAs other than those required for FE MURF2 and A6, e.g. CR4 gRNA, suggesting that gRNA selection/utilization also differed between eWT and each mutant.

Discussion

Our results indicate that specific amino acids in the OB-fold and C-terminal IDR of KREPA6 are critical for the growth and viability of BF T. brucei due to their contributions to the overall organization, protein composition and structural integrity of RECCs, and to the editing process. The data suggest that the KREPA6 functions via multiple specific interactions within RECCs, likely during dynamic conformational changes that must occur during editing [26, 30, 35, 36]. We also conclude that KREPA6 functions in the selective utilization of gRNAs based on the effects that the various mutations have on the overall extent of mRNA editing and on the sequences of edited mRNAs that were likely directed by specific known gRNAs. However, KREPA6 does not appear to have a major function in the precision by which individual gRNAs specify the number of inserted or deleted Us based on the lack of substantial effects of the mutations on the fidelity of editing, i.e. number of Us in the ESs (Supplementary Figs S4 and S5).

The structural integrity of all RECCs depends on multiple KREPA6 interactions

Our analyses of cells with mutated KREPA6 expand our understanding of its importance to the structural integrity of RECCs which was previously indicated by gene expression knockdown, co-expression, protein cross-linking MS, and protein crystal structure studies [26, 30, 35, 36, 42]. These prior studies specifically showed that the OB-fold of KREPA6 is proximal to KREPA1-5, KREPB4, and several catalytic RECC proteins [35]. Strikingly, various single amino acid substitutions in KREPA6 resulted in substantial or almost complete loss of all ∼1 MDa RECCs (Figs 1 and 2). In addition, KREPA6 contributes to the overall organization of the RECCs as indicated by the occurrence of subcomplexes in cells that may also contain ∼1 MDa RECCs, all with corresponding growth and editing defects e.g. ∼242 or ∼800 kDa subcomplexes in N96Y or Y40S cells, respectively (Figs 1 and 2), and the differential effects of several substitutions on the presence of KREPA1 and KREPA2 (Fig. 2) and the KREPA1/KRET2/KREL2 and KREPA2/KREX2/KREL1 heterotrimeric insertion and deletion subcomplexes [24, 35, 63]. Hence, KREPA6 is important to the assembly and stability of RECCs.

The various substitutions alter amino acid size, charge, hydrophobicity, polarity, or shape, and affect RECC structure and editing (Supplementary Table S3) indicating that these residues have a variety of properties which likely affect their molecular interactions and therefore function. For example, the F38L substitution, which would alter a hydrophobic pocket near OB-fold loop L12, results in loss of ∼1 MDa RECCs and of subcomplexes that contain KREPA2 (Fig. 2) [30]. A substitution at an orthologous structural position in KREPA3 (F305L) also resulted in reduced abundances of ∼1 MDa RECCs and reduced editing in BFs but to a lesser extent than KREPA6 F38L [27]. Thus, hydrophobic interactions of OB-folds are essential for RECC integrity. In contrast, N96Y substitutes a polar residue in β-strand four of the OB-fold. In these cells we observed that the ∼1 MDa RECCs lack KREPA1, in addition to the presence of ∼242 kDa subcomplexes that contain KREPA3, suggesting that this substitution altered the association of KREPA1 containing subcomplexes with RECCs by interactions that involve polar residues. Similarly, the presence of ∼1 MDa RECCs and ∼800 kDa complexes in cells with the Y40S substitution and the co-sedimentation of KREPA1 and KREPA2 containing complexes at ∼15S suggests that Y40 is in a KREPA6 region that associates with the heterotrimeric insertion and deletion subcomplexes, and that the aromatic nature of this residue is important for these interactions. Together these data, combined with our previous work, indicate that the OB-fold of KREPA6 participates in multiple types of interactions with several other RECC proteins, especially those with OB-folds, in all three RECCs.

Impacts of mutations on editing and edited mRNA sequences

The variety of consequences of KREPA6 mutations to RECC structural integrity, interactions among proteins, and to RNA editing reflect its multiple functions. RT-PCR profiling and deep sequencing analyses of MURF2 and A6 mRNAs in KREPA6 mutants revealed distinct consequences to editing and indicate that KREPA6 plays a role in gRNA selection. Each of the mutations altered the progression and extent of editing of all RNAs analyzed which was especially evident in the editing of the large A6 editing domain. Some mutations, notably F38L and D68G, resulted in a dramatic loss of editing, which can be explained by the absence or substantial loss or alteration of ∼20S RECCs and underscores a structural role for KREPA6 in all three major RECCs. More detailed comparisons of editing in mutants that retained some complex and/or subcomplex to eWT showed different patterns: in MURF2, E77K and N96Y had increased IE near the 3′ end corresponding to increased editing by MURF2 g-2 that was distinct from other mutants (Fig. 5B); in A6, N96Y and N110I retained editing matching A6 g-2 (a CR4 gRNA) and broadly reduced editing matching FE (g-1 and g-3), while other mutants lost editing matching A6 g-1 and g-3 and broadly reduced editing matching g-2. Thus, different KREPA6 mutants appear to have resulted in distinct gRNA usage patterns, indicating that KREPA6, and by extension RECCs in general, play a role in gRNA utilization. In contrast, there was no compelling evidence that KREPA6 mutations substantially altered the fidelity of editing, i.e. different numbers of added or deleted Us that specified by gRNAs. The role for KREPA6 and therefore RECCs in differential gRNA selection and usage that is demonstrated by our results was intriguing. A similar role was previously described for the REH2C, where knockdown of the RNA helicase KREH2 also resulted in increased g-2 editing in A6 mRNA although this was in PF, not in BF cells [43]. Given that RESCs are also thought to promote gRNA association with mRNA substrates [19, 21], our data raise the intriguing possibility that RECCs work in tandem with REH2C and RESC to dictate gRNA–mRNA pairing and gRNA usage, and by comparison may also do so differentially in BF versus PF cells.

Implications of the OB-fold on KREPA6 function

The propensity of KREPA6 to interact with other OB-fold proteins KREPA1-5, its proximity to almost all other proteins within the RECCs, and our results showing substantial disruption to the structure and function of BF RECCs when its OB-fold is mutated all suggest that this domain is integral to RECC structure and organization [30, 35]. This is consistent with the function of other OB-fold proteins that entail both oligonucleotide binding and protein interactions [38]. That different mutations in KREPA6 differently impact the amount and characteristics of editing suggests that this protein plays a nuanced role in the editing process that likely involve specific protein–protein and possibly protein–RNA interactions as implied by both its OB-fold and ability to bind RNA in vitro [42, 68]. Future studies will be necessary to determine the nature of KREPA6 interactions with other editing proteins and/or with substrates (gRNAs and/or mRNAs). Because mutation to KREPA6 alters gRNA selection, one possibility is that KREPA6-mediated interactions with RESCs are altered, as RESCs are thought to play a critical role in gRNA–mRNA pairing. KREPA6 is a small but essential component of RECCs, and insights gained from this study can help inform not only our understanding of RNA editing in T. brucei, but also other processes mediated by (Single Strand Binding protein) SSB-related OB-fold proteins including DNA replication and repair [69].

Implications of the C-terminal IDR on KREPA6 function

Deletion of the KREPA6 C-terminal IDR resulted in only mild effects on growth and ∼1 MDa RECC integrity but affected editing (Figs 3, 5, and 6), suggesting that this domain does not adopt a fixed structure but participates in highly dynamic interactions, like other IDRs [70]. These interactions could be with KREPA1 and KREPA2, KREPB4 and KREPB5, KREN3 and KREX2 which are proximal to the KREPA6 IDR [35]. The KREPA6 domain arrangement of an OB-fold followed by a short C-terminal IDR, suggests that it may function analogously to the related SSB protein of Escherichia coli, which has the same organization [71, 72]. SSB functions as a homotetramer in which each short C-terminal IDR contacts neighboring OB-folds or other OB-folds during DNA replication and repair [71, 72]. The IDR of KREPA6 may similarly facilitate interactions with other KREPA proteins during editing, either by stabilizing OB-fold dimers and/or tetramers or by transiently interacting with other proteins during editing [26, 30]. These two protein-binding roles for the IDR are not mutually exclusive as it remains uncertain how many copies of KREPA6 are present in each RECC. However, the immunoprecipitation of both V5-tagged and untagged KREPA6 from cells expressing both proteins (Supplementary Fig. S2E) indicates the presence of multiple KREPA6 proteins per RECC which is consistent with published crystal structures, and the substantial impact of KREPA6 loss or mutation on RECC integrity and organization [26, 30, 33, 35, 36, 42]. Thus, depending on copy number, the C-terminal IDRs of different copies of KREPA6 might each function independently during editing, i.e. the IDR from one copy might bind another OB-fold protein to stabilize a dimer or tetramer while a separate IDR from a different copy of KREPA6 interacts transiently with other editing proteins or with RNA. Future studies will be needed to determine the precise role of the KREPA6 IDR in editing.

In sum, KREPA6 is a multifunctional OB-fold protein, also with a C-terminal IDR, that is present in and essential to the structural integrity and function of the three RECCs that catalyze the editing of mitochondrial mRNAs in T. brucei. It likely contributes to the functions of these RECCs via networks of precise dynamic molecular interactions within RECCs, including with other OB-fold proteins, and likely with substrate gRNA/mRNA and with RESC and AF proteins. The multiplicity and variety of KREPA6 interactions with multiple RECC proteins indicates that KREPA6 affects multiple steps in the complicated editing process. Our results show that KREPA6 functions in the selection and subsequent utilization of gRNAs, and therefore that RECCs must be actively engaged in gRNA discrimination during the editing process.
