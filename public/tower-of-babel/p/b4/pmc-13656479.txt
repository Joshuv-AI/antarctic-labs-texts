Effectiveness and safety of PD-1/PD-L1 inhibitors plus lenvatinib with or without Gemox chemotherapy in advanced intrahepatic cholangiocarcinoma
Authors: Shuofeng Li, Guanhua Yu, Shanshan Wang, Mingming Wang, Chengpei Zhu, Mingjian Piao, Ziyu Xun, Chengjie Li, Boyu Sun, Jiashuo Chao, Jie Pan, Kang Zhou, Xinyuan Chen, Xiaobo Yang, Haitao Zhao
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Background and aimsAdvanced intrahepatic cholangiocarcinoma (ICC) is characterized by a poor prognosis. This study evaluated the efficacy and safety of programmed cell death protein 1 (PD-1) or programmed death-ligand 1 (PD-L1) inhibitors combined with lenvatinib, with or without gemcitabine and oxaliplatin (Gemox) chemotherapy.MethodsA retrospective cohort analysis was performed on patients with advanced ICC who received treatment with PD-1/PD-L1 inhibitors and lenvatinib, with or without Gemox chemotherapy, between February 2017 and February 2024. Outcomes included progression-free survival (PFS), overall survival (OS), objective response rate (ORR), disease control rate (DCR), and safety.ResultsThe study included 107 patients, comprising 67 patients treated with PD-1/PD-L1 inhibitors and lenvatinib (P-L group) and 40 patients treated with PD-1/PD-L1 inhibitors, lenvatinib, and Gemox chemotherapy (P-L-G group). In comparison to the P-L group, the P-L-G group had longer median OS (16.4 months vs. 13.7 months, P = 0.047) and median PFS (11.1 months vs. 5.6 months, P = 0.004). Furthermore, the P-L-G group had a higher ORR (60.00% vs. 22.39%) and DCR (85.00% vs. 73.13%). All patients experienced adverse events (AEs), with 33 patients (49.25%) in the P-L group and 22 patients (55.00%) in the P-L-G group reporting grade 3–4 AEs. No grade 5 AEs were observed, and all AEs were manageable. Meanwhile, among patients with TP53 mutations (P = 0.048), KRAS mutations (P = 0.037), IDH1 mutations (P = 0.033), or BAP1 wild-type status (P = 0.039 and P = 0.022), the P-L-G group exhibited a more favorable prognosis compared to the P-L group.ConclusionsAdvanced ICC patients may derive greater benefit from the triple combination therapy of PD-1/PD-L1 inhibitors, lenvatinib, and Gemox chemotherapy without an increase in AEs. This regimen holds potential as an effective first-line treatment for advanced ICC.

Introduction

Intrahepatic cholangiocarcinoma (ICC), arising from biliary epithelial cells in the liver, accounts for approximately 10% to 15% of primary liver cancer cases and is the second most common type of primary liver cancer [1, 2]. ICC, the major subtype of biliary tract cancer (BTC), is a highly invasive malignancy characterized by marked aggressiveness [3]. Due to the absence of early symptoms, most patients are diagnosed at advanced stages, resulting in a five-year survival rate of only approximately 30% [4]. Currently, surgical resection is the only treatment with curative potential for ICC; however, only a minority of patients are eligible for radical resection, as most have missed the opportunity for surgery by the time of diagnosis [5–7]. Because ICC is the predominant subtype of BTC, its systemic therapy is primarily guided by landmark BTC trials. The ABC-02 trial established gemcitabine plus cisplatin (GC) as a standard first-line regimen for advanced BTC. However, with a median overall survival (mOS) of only 11.7 months and median progression-free survival (mPFS) of only 8.0 months, the efficacy of this regimen remains limited [8].

With intensive research into immune checkpoint inhibitors (ICIs), immunotherapies have demonstrated significant efficacy in various types of cancers [9, 10]. The TOPAZ-1 study showed that patients with advanced BTC who received durvalumab plus GC had improved mOS (12.9 months) compared to those treated with GC alone [11, 12]. In 2023, the KEYNOTE-966 study also confirmed that the combination of the GC regimen and pembrolizumab significantly improved prognosis (mOS: 12.7 months) compared with GC alone [13]. These pivotal studies have thereby established both chemotherapy and chemo-immunotherapy as guideline-recommended first-line treatment options for advanced ICC. Nevertheless, these regimens yield an average mOS of only about one year, highlighting the critical need for more effective and better-tolerated alternatives.

Lenvatinib, a multi-targeted tyrosine kinase inhibitor that blocks vascular endothelial growth factor (VEGF) receptors 1–3, enhances anti-tumor activity by modulating the VEGF-VEGF receptor (VEGFR) axis [14, 15]. Several clinical studies have demonstrated the synergistic efficacy and manageable toxicity profile of combining ICIs with lenvatinib as systemic therapy for BTC, reporting an objective response rate (ORR) of 17.5–42.1% and disease control rate (DCR) of 71.6–76.3% [16–18]. These studies suggest that combining targeted therapies with immunotherapy is both safe and effective in the clinical management of BTC. In 2023, a Phase II study involving 30 individuals with advanced ICC showed that ICIs combined with targeted therapy and chemotherapy produced encouraging results, with an ORR of 80%, mOS of 22.5 months, and mPFS of 10.2 months, suggesting that triple combination therapy with ICIs, lenvatinib, and gemcitabine and oxaliplatin (Gemox) chemotherapy provides substantial survival benefits with acceptable toxicity in advanced ICC [19]. However, the comparative efficacy and safety of these two treatment strategies remain controversial, particularly given the limited sample sizes of previous clinical studies evaluating triple combination therapy with ICIs, lenvatinib, and Gemox chemotherapy in advanced ICC. Hence, we conducted a retrospective study of 107 patients with advanced ICC to evaluate the safety and efficacy of ICIs plus lenvatinib with or without Gemox chemotherapy as first-line therapy.

Materials and methods

Patients

This retrospective study included patients with advanced ICC who received programmed cell death protein 1 (PD-1) or programmed death-ligand 1 (PD-L1) inhibitors plus lenvatinib with or without Gemox chemotherapy as initial systemic therapy at Peking Union Medical College Hospital (PUMCH) between February 2017 and February 2024. After screening, 107 patients who met the eligibility criteria were enrolled (Fig. 1). The inclusion criteria were as follows: (1) histopathologically confirmed ICC and radiologically defined locally advanced or metastatic disease; (2) age ≥ 18 years; (3) Eastern Cooperative Oncology Group performance status (ECOG PS) of 0 to 2; (4) Child-Pugh class A or B; (5) presence of at least one measurable lesion according to the Response Evaluation Criteria in Solid Tumors (RECIST, v1.1); and (6) receipt of at least two cycles of a PD-1/PD-L1 inhibitor plus lenvatinib combined with or without Gemox chemotherapy. Exclusion criteria included: (1) incomplete key baseline clinicopathological data; (2) incomplete documentation of adverse events (AEs); (3) previously received antitumour therapy; (4) presence of other concomitant malignancies; (5) received local-regional therapy; or (6) incomplete follow-up records. Clinical and pathological data, including age, sex, Child-Pugh score, ECOG PS, cancer antigen 19-9 (CA19-9), American Joint Committee on Cancer (AJCC) stage, histologic grade and metastatic sites, were collected and analyzed. Treatment protocol

We systematically collected data over the entire course of treatment, including treatment initiation and discontinuation dates, initial doses, laboratory findings, radiological assessments, and any AEs observed during treatment. ICIs were administered intravenously every 3 weeks, with the dosage and regimen determined according to the drug specifications and prescribing information. Specifically, pembrolizumab, sintilimab, tislelizumab, and camrelizumab were administered at 200 mg per dose, toripalimab and nivolumab at 240 mg per dose, and durvalumab at 1500 mg per dose. Lenvatinib was administered orally once daily at a dose of 12 mg (for patients ≥ 60 kg) or 8 mg (for patients < 60 kg). Systemic chemotherapy was administered using the Gemox regimen, consisting of gemcitabine (1000 mg/m2 on days 1 and 8) and oxaliplatin (100 mg/m2 on day 1). All chemotherapy cycles were administered at three-week intervals. Oxaliplatin was generally discontinued after a maximum of eight cycles to limit cumulative toxicity, whereas gemcitabine, ICIs, and lenvatinib were continued until disease progression or intolerable adverse events. Genetic testing results were also collected for patients with available genetic testing reports.

Outcome assessment and safety evaluation

Two experienced radiologists at PUMCH evaluated tumor responses using computed tomography (CT) or magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) scans. These assessments were conducted at baseline and subsequently every six to nine weeks, following RECIST v1.1 guidelines [20]. The outcomes included overall survival (OS) and progression-free survival (PFS), along with ORR, DCR and safety. OS was the time from the first treatment to death, whereas PFS was the time interval from the first treatment to disease progression or death. DCR was defined as the percentage of patients who achieved disease control, including complete response (CR), partial response (PR), or stable disease (SD). ORR was defined as the proportion of patients who obtained either CR or PR. Safety was assessed using laboratory testing, electronic medical records, and physical examinations. AEs were graded according to the Common Terminology Criteria for Adverse Events (CTCAE), version 5.0 [21].

Statistical analysis

Survival outcomes, including OS and PFS, were estimated using the Kaplan–Meier method and compared via the log-rank test. The t-test or Mann–Whitney U test for continuous variables and the chi-square test or Fisher's exact test for categorical data were used to compare individual variables. Statistical significance was established when the two-sided P-value was less than 0.05, and SPSS (version 25.0) and R (version 4.2.0) were used for all statistical analyses.

Treatment and efficacy

As of the data cutoff date of September 15, 2024, all patients had undergone radiographic response assessment. In the P-L group, 15 patients (22.39%) achieved an objective response, with 2 patients (2.99%) achieving CR and 13 patients (19.40%) achieving PR. SD was observed in 34 patients (50.75%), while 18 patients (26.87%) experienced progressive disease (PD). Overall, the ORR was 22.39% and the DCR was 73.13% in the P-L group. In the P-L-G group, 24 patients (60.00%) had an objective response, including 1 patient (2.50%) with CR and 23 patients (57.50%) with PR. Additionally, 10 patients (25.00%) had SD, and 6 patients (15.00%) had PD. Safety

AEs of different grades were observed in all patients (Table 3). In the P-L group, the most common AEs of any grade were alanine aminotransferase (ALT) or aspartate aminotransferase (AST) levels increased (36/67, 53.73%), followed by fatigue (28/67, 41.79%), hypertension (28/67, 41.79%), and increased total bilirubin levels (23/67, 34.33%). Thirty-three (49.25%) patients in the P-L group experienced grade 3–4 AEs, which were predominantly fatigue (7/67, 10.45%), hypertension (5/67, 7.46%), hepatic encephalopathy (5/67, 7.46%), increased total bilirubin levels (4/67, 5.97%), ALT or AST levels increased (3/67, 4.48%), and diarrhea (3/67, 4.48%). In the P-L-G group, fatigue was the most frequently occurring AE of any grade (22/40, 55.00%), followed by ALT or AST levels increased (18/40, 45.00%), myelosuppression (14/40, 35.00%), and vomiting (14/40, 35.00%). Twenty-two (55.00%) patients in the P-L-G group experienced grade 3–4 AEs, which were predominantly fatigue (9/40, 22.50%), hypertension (7/40, 17.50%), and myelosuppression (4/40, 10.00%). Overall, most AEs were manageable with dose modifications or supportive care, and no treatment-related deaths (grade 5 AEs) occurred in any treatment group. Discussion

In this study, we compared two therapeutic strategies for advanced ICC: ICIs plus lenvatinib and the triple combination of ICIs plus lenvatinib and Gemox chemotherapy. Our results showed that, compared with ICIs plus lenvatinib, the triple-therapy regimen was associated with more favorable clinical outcomes, including a higher ORR (60.00% vs. 22.39%), a higher DCR (85.00% vs. 73.13%), longer mPFS (11.1 vs. 5.6 months), and longer mOS (16.4 vs. 13.7 months). All patients enrolled in this study experienced varying degrees of AEs, but no grade 5 AEs occurred, and most AEs were relatively manageable in either group.

Cancer treatment has advanced substantially with the introduction of immunotherapies, such as PD-1/PD-L1 inhibitors, particularly for patients with initially unresectable tumors [22]. The triple combination therapy demonstrated superior clinical outcomes compared to the immunotherapy-targeted therapy combination. This is likely due to the synergistic effects of chemotherapy, which enhance the efficacy of immunotherapy. Chemotherapy also facilitates the upregulation of antigen presentation, selectively reduces the infiltration of immune suppressive cells such as myeloid-derived suppressor cells, and promotes the immunogenic cell death of tumor cells [23–25].

In addition to its clinical benefits, triple combination therapy demonstrated tolerable and manageable AEs in this study. A total of 22 (55.00%) patients who received the triple combination therapy experienced grade 3–4 AEs, which were primarily characterized by fatigue, hypertension and myelosuppression. One common side effect of chemotherapy is myelosuppression. In our study, the incidence of myelosuppression in patients treated with ICIs plus lenvatinib and Gemox chemotherapy was notably higher than in those treated with ICIs and lenvatinib (35.00% vs. 7.46%). Meanwhile, the rate of grade 3–4 myelosuppression was higher in the P-L-G group than in the P-L group (10.00% vs. 1.49%). Nevertheless, the treatment-related toxicities were clinically manageable through supportive care and temporary dose modifications.

Among the 107 patients included in this study, 67 had available gene profiling results, including 40 patients in the P-L group and 27 patients in the P-L-G group. Subgroup analyses of genomic alteration profiles, prognosis and objective response were subsequently performed in these 67 patients. The analysis revealed that the most frequently mutated genes were TP53 (22.39%), KRAS (19.40%), IDH1 (16.42%), and BAP1 (13.43%). TP53 is a tumor suppressor gene and one of the most frequently mutated genes in human cancers, with a reported mutation rate of approximately 17% in ICC [26, 27]. In our cohort, TP53 was the most frequently altered gene, and patients with TP53 mutations achieved significantly longer PFS in the P-L-G group than in the P-L group (P = 0.048). KRAS is a common proto-oncogene driver involved in tumorigenesis and progression [28]. In our study, KRAS was the second most frequent alteration; among patients with KRAS mutations, triple combination therapy was associated with significantly improved OS (P = 0.037), while among those with KRAS wild-type tumors, it was associated with significantly improved PFS (P = 0.022). IDH1 mutations occur in approximately 20% of ICCs and have been implicated in immune evasion [27, 29]. In our analysis, patients with IDH1 mutations in the P-L-G group had significantly longer PFS than those in the P-L group (P = 0.033). BAP1, another tumor suppressor gene, is mutated in approximately 15% of ICC [27, 30]. Although no significant survival difference was observed among patients with BAP1 mutated tumors, those with BAP1 wild-type tumors in the P-L-G group showed significantly better PFS (P = 0.039) and OS (P = 0.022), indicating that BAP1 wild-type status may be associated with greater benefit from triple combination therapy.

From a broader perspective, the next five years of research in advanced ICC are likely to move toward biomarker-guided precision treatment. Although combination systemic therapies have improved outcomes in some patients, several critical unresolved issues remain, including how to identify those most likely to benefit from specific regimens and how to integrate molecularly targeted therapies into treatment strategies. In the future, treatment selection may increasingly rely on biomarkers and gene alterations, such as KRAS, IDH1, HER2 and FGFR alterations, among others, together with clinicopathologic features and treatment tolerance. More importantly, targeted therapies directed against BTC-specific oncogenic drivers may be further combined with systemic approaches, including chemotherapy, immunotherapy, antiangiogenic therapy and local–regional therapy, to achieve more individualized and effective treatment. In addition, prospective multicenter studies with integrated biomarker analyses will be essential to refine patient selection, optimize combination strategies, and advance precision treatment in BTC.

However, this study had some limitations. First, this was a single-center retrospective study with non-randomized treatment allocation, and selection bias could not be fully excluded. Although propensity score methods may help balance baseline characteristics in retrospective studies, propensity score matching was not performed in this cohort, primarily because the limited sample size would have led to a substantial reduction in the effective sample size after matching, thereby compromising statistical power. Second, the use of multiple types of ICIs in this study may have generated bias in the assessment of safety and efficacy outcomes. Third, the lack of a dedicated analysis of immune-related adverse events (irAEs) is an important limitation of the current study. Owing to the retrospective design, irAEs could not be reliably distinguished from adverse events related to lenvatinib, Gemox chemotherapy, infection, tumor progression, biliary obstruction, or underlying hepatic dysfunction. Despite these limitations, this clinical study demonstrated the superior efficacy of the triple combination therapy involving ICIs, lenvatinib, and Gemox chemotherapy compared to the combination of ICIs and lenvatinib for advanced ICC. It provided a direct comparison of the safety and effectiveness of both regimens. These results offered valuable guidance for treatment strategies in advanced ICC and have important implications for future clinical trial design.

Conclusions

In this study, the combination of ICIs, lenvatinib, and Gemox chemotherapy was associated with greater survival benefits than ICIs plus lenvatinib, with a manageable safety profile, suggesting that this triple-therapy regimen may be a promising and well-tolerated first-line treatment option for patients with advanced untreated ICC.
