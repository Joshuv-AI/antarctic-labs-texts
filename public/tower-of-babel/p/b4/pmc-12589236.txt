Evaluating inconsistences, generalizability, and heterogeneity in analysis of carbetocin versus oxytocin after cesarean delivery in high-risk women
Authors: Marcela da Silva Kazitani Cunha, Lucas M Barbosa, Maria Beatriz de Moraes Bastos Gonçalves
Published: 2025

We commend El-Goly et al. [1] for addressing the critical issue of postpartum hemorrhage prevention in high-risk women undergoing cesarean delivery by comparing carbetocin and oxytocin. However, we have identified several methodological concerns that limit the robustness of their findings and merit further discussion.

First, the statistical methods employed raise significant issues. The authors conducted tests for overall effect within subgroup analyses, which is inappropriate as it leads to overlapping populations and artificially inflates the precision of subgroup findings. Subgroup analyses should focus on interaction tests to explore group differences rather than recalculating an overall pooled effect that duplicates patients already included in the primary analysis. This is particularly concerning as the results section states that 3068 participants were included, yet Fig. 9 analyzes 3652 patients, potentially misleading readers to an inaccurate conclusion [2].

While the authors’ use of the GRADE assessment to evaluate evidence quality is commendable, their reported certainty appears misleading. GRADE evaluates domains, such as risk of bias, inconsistency, indirectness, imprecision, and publication bias [3]. For example, in the blood loss endpoint, the authors did not address publication bias, despite Cochrane's recommendation to report it when more than ten studies are involved [2]. Although they indicated a low risk of bias for this endpoint, Fig. 3 reveals that 8 of the 11 studies had a high risk of bias in at least one domain, with heterogeneity reaching 97%.

Moreover, the absence of subgroup analyses in the blood loss endpoint, despite high heterogeneity, fails to clarify the source of inconsistency. No leave-one-out sensitivity analysis was performed to determine if a single study disproportionately influenced the pooled result [2]. Without these evaluations, the robustness of the primary outcome is highly questionable. Consequently, the true certainty of the evidence should be downgraded to at least low. This issue with GRADE assessment and heterogeneity also extends to other endpoints, such as “the need for additional uterotonic agents”.

Another significant concern is the inappropriate inclusion of reference 20 [4]. This trial compared carbetocin with oxytocin in combination with misoprostol, introducing a confounding factor. The use of an additional uterotonic alters physiological effects and risks biasing pooled estimates in favor of one intervention. Including this study under the premise of a direct carbetocin-versus-oxytocin comparison undermines the validity of the meta-analysis.

Lastly, the consistent use of odds ratios (OR) as effect measures poses additional issues. While ORs are widely applied, they can substantially overestimate or underestimate effect magnitude when the incidence of outcomes is high, as seen in postpartum hemorrhage. Risk ratios (RR) would have offered a more accurate and clinically interpretable estimate of treatment effect, particularly for decision-making in obstetric practice [5].

Meta-analyses depend on the rigor and characteristics of the included studies. Findings are not generalizable when the design is biased or clinically implausible. We appreciate the authors’ focus on an underrepresented population and hope this feedback will enhance future research in this vital area.
