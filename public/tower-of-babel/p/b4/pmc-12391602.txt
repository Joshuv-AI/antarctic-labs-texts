An in vivo comparative study of pure Mg and Mg-Zn alloy plates implanted in the mandible of New Zealand rabbits
Authors: Altaib Abdel Razik Mohammed, Wafaa Yahia Alghonemy, Naglaa I. Sarhan, Abdalla Abdal-hay, Reda Gaber Saleh
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
BackgroundBiodegradable magnesium-based implants present significant potential for maxillofacial applications, particularly in mandible fixation, due to their osteoconductive properties and elimination of secondary removal surgeries.ObjectiveThe present study aimed to assess the in vivo biocompatibility and degradation behavior of pure magnesium (Mg) and Mg-Zn-Ca alloy plates implanted in the mandibles of New Zealand rabbits, providing insights into their clinical viability.Materials and methodsTwenty-four male New Zealand White rabbits were divided into two groups and received titanium, pure Mg, and Mg-Zn-Ca alloy plates passively implanted into the mandibular bone surface. Animals were evaluated histologically and radiographically at 4 and 8 weeks postoperatively.ResultsHistomorphologic analysis demonstrated that all implants showed clinical stability throughout the observation period. While no signs of inflammation were observed in the pure Ti and pure Mg groups, the Mg-Zn-Ca alloy group exhibited moderate to severe inflammatory cell infiltration at 4 weeks and localized bone resorption at 8 weeks, as confirmed histologically. Remarkably, new bone formation was observed surrounding the Mg implants, with no evidence of fibrous capsule development. Mg-Zn-Ca biodegradation had no adverse systemic effects as the histological evaluations using H&E staining of major organs revealed no significant abnormalities.ConclusionMg-based materials show promise for maxillofacial fixation applications due to their osteogenic potential and biodegradability. However, the observed inflammatory responses and soft tissue effects highlight the need for further optimization of alloy composition and degradation control before clinical translation.

Introduction

In craniofacial fractures and orthopedic surgeries, internal fixation using metal plates is a standard treatment approach.1 Titanium (Ti) and its alloys are considered the gold standard materials for bone fracture fixation, primarily due to their excellent corrosion resistance, biocompatibility, and suitable mechanical properties.2,3 Despite these advantages, Ti and its alloys are associated with several postoperative complications, including tissue irritation, inflammation, infection, pain, thermal sensitivity, implant exposure, and aesthetic concerns. Furthermore, they often require a second surgical procedure for removal, which can increase patient stress, recovery time, and healthcare costs.4, 5, 6

To address these limitations, recent research has shifted towards biodegradable metallic implants, particularly those made from magnesium (Mg) and its alloys. These materials can potentially eliminate the complications associated with permanent implants, as they naturally degrade within the body. Using biodegradable implants like Mg reduces the need for a secondary removal surgery, minimizing surgical risks, lowering the chances of postoperative stress in the bone, and improving patient outcomes.7

Mg and its alloys are considered ideal candidates for biodegradable metallic materials due to their excellent biocompatibility, bioresorbability, and osteogenic potential.8 During degradation, Mg ions are released that promote bone healing and accelerate bone formation.9, 10, 11 These properties have made Mg-based materials widely applicable in various bone fixation devices, such as plates, screws, pins, rods, trabecular or lattice structures, and modular components.12 Pure Mg plates have been studied extensively in maxillofacial surgery, demonstrating favorable biocompatibility, osteogenic properties, cell viability, and low infection rates.11,13,14 Moreover, tissue culture assays have confirmed that Mg plates' corrosion products are cytocompatible.13, 14, 15 Studies have reported significant mineral deposition and enhanced bone density around Mg implants.9,16

However, the rapid degradation of pure Mg leads to excessive hydrogen gas production, which has been a significant barrier to clinical success.17,18 Noviana et al.19 Hydrogen gas can accumulate from muscle to subcutaneous tissue, causing severe emphysema. Witte18 Identified hydrogen gas generation and cavity formation as key factors for discontinuing the use of Mg plates in clinical settings. To mitigate these issues, recent research has focused on alloying Mg to control its degradation rate19.

Advanced Mg alloy development has demonstrated promising outcomes, including superior mechanical properties, enhanced biocompatibility, and controlled degradation rates.20, 21, 22, 23 One of the best candidate alloys is Mg-Zn, which has demonstrated excellent biocompatibility and controlled degradation rates. Zinc is an essential trace element known for its biodegradation and antibacterial properties, vital for physiological processes in mammals24, 25, 26. Zheng et al.7 Highlighted that zinc exhibits a moderate corrosion potential (0.76 V), which falls between that of magnesium (−2.37 V) and iron (−0.44 V), making it a strong contender for use in biodegradable alloys.

Thus, the current work uses a rabbit model to investigate the in vivo degradation behavior of pure Mg and Mg-Zn alloy plates when applied to mandibular bone fixation. New Zealand white rabbits are a well-established animal model for studying bone healing, remodeling, and osseointegration due to their reliable clinical and histological properties27,28 This research will comprehensively analyze the biocompatibility and degradation characteristics of pure Mg versus Mg-Zn alloy plates implanted in the mandibles of New Zealand rabbits.

Materials and methods

Sample preparation and sterilization before implantation

The pure Ti, pure Mg, and Mg-Zn-Ca alloy plates, each measuring 15 mm in length, 10 mm in width, and 3 mm in thickness, were manufactured by the Egyptian Central Metallurgical Research & Development Institute (CMRDI), Cairo, Egypt. All samples were sterilized under dry autoclave conditions before implantation.

Sample size calculation

The minimal sample size was calculated using data based on a previous study29 The sample size was calculated based on the bone-fixation plate's biodegradable Mg alloy. Based on Byun et al.29 Results, adopting a power of 80 % (β = 0.20) to detect a standardized effect size bone fixation (primary outcome) of 0.745 and a level of significance of 5 % (α error accepted = 0.05), the minimum required sample size was 24 male New Zealand white rabbits. Any specimen loss from the study sample due to processing error will be replaced to maintain the sample size.

In vivo study

This study was reviewed and approved by the Ethical Committee of the Faculty of Dentistry at Tanta University, adhering to the ethical guidelines for medical research involving experimental animals according to ARRIVE guidelines. 24 male New Zealand white rabbits, weighing 2.5 and 3.0 kg, weighing 2.5 and 3.0 kg were sourced from a local breeder. The animals were housed in a controlled environment with regulated lighting and temperature, and they were provided with standard food and water throughout the study.

All animals in Group I underwent a sham operation. In contrast, animals in Groups II, III, and IV received surgical implantation of their respective plates against the bone surface of the mandible. Six rabbits were randomly euthanized at each of the 4-week and 8-week time points post-surgery using cervical dislocation. This split-mouth design allowed for intra-animal comparison while minimizing inter-animal variability and potential material interference.

Samples characterization

The pure Mg and Mg-Zn alloy plates were analyzed using the X-ray fluorescence (XRF) technique to determine their elemental compositions. A crucial stage in sample preparation involves smoothing the sample's surface to minimize X-ray diffraction. The plates were analyzed in the central lab of an XRD device.

Surgical procedure

All surgical procedures were conducted under general anesthesia. Premedication was achieved with intramuscular (IM) administration of atropine sulfate (0.4 ml/kg). Anesthesia was induced with ketamine hydrochloride (10 %; 0.5 ml/kg) and xylazine (2 %; 0.2 ml/kg) administered intramuscularly into the hind limb thigh muscle. Local anesthesia was provided by infiltration of mepivacaine hydrochloride (2 %) with adrenaline (1:80,000) along the planned surgical site.

Following aseptic preparation with a povidone-iodine solution, a skin incision was made in the posterior submandibular region. Blunt and sharp dissection exposed the inferior border of the mandible. Meticulous hemostasis was maintained throughout the procedure. The metal plates were securely implanted against the mandible's outer cortex of the right ramus as onlay grafts and gently positioned within the pocket without mechanical fixation (i.e., no screws), to prevent stress-induced bone resorption and to mimic passive biological integration Fig. 1. The soft tissue was then sutured tightly to stabilize the implant and to avoid movement. This technique is consistent with previously published methodologies.30 Postoperative care included the administration of antibiotics and analgesics for 5 days.Fig. 1A. submandibular approach. Cone beam computed tomography (CBCT) examination

Following surgical removal, the whole mandible specimens, including the implanted plates, were immediately fixed in Karnowski's solution (5 % paraformaldehyde + 4 % glutaraldehyde in 0.1 M phosphate buffer, pH 7.4) for 24 h. This fixation protocol ensured optimal bone and soft tissue preservation for subsequent analysis. Cone beam computed tomography (CBCT) imaging was performed using a SCANORA 3Dx CBCT dental unit (Soredex, Helsinki, Finland) to evaluate the position and integrity of the plates and the surrounding mandibular bone and soft tissues. Tissue preparations

Following CBCT examination, the mandible specimens were further processed for histological analysis. The implanted plates were carefully removed and preserved in Karnowski's solution for future scanning electron microscopy (SEM) evaluation. To prepare the specimens for light microscopy (LM), they underwent a decalcification process to remove the mineralized bone component. This allowed for the creation of thin sections suitable for microscopic examination. The decalcified specimens were then processed and embedded in paraffin wax, and sections were cut and stained with hematoxylin and eosin (H&E). This standard staining technique allows for visualization of cellular and tissue morphology.

The stained sections were examined using a Leica DM500 light microscope equipped with a Leica ICC50 HD camera system. This high-quality imaging system allowed for detailed assessment of the soft tissue and mandibular bone surrounding the implant sites, enabling evaluation of tissue response, inflammation, and bone healing. In addition to the local tissue analysis, systemic effects were also investigated. The liver, kidney, and lung were harvested from all animals and processed for histopathological examination. This analysis aimed to identify any potential systemic effects related to the implanted materials or the surgical procedure.

Scanning electron microscopic examination

For SEM analysis, the metal plates were carefully removed from Karnowski's solution and subjected to dehydration. This involved increasing concentrations of ethanol washes, ultimately removing all water from the specimens. Following dehydration, the plates were treated with hexamethyldisilazane (HMDS), a drying agent that helps prevent surface tension-induced damage during the final drying stage.

The prepared plates were then mounted onto appropriate SEM stubs using a conductive adhesive to ensure proper grounding. To enhance conductivity and image quality, the specimens were coated with a thin layer of gold using a Blazers SCD-050 ion sputtering evaporator device. This gold coating minimizes electron charging effects during SEM imaging.

Finally, the surface morphology of the metal plates was analyzed using a JSM-IT200 scanning electron microscope (JEOL). This high-resolution imaging technique allowed for a detailed examination of the plate surfaces, enabling assessment of surface topography, any signs of corrosion or degradation, and cellular adhesion.-This study was based on qualitative assessments including histopathological, radiological, and morphological observations. Surface analysis

Studying the morphology and structural characteristics of commercially pure Ti, pure Mg, and Mg-Zn alloys is crucial when assessing their potential for use in biomedical applications, such as bone tissue engineering. In this context, examining the surface properties can provide valuable insights into the materials' biocompatibility and degradation behavior of the plates before and after implantation. In this study, pure Mg and a Mg-Zn alloy plate were compared, with a particular focus on their morphology and surface damage. Clinical study

The postoperative period passed smoothly, with good wound healing. However, there was obvious edema on both sides, and it was larger at the site of Mg-Zn alloy, which was during sample preparation. In some rabbits, the edema decreased with the aspiration of generated gases. Experimental groups (groups III and IV)

In Group III (pure Mg-plate), histological analysis at 4 weeks post-surgery revealed that the mandibular bone exhibited a nearly standard structure with mild inflammatory cell infiltration. However, the skeletal muscle adjacent to the Mg plate was in an advanced degeneration phase, as shown in Fig. 7-B1. Additionally, large gas spaces and moderate inflammatory cell infiltration were observed between remodeled muscle fibers (Fig. 7-B4). By 8 weeks post-surgery, the mandibular bone demonstrated a completely normal histological structure. Nevertheless, the skeletal muscle displayed specific abnormalities, including areas of dilated blood capillaries near the Mg plate, small gas spaces between muscle fibers, and fibrous tissue formation surrounding the plate (Fig. 7-B3 and B4). In Group IV (Mg-Zn alloy plate), histological analysis at 4 weeks post-surgery revealed an intact periosteum but extensive bone surface resorption. Moderate to severe inflammatory cell infiltration was observed beneath the Mg-Zn alloy plate. Additionally, the skeletal muscle displayed advanced degeneration, characterized by large gas spaces (Fig. 7-B5 and B6).

By 8 weeks post-implantation, the mandibular bone exhibited significant bone resorption, with large gas-filled spaces replacing the resorbed bone. A thin layer of granulation tissue was observed near the Mg-Zn alloy plate. Meanwhile, skeletal muscle fibers showed evidence of remodeling, indicating a transitional phase of recovery (Fig. 7-B7 and B8). These findings underscore the dynamic tissue response to Mg-alloy plates, marked by progressive bone resorption and localized skeletal muscle remodeling over time.

H&E-stained sections of the renal cortex of Groups I & II after 4 & 8 weeks demonstrated normal renal architecture in the form of Bowman's capsule surrounded by normal tubules. However, focal glomerular sclerosis and vascular congestion and dilatation were observed in renal sections of rats in groups III and IV opened after 4 weeks. Furthermore, the renal cortex of the Mg implantation group opened after 8 weeks revealed standard renal architecture in the form of many normal-looking renal tubules surrounding Bowman's capsule (Fig. 8 D, E, F).

Lung

Lung sections of the groups (G1& GII) after 4 and 8 weeks showed standard pulmonary architecture in the form of alveoli, alveolar sac, alveolar duct, respiratory bronchiole, and terminal bronchiole with thin interalveolar septa, and pulmonary blood vessels. On the other hand, those groups III &IV sacrificed after 4 weeks showed thickened interalveolar septal hemorrhage in association with extensive dilatation of alveoli, in association with focal inflammatory cells in a few areas. The lung of the Mg implantation group (GIII &IV) opened after 8 weeks and displayed standard pulmonary architecture (Fig. 8 G, H, I).

The SEM analysis of pure Mg revealed several noteworthy findings. First and foremost, the Mg-Zn alloy exhibited larger cracks and more pronounced surface damage (as shown in Fig. 9) compared to the pure Mg plate. However, the control samples of pure Ti did not show any significant difference in the 2 and 8 weeks of implantation. These cracks and surface irregularities on Mg implants indicate the degradation process experienced by the Mg alloy when exposed to direct contact with the surrounding physiological conditions during implantation. Discussion

According to a previous study, the metal blocks were sterilized by autoclaving because it does not cause structural changes, while dry heating causes surface cracks. Clinically, magnesium alloy showed no adverse reaction in this study, and this is due to its biocompatibility. The obvious complication in this study was the edema due to hydrogen release during magnesium degradation; however, according to Byun et al.,29 the hydrogen gas is nontoxic and used for medical purposes.29 Through this study, it was noticed that edema in the case of pure magnesium is less than that associated with magnesium alloy, and this is in accordance with the study of Hofstetter. Edema can be decreased by using interrupted sutures during surgery, and if it reaches a large size, it can be reduced by aspiration using a wide-pore syringe.31, 32, 33 XRF analysis revealed the presence of trace impurities as lead (0.52 wt%) and titanium (0.21 wt%) in the Mg-Zn-Ca alloy. Pb is known to be cytotoxic and pro-inflammatory, even at low concentrations, and may have contributed to the moderate-to-severe inflammatory response and bone resorption observed at 4–8 weeks in Group IV. Although Ti is generally biocompatible, its inclusion as an unintended impurity raises concerns regarding alloy homogeneity and purity.34,35 These findings underscore the importance of refining alloy processing and purification methods to eliminate trace contaminants that may impair biocompatibility and accelerate corrosion.

Histologically, the mandibular bone showed nearly standard histological structure of bone in both control and experimental groups throughout the two follow-up periods, except in the 8th week of the Mg-alloy group, which showed extensive areas of bone resorption. This was in agreement with Naujokat et al.,36 who studied the Pure-Mg plates and screws for internal fixation of a cranio-osteoplasty in minipigs and found that Pure-Mg plates led to uninterrupted bone healing in all cases, and the biocompatibility of Mg with bone and soft tissue is sufficient. Also, Byun et al.29 Concluded that the histological examination of the newly formed bone showed no specific differences between the Ti-plate and the Mg-Zn alloy (WE43) plate.

The fundamental cellular and developmental processes involved in muscle biology are essential indicators of the muscle's response to its surroundings. Muscle remodeling in the present study appeared on the negative control, Ti-plate, and Mg-alloy plate after 8 weeks. In the negative control group, this remodeling is considered an adaptive response that maintains a balance between physiological demands for contractile work and the capacity of skeletal muscle to meet those demands.37 For the Ti-plate and Mg-alloy plate group, this remodeling is considered a response to injury and physiological or pathological repair.38 However, skeletal muscle degeneration appeared in the 4th week of both group III and group IV. Also, muscle fibrosis appeared in the 8th week of group III. After skeletal muscle injury, they undergo a sequence of events, including degeneration, inflammation, repair, regeneration, and scar formation.39 The degree of this response and the sequence's rate depend upon the injury's magnitude, the anatomic location of the insult, and the environmental factors.40 Interestingly, advanced muscle degeneration was observed adjacent to the implantation site in Group III (pure Mg) at 4 weeks (Fig. 7-B1). This may be attributed to the local release of hydrogen gas and rapid corrosion by-products, such as hydroxide and magnesium ions, which can alter pH and create an unfavorable microenvironment for surrounding soft tissues. Similar findings have been reported in previous studies involving rapidly degrading Mg implants, where gas accumulation and local alkalinity contributed to muscle fiber degeneration and tissue necrosis.12 This highlights the need to improve the corrosion resistance and surface modification of pure Mg to minimize soft tissue damage.

Inflammatory cell infiltration was detected in the 4th week for groups II, Mg-plate, and Mg-alloy. Moreover, in the 8th week, the fibrous tissue was depicted in the Mg-plate group, and the granulation tissue in the Mg-alloy group. The inflammatory infiltration is due to the inflammatory response related to the anesthetized injury, which could be considered a surgical inflammation.41 This surgical inflammation comprises a series of overlapping successive phases, the second phase after injury.42 The fibrous tissue is secondary to excessive traumatic tissue necrosis and is considered a mark of unhealthy wound healing.43 Importantly, the formation of granulation tissue is a factor in the wound healing process, which guards the wound surface against microbial invasion and further injury, seals the wound with new tissue and vasculature, and substitutes the necrotic tissue until replacement by scar tissue43, 44, 45.

Gas spaces appeared in both Mg-plate and Mg-alloy groups and extended throughout the two follow-up periods, in agreement with Byun et al.,29 who found swelling and gas formation in the dogs of the Mg-alloy (WE43) group starting at 8 weeks. Hydrogen gas usually develops due to the corrosion reaction of Mg at normal body temperature. The amount may reach 1 L of hydrogen gas from the degradation of 1 g of pure Mg, which accumulates under the muscles, forming a cavity.18,46 Also, Kraus et al.47 discovered that hydrogen gas initiates pressure on the bone, decreasing its regeneration and increasing the possibility of callus formation. It also changes the pH, which affects the viability of cells and may cause tissue inflammation.48 This might account for the bone resorption in group IV in the 8th week.

Light microscopic examination of the liver, kidney, and lung of group I and II after 4 & 8 weeks revealed their well-known normal histological features. Former studies focused on systemic titanium burden, indicating that titanium mainly accumulated in the liver of mice after TiO2 particle treatment, but the activities of liver enzymes AST and ALT did not change much.49 Another study carried out on titanium and titanium oxide-coated plates in rat tibiae recorded an initial increase in liver enzymes AST, ALT, and LDH after one month and a decrease after 2 months. Similarly, serum urea increased significantly after one month, and normalization was achieved after 2 months without effect on serum creatinine.50 The absence of remarkable effects in the tested organs in our study could be attributed to the presence of a unilateral implant on the right side. These results were opposite to those mentioned in the study of Swalsky et al.51 Regarding the systemic response to titanium implants, which could be attributed to the use of TiO2 nanoparticles.

On the other hand, the experimental group (groups III & IV) showed hepatic, renal, and pulmonary histological alterations in the form of fibrosis, and dilated congested blood vessels associated with inflammatory cellular infiltration after 4 weeks. Magnesium implants, especially in non-bioresorbable forms or those with excessive magnesium release, may induce local inflammation or fibrosis in the liver, kidney, and lung tissue associated with inflammatory reactions to foreign materials. However, most biodegradable magnesium alloys are designed to minimize this risk, as reported by Zhang et al.52 Applying Mg-based materials as biomedical implants revealed rapid degradation of Mg, leading to increased local pH and H2 release shortly after implantation and possible premature mechanical failure of the implants.

Short-term toxicity of the degradation products of magnesium has attracted the attention of many researchers. Inserting magnesium-rich implants into the tissue produces different decomposition substances like hydroxides, oxides, chlorides, and magnesium apatites in addition to Mg ions, hydrogen gas, and OH2 ions. These decomposition products are generated because of the reaction of implant materials with their environment. The burst release of these corrosion products can overburden the physiological regulating mechanisms like physiological buffering, diffusion, and convection, which subsequently negatively impact cell behavior, tissue response, and healing. The physiological regulating mechanism might aid in excreting these degradation products or integrating them into the natural metabolic process.53

After eight weeks, Groups III and IV showed that the histological changes had been resolved. Tie et al.54 recorded similar results when they carried out their work on Mg–1Sr alloy and pure Mg plates and screws that were implanted into the femurs of 18 rabbits and were followed up for 16 weeks. Histopathology of peri-implant muscle, spleen, kidney, and liver, together with testing of hematological, inflammatory, cardiac, and hepatic responses of samples, demonstrated that the implantation of Mg–1Sr alloy and pure Mg did not trigger significant inflammation and did not induce adverse effects.

From SEM images, pure Mg showed a considerable amount of damage compared to Mg-Zn alloy. This suggests that the Mg alloy might experience a lower degradation rate under the same conditions. The surface of the pure Mg appeared to be more structurally stable, with fewer cracks and signs of damage. The observed differences in surface morphology between pure Mg and the Mg alloy have important implications for their potential applications in bone tissue engineering. The larger cracks and surface damage in Mg alloy indicate that it degrades more rapidly when exposed to physiological environments55,56. This is a critical factor to consider when selecting materials for biomedical implants, as a slower degradation rate can lead to longer-lasting implants.56 Excessive ion release from degrading materials can adversely affect the body, causing inflammation and tissue damage. The pure Mg's ability to maintain its structural integrity while degrading slowly indicates that it may release ions at a more controlled and biocompatible rate. One of the key concerns when using Mg-based materials in biomedical applications is the potential for hydrogen gas formation during degradation.57 Excessive hydrogen gas production can be detrimental to surrounding tissues. Pure Mg induced less surface damage, and during observation of the samples during implantation, it was found that pure Mg has lower hydrogen gas formation around the tissue and, subsequently, less necrosis than the Mg-Zn alloy. Likely, the presence of zinc, Ca, and Oxides around the Mg alloy implant influences the absorbance of hydrogen gas compared to the pure Mg.56,58,59 Furthermore, osteoblast cells seem attached to the pure Mg samples after implantation for 2 and 8 weeks (Fig. 3). This further verifies that pure Mg forms a passive layer and, subsequently, has higher surface stability and lower surface degradation. After 8 weeks of implantation, both pure and Mg alloy samples induced mineralization/corrosion product formation onto their surface.

The degradation behavior of the tested materials played a central role in the observed biological responses. Pure magnesium (Group III) exhibited a relatively slower degradation rate, which limited early structural damage and maintained implant integrity. However, histological evaluation revealed advanced muscle degeneration adjacent to the implant at 4 weeks, likely due to localized pH changes, hydrogen gas release, and ionic by-products from corrosion60,61 In contrast, the Mg-Zn-Ca alloy (Group IV) degraded more rapidly, as evidenced by surface cracking on SEM and loss of material integrity. This accelerated degradation was associated with a more pronounced inflammatory response, including moderate to severe inflammatory cell infiltration at 4 weeks, and bone resorption at 8 weeks, indicating that the higher corrosion rate may have overwhelmed the tissue's ability to remodel and repair.53,61 These results emphasize the importance of controlling degradation rates to avoid adverse effects on both soft and hard tissues.

Although this study focused on the in vivo degradation behavior of Mg-based materials, we recognize the importance of complementary in vitro analysis. Previous research has extensively addressed the in vitro degradation mechanisms of Mg alloys.62 However, in vivo studies remain limited and are crucial to understanding real-time biological responses. Our decision to concentrate on in vivo analysis was made to avoid redundancy and to emphasize clinically relevant outcomes. Future studies should integrate both in vitro and in vivo approaches for a more comprehensive evaluation.

Conclusion

This study provides compelling evidence that biodegradable magnesium-based implants, particularly Mg-Zn alloys, hold significant promise for maxillofacial applications, specifically in mandible fixation. In Vivo, findings demonstrate excellent biocompatibility, complete implant stability, and robust bone regeneration without adverse systemic effects or inflammatory responses for pure Mg. Notably, the absence of fibrous capsule formation further supports the osteoconductive potential of these materials. The Mg-Zn-Ca alloy demonstrated promising biocompatibility and initial mechanical stability; however, its degradation rate was relatively rapid, as confirmed by surface cracking and associated inflammatory response. Further alloy optimization may be required to achieve more controlled degradation and minimize adverse reactions. The. These results underscore the clinical viability of ultra-pure Mg-based implants, paving the way for further translational studies and potential adoption in surgical practice. Future investigations should focus on refining degradation kinetics and conducting long-term clinical trials to establish these degradable biomaterials as a standard alternative to conventional fixation systems.

Study limitations

The presence of toxic impurities such as Pb in the Mg-Zn-Ca alloy may have influenced the local tissue response and degradation behavior. Future studies should use high-purity alloy compositions to isolate the effects of the principal elements and improve biocompatibility. In addition, the short-term systemic toxicity observed in vital organs at 4 weeks underscores the need for extended biocompatibility evaluation and long-term safety studies in larger animal models. While the qualitative findings offer valuable insight into the biological response to different materials, future studies should include measurable endpoints and statistical analysis, such as quantitative degradation kinetics as well as Masson's trichrome staining and/or bone morphometric analysis to validate and strengthen these observations.
