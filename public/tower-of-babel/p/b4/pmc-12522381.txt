Association between game addiction and lifetime psychotic-like experiences among high school students: a cross-sectional study
Authors: Li-Hua Lin, Wei-Hao Luo, Jia-Li Wang, Rui Wang, Meng Sun, Liang Zhou
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundPsychotic-like experiences (PLEs) and game addiction are common among young individuals. The present study aimed to explore the association between game addiction and lifetime PLEs among high school students in Guangzhou, China.MethodsData for this study was derived from the Mental Health and Behavior Disorders among Children and Young People in Guangzhou Survey, which utilized a multistage stratified cluster sampling design. The 15-item positive subscale of the Community Assessment of Psychic Experiences (CAPE-15) was used to assess lifetime PLEs. Univariate and multivariate logistic regression analysis were applied to explore the lifetime PLEs-related factors.ResultsA total of 5581 high school students aged 13 to 20 years were included in the analysis. The prevalence of lifetime PLEs and game addiction among high school students was 44.9% (n = 2507) and 15.0% (n = 835), respectively. Multivariate logistic regression analysis showed that age (OR = 0.86, 95%CI: 0.80–0.93),single child (OR = 1.17, 95%CI: 1.03–1.34), perceived academic stress (OR = 1.10, 95%CI: 1.07–1.14), loneliness (OR = 1.10, 95%CI: 1.08–1.12), social support (OR = 0.93, 95%CI: 1.18–1.23), PHQ-9 score (OR = 1.20, 95%CI: 1.18–1.23), game use time for more than 4 h per week (vs. 0 h) (OR = 1.53, 95%CI: 1.21–1.93), and game addiction (OR = 1.56, 95%CI: 1.29–1.88) were influencing factors of lifetime PLEs (P < 0.05).ConclusionThe prevalence of lifetime PLEs is high among high school students, and PLEs are related to a variety of psychosocial and behavioral factors. Lifetime PLEs are strongly related to game addiction and long game use time among high school students.Keywords: Game addiction, Game use time, Lifetime psychotic-like experiences

Background

Psychotic-like experiences (PLEs) were defined as experiences similar to psychotic symptoms at a subclinical level, involving paranoia, hallucination, and cognitive symptoms [1]. PLEs are common among young individuals, with a 12-month prevalence of 72% in those aged 15 to 25 years in the USA [2].

PLEs are correlated with psychotic disorders. A substantial amount of literature has documented that people with PLEs have a higher risk of mental disorders [3–6]. A meta-analysis of 14 studies found that PLEs were linked to a three-times increased risk of having a diagnosis of any mental disorder in childhood and adolescence. According to a 15-year longitudinal study, adolescents with PLEs have a 5 to 16 times increasing risk of schizophrenia-spectrum disorder [4]. Approximately 20% of individuals with subclinical psychotic experiences grow into persistent psychotic experiences, and 7.4% develop a psychotic disorder outcome, with an annual transition rate below 1% [5]. Based on the data from the Global Burden of Disease (GBD), psychotic disorder has posed an immense economic burden for health-care systems [7]. Therefore, understanding the risk factors of PLEs was crucial for the early detection and intervention of psychotic disorders.

Game addiction was first formulated as a type of internet addiction by Young in 2007 and performed three basic characteristics of behavioral addiction, involving a lack of self-control, withdrawal symptoms, and tolerance [8, 9]. A meta-analysis reported that the prevalence of game addiction was 8.8% among adolescents [10]. In China, high school students are undergoing huge academic stress due to college entrance examinations and may engage in video game playing as a means of alleviating academic stress [11]. However, playing games for a long time and game addiction could also impair learning and sleep quality, even leading to physical and mental health problems [9, 12].

The adolescent period is a critical window for the prevention and intervention of mental disorders [13]. Both PLEs and game addiction may be related to poor mental health in adolescents [14]. PLEs and game addiction shared similar related factors, such as loneliness, depression, and academic stress in student groups [15–17]. Previous studies have studied the association between PLEs and media or internet use. A population-based longitudinal study reported that adolescent media use was associated with lifetime PLEs [18]. Greater media use and problematic internet use were positively related to higher current PLEs [19, 20]. The specific relationship between game addiction and PLEs is still unclear. Game addiction is considered a modifiable behavioral factor in adolescents. The relationship between game addiction and PLEs deserves further investigation.

Thus, the present study had two aims: (1) estimating the prevalence of lifetime PLEs and game addiction among high school students in Guangzhou, and (2) examining the association between game addiction and lifetime PLEs, game use time and lifetime PLEs among high school students. Based on the results of previous studies, it was hypothesized that game addiction and long game use time were strongly associated with lifetime PLE.

Methods

Study design and participants

A cross-sectional study was conducted in Guangzhou, Guangdong Province, China. Data for this study were derived from the Mental Health and Behavior Disorders among Children and Young People in Guangzhou Survey, which utilized a multistage stratified cluster sampling design. Firstly, the 11 districts of Guangzhou were divided into 2 layers according to the main urban area and the non-main urban area, and 2 districts were randomly selected from the 4 districts in the main urban area and 4 districts from the 7 districts in the non-main urban area. Then, the schools in the 6 districts were divided into 36 layers by school level (primary school, junior high school, senior high school) and school type (whether provincial demonstration or not), and 1 school was randomly selected from each layer. A total of 36 schools were selected, including 12 primary schools, 12 junior high schools, and 12 senior high schools. Finally, classes were randomly selected from the selected schools. In primary schools, 6 classes were randomly selected from grades 1–3, and 7 classes were randomly selected from grades 4 to 6. A total of 6 classes were randomly selected from each middle school and high school. Due to the impact of COVID-19, only information of students in grades 10 to 12 from 5 selected high schools was collected. This study started in December 2020 and ended in May 2021 [21].

This study was conducted in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki and approved by the Ethics Committees of the Affiliated Brain Hospital of Guangzhou Medical University (2020003). All parents of participants agreed to attend this survey and provided written informed consent. Written informed consent was obtained from all participants and their parents. The Strengthening the Reporting of Observational Studies in Epidemiology (STROBE) guideline was used to report this observational study.

Data collection and variables

Participants filled in the questionnaire online. Investigation covariates involved age, gender (male/female), grade, ethnicity (Han/others), residence (city/town/village), parental marital status (currently married/currently unmarried), single child (yes/no), self-rated academic performance, perceived academic stress, boarders (yes/no), social support, and game addiction (yes/no). After the investigation was completed, the researchers reviewed the questionnaires and deleted the invalid ones. Questionnaires that were missing more than 20% of the content or completed within 2 min were defined as invalid questionnaires.

Assessment

Lifetime psychotic-like experiences

Lifetime psychotic-like experiences (PLEs) were assessed by the 15-item positive subscale of the Community Assessment of Psychic Experiences (CAPE-P15). CAPE-P15 was an effective tool for screening PLEs among senior high school students [22, 23]. It consists of two subscales: the frequency and associated distress subscale. Every item concluded with four options: “1 = never”, “2 = sometimes”, “3 = often” and “4 = almost”. Refer to previous research; those who scored more than 3 points on any items of the frequency subscale were considered as suffering lifetime PLEs in this study [24]. The Cronbach’s alpha coefficient for the frequency subscale was 0.865.

Game use time

Questions about game use time were adapted from internet addiction-related items of the Chinese Youth Risk Behavior Survey (YRBS), which was conducted by the Chinese Center for Disease Control and Prevention. The information was collected by the following question: “How long did you usually play video games each day in the past week?”. Game use time was classified as never, less than 1 h, 1 to 2 h, 3 to 4 h, and more than 4 h. Game use overtime was considered as spending two hours or more playing games daily in this study.

Game addiction

The game addiction scale was adapted from the Internet Gaming Disorder Short Form Scale (IGDS9-SF) and the internet addiction-related items of YRBS [25]. Students who answered with 4 or more positive answers in 9 game experience- related items were defined as having game addiction. The Cronbach’s alpha coefficient for game addiction scale was equal to 0.743 in the present study.

Depressive symptoms

Depressive symptoms were assessed by the 9-item Patient Health Questionnaire (PHQ-9). The PHQ-9 is one of the most widely used instruments for evaluating depressive symptoms worldwide [26, 27]. The total score of PHQ-9 varies from 0 to 27, with a greater score suggesting worse depressive symptoms The Cronbach’s alpha coefficient for PHQ-9 was equal to 0.877 in the present study.

Loneliness

Loneliness was assessed with the 6-item UCLA Loneliness Scale (ULS-6). It was adapted from ULS-8. The total score ranges from 6 to 24 points, with a higher score indicating a higher degree of loneliness [28]. The Cronbach’s alpha coefficient for ULS-6 was 0.924 in the present study.

Social support

The social support scale in the study was adapted from the subjective support subscale of the Social Support Rating Scale (SSRS) with the permission of the authors. The SSRS was designed by Xiao et al. and widely adopted in China [29, 30]. The social support scale had a 20-point maximum score, with higher scores indicating greater subjective social support. The Cronbach’s alpha coefficient for the social support scale was 0.814 in the present study.

Self-rated academic performance and perceived academic stress

Self-rated academic performance was collected by the following question: “How do you rank in your class in the last year?”. The answers were categorized into ranking in the top 25% of the class, 26 to 50%, 51 to 75%, exceeding 76% and unclear. Participants were asked to evaluate perceived academic stress in the past month on a 0 to 10 points scale, with higher points indicating greater stress.

Statistical analysis

Samples with missing data were deleted (missing data n = 1, 0.02%). Descriptive statistics were used to describe the distribution characteristics of samples. Normality was tested by the Kolmogorov-Smirnov (K-S) test. Univariate and multivariate logistic regression analysis were applied to explore the lifetime PLEs-related factors. Variables with significant associations to PLEs in univariate analysis will be part of the multivariate logistic regression analysis. The backward elimination method was used in performing multivariate logistic regression analysis. Statistical significance was set at P < 0.05 (two-tailed). All analysis was performed with SPSS version 26.0 (IBM).

The prevalence of lifetime PLEs among high school students was 44.9% (n = 2057). A total of 2888 (51.7%) participants met the criteria of game use over time. The prevalence of game addiction was 14.96% (n = 835) among Guangzhou high school students.

Discussion

The study found that the prevalence of lifetime PLEs and game addiction among high school students in Guangzhou was 44.9% and 15.0%, respectively. More importantly, game addiction and long game use time were strongly associated with lifetime PLEs among high school students, even statistically controlling the impact of social supports, loneliness, and academic stress.

The result was similar to the study by Lin et al. on the relationship between internet addiction and PLEs among Korean high school student groups and the study by Vadlin et al. on the relationship between problematic gaming and PLEs [31, 32]. The findings may be explained by loneliness and perceived stress. Among high school students, gaming is one of the means of relieving loneliness and academic stress. Extensive literature has confirmed the bidirectional correlations between game addition and loneliness, game addiction, and perceived stress in adolescents [33–36]. Zhu et al. found that lonelier students were more likely to suffer from game addiction [37]. Studies by Sanne et al. in adolescent groups found that loneliness could increase the likelihood of hearing human voices (such as voices or laughter) or feeling human touch loneliness [15]. The students who are lonely and under great pressure may be more likely to become addicted to games and be more prone to having PLEs. Although we have statistically adjusted for the effects of loneliness and perceived academic stress in this study, the potential influence of loneliness and other stresses, such as social pressure, cannot be excluded.

The results can also be explained from the perspective of addiction. Addiction usually involves compulsive behavior, a compulsive and persistent use of a substance or behavior. Literature has consistently reported a robust correlation between addiction and PLEs. Both transversal and longitudinal studies found that internet addiction was positively associated with PLEs [20, 31]. The study by Dong et al. suggested that students with internet addiction had higher scores on psychiatric symptoms [38]. A meta-analysis stated that substance use might be an indicator for psychosis vulnerability [3]. Notably, substance use, internet addiction, and game addiction shared similar mechanisms, involving the abnormal cognitive and motivational functioning, cognitive decision-making that favors reward over risk, impulse control disorders, etc [39, 40].

We also tested the relationship between game use time and PLEs and substantiated that long game use time (≥ 4 h) was strongly associated with PLEs among high school students as well. Video gaming was a typically sedentary nighttime activity that required intense concentration. Excessive game use time could result in prolonged periods of inactivity, reduced sleep duration and quality, diminished social support, and decreased engagement in physical activities, all of which can further compromise mental health outcomes [41–43]. The relationship between game use time and mental health outcomes was controversial in previous studies and did not follow a linear pattern in this study [44, 45]. A longitudinal cohort study in Canada found that game use time was positively correlated with depressive symptoms and attention deficit and hyperactivity disorder symptoms among youth [46]. Each of these factors was associated with PLEs [47, 48]. There were also studies that found no such significant associations [45]. More research is needed to further verify this cut-off value of 4 h among high school students and the association between game use time and PLEs.

There were also studies that differed from our findings. In a longitudinal study of Canada to assess the association between adolescent media use and lifetime PLEs, they did not find a relationship between game use and PLEs [18]. But in that study, the researchers only measured the participants’ game use time from 12 to 15 years old and did not investigate other symptoms of addiction. In addition to this, the cultural differences between the two countries need to be taken into account. Firstly, the education system in Canada places emphasis on cultivating students’ independent learning and creativity. Students are encouraged to think independently and develop their own interests and talents. And it also emphasizes students’ participation and cooperation, respects individual differences, and gives students more choices. In contrast, the education system in China is more focused on traditional utilitarian education. Students are required to complete a large amount of homework and take numerous exams, and the pressure to achieve high scores and advance to the next grade is relatively high. The educational pressure in China may be more intense and uniform, which could lead adolescents to rely more heavily on gaming as a coping mechanism. Secondly, differences in digital culture penetration, regulatory environments, and the availability of offline stress-relief resources (e.g., extracurricular activities, psychological support services) may also contribute to the observed discrepancy.

The prevalence of lifetime PLEs in this study was higher than the 15.6% observed in a separate group of high school students [23]. It was important to note that the previous study measured PLEs over a period of approximately three months, whereas the current study examined lifetime PLEs. The prevalence of lifetime PLEs also varied according to geographical location and ethnicity, with a rate of 28.4% in America and 75% in England, respectively [49, 50].

This study also found that being a single child was positively related to lifetime PLEs, whereas social support was negatively related to lifetime PLEs. This finding aligned with the results obtained from a separate research endeavor conducted by our team [23]. Social support was considered a strong protector of mental health. A longitudinal study conducted in Canada showed that social support could decrease the risk of depression, anxiety, and suicide-related outcomes among adolescents [51]. A psychological autopsy study also proved that social support was a mediating factor in the relationship between being left behind and suicide among rural elders [52]. Our study reinforced the association between social support and PLEs among high school students.

Limitations and implications

The study had several limitations. First, the cross-sectional design prevented the establishment of causal relationships between game addiction and PLEs. To validate these findings, prospective cohort studies are necessary. Second, the reliance on self-reported information introduced the possibility of recall bias. And the academic stress was only assessed through self-reporting rather than a relative scale. Future studies could adopt internationally standardized scales. Third, conducting the survey during the COVID-19 pandemic may lead to an overestimation of the prevalence of game addiction. Fourth, PLEs are experiences rather than diagnosis and the lack of a standardized definition for game addiction hindered the comparability of this study with other studies.

Despite these limitations, our findings had some implications. Lifetime PLEs were strongly related to game addiction and long game use time, and the strength of the association was even stronger than the association between PLEs and psychological problems such as depression and loneliness. We should not only pay attention to the psychological problems of participants but also care about their behavioral problems in the future study on PLEs.

Conclusion

The prevalence of lifetime PLEs is high among high school students, and PLEs are related to a variety of psychosocial and behavioral factors. Lifetime PLEs are strongly related to game addiction and long game use time among high school students.
