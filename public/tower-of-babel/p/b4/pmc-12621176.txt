Triple Burden of Cannabis Use, First Episode Psychosis and HIV in KwaZulu‐Natal Province, South Africa
Authors: Usha Chhagan, Vuyokazi Ntlantsana, Enver Karim, Andrew Tomita, Bonginkosi Chiliza, Saeeda Paruk
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTAimTo describe the current and lifetime patterns of substance use, particularly cannabis, and its association with clinical presentation of individuals presenting with first episode psychosis, living with and without HIV, in KwaZulu‐Natal Province, South Africa.MethodsAdult patients, aged 18–45 years, diagnosed with a psychotic disorder meeting DSM‐5 criteria and screened using the Mini International Neuro‐psychiatric Interview, were recruited to the study (N = 182). Psychiatric tools, including the Positive and Negative Syndrome Scale, International HIV Dementia Scale, Patient Health Questionnaire and the Extra‐pyramidal Symptom Rating Scale, were used to gather clinical data. Substance use was assessed using the WHO Alcohol, Smoking and Substance Involvement Screening Test; HIV ELISA testing was used to assess HIV status.ResultsMost were males (n = 129, 71.0%), mean age being 26.27 (SD = 7.22), with 39 individuals (21.3%) living with HIV. Those with lifetime cannabis use scored significantly higher on the Positive and Negative Syndrome Scale total score (p = 0.009), disorganised (p = 0.009), excitement (p < 0.001) and positive symptom domain (p = 0.003), and lower on the depression domain (p = 0.002). People living with HIV and having lifetime cannabis use scored significantly higher on the positive (p = 0.023) symptom domain. HIV‐negative participants with lifetime cannabis use scored significantly higher on the excitement symptom domain (p = 0.013) and lower on the depression domain (p = 0.023).ConclusionThere is a high prevalence of comorbid cannabis use in persons with first‐episode psychosis. An integrated mental health service program that addresses the triple diagnosis of psychosis, substance use and HIV is required to help curb the increasing disease burden.Keywords: HIV, psychosis, South Africa, substance use

Introduction

Mental health and substance use disorders are among the main contributors to the increase in years lived with disability (YLD) rates per person; this is true for most countries. There has been a doubling of the total number of disability‐adjusted life years attributable to mental illness in the last three decades in South Africa, with mental illness now ranking among the top 10 causes of disease burden in the country (GBD 2019 Mental Disorders Collaborators 2022; Ruffieux et al. 2023). Importantly, HIV/AIDS was also identified as especially contributing to increasing YLDs in sub‐Saharan Africa (Naghavi et al. 2015). In South Africa particularly, HIV/AIDS is of major health concern, with the country having the highest number of people afflicted with the disease of any country. An estimated 8.45 million people were infected with HIV by 2022. The province of KwaZulu‐Natal (KZN) remains the disease's epicentre (Vandormael et al. 2020; Zuma et al. 2022).

There remains a paucity of published literature on HIV and psychosis in lower and middle‐income countries in this region. In South Africa, substance use also remains a critical public health challenge that needs to be further investigated (Wang et al. 2007). With resource constraints, it is essential that we have a clearer understanding of the nature of associations between HIV, substance use, and first episode psychosis. Early focus on the association of substance use and HIV infection on psychosis onset may help improve outcomes in psychosis in those with a dual or triple burden of disease with HIV and/or substance use.

Using the Structured Clinical Interview for DSM‐IV (SCID) and the St George's Substance Abuse Assessment Questionnaire, Barnett and colleagues (Barnett et al. 2007) reviewed 123 referrals to a specialist service, and compared patterns of substance use in people with first episode psychosis (FEP) with general population prevalence. They found that substance use among patients referred with FEP was double that of the general population, with 51% of patients reporting cannabis abuse and 43% alcohol abuse. Age at first use of substances was significantly associated with age at first psychotic symptoms. In people with FEP, the high prevalence of substance use emphasised the need for its identification during the mental health assessment (Barnett et al. 2007).

On the African continent, demographic surveys in East Africa have highlighted that this region bears the burden of the world's highest rates of substance users (Fentaw et al. 2022), the rates of cocaine, stimulants and heroin having shown an upward trend (Acuda et al. 2011). South Africa, in comparison to data from other countries, has a high lifetime prevalence of substance use disorders with an early age of onset (Stein et al. 2008). Drug use, combined with high HIV prevalence, has been described as a neglected epidemic (Raguin et al. 2011), with a study in the Eastern Cape Province confirming a high prevalence of substance use in patients with FEP. In a 2019 publication by Thungana et al. (2019), 81% of the 117 patients had a history of active or prior substance use. Cannabis was the most used substance (59.8%), with similarly high rates for alcohol (57.3%) and stimulants (46.4%).

Substance use may also increase an individual's susceptibility to developing psychosis if they are living with HIV (Sewell 1996). The reverse is also documented: that in the presence of psychosis, an individual may be more prone to contract HIV due to a multitude of factors, including substance abuse (Remien et al. 2019; Subedi et al. 2013). International literature has reported an association between substance use in FEP and HIV infection (Nebhinani and Mattoo 2013; Sewell 1996; Subedi et al. 2013). In a study comparing new‐onset psychosis with a non‐psychotic HIV‐positive group, Sewell et al. (1994) found a greater lifetime prevalence of stimulant and sedative‐hypnotic abuse or dependence in those living with psychosis, who also had greater neuropsychological impairment and a higher mortality rate.

In a South African study by Brink et al. (2003), the relationship between substance abuse and FEP was compared between a control group (n = 36) and patients with psychosis (n = 33). The FEP group had onset of substance use at a significantly younger age (10–15 years) than the controls (15–20 years). Substance abusers had significantly higher Positive and Negative Syndrome Scale (PANSS) total score (p = 0.01), PANSS positive score (p = 0.01) and PANSS general psychopathology score (p = 0.04). These differences were found only at baseline and were not demonstrated at follow‐up interviews, the findings providing further evidence that substance abuse is of significant concern among FEP patients (Brink et al. 2003).

While international studies have made some strides in exploring this bidirectional relationship, there are gaps in the literature within sub‐Saharan Africa. The presence of the triple burden of severe mental illness, substance use and HIV has potential implications for illness severity, course and management, and thus needs further investigation. This is particularly urgent in low‐income settings, where budget constraints and resource limitations add to the burden of managing patients presenting with comorbid conditions.

There is thus an urgent need to understand the interacting variables and implement intervention strategies to address psychiatric and substance use disorders as well as HIV infection in patients presenting with this triple diagnosis (Zahari et al. 2010). This paper aims to describe the current and lifetime patterns of substance use, particularly cannabis, and its association with the socio‐demographic and clinical variables of individuals with FEP, living with and without HIV, in a low‐resourced, high HIV prevalence region of KZN, South Africa.

Research Questions

The burden of psychosis is costly to patients, families and healthcare systems. We are ideally positioned to study the clinical presentation of psychosis and its association with substance use, with a focus on cannabis and/or HIV infection.

In the clinical setting there were several areas identified that required further interrogation and these formed the basis of the research questions. This included the need to better understand the socio‐demographic profile of HIV‐infected and non‐infected patients with FEP, the symptomatology of FEP among HIV‐infected patients, and how that differs from those non‐infected with HIV. Furthermore, the clinical relevance of substance use on clinical variables in patients presenting with first‐episode psychosis was in question. Lastly, the difference between substance use patterns of HIV‐infected and non‐infected patients with FEP warranted further investigation.

Thus, the hypothesis that individuals presenting with FEP (HIV infected and non‐infected) are socio‐demographically and clinically different with different substance use patterns at illness presentation was tested using a quantitative approach.

Method

Study Design

This is a comparative cohort study that is quantitative and descriptive in design, and consists of adult patients presenting with FEP who are either HIV‐infected or non‐infected. A full description of the methods has been outlined in an earlier publication (Chhagan, Ntlantsana, Tomita, Naidu, et al. 2021).

Sites and Participants

The study was conducted at the psychiatry units of five district‐level public sector hospitals in the eThekwini Municipality, KwaZulu‐Natal Province. Consisting of both inpatient and outpatient facilities, these units are serviced by specialist psychiatrists as well as medical officers and registrars in training.

Participants for this study consisted of 182 people presenting with FEP of whom 39 (21.3%) were living with HIV Male and female patients aged between 18 and 45 years, with first presentation to mental health care service and diagnosed with a psychotic disorder meeting DSM‐5 criteria, confirmed by the Mini International Neuro‐psychiatric Interview (MINI) interview for psychosis (Sheehan et al. 1998), were invited to participate. The participants were categorised into two groups based on their HIV status, namely positive and negative. FEP was defined as the first treatment contact at which they met the criteria for psychosis and within 6 weeks of first clinical presentation.

Those who refused consent, had intellectual disability or less than 7 years of formal schooling were excluded from the study. Patients with other significant general medical conditions that may be associated with FEP, such as epilepsy, neurological disorders, syphilis, traumatic brain injury, physical examination or haematological tests were also excluded. All patients with psychosis received antipsychotic treatment, as per the National Essential Drug List for adults (Republic of South Africa 2015) at the hospital level. Those who were living with HIV at the time of the study assessment were referred to the antiretroviral services for initiation/continuation of treatment.

Measures

The study utilised a clinical interview, physical examination and a host of psychiatric tools to measure a number of variables. The study method, with detailed description relating to sites, participants, inclusion criteria and measures, is described in an earlier publication (Chhagan, Ntlantsana, Tomita, Chiliza, and Paruk 2021; Chhagan, Ntlantsana, Tomita, Naidu, et al. 2021). This paper extracts the cross‐sectional baseline data of participants' socio‐demographic profile, HIV status by HIV ELISA, clinical symptomatology and substance use.

The following scales were used to gather clinical data: MINI (Sheehan et al. 1998) (to confirm psychosis), Positive and Negative Syndrome Scale (PANSS) (Kay et al. 1987), Patient Health Questionnaire‐9 (PHQ‐9) (Cholera et al. 2014; Kroenke et al. 2001), Extra‐pyramidal Symptom Rating Scale (ESRS) (Chouinard and Margolese 2005), Cognitive Assessment Tool (CAT‐rapid) (Joska et al. 2016, 2011), International HIV Dementia Scale (IHDS) (Sacktor et al. 2005), with quality of life being measured with World Health Organization Quality of Life questionnaire (WHO QOL‐BREF) (Whoqol Group 1998). The WHO Encounter (World Health Organization 1987), together with a clinical interview, was used to establish the duration of untreated psychosis (DUP). Using the five‐factor model to score the PANSS (Emsley et al. 2003), the data were categorised into negative, positive, disorganised, excited and depression/anxiety domains.

Patterns of Substance Use

Substance use was assessed using the Alcohol, Smoking and Substance Involvement Screening Test, the screening questionnaire having been developed by the WHO to assess the use of psychoactive substances (WHO ASSIST Working Group 2002). The questionnaire, which is easily administered, covers tobacco, cannabis, alcohol, cocaine, stimulants, sedatives, hallucinogens, opiates and other drugs. The test provides information about lifetime substance use, current, adverse effects, potential harm, dependence and injecting drug use. Using the WHO‐Assist scale, participants who scored 4–26 were classified as requiring a brief intervention, while those who scored 27 and above required intensive intervention. It is comprehensive and has been validated internationally, having been shown to be cross‐culturally useful and applicable to the South African context. A high degree of inter‐observer agreement was attained with a weighted kappa score of > 0.9.

Data Analysis

Data was entered onto a RED Cap (Harris et al. 2019) database by three independents investigators (U.C., V.N., E.K.) and analysed using Stata version 17.0. For the continuous data, means, standard deviations, medians and interquartile ranges were used, while proportions and percentages were used to present the categorical variables. Pearson's chi‐squared test was used to test for associations between the categorical variables, and Fisher's exact test was used where a category contained five or fewer observations. The T‐test was used to test for association between continuous and categorical variables, with the Wilcoxon rank‐sum test being used where data were not normally distributed, the level of significance being set at p = 0.05.

Ethical Considerations

Ethical approval was obtained from the University of KwaZulu‐Natal Biomedical Research Ethics Committee (BREC ref no.: BE276/19), and permission was obtained from the Provincial Department of Health as well as the five clinical sites. Written, informed consent was obtained from all participants and/or their guardians.

Results

A total of 190 participants were approached for enrolment, of whom seven refused and one subsequently withdrew consent, resulting in the analysis of data from 182 participants. Of these, 53 (29.0%) were female, the age range being 18–45 years, the mean age being 26.27 (SD 7.22), and 39 (21.4%) were people living with HIV (PWH) (Table 1). Most of the participants were single (88.4%) and had obtained a secondary school level of education (more than 7 years of formal schooling) (85.2%). After controlling for age, males were less likely to be infected with HIV than females presenting with FEP (OR 0.098; p = 0.0001, CI 0.042–0.23). All participants with HIV were on anti‐retroviral treatment.

The most commonly used substances identified were tobacco products, alcohol and cannabis. Among the participants with lifetime cannabis use (n = 123), the majority were single (n = 113, p = 0.003) and males (n = 107, p < 0.001) who had achieved a primary school or secondary level of education (90.2%). Of the 104 current cannabis users, 102 (98.08%) required some intervention, based on their WHO ASSIST score, with 82 (78.85%) requiring a brief intervention and 20 (19.23%) an intensive one. Of the 91 current alcohol users, 50 (55%) required some intervention, based on the WHO ASSIST score.

Of the participants with lifetime cannabis use (n = 123), 15 were PWH (12.2%) and 108 were HIV negative (87.8%) (Table 2). Clinically, participants with lifetime cannabis use scored significantly higher on the PANSS total score (p = 0.009), disorganised (p = 0.009), excitement (p < 0.001) and positive symptom domain (p = 0.003), with lower scores being found on the depression domain (p = 0.002). There was no significant association between clinical factors, including cognitive screening and current cannabis use. People with FEP and a lifetime history of cannabis use were less likely to have impairment on IHDS, irrespective of HIV status. There was also no significant difference in the PHQ‐9 and CAT between the PWH and the HIV negative group.

Discussion

The key finding of this study was that at least a fifth of people presenting with FEP may have the triple burden of substance exposure, HIV and psychosis. Those with this triple burden and lifetime substance use (15 of 182, 8.2%) had increased psychosis symptom severity, with significantly higher scores on the positive (p = 0.023) symptom domain of the PANSS, compared to those who did not have cannabis exposure. This may be due to increased biological vulnerability associated with having to cope with the biopsychosocial implications of HIV, which remains a stigmatised illness and contributes to greater vulnerability to mental illness and substance use. It could also be attributed to HIV‐related neurological vulnerability, which may be seen in individuals having opportunistic infections (Armoon et al. 2022; Nakasujja et al. 2025; Turan et al. 2017).

Furthermore, 21.4% of people presenting with FEP had been living with HIV, the prevalence of HIV in this population being consistent with other recent sub‐Saharan publications (Chhagan, Ntlantsana, Tomita, Chiliza, and Paruk 2021). Currently, as of mid‐2024, the approximate overall HIV prevalence rate is 13.9% (8.45 million) and an estimated 19.6% of the general population (adults aged 15–49 years) is living with HIV infection (Statistics South Africa 2024). This is discouraging, as it suggests that while there is now a robust anti‐retroviral treatment program in South Africa (Klausner et al. 2011), the prevalence of HIV remains over 20% in this vulnerable group. This emphasises the need to review the factors driving this HIV risk and how to best address them.

In this study, females (even after controlling for age), remained at higher risk for HIV infection than males, which is consistent with the literature in sub‐Saharan Africa (Griesbeck et al. 2016). Significantly, poorer health‐related quality of life in multiple domains has also been found in women with HIV than in men (Mrus et al. 2005), with gender differences in quality of life not being explored in this paper. However, this suggests that the female population remains a particularly vulnerable group of patients to HIV. Awareness of these differences in clinical profile and gender as factors that may precipitate or continue to influence outcomes is essential when developing and implementing preventative and treatment strategies that reduce the disease burden in this patient population (Griesbeck et al. 2016).

In a retrospective chart review, Mona and colleagues (Mona et al. 2022) assessed the use of cannabis in all in‐patients 13 years and older meeting the criteria for schizophrenia spectrum and other psychotic disorders. A high prevalence was found, with 48.9% reporting current and 51.1% lifetime cannabis use, with a significant association between being male and current and lifetime cannabis use. Substance use was found to be higher in males with FEP than in females, with a sample of 54 patients with FEP in KZN by Burns et al. (2010) reporting comorbid cannabis use in 35% of their sample population. Internationally, in a meta‐analysis of cannabis use prevalence in FEP, an estimated rate of 33.7% was documented (Myles et al. 2016). There is a need to address co‐occurring cannabis use as it may have significant implications on clinical variables like cognition and hence prognosis. Thus, the data from this study provides evidence for the need for an integrated and comprehensive program that addresses severe mental illness, co‐occurring substance use and medical conditions such as HIV to improve outcomes.

There was a high prevalence of self‐reported cannabis exposure in this study, with 123 of 182 (67.6%, two‐thirds) participants self‐reporting lifetime cannabis use, and 104 having current use. Of the group who were current cannabis users, 102 (98.08%) required some intervention, as per the WHO risk score, with more than three‐quarters requiring a brief and one‐fifth an intensive intervention. The high number of patients in this study requiring intervention for cannabis use demonstrates that this community remains affected by the dual burden of substance use and psychosis. Substance use as a risk factor may also negatively impact adherence to treatment as well as prevention recommendations (Norman et al. 2009).

The results of this study also show that lifetime cannabis use in people with FEP was associated with significantly higher scores on the PANSS total score (p = 0.009), disorganised (p = 0.009), positive (p = 0.003) and excitement symptom domain (p < 0.001). In another study of individuals with FEP, cannabis users showed greater positive and dissociative symptoms (Dissociative Experiences Scale – II) as well as impairment in functioning, even while on comparable anti‐psychotic treatment, suggesting a lesser response to treatment (Ricci et al. 2021). The differences in symptomatology persisted at the eight‐month assessment, with a worsening of global functioning, although an improvement in functioning was noted in those who stopped using cannabis (Ricci et al. 2021). Similarly, in our study, lifetime cannabis use was associated with significantly higher scores in multiple domains on the PANSS scale. These results suggest that cannabis use may have an impact on clinical presentation, which may necessitate more aggressive interventions to effect better outcomes. While our study did not demonstrate any association with negative symptoms of psychosis, we used a larger, more representative sample size and considered the triple diagnosis of psychosis, using both HIV negative and PWH.

The WHO World Mental Health Survey identified Africa, particularly South Africa, as one of the countries where there are gaps in the provision of treatment for substance abuse (Wang et al. 2007). Studies have pointed to a bidirectional relationship between HIV and substance abuse with mental illness (Singh et al. 2020), namely comorbid HIV and substance abuse pose an increased risk for mental illness, which in turn increases the risk for HIV plus substance abuse. Studies have also suggested that the presence of these multiple conditions in an individual impacts their quality of life, negatively affects treatment adherence and adds to the caregiver and healthcare burden (Bing et al. 2001). A review of the existing literature within sub‐Saharan Africa has demonstrated the lack of research around this triple burden of disease, the findings of our study emphasising the need for further multi‐centre studies on this aspect of mental illness.

Limitations

The limitations of this study include the cross‐sectional design, lack of a control group as well as the relatively small sample size for PWH compared to the HIV‐negative participants. This reflects the HIV prevalence in the study region, where we showed a seropositivity of 22%.

Conclusion

The link between mental illness and cannabis use is highlighted, with particular reference to PWH. This study also suggests a high prevalence of comorbid cannabis use in people living with psychosis, requiring intervention, and that at least a fifth of those presenting with FEP may have the triple burden of substance exposure, HIV and psychosis. The integration of mental health treatment strategies with substance use prevention and targeted treatment programmes will assist in addressing the triple burden of comorbidities in PWH. A reduction in HIV transmission is essential to address the morbidity associated with this chronic disease, and through optimised, holistic treatment approaches, the neuropsychiatric consequences of this epidemic can be effectively reduced and more positive patient outcomes realised.
