Association between nasal airway metabolome signatures and lung function at age 6 years among children with post-bronchiolitis asthma
Authors: Heidi Makrinioti, Hideaki Miyachi, Ryohei Shibata, Janice A. Espinola, Ashley F. Sullivan, Carlos A. Camargo, Zhaozhong Zhu
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT

Introduction

Asthma, a disorder marked by variable and reversible airway obstruction, inflammation and hyperresponsiveness, is one of the most prevalent chronic paediatric respiratory conditions globally; post-bronchiolitis asthma represents a frequent phenotype [1]. Notably, the early onset of lung function impairment in asthma (including post-bronchiolitis asthma) is associated with an increase in the number of exacerbations, severity, and long-term respiratory sequelae [2–4]. Thus, identifying biological mechanisms for lung function impairment in children with post-bronchiolitis asthma could crucially provide opportunities for early intervention to protect against adverse long-term respiratory sequelae [5].

To identify biological mechanisms for lung function impairment in post-bronchiolitis asthma, it is essential to first acknowledge that post-bronchiolitis asthma is a heterogeneous disease with varying underlying biological pathways [6]. Because metabolites are inherently sensitive to subtle alterations in biological pathways, metabolomics can provide clinically interpretable insights into asthma and associated lung function impairment pathogenesis pathways [7, 8]. In regard to asthma development, cohort studies show that peripheral blood metabolomic profiles enriched in lipids and amino acid metabolism are associated with impaired lung function in school-aged children and adolescents [9, 10]. In addition, our recent study from the 35th Multicenter Airway Research Collaboration (MARC-35) severe bronchiolitis cohort showed that nasopharyngeal airway metabolomic profiles enriched in amino acid (e.g. methionine, betaine, histidine, glutathione) and α-linolenic acid metabolism are associated with an increased risk of asthma development by age 5 years [11]. However, we are yet to understand whether upper airway metabolomic profiles are associated with impaired lung function in children with asthma. Filling the latter knowledge gap would offer valuable insights into associated lung function impairment, enhancing our understanding of the underlying factors and mechanisms contributing to its development in post-bronchiolitis asthma.

To address this knowledge gap, our study aimed to define nasal airway metabolome signatures that are associated with lung function impairment, including forced expiratory volume in 1 s (FEV1), forced vital capacity (FVC), FEV1 to FVC ratio and bronchodilator response (BDR), in children with post-bronchiolitis asthma. We analysed nasal airway metabolomic data from children with post-bronchiolitis asthma and linked these to the available lung function data. Specifically, we applied weighted gene co-expression network analysis (WGCNA) [12, 13] in the nasal airway metabolome data to identify distinct groups of highly correlated metabolites (metabolite modules) and provide insight into the molecular mechanisms underlying lung function impairment in children with post-bronchiolitis asthma.

Methods

Study design, setting and participants

We analysed data (i.e. nasal airway metabolomic profiling and spirometry data at age 6 years) from a multicentre prospective cohort study of infants hospitalised for bronchiolitis, the MARC-35 study [14–18]. MARC-35 is coordinated by the Emergency Medicine Network (www.emnet-usa.org), an international research collaboration with 247 hospitals (table S1). At 17 sites across 14 US states, MARC-35 enrolled infants (aged <1 year) who were hospitalised with an attending physician diagnosis of bronchiolitis during one of three bronchiolitis seasons (1 November to 30 April) from 2011 to 2014 [11, 14, 19]. The diagnosis of bronchiolitis was made according to the American Academy of Pediatrics bronchiolitis guidelines, defined as acute respiratory illness with a combination of rhinitis, cough, tachypnoea, wheezing, crackles or retraction [20, 21]. We excluded infants with pre-existing heart and lung disease, immunodeficiency, immunosuppression, gestational age of <32 weeks, history of previous bronchiolitis hospitalisation or those who were transferred to a participating hospital >24 h after initial hospitalisation [11, 14, 19]. All patients were treated at the discretion of the treating physicians [22]. The institutional review board at each participating hospital approved the study, and all study procedures were in accordance with the World Medical Association Declaration of Helsinki [23].

Among 919 children enrolled in the longitudinal cohort, 861 (94%) completed sufficient follow-up for parent-reported asthma diagnosis at age 6 years, and 150 (17% of 861) had nasal swab metabolome data from the age 6 years in-person visit. Of these 150, 116 (77%) had doctor-diagnosed asthma (figure S1). Among them, 84 (72%) completed the age 6 years visit for spirometry (pre- and post-bronchodilator). Spirometry-specific exclusions included bronchodilator use prior to testing: short-acting β-agonist within 4–6 h, short-acting muscarinic antagonist within 12 h, long-acting β-agonist within 24 h, and long-acting muscarinic antagonist within 36–48 h [24].

The institutional review board at each participating hospital approved the study. Written informed consent was obtained from the parent or guardian, and from the child when developmentally appropriate.

Data collection

At enrolment, a structured interview assessed participants’ demographic, medical and environmental characteristics and family history. Parental phone interviews followed at 6-month intervals [24].

An in-person examination at age 6 years included spirometry, performed in paediatric pulmonary function laboratories by trained respiratory therapists following the American Thoracic Society and European Respiratory Society guidelines [25]. The spirometry procedures have previously been described in detail [24]. Nasal swab specimens were collected during the in-person examination at age 6 years.

Exposure

Nasal airway metabolome profiling was conducted by Metabolon (Durham, NC, USA) using liquid chromatography–tandem mass spectrometry. The laboratory was blinded to all specimens taken at age 6 years, which were processed in order. Instrument variability was 4%, as determined by calculating the median relative standard deviation for the internal standards. The metabolome data contain 311 metabolites from 271 sub-pathways within eight super-pathways (amino acids, carbohydrates, cofactors and vitamins, energy, lipids, nucleotides, peptides, and xenobiotics) [11, 26].

Outcome measures

The primary outcomes included the FEV1 % predicted and the BDR at the age 6 year examination, as defined by the Expert Panel Report 3 [27]. The secondary outcomes included the FVC % predicted and FEV1/FVC % predicted [27]. The details of spirometry measurement are described in an earlier study [27]. In the current analysis, BDR was treated as a dichotomous variable, where a value of 1 indicated a positive response, defined as an increase of >10% in FEV1, following bronchodilation [28].

Statistical analysis

Using a bioinformatics approach, metabolomic signatures were defined as metabolite modules. Our objectives were 1) to identify highly correlated nasal airway metabolite modules among children with post-bronchiolitis asthma at age 6 years and 2) to examine the association of these highly correlated nasal airway metabolite modules with the primary and secondary outcomes. Data processing

We used Metabolon data after removing batch variability by dividing each metabolite's values by the median within each instrument batch, giving each batch a median of 1. For each metabolite, the minimum value across all batches in the median-scaled data was imputed for missing values. We removed unidentified metabolic compounds (n=40) and metabolites with near-zero variance (n=6) (figure S2). All downstream analyses, including WGCNA and regression models, were performed on the same pre-processed dataset.

Weighted correlation network analysis

We performed a network analysis using the WGCNA approach to the metabolome data to identify distinct groups of highly correlated metabolites (R package WGCNA version 1.72–5; www.r-project.org) that may reflect metabolic pathways underlying asthma-related lung function impairment [29]. We first identified the optimal power for the network's scale-free topology. The power (β) amplified strong correlations and reduced weaker ones, adhering to the scale-free property. We determined a soft-thresholding power (β=10) for network construction by analysing the scale-free topology fit index as a function of the β (figure 2a). We constructed a network and detected modules (clusters of co-expressed metabolites). We set the minimum number of metabolites in a module to 10. In this way, we identified groups of co-expressed metabolites (modules) with WGCNA, reflecting biologically meaningful clusters that may relate to metabolic pathways or responses (figure 2b). A module eigenvalue of a given metabolite module was determined for each sample by calculating the first principal component of the metabolomic module. The module representing uncorrelated metabolites (grey) was removed from the analysis. Then, Spearman's correlation coefficients between the module eigenvalue of a module and the primary and secondary outcomes (i.e. FEV1 % predicted, FVC % predicted, FEV1/FVC % predicted and BDR) were calculated to explore general trends and potential linear relationships to guide the selection of specific modules for regression analyses.

We additionally examined associations between module eigenvalues and primary or secondary outcomes using regression models. Specifically, we fitted unadjusted and adjusted logistic and linear regression models, adjusting for age at nasal swab collection, sex, parental history of atopy and body mass index. For a sensitivity analysis, we included additional covariates, such as ancestry, study centre and treatment with corticosteroids at nasal swab age. For eigenvalues significantly correlated with any outcome, we tested associations between each module's metabolites and the outcomes using multivariable regression, adjusting for covariates. Multiple testing was corrected using the Benjamini–Hochberg false discovery rate (FDR) method [30].

Pathway analysis

We used MetaboAnalyst 6.0 (Wishart Research Group, University of Alberta, Canada) to conduct pathway analysis based on the metabolites from the modules significantly correlated with the primary and secondary outcomes. The software performed overrepresentation analysis to identify significantly enriched metabolic pathways and calculated pathway impact scores, providing insights into the biological relevance of our findings [31]. For visualisation, we constructed a network of the enriched pathways using Cytoscape 3.10.3 (http://cytoscape.org) in combination with the STRING database (https://string-db.com). Each node in the network represents a metabolic pathway, and edges illustrate potential associations or overlaps between pathways, as defined by shared metabolites or functional connections through the Kyoto Encyclopedia of Genes and Genomes database. This representation allows for the exploration of interconnections between pathways that may underlie the observed associations. The network represented the interconnections between pathways and was constructed with Cytoscape 3.10.3 and STRING [32].

Results

Patient demographic and clinical characteristics

Among the analytic cohort (n=116), the median age at nasal swab collection was 6.9 years (interquartile range 6.5–7.5 years), 75 (65%) were male, 53 (46%) were non-Hispanic white, 24 (21%) were born preterm (i.e. 32–36.9 weeks) and 63 (54%) had public insurance (table 1). These demographic data did not differ significantly in the non-analytic cohort with an exception of history of asthma and eczema, as expected given that the analytic cohort consisted only of asthma patients (table S2). The majority (62%) had a history of recurrent wheezing by age 3 years. Approximately half (52%) had at least one parent with a history of asthma. Lung function measurements were completed by 84 out of 116 children (72%). The mean FEV1 was 99.1% predicted, the mean FVC was 94.9% predicted and the mean FEV1/FVC was 103.1% predicted (table 1).

Associations between metabolite modules and outcomes

WGCNA identified seven nasal airway metabolite modules with dominant metabolic functions per module (table S3). Among the seven modules shown in figure 2c, the peptide and amino acid derivative metabolism module was significantly correlated with BDR at age 6 years (FDR=0.04). The peptide and amino acid derivative metabolism module; urea cycle, aromatic and branched-chain metabolism module; and the N-acetylated amino acid metabolism module were all significantly correlated with FVC % predicted (all FDR=0.048). Thus, we considered the peptide and amino acid derivative metabolism; urea cycle, aromatic and branched-chain metabolism; and N-acetylated amino acid metabolism modules as the outcome-related modules for downstream analysis.

Next, we investigated the association between the three outcome-related modules and BDR using a logistic regression model adjusted for age at nasal swab collection, sex, parental history of atopy and body mass index. After correcting for multiple testing, only the peptide and amino acid derivatives metabolism module showed a statistically significant association with an increased risk of positive BDR (FDR=0.043). Associations between the peptide and amino acid derivative metabolism; urea cycle, aromatic and branched-chain metabolism; and N-acetylated amino acid metabolism modules and reduced FVC % predicted were not significant when adjusting for multiple testing (FDR>0.05). Results from a sensitivity analysis including ancestry, study centre and treatment with corticosteroids at the nasal swab age as additional covariates are depicted in tables S4 and S5.

Association of individual metabolites with outcomes

A total of three metabolites in the peptide and amino acid derivative metabolism module were significantly associated with an increased risk of positive BDR at age 6 years (palmitoyl-sphingomyelin: odds ratio (OR) 1.62, 95% confidence interval (CI) 1.07–2.99, FDR=0.047; N-acetylthreonine: OR 1.63, 95% CI 1.20–2.43, FDR=0.048; pyridoxate: OR 3.81, 95% CI 1.13–4.40, FDR=0.048; table 2). We found that metabolites in the peptide and amino acid derivative metabolism module, specifically palmitoyl-sphingomyelin (β= −2.99, FDR=0.047) and N-acetylserine (β= −2.5, FDR=0.043), were significantly associated with reduced FVC % predicted. In addition, hypoxanthine in the N-acetylated amino acid metabolism module was significantly associated with reduced FVC % predicted (β= −2.30, FDR=0.047). Finally, from the urea cycle, aromatic and branched-chain metabolism module, N-acetyllysine was also associated with reduced FVC % predicted (β= −3.02, FDR=0.043) (table S6).

Pathway analysis

The analysis identified 25 significantly enriched metabolic pathways (FDR<0.05), illustrated in figure 2. Prominent pathways with high enrichment ratios included arginine biosynthesis; β-alanine metabolism; glycine, serine, threonine metabolism; histidine metabolism; phenylalanine metabolism; and purine metabolism (in aqua, figure 3a, table S7). These pathways were enriched based on the input metabolite list, suggesting a key role in the metabolic processes underlying observed clinical traits (i.e. FVC % predicted). Additional pathways also showed notable enrichment ratios, offering insights into mechanisms associated with the clinical traits (figure 3a). Discussion

Using a WGCNA approach in nasal airway metabolomic data from 116 children with post-bronchiolitis asthma, we conducted a cross-sectional analysis at age 6 years. We identified distinct modules of co-abundant nasal metabolites that were significantly associated with BDR and FVC % predicted. Specifically, metabolites such as palmitoyl-sphingomyelin, N-acetylthreonine and pyridoxate were positively associated with increased risk of positive BDR, while palmitoyl-sphingomyelin, N-acetylserine and hypoxanthine were negatively associated with FVC % predicted, suggesting potential links with impaired lung function. To explore the biological context, we performed pathway enrichment analysis on the metabolite modules that were significantly correlated with BDR and FVC % predicted. Our results highlight the potential of nasal airway metabolomic profiles to reflect lower airway pathobiological processes leading to lung function impairment in children with post-bronchiolitis asthma.

Although our findings derive from a relatively small sample of children with post-bronchiolitis asthma, they serve as proof-of-concept that upper airway metabolomic profiling can identify pathobiological signals of lower airway disease progression. By leveraging noninvasive nasal sampling and network-based approaches, we demonstrate that nasal airway metabolites can reflect biological processes linked to bronchodilator responsiveness and lung function. Specifically, we highlight the potential role of sphingolipids, amino acid-related metabolites and vitamin B6 derivatives in modulating airway responsiveness and in lung function impairment. Pathway enrichment analysis, performed on modules significantly correlated with BDR and/or FVC, revealed an overrepresentation of several biologically relevant pathways, including β-alanine metabolism and glycine, serine and threonine metabolism. Although our enrichment results were based on significant correlations rather than significant associations, they offer mechanistic insight by showing that metabolites associated with clinical traits cluster within defined biochemical pathways. To our knowledge, this is the first study to date highlighting these results using noninvasive upper airway samples.

Current evidence indicates an increase in certain sphingomyelins, which are ceramide-derived, in the airways of patients with asthma and a link to airway hyperresponsiveness, epithelial cell dysfunction and immune modulation [33]. Reinke et al. [34] identified distinct metabolic profiles in patients with asthma, showing that metabolites, including sphingolipids (a broad lipid class that includes sphingomyelins) such as sphingosine-1-phosphate, were dysregulated in patients with severe asthma. Their study revealed that sphingolipids, particularly in moderate and severe asthma, may activate the transient receptor potential vanilloid type 1 (TRPV1) receptor, driving inflammation and airway pathogenesis [34]. In addition, short-term sphingomyelin inhibition has been shown to have anti-apoptotic effects and later enhanced endothelium repair signals (improved autophagy completion and increased cell proliferation). Therefore, inhibition of sphingolipids is associated with protection against acute lung injury [35]. These findings indicate that sphingomyelins and their derivatives may contribute to acute lung injury and asthma-related metabolic shifts and serve as biomarkers or therapeutic targets, particularly in severe asthma, because they are implicated in key inflammatory pathways independent of corticosteroid treatment [34]. Palmitoyl-sphingomyelin, as part of the sphingomyelin family, might be a marker for airway inflammation or metabolic dysregulation in asthma [34]. However, specific studies on palmitoyl-sphingomyelin in lung function impairment in asthma remain limited.

There is also an increasing number of scientific reports linking amino acid metabolism to the pathophysiology underlying severe asthma and lung function impairment. Recently, Cottrill et al. [36] investigated differences in plasma metabolomic profiles between children with exacerbation-prone asthma and those with non-exacerbation-prone asthma. Their study identified dysregulation in arginine, lysine and methionine metabolism pathways. Specifically, metabolomic profiles differentiating patients with high and low asthma exacerbations included metabolites like citrulline, methionine sulfoxide and ornithine [36]. These pathways have been implicated in nitric oxide production, redox balance and T-cell activation, all of which are implicated in asthma pathophysiology [37]. These and other studies’ findings suggest that specific amino acids may act as potential biomarkers for exacerbation-prone asthma, and targeting metabolic dysregulation may be associated with improvement in asthma outcomes [38, 39].

Our study aligns with and extends the findings of our previous work related to asthma development risk after severe bronchiolitis, highlighting similar trends in metabolomic profiling [11, 40–45]. For instance, we recently identified distinct metabolic subtypes in infants hospitalised with severe bronchiolitis associated with asthma development by age 5 years [11]. Five metabolic subtypes were identified via unsupervised clustering of metabolomics data measured in nasopharyngeal samples. Among these, the metabolic subtypes characterised by high levels of inflammatory amino acids (e.g. methionine, histidine, glutathione) and low levels of polyunsaturated fatty acids were strongly associated with a higher risk of asthma compared to the reference metabolic subtypes [11]. Another recent study from our team identified distinct omics clusters of 401 transcripts and 38 metabolites that discriminated severe cases, with an area under the curve of 0.828 in predictive modelling [45]. Pathway and network analyses revealed that dysregulation of amino acid metabolic pathways may underlie the current associations with reduced FVC % predicted and increased risk of positive BDR. Given that T2-high asthma is less common in children than in adults, it will be important to validate our findings in a separate bronchiolitis cohort so that we can conclude whether non-eosinophilic or mixed inflammatory endotypes that are linked to post-severe bronchiolitis school-age asthma are associated with small airway dysfunction.

Our study has strengths and limitations. First, we followed a hypothesis-driven analytical approach starting from the correlation of metabolite modules with clinical outcomes, which led to an analysis of metabolic profiles capturing module-wide contributions [46]. However, the study design does not allow the identification of any causal or mechanistic insights. Additionally, the small sample size and the use of nasal airway samples may have limited the generalisability and the direct applicability of findings to lower airway pathology. The absence of statistically significant associations with FEV1 % predicted should be interpreted with caution, given our modest sample size. Larger analyses will be needed to determine whether similar trends emerge when metabolomics profiling is available for the full cohort. Our reported associations may reflect a pattern linked to small airway dysfunction and relief of gas trapping rather than improvement in large-airway flow [47]. Despite these limitations, our approach highlighted biologically relevant pathways and provided insights for future research to explore mechanisms and validate findings in larger, longitudinal cohorts.

Our findings suggest that high-abundance metabolites within key modules play a central role in driving significant associations with lung function traits such as BDR and FVC % predicted. Sphingolipid and amino acid metabolism pathways contribute to airway inflammation, oxidative stress and lung function regulation, highlighting their relevance to asthma pathogenesis. We encourage future studies to focus on elucidating the mechanistic roles of these metabolites and to validate their potential as therapeutic targets or biomarkers for asthma-related lung function impairment.

Conclusion

Our study identifies high-abundance metabolites linked to sphingolipid and amino acid metabolism as possible drivers of lung function impairment in children with post-bronchiolitis asthma. Sphingolipid and amino acid metabolism pathways have been considered to play a central role in airway inflammation, oxidative stress and lung function regulation in asthma. Our findings align with prior evidence linking sphingolipid dysregulation to asthma severity and bronchiolitis outcomes, supporting its emerging role as a potential therapeutic target or biomarker pathway. Nevertheless, validation in larger cohorts or mechanistic studies is needed before these metabolomic signatures can be translated into risk stratification tools or preventive interventions for post-bronchiolitis asthma.
