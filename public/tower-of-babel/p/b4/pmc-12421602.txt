Complex management of a patient with impalement injury to the neck
Authors: Kevin T Petersen, Brent Blackwell, Harris Kashtan, Deborah A Kuhls, Allison G McNickle
Published: 2025

What would you do

An adult construction worker was working on the second story of a construction site and fell backwards onto exposed rebar sustaining multiple impalements (figure 1). A prolonged extrication was required to cut the rebar to free the patient from the structure and air transport took approximately 45 min to our level I trauma center. On arrival, the patient was noted to have been impaled by two separate pieces of rebar, one through the right neck and the left chest, and one entering and exiting the left upper arm.

Picture taken at the construction site prior to extrication.

Advanced trauma life support primary survey showed the airway was clear of blood, but the patient was unable to phonate. The patient had clear breath sounds bilaterally with symmetrical chest wall expansion. There were palpable distal pulses, and his Glasgow Coma Scale score was 13 (eyes 4, verbal 3, motor 6). Chest X-ray showed a metallic foreign body traversing the thorax, without hemothorax or pneumothorax. There were no other injuries on the primary or secondary survey. X-ray images of the left arm showed no fracture, and the rebar was within the soft tissue and was removed at bedside.

What we did and why

Although the rebar appeared to be traversing the airway, the patient was not in respiratory distress for several hours; thus, we obtained imaging. Scans demonstrated the rebar entering below his right temporomandibular joint (TMJ), a fracture of the right mandibular ramus and dislocation of TMJ. The rebar traversed the oropharynx and neck. CT angiography showed the rebar abutting the bilateral carotids without a hematoma; however, the imaging was degraded by scatter. Torso imaging showed the rebar passing anterior to the left clavicle, and within the subcutaneous tissue, exiting at the anterior axillary line of the left chest (figure 2). The radiographic studies confirmed the patient did not have an immediate life-threatening injury, and we had time to prepare our team as well as consult the necessary subspecialties.

Three axial images of CT angiography of the cervical spine show the rebar as it passes the patients right temporomandibular joint, closely abutting the right internal carotid artery at the base of the skull, as the rebar passes the posterior oropharynx just above the larynx, and as the rebar traverses down the left neck in close approximation of the carotid artery at its bifurcation. Three-dimensional reconstruction of the rebar passing above the left thorax and exiting at the anterior axillary line.

We proceeded with securing the airway. With anesthesia and otolaryngology (ENT), an awake, nasotracheal intubation was performed using fiber optics. The rebar was traversing the pharynx with the larynx directly inferior. Local anesthesia was provided transcutaneously, and an endotracheal tube was passed over the camera, abutting the rebar, and into the trachea to secure the airway.

The patient was then sedated and taken to interventional radiology to better visualize the carotid arteries. Angiography showed no evidence of vascular injury but close approximation of the carotids with the rebar. At this point, we involved vascular surgery to evaluate the carotids and assist with management of this patient.

With a plan to pull the rebar from the left chest, the focus turned to minimizing the amount of rebar that would pass through the patient. The fire department cut down 30 cm of the rebar from the right neck. Due to fire risk, the operating room was not an ideal place to perform this task because of the oxygen supply. The patient was brought to a positive pressure room in the post-anesthesia care unit. In preparation, the patient was weaned down to an FIO2 (fractional inspired oxygen) of 21% and had their face and body covered with a wet blanket. The fire department cut and grinded the rebar to facilitate removal.

The patient was brought to the operating room with trauma, ENT, and vascular surgery. A tracheostomy was performed, and the nasotracheal tube was removed. Bilateral neck dissections controlled the carotids and visualized the rebar (figure 3). The rebar was removed without incident. The neck incisions were closed over drains, and the oropharynx was repaired transorally. The wound on the left chest was left open, and the left arm wound was closed over a drain.

Intraoperative photo demonstrating the tracheostomy with a reinforced, long endotracheal tube, and bilateral neck dissections with carotid artery exposures. Also noted is the rebar traversing the neck exploration of the left neck.

The patient was brought to the trauma intensive care unit and weaned from the ventilator. The patient was transferred to the floor maintaining the trach collar, and a diet was started after a successful swallow study. Two weeks after the injury, the patient’s tracheostomy was decannulated, and after 24 hours of monitoring for respiratory compromise, was discharged home. This challenging case, which focused on the complex management of a difficult airway, demonstrated the importance of allowing clinical status to guide your management of trauma patients.
