Clinical outcomes and risk factors of cytomegalovirus reactivation in teclistamab-treated multiple myeloma patients
Authors: Hira Cheema, Asis Shrestha, Syed Naqvi, Sanjay Muttineni, Sasya Dronavalli, Jawad Alrawabdeh, Marah Alzu’bi, Carolina D Schinke, Sharmilan Thanendrarajan, John D Shaughnessy, Fenghuang Zhan, Maurizio Zangari, Frits van Rhee, Samer Al Hadidi
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractTeclistamab, a bispecific T-cell engager targeting BCMA and CD3, has demonstrated efficacy in relapsed/refractory multiple myeloma but may increase infection risk due to immunosuppression. Cytomegalovirus (CMV) reactivation is a concern in immunocompromised patients, yet its incidence and clinical significance in teclistamab-treated patients remain poorly characterized. We conducted a retrospective analysis of 177 multiple myeloma patients who received teclistamab between December 2022 and December 2024. We assessed CMV reactivation incidence, risk factors, and clinical outcomes. Binary logistic regression identified predictors of CMV reactivation. CMV testing was done in 173 (98%) patients. CMV reactivation occurred in 38 patients (22%) during teclistamab treatment, with a median initial viral load of 87.2 IU/mL (range: 37.1–4807.0). The median highest CMV level was 288 IU/mL (range: 45.3–20,000). Most reactivations were asymptomatic (89.5%), with only four symptomatic cases, three requiring treatment, and no end-organ damage. Eight patients (21.1%) experienced second viremia episodes. Multivariable analysis identified prior CMV reactivation as a significant predictor (OR = 3.34, p = 0.005). CMV reactivation did not impact overall survival (p = 0.673). Prophylactic IVIG use was associated with improved survival (HR = 0.49, p = 0.011). CMV reactivation occurs in approximately one-fifth of teclistamab-treated multiple myeloma patients but is typically asymptomatic and does not affect survival. Prior CMV reactivation predicts subsequent reactivation. These findings support risk-stratified CMV monitoring approaches in this population.Subject terms: Myeloma, Myeloma

Introduction

Multiple myeloma (MM) survival outcomes continue to improve, however many patients experience multiple relapses despite advances in therapeutic options [1–3]. Teclistamab, a bispecific T-cell engager (BiTE) antibody that simultaneously binds B-cell maturation antigen (BCMA) on myeloma cells and CD3 on T cells, has emerged as an effective treatment option for heavily pretreated relapsed/refractory multiple myeloma (RRMM) patients [4–6]. The MajesTEC-1 trial demonstrated overall response rates exceeding 60% in patients who had received a median of five prior lines of therapy, leading to FDA approval in 2022 [5].

However, bispecific antibodies and other T-cell engaging therapies are associated with significant immunosuppression, manifesting as hypogammaglobulinemia, lymphopenia, and increased susceptibility to opportunistic infections [7]. Cytomegalovirus (CMV), a ubiquitous herpesvirus with latency in 50–90% of adults, can reactivate in immunocompromised hosts, potentially causing serious complications including pneumonitis, gastrointestinal disease, retinitis, and end-organ damage [8, 9]. CMV reactivation has been well-documented in hematopoietic stem cell transplant recipients and patients receiving other immunosuppressive therapies, but its incidence and clinical significance in patients treated with bispecific antibodies remain incompletely characterized.

Previous reports of CMV reactivation in MM patients have primarily focused on those undergoing autologous stem cell transplantation, with limited data available for patients receiving novel immunotherapies. Early clinical trials of teclistamab reported infections as common adverse events, but detailed analyses of CMV reactivation patterns, risk factors, and outcomes are lacking. Understanding the epidemiology of CMV reactivation in this population is crucial for developing appropriate monitoring and prophylaxis strategies.

We conducted this retrospective study to characterize the incidence, risk factors, and clinical outcomes associated with CMV reactivation in MM patients receiving teclistamab therapy. Our objectives were to determine the frequency of CMV reactivation, identify predictive factors for reactivation, assess the clinical severity of reactivation events, and evaluate the impact of CMV reactivation on overall survival.

Methods

Study design and patient population

This retrospective cohort study included all MM patients who received teclistamab between December 30, 2022, and December 19, 2024, at the University of Arkansas for Medical Sciences (UAMS). Patients were included if they had a confirmed diagnosis of MM, received at least one dose of teclistamab, and had available CMV monitoring data. CMV testing was done as part of standard of care at UAMS.

Ethics and inclusion

This study was approved by the Institutional Review Board at UAMS. All procedures were conducted in accordance with relevant guidelines and regulations. As this was a retrospective study, the requirement for patient informed consent was waived.

Data collection

Medical records were reviewed to extract demographic information, disease characteristics, treatment history, laboratory values, and clinical outcomes. Collected variables included age at diagnosis, sex, race, ethnicity, Eastern Cooperative Oncology Group (ECOG) performance status, immunoglobulin heavy and light chain subtypes, time from diagnosis to first teclistamab dose, prior CMV reactivation history, concomitant immunosuppressive medications, and prophylactic intravenous immunoglobulin (IVIG) use.

CMV Monitoring and Definitions

CMV reactivation was defined as detection of CMV DNA by quantitative polymerase chain reaction (PCR) in plasma at any level above the assay’s lower limit of detection. The frequency of CMV monitoring was determined by institutional protocols and treating physician discretion. For patients with CMV reactivation, we recorded the number of tests performed before detection, initial positive viral load, highest viral load during the episode, presence of symptoms, need for antiviral treatment, and occurrence of end-organ disease. Symptomatic CMV infection was defined as presence of clinical manifestations attributable to CMV in conjunction with detectable viremia. A second CMV viremia episode was defined as recurrence of detectable CMV DNA after documented clearance. CMV end-organ disease was defined as tissue-invasive CMV infection with direct viral cytopathic damage to specific organ systems, documented by the detection of CMV (via PCR, immunohistochemistry, or culture) in the affected tissue along with histopathological evidence of organ dysfunction and characteristic clinical manifestations.

The majority of patients (169/177, 95.5%) were tested for CMV prior to therapy. Almost all patients (173/177, 97.7%) had at least one CMV test during treatment. CMV viral load was quantified using the cobas® CMV test on the Roche cobas® 6800 System, an FDA-cleared quantitative polymerase chain reaction (qPCR) assay. The assay quantifies CMV DNA over a range of 34.5 IU/mL to 1.0 × 10⁷ IU/mL (log₁₀ 1.54–7.0). Viral load more than 37.1 IU/ml was considered positive. The assay targets a CMV-specific DNA sequence within the highly conserved regions of the CMV DNA polymerase (UL54) gene, which is not homologous to other herpesvirus family members. To mitigate the risk of under-quantification or false-negative results due to potential mutations in primer or probe binding sites, the cobas® CMV test employs redundant amplification primers to ensure robust viral detection and quantification.

Per institutional protocol, patients were treated if they had detectable CMV with symptoms or if they had persistently rising titers despite being asymptomatic and per the treating physician decision.

Prior CMV reactivation was defined as any documented CMV viremia before initiation of teclistamab therapy. For patients with prior CMV reactivation, we recorded whether the episode was attributed to transplantation, presence of symptoms, antiviral treatment received, and highest viral load.

Laboratory parameters

Laboratory data collected during teclistamab treatment included complete blood counts (hemoglobin, white blood cell count, absolute neutrophil count, absolute lymphocyte count, platelet count), liver function tests, lactate dehydrogenase (LDH), total IgG, and functional IgG levels. For each patient, the lowest value during treatment was recorded. Functional IgG was measured in patients with IgG myeloma to assess non-myeloma immunoglobulin production.

Viral panel testing results were recorded when available, including Epstein-Barr virus (EBV), human herpesvirus 6 (HHV-6), parvovirus B19, and adenovirus. Specific viral testing, apart from CMV testing, was done per treating physician.

Statistical analysis

Descriptive statistics were used to summarize patient characteristics, with continuous variables reported as medians and ranges, and categorical variables as frequencies and percentages. Binary logistic regression was performed to identify factors associated with CMV reactivation during teclistamab treatment. Variables were first analyzed in univariable models, and those with p-values < 0.10 were considered for inclusion in the multivariable model. Multicollinearity was assessed using tolerance and variance inflation factor (VIF) values, with VIF > 10 indicating problematic collinearity.

Overall survival was calculated from the date of first teclistamab dose to death or last follow-up. Survival analyses were performed using Kaplan-Meier method, with log-rank tests to compare survival between groups. Hazard ratios (HR) with 95% confidence intervals were calculated using Cox proportional hazards models. Statistical significance was defined as p < 0.05 (two-tailed).

Results

Patient characteristics

A total of 177 MM patients who received teclistamab were included in this analysis. The median age at diagnosis was 63.8 years (range: 27.8–82.6 years), with male predominance (60.5%). The majority of patients were non-Hispanic (97.2%) and White (78.5%), and with 17.5% Black. ECOG performance status at teclistamab initiation was 0 in 26.0%, 1 in 48.0%, 2 in 22.6%, and 3 in 3.4% of patients. The most common heavy chain subtype was IgG (55.4%), followed by IgA (23.7%), with kappa light chain predominance (67.2%) (Table 1).

Prior CMV history and baseline characteristics

Prior to teclistamab initiation, 35 patients (19.8%) had documented CMV reactivation. Among these, six episodes were attributed to prior stem cell transplantation, two were symptomatic, and three patients received antiviral treatment. The median prior CMV viral load at time of previous reactivation was 222 IU/mL (range: 35–29,051 IU/mL).

The median time from MM diagnosis to first teclistamab dose was 6.15 years (range: 0.59–23.1 years), reflecting heavily pretreated patients. The majority of patients (85.9%) had a baseline testing with undetectable CMV DNA at teclistamab initiation.

During teclistamab treatment, the median number of CMV surveillance tests performed was 4 (range: 0–44). The median lowest absolute lymphocyte count (ALC) was 0.15 × 10³/µL (range: 0.00–11.00), median lowest total IgG was 395 mg/dL (range: 22–6,955), and median lowest functional IgG was 290 mg/dL (range: 8–1419), demonstrating significant immunosuppression in this population.

Viral panel PCR testing showed coinfections in a subset of patients: three had EBV reactivation, two had HHV-6 reactivation, seven had parvovirus B19 reactivation, and one had adenovirus reactivation (Table 2).

CMV reactivation during teclistamab treatment

CMV reactivation occurred in 38 of 173 patients with CMV testing (22.0%) during teclistamab therapy. The median number of CMV tests performed before initial detection was 3 (range: 0–11). The median initial positive CMV viral load was 87.2 IU/mL (range: 37.1–4807.0 IU/mL), and the median highest viral load during the reactivation episode was 288 IU/mL (range: 45.3–20,000 IU/mL).

Despite detectable viremia, most reactivations were clinically silent. Symptoms included fever, malaise, cytopenia, or transaminitis. Four patients (10.5%) developed symptomatic CMV infection, three patients (7.9%) required antiviral treatment, and notably, no patients developed clinically significant CMV end-organ disease. Eight patients (21.1%) experienced a second CMV viremia episode after initial clearance while continuing teclistamab therapy. We found no association between earlier use of tocilizumab and/or step-up steroid dosing and the risk of CMV reactivation.

CMV reactivation occurred predominantly in the early treatment period. Median time to first CMV reactivation: 5 weeks (range 0.14–29.86 weeks). Approximately 90% of patients who developed CMV reactivation did so within 3.3 months of teclistamab initiation, and around 75% experienced reactivation within 2.3 months, suggesting the highest risk period is within the first 2–3 months of therapy [Fig. 1].

Importantly, no patient discontinued teclistamab due to CMV reactivation. More than half of all patients (61.6%) received primary prophylactic IVIG during teclistamab treatment (Table 3).

Risk factors for CMV reactivation

Univariable logistic regression analysis identified three variables significantly associated with CMV reactivation: prior CMV reactivation (p < 0.001, OR = 4.56), increasing age at diagnosis (p = 0.005, OR = 1.06), and longer time from diagnosis to first teclistamab dose (p = 0.010, OR = 0.89). Primary prophylactic IVIG use showed a trend toward lower CMV reactivation (p = 0.074). Sex, CMV status at teclistamab initiation, and other viral reactivations (EBV, HHV-6, parvovirus B19, adenovirus) were not associated with CMV reactivation.

In multivariable analysis including prior CMV reactivation, age at diagnosis, and time to first teclistamab dose, only prior CMV reactivation remained statistically significant (p = 0.005, OR = 3.34), indicating that patients with prior CMV reactivation had more than three-fold increased odds of reactivation during teclistamab therapy. Age at diagnosis and time to first teclistamab dose lost statistical significance in the multivariable model (Table 4).

Survival outcomes

The median overall survival from first teclistamab dose was 24.2 months. CMV reactivation did not significantly impact overall survival (p = 0.673, Fig. 2), with similar survival curves observed in patients with and without CMV reactivation during treatment.

Discussion

This retrospective analysis provides comprehensive data on CMV reactivation in MM patients receiving teclistamab, demonstrating a 22% incidence rate that, while notable, is predominantly characterized by asymptomatic viremia without significant clinical consequences or impact on survival. Our findings have important implications for CMV monitoring and management strategies in this growing patient population.

The 22% CMV reactivation rate in our cohort is consistent with infection rates reported in early teclistamab clinical trials but provides more granular detail regarding CMV-specific patterns [5, 7]. This incidence is lower than rates observed following allogeneic stem cell transplantation but higher than those seen with autologous transplantation or conventional chemotherapy [10–13]. The intermediate reactivation rate may reflect the unique immunosuppressive profile of bispecific antibodies, which cause profound but potentially reversible T-cell engagement and redistribution.

An important finding of our study is the overwhelmingly asymptomatic nature of CMV reactivation in teclistamab-treated patients. Only 10.5% of patients with detectable viremia developed symptoms, only 7.9% required antiviral therapy, and no patients developed clinically significant end-organ disease. The need for treatment was based on clinical judgment of treating physician. This contrasts markedly with transplant populations, where symptomatic CMV disease and end-organ complications (example: pneumonitis) are more common and associated with significant morbidity and mortality [13, 14]. The generally low viral loads observed (median highest level 288 IU/mL) may explain the benign clinical course, as higher viral burdens typically correlate with increased risk of symptomatic disease and end-organ involvement.

The lack of impact of CMV reactivation on overall survival in our cohort is reassuring and suggests that current monitoring practices may be adequate. This finding differs from transplant literature, where CMV reactivation has been associated with increased mortality through both direct effects and indirect immune modulation [13, 14]. The absence of survival impact in our study may relate to the asymptomatic nature of most reactivations, low viral loads, or the fact that teclistamab was not held or discontinued for CMV management, allowing continued myeloma therapy.

Our multivariable analysis identified prior CMV reactivation as the strongest and only significant predictor of CMV reactivation during teclistamab treatment, with a more than three-fold increased risk. This finding parallels observations in transplant populations and suggests that CMV seropositivity alone may be insufficient for risk stratification—rather, a history of prior reactivation identifies patients who may benefit from more intensive monitoring.

The high rate of second viremia episodes (21.1%) among patients with initial reactivation is noteworthy and suggests that CMV reactivation in this population may follow a relapsing pattern rather than representing single isolated events. This pattern may reflect ongoing immunosuppression from continued teclistamab therapy and supports the need for continued monitoring even after viral clearance. However, the clinical insignificance of these episodes argues against routine preemptive therapy for asymptomatic low-level viremia.

The immunologic parameters observed in our cohort—profound lymphopenia (median lowest ALC 0.15 × 10³/µL) and hypogammaglobulinemia (median lowest IgG 395 mg/dL, functional IgG 290 mg/dL)—highlight the significant immunosuppression induced by bispecific antibodies. These findings are consistent with the known mechanisms of T-cell engaging therapies and may explain both the increased infection risk and the pattern of opportunistic infections observed. The observation that teclistamab was never discontinued for CMV reactivation suggests that clinicians prioritized myeloma disease control over asymptomatic viremia, a reasonable approach given our finding of no survival impact.

Our study has several limitations. The retrospective design introduces potential for selection and information bias, and CMV monitoring frequency varied based on institutional protocols and physician discretion, potentially leading to underdetection of transient low-level viremia. The study also has notable strengths, including a relatively large cohort size for this recently approved therapy, real-world data reflecting routine clinical practice, comprehensive viral monitoring data, and detailed laboratory parameters characterizing the degree of immunosuppression.

Our findings suggest that current CMV monitoring approaches are reasonable, but routine universal prophylaxis is likely unnecessary given the low rate of symptomatic disease and absence of survival impact. We recommend risk-stratified monitoring, with more intensive surveillance for patients with prior CMV reactivation history. Low-level asymptomatic viremia may not require immediate intervention, though preemptive therapy thresholds should be established through prospective studies. Primary prophylactic IVIG should be considered for all patients with MM who are receiving T-cell engaging therapies [15–17]. Our findings align with recent consensus recommendations for infection monitoring in bispecific antibody therapy, which emphasize risk-stratified approaches to viral surveillance in this population [16, 17].

Our findings are consistent with emerging real-world data on CMV reactivation in patients receiving bispecific antibody therapy. Recent studies have reported variable rates of CMV reactivation rates, with variation largely attributable to differences in monitoring intensity and definitions used [18–20]. Across these studies, most reactivations were asymptomatic, and clinically significant CMV disease remained rare. Our study uniquely contributes by examining the largest single-institution teclistamab cohort, identifying prior CMV reactivation as the sole independent predictor, showing no impact on overall survival, and providing detailed timing data showing 90% of reactivations occur within 3.3 months—findings that support risk-stratified monitoring approaches in clinical practice.

In conclusion, CMV reactivation occurs in approximately one-fifth of MM patients receiving teclistamab but is typically asymptomatic, characterized by low viral loads, and does not impact survival. Prior CMV reactivation is the strongest predictor of reactivation during therapy. As bispecific antibodies become increasingly incorporated into earlier lines of myeloma therapy, understanding and optimizing management of infectious complications will be crucial for maximizing therapeutic benefits while minimizing risks.
