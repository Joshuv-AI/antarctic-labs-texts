SPP1 knockdown inhibits invasion, migration and paclitaxel-induced Epithelial-Mesenchymal Transition in Cervical Cancer Cells through the TGF-β/Akt/Snail pathway
Authors: Qianqian Cao, Yayin Lin, Huiwen Guan, Chunlin Dong, Yunjun Ge, Min Li, Qian Zhang, Shuhui Guo, Mingyu Qin, Jinjin Yu, Guosheng Wu
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Secreted Phosphoprotein 1 (SPP1) was reported to promote the progression of multiple tumors by modulating the immune microenvironment and facilitating metastasis, but its role in cervical cancer remains unclear. Our purpose was to explore the effect and the potential mechanisms of SPP1 on cervical cancer progression and paclitaxel (PTX)-induced epithelial-mesenchymal transition (EMT). The Gene Expression Omnibus (GEO) and the Cancer Genome Atlas (TCGA) database were used to detect SPP1 expression in human cervical cancer and para-carcinoma non-tumor tissues, immunohistochemistry and western blotting experiments were carried out to confirm the expression of SPP1 in clinical samples. Cancer cell proliferation and metastasis assays were conducted to detect the effects of SPP1 on cancer progress using normal and SPP1-knockdown cervical cancer cells. Transcriptomics data and protein expression assays were utilized to investigate the mechanism underlying the effect of SPP1 on PTX-induced EMT. The mouse metastasis model was used to detect the effect of SPP1 on PTX-induced EMT during cervical cancer metastasis. SPP1 is highly expressed in cervical cancer clinical samples and is associated with low survival rates, SPP1 knockdown inhibits cervical cancer cell migration and invasion but doesn’t affect cell proliferation. Further studies revealed that SPP1 knockdown suppresses tumor cell EMT through the TGF-β/Akt/Snail pathway. Both in vivo and in vitro data confirmed that SPP1 knockdown alleviates PTX-induced EMT. SPP1 knockdown inhibits the invasion, metastasis and PTX-induced EMT of cervical cancer by suppressing TGF-β/Akt/Snail signaling pathway, and SPP1 is a potential therapeutic target for cervical cancer treatment.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1038/s41598-026-53597-y.

Introduction

Globally, cervical cancer poses a significant threat to women’s health as a malignant tumor and ranks as the fourth leading cause of cancer-related deaths among women1. In 2020, statistics indicate that over 600,000 women worldwide will receive a diagnosis of cervical cancer, with approximately 350,000 succumbing to the disease2. Limited and ineffective treatment options are available for patients with metastatic and recurrent cervical cancer. The failure of treatment and grim prognosis associated with cervical cancer primarily stem from the progression of localized infiltration and distant metastasis, the molecular underpinnings of which remain elusive3. Therefore, persistent endeavors are imperative to elucidate the molecular mechanisms involved and pinpoint novel therapeutic targets for cervical cancer metastasis.

Secreted phosphoprotein 1 (SPP1), also known as osteopontin, is situated in the 4q22.1 region of chromosome 44. It is a phosphorylated glycoprotein secreted with multiple biological functions, abundant in aspartic acid, glutamic acid, and serine residues5. SPP1 is predominantly expressed under normal conditions by various tissues, including osteoblasts, cardiac fibroblasts, and epithelial tissue6. In pathological conditions, especially in inflammatory and tumor microenvironments, immune cells (such as macrophages) and tumor cells can significantly upregulate the expression of SPP17. SPP1 plays a crucial role in both physiological and pathological processes by activating diverse intracellular signaling pathways through interactions with its receptors. The principal receptors for SPP1 comprise members of the integrin family (such as αvβ3) and CD44 variants (such as CD44v6), which exhibit cell type-specific distribution8. Upon binding to these receptors, SPP1 initiates a complex signaling cascade that modulates various physiological and pathological processes such as biomineralization and bone remodeling, inflammation and immune response, as well as tumor proliferation and invasion9–12.

Paclitaxel (PTX) is a first-line chemotherapeutic agent for postoperative and neoadjuvant treatment of cervical cancer13. The anticancer mechanism of PTX primarily involves potent mitotic arrest, induction of apoptosis, autophagy and triggering pyroptosis. Microtubule stabilization and mitotic arrest induced by PTX trigger a series of downstream events, such as activating the ROS pathway, inhibiting the PI3K/Akt/mTOR pathway, and activating the p53 pathway, thereby leading to cancer cell death. However, PTX also faces several challenges in clinical application, including low selectivity, high systemic toxicity, and the induction of epithelial-mesenchymal transition (EMT) in tumor cells14–16. PTX can trigger the upregulation of N-cadherin and the downregulation of E-cadherin, enhance the activity of matrix metalloproteinase 9 in cancer cells. Recent studies reported that PTX can induce EMT and pro-oncological effects in cervical cancer17,18, suggesting an increased risk of PTX-promoted tumor metastasis.

EMT is a biological process through which epithelial cells transition to a mesenchymal phenotype19. EMT is a key initial step through which cancer cells gain invasive and metastatic potential20. Cancer cells undergoing EMT will lose epithelial markers (such as E-cadherin) and obtain interstitial markers (such as N-cadherin), which change cell morphology and enhance cell motility. The TGF-β signaling pathway is one of the most potent and widely studied pathways for inducing EMT21. It can activate the above-mentioned EMT transcription factors through both SMAD-dependent and non-SMAD-dependent pathways. The Wnt/β-catenin pathway, Notch pathway, Hedgehog pathway, etc., also play important roles. Growing evidence illustrates that EMT plays a key role in tumor metastasis22. Specially, EMT plays an important role in cervical cancer progression and metastasis23.

Numerous studies have revealed the high expression of SPP1 in various malignant tumors, facilitating tumor advancement and spread through the regulation of tumor cell diversity, immune microenvironment restructuring, and therapy resistance24. For instance, Zhang et al. confirmed a significant upregulation of SPP1 in papillary thyroid carcinoma, correlating closely with clinical stage, race, and pathological subtype25. Kzhyshkowska et al. illustrated that SPP1 blockade in hepatocellular carcinoma triggers the activation of PD-1-expressing CD4 and CD8 T cells, inducing the shift of macrophages from M2 to M1 phenotype, thereby restraining tumor progression26. Previous studies have reported that SPP1 is highly expressed and is associated with poor prognosis in a variety of tumors, including ovarian cancer27, glioblastoma28, hepatocellular carcinoma29, and head and neck squamous cell carcinomas30. However, the role of SPP1 in cervical cancer and its associated mechanisms have not yet been elucidated.

In this study, we demonstrated SPP1 is overexpressed in cervical cancer, and SPP1 knockdown in cervical cancer cells inhibit cell migration, invasion while have no effect on cell proliferation. Further studies revealed TGF-β/Akt/Snail signaling pathway is involved in SPP1-knockdown-induced EMT inhibition. We also found that SPP1 knockdown could alleviate PTX-induced EMT, which suggests SPP1 may be a potentially therapeutic target in cervical cancer treatment.

Materials and methods

Online data resources

The expression profile data of cervical cancer were downloaded from the Gene Expression Omnibus databases (GSE7803, GSE9750, GSE138080, GSE44001, and GSE75034, https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/geo/) and the Cancer Genome Atlas Program (TCGA, http://cancergenome.nih.gov/) datasets. GSE7803 includes 28 cervical cancer and 10 normal cervical tissues, GSE9750 includes 33 cervical cancer and 24 normal cervical tissues, and GSE138080 includes 10 cervical cancer and 10 normal cervical tissues, collectively covering normal cervix, cervical intraepithelial neoplasia (CIN I-III), and cervical cancer. The expression profile data of normal cervical tissue were downloaded from the Genotype-Tissue Expression Project (GTEx, https://www.gtexportal.org). SPP1 expression data in cervical cancer cell lines were acquired from Cancer Dependency Map Portal (https://depmap.org/portal/). All three datasets were generated using the Affymetrix HG_U133A Array platform.

Bioinformatic analysis

RNA sequencing data from the GEO database and TCGA dataset were used to analyze the differentially expressed genes (DEGs) between normal cervical epithelial and cervical cancer tumor tissues. RNA sequencing data were analyzed using R software (v4.3.3, R core team, 2022). The DESeq2 package (v1.42.0, Michael Love, 2022) was used to normalize gene expression, and read counts were normalized to transcripts per million (TPM). The raw gene expression matrix from RNA-seq was preprocessed and normalized in the R environment using log2 transformation for background correction, followed by quantile normalization and probe merging. Differential expression analysis was then performed with the limma package using the cutoff criteria of |log2FC| > 1 and FDR < 0.05. This study complied with the publication guidelines provided by TCGA. Gene Ontology (GO), the Kyoto Encyclopedia of Genes and Genomes (KEGG), and Gene Set Enrichment Analysis (GSEA, http://software.broadinstitute.org/gsea/index.jsp) enrichment analyses using DEGs were performed with R language with the aid of packages clusterProfiler (v4.13.0; Yu et al., 2024), enrichplot (v1.25.0; Yu et al., 2024), and ggplot2 (v3.5.0; Wickham, 2024). Only terms with both p-value of < 0.05 were considered significantly enriched.

Tissue collection

The paraffin-embedded tissues were collected from fifty-five patients between January 2024 and December 2024 at the Affiliated Hospital of Jiangnan University. Two pairs of fresh cervical cancer tissue (T) and adjacent normal tissue (ANT) samples were collected post-operation and promptly frozen at −80℃ for subsequent total protein isolation. The study was authorized by the Research Ethics Committee of Affiliated Hospital of Jiangnan University (approval number LS2021001). Detailed patient information is referred to Table S1(see Additional file).

Hematoxylin and eosin (H&E) staining and immunohistochemistry (IHC) staining

The embedded tumor tissues were cut into 4-µm thick slices. H&E staining was conducted using hematoxylin (Bioshark, China) and eosin (Bioshark, China) as primary reagents and was performed to evaluate histopathological features. IHC staining was performed to detect SPP1 protein levels. Slides were incubated with the corresponding primary antibodies overnight at 4 °C. The specimens were detected utilizing the chromogenic substrate DAB, counterstained with hematoxylin to label the nuclei and were transparently sealed with xylene (Shanghai Sangon, China). The subsequent analysis was completed using a digital section scanning system (3DHISTECH, Hungary). The IHC score was calculated as the product of the staining intensity score and the percentage of positive cells score. The staining intensity was scored as follows: negative = 0, low positive = 1, positive = 2, high positive = 3. The percentage of positive cells was scored as: ≤25% = 1, 26%−50% = 2, 51%−75% = 3, > 75% = 4. Statistical analysis was performed using a paired t-test.

Cell culture

Cell lines HEK293T, HeLa (HPV-18-positive), SiHa (HPV-16-positive), and C-33 A (HPV-negative) were purchased from the cell bank of the Chinese Academy of Sciences (Shanghai, China). All cell lines were cultured in high-glucose Dulbecco’s Modified Eagle’s Medium (DMEM, Gibco, CA, USA) supplemented with 10% fetal bovine serum (FBS, Sigma, St. Louis, MO, USA) at 37 °C in 5% CO2.

Vector construction and transfections

SPP1 sequence was cloned and inserted into the pLV3-H1 expression vector. Short hairpin RNA (shRNA) targeting SPP1 and negative controls were obtained from Shanghai Gemma Genetics (China). The synthesized pLV3-H1-shSPP1 and pLV3-H1-shNC plasmids, along with the envelope plasmid pMD2G and the packaging plasmid psPAX2, were introduced into 293 T cell culture dishes. Virus particles were harvested 48 h after plasmids transfection and then separately used to infect cervical cancer cell lines.

Western blotting

Cell and tissue samples were lysed in RIPA lysis buffer (BeyotimeBio, China) with protease inhibitors. The protein concentration was quantified using the BCA method (Thermo reagent kit). Protein samples were loaded for electrophoresis on SDS-PAGE and transferred to PVDF membranes. Western blotting was performed using primary antibodies against SPP1, TGF-β1, Smad2/3, Akt, N-cadherin, E-cadherin, Vimentin, Snail, GAPDH, and β-actin (overnight at 4℃). The membranes were then incubated with horseradish peroxidase-conjugated secondary antibodies (1:5000; Cell Signaling Technology, 7074 S) for 2 h at room temperature. The membrane was incubated and developed using a hypersensitive ECL chemiluminescence developer (Yeasen Biotech Co., Ltd., Shanghai, China), analysis was performed with Image Lab software. The antibodies used in this study and their dilution ratios are referred to in Table S2 (see Additional file).

Quantitative real-time PCR

Total RNA was isolated using trizol reagent (VazymeBio, China), and was reversely transcribed into complementary DNA (cDNA) using 4×HiScript III SuperMix Plus (Yeasen Biotech Co., Ltd., Shanghai, China). Expression of mRNA was quantified with the LightCycler 480 System (Roche; Basel, Switzerland) using the SYBR Green I Master, following the manufacturer’s instructions. The expression of target genes was analyzed by the 2−∆∆Ct method with β-actin and GAPDH as internal controls. The primers used in our study are listed as follows:

SPP1 F: 5’-AGTCCAGATTATATAAGCGGAA-3’ and R: 5’-CTTTTGGGGTCTACAACCAG- 3’;

β-actin F: 5’-TGACGTGGACATCCGCAAAG-3’ and R: 5’-CTGGAAGGTGGACAGCGAGG-3’;

Smad7 F: 5’-CCAACTGCAGACTGTCCAGA − 3’ and R: 5’-TCTCCTCCCAGTATGCCAC-3’;

GAPDH F: 5’-ACCACAGTCCATGCCATCAC-3’ and R: 5’- TCCACCACCCTGTTGCTGTA-3’.

MTT assay

Cells were seeded in a 96-well microplate overnight and per well was added with 20 µL the 3-(4,5-dimethylthiazol)2,5-diphenyltetrazolium bromide (MTT) reagent and incubated for 4 h at 37 °C. Afterward, the supernatant was discarded, and 150 µL dimethyl sulfoxide was added to the cells for 10 min. The light absorbance value was measured at 490 nm using a BioTek Synergy H4 microplate reader (Vermont, USA). The cell viability was calculated by normalizing to the blank wells.

Colony formation assays

The single-cell suspension was seeded with 800 cells per well in 6-well plates and incubated at 37 °C in a 5% CO₂ incubator for 14 days. The medium was changed regularly and colony formation was monitored. Cells were fixed in 4% paraformaldehyde (Biosharp, China) for 30 min and stained with crystal violet for 15 min (BeyotimeBio, China). The number of colonies containing more than 50 cells was then counted, and the cloning efficiency was assessed by calculating the colony formation rate and evaluating the size of the colonies.

Flow cytometry

Knockdown and non-knockdown cancer cells were harvested using EDTA-free trypsin and then resuspended in binding buffer. The cells were stained with Annexin V-FITC and propidium iodide (PI) for 30 min at room temperature in the dark according to the manufacturer’s instructions. The DNA content was then assessed using BD C6 Flow Cytometer (Becton Dickinson and Company, San Jose, CA, USA), and the distribution of cells across the G0/G1, S, and G2/M phases was analyzed using FlowJo software.

Wound healing assay

Knockdown and non-knockdown cancer cells were plated in 6-well plates and cultured until a monolayer of fused cell membranes was established. A straight line was evenly scratched on the bottom of the plate using the tip of a sterile 200 µL pipette. The cells were gently washed with PBS and then added to 5% serum medium. The width of the scratch was photographed and recorded under an inverted Zeiss microscope at 0 h, 24 h, and 48 h. The area of the scratch was measured using ImageJ software, and the migration rate was calculated using the formula (% migration rate = initial scratch width/remaining scratch width × 100%).

Transwell assays

For the migration experiment, Transwell chambers without matrix gel (Corning, USA) were plated in 24-well plates. The upper chamber was inoculated with cell suspensions deprived of serum-free medium (1 × 10⁵ cells/well) and a total of 500 µL of medium containing 10% FBS was added to the lower chamber. The cells were fixed with 4% paraformaldehyde after 24 h and stained with 0.1% crystal violet. The cells in the upper chamber were removed and the remaining cells were photographed under an inverted fluorescence microscope (Zessi, USA). For the invasion experiment, the upper chamber was pre-coated with matrix gel (50 µL, 1 mg/mL), and the remaining procedures followed those of the migration experiment. The invasive ability was assessed by comparing the number of cells that penetrated the membrane.

Cell adhesion assay

Knockdown and non-knockdown cancer cells (1 × 10⁴ cells/well) were plated in 96-well plates precoated with matrix gel (5–20 µg/mL) and cultured at 37 °C for 2 h. The adhesion rate was assessed by measuring the OD value of the adherent cells using the MTT assay and crystal violet staining (Adhesion rate% = number of adherent cells/number of inoculated cells × 100%).

Immunofluorescence (IF) assay - ghost pen cyclic peptide staining

The cells were fixed with 4% paraformaldehyde for 15 min and permeabilized with 0.1% Triton X-100 for 5 min to expose the cytoskeleton. The samples were incubated with FITC/TRITC-labeled ghost pen cyclic peptide (5–10 µg/mL) for 30 min to label the microfilament structure of F-actin and re-stained the nuclei of the cells with DAPI (1 µg/mL) for 5 min. Images were captured using Zeiss LSM880 laser scanning confocal microscope (Oberkochen, Germany). The cellular morphology parameters (the cellular filamentous pseudopod) were analyzed using ZEN software to evaluate their migration ability.

RNA-seq

Total RNA was isolated from cells for RNA sequencing using trizol reagent following standard protocols. The quality of the RNA was evaluated with an Agilent 2100 Bioanalyzer and a Nanodrop 2000. The libraries were constructed using the standard Illumina library preparation method. Each library underwent sequencing in 150 PE mode on an Illumina NovaSeq 6000, facilitated by Beijing Berry Biotechnology (Co., Ltd. China). The analysis method can be found in Bioinformatic analysis.

Animal experiments

Healthy immune-deficient female BALB/c-nude mice (4–6 weeks old, 14–16 g) were purchased from Spfbiotech (China) and cared for under a strict pathogen-free environment at Jiangnan University. They were randomly divided into different groups (n = 5 per group) and injected via the tail vein with SiHa GFP-shNC or SiHa GFP-shSPP1 (1 × 10⁶ cells/100 µL per mouse). Drug administration commenced four weeks post-transplantation. Administration groups received intraperitoneal injections of 10 mg/kg PTX (Shanghai Taoshu, China) in PBS every three days. The other two groups received intraperitoneal injections of PBS as a control. Mice’s body weight was recorded throughout the administration period. Four weeks after treatment, mice were anesthetized with isoflurane (3–5% for induction, 1.5–2% for maintenance) in 100% oxygen at 1 L/min. Bioluminescence images were then acquired using a chemiluminescence imaging system (PerkinElmer, USA) and analyzed with Living Image software. At the experimental endpoint, mice were placed in a dedicated CO₂ euthanasia chamber and exposed to a gradual fill of CO₂. Following confirmation of respiratory arrest and unconsciousness, cervical dislocation was performed to ensure death. Then, internal organs from mice exhibiting metastatic nodules were subjected to H&E staining for histological assessment of metastasis. All animal experiments were performed following the regulations of Jiangnan University Medical Experimental Animal Care Commission (JN.NO 20250515B0400815 [277]).

Statistical analysis

The results obtained were based on at least three independent experiments, and the data were presented as means ± SEM. Statistical analyses were conducted using GraphPad Prism version 10.0 (GraphPad Software, San Diego, CA, USA). Two-tailed Student’s t-test was used to determine the difference between two groups of datasets with similar variance, and analysis of variance (ANOVA) was used to determine the difference between more than two groups of datasets. P-value < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Results

SPP1 is highly expressed in cervical cancer and significantly correlated with poor clinical outcomes of cervical cancer patients

To explore the potential mechanisms driving cervical cancer progression, we analyzed RNA-seq data from three GEO datasets: GSE7803, GSE9750, and GSE138080. We examined the most DEGs across these standardized GEO datasets, presenting the top 20 genes with the highest expression differences in heat maps (Fig. 1A-C). We identified 34 overlapping DEGs in three GEO databases which are illustrated in a Venn diagram (Fig. 1D). Next, we analyzed the TCGA_CESC dataset and identified 423 genes associated with the overall survival (OS) of patients with cervical cancer (Fig. 1E). A comparison of the 34 overlapping DEGs with the OS-related genes revealed that SPP1 was the sole gene common to both lists (Fig. 1 F).

Analysis of SPP1 expression in the GSE7803, GSE9750, and GSE138080 datasets confirmed that SPP1 was significantly overexpressed in cervical cancer samples (Fig. 1G-I). Further validation in the TCGA+GTEx dataset revealed that SPP1 was markedly elevated in the tumor tissues of cervical cancer patients (Fig. 1J). In order to assess the clinical significance of SPP1 expression, we found that high levels of SPP1 were associated with tumor grades in cervical cancer patients (Fig. 1M), lymph node metastasis (Fig. 1L), and the predominant tumor type, squamous cell carcinoma (Fig. 1N). The relationship between SPP1 expression and patient prognosis was further investigated through Kaplan-Meier analysis to assess its association with survival in cervical cancer patients. The median OS for patients with high SPP1 expression was 20 months, compared to 24 months with low or no expression (Fig. 1K). Furthermore, progression-free survival (PFS) analysis based on the TCGA dataset (Fig. 1O), and disease-free survival (DFS) analysis using both TCGA and GEO datasets (Fig. 1P). These results indicated a significant negative correlation between SPP1 expression and the overall survival duration of cervical cancer patients. Subsequent western blotting analysis confirmed the upregulation of SPP1 protein expression in cervical cancer tissues (Fig. 1R-S). Next, we performed IHC analysis on both tumor and adjacent normal tissues from the 55 patients. Notably, we observed increased SPP1 staining in the tumor tissues compared with ANT in 73% of the patients (Fig. 1Q). Comprehensive analysis indicated a strong association between SPP1 overexpression and cervical cancer.

SPP1 does not affect cervical cancer cell proliferation

With the aim of elucidating the specific role of SPP1 in cervical cancer, we generated stable SPP1-knockdown lines in different cervical cancer cell lines, including C-33 A and SiHa. We confirmed the knockdown efficiency through western blotting (Fig. 2A-D) and RT-qPCR experiments (Supplementary Figure. 3). Based on careful evaluation, we selected the cervical cancer cell line demonstrating the highest stable knockdown efficiency via the shSPP1-355 sequence for use in subsequent functional assays. The MTT assay (Fig. 2E-F) indicated that SPP1 didn’t affect the viability or proliferation of cervical cancer cells. Furthermore, colony formation experiments (Fig. 2K-M) demonstrated that the proliferative capabilities of both cervical cancer cell lines remained unchanged. For further validation, flow cytometry was performed to analyze the cell cycle in cervical cancer cells with and without SPP1 knockdown (Fig. 2G-H, I-J). The results indicated that SPP1 knockdown didn’t modify the cell cycle of cervical cancer cells. In summary, SPP1 had no significant effect on the proliferation of cervical cancer cells.

SPP1 promotes cervical cancer cell migration and invasion

The analysis showed that SPP1 knockdown didn’t affect the proliferation of cervical cancer cells. Considering, the overexpression of SPP1 was associated with the malignant phenotype of cervical cancer (Fig. 1L-N), indicating a potential role of SPP1 in enhancing the invasiveness and migratory capacity of these cells. Consistent with expectations, wound healing assays (Fig. 3A-B, C-D) revealed that SPP1 knockdown in both cell lines suppressed the migratory capability of cervical cancer cells. Using the analysis of Expression Public 24Q2 and GEO (GSE9750 and GSE75034) databases (Fig. 3E), we examined SPP1 expression in three cervical cancer cell lines and confirmed the results by western blotting to ensure reliability (Fig. 3F-G). The analysis demonstrated that among the three cervical cancer cell types, SiHa cells exhibited the highest SPP1 expression. Additionally, as SPP1 was predominantly highly expressed in squamous cell carcinoma (Fig. 1N), SiHa cells were selected for further investigation.

Transwell assays indicated that SPP1 knockdown inhibited the migration (Fig. 3K-L) and invasion (Fig. 3H-I) of cervical cancer cells. Considering the role of cell adhesion ability in tumor metastasis, we also examined the adhesion ability of SPP1-knockdown SiHa cells (Fig. 3J, M, N). The results indicated that the adhesion ability of SPP1-knockdown cells was enhanced compared with the control group. This meant that the metastatic ability of cervical cancer cells had been inhibited.

SPP1 knockdown suppresses cervical cancer cell migration by inhibiting EMT through the TGF-β/Akt/Snail signaling pathways

RNA-seq analysis was performed on SPP1-knockdown and control SiHa cells to assess the transcriptomic alterations associated with SPP1’s regulatory role in cervical cancer metastasis and performed GSEA enrichment analysis of DEGs (Supplementary Figure. 4). We observed that SPP1 was enriched in key terms such as “TGF-β signaling pathway” and “Epithelial-mesenchymal transition”. To further enquiry the mechanisms underlying cervical cancer metastasis, the GO enrichment analysis (Fig. 4A) suggested that SPP1’s role in cervical cancer metastasis was closely linked to EMT. In testing this hypothesis, we performed western blotting (Fig. 4C-D) which revealed significant changes in EMT-related proteins in SPP1-knockdown cervical cancer cells. Notably, the expression of E-cadherin increased in SiHa cells after SPP1 knockdown, while the expression levels of N-cadherin, Snail, and Vimentin exhibited varying degrees of decrease. These findings suggested that SPP1 knockdown hinders the EMT process in cervical cancer cells.

Our investigation into how SPP1 induced EMT in cervical cancer, employing KEGG enrichment analysis of DEGs (Fig. 4B), revealed a strong correlation between SPP1 and TGF-β signaling pathways. For the purpose of confirming this association, we performed western blotting to analyze key proteins within the TGF-β signaling pathway (Fig. 4 F). The results showed that SPP1 knockdown significantly reduced phosphorylated (p-) Akt expression in SiHa cells, increased p-Smad2/3, and decreased TGF-β1 levels (Fig. 4E). Based on existing literature, we proposed that SPP1 knockdown suppressed cervical cancer progression by inhibiting p-Akt and reducing TGF-β1, thereby decreasing Snail expression to ultimately inhibit EMT and reduce tumor metastasis.

To validate whether SPP1 knockdown suppresses EMT specifically through the TGF-β/Akt/Snail pathway, we performed rescue experiments using the Akt activator SC79. SC79 treatment did not significantly affect the migration ability of SiHa-shNC cells. However, while SiHa-shSPP1 cells exhibited markedly reduced migration compared with SiHa-shNC cells, treatment with SC79 partially restored the migration ability of SiHa-shSPP1 cells relative to the untreated SiHa-shSPP1 group (Fig. 4G, H). Similar trends were observed for cell invasion under the same conditions (Fig. 4I, J). Western blotting analysis (Fig. 4K) revealed that SC79 treatment had no obvious effect on the expression of EMT-related proteins in SiHa-shNC cells. In contrast, in SiHa-shSPP1 cells, SC79 treatment led to decreased E-cadherin expression (Fig. 4L) and concurrent upregulation of N-cadherin (Fig. 4M), Vimentin (Fig. 4N), and Snail (Fig. 4O) compared with untreated SiHa-shSPP1 cells. These results indicate that the Akt activator SC79 rescues the EMT inhibition induced by SPP1 knockdown. Collectively, these findings demonstrate that SPP1 knockdown suppresses EMT specifically through the TGF-β/Akt/Snail pathway.

SPP1 knockdown alleviates PTX-induced EMT in vitro

Studies showed that PTX increases the risk of tumor metastasis through the induction of EMT. We separately exposed SPP1-knockdown and control SiHa cells to low-dose PTX to assess the potential of SPP1 in reversing PTX-induced metastasis. This study found that without affecting the proliferation activity of cells, the migration ability of SiHa cells treated with PTX was significantly stronger than that of the untreated group. Conversely, the migratory ability of SPP1-knockdown SiHa cells treated with PTX was notably diminished compared to the control group treated with PTX (Fig. 5A-B). We performed RNA-seq transcriptome and subsequent differential gene expression analysis on PTX-treated SPP1-knockdown and control SiHa cells to elucidate the precise mechanism underlying SPP1’s inhibition of PTX-induced migration. Heat maps displaying the top 15 genes exhibiting up-regulation or down-regulation were generated (Fig. 5C). Subsequent KEGG (Fig. 5J) and GO enrichment analyses (Fig. 5D) of the DEGs revealed significant enrichment of SPP1 in the “TGF-β signaling pathway” and “Epithelial-mesenchymal transition”.

To validate the findings from the enrichment analysis, we assessed the expression of EMT-related proteins in PTX-treated and untreated SPP1-knockdown SiHa cells, along with their respective controls, using western blotting (Fig. 5F). The results indicated that, compared to PTX-treated SiHa cells, E-cadherin expression was elevated in PTX-treated SPP1-knockdown SiHa cells (Fig. 5E). In contrast, the expression levels of N-cadherin (Fig. 5G), Snail (Fig. 5H), and Vimentin (Fig. 5I) were diminished to varying extents. Literature indicated a strong association between EMT and the cytoskeleton, which was a dynamic network primarily composed of microfilaments (actin filaments), microtubules, and intermediate filaments. Filamentous pseudopods were F-actin-rich membrane protrusions that were indicative of heightened cell motility. We performed an IF assay to visualize filamentous pseudopods by staining F-actin with phalloidin (Fig. 5K), revealed a reduction in filamentous pseudopods in SPP1-knockdown cervical cancer cells and an increase in PTX-treated cervical cancer cells. Importantly, SPP1 knockdown significantly decreased the number of filamentous pseudopods in PTX-treated cervical cancer cells compared to those in PTX-treated cervical cancer cells. This finding suggested that the reduction in actin-rich filamentous pseudopod formation due to SPP1 knockdown also counteracted the PTX-induced alterations in the cytoskeleton, thereby inhibiting metastasis.

SPP1 knockdown alleviates PTX-induced EMT in vivo

We constructed a mouse lung metastasis model using SPP1-knockdown SiHa cells to evaluate the protein’s role in cervical cancer cells. Throughout the treatment period, there were no significant changes in the body weight of mice across all groups (Fig. 6C) or in the rate of body weight change (Fig. 6D). These findings suggested that SPP1 and 10 mg/kg PTX didn’t exert notable toxic or side effects on the growth of either the mice or the tumors. In vivo imaging of small animals revealed that the average fluorescence intensity in the SPP1 knockdown group was lower than that in the control group. Additionally, the average fluorescence intensity of the SPP1 non-knockdown group treated with PTX was significantly enhanced compared to the control group. However, the average fluorescence intensity of the PTX-treated SPP1 knockdown group was lower than that of the PTX-treated SPP1 non-knockdown group (Fig. 6A-B). These findings indicate that SPP1 knockdown can reverse PTX-induced tumor metastasis in lung metastasis models. Building on this, we performed chemiluminescence imaging of the lungs from each mouse group (Fig. 6E-F), obtaining results consistent with the in vivo imaging. Subsequently, we performed H&E staining on the internal organs of the mice (Fig. 6G) and statistically analyzed the number of pulmonary metastatic nodules (Fig. 6H). The analysis revealed that the pulmonary metastatic nodules in the mice correlated with the fluorescence imaging results, while no significant changes were observed in the tissue morphology of other internal organs.

Discussion

Cancer statistics (2025) indicate that approximately 13,360 new cases of cervical cancer and about 4,320 deaths occurred in the U.S. alone within a year. Notably, the incidence of cervical cancer among women aged 30 to 44 is increasing31. Despite a significant decrease in overall cervical cancer incidence and mortality since the 1970 s due to vaccination and early screening, challenges persist with tumor metastasis and recurrence. Recurrence rates range from 10 to 20% in early-stage patients to 80–90% in locally advanced or metastasized cases32. Cervical cancer metastasis significantly correlates with poor patient prognosis and low 5-year survival rates. Hence, there is an urgent imperative to elucidate the precise mechanisms of cervical cancer metastasis and identify effective therapeutic targets.

SPP1 was first identified in the bone matrix and recognized as a significant non-collagenous bone matrix protein due to its strong affinity for hydroxyapatite7. Subsequent research found that SPP1 is broadly distributed across various tissue cells, including cardiac fibroblasts, bone cells, macrophages, smooth muscle cells, endothelial cells, tumor cells, and perivascular stromal cells. The structure of the SPP1 protein features multiple key functional domains, such as the integrin binding site, characterized by the classic RGD sequence, and the CD44 receptor binding region. These structural attributes allow SPP1 to function as a bridge molecule, linking cell surface receptors with extracellular matrix components, thereby initiating downstream signal transduction and mediating essential biological processes, including cell adhesion and signal transduction4. There is substantial evidence indicating that SPP1 is associated with tumor immunity and metastasis. Shen Lin et al. revealed that the SPP1/CXCL12 axis is a potential target and biomarker for precise regulation of colorectal cancer immunotherapy by colorectal cancer liver metastasis33. Bo Wei et al. identified that SPP1 + macrophages in liver metastases induce a distinct stress response T-cell subset via SPP1/CD44/PI3K/Akt signaling, facilitating cancer immune evasion. SPP1 inhibition can reverse the T-cell stress state, suppress liver metastasis growth, and present a novel strategy to enhance immunotherapy efficacy for colorectal cancer liver metastases34. Recent research also revealed that SPP1 serves as a core regulator for maintaining the fate of mesenchymal-type cells in pancreatic ductal adenocarcinoma24. SPP1 monoclonal antibody therapy can disrupt the generation of mesenchymal-type cells through the CD61/BMP2/GREM1 pathway, thereby markedly restrained tumor growth and distant metastasis, prolonged survival, and exhibited substantial potential for clinical translation.

In this study, we examined gene expression profiles of various cervical cancers to identify genes linked to clinical prognosis. The data from the clinic sample revealed a high expression of SPP1 in cervical cancer, and bioinformatic data suggested that SPP1 expression correlates with poor prognosis, tumor grade, indicating its involvement in cervical cancer progression. Our gene selection may have missed some relevant genes due to its focus on reproducibility; future validation with more diverse datasets is required. In line with the cellular biological function of SPP1, its knockdown doesn’t inhibit tumor proliferation but suppresses migration and invasion by inhibiting the EMT of cervical cancer cells (downregulation of N-cadherin and Vimentin, upregulation of E-cadherin). In contrast to the canonical TGF-β/Smad signaling pathway, SPP1 knockdown activates the phosphorylation of Smad2/3 and inhibits the phosphorylation of Akt35. Considering Smad7 is the core negative regulatory protein of the TGF-β signaling36–38, and the decrease in Smad7 levels makes cells abnormally sensitive to TGF-β signaling, we detected the expression of Smad7, found SPP1 knockdown leads to downregulation of Smad7 (Supplementary Figure. 5). Further rescue experiments using the Akt activator SC79 confirmed the essential role of the TGF-β/Akt/Snail pathway in mediating this effect. SC79 treatment partially reversed the migration and invasion defects caused by SPP1 knockdown, accompanied by restoration of the mesenchymal markers N-cadherin, Vimentin, and Snail, as well as suppression of the epithelial marker E-cadherin. Notably, SC79 did not alter these phenotypes in control cells, ruling out off-target effects. These findings provide direct mechanistic evidence that SPP1 promotes EMT and tumor progression via activating the TGF-β/Akt/Snail axis. Overall, SPP1 knockdown inhibists EMT through TGF-β/Akt/Snail signaling, finally suppressing cell invasion and metastasis in cervical cancer.

PTX is a commonly used first-line chemotherapeutic agent for clinical cervical cancer. However, recent findings suggest that PTX might enhance tumor invasion and metastasis by inducing EMT alongside its cytotoxic effects. The PTX-triggered EMT plays a crucial role in tumor metastasis and drug resistance15. Research indicates that PTX resistance and EMT induction involve the activation of the aromatic hydrocarbon receptor (AhR) pathway, leading to upregulation of the CYP1B1 enzyme, which is associated with PTX resistance and EMT progression. The reversal of PTX resistance and EMT inhibition can be achieved through the use of highly selective CYP1B1 inhibitors such as Compound B1439. Research has shown that in the gastric cancer peritoneal metastasis model, elevated THSD4 protein levels were linked to PTX resistance through interactions with the midkine and EMT pathways40. Nevertheless, the mechanism by which PTX-induced EMT potentially facilitates metastasis in cervical cancer remains unclear. In this study, we found that SPP1 knockdown can alleviate PTX-induced EMT. Transcriptomics analysis revealed that the mechanism primarily involves the TGF-β signaling pathway. In vivo experiments further confirmed the role of SPP1 in cervical cancer metastasis and PTX-induced EMT, suggesting that SPP1 could serve as a potential target for further investigation in cervical cancer therapy.

In summary, this study is the first to clarify the role of SPP1 in tumor cell mesenchymal transformation in cervical cancer, providing a theoretical basis for SPP1 as a therapeutic target for cervical cancer metastasis. Further research will be conducted to elucidate the specific molecular mechanism by which SPP1 regulates the TGF-β signaling pathway.

Conclusions

In conclusion, our study confirmed that SPP1 is highly expressed in cervical cancer clinical samples and is associated with low survival rates. Although SPP1 does not influence cell proliferation of cervical cancer cells, it truly promotes cell invasion and migration. SPP1 knockdown inhibits the EMT through suppressing the TGF-β/Akt/Snail signaling pathway, and alleviates PTX-induced EMT both in vitro and in vivo. These findings collectively suggest that targeting SPP1 may represent a promising therapeutic strategy to inhibit metastasis in cervical cancer.
