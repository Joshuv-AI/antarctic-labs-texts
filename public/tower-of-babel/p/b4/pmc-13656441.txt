Shifts in tuberculosis treatment outcomes despite timely treatment initiation during and after the COVID-19 pandemic: a retrospective cohort study in a tertiary-level hospital in Mexico
Authors: Luis Eduardo del Moral-Trinidad, Melva Guadalupe Herrera-Godina, Esteban González-Díaz, Sara Alejandra Aguirre-Díaz, Gilberto Silva-Bañuelos
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundThe COVID-19 pandemic disrupted tuberculosis (TB) services worldwide, primarily affecting diagnosis and treatment initiation. However, the impact of the post-pandemic period on TB treatment outcomes remains poorly understood. We aimed to evaluate changes in TB treatment outcomes during and after the COVID-19 pandemic and to determine whether these changes occurred independently of delays in treatment initiation.MethodsWe conducted a retrospective cohort study including adult patients diagnosed with TB at a tertiary referral hospital in Mexico between 2020 and 2025. Patients were categorized into COVID (2020–2022) and post-COVID (2023–2025) periods. The primary outcome was unfavorable treatment outcome (death, loss to follow-up, or treatment failure). Time from diagnosis to treatment initiation was assessed, and clinically relevant delay was defined using multiple thresholds. Multivariable logistic regression and Cox proportional hazards models were used to evaluate associations between period and outcomes, adjusting for age, sex, HIV status, undernutrition, and TB characteristics. Sensitivity analyses included exclusion of patients with HIV and socially vulnerable populations.ResultsA total of 1,299 patients were included. The proportion of unfavorable outcomes increased from 27.1% in the COVID period to 34.4% in the post-COVID period (p < 0.001), driven by a marked increase in TB-related mortality (2.1% vs. 10.6%). Time to treatment initiation did not differ between periods (median 0 days in both groups). In multivariable analysis, the post-COVID period was independently associated with unfavorable outcomes (adjusted OR 1.39, 95% CI 1.03–1.88) and higher hazard of adverse events (HR 1.33, 95% CI 1.09–1.63). Sensitivity analyses showed attenuation of this association after excluding patients with HIV (aOR 1.21, 95% CI 0.89–1.65), though the direction of effect remained consistent. The post-COVID cohort exhibited higher prevalence of diabetes and substance use, alongside shifts in sociodemographic characteristics.ConclusionTuberculosis treatment outcomes worsened in the post-COVID period despite largely preserved overall timeliness of treatment initiation. These findings suggest that factors beyond treatment initiation delay, including clinical complexity, comorbidity burden, and potential challenges in continuity of care, may have contributed to adverse outcomes.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at https://doi.org/10.1186/s12879-026-13999-9.Keywords: Tuberculosis, COVID-19, Treatment outcomes, Risk factors, Health systems, Mexico

Background

Tuberculosis (TB) remains a major global public health challenge, with an estimated 10 million new cases and 1.5 million deaths annually worldwide [1]. In Mexico, TB continues to represent a significant burden, with an incidence of approximately 19 cases per 100,000 population and a mortality rate of 1.68 per 100,000 prior to the COVID-19 pandemic, underscoring the need for sustained and effective control strategies [2].

The emergence of the COVID-19 pandemic profoundly disrupted health systems worldwide, affecting the diagnosis, treatment, and follow-up of chronic infectious diseases such as tuberculosis [3]. Global reports have documented substantial declines in TB notifications, delays in diagnosis, and interruptions in care during the pandemic period, largely driven by reduced healthcare access, resource reallocation, and changes in health-seeking behavior [4–6].

Successful tuberculosis control relies not only on timely diagnosis but also on sustained adherence to prolonged treatment regimens. However, treatment outcomes remain suboptimal in many settings, with a considerable proportion of patients experiencing unfavorable outcomes, including death, treatment failure, or loss to follow-up [7]. These outcomes are influenced by a complex interplay of factors, including socioeconomic conditions, access to care, comorbidities, and behavioral determinants [8–10]. Importantly, patients who do not complete treatment are at increased risk of mortality and contribute to ongoing transmission and the development of drug-resistant tuberculosis, which further complicates management and increases healthcare costs [1, 8].

Beyond direct healthcare disruptions, the COVID-19 pandemic may have reshaped the clinical and social profile of patients with tuberculosis. Emerging evidence suggests that delayed care-seeking, accumulated undiagnosed cases, and worsening social and mental health conditions may have altered both disease severity at presentation and patients’ ability to complete treatment [11, 12]. These changes raise concern that the impact of the pandemic on TB outcomes may extend beyond the acute phase of disruption.

Despite growing evidence on the effects of COVID-19 on TB diagnosis and service delivery, data on treatment outcomes in the post-pandemic period remain limited, particularly in middle-income countries and tertiary care settings [13]. In Mexico, where tertiary hospitals manage a substantial proportion of complex cases—including patients with comorbidities such as HIV infection—the long-term impact of the pandemic on treatment outcomes has not been well characterized [14].

Therefore, the aim of this study was to evaluate temporal changes in tuberculosis treatment outcomes among patients diagnosed at a tertiary-level hospital in Mexico during and after the COVID-19 pandemic, and to determine whether these changes occurred independently of delays in treatment initiation. By examining both clinical outcomes and temporal patterns, this study seeks to provide insight into the evolving impact of the pandemic on tuberculosis care and to inform strategies to improve patient management in the post-pandemic era.

Methodology

Study design and setting

We conducted a retrospective cohort study using routine epidemiological surveillance records to evaluate TB treatment outcomes and associated factors among patients diagnosed at the Hospital Civil de Guadalajara Fray Antonio Alcalde, a large tertiary referral center serving western Mexico, between January 2020 and December 2025. This health facility is one of the largest in the region and serves as a reference site for surrounding cities and states.

By the end of 2023, a total of 791 cases of respiratory tuberculosis were reported in the state of Jalisco, of which 292 were diagnosed in this hospital [5].

Study population and sample size

All patients who were diagnosed during the period of the study were included. A total of 1,299 patients with complete treatment outcome data were included in the final analysis. We excluded patients with drug-resistant TB, those with missing outcome data, and individuals who did not initiate treatment or were not traceable within the national registry system. Patients whose treatment was ongoing at the time of database closure were excluded because a final treatment outcome could not yet be assigned. This included 23 patients who were still receiving treatment and therefore were not eligible for the treatment outcome analysis. The selection process is summarized in Fig. 1.

Study procedure

Data were obtained from institutional epidemiological surveillance records rather than detailed clinical charts. These registries are routinely used for tuberculosis reporting and include standardized sociodemographic, clinical, and treatment outcome variables. While this approach ensured complete follow-up through linkage with the national TB surveillance system, it limited the availability of detailed clinical information such as disease severity at presentation, radiographic findings, and length of hospitalization.

Study variables

Outcome: The primary outcome was tuberculosis treatment outcome, classified according to national guidelines and World Health Organization definitions. For analytical purposes, outcomes were dichotomized into favorable (cure or treatment completion) and unfavorable (death, treatment failure, or loss to follow-up) [6].

Deaths were classified according to the cause recorded in the death certificate and the national tuberculosis surveillance registry following national tuberculosis program definitions and WHO treatment outcome definitions [6]. Deaths were considered TB-related when tuberculosis was recorded as the underlying or contributing cause of death, or when the registry classified the treatment outcome as death due to TB. Deaths occurring during treatment but attributed in the certificate or registry to causes other than tuberculosis were classified as deaths from other causes. No independent clinical adjudication of mortality attribution was performed.

Exposure of interest: The main exposure of interest was the period of diagnosis, defined a priori based on the epidemiological context in Mexico. The COVID period included patients diagnosed between January 2020 and December 2022, corresponding to the major pandemic waves, while the post-COVID period included those diagnosed from January 2023 to December 2025, reflecting the transition to routine healthcare conditions. In addition, calendar year of diagnosis was analyzed as a continuous variable to explore temporal trends. This classification was based on the epidemiological context and national reports describing recovery of health service utilization after the acute pandemic phase [3, 13]. To evaluate whether our findings depended on this predefined categorization, calendar year of diagnosis was additionally modeled as a continuous variable and explored using spline-based analyses in sensitivity analyses.

Treatment initiation delay was defined as the time between the date of TB diagnosis and the date of treatment initiation. Because there is no universally accepted cut-off for treatment initiation delay in TB, two thresholds were evaluated. A > 7-day threshold was selected based on previous programmatic and epidemiological studies that have assessed treatment initiation delay after TB diagnosis using this cut-off [15]. A > 14-day threshold was additionally evaluated as a more conservative definition of prolonged delay, supported by prior literature reporting treatment-delay distributions around this interval and by studies defining failure to initiate TB treatment within 14 days as a relevant pre-treatment care gap [15].

Covariates included demographic, clinical, and behavioral variables. Age was analyzed as a continuous variable, and sex as a binary variable. HIV status, undernutrition, and diabetes mellitus were included as key clinical comorbidities. Undernutrition was defined as a body mass index (BMI) < 18.5 kg/m² at the time of tuberculosis diagnosis, according to the World Health Organization classification. Behavioral factors included active smoking, alcohol use, and illicit drug use at the time of diagnosis. Socioeconomic variables included educational level and occupation, both grouped into categories to ensure analytical stability. Social vulnerability was defined as a binary variable. Patients were classified as socially vulnerable if they were recorded as experiencing homelessness or being deprived of liberty at the time of diagnosis. Patients without either condition were classified as not socially vulnerable. No weighted score was used.

Disease characteristics included tuberculosis form, categorized as pulmonary or extrapulmonary, and type of patient (new, relapse, return after loss to follow-up, or return after treatment failure). Due to sparse data and unstable estimates, type of patient was not retained in the final multivariable models but was examined in stratified and exploratory analyses.

Data analysis

All analyses were performed in R version 4.6.0. Baseline characteristics were summarized using means and standard deviations or medians and interquartile ranges, as appropriate, while categorical variables were described as frequencies and percentages. Comparisons between the COVID and post-COVID periods were conducted using chi-square tests for categorical variables and Student’s t-test or Wilcoxon rank-sum test for continuous variables, depending on distributional assumptions.

The primary analytical approach used multivariable logistic regression to estimate adjusted odds ratios (aORs) and 95% confidence intervals (CIs) for unfavorable treatment outcomes. The main exposure was period of diagnosis, categorized as COVID (2020–2022) and post-COVID (2023–2025). Covariates included in the adjusted models were selected on clinical and epidemiological grounds and comprised age, sex, HIV status, undernutrition, diabetes mellitus, educational level, occupation, substance use variables, and tuberculosis form. Because some strata of treatment history were sparse and yielded unstable estimates, type of patient was not retained in the main parsimonious models, but its potential influence was evaluated through adjusted sensitivity analyses, restriction to new cases, and interaction testing.

Treatment delay was analyzed descriptively using time from diagnosis to treatment initiation. Because same-day treatment initiation was highly frequent, alternative thresholds were explored to identify delays of greater clinical relevance. Delay was first described using a > 7-day threshold, and a second threshold of > 14 days was used in multivariable models to capture clinically meaningful delays. Additional exploratory analyses using lower cutoffs were conducted as sensitivity analyses.

Sensitivity analysis

To evaluate whether the association between period and outcomes was robust to different specifications of time, calendar year of diagnosis was also modeled as a continuous variable. In addition, non-linear temporal patterns were explored using spline-based regression models, from which predicted probabilities of unfavorable outcomes across calendar years were derived.

Time-to-event analyses were performed using Kaplan–Meier methods and Cox proportional hazards regression, defining follow-up time as the interval between diagnosis and the recorded treatment outcome. Hazard ratios (HRs) and 95% CIs were estimated for unfavorable outcomes. The proportional hazards assumption was assessed using Schoenfeld residuals; because this assumption was not fully met for some variables, Cox estimates were interpreted as average effects over follow-up and used as supportive rather than primary analyses.

Other analysis conducted included exclusion of patients living with HIV, deprived of liberty, restriction to new TB cases, adjustment for type of patient, and interaction testing between period and type of patient. Statistical significance was defined as a two-sided p-value < 0.05.

Ethics

Given the retrospective nature of the study, informed consent was not required. A full study protocol which included an informed consent waiver form was prepared and approved by the institutional bioethics committee (registration number CEI 53/23, approved in May 2025). To ensure data confidentiality, sensitive data collection was omitted, and alphanumeric identifiers were used to prevent identification. This project adheres to the principles of the Declaration of Helsinki and guidelines established by the hospital’s ethics committee.

Results

Study populations and baseline characteristics

A total of 1,299 patients were included, with 580 diagnosed during the COVID period and 719 in the post-COVID period. Patients in the post-COVID period were slightly older (mean age 42.8 vs. 40.1 years; p = 0.004). Sex distribution was similar across periods (27.8% female in both groups; p = 1.000).

Significant differences were observed in socioeconomic and clinical characteristics. Post-COVID patients had higher levels of education (secondary or higher: 61.6% vs. 55.0%; p = 0.021) and were more likely to be formally employed (17.4% vs. 8.3%; p < 0.001), while the proportion of socially vulnerable individuals decreased (33.9% vs. 40.9%).

Comorbidities and behavioral factors also differed. The prevalence of diabetes increased in the post-COVID period (14.0% vs. 8.8%; p = 0.004), while undernutrition decreased (9.7% vs. 15.0%; p = 0.005). Alcohol use, smoking, and drug use were all more frequent in the post-COVID period (all p < 0.001). HIV prevalence remained stable (29.0% vs. 28.8%; p = 0.994). The distribution of pulmonary versus extrapulmonary tuberculosis did not differ significantly between periods (p = 0.176) (Table 1).

Similarly, the time from diagnosis to treatment initiation was comparable, with a median of 0 days in both groups (p = 0.579). Despite this, treatment outcomes differed substantially. The proportion of unfavorable outcomes increased from 27.1% during the COVID period to 34.4% in the post-COVID period (p < 0.001) (Table 2).

The distribution of patient types was broadly similar between periods, although an increase in patients returning after treatment failure was observed in the post-COVID period (8.3% vs. 2.6%). Unfavorable outcomes varied by patient type, being lowest among new cases (30.7%) and highest among patients returning after treatment failure (45.3%). Multivariable analysis

In adjusted models, the post-COVID period was associated with increased odds of clinically relevant delay (> 14 days) (aOR 1.84, 95% CI 1.19–2.90), indicating that longer delays were more frequent in a subset of patients. However, at the population level, time to treatment initiation remained similar between periods, with a median of 0 days in both groups and no significant difference using the > 7-day delay threshold. The post-COVID period was also independently associated with unfavorable treatment outcomes (aOR 1.39, 95% CI 1.03–1.88).Male sex (aOR 1.61, 95% CI 1.14–2.28) and HIV infection (aOR 1.66, 95% CI 1.06–2.60) were also associated with higher odds of unfavorable outcomes. Pulmonary tuberculosis was associated with a modest increase in risk (aOR 1.32, 95% CI 1.01–1.72). Notably, treatment delay > 7 days was not associated with unfavorable outcomes (aOR 0.78, 95% CI 0.52–1.15) (Table 5).

Sensitivity and temporal analyses

Sensitivity analyses confirmed the robustness of the findings. The association between the post-COVID period and unfavorable outcomes persisted after adjustment for patient type and in analyses restricted to new cases (Supplementary Table S1, S2 and S3).

Modeling calendar time as a continuous variable showed that each additional year was associated with increased odds of unfavorable outcomes (aOR 1.21, 95% CI 1.12–1.31; p < 0.001) (Supplementary Table S4).

Non-linear modeling using spline functions demonstrated a progressive increase in the predicted probability of unfavorable outcomes over time (Figure S1).

In sensitivity analyses excluding patients with HIV, the association between the post-COVID period and unfavorable outcomes was attenuated and no longer statistically significant (aOR 1.21, 95% CI 0.90–1.63; p = 0.215), suggesting that the high burden of HIV in this cohort contributes substantially to the observed differences in outcomes. However, the direction of the association remained consistent, indicating that temporal changes beyond HIV status may also play a role (Supplementary Table S5).

Time-to-event analysis using Kaplan–Meier methods showed a significantly higher probability of unfavorable outcomes over time among patients diagnosed in the post-COVID period compared to the COVID period (log-rank p = 0.003) (Figure S2). The divergence between curves became more apparent during follow-up, indicating a sustained increase in risk rather than an early transient effect. These findings were consistent with the multivariable Cox regression analysis, which demonstrated an increased hazard of unfavorable outcomes in the post-COVID period (aHR 1.33, 95% CI 1.09–1.63; p = 0.005) (Supplementary Table S6).

Results were consistent after excluding individuals deprived of liberty, with a persistent association between the post-COVID period and unfavorable outcomes (aOR 1.52, 95% CI 1.19–1.95; p < 0.001) (Supplementary Table S7).

Discussion

In this cohort of patients with tuberculosis treated at a tertiary referral center, treatment outcomes were worse in the post-COVID period despite similar timeliness of treatment initiation. The proportion of unfavorable outcomes increased from 27.1% to 34.4%, driven primarily by a rise in TB-related mortality (2.1% vs. 10.6%), which also occurred earlier after diagnosis. These findings suggest that factors beyond delays in treatment initiation contributed to the deterioration in outcomes.

The observed changes are consistent with two non-mutually exclusive mechanisms that may explain the paradoxical worsening of outcomes despite preserved treatment initiation. First, patients presenting in the post-COVID period may represent a cohort with more advanced or accumulated disease. During the acute phases of the pandemic, disruptions in healthcare access and changes in care-seeking behavior likely resulted in delayed or missed diagnoses, particularly among patients with less severe symptoms [16]. As a result, those presenting in the post-COVID period may disproportionately reflect individuals who either progressed without care or re-entered the health system with a higher burden of disease. This interpretation is supported by the marked increase in TB-related mortality and its earlier occurrence after diagnosis in our cohort.

Second, the pattern of loss to follow-up suggests that the challenge may not lie in initiating treatment but in sustaining it. Although the overall proportion of patients lost to follow-up changed only modestly, the substantially later timing of these events in the post-COVID period indicates that disengagement occurs after initial linkage to care. In decentralized treatment systems such as ours, where therapy is continued at primary-level facilities, this pattern may reflect disruptions in continuity of care, weakened follow-up structures, or broader social instability affecting long-term adherence [17].

A growing body of evidence has documented the disruptive impact of the COVID-19 pandemic on tuberculosis diagnosis and care pathways. Multiple studies have consistently shown increased diagnostic delays during the pandemic period, driven by both patient-related and system-level factors, including fear of healthcare settings, reallocation of resources, and overlapping clinical presentations with COVID-19 [18–21].

For example, national data from Korea demonstrated a significant increase in time to treatment initiation during 2020 [18], while data from Italy showed substantially prolonged patient delays and more severe clinical presentations during the first wave [19]. Similarly, qualitative and mixed-methods evidence from Peru highlighted how patients experienced delays due to misattribution of symptoms to COVID-19 and reduced access to diagnostic services [20]. Studies conducted in Mexico have shown substantial declines in TB case detection and treatment success during the pandemic, alongside disruptions in surveillance and access to care, particularly in high-burden regions such as Baja California [22].

However, our findings diverge from this established pattern in an important way. In contrast to prior studies that focused on delays during the acute pandemic phase, we observed no meaningful differences in time to treatment initiation between periods. This aligns with emerging longitudinal data suggesting that, in some settings, diagnostic delays may have improved or normalized after the initial pandemic disruption [23]. At the same time, evidence indicates that the impact of COVID-19 on healthcare-seeking behavior and diagnostic processes has been heterogeneous across regions and over time [24]. Together, these findings suggest that delays alone cannot fully explain post-pandemic patterns in tuberculosis outcomes.

Instead, our results support the hypothesis that the post-COVID period is characterized by a shift in the underlying clinical profile of patients. This interpretation is consistent with reports describing reduced TB notifications during the pandemic followed by concerns about accumulated undiagnosed cases and more advanced disease at presentation [25]. Additional evidence from post-pandemic settings indicates that diagnostic delays and advanced disease presentations persist even after healthcare systems begin to recover, reflecting ongoing disruptions in care-seeking behavior and healthcare access [16]. In our study, the marked increase in TB-related mortality and its earlier occurrence after diagnosis provide indirect evidence supporting this mechanism.

The observed patterns are also consistent with the broader concept of delayed disease burden and post-disruption effects. Studies conducted in different epidemiological contexts have shown that disruptions caused by COVID-19 may lead not only to reduced case detection but also to downstream increases in disease severity and mortality [21]. This phenomenon has been described in other infectious disease contexts and reflects the cumulative impact of delayed diagnosis, interrupted care, and health system strain [17].

Beyond clinical severity, our findings highlight the role of patient complexity and behavioral risk factors in shaping outcomes. The post-COVID cohort exhibited higher prevalence of diabetes and substantially increased levels of alcohol, tobacco, and drug use, suggesting a shift toward a more complex and vulnerable patient population. This is particularly relevant given the well-established interaction between tuberculosis and diabetes, where comorbidity is associated with increased risk of treatment failure, mortality, and delayed microbiological response, especially in low- and middle-income settings [26]. Previous studies have identified comorbidities and social determinants as key drivers of diagnostic delay and poor outcomes in tuberculosis [27], reinforcing the importance of considering these factors in post-pandemic analyses.

At the same time, an additional and less explored explanation emerges from our data: a shift in the sociodemographic composition of the population accessing care. Patients in the post-COVID period exhibited higher educational attainment and a greater proportion of formal employment, suggesting a relative underrepresentation of socially vulnerable groups. Evidence from Mexico indicates that individuals with lower education, rural residence, and higher social marginalization—particularly indigenous populations—experience worse TB treatment outcomes and face substantial barriers to care [28]. In this context, the apparent improvement in socioeconomic indicators within our cohort may not reflect true population-level changes, but rather differential access to tertiary care services.

This raises the possibility that the most vulnerable populations—those at highest risk of adverse outcomes—may be underdiagnosed or excluded from referral pathways in the post-pandemic period. If so, our findings may represent a conservative estimate of the true deterioration in TB outcomes, with broader implications for health equity and disease control at the population level.

Although diabetes mellitus was more frequent among patients diagnosed in the post-COVID period, it was not disproportionately concentrated among those with adverse outcomes. Therefore, diabetes may reflect a higher comorbidity burden in the post-COVID cohort, but it does not fully explain the observed increase in poor outcomes and mortality. These findings suggest that the post-COVID deterioration in TB outcomes may be better interpreted as a multifactorial phenomenon related to accumulated diagnostic delays, altered referral patterns, residual health-system disruption, and delayed presentation to tertiary care.

The role of healthcare system strain and behavioral changes further supports this interpretation. The COVID-19 pandemic overwhelmed health systems, reallocated resources, and disrupted essential services, including TB programs [16, 24] These effects were not uniform and may have disproportionately impacted continuity of care in decentralized systems such as ours, where long-term treatment depends on coordination between tertiary and primary-level facilities.

The high prevalence of HIV infection in our cohort further underscores the complexity of this population. Evidence suggests that coexisting infections and comorbidities can exacerbate disease severity and worsen outcomes in tuberculosis patients [29, 30]. In sensitivity analyses excluding individuals with HIV, the association between the post-COVID period and unfavorable outcomes was attenuated, suggesting that HIV modifies the impact of temporal changes rather than acting solely as a confounder.

Importantly, the consistency of our findings across multiple analytical approaches—including logistic regression, time-to-event analyses, and temporal modeling—supports the robustness of the observed effect. This triangulation strengthens the interpretation that the deterioration in outcomes reflects a sustained epidemiological shift rather than a transient artifact of the pandemic period.

The attenuation of the post-COVID effect after excluding patients with HIV suggests that the excess risk of unfavorable outcomes may have been partly concentrated among individuals with TB/HIV coinfection. In this tertiary-care cohort, HIV represented a highly prevalent comorbidity and likely identified a subgroup with greater clinical complexity, including potential immunosuppression, higher risk of severe or disseminated disease, competing causes of mortality, and greater dependence on continuous linkage to care [31, 32]. Therefore, the attenuation observed in the sensitivity analysis should not be interpreted only as statistical confounding, but also as a possible indication that post-pandemic disruptions may have disproportionately affected clinically vulnerable patients. This finding supports the need for integrated TB/HIV care models and closer follow-up of coinfected patients during health-system recovery periods.

Taken together, our findings extend the existing literature by demonstrating that the indirect effects of the COVID-19 pandemic may persist beyond its acute phase and manifest as worse treatment outcomes, even when diagnostic processes appear to have recovered. This distinction is critical, as most prior studies have focused on delays in diagnosis rather than downstream clinical outcomes [19, 30].

These findings have important implications for tuberculosis control strategies. While ensuring timely diagnosis remains essential, our results suggest that this alone may be insufficient to improve outcomes in the post-pandemic context. Strengthening continuity of care, particularly at the interface between hospital and primary-level services, is critical. In addition, integrating mental health and substance use services into tuberculosis programs may be necessary to address the increasingly complex clinical and social profile of affected patients.

Conclusions

In this study, tuberculosis treatment outcomes worsened in the post-COVID period despite largely preserved overall timeliness of treatment initiation, suggesting that factors beyond access to diagnosis—particularly increased clinical complexity, comorbidity burden, and potential challenges in continuity of care—are driving adverse outcomes. Although longer treatment delays (> 14 days) were more frequent in a subset of post-COVID patients, treatment initiation delay alone did not explain the observed deterioration in outcomes. These findings suggest that factors beyond treatment initiation timeliness, including clinical complexity, comorbidity burden, and potential challenges in continuity of care, may have contributed to adverse outcomes in the post-COVID period.

Limitations

This study has several strengths, including a large real-world cohort, comprehensive outcome ascertainment, and the use of multiple complementary analytical approaches. However, important limitations must be acknowledged. The study is based on a single tertiary referral center and may not be generalizable to community-level populations. Additionally, data were derived from epidemiological surveillance systems rather than full clinical records, limiting the availability of detailed measures of disease severity at presentation, including radiographic extent, smear grading, ICU admission, and hospitalization duration. The absence of these severity indicators is particularly relevant because patients in the post-COVID period may have presented with more advanced disease or different clinical profiles, which could have influenced treatment outcomes and contributed to residual confounding. Additionally, classification of death as TB-related or due to other causes was based on death certificates and surveillance registry records, without independent clinical adjudication, which may have led to some degree of misclassification.

Furthermore, because the surveillance database only included patients who initiated treatment, we were unable to evaluate pre-treatment loss to follow-up, which represents another important consequence of the COVID-19 pandemic. The exclusion of patients with ongoing treatment may have slightly reduced the representativeness of the most recent diagnostic period; however, this group represented a small proportion of otherwise eligible patients.Finally, residual confounding due to unmeasured factors—such as pre-hospital delays, clinical severity at diagnosis, and health system factors affecting timely access to care—cannot be excluded. Despite these limitations, the internal consistency of the findings supports the interpretation of a real and clinically relevant shift in tuberculosis outcomes in the post-COVID era.
