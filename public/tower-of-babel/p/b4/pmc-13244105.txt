Reply
Authors: Shengxian Tu, Chenguang Li, Daixin Ding, Junbo Ge
Published: 2026

We thank Dr Skalidis and colleagues for their insightful comments, which allow more discussions on how radial wall strain (RWS) might fit into the clinical landscape alongside physiology and imaging for intermediate coronary lesions.1

Their first question asks whether RWS simply refines physiologic uncertainty or offers something genuinely distinct. In lesions with Murray law-based quantitative flow ratio (μFR) suggesting medical therapy but subsequently changed to revascularization by showing RWS data, we found something noteworthy: among lesions stratified by μFR into the ranges of 0.81 to 0.85, 0.86 to 0.90, and 0.91 to 1.00, the proportions of positive RWS (≥13.0%) were 50.0% (n = 2 of 4), 28.6% (n = 2 of 7), and 42.9% (n = 3 of 7), respectively. This suggests that physiologically mild lesions might still have high RWS, a biomechanical marker for vulnerable plaque.

The second question—should RWS guide revascularization or prompt personalized preventive therapy—is fundamental. Our survey reveals these interesting data. While 223 (11.8%) decisions transitioned from medical therapy to revascularization after RWS disclosure, a much larger group (855 decisions [43.3%]) shifted instead to intensive medical therapy. This pattern reveals a preference among participants for preventive pharmacotherapy over intervention for vulnerable plaques. Whether revascularization is a better strategy for some of these lesions is now being tested in the FAVOR V AMI (Functional and Angiography-Derived Strain Guided Multi-Vessel/Lesion Revascularization Strategy in Patients With Acute Myocardial Infarction) trial (NCT05669222). This study randomizes nonculprit lesions in patients with acute myocardial infarction and multivessel disease to either standard angiography guidance or integrated μFR and RWS guidance, in which revascularization is performed if μFR is ≤0.80 or RWS is ≥13%, and deferral if μFR is >0.80 and RWS is <13%. The primary endpoint is major adverse cardiac events defined as a composite of all-cause death, myocardial infarction, or ischemia-driven revascularization at follow-up.

In our survey, RWS-guided decisions aligned more closely with those based on quantitative lipid-to-cap ratio (LCR) than with qualitative optical coherence tomography (OCT) interpretation. This echoes our earlier findings: RWS correlated with LCR (r = 0.58, P < 0.001), with perfect colocalization of segments showed maximal values,2 but only weakly with OCT-derived vulnerable features such as thin-cap fibroatheroma, complicated plaque, healed plaque, and minimal lumen area ≤2.5 mm2 (r = 0.20, P < 0.001).3 The affinity between RWS and LCR is conceptually intuitive: vulnerable plaques, characterized by larger lipid burden and thinner fibrous caps (precisely what LCR quantifies), tend to undergo greater luminal deformation under pressures, and RWS captures this biomechanical response. Both tools also bring practical strengths: RWS can be computed with a single angiographic view without extra instrumental devices, and LCR can be derived automatically from OCT images.2 More importantly, LCR outperformed thin-cap fibroatheroma in predicting recurrent cardiovascular events in nonculprit vessels,4 and RWS provided incremental prognostic value beyond OCT alone.3 Together, these observations point toward a more reproducible, risk-calibrated framework for assessing plaque vulnerability. Whether this approach improves clinical outcomes, however, awaits rigorous evaluation in future studies.
