Efficient genome editing by CRISPR-Cas12a in common wheat via shoot apical meristem delivery
Authors: Weifeng Luo, Selda Ors, Hong Fang, Minting Zeng, Yanhao Cheng, Andrew Emil Sidawy, Yiping Qi
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBiolistic delivery of intron-optimized LbCas12a and its variants into shoot apical meristems results in efficient multiplexed genome editing in common wheat.

Dear Editor,

The CRISPR-Cas12a system has emerged as a powerful genome editing (GE) tool, particularly suited for targets with T-rich (TTTV or TTV) protospacer adjacent motifs (PAMs) (Tuncel et al. 2025). While Cas12a has been successfully applied in a variety of plant species, including Arabidopsis thaliana, rice (Oryza sativa), soybean (Glycine max), and tobacco (Nicotiana benthamiana) (Endo et al. 2016; Begemann et al. 2017; Hu et al. 2017; Kim et al. 2017; Tang et al. 2017; Xu et al. 2017; Yin et al. 2017), its efficiency in common wheat (Triticum aestivum) remains limited. A prior study reported low Cas12a-mediated GE efficiency in wheat using Agrobacterium-mediated transformation (Wang et al. 2021), which has hindered broader adoption of the system in wheat. To overcome the limitation of conventional tissue culture-based transformation methods, a Shoot Apical Meristem (SAM)-based biolistic delivery method has been developed for genotype-independent CRISPR-Cas9 mediated GE in wheat (Hamada et al. 2017; Liu et al. 2021; Kumagai et al. 2022). The sub-epidermal (L2) cells of SAM maintain the potential to develop into germline cells, therefore mutations that occur in L2 cells can lead to stably edited plants in the next generation. In this study, to augment CRISPR-Cas12a applications in wheat, we investigated factors that could improve Cas12a-mediated GE efficiency in wheat using the SAM-based delivery approach.

Initially, we constructed a LbCas12a construct targeting TaPDS and delivered it into the SAMs to assess GE efficiency in the common wheat variety Fielder (Supplementary Fig. S1A, Supplementary Methods). In our system, both Cas12a and crRNA were expressed under the maize ubiquitin promoter (pZmUbi), with the crRNA flanked and processed by hammerhead (HH) and hepatitis delta virus (HDV) ribozymes (Tang et al. 2017). No detectable editing events were observed when SAMs were cultured at 22 °C after bombardment following previously established protocols (Supplementary Fig. S1B) (Hamada et al. 2017; Liu et al. 2021; Luo et al. 2024). Cas12a activity has been reported to be temperature-sensitive in rice, maize, and Arabidopsis (Malzahn et al. 2019), but its temperature response in wheat SAMs has not been comprehensively studied. To address this, we optimized the experimental protocol by introducing a high-temperature incubation step after bombardment (Fig. 1A). SAMs were cultured at elevated temperatures (26 °C, 30 °C, and 34 °C) in comparison to the standard 22 °C for 7 days after bombardment before being transferred to 22 °C for further growth. Our results showed that incubation at 30 °C after bombardment significantly increased GE efficiency at the TaPDS site in both T0 and T1 generations compared to the other temperatures tested (Supplementary Fig. S1, C and D, Supplementary Table S1). Based on next-generation sequencing (NGS) of PCR amplicons, the GE efficiency at 30 °C was approximately 2-fold higher than the one at 26 °C after bombardment. Two independent experiments consistently supported these findings (Supplementary Table S1). These data demonstrate that elevated culture temperatures immediately after bombardment enhance Cas12a activity in wheat via the SAM delivery method. Notably, a decline in plant survival was observed at 34 °C, indicating the need to balance editing efficiency with tissue viability. Thus, 30 °C was selected as the optimal post-bombardment incubation temperature for subsequent experiments.

Building on previous research that intron-optimized Cas12a variants improve GE efficiency in Arabidopsis (Schindele et al. 2023), we evaluated two promising LbCas12a variants, ttLbCas12a (Schindele and Puchta 2020) and LbCas12a-RRV (Zhang et al. 2023), and their corresponding intron-containing variants in wheat using the optimized SAM transformation protocol (Supplementary Fig. S2). The TaPDS site was used as the benchmark target for the six Cas12a constructs in two independent biological replicates via the SAM delivery (Fig. 1A). Although ttLbCas12a and LbCas12a-RRV were previously demonstrated to confer higher GE efficiency than wild-type LbCas12a in multiple plant species (Schindele and Puchta 2020; Zhang et al. 2023), we did not observe significant differences in GE efficiency among wild-type, ttLbCas12a, and LbCas12a-RRV variants under our experimental conditions in wheat (Fig. 1B and C; Supplementary Table S2). In contrast, intron-containing variants consistently showed over a two-fold increase in GE efficiency compared to their non-intron counterparts in both T0 and T1 generations (Fig. 1B and C; Supplementary Table S2). These results suggest that the intron-mediated enhancement of mRNA expression and stability could be a major contributor of improved Cas12a performance in wheat. Phenotypic and molecular analyses confirmed successful editing of the TaPDS site: representative T0 chimeric plants displayed albino sectors in their leaves (Fig. 1D and E), indicating knockout events across all six copies of TaPDS. Interestingly, the green tissues from the same plant could serve as source tissues that supported the growth and development of the albino lineage into spikes that had fertile seeds, which helped us confirm that mutations were stably inherited into the T1 generation (Fig. 1F and G).

To further validate the benefits of intron-containing Cas12a variants, we selected four additional guide RNAs (Ta-TS106-A, Ta-TS112-A, Ta-TS105-B and Ta-TS121-D) (Gaillochet et al. 2023) to assess multiplexed genome editing via the SAM delivery. Six multiplexed Cas12a vectors were constructed using the dual Pol II promoter and dual ribozyme system (Fig. 2A and B) (Zhang et al. 2021). Wheat SAMs were cultured at 30 °C for 7 days after bombardment and then transferred to 22 °C for further growth. NGS analysis of PCR amplicons confirmed that intron-containing variants again achieved significantly higher GE efficiencies, more than two-fold greater than non-intron variants, across all four target sites (Fig. 2C-F; Supplementary Table S3). As with our earlier observation, no significant differences were observed among the intron-containing variants themselves. Multiplex gene editing events were detected (Supplementary Table S4). Two independent experiments yielded consistent results (Fig. 2C-F; Supplementary Table S3, Supplementary Table S4). Genomic PCR results indicate that most positive T0 plants were created through transient expression of the CRISPR-Cas12a system in the SAM, while some plants contained integrated vector fragments in the genome (Supplementary Fig. S3). We further performed genome editing in two additional wheat cultivars and consistently obtained edited plants (Supplementary Table S5 and S6), demonstrating this approach is genotype independent.

In summary, our results demonstrate that post-bombardment incubation at 30 °C and the use of intron-containing Cas12a variants markedly enhance GE efficiency in wheat via the SAM-based biolistic delivery. These findings corroborate with previous studies highlighting the temperature sensitivity of Cas12a (Malzahn et al. 2019) and the efficacy of intron-containing Cas12a variants in wheat and barley (Schindele et al. 2023; Lawrenson et al. 2024). Moreover, our method achieved a GE efficiency of 5.4% (T1 progeny), which appeared to be higher than previously reported efficiencies (∼2.2%) using Agrobacterium-mediated transformation under high-temperature culture conditions (Wang et al. 2021). Coupling intron-containing LbCas12a and a high temperature treatment, we established an efficient CRISPR-Cas12a genome editing method via SAM delivery, which is genotype and tissue culture independent. Our method paves the way for more efficient functional genomics research and trait improvement efforts in wheat, facilitating the development of resilient, high-yield cultivars through precise genome modifications.
