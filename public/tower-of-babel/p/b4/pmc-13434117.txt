Distinct molecular profile in Sjögren’s syndrome is associated with disease activity and clinical manifestations
Authors: Carlo Perricone, Giacomo Cafaro, Martina Nicchi, Giulia Cruciani, Biancamaria Pianese, Lorenza Bruno, Roberto Dal Pozzolo, Roberta Cecchetti, Matteo Antonucci, Fabiana Topini, Onelia Bistoni, Patrizia Mecocci, Roberto Gerli, Elena Bartoloni
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Sjögren’s disease (SjD) is a systemic autoimmune condition for which reliable diagnostic and stratification biomarkers are still lacking. Several molecules have been implicated in its pathogenesis, and this study aimed to evaluate their potential as biomarkers in a monocentric cohort of SjD patients. Sera from 40 SjD patients (2016 ACR/EULAR criteria) and 41 matched controls were analyzed for 23 soluble inflammatory mediators using a Luminex® multiplex immunoassay. Clinical and laboratory correlations were examined, and multivariate logistic regression was performed. Compared with controls, SjD patients had significantly higher levels of IL-15 (11.52 ± 24.95 vs. 1.37 ± 1.26 pg/ml, P = 0.0009), monocyte chemoattractant protein (MCP)-1 (683.6 ± 1127 vs. 654.8 ± 608.9 pg/ml, P = 0.0203), TNFRII (8588 ± 2889 vs. 5505 ± 1007 pg/ml, P < 0.0001), and MMP-8 (6382 ± 12,697 vs. 6247 ± 6950 pg/ml, P = 0.0141), while IL-1RII was reduced (6267 ± 1699 vs. 8514 ± 2255 pg/ml, P < 0.0001). IL-15 correlated with hypergammaglobulinemia and monoclonal component, MCP-1 with focus score and sicca severity, and IL-1RII with anti-SSB positivity. Logistic regression identified TNFRII and IL-1RII as the strongest independent predictors of SjD (AUC = 0.89). This study supports the association of IL-15, TNFRII, and MCP-1 with immune dysregulation in SjD and highlights MMP-8 and IL-1RII as promising serological biomarkers. Validation in larger, longitudinal cohorts is warranted.

Introduction

Primary Sjögren’s disease (SjD) is a systemic chronic autoimmune disease of unknown origin that mainly affects exocrine glands such as the salivary and lachrymal glands leading to xerostomia and xerophthalmia1. Although rarely, the disease can potentially involve any organ system with pleomorphic clinical manifestations; this heterogeneity may be due to the multiple factors which contribute to the disease pathogenesis. In susceptible individuals several environmental factors, especially infectious agents, might play a crucial role in triggering the disease onset through a dysregulation of the immune system1,2.

Recent understanding of the pathogenic mechanisms of SjD has highlighted that several surrogate biomarkers might be useful for diagnosis, choice of treatment and prognosis. Among them, the role of autoantibodies anti-Ro52/SSA, anti-Ro60/SSA and anti-La/SSB is well established, and their presence has been associated with lower mean age at diagnosis and higher disease activity measured by the EULAR Sjögren’s Syndrome Disease Activity Index (ESSDAI)3,4. Reduced serum C3 and C4 levels seem to be associated with younger age at diagnosis and higher ESSDAI score. However, apart from autoantibodies, no other biomarker has yet been incorporated into the classification criteria for SjD, and the role of additional molecules remains under debate.

In this context, our study evaluated a panel of potential biomarkers in a monocentric cohort of SjD patients compared with a control group of healthy participants. In particular, we included molecules involved in interferon signaling, lymphocyte activation, monocyte recruitment, and ectopic germinal center formation that appear to play a major pathogenic role. Interleukin (IL)-15 was selected because of its known involvement in T-cell proliferation, survival of CD8 + memory T cells, and maintenance of chronic inflammatory responses within salivary glands5. Previous studies demonstrated increased IL-15 expression both in salivary gland tissue and serum of SjD patients, suggesting a potential role in disease activity and lymphocytic organization6. Monocyte chemoattractant protein 1 (MCP-1/CCL2) was included because of its capacity to recruit monocytes and macrophages into inflamed tissues and its reported association with salivary gland infiltration and germinal center formation7. Similarly, CXCL13, BAFF, IL-21, IL-23, and CCL19/MIP-3B were selected due to their established role in B-cell activation, lymphoid neogenesis, and adaptive immune-cell trafficking in SjD8.

We also investigated soluble inflammatory receptors involved in immune regulation and cytokine signaling. Tumor necrosis factor receptor 2 (TNFRII) was selected because TNF-related pathways have been associated with epithelial injury, T-cell activation, and systemic inflammation in SjD9. Likewise, IL-1RII was included due to its role as a decoy receptor capable of modulating IL-1–mediated inflammatory signaling and maintaining the balance between pro-inflammatory Th17 responses and immune regulation. Alterations in these pathways may reflect dysregulated immune homeostasis in SjD patients10.

In addition, we evaluated matrix metalloproteinase-8 (MMP-8) which is involved in extracellular matrix degradation and tissue remodeling. Although matrix metalloproteinases have been extensively studied in periodontal disease and chronic inflammatory conditions, their role in SjD remains poorly characterized. Given the relevance of glandular tissue damage, epithelial remodeling, and stromal-immune interactions in SjD pathogenesis, we hypothesized that MMP-8 might represent a marker of inflammatory tissue remodeling and glandular injury11.

Finally, additional cytokines implicated in Th1-, Th17-, and interferon-mediated immune responses, including IFN-α2, IL-6, IL-7, IL-12, IL-13, IL-17 A, TNF-α, TSLP, and IL-33, were analyzed to provide a broader characterization of inflammatory pathways potentially involved in SjD12. By simultaneously evaluating a wide panel of soluble inflammatory mediators, we aimed to identify molecular profiles associated with disease manifestations and to explore novel candidate biomarkers potentially useful for patient stratification and precision medicine approaches in SjD.

Methods

A cohort of consecutive patients with SjD diagnosed according to the 2016 ACR/EULAR criteria13 were enrolled. Age- and sex-matched healthy participants were included as controls. Blood samples were collected in EDTA tubes and centrifuged at the end of the coagulation period (30–60 min) for 15 min at approximately 2,000 × g at room temperature. The serum was then aliquoted and stored at -80 °C. Prior to analysis, the samples were thawed at room temperature.

Analysis of a panel of 23 molecules was performed using the Bio-Plex technology (Bio-Rad Laboratories), namely CCL2/MCP-1, IP-10/CXCL10, Flt3 Ligand, IFNa2, IL6, IL7, IL12, IL13, IL15, IL17A, TNFa, IL-1Ra, CXCL13, IL21, IL23, IL33, TSLP, IL22Ra, IL1RII, TNFRII, BAFF, CCL19/MIP-3B, and MMP-8, using the Luminex® multiplex immunoassay (Bio-Rad BioPlex 200 System - Luminex x-Map technology, R&D Systems, USA) according to the manufacturer’s instructions and customized procedures. These molecules were chosen among most promising following a revision of the literature performed by MN and CP. Each sample was run in triplicate in accordance with the protocol of the manufacturer. A regression formula was used to calculate the sample concentrations from the standard curves. Data were analyzed by using either spline curve-fitting method as recommended by the manufacturer. Type of curve-fitting method was chosen for each molecule with respect to the lowest residual variance (< 5%). Clinical data were collected, including age at diagnosis and at enrolment, history of xerophthalmia, xerostomia, extraglandular manifestations according to ESSDAI items, lymphoproliferative disorders, presence of Raynaud’s phenomenon, purpura and recurrent swelling of the parotid glands. Moreover, the following disease-specific laboratory serum markers were collected: blood count, C3 and C4 levels, protein electrophoresis, rheumatoid factor (RF, measured by nephelometry), anti-nuclear antibodies (ANA, measured by indirect immunofluorescence), anti-Ro/SSA, anti-La/SSB antibodies (measured by immunoblotting), and cryoglobulins.

Current and previous therapies were recorded, including glucocorticoids (GC) (prednisone equivalents), immunosuppressive agents (IS), and hydroxychloroquine. Disease activity was assessed using ESSDAI and disease related symptoms by EULAR Sjögren’s Syndrome Patient Reported Index (ESSPRI)14,15.

Each SjD patient underwent minor salivary gland biopsy at diagnosis. The grade of lymphocytic infiltration was evaluated by the Focus Score (FS) which corresponds to the mean number of foci in an area of tissue of 4 mm213.

The study was conducted in accordance with principles of the Declaration of Helsinki, and approval was granted by the Regional Ethics Committee of Umbria (3780/19). Each patient provided written informed consent.

Statistical analysis

The D’Agostino-Pearson’s test to assess data distribution, followed by the application of inferential tests appropriated for data distribution. The Mann-Whitney test or Student’s t-test were used accordingly; correlation analyses were conducted using Pearson’s or Spearman’s tests. To assess the discriminatory power of individual cytokines in differentiating SjD patients from healthy controls, we performed a multivariate logistic regression analysis using standardized cytokine values. First, cytokine concentrations were standardized (z-scores) to account for differences in measurement scales among variables. Multicollinearity between predictors was assessed using variance inflation factors, and no significant multicollinearity was identified. A model including MCP-1, IL-1RII, TNFRII, and MMP-8 was first applied to the full dataset (N = 81). Model performance was assessed using the area under the receiver operating characteristic (ROC) curve (AUC), and variable importance was evaluated through standardized coefficients. A two-tailed P value ≤ 0.05 was considered significant. Data were analysed using GraphPad Prism V. 8 (GraphPad Software, Inc.).

Results

Forty patients with SjD and 41 healthy participants were analysed. Demographic, clinical, and laboratory characteristics of the two groups are represented in Table 1. Patients with SjD were mainly females (97.5%), while the control group was entirely composed by females. The mean age was 54.2 ± 16.6 years in the SjD group and 57.6 ± 12.8 years in the control group (P = ns). Nearly three quarters of SjD patients complained about sicca symptoms (32/40, 80%) and about half reported articular symptoms (18/40, 45%) and extra-glandular organ involvement (18/40, 45%).

Soluble inflammatory mediators in SjD patients and healthy controls

The analysis of the 23-molecules panel demonstrated significant differences between SjD patients and healthy controls in 5 of these (Table 2). Specifically, significantly higher mean levels of IL-15 were observed in SjD patients (11.52 ± 24.95 pg/ml) compared to controls (1.37 ± 1.26 pg/ml, P = 0.0009) (Fig. 1a). Similarly, levels of MCP-1 (683.6 ± 1127 vs. 654.8 ± 608.9 pg/ml, P = 0.0203) (Fig. 1b), TNFRII (8588 ± 2889 vs. 5505 ± 1007 pg/ml, P < 0.0001) (Fig. 1c), and MMP-8 (6382 ± 12697 vs. 6247 ± 6950 pg/ml, P = 0.0141) (Fig. 1d) were significantly higher in SjD than in the control group. Conversely, IL-1RII levels in the SjD population were significantly reduced in comparison to healthy controls (6267 ± 1699 vs. 8514 ± 2255 pg/ml, P < 0.0001) (Fig. 1e).

Associations with clinical and laboratory features

The analysis of the association between all tested molecules and disease-related clinical and serologic features showed that IL-15 levels were significantly higher in patients with monoclonal component compared to those without (25.46 ± 32.38 vs. 6.69 ± 20.4 pg/ml, P = 0.003) (Fig. 2a). Similarly, elevated IL-15 levels were detected in patients with hypergammaglobulinemia (15.54 ± 30 vs. 4.7 ± 10.3 pg/ml, P = 0.005) (Fig. 2b). The focus score values correlated with MCP-1 levels (P = 0.049, r = 0.273) (Fig. 3a). Indeed, patients with a FS > 1 exhibited significantly higher serum levels of MCP-1 (792.2 ± 1230 vs. 235.9 ± 240.3 pg/ml, P = 0.02) (Fig. 3b). The levels of MCP-1 also correlated with the severity of sicca symptoms (P = 0,048, r = 0,392, Fig. 3c). No significant correlations were observed between circulating MMP-8 levels and total ESSDAI score, individual ESSDAI domains, or articular involvement.

To evaluate the predictive value of selected cytokines in discriminating SjD from controls, we performed a multivariate logistic regression using concentrations of MCP-1, IL-1RII, TNFRII, and MMP-8 (Table 3). The analysis revealed that TNFRII was the most robust independent predictor, showing a strong inverse association with case status (standardized β = − 3.08, OR = 0.046, p = 0.0003). IL-1RII also emerged as a significant positive predictor (β = +1.50, OR = 4.49, p = 0.0029). The model confirms that TNFRII and IL-1RII are the strongest independent predictors of case status, while IL-15 and MCP-1 do not retain significance when included alongside the other markers. The overall discriminative performance of the model was excellent, with a receiver operating characteristic (ROC) curve yielding an area under the curve (AUC) of 0.89, indicating high sensitivity and specificity in distinguishing SjD cases from healthy controls (Fig. 5).

Discussion

In this study, we evaluated the levels of 23 potentially pathogenic molecules in 40 patients affected by SjD in comparison to a control group of healthy participants. Circulating molecules significantly contribute to the pathogenesis of SjD, being responsible for both the initiation and perpetuation of the autoimmune process. Moreover, they could potentially be used as biomarkers for the diagnosis and management of this disease16. In this study, we found significant differences in circulating levels of five molecules (IL-15, MCP-1, IL-RII, TNF-RII, and MMP-8) between SjD patients and healthy participants.

Firstly, we confirmed that patients with SjD exhibit higher levels of IL-15. A previous study conducted by Sisto et al. had already identified IL-15 as a potential target molecule in SjD. This molecule is a pro-inflammatory cytokine which is expressed both by monocytes and dendritic cells, although it is also known as a T-cell growth factor. Indeed, IL-15 induces migration of T-cells and proliferation of CD8 + memory T cells which may explain their higher expression levels in patients with germinal centres17. Sisto et al. found increased expression of this cytokine in the salivary glands of a group of patients affected by SjD. They also suggested its use as a laboratory parameter useful to assess disease activity17. Additionally, increased levels of IL-15 have been observed in rheumatoid arthritis (RA) and other autoimmune conditions such as celiac disease, inflammatory bowel disease, and psoriasis18. Interestingly, in our cohort, we found that IL-15 levels were higher in patients with hypergammaglobulinemia/monoclonal component. The involvement of this cytokine is in the Ras/MAPK signalling to prevent cell death and increased production may lead to enhanced autoreactivity and survival of T lymphocytes19.

Recent bioinformatic and translational studies further support a potential pathogenic role for IL-15 in SjD. Liu et al. identified IL-15 as one of four key inflammation-associated biomarkers significantly overexpressed in SjD across multiple independent transcriptomic datasets, together with LY6E, EIF2AK2, and CXCL10. Importantly, IL-15 expression was associated with interferon-related immune pathways and alterations in immune-cell infiltration, suggesting that IL-15 may contribute to the maintenance of chronic immune activation in SjD20.

In parallel, IL-15 plays a central role in sustaining tissue-resident memory (TRM) T-cell survival and function through JAK/STAT-dependent signaling pathways. In the context of SjD, it was proposed that IL-15-driven TRM-cell persistence within salivary glands may promote local cytotoxicity, epithelial injury, and progressive glandular dysfunction, thereby contributing to sicca manifestations and chronic tissue inflammation21

Our study confirmed that MCP-1 is present at higher levels in patients affected by SjD than in healthy controls22. MCP-1/CCL2 is a chemokine that regulates the migration and infiltration of monocytes and macrophages into tissues23. Mechanistic evidence supporting the pro-inflammatory role of the CCL2/CCR2 axis derives from experimental studies showing that mesenchymal stromal cells can promote myeloid-cell differentiation through CCL2-dependent signaling pathways, thereby potentially contributing to the maintenance of chronic inflammatory niches and monocyte/macrophage accumulation observed in autoimmune tissues, including Sjögren’s disease24. The binding between MCP-1 and CCR2 receptor, facilitated by IFN-γ, perpetuates the infiltration of monocytes and macrophages into the salivary glands of patients with SjD inducing their proliferation and maintenance of pro-inflammatory status25. Iwamoto et al. demonstrated increased MCP-1 expression in salivary gland cells in SjD patients, especially in those with germinal centres25. Indeed, accumulating evidence supports a relevant pathogenic role for MCP-1/CCL2 in SjD, particularly in sustaining immune-cell recruitment and the inflammatory salivary gland microenvironment. Experimental studies have demonstrated that activation of MyD88-dependent Toll-like receptor pathways in salivary tissue induces MCP-1 production, which contributes to local inflammatory cytokine secretion and chronic glandular inflammation26. Notably, spontaneous salivary gland production of MCP-1 was shown to depend on MyD88 signaling in murine models of SjD, supporting a mechanistic link between innate immune activation and chemokine-driven tissue damage26. More recent data suggest that salivary gland epithelial cells themselves actively contribute to MCP-1–mediated inflammation. Chivasso et al. demonstrated that hyperosmotic stress, a condition likely associated with dysfunctional salivary secretion in pSS, markedly increased CCL2/MCP-1 expression in salivary gland epithelial cells27. Moreover, serum CCL2 levels correlated with systemic disease activity (ESSDAI), biological activity, and cutaneous involvement, suggesting that MCP-1 may also reflect disease severity and systemic immune activation27. In parallel, spatial transcriptomic analyses have recently identified a pathogenic CCL2^high fibroblast–ACKR1^high endothelial cell axis within lymphoid foci of Sjögren’s disease salivary glands. This inflammatory niche was strongly associated with immune-cell infiltration, interferon responses, and B/T-cell activation, indicating that MCP-1 may participate in the spatial organization of immune-stromal crosstalk sustaining chronic glandular inflammation28. Interestingly, although one clinical study reported reduced tear CCL2 concentrations in patients with SjD compared with healthy controls, lower CCL2 levels still correlated with objective ocular damage parameters, highlighting the complex compartment-specific regulation of this chemokine in exocrine tissues29. For the first time, our study showed an association between MCP-1 levels and sicca symptoms and a correlation between MCP-1 levels and FS. Patients with FS > 1 at the salivary gland biopsy were those with higher MCP-1 levels. Thus, we suggest that MCP-1 could represent a marker of more severe glandular involvement in SjD patients.

TNF is a cytokine whose actions are mediated by TNF receptor 1 (TNF-RI) and TNF receptor 2 (TNF-RII). TNF-RII seems to be involved in the signaling of CD4 + T-cells, increasing resistance to regulatory T cells (Treg)-mediated suppression; moreover, it seems to be capable of enhancing T cell receptor (TCR)-dependent activation of both CD4 and CD8 T cells by increasing IL-2R levels30. While Szodoray et al. have documented reduced levels of TNF-RII in SjD patients, particularly those with extraglandular manifestations, our study observed higher levels of this cytokine in the SjD group than in the control group31. Once again, the small sample size in the study by Szodoray et al. and the different demographic characteristics of the populations studied might explain these opposite results. Consistent with our results, Loureiro-Amigo et al. observed slightly elevated TNF-RII levels in SjD patients, particularly those with an ESSDAI > 0 32. Similarly, Nishikawa et al. suggested a correlation between TNF-RII levels and the biological domains of ESSDAI. Still, higher levels of TNF-RII might be associated with vascular and epithelial injury of the salivary glands33.

Matrix metalloproteinases, calcium-dependent and zinc-containing endopeptidases, are involved in the degradation of the extracellular matrix and non-matrix protein such as cytokine34. Among them, relevant for our study is MMP-8 which has been previously studied by Konttinen et al., suggesting its expression by the interstitial or vascular structures of the salivary gland only in a small percentage of patients35. Our study is the first to highlight an increase in circulating levels of MMP-8 in SjD patients. Indeed, it is interesting that higher expression of MMP-8 and MMP-9 is considered a valuable serological marker for assessing disease activity in periodontal disease36. Although no available studies have identified significant differences in the periodontal status of SjD patients compared with control group, it is likely that reduction of saliva flow experimented by these patients might be related to higher incidence of periodontitis37. In this context, further research is needed to assess a possible correlation between the expression of MMP-8 and the increased occurrence of periodontitis. Therefore, elevated MMP-8 levels may reflect systemic inflammatory tissue remodeling rather than a specific clinical phenotype.

IL-1, in its two isoforms (IL-1α and IL-1β), is a pivotal cytokine for local and systemic inflammation which expresses its role binding with two functional receptors, IL-1RI and IL1-RII. While IL-1RI is ubiquitously expressed, IL-1RII is restrictedly expressed by monocytes, neutrophils, B cells and some T cells38. Moreover, some studies have hypothesized that this cytokine might be involved in maintaining the balance between Th17 and T reg cells: a reduced expression of IL-1RII seems to be central for the pathogenesis of Th17-related disorders, such as SjD39. In fact, it is known that IL-1β facilitates binding of IL-1RI to the accessory protein IL-1RAcP, which in turn activates the NF-kB and MAPK pathways, leading to an inflammatory response and activation of cellular immunity. The downregulation of IL-1RI is mediated by IL-1RII, which in combination with the IL-1 antagonist (IL-1Ra), acts as a silencing protein for pro-inflammatory signal transmission. Bardsen et al. have demonstrated that the expression of this cytokine in the cerebrospinal fluid of SjD patients could contribute to modulating the onset of fatigue which is induced by IL-1β40. It is plausible that reduced levels of sIL-1RII observed in the present study are permissive for an increased IL-1β-mediated inflammatory response.

Beyond its immunoregulatory role as a decoy receptor, increased circulating IL-1RII may also have potential translational implications. Previous studies have demonstrated that IL-1-related signaling contributes to fatigue generation and sickness behavior in primary SjD, while Toll-like receptor-mediated innate immune activation represents a central upstream mechanism driving IL-1-dependent inflammatory pathways in connective tissue diseases. In this context, IL-1RII could potentially serve as a biomarker to distinguish patients with predominant inflammatory activity from those characterized mainly by fatigue and subjective symptom burden despite limited objective inflammatory manifestations41.

Finally, our multivariate modeling consistently identified TNFRII and IL-1RII as the strongest independent predictors of SjD. These findings highlight the potential utility of these cytokines as biomarkers for disease classification or risk stratification and align with prior evidence implicating TNF and IL-1 signaling pathways in the pathogenesis of SjD42.

Several limitations should be acknowledged. The monocentric design and limited sample size may reduce generalizability. The cross-sectional nature of the study precludes causal inference and longitudinal assessment of biomarker fluctuations. In addition, multiple comparisons were not adjusted because of the exploratory design, and therefore the findings should be interpreted cautiously pending external validation.

Conclusion

This study confirmed increased serum levels of IL-15, TNF-RII, and MCP-1 in patients with SjD compared with healthy controls and additionally identified, for the first time, increased circulating MMP-8 together with reduced sIL-1RII levels. Among the analyzed molecules, TNFRII, IL-1RII, and MMP-8 emerged as the variables most strongly associated with SjD in multivariate analysis, supporting their potential relevance as biomarkers of disease-related immune dysregulation. These findings reinforce the role of innate immune activation and inflammatory signaling pathways in the pathophysiology of SjD. Although the cross-sectional design and the lack of longitudinal assessment limit conclusions regarding causality and disease progression, the multiplex evaluation of cytokines and chemokines allowed a comprehensive characterization of inflammatory patterns associated with SjD. Further prospective studies in larger and independent cohorts are warranted to validate these findings and to clarify the potential clinical utility of these biomarkers for disease stratification and monitoring.
