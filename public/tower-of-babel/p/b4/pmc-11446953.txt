Optimization and pharmacological evaluation of phytochemical-rich Cuscuta reflexa seed extract for its efficacy against chlorpyrifos-induced hepatotoxicity in murine models
Authors: Tahira Batool, Sobia Noreen, Fozia Batool, Gamal A Shazly, Shahid Iqbal, Ali Irfan, Saira Batool, Shazia Akram Ghumman, Maryam Basharat, Sara Hasan, Samina Aslam, Yousef A Bin Jardan
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
AbstractThe popular organophosphorus (OP) compound chlorpyrifos (CP) has recently gained significant attention due to its health risks, particularly among farmers exposed to OP pesticides. This study aimed to evaluate the acute toxicity of Cuscuta reflexa seed extract (CRSE) and its efficacy of mitigating the adverse effects of CP in albino male mice. For acute toxicity analysis, the first group was served as the control group, while the second group was received CRSE (200 mg/kg/bw) on the first day of the 14-day experiment. For hepatotoxicity analysis, the first group was the control group, the second group (vehicle control) received corn oil (CO) (2 mL/kg/bw), the third group was given CP (20 mg/kg/bw) dissolved in corn oil and the fourth group was given CP (20 mg/kg/bw) along-with CRSE (200 mg/kg/bw) orally via gavage once daily for 21 days. The acute toxicity examination revealed no statistically significant differences between the CRSE-treated and control groups in serum biochemical indicators and histopathological analyses of various organs, suggesting that CRSE as safe at a dosage of 200 mg/kg/bw, with an oral LD50 in mice higher than 200 mg/kg. The hepatotoxicity study demonstrated that the CP administration resulted in liver damage and oxidative stress, while CRSE acted as an antioxidant and attenuated the signs of oxidative stress in liver damage. Hence, a promising therapeutic approach for lowering CP hepatotoxicity is co-treatment with CRSE.Keywords: Cuscuta reflexa seed extract, Hepatotoxicity, Oxidative stress, Pesticides, Antioxidants, Serum biochemical markersSubject terms: Biochemistry, Biological techniques, Drug discovery, Plant sciences, Environmental sciences, Biomarkers, Molecular medicine, Chemistry

Introduction

Pesticides are used frequently and massively around the world, particularly in underdeveloped countries. They have immensely benefited mankind by ameliorating crop yields and agricultural productivity1. Several chemical classes may be used to categorize pesticides; each class has a unique biological activity and, hence, a unique potential to cause harm to humans and other organisms2. A broad-spectrum organophosphorus pesticide, chlorpyrifos (CP; [O, O-diethyl-o-(3,5,6-trichloro-2-pyridyl) phosphorothionate]), a heterogeneous class of agricultural chemicals, is frequently employed in agriculture3. It is effective against two common household pests, mosquitoes, and flies4. Hepatocytes are assumed to be the primary site of CP metabolism, where it is converted to the most hazardous CP metabolite, CP oxon (oxygen analog)5. Innumerable adverse outcomes have been linked to both immediate and prolonged exposure to it, including impairment of liver function, immunological abnormalities, hematoxicity, neurotoxicity, embryotoxicity, and genotoxicity6–9. Several publications have revealed the involvement of oxidative stress, inflammation, and cell death in CP toxicity10, even though the precise mechanism is still uncertain. To put it simply, oxidative stress takes place when the body’s antioxidant defence capability is outweighed after the production of reactive oxygen species (ROS)11. Hydroxyl radicals and hydrogen peroxide are examples of ROS that may interact with biological molecules, leading to membrane and tissue damage12. There is evidence that many pesticides cause damage to membranes by generating lipid peroxidation (LPO)13. This is because many pesticides are hydrophobic compounds that readily get attached to biological membranes, particularly phospholipids bilayers.

Nutritional compounds (glycogens, protein, and fat) and a wide range of intrinsic and extrinsic chemicals (pesticides, metals, and drugs) are metabolized in the liver14. The liver is especially vulnerable to chemically induced toxicity and is vulnerable to a broad variety of disorders because of the development of metabolites which may be marginally or significantly detrimental than the primary compound15. Because of its portal blood supply, the liver is the initial primary organ to interact with the ingested toxins, after the gastrointestinal tract. This first run through the body’s bloodstream may be effective at filtering out some toxins, protecting other organs but increasing the risk of hepatic damage16. The impact of any agent on the liver, which results in a divergence from normal function and morphology and implies chemical, pharmacological, or microbial-driven liver impairment, can be characterized as hepatotoxicity17. The hepatocytes, biliary epithelial cells, and/or hepatic vasculature may be harmed as a direct consequence of the toxicity of the initial chemical and/or a reactive metabolite or as a result of an immune-mediated reaction18. Exogenous molecules of therapeutic significance are known as hepatotoxicants, and they may come in the form of microcystins, overdoses of certain pharmaceuticals, or even natural chemicals like those found in herbs and supplements19. The extensiveness of liver disorder and the associated death rate have both emerged as major worldwide medical concerns. By 2030, the WHO expects it to be the eleventh major reason for death worldwide20. The effects of xenobiotics on the liver should be observed and interpreted, and measures to protect the liver against toxicity should be researched, for these reasons. Conventional toxicity studies commonly employ clinical biochemistry measures such as total cholesterol, enzymes, lipids, and proteins to assess liver toxicity. Additionally, they evaluate the liver’s relative weight and the presence of more than two enzymes, including alanine aminotransferase, aspartate aminotransferase, alkaline phosphatase, and lactate dehydrogenase, that indicate hepatocellular effects21.

Several investigations have shown the prophylactic and therapeutic effectiveness of plants as hepatoprotective agents, and this practice dates back to ancient times22. Consequently, natural antioxidants derived from plants have recently received a lot of interest for their protective properties against CP-induced oxidative processes and consequent adverse effects by extinguishing reactive oxygen species (ROS)23. One of the herbal therapeutic plants, Cuscuta reflexa (CR), is often referred to as “amarvela” or “akashbel” in the local vernacular24. Giant dodder is an angiospermic, parasitic, leafless, epiphytic plant of the family Cuscutaceae. It is widespread across Thailand, as well as in Pakistan, western Bengal, the lowlands of India, Ceylon, and Bangladesh25. It’s a perennial parasitic climber on host plants and is bright yellow in color. Because it lacks chlorophyll, CR must penetrate its hosts and connect to its xylem and phloem to obtain water and complex nutrients like sugars and amino acids. To do this, it uses specialized branching organs called haustoria to entwine and cling to its victims26. The names “beggar weed,” “hell weed,” “devil’s gut,” “strangle tare,” and “scald weed” all refer to this plant. However, it possesses remarkable therapeutic capabilities while being destructive to the plants it inhabits and relies on for survival27. Jaundice, gout, bodily pains, constipation, flatulence, and liver diseases are all treated with CR extract in traditional medicine28. Species of the genus Cuscuta have cancer-fighting and immune-boosting properties. There is evidence that it shows anti-inflammatory, antipyretic, pesticidal, and antiviral activities24. It has several medicinal purposes, including those of a purgative, anti-itch salve, and fever prolonger. The seeds have a bitter terrible taste, and are sedative, emmenagogue, and diuretic, the infusion is administered in ophthalmia, and the decoction in biliousness as a purgative (Unani). They are also effective in disorders of the liver and the spleen, quartan fever, chronic fevers, griping, hiccoughs, purify the blood, and cleanse the intestines29. It may also help with conditions including schizophrenia, intestinal worms, diseases caused by an excess of melanin, bronchial obstruction, eye infections, cough, splenomegaly, eczema, constipation, heart disease, epilepsy, and paralysis. The antimicrobial, antisteroidogenic, diaphoretic, and demulcent characteristics are exhibited by the methanolic extract of CR. Phytochemical investigation exposed the presence of flavonoids, alkaloids, glycosides, cuscutalin, cuscutin, steroids, and amarvelin, with flavonoids being the fundamental active chemical component and responsible for the plant’s sedative effects30.

The objective of this investigation was to explore the Bioactive-enriched fractionation of Cuscuta reflexa seeds extract (CRSE) using liquid-liquid partitioning (solvent-solvent partitioning) and optimizing the extraction conditions using response surface methodology (RSM) and artificial neural networking (ANN) followed by the identification and quantification of possibly novel bioactive compounds by the determination of antioxidant potential in addition to the primary focus to evaluate the hepatotoxicity of CP and ameliorating efficacy of an extract of Cuscuta reflexa seeds in albino male mice by measuring body weight, several serum biochemical parameters including Liver functioning tests (LFTs), cholesterol, albumin, and histopathological findings. Another objective was to examine the potential toxicity of CRSE by acute oral administration. The outcomes of the mammalian model gave rise to optimism that it would prove to be a viable therapeutic option for humans.

Materials and methods

Chemicals

Folin–ciocâlteu (FC) reagent, sodium carbonate, dimethyl sulfoxide, potassium acetate, aluminum chloride, gallic acid, ascorbic acid, ferric chloride, potassium ferricyanide, trichloroacetic acid, phosphate buffer, quercetin, chlorpyrifos (CP), corn oil, chloroform, formaldehyde, and solvents including methanol, ethanol, and n-hexane were used. The bioactive-enriched fraction was extracted and the solutions were prepared using deionized water. Unless otherwise stated, all chemicals and reagents utilized were bought from E. Merck or Sigma-Aldrich and were of analytical quality.

Plant material collection and pretreatment

The plant material was chosen because of it has a long history of medical use and is currently receiving a lot of attention from scientists who are interested in learning more about its pharmacological properties. Seeds of Cuscuta reflexa (CR) were collected from widely growing wild trees in Southern Punjab, Pakistan, and were sold in local herbal stores. For the present study, seeds were purchased from the Chinioti Davakhana, Sargodha-Pakistan, in December 2020 and were validated by taxonomist Prof. Amin Ullah Shah from the University of Sargodha’s Department of Botany. For further reference, a voucher specimen (No. UOS-PA-21-15) was deposited at the University of Sargodha’s herbarium. The seeds were first washed with fresh water to remove dust particles and then washed again in deionized water to remove any remaining contaminants. Using a commercial grinder, the shadow-dried seeds were reduced to a powder. The samples were kept in polythene bags sealed with airtight seals and kept at room temperature until further examination.

Extraction of bioactive enriched fraction

Seed powder of Cuscuta reflexa was extracted with a 1:10 ratio of 80% ethanol. The mixture was filtered through Whatman paper after it was shaken on an orbital shaker for the time intervals (X1) 2, 4, and 6 h, while the rotation speed (X2) was varied between 100, 150, and 200 rpm and the temperature (X3) was varied between 30, 40, and 50 °C, respectively. A water bath was used to dehydrate the filtrate after three days of continuous extraction. After treating the filtrate with 70% n-hexane to isolate the bioactive-enriched fraction, the mixture was dried, weighed, and the percentage yield (w/w) was calculated before being kept for further analysis.

Experimental design and optimization

A 3-level/3-factor central composite design (CCD) consisting of 21 runs was used to assess the impact of independent variables on the extraction yield. Independent variables, namely time (hours), speed (rpm), and temperature (°C), were selected based on literature and preliminary trials. Triplicate measurements were taken for the percent yield, and the mean values were subjected to regression analysis. Design Expert version 13 was used for predictive modeling and to identify optimized conditions for extraction. Response surface and contour plots generated from response surface methodology helped visualize the interplay of parameters and their combined effects on extraction yield. Assessment of antioxidant potential of CRSE

Total phenolic content (TPC) determination

Total phenolic content (TPC) was calculated using the Folin-Ciocalteu (FC) reagent. For this purpose, a crude extract of Cuscuta reflexa seeds (CRSE 1), as well as a phenolic-rich extract of Cuscuta reflexa seeds (CRSE 2) (1 mg/mL), was dissolved in DMSO (dimethyl sulfoxide). Following a 5-minute incubation period, a reaction mixture containing CRSE (0.1 mL) and FC (0.25 mL) reagent was formed. Following incubation, a solution of 20% Na2CO3 (1.5 mL) was supplemented to the reaction container. The subsequent volume of the mixture was then adjusted to 5 mL using distilled water. Blank was also prepared along with, and treated in the same way as the sample and standard, with the only difference that CRSE was not added to it. After incubation for 2 h, the spectrophotometer (UV-1700 Pharmaspec Shimadzu, Kyoto, Japan) measured the absorbance of the mixture at 760 nm. The study’s findings were reported as gallic acid equivalent (GAE) in µg GAE/mg of extract as the Gallic acid (GA) was used as standard. The experiment was executed in triplicate33.

In this context, y belongs to the absorbance, whereas x is the quantity of gallic acid measured in micrograms (µg).

Maintenance of experimental animals

Albino male mice were used to investigate hepatoprotective effects and acute toxicity. Thirty mice (n = 30) were obtained from the Animal House of the University of Veterinary and Animal Sciences (UVAS) in Lahore, Pakistan. The mice were 6 to 8 weeks old and weighed 30 ± 5 g. The experimental animals were maintained in polypropylene cages equipped with paddy husks. They were subjected to a temperature range of 25 ± 5 °C and a 12-hour day/night cycle at the animal house located at the University of Sargodha (UOS), Sargodha. The mice were chosen at random, labeled so that they could be identified, and acclimatized to the lab environment for 7 days before being dosed. The mice were given free access to standard commercial food and water. All in vivo study experiments were authorized by the institutional committee on animal ethics reference no (SU/ORIC/1395) before they were conducted. ARRIVE guidelines approved by the University of Sargodha were followed in all animal care and handling.

Experimental design

Acute toxicity analysis

Ten albino male mice ranging in weight from 30 ± 4 g and in age from 6 to 8 weeks were chosen at random. Prior to the trial, the experimental animals were housed for four days under normal standard environmental conditions. Mice were haphazardly distributed into two groups for the acute toxicity testing. Group I served as the control and was fed a regular diet, whereas Group II was given 200 mg/kg/bw CRSE via oral gavage once in the whole 14 days of trial36. Mice had access to water ad libitum but were fasted for 2–4 h before being dosed. Each animal was housed in a metal cage and observed after treatment for the first five minutes for any indication of regurgitation. Then, each was carefully monitored for behavioral indications of toxicity including alteration in hair, eyes, skin, respiratory, mucous membranes, motor activity, autonomic, circulatory, and central nervous systems, salivation, tremors, convulsion, diarrhoea, urinary incontinence, sluggishness, or sleep, including mortality, every 15 min for the first 4 h after the dose, every 30 min for 6 h, and every day for the next 48 h. Animals were monitored for fourteen days to determine their likelihood of long-term lethality. On the first and fourteenth days of the investigation, the weight of the mice was tracked and noted. After the treatment period ended, blood was collected from the animals by direct cardiac puncture into tubes containing heparin as an anti-coagulant while they were under chloroform anesthesia to assess the enzyme activities in the serum. After the animals were sacrificed followed by cervical dislocation, the organs were immediately removed for histological analysis of the liver, heart, lungs, and kidneys37.

In vivo CRSE’s activity against hepatotoxicity

Twenty albino male mice, aged 6–8 weeks and weighing 30 ± 4 g, were chosen at random. Weights were recorded and the mice were split into four groups (n = 5)38. The following steps made up the hepatotoxicity induction protocol: Four groups of rats were given oral doses of Chlorpyriphos and CRSE via gavage once per day: Group I (control) was administered a normal diet, Group II (vehicle control) was given corn oil (CO) (2 mL/kg body weight), Group III was dosed 20 mg/kg of CP dissolved in CO, and Group IV received CP + CRSE (20 mg/kg + 200 mg/kg body weight) orally daily through gavage39,40. The duration of the trial was 21 days. Mice were anaesthetized with chloroform and sacrificed followed by cervical dislocation 24 h after the last dosage. After a cardiac puncture, blood was collected in an EDTA vacutainer, subjected to serum separation, and preserved at -20 °C for further biochemical investigation. After sacrifice the liver was immediately removed, washed, weighed, and resected. For histological analysis, the liver was preserved in 10% formaldehyde and slides were prepared according to the method described in “section Statistical analysis”. To monitor the effects of the medication, the weight of each animal was recorded, daily.

Body and organ weights’ measurement

Each animal was weighed on the first and last day of the experiment. After the trial period mice were anesthetized by chloroform to collect blood, then sacrificed after cervical dislocation, and the heart, liver, lungs, and kidneys were removed, weighed, and examined macroscopically. The relative weight of each extracted organ was determined by employing the expression (6)41.

Serum biochemical analysis

Serum was separated from blood via centrifugation at 4000 rpm for 5 min at 4 ºC for biochemical analysis. The serum was then placed in numbered Eppendorf tubes for long-term storage at a temperature of -20 °C. Liver function tests (LFTs) were determined by analyzing serum samples for biochemical markers such as ALT, AST, ALP, total protein, albumin, globulin, bilirubin, and A/G ratio using commercially available assay kits supplied by the Biolabo and Biomerieux companies. The procedures were carried out precisely as described in the kits42. While a semiautomated chemical analyzer (Microlab 300) was used to compile the lipid profile results i.e., triglycerides, cholesterol, high-density lipoprotein (HDL), low-density lipoprotein (LDL), and very low-density lipoprotein (VLDL) levels43.

Histopathological evaluations

Small slices of the liver for hepatotoxicity analysis while those of the liver, heart, lungs, and kidneys for acute toxicity analysis were embedded in paraffin after being fixed in 10% formalin to analyze histopathological alterations, i.e., the varying degrees of liver damage, by creating serial slices of 5 µ thickness, by a rotary microtome (Laborgerate GmbH). Then they were stained with hematoxylin and eosin (H&E) and Masson’s trichrome reagents, and examined under a high-resolution microscope, finally, photomicrographs were taken from a separate slide for every single animal. Digital images of hematoxylin and eosin-stained sections were obtained at 40x magnification utilizing a digital camera of Sony company (Sony DSC-WX800 CyberShot 4 K) mounted on a Labomed FN18 trinocular microscope17.

Statistical analysis

Mean ± SEM (standard error of the mean) was employed to characterize the data (n = 5). The latest version of GraphPad Prism 9.5.1 was used to execute all statistical computing. Statistical analysis relied on the three replicates of each assay. One-way analysis of variance (ANOVA) accompanied by a Post hoc test (Dunnett’s “t” test) was utilized to establish the statistical significance to compare the treatment and control group data. It was decided that a significance level of P < 0.05*, 0.01**, and 0.001*** was necessary.

Results and discussion

Percentage yield

Although solid/liquid extractions are often thought to be the most effective, it has been found that the yield of any applied extraction approach is dependent on numerous aspects, including the nature of the solvents used, the amount of sample used, and the sample’s physical parameters44. In the current study, the extract yield was estimated by comparing the total weight of the CRSE to the total weight of the raw materials (Cuscuta reflexa seeds powder). Since the solvent employed (ethanol 80%) shares similar polarity qualities as most of the components present in CRSE, it plays a role in obtaining maximum percentage yield. In-depth yield analysis showed that polar solvents are superior to non-polar ones for extracting phytochemicals from giant dodder seeds45.

Response surface methodology

The application of a central composite design, a robust statistical method, allowed the simultaneous exploration of interactions among multiple factors and their impact on dependent variables. In the current investigation, a 33-CCD was employed to optimize the extraction process of CRSE. Table 1 outlines the 21 recommended runs generated by software, with time, speed, and temperature as independent variables and % yield as dependent variables. Enhancing the comprehension of the correlation between factors and response, as depicted in Fig. 1, the relationship between response and factors was further explored through ANOVA. The statistical analysis employed DES software, fitting observed values to numerous models, including linear, two-factor interaction, quadratic, and cubic models. Model selection relied on statistical indicators such as correlation coefficient; R2, expected R2, adjusted R2, and PRESS (predicted residual sum of squares). The linear model was found suitable for the % yield. F and p-values for % yield indicated significance, affirming the models’ improved fit. The coefficient of variation values and adequate precision values suggested repeatability and signal sufficiency. The non-significance of lack of fit values for %yield was underscored by the models’ good fitting. Assessment of antioxidant potential of CRSE

Total phenolic content (TPC)

The total phenolic content presented in Fig. 3, as determined from the gallic acid standard curve (R2 = 0.9916), varied between 24.56 and 70.44 µg GAE/mg in CRSE 1, 34.88 to 78.24 µg GAE/mg in CRSE 2, and 43.92 to 89.52 µg GAE/mg in the standard, ordered from lowest to highest concentration46,47, while the total flavonoid content varied between 23.62 and 65.58 µg QCTE/mg in CRSE 1, 32.17 and 73.04 µg QCTE/mg in CRSE 2, and 45.75 and 91.88 µg QCTE/mg in the standard, ordered from lowest to highest concentration48. Because of their ability to form complexes with metals like iron and copper, phenolic compounds possess redox characteristics and can function as antioxidants49. Since their hydroxyl groups help them scavenge free radicals, the total phenolic content may serve as a foundation for quick screening of antioxidant activity. Plant secondary metabolites known as flavonoids including flavones, flavanols, and condensed tannins are antioxidants that rely on the availability of free OH groups, particularly 3-OH. Flavonoids derived from plants exhibit antioxidant properties both in vitro and in vivo. Upon exposure to polyphenols, which are recognized for their ability to reduce metabolites, the FC reagent becomes blue in color. Absorbance is used to quantify color intensity50.

Ferric reducing activity of antioxidants (FRAP)

The assessment of Fe2+ reducing power by CRSE was conducted utilizing the calibration curve of equivalent ascorbic acid per milligrams of extract (AAE) (R2 = 0.9882), which illustrated a reducing power that was dependent on concentration (Fig. 3). The justification for employing ascorbic acid as a standard solution originated from its capability to function as a secondary antioxidant, thereby efficiently eliminating free radicals and impeding chain reactions. A possible indicator of antioxidant activity is the capacity of compounds to undergo a transmutation from Fe3+/ferricyanide complex to Fe2+/ferrous form51. Depending on the extracts’ or compounds’ capability for reduction, the FRAP assay’s yellow color test solution might turn green or blue. The FRAP values for the present investigation varied from 21.28 to 60.88 µg AAE/mg for CRSE 1, 28.84 to 79.6 µg AAE/mg for CRSE 2, and 47.24 to 93.76 µg AAE/mg for the standard, moving from the lowest to the highest concentration. Results from the antioxidant activity test indicated that as the extract content increased, so did the antioxidant activity. CRSE at its maximum concentration demonstrated the most pronounced antioxidant activity35. Findings indicate that the FRAP antioxidant test is simple, repeatable, and concentration-dependent in a linear fashion.

Acute toxicity studies and the impact of CRSE; clinical indications

There were no indications of toxicity, morbidity, or mortality attributed to the extract in either the control group or the group treated with CRSE throughout the fourteen-day observation period. During the trial period, the mice demonstrated normal fecal consistency and frequent bowel movements, suggesting that they were well-nourished and physically active. Huddling was the only clinical manifestation after CRSE administration. Comparing the body and organ weights of the experimental animals to those of the control group, it was observed that all the animals remained alive and in good health until the very last day of the trial. This suggests that the CRSE extract may be safe at a dose of 200 mg/kg/bw and that the oral LD50 in mice may be higher than 200 mg/kg52,53.

Histopathological examination

Liver histological findings among groups of animals receiving various treatments are shown in Fig. 10. Multiple histological criteria revealed the severity of the injuries in the different treatment groups. Histological evidence backed the protective efficacy of CRSE. Histological analysis of the livers of control mice using a combination of hematoxylin and eosin (H & E) and Masson’s trichrome staining exhibited normal hepatocytes and hepatic lobules with preserved cytoplasm, prominent nuclei, nucleoli, the normal position of the portal vein and bile ducts, unharmed portal spaces, and a hexagonal array in the hepatic plates. Hepatocytes in the livers of mice treated with corn oil looked histopathologically like those in the control group, with normal cell architecture, transparent cytoplasm, and well-integrated hepatic cells. Leukocyte infiltration in the portal region, vacuolations, cytoplasmic vacuolations, and dilated sinusoids, with eosinophilic substances indicative of cellular damage, were all observed in CP-induced mice. Histological changes triggered by CP were blocked by co-supplementation with CRSE, as seen in Fig. 10. Deterioration, fatty change, and coagulative necrosis in hepatocytes diffuse Kupffer cell proliferation in the portal area as well as in between hepatocytes, and dilatation in the central vein were all reduced to a lesser or greater extent in the livers of the CP + CRSE treated group than in the CP treated groups. Our findings were aligned with previously reported studies53,58.

Conclusion

As a conclusion, the current study demonstrated that CP exposure triggers liver injury that is mediated by oxidative stress, inflammation, and cell death. In albino male mice, CP administration resulted in functional and histological changes, as well as oxidative damage and hepatotoxicity.

In contrast, CRSE protected against functional, physical, biochemical, and histological alterations in the liver, suggesting that it reduced oxidative stress. Because of these therapeutic effects of CRSE, CP-induced hepatotoxicity and oxidative damage were attenuated. Our current findings suggest that CRSE may be used as a therapeutic strategy towards toxins-induced hepatotoxicity/hepatic damage with minimal to no adverse effects. It has also been proposed that a dose concentration of CRSE of 200 mg/Kg was proven to be non-toxic to the body. It has already been established that phytochemical components such as alkaloids, flavonoids, glycosides, and sterols possess hepatoprotective action59. The presence of these phytoconstituents in the CRSE is what gives it its hepatoprotective quality. In addition, pure phytoconstituents will need to be separated in the future to particularly investigate the hepatoprotective agent that is present in CRSE.
